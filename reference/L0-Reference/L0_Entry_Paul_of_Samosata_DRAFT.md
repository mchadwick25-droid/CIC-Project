## 2. Dates: episcopate and synods

**Episcopate.** Eusebius, *HE* 7.27.1 dates Paul's accession by synchronism: "About the same time Demetrianus died in Antioch, and Paul of Samosata received that episcopate" — the "same time" being Dionysius's succession to Xystus at Rome. **That synchronism is unusable as given, and the NPNF editor says so in the sharpest terms available to him.** Footnote 2375 on the same page: "Xystus II. was bishop only eleven months, not eleven years… Eusebius' chronology of the Roman bishops of this time is in inextricable confusion." Footnote 2376 supplies the firmer anchor from outside Eusebius — Dionysius of Rome became bishop 22 July 259 (Liberian catalogue), holding office, per Lipsius, until 27 December 268. The editor's own resulting estimate for Paul's accession is **between 257 and 260**.

Smith & Wace's *Dictionary of Christian Biography* (a Doc_00 §4 named Tier A reference work) gives the episcopate as **260–270**, with deposition in **269**. The *New Schaff-Herzog* entry says only that Paul "began expressly to promulgate" his teaching "about 260."

So: the conventional "c. 260" is a modern reconstruction from a corrected Roman episcopal list, not a documented date, and the reference works do not agree with each other on the terminal year. — **Historical Confidence: Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for "c. 260" as the accession; **Documented** for the fact that Eusebius's own synchronism here is defective (the editor states it outright). **Sourcing Provenance: `Primary-Open`** for Eusebius's text; **`Standard-Reference-Open`** for footnote 2375/2376 and for the DCB and Schaff-Herzog date ranges — these are 19th/early-20th-century editorial and reference material, not primary text, and are tagged accordingly rather than absorbed into the primary tier.

**Synods — multiple, and the count is agreed while the dates are not.** Eusebius is explicit that the process was iterative, not a single hearing: *HE* 7.28.2, "When all of these assembled at different times and frequently to consider these matters, the arguments and questions were discussed at every meeting"; and *HE* 7.30.3–4, the synodal letter's own first-person testimony that "Firmilianus came twice and condemned his innovations, as we who were present know and testify." *HE* 7.30.5 then records Firmilian setting out for Antioch a further time, reaching Tarsus, and dying there while the assembled bishops were "calling for him and awaiting his arrival" — a datum that fixes the final synod *after* Firmilian of Caesarea's death.

The date estimates actually on the table, from the sources read this pass:

- **NPNF2 vol. 1 editorial note (fn. 2379):** the synod Dionysius of Alexandria was invited to "was not the last one, at which Paul was condemned, but one of the earlier ones"; and the condemning synod is "ordinarily fixed at 268 or 269, but it cannot have occurred in 269, and probably occurred earlier than 268."
- **NPNF2 vol. 4 editorial note (fn. 3633, on Athanasius *De Syn.* 43):** "There were three Councils held against Paul of Samosata, of the dates of 264, 269, and an intermediate year. The third is spoken of in the text, which contrary to the opinion of Pagi, S. Basnage, and Tillemont, Pearson fixes at 265 or 266."
- **Smith & Wace DCB:** three synods — first in 265 under Firmilian, a second at an uncertain date also under Firmilian, and a third "towards the close of 269," the one at which Malchion conducted the examination.
- **New Schaff-Herzog:** a great synod at Antioch in **264**, a second, and "a third synod, held at Antioch between 266 and 269 (probably in 268)."
- **Modern scholarship** titles its own work either "268" (Giulea 2018) or "268/9" (Lang 2000) — see §5.

**The honest statement.** That there were **multiple synods, most commonly reckoned three, culminating in a condemnation in the late 260s** is well founded; the *specific* year of the final synod is not settled, and the two public-domain reference works read here disagree with each other (269 vs. probably 268) while the NPNF2 vol. 1 editor rules out 269 altogether. "268" is a defensible convention that current specialist literature uses; it should be cited as a convention, not as a documented date. — **Historical Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the multi-synod sequence and a late-260s condemnation; **Contested** for the specific year. **Sourcing Provenance: `Primary-Open`** for the sequence (Eusebius's own text); **`Standard-Reference-Open`** for the competing date estimates.

**Expulsion.** *HE* 7.30.19: Paul refused to give up the church building, "the Emperor Aurelian was petitioned; and he decided the matter most equitably, ordering the building to be given to those to whom the bishops of Italy and of the city of Rome should adjudge it… Thus this man was driven out of the church, with extreme disgrace, by the worldly power." Eusebius supplies no year. Aurelian reigned 270–275, so the expulsion is *after* the deposition by a real interval — the deposed bishop kept physical possession — and the conventional date of **272**, tied to Aurelian's defeat of Zenobia, is a synchronization added by later reconstruction, not something Eusebius states. — **Historical Confidence: Documented** that Aurelian was petitioned and adjudicated as described, and that Paul was removed by imperial rather than ecclesiastical force; **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for the specific year 272. **Sourcing Provenance: `Primary-Open`.**

**Afterlife, and a datable one.** Nicaea, Canon XIX (325), read in full this pass: "Concerning the Paulianists who have flown for refuge to the Catholic Church, it has been decreed that they must by all means be rebaptized; and if any of them who in past time have been numbered among their clergy should be found blameless and without reproach, let them be rebaptized and ordained by the Bishop of the Catholic Church; but if the examination should discover them to be unfit, they ought to be deposed. Likewise in the case of their deaconesses…" The NPNF editorial note identifies the Paulianists as Paul's followers and flags "great difficulty about the text" on manuscript variants. This establishes, from a conciliar document and not from a hostile narrative, that an identifiable Paulianist body with its own clergy and its own women in the diaconal habit still existed and was still being received back **fifty-seven years after the condemnation** — the same order of institutional persistence Syriac Doc_01 §5.5 documents for Bardaisanism. The canon does **not** locate them geographically. — **Historical Confidence: Documented** for the canon's existence and content; **Inferential-Thin** for anything about where the Paulianists actually were. **Sourcing Provenance: `Primary-Open`** for the canon, **`Standard-Reference-Open`** for the editorial note.

## 3. The primary source for the condemnation: what Eusebius actually says

The assignment named *HE* 7.27–30 as the traditional citation range and asked whether it is really there and what it really says. It is there, in NPNF2 vol. 1, at the four URLs in §1, and the range is correct: four consecutive chapters, of which two are narrative and one is a long excerpt of the synod's own encyclical.

- **7.27 — "Paul of Samosata, and the Heresy introduced by him at Antioch."** §1 the succession; §2 the doctrinal charge and the convening. Main-body text, §2: Paul "held, contrary to the teaching of the Church, low and degraded views of Christ, namely, that in his nature he was a common man"; Dionysius of Alexandria was invited but "being unable to come on account of age and physical weakness, he gave his opinion on the subject under consideration by letter"; the other bishops "made haste to assemble at Antioch, as against a despoiler of the flock of Christ."
- **7.28 — "The Illustrious Bishops of that Time."** Names Firmilian of Caesarea in Cappadocia; Gregory and Athenodorus in Pontus; Helenus of Tarsus; Nicomas of Iconium; Hymenaeus of Jerusalem; Theotecnus of Caesarea; Maximus of Bostra. Records the iterative meetings (quoted in §2 above), then Dionysius of Alexandria's death "in the twelfth year of the reign of Gallienus, having held the episcopate of Alexandria for seventeen years," with Maximus succeeding; and the imperial succession Gallienus → Claudius → Aurelian.
- **7.29 — "Paul, having been refuted by Malchion, a Presbyter from the Sophists, was excommunicated."** The examination that produced the deposition: Malchion, "a man learned in other respects, and principal of the sophist school of Grecian learning in Antioch," holding a presbyter's office, "conducted a discussion with him, which was taken down by stenographers," and "was alone able to detect the man who dissembled and deceived the others"; Paul's "false doctrine clearly shown before all, and he was excommunicated from the Catholic Church."
- **7.30 — "The Epistle of the Bishops against Paul."** The encyclical, "To Dionysius and Maximus, and to all our fellow-ministers throughout the world, bishops, presbyters, and deacons" — i.e. to Rome and Alexandria and thence generally — signed by Helenus, Hymenaeus, Theophilus, Theotecnus, Maximus, Proclus, Nicomas, Aelianus, Paul, Bolanus, Protogenes, Hierax, Eutychius, Theodorus, Malchion, and Lucius. Doctrinal charge, §11: Paul "is unwilling to acknowledge that the Son of God has come down from heaven," and "he says 'Jesus Christ is from below.'" §16: "he has scorned the mystery and struts about in the abominable heresy of Artemas." §17: "we have appointed Domnus, who is adorned with all the qualities becoming in a bishop." §19: the Aurelian petition, quoted in §2 above. Alongside the doctrine, the letter carries a heavy load of conduct charges: financial dealings, a raised tribunal and throne, theatrical self-presentation, women in his household, and the suppression of hymns to Christ in favour of songs praising himself.

Three observations that bear on how much weight this range can carry.

**(a) The genre matters and the letter names its own interest.** *HE* 7.30 is not a neutral report; it is the prosecuting synod's own circular, written to secure recognition of a deposition and the installation of a rival. Eusebius transmits it because he agrees with it. The doctrinal charge and the conduct charges arrive fused in a single rhetorical package. This is the identical structural caution Syriac Doc_01 §5.1 applies to Ephrem and Epiphanius on Bardaisan, and Doc_01 §5.1's own methodological authorities (Le Boulluec on the construction of the category "heresy"; Bauer) apply here with equal force.

**(b) It is nonetheless a materially *better* source than what Bardaisan's determination had to work with**, and the difference should be stated because it changes the confidence available. Eusebius's witnesses here are contemporaries and participants writing in the first person — "as we who were present know and testify" — not a heresiologist writing 150 years later from later followers' books. The distance from event to document is effectively nil.

**(c) The two things that would matter most are exactly the two that do not survive.** Paul's own writings: nothing. Malchion's stenographic *Acta*: fragments only. So even at its best, the record is his prosecutors' account of what he said, plus a transcript we mostly don't have of a debate he lost.

— **Historical Confidence: Documented** that Eusebius reports the above and that a synod at Antioch deposed Paul on a Christological charge, appointed Domnus, and later secured the building through Aurelian; **Widely Accepted** that the synodal letter is a near-contemporary participant document. **Sourcing Provenance: `Primary-Open`** throughout this section.

## 4. The Christology as reported, and how confidently it can be attributed to Paul

**What the near-contemporary primary text supports.** From *HE* 7.27.2 and 7.30.11 taken together: Paul held that Christ "in his nature… was a common man"; he was "unwilling to acknowledge that the Son of God has come down from heaven"; his position was summarized by his opponents as "Jesus Christ is from below"; and he was aligned with "the heresy of Artemas" — i.e. placed by the synod in an existing lineage of what later terminology calls adoptionism or dynamic monarchianism. Nothing more technical than that is in Eusebius's own text. — **Historical Confidence: Widely Accepted** that Paul denied the personal, pre-existent descent of the Son and located Christ's origin in the human; **Sourcing Provenance: `Primary-Open`.**

**The fuller, more familiar description — and where it actually comes from.** The account usually given of Paul (the Logos as impersonal divine wisdom or power indwelling a human Jesus, differing from the prophets in degree rather than kind, Christ becoming divine by moral progress) is **not** in Eusebius. It is assembled from three later or secondary layers, which must be kept apart:

1. **Fourth-century witnesses to the debate**, principally Athanasius, *De Synodis* 45, whose report is discussed at §6 below and which supplies one of the few sentences purporting to be Paul's own reasoning.
2. **Nineteenth-century editorial synthesis.** Smith & Wace's DCB gives the full developmental picture — Christ's "pure humanity"; "He was not before Mary, but received from her the origin of His being"; pre-existence only "in the divine foreknowledge"; the Logos indwelling as a quality rather than a person; Christ becoming "worthy of union with God" through "progressive development." The *New Schaff-Herzog* gives the same shape: "The Redeemer is, therefore, human in essence… The union of the Logos with the man Jesus is to be considered an indwelling… the Logos being the greater." **These are reference-work reconstructions, not primary text.** The identical trap caught l0fig001 at review — a Schaff footnote had been quoted as if it were Eusebius. Tagged here as what it is: **`Standard-Reference-Open`**, not `Primary-Open`.
3. **The fragment collections and the *Acta***, on which see §5.

**The attribution problem, stated at the strength the evidence actually supports.** Paul's own words do not survive. Every description of his theology reaches us through people who deposed him, or through fourth-century partisans quoting him inside their own controversy, or through modern reconstructions built from those. The general shape of the charge — a denial that the Son personally and pre-existently descended, with Christ's being originating in the man — is about as securely attested as anything can be in this situation, because it is stated in near-contemporary participant documents, appears consistently across independent fourth-century witnesses who were arguing with each other about its significance, and is corroborated institutionally by Nicaea's need to legislate about a surviving Paulianist clergy. **The technical detail is a different matter and should not be carried at the same confidence as the general shape.** Whether Paul described the Logos as impersonal "wisdom" or "power," whether he taught a progressive divinization, and what he did with the human soul of Christ are reconstructions from contested fragments, and §5 shows that specialists have specifically doubted whether the sharpest formulations are third-century at all. — **Historical Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the general shape; **Contested** for the technical formulations; **Contested** for any specific sentence presented as Paul's own words.

## 5. Does reputable modern scholarship treat the synodal account as reliable?

Four real, named, bibliographically verified items. Each was verified through the publisher's own record; none of the four full texts was read this pass, and §11 says so. **No citation in this section is invented; each carries the access path by which it was verified.**

**Robert L. Sample, "The Christology of the Council of Antioch (268 C.E.) Reconsidered," *Church History* 48, no. 1 (March 1979): 18–26, DOI 10.2307/3163921.** Verified via a Cambridge Core PDF that was openly retrievable this pass. This is the most directly on-point item for the reliability question, and its answer is *not* a simple yes. Sample argues that the bishops opposing Paul did not hold a unified doctrinal position, that calling them "Origenists" is misleading since they show no record of conscious appeal to Origen, and — critically — that the two surviving document-classes must be separated: the *Acta* (the Malchion disputation) and the *Letter of Six Bishops* express significantly different Christological concerns. He engages the fragment-authenticity debate directly, naming Bardy and Loofs as collectors, de Riedmatten on the *Acta*'s authenticity, Schwartz as editor, and **Henry Chadwick on anachronistic language in Malchion's reported statements that reads as inappropriate for third-century theology** — and questions whether Malchion's precise formulations are original or a later redactional layer. — **Historical Confidence: Widely Accepted** that fragment authenticity is a live scholarly problem and that the sharpest formulations attributed to the synod are suspected of later redaction; **Sourcing Provenance: `Standard-Reference-Open`** (openly retrievable publisher PDF; argument summarized, text not quoted at length).

**Uwe Michael Lang, "The Christological Controversy at the Synod of Antioch in 268/9," *Journal of Theological Studies* 51, no. 1 (2000): 54–80, DOI 10.1093/jts/51.1.54, ISSN 0022-5185.** Full bibliographic record verified at the CNRS Pascal-Francis database (`pascal-francis.inist.fr`, record 1562880). The record carries no abstract. From secondary description only, the article works from the fragments of the synod's *Acta* and centres on the substantial union of divinity and humanity and on the soul-body analogy in Christology. — **Historical Confidence: Documented** for the citation itself; **Sourcing Provenance: `Standard-Reference-Open`** for the citation, **`Web-Lead (needs upgrade)`** for the characterization of its argument. Do not cite Lang's position from this entry without reading him.

**Dragoș Andrei Giulea, "Antioch 268 and Its Legacy in the Fourth-Century Theological Debates," *Harvard Theological Review* 111, no. 2 (April 2018): 192–215, DOI 10.1017/S0017816018000056.** Abstract read verbatim at Cambridge Core. Giulea's own stated finding: the synodals "employed the two terms" *ousia* and *hypostasis* "interchangeably to denote the individual entity or person rather than the common essence or nature of the Father and Son," and "defended Christ's divinity before time and simultaneously assumed a certain subordinationism." **Two consequences matter here.** First, Giulea speaks of "the authentic reports preserved from the assembly" — so a current specialist does treat part of the record as authentic, which is why §4's general shape is carried at Widely Accepted and not lower. Second, and more interesting: on Giulea's reading *the condemning bishops were themselves subordinationist to a degree*, which means the 268 synod cannot be read as Nicaea-in-advance, and "Paul was the subordinationist and the synod was the orthodoxy" is not the right frame for what happened. — **Historical Confidence: Documented** for the citation and for the abstract's own wording; **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for Giulea's substantive thesis (one specialist's argued position, published in a leading journal, not a field consensus). **Sourcing Provenance: `Standard-Reference-Open`** for citation and abstract; the article body was not read (Academia.edu returned 403).

**Fergus Millar, "Paul of Samosata, Zenobia and Aurelian: the Church, Local Culture and Political Allegiance in Third-Century Syria," *Journal of Roman Studies* 61 (1971): 1–17, DOI 10.2307/300003.** Citation verified at Cambridge Core. Directly relevant to §7, and worth flagging that **Millar is already a cited authority in the Syriac world's own Doc_01 §3** (*The Roman Near East, 31 BC–AD 337*), so this is the same scholar the Syriac build already relies on, writing on precisely this figure. From the publisher's abstract/preview page only: Millar argues *against* reading Paul as representing native Semitic culture against Hellenism, stresses the region's cultural complexity, is sceptical of a real political alliance between Paul and Zenobia, and situates Paul within Hellenized Syrian Christianity and Roman administrative structures. — **Historical Confidence: Documented** for the citation; **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for the argument as characterized. **Sourcing Provenance: `Standard-Reference-Open`** for the citation; **`Web-Lead (needs upgrade)`** for the argument summary. **Explicit caution: phrasings the preview surfaced must not be re-quoted from this entry as verified quotations of Millar.** The upgrade path is Tier B (JSTOR carries *JRS* vol. 61) or a library copy.

**Verdict on the reliability question.** Modern scholarship does **not** treat the synodal account as straightforwardly reliable, and it does not treat it as fabricated either. The position visible across these four items is more specific than either: the *general* charge against Paul is accepted as historical, part of the record is treated as authentic, and the *precise theological formulations* — especially Malchion's — are under active suspicion of later redaction, with the two document-classes pulling in different directions. — **Historical Confidence: Widely Accepted** that this is the state of the question; **Sourcing Provenance: `Standard-Reference-Open`.**

## 6. *Homoousios* at Antioch: real, citable, and more interesting than the usual version of it

The assignment asked whether there is a real, verifiable connection between the 268 synod and *homoousios*, or whether this is a common misconception. **It is real, it is verifiable in public-domain primary texts, and it is more complicated than the way it is usually told.** Four findings, in order of security.

**6.1 The term does not appear in Eusebius's account.** Independently reconfirmed this pass: *homoousios*, "of one substance," and "consubstantial" appear nowhere in the main body or the footnotes of *HE* 7.30, and nowhere in 7.27. **Whatever happened about the word at Antioch, our near-contemporary source is silent about it.** This is the single most important qualification on the whole story and it is the one most often dropped. — **Historical Confidence: Documented; Sourcing Provenance: `Primary-Open`.**

**6.2 Two independent fourth-century witnesses do report it, and one of them quotes Paul.** Read in full this pass:

*Athanasius, De Synodis* 43 introduces the datum as **his opponents' allegation, not his own report**: "But since, as they allege, the Bishops who condemned the Samosatene have said in writing that the Son is not coessential with the Father…" He then accepts rather than denies it, and in §45 explains it: "For they who deposed the Samosatene took Coessential in a bodily sense, because Paul had attempted sophistry and said, 'Unless Christ has of man become God, it follows that He is Coessential with the Father; and if so, of necessity there are three essences, one the previous essence, and the other two from it;' and therefore guarding against this they said with good reason, that Christ was not Coessential." §45 also gives Athanasius's summary of Paul's position — "the Samosatene held that the Son was not before Mary, but received from her the origin of His being" — and concedes something significant about the synod he is defending: "concerning the Son's Godhead writing in simplicity, they arrived not at accuracy concerning the Coessential." §43 and §45 both refer to the condemning body as "the seventy."

*Basil, Ep.* 52 gives the same substance independently: "the members of the synods which met to discuss the case of Paul of Samosata did find fault with the term as an unfortunate one," because "they maintained that the homoousion set forth the idea both of essence and of what is derived from it, so that the essence, when divided, confers the title of co-essential on the parts into which it is divided" — Basil's own illustration being bronze and coins struck from it, and his own objection that this is impious of Father and Son since there is no substance "anterior or even underlying both."

So the core datum is attested by **two mutually independent fourth-century writers on the pro-Nicene side, one of whom explicitly frames it as something his opponents were pressing on him.** That last point cuts both ways and both should be said: it is *evidence-strengthening*, because the claim's circulation was not in Athanasius's interest and he had to accommodate it rather than invent it; and it is *evidence-weakening*, because the entire attestation lives inside a fourth-century controversy about a fourth-century word, with no third-century document quoted anywhere in it. — **Historical Confidence: Widely Accepted** that the Antiochene bishops who condemned Paul objected to *homoousios* in some sense, and that this was a known, awkward, and argued-over fact by the mid-fourth century; **Contested** for the mechanism. **Sourcing Provenance: `Primary-Open`** for both texts.

**6.3 The ancient explanations of *why* contradict each other, and the reference works say so.** On Athanasius's and Basil's account, Paul did **not** affirm *homoousios* — he deployed it as a *reductio* against his opponents ("if so, of necessity there are three essences"), and the synod refused the word to avoid conceding his premise. On Hilary of Poitiers's account (*De Synodis* 81–86), Paul himself **admitted** the term, in a Sabellian sense of one substance-or-person, and thereby forced the council off the word. These are different events, not different emphases. The *Catholic Encyclopedia* (1913) sets the two side by side and then declines to choose, in a sentence this entry adopts as its own: **"but the question is not clear."** Smith & Wace's DCB independently records that Athanasius endorsed the datum "with some hesitation," noting he had not seen the original documents — which is exactly what *De Syn.* 43's "as they allege" looks like from the inside. — **Historical Confidence: Contested** for the mechanism, and explicitly *not* resolvable from the sources read here; **Sourcing Provenance: `Primary-Open`** for Athanasius, **`Standard-Reference-Open`** for the DCB and *Catholic Encyclopedia* framing, **`Web-Lead (needs upgrade)`** for Hilary's version (see §11 — I did not reach the primary text).

**6.4 Modern handling.** Rebecca Lyman's "The Theology of the Council of Nicaea" in the *St Andrews Encyclopaedia of Theology* (2024) — an open-access academic reference work, credentialed and freely readable — states the datum in passing and without hedging: Dionysius of Alexandria had rejected *homoousios* as "Sabellian" and, "as would be later discussed by Athanasius, it had been denounced at the Council of Antioch in 268 against Paul of Samasota" [*sic*, spelling as printed]. Giulea 2018 (§5) goes further and makes the rejection intelligible rather than merely embarrassing: if the Antiochene synodals used *ousia* and *hypostasis* interchangeably for the *individual* entity, then *homoousios* would have carried a meaning they had good reason to refuse, and the fourth-century "contradiction" is a semantic shift rather than a reversal. — **Historical Confidence: Widely Accepted** that current scholarship treats the 268 rejection as historical; **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for Giulea's semantic explanation. **Sourcing Provenance: `Standard-Reference-Open`.**

**So: not a misconception, but routinely told wrong.** The defensible version is: *the bishops who condemned Paul refused the word* homoousios *in a sense they associated with his argument, fifty-seven years before Nicaea affirmed it in a different sense; this is reported only by fourth-century writers in the middle of their own fight about that word, our near-contemporary source says nothing about it, and the ancient explanations of the mechanism contradict each other.* Compressing that to "the same word was condemned at Antioch and adopted at Nicaea" is true in outcome and misleading in everything else.

## 7. Geographic and ecclesiastical scope: Antioch relative to Edessa-Nisibis

This is the question that actually decides the disposition, and it needs to be answered about *this* world's own stated boundaries rather than about "Syriac Christianity" as a general category.

**What the Syriac world says its own scope is.** Doc_01 §3: core geography Edessa (Urhoy), capital of former Osroene, and Nisibis, a frontier fortress-capital — "both in upper Mesopotamia, in the Aramaic/Syriac-speaking zone" — with §3's further working definition extending the world to "the Syriac-speaking Christian ecology across the Roman-Persian Mesopotamian frontier zone," including Sasanian Adiabene for Aphrahat. Doc_01 §4: the language is Syriac, and the normative Gospel text is the Diatessaron. Doc_01 §2: c. 200–410 CE.

**Where Antioch sits relative to that.** Antioch-on-the-Orontes is west of the Euphrates, in Roman Syria proper — not upper Mesopotamia, not Osroene, not the Persian frontier. Its Christian literary and liturgical language in this period is Greek; Eusebius's own account makes the point incidentally and decisively when it identifies Malchion as "principal of the sophist school of **Grecian** learning in Antioch," and when it shows the synod circulating its findings to Rome and Alexandria — the Greek-Latin metropolitan network — with no Mesopotamian see among the sixteen signatories or the named bishops of *HE* 7.28 (Cappadocia, Pontus, Tarsus, Iconium, Jerusalem, Caesarea, Bostra). — **Historical Confidence: Documented; Sourcing Provenance: `Primary-Open`.**

**And the Syriac world's own documents treat Antioch as an outside pole it defines itself against, not as its own territory.** Doc_01 §7, on what this world was refusing: "full assimilation into the Greek-language imperial-ecclesiastical apparatus centered at Antioch and Constantinople, in favor of a vernacular Aramaic register and locally rooted institutions (the qyama)." Doc_01 §2 mentions Antioch only once more, and in the same register — the 424 Synod of Dadisho declaring the Church of the East's independence *from* Antioch, an event outside this world's window. **Antioch appears in this world's own boundary document exclusively as the external Greek centre.** (Recorded precisely because l0fig001's review caught that entry citing "Doc_01 §3" for this, which does not mention Antioch at all; the correct citations are §7 primarily and §2 incidentally.) — **Historical Confidence: Documented** for what Doc_01 says; **Sourcing Provenance:** internal governing document, not a historical-sourcing tier.

**The strongest form of the counter-argument, engaged rather than skirted.** Doc_01 §5.6's whole method is to weigh evidence that cuts *against* the exclusion, so the same is owed here. Four real considerations pull toward including Paul in this world's orbit, and each fails on inspection:

1. *He is "of Samosata."* Samosata (Samsat) is on the upper Euphrates in Commagene — genuinely closer to the Syriac-speaking zone than Antioch is, and a plausible Aramaic-speaking origin. But an episcopal career, a Greek-sophist prosecutor, and a Greek-language synodal circular are what his historical footprint consists of; a toponym of origin is not a formation ecology. Nothing read this pass attests anything about his language of formation or his scriptural text.
2. *Broad "Syriac-adjacency."* Roman Syria and upper Mesopotamia are culturally continuous in a wide sense, and the Syriac world's own scope statement is deliberately ecological rather than administrative. But that statement is specific about *which* ecology — Aramaic/Syriac-speaking, Diatessaron-reading, qyama-organized. None of the three is attested of Paul or his followers.
3. *A claim I found and could not verify, recorded because someone else will find it too.* A web summary surfaced during this pass asserted that the 264 synod considered, among the charges against Paul, "his encouragement of East Syrian usages." **If true this would be directly on point.** I could not verify it in any source: it is not in *HE* 7.27–30, not in Smith & Wace, not in Schaff-Herzog, not in the *Catholic Encyclopedia*, and the Wikipedia article — which I fetched specifically to find the claim's citation — makes **no claims whatsoever** connecting Paul to Syriac, East Syrian, or Semitic usage, or to Edessa or Mesopotamia, and never mentions *homoousios* either. **Treated as unverified and not relied on.** Anyone who meets this claim should demand a citation before accepting it. — **Sourcing Provenance: `Web-Lead (needs upgrade)`, and specifically flagged as failing verification, not merely awaiting it.**
4. *The older "Semitic reaction" reading of adoptionism.* There is a real scholarly tradition of reading third-century Antiochene adoptionism as a native Semitic current resisting Hellenistic Christology — which, if sustained, would make Paul culturally adjacent to this world after all. **This is precisely the reading Millar 1971 argues against** (§5), situating Paul instead within Hellenized Syrian Christianity and Roman administrative structures. That the Syriac build already leans on Millar elsewhere makes this the most economical available answer, and it points away from inclusion.

**Determination.** Paul of Samosata falls **inside** Level 0's Phase One temporal window (Doc_00 §1: 70–451) and **inside** its geographic footprint in the broad sense (Doc_00 §2 lists "Antioch and wider Roman Syria" in the union of Phase One's nine worlds' regions). He falls **outside the Syriac Edessa-Nisibis world's own milieu** — different city, different region, different ecclesiastical network, different primary language community, and named in that world's own boundary document only as the external Greek centre it defined itself against. **This is a materially different relationship from Bardaisan's**, and the difference should not be blurred by giving them the same-shaped entry: Bardaisan was geographically native to Edessa, contemporary with the world's own formation, and explicitly and repeatedly refuted by its primary voice — which is what earned him "Excluded / Named Comparandum." Paul meets none of those three conditions. — **Historical Confidence: Documented** for the geographic, linguistic, and ecclesiastical facts; **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for the conclusion that this places him outside this world's milieu (a reasoned application of the world's own already-argued boundary, plus Millar, not a fresh primary-source finding).

## 8. Cross-world scan: does any currently-built world claim him?

Six worlds are built: `01-Post-Apostolic-House-Church`, `Alexandria-Catechetical-School`, `Desert-Monasticism`, `Hieronymian-Ascetic-Literary`, `Imperial-Juridical-Christianity`, `Syriac-Christianity-Edessa-Nisibis`.

**Textual scan.** "Samosata" across all of `World-Builds/` returns three hits, all **Lucian** of Samosata (the satirist, a registered source in World #1's Doc_02 §6 and Doc_09 for *The Passing of Peregrinus*) — a different man, and a namesake trap worth flagging in its own right for anyone grepping. A wider scan across the whole repository returned 21 hits, every one of them Lucian, in the same World #1 files and their duplicates in git worktrees and `cic-poc/backend/data/pahc_world/`. **Paul of Samosata appears nowhere in any built world's documents or record store.** This independently reproduces l0fig001's grep finding by a different route.

**But absence from the files is not absence from the milieu, and one world's own Native primary voice is documentedly in this story.** This is where this entry parts company with l0fig001.

**The Alexandria finding.** The Alexandria-Catechetical-School world's stated temporal scope is c. 150–400 CE (its Doc_01 §2.1, §2.3) — Paul's entire career falls inside it. Its Doc_02 §3.6 registers **"Dionysius of Alexandria (bishop c. 248–264 CE) — primary voice, thinly and mediately attested,"** with a full five-dimension Author Gravity entry, and its Doc_01 §1.2 places Dionysius in the *didaskaleion* succession (Pantaenus → Clement → Origen → Heraclas → Dionysius → … → Didymus). And Eusebius, *HE* 7.27.2 — read directly, main body — records that **Dionysius of Alexandria was invited to the Antioch synod against Paul, was unable to attend on account of age and physical weakness, and gave his opinion on the matter by letter.** *HE* 7.28.3 then records his death and Maximus's succession; and *HE* 7.30.2 shows the final synod's encyclical addressed "To Dionysius and Maximus" — Dionysius of Rome and **Maximus of Alexandria**, Dionysius's own successor. Two consecutive Alexandrian bishops, one of them a registered Native primary voice of that built world, are documented participants in this affair.

The entanglement goes deeper than attendance. Athanasius, *De Synodis* 43–45 — the passage that carries the *homoousios* datum — argues its whole case *through* Dionysius of Alexandria: §43 recounts that Dionysius of Alexandria was accused before Dionysius of Rome of saying the Son was made and not coessential, that the Roman synod objected, and that the Alexandrian answered with a book "of Refutation and Defence"; §45 then reconciles Antioch-268 with Nicaea by appeal to "the illustrations used in Dionysius's case, the 'fountain,' and the defence of 'Coessential.'" Lyman 2024 states the same linkage compactly: Dionysius of Alexandria had himself rejected *homoousios* as "Sabellian." **The Alexandria world's own primary voice is on the other side of the very terminological question that makes Paul of Samosata interesting.**

**Two hard limits on that finding, so it is not overstated.** First, geography still fails: Antioch is not in Alexandria's stated scope either (its Doc_01 §3.1: Alexandria, the Nile Delta and Egyptian urban network, the Egyptian *chora*). Paul is external to Alexandria's milieu too. Second, and more sharply — **the extant letter of Dionysius on Paul is judged spurious.** The *Catholic Encyclopedia* states it flatly: "The letter of St. Dionysius is spurious. That of six bishops to Paul is usually rejected, but Harnack thinks it genuine." So the engagement is attested by Eusebius's narrative; the document that would let us hear Dionysius on Paul is not authentic. That caveat belongs in any registry entry built from this.

**What the relationship therefore is.** Not "Native" to any built world. Not "Named Comparandum" in the Syriac sense — Paul is not refuted by any world's primary voice in that world's own formative program. But also **not** the "in-time, out-of-place, never engaged by the primary voice" pattern Doc_00 §0 identifies as recurring across four worlds, because here the primary voice *was* engaged: **in-time, out-of-place, and documentedly engaged by a Native primary voice of a world other than the one he was first assessed against.** — **Historical Confidence: Documented** for Dionysius's and Maximus's participation as Eusebius reports it, and for the Athanasian argument running through Dionysius; **Widely Accepted** that no genuine Dionysian text on Paul survives; **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for the classification. **Sourcing Provenance: `Primary-Open`** for the Eusebius and Athanasius texts; **`Standard-Reference-Open`** for the spuriousness judgement; internal-document reading for the world-scope claims.

## 9. The A2 continuity test (Constitution Article 4)

Applied in the form Syriac Doc_01 §5.5 insists on: **the A2 test asks whether a figure's theology, followed to its own conclusion, lands within Nicene-Constantinopolitan content — a question kept explicitly separate from whether the figure held clerical office or died in communion.** Paul plainly held office; he was a metropolitan bishop of one of the great sees. That is not the question and does not bear on the answer. And because he predates Nicaea by roughly fifty-seven years, the test is continuity of substance, not conformity to the Creed's later wording.

| Article 4 commitment | What the sources actually support | Verdict |
|---|---|---|
| One God, the Father, maker of all things | Not charged against him in anything read this pass; the synod's complaint is Christological, not theological-in-the-narrow-sense | **Clears** |
| The Son eternally begotten, of one Being with the Father, come down from heaven | Denied. *HE* 7.30.11: "unwilling to acknowledge that the Son of God has come down from heaven"; *HE* 7.27.2: "in his nature he was a common man"; Athanasius *De Syn.* 45: "the Son was not before Mary, but received from her the origin of His being" | **Fails** |
| Truly human | Affirmed, emphatically — this is the content of the charge, not a defect in it | **Clears** |
| Crucified, risen, ascended, returning | Not charged; no evidence located either way | **Not assessed** |
| The Holy Spirit, worshipped with the Father and the Son | **No primary-text evidence located in either direction.** The familiar claim that Paul reduced Logos and Spirit alike to impersonal divine powers rests on Schaff's editorial footnote to *HE* 7.27 (l0fig001's Round 1 review identified this precisely) and on the reference-work reconstructions at §4 — not on Eusebius | **Open** |

**The failing clause is the load-bearing one, and it fails on unusually good evidence for a figure of this kind.** The denial of the Son's personal pre-existent descent is attested in a first-person participant document written at essentially zero temporal distance, is independently reflected by two fourth-century witnesses who disagreed with each other about its significance, and is corroborated institutionally by Nicaea's need to legislate about surviving Paulianist clergy. That is a materially firmer evidentiary base than the one on which Syriac Doc_01 §5.4–5.6 reached its Contested finding against Bardaisan, and the confidence should be set accordingly rather than reflexively matched to the Bardaisan precedent.

**One clause fails; one clause is genuinely open; the rest clear or are not assessed. Paul of Samosata does not clear the Nicene-trajectory eligibility floor.** — **Historical Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the failure on the eternal-begottenness/descent clause; **Inferential-Thin** for the Holy Spirit clause, which should not be reported in either direction. **Sourcing Provenance: `Primary-Open`** for the failing clause; **`Standard-Reference-Open`** for the reconstructions that are *not* being relied on. This independently confirms l0fig001 §4's determination, reached here from the sources rather than inherited.

**A distinction the failure does not license.** He fails the floor as a *founding-source voice*. That says nothing about whether he may be *studied, named, and described* by the project — the floor governs whose voice a Representative may speak in, not what history the project may tell. Bardaisan's own disposition in Syriac Doc_01 §5.6 turns on exactly that distinction.
