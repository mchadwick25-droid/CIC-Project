## Mark's recommendation (verbatim)

Aim for approximately CEFR B2 (Upper Intermediate English), which
roughly corresponds to US Grades 8–10.

This is a sweet spot because it is:

- Accessible to most high school students
- Readable by many non-native English speakers
- Comfortable for adults without specialized education
- Still capable of expressing sophisticated ideas

Many respected organizations intentionally write at this level:

| Organization | Approximate Level |
|---|---|
| BBC News | Grade 8-10 |
| Smithsonian Magazine | Grade 9-11 |
| National Geographic | Grade 8-10 |
| Harvard Business Review (best articles) | Grade 10-12 |
| Many university public outreach publications | Grade 8-10 |

Notice they are not written at graduate-school level.

### What creates the feeling of rigor?

Research suggests readers perceive expertise from different signals than
vocabulary.

**1. Precise language**

Instead of

> Salvation is fundamentally realized through participatory ontological
> incorporation.

Say

> Salvation means being brought into Christ's life and becoming part of
> God's new creation.

Nothing important was lost.

**2. Define specialized words**

Good scholarship doesn't avoid technical words.

It introduces them.

Example:

> We often spoke about theosis—becoming increasingly shaped by God's
> life and character.

After that, "theosis" can be used naturally.

This is exactly how Tim Mackie teaches.

**3. Short sentences**

Instead of

> The Alexandrian tradition demonstrates an interpretive methodology
> predicated upon a christological hermeneutic integrating both literal
> and spiritual readings.

Say

> We read Scripture through Christ. We believed many passages had both a
> literal meaning and a deeper spiritual meaning.

Same scholarship.

Far easier.

**4. Transparent uncertainty**

Nothing increases trust more than saying

> Historians disagree.

or

> We cannot know for certain.

or

> The evidence suggests...

rather than

> This is what happened.

**5. Show your evidence**

One sentence explaining why you think something is true often creates
more credibility than three paragraphs of sophisticated language.

Example

> We believe this because Athanasius repeatedly connects these ideas in
> On the Incarnation.

### Cognitive load matters more than vocabulary

There is a difference between

Simple ideas

and

Simple language.

You want

**Complex ideas expressed simply.**

Not

Simple ideas.

Richard Feynman was famous for this.

So was C. S. Lewis.

So are teachers like Tim Mackie.

### One idea per paragraph

Research in instructional design consistently shows that comprehension
increases dramatically when paragraphs contain one main idea.

For conversation, even more so.

Instead of

> We believed Christ fulfills Israel, restores humanity, defeats death,
> renews creation, forms the Church, and brings the kingdom.

Say

> We believed Jesus fulfilled Israel's story.

Pause.

Then

> Because of that, we believed he also restored humanity.

The information is identical.

The processing cost is much lower.

### The "expert but human" voice

Avoid academic habits like:

- excessive passive voice
- nominalizations ("participation" instead of "participate")
- unnecessary Greek and Latin
- nested clauses
- sentences longer than about 25 words

Instead:

- use active voice
- use concrete verbs
- explain before naming
- define terms naturally

For example:

> We often used the word economy. By that we meant God's way of carrying
> out his saving work throughout history.

That is rigorous.

### A practical writing standard

For Church in Conversation, I would adopt something like this:

| Element | Standard |
|---|---|
| Reading level | Grade 8–10 (CEFR B2) |
| Average sentence | 12–20 words |
| Paragraph length | 1–4 sentences |
| One new concept | Per paragraph |
| Technical terms | Always introduced before use |
| Uncertainty | Explicitly stated |
| Evidence | Mentioned naturally |
| Tone | Curious, humble, confident |
| Vocabulary | Common English whenever possible |

### What I think fits your project's philosophy

From everything we've discussed about Church in Conversation, your aim
is not to impress people with scholarship. It is to make serious
scholarship inhabitable.

That suggests a communication principle like this:

> Every participant, regardless of education or first language, should
> be able to follow the conversation without feeling talked down to. At
> the same time, every historian, pastor, or seminary student should
> recognize that the conversation is grounded in careful scholarship,
> transparent reasoning, and faithful representation of the historical
> evidence.

I think that's the standard you're really aiming for: not simplifying
the scholarship, but lowering the barrier to entering it. That
distinction is important. It means the AI's job is to translate
scholarly understanding into clear, faithful language without diluting
the complexity of the ideas themselves.
