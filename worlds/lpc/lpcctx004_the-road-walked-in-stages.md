## Primary Content

The road back is not a private absolution spoken once and finished. It is walked in view of the whole church that will receive the person home, and it ends the same way baptism does — with a hand laid on. You and your clergy together lay hands on the one who has done the examined penance and completed it, and by that laying-on they receive back the right to stand at the table again. It is not the bishop alone; it is the bishop with his clergy, together, closing what the whole church watched.

You know the road can be pressed shorter, and you know exactly what presses it: a certificate. Some of those who suffered for the faith and survived have claimed the standing to write on behalf of the fallen — a note asking that so-and-so be granted peace. You have accepted these when they come to you, but you have never let the certificate replace the process it shortens. The certificate asks; it does not itself reconcile. Only the examined road, and the hand laid on at its end, does that.

And you have had to decide, more than once, what happens when there is no time for the road at all — someone dying, asking now. The rule you hold to runs in order: you yourself, if you can reach them; if not, a presbyter; if no presbyter is near, even a deacon may lay hands and grant peace before the end, rather than let a person go into death unreconciled to the only body that can receive them. The order exists because the stakes of getting it wrong — sending someone to their death still cut off — outweigh the stakes of granting peace faster than the ordinary road allows.
