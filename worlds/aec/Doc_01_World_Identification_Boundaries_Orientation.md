## 1. Formation World Identity

**World Name:** Antiochene Exegetical Christianity (Chrysostom-centered).

**Short Description:** The formation of ordinary Christian life at Antioch, and later Constantinople, through sustained, plain-sense exposition of scripture — a presbyter and later bishop expounding a biblical text verse by verse and drawing from it a concrete ethical demand, addressed to a real congregation's real conduct: wealth and its use, civic behavior, family life, and, at moments of acute crisis (a riot against the emperor's own statues in 387; his own later exile), the congregation's fear and grief directly.

**Core Identity:** This is a world formed by a method of reading, not primarily by an office or a jurisdictional claim. Its formation logic is Antiochene historical-grammatical exegesis — reading scripture in its plain, literal, historical sense rather than the allegorical mode associated with Alexandria — put to sustained, practical, ethical use for an ordinary urban congregation, across roughly 3.3 million surviving words, nearly all of it continuous scriptural exposition rather than administrative or doctrinal-controversial writing.

**Distinctive Contribution:** No other confirmed or in-progress world in this project's current portfolio reconstructs Christian formation through the specific mechanism of continuous, plain-sense scriptural preaching as its own organizing subject. This is a substantively different claim from World #8's own contribution (Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity): World #8 reconstructs episcopal authority exercised "through office, sacrament, and territorial care of an entire flock" (`CiC_Step0_Conclusion_FINAL_v2.docx`); this world reconstructs formation exercised through a specific interpretive method, independent of the office that happens to deliver it — the finding `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §3 (B3) develops in full, discharging the Step 0 Conclusion's own deferral condition.

**Living Tradition Status:** Not confirmed. Per this project's established practice (`worlds/ijc/Doc_01...md` §1), Living Tradition Status confirmation is a project-lead act this document cannot perform. Noted for the record, not decided here: John Chrysostom is venerated across Eastern Orthodox, Oriental Orthodox, Roman Catholic, and Anglican traditions alike, and is one of the three Ecumenical Teachers and Hierarchs of Eastern Orthodoxy (with Basil the Great and Gregory of Nazianzus) — an unusually broad, cross-traditional living continuity by this project's own standards, though whether and how to name it is the project lead's own call. **Status: PENDING.**

## 2. Historical Boundaries

**Approximate Time Horizon:** c. 350–430 CE.

- **Beginning point (c. 350):** the census's own window reaches back to roughly Chrysostom's own birth (c. 349, per the standard biographical record) and the generation of Antiochene Christian and civic life immediately preceding his formation — including, within this window, the emperor Julian's own residence at Antioch (362–363), already carried on this world's own corpus-map (`ammianus-marcellinus_roman-history_yonge1862`, added 2026-09-25) as `context`: a pagan eyewitness's account of the burning of the Temple of Apollo at Daphne and Julian's retaliatory closure of Antioch's chief church, a formative civic memory of church-state hostility a generation before Chrysostom's own ministry there. This is a defensible beginning point because it captures the civic and religious climate that formed the Antioch Chrysostom was himself later formed by and preached in, not merely his own birth year in isolation.
- **Ending point (430):** twenty-three years after Chrysostom's own death (407), and one year before the Council of Ephesus (431) condemns Nestorius — a boundary chosen, per Step 0 §2 (A1), specifically to close before the Nestorian controversy that later implicates the wider Antiochene exegetical tradition's own Christological method, keeping this world's own window inside the period where its Native voices are not themselves doctrinally contested.

**Geographic Centers:** Antioch, primarily; Constantinople, secondarily, during Chrysostom's own episcopate (397–404) and exile (404–407).

- **Antioch:** the third city of the later Roman Empire by standard reckoning (behind Rome and Alexandria), a major center of Hellenistic urban culture, rhetorical education, and — directly relevant to this world's own required disclosure (Step 0 §2, A5) — a large, socially established Jewish community whose continued attraction for some Antiochene Christians is the documented occasion for the *Adversus Judaeos* homilies, a source this world's own build does not currently hold (Step 0 §4.2).
- **Constantinople:** the site of Chrysostom's own episcopate and the church-state confrontations (the Eutropius affair; the conflict with the Empress Eudoxia; his eventual deposition and exile) already correctly carried, per the existing corpus-map's own "second id" pattern, as material this world shares with `imperial-juridical-christianity` rather than this world's own primary geography.

**Cultural Environment:** Greek-speaking, rhetorically sophisticated urban late antiquity. Chrysostom's own formation is directly relevant here and independently attested across standard biographical scholarship (not verified against a specific vendored primary source this session, and tagged accordingly): he studied rhetoric under the celebrated pagan sophist Libanius before his own conversion to ascetic and ecclesiastical life — named in the census's own list of this world's voices, alongside Chrysostom, Diodore, Theodore, and Theodoret. This dual formation (classical rhetorical training, then Christian ascetic and exegetical vocation) is directly legible in the surviving homilies' own rhetorical craft, and is Widely Accepted rather than independently re-verified against a primary source this session.

**Historical Pressures:**
- Julian's brief pagan restoration and residence at Antioch (362–363), and the city's own documented hostility to him (the *Misopogon*, Julian's own bitter response to Antiochene mockery, already carried as context via the Ammianus row).
- The "Statues" crisis of 387: an Antiochene riot against imperial tax policy resulted in the overthrow of the emperor Theodosius I's own statues, and the city awaited the emperor's judgment (possible destruction of the city itself) while Chrysostom preached the twenty-one *Homilies on the Statues* — this world's own most direct, sustained record of a congregation's fear meeting scriptural exhortation in real time.
- The ongoing tension, at Constantinople, between Chrysostom's own uncompromising preaching against luxury and injustice and the imperial court's own expectations of its bishop — directly evidenced in the Eutropius homilies and the conflict with Eudoxia, culminating in his deposition (the Synod of the Oak, 403) and final exile (404).
- The large, socially confident Jewish community at Antioch and its continued attraction for some Christians, the documented occasion for the *Adversus Judaeos* material (Step 0 §2, A5) — named here as historical context for that disclosure obligation, not as license to characterize the homilies' own content before the primary text is vendored.

**Historical Catalysts:** Chrysostom's ordination as presbyter at Antioch (386) and the start of his major preaching career there; the Statues riot and its aftermath (387); his elevation to the see of Constantinople (397); the Synod of the Oak and his first deposition (403); his final exile and death (407).

## 3. Distinct World Criteria

- **What recurring formation ecology exists?** An ecology of homiletic exegesis: a congregation formed, week by week, through continuous exposure to scripture expounded in its plain sense and applied directly to conduct — the specific mechanism named in §1 above, evidenced by the sheer compositional shape of the surviving corpus (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §3, B3).
- **What recurring gravities exist (preliminary, subject to Doc_04)?** Provisionally: historical-grammatical exegesis as the primary vehicle of ethical formation; uncompromising moral exhortation addressed to wealth, status, and civic conduct, applied without regard to the hearer's own social rank (a documented, recurring theme across the Statues homilies, the Eutropius homilies, and the general moral-homiletic corpus); the tension between rhetorical eloquence (Chrysostom's own inherited Libanian training) and plain, direct address to ordinary hearers. Named as candidates for Step 4 testing, not confirmed gravities.
- **What recurring patterns of worship, formation, interpretation, belonging, and authority exist?** Interpretation and Formation dominate and are, in this world, substantially the same activity (§1). Worship has real, direct material (the Statues homilies as a Lenten preaching sequence; the general homiletic setting itself as a liturgical event). Authority is present but not this world's own center of gravity in its Antiochene phase — it becomes more prominent, and more institutional/juridical in character, specifically during the Constantinopolitan phase (§6 below), material this world already correctly shares with World #6 rather than claims as its own primary register throughout.
- **What distinguishes this world from adjacent worlds?** See §6 below.

## 4. Strand Determination

Per Constitution Article 21, strand is "a finding, never a presupposed universal schema," accountable to evidence, and "never assigned to satisfy an architectural preference for plurality."

**Finding: one primary strand — Chrysostom's own exegetical-homiletic-moral voice — continuous across a real change of office and city, not split by it.** Chrysostom's own ground of authority does not change between his Antiochene presbyterate (386–397) and his Constantinopolitan episcopate (397–404): in both settings, his own claim to speak is grounded in faithful exposition of scripture applied without concession to worldly rank — the same mode that produces the Statues homilies at Antioch produces the Eutropius homilies and the conflict with Eudoxia at Constantinople (§1, §2 above). Applying Article 21 honestly here, as `roman-church-gregorian`'s own Doc_01 did for a comparable single-figure case, means recognizing this as Article 15's temporal/geographic development of one continuous formation logic, not a division between two authority claims.

**Theodoret's own material: a named, unresolved edge voice, not yet classified as a second strand.** Theodoret writes a generation later, from a different city (Cyrrhus, not Antioch), in a genuinely different literary mode (doctrinal-Christological dialogue and polemic — the *Eranistes*, the Counter-statements to Cyril's Anathemas — rather than homiletic moral exposition), addressing a controversy (two-nature Christology against Nestorianism and its critics) this world's own window is deliberately drawn to close just short of (Step 0 §2, A1). This is a real candidate for a second, distinct ground of authority (doctrinal-precision-seeking argument, rather than homiletic moral exposition) — but building a confident strand finding on a voice whose own inclusion is already flagged `provisional` at the corpus-map level, and whose own fit is an open characterization question per the Source Readiness Dossier §5, would be premature. **This document does not classify Theodoret's material as a second strand; it names the question directly and carries it forward to Doc_04, rather than either forcing a strand finding or silently absorbing Theodoret into Chrysostom's own strand as if the two voices were doing the same kind of thing.**

**Governing consequence for later steps:** Doc_04's formal cross-strand testing does not apply the way it did for World #6's confirmed three strands, since this document finds one confirmed strand — but Theodoret's own possible second-strand status, and his edge-of-window fit generally, should be tested directly at Doc_04 rather than left permanently unresolved.

## 5. Preliminary Forces Identification

Per the Forces Framework's Step 1 requirement, this is orientation for Step 2's scope, not the complete forces analysis compiled at Step 8.

**What generated this world:** Chrysostom's own formation — classical rhetorical training under Libanius, followed by ascetic withdrawal and then ordination — combined with Antioch's own established culture of expository preaching (Diodore of Tarsus, his own teacher in exegesis per the standard historical record though not himself part of this world's currently vendored Native base, per Step 0 §2 and §4.4) produced a preacher equipped to apply rhetorical skill to plain-sense scriptural exposition at unusual scale and sustained intensity.

**What larger world was it embedded in:** the Greek East of the later fourth and earlier fifth centuries — doctrinally, the settled post-381 Nicene-Constantinopolitan consensus (this world's own actors are not themselves party to the Trinitarian controversy the way World #6's earlier figures were); politically, an empire whose Constantinopolitan court increasingly set the terms even for a see as prominent as Antioch's, a condition this world's own Constantinopolitan phase makes directly visible.

**What was it responding to:** at Antioch, ordinary congregational life under real but not acute political pressure, punctuated by the Statues crisis's own acute emergency; at Constantinople, an imperial court whose own expectations of its bishop's public conduct Chrysostom's own preaching repeatedly, deliberately violated.

**What was it refusing:** moral compromise with wealth, status, and imperial favor, applied consistently rather than selectively — the documented throughline connecting the Statues homilies (addressed to a whole frightened city, elite and poor alike), the Eutropius homilies (addressed to a specific fallen minister, at the literal altar), and the conflict with Eudoxia (addressed, implicitly, to the empress herself). Not a refusal of imperial power as such (contrast Donatism's own more total refusal, or World #6's own more equivocal accommodation), but a refusal to let imperial or elite status exempt anyone from the same scriptural standard applied to an ordinary hearer.

**Preliminary six-cell sketch (Layer 1 only):**

| | External | Internal |
|---|---|---|
| **Initiating** | Antioch's own established culture of rhetorical education and, more specifically, an existing local tradition of historical-grammatical exegesis (associated with Diodore of Tarsus) available for Chrysostom to inherit — Cell 1A | Chrysostom's own conversion from a rhetorical to an ascetic-then-clerical vocation, redirecting inherited rhetorical skill toward scriptural exposition — Cell 1B |
| **Ongoing** | The Statues crisis (387); the ordinary, recurring pressures of urban civic and congregational life at Antioch; later, the Constantinopolitan imperial court's own expectations of episcopal conduct — Cell 2A | The sustained, serial homiletic practice itself — verse-by-verse exposition across entire biblical books, delivered and (unusually completely, for this period) transcribed and preserved; **Transmission dimension:** the sheer completeness of the surviving corpus (§Step 0, B1) likely reflects both Chrysostom's own enormous contemporary reputation and later centuries' own sustained interest in preserving a preacher regarded, across multiple traditions, as a model — a point of continuity with this project's other worlds' own recurring finding that institutional prestige shapes what survives — Cell 2B |
| **Ending/Transforming** | Chrysostom's deposition (403) and exile (404–407), and his death in exile — Cell 3A | The posthumous rehabilitation of his memory (his relics returned to Constantinople under Theodosius II, outside this world's own window and not developed here) and the tradition's own later continuation through Theodoret's generation, whose own fit this document leaves open (§4) — Cell 3B |

This sketch is preliminary and Layer-1-only by design; it governs Step 2's source-ecology scope and is not a substitute for Doc_08.

## 6. World Continuity & Distinction

**What this world inherits from prior and adjacent worlds:** the settled post-381 Nicene-Constantinopolitan doctrinal consensus this world's own actors take for granted rather than themselves establish (contrast World #6's own earlier, contested phase); at further remove, the historical-grammatical exegetical tradition associated with Diodore of Tarsus, Chrysostom's own teacher — not itself part of this world's currently vendored Native base (Step 0 §4.4), an inheritance named for orientation, not reconstructed here.

**What this world transmits to subsequent worlds/history:** Chrysostom's own enormous and enduring homiletic influence across the whole of later Christian preaching, well beyond this project's own window; the wider Antiochene exegetical and Christological tradition's own later, more contested trajectory through Theodore of Mopsuestia and Nestorius (deliberately kept outside this world's own 430 close, per Step 0 §2, A1) and through Theodoret's own edge-of-window continuation (§4 above). Neither continuation is this document's own to reconstruct.

**Distinctness from adjacent worlds, carried forward as binding boundaries:**
- **World #8 (Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity, c. 240s–430, not yet built):** the specific, required primary-gravity contrast developed in full at `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §3 (B3) — exegetical-formational method here, versus institutional-pastoral-sacramental office there — discharging the Step 0 Conclusion's own deferral condition.
- **World #6 (Imperial and Juridical Christianity, c. 312–451, built):** no meaningful overlap in gravity; real, already-correctly-shared content (the Eutropius homilies, the Olympias and Innocent letters) via the corpus-map's own existing "second id" pattern, not a boundary risk.
- **`roman-church-gregorian` (this same handoff, not yet admitted):** no overlap — different century, region, language, and gravity (administrative-pastoral necessity there; exegetical-formational method here).
- **Diodore of Tarsus and Theodore of Mopsuestia (not includable, no PD path):** named as this world's own honest scoping limit (Step 0 §4.4) — "Chrysostom-centered," not a full Antiochene-school reconstruction.
