## 1. Formation World Identity

**World Name:** The Methodist Revival.

**Short Description:** An evangelical revival within, and eventually separating from, the Church of England, beginning with John Wesley's Aldersgate Street experience of 24 May 1738 ("I felt my heart strangely warmed") and organized through a distinctive discipline of weekly small-group accountability (the "class meeting"), itinerant lay and ordained preaching, and congregational hymn-singing. Two strands, bridged by Wesley's own personal authority until his 1791 death and by shared doctrine and hymnody throughout: a **British strand**, which remained formally inside the Church of England as a connexion of societies governed by an annual Conference, and an **American strand**, which organized itself as an independent, episcopally governed church (the Methodist Episcopal Church) at the 1784 Christmas Conference in Baltimore — seven years before Wesley's own death, and against his own explicit later discomfort with the title his appointed "superintendents" soon adopted (§5 below). Window: 1738–1815, per the census's own already-decided continues-cap convention (Era 8 Freeze) — an administrative closing point for a still-living tradition, not a historical rupture (§2 below).

**Core Identity:** A world of disciplined small-group formation set inside mass open-air evangelism — the class meeting's weekly, spoken, individually-accountable "account of the soul" is this world's own distinctive formation mechanism (Article 4 commitments (1)–(3) are directly affirmed by Wesley's own uncontested Anglican Trinitarian and Christological orthodoxy, per Step 0 §2 A1; commitments (4)–(5) are likewise affirmed by the same confessional inheritance but were not individually checked against the Twenty-Four Articles' own text this pass — carried forward as an open item, §7 below, distinct from an upgraded confidence claim). Salvation is preached as a present, felt assurance ("the witness of the Spirit," directly vendored — Doc_02) available to anyone regardless of social station, joined to a doctrine of entire sanctification (Christian Perfection) most other contemporary Protestant traditions this project has built or surveyed do not share in the same strong form.

**Distinctive Contribution:** No other confirmed or in-build world in this project's portfolio reconstructs a movement whose primary formation mechanism is a *weekly, spoken, small-group, peer-witnessed account of one's own present spiritual condition*, deliberately decoupled from ordained clergy (a class leader was typically a layperson, often a woman — Widely Accepted, not independently vendor-verified this pass) and from any fixed catechetical text. Set against this batch's own era-8 or earlier magisterial-Protestant comparisons (Lutheran Wittenberg, VI.1; the Reformed Cities, VI.2): both of those worlds root formation in catechesis backed by a coercive civic or territorial institution (the Consistory, the territorial church); this world roots formation in voluntary, itinerant, non-coercive small-group accountability that operated *inside* an establishment (the Church of England) it did not initially seek to leave, and which reached, by explicit design, an audience (industrial and agricultural laborers, colliers, the unchurched urban poor) those worlds' own confessional and civic mechanisms did not directly target. The census's own `relationsSummary` names this world as a parent tradition of both the Holiness movement (and through it, Pentecostalism) and, through Richard Allen's 1816 African Methodist Episcopal Church, of the Black Church in America — two live, present-day transmission lines this document does not resolve (Living Tradition Status, below) but does not obscure either.

**Living Tradition Status:** Not confirmed; squarely triggered, not merely possible. Article 29 attaches "where a formation world's reconstruction touches a tradition that continues into the present" (quoted verbatim, `reference/L1-Foundation/CiC_L1_Constitution_V2_2.docx`, independently re-verified this pass by direct extraction from the file itself rather than copied from a prior world's document — see Step 0 §0). This world's own case is unusually strong and unusually plural: the census's own `continuesAs` field names `nineteenth-century-methodism` directly, its `streams` field names `wesleyan-holiness-pentecostal`, and its `relationsSummary` names a second, independent line through Richard Allen's AME (1816) and AME Zion (1821) churches into the Black Church in America. **Both descendant lines are live, named, and distinct — this document does not collapse them into one continuation for simplicity.** Per Blueprint V7.3 §17's own three-part confirmation mechanism (naming the present-day tradition(s); documenting material divergences in the Representative Construction Notes; project-lead confirmation with a date — precedent: `rzg_World_Profile.md`-style handling), this remains the project lead's own act, reserved for a later build stage; this document names both lines plainly rather than pre-judging which (or whether both) should anchor the eventual confirmation.

## 2. Historical Boundaries

**Approximate Time Horizon:** 1738–1815 CE, adopted directly from the census's own already-decided window (`"start": 1738, "end": 1815`). The census's own `statusDescription` states the reasoning directly: "Its end moved to 1815 under the continues-cap convention, with Wesley's death (1791) and the Plan of Pacification (1795) named as the tradition's own hinges — the discarded round-1800 sat astride both without being either." This world's own 1815 close is Francis Asbury's own Journal's stated end-date (7 December 1815, one year before his own 1816 death) — a coincidence this document notes rather than claims as the census's own stated reasoning (which names Wesley's death and the 1795 Plan of Pacification, not Asbury's journal, as the operative hinges).

**What developments remain internal to this world's own window, and what indicate transition:** internal — the 1738 Aldersgate experience and its immediate aftermath; the 1739–41 Wesley/Whitefield doctrinal breach (§4 below, held as a named contemporary-movement boundary, not internal strand evidence); the 1740 Fetter Lane breach with the Moravians (§7 below); the 1743 General Rules and 1744 first Conference; the 1784 Christmas Conference; Wesley's 1791 death; the 1795 Plan of Pacification (granting British Methodist preachers limited authority to administer the sacraments, a real step toward formal separation from the Church of England, taken four years after Wesley's own death — Widely Accepted, not independently vendor-verified this pass). **Transitional, at the window's very edge:** Richard Allen's own 1816 founding of the AME church falls one year past this world's own 1815 close; the census's own source list flags this explicitly ("Allen's conversion c. 1777 at the era's edge... Allen's narrative wasn't published until 1833"), and this document treats the AME founding itself as the opening event of a *descendant* tradition, not as internal to this world's own window — a boundary this document states plainly rather than stretches to include a tidy founding-to-founding narrative.

- **Beginning point (24 May 1738):** John Wesley's Aldersgate Street experience, verified directly against the vendored Curnock Journal (`cic/texts/wesley-j_journal-v1_curnock1909.txt`, lines 27070–27074): "I felt my heart strangely warmed. I felt I did trust in Christ, Christ alone for salvation; and an assurance was given me that He had taken away my sins, even mine, and saved me from the law of sin and death." **Corrected at independent review — a claimed OCR defect did not exist.** This document's own Revision 1 stated that the entry's day-heading digit read "14."/"15." (rather than "24."/"25.") because of an OCR misread, "a documented pattern in this same scanned edition." No such pattern exists anywhere in the file, and the claim was itself wrong about what "14." and "15." even are: read in context (line 26503, "What occurred on Wednesday the 24th, ... I think best to relate at large"; line 26508, "1. I believe, till I was about ten years old..."), Wesley states the 24th directly in running prose and then numbers his own paragraphs — 1, 2, 3... — through a single continuous retrospective account of that day; "14." (line 26957) and "15." (line 27076) are his own paragraph numbers fourteen and fifteen, not day-of-month headings at all, and were never garbled by OCR. The quotation above is paragraph 14 of that retrospective, and the date is exactly what Wesley himself states in his own prose, not merely corroborated by an editorial footnote.
- **Ending point (1815):** adopted from the census as an administrative, not historical-rupture, closing point (per the `statusDescription` quoted above). The pattern this world reconstructs continues unbroken past 1815 into at least two living descendant traditions (§1 above), a finding Doc_08's own Ending/Transforming cell must record honestly per Article 22.

**Geographic Centers:**

- **London:** the Aldersgate Street society (1738); the Foundry, Moorfields (from 1739, Wesley's own headquarters society and the site the 1780 hymn collection was printed at, per its own title page — directly verified, Doc_02); Fetter Lane (the Moravian-influenced society Wesley broke from in 1740, §7 below).
- **Bristol and Kingswood:** the site of Wesley's own first open-air preaching (2 April 1739, taking over from Whitefield, per the census's own `documentedStories` field — not independently vendor-verified against a primary source this pass, since Whitefield's own journal is not vendored).
- **Oxford:** the "Holy Club" (from 1729), the movement's own true point of origin per its own later self-account — directly verified against the vendored Large Minutes (`cic/texts/wesley-j_large-minutes_1850.txt`, lines 379–381): "Q. 2. WHAT was the rise of Methodism, so called? A. In [1729] the late Mr. Wesley and his brother, upon reading the Bible, saw they could not be saved without holiness; they followed after it, and incited others to do the same." (This edition's own OCR renders the year as "1/29"; 1729 is the well-attested date for the Holy Club's own founding, and this document treats the OCR string as a character-level slash/digit misread, not a substantive dating claim of this world's own.)
- **Savannah, Georgia:** Wesley's own 1735–1737 missionary posting under the Society for the Propagation of the Gospel (SPG — corrected at independent review from an earlier, wrong "Society for Promoting Christian Knowledge," the SPCK, a different and separate body; directly verified against the vendored Journal, `cic/texts/wesley-j_journal-v1_curnock1909.txt`, lines 7746–7747, Curnock's own editorial note: "He had come to America as a missionary in the service of the Society for the Propagation of the Gospel") — by his own later judgment, and Widely Accepted historiography, a failure (a difficult relationship with Sophia Hopkey and the resulting parish discipline dispute forced his return; this document does not claim, as an earlier revision did, that the two were formally engaged, since this pass found no vendored confirmation of that specific claim) — but the voyage out is also, per Widely Accepted historical record and directly attested in the vendored Journal itself (lines ~6950–6990, Wesley recounting the Moravians' own calm during a mid-Atlantic storm, and their captain Bishop Nitschmann named at line 5224), this world's own **first**, not second, contact with the Moravians, more than two years before Aldersgate (§7 below) — corrected at independent review: an earlier revision of this document flagged this specific passage as "not searched," when it is in fact present and now directly cited.
- **Baltimore:** Lovely Lane Chapel, site of the 1784 Christmas Conference organizing the Methodist Episcopal Church in America — Widely Accepted, not independently vendor-verified this pass.
- **The American circuit:** the whole of the Eastern Seaboard and, increasingly across this world's own window, the trans-Appalachian frontier, covered by Asbury's own itinerant ministry, directly verified across his complete, three-volume vendored Journal (Doc_02).

**Cultural Environment:** A movement that began among, and was largely led by, university-educated Anglican clergy (Wesley, Charles Wesley, Whitefield — all Oxford) but which deliberately, and against considerable social resistance, addressed itself to audiences excluded from ordinary parish life: industrial colliers, agricultural laborers, and, in America, a socially and geographically far more dispersed frontier population Asbury's own itinerant "circuit rider" system was built to reach. A real, internal social-register tension runs through the whole window: Wesley himself never abandoned a High Church, sacramentally serious Anglican self-understanding, even as his own movement's lay preachers and class leaders were drawn overwhelmingly from outside the clergy and, not infrequently, from outside literate society.

**Historical Pressures:**
- The Church of England's own parish-based, geographically fixed clergy system, which structurally could not (and, in many recorded cases, would not) serve an increasingly mobile industrial and frontier population — the specific gap field preaching and itinerancy were built to fill.
- Recurring clerical and, at times, mob hostility to open-air preaching and to the class-meeting system as irregular and enthusiastic — Widely Accepted, general historical texture, not independently vendor-verified this pass to a specific incident beyond the Bristol/Kingswood episode named above.
- The American War of Independence (1775–1783), which placed Asbury — a British subject who, unlike nearly all of Wesley's other British-born itinerant preachers in America, chose to remain in the country throughout the war rather than return to England, per Widely Accepted historiography (the precise count of who else stayed is not independently vendor-verified this pass, and this document does not claim Asbury was the sole exception) — in a genuinely difficult position, and which is the direct precondition for American Methodism's own 1784 organization as a fully independent church rather than a colonial extension of the British connexion.
- Chattel slavery in America: the 1784 Christmas Conference's own initial rules included strong anti-slavery provisions that were substantially weakened within a few years under pressure from Southern membership — Widely Accepted, not independently vendor-verified this pass; directly relevant to, and in real tension with, this world's own transmission into the Black Church in America through Richard Allen (§1 above), a tension this document names rather than smooths into a single uncomplicated legacy narrative.

**Historical Catalysts:** The Holy Club at Oxford (from 1729); Wesley's Georgia mission and first Moravian contact (1735–1737); the Aldersgate experience (24 May 1738); Wesley's visit to Herrnhut (1738, §7 below); the Bristol/Kingswood open-air preaching start (1739); the Wesley/Whitefield "Free Grace" breach (1739–41, §4 below); the Fetter Lane/Moravian breach (1740, §7 below); the General Rules (1743) and first Conference (1744); the 1784 Christmas Conference and the ordination of Coke and Asbury as "superintendents" (a title Wesley himself endorsed but whose informal escalation to "Bishop" he later, famously, objected to in writing — Widely Accepted, not independently vendor-verified this pass); Wesley's death (1791); the Plan of Pacification (1795).

## 3. Distinct World Criteria

- **What recurring formation ecology exists?** Weekly small-group, peer-witnessed spoken accountability (the class meeting) joined to itinerant open-air and chapel preaching and to congregational hymn-singing as a vehicle for shared doctrine — three distinct, mutually reinforcing mechanisms operating together, none of which this world's own tradition treats as sufficient alone.
- **What recurring gravities exist (preliminary, subject to Doc_04)?** Provisionally: present, felt assurance of salvation (the "witness of the Spirit," directly vendored); entire sanctification / Christian Perfection as an attainable state in this life; free grace, offered to all rather than limited by predestination (directly counterposed to Whitefield's own Calvinism, §4 below); disciplined, examined communal life as the ordinary means of growth in holiness. Named here as candidates for Step 4 testing.
- **What recurring patterns of worship, formation, interpretation, belonging, and authority exist?** Belonging and formation are the most developed in the vendored corpus so far (the Large Minutes' own catechism; the class-meeting structure it prescribes). Worship is strongly attested through Charles Wesley's hymns, directly vendored and confirmed genuinely present in this specific 1780 edition. Authority shows a real, internal, disclosed asymmetry rather than one settled pattern: British Methodism's own connexional-conference structure, with no bishop, against American Methodism's own episcopally titled superintendency from 1784 (§5 below).
- **What distinguishes this world from adjacent worlds?** See §7 below.

## 4. World Separation Criteria and the Whitefield Question

**Substantially re-argued at independent review (Round 2) — this section's own Revision 1 both inverted the six-question test's own scoring and overclaimed that its conclusion required no escalation. Both are corrected below; the escalation itself is not resolved by this document, per §9.**

Before Strand Determination (§5), this document runs the Framework's own six-question test (Construction Framework V7.4, "World Separation Criteria": has a new gravity emerged; has a major gravity disappeared; has formation changed substantially; has worship changed substantially; has authority changed substantially; has interpretation changed substantially) on the one candidate relationship genuinely capable of being read as a *separate world* rather than an internal strand: George Whitefield's own Calvinistic Methodism, run here at the level of his own institutional legacy specifically (his post-1741 organized Calvinistic Methodism), not at the level of his own person, who was directly present within this world's own 1738–41 founding events (§9 below).

- **Has a new gravity emerged, or a major one disappeared?** **Yes** — corrected from Revision 1's "No." §3 above names this world's own candidate gravities as including free grace (offered to all, not limited by predestination) and entire sanctification (Christian Perfection). Whitefield rejected both: his own Calvinism holds unconditional election, and his own soteriology has no place for a doctrine of sinless perfection attainable in this life. Two of this world's four named candidate gravities are absent or directly contradicted in Whitefield's own theology — a real gravity divergence, not merely a pre-existing disagreement that leaves the shared ecology's own gravities untouched, which is what Revision 1 incorrectly concluded.
- **Has authority changed substantially?** Yes — the 1743 Calvinistic Methodist Association (with Whitefield himself as moderator) and, later, the Countess of Huntingdon's own Connexion are genuinely separate organizational structures; no shared Conference or class-meeting discipline ever bound Whitefield's own following to Wesley's.
- **Has interpretation changed substantially?** Arguably yes — the predestination dispute is itself a substantive interpretive disagreement over how scripture's own teaching on grace and election is to be read, not merely a difference of practical emphasis.
- **Has formation changed substantially?** Partial, not decisive — corrected from Revision 1's unqualified "Yes, and decisively... never built, and by most accounts never sought to build, anything resembling Wesley's own class-meeting system." That overclaimed the case: Whitefield built his own Tabernacle society, presided as moderator of the 1743 Association, and the Welsh Calvinistic Methodist movement's own *seiat* (experience meeting) is described by the census's own VII.10 entry as itself "comparable to Methodism's class meetings." Whitefield's own formation mechanisms were real, if less durable and less systematized than Wesley's own — a genuine but partial divergence, not the clean, decisive one Revision 1 claimed.
- **Has worship changed substantially?** No — both preached the same new-birth doctrine in substantially the same open-air idiom.

**Corrected tally and finding.** Revision 1 folded these six questions into four bullets, answered gravity "No," and then counted "the absence of any shared gravity-emergence" as a point *favoring* separation — backwards: a "No" on the gravity questions is evidence *for* one world, not against it. On Revision 1's own (miscounted) logic the actual tally was 2 of 6, not the claimed 4 of 6. Run correctly, with the gravity answer corrected to Yes: gravity yes, authority yes, interpretation arguably yes, formation partial, worship no — a real, if not unanimous, case for treating Whitefield's own **post-1741 organized Calvinistic Methodism** as institutionally and doctrinally distinct from this world, resting on stronger and more honestly-argued grounds than Revision 1's own inverted count produced.

**What this finding does not settle, and Revision 1 wrongly claimed it did.** Revision 1 asserted the census "corroborates" this reading because "no Whitefield primary text is named among this entry's own source list (only Wesley's, Charles Wesley's, and Asbury's are)" and "none of the census's own named descendant traditions... descend through Whitefield's own line." **Both claims are false, independently checked directly against the live census file:** Whitefield is one of this world's own six named `voices` ("the revival's most powerful open-air preacher on both sides of the Atlantic, and Wesley's Calvinist counterpart and friendly rival"); two of this world's own three `documentedStories` feature him directly, one ("Whitefield Asks for Slaves") entirely about him; and the census's own `sources` list does not name Asbury at all (the claim that it named "only Wesley's, Charles Wesley's, and Asbury's" work was itself inaccurate on its own terms). Whitefield is also a named `voice` of the Evangelical Awakening in New England (Atlas VII.6), and the Welsh Revival Tradition (Atlas VII.10, "sibling of Methodism") is itself the Calvinistic Methodist line his own preaching helped set going. **Revision 1 also claimed "Article 21 does not apply... touches no other census entry's own boundary" and treated this as a considered, non-escalating reading. That claim does not survive the correction above and is withdrawn, not merely softened:** treating Whitefield's own institutional legacy as belonging outside this world's own scope, when the census itself names him as one of this world's six voices and builds two of three of its documentedStories around him, is not a scope question this world's own build thread can settle by itself — it implies a reallocation of a census-named voice toward other entries (VII.6, VII.10, and no entry currently exists for the English Calvinistic Methodist/Huntingdon Connexion line specifically), which is exactly the class of cross-world, portfolio-level decision `cic-build-cycle`'s own escalation categories name, on the same footing as the rzg/Dort precedent this document's own Revision 1 cited without applying to itself. **This document does not decide the allocation question. ## 5. Strand Determination

Per Constitution Article 21: strand is "a meaningfully distinct pattern of formation emphasis, practice, authority structure, or ecological orientation within a single world, not merely a variation in detail."

**Finding: two strands — the British strand (Wesley, then the post-1791 Wesleyan Conference) and the American strand (Asbury, from 1771, organized as the Methodist Episcopal Church from 1784) — bridged by shared doctrine, hymnody, and the Large Minutes' own disciplinary inheritance even after formal organizational separation. This finding holds; two of its own supporting claims were overstated in Revision 1 and are corrected below, not merely restated.**

Run against the same six-question test §4 applies to Whitefield (added at independent review — Revision 1 asserted this test had been run, per its own Round 1 review finding L3, when it had not): **authority, yes** (the clearest divergence, below); **formation/ecological orientation, yes** (a genuinely different practical problem, though built on shared underlying mechanisms — corrected below, not the clean "different solutions" claim Revision 1 made); **gravity emergence/loss, no** (both strands hold the same free-grace, present-assurance, entire-sanctification core); **worship, no** (shared hymnody); **interpretation, no** (shared doctrine and scriptural reading). Either authority or formation alone meets Article 21's own definition of a strand; both together make the case solid.

1. **Authority structure — the clearest and most permanent divergence.** British Methodism retained, throughout this world's own window, a connexional structure with no bishop: an annual Conference of preachers, and the Plan of Pacification (1795) as the first formal step toward full independence from the Church of England. American Methodism, from the 1784 Christmas Conference onward, was organized as an *episcopally governed* church, with Coke ordained by Wesley at Bristol in September 1784 and Asbury — who insisted on his own election by the American preachers before accepting ordination, itself the strongest single piece of authority-divergence evidence this document has, not fully used in Revision 1 — ordained deacon, elder, and superintendent within the same Christmas Conference. "Superintendent" came to be rendered "Bishop" within a few years, over Wesley's own later written objection (Widely Accepted; the specific 1788 letter is not independently vendor-verified this pass, since no Wesley correspondence collection is vendored). This is not a minor administrative variance: it is a different form of church government, adopted while Wesley himself was still alive and, on the evidence available, without his full approval — a divergence that in fact deepened after 1784, not merely at the founding moment: in 1787, per Widely Accepted historiography (not independently vendor-verified this pass, no Conference Minutes proper being vendored), the American Conference declined Wesley's own direction to appoint Richard Whatcoat as joint superintendent and struck his name from the printed Minutes — corrected at independent review, since Revision 1's own account of "Wesley's personal authority... until 1791" did not disclose this and so overstated how far that authority actually extended in practice.
2. **Formation emphasis and ecological orientation — real, but resting on shared mechanisms, not two unrelated solutions.** Corrected at independent review: Revision 1 stated the two strands arrived at "genuinely different practical solutions, not the same solution applied in two places" — wrong on its own terms, since itinerancy, the circuit system, and the class meeting were themselves British-connexion mechanisms Asbury carried directly to America, not independently invented there. What differs is not the mechanism but the ecological problem it was stretched to solve: the British strand's own formation problem was reaching an increasingly urban and industrial population inside an already-existing, geographically fixed parish system; the American strand's own problem — Asbury's own itinerant "circuit rider" system, directly attested across his complete vendored Journal — was covering an enormous and rapidly expanding frontier with a chronic shortage of preachers relative to territory, using the same connexional toolkit at a different scale and under different pressure. This is real divergence in ecological orientation, argued honestly as such rather than as two unrelated inventions.
3. **Practice.** Both strands retain the class meeting and congregational hymn-singing (Charles Wesley's own hymns were used on both sides of the Atlantic, per Widely Accepted historiography) — this is what bridges rather than separates the strands, alongside shared doctrine.

**In-window divisions within the American strand, not surveyed in Revision 1 — added at independent review.** The strand finding above concerns British/American divergence, not internal uniformity within either strand, and this document does not let the two-strand finding imply either strand was itself undivided: the O'Kelly schism (1792, over Asbury's own episcopal authority) and the Methodist New Connexion (1797, a British secession over lay representation) both fall inside this world's own 1738–1815 window; the Primitive Methodists (from the 1807 Mow Cop camp meeting, a settled connexion by 1811–12) sit at the window's very edge and are the direct subject of a separate, already-registered census entry, Nineteenth-Century Methodism (Atlas VIII.34, this world's own `continuesAs` target). None of these is resolved here; they are named so Doc_04/Doc_08 do not treat either strand as internally monolithic across the whole window.

**A further cross-world overlap, not a strand question but worth naming here rather than only in §7:** Francis Asbury is himself a named `voice` of Atlas VIII.2 ("The Second Great Awakening & Camp-Meeting Tradition"), whose own `relationsSummary` calls that world a "child of Methodist/Edwardsean lines." This world's own American strand and VIII.2 share a figure, not merely an era — named for Doc_02/Doc_07's own awareness, not resolved here.

**What bridges, rather than separates, the strands:** the shared class-meeting system; the shared doctrinal core (free grace, present assurance, entire sanctification); the shared hymnody; the Large Minutes' own disciplinary inheritance (the American church's own 1784 "Sunday Service" and discipline derive directly from Wesley's own text, per Widely Accepted historiography, not independently vendor-verified this pass).

**Cross-strand implication for Step 4 and Step 10:** per Article 21, convergence across strands is itself a test of a gravity's centrality — the free-grace/present-assurance gravity and the class-meeting formation mechanism are visible in both strands and should be tested for convergence at Doc_04. This finding also directly bears on the Representative-construction question reserved for the project lead at Step 10 (a British-strand figure such as Wesley himself, an American-strand figure such as Asbury, or a genuine multi-figure Representative) — this document does not decide that question itself, per this build's own escalation categories.

## 6. Preliminary Forces Identification

Per the Forces Framework's Step 1 requirement (this world's Doc_01 is one of the Framework's own six integration points), this is orientation governing Doc_02's scope, Layer 1 (Historical Event) only, per Article 22 and the Forces Framework's own Section 5 register rule for Layer 1.

**What generated this world:** a felt inadequacy in the Church of England's own capacity to reach an industrializing, increasingly mobile population, met by a small group of Oxford-trained clergy (the Holy Club, from 1729) whose own devotional rigor had already earned them a mocking nickname, and catalyzed into a mass movement by Wesley's own 1738 assurance experience and Whitefield's prior example of open-air preaching (from which Wesley initially recoiled on grounds of propriety, then adopted, per the census's own account, directly attested).

**What larger world was it embedded in:** the Church of England's own eighteenth-century established order, itself shaped by the Restoration settlement and by a broader, contested "vital religion" current running through Puritan devotional writing, Anglican High Church piety (the Non-Jurors; William Law's own mystical-ascetic writing, a documented direct influence on Wesley — Widely Accepted, not independently vendor-verified this pass), and, decisively, Continental Pietism transmitted through the Moravians (§7 below). The census's own `relationsSummary` states this plainly: "Methodism grew from Puritan piety and Moravian influence inside the Church of England" — this document treats "Puritan piety" here as the general devotional-experiential inheritance (not institutional Puritanism or the Reformed confessional tradition directly; no organizational link to the Reformed Cities world, VI.2, is claimed or found), a distinction worth stating rather than leaving the census's own loose wording to imply more institutional continuity than the evidence shows.

**What was it responding to:** a felt absence, in ordinary parish religion as most laypeople experienced it, of assurance, moral seriousness, and communal accountability — not, unlike Wittenberg or the Reformed Cities, a doctrinal crisis over justification's own theological mechanism (Wesley's own theology of grace is a real development, but he explicitly understood himself as recovering primitive and Anglican, not as refounding, Christianity) nor a sacramental-authority crisis against Rome (this world has no Roman Catholic antagonist comparable to the Reformation-era worlds').

**What was it refusing:** nominal, unexamined church membership without a corresponding changed life ("The Almost Christian," directly vendored — Doc_02); a fixed, exclusively parish-bound model of pastoral care; and, on Wesley's own side specifically, unconditional predestination (refused directly against Whitefield, §4 above) and the Moravian "stillness" doctrine that outward means of grace should be set aside pending an inward experience of full assurance (refused directly against the Fetter Lane Moravians, §7 below).
