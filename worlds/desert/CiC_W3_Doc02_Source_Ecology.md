## 1. Primary Voices

Each entry below carries a full five-dimension Author Gravity Assessment per Framework Part II (visibility, representativeness, influence, limitations, and — per the v1.6 addition — transmission history).

### 1.1 Athanasius of Alexandria, *Life of Antony* (*Vita Antonii*), c. 356–362

- **Visibility:** the single most influential text this world produced, in any direction. Written by Alexandria's own bishop, not a desert participant.
- **Representativeness:** low as a window onto ordinary participants' own experience; high as a witness to how this world's founding figure was *presented* to, and received by, both Egyptian and international (via early Latin translation) audiences.
- **Influence:** decisive and disproportionate — this is the text that made Antony, and by extension this world, known outside Egypt at all. Jerome, Augustine (*Confessions* 8.6), and the Latin monastic tradition all trace back to it.
- **Limitations:** Athanasius was not present for the events he narrates in detail and wrote with an explicit theological and political agenda (Doc_01, Section 10) — presenting Antony as anti-Arian and doctrinally orthodox, and as deferential to episcopal authority in a way that reads, per David Brakke's critical treatment, as serving Athanasius's own contested claim to Alexandrian ecclesiastical authority as much as it documents Antony's actual life.
- **Transmission history:** the Greek text survives complete; an early Latin translation (Evagrius of Antioch, not to be confused with Evagrius Ponticus) circulated within a generation and is the version that reached the West, including Augustine. This means most of this world's international reception ran through a translator's hand, not the Greek original — a transmission dependency this document flags rather than assumes away.
- **Confidence in this document's use of it:** Documented as to the text's existence, authorship, and approximate date; Contested as to its reliability for specific biographical incident and for the "unlettered rustic" characterization it advances (see Section 1.3 below and Doc_01, Section 10).

### 1.2 Pachomian corpus — the Rules and the *Lives of Pachomius*

- **Visibility:** substantial but linguistically fragmented — the Rules survive complete only in Jerome's Latin translation (404 CE, made from a Greek intermediary Jerome says was itself translated from Coptic), with partial Coptic originals also extant. The *Lives* survive in multiple, only partially overlapping versions: Sahidic and Bohairic Coptic, and Greek (the *Vita Prima Graeca* among others).
- **Representativeness:** the most direct textual link this world has to its own cenobitic (Strand B) internal governance — these are working administrative and hagiographic documents produced within the Pachomian federation itself, not by outside visitors.
- **Influence:** the Pachomian Rule, via Jerome's Latin translation, fed directly into the Western monastic rule tradition (Cassian, and eventually Benedict draws on this lineage).
- **Limitations:** the relative priority and historical reliability of the different *Life* versions for specific incident-level detail remains a genuinely live scholarly question (Doc_01, Section 10, citing Rousseau and Veilleux's differing positions) — this document does not adjudicate that debate and uses the corpus only for claims consistent across versions.
- **Transmission history:** a documented, multi-stage translation chain (Coptic → Greek → Jerome's Latin for the Rules; multiple independent Coptic and Greek recensions for the *Lives*) — each stage a real opportunity for editorial shaping that this world's own most internally-produced textual evidence has already passed through before reaching modern readers.
- **Confidence:** Widely Accepted as to the corpus's existence, general content, and Pachomian origin; Contested as to specific incident-level historical reliability and version-priority.

### 1.3 The Letters of Antony (seven letters attributed to Antony)

- **Visibility:** low relative to the *Life of Antony*, and entirely dependent on Samuel Rubenson's 1990/1995 critical edition and argument for bringing them to serious scholarly attention as likely-authentic primary material rather than pseudepigraphic devotional literature.
- **Representativeness:** if authentic (see Doc_01, Section 10), this is the only surviving material in Antony's own voice, rather than in Athanasius's — a genuinely different evidentiary category from the *Life*, and the one piece of evidence bearing directly on the Rubenson/Athanasius tension over Antony's own literacy and philosophical formation.
- **Influence:** minimal historically (the Letters were largely unknown to the later Greek monastic tradition, surviving chiefly through Georgian, Latin, and Syriac translation) but potentially significant for this world's own self-understanding if Rubenson's authenticity case holds.
- **Limitations:** the Coptic original is almost entirely lost; the case for authenticity, while substantive (convergent early manuscript attribution, Jerome's independent mention of seven Antonian letters, citations by Shenoute and Besa, echoes in the fifth-century *Apophthegmata*), remains contested specifically as to how far the Letters' Origenist-Platonist theological register can be generalized into a claim about Antony's own education (Graham Gould's counter-position, Doc_01 Section 10).
- **Transmission history:** transmitted almost entirely through translation (Georgian, Latin, Syriac) with only Greek and Coptic fragments surviving directly — an unusually thin and indirect transmission chain for material this potentially significant, and a genuine limitation on how much weight this document places on it.
- **Confidence:** Contested as to authenticity (leaning toward authentic, per the balance of manuscript and citation evidence Rubenson assembles, but not Documented); Contested, and more sharply so, as to the Origenist-influence reading specifically.

### 1.4 Evagrius Ponticus — the *Praktikos*, *Chapters on Prayer* (*De oratione*), and the *Antirrhetikos*

- **Visibility:** the most systematic surviving theological-psychological account of the ascetic life produced within this world (Strand C, Kellia) by a participant rather than an outside observer.
- **Representativeness:** low for the movement as a whole — Evagrius was a Greek-educated theologian trained under the Cappadocian Fathers before settling at Kellia (Doc_01, Section 4), making him this world's most atypical participant-author by education, even as he became its most systematic voice.
- **Influence:** decisive for the later contemplative tradition (via Cassian's transmission to the West, and via Syriac and Armenian translation to the East), but this influence was exercised largely under other names (see transmission history below) rather than under his own, following his posthumous condemnation.
- **Limitations:** Evagrius's own speculative cosmological-theological positions were condemned alongside Origen's at the Second Council of Constantinople (553 CE) — well outside this world's own c. 430 closing boundary, but with direct consequences for how his corpus survives (below).
- **Transmission history:** this is this world's single most dramatic transmission-history case. Following the 553 condemnation, Evagrius's more speculative works survive substantially in Syriac and Armenian translation — traditions unaffected by the Constantinopolitan decision — while his Greek manuscripts frequently transmit several of his ascetic-practical works — the *Chapters on Prayer* above all (not the *Praktikos*, which generally transmits under Evagrius's own name and anchors Guillaumont's critical edition) — under pseudonymous attribution, chiefly to Nilus of Ancyra, and occasionally to Basil or Gregory of Nazianzus. A reader encountering "Nilus" in a Byzantine or Athonite manuscript may be reading Evagrius. This is Constitution Article 22's Transmission History dimension in its starkest possible instance for this world.
- **Confidence:** Widely Accepted as to authorship and general content for the ascetic-practical works; Documented as to the 553 condemnation and its effect on transmission; the specific pseudonymous-attribution pattern is Widely Accepted among specialists in the manuscript tradition specifically.

### 1.5 The *Apophthegmata Patrum* (Sayings of the Desert Fathers)

- **Visibility:** this world's single most widely read primary corpus, then and now — the terse, orally transmitted sayings later gathered into the Alphabetical and Systematic collections (compiled in something like their surviving written form across the 5th–6th centuries, i.e., substantially after this document's own c. 430 closing boundary, even though the oral material collected clearly originates within it).
- **Representativeness:** the broadest surviving witness to this world's actual teaching content and named participants, including its *ammas* (Section 1.6) — but reaching modern readers only through the filter of later compilers who selected, arranged, and in places clearly harmonized material from across all three strands (Doc_01, Section 6) into a single collected tradition.
- **Influence:** the primary vehicle by which this world's own teaching content and idiom (rather than its institutional history) reached the later monastic tradition, East and West.
- **Limitations:** compiled after this document's own closing boundary, by editors whose own selection criteria and possible harmonization across strands are themselves a live methodological question (Doc_01, Section 6, open item 5) rather than a transparent window onto original practice.
- **Transmission history:** oral transmission across at least one, likely several, generations before written compilation; the compilers themselves function as a distinct Author Gravity layer (shapers, not merely transcribers) and should be assessed with the Framework's Narrative Source Author Gravity criteria, not treated as a neutral archive.
- **Confidence:** Widely Accepted as to the corpus's general origin in this world's own oral teaching tradition; Contested as to how faithfully any individual saying preserves its original strand-specific context versus reflecting later compilers' own arrangement.

### 1.6 The *ammas* — Syncletica, Theodora, Sarah

- **Visibility:** thin relative to the male-authored and male-centered bulk of the surviving material, but real and specifically named, not merely inferred (Doc_01, Section 4).
- **Representativeness:** the only direct textual evidence this world offers for women's own teaching voice, as distinct from women's presence as a documented but voiceless minority.
- **Influence:** limited historically relative to male figures, but genuinely present within the *Apophthegmata* tradition and carried forward alongside it.
- **Limitations:** transmitted entirely through the same later-compiled *Apophthegmata* apparatus as the male-attributed sayings (Section 1.5), meaning every limitation named there applies here as well, compounded by the much smaller sample size available for any individual cross-check.
- **Transmission history:** identical mechanism to Section 1.5; no independent transmission stream exists for the *ammas'* material.
- **Confidence:** Widely Accepted that named *ammas* and their sayings are a genuine, if thin, part of the *Apophthegmata* tradition; Inferential / Thin for any claim beyond what the surviving sayings themselves state.

## 2. Secondary Voices / Narrative Sources (per the Framework's Narrative Source Author Gravity requirement)

These are historians and observers who function as *accounts of* this world rather than voices *within* it, and require the same five-dimension assessment as primary voices.

### 2.1 Palladius of Galatia, *Lausiac History* (c. 419–420)

- **Visibility:** high — alongside the *Historia Monachorum*, this world's principal narrative-history source.
- **Representativeness:** Palladius was himself a monk who spent substantial time in Egypt before becoming bishop of Helenopolis, giving him closer personal participant-adjacent standing than a purely external visitor, though he wrote the *History* retrospectively, for the imperial chamberlain Lausus, years after his own Egyptian residence.
- **Influence:** among the most-read sources for this world in both the Greek East and (via later Latin translation) the West.
- **Limitations:** written as a retrospective compilation addressed to an outside patron, after this world's own most intense period of formation — an inherently curatorial relationship to the material (Doc_01, Section 2.3).
- **Transmission history:** the Greek original is complicated by an extensive later reception history, including a considerably expanded Latin recension; textual criticism of the *Lausiac History*'s manuscript tradition is itself a specialist field this document has not independently adjudicated.
- **Confidence:** Widely Accepted as to authorship, approximate date, and general content; Inferential / Thin as to specific numerical claims (e.g., the Nitria population figure, Doc_01 Section 2.2, attributed to Nitria alone).

### 2.2 The anonymous author of the *Historia Monachorum in Aegypto* (c. 394–395) and Rufinus of Aquileia's Latin translation/adaptation (c. 403)

- **Visibility:** high, alongside the *Lausiac History*, though frequently confused with it — this document notes explicitly that the two are independent works by different authors, not variant titles of the same text.
- **Representativeness:** the Greek original was written by one of a group of monks from Rufinus's own monastic community on the Mount of Olives who traveled through Egypt and recorded what they saw and heard — closer to a travelogue by short-term visitors than to Palladius's longer personal residence.
- **Influence:** substantial, particularly in the West once Rufinus's Latin version (which adds material of his own beyond straight translation) circulated.
- **Limitations:** authorship is genuinely anonymous, not merely lost — this is not a case of a known author whose work is imperfectly preserved, but of a text whose author chose not to be named, or whose name did not survive transmission.
- **Transmission history:** an early and unusually well-documented case — the relationship between the Greek original and Rufinus's Latin version was itself historically disputed (an older view, since superseded, held Rufinus's Latin was the original and the Greek a back-translation); the now-dominant scholarly view (held at Widely Accepted, not Documented — "consensus" is Framework-prohibited language below that level), following C. Butler's philological demonstration, holds the Greek is original and Rufinus's Latin a translation-with-additions.
- **Confidence:** Widely Accepted as to the Greek-original-then-Latin-translation relationship (this rests on Butler's 1898 philological demonstration resolving a real prior dispute with E. Preuschen and R. Reitzenstein over Latin priority; that is a scholarly demonstration achieving current consensus, not a directly and multiply attested fact free of any serious historical dispute, so Widely Accepted is the more defensible level); Inferential / Thin as to the anonymous author's specific identity.

### 2.3 Formation Narrative Sources Assessment

Framework Part II requires a discrete Formation Narrative Sources assessment — for each hagiographic/biographical/sayings source, its authorship and date, proximity to the events or persons described, the genre conventions shaping the account, what formation ecology it actually reveals, and its Author Gravity risk. Sections 1 and 2 above already carry authorship/date, proximity, and Author Gravity risk for each source; this subsection completes the assessment by applying the two framework axes those sections under-served: genre convention and formation ecology revealed.

- **Athanasius, *Life of Antony* (Section 1.1).** Genre: episcopal hagiography written to construct an exemplar, following (and shaping) the conventions of Greco-Roman *bios* literature adapted to Christian sanctity — a genre that expects idealization, typological patterning (Antony's progressive withdrawal mirrors a spiritual ascent narrative), and a didactic through-line, not neutral chronicle. Formation ecology revealed: less the texture of ordinary desert life than the ideal of solitary combat and progressive withdrawal as a model to be imitated — valuable precisely as evidence of what this world's own leadership wanted formation to look like, distinct from evidence of what it actually was for most participants.
- **The Pachomian *Lives* (Section 1.2).** Genre: monastic community hagiography, produced and revised within the federation itself across multiple recensions, a genre oriented toward institutional legitimation (establishing Pachomius's authority and the Rule's divine sanction) as much as biography. Formation ecology revealed: the *Lives*' own narrative emphasis on obedience, communal labor, and hierarchical correction directly reflects Strand B's own formation logic (Doc_01, Section 6) — the genre and the ecology it depicts are not independent of each other.
- **The *Apophthegmata Patrum* (Section 1.5).** Genre: a collected-sayings genre, oriented toward terse, memorable, practically applicable teaching rather than narrative or chronicle — its very brevity is a formation technique (a saying meant to be turned over in the mind during solitary labor) as much as a literary convention. Formation ecology revealed: the oral, elder-to-disciple transmission mechanism this world used to teach (Doc_01, Section 1) is not merely described by this genre but performed by it — the collection's own form is itself primary evidence for how teaching actually moved through this world.
- **Palladius, *Lausiac History* (Section 2.1), and the *Historia Monachorum* (Section 2.2).** Genre: both are travel-memoir/monastic-history hybrids, addressed to outside patrons or communities, organized around notable encounters with named holy figures rather than around ordinary participants' daily texture. Formation ecology revealed: comparatively more of this world's geographic and demographic scale (settlement locations, rough population claims) than its interior teaching content, which both works tend to summarize rather than transcribe — a genre-driven complement to the *Apophthegmata*'s opposite emphasis.

This document does not produce that inventory (reserved for Doc_09 per the Framework's own sequencing) but flags the following as its most likely early candidates, drawn directly from the sources above: Antony's withdrawal narrative (Section 1.1); the *Apophthegmata*'s named-*amma* sayings (Section 1.6); and the Nag Hammadi burial episode, contingent on Section 5.3's unresolved theory — each carried forward as a provisional lead, not a tiered classification, which is Doc_09's job.

## 3. Institutional Evidence

The Pachomian Rule itself (Section 1.2) is this world's primary institutional-administrative document — specifying communal offices (housemasters, stewards), work assignments, and internal discipline in a way no Strand A or Strand C material attempts. Beyond the Rule, the papyrological Nepheros archive (Section 5 below) provides independent institutional evidence of a different kind: everyday administrative and legal correspondence generated by an actual, functioning monastic community, rather than a normative rule text describing how a community *should* function.

## 4. Liturgical Evidence

Evidence for this world's actual liturgical practice is comparatively thin relative to its ascetic-practical teaching. What survives: the weekly *synaxis* structure across Strand C settlements (Doc_01, Section 4) — a Saturday-to-Sunday gathering for common vigil, liturgy, and a shared meal, attested across the *Apophthegmata* and the narrative histories (Sections 1.5, 2.1, 2.2) but not preserved in any surviving liturgical text specific to this world (no distinct "Desert Rite" text survives, unlike the later, better-documented liturgical traditions of settled dioceses). The Pachomian communities' own daily prayer rhythm — a fixed cycle of communal prayer interspersed with manual labor — is attested in the Rule (Section 1.2) with more structural specificity than Strand A or C material offers, but even here the Rule specifies structure (how often, in what groupings) far more than liturgical content (what was actually prayed, beyond the Psalter and the Lord's Prayer, both widely attested across all strands). This document treats liturgical content specifically, as opposed to liturgical rhythm and structure, as a genuinely thin area of this world's evidence base and flags it for explicit proportionality treatment at Doc_05 rather than allowing thin evidence to be silently padded into false specificity.

## 5. Material Culture and Daily Life Sources

### 5.1 The Kellia excavations

Kellia was rediscovered by Antoine Guillaumont in 1964 and excavated by French and Swiss archaeological teams from 1965 to 1990, across a site covering some 125 square kilometers with over 1,500 identified structures. The excavations recovered a physical range from single-occupant cells to larger multi-room hermitages with attached oratories, chapels, and towers, plus communal-service clusters and at least one commercial center — direct physical corroboration of Strand C's semi-anchoritic settlement pattern (individual or small-group cells plus shared communal infrastructure) described textually in Doc_01, Section 6. Most recovered artifacts are pottery; some cell walls carry inscriptions, graffiti, and paintings, offering a rare direct (non-literary) window into resident monks' own devotional and daily concerns. **Confidence:** Documented as to the excavation's existence, dates, teams, and general findings; Inferential / Thin as to connecting any specific excavated structure to a specific named figure from the textual tradition.

### 5.2 Papyrological evidence: the Nepheros archive

A fourth-century archive of monastic correspondence, associated with a Melitian (Doc_01, Section 4) monastic community near Hipponon in the Heracleopolite nome, centered on the letters of the monks Paieous and Nepheros. This is direct documentary evidence of a functioning monastic community's ordinary economic and legal life — correspondence about practical business, not devotional or hagiographic composition — and offers a genuinely different evidentiary register from the literary sources in Sections 1–2. The editors of this archive read the underlying community as an "intermediary" system, structurally something between a fully centralized cenobium and a loose scatter of independent hermits — a possible fourth organizational pattern, or a documented real-world variant that does not map cleanly onto any single one of Doc_01's three strands. This document flags this as a genuine complication for Doc_01's strand typology rather than silently absorbing it into one of the three named strands (see Section 11).

**A necessary qualification:** this archive is Melitian, not part of the Nicene-communion mainstream this document otherwise centers (Doc_01, Section 8.2). It is nonetheless included here as material and institutional evidence for what an actual fourth-century Egyptian monastic community's documentary footprint looked like, on the working assumption — not yet independently verified — that Melitian and Nicene-communion ascetic practice were not organizationally distinct in most day-to-day respects, only in their ecclesial allegiance. This assumption itself is one of the open items Doc_01 already carries forward (Doc_01, Section 11, item 1) and is not resolved here.

### 5.3 The Nag Hammadi codices and the contested Pachomian-proximity theory

Discovered in 1945 near Nag Hammadi in Upper Egypt, not far from the Pachomian federation's own territory, the codices' relationship to this world is a live and unresolved scholarly question rather than settled fact, and this document treats it accordingly. One substantial line of scholarship (most associated with James Robinson, and developed further by Hugo Lundhaug and Lance Jenott) argues the codices were produced and read within a Pachomian monastic milieu — citing monastic documentary material used as cartonnage (binding stuffing) in several of the codices' covers, most prominently Codex VII, and proposing that Athanasius's 39th Festal Letter of 367 CE, which explicitly condemns "apocryphal" texts alongside its list of canonical scripture, may have prompted Pachomian monks to bury texts they could no longer safely keep. A substantial competing position holds this monastic-origin and burial-as-response-to-Athanasius theory is not well supported — some scholars find it difficult to reconcile with the Pachomian federation's own general reputation for doctrinal conformity, and an alternative hypothesis holds the codices may derive from private individual ownership and were buried as grave goods, a separately well-attested Egyptian practice, unconnected to any monastic community at all. **This document does not adopt either position.** If the Pachomian-proximity reading is correct, it would be significant material evidence for what at least some participants in Strand B were reading — including material well outside this document's own current source base — and would bear directly on Doc_02's own Source Asymmetry discussion (Section 6) and on Doc_04's gravity discovery. This is flagged as an open item requiring further, more specialized research before Doc_04, not resolved here (Section 11).

## 6. Ordinary Participant Evidence and Source Asymmetry (the Affirmative Duty, Constitution Article 20)

Whose voices does this world's source record structurally suppress, and why?

**The overwhelming majority of this world's actual participants — the thousands of monks Palladius and the Pachomian *Lives* report by the hundreds and thousands — left no surviving text of their own composition.** What evidence exists for their lives comes through four filtered channels, each already assessed above: (1) literary portrayal by educated outside observers (Athanasius, Palladius, the *Historia Monachorum* author) whose own agendas and social distance from their subjects are independently documented; (2) the *Apophthegmata*'s later-compiled sayings, themselves filtered through unnamed editors' selection and arrangement; (3) the Pachomian Rule and *Lives*, which are institutional and hagiographic rather than personal testimony; (4) papyrological survival (the Nepheros archive, Kellia's material remains), which is genuinely closer to unmediated ordinary evidence but exists only where chance preservation and, in Kellia's case, twentieth-century archaeological excavation happened to recover it.

**This structural suppression falls along at least three axes this document names explicitly, per the Affirmative Duty:**

1. **Literacy and language.** Athanasius's own portrait of Antony as *agrammatos* (Doc_01, Section 4) — whatever its accuracy as applied to Antony specifically (Section 1.3 above) — likely describes the actual condition of a substantial share of this world's Coptic-speaking participants, who are for that reason structurally absent from a source record composed almost entirely in Greek by literate, often non-native, observers.
2. **Gender.** Women's participation (the *ammas*, Section 1.6; Pachomius's own sister's women's houses, Doc_01 Section 2.1) is documented as real but is transmitted through a source base overwhelmingly authored by and about men, with the thinness of the *ammas'* own material (Section 1.6) a direct, measurable consequence.
3. **Ecclesial allegiance.** Melitian ascetic communities (Section 5.2; Doc_01, Sections 4 and 8.2) are documented as real and contemporaneous but are structurally under-visible in a literary tradition (Athanasius above all) produced by their doctrinal opponents — Athanasius's own authority over how this world's Nicene-communion majority is remembered is not incidental to his simultaneous role as the Melitian schism's most powerful ecclesiastical adversary.

**What naming this structural absence honestly tells us about the formation ecology itself:** this is a world whose own self-understanding, as transmitted, privileges withdrawal, orality, and anonymity as positive ascetic values (a monk seeking to be forgotten, to leave no name, is in some strands of this tradition's own logic succeeding rather than failing) — meaning some of this source thinness is not merely an accident of preservation but may partially reflect the ascetic project's own values working exactly as intended. This document flags this as a genuine interpretive possibility for Doc_05's Ecology → Theology work, not as a settled reading that would let the source asymmetry off the hook as somehow unproblematic.

## 8. Forces lens applied to source ecology (Framework Step 2 / Forces Framework Step 2)

**Which sources speak to external forces?** The Nepheros archive (Section 5.2) speaks most directly to economic and legal pressure — the ordinary business of sustaining a community materially under Roman provincial administration. Athanasius's *Life of Antony* (Section 1.1) and the Pachomian *Lives* (Section 1.2) speak to demonic/spiritual pressure as their own primary vocabulary for external threat, a genuinely different register from documentary economic evidence.

**What do silences reveal?** The near-total absence of a distinct liturgical text corpus (Section 4) may itself be evidence of this world's own oral, elder-mediated transmission logic (Doc_01, Section 1) rather than simple accidental loss — a world that transmitted teaching primarily through direct relationship rather than fixed liturgical text would be expected to leave exactly this kind of thin textual liturgical record, whether or not anything was in fact lost.

**What survivorship patterns indicate which forces were most threatening?** Evagrius's own transmission history (Section 1.4) — surviving substantially through pseudonymous Greek attribution and unaffected Syriac/Armenian translation streams — is direct, measurable evidence of the 553 Origenist condemnation's power to suppress a named author's own attribution, decades after this world's own closing boundary, while failing to suppress the underlying content itself. This is a striking, concrete instance of a broader pattern this document expects Doc_08's Forces Document to develop further: doctrinal condemnation targeting a name and a cosmological system, while proving far less effective against the practical-ascetic content that outlived it.

## 9. Confidence Map (summary, using the Framework's actual five-level vocabulary)

- **Documented:** Antony's and Pachomius's approximate dates and the broad outline of their careers (Doc_01, Section 2); the Kellia excavations' existence, dates, and general findings (Section 5.1); the 553 condemnation's effect on Evagrius's transmission (Section 1.4).
