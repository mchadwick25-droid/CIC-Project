## Quick Meaning

In Cassian's Marseilles, the power that "always remains free in man" to "neglect or delight in the grace of God" — real enough that the monk's fasting, watching, and labour are truly his and truly required, weak enough that "the assistance and grace of God are necessary to it every day and hour" — never denied and never sufficient.

## World Meaning

The question is asked by a Gaul. Germanus, sitting with Abbot Paphnutius, hears that God begins and completes every good work and asks: "Where then is there room for free will, and how is it ascribed to our efforts that we are worthy of praise, if God both begins and ends everything in us which concerns our salvation?" The old man's answer holds both. The will is weak — "Being pushed I was overturned that I might fall," the prophet sings, "where the weakness of our free will is shown," and then "the Lord sustained me," which "shows that the Lord's help is always joined to it." But the will is real: "we have no wish to do away with man's free will by what we have said, but only to establish the fact that the assistance and grace of God are necessary to it every day and hour." It is "in our own power to follow up the encouragement and assistance of God with more or less zeal," and "accordingly we are rightly visited either with reward or with punishment."

Abbot Chaeremon says the same more fully. "It cannot then be doubted that there are by nature some seeds of goodness in every soul implanted by the kindness of the Creator: but unless these are quickened by the assistance of God, they will not be able to attain to an increase of perfection." "And therefore the will always remains free in man, and can either neglect or delight in the grace of God." He puts grace and free will side by side and refuses to drop either: "These two then; viz., the grace of God and free will seem opposed to each other, but really are in harmony, and we gather from the system of goodness that we ought to have both alike, lest if we withdraw one of them from man, we may seem to have broken the rule of the Church's faith." God works in all "so as to incite, protect, and strengthen, but not to take away the freedom of the will which He Himself has once given," "in such a way that the choice of free will is open to either side." And the last word is a limit rather than a solution: "how God works all things in us and yet everything can be ascribed to free will, cannot be fully grasped by the mind and reason of man."

The monk feels this in his own practice before he hears it argued. The formation manual commands fasting, watching, labour, the psalms, and the disclosure of thoughts; it blames the deserter who leaves his cell; it tells the junior "to conquer his own wishes." Then, at the summit, on pride, it tells him what his will is: "this very free will of ours, which is more readily inclined to sin, is turned by Him to a better purpose, and by His prompting and suggestion, bent towards the way of virtue"; God's grace is "bestowed only upon those who labour and exert themselves," and yet "we cannot even make the effort to obtain perfection without the mercy and inspiration of God." The will is the thing that labours; the labouring is not its own.

The boundary on one side is named by Vincent as well as Cassian: Pelagius, who "attributed so much antecedent strength to Free-will, as to deny the necessity of God's grace to aid it towards good in every single act" — "the profane notion of some who attribute everything to free will and lay down that the grace of God is dispensed in accordance with the desert of each man." On the other side stands what the men of Marseilles were reported to hold by those who reported them to Africa: that "men's wills are anticipated by God's grace," that "no one can suffice to himself either for beginning or for completing any good work," and that they were nonetheless "in darkness" about predestination — a word they did not use of themselves. Later, from Lérins, Faustus is remembered for teaching that "whatever gain that freedom of will may attain for its pious effect, is not its own desert, but the gift of grace."

At Tours none of this is argued. Martin obeys, fasts, and prays, and Sulpitius writes of what Martin did; the question of whether the doing was his own was not one Martin's house had been asked.

## Ecological Function

The second term of the pair through which G3 is spoken (Supporting at world level; Primary within the south — Doc_04; strengthened by Doc_05 §2.4(v)). Its ecological weight is that it *underwrites the formation program*: every commanded practice in Doc_05 §2.2 presupposes that the will is real and required, and *Inst.* XII's teaching on the will is the program's own last word on why the practices are neither optional nor sufficient. It connects to G7 (the practices aimed at purity of heart), G8 (the athlete's own effort under the general's protection), G4 (defended as "the rule of the Church's faith" and the fathers'), and G9 (the position reported to Rome and held against Celestine's letter — Gibson's prolegomena, editorial). Dependencies: grace (5.1), beginning of a good will (5.3), co-operation (5.6), merit (5.7), perseverance (5.4), predestination (5.5), Pelagians (5.9), Massilians (5.8), obedience (3.9), thoughts (4.4). A participant who understands this term understands why a Massilian monk can say both that his will is anticipated by grace and that his fast is required of him.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
Free will as a philosophical question about determinism; or as the Pelagian half of a grace-versus-works binary; or as self-reliance — the idea that this world taught that people save themselves by effort.

**World Hearing:**
A weak but real freedom that "always remains" and is "open to either side," which grace incites and strengthens without removing, and which the monk exercises in every fast and vigil while confessing the labouring is not his own; a boundary drawn against Pelagius by name and against the denial that God wills all to be saved; a relation explicitly declared beyond reason's grasp.

## CT Contest Type

**Meaning:** whether Chaeremon's "the will always remains free in man" and "seeds of goodness in every soul" (*Conf.* XIII.12) amount to what Augustine himself grants — a freedom that grace anticipates and incites — or to the claim Prosper's *Contra Collatorem* extracted and condemned; the same Chadwick-versus-Casiday contest that attaches to "grace (of God)" (rows 21–22; Doc_01 §7), and inseparable from the Contested chronology of *Conf.* XIII relative to Augustine's Gallic treatises.

**Application to this world:** whether "semi-Pelagian" — anachronistic per Doc_01 §7, and present in this build's files only in the editorial apparatus — fairly describes a position whose own best later witness (Faustus, per Gennadius) is grace-prevenient, and whether that position was held at Lérins and by Vincent at all (Contested; Heurtley's editorial reading, Doc_02 §1.3).
