## Quick Meaning

For this world virginity is a consecrated state with its own dress and its own "glory": Arborius's daughter "consecrated ... to God, and devoted ... to perpetual virginity" by Martin's "placing upon her the dress characteristic of virginity"; "the choir of virgins" at his funeral; "nothing is to be compared with virginity"; and the virgin praised because "she did not admit to her presence Martin himself" — while the south's own conference "On Chastity" is missing from this world's English text.

## World Meaning

At Tours the virgin is made by the bishop's hands and honoured by his refusal to be seen. Arborius "at once consecrated the girl to God, and devoted her to perpetual virginity," and "would not suffer her to be consecrated by any other than Martin, through his placing upon her the dress characteristic of virginity." Martin's parable at Sulpitius's side sets the meadow in order: the grazed part is marriage, the ruined part fornication, the flowered part virginity — "Blessed is such beauty and worthy of God; for nothing is to be compared with virginity"; "marriage belongs to those things which may be excused, while virginity points to glory, and fornication must incur punishment." To the soldier who wants his wife in the desert: "let the woman keep herself within the protection of the walls. She, too, certainly has her own glory, if, when her husband is absent, she maintains her chastity; and the first excellence, as well as completed victory of that, is, that she should not be seen." And the virgin "who had so completely withdrawn herself from the eyes of all men, that she did not admit to her presence Martin himself," is praised above priests: "her blessing was by no means to be rejected by a priest, since she was indeed to be placed before many priests." At his funeral "there was the choir of virgins, abstaining out of modesty from weeping"; at the altar, "one of the virgins" was among the five who saw the fire. The letter to Claudia calls virgins "the brides of Christ," veiled "after the manner of brides."

In the south the word is Elijah's — "the flowers of a virgin life and an example of chastity and continence" — and a married layman's, who kept his wife "as a virgin in the place of a sister." What Cassian's fathers taught at length about chastity was said in a conference this world's English text does not contain: of *Conferences* XII the edition says only "Not translated"; of the book on fornication, "We have thought best to omit altogether the translation of this book"; and the conference "On Nocturnal Illusions" "is omitted." What survives is the edge: the night's purity the dream must not spoil; "familiarity with women" as a fault "atoned for by expulsion"; "that religious woman, devoted to the service of God, who is deprived of all support of kindred," offered by accidie as a pretext. Two women's houses stood, at Tours and at Marseilles; no woman in either speaks.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
Virginity as sexual abstinence merely, or as repression; "nuns" under a later rule; the enclosed virgin's invisibility as oppression the world should have recognized; or, oppositely, the assumption that the south had nothing to say about chastity.

**World Hearing:**
A dressed, episcopally-conferred state that "points to glory," whose "completed victory" is not to be seen, honoured above priests, present as a choir at the altar and the grave; and a southern teaching on chastity that existed, was written, and was removed from this world's English text by a nineteenth-century hand — the world's word, and a silence not the world's.
