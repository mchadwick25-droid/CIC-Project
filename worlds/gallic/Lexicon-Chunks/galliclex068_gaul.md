## Quick Meaning

For this world Gaul is where one is, named in two registers: Cassian's "this province that is frozen as it were with the cold of Gaul," "a new establishment in the West, in the parts of Gaul," a country whose climate forbids Egypt's dress and hours and whose monks are told "some of us do in this country" what Egypt does not; and Sulpitius's "as you are wont to say, when you are accused of being too fond of eating, 'We are Gauls,'" with the charge to tell Egypt that "Christ has by no means forsaken Gaul, since he has granted it to possess such a man as Martin."

---

## World Meaning

In the south Gaul is the cold, later, weaker place. Cassian writes Egypt's institutes for "a new establishment in the West, in the parts of Gaul" because he does not believe such a place "could find anything more reasonable or more perfect" than Egypt's customs; where those are "impossible in these countries" because of "the severity of the climate," he adapts, since "in this country neither the climate nor the weakness of our constitution would permit" the Egyptians' hours and food; and the way itself is "difficult and almost unknown in this country." His running correction is in the first person: "as some of us do in this country, who, before the Psalm is fairly ended, make haste to prostrate themselves"; the Gloria "we have observed in this country" and "never heard anywhere throughout the East"; "some in this province ... go back again to bed"; and of the Egyptians' perseverance, "we cannot recollect any one who joined our monasteries keeping it up unbroken even for a year." One of his own dedicatees longed "to make his way to Egypt to be edified by the sight of these in the flesh, that he might leave this province that is frozen as it were with the cold of Gaul." To be a Gaul at Marseilles is to be Egypt's debtor and to know it.

At Tours the same debt is turned into a joke and a boast. Martin sits on a stool "which we Gallic country-people call tripets"; Sulpitius teases Gallus that when accused of eating too much, "as you are wont to say ... 'We are Gauls'"; and Gallus, the "Gallic country-person" set against "you men of learning, or those at least who are from Greece," is the one who has the story. Then the charge to Postumianus going east: tell them "that Christ has by no means forsaken Gaul, since he has granted it to possess such a man as Martin. But when you have come as far as Egypt, although it is justly proud of the numbers and virtues of its own saints, yet let it not disdain to hear how Europe will not yield to it, or to all Asia, in having only Martin." In Salvian's Marseilles the word is a ruin: "the country of the Belgae burst into flames, then the rich estates of the luxurious Aquitanians, and after these the whole body of the Gallic provinces." And Gennadius, at the century's end, calls Vincent simply "the Gaul."

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
"France" and a national identity; Gallic pride; or Gaul as merely a geographical setting for a "Western" monasticism that thinks of itself as original.

**World Hearing:**
The cold, late province that receives Egypt's rule and knows it falls short — measured by hours it cannot keep and hoods it cannot wear — and, at Tours, a self-mocking country-people's name and one saint by whom "Europe will not yield" to Egypt; in one Marseilles voice, a body of provinces on fire.
