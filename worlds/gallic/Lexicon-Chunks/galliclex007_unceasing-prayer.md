## Quick Meaning

Two things at once: at Marseilles a fixed "canonical system" of psalms and prayers at appointed hours, received from Egypt as "no appointment of man's invention, but ... brought down from heaven to the fathers by the ministry of an angel"; and, at both Tours and Marseilles, the aim that system exists to scaffold — prayer that never stops, "just as it is the custom of blacksmiths, in the midst of their work to beat their own anvil."

---

## World Meaning

The aim is the same in both houses. Of Martin: "Never did a single hour or moment pass in which he was not either actually engaged in prayer; or, if it happened that he was occupied with something else, still he never let his mind loose from prayer. In truth, just as it is the custom of blacksmiths, in the midst of their work to beat their own anvil as a sort of relief to the laborer, so Martin even when he appeared to be doing something else, was still engaged in prayer." At Marmoutier "the elders spent their time in prayer," and no one left his cell "unless when they assembled at the place of prayer." Of every monk, Abbot Isaac says: "The aim of every monk and the perfection of his heart tends to continual and unbroken perseverance in prayer." Cassian's own plan for his books is to climb "from the system of the canonical prayers" to "that continuance in unceasing prayer, which the Apostle enjoins."

At Marseilles the system is the second lesson of the monk's life, after the girdle: "Girt, therefore, with this twofold girdle ... the soldier of Christ should next learn the system of the canonical prayers and Psalms." Its number is not anyone's to change. The fathers, arguing over how many psalms to sing, watched one stand up and sing "eleven Psalms, separated by prayers introduced between them," finish "the twelfth with a response of Alleluia," and vanish — so that twelve at evening and twelve at night "was no appointment of man's invention." The day hours are memorials: "at the third hour the Holy Spirit ... descended," at the sixth the Lord was offered, at the ninth He harrowed hell, in the evening He gave the supper; and with the morning office the day fulfils "Seven times a day do I praise Thee." The system is honest about what it is for. Continuous prayer joined to work is worth more than prayer at intervals, "and more acceptable as a free gift than the duties which are performed by the compulsion of a rule"; the fixed hours are for those "who, unless we are compelled, as it were, by some rule to discharge these pious offices at least at stated times, either through sloth or through forgetfulness, or being absorbed in business, spend the whole day without engaging in prayer." The scaffold confesses that the building is beyond it.

Keeping it is a discipline of the whole body. Prayers are "short and offered up very frequently," so that the devil's darts find nothing to strike; "ten verses ... sung with understanding and thought" are better than "a whole Psalm ... poured forth with a bewildered mind"; the monk goes down to the ground only for a breath and rises again; after the night office no one sleeps, lest the dream spoil what the psalms have cleaned. And the system is the house's hardest sanction short of expulsion: "if one of them has been suspended from prayer for some fault which he has committed, no one has any liberty of praying with him," because the suspended man is, "as the Apostle says, 'delivered unto Satan.'" To belong is to be allowed to pray with the brethren.

Gaul keeps it in its own way, and Cassian says so, in the first person, from inside the office. In "this country," "before the Psalm is fairly ended," some "make haste to prostrate themselves for prayer, in their hurry to finish the service." Here, "while one sings to the end of the Psalm, all standing up sing together with a loud voice, 'Glory be to the Father and to the Son and to the Holy Ghost' — we have never heard anywhere throughout the East." The morning office is "generally celebrated after a short interval after the Psalms and prayers of Nocturns in the monasteries of Gaul," and "some in this province, not knowing the reason why this office was appointed and introduced, go back again to bed." A Gallic monk of the 420s owns one thing in the office outright — the loud Gloria — and is charged with hurrying and oversleeping the rest.

What Marmoutier sang, and at what hours, no one wrote down. There is "the place of prayer," a bishop who "proceeds to offer the sacrifice to God" before a great multitude, and, after his death, the new form his own prayer took: "Martin is praised with the divine psalms, Martin is honored in heavenly hymns."

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
"The Divine Office" or "Liturgy of the Hours" as a settled, uniform book — Benedictine or Roman, with Lauds, Prime, and Compline — that all monks everywhere recited; or "prayer" as private devotion and feeling.

**World Hearing:**
An Egyptian pattern received as angelic and therefore not to be altered, yet still being argued (a new morning office instituted in Cassian's own day), kept imperfectly in Gaul with one loud Gallic addition, and whose whole point is to be exceeded by prayer without hours; the later office-names are a nineteenth-century editor's; and what Martin's own house sang is unknown.
