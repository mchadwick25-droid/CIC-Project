## Quick Meaning

For this world the second sense of "grace" — a bestowed power or gift: "the gift of accomplishing cures was so largely possessed by Martin," the "grace" Martin felt "diminished" by a bad communion and "repaired, with manifold interest," Abbot John "exalted even to the grace of prophecy for his admirable obedience," "the gift of discretion ... the greatest prize of divine grace" — to be kept distinct from the grace argued over at Marseilles, which is the initiative of salvation.

---

## World Meaning

At Tours the word names something a saint has more or less of. "The gift of accomplishing cures was so largely possessed by Martin, that scarcely any sick person came to him for assistance without being at once restored to health." God, "glorifying his own holy follower everywhere, conferred upon that one man the gifts of various graces." It rises and falls: after his one coerced communion with the Ithacians "he cured some of the possessed more slowly and with less grace than usual," and then, Gallus says, "as we experienced, he repaired, with manifold interest, his grace, which had been diminished for a time." The "abundance of power" Martin had as a monk and lost as a bishop is, in the *Life*'s own Latin, an abundance of *grace*. This grace is measured by results, transferable to threads and letters, and injured by a wrong fellowship.

At Marseilles the same sense is used of the Egyptian fathers and is kept in its place. Abbot John of Lycon "was exalted even to the grace of prophecy for his admirable obedience"; Abbot Moses says "the gift of discretion is no earthly thing and no slight matter, but the greatest prize of divine grace"; each monk is to hold to the line "which he has chosen as the grace which he has received"; Abbot John marvels at a married layman's "so evident grace" in casting out a demon; and "the special grace of the Presbyter Isidore himself" could not deliver an envious brother from his tormentor. Abbot Nesteros gives the whole account of "divine gifts" — threefold, the first "for the sake of healing" — and then warns that "the working of miracles and signs is not always necessary, nor good for all, nor granted to all." In both places grace-as-gift is real, particular, and bestowed; in neither is it the grace that "always co-operates with our will," which is the beginning and end of a soul's salvation and not a power one man has more of than another.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
One word, one doctrine — Martin's "grace" read as the grace of the Massilian argument, or vice versa; "charism" as charisma or personality; "spiritual gifts" as a modern charismatic category.

**World Hearing:**
A bestowed and measurable power — cures, prophecy, discretion, command over demons — that a saint possesses in greater or lesser abundance, can lose by a bad communion, and can have repaired; at Marseilles, a gift held under humility and never proclaimed; and never the same thing as the grace that begins salvation.
