## 1. Formation World Identity

**World Name:** The Roman Church in the Age of Gregory the Great.

**Short Description:** The Roman episcopate governed, in practice, by one former monk turned pope — administering a shrunken, Lombard-besieged city; managing the church's own Sicilian and Italian estates as its last reliable source of grain and revenue; codifying what a bishop's pastoral office actually requires; reaching, through personal correspondence, to Visigothic Spain, Merovingian Gaul, and a newly-planted mission in Anglo-Saxon Kent; and continuing, from his own earlier years as papal legate at Constantinople, to operate as an engaged participant in the same imperial church even as Byzantine power proved unable to defend or provision Italy itself (see the Distinctive Contribution immediately below and §6).

**Core Identity:** This is a world of pastoral administration exercised as necessity, not a world of jurisdictional assertion against a rival. Its formation logic is the *Pastoral Rule*'s own governing question — what does a bishop actually owe the souls in his care, in both preaching and personal conduct — worked out not as abstract theology but as the daily business of feeding a starving city, ransoming captives from the Lombards, disciplining a scattered Italian and Sicilian clergy, and answering, one letter at a time, for a see whose material power had collapsed even as its pastoral and administrative responsibility had not.

**Distinctive Contribution:** No other confirmed or in-progress world in this project's portfolio reconstructs the Roman episcopate at the specific moment its own primacy claims come to rest as much on administrative indispensability as on juridical assertion — Rome as the institution that still feeds people, still ransoms captives, still negotiates truces, precisely because the Byzantine imperial structure that still, formally, governs Italy can no longer reliably do any of those things itself, even as Gregory himself continues to operate as an engaged, loyal participant in that same imperial church (his own Constantinopolitan legation, §2 below; his own petitioning of the emperor over the "Universal Bishop" claim, §5 below; and the emperor's own direct order restraining him over the Istrian schism, Doc_02 §1, Book II Ep. XLVI). This is a narrower and more precisely stated claim than World #6's own distinctive contribution (§6 below): World #6 reconstructs Roman primacy *consolidating itself* inside a still-functioning imperial partnership; this world reconstructs Roman authority absorbing new, administrative-necessity functions *alongside* a continuing, but locally incapable, imperial partnership, not a world without one.

**Living Tradition Status:** Not confirmed. Per this project's established practice (`worlds/ijc/Doc_01...md` §1, citing `worlds/syr/Open_Gaps_Tracking.md`, item 10, the "PENDING project-lead confirmation" precedent dated 2026-07 in that log), Living Tradition Status confirmation is a project-lead act this document cannot perform. Noted for the record, not decided here: Gregory I is a Doctor of the Church specifically in the Roman Catholic tradition; Eastern Orthodoxy has no equivalent formal "Doctor of the Church" category, though Gregory is venerated there as a saint, known as Gregory the Dialogist — a comparatively direct and traceable line of living continuity across two traditions, on different formal terms in each, though whether and how to name it is the project lead's own call, not this document's. **Status: PENDING.**

## 2. Historical Boundaries

**Approximate Time Horizon:** c. 560–604 CE.

- **Beginning point (c. 560):** the census's own window reaches back roughly thirty years before Gregory's election (590), into his own formation as a Roman civic official (he served as urban prefect of Rome, *praefectus urbi*, in the early 570s, per the standard biographical record), then as a monk after converting his family's Caelian Hill estate into a monastery, and then, c. 579–c. 586, as papal representative (*apocrisiarius*) at the imperial court in Constantinople — verified directly against Gregory's own words in the Moralia's dedicatory epistle to Leander of Seville: *"When I knew you long since at Constantinople, my most blessed brother, at the time that I was kept there..."* This is a defensible beginning point under the Framework's Temporal Scope test because it marks the formation of the man whose pontificate this world reconstructs, not merely the start of the pontificate itself — the monastic conversion, the civic-administrative career, and the Constantinopolitan legation are all load-bearing for understanding the pope who later governed as he did: the *Moralia* was begun orally during the Constantinopolitan phase and completed and dedicated only later, well into his pontificate (Doc_02 §1), and that same Constantinopolitan phase is what makes Gregory, throughout his pontificate, a continuing, informed participant in the imperial church's own affairs, not a figure operating apart from it (see the World #6 contrast, §1 above and §6 below).
- **Ending point (604):** Gregory's death, closing the pontificate and this world's own window with it. Defensible without further argument — no comparable single later event competes for the boundary the way Chalcedon's ambiguous aftermath complicated World #6's own choice of 451.

**Geographic Centers:** Rome and its Sicilian and Italian estates; Constantinople, during Gregory's own pre-pontificate legation; the mission field reached through correspondence (Kent, under Anglo-Saxon Christianity's own separate census entry; Visigothic Spain; Merovingian Gaul).

- **Rome:** the seat of Gregory's own office, materially diminished from its imperial-era scale — repeated Lombard sieges and a much-reduced population are directly attested in the *Register* itself, not merely inferred from external history.
- **The Sicilian and Italian patrimonies:** the church's own landed estates, administered through the *Register*'s own extensive correspondence with its rectors — the material base that let Rome feed itself and its refugees when the old imperial grain supply (the *annona*) no longer reliably could.
- **Constantinople:** the site of Gregory's own c. 579–c. 586 legation — not a mission field described at a distance, but a place Gregory himself lived in and formed relationships from (his own friendship with Leander of Seville, later the dedicatee of the Moralia, began there), directly relevant to this world's own account (§1 above and §6 below) of Gregory's continuing engagement with, not absence from, the imperial church.
- **The mission field beyond:** reached only through Gregory's own letters, never visited by him in person within this world's window — correspondence with Æthelberht of Kent and Bertha concerning the mission Gregory sent under Augustine of Canterbury (596); with the Visigothic court following Reccared's own personal conversion from Homoian Arianism to Nicene Christianity (587) and its public ratification at the Third Council of Toledo (589); and with the Merovingian court, principally Queen Brunhild. These are already correctly held as `context`, not `tradition`, on the separate census entries they describe (Source Readiness Dossier §1) — this world's own boundary reaches only as far as Gregory's own voice, not into the mission fields' own internal formation.

**Cultural Environment:** Late-sixth/early-seventh-century Latin Italy under active military and demographic crisis — a world of siege, famine, and administrative improvisation rather than the settled imperial-elite culture World #6 reconstructs. Gregory's own formation straddles two registers rarely combined this directly elsewhere in the portfolio: Roman senatorial-administrative culture (his own family was aristocratic; his career began in civil office) and coenobitic monastic culture (his own conversion and, throughout his pontificate, his continued self-understanding as a monk pressed into pastoral service against his own preference — a tension named directly in his own writing, not read into it; see §5).

**Historical Pressures:**
- The Lombard invasion and ongoing occupation of much of Italy from 568 onward, the single most consequential external fact of this world's own window.
- Byzantine imperial authority in Italy reduced to a distant, underresourced Exarchate at Ravenna, unable reliably to defend Rome itself — the practical vacuum Gregory's own administration fills, directly relevant to this world's own distinctive contribution (§1).
- The unresolved Istrian ("Three Chapters") schism: the underlying imperial condemnation of the Three Chapters dates from 553 (the Second Council of Constantinople), but the actual rupture in communion followed Pope Vigilius's and Pope Pelagius I's own later assent to that condemnation (c. 554–557), not 553 itself, and it involved not only Aquileia and Istria/Venetia but also Liguria under the metropolitan of Milan. It remained fully open throughout Gregory's own pontificate, including his own recorded, ultimately unsuccessful efforts to bring the Istrian bishops back to communion (verified directly against `npnf212`, Doc_02 §1).
- Persistent material want: famine, plague (Gregory's own election followed a major outbreak in Rome, per the standard biographical record), and the logistics of feeding a city that could no longer feed itself.

**Historical Catalysts:** Gregory's election (590), following the death of Pope Pelagius II amid plague; the negotiation of truces and tribute with the Lombard king Agilulf; the dispatch of the mission to Kent under Augustine of Canterbury (596); Reccared's Nicene conversion (587) and Gregory's own correspondence marking it; Gregory's sustained protest against the Constantinopolitan patriarch John IV's use of the title "Ecumenical."

## 3. Distinct World Criteria

Applying the Framework's own four questions directly:

- **What recurring formation ecology exists?** An ecology of pastoral administration under material constraint: a bishop's office formed less by doctrinal contest (compare World #6) than by the daily, recurring demand to feed, defend, discipline, and correspond — with the *Pastoral Rule* standing as this world's own explicit, self-aware theory of what that office actually requires of the person holding it.
- **What recurring gravities exist (preliminary, subject to Doc_04)?** Provisionally: pastoral stewardship exercised as necessity rather than jurisdictional assertion; the subordination of contemplative/monastic vocation to active pastoral duty (Gregory's own recurring theme, *contemplata aliis tradere* — to hand over to others what has been contemplated); administrative indispensability as the practical ground of Roman authority in this specific window. Named here as candidates for Step 4 testing, not confirmed gravities.
- **What recurring patterns of worship, formation, interpretation, belonging, and authority exist?** Authority and Formation dominate, closely bound together in the *Pastoral Rule*'s own subject matter; Interpretation is substantial via the *Moralia*'s own allegorical-ascetical mode; Worship has real but more circumstantial material (Gregory's own later liturgical reputation requires careful confidence-tagging, per Step 0 §4.6); ordinary lay Formation Ecology, as with World #6, is thinner than the elite-authorial material — this world's surviving voice speaks from and about a bishop's office, not about how an ordinary sixth-century Roman layperson experienced formation day to day.
- **What distinguishes this world from adjacent worlds?** See §6 below.

## 4. Strand Determination

Per Constitution Article 21, strand is "a finding, never a presupposed universal schema," accountable to evidence, and "never assigned to satisfy an architectural preference for plurality."

**Finding: no plural strands identified — one continuous formation logic, expressed across several genres of one man's own writing.** World #6's own three-strand finding rested on three actors asserting authority on three genuinely different grounds (apostolic succession; imperial proximity; sacramental/moral independence). This world does not present a comparable case: the *Register*, the *Pastoral Rule*, the *Moralia*, and the Dialogues are four different genres — administrative, prescriptive, exegetical-ascetical, hagiographical — but every one of them is authored by the same office-holder asserting authority on the same ground throughout: Petrine/apostolic succession, exercised pastorally, under conditions that make administrative indispensability rather than jurisdictional contest the actual operative form that authority takes. Applying Article 21 honestly here means not manufacturing plurality where the evidence shows one continuous throughline expressed in several registers, exactly as the Article warns against.

**A named internal tension, distinguished here from a strand rather than elevated to one.** Gregory's own writing repeatedly names a real tension between his monastic, contemplative preference and the active demands of pastoral office — most directly in the *Moralia*'s own preface and echoed in the *Pastoral Rule*'s opening chapters, where he writes of the danger and burden of a contemplative being compelled into rule over others. This is well-documented in Gregory's own words, not inferred, and is a recurring theme of modern scholarship on him (Carole Straw, *Gregory the Great: Perfection in Imperfection*, Confidence B — not independently re-checked this session, cited for this specific, well-known thematic point only). It is named here as a real, load-bearing internal tension — a strong candidate for Doc_04's Tensional classification — but it is not a second strand under Article 21's own test, because it is not a competing claim about *where authority is grounded*; both the contemplative and the active-pastoral sides of the tension are voiced by the same man claiming the same Petrine, pastorally-exercised authority throughout. It is a tension internal to one office-holder's own vocation, not a division between two authority claims.

**A counter-example directly engaged, not assumed away — the abbot Equitius (Dialogues, Book I).** Round 1 review raised, correctly, whether this document's one-strand finding too quickly conflated single authorship with a single ground of authority, citing Gregory's own account of Equitius: an unordained abbot who preached and taught "not having received holy orders, nor yet licence of the Bishop of Rome," his authority instead resting on a vision commissioning him directly. This is real, and it names a genuinely different ground of authority — charismatic, vision-authorized, independent of ecclesiastical office — appearing within this world's own Native corpus. It does not, on examination, change this document's own strand finding, for a reason worth stating precisely rather than assumed: **Strand Determination asks what ground of authority *this world's own defining voice* — Gregory himself — claims for himself**, not what authority structures Gregory, as narrator, records other figures holding. Gregory himself never claims charismatic, vision-authorized authority of this kind for his own office anywhere in this world's Native corpus; his own claim remains Petrine and pastorally-exercised throughout, exactly as this document already argues.

**The episode itself, verified directly, strengthens rather than weakens this reading.** The episode continues (Dialogues, Book I, ~lines 2109–2204, verified): some of the Roman clergy complain to "the Bishop of this Apostolic see" that Equitius, lacking ordination, "presumeth to usurp unto himself the office of our Apostolical Lord," and press for him to be summoned to Rome "that he may taste of the severity of ecclesiastical discipline." The then-pope (not Gregory himself, who narrates the episode as a later successor; the interlocutor Peter is Gregory's own dialogue partner in the frame, not a participant in the story) consents and sends one Julianus to fetch Equitius — but Julianus's own messenger, sent ahead, is struck with such terror on merely approaching Equitius in the field that "he could scarce go upon his legs." The next morning a rider brings Julianus a letter "commanding him not to presume to molest or to draw the servant of God out of his monastery," because, the messenger explains, "the next night after his departure, the Pope was terribly frighted in a vision, for presuming to send for the man of God." The summons is withdrawn outright, on the pope's own word, on no ground but the vision itself. The episode shows the Roman see's own institutional authority genuinely yielding, in practice, to a charismatic holy man's own independent standing — a real, concrete instance of the tension this document names, not a hypothetical one. This sharpens, rather than merely permits, the Tensional-gravity candidate below: it is not only that Gregory himself records and respects charismatic authority he does not personally claim, but that this world's own record shows the Roman see's own institutional authority, in at least one specific case, visibly deferring to it.

What Equitius's own case genuinely establishes is not a second strand of this world's own authority claim, but a recurring feature of this world's own *ecology* — charismatic, monastic holiness as a recognized, real, and theologically significant alternate authority mode that this world's own record shows both Gregory personally and the Roman see institutionally taking seriously, even while neither one adopts it as their own office's own ground. This is named here as real ecological texture, carried forward to Doc_04 as a further candidate Tensional gravity (institutional/Petrine authority acknowledging, and in this one attested case yielding to, without adopting, charismatic/monastic authority) alongside the contemplative/active tension already named above — not folded silently into the one-strand finding, and not allowed to expand it without the evidence to support that expansion.

**Governing consequence for later steps:** Doc_04's cross-strand gravity testing (per the Framework, formally run at Step 4) does not apply the way it did for World #6's three strands, since there is only one strand here — but the contemplative/active tension and the institutional/charismatic-authority tension named above should both be tested directly as Tensional-gravity candidates in their own right.

## 5. Preliminary Forces Identification

Per the Forces Framework's Step 1 requirement, this is orientation for Step 2's scope, not the complete forces analysis compiled at Step 8.

**What generated this world:** Gregory's own election to the papacy (590) at a moment of acute crisis — plague, famine, and an unresolved, decades-old Lombard occupation — combined with a formation (civic-administrative, then monastic) that made him unusually equipped to respond to exactly that crisis administratively rather than only theologically.

**What larger world was it embedded in:** the wreck of the Western Roman imperial order, nominally still governed from Constantinople through the Exarchate of Ravenna but unable, in practice, reliably to defend or provision Rome itself — the specific condition (§1, §2) that gives this world its own distinctive shape, in direct contrast to World #6's embeddedness in a still-functioning, contested-but-present imperial partnership.

**What was it responding to:** the Lombard invasion and its consequences directly (famine, siege, captivity, a fractured Italian political landscape); more distantly, the general administrative and demographic collapse of urban Italy across the sixth century, of which Gregory's own pontificate is responding to symptoms (a starving city, an underpaid clergy, undefended estates) rather than causes.

**What was it refusing:** Lombard, and more broadly Homoian/Arian, religious and political domination of Italy, without refusing negotiation or coexistence with the Lombards as a political fact (Gregory's own letters negotiate truces and tribute directly, a pragmatic stance distinct from a doctrinal refusal); the Constantinopolitan patriarch's claim to the title "Ecumenical," which Gregory read as an overreach threatening the collegial equality of the major sees. Gregory's own self-designation "servant of the servants of God" (*servus servorum Dei*) is not confidently read as a deliberate rebuke of that claim: the formula heads Gregory's very first extant letter as pope (Epistle I.1, dated September 590), five years before the "Universal Bishop" dispute (595), and the NPNF apparatus's own endnote at that locus records that the designation had already been used by Pope Damasus and by Augustine before Gregory. This document carries the formula as a self-designation of genuinely Contested significance, attested only four times in the Registrum itself (Ep. I.1, I.36, VI.51, XIII.1, per the same endnote) — the endnote's own editor suggests Gregory "may have been the first to use it habitually" and that its rarity in the surviving Registrum likely reflects how the Roman copies were kept, since it also appears in Bede's own record of the English-mission letters where the Registrum's own copies lack it. Real and worth noting either way, but not confidently read as a rebuke of any specific later claim.

## 6. World Continuity & Distinction

**What this world inherits from prior and adjacent worlds:** the Roman primacy claims World #6's own Strand A (Damasus through Leo I) established are this world's own direct institutional inheritance — Gregory governs an office whose jurisdictional self-understanding World #6 already reconstructs the consolidation of, though on different practical grounds (§1 above). Gregory's own monastic formation draws, at further remove, on the coenobitic tradition this project's Desert Monasticism world reconstructs at its origin, and directly on the Benedictine Rule specifically — his own Dialogues Book II is, on the current state of scholarship, the earliest and only surviving connected account of Benedict of Nursia's life, making this world's own relationship to `early-benedictine-italian-monasticism` (not yet built) a direct transmission link, not merely a thematic adjacency.

**What this world transmits to subsequent worlds/history:** the institutional and administrative pattern of the medieval papacy more broadly (well beyond this project's own window); the *Pastoral Rule*'s own long reception (Carolingian episcopal legislation; Alfred the Great's Old English translation, three centuries on); the Gregorian mission to Kent, which `anglo-saxon-christianity`'s own build (not yet started) would need to treat as an inheritance from this world, not an independent origin; the Benedictine Rule's own wider diffusion, promoted in part through the Dialogues' own popularity, a transmission question `early-benedictine-italian-monasticism` will need to address from its own side. None of these continuations is this document's own to reconstruct — noted for orientation only.

**Distinctness from adjacent worlds, carried forward as binding boundaries:**
- **World #6 (Imperial and Juridical Christianity, c. 312–451, built):** distinguished on substantive, not merely chronological, grounds, in the corrected, narrower form developed at §1 above (the Distinctive Contribution paragraph) — administrative/pastoral necessity layered onto a continuing but locally incapable imperial partnership, versus World #6's own jurisdictional consolidation inside an actively functioning one (per `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §3, B3).
- **`early-benedictine-italian-monasticism` (not yet built):** overlap "in person rather than in kind" (census `relationsSummary`) — real and direct via Dialogues Book II specifically, requiring active vigilance in Doc_02 rather than an assumed-obvious boundary, per Step 0 §3 (B3) and §4.3.
- **`byzantine-imperial-church-justinianic`, `anglo-saxon-christianity`, `merovingian-gallic-christianity` (built or on record):** each already correctly holds Gregory's own letters as `context` rather than `tradition`, per the census's own `relationsSummary` (§1 above) — no overlap risk; this world's own boundary is Gregory's own voice, and only that. (Surviving Donatism's own link is via a separate source, the Turchi Latin letters, named at §7 item 5 below — distinct from this list.)
