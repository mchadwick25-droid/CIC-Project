## 1. Formation World Identity

**World Name:** Imperial and Juridical Christianity.

**Short Description:** The formation of Christianity as a claimant, instrument, and contested partner of Roman imperial and juridical power — from Constantine's alliance with the church (312) through Ambrose of Milan's assertions of episcopal authority over an emperor, to the Roman episcopate's own self-consolidation of jurisdictional primacy under Damasus through Leo I, closing at the Council of Chalcedon (451).

**Core Identity:** This is a world of office-holders, not congregants — emperors, court bishops, metropolitan bishops, and the Roman episcopate itself, formed by and forming the question of where final authority over the church actually resides: the emperor, an ecumenical council, or the bishop of Rome. Its formation logic is juridical and institutional before it is devotional: canon, decretal, conciliar acts, and imperial edict are this world's characteristic literary output, in a way true of none of the other eight worlds in this project's current portfolio.

**Distinctive Contribution:** No other confirmed world in this project's portfolio reconstructs Christianity's own alliance with and juridical consolidation of worldly political power as its organizing subject — the church governing *through* and *by* the state's own authority, not merely operating in the state's presence. Where other worlds ask how a community worshiped, believed, or endured, this world asks how a community governed itself and was governed — and how those two questions collided, repeatedly, across 140 years. This is a narrower claim than "reconstructs Christianity's encounter with political power" as such: World #4 (Donatism, §6 below) reconstructs a different, opposite answer to a related question — refusal of that same power's legitimacy from inside a schism, not consolidation of it from inside communion — and is not this document's rival for uniqueness so much as its clarifying contrast.

**Living Tradition Status:** Not confirmed. Per Constitution Article 29 and this project's established practice (see `worlds/syr/Open_Gaps_Tracking.md`, item 10), Living Tradition Status confirmation is a project-lead act this document cannot perform on its own behalf. Noted for the record, not decided here: this world's core content — Roman episcopal jurisdictional primacy under Damasus through Leo I — has the most direct, traceable living-tradition continuity of any world yet confirmed in this portfolio, running forward into the present-day papacy's own claimed lineage; its Constantinopolitan strand (§4 below) is comparably continuous with Eastern Orthodoxy's own historical self-understanding of Constantinople's status. **Status: PENDING.**

## 2. Historical Boundaries

**Approximate Time Horizon:** c. 312–451 CE.

- **Beginning point (312):** the Battle of the Milvian Bridge and Constantine's subsequent alliance with the church, formalized in the Edict of Milan (313) — the event that first makes ecclesiastical office a form of state-adjacent power at all. This is a defensible beginning point under the Framework's own Temporal Scope test ("what beginning point is appropriate?") because it marks a genuine ecological rupture, not an arbitrary date: before 312, church office carried no juridical weight the Roman state was bound to recognize; after it, that changed permanently, for every subsequent phase this world reconstructs.
- **Ending point (451):** the Council of Chalcedon. Defensible under the same test on two independent grounds, not merely because Leo I's own role there is the world's stated closing episode: (1) Chalcedon is the last of this window's ecumenical councils at which a still-nominally-unified imperial church convenes and legislates as though universal juridical consensus were achievable — its own aftermath (§5 below) demonstrates that this was no longer true; (2) the process of Western imperial collapse this world's Western strand ultimately depends on is already visibly underway within the window itself (Alaric's sack of Rome, 410, a documented internal shock) and reaches its conventional terminus a generation later (the last Western emperor, Romulus Augustulus, deposed 476) — 451 sits inside that unraveling process, not before it starts. Ending the window in 451 rather than reaching for a later date (461, Leo I's death, this project's own earlier working boundary per the Step 0 Conclusion) keeps the world's temporal scope anchored to a real ecological event rather than a single figure's biography.

**Geographic Centers:** Rome, Constantinople, Milan.

- **Rome:** seat of the bishop whose jurisdictional claims this world's Damasus-through-Leo phase consolidates; increasingly a political backwater relative to the imperial court by the later 4th century, a fact directly relevant to why ecclesiastical primacy became available as compensation for lost political centrality (§5, Cell 2A).
- **Constantinople:** dedicated 330 as Constantine's new imperial capital — "New Rome." Its own bishop's claimed ecclesiastical rank rises in direct proportion to the city's political rank across this window (Council of Constantinople 381 Canon 3; Chalcedon 451 Canon 28), making it not merely a third geographic center but the origin point of this world's second internal strand (§4).
- **Milan:** the West's principal imperial residence for large stretches of the 4th century (Valentinian I, Gratian, Valentinian II, and periodically Theodosius all held court there), which is precisely why Ambrose's episcopate there — not Rome's — becomes the site of the West's first sustained direct confrontation between an emperor and a bishop.

**Cultural Environment:** Late Roman and early Byzantine imperial elite culture, spanning both linguistic halves of the empire — a feature distinctive among this project's confirmed worlds. Rome and Milan operate in Latin; Constantinople operates predominantly in Greek. Participation in this world is not defined by ordinary lay life but by proximity to imperial or episcopal office: senatorial aristocracy, court functionaries, metropolitan clergy, and — increasingly across this window — the episcopal office itself remade partly in the image of Roman civic magistracy (fixed jurisdictional territory, appellate authority, written decretals functioning as a body of law).

**Historical Pressures:**
- The empire's own political fragmentation: division of imperial administration after Theodosius I's death (395) into functionally separate Western and Eastern courts; the West's accelerating military and administrative weakening across the 5th century, visible within this window in Alaric's sack of Rome (410).
- The Homoian ("Arian") imperial establishment: under Constantius II (337–361) and Valens (364–378), the *imperial church itself* — not merely a rival minority — was Homoian for long stretches of the exact period this world reconstructs (see §5, Cell 2A, and the disclosure obligation carried forward from Step 0 §4.1).
- Recurring Christological controversy requiring conciliar resolution: Nicaea (325), Constantinople (381), Ephesus (431), the "Robber Synod" of Ephesus (449, whose verdict Chalcedon overturns), and Chalcedon itself (451).

**Historical Catalysts:** Milvian Bridge and the Edict of Milan (312/313); Nicaea (325); the Edict of Thessalonica (380), making Nicene Christianity the empire's establishment religion under Theodosius I; Ambrose's confrontations with Theodosius over the Thessalonica massacre (390) and the Callinicum synagogue affair (388); Damasus's assertion of Roman primacy claims (366–384); Leo I's Tome to Flavian and his role at Chalcedon (451).

## 3. Distinct World Criteria

Applying the Framework's own four questions directly:

- **What recurring formation ecology exists?** An ecology of office and jurisdiction: bishops and emperors repeatedly negotiating, asserting, and contesting where final authority over doctrine and discipline resides. Canon law, conciliar process, and apostolic-succession claims (Petrine primacy for Rome specifically) are this world's recurring formative material, in a way not shared by any other confirmed world.
- **What recurring gravities exist (preliminary, subject to Doc_04)?** Provisionally: juridical primacy-claiming; church-state alliance and its limits; orthodoxy-enforcement exercised through, not merely alongside, imperial power. These are named here as candidates for Step 4 testing, not as confirmed gravities — Doc_01 does not perform Doc_04's work.
- **What recurring patterns of worship, formation, interpretation, belonging, and authority exist?** Authority dominates; the other four are present but structurally secondary. Worship has real, documented content (Ambrose's introduction of antiphonal psalm-singing and Latin hymnody at Milan, directly tied to the 386 basilica standoff — Augustine, *Confessions* 9.7), but this world does not reconstruct ordinary believer formation with the depth Desert Monasticism or Post-Apostolic House-Church Christianity can — a limitation already named in Step 0 §3 (B2) and carried forward as binding on Doc_05.
- **What distinguishes this world from adjacent worlds?** See §6 below.

## 4. Strand Determination

Per Constitution Article 21: strand is "a finding, never a presupposed universal schema," accountable to evidence, and "never assigned to satisfy an architectural preference for plurality." This determination is made here, for the first time — Step 0 correctly deferred it as Step 1's own work (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §2, A5).

**The question this section must not confuse itself about:** the Step 0 Conclusion's own merge reasoning — not reopened here — establishes that this world's three named components (Constantinian alliance; Ambrose's confrontations; Damasus-through-Leo consolidation) are "sequential, causally-linked phases of one process," which is the reasoning for why they form *one world* rather than three. That is a temporal-development finding (Article 15, Historical Development and Instability), not a strand finding. Treating those same three sequential phases as three parallel "strands" would misuse Article 21 exactly as it warns against — imposing plurality on what the evidence already shows to be a single developing throughline. Strand Determination asks a different question: does this one, developing world *also* contain a genuinely simultaneous, evidence-supported divergence in authority structure or ecological orientation, independent of its sequential development?

**Finding: strands identified with named evidence — three.** Step 0 itself (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §2, A5) flagged an illustrative candidate structure — "an Eusebian/moderate-episcopal strand versus a Damasus-Leo/Roman-primacy strand versus Ambrose's confrontational-episcopal strand" — as Step 1's own work to test, not Step 0's to decide. Ambrose's own authority claim, on the documentary record, is not grounded in Petrine succession at all (he was bishop of Milan, not Rome) — it rests on sacramental and moral leverage (the threat and use of excommunication) and a general claim that the church's spiritual authority is independent of, and binds, imperial command (his own recorded assertion, preached against Auxentius: the emperor is within the Church, not over it). That is a different ground of authority than either of the primacy-among-sees claims below, not a variant of one of them.

- **Strand A — Roman/Apostolic-Primacy strand.** Authority grounded in claimed apostolic succession from Peter, exercised through office, decretal, and canon law, independent of a given see's political proximity to the emperor. Evidenced across Damasus's assertion of Roman primacy (366–384) through Leo I's Tome to Flavian and his conduct at and after Chalcedon.
- **Strand B — Constantinopolitan/Imperial-Proximity strand.** Authority grounded in a see's political proximity to imperial power, not apostolic succession. Its theological seed is visible earlier than 381, in Eusebius's own court theology — Eusebius reports Constantine describing himself as a "bishop of those outside [the Church]" (*Life of Constantine* 4.24, Documented as a textual claim; what it reveals about imperial self-understanding versus Eusebius's own framing is more properly Contested, and is not resolved here) — a vision of imperially-adjacent religious authority this strand's later conciliar claims make institutionally explicit. Evidenced directly at the institutional level: Canon 3 of the Council of Constantinople (381), granting that see primacy of honor after Rome specifically because it is the New Rome (rendered here as a close paraphrase of the Greek — τὰ πρεσβεῖα τῆς τιμῆς — rather than a quotation of any one numbered published translation, which vary in wording, e.g. Percival/NPNF renders it "prerogative of honor"); Canon 28 of Chalcedon (451), extending the same reasoning, directly and explicitly rejected by Leo I, who refused to ratify it, objecting on the documentary record that ecclesiastical rank should not follow civil rank.
- **Strand C — Ambrosian/Sacramental-Independence strand.** Authority grounded in a bishop's sacramental and moral leverage over any ruler, regardless of that bishop's own see's rank or apostolic pedigree — a claim about the church's independence from imperial command as such, not a claim about which see outranks which. Evidenced in Ambrose's confrontations with Theodosius (the 390 response to the Thessalonica massacre; the 388 Callinicum affair) and his 386 basilica standoff against the Homoian imperial court of Justina and Valentinian II. This strand does not persist as an independent claim-making stream to this world's 451 close — no later actor in this window asserts primacy on Ambrosian sacramental-leverage grounds specifically — but its historical legacy is real and distinct from Strand A's own ground: later Western assertions of ecclesiastical authority against secular rulers draw on Ambrose's model, without that model becoming identical to, or merging into, Rome's specifically Petrine claim. Named as its own strand, evidenced for the phase where it is attested, rather than silently absorbed into Strand A's account of itself.

All three strands share a common root in the initiating Constantinian alliance (§5, Cell 1A/1B below). This finding satisfies Article 21's own evidentiary bar for each strand independently: each rests on a distinct, named ground of authority (apostolic succession; imperial proximity; sacramental/moral independence), each is supported by primary evidence specific to it (Damasus/Leo's decretals and Leo's Tome; Canon 3, Canon 28, and Leo's rejection of the latter; Ambrose's own recorded confrontations and sermon), and none is inferred merely to satisfy a preference for plurality — Strand C in particular is retained despite not persisting to 451, because Article 21 tests for a meaningfully distinct pattern within the world, not for persistence through the world's full temporal span.

**Governing consequence for later steps:** per the Framework, cross-strand gravity testing occurs formally at Step 4. A candidate gravity that holds across all three strands (for example, the shared conviction that ecclesiastical authority *should* be juridically fixed and defensible, even where the three strands disagree sharply about its ground) is a stronger candidate for the world's irreducible core than one that holds only within one or two.

## 5. Preliminary Forces Identification

Per the Forces Framework's Step 1 requirement, this is orientation for Step 2's scope, not the complete forces analysis compiled at Step 8.

**What generated this world:** Constantine's political and military victory at the Milvian Bridge (312) and the subsequent legal toleration of Christianity (Edict of Milan, 313), converting Christian episcopal office from a persecuted status into a form of state-recognized, and increasingly state-adjacent, authority.

**What larger world was it embedded in:** the late Roman imperial state itself — its legal, administrative, and military apparatus — and, within Christianity, the broader Nicene-trajectory church whose eligibility this world's own Step 0 confirmation already tested. For long stretches of this exact window, the "larger world" this world was embedded in was not securely Nicene: under Constantius II and Valens, Homoian Christianity *was* the imperial church (Step 0 §4.1, binding on Doc_02).

**What was it responding to:** the preceding era of persecution (the Diocletianic persecution, ending 311–313) and the unprecedented practical problem of maintaining doctrinal coherence across a newly-patronized, empire-wide church — hence the conciliar mechanism this world relies on throughout. Rome specifically was responding to its own declining political centrality as emperors resided elsewhere (Milan, then increasingly Ravenna and Constantinople), a condition under which ecclesiastical primacy became available as a form of continuity Rome's political standing could no longer supply on its own.

**What was it refusing:** for the Nicene actors this world centers, Homoian/Arian imperially-favored theology; for Ambrose specifically, unlimited imperial authority over the church (a refusal of totalizing Caesaropapism, asserted from within the Latin West); for Leo specifically, the reduction of ecclesiastical rank to a function of civil/political rank (his rejection of Canon 28) — three distinct refusals, at three different points in this world's development, but a recognizable common thread: this world's own actors consistently refuse to let a purely external, political logic be the final word on ecclesiastical authority, even while depending on political power to establish and defend that authority in the first place. That dependence-and-refusal tension is itself likely candidate material for Doc_04.

## 6. World Continuity & Distinction

**What this world inherits from prior worlds:** the emerging monepiscopal office structure already visible in Post-Apostolic/Sub-Apostolic House-Church Christianity (World #1, c. 70–200) is this world's own starting institutional form, developed rather than invented here; the Nicene theological consensus this world's own actors help finalize and then defend draws on doctrinal work already underway before 312 (including, at its Alexandrian edge, work adjacent to World #2's own catechetical tradition, though this world does not reconstruct that adjacency in depth).

**What this world transmits to subsequent worlds/history:** the Roman episcopal primacy claims this world's Strand A consolidates continue directly into the medieval and modern papacy, well beyond this project's Phase One (70–451 CE) window; the Constantinopolitan primacy claims of Strand B continue into Byzantine and Eastern Orthodox ecclesiology on a comparable trajectory; Strand C's own legacy is narrower and less institutionally continuous — not a see's claim to rank at all, but a model of ecclesiastical independence from secular command that later Western assertions of church authority against rulers draw on without it becoming its own persisting institutional lineage the way Strands A and B do (§4). None of the three continuations is this document's to reconstruct — noted for orientation, not developed.

**Distinctness from adjacent worlds, confirmed and carried forward as binding boundaries, not redrawn here:**
- **World #8 (Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity, not yet built — Cyprian and Augustine):** already confirmed distinct in the Step 0 Conclusion and reaffirmed in `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §3 — non-overlapping authority structure (juridical/state-adjacent here; pastoral/sacramental/territorial-flock-care there), orthogonality to state power, temporal overlap rather than sequence.
- **World #5 (Cappadocian Nicene Pastoral-Monastic Tradition, c. 360s–380s, Cappadocia/Anatolia):** adjacency examined in `Archive/Syriac-Build-2026-07/CiC_Coach3_Step0_Critique_2026-07-06.md` and adopted in `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §3 — the only overlap point (Gregory of Nazianzus's brief, failed Constantinople tenure) is not a power-consolidation narrative the way it is for this world's own figures; World #5's gravity is theological-ascetic, this world's is institutional-power. Both worlds share the Homoian-recentering disclosure obligation (Step 0 §4.1), which is a shared *evidentiary* obligation, not evidence of shared *formation ecology*.
- **World #4 (Donatism, c. 312–430s, North Africa):** already examined in `Archive/Syriac-Build-2026-07/CiC_Coach3_Step0_Critique_2026-07-06.md` and adopted here, credited rather than re-derived (the same discipline applied to the World #5 comparison above): *"These are two sides of the same imperial-religious question: #6 is the church that aligns with and juridically consolidates around state power; #4 is the church that permanently splits from the mainstream specifically in refusal of that same power."* No overlap risk — a point of productive contrast, not a boundary question requiring vigilance the way World #5 does — flagged here as context Doc_02 may find useful when situating this world's own internal "refusal of totalizing Caesaropapism" thread (§5 above, and Strand C in §4) against a starker external counterexample: this world refuses total imperial control of the church while remaining inside imperial communion; Donatism refuses the same thing by leaving it.
