## 1. Distinct World Criteria

What makes this a distinct formation world, not merely "17th-century
Russian Orthodoxy in general":

- **A single, datable rupture with a named cause.** In the 1650s, Patriarch
  Nikon corrected the Russian liturgical books and certain ritual
  practices against contemporary Greek Orthodox usage. The sign of the
  cross made with three fingers instead of two is **[Documented]**,
  verified directly against this world's own vendored source (§9, the
  pp. 120-121 dialogue). The spelling of the name of Jesus (Isus →
  Iisus), the direction of processions around a church, and the number
  of prosphora used in the liturgy are **[Widely Accepted]** as part of
  the standard historical account of Nikon's corrections, not yet
  independently verified against a vendored primary source. A
  substantial part of the Russian church refused these changes as
  innovations rather than corrections. The Moscow council of 1666-1667
  anathematized those who refused — **[Widely Accepted]**, on the
  census's own record and the Decision-Log's own Era 8 gate entry
  (§2.1). This is a single, nameable event with a named cause (Nikon's
  reforms) and a named consequence (anathema), not a diffuse regional or
  generational drift.
- **A recurring, sharply evidenced self-understanding: fidelity to
  inherited ritual practice as a central part of the case — though, per
  §9, not the whole of it.** Archpriest Avvakum, in his own dialogue with
  the Eastern patriarchs recorded in his own autobiography, invokes "the
  tradition of our holy fathers" and names four authorities for the
  two-fingered sign of the cross, quoted exactly as each vendored file
  prints them:

  - English (p. 121): "Meletina of Antioch, Theodoret, the Heart Bishop of
    Cyrene, Peter of Damascus and Maxim the Greek."
  - Russian (same locus): "Мелетия антиохийскаго и Феодора Блаженнаго,
    епископа киринейскаго, Петра Дамаскина и Максима Грека" — "Meletios
    of Antioch and Theodore the Blessed, bishop of Cyrene, Peter of
    Damascus and Maxim the Greek."

  Read against each other, the two witnesses name the same **four**
  authorities: Meletios/"Meletina" of Antioch **[Documented]**; Theodore/
  "Theodoret," bishop of Cyrene — "Cyrene" is the faithful reading of
  Russian *киринейскаго*, and the English "Heart" is almost certainly an
  OCR misreading of "Blest"/"Blessed" (Russian *Блаженнаго*); Peter of
  Damascus; and Maxim the Greek. Whether the English translators'
  "Theodoret" for the Russian "Феодора" (Theodore) is their own rendering
  choice, a transliteration slip, or reflects a different manuscript
  reading is **[Contested]** — and the identity of the "Феодорит"/
  "Феодор" whom Old Believers cited as authority for the two-fingered
  sign (the pseudepigraphic *Slovo Feodoritovo*) is itself a live
  question in the secondary literature, registered here as a
  `contested_claim` candidate for Doc_02/Doc_03.
- **A recurring pattern of authority, suffering, and testimony.** Exile,
  underground imprisonment, and execution for refusing the corrected rite
  recur across the movement's own record — Avvakum's own exile to Siberia
  (1653-1664) and his final fourteen years imprisoned in an earthen pit at
  Pustozersk before his burning in 1682; the eight-year siege and fall of
  the Solovetsky Monastery (1668-1676); the mass self-immolations of the
  1670s-1680s. These are not isolated incidents but a recurring pattern
  the movement's own literature — a martyrology genre, by its own later
  practitioners' own naming (Semyon Denisov's own histories of the
  Solovki fathers and sufferers, and the Vyg community's *Pomorskie
  otvety* of 1723, whose primary author is Andrei Denisov, with Trifon
  Petrov and Semyon Denisov as participants) — treats as constitutive of
  what the movement understands itself to be.
- **What distinguishes this world from its nearest neighbor.** From the
  official Synodal Russian church (Atlas entry unbuilt as of this
  writing), this world is the ritual-and-textual position the 1666
  council's own anathema was directed against, not the anathematizing
  body. This world (VII.7) is a separate Atlas entry from the Synodal
  church's own entry, already banked before the Era 8 gate pass that
  drafted the Synodal church's own entry alongside it: the 2026-08-02
  Era 7 Frozen entry records "NOT written: VI.24→VII.4, VI.23→VII.7
  (era 8's)," and the same entry's own forward-reference note names "Old
  Believers at era 8" among Era 8's banked flags (§2.2). §9 states
  plainly why the ritual/doctrinal distinction between these two
  entries is real, and narrower than a purely ritual dispute.

## 2. Temporal Scope

### 2.1 Beginning point: 1666, the Moscow council's anathema

The census's own `start` field gives 1666. Nikon's own liturgical
corrections began circulating from the mid-1650s (the printed service
books of 1653-1656), and resistance to them predates the council — but
1666-1667 is the point at which refusal to accept the corrections became
a formally anathematized position rather than one live option among
others inside the church, which is the boundary this world's own identity
turns on (§1: a single, named rupture). **[Widely Accepted]**, on the
census's own record; this document's own vendored source does not carry
an independent, dated account of the council itself (Avvakum's
autobiography narrates his own life and exile, not the council's own
proceedings as an institutional event) — a genuine limit of this world's
current library, carried to Doc_02 and `Open_Gaps_Tracking.md`.

### 2.2 Ending point: 1815 — the Era 8 portfolio boundary, not an argued
movement-specific closing point

Era 8 is fleet-scoped as 1650-1815, per a forward-reference note at the
close of the immediately preceding Era 7 Frozen entry
(`Ministry/Features/Atlas-World-Map/Decision-Log.md`, 2026-08-02):
"Next: A1.E8 (1650–1815) with its banked forward flags... Old Believers
at era 8..." — this world is named directly, confirming it was already
in view when this boundary was set. The Era 8 gate's own Frozen entry
(2026-08-03, "ERA 8 FROZEN by Mark") separately records that this gate
pass "replaced" a "round-1800 artifact cluster" across several Era 8
entries "with honest 1815 caps" — i.e., 1815 is the fleet's own
portfolio-level closing year for the whole era, applied across multiple
movements at the same gate pass, not a date independently argued from
this movement's own history.

Within that boundary, the nearest genuinely Old-Believer-relevant event is
**Edinoverie's establishment in 1800** — the first formal reconciliation
mechanism between the Synodal church and old-rite worship, permitting
communities to keep the pre-Nikonian rite under Synodal episcopal
oversight. The census's own `statusDescription` names this as giving the
mechanical 1815 cap a substantive nearby anchor. Edinoverie (1800) is the
real, substantive event nearest the window's close; 1815 itself is the
administrative boundary — a distinction Doc_02 and later documents
should preserve rather than collapse.

**Explicitly out of scope, as a later development:** the priestly Old
Believers' restoration of their own episcopate, the Belokrinitsa
hierarchy, in 1846 — thirty-one years after this window's own close.

### 2.3 What remains internal vs. what indicates transition

**Internal to this world, across the whole 1666-1815 span:** the refusal
of the corrected rite as the movement's own constitutive act; martyrdom
and suffering under state persecution as a recurring, expected pattern;
and the movement's own literature of self-witness (autobiography,
petition, martyrology) as the primary way it records itself, from
Avvakum's own Life (c. 1673) through Denisov's histories of Solovki and
the Vyg community's Pomorian Answers (1723).

**Indicates transition, within the window:**

- **1668-1676 — the Solovetsky Monastery siege and fall.** The monastery's
  own fifth petition (1667) is, per the census's own sourcing note, "the
  fullest early statement of the Old Believer case," and its fall by
  betrayal in January 1676, with the tsar's own death a week later, was
  read by the movement itself as judgment. Neither the petition nor a
  siege account is vendored (Doc_02 §3): **[Widely Accepted]**, on
  secondary works named in the census (Crummey; Michels), not
  independently re-checked this session.
- **1670s-1680s — the mass self-immolations, and the movement's own
  internal disagreement about them.** Not a single settled practice but a
  contested one within the movement's own record. Avvakum's own
  Pustozersk epistles are cited (census; not yet independently verified
  against a vendored file) as approving the practice; Evfrosin's
  *Otrazitel'noe pisanie o novoizobretennom puti samoubiistvennykh
  smertei* (1691) is a contemporary Old Believer tract arguing against
  it. Self-immolation is treated here as a real, disputed practice
  within the movement's own record, not a univocal position the whole
  movement held — the single most important framing point for Doc_09's
  later story work. Death-toll figures (e.g. the ~2,700 figure at
  Paleostrovsky Monastery in 1687) are **[Contested]**: state reports,
  Old Believer martyrologies, and hostile accounts "agree on the fact
  and disagree on the numbers," per the census's own sourcing note.
- **1723 — the Pomorian Answers.** The Vyg community's formal, written
  self-defense (bespopovtsy), primarily authored by Andrei Denisov,
  marks the point at which the priestless position moves from practical
  improvisation to an articulated, systematic theological argument. Not
  yet vendored (Doc_02 §3).
- **1800 — Edinoverie**, already discussed at §2.2, is also a genuine
  internal transition: the point at which formal reconciliation with the
  Synodal church, on terms preserving the old rite, becomes possible for
  the first time within this window.

## 3. Geographic Scope

**Core:** Central and northern Muscovite Russia at the movement's own
founding — Moscow (the 1666-1667 council; Avvakum's own early ministry
and disputations), and the Russian north — the White Sea coast and the
Solovetsky Monastery, the Vyg community on Lake Onega's own watershed.
The census's own `region` field states "Russian north, Siberia."
**[Widely Accepted]** on the census's own record.

**Siberia**, specifically the Dauria region beyond Lake Baikal and the
Tobolsk-Yeniseysk corridor, enters this world's own geography directly
through Avvakum's own exile there (1653-1664, per the census's own
account of his return to Moscow), narrated at length in his own
autobiography — this is not a secondary or inferred detail but the
setting of a large fraction of the Life's own narrative. **[Documented]**,
verified directly against the vendored file (the exile narrative runs
continuously across a large portion of the autobiography's own middle
section, including the Markovna passage at p. 80, discussed at §9).

**Expansion within window:** the movement's own later dispersal into
Siberia (as ongoing refuge, not only Avvakum's own imposed exile), the
Baltic communities (Vetka, later dispersed), and — per the census's own
`legacy` field, describing the movement's continuing life well past this
window's own close — eventual communities as far as Alaska, Oregon, and
South America. The far dispersal (post-1815) is outside this world's own
window (§2.2); the Siberian/northern core is the geography this world's
own construction should center on.

## 4. Cultural Scope

**Language:** Russian and Church Slavonic — the liturgical and literary
register of the movement's own self-record. Avvakum's autobiography is
written in "the plain, angry, comic Russian of speech rather than of
books," per the census's own characterization (`documentedStories[0]`)
— **[Widely Accepted]**, a project-internal characterization rather than
a claim independently verified against the vendored preface or edition
matter. A genuinely distinctive register within this world's own
literature is directly evidenced, though: a deliberately plain, spoken
idiom set against the literary Church Slavonic of official church
writing.

**An edition finding, established by direct comparison of both vendored
witnesses: the 1924 Harrison & Mirrlees English translation omits
Avvakum's own plain-speech apologia from an otherwise positionally
aligned opening.** Both witnesses' openings begin with the same
Epiphanius attribution, close that paragraph with "Amen," and are
immediately followed by an identical next paragraph (the Holy Trinity
invocation) — so the English opening is the same passage as the
Russian's, not a different one, with Avvakum's own declaration on plain
speech and "philosophical verses," and his Pauline citation, dropped out
of the middle of it. A search of the whole English file for any
equivalent of that declaration returns nothing; it is not relocated
elsewhere in the translation. **[Widely Accepted]**, on this document's
own direct comparison of both files.

The Russian text of the omitted passage, quoted exactly as the vendored
file prints it, offered here to support the edition-comparison finding
above (an original-language text is a second witness under this
project's own sourcing rule — usable to cross-check an English
rendering, not to carry a substantive claim on its own):

> "не позазрите просторечию нашему, понеже люблю свой русской природной
> язык, виршами философскими не обык речи красить, понеже не словес
> красных бог слушает, но дел наших хощет."

In substance — a working gloss offered only to support the
edition-comparison finding above, not a free-standing translation:
Avvakum asks the reader not to scorn his plain speech, since he loves
his own native Russian tongue and is not in the habit of decorating his
speech with philosophical verses (*вирши философские*), because God
does not listen to fine words but wants deeds. **A disclosed hazard for
any future quotation of this file: the vendored Russian text carries
roughly ninety bracketed modern-Russian editorial glosses interpolated
directly into the running sentences** (e.g. `[правое]`, `[потому что]`,
`[так]`) — a modernized reading edition's own apparatus, not Avvakum's
words; any future quotation must strip them. See Doc_02 §7 for the full
disclosure, including why this file's own `orv` (Old East Slavic)
language tag is looser than its modernized orthography actually
supports.

**Material and visual culture**, per the census's own `legacy` field: old
icon-painting styles, znamenny chant, and manuscript-copying practices
that the official church had modernized away survived specifically in
Old Believer communities and were later recovered from them by
collectors and scholars. **[Widely Accepted]**, not yet independently
verified against a vendored source.

## 5. World Separation Criteria

This world is separated from the Synodal Russian church (its immediate
institutional origin and ongoing adversary) by the single ritual-and-
partial-textual rupture named at §1 and §9. It is separated from
`cappadocian-nicene-pastoral-monastic-tradition` — the one other Built &
Live world in the same "Greek East & Orthodoxy" lane — by century,
empire, and register: fourth-century Cappadocia's own formation logic is
Trinitarian-doctrinal, this world's is ritual-and-authority, and no
figure, text, or controversy is shared between them. No genuine
cross-world overlap or boundary tension was found with `cappadocian`, or
with any other built or candidate world in this fleet (Doc_02 §8
confirms the same check independently).

## 6. Strand Determination (Constitution Article 21)

**Not decided at this stage — carried forward as an explicit open
question, per Step 0 §4.** The movement divided, within this window,
between those who retained priests ordained before 1666 or received from
elsewhere (popovtsy) and those who concluded, over time, that a valid
priesthood could no longer be had at all (bezpopovtsy) — a real,
consequential internal division, though one that (per §9) does not touch
the Creed's own content. The Vyg community and the Pomorian Answers
(1723) are a bezpopovtsy production; the Belokrinitsa hierarchy (1846,
out of scope, §2.2) is the eventual popovtsy resolution.

This document does not force a strand determination now, for two
reasons. First, full Article-21 strand analysis (the shape the PAHC/IJC
precedent worlds used) is substantial work properly done once the
library supports it — this world's current vendored library is Avvakum's
own voice only, and Avvakum himself died in 1682, decades before the
popovtsy/bezpopovtsy division hardened into two distinct, self-conscious
positions. Reasoning about the strand question from Avvakum's own text
alone risks reading a later division backward into a source that
predates it. Second, the Representative-identity decision (the M1
checkpoint, after Doc_09 in this project's own document sequence, per
`reference/method/CiC_Record_Native_World_Build_Process_V1.5.md`) is the
point at which this question's practical weight actually bears — and
deciding a strand determination now, before that library is built out,
risks constraining that later, more consequential decision on a thinner
evidentiary base than it deserves.

**Carried to `Open_Gaps_Tracking.md` (entry 1)** as a live boundary
question for the next stage of this world's own build to resolve once
further research has filled in the priestless/priestly division's own
evidentiary base.

## 9. The floor: a ritual-and-textual schism, not a doctrinal one

**No question of a rival confession of faith arises here** — but the
schism reached the Creed's own wording, not only ritual practice, and
this section states that precisely rather than as an absolute negative.

At p. 34, arguing the wording of the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed's
own eighth article — the Nikonian correction's removal of "истиннаго"
("the True") from the phrase "and in the Holy Spirit, the Lord, the True
and Life-giving" — Avvakum writes, quoted verbatim:

> "It were better in the Creed not to pronounce the word Lord, which is
> an accidental name, than to cut out "True", for in that name is
> contained the essence of God. But we, the True Believers, confess both
> names, and we believe in the Holy Spirit, the True and Life-giving
> Lord, our Light, worshipped together..."

And the same passage in the vendored original-language file (verified
directly, exact string match):

> "Лучше бы им в Символе веры не глаголати господа, виновнаго имени, а
> нежели истиннаго отсекати, в нем же существо божие содержится. Мы же,
> правовернии, обоя имена исповедаем..."

This is not an argument about ritual gesture or calendar practice. It is
an argument that a specific word's removal from the Creed's own text
empties it of "the essence of God" — a claim made in the register of
divine essence, by this world's central voice, about the Creed's own
wording. The same opening section of the file ties the threefold
"Alleluia" dispute directly to the Latin *filioque* ("по римской
бляди… духу и от сына исхождение являют") and pronounces anathema on
those who sing it fourfold ("Да будет проклят сице поюще").

**The floor, stated at the precision the evidence supports:** the
schism was not a collision between two different confessions of faith.
Neither side charged the other with a new doctrine of God or of Christ;
both professed the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed as their own; the
central and recurring dispute, across the movement's own record as a
whole, was over corrections to inherited practice and the authority to
make them — amply and directly evidenced at pp. 120-121 below. **But
the disputed corrections included the wording of the Creed itself, and
the Old Believers argued that particular change in explicitly
theological terms at the very opening of this world's own central
text.** The world clears Constitution Article 4's floor test —
comfortably, on the Creed's overall content and both sides' own shared
profession of it — on this accurate statement, registered as a
`contested_claim` candidate for Doc_02/Doc_03 rather than smoothed over.

A second, later, and separate tension deserves the same honesty: the
bezpopovtsy branch's own later reasoning — that a valid priesthood could
no longer be had at all, because the visible church itself had fallen to
Antichrist — edges toward ecclesiological territory that is
doctrine-adjacent, decades downstream of Avvakum's own generation (§6
declines to resolve the strand question for this same reason). The
Creed-wording dispute above and this later ecclesiological question are
two separate, real reasons the floor claim needs the qualification this
section gives it, not one.

**Also worth naming as an out-of-library caution, not a sourcing
failure:** Avvakum's own later Trinitarian speculation (the
*tresushchnaya troitsa*, "three-essence Trinity") was itself judged
heterodox by his own fellow Old Believer prisoners at Pustozersk and
split them internally — a fact outside the vendored *Zhitie* and outside
this library, named here so a future document does not treat "no
doctrine at all" as if it were true of the movement's whole internal
history rather than of the 1666-1667 rupture specifically.

**The pp. 120-121 exchange** (page numbers established from this file's
own pagination convention: printed at the foot of each page, followed
immediately by the next page's own running head, confirmed independently
at two separate page breaks in this file — the patriarchs' own question
spans pp. 120-121, and Avvakum's own reply is entirely on p. 121):

> "Why," said they, "art thou stubborn? The folk of Palestine, Serbia,
> Albania, the Wallachians, they of Rome and Poland, all these do cross
> themselves with three fingers, only thou standest out in thine
> obstinacy and dost cross thyself with two fingers; it is not seemly."

And Avvakum's own reply, quoted verbatim from p. 121:

> "By the gift of God among us there is autocracy; till the time of
> Nikon, the apostate, in our Russia under our pious princes and tsars
> the orthodox faith was pure and undefiled, and in the Church was no
> sedition. Nikon, the wolf, together with the devil, ordained that men
> should cross themselves with three fingers, but our first shepherds
> made the sign of the cross and blessed men as of old with two fingers,
> according to the tradition of our holy fathers..."

This exchange itself does not raise a question about the Creed's
content — it is fought entirely on the ground of inherited practice and
who had the authority to alter it. That narrower claim, scoped to this
one exchange, is accurate and stands. The movement's own fierce,
uncompromising language about Nikon here ("the wolf," "the apostate,"
"together with the devil") is the language of a ritual betrayal argued
in the highest possible register in this passage specifically — it is
the p. 34 passage above that shows the dispute also reaching the Creed's
own wording.
