## Story Text

Zwingli left Zurich for the front early on 11 October 1531, and was killed that same afternoon. His close friend Oswald Myconius was in the city that day, and later wrote down what he remembered.

He remembered warnings that, looking back, seemed to have foretold it. Within the two weeks before Zwingli's departure, Myconius had heard him say from the pulpit, amid general excitement, "I know, I know what all this means. It means my death, everything is done to put me out of the way." Zwingli had also spoken privately of a comet visible for weeks beforehand, which he believed would prove fatal to him and to one other person — a warning Myconius and others took to mean Oecolampadius.

What Myconius watched leave the city that morning did not look, to him, like an army prepared for battle. Five thousand men had been summoned; fewer than four thousand five hundred actually mustered. There was no real order, no plan, people running about with frightened faces before and behind the banner. Zwingli rode out in the rear, armed in the ordinary manner of the time. Myconius watched him go and could scarcely stand, he wrote, for a sudden pain of heart that seemed to warn him of the worst. The city spent that day in continual sighing and prayer.

That night, the news came back: the fight had been sharp and lost, and Zwingli was dead. It was reported that he had been struck down three times in the fighting and had struggled back to his feet each time; the fourth blow, a spear under the chin, brought him to his knees. He was reported to have said, as he fell, "What evil is there in this? They are able, it is true, to kill the body but not the soul" — and then he died.

After the battle, once the victors had time to search among the fallen, Zwingli's body was found, given a mock trial, and burned. When his friends came to the field afterward, hoping to recover some remains, they found — Myconius records this as something strange, reported to him rather than seen himself — that his heart had come through the fire whole and unburned.

## Tier Justification

This is Tier 1 material: direct textual attestation from a named author (Oswald Myconius) with an identifiable, well-established social location — "his bosom friend" (line 412 of the same volume, the translator's own editorial note identifying Myconius's relationship to Zwingli) and a Reformed pastor in his own right — datable to the events of 11 October 1531 and written close to them, and a historically credible narrative claim with the caveats Tier 1's own definition names.

**Two disclosed caveats, not smoothed over.** First, Myconius was in Zurich, not on the battlefield — his own account of the battle itself ("it was reported that...") is explicitly secondhand, relayed to him afterward, a distinction his own text preserves and this story preserves in turn (the direct quotations of Zwingli's own words are themselves reported speech within Myconius's own account, not claimed as something Myconius personally heard on the field). Second, the account's own final detail — Zwingli's heart found "whole and uninjured" in the ashes — is presented in Myconius's own text as something reported to him ("strange to say"), not as his own direct observation; this story preserves that same hedge rather than upgrading it to unqualified fact.

**A page-break interruption in the vendored text, disclosed rather than silently bridged.** The direct quotation of Zwingli's own last words is split across a page boundary in this volume's own layout (lines 1586–1587, then the running header "Zwingli's Death 23," then the quotation's own completion at lines 1592–1593) — the same interleaved-pagination pattern this world's build has now encountered more than once in this volume's own OCR (compare `rzgstory001`'s own disclosed Hegenwald/Jackson interleaving in a different file). The quoted words themselves are continuous and unaffected; only the citation's own line range needs to show the break.

## Usage Guidance

The Representative may draw on this story as remembered history with strong confidence — a named, close friend's own account, written near the events it describes. The comet, the pulpit prediction, and the heart found unburned in the ashes should each be offered with the same hedge Myconius's own text applies: reported and believed within this world's own community, not independently verified fact. The battlefield details Myconius did not personally witness ("it was reported that...") may be offered as this world's own received account of the battle, distinguished, if a participant presses for precision, from what Myconius says he witnessed directly in Zurich itself.
