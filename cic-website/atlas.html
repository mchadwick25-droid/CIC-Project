The Whole Span

The ten eras of the Church's story

Church in Conversation frames the Church's history in ten eras, from the first gatherings after the apostles to the present day. Only the first two eras have a built, live chapter so far — Church in Conversation: The First Centuries. Every later era is planned, each its own careful, review-gated build.

Live Today

The five worlds you can sit down with right now

Each of these is a reconstructed community from the Church's first four centuries, built through a review-gated scholarly process — sourced from that world's own letters, sermons, sayings, and disputes. Tap a world to read more about what it can and can't speak to.

The House-Churches 70–200 CE · Antioch, Asia Minor & Rome Era I

The scattered gatherings that held together after the apostles were gone, connected by letters, formed around the table, still discerning who should lead and what the body's suffering truly means — shaped by the words of Ignatius, Polycarp, Justin Martyr, and Hermas.

Richest in the shared life of an ordinary early gathering — the room, the reading, the meal, the collection carried out that week. Thinner on any single person's own interior journey, since almost nothing survives in one voice apart from what the whole community held in common.

Represented at the Table by Chloe, host of the assembly — one whose door opens for the gathering and who carries this people's whole life, from Antioch to Rome.

SourcesThe letters of Ignatius of Antioch, Polycarp's letter to the Philippians, Justin Martyr's First Apology, and the Shepherd of Hermas.

TourA guided-tour demo exists and is the strongest of the five — still pre-launch, not yet in the live app.

Desert Fathers and Mothers c. 320–430 CE · Nile Valley & Desert, Egypt Era I

Communities who left settled village life to wage a lifelong combat against the thoughts that trouble a person from within — some alone in solitary cells tested by elders one at a time, others gathered under Pachomius into a shared rule they called koinonia. They never agreed which pattern was truer.

Shaped by the example of Antony, the sayings of Amma Sarah, and the rule of Pachomius. The smallest body of surviving material of any world here, concentrated almost entirely on the inner combat with one's own thoughts — real depth on struggle and discernment, but only a handful of stories told in full, and it says so plainly rather than inventing more.

Represented at the Table by Papnoute, an elder of the desert — formed by withdrawal and the long combat against the thoughts that trouble a person from within.

SourcesAthanasius's Life of Antony, the Sayings of the Desert Fathers (Apophthegmata Patrum), and the Rule of Pachomius.

TourPlanned, not yet built — this world's own record has no worship-service tour to draw on, so any tour will be partial by nature.

Alexandrian Christianity c. 150–400 CE · Alexandria, Egypt Era I–II

A community of readers who received seekers into a life of accompanied reading, convinced that Scripture's surface is a door onto the Logos's own inexhaustible depth, and that a knowing which leaves the knower unaltered is no knowing at all.

Formed by the didaskaleion tradition of Clement and Origen, tested by rival wisdom, persecution, and the Nicene settling of who the Son is. Richest in the theology and practice of accompanied catechetical reading — thinner on the ordinary household, women's voices, and how most of the city's Christians were actually formed, since the surviving record concentrates on the teacher's own room.

Represented at the Table by Theon, a catechetical teacher — a didaskalos who reads Scripture beside a seeker until the seeker's own eye opens onto the depth the Word has placed there.

SourcesClement of Alexandria's Stromateis and Origen's On First Principles and homilies.

TourPlanned, not yet assessed — this world joined after the initial tour-feasibility review.

Syriac Christianity 200–410 CE · Edessa & Nisibis Era II

A community shaped by persecution under Persian rule, holding together through covenant vows and typological reading of Scripture. Their bishops were martyred, their see stood empty for decades, yet their teaching endured.

Carried in the hymns of Ephrem, the demonstrations of Aphrahat, and the witness of Jacob of Nisibis. Richest in the formal vocabulary, institutions, and disputes of a demanding covenant tradition — thinner on the everyday, personal texture of an ordinary member's life, since what survives is mostly doctrinal and institutional rather than personal.

Represented at the Table by Mar Yausep, a teacher of the covenant order — formed within the community that reads Scripture by raza, the hidden truth bound within the old stories, and keeps the qyama vow.

SourcesEphrem the Syrian's hymns, Aphrahat's Demonstrations, and the witness of Jacob of Nisibis.

TourPlanned, qualified yes — needs a pronunciation pass on the Syriac terms before it's ready.

The Bethlehem Circle c. 382–420 CE · Rome & Bethlehem Era II

A circle of scholars and ascetics who gave up wealth and rank to test Scripture's Latin translation against the Hebrew it was first given in, holding together through earned trust rather than any office — even when that conviction cost them dearly among their own.

Shaped by the example of Jerome, Paula, Marcella, and Eustochium. The richest surviving written record of any world here — though much of it channels through one extraordinarily prolific author's own hand rather than a broad chorus of independent voices.

Represented at the Table by Albina, a widow of the household — formed by renunciation and the scholarly labor of testing Scripture's Latin words against the Hebrew they were first given in.

SourcesJerome's letters and prefaces to his Vulgate translation, and the letters of Paula and Eustochium.

TourPlanned, partial — no liturgical tour available in the record; would be produced last of the five.

Era assignments above are approximate groupings for orientation, not a claim about exact boundaries — several of these worlds' own dates run right across an era line.
