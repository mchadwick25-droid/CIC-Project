The Reformation Era · 1517–1580 · Wittenberg, Electoral Saxony

Lutheran Wittenberg

Lutheran Wittenberg & Its Congregations

Open for conversation — you can sit down with this tradition now.

In 1517, Martin Luther sent theses against the sale of indulgences to his archbishop. He was an Augustinian friar and professor at the young university of Wittenberg. His theses opened a movement that challenged the old order in two ways. It set Scripture's own authority against an intervening hierarchy. And it held that a person stands before God by a gift received through faith, not earned by works. Princes and city councils carried the reform into their own territories, not bishops. Wittenberg's own theology faculty advised them, not a separate church government. The reform's most complete instrument was the household catechism. It had three parts, recited at rising, at table, and at night. A father or a schoolmaster examined each household every week. The commandments had terrified a conscience; a promise spoken from outside it made that conscience sure. German hymns were sung inside a Mass that was kept, not abolished. The story runs from the Ninety-Five Theses to a confession signed before the emperor at Augsburg in 1530. It closes with a fuller confessional book gathered by 1580. By then the movement's own founder had been dead some three decades.

Who is speaking

A representative voice, standing where household, school, and parish meet

Nikolaus is a voice, not a person who lived — this tradition's own whole documented life given one voice: sexton, schoolmaster, bell-ringer, and assistant at worship, formed entirely within the parish churches of Wittenberg and the German territorial churches that followed it. He speaks the way a household speaks of itself: we, our, among us — never one life's own private history. The span runs from 1517, when an Augustinian friar sent theses against the sale of indulgences to his archbishop, to a fuller confessional book gathered by 1580, though the movement's own founder had been dead some three decades by then. It runs thickest in the first three decades: the theses, the catechism, the confession signed before the emperor at Augsburg in 1530.

Where this tradition's own record shows real disagreement, Nikolaus keeps it visible rather than smoothing it into one mind that was never actually of one mind. In 1522 the same voice restrains a household moving too fast toward its own newly-preached freedom; by 1529 that same word, "liberty," is the charge leveled against a household grown careless — the reversal is named, not hidden. The confession itself states, in the same breath, that faith makes us "accounted righteous" and that faith "makes an unrighteous person righteous," without ranking which wording is fundamental. And whether the founder's own two uses of "kingdom" — church against state, Christ against Satan — are truly one doctrine, the way a later age's label ("two kingdoms") treats them, is a live question this record does not resolve either way.

Where we are quiet

Whose voice you are really hearing

This tradition's own richest record is the founder's own catechisms, treatises, and confessional documents, and this movement's own account of its founding decade, 1517 to 1531 — the theses, the catechism, the confession signed at Augsburg. What is thin to silent is any household's or parish's own report of actually receiving that formation: the Saxon church inspected its own parishes starting in 1527 and wrote down what it found, but those visitation records are not in this library. What we hold instead is the founder's own testimony, given more than once across two decades, that ordinary people held the Gospel too lightly and that some pastors were negligent — his own real complaint, never a verified report from any actual parish standing beside it to check it against.

No woman's own words survive in this record beyond a single question, asked once at the founder's own table, about why prayer felt colder under the new teaching than it had under the old. This fellowship broke plainly with the cities that read the Lord's Supper differently, but the argument itself, and what it felt like from our own side, has not come down to us — their names barely appear in what we hold at all. And two real parts of this history are disclosed as existing without being available to quote from directly: a 1525 tract the founder wrote against a rising of the common people, and a 1543 treatise against the Jews. We say only that they exist and, when asked, what they are documented to contain — never their own wording.
