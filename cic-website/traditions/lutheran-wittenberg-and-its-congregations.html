VII. The Reformation1517-1650

1517-1580 · Saxony, then N. Europe

Lutheran Wittenberg

Lutheran Wittenberg & Its Congregations

In 1517, Martin Luther sent theses against the sale of indulgences to his archbishop. He was an Augustinian friar who taught theology at the university of Wittenberg. His theses opened a movement that challenged the old order in two ways. It set Scripture's own authority against an intervening hierarchy. And it held that a person stands before God by a gift received through faith, not earned by works. Princes and city councils carried the reform into their own territories, not bishops. Wittenberg's own theology faculty advised them, not a separate church government. The reform's most complete instrument was the household catechism. It had three parts, recited at rising, at table, and at night. A father or a schoolmaster examined each household every week. The commandments had terrified a conscience; a promise spoken from outside it made that conscience sure. German hymns were sung inside a Mass that was kept, not abolished. The story runs from the Ninety-Five Theses to a confession signed before the emperor at Augsburg in 1530. It closes with a fuller confessional book gathered by 1580. By then the movement's own founder had been dead some three decades.

People

The people behind this account

Doctor Martin Luther

Luther was the friar and Wittenberg professor who wrote to Archbishop Albrecht about indulgences in 1517. He later told his students of facing a papal legate in 1518 and the Empire's assembly at Worms in 1521. In 1522 he came home to calm his own congregation. This record holds him in his own letters, sermons, catechisms, and a ballad. It also holds table talk that students collected. His life closed in the mid-1540s.

Melanchthon

Melanchthon wrote the Augsburg Confession. It was read to the Emperor in 1530, and princes and city senates signed it. It was a shared statement of what the movement taught.

Kate, Luther's wife

Katharina von Bora was Luther's wife. One question of hers survives in this record. She asked why the old church prayed with such fervor while their own household prayed so coldly. Luther told it at table, and a student wrote it down years later.

Johann Walter, Luther's musical collaborator

Johann Walter worked with Luther on the music. He remembered being kept three weeks at Wittenberg while the two of them chose how to sing the Epistle and Gospel in German. His letter is the one account of worship there that does not come from Luther himself.

A Letter to an Archbishop

31 October 1517, Wittenberg

A friar wrote to the archbishop in whose name indulgences were being preached. He asked for correction and sent ninety-five statements for debate.

Luther greeted the Archbishop as "Most Reverend Father in Christ and Most Illustrious Prince." He called himself "the dregs of humanity," amazed at his own boldness in writing.

Indulgences for the building of St. Peter's in Rome were being preached under Albrecht's name. Luther said he had not heard the preaching himself, and he grieved over what its hearers now believed. They thought a bought letter of indulgence made their salvation sure. They thought souls flew out of purgatory as soon as a gift went into the money-box. "O God, most good!" he wrote. "Thus souls committed to your care, good Father, are taught to their death."

He asked the Archbishop to withdraw the instruction the preachers followed. Otherwise someone else might do it in public, to the Archbishop's own shame. He signed himself "Brother Martin Luther," and added one line more. If it pleased him, the Archbishop might look over "these my Disputations." They were ninety-five statements for university debate, sent along with the letter.

The next day Luther came again and would not recant anything. In Luther's memory, the Cardinal asked whether he thought the Pope cared for Germany, or that princes would raise armies to defend him. Where, Cajetan asked, would he be safe then?

"Under Heaven," Luther answered.

1521, Worms, as Luther told it years later at his table

Luther told his students he went to Worms despite warnings. He said he would accept correction in everything except what touches Scripture.

At Erfurt, on the road to WormsWormsThe city where the Diet met in 1521. Borbetomagus, the ancient place at Worms, lies in today's Germany.Place · pleiades.stoa.org, a herald asked Luther whether he still meant to go on. Word had spread that he was condemned.

So Luther told it, years later, to the students at his table. He had been summoned to the Diet under an imperial safe-conduct, and within a day of his leaving it was broken. His books were burned at Worms before he arrived. He answered the herald that he would go, "although in Worms there were as many devils as there are tiles on the houses."

Before the Emperor and Electors, Dr. Eck asked whether the books on the table were his. He said they were his. Some were books of doctrine, and those he could not recant. He asked for time to consider, and was given one day and one night.

The next day the Bishops pressed him. God's Word, he told them, was not his word, so he did not know how to give it away. In whatever did not touch the Holy Scriptures, he would obey.

Word reached the Emperor, wrongly, that he had recanted. A Bishop came to ask if he had left his cause to the Emperor. He had not, Luther said, and never meant to.

While he was away, the reform had run ahead of him under his colleague Karlstadt and others. The town had fallen into disorder over how fast the old forms of worship should be swept away.

Luther preached eight days running. A witness named Kessler was there, and he said Luther first explained the day's Gospel. Then he "dropped the text and took up the present affair."

"And here, dear friends," Luther said, "have you not grievously failed? I see no signs of love among you." They had learned the doctrine, he told them plainly, without living it. "Let us beware lest Wittenberg become Capernaum."

The kingdom of God, he said, lies in deeds and works. "God does not want hearers and repeaters of words, but doers and followers," he said. They were to practice the faith that works by love. A faith without love, he told them, was no faith at all. It was "a counterfeit of faith."

On the first of July, 1523, two young monks named John and Henry were burned at Brussels. Theologians from Louvain had examined them and pressed them to give up the evangelical teaching, and they refused. Word reached WittenbergWittenbergThe town where Luther taught at the university and wrote his 1517 letter.Place quickly, and within months Luther had written his song.

It calls them "two youths, highly gifted with rich and heavenly graces." In the song, the Louvain theologians try every device to make them recant, and the two stand firm "like rocks." In its words, "for God's dear Word they shed their blood," and "they won the crown of martyrs."

The ballad says their monks' habits were stripped from them before the burning. They answered, it says, with "a cheerful Amen," glad to be free of "Satan's mock and masquerade."

What a Household Was Told to Do

1529, as the catechisms prescribed

A father was to question his children and servants every week. The young were to get no food or drink until they could say the parts.

Read the full account

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

The two catechisms set out a program for every household, in detail. No source names a single household that kept it.

Mornings were to begin with the sign of the cross and a short prayer. It asked that "the evil enemy will not gain power over me." Afterward one might "go about your work and perhaps sing a song inspired by the Ten Commandments or your own thoughts." A father was to teach the Commandments in what the Small Catechism calls "the simple way," one at a time.

The Small Catechism gave a servant words for confession. The servant was to say he had "served my master unfaithfully." The master or lady of the house was given words too, confessing they had "not faithfully led my children, servants or wife to God's glory."

The Large Catechism's preface made the weekly duty plain. A father was "to question and examine his children and servants at least once a week." The youngest were to learn the parts "word for word" and recite them each day. The preface says that "until they repeat them, they should be given neither food nor drink."

A head of the house was to hold his servants to the same standard. He was "not to keep them in his house if they do not know these things and are unwilling to learn them."

Patience and a Question About Prayer

Remembered at Luther's table, between 1531 and 1546

At table, Luther said his whole life was patience. He also told of a question from his wife, Katharina von Bora. Why did the old church pray so hard, and their own house so coldly?

Read the full account

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

His students wrote down what Luther said one day at table about patience. "I must have patience with the Pope," he told them. He went on to heretics and seducers, to the roaring courtiers, and to his own servants. "I must have patience with Kate my wife; to conclude, the patiences are so many, that my whole life is nothing but patience."

In another conversation, Luther told how his wife had been sick, and how he had prayed for her to recover. He believed God answered that prayer, and gave the household a good farm and a fruitful year besides.

At that time, Katharina herself is remembered asking him a question. She asked him why, under the Pope, people prayed so often and so hard, while their own household was cold and careless in prayer.

Luther answered, as his students recorded it, that the devil drives his own servants on continually. They are diligent in their false worship, while we are ice cold and negligent in prayer.

Legacy

What came after

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

This world's own record says that a confessional family grew from these churches. It still teaches a catechism like this world's own and still sings in the language of the people, under many names. This record speaks from the world's own formation, as it was lived. It makes no claim about what those communities believe or practice now.

Three things are not carried forward. This record does not say what came after the prince-and-council church that this world lived under. It closes within a few years of the founder's death, though the confession it holds was gathered by 1580. The tradition went on for centuries beyond that point, in ways not told here. The 1543 treatise is real and part of this world's own history. This record does not say how the churches that came after have answered for it.

Related traditions

How this tradition relates to others

This world knew itself partly by what it refused. It refused the claim that authority over doctrine sits with the pope, and it refused the sale of indulgences. Rome answered with a bull in 1520 and a written reply at Augsburg in 1530. It also set limits against the radicals of 1522, and its confession names and condemns the Anabaptists. And it broke with the cities that read the Supper differently, at Marburg in 1529. The Roman side reaches this record only as quoted by the side that answered it.

In its own words

A few terms and lines worth knowing

And in Jesus Christ, his only Son, our Lord. He was conceived by the Holy Spirit and born of the Virgin Mary. He suffered under Pontius Pilate, was crucified, died and was buried. He descended to Hell. On the third day he rose again from the dead. He ascended to Heaven and sat down at the right hand of God the Almighty Father. From there he will come to judge the living and the dead. What does this mean? I believe that Jesus Christ is truly God, born of the Father in eternity, and also truly man, born of the Virgin Mary. He is my Lord! He redeemed me, a lost and condemned person. He bought me and won me from all sins, from death and from the authority of the Devil. It did not cost him gold or silver, but his holy, precious blood, his innocent body -- his death! Because of this, I am his very own. I will live under him in his kingdom. I will serve him in righteousness, innocence and blessedness forever, just as he is risen from death, lives and reigns forever. Yes, this is true.

the Small Catechism's Second Article -- the creed and its explanation, said by the household itself, in its own first person, as 'I believe'

They also teach that one holy Church will go on forever. The Church is the community of saints, in which the Gospel is rightly taught and the sacraments are rightly given. For the true unity of the Church, it is enough to agree on the Gospel's teaching and on how the sacraments are given. Nor do human traditions -- that is, rites or ceremonies set up by people -- need to be alike everywhere. As Paul says: one faith, one baptism, one God and Father of all, and so on.

the Augsburg Confession, Article VII -- signed at the 1530 Diet of Augsburg by an Elector, princes, and two city councils who confessed it, not one preacher's own private teaching

But beware of this. The poor man may come to you, and there are so many now. He must buy with the penny of his daily wages and live on it. You may be harsh to him, as though everyone lived by your favor. You may skin and scrape to the bone. Besides that, with pride and haughtiness, you may turn away the man you ought to give to for nothing. Then he will go away wretched and sorrowful. He can complain to no one, so he will cry and call to heaven. Then beware, I say again, as of the devil himself. For such groaning and calling will be no joke. It will have a weight that will prove too heavy for you and all the world. For it will reach Him who takes care of the poor, sorrowful hearts. He will not allow them to go unavenged.

the Large Catechism, the Seventh Commandment's own household explanation
conscience -- 'terrified,' 'anxious,' 'captive'; the court where our doctrine is decided
Conscience is the inner court where our whole teaching plays out. Under God's Law, it feels terror. Under God's promise, it finds comfort. It is not a guide that tells us right from wrong. It is a court that stands accused, then is set free.
faith -- trust that clings to God's promise, 'faith alone'
Faith is trust that holds onto God's own promise. It is not simply believing a story is true. This trust alone makes us right with God, apart from our works. But faith never stands on nothing. It rests on the Word and the sacraments.
the household -- father, wife, children, servants; the catechism's own site
The household is where our catechism was taught. Our books told a father to question his family and servants at least once a week. He was to lead them in prayer morning, evening, and at meals. That is how ordinary people were meant to learn the faith.
the two governments -- God's spiritual rule by the Word, and secular rule by the sword
God rules us two ways. Through the Word, he rules the spiritual kingdom, and makes us Christians. Through the sword, he rules the civil kingdom, and restrains evil. We keep both, and confuse neither with the other.
'we are all priests' -- consecrated in baptism, no estate difference, only office
Baptism makes every one of us a priest. There is no rank among us, only different tasks. A father is a priest in his own home. Still, no one may preach in public without being properly called.
