The Imperial Church Era · c. 311–439 CE · Carthage & Numidia, Roman North Africa

The Church of the Martyrs

Donatism

Open for conversation — you can sit down with this tradition now.

In 311/312, Caecilian was consecrated bishop of Carthage by a man accused of surrendering his copy of the scriptures when ordered to — and a rival was consecrated beside him because of it, not in his place. From that day the same see held two bishops at once, two parallel hierarchies contesting town after town across Roman North Africa, each holding its own basilicas and its own unbroken line of ordination. This is the rigorist alternative communion that grew from that refusal: not a doctrinal heresy — its confession is standard North African Latin Trinitarian and Christological orthodoxy — but a communion built on one unyielding conviction, that a tainted hand cannot give what it claims to give, lived out through rebaptism, the martyrs' graves read aloud once a year, and a refusal of the Christian emperor's own standing to judge the church. It closes in 439, when a Vandal army took Carthage and removed the Roman state this whole refusal was defined against.

Voices

The people behind this account

Donatus, our bishop at Carthage

Donatus followed Majorinus as bishop of Carthage around 313. He led his church for more than thirty years, and history names the whole movement after him. He left no writing of his own. His best-known words come from Optatus, the bishop who wrote against him. When the emperor's men came to Carthage with aid, Optatus says, Donatus asked what the emperor had to do with the church. He was sent into exile after the repression under Macarius, and he died there.

Petilian, our bishop at Constantina

Petilian became bishop of Constantina, the new name for Cirta, in the 390s. More of his words survive than of any other Donatist, because Augustine quoted his letters clause by clause in three books to refute them. At the Conference of Carthage in 411 he spoke on an open transcript. He thanked the judge for the offer of a chair and refused it. Christ himself, he said, had not refused to stand before his governor.

Emeritus, our bishop at Caesarea

Emeritus, bishop of Caesarea in Mauretania, carried the Donatist case for three days at the Conference of Carthage in 411. An imperial notary took down his words as he spoke them. No other figure of his church is heard so directly. He would not let the court touch the case itself until it had settled the day, the mandates and who was bringing the charge. When the argument reached Scripture, he answered a reading of the wheat and the weeds verse for verse, from the same books. He signed the notary's record of most of what he said. At times he added that he kept his right to appeal.

Marculus, blessed martyr

Marculus is remembered as a man who had given up worldly advancement before the persecution found him. He was seized during the repression under Macarius, flogged, and paraded through several towns of Numidia. Then he was held four days at a cliff called Novapetra. His church's own account says he fasted there and was shown a vision of a cup, a crown and a palm. Before dawn he was thrown from the cliff.

Tyconius

Tyconius was a Donatist writer and preacher, and the one Donatist thinker whose own book survives entire. He argued from Scripture that the church was spread across the whole earth. His own bishop at Carthage, Parmenian, told him never to preach it again. He did not take it back, and he did not go over to the other side. He went on arguing from inside the party that had told him to stop. It is reported that a council of his own church then condemned him.

Legacy

What came after

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Tyconius wrote the Book of Rules. A council of his own party is reported to have condemned him, but the book lasted longer than that party. It passed to the rival church, and it shaped how Augustine read Scripture.

The contest between the two churches did not end in 439. It went on under Vandal and then Byzantine rule for about another century and a half. In the 590s Pope Gregory the Great wrote letters pressing for an inquiry into Donatist rebaptism still going on in Numidia. No line from the Donatists to any church a person could visit today has ever been shown.

In its own record

How this world relates to others

The Donatists denied that the state could judge the church. A neighboring tradition, Imperial and Juridical Christianity, took the opposite path. There, church office grew close to the power of the state from Constantine's time on. The same Constantine whom the Donatists asked for help in 313, and then refused as a judge, is where that tradition begins. Donatist rebaptism, given once for all, also stands against the Cappadocian answer to the same question. How does someone from the other side come back in? The Cappadocians let people back in by stages, with graded penance, as a tool of exact care and not as a wall.

Sources

What this account is built from

Nearly all the written evidence passed through two Catholic writers, Optatus of Milevis and Augustine of Hippo, before it reached us. That is the central problem with the sources. Four short texts speak as Donatists rather than about them. The transcript of the 411 hearing also keeps their bishops' own words, chosen by no opponent. The clearest exception is two words, Deo laudes, cut into stone. No report or plan of the Numidian churches where such words were spoken is among the sources. One more scholarly dispute runs through all the evidence. Was the hostile picture of the Circumcellions, rural members of the movement, true to them, or mainly their opponents' polemic?

A few terms and lines worth knowing

Praise to God.

Anonymous inscribers of this communion, on two pillars near Bagai (CIL VIII 17732), with three further stones carrying the same words

What business does the emperor have with the church?

Donatus, our bishop at Carthage

Truth is hidden by a great device: when we ask, the other side puts forward a small part, and the rest is kept silent.

Emeritus, our bishop at Caesarea

What we look for is the conscience of the giver, the one who gives in holiness, to cleanse the conscience of the one who receives. For whoever knowingly takes faith from the faithless does not receive faith, but guilt.

Petilian, our bishop at Constantina
traditor / traditio
When the persecutor came, some clergy handed the scriptures over to be burned. We called such a man a traditor. We held that his hand was no longer clean, and that he could not ordain, baptize, or bless. What flowed from that hand was nothing at all.
rebaptizare
We did not think of this as baptizing twice. Nothing happened the first time. A hand from a tainted line washes nothing. So when someone came to us from the other church, we gave the first real baptism they had had.
martyr / martyrium
A martyr dies for the true faith. But our best-known dead were not killed by pagans. They were killed by imperial troops sent at the urging of a rival Christian party. That party did not grant them the word. We did not accept its account of our own dead.
ecclesia
There was no neutral word for the church here. The word itself was the argument. Two whole rival bodies claimed it, town by town, bishop for bishop. We held that we were the true one, and that our rival's favour with the state settled nothing.
Quid est imperatori cum ecclesia? ("What has the emperor to do with the church?")
We held that the state had no standing to say which church was the true one. 'What has the Emperor to do with the Church?' -- so our primate is reported to have asked. Yet three times we went to that same court when it served us. We held both facts at once, and called neither one a lie.
