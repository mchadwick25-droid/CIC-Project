The Imperial Church Era · c. 311–439 CE · Carthage & Numidia, Roman North Africa

The Church of the Martyrs

Donatism

Open for conversation — you can sit down with this tradition now.

In 311/312, Caecilian was consecrated bishop of Carthage by a man accused of surrendering his copy of the scriptures when ordered to — and a rival was consecrated beside him because of it, not in his place. From that day the same see held two bishops at once, two parallel hierarchies contesting town after town across Roman North Africa, each holding its own basilicas and its own unbroken line of ordination. This is the rigorist alternative communion that grew from that refusal: not a doctrinal heresy — its confession is standard North African Latin Trinitarian and Christological orthodoxy — but a communion built on one unyielding conviction, that a tainted hand cannot give what it claims to give, lived out through rebaptism, the martyrs' graves read aloud once a year, and a refusal of the Christian emperor's own standing to judge the church. It closes in 439, when a Vandal army took Carthage and removed the Roman state this whole refusal was defined against.

Fidelis is a representative voice for this tradition, not a person who lived — built from its own letters and records, and honest about where they run out.

The Story

How this tradition took shape

This world's own record runs from 311 or 312 to 439, centered on Carthage and the Roman provinces of North Africa - Africa Proconsularis, Numidia, Byzacena, and Mauretania. It is not a doctrinal heresy. Both this communion and the rival it split from confessed the same Nicene faith, word for word. What was contested was narrower and sharper: whether a bishop's own conduct under persecution could void what his hand later gave. One conviction runs through everything this world did - purity, rite, memory, and refusal are four faces of a single claim: this is the pure, persecuted, true church, proved by what it will not concede and by what it has suffered for refusing to concede it.

The conviction was not new in 311. Half a century earlier, eighty-seven African bishops meeting at Carthage under Cyprian had already ruled that baptism given outside the true church washes nothing. This world's founders read themselves as continuing that African ruling, not inventing one - a claim their own opponents had to answer at length, arguing that Cyprian, however holy, was not a canonical authority. Then came the Diocletianic persecution of 303 to 305, and a demand no earlier dispute had tested: that clergy surrender the scriptures themselves, on pain of death. Some did. Some refused and suffered for it. What a minister did in that one moment, this world held, was not incidental to whether his hand could be trusted afterward.

The rupture itself began, as this world's own record tells it, with a personal rebuke. A wealthy laywoman of Carthage, Lucilla, kissed a martyr's bone before communion; the archdeacon Caecilian rebuked her for it in public, and she did not forget it. When persecution passed and the bishop who had led Carthage through it did not return, Caecilian was elected in his place over two disappointed rivals - and the man who consecrated him, Felix of Aptungi, was accused of having surrendered the scriptures himself. On this world's own doctrine, that accusation, if true, voided the consecration and everything flowing from it. A rival party would not accept it: they consecrated Majorinus, from Lucilla's own household, beside Caecilian rather than in his place. Majorinus's successor Donatus gave the movement the name history remembers it by, though nothing suggests his own people chose that name for themselves. Lucilla is the one woman this world's own record names - and she reaches us only inside the hostile narrative that blamed her for the whole split.

Two founding-era inquiries complicate any tidy version of this story, and this world's own record keeps both rather than smoothing them away. Before the schism hardened, a council of Numidian bishops at Cirta asked itself the same question it would soon put to Caecilian: had any of them handed over the scriptures? Several said yes. When the question turned back on the bishop presiding, the room could not finish it, and simply agreed to leave the matter to God. And when Constantine ordered a formal inquiry into the accusation against Felix of Aptungi, it did not vindicate the accusers: a notary confessed, under threat of torture, that he had forged the letter used to prove the charge - working, he said, for the rigorist side. This world's own tradition never claimed that inquiry as a win. It held instead that the wider dispute involved more people and more evidence than one proceeding could settle.

Seven years after the rival consecration, a Roman official investigating the same money heard testimony that damages this world's own founding generation more than any opponent's own argument does. Letters from its own bishops, read into a court record in 320, urge each other to keep a true accusation quiet rather than let it reach a public court. One admits outright that he knows the charge against a fellow bishop, Silvanus of Cirta, is true. This world's own record does not hide the letters. It keeps them, because hiding them was never this world's own discipline.

The doctrine had one enacted consequence, repeated for a century: anyone crossing from the rival communion was washed again - though this world never called it a second baptism. A hand from a broken line, it held, had given nothing the first time, so what happened at its own font was a person's first true entry into the church, not a repetition of an empty rite. Successive imperial edicts named the practice specifically as an offense, which tells its own story about how public an act it was. The one clean exception on record is a group of Maximianist clergy, discussed below, received back without the rite repeated at all - the door was not always as narrow as this world's own reputation for rigor suggests.

This world held two persecutions apart in memory, deliberately. The Diocletianic terror was suffered alongside the eventual rival, before the schism existed, and it supplied the founding accusation. The Macarian repression of 347 to 348 was suffered specifically at that same rival's own urging, through the emperor's commissioners - and it supplied the dead this world actually commemorates. A bishop named Marculus, who had already given up worldly advancement before persecution ever reached him, was shown a vision of a cup, a crown, and a palm during four days of fasting, then thrown from a cliff called Novapetra. Two laymen of Carthage, Isaac and Maximianus, were flogged and their bodies weighted and sunk in the sea; their own bishop, Macrobius, wrote to the surviving congregation that what had happened to those two might yet be asked of any of them. Every year, at the grave, on the appointed day, this world read such accounts aloud again - not to remember something finished, but to be shown again what it already believed itself to be. On two pillars near one of its own principal towns, Bagai, two words are still legible, cut into stone with no hostile hand between them and any later reader: Deo laudes. Praise to God. It is the one piece of this world's own voice that reached us unmediated.

By the fourth century's later decades this was not a fringe protest but a complete, parallel church: its own bishops, its own basilicas, its own councils, contesting the same towns as its rival across four Roman provinces. At the great Conference of Carthage in 411, two hundred and seventy-nine of its bishops answered a roll call against two hundred and eighty-six from the other side. In Numidia specifically, this world's own record states plainly, it was not a minority current but simply the ordinary church of the countryside.

The clearest statement of what this world would not concede is five words remembered from its own founding bishop, reported inside the very work written to refute him: what has the emperor to do with the church? Rome's hearing in 313 and the Council of Arles in 314 both ruled against this communion, and both rulings were rejected - not because a council cannot decide anything, but because a court whose venue and rules already belong to a state that has picked a side is not the church judging itself. This world's own record does not pretend the refusal was ever absolute. Three times, at three real moments, it turned to the very machinery it otherwise refused to recognize: petitioning Constantine himself in 313, petitioning Julian for confiscated basilicas back in 361, and invoking the empire's own anti-heretical law against its own breakaway Maximianist dissidents in the 390s. Each turn came at a moment the state briefly offered something to gain by engaging it. This world's own texts state the exceptions in the same breath as the principle, and never treated naming the gap as requiring that it be closed.

This world produced exactly one systematic theologian, and cut him off. Tyconius, a layman, argued from Scripture - at length, and too well to simply dismiss - that the church was spread across the whole earth, which meant, followed to its own conclusion, that the rival communion still in that wider fellowship had not lost anything by staying in it. His own bishop at Carthage told him never to preach it again. He did not recant, and did not leave; he kept arguing from inside the party that had silenced him, and was cut off for good, reportedly by a council whose own act does not survive to be checked. His one surviving book, the Book of Rules, crossed over anyway - read for centuries afterward by the Catholic bishop, Augustine, who had helped defeat the party that condemned its author.

This world's own hardest tension is internal, not external, and it kept the tension visible rather than resolving it. In 393 a deacon named Maximian broke away and was consecrated a rival primate; the following year a much larger council at Bagai, three hundred and ten bishops strong, condemned his party in language about shipwrecked bodies that could not even find burial. Then, within a few years, that same council took two of the condemned bishops back into full office - without repeating either their baptism or their ordination. No explanation for holding both positions survives from this world's own side. It did not hide the exception. It is stated in the same conciliar record that states the doctrine at its most absolute.

The clearest and least mediated look at this world's own bishops arguing for themselves comes from the very end of its recorded life. At the great Conference of Carthage in 411, an imperial judge offered the standing bishops chairs; Petilian of Constantina thanked him and refused, since Christ himself had not refused to stand before his own governor. Emeritus of Caesarea carried the argument for three days, holding the court to its own procedure before its substance, and when the argument at last reached Scripture, answered a wheat-and-tares reading verse for verse out of the same books. He signed the transcript of his own words, again and again, with his appeal reserved - putting his name to the record while refusing, in the same stroke, to concede the proceeding itself. The judge conceded, out loud, that the name Catholic belonged to whichever side the truth was found with - then ruled that he was nonetheless bound by an imperial rescript that had already given the name to the other side.

The window this account covers closes in 439, not because the two-party contest itself ended there but because that year a Vandal army took Carthage and removed the one power - the Roman, Catholic-aligned imperial state - this world's whole refusal was defined against. The contest itself went on for a further century and a half under Vandal and then Byzantine rule, still legislated against, until Pope Gregory the Great wrote letters in the 590s pressing for an inquiry into Donatist rebaptism still being practiced in Numidia. After that, this world's own record simply falls silent. No line from it to any communion a person could visit today is documented anywhere, and this world's own Living Tradition Status has never been confirmed. Almost everything recounted above was written down by two men arguing against this world, not by this world itself - and of the handful of women its own record allows any real agency to, not one left a word in her own voice.

Voices

The people behind this account

Donatus, our bishop at Carthage

Donatus succeeded Majorinus as bishop of Carthage around 313 and led this communion for more than three decades - long enough that history remembers the whole movement by his name, though nothing suggests his own people chose that name for themselves. He left no writing of his own. His single most quoted line survives only because the bishop who opposed him recorded it in order to answer it: what has the emperor to do with the church? He was exiled after the Macarian repression of 347-348 and died in exile.

Petilian, our bishop at Constantina

Petilian became bishop of Constantina, the renamed Cirta, in the 390s and was, by volume, this world's fullest surviving voice - because Augustine quoted his letters clause by clause across three books in order to refute them. At the great Conference of Carthage in 411 he argued in a different register entirely: on an open transcript, thanking the judge for the offer of a chair and refusing it, because Christ himself had not refused to stand before his own governor.

Emeritus, our bishop at Caesarea

Emeritus, bishop of Caesarea in Mauretania, carried this communion's case for three days at the Conference of Carthage in 411, on the best evidentiary footing any figure in this world's record holds: his own words, taken down by an imperial notary as he spoke them. He held the court to its own procedure before its substance, and when the argument finally reached Scripture, answered a reading of the wheat and the tares verse for verse, out of the same books. He signed the transcript of his own words again and again with the same qualified formula: his appeal reserved.

Marculus, blessed martyr

This world remembers Marculus as a man who had already given up worldly advancement before the persecution that killed him ever found him. Seized during the Macarian repression, he was flogged, paraded through several Numidian towns, and held four days at a cliff called Novapetra, where he was shown, in the fast, a vision of a cup, a crown, and a palm. He was thrown from the cliff before dawn.

Tyconius

Tyconius was the one systematic theologian this world produced, and the one it silenced. A layman, he argued from Scripture that the church was spread across the whole earth - an argument built well enough that his own bishop at Carthage told him never to preach it again. He did not recant, and did not defect to the other side either; he kept saying it from inside the party that had told him to stop, and was cut off for good.

Documented stories

Stories worth telling in full

Four Hundred Pieces of Silver, and Letters Asking for Silence

320 (the inquiry before Zenophilus); the money and consecration it investigates date to c. 305-312

A Roman official puts named witnesses under oath and reads a bishop's own letters into the record - letters that admit an accusation was true and beg that no one find out.

Read the full account

In 320, before the consular official Zenophilus, a formal inquiry was opened into what had actually happened to the church's own money. The complaint had been laid by Nundinarius, a deacon degraded by his own bishop, and it named names. Silvanus of Cirta, it charged, was a betrayer and a thief of the goods of the poor - and all of them, the bishops and priests and deacons and elders, knew it, because of the four hundred pieces of silver given by the noble woman Lucilla, for the sake of which they had conspired together that Majorinus should be made bishop, and out of which the schism came. Victor the fuller, it added, had paid twenty pieces to be made a priest. Letters were read into the record, and the letters are what a modern reader will find hardest. Purpurius, a bishop, had written to Silvanus urging him to settle with the deacon quietly, before the flame burst out that could not afterward be put out without spiritual bloodshed - and adding, in the same letter, that he knew the things written in the bill of accusation were true. He wrote to the clergy and elders of Cirta in the same terms, and closed by warning them to see that no one learned the story of this conspiracy. A bishop named Fortis wrote to Silvanus in the same vein: let us not come into a public court and be condemned by the gentiles. A bishop named Sabinus wrote twice. Every one of the letters ends the same way. Let no one know about it. Then the witnesses were questioned directly, under record. Zenophilus said that from the acts and letters read aloud it was clear that Silvanus was a betrayer, and turned to a deponent named Victor: say frankly whether you know he handed anything over. He handed it over, Victor said, but not in my presence. What office did he hold at the time? Sub-deacon, said Victor; the persecution broke out when Paul was bishop. And when he came to be made bishop, Nundinarius said, the people answered: let it be another - hear us, God. Did the people cry out that Silvanus was a betrayer? I myself fought against his being made bishop, Victor said. So you knew he was a betrayer - confess it. He was a betrayer, Victor said. We wanted one of our own townsmen, a man of integrity; I knew that if the office went to such as he, we should end up before the emperor's court. Decades afterward Augustine, no friend to our cause, referred back to this same episode in a letter of his own, recalling that Nundinarius, in the heat of passion, revealed many secrets - among them that the founding of a rival altar at Carthage had been made possible by money that came from Lucilla. Two witnesses, on opposite sides of the schism and decades apart, describe the same underlying fact.

Condemned as Shipwrecked, Then Taken Back Without a Word

393 (the Cebarsussi rupture); 394 (the Bagai council and reception)

Three hundred and ten bishops condemn a breakaway party in the harshest language this world ever used on itself - then take two of the condemned back into office without repeating a single rite.

Read the full account

In 393 a deacon named Maximian was elected as a rival primate at Cebarsussi, in direct defiance of Primian, the sitting primate at Carthage. It was, in miniature, the same act our own founders had once taken against Caecilian: a rival consecration, answering a grievance with a new bishop rather than submission to the one already in place. The response was neither quiet nor measured. A much larger council - three hundred and ten bishops - met at Bagai in 394 and condemned the Maximianists in language that did not soften what it meant. The decree, preserved in Augustine's own quotation of it, describes the schismatics as shipwrecked men dashed by the waves of truth upon the sharp rocks, their bodies covering the shore, so thoroughly that they fail to find so much as burial. And then, having said this, the council did something its own decree's language did nothing to prepare a listener for. When Felicianus of Musti and Praetextatus of Assuris, two of the condemned Maximianist bishops, were brought back into the fold, it was done without rebaptism and without reordination. A general named Optatus Gildonianus enforced the reconciliation with military force. The very men whose ordination the council had just described in the imagery of shipwreck and unburied death were received back into full office, exactly as they had been ordained under the schism the council had just condemned in the harshest terms it had. Augustine, no friend to any argument of ours, seized on the contradiction and made it the centre of his own case: if reordination could be waived here, on the mainstream party's own authority, then the absolute rule - that invalidly ordained clergy must always be rebaptized and reordained, without exception - did not hold even for the party that preached it most fiercely.

Three Days Standing Before a Judge Already Told Who Was Right

1-8 June 411

Two hundred and seventy-nine bishops refuse an imperial judge's offered chairs and argue for three days against a rescript that had already named the other side the church.

Read the full account

In the summer of 411 the emperor ordered the whole quarrel settled at law, and sent Flavius Marcellinus, a tribune and notary, to Carthage to hear it. Both communions were summoned. Our bishops came, and were counted: the roll of subscribed mandates was read out name by name, and as each name was read the bishop it belonged to came forward and said the same six words - I gave the mandate and I signed. When the roll reached one of ours he added something the notary set down with the rest: that he had no traditores in his own congregation, nor in his whole diocese. Bishops from the other side interrupted the reading to dispute particular entries - one of ours, they said, had been a Catholic deacon, had been rebaptized, made a presbyter, thrown out for adultery, and only then made a bishop - and Petilian answered from our benches that the man had chosen to stand in the place of an accuser. Seven were then chosen from each side to plead for all. Marcellinus, sitting, offered our bishops chairs. He said plainly that it weighed on him to sit while so many venerable men stood. Petilian thanked him for the courtesy and refused it. We do not blush, he said, we do not fear, we do not avoid standing willingly while you sit; and since Christ the Lord himself did not avoid it, when he deigned to stand before the governor, how much less may we refuse. Our bishops stood for three days. Emeritus of Caesarea carried the argument. He would not let the court reach the substance of the case before it had settled how the case got there. First the day, he said, then the mandate, then the persons, then the cause - and only then the merits. To you, truest of judges, nothing else falls but to hold to the truth: judge, exalted man, in a Christian matter, because it is God who judges this case. He held the court to the emperor's own four-month term, and would dispute nothing of the merits until the day itself had been ruled on. When the other side declined to name its own representatives, their rank, or their mandate, he said that the truth was being hidden by a great contrivance. When they complained that he was hunting for persons instead of arguing the case, and asked why he had come at all, Petilian answered for him that every case begins with the persons; and Emeritus pressed the point to its hinge - no one can be called a respondent unless someone first challenged him, so let them state the action they bring, and then we will answer it. The name itself was fought over. Their side asked that the accusations we were said to make against a church spread through the whole earth be heard that very day, and be found true or false. Petilian answered that before the case is decided the name they claim is an empty one, and that whoever is found to be a Christian will hold it at the end. Marcellinus agreed - it is certain, he said, that after the contest the name Catholic belongs to those with whom the truth is found - and then said that he was nevertheless bound to follow the imperial rescript, which had already been pleased to call them by it. That answer is the whole shape of the day for us: a judge who conceded the principle and was not free to act on it. On the third day the argument finally reached Scripture. Augustine argued from the wheat and the tares that the church holds good and bad together, and that what is to be saved is the world. Emeritus answered him out of the same books, verse against verse: the world did not know him - so the church did not know God, if the church is the world; that every mouth may be stopped and all become guilty before God; if you were of the world, the world would love its own, but because you are not of the world, therefore it hates and persecutes you; they are of the world, and the world hears them; if any man love the world, the love of the Father is not in him. When Augustine went on at length, Emeritus said only that a man who runs on that long drowns himself out. Emeritus signed the record of that exchange as he signed most of what he said there - I, Emeritus the bishop, have reviewed this, my appeal reserved. He put his name to the notary's account of his own words and refused, in the same stroke, to accept the proceeding they were being recorded for.

Legacy

What came after

One thread carried past this world's own horizon in a way its own founders could hardly have predicted. The Book of Rules, the one substantial work of biblical interpretation this world produced, survived the council that condemned its own author. It crossed over to the side that had defeated this communion and shaped how Augustine, and generations of readers after him, learned to read Scripture - a transmission this world's own record does not explain and does not claim to.

The two-party contest itself did not end when this world's own window closes in 439. It continued under Vandal and then Byzantine rule for roughly a further century and a half, still legislated against, until Pope Gregory the Great wrote letters in the 590s pressing for an inquiry into Donatist rebaptism still being practiced in Numidia. After that, this world's own record simply falls silent. No line from it to any communion a person could visit today is documented anywhere, and this world's own Living Tradition Status has never been confirmed.

In its own record

How this world relates to others

This world's own refusal to grant the state any standing to judge the church is the mirror image of a neighboring world's own opposite condition: Imperial and Juridical Christianity, where church office became a form of state-adjacent power from Constantine's own reign onward, and where the same Constantine this world petitioned once and then refused for a century is that other world's own initiating figure. And this world's once-for-all rebaptism is the opposite answer Cappadocian Christianity gave to the identical question - how someone from the other side comes back in - which trained its own people to hold graded penance and graded reception as instruments of exactness rather than as a wall. Neither comparison is a claim that either world resolves the other; both are genuine divergences this world's own records name directly, not thematic echoes assumed from outside.

Sources

What this account is built from

This world's evidence survives in inverse proportion to how directly it can be checked without a hostile hand mediating it. Nearly the whole textual record passed through two Catholic writers, Optatus of Milevis and Augustine of Hippo, before reaching us - and that concentration is named as this world's own central evidentiary problem, not a background caveat. Four short texts speak as this communion rather than about it, plus the transcript of the 411 Conference, where for once no adversary chose which words to keep. The clearest exception of all was never a text to begin with: two words, Deo laudes, cut into stone. What this world's own record cannot supply at all is any specific report, excavation, or plan of the rooms these words were spoken in - Numidian basilica archaeology remains honestly undone in this world's own source ecology, so this world knows its own shout far better than the room it was shouted in. And one further live scholarly contest runs through the whole record rather than sitting in one place: whether the hostile portrait of this world's rural members, the Circumcellions, reflects their real character or mainly their opponents' polemic. That question is not adjudicated here.

  • Optatus of Milevis, Against the Donatists (De schismate Donatistarum), Books I-VII (c. 366-367, revised c. 385) The earliest sustained narrative of this world's own founding decade, written roughly five decades after the events by the Catholic bishop this communion regarded as its opponent - and the source through which most of what survives of that founding decade actually reaches us.
  • Augustine of Hippo, Answer to the Letters of Petilian, the Donatist (Contra litteras Petiliani), 3 books (Book I c. 400, Book III c. 401-402 on the NPNF Prolegomena's own dating) Three books quoting a Donatist bishop's own letters clause by clause in order to refute them - the fullest surviving Donatist voice, reaching us because it was chosen for answerability, not because anyone wanted it preserved for its own sake.
  • J.-P. Migne (editor); various late-antique authors and the recorders of the 411 Conference acts, Patrologiae Cursus Completus, Series Latina, Tomus XI -- the volume bundling Zeno of Verona's works with a substantial Optatus/Donatism cluster, including the Gesta Collationis Carthaginiensis itself (col. 1223) The transcript of the 411 Conference of Carthage - the one place in this world's record where its own bishops argue their own case, in their own recorded words, with no adversary curating what survived. Also this corpus's worst-damaged scan, so exact wording throughout is a careful reading of a difficult copy, not a settled critical edition.

In this world's own words

A few terms and lines worth knowing

Praise to God.

Anonymous inscribers of this communion, on two pillars near Bagai (CIL VIII 17732), with three further stones carrying the same words

What business does the emperor have with the church?

Donatus, our bishop at Carthage

A clever trick can hide the truth. We answer in full. They give us just a little bit, and call the rest closed.

Emeritus, our bishop at Caesarea

What matters is the giver's own character - that's what makes the one receiving it clean. Someone who knowingly takes their faith from a faithless hand doesn't receive faith at all - only guilt.

Petilian, our bishop at Constantina
traditor / traditio
When the persecutor came, some clergy handed the scriptures over to be burned. We call such a man a traditor. His hand is no longer clean. He cannot ordain, baptize, or bless. What flows from that hand is nothing at all.
rebaptizare
We do not think of this as baptizing twice. Nothing happened the first time. A hand from a tainted line washes nothing. So when someone comes to us from the other church, we give the first real baptism they have had.
martyr / martyrium
A martyr dies for the true faith. But our best-known dead were not killed by pagans. They were killed by imperial troops sent at the urging of a rival Christian party. That party has never once granted them the word. We do not accept its account of our own dead.
ecclesia
There is no neutral word for the church here. The word itself is the argument. Two whole rival bodies claim it, town by town, bishop for bishop. We hold that we are the true one, and that our rival's favour with the state settles nothing.
Quid est imperatori cum ecclesia? ("What has the emperor to do with the church?")
The state has no standing to say which church is the true one. 'What has the emperor to do with the church?' -- so our primate is reported to have asked. Yet three times we went to that same court when it served us. We hold both facts at once, and call neither one a lie.
