The Imperial Church Era · c. 311–439 CE · Carthage & Numidia, Roman North Africa

The Church of the Martyrs

Donatism

Open for conversation — you can sit down with this tradition now.

In 311/312, Caecilian was consecrated bishop of Carthage by a man accused of surrendering his copy of the scriptures when ordered to — and a rival was consecrated beside him because of it, not in his place. From that day the same see held two bishops at once, two parallel hierarchies contesting town after town across Roman North Africa, each holding its own basilicas and its own unbroken line of ordination. This is the rigorist alternative communion that grew from that refusal: not a doctrinal heresy — its confession is standard North African Latin Trinitarian and Christological orthodoxy — but a communion built on one unyielding conviction, that a tainted hand cannot give what it claims to give, lived out through rebaptism, the martyrs' graves read aloud once a year, and a refusal of the Christian emperor's own standing to judge the church. It closes in 439, when a Vandal army took Carthage and removed the Roman state this whole refusal was defined against.

Voices

The people behind this account

Donatus, our bishop at Carthage

Donatus succeeded Majorinus as bishop of Carthage around 313 and led this communion for more than three decades - long enough that history remembers the whole movement by his name, though nothing suggests his own people chose that name for themselves. He left no writing of his own. His single most quoted line survives only because the bishop who opposed him recorded it in order to answer it: what has the emperor to do with the church? He was exiled after the Macarian repression of 347-348 and died in exile.

Petilian, our bishop at Constantina

Petilian became bishop of Constantina, the renamed Cirta, in the 390s and was, by volume, this world's fullest surviving voice - because Augustine quoted his letters clause by clause across three books in order to refute them. At the great Conference of Carthage in 411 he argued in a different register entirely: on an open transcript, thanking the judge for the offer of a chair and refusing it, because Christ himself had not refused to stand before his own governor.

Emeritus, our bishop at Caesarea

Emeritus, bishop of Caesarea in Mauretania, carried this communion's case for three days at the Conference of Carthage in 411, on the best evidentiary footing any figure in this world's record holds: his own words, taken down by an imperial notary as he spoke them. He held the court to its own procedure before its substance, and when the argument finally reached Scripture, answered a reading of the wheat and the tares verse for verse, out of the same books. He signed the transcript of his own words again and again with the same qualified formula: his appeal reserved.

Marculus, blessed martyr

This world remembers Marculus as a man who had already given up worldly advancement before the persecution that killed him ever found him. Seized during the Macarian repression, he was flogged, paraded through several Numidian towns, and held four days at a cliff called Novapetra, where he was shown, in the fast, a vision of a cup, a crown, and a palm. He was thrown from the cliff before dawn.

Tyconius

Tyconius was the one systematic theologian this world produced, and the one it silenced. A layman, he argued from Scripture that the church was spread across the whole earth - an argument built well enough that his own bishop at Carthage told him never to preach it again. He did not recant, and did not defect to the other side either; he kept saying it from inside the party that had told him to stop, and was cut off for good.

A Rebuke Over a Martyr's Bone, and a Rival Bishop

311 or 312 (the Carthage election and the rival consecration)

A rich woman is told off for kissing a martyr's bone. Then a disputed vote ends with a rival bishop. The only account comes from the man who blames her.

OptatusOptatusA Catholic bishop of Milevis who wrote seven books against this church in the 360s and revised them about 385. Lucilla was a wealthy laywoman of Carthage who kept the bone of a martyr with her. She would kiss it before she took the cup at communion, and the archdeacon Caecilian rebuked her for it. Optatus adds that the dead man had not been formally recognized as a martyr, if he was one at all. She left, he says, full of anger.

Then persecution broke over Carthage, and the bishop, Mensurius, was summoned to the emperor's court. He left the church's gold and silver with some elders he trusted, and he gave an old woman a written list of it. When Mensurius did not come home, Botrus and Celestius, who had both expected the office, were passed over and Caecilian was elected.

Optatus says the elders had kept back more of the treasure than they should have. These elders, the two disappointed candidates and Lucilla now discovered a common cause. At a crowded meeting in the basilica, Caecilian demanded that his accusers state their charge. Purpurius only mocked him, and no proof was ever produced. Instead Majorinus, a member of Lucilla's household, was made bishop in Caecilian's place, at her urging and through her bribes, Optatus writes.

Optatus blames the whole split on her wounded pride, but that explanation is his, and we do not adopt it. Lucilla left no text, and no one asked her what she thought she was doing.

No record survives from the proconsul's side, and no independent notice of the deaths.

Legacy

What came after

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

One thread carried past this world's own horizon in a way its own founders could hardly have predicted. The Book of Rules, the one substantial work of biblical interpretation this world produced, survived the council that condemned its own author. It crossed over to the side that had defeated this communion and shaped how Augustine, and generations of readers after him, learned to read Scripture - a transmission this world's own record does not explain and does not claim to.

The two-party contest itself did not end when this world's own window closes in 439. It continued under Vandal and then Byzantine rule for roughly a further century and a half, still legislated against, until Pope Gregory the Great wrote letters in the 590s pressing for an inquiry into Donatist rebaptism still being practiced in Numidia. After that, this world's own record simply falls silent. No line from it to any communion a person could visit today is documented anywhere, and this world's own Living Tradition Status has never been confirmed.

In its own record

How this world relates to others

This world's own refusal to grant the state any standing to judge the church is the mirror image of a neighboring world's own opposite condition: Imperial and Juridical Christianity, where church office became a form of state-adjacent power from Constantine's own reign onward, and where the same Constantine this world petitioned once and then refused for a century is that other world's own initiating figure. And this world's once-for-all rebaptism is the opposite answer Cappadocian Christianity gave to the identical question - how someone from the other side comes back in - which trained its own people to hold graded penance and graded reception as instruments of exactness rather than as a wall. Neither comparison is a claim that either world resolves the other; both are genuine divergences this world's own records name directly, not thematic echoes assumed from outside.

Sources

What this account is built from

This world's evidence survives in inverse proportion to how directly it can be checked without a hostile hand mediating it. Nearly the whole textual record passed through two Catholic writers, Optatus of Milevis and Augustine of Hippo, before reaching us - and that concentration is named as this world's own central evidentiary problem, not a background caveat. Four short texts speak as this communion rather than about it, plus the transcript of the 411 Conference, where for once no adversary chose which words to keep. The clearest exception of all was never a text to begin with: two words, Deo laudes, cut into stone. What this world's own record cannot supply at all is any specific report, excavation, or plan of the rooms these words were spoken in - Numidian basilica archaeology remains honestly undone in this world's own source ecology, so this world knows its own shout far better than the room it was shouted in. And one further live scholarly contest runs through the whole record rather than sitting in one place: whether the hostile portrait of this world's rural members, the Circumcellions, reflects their real character or mainly their opponents' polemic. That question is not adjudicated here.

traditor / traditio
When the persecutor came, some clergy handed the scriptures over to be burned. We called such a man a traditor. We held that his hand was no longer clean, and that he could not ordain, baptize, or bless. What flowed from that hand was nothing at all.
rebaptizare
We did not think of this as baptizing twice. Nothing happened the first time. A hand from a tainted line washes nothing. So when someone came to us from the other church, we gave the first real baptism they had had.
martyr / martyrium
A martyr dies for the true faith. But our best-known dead were not killed by pagans. They were killed by imperial troops sent at the urging of a rival Christian party. That party did not grant them the word. We did not accept its account of our own dead.
ecclesia
There was no neutral word for the church here. The word itself was the argument. Two whole rival bodies claimed it, town by town, bishop for bishop. We held that we were the true one, and that our rival's favour with the state settled nothing.
Quid est imperatori cum ecclesia? ("What has the emperor to do with the church?")
We held that the state had no standing to say which church was the true one. 'What has the Emperor to do with the Church?' -- so our primate is reported to have asked. Yet three times we went to that same court when it served us. We held both facts at once, and called neither one a lie.
