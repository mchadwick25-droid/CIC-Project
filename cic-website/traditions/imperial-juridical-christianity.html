Church and Empire

Imperial and Juridical Christianity

Bishops in Rome, Constantinople, and Milan lived through a real change. It ran from Constantine's victory in 312 to the Council of Chalcedon in 451. They stopped being outlaws beneath the sword. They became office-holders beside a throne instead — settling their own disputes in council, managing imperial funds, summoned to councils at the emperor's own expense. This was not only a fight over power. At Chalcedon in 451, the council took a letter from Leo of Rome as the final word on what it means to say Christ has two natures. Whole regions, starting in Egypt, rejected that settlement at once. The split never healed. The same council also extended Constantinople's own claim to rank second among the churches, over the protest of Rome's own delegates. Three rival grounds of authority rose across this whole window. Rome claimed it from Peter. Constantinople claimed it from nearness to the throne. And Ambrose of Milan claimed that the emperor stood inside the church, not over it. The question was never settled. This Christian tradition's worst hours were caused, not suffered. One was Damasus's own disputed election to the bishopric of Rome, so violent that a hundred and thirty-seven people died in a single day. Another was Theodosius's massacre at Thessalonica. His own bishop refused him the sacrament over it until he repented. Ambrose used that same refusal two years earlier for the opposite end. He shielded a bishop who had incited a mob to burn the synagogue at Callinicum. The bishop never had to pay to rebuild it, and that community got no recourse.

People

The people behind this account

Constantine (c. 272/273–337, baptized on his deathbed)

Constantine won the battle that made him sole ruler of the West, and turned an empire that had hunted the church into one that funded it. Within a year of his victory, toleration was law. He convened the council of Nicaea and called himself a bishop of those outside the church. He was baptized only on his deathbed, decades later.

Ambrose (c. 339–397)

Ambrose, bishop of Milan, held a basilica against an emperor's own soldiers. He preached that the emperor stands inside the church, not above it. Four years later, he used that same sacramental leverage again. This time he forced an emperor into public penance for a massacre. And two years before that, he used the same leverage a third time. That time, he shielded men who had burned a synagogue - so they never had to pay for it.

Damasus (c. 305–384, "in the reign of the Emperor Theodosius")

Damasus, bishop of Rome, built the see's own claim into stone. He paid for verses to be carved at the martyrs' graves. Pilgrims could then read Rome's own memory of its descent from the apostles. His own election, in 366, turned violent. A hostile pagan witness records a hundred and thirty-seven dead, in one church, in one day.

Leo (unknown–461 - a decade past this world's own 451 close; his death is outside the window and nothing after Chalcedon's immediate aftermath is this world's to narrate)

Leo, bishop of Rome, wrote the letter that closed this world's own argument over Christ's two natures. It was read aloud at Chalcedon. Those who received it well heard it as the see of Peter speaking. Leo then refused that same council's ruling on Constantinople's own rank. He wrote to the emperor, the empress, and the bishop alike, to say so.

Justina (unknown–c. 388)

Justina was the mother of the young emperor Valentinian II. She was the real power behind him. She held the Homoian faith herself, and demanded a Milan basilica for it. When Ambrose refused, real force moved through her court. Merchants in the city were fined. People were locked up during Lent.

Pulcheria (399–453)

Pulcheria became empress in 414. She was one of two people whose own convening power actually brought about the Council of Chalcedon, in 451. Leo himself records that she commanded the council to be held. He had asked to hold it in Italy instead. She refused. He answered by sending his legates anyway, without protest.

Legacy

What came after

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

This world's own final council did not close in agreement. Chalcedon's settlement on Christ's two natures was final for those who accepted it. But whole regions rejected it at once, starting in Egypt, at the council itself. This rejection did not fade the way earlier disputes had. It hardened into lasting division. This world's own record ends right as that fracture opens. It does not know how the fracture will end.

The two rival claims this world argued over never stopped being argued over. Does this world's own record already amount to a settled papacy? Or is it a claim still being worked out? Later traditions still argue that question, from these same documents. Constantinople's own rank raises a second, related fight. This world's own closing council granted that rank on one ground: nearness to the throne, not an apostle's grave. The tradition that received the rank reads it one way. The see that refused it reads it another way. This world's own record opens that disagreement. It does not close it.

Related traditions

How this tradition relates to others

This world argued with real rivals, not invented ones. Inside the church itself, for roughly two decades, the Homoian confession held the imperial throne. It was not a fringe opponent working from outside power. It was the establishment. Its rivals had to work against it from underneath. In Africa, a separate church called the Donatists claimed to be the true church. They held basilicas the Catholics claimed as their own. Constantine's own courts ruled against them twice. Even so, the emperor paid to build a second church, rather than send troops. On the eastern frontier, monks burned a meeting-house that belonged to a different Christian group, the Valentinians. A mob burned a synagogue on the very same day. And outside the church altogether, in 384, Rome's last great pagan senator made his own case in writing. He argued that the old gods' rites should be restored to the Senate house. This is a rare moment. This world's record keeps its own defeated opponent's case, in his own words, answered right beside it rather than erased.

"Of one substance with the Father." The Nicene word that binds the Son to the Father as one and the same being. Not merely like God - sharing the very being that makes the Father God.
homoios
"Like the Father." The confession that the Son is like the Father, as the scriptures say - and no further word about shared being. For years the imperial church itself held this, at the emperor's command.
primatus
Rome's claim to first standing among the churches. Rome held that what was given to Peter passed to each bishop who sat in his seat after him, so it guarded the claim as an inheritance and did not treat it as an honor others grant.
presbeia (tes times)
The "prerogative of honor" was the new capital's claim to rank second among the churches. The emperor reigned there. No apostle had founded it. Rank followed the throne.
Imperator intra Ecclesiam, non supra Ecclesiam
"The emperor is within the Church, not above it." Ambrose said this in the basilica crisis, calling the emperor a son of the Church. The emperor's soldiers were real, yet Ambrose still held that what belongs to God is not Caesar's to take.
