The Imperial Church Era · c. 312–451 CE · Rome, Constantinople, Milan

Church and Empire

Imperial and Juridical Christianity

Open for conversation — you can sit down with this tradition now.

Bishops in Rome, Constantinople, and Milan lived through a real change. It ran from Constantine's victory in 312 to the Council of Chalcedon in 451. They stopped being outlaws beneath the sword. They became office-holders beside a throne instead — settling their own disputes in council, managing imperial funds, summoned to councils at the emperor's own expense. This was not only a fight over power. At Chalcedon in 451, the council took a letter from Leo of Rome as the final word on what it means to say Christ has two natures. Whole regions, starting in Egypt, rejected that settlement at once. The split never healed. The same council also extended Constantinople's own claim to rank second among the churches, over the protest of Rome's own delegates. Three rival grounds of authority rose across this whole window. Rome claimed it from Peter. Constantinople claimed it from nearness to the throne. And Ambrose of Milan claimed that the emperor stood inside the church, not over it. The question was never settled. This world's worst hours were caused, not suffered. One was Damasus's own disputed election to the bishopric of Rome, so violent that a hundred and thirty-seven people died in a single day. Another was Theodosius's massacre at Thessalonica. His own bishop refused him the sacrament over it until he repented. Ambrose used that same refusal two years earlier for the opposite end. He shielded a bishop who had incited a mob to burn the synagogue at Callinicum. The bishop never had to pay to rebuild it, and that community got no recourse.

Marius is a representative voice for this tradition, not a person who lived — built from its own letters and records, and honest about where they run out.

Who is speaking

A representative voice, speaking for a whole people

Six figures anchor this world's own voice. Constantine began the alliance that made everything else here possible. Ambrose, bishop of Milan, held a basilica against an emperor's own soldiers. He used that same sacramental leverage twice more. Once, to force an emperor into public penance. Once, to shield men who had burned a synagogue. Damasus built Rome's own claim into martyr-shrine stone. His own election was so violent it left more than a hundred dead. Leo was Rome's fullest voice. He sent a letter that closed this world's own argument over Christ's two natures. Then he refused a council's own ruling on where authority itself belonged. Justina and Pulcheria are the two women this world's record shows wielding real command. One was defeated. One convened the council that closed the age. Neither left us her own words.

The Story

How this tradition took shape

This world's own record runs from 312 to 451 CE, in Rome, Constantinople, and Milan. It opens when the emperor Constantine allied himself with a church he had once hunted. It closes at the Council of Chalcedon, the last council of the age to legislate as though everyone could still agree. Three claims run through the whole span. Rome grounds its authority in the apostle Peter. Constantinople grounds its rank in nearness to the emperor. And a third voice, from Milan, grounds authority in a bishop's own sacramental and moral standing over any ruler.

In 312, at the Milvian Bridge, Constantine won the battle that made him sole ruler of the West. What exactly turned him toward Christ is not settled, even in this world's own record. Eusebius, his own admiring historian, says he saw a cross of light at midday, with his whole army watching. Lactantius, writing earlier and independently, has him ordered in a dream, the night before, to mark his soldiers' shields with Christ's sign. The two stories do not agree, and this world does not force them to. What is certain is the consequence. Within a year, toleration was law, and a persecuted church became a funded one.

That alliance is the fact everything else in this world moves within. Emperors now protected bishops. They asked for their advice. They made laws about their faith. And they expected obedience in return. But the alliance never held one shape for long. Which faith the empire favored changed with whoever wore the crown. Every side in this world had to live inside that instability.

For roughly two decades, under the emperors Constantius II and Valens, the empire's own church held a different creed. It confessed the Son as merely "like" the Father, not of one substance with him. This confession was called Homoian. It was not a fringe belief in this window. It was the establishment itself, held at the emperor's own command. Bishops who confessed the rival word - homoousios, "of one substance" - were the ones removed and exiled. What survives of the Homoian side's own voice is almost nothing. One damaged fragment survives. A fellow believer preserved it, then partly erased it as heresy before it reached later readers. Nearly everything else comes down through the account of the side that eventually won.

Ambrose, bishop of Milan, answered that whole imperial logic from inside it. In 386, an imperial court that itself held the Homoian faith demanded one of Milan's churches for its own worship. Ambrose refused to give it up. Soldiers surrounded the building. His congregation stayed inside with him, not knowing whether the orders outside meant to wait or to storm. Augustine, then a young man in the same city, remembered that it was during that siege that Milan's people first sang hymns in a manner borrowed from the Eastern churches, so the waiting did not pass in silence - a practice, he wrote, that was kept from then on. In the middle of it, Ambrose preached the sentence this world remembers him for: the emperor is within the church, not above it. The court did not take the building.

That same leverage was not always used to restrain power. Two years earlier, in 388, a Christian mob burned a synagogue at Callinicum, on the eastern frontier. The local bishop had urged them on. Monks separately burned a meeting-house that belonged to a different Christian group, the Valentinians. The emperor Theodosius ordered the normal remedy. The bishop would pay to rebuild the synagogue. The monks would be punished. Ambrose wrote to have that order reversed. He did not deny the burning. He judged it intolerable for a bishop to be made to fund a synagogue. When the order stood, he refused the emperor the sacrament. Theodosius gave in and withdrew it. The same tool this world elsewhere shows restraining a throne here shielded the men who burned a Jewish community's synagogue. It left that community no recourse. This world's own record does not soften that.

Two years later, the same leverage was turned the other way. In 390, Theodosius's soldiers massacred the crowd at Thessalonica. It is said seven thousand died. Ambrose wrote him a private letter. He dared not offer the sacrifice with the emperor present, he said. Theodosius had to do public penance before he could stand at the altar again. A bishop with no soldiers had closed the altar to the man who commanded them all. And the man submitted.

On a different front, the bishops of Rome built a rival kind of authority. This was not sacramental leverage. It was a legal claim: the right to review any church's own judgment. In 341, a council in the East deposed the bishop of Alexandria. It did not consult Rome first. Julius, bishop of Rome, wrote back. He questioned the council's right to decide the matter at all. Decades later, Damasus built that same claim into stone. He paid for verses to be carved at the martyrs' graves. Pilgrims could then read Rome's own memory of its descent from the apostles, for themselves. His own election to that see, in 366, turned violent. A hostile pagan witness records a hundred and thirty-seven bodies, in one church, in one day.

Behind every one of these contests stood a drive to say the faith in words exact enough to bind. At Nicaea in 325, the council chose the word homoousios: "of one substance." That word is not in scripture. The council chose it over a looser, more scriptural-sounding word, because the looser word left the door open to error. Even a bishop who signed the creed at Nicaea itself, Eusebius of Caesarea, wrote home to explain his own hesitation over that word. The argument over what it meant, and whether it was needed, ran on for the rest of this world's span.

Two different grounds of authority ran through this whole world. They never merged. One ground was sanctity and sacrament - a bishop's own moral leverage. The other was office and legal standing - a see's own rank. Ambrose used the first kind directly against an emperor. Damasus did something different. He folded the memory of dead martyrs into Rome's own claim to rank, using their sanctity to argue for the living see's own standing. The tension was never resolved. Different men only ever managed it, each in his own way.

This world closes at Chalcedon in 451. Leo, bishop of Rome, did not attend; his letter went in his place. When it was read to the assembled bishops, the session record has them crying out that Peter had spoken through Leo. But before the council dispersed, it also granted Constantinople rank just after Rome - not because an apostle had ever taught there, but because the emperor now reigned there. Leo's own representatives objected on the spot. Leo himself, once word reached him, refused to accept it. The council that had just settled who Christ is left open the very question it had spent this world's whole span arguing over: where, on earth, the church's own final authority actually sits.

Voices

The people behind this account

Constantine (c. 272/273–337, baptized on his deathbed)

Constantine won the battle that made him sole ruler of the West, and turned an empire that had hunted the church into one that funded it. Within a year of his victory, toleration was law. He convened the council of Nicaea and called himself a bishop of those outside the church. He was baptized only on his deathbed, decades later.

This world's own hedge: Two accounts of what turned Constantine toward Christ survive. This world's own record does not force them to agree. One was written within a few years, by a Christian at his own court. The other came decades later, from his own admiring biographer. That later account has Constantine and his whole army seeing a vision at midday. What is certain is the alliance that followed - not the experience behind it.

Ambrose (c. 339–397)

Ambrose, bishop of Milan, held a basilica against an emperor's own soldiers. He preached that the emperor stands inside the church, not above it. Four years later, he used that same sacramental leverage again. This time he forced an emperor into public penance for a massacre. And two years before that, he used the same leverage a third time. That time, he shielded men who had burned a synagogue - so they never had to pay for it.

This world's own hedge: Almost everything we know of Ambrose comes from his own account of his own fights. The 386 standoff does have a real witness from outside - Augustine's own memory. Not every event does. And this world's own record will not let him look good every time. The same tool that once made an emperor kneel also once shielded men who had burned a Jewish community's synagogue.

Damasus (c. 305–384, "in the reign of the Emperor Theodosius")

Damasus, bishop of Rome, built the see's own claim into stone. He paid for verses to be carved at the martyrs' graves. Pilgrims could then read Rome's own memory of its descent from the apostles. His own election, in 366, turned violent. A hostile pagan witness records a hundred and thirty-seven dead, in one church, in one day.

This world's own hedge: This world tells that hard fact about Damasus's own election. It does not hide it. A body of decrees also circulates under his name. This record does not use them. Modern scholarship judges much of that corpus a later forgery, backdated to look older than it is. His genuine synodical letters, and his own verse-inscriptions, carry his record instead.

Leo (unknown–461 - a decade past this world's own 451 close; his death is outside the window and nothing after Chalcedon's immediate aftermath is this world's to narrate)

Leo, bishop of Rome, wrote the letter that closed this world's own argument over Christ's two natures. It was read aloud at Chalcedon. Those who received it well heard it as the see of Peter speaking. Leo then refused that same council's ruling on Constantinople's own rank. He wrote to the emperor, the empress, and the bishop alike, to say so.

This world's own hedge: Leo's own letters assert the very authority they are offered as evidence for. A bishop of Rome states his own standing, in his own words. Did the wider church actually accept that claim as settled? Or was it only ever stated, never fully agreed? This world's own closing argument, Canon 28, leaves that question open.

Justina (unknown–c. 388)

Justina was the mother of the young emperor Valentinian II. She was the real power behind him. She held the Homoian faith herself, and demanded a Milan basilica for it. When Ambrose refused, real force moved through her court. Merchants in the city were fined. People were locked up during Lent.

This world's own hedge: Justina reaches this record only through the writing of the bishop who defeated her. His own letter names the emperor, not her, as the one who used that force. It is this record's own chronology that names her instead. This world states that difference. It does not resolve it.

Pulcheria (399–453)

Pulcheria became empress in 414. She was one of two people whose own convening power actually brought about the Council of Chalcedon, in 451. Leo himself records that she commanded the council to be held. He had asked to hold it in Italy instead. She refused. He answered by sending his legates anyway, without protest.

This world's own hedge: Leo's letters address Pulcheria as a real political power, not as a courtesy. That is direct evidence of her own agency. But nothing of her own words survives anywhere in this world's record - not on her own terms, not anywhere.

Documented stories

Stories worth telling in full

Ambrose Defends the Men Who Burned a Synagogue

388, on the eastern frontier

A Christian mob burns a Jewish community's synagogue. The same bishop who elsewhere makes an emperor kneel uses his leverage here to shield the arsonists from paying for it.

Read the full account

At Callinicum, on the eastern frontier, a Christian mob burned the town's synagogue - at the local bishop's own urging. Monks separately burned a Valentinian meeting-house. Theodosius heard of it and ordered the ordinary remedy: the bishop would pay to rebuild the synagogue, and the monks would be punished. Ambrose wrote directly to the emperor to get the order reversed. Not because he denied the burning. He judged it intolerable for a bishop to be forced to fund a synagogue - and worse, in his own words, for the burning of a Valentinian house, which he called worse than heathen, to be answered at all. When the order stood, Ambrose preached in front of Theodosius in church. He refused to offer the sacrament until the emperor promised, on the spot, to withdraw the order. Theodosius gave in. Refusing the emperor the sacrament was the same tool Ambrose used, in another story, to make an emperor repent of a massacre. Here the same tool was used to protect the arsonists, so they never had to pay for what they burned. It left a burned community with no recourse.

The Altar of Victory: Rome's Old Religion Makes Its Case

384, the Senate house at Rome

A pagan senator asks the state to fund the old rites again. The bishop of Milan answers him twice - and the establishment wins, with the loser's own eloquent plea preserved right beside it.

Read the full account

The altar of Victory had stood in the Senate house for centuries. The emperors had it removed. In the year 384, Symmachus, prefect of Rome and the old religion's most eloquent voice, petitioned the young emperor to bring it back. His plea survives, grave and moving: Rome's ancient worship had guarded Rome's ancient greatness, and so great a secret could not be reached by one road alone. Ambrose answered him twice, in writing, and the answers survive beside the plea. The emperor is a Christian, he argued. The state may not fund what the faith knows to be false. And what Symmachus called reverence for the past was the past's own long error. The altar stayed removed. The exchange is our establishment speaking at its most confident. It is also the rare place in our record where the defeated side is heard making its own case, in its own words, at full strength.

The Man Who Abolished Sanctuary Has to Use It

399, Constantinople

A fallen chamberlain who stripped the church of its right of refuge runs to the altar himself. The bishop who shelters him preaches his ruin as a warning - and loses him to execution anyway.

Read the full account

Eutropius was a eunuch who rose to be chamberlain to the emperor Arcadius, and in 399 he became the first eunuch ever made consul. Among the things he did with that power: he procured laws that cut off the church's ancient right of refuge, so that a fugitive at the altar could be taken. Then he fell. He ran to the Great Church and grabbed the altar - the one protection he had himself destroyed - and the soldiers came for him. The bishop, John, preached with him lying there. He did not preach comfort. He preached the emptiness of everything Eutropius had spent his life getting, in front of the man and in front of the city: where is the consulship now, where are the torches, the flatteries, the swarm of hangers-on? Then he turned on his own congregation, who wanted the man given up. He told them they were servants of a crucified man who had said, forgive them. Yes, he granted - this is the man who abolished refuge here. And he has learned by experience what he did. The emperor held the army off, but it did not hold. Eutropius left the church, was taken, exiled, and executed. John preached a second time, about that too.

Where we are quiet

What the record does not say

You ask for an ordinary day among us: the food, the work, the children, the women's own lives, the sick and the dying. Here is our problem. Our record comes from courts and bishops, and it noticed ordinary people mostly when they filled a basilica or a riot. We know the crowds of our cities were there. On one besieged night they enter the record singing, and for a moment we hear them. The only women our record shows acting are two empresses, and even they speak only through other men's accounts. The daily bread, the household, the deathbed of an ordinary believer: those were lived, not written, and we will not invent them.

Legacy

What came after

This world's own final council did not close in agreement. Chalcedon's settlement on Christ's two natures was final for those who accepted it. But whole regions rejected it at once, starting in Egypt, at the council itself. This rejection did not fade the way earlier disputes had. It hardened into lasting division. This world's own record ends right as that fracture opens. It does not know how the fracture will end.

The two rival claims this world argued over never stopped being argued over. Does this world's own record already amount to a settled papacy? Or is it a claim still being worked out? Later traditions still argue that question, from these same documents. Constantinople's own rank raises a second, related fight. This world's own closing council granted that rank on one ground: nearness to the throne, not an apostle's grave. The tradition that received the rank reads it one way. The see that refused it reads it another way. This world's own record opens that disagreement. It does not close it.

In its own record

How this world relates to others

This world argued with real rivals, not invented ones. Inside the church itself, for roughly two decades, the Homoian confession held the imperial throne. It was not a fringe opponent working from outside power. It was the establishment. Its rivals had to work against it from underneath. In Africa, a separate church called the Donatists claimed to be the true church. They held basilicas the Catholics claimed as their own. Constantine's own courts ruled against them twice. Even so, the emperor paid to build a second church, rather than send troops. On the eastern frontier, monks burned a meeting-house that belonged to a different Christian group, the Valentinians. A mob burned a synagogue on the very same day. And outside the church altogether, in 384, Rome's last great pagan senator made his own case in writing. He argued that the old gods' rites should be restored to the Senate house. This is a rare moment. This world's record keeps its own defeated opponent's case, in his own words, answered right beside it rather than erased.

Sources

What this account is built from

This world's record is richest in one shape: the paper trail of office. Council acts, church laws, imperial laws, and letters between bishops and emperors carry nearly everything that survives. Elsewhere, it runs thin, or falls silent. No ordinary believer's own words about any event survive. Only bishops, emperors, and their courts speak. Women show up as real political powers, not just as obstacles. But they never speak in their own words. Even the two who held real, proven power reach us only through men with their own stake in the story. And for roughly two decades, the empire's own church held a creed whose own fullest voice barely survives at all. We know it mostly through the side that later beat it.

In this world's own words

A few terms and lines worth knowing

I have no reason, I say, to charge you with defiance; but I do have reason to fear. I dare not offer the sacrifice if you intend to be present. Is something that is not allowed after the blood of one innocent person has been shed, allowed after the blood of many? I do not think so.

Ambrose

We are the servants of the crucified one, who said, 'Forgive them, for they do not know what they are doing.' But, you say, this man cut off the right of refuge here with his decrees and his many kinds of laws. Yes! And now he has learned by experience what it was he did. He himself, by his own actions, has been the first to break his law, and he has become a spectacle to the whole world.

John Chrysostom, bishop of Constantinople, preaching in 399 while Eutropius clung to the altar behind him

Let the saint rejoice: he draws near to victory. Let the sinner be glad: he is invited to pardon. Let the gentile take courage: he is called to life.

Leo

The most distinguished Presidents and the most honorable Assessors said: 'These vulgar outcries are not worthy of bishops, and they will not help either side. Allow, therefore, the reading of all the documents.'

the imperial Presidents and Assessors at Chalcedon, Session I
homoousios
"Of one substance with the Father." The Nicene word that binds the Son to the Father as one and the same being. Not merely like God - sharing the very being that makes the Father God.
homoios
"Like the Father." The confession that the Son is like the Father, as the scriptures say - and no further word about shared being. For years the imperial church itself held this, at the emperor's command.
primatus
Rome's claim to first standing among the churches. What was given to Peter, Rome holds, was given to each bishop who sits in his seat after him. Not an honor others grant - an inheritance Rome guards.
presbeia (tes times)
The "prerogative of honor": Constantinople's claim to rank second among the churches. Not because an apostle founded it - because the emperor now reigns there. Rank follows the throne.
Imperator intra Ecclesiam, non supra Ecclesiam
"The emperor is within the Church, not over the Church." Ambrose's word from the basilica crisis. A ruler is a son of the Church, under her discipline like any believer. His soldiers are real - but he cannot command what belongs to the altar.
