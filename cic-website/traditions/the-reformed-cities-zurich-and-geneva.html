The Reformed Cities

The Reformed Cities — Zurich & Geneva

Open for conversation — you can sit down with this tradition now.

On 1 January 1519, Huldrych Zwingli, newly appointed pastor at Zurich's Grossmünster, began preaching straight through the Gospel of Matthew rather than following the fixed lectionary — the first act of a reform that tested every inherited practice against Scripture and kept only what the text would warrant. Geneva's own reform opened separately and was joined in 1536 by John Calvin, whose ministry built a Consistory of pastors and lay elders to hold the whole city's ordinary conduct to account. Zwingli and Calvin never met; what formally bridges the two cities is the Consensus Tigurinus (1549), two reforms arriving independently at language neither could disown. This pattern continues unbroken into the Scottish Kirk, the Huguenots, the Dutch Reformed, and English Puritanism.

The Story

How this tradition took shape

This world's own record runs from 1519 to 1650, and it holds two cities' reforms together without ever merging them into one. Zurich's reform opens first, five years before Geneva's, and neither founding figure ever had occasion to meet the other. One conviction sits underneath both cities all the same: Scripture alone is sufficient to test what a church may require, and what it will not yield, no pope, council, or custom may set aside.

Zurich's reform began on 1 January 1519, when Huldrych Zwingli, newly appointed as the city's people's priest, chose to preach straight through the Gospel of Matthew rather than follow the fixed yearly calendar of readings. Three years later, a Lenten meal at a printer's house, where several guests ate meat during the fast, drew the city council's own attention and pushed the argument into the open. By January 1523, six hundred people filled Zurich's Town Hall for the First Disputation, where Zwingli's own case was tested article by article against Scripture. A Zurich schoolmaster named Erhart Hegenwald sat through the whole day and wrote it down. The council ruled for the reform and bound every priest in its territory to preach the same way.

Geneva's own reform enacts the identical conviction a different way. Its 1526 alliance with Bern and its 1536 break from Savoy opened the civic ground; Calvin arrived that same year, was exiled to Strasbourg for three years, and was recalled in 1541 to consolidate the Ecclesiastical Ordinances. Those Ordinances created a Consistory of pastors and lay elders with real disciplinary standing - not a body answerable to the civil council alone. Where Zurich tests a claim aloud before the whole city, Geneva builds the same authority up article by article, through Calvin's own Institutes and Catechism, taught to the whole population.

This world's own record treats Disputation and catechesis as two enactments of the same authority, not two churches that happen to agree. Neither city borrowed the other's method, and neither needed to: the same scriptural authority produced doctrine independently in both places. What the two methods never did, across this world's whole span, is converge into one arrangement. Zurich's council still governed church and city as a single body; Geneva's Consistory fought for, and only by 1555 substantially won, its own independence from exactly that kind of council control.

God's own sovereign election is this world's own second load-bearing conviction, present in seed form in Zwingli's 1527 exposition and built into full system in Calvin's Institutes. It is held as the ground of a settled life, not a threat: whoever is joined to Christ by true faith already has, in that faith, the only evidence of election anyone is given. Bullinger's own Second Helvetic Confession states the same pastoral answer directly - look to Christ, not to your own conscience, for proof of election. In 1551, Jerome Bolsec publicly challenged this doctrine at Geneva, forcing it to be defended as a live pastoral question before the whole congregation, not only stated as settled teaching.

On the Lord's Supper, this world refuses two claims at once: Rome's claim that the Mass repeats Christ's own sacrifice, and Wittenberg's claim that Christ's body sits locally in the bread. Zurich's own 1523 refusal of the Mass matured, at the Marburg Colloquy in 1529, into a further refusal aimed specifically at Luther. Calvin, then a young law student, was not present at Marburg, and Geneva was not yet reformed. Twenty years later, in 1549, Calvin travelled to Zurich himself with his colleague William Farel rather than trust the question to a letter, and what they worked out together with Zurich's own pastors became the Consensus Tigurinus.

Whether the Consensus deepens Zwingli's own earlier "remembrance" language or simply restates it more fully is a question this world's own record does not resolve. Both texts are held together rather than one being read as correcting the other: Zwingli's 1523 word that the Supper is "a remembrance of the sacrifice," and the Consensus's own 1549 formula that the sign and the thing it signifies are distinguished but never disjoined. This is this world's own genuinely open question, not a settled scholarly finding that can be reported as fact.

This world's own founding circle at Zurich also produced a schism from within. On 21 January 1525, Conrad Grebel baptized Georg Blaurock at Felix Manz's own house, and Blaurock baptized the others present - the first Swiss Brethren baptisms, only two years after the First Disputation. Grebel and Manz had both stood with Zwingli before breaking from him: their own case was that a church gathered only by a believer's own profession, not a whole city, was the more scripturally consistent reading. Zurich's council answered with more than argument - it moved to suppress the movement by civil force in the years that followed, a fact this world's own record does not hide.

At Geneva, the Consistory's own independence from civil control was tested hardest by the Perrinist faction, which contested its standing directly. The fight was substantially resolved in the Consistory's own favor only by 1555, a full generation after Calvin's 1541 recall - the sharpest, most concentrated proof that Zurich's and Geneva's own answers to who governs church discipline never became one arrangement.

Zwingli did not survive to see any of this settled. He rode out with Zurich's own muster to the Second War of Kappel on 11 October 1531 and was killed that same afternoon, serving as a field chaplain in a war neither city had chosen alone. His close friend Oswald Myconius, who was in the city that day, later wrote down what he remembered: a sudden, unaccountable pain of heart as he watched Zwingli ride out, and Zwingli's own reported last words, that his enemies could kill the body but not the soul. Heinrich Bullinger succeeded him and held Zurich's own pastorate for four decades.

Bullinger's own Second Helvetic Confession, written in 1566 - decades after Zwingli's death - and the Heidelberg Catechism of 1563, a third, non-Zurich and non-Geneva Reformed voice, together fixed this movement's shared confessional core in writing once a founding generation's own method needed a text to outlive it. At the Synod of Dort in 1618-19, an international Reformed delegation, reportedly including Geneva and the Swiss cantons, pressed the same conviction about God's own free election to its sharpest statement yet, carried forward at one remove through Theodore Beza's own doctrinal throughline.

This world's own record is doctrine and confession, almost to the exclusion of everything else. It keeps no saints' lives and no martyrs' cults anywhere - its own confessional core refused that devotional genre from its first founding statement onward, and the absence is evidence the refusal worked, not a missing source. What a stranger walking into one of its own churches would have actually seen - no altar, a plain table, for years no instrument answering the voice in worship - is reasoned from doctrine, not read from a witness who stood there. Catholic pressure never let up across the whole window, from the Jesuits at Fribourg to the 1590s reconquest fought on Geneva's own doorstep, without ever converting into an event that closed this world down.

Voices

The people behind this account

Huldrych Zwingli (1531)

Zwingli was Zurich's own founder. He began preaching straight through Matthew in 1519 rather than follow the fixed lectionary, and by 1523 had defended his own case at the First Disputation, staking everything on being corrected only from Scripture itself. He taught that the Supper is chiefly a remembrance of Christ's finished sacrifice, not a repetition of it. He died at the Second War of Kappel in 1531, serving as a field chaplain.

John Calvin

Calvin was Geneva's own pastor from 1536, exiled to Strasbourg for three years, then recalled in 1541 to consolidate the Ecclesiastical Ordinances and the Consistory they created. His Institutes and Geneva Catechism built up, article by article, the doctrine this world holds most fully: that God's own free election, not anything earned, decides who is saved, held as comfort rather than threat.

Heinrich Bullinger

Bullinger succeeded Zwingli at Zurich in 1531 and held that pastorate for four decades. His own Second Helvetic Confession (1566) gave this world's shared conviction its mature, lasting written form, including its own pastoral answer to doubt: look to Christ, not to your own conscience, for proof of election.

Oswald Myconius

Myconius was Zwingli's own close friend, in Zurich on the day Zwingli rode out to the Second War of Kappel and was killed. He later wrote down what he remembered: a sudden pain of heart as he watched his friend leave, and the words Zwingli was reported to have said as he fell.

Documented stories

Stories worth telling in full

Six Hundred People and a Council That Ruled

29 January 1523, Zurich's Town Hall

Enemies of the new preaching mocked the gathering in advance as fit only for tinkers. A schoolmaster sat through the whole day and wrote down why they were wrong.

Read the full account

The city council of Zurich did not simply permit the argument that launched our own Reformation there. It summoned it. On 29 January 1523, six hundred people filled the Town Hall - priests and laymen from across the canton, and delegates from the bishop of Constance. Before it began, enemies of the new preaching mocked the whole gathering as a "tinker's day," saying nothing but tinkers would attend. That mockery is why our own record of the day survives in such detail: a Zurich schoolmaster named Erhart Hegenwald sat through the whole disputation and afterward wrote an account specifically to answer the sneer. "I was there myself and sat with them," he wrote, "heard and understood and remembered all that was said there." The council did not merely watch. When the arguments were finished, it found for our own side, and ordered every priest in the canton to preach the same way. An argument some had already dismissed in advance became an official act of civic reform.

Why Calvin Made the Journey Himself

1549

Worried that Geneva's and Zurich's own teaching on the Supper might quietly disagree, Calvin did not trust a letter. He went to Zurich in person, with Farel at his side.

Read the full account

By 1549, a quiet worry had spread among people who respected both our own churches: did Calvin's own teaching on the Supper actually agree with what Zurich taught? Calvin heard the worry directly, from people who revered both churches and did not want an appearance of disagreement to slow their own faith. A letter would not settle it. He went himself, and asked his own colleague William Farel - "indefatigable soldier of Christ," he called him - to come too. Farel needed no persuading; he had suggested the visit himself. What the two of them worked out with Zurich's own pastors became the Consensus Tigurinus. Calvin's own letter afterward is careful about what it actually is: not a new invention, but a faithful record of the conference - and not his and Farel's own private business, but binding on every colleague serving Christ under Geneva's own jurisdiction as well.

What His Friend Remembered of the Day He Died

11 October 1531, the Second War of Kappel

Zwingli rode out to battle and was killed that same afternoon. His closest friend wrote down what he remembered - and was careful to say which parts he saw and which he was only told.

Read the full account

Zwingli left Zurich for the front early on 11 October 1531, and was killed that same afternoon. His close friend Oswald Myconius was in the city that day and later wrote down what he remembered - warnings that, looking back, seemed to have foretold it; a disorganized muster, fewer than four thousand five hundred where five thousand had been summoned; a sudden pain of heart Myconius felt as he watched Zwingli ride out in the rear. That night the news came back: the fight was sharp and lost, and Zwingli was dead, struck down three times and rising each time before a fourth blow brought him to his knees. He was reported to have said, as he fell, "What evil is there in this? They are able, it is true, to kill the body but not the soul." His body was found by the victors afterward, given a mock trial, and burned. When his friends searched the field, they found - Myconius records this as something reported to him, not seen - that his heart had come through the fire whole and unburned.

Legacy

What came after

This world's own gravities transmit, unbroken, into four named descendant traditions: the Scottish Kirk, the Huguenots, the Dutch Reformed, and English Puritanism. No external or internal force closes this world within its own 1519-1650 window - its own administrative close is an editorial boundary for a still living tradition, not a historical ending.

At the Synod of Dort (1618-19), an international Reformed delegation pressed this world's own doctrine of election to its sharpest statement yet, carried forward at one remove through Theodore Beza's own doctrinal throughline from Geneva. This sharpens the conviction already held; it does not close or complete it - Dort falls thirty-two years before this world's own window ends.

In its own record

How this world relates to others

This world knew itself partly by what it refused, against three real neighbors. It refused Rome's own repeated sacrifice and its images, inherited from the late-medieval church both this world and Lutheran Wittenberg contested. It refused Wittenberg's own claim that Christ's body sits locally in the bread, pressed specifically at the Marburg Colloquy in 1529. And it refused a third claim from inside its own founding circle at Zurich - that a church gathered only by a believer's own profession was the more scripturally consistent reading - answered in the years after 1525 with civil force, not only argument.

Sources

What this account is built from

This world's own record is richest in confession and doctrine: Calvin's Institutes and Catechism, Zwingli's own founding statements, the jointly signed Consensus Tigurinus, and Bullinger's Second Helvetic Confession all survive and are well documented. It goes thin or silent elsewhere. No surviving account describes an ordinary Sunday service, a Consistory summons, or an ordinary citizen's own experience of either reform. Geneva's own consistory registers - the very archive this world was selected for, and the reason it has been described elsewhere as one of the best ordinary-life archives in Christian history - remain unacquired; the general doctrine of discipline is Documented, but no single real case currently survives in this world's own record. Three of this world's own most consequential disputes - the Marburg Colloquy, the Bolsec controversy, and the Perrinist crisis - have no surviving primary narrative comparable to this world's own three built stories. And no woman's own words survive: Marie Dentiere wrote in Geneva's own defense and was prosecuted for it, but her own argument has not yet reached us.

  • John Calvin, Institutes of the Christian Religion, Books II-III (Of the Knowledge of God the Redeemer; Of the Mode of Obtaining the Grace of Christ) Geneva's own load-bearing doctrinal core - Christ the Redeemer, faith, and predestination. Calvin's own surviving corpus runs roughly four times Zwingli's by volume in this world's own record, a disclosed concentration worth reading with that asymmetry in mind.
  • Huldrych Zwingli, The Sixty-Seven Articles (1523) Zurich's own founding doctrinal statement, defended article by article at the First Disputation in 1523. Article XVIII carries the earlier, sharper pole of this world's own unresolved tension over what the Supper actually is.
  • John Calvin and the Zurich pastors (jointly negotiated and signed), Mutual Consent in Regard to the Sacraments (the Consensus Tigurinus, 1549/1554) The one document in this world's entire corpus that both cities' own hands actually touched - a jointly negotiated 1549 statement on the Supper, not a record of prior contact between Zwingli and Calvin, who never met.

In this world's own words

A few terms and lines worth knowing

We reject those who seek, apart from Christ, whether they are chosen. Let Christ be the mirror in which we behold our own election.

Heinrich Bullinger

Christ sacrificed himself once. That sacrifice is, to eternity, a certain and valid offering for the sins of all the faithful. From this it follows that the Mass is not a sacrifice. It is a remembrance of that sacrifice, and an assurance of the salvation Christ has given us.

Huldrych Zwingli

What evil is there in this? It is true, they can kill the body, but not the soul.

Huldrych Zwingli, as reported by Oswald Myconius
Sola Scriptura
Scripture alone, not church tradition or a pope's decree, settles what we believe and how we govern our churches. Zurich tested this in public debate. Geneva built it up through sustained teaching. Both cities held the same conviction.
Predestination and Election
God chose, before the world began and by his own free will alone, who would be saved. Both Zurich and Geneva held this. Geneva built it into a full system. Zurich stated it more briefly, as pastoral comfort.
The Lord's Supper and Spiritual Presence
We refuse two claims about the Lord's Supper. It does not repeat Christ's own sacrifice. His body does not sit physically inside the bread. What we affirm took two cities and a 1549 agreement to state together: Christ is truly given, by the Spirit, to whoever receives him believing.
Consistory
At Geneva, the Consistory is our body of pastors and lay elders. It holds our whole population's daily conduct to account. Zurich has no equivalent. Its council governs church and city directly.
Disputation
A Disputation was a public debate held before Zurich's own city council. Our reforming side defended its case from Scripture. The old church's side answered. This is how Zurich actually decided to reform - not a private argument, a civic one.
