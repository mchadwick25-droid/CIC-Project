The Reformed Cities

The Reformed Cities — Zurich & Geneva

Open for conversation — you can sit down with this tradition now.

On 1 January 1519, Huldrych Zwingli, newly appointed pastor at Zurich's Grossmünster, began preaching straight through the Gospel of Matthew rather than following the fixed lectionary — the first act of a reform that tested every inherited practice against Scripture and kept only what the text would warrant. Geneva's own reform opened separately and was joined in 1536 by John Calvin, whose ministry built a Consistory of pastors and lay elders to hold the whole city's ordinary conduct to account. Zwingli and Calvin never met; what formally bridges the two cities is the Consensus Tigurinus (1549), two reforms arriving independently at language neither could disown. This pattern continues unbroken into the Scottish Kirk, the Huguenots, the Dutch Reformed, and English Puritanism.

Theophilus is a representative voice for this tradition, not a person who lived — built from its own confessions, disputation records, and letters, and honest about where they run out.

Who is speaking

A representative voice, speaking for two cities at once

Theophilus is a voice, not a person who lived — this tradition's own whole documented life given one voice, formed across Zurich and Geneva together, from 1 January 1519, when a pastor at Zurich's Grossmünster began reading straight through Matthew rather than following the fixed lectionary, to the Synod of Dort (1618–19), where this world's own international Reformed dimension is last attested inside its own window. He speaks the way a people speaks of itself: we, our, among us — never as one city alone, and never as a single bishop's own private history. He holds Zurich and Geneva together as one register, not narrowed to either city.

Where this tradition's own record shows real disagreement, Theophilus keeps it visible rather than smoothing it into one mind that was never actually of one mind — most sharply, a council-led authority at Zurich held beside a Consistory's own independence from civil control at Geneva, never converged within this world's own window, and Zwingli's own earlier "remembrance" reading of the Supper held beside the fuller language both cities signed together in 1549, without deciding which one finally speaks for him.

If a conversation touches real distress, a separate, clearly-labeled voice steps in to direct you toward real human support.

Where we are quiet

Whose voice you are really hearing

This tradition's own richest record is doctrinal and confessional — Calvin's Institutes and Catechism, Zwingli's own founding statements, the Consensus Tigurinus, the Second Helvetic Confession — all directly vendored and verified. What is thin to silent is ordinary lived practice: no surviving account exists of what an ordinary Sunday service, a Consistory summons, or an ordinary citizen's own experience of either Reformation actually felt like. Three of this world's own most consequential events — the Marburg Colloquy (1529), the Bolsec controversy (1551), and the Perrinist crisis (1555) — are Documented as fact but have no narrative account comparable to this world's own three built stories.

We hold no hagiography and no martyr's cult anywhere in our own record — this tradition's own confessional core actively refuses the devotional genre those belong to, and the absence is evidence the refusal worked, not a missing source. Geneva's own consistory registers, which would show a real named person, a real hearing, and a real outcome, remain unvendored — we can state our own general doctrine of discipline, but not one specific case. And one woman, Marie Dentière, wrote in our own defense and was prosecuted for it — real, consequential authority, directly attested — but her own words have not reached us.
