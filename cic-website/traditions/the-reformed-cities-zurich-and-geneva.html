The Reformed Cities

The Reformed Cities — Zurich & Geneva

On 1 January 1519, Huldrych Zwingli, newly appointed pastor at Zurich's Grossmünster, began preaching straight through the Gospel of Matthew rather than following the fixed lectionary — the first act of a reform that tested every inherited practice against Scripture and kept only what the text would warrant. Geneva's own reform opened separately and was joined in 1536 by John Calvin, whose ministry built a Consistory of pastors and lay elders to hold the whole city's ordinary conduct to account. Zwingli and Calvin never met; what formally bridges the two cities is the Consensus Tigurinus (1549), two reforms arriving independently at language neither could disown. This pattern continues unbroken into the Scottish Kirk, the Huguenots, the Dutch Reformed, and English Puritanism.

People

The people behind this account

Huldrych Zwingli (1531)

Zwingli was Zurich's own founder. He began preaching straight through Matthew in 1519 rather than follow the fixed lectionary, and by 1523 had defended his own case at the First Disputation, staking everything on being corrected only from Scripture itself. He taught that the Supper is chiefly a remembrance of Christ's finished sacrifice, not a repetition of it. He died at the Second War of Kappel in 1531, serving as a field chaplain.

John Calvin

Calvin was Geneva's own pastor from 1536, exiled to Strasbourg for three years, then recalled in 1541 to consolidate the Ecclesiastical Ordinances and the Consistory they created. His Institutes and Geneva Catechism built up, article by article, the doctrine this world holds most fully: that God's own free election, not anything earned, decides who is saved, held as comfort rather than threat.

Heinrich Bullinger

Bullinger succeeded Zwingli at Zurich in 1531 and held that pastorate for four decades. His own Second Helvetic Confession (1566) gave this world's shared conviction its mature, lasting written form, including its own pastoral answer to doubt: look to Christ, not to your own conscience, for proof of election.

Oswald Myconius

Myconius was Zwingli's own close friend. He was in Zurich on the day Zwingli rode out to the Second War of Kappel and was killed. He later wrote down what he remembered. He felt a sudden pain of heart as he watched his friend leave. He also recorded the words Zwingli was reported to have said after he fell.

There were warnings that, looking back, seemed to have foretold it. The muster was disorganized: five thousand had been summoned, and fewer than four thousand five hundred came. As Myconius watched Zwingli ride out in the rear, he felt a sudden pain of heart.

Zwingli was killed that same afternoon. The news reached the city that night. The fight had been sharp, and it was lost.

Myconius was not on the battlefield, and he tells of the fighting as it was reported to him. Zwingli was said to have been struck down three times. Each time he rose, until a fourth blow brought him to his knees. On his knees, he was reported to have said, "What harm is there in this? It is true, they can kill the body, but not the soul."

The enemy found his body afterward. They tried and condemned it, and then they burned it. After they withdrew, friends of Zwingli went to look for any remains. Myconius was told, though he did not see it, that they found his heart whole and unharmed in the ashes.

Legacy

What came after

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

This world's own gravities transmit, unbroken, into four named descendant traditions: the Scottish Kirk, the Huguenots, the Dutch Reformed, and English Puritanism. No external or internal force closes this world within its own 1519-1650 window - its own administrative close is an editorial boundary for a still living tradition, not a historical ending.

At the Synod of Dort (1618-19), an international Reformed delegation pressed this world's own doctrine of election to its sharpest statement yet, carried forward at one remove through Theodore Beza's own doctrinal throughline from Geneva. This sharpens the conviction already held; it does not close or complete it - Dort falls thirty-two years before this world's own window ends.

Related traditions

How this tradition relates to others

This world knew itself partly by what it refused, against three real neighbors. It refused Rome's own repeated sacrifice and its images, inherited from the late-medieval church both this world and Lutheran Wittenberg contested. It refused Wittenberg's own claim that Christ's body sits locally in the bread, pressed specifically at the Marburg Colloquy in 1529. And it refused a third claim from inside its own founding circle at Zurich - that a church gathered only by a believer's own profession was the more scripturally consistent reading - answered in the years after 1525 with civil force, not only argument.

A few terms and lines worth knowing

Christ sacrificed himself once. He is forever a sure and valid sacrifice for the sins of all the faithful. From this it follows that the mass is not a sacrifice. It is a remembrance of the sacrifice and an assurance of the salvation which Christ has given us.

Huldrych Zwingli

Huldrych Zwingli, as reported by Oswald Myconius
Sola Scriptura
Scripture alone, not church tradition or a pope's decree, settled what we believed and how we governed our churches. Zurich tested this in public debate. Geneva built it up through sustained teaching. Both cities held the same conviction.
Predestination and Election
God chose, before the world began and by his own free will alone, who would be saved. Both Zurich and Geneva held this. Geneva built it into a full system. Zurich stated it more briefly, as pastoral comfort.
The Lord's Supper and Spiritual Presence
We refused two claims about the Lord's Supper. It did not repeat Christ's sacrifice. His body did not sit in the bread. What we affirmed took two cities and a 1549 agreement to state together. Christ is truly given, by the Spirit, to whoever receives him believing.
Consistory
At Geneva, the Consistory was our body of pastors and lay elders. It held everyone to account for how they lived each day. Our record shows no such body at Zurich. There the council ruled church and city directly.
Disputation
A Disputation was a public debate before Zurich's own city council. Our reforming side defended its case from Scripture. The old church's side answered. This was how Zurich chose to reform.
