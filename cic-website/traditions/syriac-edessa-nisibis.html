Syriac Christianity

Syriac Christianity of Edessa and Nisibis

Open for conversation — you can sit down with this tradition now.

In Edessa and Nisibis, from 200 to 410 CE, Christians taught their faith in hymn. They read Scripture as a world of types, each one answering another. They also read the four Gospels as one continuous story, not four separate books. A harmony woven a generation earlier combined the four accounts. Preachers used it for as long as this world lasted. At the heart of this community's ascetic life stood the covenant. Men and women vowed themselves to celibacy for life, serving their own congregation instead of leaving for the desert. Whether authority rested more in that vow or in a bishop's office was never settled in their own record. On the Persian side of the frontier, the king's persecution killed the bishop Simeon bar Sabbae and his clergy, and bishops after him were killed in turn. After the last of them, the bishopric of Seleucia-Ctesiphon stood empty for twenty years. Taking the post meant near-certain death.

Mar Yausep is a representative voice for this tradition, not a person who lived — built from its own letters and records, and honest about where they run out.

The Story

How this tradition took shape

This world lived on a border. From about 200 to 410 CE, Syriac-speaking Christians built their faith in two cities pulled between two empires. Edessa stood under Rome. Nisibis was a fortress city that changed hands partway through. Aphrahat wrote from further inside Persia still. The record opens with this world's oldest hard trace: a church at Edessa, destroyed in a flood in 201. It closes in 410, when Persia's own church finally settled into one official structure.

Doctrine here was sung, not argued in a philosopher's style. Scripture and the world itself were read as a raza: a symbol bound to the truth, or shrara, it carries and shows. Eden answers the cross. The Jordan river answers the baptismal font. Ephrem, this world's greatest hymn-writer, could build a whole argument out of one image - a pearl held up to the light, showing him the mysteries of God's kingdom.

For this whole world, the Gospel was one continuous story, not four separate books. A generation before this world's own window opens, a man named Tatian wove Matthew, Mark, Luke, and John into a single harmony. Both of this world's chief teachers, Ephrem and Aphrahat, read and preached from that harmony for as long as this world lasted.

At the center of this world's ascetic life stood the covenant, the qyama. Men and women vowed themselves to lifelong celibacy and service. They stayed inside their own ordinary congregation, rather than leaving for a desert monastery. Aphrahat calls them ihidaye, "single ones." The name joins their undivided life to Christ. He carries that very same title: the Only-Begotten.

Who actually held authority here was never settled. Bishops led, and this world's own record remembers them warmly. But the vowed covenant life carried a standing of its own, and a respected teacher's word could matter just as much as an office. Aphrahat himself once drafted a letter in a synod of bishops' own name - yet whether he was a bishop himself is a question this world's own record never answers. In Persia, the one bishop who claimed to lead them all, Papa, was openly contested by his own colleagues while he still lived.

This world also knew its own faith by naming its rivals. Three men preached in the very same cities, in the very same tongue: the philosopher-poet Bardaisan, the teacher Marcion, and the prophet Mani. Ephrem answered all three by name, in both prose and song. He even turned Bardaisan's own sweet hymn-forms back against his followers.

On the Persian side, faithfulness meant endurance under a hostile crown. From the 340s, King Shapur II turned on his Christian subjects. Bishop Simeon bar Sabbae refused to collect a punishing new tax and refused to worship the sun. He was executed with his own clergy, the first of many bishops the persecution killed. After the last of them died, the church's chief seat in Persia stood empty for twenty years, since taking the post meant near-certain death. Roman-side Edessa, across the frontier, knew rival teachers, not this kind of danger - the two halves of this one world lived very different fourth centuries.

This world's own record ends in 410. A Roman bishop, Marutha, used his embassies to the Persian court to help broker a synod under the protection of King Yazdegerd I. For the first time, Persia's church received a single, settled, official structure. The ambiguity this world had lived with for two centuries did not resolve itself from within. It arrived, at the very end, from outside.

Voices

The people behind this account

Ephrem (c. 306–373, at Edessa)

Ephrem was a deacon, first at Nisibis and then, after 363, at Edessa - and this world's most prolific poet. Hundreds of his teaching hymns carried this world's doctrine straight into congregational song. In the last year of his life, famine struck Edessa. He came out of his quiet cell, took charge of relief for the whole city, and died within a month of the famine's end.

Jacob (338 vs 350 - the Martyrologium Hieronymianum implies 338)

Jacob was bishop of Nisibis from about 309. He signed the Nicene Creed himself, at the council in 325. When a Persian army besieged his city, tradition remembers him climbing the wall to pray. He prayed not for the army's destruction, but for its withdrawal.

Marutha (unknown)

Marutha was a Roman bishop on the frontier. His embassies to the Persian court helped open the way to a lasting settlement. His diplomacy with King Yazdegerd I helped bring about the synod of 410. That synod finally gave the Persian church one settled structure.

Simeon (traditionally 341; Kosinski and Burgess argue c. 344 - the anchor date for the whole chronicle-derived succession, held open and never resolved either way)

Simeon bar Sabbae was bishop of Seleucia-Ctesiphon. King Shapur II ordered Persia's Christians taxed into ruin. Simeon refused to collect that tax. He also refused to worship the sun. He was executed along with his own clergy. This world remembers him as the first of the persecution's bishops to die for the faith.

Documented stories

Stories worth telling in full

Ephrem Leaves His Cell for the Famine

372-373 (the last year of Ephrem's life)

Famine strikes Edessa, and the rich say they trust no one to hand out relief honestly. An old teacher volunteers himself.

Read the full account

In the last year of Ephrem's life, famine struck Edessa. The old teacher came out from his quiet cell and went to the rich of the city, and rebuked them: why do you let your neighbors die while your wealth rots into your own condemnation? They answered that they trusted no one to handle the giving honestly. And he asked them: what do you think of me? They said: you are a man of God. Then trust me, he said - I appoint myself keeper of the strangers' house. He took their money and had some three hundred beds set up in the public porches. There he nursed the starving and the sick, strangers and townsfolk alike, and buried those who died, until the year turned and the famine broke. Then he went back to his cell, and within a month he died.

Jacob of Nisibis and the Siege

338, 346, or 350 (this world's own record does not settle which siege)

A Persian army dams the river against the city walls. The aged bishop climbs a tower to pray - and asks God for nothing worse than a swarm of gnats.

Read the full account

Shapur's army lay against the walls of Nisibis, and the river had been turned against the city. The tradition tells that Ephrem urged the aged bishop Jacob to climb the wall and let prayer, not strength, be the city's defense. The old man agreed and went up into a tower. Looking out over the numberless camp, he asked God not for the enemy's death, but for the smallest of plagues: mosquitoes and gnats. The clouds of them came. They maddened the elephants and the horses. And the army that a river could not stop broke camp before the smallest things God had made. The city sang of its rescue afterward. Ephrem himself, who had urged it and watched it, put it into the hymns Nisibis kept.

The Story We Told of Our Own Beginning

set around the year 30; first attested in writing centuries later

A sick king writes to Jesus asking to be healed, and says a reply came back. This community told the story as its own for as long as it lasted.

Read the full account

This is how we told our own beginning. King Abgar the Black lay sick in Edessa. He heard of Jesus and his healings from envoys returning through Jerusalem, and he wrote Jesus a letter: come to me and heal me, and share my small and beautiful city, safe from those who seek your life - for you must be God come down from heaven, or the Son of God. The Lord answered, the story says, that he had to finish his work where he was. But after his ascension he would send one of his own. And he blessed the city. So after the ascension, Addai came - one of the seventy-two. He lodged with Tobia the Jew, healed the king, and preached to the city, calling it away from its old gods - Bel, Nebo, and Tar'atha - to the one true God. Before he died, Addai appointed Aggai. Aggai was struck down by a rival before he could ordain a successor. So Palut traveled to Antioch and was ordained by Serapion - binding Edessa's church, through Antioch, into the line running back to Peter.

Legacy

What came after

This world's own woven Gospel did not last forever. Within a generation of this world's close, a new translation split the four Gospels apart again. Tradition credits Bishop Rabbula of Edessa with the change. But this world's own record leaves open how much of it was really his doing. Once the harmony stopped being copied, the original Syriac text itself did not survive. It is known today only through fragments, quotations, and translations made from it in other languages.

Sources

What this account is built from

This world's record is richest in hymns and doctrinal teaching. Ephrem's enormous corpus stands first, then Aphrahat's twenty-three dated Demonstrations. It is also strong on the persecution as it was lived on the Persian side, and on the frontier cities' own civic memory. It thins out, and sometimes falls silent, in a few places. Ordinary believers never speak in their own words. The daughters of the covenant sang in every church, but their own voices do not survive - only men's writing about them does. Aphrahat's dispute with his Jewish neighbors is recorded only from his own side. Anyone enslaved, or of low status, left no voice at all. The years after Ephrem's death in 373 thin out fast too, down to little more than a list of bishops.

  • Ephrem the Syrian (c. 306-373 CE), Nisibene Hymns (Carmina Nisibena) - 46 of the original 77 hymns; I-XXI (the Nisibis-and-its-bishops cycle) complete, with selections from the rest including the Death-and-Satan cycle This world's greatest hymn-writer remembering the siege of his own city and its bishops in verse - the closest thing this world has to an eyewitness account, and sung rather than told.
  • Aphrahat, 'the Persian Sage' (fl. c. 337-345; a 510 CE colophon calls him 'Mar Jacob, the Persian sage'; the name 'Aphrahat' first appears in the tenth century), Select Demonstrations: I Of Faith; V Of Wars; VI Of Monks (the bnay qyama teaching); VIII Of the Resurrection of the Dead; X Of Pastors; XVII Of Christ the Son of God; XXI Of Persecution; XXII Of Death and the Latter Times The Persian side's own primary voice - dated letters written from inside the empire, the last of them composed while the persecution was already underway.
  • Tatian (composed c. 172 CE); the surviving text is an English rendering of the Arabic recension, The Diatessaron of Tatian - the single continuous-narrative Gospel harmony that served as this world's standard Gospel text until the early fifth century The one woven Gospel this whole world read as 'the Gospel' for two centuries - though this particular English text stands several steps removed from Tatian's own lost Syriac wording.

qyama (bnay qyama / bnat qyama)
The covenant: our own vowed order. Its members - the sons and daughters of the covenant - promised a celibate life for good. But they did not leave for the desert. They stayed in town, among their own kin, and served the congregation: the fast, the watch, the vigil, the singing.
ihidaya (pl. ihidaye)
The Single One. One word does double duty among us. It names the vowed celibate - single-minded, undivided for God - and it is also the Syriac title of Christ as the Only-Begotten. The two senses share one root: to live single is to be joined, by your own name, to Christ's own oneness.
raza / shrara
Symbol and truth, held as one pair. A raza is a thing in Scripture or in nature that shows a hidden truth - the shrara. The raza is bound to the truth it shows. It even carries some of that truth's hidden power. It is not a stand-in that a reader picks at will.
madrasha (pl. madrashe)
The teaching hymn: a sung poem with stanzas and a refrain, made to be performed. Among us, doctrine was taught by singing it. The melody and the refrain carry the argument into the body, so the congregation learns the faith by keeping the tune.
Ewangeliyon da-Mhallete
The 'Gospel of the Mixed': the one continuous Gospel story we read and heard in worship. Tatian wove the four accounts into a single narrative around 172. For some two hundred years, 'the Gospel' in our churches meant that one unfolding story, not four separate books.
