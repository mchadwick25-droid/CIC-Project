Syriac Christianity

Syriac Christianity of Edessa and Nisibis

In Edessa and Nisibis, from 200 to 410 CE, Christians taught their faith in hymn. They read Scripture as a world of types, each one answering another. They also read the four Gospels as one continuous story, not four separate books. A harmony woven a generation earlier combined the four accounts. Preachers used it for as long as this Christian tradition lasted. At the heart of this community's ascetic life stood the covenant. Men and women vowed themselves to celibacy for life, serving their own congregation instead of leaving for the desert. Whether authority rested more in that vow or in a bishop's office was never settled in their own record. On the Persian side of the frontier, the king's persecution killed the bishop Simeon bar Sabbae and his clergy, and bishops after him were killed in turn. After the last of them, the bishopric of Seleucia-Ctesiphon stood empty for twenty years. Taking the post meant near-certain death.

People

The people behind this account

Ephrem (c. 306–373, at Edessa)

Ephrem was a deacon, first at Nisibis and then, after 363, at Edessa - and this world's most prolific poet. Hundreds of his teaching hymns carried this world's doctrine straight into congregational song. In the last year of his life, famine struck Edessa. He came out of his quiet cell, took charge of relief for the whole city, and died within a month of the famine's end.

Jacob (338 vs 350 - the Martyrologium Hieronymianum implies 338)

Jacob was bishop of Nisibis from about 309. He signed the Nicene Creed himself, at the council in 325. When a Persian army besieged his city, tradition remembers him climbing the wall to pray. He prayed not for the army's destruction, but for its withdrawal.

Marutha (unknown)

Marutha was a Roman bishop on the frontier. His embassies to the Persian court helped open the way to a lasting settlement. His diplomacy with King Yazdegerd I helped bring about the synod of 410. That synod finally gave the Persian church one settled structure.

Simeon (traditionally 341; Kosinski and Burgess argue c. 344 - the anchor date for the whole chronicle-derived succession, held open and never resolved either way)

Simeon bar Sabbae was bishop of Seleucia-Ctesiphon. King Shapur II ordered Persia's Christians taxed into ruin. Simeon refused to collect that tax. He also refused to worship the sun. He was executed along with his own clergy. This world remembers him as the first of the persecution's bishops to die for the faith.

The Story We Told of Our Own Beginning

set around the year 30; first attested in writing centuries later

In the story, the sick King Abgar of Edessa writes to Jesus for healing. Jesus promises to send a disciple, and Addai comes. We told it as the story of our own beginning. It was first written down centuries later, and nothing from Abgar's own day confirms it.

Jesus answered, the story says, that the work he was sent for was now finished and he was going up to his Father. After that he would send one of his disciples to heal the king, and the city would be blessed.

After the ascension Addai came, one of the seventy-two, and stayed with a Jewish man named Tobias. The story says he healed the king and preached to the city, calling it away from Nebo, Bel and Taratha to the one true God.

Before he died, Addai made Aggai leader in his place. Years after Abgar died, one of his sons had Aggai's legs broken as he sat teaching in the church. Aggai died too quickly to ordain Palut. So Palut went to Antioch and was ordained by its bishop, Serapion. Serapion had himself been ordained by Zephyrinus, bishop of Rome, in the line of Simon Peter.

The Paradise says one of the holy fathers saw a dream in which the holy angels came down from heaven at God's command. One of them held a roll written on the inside and on the outside. The angels asked each other who was fit to be trusted with it.

Some angels named one man, some another. Each time the answer was that these were holy and righteous, but not enough for this. They counted up many names of saints. At last they said that no one was fit for it except Ephrem, and they gave him the roll.

When the dreamer got up in the morning, he heard people saying that Ephrem was teaching. His words, they said, flowed from his mouth like water from a fountain. Then, it says, the old man knew within himself that the words coming from Ephrem's lips were of the Holy Spirit.

The second dream is one Ephrem himself is said to have had as a youth. A vine grew out of his tongue and filled the whole space beneath the heavens, loaded with clusters of grapes. All the birds of heaven came and ate from it. The whole time they ate, the clusters grew in number and in size.

A Morning of the Covenant at Nisibis

Before dawn in NisibisNisibisA fortress city, Roman from 298 until 363, when it was ceded to Persia. Its site is Nusaybin, on today's Turkey–Syria border.Place · pleiades.stoa.org, those bound by the covenant gather for the vigil. No single morning like this was written down. This one is pieced together from separate sources, from the years before the city was handed to Persia in 363.

The covenanters are men and women who have taken the vow and stay unmarried. They walk through the dark streets from their own family houses, since there is no cloister. Among them stands the choir of the daughters of the covenant, who will raise the madrasha, the teaching hymn.

The hymn carries the teaching on its tune, stanza answering refrain, until the whole gathering holds the argument by heart. The Gospel read out is the harmony, the Gospels woven into one unbroken story.

The feast this season keeps the Lord's birth and his baptism together in one observance, as this city then did. When the light is full, the covenanters keep their fast and go back to their own households and their work in town.

Simeon Before the King of Persia

341 by the traditional count (some argue about 344)

The tradition tells that Simeon, bishop of Seleucia-Ctesiphon, is brought in chains before King Shapur. He will not worship the sun, and he is put to death with his clergy.

"As time went on, the Christians grew in number. They began to form churches, and they appointed priests and deacons." So SozomenSozomenA church historian of the fifth century. Simeon was bishop of Seleucia and Ctesiphon, the royal cities of Persia, and he was accused of being a friend of the Roman emperor. King Shapur laid heavy taxes on the Christians, hoping poverty would force them to give up their faith. The year of what followed is disputed, 341 or about 344.

The tradition tells that Simeon was brought before the king in chains and would not bow down. He said he stood there now in defense of godliness and of our faith. Shapur offered him gifts, then threatened to destroy him and all the Christians. Each time Simeon refused to worship the sun or betray his religion.

As Simeon was led to prison, Usthazanes, the king's aged foster-father, rose to honor him. Usthazanes had been a Christian, but he had given way and worshipped the sun. The bishop rebuked him and turned his face away. Usthazanes then confessed himself a Christian before the king, and Shapur had him beheaded.

The next day Simeon again refused to worship the king or the sun. A hundred other prisoners were put to death that day while he watched. Simeon was killed last of all, with two aged priests of his own church.

For many years Ephrem had lived quietly in his cell, but now he left it and went to the rich of the city. He asked them why they had no pity on people dying around them, while their wealth spoiled into the condemnation of their own souls.

The rich answered that they had no one they could trust with the giving, because everyone was dishonest in such business. Ephrem asked what they thought of him, and they said they knew him to be a man of God. "Then trust me," he said. "See, on your behalf I appoint myself to take in and care for the needy."

He took their money and set up some three hundred beds in the public porches. There he nursed the starving and the sick, strangers and local people alike, and he buried those who died. When the year was over and good times returned, he went back to his cell. He died soon after, a month later in Palladius's account and a few days later in Sozomen's.

Legacy

What came after

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

This world's own woven Gospel did not last forever. Within a generation of this world's close, a new translation split the four Gospels apart again. Tradition credits Bishop Rabbula of Edessa with the change. But this world's own record leaves open how much of it was really his doing. Once the harmony stopped being copied, the original Syriac text itself did not survive. It is known today only through fragments, quotations, and translations made from it in other languages.

qyama (bnay qyama / bnat qyama)
The covenant: our own vowed order. Its members - the sons and daughters of the covenant - promised a celibate life for good. But they did not leave for the desert. They stayed in town, among their own kin, and served the congregation: the fast, the watch, the vigil, the singing.
ihidaya (pl. ihidaye)
The Single One. One word does double duty among us. It names the vowed celibate, single-minded and undivided for God. It is also the Syriac title of Christ as the Only-Begotten. The two senses share one root, so the one who lives single shares a name with Christ.
raza / shrara
Symbol and truth, held as one pair. A raza is a thing in Scripture or in nature that shows a hidden truth, the shrara. The raza is bound to the truth it shows. It is not a stand-in that a reader picks at will.
madrasha (pl. madrashe)
The teaching hymn: a sung poem with stanzas and a refrain, made to be performed. Among us, doctrine was taught by singing it. The melody and the refrain carry the argument into the body, so the congregation learns the faith by keeping the tune.
Ewangeliyon da-Mhallete
The 'Gospel of the Mixed': the one continuous Gospel story we read and heard in worship. Tatian wove the four accounts into a single narrative around 172. For some two hundred years, 'the Gospel' in our churches meant that one unfolding story, not four separate books.
