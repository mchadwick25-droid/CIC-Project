The Imperial Church Era · c. 320–430 CE · Nile Valley & Desert, Egypt

Desert Fathers and Mothers

Desert Monasticism

Open for conversation — you can sit down with this tradition now.

Between about 320 and 430 CE, Christians in Egypt began leaving their villages for the desert's edge, in a movement that never really stopped. Persecution had just ended, so they invented a different kind of struggle: fighting the tempting thoughts that rise up from inside a person. This way of life took three real, different shapes. Some lived alone, testing their own choices against an elder's hard-earned wisdom. Others joined communities governed by a rule set down in writing, under an appointed leader. A third group kept mostly to their own cells, then walked in once a week to pray and share a meal together. These two kinds of authority, personal and official, existed side by side and never merged into one. None of this was as cut off from ordinary life as it might sound. Digs at one settlement found a marketplace standing among the monks' own cells. Joining this life meant giving up land, family ties, and a settled home. That cost came gradually, one step at a time, over years, not in a single break.

Voices

The people behind this account

Antony (c. 251–356, at the inner mountain - narrated at Vita SS92-93, written by Athanasius shortly after)

Antony heard one Gospel reading in church and took it as spoken to him directly. He gave away his family's land that same day. Then he withdrew in stages: first to the village's edge, then to an abandoned fort, and finally to a remote inner mountain, where he stayed for the rest of his life.

Pachomius (c. 292–346 - by which point the federation he founded numbered nine houses for men and two for women, membership in the low thousands)

Pachomius founded the desert's first rule-governed community at Tabennesi around 320. By his death in 346, tradition says it had grown into a federation of nine houses for men and two for women. A written rule and named offices governed it, not just one elder's own example.

Sarah

Sarah was one of a small number of women remembered by name in this world's own teaching tradition. Six of her sayings survive. She used to say to her brothers that she was a man, and they were women.

Evagrius (c. 345–January 399, at Kellia - communicating in church at Epiphany, per Palladius ch. XXXVIII)

Evagrius was the most formally educated man the desert produced. Basil of Caesarea ordained him a reader. Gregory of Nazianzus made him a deacon. Then he withdrew to Kellia. There, he organized this world's practical wisdom about tempting thoughts into its most complete scheme. He died in January 399, months before a controversy his own circle could not survive.

c. 270 (a few months after his parents' deaths)

Less than six months later, he was going into church as he always did. He was thinking of how the apostles had left everything to follow the Saviour. The Gospel was being read. He heard the Lord say to the rich man: "If you want to be perfect, go and sell what you have and give to the poor. Then come and follow me, and you will have treasure in heaven."

Antony went out of the church at once, as though the passage had been read on his account. He gave the villagers his family's land, three hundred Egyptian field measures of good ground. He sold everything else he could move and gave the money to the poor, keeping back a little for his sister.

Later he went into church again and heard the Lord say, "Do not be anxious about tomorrow." He could stay no longer. He went out and gave the rest to the poor as well. He placed his sister with known and faithful virgins to be brought up. From then on he gave himself to a life of discipline outside his house, training himself with patience.

In the next village lived an old man who had been a hermit from his youth. After Antony had seen him, he copied his devotion.

Antony mocked them: "If you had any real power, it would have been enough for just one of you to come. But the Lord has made you weak. So you try to frighten me with your numbers."

Then the roof seemed to open, and a ray of light came down. The demons vanished and the pain stopped. Antony asked, "Where were you? Why did you not appear at the start, to end my pain?" A voice answered: "Antony, I was here, but I waited to see your fight. You have endured and have not given way. So I will always help you, and I will make your name known everywhere."

The perfect, he said, need no rule, because alone in their cells they have given their whole lives to the contemplation of God.

In his own day, Palladius says, the monasteries that kept this rule held some seven thousand men. The first and great monastery was the one where Pachomius himself had lived, and he gives it thirteen hundred members.

These things, he said, he had ordained for those who have no advisers.

Antony had often urged the bishops to command the people against this, and he had taught that it was neither lawful nor holy.

He went back to the inner mountain, where he was used to living, and a few months later he fell sick. Two men had stayed with him there for fifteen years, serving him because of his age. He called them and told them to let no one take his body into Egypt. "Bury my body and hide it underground yourselves," he said. "Keep my words, so that no one may know the place but you alone."

He told them to divide his garments. One sheepskin and the garment he lay on were for the bishop Athanasius. The other sheepskin was for the bishop Serapion, and the two men were to keep the hair garment. Then he said farewell, and died.

They buried him as he had commanded. Athanasius writes that to this day no one knows where he lies, except those two.

Legacy

What came after

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

This world's own eight named categories of tempting thought did not stay here. Passed west through Cassian's Conferences and narrowed two centuries later by Gregory the Great, they became the seven deadly sins of later Western moral teaching.

One popular link does not actually hold. This world's own word for the stillness it sought, hesychia, is often assumed to be the same practice as the later Byzantine Hesychast tradition and its Jesus Prayer technique. That systematized method belongs to a separate, later tradition and is not part of this one's own record.
