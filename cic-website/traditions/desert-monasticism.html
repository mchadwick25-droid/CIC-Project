The Imperial Church Era · c. 320–430 CE · Nile Valley & Desert, Egypt

Desert Fathers and Mothers

Desert Monasticism

Open for conversation — you can sit down with this tradition now.

Between about 320 and 430 CE, Christians in Egypt began leaving their villages for the desert's edge, in a movement that never really stopped. Persecution had just ended, so they invented a different kind of struggle: fighting the tempting thoughts that rise up from inside a person. This way of life took three real, different shapes. Some lived alone, testing their own choices against an elder's hard-earned wisdom. Others joined communities governed by a rule set down in writing, under an appointed leader. A third group kept mostly to their own cells, then walked in once a week to pray and share a meal together. These two kinds of authority, personal and official, existed side by side and never merged into one. None of this was as cut off from ordinary life as it might sound. Digs at one settlement found a marketplace standing among the monks' own cells. Joining this life meant giving up land, family ties, and a settled home. That cost came gradually, one step at a time, over years, not in a single break.

The Story

How this tradition took shape

By the early fourth century, the long Roman persecution of Christians had ended. For people who had once measured their faith by a willingness to die for it, that ending left a real question: what kind of struggle was left? This world's answer was to leave ordinary village life for the desert's edge, and to treat that leaving itself as the whole work of a Christian life, not just its starting point. The struggle once fought against a persecutor from outside was now fought against tempting thoughts from within.

One young Egyptian landowner named Antony gave this pattern its clearest shape. He heard a Gospel reading in church and took it as spoken to him alone: sell what you have, give it to the poor, and follow me. He gave away his land that same day, and weeks later gave away everything else after hearing a second reading. He placed his sister with a community of women, then began the discipline himself - first close to home, then further out, until he had spent decades at what he called the inner mountain.

Not everyone lived Antony's way. Around 320, a man named Pachomius founded the desert's first community bound by a written rule rather than one elder's own example. By his death in 346, tradition says his federation had grown to nine houses for men and two for women, with membership in the low thousands - a rough count from a witness writing decades later, not a precise census. Authority here came from a rule and named offices, not only from a person's own earned reputation.

This world had two forms of authority. One was a life earned through years of discipline. The other was an office conferred by a written rule. The two existed side by side for this world's whole history, and neither one ever fully replaced the other. Even Pachomius, the rule's own founder, was remembered afterward less as an officeholder and more as a father whose own example had formed his community.

What kept any of this from tipping into excess was discernment. This world's own word for it was diakrisis. There was no fixed program here - no set number of fasts, no fixed hours of prayer for everyone. An elder's real skill was judging how much a particular person could actually bear. The tradition's most common story shape is simple: an eager newcomer asks for something extreme, and an elder redirects him toward something more moderate instead.

This life was never as cut off from ordinary village life as its own words about total withdrawal suggest. Diggers at one settlement, Kellia, found a marketplace operating right alongside the monks' own cells. That is real evidence of trade running alongside the discipline of leaving the world behind.

One participant, Evagrius, stood out by his education. He was trained in Greek philosophy and ordained by two of the great Cappadocian bishops. He took this world's raw, occasion-bound wisdom about tempting thoughts and organized it into the desert's most complete scheme. It named eight categories of temptation. It also built a ladder rising from practice to a stilled, contemplative seeing of God.

That scheme cost the desert something. Evagrius died at Kellia in January 399. Just months later, a controversy broke out that his own learned circle could not survive. A bishop's letter argued against picturing God in human form. It split simple monks from educated ones, and within the year his circle had been driven out of Egypt. One old monk of Scete, Sarapion, is remembered breaking down in tears in the middle of a prayer, right after a visiting deacon persuaded him that God has no human shape. It was the human cost of a correction that, on its own terms, was probably right.

Voices

The people behind this account

Antony (c. 251–356, at the inner mountain - narrated at Vita SS92-93, written by Athanasius shortly after)

Antony heard one Gospel reading in church and took it as spoken to him directly. He gave away his family's land that same day. Then he withdrew in stages: first to the village's edge, then to an abandoned fort, and finally to a remote inner mountain, where he stayed for the rest of his life.

Pachomius (c. 292–346 - by which point the federation he founded numbered nine houses for men and two for women, membership in the low thousands)

Pachomius founded the desert's first rule-governed community at Tabennesi around 320. By his death in 346, tradition says it had grown into a federation of nine houses for men and two for women. A written rule and named offices governed it, not just one elder's own example.

Sarah

Sarah was one of a small number of women remembered by name in this world's own teaching tradition. Six of her sayings survive. When a group of elder monks visited to test her standing as a woman, she told them plainly that she was the man among them, and they were the women.

Evagrius (c. 345–January 399, at Kellia - communicating in church at Epiphany, per Palladius ch. XXXVIII)

Evagrius was the most formally educated man the desert produced. Basil of Caesarea ordained him a reader. Gregory of Nazianzus made him a deacon. Then he withdrew to Kellia. There, he organized this world's practical wisdom about tempting thoughts into its most complete scheme. He died in January 399, months before a controversy his own circle could not survive.

Documented stories

Stories worth telling in full

Antony Hears the Gospel Read for Him

c. 270 (a few months after his parents' deaths)

A young landowner walks into church one ordinary day, and the Bible reading that morning changes his whole life before nightfall.

Read the full account

Not long after he lost his parents, still a young man, Antony went into church one day and heard the Gospel being read. It was the Lord telling the rich young man: if you would be perfect, go and sell what you have, give to the poor, and come follow me, and you will have treasure in heaven. Antony took it as though it had been said to him alone. He walked out of the church, and before the day was over he had given away the land he had inherited and sold what else his family owned, keeping back only a little for his sister. Not long after, he heard the same Gospel read again - do not be anxious about tomorrow - and he gave away the rest as well. He placed his sister with women he trusted, women already given to a life of virginity. Then he began the discipline himself, close to home at first, seeking out and copying an old man who was already living that way nearby.

Buried Where No One Will Ever Find Him

356 (Antony's death)

At the end of his life, the desert's most famous monk gives one final order: hide my body so well that no one, not even you, can ever turn me into a shrine.

Read the full account

Near the end of his life, Antony made one last visit to the monks who lived on the outer mountain. He told them plainly: this is the last time you will see me. I am near a hundred and five years old, and my time is close. He urged them to keep up their discipline, to live as though dying daily, and to have nothing to do with the Meletian schismatics or the Arian heretics. Then he went back to the inner mountain, where he had lived for decades, and within a few months he grew sick. Antony had one more instruction to give, and it mattered to him more than almost anything else. In Egypt at that time, people often kept the bodies of holy men in their own houses, wrapped in linen and laid out on couches, instead of burying them in the ground. Antony hated this custom and had spent years urging bishops and ordinary people to give it up. He was afraid the same thing would be done to his own body. So he called the two monks who had lived with him and cared for him for fifteen years, and gave them exact orders. Bury me yourselves, he told them, and hide my body in the ground. Let no one know the place but the two of you alone. He divided his few belongings between them: one sheepskin and the cloak he was lying on for the bishop Athanasius, the other sheepskin for the bishop Serapion, and his rough hair garment for the two attendants themselves. When he died, the two men did exactly as he had asked. They wrapped his body and buried it in the ground, and to this day, Athanasius writes, no one knows where - except those two men alone.

They Have Taken My God Away From Me

399 (soon after a bishop's Festal Letter reached Scete)

A revered old monk finally accepts that God has no human body or face. He immediately breaks down in tears. The only way he ever knew how to pray to God is suddenly gone.

Read the full account

In the year 399, the bishop of Alexandria sent a letter to be read out in every monastery in Egypt. The letter said that God has no body, no face, and no hands - the opposite of how many simple monks had always pictured him in prayer. Most of the monks at Scete were angry and troubled. Only one presbyter there, Abba Paphnutius, welcomed the letter as sound teaching. Among the monks at Scete was an old man named Sarapion. He had lived there for years and was known for a strict, disciplined life. But he could not accept the letter's teaching. It seemed new to him, nothing his own teachers had ever taught. Then a visiting deacon named Photinus arrived from Cappadocia, a man of great learning. Paphnutius asked Photinus, in front of the gathered brothers, to explain how the churches of the East understood the words "let us make man in our own image." Photinus explained, at length and from many places in scripture, that the image of God was not a bodily one, and that nothing so vast and unseen could be shaped like a human body. Hearing this, old Sarapion was finally persuaded, and agreed with the teaching. Then something happened that none of them expected. As they all rose to give thanks and pray together, Sarapion suddenly broke down. He had always pictured God in a human shape when he prayed, and now, in the middle of his own prayer, he felt that picture torn away from his heart. He threw himself on the ground, sobbing, and cried out that they had taken his God away from him - that he now had no one to hold on to, and did not know who to worship or pray to. Cassian, who was there, says he and his companion left deeply shaken, and went straight to Abba Isaac to ask how such a thing could happen to a man like that.

Legacy

What came after

This world's own eight named categories of tempting thought did not stay here. Passed west through Cassian's Conferences and narrowed two centuries later by Gregory the Great, they became the seven deadly sins of later Western moral teaching.

One popular link does not actually hold. This world's own word for the stillness it sought, hesychia, is often assumed to be the same practice as the later Byzantine Hesychast tradition and its Jesus Prayer technique. That systematized method belongs to a separate, later tradition and is not part of this one's own record.

In its own record

How this world relates to others

This is a distinct formation world from Alexandria's own, not simply its continuation - though one disputed reading of Antony's own letters leaves the question of contact open.

Sources

What this account is built from

This is the richest of the fleet's built worlds for direct quotation. It has fifty-nine verified quote records, anchored by Antony's own Life and the Apophthegmata's elder-and-disciple sayings. It is thinner in three places: on ordinary participants outside the sayings tradition, on the Pachomian federation's own interior life beyond its founding, and on women's own voices. Only a small number of named sayings survive from women, not a fuller corpus.

  • Athanasius of Alexandria (c. 296-373 CE) - bishop, not a desert participant, Vita Antonii (Life of Antony), c. 356-362 CE - episcopal hagiography constructing an exemplar (Greco-Roman bios conventions adapted to Christian sanctity); the single most influential text about this world, written from outside it; its formation ideal is credible evidence, its miracle episodes are not historical reporting, and its 'unlettered' (agrammatos) portrait of Antony is contested (see desert.contested.antony-literacy) The founding narrative of this world's most famous figure - written by a bishop with his own theological agenda, within a few years of Antony's death.
  • Anonymous compilers (5th-6th c.), collecting the oral sayings tradition of the desert elders and ammas of the 4th-early 5th c., Apophthegmata Patrum (Sayings of the Desert Fathers) - the Alphabetical and Systematic collections; this world's central teaching corpus and the broadest witness to its named participants including the ammas (Syncletica, Theodora, Sarah). Compiled in surviving written form AFTER this world's c. 430 close, from oral material originating inside it: every citation carries the compiler screen (selection, arrangement, and possible cross-strand harmonization are the editors' work), and whether that overlap between strands reflects lived reality or later editorial arrangement remains a genuinely open question The terse elder-and-disciple sayings that carry most of this world's own teaching, gathered and arranged by later compilers rather than by the participants themselves.
  • John Cassian (c. 360-435 CE), Collationes (The Conferences), c. 426-429 - twenty-four conferences staged as the recorded teaching of named Egyptian elders (Moses, Paphnutius, Isaac, and others) at Scetis and the desert settlements; the fullest surviving presentation of this world's elder-teaching in extended discourse form, and also its most heavily literary mediation: composed in Gaul decades later, in Latin, in a dialogue genre the desert's own terse oral idiom did not use; same export screen as the Institutes The fullest surviving presentation of this world's elder-teaching in extended discourse form, written decades later in Latin for a Gallic audience - its most literary mediation as well as its most complete.

In this world's own words

A few terms and lines worth knowing

Mother Sarah used to say to her brothers: 'It is I who am the man, and you who are the women.'

Sarah

Now we must also remember the chosen and mighty women. God gave them strength of will equal to the men's. So they had no excuse for weakness in the hard work of this way of life.

Palladius, in the Syriac recension of the Paradise

I am a wretched man! They have taken my God away from me. Now I have no one to hold on to. I do not know who to worship, or who to pray to.

Abbot Sarapion of Scete, as Cassian records him - a different named elder from the Abbot Serapion of Conference V (the eight-principal-faults teaching); the surviving text marks the two with different spellings

There are eight main faults that attack people. First is gluttony. Second, sexual sin. Third, the love of money. Fourth, anger. Fifth, deep sadness. Sixth, listlessness. Seventh, showing off. And eighth, pride.

Abbot Serapion of Scetis, as Cassian records him
anachoresis
Withdrawal: leaving settled village life for the desert's edge, as the whole work of formation, not a change of address.
apophthegma
The saying: a short word from an elder, given to one person for one moment of need. It was our main way of teaching.
diakrisis
Discernment: the skill of judging rightly between thoughts, practices, and counsels - our own master virtue.
logismoi
The thoughts: unwanted, intruding thoughts and urges, treated as the main battlefield of the ascetic life.
hesychia
Stillness: quiet of body and mind, sought as both the condition and the fruit of the ascetic life.
