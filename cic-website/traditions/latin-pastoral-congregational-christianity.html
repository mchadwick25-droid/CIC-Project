The Ordinary Church

Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity

In 250, the first persecution enforced across the whole empire by plan did not mainly demand that Christians give up their faith. It demanded a recorded act of sacrifice, proved by a certificate. Many gave way, and their bishop, Cyprian of Carthage, did not write them off. He set a road back for them, examined and walked in the open. This is the ordinary church of Latin-speaking Roman North Africa, from about 246 to 430, in two cities, Carthage and Hippo. In each, one bishop was given a particular people and answered for them. A century and a half after Cyprian's death in 258, Augustine of Hippo argued that Cyprian had been wrong about baptism. He never placed him outside the church. This church argued hard about the font and about councils, but kept its bond even with a bishop it judged wrong. It closes in 430, when a Vandal army besieged Hippo and Augustine died there.

The Story

How this tradition took shape

This is the ordinary church of Latin-speaking Roman North Africa, from about 246 to 430, and it lived in two cities. CarthageCarthageThe chief city of its province and Cyprian's see. The name used here is a modern label, because this church had no special name for itself. It saw itself simply as the church, doing the ordinary work of caring for the people in front of it.

At its center stood one plain fact: a bishop was given a particular people, in one place, and he answered for them. He preached to them week after week, taught those preparing for baptism, washed them at the font and fed them at the table. When they failed, it was his own wound before it was a case to judge. Cyprian put it in one image: the shepherd is wounded most deeply when his flock is wounded. The tie ran both ways. The people could call a man to the office against his will, and they could crowd before his seat and refuse to go home.

Cyprian was a trained public speaker who turned to the church in about 246. Within two or three years the people of Carthage made him their bishop by acclaim. He was still newly baptized, and five presbyters, the clergy just below a bishop, opposed him. He later named what had put him there: the people's voice, joined to God's judgment. A century and a half later, Augustine's path ran the same way. In 391 the congregation at Hippo seized him and had him made a presbyter while he wept.

In 250 came the first persecution enforced across the whole empire by plan. It did not mainly demand that Christians give up their faith, but a recorded act of sacrifice to the gods, proved by a certificate. Some Christians sacrificed, and others paid to have the sacrifice recorded without making it. Both groups were called the lapsedthe lapsedChristians who gave in to the order to sacrifice in 250. Some sacrificed, and some bought a paper saying they had.Term. They were the church's own people, and now they asked to come back. To shut them out for good would say that one hour under pressure decides a life. To let them straight back would say the hour meant nothing.

So the church kept a road back. A failed member's name was set down, and what had truly changed in the person was watched and weighed. At the end, the same people who saw the fall received the person home, in the open. A rival claim pressed against this road. On 14 September 258 he was put to death by the sword. He had spent years deciding what to do with those who failed the first test. The second test took him, and he did not fail it.

Then this church's own voice goes quiet for about 133 years, from 258 to 391. Much happened in those years, but what survives of them comes mostly from the rival Donatist church and its opponents, not from this church's pastors. Nine old sermons, printed in a scholarly edition in 1917, may date from those years. They have not yet been assessed in building this world, so the silence is held as an open question, not a settled fact.

The second voice is Augustine. He came to the faith in 386 and was baptized at Milan in 387. At Hippo he was made a presbyter in 391 and bishop in 395 or 396. He preached to the same congregation for decades, and many of his sermons survive. By then a rival church stood in the same towns, the Donatiststhe DonatistsA rival church in the same North African towns, with its own bishops. It baptized again those who joined it.Term, with their own bishops in the same sees. They baptized again those who came over to them, and they named Cyprian's ruling as their warrant.

Augustine answered the font question the other way. Something real is given even outside the church, he argued, though it does the person no good until they come inside. So such people were received, not baptized again. In his book On Baptism he argued at length that Cyprian's ruling was wrong. Yet he never placed Cyprian outside the church. Disagreeing with a colleague, even a revered one, was not a reason to separate. The font was answered twice, and both answers stayed inside one communion.

On using force against the rival church, Augustine's mind moved in three stages. By his own later account, he first held that no one should be forced into the church. Then he and his fellow bishops asked the emperors for a narrow measure, and it was not granted. Later still, he defended a fuller use of force that was already in place. He told it as a change of mind. Scholars still dispute whether it was one, or a later way of telling the story. Cyprian never asked the state for anything.

In the 410s and 420s a second fight took hold, over grace. Augustine argued that no one's own will, left to itself, is enough to stand right before God. This argument belongs only to Augustine's years, and Cyprian never argued it in those terms.

People

The people behind this account

Cyprian, our bishop at Carthage

Cyprian was a trained public speaker who turned to the church around 246. Carthage made him its bishop within a few years, while he was still newly baptized. He led his people through persecution, plague and schism. A large collection of his letters survives, with some written to him, along with treatises such as On the Lapsed. He spoke of a shepherd wounded in his own flock's wound. He was put to death under Valerian in 258.

Pontius, our bishop's own deacon

Pontius was Cyprian's deacon. He went with his bishop into exile at Curubis and was with him on his last night. After the execution in 258 he wrote The Life and Passion of Cyprian, counted the first Christian biography. It holds the election, the plague sermon and the death.

Celerinus, a confessor among us

Celerinus was a confessor of Carthage. He had been put in prison and had not denied Christ. His letter to Lucian, another confessor in prison, survives with Lucian's reply. In it he grieves for his sister, who had sacrificed, and asks Lucian to help restore her. Neither man was yet a cleric. These two letters are the clearest lay voices this world kept.

Augustine, our bishop at Hippo

Augustine came to the faith in 386 and was baptized at Milan in 387. In 391 the congregation at Hippo seized him and had him made a presbyter while he wept. He was bishop there from 395 or 396 until his death in 430. He preached, taught those preparing for baptism, and argued against the Donatists, the Manichaeans and the Pelagians. Until his last illness he did not stop preaching.

Possidius, bishop of Calama

Possidius was bishop of Calama and Augustine's friend for nearly forty years. He wrote the Life of Augustine. He was in the house at Hippo during the siege of 430, and he watched and prayed as Augustine died.

Legacy

What came after

Augustine's books outlived this world. Inside his own church, nothing else depended on how the argument over grace came out. Later it became a foundation of Western Christian thought. Catholic and Protestant readers still draw on it, and they still disagree about what it means.

The church that kept both bishops' books kept Cyprian even where it judged him wrong, on baptism. This world has no single living heir. Its ordinary work, a pastor answerable for one local flock, makes it an ancestor of the Western church as it was before that church divided.

Related traditions

How this tradition relates to others

The rival Donatist church shared this world's provinces and its century, and it appealed to Cyprian himself. The two answered one question in opposite ways. When bishops cannot settle a disagreement, do they keep communion or build a rival church? The Donatists built their own. This church kept its bond, even with the bishop it judged wrong. Its law looks different from an imperial church's, too. There, church law orders the relations of sees and the state. Here, it exists to bring a failed member home.

In its own words

A few terms and lines worth knowing

I grieve, brothers, I grieve with you. My own integrity and my personal soundness do not lure me into soothing my griefs. That is because it is the shepherd who is wounded most deeply when his flock is wounded. I hold each one of you close to my heart, and I share the heavy burden of sorrow and mourning. I wail with those who wail, I weep with those who weep...

Cyprian, our bishop at Carthage

None of us sets himself up as a bishop over other bishops, and none of us uses a tyrant's terror to force a colleague into obedience. Every bishop has his own right to judge, in keeping with the freedom and power allowed to him. He can no more be judged by another bishop than he himself can judge another. Instead, let us all wait for the judgment of our Lord Jesus Christ. He is the only one who has the power both to place us in the government of His Church and to judge how we conduct ourselves there.

Cyprian, our bishop at Carthage

The visible form of a branch can exist even when it is separated from the vine. But the unseen life of the root can only be had in the vine. So the bodily sacraments can give “the form of godliness.” Even those who are separated from the unity of Christ’s Body carry and celebrate them. But the unseen, spiritual power of godliness cannot be in them at all. In the same way, feeling does not stay with a person's limb when it is cut off from the body.

Augustine, our bishop at Hippo

You share communion with the members of the Apostles. You share communion with the shrines of the holy martyrs, who are spread over the whole world. And you belong to my care, so that I may give a good account of you. You know what my whole account is. “Lord, You know that I have spoken. You know that I have not kept silent. You know in what spirit I have spoken. You know that I have wept before You when I spoke and was not heard.”

the flock
A bishop, for us, is not an officer of some wider institution, dropped into a posting. He is given a people. He preaches to them and baptizes them, and when they fail it is not a problem he manages but a wound he carries. The charge runs both ways: he answers for his flock, and his flock can stand in front of his seat and refuse to leave until he hears them.
the lapsed
The lapsed are our own people who complied with the order to sacrifice during the persecution -- some by sacrificing, some only by paying for a certificate that said they had. They are not strangers and not enemies. They sat where we sit, and now they are asking to come back.
reconciliation
Discipline is the road; reconciliation is the arrival. What matters is that it is a road at all. There is an order to it, the order takes time, and everyone can see it.
confessor
For us, a confessor is one who was interrogated for the Name, did not deny it, and lived. In Cyprian's time, that standing could rival a bishop's own authority.
communion
For us, communion is a formal standing within the one body. You can lose it, and be given it back. At the council of 256, Cyprian said no one would be put out of it for thinking differently.
certificate
A certificate is a letter from a confessor, given to a lapsed person. It asks for peace, or says peace is granted. Cyprian wanted each one to name the person. Some named one person and then let in his own people too, without naming them.
