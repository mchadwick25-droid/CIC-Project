The Early Church Era · c. 246–430 CE · Carthage & Hippo Regius, Roman North Africa

The Ordinary Church

Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity

Open for conversation — you can sit down with this tradition now.

Two bishops, nearly two centuries apart, held the same office over the same kind of church in Roman North Africa. The first turned from a public career to the church around 246. Within a few years his people made him bishop, over the recorded objection of five presbyters. He led his flock through a persecution that split the failed from the faithful. Then came a plague. Then his own execution in 258. The second bishop held the same office over a century and a half later, in a different city on the same coast. He preached to the same congregation for more than three decades. He died in his own city, while a Vandal army lay outside its walls. Between the two lies a silence of about a hundred and thirty years, when this world's voice falls quiet. What holds the two together is not a continuous record. It is one plain thread: a bishop answerable for his people, a road back for those who fail in public, and colleagues who argue hard without ever putting each other outside the table.

The Story

How this tradition took shape

The record runs from about 246 to 430. It centers on two cities in Roman North Africa: Carthage and Hippo Regius. For most of this span, Carthage was the biggest Latin Christian city outside Rome. Hippo Regius was a large port, in the Numidian church province. This is not a movement built around one doctrine or one crisis. It has no founding story to tell. It is the ordinary life of a bishop and his flock: preaching, teaching people before baptism, and deciding what to do with those who fail. Real differences separate the two bishops. How far coercion could go. Whether a rival ordination was valid. What a council can decide -- and that question stays open, not settled. But none of these differences clearly touches a bishop's ordinary authority over his own flock. That authority is what this world cares about most.

The first bishop was a trained speaker. He turned to the church around 246. Within a few years, Carthage made him its bishop -- while, in his own deacon's words, he was still "a neophyte, and, as it was considered, a novice." The congregation called for him. Five presbyters are recorded opposing it. Years later, he defended that same election against a faction that had never accepted it. He named two things acting together: "your suffrage and God's judgment," set against a faction's "ancient venom." This was not one single event. A century and a half later, a congregation brought the second bishop unwillingly into office, twice, in the same kind of way.

In 250 the emperor Decius ordered a certificate proving every subject had sacrificed to the gods. It split the congregation into those who had stood firm and those who had not -- and both groups were still there afterward, in the same room. Cyprian called the injury his own: "it is the shepherd that is chiefly wounded in the wound of his flock." What followed was not exclusion. A road back was built, examined and weighed, and walked in the open. A church that takes everyone back the same afternoon has no door. One that takes no one back has no master.

The road back was contested from the start. Confessors -- people who had survived interrogation without denying their faith -- claimed a standing of their own to grant peace on the spot. "Thousands of certificates were daily given, contrary to the law of the Gospel," Cyprian complained. Two of them show the claim in their own hands. Celerinus had survived imprisonment, and now wrote from freedom to Lucian, a confessor still held in prison. He asked Lucian to grant peace to his own sister, and to two other women. Lucian, expecting to die of hunger and thirst, granted it. Cyprian never fully folded that claim into his own regulated process. Both stood as real: the claim that forced a process into being, and the process that had to answer it.

A severe plague swept Carthage while Cyprian was bishop. It took the faithful and the failed alike, and it asked nothing anyone could answer rightly or wrongly. He called the congregation together and told them that caring for their own people was nothing remarkable -- anyone does that. What would set them apart was caring for the very people persecuting them. Relief went out to everyone, not only to believers, and the poor who had no money gave labor instead. The plague produced no new doctrine and no new structure. It produced a bishop's plain word to a frightened city, and then a treatise -- this world's own way of turning crisis into teaching.

Opening the council that would rule on the sharpest question of his career, Cyprian stated the rule that held this world together: "neither does any of us set himself up as a bishop of bishops... since every bishop... has his own proper right of judgment." A century and a half later, the second bishop argued at book length that the ruling reached that day was wrong. He held to his own answer firmly. But he never once suggested that the man who reached the ruling stood outside communion for it. In this world, sharp disagreement was not treated as a reason to separate.

Persecution returned under the emperor Valerian. In 258, soldiers executed Cyprian outside Carthage. His own deacon wrote about it afterward, in the form of a saint's life. He remembered Cyprian binding his own eyes and hurrying the executioner along. This is a portrait of a life fully given, not a plain report from a stranger in the crowd. The trial's own strict, documentary record survives in this world's collection. It has not yet been read. So this account is the tradition's own memory of the death. It is offered as that, not as what a bystander would have seen.

Between the two bishops lies a silence of roughly a hundred and thirty years. This world's own voice falls quiet in that time. The silence is richly attested elsewhere, but almost entirely through a neighboring communion's record, never this one's own. What crosses that gap is not a line of living teachers. It is the second bishop, reading the first one's council acts decades after his death. He argues with them as a text to weigh, not a memory to inherit: "even of the plenary Councils, the earlier are often corrected by those which follow them." A predecessor met on a page. Answered. Never able to answer back.

A rival church stood in this world's own towns throughout the second bishop's time in office. It appealed, for its central practice, to a ruling the first bishop had once given: that baptism given outside the one church washes nothing, so anyone crossing over must be washed again. The second bishop held the opposite view, at book length -- that something real is given even outside, though it does the person no good until they come home. Each bishop held his own answer firmly. What this world never resolved is which of its own two answers was right. Collegial communion was tested hardest exactly here, and it held.

The second bishop had been seized into his first office, in 391, by a congregation that laid hands on him while he stood unaware among them -- and "demanded it with great zeal and clamor, while he wept freely." He died in 430, during his own last illness, having asked to be left alone with psalms of penitence copied out and hung on the wall. He read them and wept over them for days. The city was under Vandal siege while it happened. He made no will, because he had nothing to make one from, and ordered the church's books preserved for those who came after. This world's record closes there -- not because one man's life ended, but because the same kind of violent pressure that opened this world closed it too.

Voices

The people behind this account

Cyprian, our bishop at Carthage

Cyprian became bishop of Carthage around 248, within a few years of his own conversion. He led his flock through persecution, plague, and schism. Soldiers executed him in 258. He is this world's fullest and most contested voice. His own crisis letters carry the whole shape of the lapsed controversy. His later letters still answer a faction that never accepted his election.

Augustine, our bishop at Hippo

A congregation brought Augustine into office twice, against his own wishes. He became presbyter at Hippo in 391, and bishop in 395 or 396. He preached to the same congregation for over three decades. He died there in 430, with a Vandal army outside the walls. He argued against Cyprian's own ruling on baptism, at book length. But he never treated him as outside communion for having been wrong.

Pontius, our bishop's own deacon

Pontius was Cyprian's own deacon, present with him at his banishment to Curubis. Writing afterward in the form of a saint's life, he is the source for most of what survives about Cyprian's own early years and death -- an admiring witness, not a neutral one, and this world names that mediation rather than smoothing it away.

Possidius, bishop of Calama

Possidius was Augustine's friend for nearly forty years and a bishop in his own right, present in the room for his friend's last illness and death. He is the source for the one first-person account this world holds of a bishop watched doing what he had spent his life asking of others.

Numidicus, our presbyter

Numidicus watched a group of believers die under persecution. His own wife was among them. Soldiers left him for dead, until his daughter found him still alive. Cyprian later ordained him a presbyter. He did this on the strength of what Numidicus had endured, not what he had studied -- though Numidicus, by Cyprian's own account, had not wanted to survive.

Documented stories

Stories worth telling in full

A Neophyte, Chosen by the Judgment of God and the Favour of the People

246-249 (Cyprian's own conversion and election)

A recent convert is made bishop within a few years of his own baptism. Five presbyters recorded their opposition. Years later, he calls the very acclaim that raised him God's own judgment made visible.

Read the full account

Our own deacon Pontius, writing after his bishop had been executed, passed over most of what he could have said about Cyprian's early years and settled on one fact as enough. "For the proof of his good works I think that this one thing is enough," he wrote: "that by the judgment of God and the favour of the people, he was chosen to the office of the priesthood and the degree of the episcopate while still a neophyte, and, as it was considered, a novice."

A neophyte. Newly baptised. Pontius did not soften this -- he pressed it, noting that Cyprian was "still in the early days of his faith, and in the untaught season of his spiritual life." What we saw in him was not training. It was something we thought we could already see.

Two things are claimed at once in that sentence, and for Pontius they are not in tension: the judgment of God, and the favour of the people. The second is how the first became visible to us -- and years afterward, writing to defend his own contested episcopate against a faction that had never accepted it, Cyprian himself would call that same acclamation by its proper name: our own suffrage, joined to God's own judgment.

How Two Confessors, Not Our Bishop, Decided Who Could Come Back

c. 250 (during the Decian persecution)

Celerinus had survived prison. He wrote to Lucian, a confessor still held there. Together, the two of them granted a lapsed woman's peace themselves. The letters survive only because the bishop they bypassed kept them in his own dossier.

Read the full account

Celerinus was a confessor among us -- he had been imprisoned and had not denied Christ -- and he wrote to Lucian, another confessor, in prison. He did not write about himself. "Know, nevertheless, that I am placed in the midst of a great tribulation," he began, and the tribulation was not his own captivity. His sister had sacrificed. "I ask that you will grant my desire, and that you will grieve with me at the (spiritual) death of my sister, who in this time of devastation has fallen from Christ."

She was not dead. She was alive, in Carthage, and what Celerinus grieved was a death he believed had happened to her soul -- which is why the letter carries a request. He asked Lucian to intervene, for her and for two other women, Numeria and Candida, whom Lucian also knew. He made the case for them: they had repented, and there were works to point to.

Lucian's reply came back from the prison, and it granted what was asked. Before it did, it said what the prison had been like: by the emperor's command they "were ordered to be put to death by hunger and thirst, and were shut up in two cells, that so they might weaken us by hunger and thirst." Then: "But now we have attained the brightness itself." He greeted Numeria and Candida, and named the martyrs on whose authority he answered, and signed off exhausted, greeting others "whose names I have not written, because I am already weary. Therefore they must pardon me."

How the One Who Judged Others' Penitence Was Watched Doing His Own

430 (Augustine's final illness, during the siege of Hippo)

A bishop spent his life examining other people's penance. Now he asks to be left alone with the shortest penitential psalms, copied out and hung on his wall. He weeps over them for days before he dies.

Read the full account

Possidius had been Augustine's friend for nearly forty years, and he was in the house.

He records something Augustine had said often, in private conversation: that even after baptism, exemplary Christians and priests ought not depart this life without fitting repentance. Then he records what Augustine did with his own rule. "And this he himself did in his last illness of which he died. For he commanded that the shortest penitential Psalms of David should be copied for him, and during the days of his sickness as he lay in bed he would look at these sheets as they hung upon the wall and read them; and he wept freely and constantly."

About ten days before the end he asked to be left alone -- Possidius says he "asked of us who were present that no one should come in to him, except only at the hours in which the physicians came to examine him or when nourishment was brought to him." The request was kept. Until that last illness he had not stopped preaching.

And the end, in Possidius's own first person: "With all the members of his body intact, with sight and hearing unimpaired, while we stood by and watched and prayed, 'he slept with his fathers,' as it is written, 'well-nourished in a good old age.'" He made no will, because he had nothing to make one from. He ordered that the church's library and all its books be carefully preserved for those who came after. The city was under siege by the Vandals while this happened.

Legacy

What came after

The two bishops did not write for a tradition they expected to found -- at least, that is how this world understood itself. Yet both took their own writings seriously as work that would last. Cyprian gathered and kept copies of his own letters. Near the end of his life, Augustine went back through everything he had written and corrected it. What actually outlived this world, on the record we hold, was Augustine's theological writing above all. His teaching on grace and human weakness, argued against a rival system in his later years, became load-bearing for the traditions that followed -- even as it stood on its own within this world.

This tradition is still living, but it has no single named heir. Too much of what it held -- office, penance, sacrament -- passed into many later communities. It did not pass into just one. It is close to how most historic Christian groups still understand parish or diocesan life, wherever they kept the office of bishop or pastor at all.

In its own record

How this world relates to others

A rival communion pressed on this world throughout the second bishop's time in office. It was a parallel church, holding the same towns. For its central practice, it appealed to a ruling this world's first bishop had once given. This world's record names that rival only where it presses directly on this world's own life, and no further. Its martyrs, and its convictions in its own voice, are not this world's to narrate. The two share the same theological confession. Augustine argued at length against Cyprian's ruling on rebaptism. But he grounded his own case in the bond between bishops, not in the ruling itself. And in its later years, one world turned to state power against the other. That is a real, disclosed difference between them.

Sources

What this account is built from

This world's evidence is unusually direct. Both bishops speak in their own words. They speak as bishops of the tradition itself, not as quotations inside a hostile source. This world holds the largest and most direct base of primary sources built so far. The imbalance here is narrower than that, but it is real. Two named, elite, clerical voices carry nearly the whole record, across 184 years. A deacon's own eyewitness account reaches beyond the episcopate. So do two lay confessors, writing in their own words. Women appear throughout this record. They are never audible. Roughly a hundred and thirty years between the two bishops are a genuine silence here. That silence is richly attested elsewhere -- almost entirely through a neighboring communion's own sources, which this record does not borrow from. Material evidence has not been consulted here. It is not absent. Carthage and Hippo Regius both hold real excavated Christian sites. But no site report or excavation record has been checked for this world. A pastoral letter does describe a church's own apse and seating. One more body of evidence remains unread: the acts of a 411 conference between this world's bishops and the rival communion.

  • Cyprian of Carthage, The Epistles (82 letters, including letters TO Cyprian from Rome, Cornelius, the confessors, and Firmilian of Caesarea) This is the fullest picture we have of how a real third-century North African congregation actually ran. It is crisis letters, in the bishop's own hand, sent to the very people they concern.
  • Pontius the Deacon, The Life and Passion of Cyprian, Bishop and Martyr This is the first Christian biography. An admiring eyewitness deacon wrote it after his bishop's execution. It is the source for most of what survives about Cyprian's early life and death, and it is openly a portrait, not a neutral report.
  • Possidius, bishop of Calama (author); Herbert T. Weiskotten (editor and translator), Sancti Augustini Vita Scripta a Possidio Episcopo (Life of Augustine) A friend of nearly forty years wrote this. He was in the room for Augustine's own last illness and death. It mirrors Pontius's own role, nearly two centuries later -- friendly, not hostile, and an eyewitness, not only a chronicler.

In this world's own words

A few terms and lines worth knowing

None of us sets himself up as a bishop over other bishops, and none of us uses a tyrant's terror to force a colleague into obedience. Every bishop has his own right to judge, in keeping with the freedom and power allowed to him. He can no more be judged by another bishop than he himself can judge another. Instead, let us all wait for the judgment of our Lord Jesus Christ. He is the only one who has the power both to place us in the government of His Church and to judge how we conduct ourselves there.

Cyprian, our bishop at Carthage

I grieve, brothers, I grieve with you. My own integrity and my personal soundness do not lure me into soothing my griefs. That is because it is the shepherd who is wounded most deeply when his flock is wounded. I hold each one of you close to my heart, and I share the heavy burden of sorrow and mourning. I wail with those who wail, I weep with those who weep...

Cyprian, our bishop at Carthage

For they have not forgotten their conspiracy. They still carry that old venom against my office as bishop, which means against your vote and against God's judgment. They are renewing their old attack on me, and once again they are starting their sacrilegious plots with their usual cunning.

Cyprian, our bishop at Carthage

The Catholics already knew the life and teaching of the holy Augustine, and they seized him. He was standing there among the people, feeling safe and unaware of what was about to happen... So they seized him and, as is the custom in such cases, brought him to the bishop to be ordained. They did this because all of them, with one accord, wanted it done and carried through. They demanded it with great eagerness and loud shouting, while he wept freely.

Possidius, bishop of Calama

But now I imagine that no one has come here except those who want to hear. So I am not speaking to deaf hearts, or to minds that will look down on the word. Instead, this longing expectation of yours is a prayer for me.

Augustine, our bishop at Hippo
the flock
A bishop, for us, is not an officer of some wider institution, dropped into a posting. He is given a people. He preaches to them, baptizes them, and buries them, and when they fail it is not a problem he manages but a wound he carries. The charge runs both ways: he answers for his flock, and his flock can stand in front of his seat and refuse to leave until he hears them.
the lapsed
The lapsed are our own people who complied with the order to sacrifice during the persecution -- some by sacrificing, some only by paying for a certificate that said they had. They are not strangers and not enemies. They sat where we sit, and now they are asking to come back.
reconciliation
Discipline is the road; reconciliation is the arrival. What matters is that it is a road at all. There is an order to it, the order takes time, and everyone can see it.
communion
For us, communion is a formal standing within the one body -- a status we can lose and be given back. Its hardest test is that it survives sharp disagreement between the people who hold it.
confessor
For us, a confessor is one who was interrogated for the Name and did not deny it, and lived. That standing, bought at that price, is the strongest rival we have to a bishop's own authority.
