The Bethlehem Circle

Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity

Open for conversation — you can sit down with this tradition now.

Marcella, a Roman widow, had already gathered a circle of ascetic, scripturally serious women on the Aventine hill before Jerome ever arrived in 382. Jerome joined them as their teacher, a caustic, brilliant scholar with a gift for making enemies. In 384, Paula's daughter Blaesilla took up the severe fasting Jerome taught and was dead within four months. A Roman crowd's outrage at her funeral helped drive Jerome from the city in 385. Marcella did not go with him. She stayed in Rome. Clergy still brought her their hardest scripture questions until her own death in 410. Paula sailed east with her daughter Eustochium. She spent a senatorial fortune founding a men's monastery, houses for women, and a pilgrims' hostel at Bethlehem. There, Jerome went on translating the Bible into Latin, book by book from the Hebrew. He had begun the project years earlier in Rome, revising the Latin Gospels against the Greek for Pope Damasus. Scholars later called the whole project the Vulgate. Paula led the women's community until her death in 404, and Eustochium succeeded her, leading it for another decade and more. In 416, an attack tied to the Pelagian dispute set fire to the Bethlehem monasteries. Eustochium and her young niece, also named Paula, sent Rome a report of it. Innocent said fear had kept them from saying more, and what reached him was closer to rumor than complaint. He did not write back to the women. His reply went instead to John, the bishop of Jerusalem, rebuking him for letting the violence happen. Inside that rebuke, he praised the women for one thing: they had never named the man responsible. Not one word any of these women wrote in her own hand survives. Nearly everything known of them comes through Jerome's own letters. Even the claim that clergy sought out Marcella's judgment comes from a memorial Jerome wrote two years after her death. Modern historians think he was partly defending his own reputation there, not only praising hers.

Voices

The people behind this account

Jerome (c. 331–420)

Jerome came to Rome in 382 as secretary to Pope Damasus. He stayed to become this world's central scholar, translator, and letter-writer. He spent decades turning the Old Testament into Latin, straight from the Hebrew. He defended nearly every hard word choice, in a preface of its own. He argued fiercely with anyone who challenged him: a fellow scholar, an old friend, a hostile bishop. Almost everything left behind by this world passed through his own hand first.

Paula (347–404, Bethlehem - Ep. 108 dates her death precisely and gives her age; buried by the Church of the Nativity)

Paula was a widow of the oldest Roman blood. She gave away a great fortune. She spent it building Bethlehem: a monastery for men, houses for women, and a hostel for travelers. She led the women's community she founded for nineteen years. She learned Hebrew well enough to chant the psalms in it. She died in 404, and was buried beneath the Church of the Nativity.

Eustochium (c. 368–418, 419, or 420 - contested across a three-year window depending on the reference consulted; never resolved to a single year here)

Eustochium was consecrated to a virgin's life from her youth. Jerome addressed one of his most famous letters to her. Her mother, Paula, died in 404. After that, Eustochium led the Bethlehem women's community for the rest of this world's own span. In 416, an attack burned the monasteries. Eustochium and her young niece, also named Paula, wrote to Rome to report what had happened.

Marcella (c. 325/330–410, Rome - shortly after the Gothic sack, following rough treatment in her own house)

Marcella gathered a circle of ascetic, scripture-serious women on the Aventine hill. She did this years before Jerome ever reached Rome. He joined a household she had already built - not the other way around. After he left for Bethlehem, disputed questions about scripture in Rome went to her house to be settled, Jerome wrote. She died in 410, not long after Alaric's soldiers broke into that same house. They were looking for treasure she had already given away.

He fasted, and then he read Cicero. He wept over his sins, and then he took up Plautus again. When he made himself go back to the prophets, their style seemed crude to him, and it repelled him.

Then a fever took him, so deep that his funeral was being prepared. In spirit, he said, he was dragged before the judgment seat. Asked who he was, he answered that he was a Christian. The Judge told him that he lied. You are a follower of Cicero and not of Christ, the Judge said, for where your treasure is, there your heart will be also.

Jerome said he was flogged, and his conscience burned worse than the lash. He swore an oath that if he ever again owned worldly books, he would have denied his Lord. He woke with his eyes drenched. From then on, he tells us, he read the divine books with more zeal than he had ever given to the books of men.

His enemies, Rufinus among them, later mocked him over the dream and the oath. They pointed out that his pages never stopped quoting the classics.

Legacy

What came after

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

This world's own record does not follow its central labor past its own span. It shows an argument that was still open the moment the last translator died. Within a few years, a respected fellow scholar, Augustine, made the strongest case for the other side, in writing. He agreed with every point of the Hebrew scholarship. He still refused the conclusion. The churches of Christ, he wrote, should trust the old Greek translators, not one modern scholar's own work, however sound. Did the Hebrew-based translation ever become this world's own settled Bible? That question sits outside what the surviving evidence can answer. Its own witness ends with the argument still unresolved.

In its own record

How this world relates to others

This circle's own scholarship grew out of Alexandria's Origen. Jerome and his oldest friend, Rufinus, had both translated Origen's own writing, in earlier years. Then a fight over Origen's ideas split them apart for good. Jerome came to this world already shaped by Rome. He first arrived to help a pope, Damasus, with his letters and his scripture questions. Only after that did he join the household Marcella had already built.

Sources

What this account is built from

This world's record is richer in one man's own writing than in almost anything else. Jerome's letters, prefaces, and attacks on rivals carry nearly everything that survives. That includes nearly every account of the women's own lives. The record goes silent in a few places, though. No word by Paula, Eustochium, Marcella, or Fabiola survives in her own hand - though we know all of them wrote. People held as slaves in these same homes are never named or heard at all. And no one knows what the ground at Bethlehem might show: no stone, coin, or dug-up wall tied to these homes has ever been found.
