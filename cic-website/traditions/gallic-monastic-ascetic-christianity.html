The Imperial Church Era · c. 360–450 CE · Loire Valley, Lérins & Marseilles, Gaul

The Monk-Bishops of Gaul

Gallic Monastic-Ascetic Christianity

Open for conversation — you can sit down with this tradition now.

This tradition grew up around bishops who never stopped living as monks, and around wisdom first learned in the deserts of Egypt. Its first figure, Martin, was a Roman soldier. Almost everything we know of him comes from one admirer's writing — Sulpitius Severus was not present for the events he describes. Martin told Caesar that he was now a soldier of Christ, and asked to leave the army; he even offered to walk unarmed toward the enemy line. By about 360, he had gathered a few companions near Poitiers. Some years later, the people of Tours seized him and made him their bishop. He kept living as a monk outside the city walls. Two generations later, Honoratus founded a monastery on the island of Lérins, off the Mediterranean coast. He became bishop of Arles himself, and other monks from the island followed him into the office, at Arles and at Riez. Around the same years, John Cassian founded two houses at Marseilles. He had spent years among the fathers of Egypt. He wrote down what he had learned there, adapting their customs to a colder climate and a different church. At Lérins, the monk Vincent wrote a rule for telling real growth in the faith from a corruption dressed up as one. Augustine had written to a monastery in North Africa about grace and human effort. Word of his treatise reached Gaul. It was the monks of Marseilles, not Martin's own Tours, who pushed back hardest. Augustine then wrote two more treatises answering them directly. Through the southern houses ran one argument, never fully settled: how much of a monk's progress comes from God's own gift, and how much from his own effort. The story ends around the year 450, with that question still open.

The Story

How this tradition took shape

This world's own record runs from about 360 to 450 CE, in Roman Gaul. It holds two separate households of monks that never once wrote to each other. One grew up in the Loire valley, around the town of Tours. The other grew up on an island called Lérins, off the Mediterranean coast, and in the nearby city of Marseilles. Nothing in this world's own evidence puts the two households in contact. What holds them together is a shared idea, not a shared conversation. Neither house claims to have invented its own way of life. Both call themselves keepers of what came before them, never authors of something new.

The northern household's founder was a soldier named Martin. Around 360, before a battle, the army was giving out a gift of money to the troops. When his turn came, Martin asked for his discharge instead. He told the commander, named in the record only as Julian Caesar, that he had served him as a soldier long enough; now he would serve Christ, and it was not right for him to fight. Accused of cowardice, Martin offered to walk out unarmed between the two armies the next morning, trusting only the sign of the cross. He never had to. The enemy asked for peace overnight, and the battle never happened. Martin got his discharge and went to Hilary, the bishop of Poitiers.

Years later, the people of Tours wanted Martin as their own bishop. He did not want the office. A citizen named Ruricius lied that his own wife was sick, to draw Martin out of his monastery. Once he was on the road, a crowd blocked every way back, so he could not turn around. A few bishops objected: his clothes were poor and his hair a mess, unfit for so great an honor. The reader picked to read that day's psalm could not get through the crowd. Someone else grabbed the psalter instead and read out whatever verse came open, a line about strength made perfect against enemies. The people cheered, and the objectors fell silent. As bishop, Martin kept living like a monk outside the city walls, and many monks he trained were later made bishops themselves.

Two generations after Martin, a different pattern appeared in the south. A man named Honoratus went alone to a small island off the coast, a place so overrun with snakes that no one else would live there. He went unafraid, singing a psalm, and the record says the snakes gave way before him. He built what his own community later called a camp of God, in a place made for no life at all. Honoratus wanted nothing more than to stay a monk on his island. The office found him anyway: he was made bishop of the nearby city of Arles. Another monk trained on the same island, Faustus, later became bishop of Riez. This world's own record says almost nothing about an ordinary day on the island - only how its founder arrived, and how the office came looking for him.

Both households treated the monks of Egypt as the standard to measure against. But they did it in opposite ways. In the south, the relationship was reception. Cassian's task was to bring the East's customs home to Gaul. He corrected anything built on, in his own words, "the fancy of any one who has founded a monastery." He changed only what the colder climate truly required. In the north, the relationship was comparison, not reception. Sulpitius has a traveler just back from Egypt declare that Martin alone was worth as much as all its hermits and holy men put together. Gaul was a rival to Egypt here, not a debtor. This world's own two households never argued the comparison out with each other. They simply held it in two different ways, at the same time, without ever comparing notes.

Both households also agreed that nothing true could be new. Vincent, a monk on the same island, wrote a short book meant only as a memory aid, "to provide against my forgetfulness," as he put it himself. His test for telling true faith from a corruption dressed up as one was simple: what has been believed everywhere, always, by everyone. He called himself not an author but a keeper, not a teacher but a disciple. Cassian's own writing carried the same conviction north from Egypt. He passed on a story the fathers of Egypt told about themselves: unable to agree how many psalms to sing each evening, they were still arguing when the hour for prayer arrived. One of them rose to sing anyway. He finished the twelfth psalm, said "Alleluia," and vanished. The fathers understood the number to be fixed by that one messenger, not by their own decision. Neither monk chose his own rule. Both received one, and called it received.

The south's own formation program had a name for its goal: purity of heart. Cassian taught that fasting, all-night prayer, and giving up possessions were not the goal itself, only aids toward it. What matters most is discretion, which he called the mother of all virtues - the one that keeps every other virtue from ruining itself through excess. The program named eight faults a monk had to fight in turn, from gluttony to pride, each with its own remedy handed down by the fathers. Tours had no such vocabulary at all. Martin's own record shows a similar instinct at work without ever naming it - his discernment of a false vision of Christ - but it never speaks of purity of heart, discretion, or a fault-list of its own.

The south's most famous argument began, by its own account, as an ordinary worry at prayer. Cassian and his friend Germanus had spent an evening in Egypt listening to an elder teach on chastity. The elder ended with one troubling sentence: that no one becomes master of the good by his own effort alone. The next morning, still turning it over, they asked him to explain himself. He answered with a farmer's field. A farmer who will not plow gets no harvest - his effort matters. But even a hard-working farmer gets nothing without rain he cannot make happen himself. The elder concluded that the very wish to do good starts with God, and it is for us to follow that grace day by day. That morning's answer grew into a whole argument about how much of a monk's progress is God's gift and how much is his own labor - an argument that later reached Rome, and this world's own record never settles it.

At Tours, the record is organized around a different idea entirely: power, felt and shown. Martin once found a catechumen, a new believer not yet baptized, lying dead. He shut himself alone with the body, stretched himself over it, and after two hours the man opened his eyes. Another night, the devil came to Martin dressed as Christ, in royal purple and a jeweled crown. Martin refused to believe him: the only Christ he would accept still carried the marks of his own wounds. Cassian, in the south, refused this whole way of measuring a monk. He would not, in his own words, weave a tale of God's wonders and signs, and warned that a monk who boasts of casting out demons has already failed. The two households never argued this difference out either. One built its record around power. The other built its record around refusing to.

Bishops and church councils sat at the center of this world's deepest disagreement with itself. At Tours, the office was something to be wary of: bishops resisted Martin's election, and some of his own accusers turned out to be bishops themselves. The emperor once called Martin to court, to stop tribunes being sent to hunt heretics in Spain. Martin agreed to share communion there with bishops he had refused before - the same bishops who had pushed for a heretic's execution. He did it to save lives, then regretted it for the rest of his own life. He never sat with a group of bishops again. His discipline reached his own household, too, and not always gently. A presbyter he had raised from boyhood was named Brictio. People inside Martin's own community accused Brictio of buying enslaved children - boys taken from beyond the frontier, and girls chosen, the record says, for their looks. When Martin corrected him, Brictio raged at his own master in public, calling his visions ridiculous fancies. He repented within the hour. Martin never removed him from office, saying: "If Christ bore with Judas, why should not I bear with Brictio?" In the south, by contrast, church councils were trusted as guardians of the truth, not as something to flee. This world's own record does not resolve the disagreement between its two households. It states it plainly and leaves it standing.

Almost everything this world's northern half knows comes from one writer. Sulpitius Severus was a wealthy layman who gave up his own career to follow Martin. He was never present for any of the events he describes, and he says so himself. His real sources were Martin's own household, and sometimes Martin himself. That single-author risk runs through every surviving Tours story. When Martin's own death drew near, he traveled to make peace among quarreling clergy in a nearby town. He already knew he was dying. His own monks wanted to lay him on a soft bed of straw. He refused. He chose sackcloth and ashes instead, saying he had sinned if he left his monks a different example to follow. He was carried back to Tours. A crowd the record puts at two thousand buried him: monks, old men, young soldiers who had just sworn service to Christ, and a choir of consecrated women who held back their own tears out of modesty.

Voices

The people behind this account

Martin, bishop of Tours

Martin left the army to serve Christ instead, telling his commander it was not right for him to fight. He gathered followers in the woods near Tours, then was made bishop against his own will. As bishop, he never stopped living like a monk. He healed the sick and raised the dead, by his own account, yet felt that power lessen once he took a communion he believed was wrong, just to save two men's lives. He never sat with a group of bishops again.

Cassian, the founder of the houses at Marseilles

Cassian lived for years among the monks of Egypt before founding two communities of his own at Marseilles. He wrote down what he had learned there, refusing on principle to tell any tale of wonders or signs. He passed on Egypt's own program: purity of heart as the goal, discretion as the guide, and eight faults for a monk to fight in order. He also passed on an unresolved question, one he did not shy from: how much of a monk's progress is God's own gift, and how much is his own effort.

Honoratus, the founder of the island

Honoratus went alone to an island so overrun with snakes that no one else would live there, and built a community in a place made for no life at all. He wanted nothing more than to stay a monk. The office of bishop found him anyway, and he ended his life leading the church at Arles instead of the island he founded.

Sulpitius Severus

Sulpitius Severus was rich and well-educated. He gave up his own career to follow Martin. He wrote Martin's Life while Martin was still alive. He says plainly that he was never there for any of the events in it. His real sources were Martin's own household, and sometimes Martin himself.

Documented stories

Stories worth telling in full

Martin Asks Caesar for His Discharge

c. 360, before a battle in Gaul, on the eve of Martin's discharge from the Roman army

Accused of cowardice for refusing to fight, a soldier offers to walk unarmed toward the enemy line - and the enemy surrenders before morning.

Read the full account

In his Life of St. Martin, Sulpitius Severus records how Martin left the army - the scene the whole of Tours's memory of him turns on. The barbarians, Sulpitius writes, "were rushing within the two divisions of Gaul," and Julian Caesar, gathering an army at the city of the Vaugiones, "began to distribute a donative to the soldiers." The men were called forward one by one. When it came to Martin's turn he judged it the moment to ask for his discharge, because "he did not think it would be proper for him, if he were not to continue in the service, to receive a donative," and said to Caesar: "Hitherto I have served you as a soldier: allow me now to become a soldier to God: let the man who is to serve thee receive thy donative: I am the soldier of Christ: it is not lawful for me to fight." Julian - "the tyrant," in Sulpitius's word - stormed, and said the man was withdrawing "from fear of the battle, which was to take place on the morrow, and not from any religious feeling." Martin, "all the more resolute from the danger that had been set before him," answered: "If this conduct of mine is ascribed to cowardice, and not to faith, I will take my stand unarmed before the line of battle tomorrow, and in the name of the Lord Jesus, protected by the sign of the cross, and not by shield or helmet, I will safely penetrate the ranks of the enemy." He was put back in prison, "determined on proving his words true by exposing himself unarmed to the barbarians." "But, on the following day," Sulpitius writes, "the enemy sent ambassadors to treat about peace and surrendered both themselves and all their possessions." There was no battle. Sulpitius does not leave the reader to draw the conclusion: "who can doubt that this victory was due to the saintly man?" - it was granted him that he should not be sent unarmed to the fight, and the good Lord, "that his blessed eyes might not be pained by witnessing the death of others," removed all necessity for fighting, so that "the enemy being subdued without bloodshed, no one should suffer death."

Martin Yields Once, at the Emperor's Court

385/386, after the execution of Priscillian and his companions at Trier

To stop tribunes from being sent to hunt heretics in Spain, a bishop takes communion with men he despises - and never fully forgives himself.

Read the full account

In his Dialogues, Sulpitius Severus has Gallus - one of Martin's own disciples - tell "an event which he always concealed, owing to the character of the times, but which he could not conceal from us." Sulpitius's Sacred History narrates an earlier phase of the same underlying affair, in his own voice. After Priscillian had been put to death, the emperor Maximus "protected by his royal power Ithacius the bishop, who had been the accuser of Priscillian." The bishops gathered at Treves were "daily communicating with Ithacius" and "had made common cause with him." Martin was on his way to the court "constrained ... by many serious causes of people involved in suffering" - and among the things being prepared there was an order "to send some tribunes armed with absolute power into the two Spains, to search out heretics, and, when found, to deprive them of their life or goods," a hunt in which, Gallus says, "one was deemed a heretic rather on his turning pale from fear, or wearing a particular garment, than by the faith which he professed." The bishops, "conscious of evil as they were," feared that Martin would refuse them communion and that others would follow him. They had the emperor's officials meet him on the road and forbid him the city unless he would "maintain peace with the bishops who were living there." He answered "that he would come among them with the peace of Christ," entered at night, prayed, and next day went to the palace. His chief request was "that tribunes, with the power of life and death, should not be sent into the Spains" - "not only to save from danger the true Christians in these regions ... but to protect even heretics themselves." In the Sacred History Sulpitius states the principle in his own words: Martin "maintained that it was quite sufficient punishment that, having been declared heretics by a sentence of the bishops, they should have been expelled from the churches; and that it was, besides, a foul and unheard-of indignity, that a secular ruler should be judge in an ecclesiastical cause." The bishops went to the emperor in terror, saying "that nothing had really been accomplished by the death of Priscillian, if Martin were to act the part of his avenger," and begged him to use his power against this one man. Maximus tried persuasion first, then anger; and when Martin was unmoved, "without delay, executioners are appointed for those in whose behalf Martin had made supplication." "When this became known to Martin, he rushed to the palace, though it was now night. He pledges himself that, if these people were spared, he would communicate; only let the tribunes ... be recalled." Maximus granted everything. Next day, at the ordination of a bishop named Felix, "Martin took part in the communion of that day, judging it better to yield for the moment, than to disregard the safety of those over whose heads a sword was hanging" - though the bishops could not make him sign his name to it. Leaving the city the day after, "he was filled with mourning and lamentation that he had even for an hour been mixed up with the evil communion," and near a village named Andethanna he sat down alone in the woods, "alternately accusing and defending the cause of his grief and conduct." Then, Gallus says, "an angel stood by him and said, 'Justly, O Martin, do you feel compunction, but you could not otherwise get out of your difficulty. Renew your virtue, resume your courage, lest you not only now expose your fame, but your very salvation, to danger.'" Afterwards, "when it happened that he cured some of the possessed more slowly and with less grace than usual, he at once confessed to us with tears that he felt a diminution of his power on account of the evil of that communion in which he had taken part for a moment through necessity, and not with a cordial spirit. He lived sixteen years after this, but never again did he attend a synod, and kept carefully aloof from all assemblies of bishops."

Legacy

What came after

This world's own record stops before its central argument was settled. It says so itself: what comes after is not its to tell. One generation later, the argument about grace and effort continued. But it was no longer monks writing to fellow monks. A bishop trained on the same island took it up instead, writing at the direct request of two church councils. This world's own record ends at the edge of that change, not inside it.

In its own record

How this world relates to others

This world grew up on ground another community had already fought to hold. Before Martin's own household took shape, a bishop named Hilary of Poitiers was sent into exile. He had refused to accept a creed backed by the emperor. Once he came home, Hilary was the bishop near whom Martin first gathered his own followers. This world's own record does not say what happened to Gaul's church after its own span closes in 450. It says nothing about monasticism elsewhere, either.

Sources

What this account is built from

This world's record is richest in one man's telling of another. Sulpitius Severus wrote about Martin. Cassian wrote down what Egypt's monks taught him. Both households left rich writing about ideas and virtues. But both leave two things almost entirely dark: the island's own daily life, and Tours's own daily worship. No one wrote down what the brethren sang, or when. No woman's own word survives from either house, though both kept communities of women alongside the men. And a later editor cut three whole sections on the body's own discipline from the one edition of Cassian that survives. That is a gap in what survives, not in what this world itself said.

  • Sulpitius Severus (c. 363 - c. 425), On the Life of St. Martin (Vita Martini) The whole of Martin's own story, told by a man who loved him and was never present for any of it.
  • John Cassian (c. 360 - c. 435), The Twelve Books on the Institutes of the Coenobia Egypt's customs, carried west and adapted for a colder climate and a bishop's new house.
  • Vincent of Lerins (d. before 450, probably shortly after 434), The Commonitory A short book written as a rule for telling true growth in the faith from a corruption dressed up as one.

In this world's own words

A few terms and lines worth knowing

In God there is one substance but three Persons. In Christ there are two substances but one Person.

Vincent, presbyter of the island, who wrote as Peregrinus

The Lord Jesus never said he would come back dressed in purple, with a glittering crown on his head. I will not believe Christ has come unless he comes looking the way he did when he suffered, and openly shows the marks of his wounds from the cross.

Martin, bishop of Tours

I can fast any day. But I am about to walk you on your way, and I will not have you with me for long. A fast is good and worth doing, but it is a gift I choose to give. Charity is a command, and a command has to be carried out. So since I receive Christ in you, I ought to feed him. Once I have sent you on, I can make up for the welcome I gave for his sake by fasting more strictly on my own. As the Lord said, the bridegroom's friends cannot fast while the bridegroom is with them. Once he has gone, then they will rightly fast.

one of the elders of Egypt, unnamed, as Cassian reports him (Institutes V.24)
grace (of God) / gratia
In Cassian's Marseilles, the help of God without which no effort reaches its goal. Grace "always co-operates with our will"; it goes before the will and follows it; it inspires the first desire and crowns the last perseverance. We hold it together, on purpose, with a will that stays free - in a relation "that cannot be fully grasped by the mind and reason of man."
virtus / power
At Tours, virtus is not first a moral quality but effective power. It is the "power present" Martin feels before he raises the dead, and the "abundance of power" he had as a monk and had less of as a bishop. At Marseilles Cassian uses the same word for moral virtue, and declines on principle to "weave a tale of God's miracles and signs."
discretion (discretio)
In Cassian's Marseilles, discretion is the virtue by which every other is kept from destroying itself. It is "the mother of all virtues, as well as their guardian and regulator," the "eye and light of the body," the "royal road" between excess and slackness. It is gained "only ... by true humility." That means testing every thought, like a good money-changer testing coin, against the judgment of the elders.
soldier of Christ
The picture that governs a monk's life among us. A man has changed service - from Caesar's army, or from a worldly career - to Christ's. He now stands girded, under oath, in daily combat against the devil and his own faults. The one who slackens is not a failure but a deserter.
Commonitory / "Peregrinus"
Vincent's book. By its own name it is an aid to memory, against his own forgetting. He signs it "Peregrinus, the least of all the servants of God" - a teller, not an author.
