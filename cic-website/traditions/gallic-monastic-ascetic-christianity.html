The Monk-Bishops of Gaul

Gallic Monastic-Ascetic Christianity

Open for conversation — you can sit down with this tradition now.

This tradition grew up around bishops who never stopped living as monks, and around wisdom first learned in the deserts of Egypt. Its first figure, Martin, was a Roman soldier. Almost everything we know of him comes from one admirer's writing — Sulpitius Severus was not present for the events he describes. Martin told Caesar that he was now a soldier of Christ, and asked to leave the army; he even offered to walk unarmed toward the enemy line. By about 360, he had gathered a few companions near Poitiers. Some years later, the people of Tours seized him and made him their bishop. He kept living as a monk outside the city walls. Two generations later, Honoratus founded a monastery on the island of Lérins, off the Mediterranean coast. He became bishop of Arles himself, and other monks from the island followed him into the office, at Arles and at Riez. Around the same years, John Cassian founded two houses at Marseilles. He had spent years among the fathers of Egypt. He wrote down what he had learned there, adapting their customs to a colder climate and a different church. At Lérins, the monk Vincent wrote a rule for telling real growth in the faith from a corruption dressed up as one. Augustine had written to a monastery in North Africa about grace and human effort. Word of his treatise reached Gaul. It was the monks of Marseilles, not Martin's own Tours, who pushed back hardest. Augustine then wrote two more treatises answering them directly. Through the southern houses ran one argument, never fully settled: how much of a monk's progress comes from God's own gift, and how much from his own effort. The story ends around the year 450, with that question still open.

Voices

The people behind this account

Martin, bishop of Tours

Martin left the army to serve Christ instead, telling his commander it was not right for him to fight. He gathered followers in the woods near Tours, then was made bishop against his own will. As bishop, he never stopped living like a monk. He healed the sick and raised the dead, by his own account, yet felt that power lessen once he took a communion he believed was wrong, just to save two men's lives. He never sat with a group of bishops again.

Cassian, the founder of the houses at Marseilles

Cassian lived for years among the monks of Egypt before founding two communities of his own at Marseilles. He wrote down what he had learned there, refusing on principle to tell any tale of wonders or signs. He passed on Egypt's own program: purity of heart as the goal, discretion as the guide, and eight faults for a monk to fight in order. He also passed on an unresolved question, one he did not shy from: how much of a monk's progress is God's own gift, and how much is his own effort.

Honoratus, the founder of the island

Honoratus went alone to an island so overrun with snakes that no one else would live there, and built a community in a place made for no life at all. He wanted nothing more than to stay a monk. The office of bishop found him anyway, and he ended his life leading the church at Arles instead of the island he founded.

Sulpitius Severus

Sulpitius Severus was rich and well-educated. He gave up his own career to follow Martin. He wrote Martin's Life while Martin was still alive. He says plainly that he was never there for any of the events in it. His real sources were Martin's own household, and sometimes Martin himself.

That interpretation belongs to Sulpitius himself. He was not present, the text gives no year, and no independent witness survives.

Martin cried out his thanks so loudly that the brothers rushed in and found him alive. The catechumen was baptized at once and lived for many years afterward.

The man himself used to describe his experience. He was brought before the Judge and assigned to gloomy places among common crowds. Then two angels declared that this was the man for whom Martin was praying, and they returned him to Martin. From that day, Sulpitius says, people considered Martin holy, powerful, and truly apostolic.

Sulpitius presents the man as a living witness, yet never names him. He identifies the monastery only as a place not far from the town.

Many said the slaves included boys bought from beyond the frontier and girls chosen for their looks. Brictio had been raised in the monastery by Martin himself. Pale with rage, he claimed to be the holier man, since he had grown up in the Church while Martin had been a soldier. He called Martin's visions ridiculous fantasies and nearly struck him, while Martin answered him gently.

Brictio left believing he had won, but he soon returned, threw himself at Martin's feet, and begged for forgiveness. He confessed that a demon had taken hold of him, and Martin forgave him easily. Later, whenever Brictio was accused of serious wrongdoing, Martin refused to remove him from his position as a presbyter, a priest. He did not want anyone to suspect him of avenging his own injury. He bore with Brictio, he said, as Christ had borne with Judas.

Brictio's own side is never heard, since every word of his reaches us through men hostile to him.

The Angel Who Fixed the Psalms

Set in the early days of Egypt's monks, no year given; Cassian wrote it down in Gaul

Egyptian fathers cannot agree how many psalms to sing each evening. The hour for prayer arrives, and one father sings twelve and vanishes. They take twelve as the number.

Read the full account

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Cassian passed this story on to Gaul in his Institutes. We received it as the fathers of Egypt telling how their own rule of prayer began. In the earliest days, they say, only a few of the best men were called monks. They lived like the first believers in Jerusalem, of one heart and soul, holding everything in common. The elder fathers met to arrange the daily worship for the generations that would follow them.

Each father considered only his own strength and proposed whatever number of psalms suited him. Some argued for fifty psalms, some for sixty, and some for even more. The disagreement was still unresolved when the hour for evening prayer arrived.

So they paused for worship, and one father rose to chant while the others sat listening attentively. He sang eleven psalms, each followed by its own prayer, and then he finished the twelfth with Alleluia. At that moment he vanished from their sight. The fathers understood this as the work of Providence, a rule fixed for every community through the angel's own act. From then on they kept twelve psalms for the evening and night prayers, adding two readings later by their own choice.

He and Germanus had spent the evening listening to the old man Chaeremon teach about chastity. One sentence troubled Germanus, that even striving with all one's might cannot make a person master of the good without receiving it as a gift. After a short sleep they returned for morning prayer, still whispering about it. Chaeremon noticed, shortened the service, and asked what was wrong.

Germanus argued that chastity is won by hard and lasting effort, just as a farmer earns the credit for his harvest. Chaeremon turned the farmer's example back on him. No farmer can claim the harvest by his labor alone, because he depends on rain he cannot control. Yet God gives no rich crop to a farmer who never ploughs. So, Chaeremon concluded, both the desire to do good and the opportunity to do it come from God first. The human part is to follow that grace day by day.

That morning's answer grew into the entire Conference. Later Chaeremon says that grace works together with the human will, and that the human mind cannot fully understand how both are true. What he taught about chastity the night before is missing from our edition.

Honoratus, Hilary says, left his homeland longing for the desert. Near the city lay an island that stood empty because of its excessive squalor, and no one could approach it for fear of venomous creatures. He was drawn there by the opportunity for seclusion, and by the nearness of the holy bishop Leontius, whom he loved. He longed to be cut off from the world, even by the sea. In heart and mouth he carried the psalm about walking on the asp and the basilisk, both serpents, and trampling the lion and the dragon.

So he went in unafraid, Hilary says, and his own calm scattered his companions' fear. The horror of the place fled, and the crowd of serpents gave way. Serpents were often met there, Hilary says, yet they never harmed or even frightened anyone, and he counts this among his master's miracles. Honoratus established a camp of God in that place. There the priesthood overtook the man who had fled it, and he was made a presbyter, a priest, while keeping a monk's humility.

This is the closest our record comes to Lerins itself. It tells how the founder arrived and was made a priest there. What anyone did on the island in between, it does not say.

Legacy

What came after

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

This world's own record stops before its central argument was settled. It says so itself: what comes after is not its to tell. One generation later, the argument about grace and effort continued. But it was no longer monks writing to fellow monks. A bishop trained on the same island took it up instead, writing at the direct request of two church councils. This world's own record ends at the edge of that change, not inside it.

In its own record

How this world relates to others

This world grew up on ground another community had already fought to hold. Before Martin's own household took shape, a bishop named Hilary of Poitiers was sent into exile. He had refused to accept a creed backed by the emperor. Once he came home, Hilary was the bishop near whom Martin first gathered his own followers. This world's own record does not say what happened to Gaul's church after its own span closes in 450. It says nothing about monasticism elsewhere, either.

Sources

What this account is built from

This world's record is richest in one man's telling of another. Sulpitius Severus wrote about Martin. Cassian wrote down what Egypt's monks taught him. Both households left rich writing about ideas and virtues. But both leave two things almost entirely dark: the island's own daily life, and Tours's own daily worship. No one wrote down what the brethren sang, or when. No woman's own word survives from either house, though both kept communities of women alongside the men. And a later editor cut three whole sections on the body's own discipline from the one edition of Cassian that survives. That is a gap in what survives, not in what this world itself said.

  • John Cassian, The Twelve Books on the Institutes of the Coenobia, trans. Edgar C. S. Gibson, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, 2nd series, vol. 11, ed. Schaff and Wace Egypt's customs, carried west and adapted for a colder climate and a bishop's new house.
  • Vincent of Lerins, The Commonitory, trans. C. A. Heurtley, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, 2nd series, vol. 11, ed. Schaff and Wace A short book written as a rule for telling true growth in the faith from a corruption dressed up as one.

In this world's own words

A few terms and lines worth knowing

In God there is one substance but three Persons. In Christ there are two substances but one Person.

Vincent, presbyter of the island, who wrote as Peregrinus

The Lord Jesus never said he would come back dressed in purple, with a glittering crown on his head. I will not believe Christ has come unless he comes looking the way he did when he suffered, and openly shows the marks of his wounds from the cross.

Martin, bishop of Tours

I can fast any day. But I am about to walk you on your way, and I will not have you with me for long. A fast is good and worth doing, but it is a gift I choose to give. Charity is a command, and a command has to be carried out. So since I receive Christ in you, I ought to feed him. Once I have sent you on, I can make up for the welcome I gave for his sake by fasting more strictly on my own. For "the bridegroom's friends cannot fast while the bridegroom is with them." But once he has gone, then they will rightly fast.

one of the elders of Egypt, unnamed, as Cassian reports him (Institutes V.24)
grace (of God) / gratia
In Cassian's Marseilles, the help of God without which no effort reaches its goal. Grace "always co-operates with our will," and "assists, protects, and defends it." Which one began the good, grace or the will, we left open. We held grace together with a free will on purpose - in a relation "that cannot be fully grasped by the mind and reason of man."
virtus / power
At Tours, virtus is not first a moral quality but effective power. It is the "power present" Martin feels before he raises the dead, and the "abundance of power" he had as a monk and had less of as a bishop. At Marseilles Cassian uses the same word for moral virtue, and declines on principle to "weave a tale of God's miracles and signs."
discretion (discretio)
In Cassian's Marseilles, discretion is the virtue by which every other is kept from destroying itself. It is "the mother of all virtues, as well as their guardian and regulator," the "eye and light of the body," the "royal road" between excess and slackness. It is gained "only ... by true humility." That means testing every thought, like a good money-changer testing coin, against the judgment of the elders.
soldier of Christ
The picture that governed a monk's life among us. A man had changed service - from Caesar's army, or from a worldly career - to Christ's. He now stood girded, under oath, in daily combat against the devil and his own faults. The one who slackened was, in Cassian's word, a deserter.
Commonitory / "Peregrinus"
Vincent's book. By its own name it is an aid to memory, against his own forgetting. He signs it "Peregrinus, the least of all the servants of God" - a teller, not an author.
