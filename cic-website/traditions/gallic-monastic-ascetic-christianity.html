The Monk-Bishops of Gaul

Gallic Monastic-Ascetic Christianity

Open for conversation — you can sit down with this tradition now.

This tradition grew up around bishops who never stopped living as monks, and around wisdom first learned in the deserts of Egypt. Its first figure, Martin, was a Roman soldier. Almost everything we know of him comes from one admirer's writing — Sulpitius Severus was not present for the events he describes. Martin told Caesar that he was now a soldier of Christ, and asked to leave the army; he even offered to walk unarmed toward the enemy line. By about 360, he had gathered a few companions near Poitiers. Some years later, the people of Tours seized him and made him their bishop. He kept living as a monk outside the city walls. Two generations later, Honoratus founded a monastery on the island of Lérins, off the Mediterranean coast. He became bishop of Arles himself, and other monks from the island followed him into the office, at Arles and at Riez. Around the same years, John Cassian founded two houses at Marseilles. He had spent years among the fathers of Egypt. He wrote down what he had learned there, adapting their customs to a colder climate and a different church. At Lérins, the monk Vincent wrote a rule for telling real growth in the faith from a corruption dressed up as one. Augustine had written to a monastery in North Africa about grace and human effort. Word of his treatise reached Gaul. It was the monks of Marseilles, not Martin's own Tours, who pushed back hardest. Augustine then wrote two more treatises answering them directly. Through the southern houses ran one argument, never fully settled: how much of a monk's progress comes from God's own gift, and how much from his own effort. The story ends around the year 450, with that question still open.

Voices

The people behind this account

Martin, bishop of Tours

Martin left the army to serve Christ, telling his commander it was not lawful for him to fight. Sulpitius says he could not easily be drawn out of his monastery. The people of Tours drew him out by a trick and made him their bishop. As bishop, he kept living like a monk, with his brothers two miles outside the city. Sulpitius tells of him healing the sick and raising the dead. Martin felt that power weaken after he once took communion with bishops he had refused, to save lives. After that he stayed away from every gathering of bishops.

Cassian, the founder of the houses at Marseilles

Cassian lived among the monks of Egypt as a young man. Later he founded two houses at Marseilles, one for men and one for women. He wrote down what the Egyptian fathers taught, and in his Institutes he chose not to write up the wonders he had seen. From the fathers he passed on purity of heart as the monk's goal and discretion as his guide. He listed eight faults for a monk to fight in turn. And he wrote down how a question about grace and effort troubled his friend Germanus and him at morning prayer.

Honoratus, the founder of the island

Honoratus took his followers to an island that stood empty, because it was filthy and no one went near it for fear of snakes. His disciple Hilary says he went in unafraid, with a psalm on his lips, and set up a camp of God there. He had run from church office, but on the island, as far as Hilary's sermon can be read, the priesthood caught up with him. In time he became a bishop.

Sulpitius Severus

Sulpitius Severus came from Aquitaine, from a leading family by his editors' account, and he visited Martin at Tours. He wrote Martin's Life so that its readers would be stirred to follow him. He also wrote a letter on Martin's death and the Dialogues, in which his friend Gallus tells more stories of Martin. He says he learned what he tells partly from Martin himself and partly from those who lived with him.

Martin Brings a Dead Catechumen Back

After Martin left the army and set up his monastery; the text gives no year

Martin returns to find a new believer dead before baptism. He bolts the door and stretches himself over the body. Sulpitius says that two hours later the man opens his eyes.

Martin had set up a monastery for himself not far from the town. A catechumen, a man still preparing for baptism, came to live with him and learn from his teaching and his life. Within a few days the man fell ill with fever. Martin was away for three days, and when he came back the man was dead, so suddenly that he had never been baptized.

As SulpitiusSulpitiusThe Aquitanian who wrote the Life of Martin. He says he learned it partly from Martin and partly from those who lived with him.Person tells it, Martin sent everyone out of the cell, bolted the door and stretched himself full length on the body. Martin cried out his thanks so loudly that the brothers rushed in and found alive the man they had left dead. He was baptized at once and lived for many years.

He used to tell us what had happened to him. He had stood before the Judge and been sent to a gloomy place among common crowds. Then two angels said that he was the man Martin was praying for, and he was given back to Martin. From that day, people counted Martin holy, powerful and truly apostolic.

Martin became our bishop, and as bishop he kept the dignity of the office and the plain habits of a monk.

If Christ Bore with Judas

Undated; told in Sulpitius's Dialogues, about 404 to 406

A priest Martin raised from boyhood rages at him, then soon comes back to beg pardon. Martin will not remove him. If Christ bore with Judas, why not Brictio?

Read the full account

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

One day Martin sat on his wooden seat, the one we all knew, in the little court outside his door. Gallus says Martin saw two demons on the rock above the monastery, calling out with glee, "Come here, Brictio, come here, Brictio." Then Brictio burst in, raging.

Brictio was a presbyter, a priest, whom Martin had raised in the monastery from boyhood. The day before, Martin had rebuked him, because Brictio had owned nothing when he joined the clergy and was now buying horses and slaves. Many said the slaves included boys bought from beyond the frontier and girls chosen for their looks. Pale with fury, Brictio claimed he was the holier man, since he had grown up in the Church while Martin had been a soldier. He called Martin's visions ridiculous fancies and nearly struck him, but Martin answered him gently.

Brictio stormed out, sure he had won. Soon he came back, threw himself at Martin's feet and begged for pardon, admitting that a demon had taken hold of him. Martin forgave him easily. Afterward, whenever Brictio was accused of serious wrongs, Martin would not remove him. He did not want anyone to think he was avenging an insult to himself. If Christ bore with Judas, he would ask, why should he not bear with Brictio?

The Angel Who Set the Number of Psalms

Set in the early days of Egypt's monks, no year given; Cassian wrote it down in Gaul

Egyptian fathers cannot agree how many psalms to sing each evening. At the hour of prayer, Cassian reports, one father sings twelve and vanishes. They take twelve as the number.

Read the full account

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Cassian brought us this story from the fathers of Egypt. In the earliest days, they say, only a few men were called monks, and they were the best of men. They lived like the first believers in Jerusalem, with one heart and one soul and everything shared. The elder fathers met to set a daily rule of prayer for those who would come after them.

Each father thought only of his own strength and proposed the number of psalms that suited him. Some wanted fifty, some sixty and some still more, and they were still arguing when the hour for evening prayer came.

So they stopped to pray. One father rose to sing while the rest sat listening, and he sang eleven psalms, each followed by a prayer. Then he ended the twelfth with Alleluia and vanished from their sight. The fathers saw Providence at work, for through an angel, they believed, a rule had been fixed for every community of brothers. From then on they kept twelve psalms at the evening and night prayers, and later they added two readings of their own choosing.

Honoratus and the Island of Snakes

Told in a funeral sermon by his successor at Arles; the text gives no year

A founder walks unafraid onto an island shunned for its filth and snakes, a psalm on his lips. His disciple Hilary says the snakes gave way.

Read the full account

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

When our founder Honoratus died, his disciple and successor Hilary preached his life. Honoratus, Hilary says, left his homeland because he longed for the desert. There was an island that stood empty, because it was filthy and no one could go near it for fear of poisonous snakes. He was drawn there by the chance of solitude, and by the nearness of the holy bishop Leontius, whom he loved. Many people tried to hold him back by warning him how terrible the place was. But he wanted to be cut off from the world, even by the sea.

He carried a psalm in his heart and on his lips. It promised that he would tread on the asp and the basilisk, and trample the lion and the dragon. So he went in unafraid, Hilary says, and his calm scattered the fear of his own people. The horror of the empty place fled, and the snakes gave way. Snakes were often met in those parts, Hilary adds, yet they never harmed or even frightened anyone, and he counts this among his master's miracles.

There Honoratus set up a camp of God. And there, as far as Hilary's sermon can be read, the priesthood caught up with the man who had run from it. He was made a presbyter, a priest, and he kept a monk's humility inside the priesthood.

Legacy

What came after

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

After 450 the argument over grace and effort went on, but it was no longer one monk writing to another. In the 470s Faustus, once abbot of Lérins and by then a bishop, took it up. He says he wrote at the request of church councils at Arles and Lyons, as a bishop writing for other bishops.

In its own record

How this world relates to others

Martin's household grew up in a church torn by heresy. Sulpitius says Martin found the church in Gaul in turmoil, because heretics had driven the bishop Hilary into exile. At Milan, a man named Auxentius drove Martin out as well, and he went to live on a small island. When Hilary came home, Martin followed him and built a monastery near his town.

Sources

What this account is built from

Most of what survives is one man's account of another. Sulpitius Severus wrote about Martin, and Cassian wrote down what Egypt's monks taught him. Both households wrote much about virtue. Tours also wrote of Martin's power, while Cassian set down Egypt's customs more than the days of his own houses. The island's daily life is known least of all, and the books from Tours do not say what the brothers sang or when. Both houses kept communities of women, yet no woman's own words survive. Cassian's English translator also left out three of his texts on the body and its discipline.

grace (of God) / gratia
In Cassian's Marseilles, the help of God without which no effort reaches its goal. Grace "always co-operates with our will," and "assists, protects, and defends it." Which one began the good, grace or the will, we left open. We held grace together with a free will on purpose - in a relation "that cannot be fully grasped by the mind and reason of man."
virtus / power
At Tours, virtus is not first a moral quality but effective power. It is the "power present" Martin feels before he raises the dead, and the "abundance of power" he had as a monk and had less of as a bishop. At Marseilles Cassian uses the same word for moral virtue, and declines on principle to "weave a tale of God's miracles and signs."
discretion (discretio)
In Cassian's Marseilles, discretion is the virtue by which every other is kept from destroying itself. It is "the mother of all virtues, as well as their guardian and regulator," the "eye and light of the body," the "royal road" between excess and slackness. It is gained "only ... by true humility." That means testing every thought, like a good money-changer testing coin, against the judgment of the elders.
soldier of Christ
The picture that governed a monk's life among us. A man had changed service - from Caesar's army, or from a worldly career - to Christ's. He now stood girded, under oath, in daily combat against the devil and his own faults. The one who slackened was, in Cassian's word, a deserter.
Commonitory / "Peregrinus"
Vincent's book. By its own name it is an aid to memory, against his own forgetting. He signs it "Peregrinus, the least of all the servants of God" - a teller, not an author.
