The Early Church Era · c. 150–400 CE · Alexandria, Egypt

Alexandrian Christianity

Alexandrian Catechetical Tradition

Open for conversation — you can sit down with this tradition now.

In Alexandria, from about 150 to 400 CE, teachers led seekers through a shared reading of Scripture. They read for a deeper meaning beneath the surface, one that opened onto the Logos himself. This teaching reached the literate few. Ordinary believers met the same faith another way — through baptism, the shared meal, and the yearly round of fasts and feasts. Some call this one continuous school, led by one teacher after another. Others say it was simply a series of private teachers, grouped together only in hindsight. When plague struck the city, Bishop Dionysius wrote to his own church, praising his people for nursing the sick and burying the dead. Others, he wrote, fled the city instead. Persecution could reach the school's own teachers, not only the wider community. Origen himself was imprisoned and tortured under the emperor Decius, and he died a few years later. It had already touched his boyhood, too. His father was martyred in an earlier persecution, when Origen was still just a boy.

Theon is a representative voice for this tradition, not a person who lived — built from its own letters and records, and honest about where they run out.

The Story

How this tradition took shape

This world's own record runs from about 150 to 400 CE, centered on Alexandria and the wider Egyptian church. Two channels carried the same faith to two different populations. A school channel formed the literate few through years of teaching, argument, and study. A whole-community channel - baptism, the shared meal, and the yearly round of fasts and feasts - reached everyone else, with no reading required. Both channels answered to one center: Christ the Logos, God's own Word, in whom Scripture, learning, worship, and change all became one movement.

The first teacher whose name survived here was Pantaenus, a Stoic philosopher who had converted to the faith. His pupil Clement remembered him as a bee, gathering honey from the prophets and the apostles. Clement came next, then Origen, then others after them. Was this one continuous school, with an official line of heads? Or was it a looser run of private teachers, only strung together later into one tidy story? That is genuinely disputed. Clement's own writing never once describes a formal school that he led.

Scripture was this world's deepest instrument of change. Origen read it in layers: the plain story first, then a moral sense, then a spiritual meaning that opened onto Christ. Clement and Origen called this method allegoria. God himself was always teaching, they said, through Scripture's own difficulty and through the discipline of being taught. Reading well was never just gathering information. It was supposed to change the reader.

The reader who kept reading this way was meant to be transformed, not just informed: purified, then given new sight, then drawn into God's own life. Athanasius put the whole hope in one line, late in this world's story: God became human so that humans could share God's own life. Underneath every part of this - the reading, the teaching, the worship, the change itself - stood one center. The Logos, God's own Word, was the single reason all four were really one movement, not four separate practices.

For the school's own students, learning itself was formation. It was not preparation for formation; it was the thing itself. One student, Gregory, later a bishop, described it from the inside. Origen tested his mind first, the way a farmer tests soil. Then came logic, then the natural world, then ethics lived out as habit. Only then did theology come. Gregory said something else happened underneath all of this. It felt, he said, like a spark landing on his own soul: love for the Word, and for the man who was the Word's own friend.

Teacher and bishop were two real authorities here, and this world never made them one. The strain showed early. As a young man, Origen took a hard verse about self-denial too literally and acted on it himself. His bishop, Demetrius, first admired his zeal and told him to keep teaching. Years later, once Origen had grown famous, Demetrius used that same youthful act against him, denouncing an ordination he had never approved. Origen left the city for good. He never taught in Alexandria again, and he later judged the hard verse differently himself, in writing.

Persecution reached this world's teachers as well as its ordinary members. Origen's own father, Leonides, was beheaded when Origen was sixteen. The boy wanted to die beside him. His mother stopped him the only way she could: she hid his clothes. A young woman named Potamiaena was executed by boiling pitch. The soldier who led her out was moved by her courage. He converted, and was later martyred himself. Two pictures of a formed life stood side by side here, never merged: dying as a witness, and the soul's own long, quiet climb toward God.

Disagreement inside the household did not always end in condemnation. Whole villages in the Arsinoite district had come to read Scripture's promises as a literal, thousand-year kingdom on earth, following a bishop they loved. When Bishop Dionysius disagreed, he did not send a ruling from the city. He went out to the villages and sat with the people for three days, testing the argument point by point, in the open. The leader of the other side changed his mind in front of everyone, and the account says it ended in agreement, not a split.

When plague struck Alexandria, Bishop Dionysius wrote to his own churches about who had stayed. Most of his people, he wrote, did not flee. They nursed the sick without fear, day after day, and many caught the disease and died from it. He wrote that they died gladly, carrying their neighbors' suffering onto themselves. He also wrote, in the same letter, that many of the city's pagans thrust their own sick out into the street at the first sign of illness. That difference, he said, was not an argument. It was a practice the whole city could see.

For most of this world's life, the church confessed Jesus as God's own Word without needing a single fixed formula. That changed under real pressure. When Arius taught that the Son was the highest created being, not truly God, the council of Nicaea answered with one new word: homoousios, of one same substance with the Father. Athanasius, bishop for forty-six embattled years, defended that word through five exiles. The word was new. The worship it defended, this world's own record insists, was not.

This world's teaching tradition closes with Didymus, blind since the age of four, too young ever to have read a written word himself. He held the whole of Scripture in memory instead, weighed and ordered by ear. Visitors came to him for decades. Later tradition remembers him heading the school in its final generation, though no eyewitness account actually says so. His own books, once thought entirely lost, turned up centuries later, buried in the Egyptian sand. Even now, most of them still cannot be read in English.

Almost everything above comes from one narrow group: literate, Greek-educated men. Did the same Scripture, and the same change, also shape the whole community? What about rural believers, people who could not read, or people held as slaves? That is a real, open question. This world's own record cannot answer it from the inside. The whole-community channel itself is well attested: baptism, the shared meal, the yearly fasts. What that channel actually felt like from within it is not, and this world's own record does not pretend otherwise.

Voices

The people behind this account

Origen (c. 185–c. 253/4, Tyre, after imprisonment and torture under Decius - 'At this time Origen died, being sixty-nine years of age')

Origen was this world's greatest and most contested teacher. He took over the school as a very young man, after his own father was martyred. As a youth he acted on a hard verse from Matthew far too literally, and his bishop, Demetrius, first admired him for it, then later used the same act against him. Origen wrote commentaries across almost all of Scripture, argued at length with pagan critics, and was finally imprisoned and tortured under the emperor Decius. He died of it a few years later.

Athanasius (c. 296-298–373)

Athanasius was bishop of Alexandria for forty-six years, through five separate exiles. He defended one confession under real threat: the Son is truly God, of one substance with the Father. Arius had called the Son merely the highest creature. Near the end of this world's story, Athanasius put the hope of that confession into one line: God became human so that humans could share God's own life. He also wrote the best-known portrait of Antony, the desert's founding figure, holding him up as this city's own picture of a formed life.

Dionysius (c. 264/5)

Dionysius studied under Origen before becoming bishop himself. He led the church through two of this world's hardest tests: the Decian persecution and a citywide plague. He wrote that his own people nursed the dying without fear, while others in the city fled. When whole villages in the Egyptian countryside took up a teaching he thought was wrong, he did not condemn them from a distance. He traveled out, sat with them for three days, and won them back through patient argument.

Didymus (c. 309-313–c. 398)

Didymus was blind from the age of four, too young to have ever read a written word himself. He held the whole of Scripture in memory, and taught from it for decades. Visitors came from far away to hear him. Later tradition remembers him as the last head of this world's teaching line, closing out its final generation.

Documented stories

Stories worth telling in full

Nursing the Sick While Others Fled

c. 260 (a plague year in Alexandria)

When the pestilence came, one bishop wrote of his own people who stayed to nurse the dying - and of a city that had shown them little kindness in return.

Read the full account

When the great sickness came to Alexandria, about the year 260, every house had its dead. Dionysius, the bishop, wrote to the churches at festival time. He did not write of victory over the disease. He wrote that most of the brothers and sisters had not run: they visited the sick without fear, tended them day after day, and served them in Christ. Many caught the sickness from those they nursed and died of it - and he wrote that they died most joyfully, as if drawing their neighbors' pain onto themselves. The pagans, he said in the same letter, thrust away their own sick at the first sign of illness and fled even from their dearest. The church buried its dead with open hands. That difference - not an argument, a practice - was what the city saw.

Three Days of Argument in the Villages

c. 255 (after Bishop Nepos's death)

A bishop travels to villages that loved a teaching he thought was wrong, and stays three days until the argument is settled in the open, not from a distance.

Read the full account

In the villages of the Arsinoite district, the churches followed the teaching of Nepos, a bishop they loved. He had written a book against the allegorists. The promises in Scripture, he said, meant a thousand-year kingdom of plenty on this earth, plainly read. Nepos had died, and his book ruled whole congregations. Dionysius did not send a condemnation. He went out to the villages. He called together the elders and the teachers, with any of the people who wished to come, and sat with them for three days, morning to evening, examining the book argument by argument. He wrote afterward that he loved Nepos - his faith, his hard work in the Scriptures, his psalms - 'but the truth should be loved and honored most of all.' He wrote that he rejoiced in the brethren's constancy, sincerity, and willingness to reconsider, as they weighed the arguments together, point by point. The leader of the other side, Coracion, was persuaded, and said so in front of everyone. Some of the other brethren, the account says, were glad at the spirit of agreement all had shown: it ended not in a split, but in agreement reached in the open.

Origen's Youthful Act, and How His Bishop Later Used It

c. 205 (the act itself); reused against him after his ordination, c. 230s

A young teacher takes a hard verse about self-denial too literally. His bishop first admires him for it, then turns the same act against him years later.

Read the full account

While Origen was still young and teaching the faith in Alexandria, he did something Eusebius calls both immature and a proof of unusual faith. He took a hard verse from Matthew - about those who "have made themselves eunuchs for the kingdom of heaven's sake" - in the most literal sense possible, and acted on it himself. He taught men and women together, day and night, and wanted no one to accuse him of wrongdoing because of it. He tried to keep the act secret. He could not. When his bishop, Demetrius, learned of it, he admired the young man's daring and his faith, and urged him to keep teaching. Years later, once Origen had grown famous far beyond Alexandria, the same Demetrius turned that same youthful act against him: he wrote to bishops everywhere denouncing it as foolish, at the same time using Origen's ordination by the bishops of Caesarea and Jerusalem - given without Demetrius's own consent - as fresh grounds for attack. Origen himself later judged the verse differently, and wrote against reading it so literally.

Legacy

What came after

Two things carried past this world's own horizon, in different ways. The first was a settled inheritance. This world passed on the Nicene confession, the hope of sharing God's own life, and Scripture read for a deeper meaning beneath its surface. Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nazianzus, teachers of a later generation, gathered an anthology of Origen's own writing while this world's own record was still open. The second thing was a live argument, not a settled one. Origen's boldest ideas kept being read, loved, and contested by turns. Interested translators carried them on both sides. The argument finally broke into open controversy right at this world's own edge.

In its own record

How this world relates to others

This world's own relationship to the desert monks who came after it is real, but not settled. Real transmission toward the desert is well attested: a shared vocabulary of contemplation and ascent. One stronger claim is not established: that the desert's own way of life simply continues Alexandria's own formation logic. That claim is disputed. This world's own record does not resolve it alone.

Sources

What this account is built from

This world's record is richest in teaching and argument: the works of Clement, Origen, and Athanasius above all. It thins in a few important places. Clement's fullest treatment of marriage survives only in his own Greek; no public-domain English version has ever been made of it. Whether Alexandria's teaching tradition was one continuous, official school is itself live scholarly dispute, not something that can simply be reported as settled fact. And no woman's own words survive anywhere in this world's corpus.

  • Clement of Alexandria (c. 150-215 CE), Stromateis (Miscellanies), Books I-II and IV-VIII in English; Book III present in Latin only Clement's flagship work for cultivated Greek readers - though its third book, on marriage and the body, survives in this edition only in Latin, not English.
  • Origen (c. 185-254 CE), De Principiis (On First Principles) - English of Rufinus's Latin translation, with its known doctrinal softening Origen's boldest speculative work, reaching us only through Rufinus's own softened Latin translation - read alongside the Greek-derived Philocalia where the two overlap.
  • Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 260-339 CE) - NOT an Alexandrian voice: a secondary narrative source writing from Caesarea with a Constantinian-apologetic project, Historia Ecclesiastica (Church History) - HIGH author-gravity risk on institutional/succession/biographical claims (the tidy school head-succession is his construction); materially better for his verbatim quotations of documents The backbone story for this world's own people and events. Written a century later from Caesarea, with its own agenda. It is strongest where it quotes real documents, and weakest on tidy lists of who held office when.

In this world's own words

A few terms and lines worth knowing

Question: If a boy of seven, or a grown man who is still a catechumen, is present at the offering and eats of it without knowing better, what should be done? Answer: Let him be illuminated - that is, baptized - for he has been called by God. Question: Should a woman who has just given birth keep the Paschal fast? Answer: No. Question: Is a sick man required to keep the Paschal fast? Answer: No. Question: If a clergyman is called to celebrate a marriage and hears that it is incestuous, should he comply and perform the offering? Answer: No; he must not share in other men's sins.

Timothy, bishop of Alexandria (d. 385), answering questions put to him
Logos
The Word: God's own reason and speech, through whom all things were made, and who became flesh in Jesus.
allegoria (the spiritual sense)
Reading Scripture at more than one level: the plain sense, and deeper senses about Christ and the soul.
theosis
Becoming like God: sharing in God's own life, as far as a creature can. Not becoming a god.
katechesis
The teaching given to people preparing for baptism: step by step, over a long time.
paideia
God's way of raising souls: he teaches through Scripture, through teachers, and even through hardship.
