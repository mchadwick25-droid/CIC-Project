The Early Church Era · c. 150–400 CE · Alexandria, Egypt

Alexandrian Christianity

Alexandrian Catechetical Tradition

Open for conversation — you can sit down with this tradition now.

In Alexandria, from about 150 to 400 CE, teachers led seekers through a shared reading of Scripture. They read for a deeper meaning beneath the surface, one that opened onto the Logos himself. This teaching reached the literate few. Ordinary believers met the same faith another way — through baptism, the shared meal, and the yearly round of fasts and feasts. Some call this one continuous school, led by one teacher after another. Others say it was simply a series of private teachers, grouped together only in hindsight. When plague struck the city, Bishop Dionysius wrote to his own church, praising his people for nursing the sick and burying the dead. Others, he wrote, fled the city instead. Persecution could reach the school's own teachers, not only the wider community. Origen himself was imprisoned and tortured under the emperor Decius, and he died a few years later. It had already touched his boyhood, too. His father was martyred in an earlier persecution, when Origen was still just a boy.

Voices

The people behind this account

Origen (c. 185–c. 253/4, Tyre, after imprisonment and torture under Decius: 'At this time Origen died, being sixty-nine years of age')

Origen was this world's greatest and most contested teacher. He took over the school as a very young man, after his own father was martyred. As a youth he acted on a hard verse from Matthew far too literally, and his bishop, Demetrius, first admired him for it, then later used the same act against him. Origen wrote commentaries across almost all of Scripture, argued at length with pagan critics, and was finally imprisoned and tortured under the emperor Decius. He died of it a few years later.

Athanasius (c. 296-298–373)

Athanasius was bishop of Alexandria for forty-six years, through five separate exiles. He defended one confession under real threat: the Son is truly God, of one substance with the Father. Arius had called the Son merely the highest creature. Near the end of this world's story, Athanasius put the hope of that confession into one line: God became human so that humans could share God's own life. He also wrote the best-known portrait of Antony, the desert's founding figure, holding him up as this city's own picture of a formed life.

Dionysius (c. 264/5)

Dionysius studied under Origen before becoming bishop himself. He led the church through two of this world's hardest tests: the Decian persecution and a citywide plague. He wrote that his own people nursed the dying without fear, while others in the city fled. When whole villages in the Egyptian countryside took up a teaching he thought was wrong, he did not condemn them from a distance. He traveled out, sat with them for three days, and won them back through patient argument.

Didymus (c. 309-313–c. 398)

Didymus was blind from the age of four, too young to have ever read a written word himself. He held the whole of Scripture in memory, and taught from it for decades. Visitors came from far away to hear him. Later tradition remembers him as the last head of this world's teaching line, closing out its final generation.

The bishop taught the young man and baptized him. Then he relaxed his watch. The young man fell in with idle companions, and then with worse ones. At last he ran to the hills and became chief of a band of robbers.

Years later John came back and asked the bishop to return what he had left in his keeping. The bishop wept. The young man was dead to God, he said, a robber on the mountain. The old apostle tore his clothes, called for a horse and rode out to the mountain himself.

When the young man saw him, he fled. John forgot his age and rode after him. He begged his child not to run from his own father, old and unarmed. There was still hope of life for him, John said, and he would answer for him to Christ.

The young man stopped, threw down his weapons and wept. John led him back, prayed with him and fasted beside him in the struggle. He did not leave until he had restored him to the church.

Logic came first, to make thinking honest. Next came the natural world, geometry and the stars, until the students' sense of wonder was trained. Then came ethics, formed as habits, with the teacher's own life teaching as well. Only then came theology. They read everything the philosophers and poets had said of God, with Scripture at the center.

Under all of it, Gregory says, something else was happening. Love was kindled in his inmost soul, like a spark falling there. It was love for the holy Word, and for this man who was the Word's friend. Gregory gave up the law. When at last he had to leave, he called it being driven out of paradise.

By then Didymus was old. What others took in with their eyes, he had taken in through his ears and held on to. He interpreted Scripture word by word. Palladius calls him the blind author. Jerome, who studied with him briefly, says, writing in 392 or 393, that he had already passed his eighty-third year.

Later tradition called him the seer, a name set against his blindness. Most of the books he wrote are lost to us, and only fragments were recovered, centuries later.

He died at the age of eighty-five.

Legacy

What came after

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Two things carried past this world's own horizon, in different ways. The first was a settled inheritance. This world passed on the Nicene confession, the hope of sharing God's own life, and Scripture read for a deeper meaning beneath its surface. Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nazianzus, teachers of a later generation, gathered an anthology of Origen's own writing while this world's own record was still open. The second thing was a live argument, not a settled one. Origen's boldest ideas kept being read, loved, and contested by turns. Interested translators carried them on both sides. The argument finally broke into open controversy right at this world's own edge.

In its own record

How this world relates to others

This world's own relationship to the desert monks who came after it is real, but not settled. Real transmission toward the desert is well attested: a shared vocabulary of contemplation and ascent. One stronger claim is not established: that the desert's own way of life simply continues Alexandria's own formation logic. That claim is disputed. This world's own record does not resolve it alone.

Sources

What this account is built from

This world's record is richest in teaching and argument: the works of Clement, Origen, and Athanasius above all. It thins in a few important places. Clement's fullest treatment of marriage survives only in his own Greek; no public-domain English version has ever been made of it. Whether Alexandria's teaching tradition was one continuous, official school is itself live scholarly dispute, not something that can simply be reported as settled fact. And no woman's own words survive anywhere in this world's corpus.

  • Clement of Alexandria, Stromateis (Miscellanies), trans. William Wilson, Ante-Nicene Fathers vol. 2 (1885) Clement's flagship work for cultivated Greek readers - though its third book, on marriage and the body, survives in this edition only in Latin, not English.
  • Origen, De Principiis (On First Principles), trans. Frederick Crombie, Ante-Nicene Fathers vol. 4 (1885) Origen's boldest speculative work, reaching us only through Rufinus's own softened Latin translation - read alongside the Greek-derived Philocalia where the two overlap.
  • Eusebius of Caesarea, Historia Ecclesiastica (Church History), trans. Arthur Cushman McGiffert, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers ser. 2 vol. 1 (1890) The backbone story for this world's own people and events. Written a century later from Caesarea, with its own agenda. Logos
The Word: God's own reason and speech, through whom all things were made, and who became flesh in Jesus.
allegoria (the spiritual sense)
Reading Scripture at more than one level: the plain sense, and deeper senses about Christ and the soul.
theosis
Becoming like God: sharing in God's own life, as far as a creature can. Athanasius wrote that the Word became man that we might be made God.
katechesis
The teaching given to people preparing for baptism: step by step, over a long time.
paideia
God's way of raising souls: he teaches through Scripture, through teachers, and even through hardship.
