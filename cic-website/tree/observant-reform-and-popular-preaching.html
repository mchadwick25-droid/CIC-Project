About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

The Observance began as an argument inside the religious orders: that the Rule should be kept as it was written, not as generations of dispensation had relaxed it. It started small, with Paoluccio Trinci's handful of friars at Brugliano in 1368, and spread across the Franciscans, Dominicans, Augustinians and Benedictines alike, until reformed houses formed rival provinces alongside the unreformed ones. Its public face was preaching. Bernardino of Siena drew whole towns into the piazza for sermon cycles that ran for weeks. The forty-five sermons he preached at Siena in 1427 were taken down as he spoke by a listener in the crowd, asides and audience reactions included, which is about as close as anyone gets to hearing a medieval sermon rather than reading one. John of Capistrano did the same across central Europe. The movement's darker side belongs in the same account: Capistrano's preaching also drove the persecution of Jewish communities, most notoriously at Breslau in 1453.

In Its Own Record

Sources

  • Bernardino of Siena, the 1427 Siena sermon cycle (reportatio of Benedetto di maestro Bartolomeo; standard ed. Delcorno, 1989, Italian) (primary) — Forty-five vernacular sermons taken down as they were spoken, audience reactions included - close to a live recording of a medieval sermon. English translation exists only in selections.
  • John of Capistrano: letters, treatises, and eyewitness accounts of the 1451-56 central-European preaching tours (primary) — Mass preaching at Vienna, Nuremberg, and Breslau. This includes the movement's darkest episode: at Breslau in 1453, Capistrano led the trial and burning of Jews on host-desecration charges. Those trial records are testimony extracted under coercion by the persecuting side, not neutral evidence. Largely untranslated (Acta Sanctorum; Hofer).
  • The Observance as structure: Observant constitutions and statutes (e.g. the Martinian constitutions, 1430), vicariate records, Bernardino's Latin model-sermon collections (primary) — The movement's institutional self-organization across Franciscan, Dominican, Augustinian, and Benedictine families. Latin editions only.
  • The reform's women in their own words: Colette of Corbie's Constitutions for the Poor Clares; Caterina Vigri of Bologna, The Seven Spiritual Weapons (primary) — The Observance's female branch as authors, not subjects - a woman legislating a reform, and an Observant Poor Clare's own formation book.
  • James Mixson & Bert Roest (eds.), A Companion to Observant Reform in the Late Middle Ages and Beyond (Brill, 2015) (secondary) — Current standard, cross-order by design. Treats Savonarola within the Observant frame - on his own he would not fit a movement defined by a single person, since the Observance was always broader than any one figure.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 4, ch. 29 'Observant reform in religious orders' (Roest); cf. ch. 30 'Public purity and discipline' (Rusconi) (reference) — A dedicated chapter by the Companion's co-editor - a direct fit.
