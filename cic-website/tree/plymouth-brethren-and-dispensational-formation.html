About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In the late 1820s a handful of dissatisfied Christians in Dublin — among them a dentist, a lawyer and a young Anglican curate named John Nelson Darby — began meeting to break bread together with no ordained clergy, no denominational name and no set liturgy, and assemblies on the same pattern spread through Britain and then the world. Darby also worked out a way of reading the Bible that divided history into distinct dispensations, kept Israel and the church permanently separate, and expected Christ to remove the church before a final tribulation — a scheme almost unknown before him and enormously consequential after. Alongside the theology ran an equally characteristic practice. George Müller ran orphanages in Bristol for thousands of children on the principle of never asking anyone for money and publishing the accounts. In 1848 the movement split over how far assemblies were answerable to one another, into the Open and Exclusive Brethren, and both halves carried on.

In Its Own Record

Sources

  • J. N. Darby, Collected Writings (34 vols.) and letters; the 1848 Bethesda division documents (both sides' circulars) (primary) — Darby's corpus is vast and centers heavily on him personally, worth keeping distinct from the movement's own broader record. The 1848 division documents show the Open and Exclusive streams diverging and continuing on their own, beyond Darby himself.
  • George Muller, A Narrative of Some of the Lord's Dealings with George Muller (1837-) and the Bristol orphanage annual reports; Scriptural Knowledge Institution records (primary) — Muller's own bookkeeping of answered prayer, published as an ordinary-facing genre for a general readership.
  • Periodicals and assemblies: The Christian Witness (1834-); assembly minute books; A. N. Groves's memoir and 1836 letter to Darby; women's presence chiefly in hymnody (Frances Bevan) and missionary letters (Harriet Groves) (primary) — Groves's 1836 letter to Darby is the movement's own earliest internal critique. Women's presence in the surviving record is thin - itself a reflection of the restrictions the assemblies placed on women's public roles, not an absence of women in the movement.
  • Tim Grass, Gathering to His Name (Paternoster, 2006), with F. Roy Coad, A History of the Brethren Movement (1968) and Ernest Sandeen, The Roots of Fundamentalism (Chicago, 1970) (secondary) — Sandeen is the key study of dispensationalism's formation. Scofield's later reshaping of Darby's scheme belongs to the following era, not this one.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 8, 'The growth of voluntary religion' (Bebbington) (reference) — This reference volume has no dedicated chapter on the Brethren movement; this is the nearest topical fit.
