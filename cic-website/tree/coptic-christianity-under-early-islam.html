About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Egypt's miaphysite Christians had spent the century before the Arab conquest under Byzantine pressure for refusing the Council of Chalcedon. When Arab armies took Alexandria in 641-42 their patriarch Benjamin came out of hiding and was restored to his see. What followed was the long transition that made the Coptic church what it still is. No longer persecuted for its confession but taxed and governed as a protected minority, shifting gradually from Coptic into Arabic, and beginning the long, centuries-long drift toward minority status that conversions would eventually bring. Its own record of those years, the History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria, follows the church bishop by bishop with unusual care, though it says far more about patriarchs and taxes than about ordinary Egyptian Christians. The window ends with the persecution under the caliph al-Hakim in the early eleventh century, the harshest single episode the community had yet faced.

Sources

  • History of the Patriarchs of the Coptic Church of Alexandria (attrib. Severus ibn al-Muqaffa'; ed./trans. B. Evetts, Patrologia Orientalis) (primary) — The church's own institutional memory across the conquest and after. English translation free online at tertullian.org.
  • John of Nikiu, Chronicle (trans. R. H. Charles, 1916) (primary) — Miaphysite bishop's narrative of the 642 conquest itself. Ethiopic-via-Arabic transmission; translation available online.
  • Apocalypse of Pseudo-Samuel of Qalamun (primary) — Coptic-tradition lament over Arabization and language loss — rare interior texture for a window whose ordinary voice is otherwise thin.
  • Maged S. A. Mikhail, From Byzantine to Islamic Egypt (I.B. Tauris, 2014) (secondary) — Current standard monograph on exactly this transition (religion, identity, politics after the conquest).
  • Mark N. Swanson, The Coptic Papacy in Islamic Egypt, 641-1517 (AUC Press, 2010) (secondary) — Institution-focused companion built directly on the History of the Patriarchs.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity, vol. 3, ch. 9: 'Christians under Muslim rule' (Sidney H. Griffith) (reference)
