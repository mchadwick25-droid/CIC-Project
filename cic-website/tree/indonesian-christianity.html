About This Movement

Christianity had been present in the eastern Indonesian islands since the Portuguese and the Dutch East India Company, but the churches that matter most today were built in the nineteenth century and, very quickly, by Indonesians. German missionaries reached the Batak highlands of North Sumatra in 1861, where Ludwig Nommensen spent more than fifty years. The church that grew there gave itself a written order in 1881 and is now among the largest Lutheran bodies anywhere in the world. Further west, in Java, a Javanese teacher known as Kiai Sadrach gathered thousands of followers into congregations that kept Javanese forms, Javanese leadership and Javanese ways of teaching. Which the Dutch missions regarded with deep suspicion at the time, and which is now read as the archipelago's clearest early case of Christianity on local terms. Almost everything written about all of this in the period was written by the missions; the churches' own records are what let it be read the other way round.

Sources

  • The Batak church's own records: the HKBP 1881 church order, synod minutes, congregation registers; Batak teachers' and pastors' writings and church press; first evangelists' accounts (primary) — This is an indigenous church's own institutional record, growing first inside and then beyond the mission that planted it.
  • Mission records WITH gravity: Nommensen's letters and reports (Rheinische Missionsgesellschaft archives, Wuppertal); the Joseph Kam-era Moluccan records; Minahasa mission records (primary) — This material carries a double filter: it is mission-authored, and it operates inside the Dutch colonial administration even though the mission itself was German.
  • The indigenous-initiative leg: Kiai Sadrach's Javanese communities (c. 1870s-90s) - the mission's hostile-suspicious file AND the community's own practice and continuity (primary) — This combines the mission's own suspicious file on Sadrach with the community's own practice and continuity - both sides of the record. It is the clearest case in this period of Christianity built on indigenous terms.
  • Revival testimonies: Toba conversion-era and mass-movement testimonies (1880s-1900s, largely through mission transcription); the 1965-71 Timor revival flagged as past this era's window (primary) — The Toba conversion testimonies survive largely through mission transcription, a filter worth noting. The Timor revival of 1965-71 falls outside this era's time window and belongs to a later period of the same story.
  • Jan Aritonang & Karel Steenbrink (eds.), A History of Christianity in Indonesia (Brill, 2008), with Sutarman S. Partonadi, Sadrach's Community and Its Contextual Roots (Rodopi, 1990) (secondary) — The standard history, written largely by Indonesian scholars, together with the dedicated study recovering Sadrach's community.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 8 (chapter assignment hedged): nearest ch. 32 (Francisco, Filipino Christianity) and ch. 34 (Stanley) - both numbers verified (reference) — This reference volume has no dedicated chapter on Indonesia or the Netherlands Indies; these are the nearest topical fits.
