About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Some of the Reformation's most radical figures concluded that no visible church was worth joining at all. Sebastian Franck, a former priest turned printer and chronicler, held that the true church had been invisible since the apostles and that God's word was spoken inwardly to individuals of every nation. Hendrik Niclaes, a merchant in the Low Countries, went further and founded one. The Family of Love, a secretive fellowship whose members were expected to conform outwardly to whatever church their country required while holding their real convictions inside. Familists were hunted by royal proclamation in England in 1580. And precisely because they conformed so well, historians still argue about how many there were and how long they lasted.

In Its Own Record

Sources

  • Sebastian Franck: Chronica (1531), Letter to Campanus (in Williams & Mergal, Spiritual and Anabaptist Writers, LCC, 1957), 280 Paradoxes (trans. Furcha) (primary)
  • Hendrik Niclaes's own writings: Evangelium Regni, Terra Pacis, epistles and ordinances (English prints of the 1570s) (primary) — Hendrik Niclaes's own prophetic self-presentation — elected 'godded man,' his writings styled as scripture. This is the founder-shaped side of the movement's own evidence.
  • The schism record: Hendrik Jansen van Barrefelt ('Hiel') and his separate following after breaking with Niclaes (c. 1573); the Plantin circle's quiet adherence (primary) — Authority here was contested and the movement survived the contest — evidence that cuts both ways: a founder-shaped rupture, but also a fellowship that outlived it.
  • The English Familists: the 1580 proclamation, member examinations and recantations, Rogers's The Displaying of an horrible secte (1578); against these, wills, parish offices, and outward conformity of known Familist families persisting decades after Niclaes's death (Marsh's evidence base) (primary) — Both hostile (the polemic and the proclamation) and coerced (the examinations and recantations). The wills and parish-conformity records are the counter-evidence, shaped by the community's own structure: it was a community without its prophet, invisible by design, the same pattern seen in other communities that survived by staying unseen.
  • Alastair Hamilton, The Family of Love (1981), with Christopher Marsh, The Family of Love in English Society, 1550-1630 (CUP, 1994) (secondary) — Standard continental and English studies. Marsh is the community-evidence base.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 6, ch. 3 (McLaughlin, the radical Reformation) (reference)
