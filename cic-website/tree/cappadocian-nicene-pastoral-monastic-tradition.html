About This Movement

In Cappadocia and neighboring Pontus, from the years after the last empire-wide persecutions to the death of the last of a remarkable generation, one extended family and the friends around it did more than most to settle the words Christians still use about God — how settled that settlement really was is itself now a live scholarly question. Basil of Caesarea, his younger brother Gregory of Nyssa, their elder sister Macrina, and Basil's friend Gregory of Nazianzus argued the hardest question of the century — whether the Son and the Spirit are God in the same sense the Father is — while running dioceses, burying relatives, and feeding the hungry through famine.

Basil's own preaching indicted the region's grain-hoarders by name and built a hospital, hostel, and workhouse complex outside Caesarea after the famine of 368 — a genuine, near-unprecedented undertaking, though not, as later legend sometimes claims, the first of its kind anywhere. Before that, he had already spent years answering a more radical ascetic ferment loose in the region — households dissolved, slaves urged to leave their masters, fasts kept against the church's own feasts — not by condemning the impulse but by giving it a rule, an obedience, and an open door for guests instead. Macrina turned the family estate into an ascetic household of her own and, in her brother's account of her last days, taught him theology from her deathbed — the only record of her that survives; no words of her own reach us independently of his telling.

Gregory of Nazianzus carried the argument to Constantinople, where the creed most Christians still recite took its final shape in 381. The years were not peaceful throughout: this world buried its own martyr, Eupsychius of Caesarea, executed under Julian in 362, and endured a hostile imperial church under Valens that drove Gregory of Nyssa briefly into exile. Even the letter usually cited as proof the three men agreed on the technical vocabulary — Epistle 38 — is itself contested between Basil and Gregory of Nyssa in modern scholarship.

In Its Own Record

Formed From

  • The Anatolian Church in the Third Century — Basil's grandmother Macrina was formed by Gregory Thaumaturgus' disciples, and Basil appeals to his practice as precedent for what is old.

Formed or Influenced

  • Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity — Jerome heard Gregory of Nazianzus teach in Constantinople and calls him his teacher.
  • Anomoean (Eunomian) Christianity — Basil's Against Eunomius, Gregory of Nyssa's answer to Eunomius' reply, and Gregory of Nazianzus' Five Theological Orations are all directed at this party by name.
  • Pneumatomachian ('Macedonian') Christianity — Basil's On the Holy Spirit and Gregory of Nyssa's Against the Macedonians are both directed at this party.
  • Apollinarian Christianity — Gregory of Nazianzus' letters to Cledonius are the classic reply: what is not assumed is not healed.

Sources

  • Basil of Caesarea, Hexaemeron (primary) — Basil's nine surviving homilies on the six days of creation, preached to his congregation at Caesarea.
  • Gregory of Nazianzus, Five Theological Orations (primary) — Preached at Constantinople in 380, these carried the Trinitarian argument into the public square in the year before the creed took its final form.
  • Gregory of Nyssa, Against Eunomius (primary) — Gregory's twelve-book rebuttal — the fullest surviving statement of the Cappadocian case, and the route through which most of Eunomius's own words survive at all.
  • Eunomius, Apology, in Eunomius: The Extant Works, ed. and trans. Richard Paul Vaggione (primary) — Oxford, Clarendon Press, 1987 — the rare case of a condemned opponent's own writing surviving well enough to be read on its own terms, not only through the winning side's rebuttal.
  • The Canons of the Council of Gangra (primary) — Twenty canons censuring "those around Eustathius" — read against the grain, the best surviving evidence for what the radical ascetic ferment Basil answered actually did and taught.
  • Basil of Caesarea, the Eustathius-rupture letters (Epistles 119, 128, 130, 223, 226, 244, 251) (primary) — Basil's own account of his friendship, collaboration, and break with his one-time ascetic mentor — the wounded former disciple's side; Eustathius left no writing of his own to answer it.
  • Anthony Meredith, The Cappadocians (secondary) — St Vladimir's Seminary Press, 1995 — the first English-language study to treat Basil and both Gregorys together.
