About This Movement

On opposite sides of the Appalachians, two sets of Presbyterians came independently to the same conclusion. That the creeds and denominational names Christians used to define themselves were the very things keeping them apart. Barton W. Stone and his colleagues dissolved their own presbytery in 1804 with a document called the Last Will and Testament of the Springfield Presbytery. Five years later Thomas Campbell published the Declaration and Address, and with his son Alexander built a movement around the plea for no creed but the Bible and no name but Christian. In 1832 the two groups shook hands and merged. Debating was their native genre — Alexander Campbell argued in public for days at a stretch against sceptics, Presbyterians and a Roman Catholic bishop, and published the transcripts himself — and by 1906, when the federal religious census listed the Churches of Christ separately for the first time, a movement founded to end division had recorded a division of its own.

In Its Own Record

Formed or Influenced

  • Churches of Christ — The a cappella wing of the Stone-Campbell movement, formed at the division the 1906 U.S. religious census recorded in its separate Churches of Christ listing. This was a formation, not a continuation: a single identity can't split into two separate successors.
  • Disciples of Christ & the Independent Christian Churches — The Disciples wing of the same 1906 division, on the same verified federal-census listing. No continuesAs write, for the same reason as its sibling edge..

Sources

  • Founding documents: Last Will and Testament of the Springfield Presbytery (1804); Thomas Campbell, Declaration and Address (1809) (primary) — These founding documents state the movement's 'no creed but the Bible' position in its own words.
  • The journals (native genre per census): Christian Baptist (1823-30), Millennial Harbinger (1830-70), Stone's Christian Messenger (1826-45) (primary) — These journals were the movement's own periodicals. Stone's reservations about how the doctrine of the Trinity was worded appear in the Messenger's exchanges.
  • Debate transcripts: Campbell-Walker (1820), Campbell-McCalla (1823), Campbell-Owen (1829), Campbell-Purcell (1837), Campbell-Rice (1843) (primary) — Public debate was one of this movement's characteristic genres, and these transcripts were published verbatim by the movement itself.
  • Congregational minute books; Stone's 1847 autobiography; pioneer-preacher memoir literature ('Raccoon' John Smith biography) (primary) — Treat the memoir material as retrospective and hagiographic - written well after the fact and admiring in tone. Black congregations from this era are documented mainly through white church minutes, a real filter on the record.
  • Foster, Blowers et al. (eds.), The Encyclopedia of the Stone-Campbell Movement (Eerdmans, 2004), with Richard Hughes, Reviving the Ancient Faith (Eerdmans, 1996) (secondary)
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 8, ch. 22 (Noll), with 'The growth of voluntary religion' (Bebbington) (reference) — The Bebbington chapter's exact number in this volume could not be confirmed.
