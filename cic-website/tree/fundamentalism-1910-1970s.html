About This Movement

Read by a synthesized voice — not a recording, not a re-enactment.

The word arrived in 1920: Curtis Lee Laws, writing in the Watchman-Examiner, proposed calling those willing to do 'battle royal for the Fundamentals' fundamentalists, and the name stuck. The movement was older than the word. The Niagara Bible Conference had met annually from the 1870s until 1897 and adopted a fourteen-point creed in 1878; A. A. Hodge and B. B. Warfield published 'Inspiration' in the Presbyterian Review in April 1881, giving the doctrine of scripture its most exacting American statement. Between 1910 and 1915 twelve volumes called The Fundamentals were published, financed by Lyman Stewart and given away — around three million copies. The Presbyterian General Assembly adopted a five-point doctrinal deliverance in 1910. Then came the public defeats and the separations: the Scopes trial in July 1925, Princeton Seminary reorganized in 1929 with Westminster founded in answer, and new denominations of their own from the 1930s.
