About This Movement

Alexandria was the ancient world's great city of libraries and lecture halls, and Christianity there took the same shape: a school. Teachers received enquirers, catechumens preparing for baptism, and educated pagans curious about this new philosophy, and walked them through Scripture as a text with a surface and a depth: the letter first, then the moral sense, then the spiritual meaning that opens onto the Logos himself.

Clement wrote for cultivated Greeks who wanted to know whether a Christian could keep his learning. Origen, who succeeded him, wrote commentaries across most of Scripture and produced the Hexapla, a six-column parallel edition of the Old Testament assembled to see what the Hebrew actually said — before falling out with his bishop and moving his teaching to Caesarea around 231. Historians still argue about how formal the whole thing was — whether Alexandria had one continuous institution with a succession of appointed heads, as Eusebius later described it, or a looser sequence of private Christian schools whose teachers happened to be remembered together. That tidy succession comes almost entirely from Eusebius: Clement's own writings never once describe a formal school that he headed.

The allegorical way of reading Scripture is this tradition's best-known signature, but it was never uncontested even at home. Nepos, a bishop out in the Egyptian countryside at Arsinoe, wrote a book refuting the allegorists and won real support for a plainer, literal reading — until Dionysius, by then bishop of Alexandria, spent three days in patient public conversation with Nepos's own community and brought them back around.

In Its Own Record

Formed From

  • Post-Apostolic Household-Church Christianity — Egyptian Christianity's origins are obscure in detail, but its second-century emergence out of the same scattered network is broadly accepted.
  • The Second-Century Greek Apologists — Justin's argument that the Word had sown seeds of truth among the philosophers is what Clement and Origen take much further.

Formed or Influenced

  • Desert Monasticism — The desert movement grew in Alexandria’s shadow; Athanasius of Alexandria wrote the Life of Antony that carried it to the world.
  • Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity — Origen's textual scholarship (the Hexapla) is the direct ancestor of Jerome's Hebrew-testing project — even as Jerome turned publicly against Origen's theology.
  • Coptic Christianity under Early Islam — …and of Alexandria's own catechetical tradition.
  • The Anatolian Church in the Third Century — Gregory studied five years under Origen at Caesarea and wrote the Panegyric on leaving; Methodius, a generation later, became Origen's first serious critic.
  • The Church of Roman Palestine before Constantine — Origen moved to Caesarea around 231 and taught there for twenty years; the library grew from the books he brought.
  • The Church of Aquileia — Rufinus translated Origen into Latin, and for several works his version is the only text that survives.

Sources

  • Clement of Alexandria, Stromata (Miscellanies) (primary) — Clement's case, addressed to educated Greek readers, that philosophy had served as a tutor leading toward Christ.
  • Origen, De Principiis (On First Principles) (primary) — The first attempt by a Christian writer at a systematic account of the faith; some of its speculative proposals were condemned by a church council three centuries after Origen's death.
  • Eusebius, Church History, Book VI (primary) — The source of the traditional picture of Alexandria as one continuous school with a succession of appointed heads — the very account modern scholars now question.
  • Gustave Bardy, "Aux origines de l'École d'Alexandrie" (reference) — The 1932 study that first argued, against Eusebius's tidy succession-list, that Alexandria's teaching looked more like a sequence of private Christian tutors than one formal institution — the opening move in a scholarly debate that remains unsettled today.
