About This Movement

At a small monastery in the forest near Kozelsk, south of Moscow, a hermitage founded in 1821 became the centre of something Russian Orthodoxy had never had at this scale. Elders — startsy — who kept the door open to anyone who walked up to it. Peasants, students, landowners and novelists queued outside the cell of Father Ambrose, and the elders who could not see everyone answered by letter, leaving thousands of pages of spiritual direction addressed not to monks but to ordinary people trying to live ordinary lives. They were also publishers: from the 1840s, with the Slavophile thinker Ivan Kireevsky, Optina ran a press that put the Greek ascetic fathers into Russian and made a monastic literature available to laypeople for the first time. Dostoevsky came in 1878 after the death of his small son, and turned what he found there into the elder Zosima.

In Its Own Record

Sources

  • The elders' letters: Macarius of Optina (Eng. sel. Russian Letters of Spiritual Direction, trans. de Beausobre, 1944); Ambrose of Optina's letters; Leonid; the skete's records (primary) — Thousands of letters of monastic direction addressed to ordinary lay people. Most of this correspondence remains untranslated from Russian.
  • The Philokalia-in-Russian line: Paisius's Slavonic Dobrotolubiye (1793); Optina's own publishing enterprise (from 1846, with Kireevsky); Theophan the Recluse's Russian Dobrotolubiye (1877-89) and letters; Brianchaninov, The Arena (primary) — This shows the renewal as a translation and publishing effort, with the elders themselves working as editors and translators.
  • The lay side: The Way of a Pilgrim (Kazan, 1880s); John of Kronstadt, My Life in Christ; pilgrims' memoirs of Optina (Leontiev) (primary) — The Way of a Pilgrim's author is unknown, and its textual history is debated among scholars.
  • The Dostoevsky-adjacent layer: The Brothers Karamazov's Zosima (1880, after the 1878 visit to Ambrose); Kireevsky/Slavophile essays; the Tolstoy visits (primary) — This is the renewal's literary and cultural afterlife - Dostoevsky's fiction and the Slavophile essays it inspired - rather than firsthand documentation of the movement itself.
  • Irina Paert, Spiritual Elders: Charisma and Tradition in Russian Orthodoxy (Northern Illinois, 2010), with Leonard Stanton, The Optina Pustyn Monastery in the Russian Literary Imagination (1995) (secondary)
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 5, ch. 15 'The Russian Orthodox church in imperial Russia 1721-1917' (Dixon) (reference)
