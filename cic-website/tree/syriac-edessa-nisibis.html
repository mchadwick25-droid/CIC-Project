About This Movement

The first hard evidence of a Christian community at Edessa is a flood: a church building destroyed when the river overran its banks in 201, recorded in the city's own chronicle. Edessa's Christians told an older story about their beginning too—that King Abgar himself had written to Jesus and been answered—but no text earlier than the fourth century attests it, and it is best heard as the community's own cherished memory, not as history. What the record actually shows is plainer. They were a Christian people who spoke Syriac, close to the language Jesus himself spoke, and who read Scripture as a web of symbols answering each other: Eden answering the cross, the Jordan answering the font. They met the Gospel as one continuous story rather than four separate books, in a harmony a Greek-trained convert named Tatian had woven a generation earlier; Aphrahat quoted it and Ephrem preached from it for as long as this world lasted.

At the community's ascetic heart stood the covenant: men and women who vowed themselves to lifelong celibacy while remaining inside their own congregation instead of leaving for the desert, keeping the fast and the vigil and singing what the community believed. Office and vow ran side by side for this whole world's life, and who finally spoke for it was never settled. Aphrahat, remembered simply as the Persian sage, once drafted a synodal letter in the name of a council of bishops, yet whether he held that office himself, no one now can say. Persia's own bishops fought the same question in the open: Papa of Seleucia-Ctesiphon claimed to lead them all, and was contested by his own colleagues in his own lifetime. The community also knew its faith by naming what it was not, answering by name the teachings of the philosopher Bardaisan, of Marcion, and of Mani, all preached in the same cities in the same tongue.

In 338, Shapur II of Persia besieged Nisibis; Ephrem, the city's own hymnwriter, remembered its deliverance as an act of God worked through ordinary defenders, while a later account added a swarm of gnats to the story. Bishop Jacob led that defense. The Chronicle of Edessa places his death that same year; Theodoret's own church history instead has him defending the city again in 350, and the record itself never closes the question. Nisibis held until 363, when the Roman emperor Jovian ceded it to Persia and Ephrem moved west to Edessa, where he spent his last year running famine relief before he died. On the Persian side, the persecution already underway since the 340s took bishop Simeon bar Sabbae and his clergy, and left the church's chief seat empty for twenty years, since filling it meant near-certain death. Not until 410, when the Roman bishop Marutha brokered a synod with the favor of Yazdegerd I, the Persian king, did the Persian church finally receive a single settled order—the last thing this world's own record has to say.

In Its Own Record

Formed From

  • Post-Apostolic Household-Church Christianity — Aramaic-speaking Christianity reached Edessa early, but by what route and hands scholars genuinely disagree.

Formed or Influenced

  • The Church of the East under Persia — The School of Nisibis and the Persian church carry this tradition's teaching directly onward after 410.
  • Bardaisan and the Bardaisanites — Ephraim's Prose Refutations answer Bardaisan at length, and are the reason most of his position survives.

Sources

  • Aphrahat, Select Demonstrations (primary) — Twenty-three dated letters written from inside the Persian empire, 336–345; the earliest firmly dated source for the covenant order and, in Demonstration 21, for the persecution itself.
  • Ephrem the Syrian, Nisibene Hymns (primary) — Hymns composed for the city Ephrem served until its surrender to Persia in 363, and for Edessa after his move there.
  • Tatian, The Diatessaron (primary) — A single continuous gospel woven from all four, used as the standard gospel text in Syriac churches for centuries.
  • Bardaisan of Edessa, The Book of the Laws of Divers Countries (primary) — The free-will dialogue, recorded by his student Philip; the one near-primary witness to the court philosopher Ephrem's own hymns were written to answer.
  • Eusebius, Church History I.13, IV.29, IV.30 (primary) — The earliest datable witness to the Abgar legend (as a document Eusebius himself accepted, which this world's own record does not); also the earliest Greek notices of Tatian's harmony and Bardaisan's dialogues.
  • The Chronicle of Edessa (primary) — A sober civic-and-church record, compiled c. 540s from archives; its year-201 flood entry is the earliest hard evidence of a Christian community at Edessa.
  • The Doctrine of Addai (tradition) — This world's own fuller telling of its Abgar-and-Addai foundation story — its self-understanding, not a historical record.
  • Theodoret, Ecclesiastical History II.26 (primary) — The remembered story of Bishop Jacob's prayer and the siege of Nisibis, written a century after the event.
  • Sozomen, Ecclesiastical History II.9–14, III.16 (primary) — The fullest surviving narrative of Shapur II's persecution of the Persian church, and an independent notice of Ephrem's famine relief in his last year.
  • Palladius, Lausiac History, ch. 40 (Ephraim) (primary) — The fullest account of Ephrem's famine relief and death, gathered about fifty years after the fact.
  • "Bardesanes and Bardesanites", Catholic Encyclopedia (reference) — Background on the Edessan philosopher-poet whose cosmology Ephrem's own hymns were written to refute.
  • Ute Possekel, "Bardaisan of Edessa: Philosopher or Theologian?", Zeitschrift für Antikes Christentum 10.3 (2006) (reference) — The strongest non-hostile modern reconstruction; even on this reading, Bardaisan's own cosmology likely diverges from bodily resurrection specifically.
  • Sebastian Brock, The Luminous Eye: The Spiritual World Vision of Saint Ephrem the Syrian (secondary) — Cistercian Studies 124, 1985 — by the scholar generally regarded as the leading authority on Ephrem and Syriac Christianity writing in English.
