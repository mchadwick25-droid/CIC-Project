About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In the 1650s Patriarch Nikon set out to correct the Russian service books and rituals against contemporary Greek practice — two fingers in the sign of the cross became three, the spelling of Jesus's name changed, processions reversed direction. And a great part of the Russian church refused. To them the old rite was not a detail but the faith itself, handed down and now being altered by a church they concluded had fallen; the council of 1666-67 anathematized them, and the state hunted them for generations. Their great voice was Archpriest Avvakum, who wrote his own life story from an underground prison at Pustozersk before he was burned in 1682 — the first sustained autobiography in Russian, plain-spoken, furious and unexpectedly funny. Cut off from bishops, the movement divided between those who found priests and those who concluded the priesthood had ended altogether, and survived in the far north, the forests and Siberia, in some cases by mass self-immolation rather than submission.

In Its Own Record

Sources

  • Archpriest Avvakum: The Life Written by Himself (ed./trans. Brostrom, Michigan Slavic, 1979; new trans. Gluck & Brostrom, Columbia Russian Library, 2021), with his Pustozersk letters and petitions (primary) — A first-rank inside voice, written in an underground earthen prison, 1669-75.
  • Boyarynia Feodosia Morozova: Avvakum's letters to her and the Tale of Boiarynia Morozova (trans. in Ziolkowski's edition, 2000) (primary) — A noblewoman starved to death for the old rite (1675); women were numerically central in the priestless communities.
  • The communities' own layer: the Solovki petitions (1667) and siege record (1668-76); the Vyg community - Pomorskie otvety (1723, bespopovtsy self-defense), Semyon Denisov's Vinograd Rossiiskii and Solovki-fathers history; popovtsy settlement records (Vetka, Kerzhenets); community rules (primary) — Denisov's work follows the martyrology genre, so read it with that framing in mind. The division between priestly and priestless communities appears directly in the sources. Largely available only in untranslated Russian editions.
  • The establishment record: the 1666-67 Moscow council's acts and anathemas, state persecution decrees (1685 articles), interrogation records (primary) — The schism documented by its persecutors; testimony given under threat of death, so treat it as coerced evidence.
  • Robert Crummey, The Old Believers and the World of Antichrist (Wisconsin, 1970) and Old Believers in a Changing World (2011), with Georg Michels, At War with the Church (Stanford, 1999) (secondary) — Michels complicates the movement's early coherence. The later merchant records referenced elsewhere are 19th-century, later than this movement's founding period.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 5, ch. 13 'Eastern Orthodoxy in Russia and Ukraine in the age of the Counter-Reformation', with 'The Russian Orthodox Church in imperial Russia 1721-1917' (Dixon) (reference) — The Dixon chapter's number is inconsistent across listings - cited as ch. 14 in some, ch. 15 in others.
