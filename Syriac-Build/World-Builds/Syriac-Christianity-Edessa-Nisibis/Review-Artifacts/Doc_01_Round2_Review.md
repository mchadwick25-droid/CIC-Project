# Independent Adversarial Review — Doc_01, Round 2 (World #7: Syriac Christianity, Edessa/Nisibis)

## (a) Verification of NEW Round 2 Factual Claims

**Papa bar Aggai / Simeon bar Sabbae dates and roles** — Broadly accurate but the document's framing is materially incomplete in a way that undercuts its own argument. Papa bar Aggai (d. c.327/328) did serve as bishop of Seleucia-Ctesiphon c.310s–329 and is the first credited with the title "Catholicos," and Simeon bar Sabbae did succeed him and was martyred on Good Friday, most likely 344 (dates in scholarship range 341–345). These dates are essentially right. **But** independent sources (Wikipedia, GEDSH, ResearchGate on "Resemanticizing Memory: the Conflict between Papa bar Aggai and Miles of Susa") show Papa's claim to primacy over other Persian bishops was **fiercely contested by other Persian bishops themselves** — a synod at Seleucia (c.315) was convened specifically to challenge Papa's authority, led by Miles of Susa and Aqib-Alaha, Bishop of Karka d'Baith Slok, with Papa refusing to submit to the synod's judgment. This is a serious complication the document nowhere mentions: Persia's "developing episcopal hierarchy" wasn't a smoothly parallel, uncontested structure analogous to the Roman-Edessene episcopal system — it was itself locally contested by other Persian bishops in exactly the same generation. That actually cuts against the document's clean "Persia had its own episcopal hierarchy, so no real structural asymmetry" argument, or at minimum should have been surfaced and weighed.

**"Mar Aqiba" as Aphrahat's leadership title** — **Not corroborated.** Extensive searching across Wikipedia, GEDSH, New World Encyclopedia, Britannica, and specialist Aphrahat scholarship turns up no attestation of "Mar Aqiba" as any title held by or associated with Aphrahat. The document uses this specific name as load-bearing shorthand for "Aphrahat's own non-episcopal leadership register" in both the Revision Log and Section 6, but it appears nowhere in the record I could find. Suspiciously, a real historical figure with a nearly identical name — **Aqib-Alaha, Bishop of Karka d'Baith Slok** — turns up as one of the Persian bishops who challenged Papa bar Aggai's authority in exactly this period. If "Mar Aqiba" is a garbled/confabulated reference to Aqib-Alaha, that is doubly damaging: (1) it's a factual error/hallucination risk that should be flagged, and (2) the real Aqib-Alaha was an actual *bishop*, which would directly undermine the document's premise that Aphrahat's register was distinctively non-episcopal. This needs a hard stop-and-verify before finalization — it is not a cosmetic issue, since the entire Strand Determination rests on the contrast between "Aphrahat's non-episcopal role" and "Persia's parallel episcopal hierarchy."

**Whether Aphrahat was actually "non-episcopal"** — The document treats this as settled fact. It is not. Multiple sources note Demonstration 14 (344 CE) was a circular letter Aphrahat drafted on behalf of a synod of bishops and clergy addressed to the church of Seleucia-Ctesiphon, which "is held by William Wright and others to prove that he was a bishop." Scholarly treatments hedge with "may have been a bishop" / "almost certainly a son of the covenant" rather than confidently asserting non-episcopal status. The document's Section 6 argument requires a clean, confident contrast (non-episcopal Aphrahat vs. episcopal Persian hierarchy) that the actual secondary literature does not support — his status is itself contested, which is a live third possibility the document doesn't address at all.

**Serafim Seppälä 2023 Religions 14 citation** — **Fabricated/wrong on every distinctive point.** Verified directly against the MDPI publisher page (mdpi.com/2077-1444/15/6/686):
- Actual author: **Malatius Malki Malki** (not Serafim Seppälä)
- Actual title: "Revisiting the Institution of Bnay and Bnoth **Qyōmo**" (not "Qyāmā" as spelled in the document)
- Actual journal/volume: **Religions 2024, Vol. 15, Issue 6**, article 686 (not "Religions 14" / 2023)
- Actual dates: submitted 11 Dec 2023, published 31 May 2024 (submission date may explain the "2023" confusion, but the document asserts 2023 as the publication year, which is wrong)

This is a real citation to a real article on the right general topic, but the byline, exact title spelling, year, and volume are all incorrect. This is exactly the kind of "confirmed, not left as unverified title-only citation" failure the document elsewhere claims to have fixed for Griffith (Section 3) — but here it introduces a *new*, unflagged citation error while congratulating itself elsewhere for citation diligence. Serafim Seppälä is a real, relevant Syriac-studies scholar, so this may be a model conflation of the correct topic/journal with the wrong author — but as written it is a fabricated attribution and must be corrected (either fix to Malki Malki 2024, or find and verify an actual Seppälä piece if one exists on this topic).

**Peace Treaty of 363 / "Treaty of Dura" naming correction** — Confirmed as accurate and, if anything, slightly over-corrected. Independent sources do use "Treaty of Dura" as an alternate name (Omniatlas calls its map "Treaty of Dura"), and Dura here refers to a different, much smaller site than Dura-Europos, on the Tigris near where Jovian's army was trapped — not the famous Dura-Europos on the Euphrates (destroyed 256/257 CE, "over a century earlier" per the document, which is correct — that's roughly a century before 363, so the arithmetic and the underlying distinction both check out). The document's correction is sound: the site called "Dura" in the treaty's name is not Dura-Europos, and the standard scholarly label is indeed "Peace Treaty of 363" or "Perso-Roman Peace Treaty of 363" per Wikipedia. This fix is correct and appropriately hedged.

**Jacob of Nisibis death-date hedge (338 vs. 350)** — Confirmed as a genuine, real scholarly disagreement, accurately characterized. Sources confirm both dates circulate (338 tied to the first Shapur II siege of Nisibis in 337/338; 350 tied to a later siege per the Chronicon Paschale). The document's decision to flag this as unsettled rather than pick one is appropriate and matches the actual state of the sources.

**Rabbula/Diatessaron-suppression connection** — Confirmed as accurate, with the appropriate hedge already present. Independent sources confirm Rabbula (bishop 411–435) is traditionally credited with suppressing the Diatessaron in favor of the four separate Gospels, but that his exact role and the Peshitta's actual relationship to him is genuinely contested in scholarship (Vööbus's argument that the Peshitta predates Rabbula). The document does not overclaim here — it presents this as Rabbula's "role in... Diatessaron suppression," which matches the traditional attribution scholars debate rather than asserting it as settled fact.
