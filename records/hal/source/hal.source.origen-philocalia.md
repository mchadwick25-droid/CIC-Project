THE CONTROL ON RUFINUS. That is the reason, and it is specific to this
world rather than general.

The charge at the centre of this world's rupture is that Rufinus, in
translating De Principiis, softened what Origen actually said - and
Rufinus admits in his own prologue, which this world already holds at
hal.source.rufinus-prologue-de-principiis, that he corrected passages he
judged corrupted by heretics. Jerome's answer was to make his own
translation to show what the Latin had hidden. So this world's argument
turns on a text whose Greek is lost.

Not entirely lost. Chapter I of the Philocalia says of itself that it is
drawn "From the work on 'Principles'", and the anthology preserves Greek
that Rufinus' Latin is otherwise the only route to. For the passages it
covers, the accusation can be tested rather than repeated. That is a
different thing from holding an opinion about whether Rufinus was honest.

A SECOND USE, and the one hal.contested.origenist-substance will want:
somebody in the middle of the fourth century went through Origen and
chose twenty-seven chapters worth keeping, overwhelmingly on how to read
scripture and then on free will and providence. Forty years later the
same material was a scandal. Whatever the anthology's compilers thought
they were doing, the selection is a dated measurement of which Origen was
uncontroversial BEFORE the controversy, made by admirers - and it can be
set against the list of positions the controversy actually fought over.

WHAT THIS WORLD MAY NOT DO WITH IT. Treat the Philocalia's Origen as the
whole of Origen, or infer from the selection's mildness that the
controversy was manufactured. hal.contested.origenist-substance holds
that question open and this record is evidence in it, not a verdict on it.
