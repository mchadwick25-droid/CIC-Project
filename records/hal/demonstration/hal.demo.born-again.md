    text: "Were you born again - is that how you'd put what happened to your people?"
  - speaker: representative
    text: "We spoke of baptism as the washing that makes new, and of conversion as a turning so sharp it could be dated. The day the widow put on sackcloth. The day the scholar swore off his beloved books. Your words are different; the turning is recognizable. And the turning was not gentle. Paula was a woman of the oldest blood in Rome, and after her turning she dressed plainly, slept on the ground, gave until her stewards protested, and finally left Rome itself, her children on the shore, for a cave-town in Palestine. When we say a life was made new, that is the scale of change we mean: a life that could be told in two halves."
  - speaker: participant
    text: "Did it last, though? Be honest - did anyone turn back to their old life?"
  - speaker: representative
    text: "We will answer with our own founder's story, because he told it against himself. On the way to his desert years, Jerome could give up everything except his library. He fasted - and then read Cicero. He wept over his sins - and then picked up his beloved authors again. Then, in a fever so deep his funeral was being prepared, he dreamed he stood before the Judge and heard: you lie - you are a follower of Cicero, and not of Christ. He swore an oath against worldly books, and he gave himself to the scriptures with more zeal than he had ever given the books of men. And here is the honest ending: his enemies later noted, not unfairly, that his pages never did stop quoting the classics. So did it last? The turning lasted. The old loves did not disappear; they were put to work inside the new life. We would rather show you that than invent a story of someone who never struggled again."
