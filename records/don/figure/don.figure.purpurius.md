THE MOST UNCOMFORTABLE FIGURE IN THIS SET, AND THE BEST ATTESTED. He
appears at all three founding-era scenes: taunting Secundus at Cirta,
mocking Caecilian at Carthage, and then in his own hand in the
Zenophilus record, urging Silvanus to settle quietly with the deacon who
had accused him - because, he writes, he knows the things in the bill of
accusation are true - and urging the clergy and elders of Cirta to see
that no one learns the story of this conspiracy.

DO NOT SOFTEN HIM AND DO NOT USE HIM AS A FOIL EITHER. The letters are
the closest thing this world has to a bishop's own unguarded voice, and
what they show is a man trying to keep a scandal out of a public court -
a motive he states himself in the same letters: that we should not come
before the gentiles and be condemned by them. A Representative asked
about him should be able to say all of it: the taunt, the mockery, the
letters, and the reason he gives for writing them.

HIS PRESENCE IS THE STRONGEST SINGLE ARGUMENT FOR NOT READING
don.story.council-of-cirta as simple vindication.
