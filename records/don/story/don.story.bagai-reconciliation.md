text: >-
  In 393 a deacon named Maximian was elected as a rival primate at
  Cebarsussi, in direct defiance of Primian, the sitting primate at
  Carthage. It was, in miniature, the same act our own founders had once
  taken against Caecilian: a rival consecration, answering a grievance
  with a new bishop rather than submission to the one already in place.

  The response was neither quiet nor measured. A much larger council -
  three hundred and ten bishops - met at Bagai in 394 and condemned the
  Maximianists in language that did not soften what it meant. The decree,
  preserved in Augustine's own quotation of it, describes the schismatics
  as shipwrecked men dashed by the waves of truth upon the sharp rocks,
  their bodies covering the shore, so thoroughly that they fail to find
  so much as burial.

  And then, having said this, the council did something its own decree's
  language did nothing to prepare a listener for. When Felicianus of
  Musti and Praetextatus of Assuris, two of the condemned Maximianist
  bishops, were brought back into the fold, it was done without
  rebaptism and without reordination. A general named Optatus Gildonianus
  enforced the reconciliation with military force. The very men whose
  ordination the council had just described in the imagery of shipwreck
  and unburied death were received back into full office, exactly as they
  had been ordained under the schism the council had just condemned in
  the harshest terms it had.

  Augustine, no friend to any argument of ours, seized on the
  contradiction and made it the centre of his own case: if reordination
  could be waived here, on the mainstream party's own authority, then the
  absolute rule - that invalidly ordained clergy must always be
  rebaptized and reordained, without exception - did not hold even for
  the party that preached it most fiercely.
absent_detail: No account of the Bagai council from our own side survives beyond the decree Augustine quotes,
  and nothing at all survives of the reasoning by which the same council that wrote those words received
  Felicianus and Praetextatus back unrepeated. We do not know what was said in the room, or whether anyone
  present named the inconsistency out loud.
modern_contrast: >-
  A modern reader will be tempted to treat this as a caught-out story:
  the rigorists exposed as no better than anyone else. That reading is
  Augustine's, and it is not the only one available. What the episode
  actually shows is a communion holding an absolute conviction and a
  named, undenied exception to it in the same breath - and not
  experiencing that as a contradiction requiring resolution. The
  exception was never hidden. It is stated plainly in the same body of
  texts that state the doctrine at its most absolute. Naming a gap was
  not thought to require closing it.

HOMONYM, LOAD-BEARING - don.core.donatism cautions item 7. The Maximian
of this story is a deacon deposed in 393 and made a rival primate at
Cebarsussi. He is NOT the martyr Maximianus of the Passio Isaac et
Maximiani, killed in the Macarian repression roughly forty-five years
earlier and commemorated as one of our own dead. See
don.figure.maximian-of-cebarsussi and don.figure.maximianus-martyr,
which each carry the distinction on their own face, and
don.story.macrobius-letter, which tells the martyr's story.

A SECOND HOMONYM IN THE SAME PARAGRAPH. Optatus Gildonianus, the
Donatist bishop of Thamugadi who enforced the reconciliation with
troops, is not Optatus of Milevis, the Catholic polemicist through whom
most of our founding narrative survives - the second trap
don.core.donatism cautions item 7 names by name. The story text above
uses only the epithet form, deliberately.

THE REFUSAL IS DOMINANT, NOT ABSOLUTE (don.core.donatism cautions item
8). This episode is one of three named points at which this communion
turned to imperial or proconsular machinery, here against its own
dissidents. It is not an embarrassment to be managed; modern_contrast
above states the position that our own record actually holds.

A SECOND WOMAN STANDS BEHIND THIS SCHISM AND IS NEVER NAMED. Augustine
draws the parallel to Lucilla himself, by name, in the same paragraph,
and leaves the other woman unnamed - see don.figure.lucilla's own body
note and don.source.lucilla-and-the-second-unnamed-woman.
