text: >-
  Before Caecilian's consecration ever became the dispute it became, a
  council of Numidian bishops met at Cirta to consecrate a successor of
  their own.

  Secundus of Tigisis, presiding, put the question to those gathered: had
  any of them, under the persecution, handed over the scriptures? One by
  one, several admitted that they had. Then Purpurius - the same man who
  would later mock Caecilian in the Carthage basilica - turned the
  question back on Secundus himself, taunting him not for having escaped
  but for having been released only after a long time among the soldiers,
  and the room began to mutter that he too must have given up the sacred
  books to buy that release.

  Secundus, unsettled, took advice from his own brother's son - called,
  in the record, Secundus the Less - who counselled him to remit the
  whole affair to God rather than press it further. Secundus then turned
  to the three bishops present who had not themselves been accused,
  Victor of Garba, Felix of Rotarium and Nabor of Centurio, and asked
  their judgement. They answered that a case of this kind ought to be
  reserved to the Lord. Sit down, all, Secundus said. Thanks be to God,
  the assembly answered, and did. No one present was found guilty; no one
  was cleared. The room simply agreed not to finish the question it had
  started.
absent_detail: No record of this council from our own side survives - no minute, no later defence, no explanation
  of what the phrase "reserved to the Lord" was understood to mean by the men who said it. Whether the
  admissions were what Optatus takes them to be, and what the assembly thought it was doing when it sat
  down, cannot be checked against anything.
modern_contrast: >-
  A modern reader may expect founding figures either to be consistent or
  to be exposed. This scene is neither. The same men who would soon
  insist that a bishop's own purity decides whether a sacrament is real
  at all sat in a room, found the question genuinely unanswerable about
  themselves, and stopped. It is possible to read that as evasion, which
  is how the man who recorded it read it. It is also possible to read it
  as an early and honest recognition of how little could actually be
  verified about who had done what under the persecution. The story does
  not settle which, and it should not be told as though it does.

THE THIRD-NAMED BISHOP HERE IS ANOTHER FELIX. Felix of Rotarium, one of
the three unaccused bishops asked for judgement, is neither Felix of
Aptungi nor the deacon Felix of don.story.lucilla-affair. Three
different Felixes appear across the founding-era stories in this
inventory.

SILVANUS OF CIRTA IS NOT NARRATED HERE. He is the bishop of Cirta whose
traditio is established at don.story.gesta-apud-zenophilum, and Cirta is
this council's own seat, so the temptation to place him in this room is
real. Optatus I.14 as read at the cited lines does not put him there,
and this record does not.
