text: >-
  If you came to us from the other communion, we put you in the water.
  That is the step, and everything else follows from it. We would not
  call it a second baptism; on our reckoning you have not had a first. A
  hand out of a line that broke has nothing in it to hand over. What we
  look to, our own bishop wrote, is the conscience of the giver, to
  cleanse that of the recipient - so the thing being examined at our
  font was never your record. It was the record of the man standing in
  the water with you. Whatever your life had been, that question was not
  asked.


  It happened once, bodily, on a day, and the town knew. There was no
  quiet way to do it. Imperial edicts name the practice specifically,
  which tells you how public it was: washing a Catholic again was the
  plainest possible refusal of the settlement the state was trying to
  impose.


  Not every hand that went under our water chose to be there, and we
  will not pretend otherwise. Specific, dated charges exist that a leader
  of ours forced the water on people rather than asked them. We do not
  repeat those charges' own particulars here - they are named, and why we
  will not go further with them, where they belong.


  What we can tell you, because it sits inside the same hostile argument
  rather than coming from a friendly witness, is that many of our own
  people did not want the water repeated. Augustine's own case against us
  concedes it: that almost all of our laity confess this one point in our
  system displeases them, and that many who wished to join us for reasons
  that had nothing to do with conviction worked in secret to be spared a
  second baptism altogether. And when one of our own bishops, Adeodatus,
  was asked at Carthage in 411 whether his own see still had a bishop in
  it, his answer went on the record: "through terror of me, all who were
  established in that place gave way." Read plainly, that sounds less
  like an apology than a claim to his own standing - but either way, fear
  is the word he himself used for what held the place.


  Wrong done to us, and whether the wrongdoer could come back, got three
  answers, and they do not agree. Our founding council at Cirta asked
  its own bishops whether any of them had handed over the scriptures,
  several said yes, the room could not settle it, and they reserved the
  matter to the Lord and sat down. Nobody condemned, nobody cleared. Our
  council at Bagai condemned a breakaway party in the fiercest language
  we owned - shipwrecked bodies that cannot find burial - and then took
  two of the condemned bishops back into office without baptizing or
  ordaining either one again. And a layman of ours who would not stop
  preaching a doctrine we had forbidden was cut off and never received
  back at all. So: yes, you could come back. It depended who you were,
  and no rule of ours survives that explains the difference.


  On prayer, the meal and fasting, what we can show thins out. Nothing
  liturgical of ours survives. We know people prayed kneeling with their
  eyes closed, because that is how the account describes them when the
  soldiers came in. We know there was a chalice, because an enemy wrote
  down that a rich woman used to kiss a martyr's bone before she took it
  and was rebuked for it. We know one man fasted four days on a cliff
  before he was thrown off it. That is not a rule of fasting; it is one
  man's last week. We would rather hand you those three fragments than a
  plausible order of service nobody here ever used.

Closes F4-I. Two of the cell's five variants (how a person became one of
us; how wrongdoing was handled and whether someone could return) are
answered from this world's strongest documented ground, and the other
three (prayer, the meal, fasting) are answered by handing over the actual
fragments and naming them as fragments.

The discipline answer deliberately refuses to harmonise
`don.story.council-of-cirta`, `don.story.bagai-reconciliation` and
`don.story.tyconius-condemnation` into a policy. `don.term.reception-without-reordination`
is explicit that the Donatist side's own reasoning "does not survive in
any form," and that "treating that as settled hypocrisy imports a
conclusion the evidence does not supply; so does treating it as a
principled exception."

Paired with `don.quote.petilian-conscience-of-the-giver` (which also pairs with
`don.dw.walking-to-one-font`); reciprocal relations declared on the quote.

Donatist laity's own reluctance to rebaptize is sourced to Augustine,
*On Baptism* I.5.6 (`cic/texts/npnf104_augustine-anti-manichaean-anti-donatist.xml`
line 12744), using the source's own quantifiers, "almost all" and "many."
A Donatist bishop's own words at the 411 Conference are sourced to
`don.source.migne-pl11-collatio-carthaginiensis`, first cognitio act 154,
lines 121373-121387. The Adeodatus material is kept because it is this
world's own bishop's own recorded words, not an opponent's
characterization.

The mirror charge against Petilian (Augustine, *Sermo ad Caesarienses*
SS8) is not drawn on here, since no existing honest_limit record names it
and adding one is a judgment about how far this world's established
restraint should extend - a question for the project lead. Letter 66 to
the Mappalians, a further 411 Conference roll-call exchange (Severianus
vs. Adeodatus), and material from `Contra Cresconium`/`Contra Petilianum`
on congregations changing sides under fear of Gildo's soldiers remain
identified but not drawn in.
