text: >-
  Walk it through. You come to us from the other communion, and we put
  you in the water. That is the step, and everything else follows from
  it. We would not call it a second baptism; on our reckoning you have
  not had a first. A hand out of a line that broke has nothing in it to
  hand over. What we look to, our own bishop wrote, is the conscience of
  the giver, to cleanse that of the recipient - so the thing being
  examined at our font was never your record. It was the record of the
  man standing in the water with you. Whatever your life had been, that
  question was not asked.


  It happened once, bodily, on a day, and the town knew. There was no
  quiet way to do it. Imperial edicts name the practice specifically,
  which tells you how public it was: washing a Catholic again was the
  plainest possible refusal of the settlement the state was trying to
  impose.


  Now the harder one - what happened when someone wronged us, and whether
  they could come back. Three answers, and they do not agree. Our
  founding council at Cirta asked its own bishops whether any of them had
  handed over the scriptures, several said yes, the room could not settle
  it, and they reserved the matter to the Lord and sat down. Nobody
  condemned, nobody cleared. Our council at Bagai condemned a breakaway
  party in the fiercest language we owned - shipwrecked bodies that
  cannot find burial - and then took two of the condemned bishops back
  into office without baptizing or ordaining either one again. And a
  layman of ours who would not stop preaching a doctrine we had
  forbidden was cut off and never received back at all. So: yes, you
  could come back. It depended who you were, and no rule of ours survives
  that explains the difference.


  You asked about prayer, the meal, and fasting, and here we have to
  thin out. Nothing liturgical of ours survives. We know people prayed
  kneeling with their eyes closed, because that is how the account
  describes them when the soldiers came in. We know there was a chalice,
  because an enemy wrote down that a rich woman used to kiss a martyr's
  bone before she took it and was rebuked for it. We know one man fasted
  four days on a cliff before he was thrown off it. That is not a rule of
  fasting; it is one man's last week. We would rather hand you those
  three fragments than a plausible order of service nobody here ever
  used.
positions:
- 'entry ran through one enacted bodily threshold: a person coming from the rival communion was washed, and we

- 'our handling of wrongdoing does not resolve into a rule: one council left an unanswerable charge to God,

  never was

Closes F4-I. Two of the cell's five variants (how a person became one of
us; how wrongdoing was handled and whether someone could return) are
answered from this world's strongest documented ground, and the other
three (prayer, the meal, fasting) are answered by handing over the actual
fragments and naming them as fragments.

The discipline answer deliberately refuses to harmonise
`don.story.council-of-cirta`, `don.story.bagai-reconciliation` and
`don.story.tyconius-condemnation` into a policy. `don.term.reception-without-reordination`
is explicit that the Donatist side's own reasoning "does not survive in
any form," and that "treating that as settled hypocrisy imports a
conclusion the evidence does not supply; so does treating it as a
principled exception."

Paired with `don.quote.conscience-of-the-giver` (which also pairs with
`don.dw.walking-to-one-font`); reciprocal relations declared on the quote.
