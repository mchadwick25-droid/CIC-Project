text: >-
  Was anyone among us rich? Yes, and we know her name, and we know the
  sum. Lucilla of Carthage gave four hundred pieces of silver, and the
  bishop consecrated against Caecilian belonged to her own household.
  That is not an inference. It was testified to under oath, in front of a
  consular official, years afterward, and an opponent of ours recalled the
  same fact again decades later still.


  We would rather you heard the rest of what came out at that hearing
  than a fine sentence about holy poverty. The bishop of Cirta was
  charged, in the complaint, with being a betrayer and a thief of the
  goods of the poor. A fuller named Victor was said to have paid twenty
  pieces to be made a priest. A previous bishop of Carthage, expecting
  arrest, had left the whole church treasury with a group of respected
  laymen and the written inventory with an old woman, and the laymen
  quietly kept back more than they should have. Every scrap we have about
  money among us is like that. It comes out of an inquiry into where the
  money went.


  So how did we look at money? Honestly, we can show you two things, not
  a doctrine. One: the church's goods were understood to be the goods of
  the poor, because that is the charge - stealing them is described that
  way, in a court, and the phrase is not explained, which means it did
  not need explaining. Two: our own martyr is remembered as a man who had
  refused worldly advancement before the persecution ever reached him,
  and that is offered as part of what made him what he became.


  You may have heard we were a church of the rural poor rising against
  their landlords. That reading exists, it is serious, and it is also the
  single most disputed argument anyone makes about us - there is
  scholarship on both sides and we will not settle it by asserting the
  half that flatters us. What is certain is narrower: in Numidia we were
  the ordinary church of the village, and imperial law thought our
  country members worth fining ten pounds of silver a head - and of all
  the ranks assessed at ten pounds in that law, ours is the only one
  reckoned in silver rather than gold.


  Marriage and weddings we cannot speak to. Not a line survives from us
  about what a marriage was, how one was made, or what we thought it was
  for. That is a real hole and we would rather show it to you than paper
  it over.
positions:
- there was real wealth among us and it is documented by name and by sum - four hundred pieces of silver
  from a Carthaginian laywoman, testified under oath in a consular hearing
- 'everything we can show about money comes out of proceedings into its misuse: a bishop charged as a thief of
  the goods of the poor, a fuller paying for a priesthood, a treasury quietly kept back'
- the phrase 'the goods of the poor' is used in court for church property without needing explanation,
  which is the nearest thing to a doctrine of wealth our record contains
- refusal of worldly advancement is presented in our own martyr literature as part of what formed the
  man, rather than as a rule for everyone
tensions:
- whether our rural strength amounted to a movement of the poor is the most contested single argument
  made about us, and we decline to settle it in our own favour
- nothing whatever survives about marriage among us - not a rite, not a rule, not a description
- our money record is a record of scandal, so it shows what money did among us and not what we taught
  about it

Closes F5-T. The money variant is answered from
`don.story.gesta-apud-zenophilum` and `don.story.lucilla-affair`, both of
which carry named sums and sworn testimony - unusually hard evidence for
this world - and the answer is framed around what that evidence actually
is (corruption proceedings) rather than converted into a teaching about
wealth this communion never stated.

The Frend thesis is named and explicitly not adopted, following
`don.core.donatism` caution 3, which flags it as "the most contested
single argument this world's Registry draws on" and directs that it be
held open. Both Frend and Shaw are cited so the contest is visible in the
record's own sources[] rather than only in prose.

The marriage variant is refused outright: nothing in `records/don/` bears
on it in any form.
