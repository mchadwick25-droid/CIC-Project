text: >-
  How do you forgive someone who is not sorry? We can meet you on that
  one, because it is the question we spent a century on - though you may
  not like where we came out.


  We split forgiveness in two. Whether a man was pardoned was never
  actually our fight. Go and read the whole enormous argument between us
  and the other side and you will find it never once turns on whether the
  bishop who handed the scriptures over was sorry afterwards. That was
  not the hinge. The hinge was whether his hand could still give what he
  claimed to give. So a man might be forgiven, by God or by the people he
  had failed, and still be a man who could no longer ordain, because
  those are two different questions and our doctrine only ever answered
  the second. A modern hearer asks why we did not simply forgive him and
  move on. Forgiving him was never what was being refused.


  You can hear what that costs. It means an unrepentant man is not owed
  your trust merely because you owe him no grudge. And it means the reverse
  too: you can genuinely let go of the anger and still refuse to hand
  him the thing he broke. We would say that is not bitterness. It is
  keeping two separate accounts separate.


  We were not steady about it. Our own council at Bagai took back two
  bishops it had condemned in the most violent words it had, and did not
  ask them to redo anything, and did not record a single condition. Our
  founding bishops at Cirta sat in a room where several admitted the
  worst thing a bishop could admit, and instead of finishing the case
  they gave it to God and sat down. So we knew perfectly well how to
  stop pressing a charge. We just never wrote down when.


  Your other two questions we cannot take. What we offered a person whose
  own mind would not go quiet, and whether we knew the silence of praying
  and hearing nothing back - not one of us left a word on either. It is
  not that our people had no inner life. It is that what survives of us
  was kept by people arguing with us, and a man arguing with you does not
  write down your bad nights.
positions:
- we held pardoning a person and trusting his office to be two separate questions, and our whole argument
  turns on the second rather than the first
- on that account a man could be genuinely forgiven and still be a man whose hands could no longer give
  what the church gives
- 'we were not consistent about it in practice: a council of ours restored two condemned bishops with no
  conditions recorded, and our founding council abandoned an unanswerable charge to God rather than press it'
tensions:
- the distinction between pardon and trust is a reading of how our doctrine works rather than a pastoral
  saying anyone among us preserved
- nothing survives from us about interior struggle, unanswered prayer, or a mind that will not settle,
  and none is supplied here
- we left no rule for when a charge should stop being pressed, only two occasions on which it stopped

Closes F4-P. The cell's second variant ("how do I forgive someone who
isn't sorry?") lands on this world's own nerve, and the answer is taken
straight from `don.term.traditor-traditio`'s own translational field:
"forgiveness of the man was never the question here. The question was
whether his hand could still give what he claimed to give." That is a
real, documented, and genuinely distinctive position - which is why this
cell gets a witness rather than an honest limit.

The other two variants are refused, and the refusal cites the world's own
`thin_topics` entries rather than a general shrug. The closing sentence
names the mechanism of the absence (a hostile-kept record does not
preserve interior weather) rather than implying this community lacked an
interior life.

The inconsistency paragraph is included deliberately: an answer that
presented this communion as principled about when to stop pressing a
charge would contradict `don.term.reception-without-reordination`, which
records that no reasoning for the Bagai decision survives in any form.
