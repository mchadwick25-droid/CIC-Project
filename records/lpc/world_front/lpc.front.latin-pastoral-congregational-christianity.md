      Two bishops, nearly two centuries apart, held the same office over the
      same kind of church in Roman North Africa. The first turned from a
      public career to the church around 246. Within a few years his people
      made him bishop, over the recorded objection of five presbyters. He led
      his flock through a persecution that split the failed from the
      faithful. Then came a plague. Then his own execution in 258. The second
      bishop held the same office over a century and a half later, in a
      different city on the same coast. He preached to the same congregation
      for more than three decades. He died in his own city, while a Vandal
      army lay outside its walls. Between the two lies a silence of about a
      hundred and thirty years, when this world's voice falls quiet. What
      holds the two together is not a continuous record. It is one plain
      thread: a bishop answerable for his people, a road back for those who
      fail in public, and colleagues who argue hard without ever putting each
      other outside the table.

      The record runs from about 246 to 430. It centers on two cities in
      Roman North Africa: Carthage and Hippo Regius. For most of this span,
      Carthage was the biggest Latin Christian city outside Rome. Hippo
      Regius was a large port, in the Numidian church province. This is not a
      movement built around one doctrine or one crisis. It has no founding
      story to tell. It is the ordinary life of a bishop and his flock:
      preaching, teaching people before baptism, and deciding what to do with
      those who fail. Real differences separate the two bishops. How far
      coercion could go. Whether a rival ordination was valid. What a council
      can decide -- and that question stays open, not settled. But none of
      these differences clearly touches a bishop's ordinary authority over
      his own flock. That authority is what this world cares about most.
