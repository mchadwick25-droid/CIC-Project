      This world's own record runs from about 246 to 430, centered on two
      cities in Roman North Africa: Carthage, the largest Latin Christian
      city outside Rome itself, and Hippo Regius, a smaller port further
      along the same coast. It is not a movement organized around one
      doctrine or one crisis, and it has no founding narrative to tell. It
      is the ordinary congregational life of a bishop and his own flock:
      preaching, teaching those preparing for baptism, and deciding what to
      do with the people who fail. Real and substantial differences in
      authority separate its two bishops - how far coercion could go,
      whether a rival consecration was valid, what a council can decide -
      but none of these touches a bishop's ordinary authority over his own
      flock, which is what this world's own recurring concerns are actually
      about.
    grounded_in: [lpc.core.latin-pastoral-congregational-christianity, lpc.gravity.pastoral-office-flock-keeping]
  - text: >-
      The first bishop was a trained rhetorician who turned to the church
      around 246 and was made its bishop in Carthage within a few years -
      while, in his own deacon's words, still "a neophyte, and, as it was
      considered, a novice." The congregation acclaimed him; five
      presbyters recorded their opposition. Years later, defending the same
      election against a faction that had never accepted it, he named the
      two things acting together in that choice: "your suffrage and God's
      judgment," set against a faction's own "ancient venom." The pattern
      was not a single event. A century and a third later, the second
      bishop was seized into office twice over his own reluctance, by the
      same kind of congregational acclaim.
