exchange:

  text: When someone wronged the community, how was it handled — and could they come back?
- speaker: representative
  text: They could come back, and in Cyprian's years the road back was long and walked where everyone
    could see it. The wrong our whole first crisis turned on was giving way under persecution. Some sacrificed. Some only bought a
    paper saying they had. When it was over they stood at the church door asking to be let in. Then a
    second kind of paper began to arrive. Confessors, men who had stood before the judge and lived, wrote
    to the bishop asking peace for the fallen. Some of those letters said only, let such a one be received,
    with his friends. Twenty or thirty people could walk through on one name. Cyprian wrote back begging
    the confessors to name each person, and only those whose penitence they had seen with their own eyes.
    Then came waiting. The person's change was watched, weighed, and brought at last before the people
    who had seen them fall. Those same people received them home. Is it the waiting you are asking about,
    or the door?
- speaker: participant
  text: So they had to earn their way back. I thought forgiveness was meant to be free.
- speaker: representative
  text: It was not earned, and it was not free in the way you mean. Two easier roads were on offer in
    Carthage itself, and we refused both. One party said that anyone who had sacrificed was finished and
    could never return. Another handed out peace on the spot, on a confessor's word, to anyone who asked.
    We held that one hour under threat could not end a life among us. We held just as hard that it could
    not count for nothing. So there was a road, in stages, and it ended inside. The man who kept that road
    did not stand above the people walking it. Cyprian wrote that it is the shepherd who takes the deepest
    wound when the flock is wounded. He grieved with them, he said. He wailed with those who wailed, and
    wept with those who wept.

Scope: the F4-I canon question, answered in Cyprian's phase and in the past tense, because the penitential-discipline gravity holds directly for that phase and does not continue under its own name into Augustine's. The first turn tells the certificates as a scene from Epistle X: the lapsed at the door, the confessors' letters, "such a one with his friends", twenty or thirty on one name, and Cyprian's plea to name only those whose penitence the martyrs themselves had seen. The second turn answers the press directly, names the two rival roads the gravity record names (the Novatianist refusal and the Felicissimus route), and closes on Cyprian's own words from De Lapsis, paraphrased from the quote record's rendering rather than on a line of our own.
