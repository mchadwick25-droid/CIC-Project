name: Congregational Acclamation Overriding a Reluctant Convert's Preference
kind: initiating
description: 'Cyprian was a trained public speaker who became a Christian in middle life. Roughly two or three years later, the people of Carthage chose him as their bishop by acclamation. Five presbyters are recorded as opposing him. Later he wrote that certain presbyters kept up an old venom against his office as bishop. That venom, he said, was against his people''s choice and God''s judgment. A deacon who knew him told it the same way from outside. God judged and the
  people chose, and he was made bishop while still new to the faith.


  The pattern comes back in the second phase, at both of Augustine''s offices. In 391 he was seized at
  Hippo and made a presbyter against his wishes. Possidius''s Life, chapter VIII, records how he became
  bishop. Valerius, the bishop of Hippo, told the bishops present, the whole clergy of the city and all
  the people what he meant to do. Those who heard were glad, and they loudly called for it. Augustine
  refused to be made bishop while his own bishop was alive. Then examples from overseas and from Africa
  persuaded him, and he gave way, compelled and constrained.


  The evidence is documented. It comes from Cyprian''s Epistle XXXIX, from Pontius, and from Possidius''s Life, chapters IV and VIII.


  This force gives the pastoral office its two-way shape. A bishop answers to the people who placed him,
  as well as for them. One caution is kept rather than smoothed over. The pattern is attested through
  different people and in different words, not through one recurring term. But it appears at the same
  office in both phases.


  Two conditions shaped it. An office with no legal protection is one a sensible man declines, so the
  acclamation had to override reluctance. And a church organized enough to hold factions was organized
  enough to elect a bishop over a faction''s opposition.'
