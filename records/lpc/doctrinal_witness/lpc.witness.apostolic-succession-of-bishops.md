positions:
- Augustine, with Fortunatus and Alypius, wrote a joint letter against a Donatist presbyter. The
  presbyter had claimed an "episcopal succession" for his own side. The three named the actual line
  instead. They counted from Peter, to whom the Lord said he would build his Church on this rock.
  They listed every bishop of Rome by name, from Linus and Clement down to Anastasius, the bishop
  then sitting. No Donatist bishop, they wrote, is found in this line of succession. Elsewhere, against
  Petilian, Augustine alone named a second chair the same way. He named Peter's own seat at Rome and
  James's at Jerusalem, each with the man who filled it "to-day."
tensions:
- Cyprian, writing a century and a half earlier, does not give a name-by-name list the way Letter LIII does, the joint
  letter of Augustine, Fortunatus and Alypius. He states the principle that list later fills in -- that from Peter onward, "through the changes
  of times and successions, the ordering of bishops and the plan of the Church flow onwards" -- and, separately,
  that the manner of choosing a bishop, with the people present, is itself "the practice delivered from
  divine tradition and apostolic observance." Cyprian traces the principle and the practice; Letter LIII
  traces the actual names. Neither, on its own, is the whole of what a modern listener might mean by "apostolic
  succession."
text: Was the office of bishop itself, not just this or that practice, ever traced back to the apostles by
  name? Yes, in two ways, by two different men. Augustine did it most clearly. He wrote a letter
  together with two fellow bishops, Fortunatus and Alypius. A Donatist presbyter had claimed an
  "episcopal succession" for his own side. The three bishops answered with the real one. They named
  every bishop of Rome in order. They started with Linus, the successor of Peter. They ended with
  Siricius, "whose successor is the present Bishop Anastasius." Then they added that no Donatist bishop is found in this line of succession. Against another Donatist, Petilian, Augustine alone named
  a second chair the same way. He named Peter's own seat at Rome and James's at Jerusalem. Each was
  still filled, "to-day," by a living bishop. Cyprian, a century and a half earlier, made no name
  list of his own. He stated the principle instead. From Peter onward, the ordering of bishops
  "through the changes of times and successions" carries the Church itself forward. On a different
  occasion he said that choosing a bishop with the people present is a practice handed down "from
  divine tradition and apostolic observance." The joint letter supplies the names. Cyprian supplies
  the principle and the practice they run on.

Grounded directly in Augustine, Letter LIII SS2 and Answer to Petilian II, chapter 51 (section 118), and Cyprian, Epistles XXVI SS1 and LXVII SS5 -- all four verified against the vendored text at the line numbers above. Answers the residual half of F4-E's own question that lpc.limit.apostolic-origin-undefended had wrongly called a bare "general conviction, not a traced lineage": a traced lineage for the office of bishop itself does exist in this world's own corpus, most explicitly in Letter LIII, a joint letter that Augustine signed with Fortunatus and Alypius, and in Augustine's own naming of two chairs against Petilian. What remains unanswered -- catechesis, the road back, and the teaching before the water, none traced to the apostles by name -- is narrowed in lpc.limit.apostolic-origin-undefended, not resolved here.
