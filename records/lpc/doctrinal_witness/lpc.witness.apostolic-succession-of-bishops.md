positions:
- Augustine, writing against a Donatist presbyter who had claimed his own faction's "episcopal succession,"
  named the actual line instead. Reckoning from Peter, to whom the Lord said "upon this rock will I build
  my Church," he listed every bishop of Rome by name down to Linus, Clement, and on through the centuries
  to Anastasius, the bishop then sitting. "In this order of succession no Donatist bishop is found." Elsewhere,
  against Petilian, he named a second chair the same way -- Peter's own seat at Rome, and James's at Jerusalem,
  each with the man who filled it "to-day."
tensions:
- Cyprian, writing a century and a half earlier, does not give a name-by-name list the way Augustine does.
  He states the principle Augustine's list later fills in -- that from Peter onward, "through the changes
  of times and successions, the ordering of bishops and the plan of the Church flow onwards" -- and, separately,
  that the manner of choosing a bishop, with the people present, is itself "the practice delivered from
  divine tradition and apostolic observance." Cyprian traces the principle and the practice; Augustine
  traces the actual names. Neither, on its own, is the whole of what a modern listener might mean by "apostolic
  succession."
text: Was the office of bishop itself, not just this or that practice, ever traced back to the apostles
  by name? Yes, in two different ways in two different hands. Augustine did it most directly. A Donatist
  presbyter had claimed an "episcopal succession" for his own side. Augustine answered with the real one.
  Counting back from the present bishop of Rome, Anastasius, through Damasus, Liberius, Julius, and so
  on, he reached Linus, the successor named by Peter himself -- "In this order of succession no Donatist
  bishop is found." Against a different Donatist opponent, Petilian, he named a second chair the same way
  -- Peter's own seat at Rome, and James's at Jerusalem, each still filled, "to-day," by a living bishop. Cyprian,
  a century and a half earlier, did not draw up a name list of his own. He stated the principle instead
  -- that from Peter onward, the ordering of bishops "through the changes of times and successions" carries
  the Church itself forward -- and, on a different occasion, that choosing a bishop with the people present
  is a practice handed down "from divine tradition and apostolic observance." Augustine supplies the names;
  Cyprian supplies the principle and the practice they run on.
