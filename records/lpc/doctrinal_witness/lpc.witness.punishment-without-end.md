positions:
- Augustine taught that the punishment of the lost has no end. After the final judgment, he wrote, there
  are two kingdoms. The life of one and the death of the other "shall both be without end." The lost suffer
  in degrees, he said, "one being more endurably miserable than another."
- Many around him found this hard to bear. He wrote that "some, indeed very many, make moan over the eternal
  punishment." He said they did not argue against Scripture. They "soften down everything that seems hard."
  He called their moan "in vain."
tensions:
- He let the doubters suppose, "if the thought gives them pleasure," that the pains of the damned are
  at times eased. He gave no more than that. God's mercy, he said, is shown "not in putting an end to
  their eternal punishment." It is shown, he said, "in mitigating, or in granting them a respite from,
  their torments." In the next chapter he wrote that the death of the wicked "shall abide for ever." In
  these chapters he does not name any person or group as lost.

text: Augustine taught that the punishment of the lost has no end. After the final judgment, he wrote,

Grounded directly in Augustine, Enchiridion, chapters 111, 112 and 113 (chapters CXI to CXIII in the Latin) -- verified against the vendored files at the lines above. The quoted phrases are the NPNF English word for word. Scripture-reference notes inside chapter 112 are left out. Chapter 112 gives the reasons for his answer to the doubters after the sentences quoted here, and those are not set out in this record. The Latin verb behind "make moan over" is miserantur, which means to pity, so the doubters are moved by pity for the lost. The Latin is OCR text with misreads. No year for the Enchiridion is stated here, and none is stated by the vendored sources for this world.
