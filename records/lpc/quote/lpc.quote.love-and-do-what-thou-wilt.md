text: 'A father beats a boy, and a boy-stealer caresses. If thou name the two things, blows and caresses,
  who would not choose the caresses, and decline the blows? If thou mark the persons, it is charity that
  beats, iniquity that caresses. See what we are insisting upon; that the deeds of men are only discerned
  by the root of charity. For many things may be done that have a good appearance, and yet proceed not
  from the root of charity. For thorns also have flowers: some actions truly seem rough, seem savage;
  howbeit they are done for discipline at the bidding of charity. Once for all, then, a short precept
  is given thee: Love, and do what thou wilt: whether thou hold thy peace, through love hold thy peace;
  whether thou cry out, through love cry out; whether thou correct, through love correct; whether thou
  spare, through love do thou spare: let the root of love be within, of this root can nothing spring but
  what is good.'

modern_lens_note: 'A modern listener may hear ''do what thou wilt'' as leave to do anything. Augustine
  puts the line at the end of an argument that the root decides the deed: a father beats a boy out of
  love, and a boy-stealer caresses him out of wrong. So the rule is to let love be the root, and then
  whether you keep silent, cry out, correct or spare, you do it through love.'
modern_rendering: 'A father beats a boy, and a slave-dealer caresses him. Name the two things, blows and

  beats, and wickedness that caresses. See what we are pressing on you. People’s deeds are told apart
  only by the root of love. For many things may be done that look good, and yet do not come from the root
  of love. Thorns have flowers too. Some actions seem rough, even savage. Yet they are done to train and
  correct, at love’s bidding. So once and for all, you are given a short rule: Love, and do what you will.
  If you keep silent, keep silent from love. If you cry out, cry out from love. If you correct, correct
  from love. If you spare, spare from love. Let the root of love be within you. From this root nothing
  can grow but what is good.'

Augustine, Homily 7 on the First Epistle of John (NPNF Homily VII), section 8. English in the NPNF translation at cic/texts/npnf107_augustine-homilies-john-soliloquies.xml, lines 15529-15531. Latin at cic/texts/augustine_in-ioannis-evangelium-tractatus-lat_migne-garnier1902-t3-pt2.txt, lines 50812-50829 (OCR text). The passage is the second half of section 8. The two sentences before it, which say that where deeds differ we find a man made fierce by love and winningly gentle by wrong, are left out, and so is everything after 'what is good'. In the Latin the precept reads Dilige, et quod vis fac, and radix sit intus dilectionis, non potest de ista radice nisi bonum existere is 'let the root of love be within; from that root nothing can spring but good'. The passage carries the gravity lpc.gravity.penitential-discipline in its form of correction from love.
