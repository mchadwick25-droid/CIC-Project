text: 'That which belongs, dearest brethren, both to the common joy and to the greatest glory of our Church
  ought to be told to you; for you must know that I have been admonished and instructed by divine condescension,
  that Numidicus the presbyter should be appointed in the number of Carthaginian presbyters, and should
  sit with us among the clergy,—a man illustrious by the brightest light of confession, exalted in the
  honour both of virtue and of faith; who by his exhortation sent before himself an abundant number of
  martyrs, slain by stones and by the flames, and who beheld with joy his wife abiding by his side, burned
  (I should rather say, preserved) together with the rest. He himself, half consumed, overwhelmed with
  stones, and left for dead,—when afterwards his daughter, with the anxious consideration of affection,
  sought for the corpse of her father,—was found half dead, was drawn out and revived, and remained unwillingly
  from among the companions whom he himself had sent before. But the reason of his remaining behind, as
  we see, was this: that the Lord might add him to our clergy, and might adorn with glorious priests the
  number of our presbyters that had been desolated by the lapse of some.'

modern_lens_note: 'A modern listener may hear ''divine condescension'' as God talking down. Here it means
  God''s gracious kindness. ''Burned, I should rather say preserved'' is not a cold word game: for Cyprian
  a martyr''s death completes a life. The ''lapse of some'' is the fall of presbyters who gave way in
  the persecution.'
modern_rendering: Dearest brothers, you must be told news that belongs both to our common joy and to the
  greatest glory of our Church. You should know that God, in His kindness, has warned and taught me to
  add Numidicus the presbyter to the presbyters of Carthage. He is to sit with us among the clergy. He
  is a man made famous by the brightest light of confession, raised high in honour for virtue and for
  faith. By his urging he sent a great number of martyrs ahead of himself, killed by stones and by fire.
  With joy he watched his wife, who stayed at his side, burned with the others. I should rather say she
  was preserved. He himself was half burned, buried under stones and left for dead. Afterwards his daughter,
  with the anxious care of love, searched for her father's body. He was found half dead, pulled out and
  revived. Against his will, he stayed behind the companions he himself had sent ahead. But the reason
  he stayed behind, as we see, was this. The Lord meant to add him to our clergy. He meant to fill with
  glorious priests the ranks of our presbyters, left thin by the fall of some.
