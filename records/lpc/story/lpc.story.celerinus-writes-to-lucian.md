text: 'Celerinus was a confessor among us, and he wrote to Lucian, another confessor, in prison. He told Lucian of his own confession. Then he came to his grief: he told Lucian he was in the midst of great trouble. His sister had sacrificed. He asked Lucian to grant his wish and grieve with him over her death, a death of the spirit, for in that time of ruin she had fallen away from Christ. The letter was written at Easter, as the editor''s note says, in the paschal rejoicing, and Celerinus spent his days in tears, in sackcloth and ashes.


  She was not dead. What Celerinus grieved was a death he believed had happened to her soul -- which is why the letter carries a request. He asked Lucian to intervene, for her and for two other women, Numeria and Candida, whom Lucian also knew. He made the case for them: they had repented, and there were works to point to. Of Candida he said more. She had given gifts so that she would not have to sacrifice. She seems only to have gone up as far as the Tria Fata, and then come back down. So he knew she had not sacrificed.


  Lucian''s reply came back from the prison, and it granted what was asked. Before it did, it said what the prison had been like: by the emperor''s command they had been sentenced to die of hunger and thirst, and shut up in two cells to weaken them that way. Then he wrote that now they had reached the brightness itself. He greeted Numeria and Candida, and named the martyrs on whose authority he answered. He asked that the two women have peace, once each case had been set before the bishop and confession made. He signed off exhausted, greeting others whose names he had not written because he was already too tired, and asking them to forgive him.'
telling: 'Celerinus was one of our confessors, a man who had confessed Christ, and he wrote to Lucian, another confessor, who was in prison.


  His sister had fallen away from Christ, and it seemed plain to Celerinus that she had sacrificed. He asked Lucian to grieve with him over his sister, for to him she was dead in spirit. He would keep weeping until help came to her, through Lucian or through the martyrs Lucian would ask.


  He also asked the martyrs'' pardon for two women Lucian knew, Numeria and Candida. He called what the women did a great sin, but they had repented and done good works for our banished colleagues. He trusted that Christ would have mercy on them if the martyrs asked.


  With Candida it was not so simple, for she had given gifts so that she would not have to sacrifice. She seems to have gone only as far as the Tria Fata, a spot on her way up to sacrifice, and then come back down. And so Celerinus knew she had not sacrificed.


  Lucian answered from the prison, and he too grieved for the women and for so great a crime. The emperor had ordered the confessors there put to death by hunger and thirst. Even so, he wrote, "now we have attained the brightness itself."


  Before he died, the martyr Paulus had told Lucian to grant this pardon, called peace, in his name to anyone who asked. Lucian granted the request in the name of the martyr Paulus and the other martyrs, with one condition. Each case was to be set before the bishop, and confession made. Then he asked that these women have peace, along with all whom Celerinus knew to be dear to the confessors.


  He greeted friends by name but left the rest out because he was already too weary, and he asked them to forgive him.'

telling_long: 'Celerinus, who had confessed Christ, was one of our confessors at Rome. Lucian, one of the Carthaginian confessors, was in prison, and not one letter had come from him since the two were last together.


  Then news reached Rome that Lucian had been tried and had confessed Christ before the magistrates. Celerinus was glad, and he wrote asking Lucian to pray that Celerinus too might be worthy to be crowned as a martyr with him.


  But Celerinus was in great trouble too. His sister had fallen away from Christ, and it seemed plain to him that she had sacrificed and angered our Lord. To Celerinus she was dead in spirit, and he asked Lucian to grieve with him.


  It was Easter, a time of joy, yet Celerinus wept day and night in sackcloth and ashes. He would go on weeping until the Lord came to her help, through Lucian or the martyrs who had already died.


  Two other women weighed on him, Numeria and Candida, whom Lucian knew. They too had sinned, but they had repented and done good works for our banished colleagues. So Celerinus trusted that Christ would have mercy on them if Lucian and the other confessors asked Him.


  He begged Lucian to take the case to the other confessors. Whichever of them was first to die as a martyr should forgive the two women their great sin.


  With Candida it was not so simple, for she had given gifts so that she would not have to sacrifice. She seems to have gone only as far as the Tria Fata, a spot on her way up to sacrifice, and then come back down. So Celerinus knew she had not sacrificed.


  The clergy in charge had lately heard both cases and told the women to wait as they were until a new bishop was appointed.


  Statius, Severianus and the other confessors who had come from Lucian joined the plea. The two women had met them at the harbour and brought them into the city. They had cared for sixty-five.


  When Lucian answered from his prison, he said the letter had filled him with so much joy that it almost overcame him. Lucian had confessed trembling, before only the lesser magistrates. But when Celerinus confessed, Lucian said, his words had driven back the emperor himself.


  Lucian grieved for the women too, and wished he could speak of them without remembering so great a crime.


  He explained why he could grant the pardon, which the confessors called peace. Before the blessed martyr Paulus died, he called Lucian to him. He said, "If any one, after my being called away, shall ask for peace from you, grant it in my name." The confessors dying with Lucian had all agreed, and their letters had given peace to all.


  By the emperor''s command they were to die of hunger and thirst, shut up in two cells to wear them down. The fire of their torture was more than anyone could bear. He went on, "But now we have attained the brightness itself."


  So Numeria and Candida would have peace by the order of Paulus and the other martyrs, and Lucian listed those martyrs by name. Many had already died of hunger in that prison, and he expected to join them within a few days.


  But he set one condition: each case was first to be set before the bishop, and confession made. Then he asked that these women have peace, along with all whom Celerinus knew to be dear to the confessors.


  He sent greetings to many by name but left out the rest because he was already weary, and he asked them to forgive him.'
