text: >-
  This is how it would have been, at a martyr's feast kept at a shrine
  somewhere in the countryside - reconstructed from what preachers who
  stood at such shrines themselves describe, offered as reconstruction,
  not as one dated occasion. On the vigil night before the feast, the
  faithful gathered at the shrine itself, kept awake through the dark
  hours by prayer and by the martyr's own passion retold - the homilies
  preached at these gatherings, on the Forty, on Gordius, on Theodore the
  soldier-martyr, are themselves the record of what such a night held,
  each preacher recounting the saint's own suffering to the crowd gathered
  to honor it. The feast drew more than worshippers. A crowd gathered
  whose reasons for coming were not all devout, and the preachers say so
  themselves: business went on nearby, and more than one sermon complains,
  in passing, of noise and distraction competing with the liturgy for the
  crowd's attention - a market's pull alongside the shrine's own claim on
  the same day. That market-and-crowd pattern is attested only generally,
  across several homilies together, not by any one dedicated account of a
  single festival, and no more precision than that is claimed here. The
  calendar itself - a fixed feast day, kept year after year - is
  documented from multiple festal texts naming specific dates, Gregory's
  own account of the Mamas festival among them.
absent_detail: "No specific festival at any one named shrine is described here; this is a composite built from several homilies' shared pattern, not a single dated occasion. The market-and-crowd texture rests on the weakest sourcing in this record's own set (a general regional pattern with no single dedicated text of its own, plus one of the underlying martyr homilies itself unverified in this world's own Source Registry) and is stated in the text with exactly that hedge rather than presented as more solid than it is."
modern_contrast: >-
  A modern reader might picture a religious festival and a nearby market as
  two separate events sharing a calendar date by convenience. This world's
  own preachers experienced them as one contested occasion - the same
  crowd, the same day, competing for the same attention - and preached, by
  their own account, against the pull of the second while standing inside
  the first.
