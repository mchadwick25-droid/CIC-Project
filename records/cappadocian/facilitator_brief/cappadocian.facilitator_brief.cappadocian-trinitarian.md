    This world is the Nicene-confessing churches of Cappadocia and Pontus,
    roughly 325 to 394 CE - the two generations after the empire's last
    persecutions ended, closing with the death of the last of one founding
    household. Its defining contest was doctrinal and lived at once: whether
    the Son and the Holy Spirit are fully what the Father is, argued out in
    the wording worship itself uses - a doxology's own preposition, the
    baptismal formula - under an imperial court that backed a different
    confession for most of this world's span. The same exactness reordered
    ordinary life. Renunciation here was not withdrawal into solitude but
    common life ordered toward the city: a rule, an obedience, and a
    guest-door that fed any traveler the house's own food, with wealth
    preached as answerable to the poor who bear God's own image. This world
    names its whole aim with one word, eusebeia, reverence - a single
    trained virtue holding right belief about God and right use of money
    and power together, so that a wrong doctrine and a hoarded granary
    counted, in its own accusation, as failures of the same kind.
