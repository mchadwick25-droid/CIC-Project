text: >-
  They falsely accuse me of introducing novelties about the Holy Spirit.
  Ask what the novelty is. I confess what I have received, that the
  Paraclete is ranked with Father and Son, and not numbered with created
  beings. We have made profession of our faith in Father, Son, and Holy
  Ghost, and we are baptized in the name of Father, Son, and Holy Ghost.
  Wherefore we never separate the Spirit from conjunction with the
  Father and the Son.

modern_lens_note: >-
  A modern reader who arrives here through the letter's earlier
  paragraphs - full of talk of calumny, slander, and wounded friendship
  - could easily mistake the whole rupture with Eustathius for a
  personality conflict, the kind of falling-out any long friendship
  might eventually survive. The specific charge Basil is answering was
  not vague, and it was not personal: "novelties about the Holy Spirit"
  was the live technical accusation that he had abandoned an older,
  deliberately unclear way of speaking about the Spirit that let a
  bishop avoid saying outright whether the Spirit shared the Father and
  Son's own uncreated rank or belonged among the things God made.
  Basil's answer refuses that vagueness on the spot: the Spirit is
  "ranked with Father and Son, and not numbered with created beings."
  That is precisely the line his own former mentor would not draw,
  which is why this ended in permanent separation rather than
  reconciliation.

This letter is Basil's own defense to the ascetic communities loyal to
him after the final break with Eustathius of Sebaste, and sec. 3 is
the passage where he names the actual doctrinal line at stake rather
than the surrounding calumny and politics: whether the Holy Spirit
ranks with the uncreated Father and Son or is to be numbered among
created things. It backs cappadocian.dw.catholic-and-its-rivals'
specific claim that a figure "once close enough to us to have been our
own ascetic movement's own reformer, came to deny the Spirit's own full
standing" - here is Basil, in his own voice at the moment of rupture,
stating the standing he holds and will not blur, which is exactly the
boundary Eustathius's own faction would not cross. It closes F3-T by
showing the rival-boundary claim resting on a real, citable doctrinal
sentence rather than a generic assertion of orthodoxy.
