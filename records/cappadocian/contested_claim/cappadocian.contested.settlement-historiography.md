claim: >-
  The three Cappadocian authors held one coherent, agreed technical trinitarian scheme - ousia to
  hypostasis as genus to instance - the tidy "Cappadocian settlement" that resolved the fourth
  century's trinitarian argument into a stable formula later tradition simply inherited.
held_against:
- Lewis Ayres reads "pro-Nicene" as a set of shared argumentative strategies converging across a wider
  field than three men between roughly 360 and 380, not one settled formula the Cappadocians alone
  produced and handed down whole
- John Behr's reconstruction of the period is substantially exegetical and soteriological rather than
  built around the ousia/hypostasis technical pairing at all - a genuinely different account of what
  this era's theology was doing, not a variant statement of the same settlement
- Michel René Barnes has independently argued against the older textbook narrative of a single
  Cappadocian achievement, alongside Ayres and Behr rather than folded into either
- Christopher Beeley's monograph reads Gregory of Nazianzus as differing in real, substantive ways from
  Basil's and Nyssen's own technical vocabulary and method - one voice inside the traditional "three"
  turns out not to share the scheme as cleanly as the textbook story requires
- Even Ad Ablabium, the settlement narrative's own flagship proof-text for the mature one-ousia/three-
  hypostaseis grammar, is itself contested ground - Ayres' essay "On Not Three People," in Coakley's
  edited volume, argues against reading it as a stable social-trinitarian formula, which makes the text
  most often produced to demonstrate the settlement a live participant in the debate over whether the
  settlement exists, not a neutral exhibit for it
- This world's own flagship citation for the pairing, Basil's Epistle 38, is itself attribution-
  contested between Basil and his brother Gregory of Nyssa - a substantial body of modern scholarship
  (Cavallin, Hübner, Zachhuber, with Drecoll leaning the same way) reassigns it to Nyssen, which means
  the letter usually cited as proof that "the Cappadocians" jointly worked out the vocabulary may in
  fact be one man's work, not evidence of a shared achievement at all
concedes: >-
  Not contested by any side: the three authors used the ousia/hypostasis pairing, argued over it under
  real polemical pressure, and left it load-bearing for every later reading of this era - Orthodox,
  Catholic, and Protestant dogmatics all still argue through vocabulary this circle fought to place.
  What is contested is whether their usage amounted to one shared, stable technical scheme - the
  textbook "settlement" - or a family resemblance across three distinct projects that later tradition
  flattened into a single formula for its own doctrinal and pedagogical convenience.
divergence_partners:
- A structurally similar question is carried elsewhere in this build's own fleet - whether a
  controversy's received shape in later tradition matches what the period's own sources actually show,
  rather than a tidy narrative retrojected onto them. The Hieronymian-Ascetic-Literary world's own

  genuinely doctrinal dispute, or a vehicle for other conflict dressed in doctrinal language), and
