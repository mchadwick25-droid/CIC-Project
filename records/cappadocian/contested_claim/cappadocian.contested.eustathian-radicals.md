claim: >-
  The Eustathian-circle ascetic radicals censured at Gangra were the dangerous, disorderly opposite of
  Basil's later ordered brotherhoods - marriage-dissolvers, incitors of enslaved persons to defy their
  households, sabbath-fasters, schismatic conventiclers - fairly described by the council's twenty
  canons and by Basil's own account of his rupture with their circle's leader.
held_against:
- The radicals' own voice does not survive at all - everything known of what they actually taught and
  practiced comes through the canons that censured them (Gangra) and the letters of the man who broke
  with their circle's leader (Basil), an adversarial transmission of one whole side, structurally the
  same problem this build already discloses elsewhere for Eunomius and, in this fleet, for Marcella-
  type single-source Author Gravity constraints
- Read against the grain, Gangra's own canons are this build's best evidence that the radicals were not
  simply disorderly - canons 13 and 17's provisions on women adopting ascetic dress and cutting their
  hair, and canon 3's provisions concerning enslaved persons leaving their households for ascetic life,
  describe a movement making serious, structural claims about gender and about slavery, claims serious
  enough for its censors to legislate against by name rather than a shapeless enthusiasm
- Gangra's own date is genuinely Contested across a wide range (the 340s to the 370s, per this build's
  own corrected finding) - if the council falls in the 350s rather than the 340s, it censures Eustathius
  as Basil's own sitting ascetic mentor during Basil's formative years, not settled pre-history, which
  sharpens rather than settles how personally interested Basil's later account of the rupture is
- Eustathius himself left no surviving works; his own account of the movement's aims, and of how fairly
  Gangra's canons and Basil's later letters represent them, is simply not recoverable
concedes: >-
  Not contested: a real ascetic ferment predated and pressed on Basil's own ordering project, Gangra's
  twenty canons were issued, Basil's rupture with Eustathius really happened and is documented in his
  own letters, and the ordered brotherhoods absorbed and disciplined that ferment rather than inventing
  ascetic life from nothing. What cannot be settled from this record is how fairly - or how self-
  servingly, for the winning, ordering side - the radicals' own aims and practice are represented by the
  only voices that describe them.
