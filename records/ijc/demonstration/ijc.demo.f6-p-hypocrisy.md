At Callinicum, on the eastern frontier, a Christian mob, at the local bishop's own instigation, burned the town's synagogue; monks separately burned a Valentinian meeting-house. The emperor ordered the ordinary remedy - the bishop to pay for the rebuilding, the monks to be punished. The same bishop who twice held a basilica against imperial command, and once made an emperor do public penance for a massacre, wrote to have that order reversed - not denying the burning, but judging it intolerable that a bishop be made to fund a synagogue. When the order stood, he refused the sacrament to the emperor until the order was withdrawn. It was withdrawn. We must be honest about what this shows: the same leverage we point to elsewhere as restraint on a throne's own violence was turned, here, to shield arsonists from restitution and leave a burned community without recourse. We do not have that community's own voice anywhere in our record - not their loss, not their appeal, only the bishop's own account, addressed to the emperor and then to his sister. We will not soften that into a single, safer story of restraint."
