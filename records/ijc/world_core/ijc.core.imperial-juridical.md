horizon: 'Rome, Constantinople, and Milan, c. 312-451 CE: the formation of Christianity as claimant,
  instrument, and contested partner of Roman imperial and juridical power - from Constantine''s alliance
  with the church after the Milvian Bridge (312) and the Edict of Milan (313), through Ambrose of Milan''s
  confrontations with emperors, to the Roman see''s consolidation of jurisdictional primacy under Damasus
  through Leo I, closing at the Council of Chalcedon (451) - the last council of the window at which a
  still-nominally-unified imperial church legislates as though universal consensus were achievable.'
formation_logic: 'This is a world of office-holders, not congregants - emperors, court bishops, metropolitan
  bishops, and the Roman see itself, formed by and forming one question: where does final authority over
  the church actually reside - with the emperor, with an assembled council, or with the bishop of Rome?
  Its formation logic is juridical and institutional before it is devotional: what this world forms in
  a person is standing - the capacity to make a claim that binds - and its characteristic output is canon,
  decretal, conciliar act, and imperial edict. Three strands contest the ground of authority across the
  whole window: a Roman strand grounding authority in apostolic succession from Peter (Damasus through
  Leo); a Constantinopolitan strand grounding a see''s rank in its nearness to imperial power (Canon 3
  of 381, Canon 28 of 451 - "because it is New Rome"); and an Ambrosian strand grounding a bishop''s
  authority in sacramental and moral leverage over any ruler - the emperor is within the Church, not
  over it. All three share one root: the Constantinian alliance that first made church office a form
  of state-adjacent power at all.'

  episcopal letters, imperial religious legislation reported by the church historians, and the century''s
  contest over authority told by participants themselves (Eusebius, Ambrose, Leo). Thin-to-silent, structurally:

  and instruments, not memoirs.'

  what they held stay at reduced confidence (see ijc.contested.homoian-content). 3) WINNER''S ARCHIVE:

  contest, not a neutral window onto the whole church. 4) SELF-REPORTING ACTORS: Eusebius writes as his

ABSENT STORIES (the required Doc_09 question, answered specifically and
carried forward from the reviewed legacy Doc_09_Story_Inventory SS5, with
this build's own step-4 additions):
(1) No story from an ordinary believer's own perspective on any event in
the record - the Constantinian alliance, Damasus's building program, the
386 siege, Chalcedon - because the sources are bishops, emperors, and
courts. Not filled by invention (no Tier 5, ever).
(2) No story from a Homoian Christian's own narrative perspective: the
one piece of near-primary Homoian self-testimony (Auxentius of
Durostorum's letter on Ulfila) is a theological fragment, not a
narrative; the losing side of this world's central conflict left a
theological argument but no story. Corrected note, added at review: this
letter's author is very likely (dominant scholarly identification, not
unanimous) the same man who confronts Ambrose at Milan in 386 under his
predecessor's own name (see ijc.figure.ambrose, ijc.source.auxentius-letter-ulfila) -
so the Homoian side of this world's central confrontation is not an
anonymous court bishop; it has a name and a surviving fragment of his
own theological voice. That voice remains a theological letter, not a
narrative - the absence named here is narrative form, not voice as
such, and is narrower than this document's first pass stated it.
(3) No woman's own told story: Justina and Pulcheria act, decisively, in
others' accounts; neither speaks for herself anywhere in the record.
(4) No individual martyr's own told story from Damasus's epigraphic
program - the program is well attested as a program; no single epigram's
narrative content is registered at story depth in this build's source
base (no public-domain English corpus of the epigrams exists - see
ijc.search.damasus-epigrams-english).
(5) No Tier 2 (collected community-tradition) story at all: every
narrative this world preserves arrives with a named, literate,
institutionally-placed author attached - itself evidence of how this
world transmitted itself.
(6) No deathbed or ordinary-death story: death appears as massacre
(Thessalonica), execution feared (the 386 siege), or succession event,
never as an ordinary member's ending.
