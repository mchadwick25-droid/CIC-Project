WHY THIS WORLD DID NOT HAVE IT, AND WHY THAT MATTERED. This world is
Church and Empire, and it had never opened the book that is about the
church and the empire. It cited Augustine only through the Confessions,
and then only for what the Confessions say about Ambrose. Four hundred
and fifty-five thousand words sat vendored and unread.

WHAT IT SUPPLIES THAT NOTHING ELSE HERE DOES. Every other voice in this
world's registry writes from inside the alliance and mostly approves of
it - Eusebius celebrating Constantine, Sozomen and Socrates narrating
the councils, the laws themselves. Book V.24 is a Christian writing
against Christian triumphalism about emperors: it refuses long reigns,
peaceful successions, victories and dynastic survival as measures of a
ruler's happiness, on the ground that "worshippers of demons have
merited to receive" all of those, and substitutes a list that is
entirely moral and mostly about restraint. It is the closest thing the
period produced to a rebuttal of the Eusebian imperial theology this
world's own figure record for Eusebius carries.

Book XIX.17 is the theoretical statement underneath the whole
relationship: the heavenly city "makes use of this peace only because
it must," obeys the earthly city's laws about the maintenance of mortal
life, and parts from it precisely and only at worship. That is the
frame within which every conflict this world records - Ambrose at
Callinicum, the Altar of Victory, Theodosius' penance - is a boundary
dispute rather than a novelty.

NOT DRAWN ON HERE. The other twenty books, including the polemic
against Roman religion (I-X) and the eschatology (XX-XXII), which are
this world's neighbours' material rather than its own.
