  text: >-
    This world is the church of Rome, Constantinople, and Milan, roughly
    312 to 451 CE - the century in which Christianity went from a hunted
    community to a partner, and instrument, of Roman imperial power. Its
    people are office-holders, not congregants: emperors, court bishops,
    metropolitan bishops, and the bishop of Rome. Its defining question is
    juridical - where does final authority over the church actually rest:
    with the emperor, with an assembled council, or with the bishop of
    Rome? Three claims run through the whole window without ever being
    settled. Rome grounds its claim in apostolic succession from Peter.
    Constantinople grounds its claim in nearness to the emperor. And a
    bishop of Milan, Ambrose, grounds a third and different kind of
    authority in a bishop's own sacramental and moral leverage over any
    ruler - the emperor stands inside the church, not above it. All three
    claims presuppose one prior fact: the alliance itself, forged when
    Constantine ended the persecution and began funding what he had once
    hunted. This world's native register is not the hymn or the anecdote
    but the council canon, the papal letter, and the imperial edict - a
    tradition that argues in instruments, not memoirs.

formation_strengths:
- text: >-
    This world serves participants asking whether gaining power corrupts a
    church, and it answers in both directions rather than one. Under
    Constantius II, and again under Valens, the empire's own church
    enforced a confession - the Son as merely "like" the Father - and
    deposed and exiled the bishops who refused it; under Theodosius the
    same imperial machinery reversed to enforce the opposite confession.
    The reversal is the record's own honest answer: state power really did
    shape what could be preached, in both directions. But this world also
    kept its own sharpest counter-evidence. Preached from inside a
    besieged basilica, with soldiers outside, one bishop's own formula
    holds that the emperor stands inside the church, not above it - a
    claim tested, not merely asserted, since the building was never taken.
