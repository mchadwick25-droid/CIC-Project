A SYRIAC WORLD READING ITS OWN FOUNDING POET IN A SYRIAC RECENSION. That
is the whole case for this record, and it is a good one: until now every
word this world held about Ephrem from Palladius came through Greek and
then Edwardian English, which for a world whose central claim is that its
own language reached things Greek did not is a strange place to have been
standing.

WHAT THE SYRIAC HAS THAT THE GREEK DOES NOT. Two visions, both absent
from Clarke entirely. First, a holy man dreams of angels descending with
a roll written inside and out, naming saint after saint as unfit to be
entrusted with it, until they say "No man is fit to be entrusted with
this thing except Ephraim" - and in the morning he hears that Ephraim is
teaching and the words flow from his mouth like a fountain. Second, from
Ephraim's own youth, a vine growing out of his tongue until it fills the
space beneath the heavens, laden with grapes, and every bird of heaven
eating from it while the clusters only grow. syr.story.ephrem-roll-and-vine
carries both.

WHERE THE TWO RECENSIONS SAY DIFFERENT THINGS. At the famine, the
citizens answer Ephraim. Clarke's Greek: "We know you to be a man of
God." Budge's Syriac: "O man, we know that thou art God." That is not a
small variant and this world should not smooth it. Whether it is a Syriac
idiom, a scribal loss, or Budge translating flatly, the sentence a Syriac
reader met on the page said one thing and the sentence a Greek reader met
said another.

The reply is likewise longer in Syriac - not "I appoint myself
hospitaller" but "I will set myself apart to become the keeper of a house
for receiving strangers". syr.quote.ephrem-keeper-of-strangers carries
it, deliberately alongside rather than in place of
syr.quote.palladius-hospitaller.

WHAT THIS FILE DOES NOT CONTAIN, so nobody reaches: not the Historia
Monachorum, not the Sayings, not Book II. It ends "Here endeth the first
Book".
