modern_lens_note: >-
  She had sold her property, sailed to Egypt, and gone into the mountain of Nitria. Then a governor
  jailed her, taking her for poor because of how she was dressed, and she told him who her father
  was. "My poor garb and estate" is the whole difficulty in four words: the renunciation is real
  and the rank is still there to be picked up. A modern reader may want this to be either
  hypocrisy or empowerment. It is neither - it is a consular family's daughter using the only
  instrument that would work on a provincial official, and a monastic author recording it with
  approval. What this world can say from it is that renouncing property in the fourth century did
  not renounce birth, and that nobody involved seems to have thought it should.

The square brackets are Budge's. The quotation stops before the closing
sentences, in which she instructs the governor on how to treat "senseless
and foolish men" - present in the file, not carried here, because it is
her advice on handling inferiors rather than her claim about herself, and
a record wanting it should quote it knowingly.
