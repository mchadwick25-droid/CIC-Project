world_identity:
  text: >-
    This world is monastic-ascetic Christianity in Roman Gaul, roughly 360 to
    450 CE, carried in two households that this world's own record never
    puts in contact with each other. The northern household gathered around
    Martin, a discharged soldier turned monk who was made bishop of Tours
    against his own wish and kept living as a monk inside the office. The
    southern household - the island of Lerins and the two houses at
    Marseilles - took shape a generation later around Cassian, who had lived
    among the monks of Egypt and carried their teaching home in a book, and
    around Vincent, who wrote a short rule for telling true faith from
    novelty. Its defining formation structure is the office fled and
    overtaken: renunciation of the world does not put a man outside the
    Church's reach, and every house in this world's own record tells its
    founder's rise to the episcopate as a capture, never an achievement. Both
    households share one grammar of reception - nothing a man invents binds
    anyone; the customs are the fathers', not ours - even though they never
    wrote to each other, and they measure themselves against Egyptian
    monasticism in opposite ways: a rule to receive in the south, a rival to
    match in the north. Both households also carried, without ever settling
    it, one argument about how much of a monk's progress is God's own gift
    and how much is his own effort.

formation_strengths:
- text: >-
    This world serves participants wrestling with an office or responsibility
    they did not seek and cannot escape. In every one of its founding
    stories, the man who flees a public role is the one it finds: Martin was
    drawn from his monastery by a trick and elected bishop over a few
    bishops' own objection; Honoratus went alone to a snake-infested island
    wanting nothing but to stay there, and was made bishop of Arles anyway;
    an Egyptian monk named Archebius counted his own promotion to bishop an
    expulsion. This world's own record does not let the reluctance stand as
    the last word - the office still comes - but it also does not pretend
    the men wanted it, and it never claims that wanting the office
    disqualifies a man less than not wanting it.

- text: >-
    It serves participants asking how much of their progress is grace and
    how much is effort, without forcing an answer. Cassian's own teacher
    answers with a farmer's field: the ground must be plowed, but no labor
    makes it rain. Two different conferences hold the question open rather
    than settling it. In Conference III, Paphnutius says "the beginning of
    our good will is given to us by the inspiration of the Lord." Conference
    XIII says the first stirring of a good will can also arise "from our own
    efforts." It also states that how the two fit together "cannot be fully
    grasped by the mind and reason of man." Outsiders pressed this world
    hard on the question and reported it to Africa and to Rome. Its own
    record still declines to settle it either way.
