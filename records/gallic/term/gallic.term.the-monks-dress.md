senses:
  informational: >-
    Cassian begins the whole formation with clothes: "A monk, then, as a soldier of Christ ever
    ready for battle, ought always to walk with his loins girded." Each garment is read - the small
    hood for "the simplicity and innocence of little children"; the sleeveless tunic, which teaches
    "that they are dead to all earthly conversation"; the staff, because the monk must never walk
    unarmed among "invisible beasts of spiritual wickedness"; the girdle, which "signifies that he
    bears about the mortification of those members in which are contained the seeds of lust." Then
    the adaptation, in our own voice: "the severity of the winter does not allow us to be satisfied
    with slippers or tunics or a single frock; and the covering of tiny hoods or the wearing of a
    sheepskin would afford a subject for derision." We introduce only what is "adapted to the humble
    character of our profession and the nature of the climate." At Marmoutier "most of them were
    clothed in garments of camels' hair. Any dress approaching to softness was there deemed
    criminal"; Martin goes about "in his shaggy garment, with a long black cloak."
  evidential: >-
    Systematic at Marseilles (Inst. I.1-11, II.1 - Egyptian dress by Cassian's own framing, adapted
    in his own voice), descriptive at Tours (Vita IX-X; Dial. II.3). The Latin behind "mystery" and
    the garment names is editorial, from the translators' footnotes.
  personal: >-
    The Gallic monk is dressed in Egypt's meanings and Gaul's wool. His girdle is a doctrine and his
    hood is not a sheepskin. At Tours the dress is a mark of severance and a scandal to the bishops
    who said Martin's clothing was mean.
  translational: >-
    A modern hearer pictures "the habit" - a uniform of an order, fixed by rule. We had no such
    thing. Our dress was a set of received Egyptian garments each read as a mystery of the inner
    life, altered for our winter and for the eyes of men of the world, and chosen for simplicity
    against novelty.
