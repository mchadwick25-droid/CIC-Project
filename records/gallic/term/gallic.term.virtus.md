plain_meaning: >-
  At Tours, virtus is not first a moral quality but effective power. It is the "power present" Martin
  feels before he raises the dead, and the "abundance of power" he had as a monk and had less of as a
  bishop. At Marseilles Cassian uses the same word for moral virtue, and declines on principle to
  "weave a tale of God's miracles and signs."
world_word: virtus / power
false_friend:
- virtue as moral excellence or character, read as the whole meaning
- miracle as a suspension of natural law to be believed or disbelieved, a question of evidence
- Martin's wonders as pious legend detachable from what we were about
senses:
  informational: >-
    At Tours the word is what the Life is made of. Martin stretches himself on a dead catechumen and,
    "perceiving by means of the Spirit of God that power was present," rises and waits; the man lives.
    The power is a possession and can be measured: Martin "was accustomed to say ... that such an
    abundance of power was by no means granted him while he was a bishop, as he remembered to have
    possessed before." It can be lost - after one forced communion with the Ithacian bishops he "felt a
    diminution of his power on account of the evil of that communion." It resides in things: threads
    from his garment, a letter laid in a fevered girl's bosom. It can be doubted, and the doubt is
    answered as blasphemy; inside the house the doubt has a name, Brictio. At Marseilles the same word
    means what a monk grows - "by driving out faults virtues shoot up" - and Nesteros teaches that
    "the working of miracles and signs is not always necessary, nor good for all," that the fathers
    "never reckoned those as good monks ... who professed themselves exorcists among men," and that a
    wonder-worker deserves praise "not from admiration of his miracles, but from the beauty of his
    life." At Lerins, Eucherius praises Egypt's fathers for "crying signs" - the south is not of one
    mind.
  evidential: >-
    The organizing sense is Sulpitius's (Life chs. VII, XVI, XVIII, XIX; Letter I; Dialogues II.4,
    III.13, III.15, III.17), and virtus is one of the few lemmas the editor supplies against the
    ancient text itself. The opposed sense is Cassian's (Institutes Preface, IV.43; Conferences XV.7,
    XVIII.1), framed as Egypt's teaching, and the Egyptian teacher is not answering Martin - he has
    never heard of him. Eucherius's line is Doc_04's rendering of rough OCR. Gennadius remembers
    Martin as "famous for signs and wonders and virtues." Hilary of Arles's Latin uses virtus in
    inflected forms many times, sense not yet read. Historicity of the events is not assessed.
  personal: >-
    At Tours the word organizes everything: the disciple is formed by what he sees done, the master is
    "apostolical" because powerful, office costs him power, a bad communion diminishes it, the power is
    the weapon against the devil, and his threads and straw keep it after him. When Postumianus goes
    east he is to tell Egypt, "justly proud of the numbers and virtues of its own saints," that "Europe
    will not yield to it ... in having only Martin." And a Marseilles monk who reads Nesteros has been
    told what not to be - and it is the thing Tours celebrates. We are two temperaments, and we do not
    smooth them into one.
  translational: >-
    'By "virtue" do you mean good character - and are the miracles just legends?' - at Tours virtus
    meant power felt as present, measured as more or less, and transferable to a thread, and the whole
    formation of a disciple ran on seeing it; at Marseilles it meant the moral virtue that grows when
    faults are driven out, and a good monk did not proclaim his wonders - two hearings, each ours,
    neither to be used for the other house, and neither assessed here for whether the events happened.
quick_meaning: >-
  At Tours, power - felt as present, measured, lost by a bad communion. At Marseilles, moral virtue,
  with the wonders set aside on purpose.
