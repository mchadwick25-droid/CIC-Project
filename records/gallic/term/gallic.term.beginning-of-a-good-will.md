  divergence_note: >-
    Cross-voice tension. Cassian's own text gives two phrasings - the beginning of a good will
    comes from God who "inspires us with a good will to begin with" (Conf. XIII.3, XIII.7; III.19),
    and it can "have arisen from our own efforts" (XIII.8, XIII.9) - and declares the whole beyond
    "the mind and reason of man" (XIII.18). Augustine reports the brethren as claiming "the
    beginning of faith" for themselves (Praed. ch. 3), which is a report, not the brethren's own
    words. Which phrasing is Cassian's position is exactly what Prosper's Contra Collatorem
    (unvendored) was written to settle, and the Chadwick/Casiday disagreement over whether Conf.
    XIII answers Augustine at all keeps it live. Faustus's De gratia, the in-window Lerins side,
    is unread beyond grep, so the Lerins position on this precise point cannot be stated in its
    own words.

plain_meaning: >-
  The one question our whole argument about grace turns on. It asks whether God has mercy on us
  because we first showed a good will. Or whether our good will begins because God first had mercy.
world_word: the beginning of a good will
false_friend:
- a settled "semi-Pelagian" error - man makes the first move, God does the rest
- a philosophical question about causation or determinism
senses:
  informational: >-
    At Marseilles our teacher Chaeremon puts the question in one sentence and says at once that
    both answers, pressed too far, are errors. If we say the beginning of free will is in our own
    power, what of Paul the persecutor and Matthew the publican, drawn to salvation while eager for
    blood and plunder? If we say the beginning is always due to God's inspiring grace, what of
    Zaccheus and the thief on the cross, who ran ahead of their calling by their own desire? He
    leans one way - the initiative even of good thoughts comes from God, who strikes "the very
    smallest spark of good will" out of the hard flint of our hearts - and elsewhere the other -
    God strengthens what "He sees to have arisen from our own efforts." Abbot Paphnutius says
    simply that the beginning of our good will is given by the Lord's inspiration. And the
    conclusion is not a formula: how God works all things in us and yet everything can be ascribed
    to free will "cannot be fully grasped by the mind and reason of man."
  evidential: >-
    Directly attested in Cassian, Conferences XIII.3, 7, 8, 9, 11, 18 and III.19, as Chaeremon's
    and Paphnutius's reported teaching - received from Egypt, then made our own argument. What
    Augustine says the brethren of Marseilles hold - that "we have faith itself of ourselves, but
    that its increase is of God" - is his report of reporters, not our words, and is carried here
    as context only. The Latin initium fidei has zero occurrences in the vendored English Cassian;
    "the beginning of faith" is Augustine's translators' phrase. The Lerins side (Faustus, De
    gratia) is unread beyond grep.
  personal: >-
    We were told by our own teacher that no one can grasp how both are true - that the beginning
    is God's spark struck from our flint and that Zaccheus ran ahead. We held both, as the genuine
    faith of the ancient fathers, and we did not settle it. Men who heard only one side carried our
    sentence to Africa.
  translational: >-
    A modern hearer wants to know which side we were on - did man or God make the first move? Our
    own teacher refused to answer that as a formula. He gave Scripture's cases on both sides and
    said the question is beyond human reason. The later verdict "semi-Pelagian" is a name we never
    heard and would not have owned.
quick_meaning: >-
  The exact point at issue in our argument about grace: whether our good will begins with us or with
  God's mercy. Our teacher answered on both sides and said no one can fully grasp it.
