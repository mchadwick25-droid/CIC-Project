    Contested-Tradition term, the second of the pair with grace. Meaning: whether Chaeremon's "the will
    always remains free in man" and "seeds of goodness in every soul" (Conf. XIII.12) amount to what
    Augustine himself grants - a freedom grace anticipates and incites - or to the claim Prosper's
    Contra Collatorem extracted and condemned (the same Chadwick-versus-Casiday contest, inseparable
    from the contested chronology of Conf. XIII). Application: whether "semi-Pelagian" fairly describes
    a position whose own best later witness (Faustus, per Gennadius) is grace-prevenient, and whether
    Lerins and Vincent held it at all. Weighted to Cassian; contested by Augustine reporting; Tours
    silent by chronology. The northern hagiographer's own later "Pelagianism" (Gennadius ch. XIX) is a
    contested biographical charge, not evidence of the north's position on this term. The Latin liberum
    arbitrium is not attested in the vendored translation.

plain_meaning: >-
  In Cassian's Marseilles, the power that "always remains free in man" to "neglect or delight in the
  grace of God." Real enough that a monk's fasting, watching and labour are truly his and truly
  required. Weak enough that "the assistance and grace of God are necessary to it every day and
  hour." Never denied, never sufficient.
world_word: free will
false_friend:
- a philosophical question about determinism
- the Pelagian half of a grace-versus-works binary
- self-reliance - the idea that people save themselves by effort
senses:
  informational: >-
    The question is asked by a Gaul. Germanus, sitting with Abbot Paphnutius, asks, "Where then is
    there room for free will ... if God both begins and ends everything in us which concerns our
    salvation?" The answer holds both: the will is weak - "the weakness of our free will is shown" -
    and the will is real: "we have no wish to do away with man's free will ... but only to establish
    the fact that the assistance and grace of God are necessary to it every day and hour." Abbot
    Chaeremon: "there are by nature some seeds of goodness in every soul," which cannot grow without
    God; "the will always remains free in man, and can either neglect or delight in the grace of God";
    grace and free will "seem opposed to each other, but really are in harmony," and to withdraw either
    would break "the rule of the Church's faith." The boundary on one side is Pelagius, who "attributed
    so much antecedent strength to Free-will, as to deny the necessity of God's grace"; on the other,
    the men who reported Marseilles to Africa conceded that these brethren held "men's wills are
    anticipated by God's grace."
  evidential: >-
    Cassian's own text (Conferences III.11-22, XIII.11-18; Institutes IV.8, X.3, XII.14-18), with
    Vincent naming Pelagius; Augustine's treatise is context only, and his "we have faith itself of
    ourselves" is his characterization, not the brethren's own words. Gennadius, post-window, gives
    Faustus's later position; Faustus's own De gratia was grepped only, with no hit for liber*
    arbitri* on an OCR-sensitive regex - not a negative finding. The Latin liberum arbitrium is not
    attested in the vendored translation. Salvian is excluded by licence.
  personal: >-
    We feel this in our own practice before we hear it argued. The manual commands fasting, watching,
    labour, the psalms and the disclosure of thoughts; it blames the deserter; it tells the junior to
    conquer his own wishes. Then, at the summit, it tells us what our will is: "this very free will of
    ours, which is more readily inclined to sin, is turned by Him to a better purpose." The will is the
    thing that labours; the labouring is not its own. That is why a Massilian monk can say both that
    his will is anticipated by grace and that his fast is required of him.
  translational: >-
    'Did you believe people choose God by their own free will?' - we believed the will "always remains
    free" and "open to either side," and that it is weak, and that grace incites and strengthens it
    without removing it; we drew the line against Pelagius by name and against the denial that God
    wills all to be saved, and we said plainly that how both are true "cannot be fully grasped."
quick_meaning: >-
  A real but weak freedom that "always remains" in man, which grace strengthens without removing.
  Our fasts are truly ours and truly required; the labouring is not our own.
