plain_meaning: >-
  The man whose judgment stands in for the junior's own - the senior to whom every thought is laid
  bare, and by whose "examination" a thing is "good or bad." "Abbot" is the title of every Egyptian
  father Cassian reports. At Tours the same place is held by one master, Martin, who himself consults
  "his elders, whether among the presbyters or clerics."

senses:
  informational: >-
    In Cassian's book the senior is the first fact of a young monk's life: the juniors are taught "to
    lay them bare to the senior, and, in forming a judgment about them, not to trust anything to their
    own discretion, but to take it on trust that that is good or bad which is considered and
    pronounced so by the examination of the senior." The house is built of such men - an Elder at the
    gate for guests, another "placed over ten of the juniors," the Abbot whose hands clothe the new
    brother, and at the top the one who presides, who may not do so "before he has ... learnt by
    obedience what he ought to enjoin." Every father in the Conferences is "Abbot": Abbot Moses, Abbot
    Piamun. Of the Gallic head of a house Cassian says only that he is "presiding as he does over a
    large monastery." And not every grey head is an elder: the majority "pass their old age in a
    lukewarmness which they contracted in youth." At Tours the elder is one man and needs no title -
    Sulpitius, offered the place of honour, "deemed it unlawful to do anything but acquiesce" - and
    Martin too asks "his elders, whether among the presbyters or clerics" before he will honour a tomb.
  evidential: >-
    Cassian, Institutes II.3, IV.5-9 and Conferences I.1, II.10-13, Preface II; Sulpitius, Life chs. X,
    XI, XXV; Gennadius on Faustus as "first abbot" at Lerins. The offices are Egyptian by Cassian's
    framing; Gibson's note on decanus is editorial. No Gallic superior is called Abbot in what was
    read.
  personal: >-
    The man to whom our thoughts are shown before they are acted on, whose examination replaces our
    own discretion. A grey head is no guarantee, and the man who appoints himself Abbot before he has
    professed himself a disciple has "grown cold through pride." Even the master who is obeyed without
    argument submits what he does not know to those older in the Church.
  translational: >-
    'So the abbot was the boss - like a Benedictine abbot with a crozier?' - no: for Cassian "Abbot"
    is the honorific of an Egyptian father, never applied in what survives to a Gallic superior; what
    matters is the senior's examination of every thought, which replaces one's own judgment, and at
    Tours a master obeyed without a rule, who himself defers to "his elders" on what antiquity allows.
quick_meaning: >-
  The senior whose judgment replaces the junior's own, to whom every thought is shown. "Abbot" is an
  Egyptian father's title; at Tours it is one master with no title at all.
