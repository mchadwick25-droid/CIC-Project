    Single-voice (Salvian of Marseilles), a fourth Native voice outside the founding three, as
    government-of-god and lukewarmness already carry - a presbyter's indictment of Gaul's own
    ordinary provincial life, not a survey (his own translator's caution, carried throughout this
    world's own Salvian-derived terms). "Bagaudae" names revolted Roman peasantry, not barbarians
    proper (a distinction the translator's own note makes and this record keeps); Salvian's own text
    treats the distinction as beside his point - his target is Roman injustice, and the Bagaudae are
    its evidence, not its subject. This is the one place in this world's whole record where an
    ordinary rural Gallic Christian's own economic and legal suffering is named directly, rather than
    appearing only as the object of a bishop's mission (contrast gallic.term.heathen-rustics) - still
    one voice, one book, and still "thin relative to the historical scale of the collapse"
    (gallic.force.barbarian-fiscal-ruin's own words, which cites the same locus in one line; this
    record gives it a retrievable shape a participant can actually reach). Not licensed for the
    grace/free-will controversy (gallic.source.salvian-on-the-government-of-god's own restriction) -
    this term never touches it and does not need to.

plain_meaning: >-
  Salvian's name for Roman peasants driven to revolt, or to flee to the barbarians, by their own
  officials' greed. Not a barbarian people. Roman citizens, made into what their own rulers then
  called rebels.
world_word: Bagaudae (the many, proscribed by the few)
false_friend:
- Bagaudae as barbarian invaders - they were revolted Roman provincials
- a colorful footnote to the "real" history of monks and bishops
- the poor as passive victims only - some fled, some revolted, and Salvian names both as rational choices
senses:
  informational: >-
    Salvian's complaint does not stay inside the monastery. Of Gaul's own cities and villages he
    asks, "what cities are there, or even what municipalities and villages, in which there are not
    as many tyrants as curials?" - the local officials responsible for collecting the tax indiction
    behaved, in his account, as brigands, and "the very lifeblood of widows and orphans is not
    drained by the leading men of their states" alone but by the whole apparatus beneath them. When
    the wronged looked to the church for relief, Salvian says plainly that "even the priests of the
    Lord make no resistance to the violence of the unscrupulous. The majority of the clergy either
    say nothing, or, if they do speak, their words are no more effective than silence" - out of what
    they called prudence. So the poor were robbed, and some chose exile: they crossed over to the
    Goths or to the Bagaudae, "for they would rather live as free men, though in seeming captivity,
    than as captives in seeming liberty," and the name of Roman citizen, "once not only much valued
    but dearly bought," was "voluntarily repudiated and shunned." Others did not leave but were
    driven to revolt outright - the Bagaudae, "despoiled, afflicted, and murdered by wicked and
    bloodthirsty magistrates," whom Rome then named rebels for the crime Rome's own officials had
    forced on them: "by what other causes were they made Bagaudae? save by our unjust acts, the
    wicked decisions of the magistrates." Salvian's own verdict on the officials: "like wild beasts,
    instead of governing those put under their power," they "devoured them."
  evidential: >-
    Directly attested in Salvian alone (Gov. V.4-6, in Sanford's English), the one voice in this
    world's whole corpus that speaks of the rural and civic poor as its own subject rather than as a
    mission field. No corresponding material appears in Cassian, Sulpitius, Vincent, or the Lérins
    circle's own letters as read - this world's ascetic literature simply does not look at this layer
    of Gallic society. Salvian's own translator and this world's own force record (Doc_08 Cell 2A,
    Force 2A-5) both flag his account as an indictment, not a survey, and its scale as thin relative
    to the actual collapse - a limit this term states rather than corrects.
  personal: >-
    Cassian wrote for men who had already renounced the world; Sulpitius wrote for a saint's
    admirers; Vincent wrote for the antiquity of doctrine. Only one of us, a presbyter in the same
    city Cassian's own houses stood in, wrote for the ones who never got a Life written about them -
    the tenant driven off his own land, the widow the curials never stopped billing, the man who
    walked to a Gothic camp rather than stay a Roman.
  translational: >-
    "Peasant revolt" undersells it as far as Salvian is concerned - his case is that Rome, not the
    Bagaudae, committed the crime, and that fleeing to the barbarians was the rational choice of a
    free man, not a betrayal. A modern reader expecting this world's record to be only about monks
    finding God in the desert or bishops arguing about grace will not expect this passage at all.
quick_meaning: >-
  Salvian's own word for the ordinary Roman poor of Gaul. Their own officials' greed drove them to
  flee to the barbarians, or to revolt outright - and then they were blamed for it. The one place in
  our own record where the rural poor speak as a subject, not a mission field.

This term is not part of the original 81-term Doc_06 lexicon batch. Built directly
from a direct read of Gov. V.4-6 (not secondhand from
gallic.force.barbarian-fiscal-ruin's brief citation of the same locus), in response to a standing
finding from this world's own Doc_05 (Ecological Reconstruction) §10A Proportionality Assessment:
"Ordinary believers / rustics" rated **under** relative to probable ecology - "thin... not
reconstructable without inferring from silence." This term does not contradict that finding (the
material remains single-voice and thin relative to the historical scale of the collapse, disclosed
in divergence_note rather than smoothed) - it gives the one real, sourced piece of that thinness a
retrievable shape, following the project lead's own instruction to expand this world's use of
Salvian specifically because he is "the one Native voice... addressed outward to the lapsed
layperson rather than inward to the monk" (per this session's own prior turn).

Licensing check: gallic.source.salvian-on-the-government-of-god restricts Salvian from the
grace/free-will controversy only. This term draws on Book V's own social material, unrelated to that
controversy, and is licensed on the same basis government-of-god and lukewarmness already used his
text for lexicon content.
