plain_meaning: >-
  Our second sense of "grace" - a bestowed power or gift, such as healing or prophecy, which a
  saint has more or less of. It is not the grace that begins salvation.
world_word: grace as charism ("the gift of accomplishing cures")
false_friend:
- one word, one doctrine - Martin's healing "grace" read as the grace of the Massilian argument, or vice versa
- '"charism" as charisma or personality'
- '"spiritual gifts" as a modern charismatic category'
senses:
  informational: >-
    At Tours the word names something a saint has more or less of. "The gift of accomplishing
    cures was so largely possessed by Martin" that scarcely any sick person came to him without
    being restored. It rises and falls: after his one coerced communion with the Ithacians he cured
    the possessed "more slowly and with less grace than usual," and then, Gallus says, "he
    repaired, with manifold interest, his grace, which had been diminished for a time." At
    Marseilles the same sense is used of the Egyptian fathers and kept in its place: Abbot John was
    "exalted even to the grace of prophecy for his admirable obedience"; the gift of discretion is
    "the greatest prize of divine grace"; and Nesteros warns that miracles are "not always
    necessary, nor good for all, nor granted to all." In neither place is it the grace that
    "always co-operates with our will."
  evidential: >-
    Directly attested in both nodes in the same sense: Sulpitius (Vita XVI; Dial. II.4, III.10,
    III.13-14) and Cassian (Inst. IV.23; Conf. II.1, XIV.5, XIV.7, XV.1, XV.7, XVIII.15). The Latin
    gratia behind the English is supplied by Roberts's editorial footnotes - one of the few lemmas
    attested in the ancient text itself. Whether Martin's cures happened is not what this record
    asserts.
  personal: >-
    When we say Martin's grace was diminished by a bad communion and repaired with interest, we mean
    a power measured by what it does - cures, prophecy, command over demons. When we say grace
    anticipates the will, we mean the beginning and end of a soul's salvation. We hold the two
    apart without effort; they are not the same sentence.
  translational: >-
    A modern hearer meets one word, "grace," and expects one doctrine. We have two senses that share
    only the word. This one is a bestowed, measurable, losable power a saint possesses - closer to
    what a modern reader might call a gift of healing - and it must never be read as the grace of
    the Massilian argument.
quick_meaning: >-
  Our other sense of "grace" - a gift or power a saint holds, more or less. Martin's cures, a
  father's prophecy. It can be lost and repaired. Not the grace that saves.
