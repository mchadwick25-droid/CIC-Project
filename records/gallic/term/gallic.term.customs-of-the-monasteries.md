plain_meaning: >-
  What Cassian carried from Egypt and Palestine to a Gallic bishop's "new monastery." Not a written
  Rule of his own. Rather, "the customs of the monasteries which we have seen observed" there, "as
  they were there delivered to us by the Fathers." We keep them where Gaul's cold and our weak bodies
  allow. Where they do not, we balance them from Pontus and Mesopotamia.
world_word: the customs of the monasteries / the Institutes (instituta)
false_friend:
- a codified legal Rule - the Rule of Benedict, with a canonical abbot, a habit, and a novitiate
- Institutes as a theology textbook (Calvin's)
- customs as trivial house habits
senses:
  informational: >-
    A bishop asks for them: Castor of Apta Julia wants "the institutions of the East and especially of
    Egypt" established in a province "at present without monasteries." Cassian's rule for judging what
    he finds already in place is that anything added "according to the fancy of any one who has founded
    a monastery" yields to "the rule which I have seen followed in the monasteries anciently founded
    throughout Egypt and Palestine." The customs are not his; that is their whole authority. Their
    content is the twelve books: the dress, the canonical psalms and prayers, the receiving and
    training of renunciants, the eight faults and their remedies. And they are adapted in Cassian's own
    voice - "the severity of the winter does not allow us to be satisfied with slippers or tunics or a
    single frock"; the sheepskin "would afford a subject for derision" - and arrive as a standing
    correction of what Gaul already does: the hurried prostration, the loud Gloria "we have never heard
    anywhere throughout the East," going back to bed after Mattins, and no one persevering "even for a
    year." At Tours Marmoutier has its customs without the word: common property, no buying or selling,
    one meal, no wine, camel's hair - a discipline with no named rule, ordered by a master's example.
  evidential: >-
    The word is Cassian's alone (Institutes Preface, I.1-2, I.10, II.2-3, II.7-8, III.5, IV.2, IV.11;
    Conferences Prefaces II and III), and Gennadius corroborates the Institutes' contents. Sulpitius
    describes the thing at Marmoutier (Life ch. X) without the word. The Latin de institutis
    renuntiantium reaches us only through the editor's Prolegomena; the volume's regula witnesses are
    the editor's own Benedict and Pachomius footnotes, not this world's text. Institutes VI is omitted
    from the edition; VII-IX were read only for titles.
  personal: >-
    We hold these customs as debtors, in a province "frozen as it were with the cold of Gaul." What is
    ours is the refitting for the winter, the loud Gloria at the end of the psalm, and the honest
    admission that none of us has kept Egypt's perseverance a year. What is Egypt's is the rest - and
    we say so, because a custom's whole point is to keep a monk from being his own master, which is
    the Sarabaite's fault.
  translational: >-
    'So you had a monastic Rule, like Benedict's?' - no: we had no Rule of our own, and were proud of
    it; what we had was Egypt's customs, received as delivered by the Fathers, read in the cell as a
    correction of what we already did badly, and adapted only where the cold and our constitution
    forced it - the cross-references to Benedict in our English edition's footnotes are a
    nineteenth-century editor's, not ours.
quick_meaning: >-
  Egypt's monastic customs, received from the Fathers, not invented. Kept in Gaul as far as the cold
  allows, and admitted to be kept badly.
