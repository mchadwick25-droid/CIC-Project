plain_meaning: >-
  A monk, for us, is a man who has cut himself off from marriage, kin, property and the life of the
  world for a stricter life in Christ's service. The cut shows in his body and his dwelling. But at
  Tours a soldier still in the ranks could be "regarded as a monk," and so could a bishop in his see.
world_word: monk / solitary (monachus)
false_friend:
- a cloistered professional religious under a written Rule and an abbot
- someone who by definition is not a soldier, not a bishop, and not to be found in a city
- medieval monasticism in dress and discipline
senses:
  informational: >-
    The word names a cut before it names a place. Abbot Piamun, explaining the name to two visiting
    Gauls, says the first monks "abstained from marriage and cut themselves off from intercourse with
    their kinsmen and the life of this world," and were called monks or solitaries "from the strictness
    of their lonely and solitary life." His mark is that nothing is called his own. At Tours the word
    is worn by Martin while still a soldier, and kept by him as bishop: he "kept up the position of a
    bishop properly, yet in such a way as not to lay aside the objects and virtues of a monk." At
    Marseilles the word opens Cassian's book - "a monk, then, as a soldier of Christ" - and the kinds
    of monk are Egypt's kinds, received: coenobites under one Elder, anchorites trained first in the
    coenobium, and the Sarabaites, the reprehensible third kind who are their own masters. In the same
    city Salvian, writing for lapsed Romans, hardly uses the word; his name for the same people is
    "the religious," and when he does say "monks" he glosses it at once: "the servants of God."
  evidential: >-
    Attested directly in Sulpitius (Life chs. II, X; Dialogues II.11) and Cassian (Institutes I.1,
    IV.2; Conferences XVIII.4-7), independently of each other; corroborated by Gennadius ("Martin,
    monk and bishop") and by Salvian. Vincent calls himself a "Peregrinus" in "the seclusion of a
    Monastery" and never uses the word of himself. Neither Sulpitius nor Cassian defines the monk by a
    written Rule; neither has one. The Latin lemma monachus is attested in Hilary of Arles's own Latin
    and in the editor's apparatus, not against the vendored translation itself.
  personal: >-
    We know a monk by what he has given up and by how strictly he keeps the severance - not by where he
    sleeps. And among us the severed man is never out of the Church's reach: the churches come to
    Marmoutier for their priests, and we have seen numbers of these monks afterwards made bishops.
    We also know we fall short of Egypt's standard; Cassian could not recall anyone who joined our
    monasteries keeping Egypt's perseverance unbroken even for a year.
  translational: >-
    'Wasn't a monk by definition cloistered and withdrawn - the opposite of a soldier or a bishop?' -
    not for us: Martin was called a monk while still under arms and stayed one as bishop of Tours,
    because the word names a severance from kin, marriage and property kept strictly, not an
    enclosure, and no written Rule of our own defined him.
quick_meaning: >-

  Christ. At Tours even a soldier and a bishop wore the name.
