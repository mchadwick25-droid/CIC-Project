    Single-voice (Salvian of Marseilles), as government-of-god, lukewarmness, and
    gallic.term.bagaudae already carry - a presbyter's indictment of ordinary lay Christian practice
    in the same cities Cassian's own houses stood in, not a survey of how often churches actually

    argument (Sanford's Introduction, editorial, already flagged by this world's own
    government-of-god), and this term keeps that caution rather than reading "we" and "every day" as

    ordinary Gallic Christians' own worship attendance - not a monk's discipline, not a bishop's
    office, but whether the layperson down the street went to church or to the games. Distinct from
    gallic.term.lukewarmness (a monk's own interior cooling) - this is a description of a body's
    behavior, not a soul's temperature, though the two terms are plainly the same complaint aimed at
    two different audiences.

senses:
  informational: >-
    Salvian puts the comparison as a question to conscience: on a day when "we are celebrating a
    feast of the church and the public games," which draws the greater crowd of Christians - "the
    rows of seats at the public games or the court of God"? His answer, stated as observed fact, not
    rhetorical flourish alone: "on every day when the fatal games are given, whatever festival of the
    church it may be, not only do men who claim to be Christians fail to come to the services, but
    any who do happen to have come unwittingly, if they chance to hear, while in the church, that
    games are being given, leave the building at once." "The temple of God is scorned for a rush to
    the theater; the church is emptied and the circus filled; we leave Christ alone on the altar and
    feast our adulterous eyes on the foulest sights of the vile games." He grounds the charge in the
    baptismal vow itself: "the first confession of faith made by Christians in baptism" is "their vow
    to renounce the devil and his pomps and spectacles and his works" - so, in his own words,
    "spectacles and pomps are the works of the devil," renounced at the font and returned to on the
    very next feast day.
  evidential: >-
    Directly attested in Salvian alone (Gov. VI.5-7, in Sanford's English). No corresponding
    complaint about lay worship attendance appears anywhere in Cassian, Sulpitius, or Vincent as
    read - all three write about the formed, the already-committed, and none of them describes what
    an ordinary layperson actually did on a Sunday. Salvian is a preacher making an argument, and his
    numbers ("we," "every day") are rhetorical, not a survey - a caution this term keeps rather than
    smooths away.
  personal: >-
    We who left the world to keep the hours have one voice among us who stayed close enough to the
    world to watch it not keep them. He does not write about a monk's cell going cold. He writes
    about a church standing empty on its own feast day while the circus next door is full - and he
    names that, not a monk's private lukewarmness, the deeper wound.
  translational: >-
    Not "some people skipped church" as a mild modern shrug - Salvian's charge is closer to: people
    who had just promised, at their own baptism, to renounce exactly this, walked out mid-service the
    moment they heard the games had started, on the same day they were supposedly keeping a feast.
quick_meaning: >-
  Salvian's own charge against us: on a feast day, when the games ran too, church emptied and the
  circus filled. The one place in our own record that shows what lay worship really looked like -
  and how often it lost.

This term is not part of the original 81-term Doc_06 lexicon batch. Built directly
from a direct read of Gov. VI.5-7, alongside gallic.term.bagaudae and gallic.story.circuses-amid-the-
ruins, in response to the same Doc_05 §10A finding and the same project-lead instruction those two
records name. Kept distinct from gallic.term.lukewarmness by audience and register (a monk's interior
cooling vs. a layperson's public behavior) rather than folded into it, since the two terms answer
different participant questions and Salvian himself addresses two different audiences even within the
same book.

Licensing check: as gallic.term.bagaudae - this term draws on Book VI's own material on lay worship
and the games, unrelated to the grace/free-will controversy Salvian is not licensed for.
