plain_meaning: >-
  In Cassian's Marseilles, the sixth fault, "which we may term weariness or distress of heart,"
  "especially trying to solitaries." The noon assault that "produces dislike of the place, disgust
  with the cell, and disdain and contempt of the brethren." It makes the monk gaze at the sun "as if
  it was too slow in setting," and dresses his escape as pious duty until "the soldier of Christ
  becomes a runaway from His service, and a deserter."
world_word: accidie (acedia)

senses:
  informational: >-
    "Our sixth combat is with what the Greeks call akedia, which we may term weariness or distress of
    heart. This is akin to dejection, and is especially trying to solitaries"; it attacks "about the
    sixth hour," so that some of the elders "declare that this is the 'midday demon' spoken of in the
    ninetieth Psalm." Cassian describes it from inside the cell: "it produces dislike of the place,
    disgust with the cell, and disdain and contempt of the brethren"; the monk thinks other monasteries
    better and his own useless, "often goes in and out of his cell, and frequently gazes up at the sun,
    as if it was too slow in setting, and so a kind of unreasonable confusion of mind takes possession
    of him like some foul darkness." The escape it offers is dressed as charity - visiting the sick, or
    "that religious woman, devoted to the service of God, who is deprived of all support of kindred" -
    "until little by little he is drawn away from his cell, and begins to forget the object of his
    profession ... and so the soldier of Christ becomes a runaway from His service, and a deserter."
    The attack is twofold, sleep or flight; the remedy is the cell, held by labour.
  evidential: >-
    Cassian only (Institutes X.1-5), the elders' teaching by his framing. The lemma is the editor's
    supply; his Chaucer footnote is editorial and not used. Tours has no counterpart.
  personal: >-
    For a Gallic monk with the book in his cell, accidie is the name of what he feels at noon and the
    diagnosis of every good reason he has for leaving. That is why the south's discipline is so much
    about staying put, and why a charitable errand can be the devil's. The true Christian athlete
    "should hasten to expel this disease also from the recesses of his soul."
  translational: >-
    'Sloth - laziness? Or depression, burnout?' - none of these exactly: a specifically monastic,
    cell-bound, hour-bound assault by a spirit, the sixth of eight, that makes the cell hateful and
    the brethren contemptible, offers pious pretexts for leaving, and ends in desertion; fought like a
    combat by staying, and named as Egypt's elders named it.
quick_meaning: >-
  The noon assault on a monk in his cell. He hates the place, watches the sun, finds pious reasons to
  leave - and deserts unless he stays put.
