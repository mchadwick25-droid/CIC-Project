plain_meaning: >-
  Two things at once. At Marseilles, a fixed "canonical system" of psalms and prayers at set hours,
  received from Egypt as "brought down from heaven to the fathers by the ministry of an angel." At
  both Tours and Marseilles, the aim that system serves: prayer that never stops, as a blacksmith
  "in the midst of their work" beats his anvil.
world_word: unceasing prayer / the canonical system of psalms and prayers
false_friend:
- the Divine Office or Liturgy of the Hours as a settled, uniform book that all monks everywhere recited
- prayer as private devotion and feeling
senses:
  informational: >-
    The aim is the same in both houses. Of Martin: "Never did a single hour or moment pass in which he
    was not either actually engaged in prayer; or, if it happened that he was occupied with something
    else, still he never let his mind loose from prayer" - the blacksmith beating his anvil between
    strokes. Of every monk, Abbot Isaac: "The aim of every monk and the perfection of his heart tends
    to continual and unbroken perseverance in prayer." At Marseilles the system is the monk's second
    lesson after the girdle, and its number is not anyone's to change: the fathers watched one stand
    up, sing eleven psalms with prayers between, finish "the twelfth with a response of Alleluia," and
    vanish, so that twelve at evening and twelve at night "was no appointment of man's invention."
    The day hours are memorials of the third, sixth and ninth hour; the scaffold confesses that the
    building is beyond it - fixed hours are for those who "unless we are compelled ... by some rule"
    would spend the whole day without prayer. It is also the house's hardest sanction short of
    expulsion: the man "suspended from prayer" is "delivered unto Satan," and no one may pray with him.
  evidential: >-
    The system is attested in Cassian alone (Institutes II-IV, read in full at Doc_05; Conferences
    Preface I, IX.2), Egyptian in content, with Cassian's own first-person remarks on Gaul: the
    hurried prostration "in this country," the loud Gloria "we have never heard anywhere throughout
    the East," the morning office "in the monasteries of Gaul," and some "in this province" going back
    to bed. Gennadius corroborates the title. Unceasing prayer as aim is attested at Tours (Life chs.
    X, XXVI; Letter III; Dialogues II.2) - the aim without the system. What Marmoutier sang, and at
    what hours, no one wrote down; Gibson's footnotes supplying synaxis, missa and the later
    office-names are editorial and excluded from the world's voice.
  personal: >-
    One thing in the office is ours outright - the loud "Glory be to the Father" sung standing at the
    end of the psalm - and for the rest we are charged with hurrying and oversleeping. But the point of
    the hours is to be exceeded: continual prayer joined to work is worth more, "and more acceptable as
    a free gift than the duties which are performed by the compulsion of a rule." To belong among us is
    to be allowed to pray with the brethren.
  translational: >-
    'Did you all pray the Divine Office, with Lauds and Prime and Compline?' - the later names are a
    nineteenth-century editor's; what Cassian's houses had was an Egyptian pattern of twelve psalms
    received as angelic and still being argued over (a new morning office was admitted "after long
    discussion" in his own day), kept imperfectly in Gaul with one loud Gallic addition, and what
    Martin's own house sang is unknown - the aim, prayer that never stops, is the only part attested
    everywhere.
quick_meaning: >-
  Prayer that never stops, the aim of every monk. At Marseilles, a received Egyptian system of twelve
  psalms at fixed hours built to serve that aim.
