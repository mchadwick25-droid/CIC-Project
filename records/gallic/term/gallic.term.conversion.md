plain_meaning: >-
  In Cassian's Marseilles, "conversion" is not a change of religion. It is the act of turning to the
  monastic life: "the early days of their conversion were so bright"; a murderer's "compulsory
  conversion" turned "into a voluntary one." At Tours the same English word names what a robber or a
  rustic does when he turns to Christ.
world_word: conversion (conversio)
false_friend:
- a private inner event of coming to faith - evangelical conversion, "born again"
- a change of religion, so that a monk's "conversion" is read as his becoming a Christian
senses:
  informational: >-
    In the south the word marks the beginning of a monk. Paphnutius warns that a bright start
    guarantees nothing - "it was of no avail that the early days of their conversion were so bright" -
    and Moses of Calamus, who fled to the desert to escape death for a murder, "made such use of his
    compulsory conversion that with ready zeal he turned it into a voluntary one." Germanus confesses
    that "in the fervour of the early days of my conversion I made light of the mere worldly
    substance" while the inner man stayed entangled; the manual's ladder puts "a start in conversion"
    first. Salvian, in the same city, knows the same use and its failure: the religious who "now seek
    after new honors - do they not repent of their conversion?"; the man professing continence of body
    and running riot in spirit: "this is not conversion to God but aversion from him." At Tours the
    word is what happens to others: "the robber believed"; Martin "could not visit the house of an
    unconverted heathen." No one there calls entry to Marmoutier a conversion.
  evidential: >-
    Cassian (Conferences III.5, III.7; Institutes IV.39) and Salvian (V.10, III.11, within his
    licence) for the monastic sense; Sulpitius (Life chs. V, XIII, XVII) for the other referent. The
    Latin lemma is attested only editorially, in an unread section.
  personal: >-
    A conversion here is what a man does with his feet and his goods; its test is whether he keeps it
    warm. When a southern text of ours says "conversion" it is talking about the day a man entered the
    monastery, and the bright start is the thing we most fear to see cool. Martin's own turning was a
    discharge before Caesar, and the eighty at Marmoutier are "disciples," not converts.
  translational: >-
    'So a monk's "conversion" was when he became a Christian?' - no: in the south it was the day he
    entered the monastic life, voluntary or "compulsory," judged by whether its early fervour was kept;
    at Tours the same word meant a robber or a rustic household turning to Christ under Martin's
    preaching - two referents that the modern "born again" hearing runs together.
quick_meaning: >-
  In the south, the day a man turned to the monastic life, not a change of religion. At Tours, a
  robber or a rustic turning to Christ.
