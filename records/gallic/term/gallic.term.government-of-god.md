senses:
  informational: >-
    Salvian begins with the objection he writes to answer: "By certain men God is said to be
    careless and neglectful of human actions, on the ground that he neither protects good men nor
    restrains the wicked." His answer is the pilot: as the helmsman "never takes his hand from the
    tiller, so God" governs, and "never turns his most gracious eyes from the whole extent of the
    world." God sees, governs, and judges - and judges now. "We are judged by the ever-present
    judgment of God, and thus a most slothful race has been aroused to accomplish our destruction
    and shame"; "the country of the Belgae burst into flames, then the rich estates of the luxurious
    Aquitanians, and after these the whole body of the Gallic provinces." The ruin of Gaul is not an
    accident God permitted but a sentence God is passing, on Christians, for the sins he spends
    eight books naming.
  evidential: >-
    Directly attested in Salvian alone (Gov. I.1, VII.10, VII.12, in Sanford's English), with
    Gennadius as ancient witness to the title. The founding monastic voices have judgment but not
    this one: theirs is a judgment to come, an expectation or a motive, not a judgment being carried
    out in the provinces. Salvian is the one voice of ours that admits the barbarians into its text
    at all.
  personal: >-
    One of us, a presbyter at Marseilles, had a word for the age's catastrophe, and he heard the
    catastrophe as a sentence. The rest of us expected judgment soon; he said it had begun, and was
    ours to answer for.
  translational: >-
    A modern hearer may take Salvian for a historian of Rome's fall, or take "providence" as a
    comfort and "judgment" as the end of the world. His is a preacher's indictment, not a survey: a
    God who never leaves the tiller and is judging now, in the flames, for what Roman Christians
    have done.
