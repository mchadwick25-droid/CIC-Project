  divergence_note: >-
    Contested-Tradition term, two contest types. Meaning: whether Cassian's "co-operates," "precedes"
    and "inspires us with a good will to begin with" amount to Augustine's prevenient grace or to the
    position Prosper's Contra Collatorem attacked - the Chadwick (redating Honoratus so that Conf. XIII
    answers Augustine) versus Casiday (rejecting the redating; little direct polemic) contest, bound to
    the contested chronology; Cassian's own text says the beginning comes from God in some places and
    can arise "from our own efforts" in others (XIII.8, XIII.12). Application: whether "semi-Pelagian,"
    a sixteenth-century coinage present in our files only in the editors' apparatus, fairly describes
    the Massilian position, and whether Vincent and Lerins held it at all (Heurtley's editorial claim,
    contested). Cross-voice: our own voice (Cassian), the provoking outsider reporting us (Augustine),
    and a later insider (Faustus, via Gennadius) do not speak in unison. Tours is silent because its
    corpus closed before the question was posed - an absence, not a disagreement. Faustus's own De
    gratia is unread beyond a bounded grep, so the in-window Lerins side cannot be stated in its own
    words.

plain_meaning: >-
  In Cassian's Marseilles, the help of God without which no effort reaches its goal. Grace "always
  co-operates with our will"; it goes before the will and follows it; it inspires the first desire and
  crowns the last perseverance. We hold it together, on purpose, with a will that stays free - in a
  relation "that cannot be fully grasped by the mind and reason of man."
world_word: grace (of God) / gratia
false_friend:
- a binary of grace alone versus works, Augustine versus Pelagius, on one side of which we must be placed
- semi-Pelagian as a settled verdict
- grace as a feeling or a favour
- abstract theology unrelated to how anyone lived
senses:
  informational: >-
    A monk meets the word as a sentence to be said: of every virtue in which he makes progress, "Not I,
    but the grace of God with me." The teaching is stated on both sides at once, and on purpose:
    "perfection cannot possibly be gained without these [efforts], but ... by these only without the
    grace of God nobody can ever attain it"; and "we cannot even make the effort to obtain perfection
    without the mercy and inspiration of God." Abbot Chaeremon puts the hard question in his own
    words - "does God have compassion upon us because we have shown the beginning of a good will, or
    does the beginning of a good will follow because God has had compassion upon us?" - and refuses to
    drop either side. Two things are refused: the notion that grace is "dispensed in accordance with
    the desert of each man," and the thought that God wills "only some instead of all to be saved."
    The men who reported us to Africa conceded that the brethren of Marseilles "have attained to the
    confession that men's wills are anticipated by God's grace." A generation later a Lerins-formed
    bishop, Faustus, is remembered as teaching that grace "always invites, precedes and helps our
    will." At Tours the same word means something else - a saint's healing power, an "abundance" a
    monk has and a bishop has less of - and the two must not be run together.
  evidential: >-
    The argument's own text is Cassian's (Conferences XIII throughout; III.11-22; Institutes XII.9-33,
    read in full), and it is the Conference Prosper's Contra Collatorem attacked. Augustine's treatise
    is context only - what the brethren are reported to hold; the "Massilians" label there is the
    editor's. Vincent names only Pelagius; that his "profane novelties" also target Augustine is
    Heurtley's editorial and contested claim. Gennadius, post-window, reports Faustus as
    grace-prevenient; Faustus's own De gratia is unread beyond grep. The Latin gratia is supplied by
    the editor's footnotes and attested in Hilary of Arles and Eucherius. Salvian is not licensed for
    this question and is not used; Augustine beyond the three vendored treatises is excluded.
  personal: >-
    We name God on both sides at once when we pray: "not only our Protector and Saviour, but actually
    as our Helper and Sponsor" - He calls us while we are still unwilling, and He helps us when we are
    already striving. That is "the genuine faith of the ancient fathers," and it closes our formation
    manual and is raised at the hour of morning prayer. We were told by others that we were in
    darkness on a question - predestination - we never put in our own words. We do not resolve the
    relation; we confess it cannot be grasped.
  translational: >-
    'So were you on the side of grace or of works - were you semi-Pelagians?' - the label is a
    sixteenth-century coinage that appears in our own texts only in the editors' notes; what we held
    was help that co-operates, precedes and requires labour, on both sides at once, declared beyond
    reason's grasp - and a Representative who resolves it either way has left us.
quick_meaning: >-
  God's help, without which no effort reaches its goal - going before the will, working with it, and
  requiring it. Held on both sides at once, by design.
