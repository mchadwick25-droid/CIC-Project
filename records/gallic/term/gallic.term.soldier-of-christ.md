plain_meaning: >-
  The picture that governs a monk's life among us. A man has changed service - from Caesar's army, or
  from a worldly career - to Christ's. He now stands girded, under oath, in daily combat against the
  devil and his own faults. The one who slackens is not a failure but a deserter.
world_word: soldier of Christ
false_friend:
- spiritual warfare as a private inner metaphor for temptation
- the rhetoric of a militant, crusading Christianity
- a devotional phrase with no institutional or biographical content
senses:
  informational: >-
    At Tours the picture is a biography before it is a figure of speech. Martin, on the eve of battle,
    refuses the emperor's donative: "Hitherto I have served you as a soldier: allow me now to become a
    soldier to God ... I am the soldier of Christ: it is not lawful for me to fight." He offers to
    stand unarmed before the line "protected by the sign of the cross, and not by shield or helmet."
    At Martin's funeral the monks walk as "young soldiers who had just taken the oath of allegiance to
    Christ." At Marseilles the same phrase opens Cassian's whole program of formation as a matter of
    dress and order: the girdle first, then the canonical psalms, then the faults fought in sequence -
    "our first struggle," "our sixth combat" - and the man who leaves his cell becomes "a runaway from
    His service, and a deserter." Even the argument about grace is fought in this idiom: Job is God's
    "well tried athlete." At Lerins Vincent names only what he has left, "the tempests of secular
    warfare," and calls the monastery a harbour.
  evidential: >-
    Directly attested in Sulpitius (Life chs. I, IV; Letter III; Dialogues II.11) and in Cassian
    (Institutes I.1, I.11, II.1, V.19, X.3; Conferences XIII.14), the two never citing each other.
    Vincent gives the antonym only. The Latin miles Christi is not attested in the vendored
    translation and was not found in the bounded grep of the Lerins Latin; militia appears only as
    Vincent's secularis militia in the editor's Introduction.
  personal: >-
    For us this is not a metaphor we chose but a discharge that happened: Martin stood before Caesar
    and said it was not lawful for him to fight. Everything after keeps the soldier's grammar - the
    girdle is armour, the faults are an order of battle, the devil is an enemy who is really there,
    growing cold is desertion, and the deathbed is a last stand. And our whole warfare is fought with
    the cross, prayer, fasting, watching and the cell, never with weapons.
  translational: >-
    'Isn't "soldier of Christ" just a metaphor - or, worse, the language of holy war?' - for Martin it
    was a literal change of service, a real discharge before Caesar and a real oath, and it forbade
    fighting rather than licensing it; the enemy is the devil and one's own faults, and the weapons are
    the cross, prayer and the cell.
quick_meaning: >-
  A man who has changed service, from Caesar's to Christ's, and now fights the devil and his own
  faults under oath - with the cross, not with weapons.
