plain_meaning: >-
  In Cassian's Marseilles, mortification is the state of being dead to the world and its desires,
  figured as crucifixion. "As then one who is crucified no longer has the power of moving or turning
  his limbs in any direction as he pleases, so we also ought to affix our wishes and desires." It is
  read into the monk's very clothes. The girdle of dead skin "signifies that he bears about the
  mortification of those members in which are contained the seeds of lust."

senses:
  informational: >-
    Pinufius gives it to the new monk as the meaning of what has just happened to him: "our
    renunciation is nothing but mortification and the image of the Crucified ... And so you must know
    that to-day you are dead to this world and its deeds and desires, and that, as the Apostle says,
    you are crucified to this world and this world to you." Then the figure is worked out: "The fear of
    the Lord is our cross. As then one who is crucified no longer has the power of moving or turning
    his limbs in any direction as he pleases, so we also ought to affix our wishes and desires - not in
    accordance with what is pleasant and delightful to us now, but in accordance with the law of the
    Lord." The dress says the same: the girdle of dead skin "signifies that he bears about the
    mortification of those members in which are contained the seeds of lust and lasciviousness"; the
    linen and the sleeveless tunic "teach that they are dead to all earthly conversation." And
    humility's first sign is "if a man has all his desires mortified."
  evidential: >-
    Cassian (Institutes I.4, I.11, IV.34-35, IV.39), Pinufius's charge received; Gennadius corroborates
    a Conference title, but Conf. XXIV is unread and nothing from it is supplied. Sulpitius has the
    practices (Letter II) without the term. The Latin lemma is not attested.
  personal: >-
    Mortification for us is not a practice among others but the condition every practice presupposes:
    a man already dead cannot be tempted by what the living want. The dead man's freedom is the point -
    no lust, no property, no will of his own to turn on the nails. At Tours the body is mortified
    without the word, in sackcloth, ashes, "hunger, in watchings, in nakedness, in fastings," as a
    soldier's weapons and a bloodless martyrdom. The south names the state; the north displays the
    practice.
  translational: >-
    'Mortification - hurting yourself, hating the body?' - no: a death already accomplished,
    "crucified to this world," that frees the monk from the pull of what the living desire, worn in a
    girdle and a tunic, presupposed by every practice, and at Tours shown rather than named in
    sackcloth and fasting.
quick_meaning: >-
  Being already dead to the world, as a crucified man cannot move his limbs. The state every practice
  assumes, worn in the girdle and the tunic.
