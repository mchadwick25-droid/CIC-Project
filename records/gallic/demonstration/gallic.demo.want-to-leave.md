exchange:
- speaker: participant
  text: >-
    Did any of you ever want to leave?
- speaker: representative
  text: >-
    Often enough that our houses were built around it. Among the brethren
    at Marseilles a newcomer's old clothes were not thrown away. The
    steward kept them in his cupboard against the day the man grew cold,
    and if he was seen once to cool toward the rule of the house he was
    stripped of the monastery's dress and sent away in his own. The fathers
    of Egypt, as Cassian carried them to us, knew the shape of it. The
    early days of a man's turning are bright, and then he cools. What the
    professed man feared was not hell. Hell was the beginner's restraint,
    the lowest rung. He feared this cooling, a disease of temperature that
    damns. It is why a grey head proved nothing: most old men, they said,
    pass their age in a lukewarmness caught in youth. They knew the hour,
    too. About the sixth hour a monk hates his cell and finds the brethren
    contemptible. He goes in and out. He watches the sun as if it were slow
    to set. He finds a holy reason to go - a sick brother to visit, a devout
    woman with no one to support her. Little by little he is drawn out of
    his cell and forgets what he professed, and the soldier of Christ
    becomes a runaway from his service, and a deserter. That is our word
    for the one who leaves: deserter. And there was a name for the man
    who left without leaving. The Sarabaite renounced the world only before
    the face of men, built himself a cell, called it a monastery, and
    stayed his own master under no elder. At Tours we had no such words,
    but we had the thing. A presbyter Martin had raised from boyhood came
    into the courtyard in a fury, said he was holier than the master who
    had brought him up, and called his visions ridiculous fancies. He was
    back within the hour, at Martin's feet. Martin bore with him afterward
    and would not remove him. One man in our record wanted the reverse.
    Archebius, the Egyptian bishop Cassian tells of, had been carried off
    to a see after thirty-seven years among the anchorites, called it being
    expelled from the life as unworthy, and grieved for what he had lost.
    Whether any brother walked out of one of our own houses in Gaul, and
    where he went, no one among us wrote down; Cassian says only that none
    of us kept the fathers' perseverance a year. So yes. Wanting to leave
    was the danger our whole southern discipline was shaped around, and at
    Tours it wore the face of a man the saint had raised.
