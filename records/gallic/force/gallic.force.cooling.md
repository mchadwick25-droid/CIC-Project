description: >-
  The failure the southern program names most often is a loss of heat. The renunciant who 'grow[s]
  cold in regard to the rule of their institution' is stripped and sent away (Inst. IV.6). The monk
  of accidie is 'a runaway from His service, and a deserter' (X.3). The house measures itself
  against the primitive Church, while its fervent faith 'had not yet grown lukewarm by being
  dispersed among the many' (II.5). The Sarabaite has made 'renunciation only as a public
  profession.' He is cast down 'by the lukewarmness of their purpose' (Conf. XVIII.7). And the
  standard itself is one no Gallic monk kept 'unbroken even for a year' (IV.2).

  For the brethren, a man's conversion was bright at first, and then he cooled. His worldly clothes
  waited with the steward for that day. Cooling was desertion, and the deserter's name was
  Sarabaite. He was his own master, living two or three together, calling his cell a monastery. And
  the province was cold.

  Cooling was the named failure of the interior road, the south's program of purity of heart. It was
  also the shape of that program's inner story: fervour, then cooling. The received standard could
  not be kept, so it bred this fear. The fear kept the program severe: a community that fears
  cooling keeps stripping and re-clothing. The vocabulary is southern.
