text: >-
  The tradition tells - as Abbot Piamun told it to Cassian and Germanus, and as Cassian wrote it down
  for Gaul - of the patience of Paphnutius, when he was young.

  Paphnutius, Piamun said, loved the desert of Scete so much that the other anchorites called him
  "Bubalis" "because he always delighted in dwelling in the desert as if with a sort of innate liking."
  Even as a boy he was so grave and steadfast that the great men of the time "put him on a level with
  the Elders out of regard for his virtues, and thought fit to admit him to their order." And that was
  the trouble: "the same envy, which formerly excited the minds of his brethren against the patriarch
  Joseph, inflamed one out of the number of his brethren with a burning and consuming jealousy."

  So this brother, wanting "to mar his beauty by some blemish or spot," waited until Paphnutius had
  gone to church on a Sunday, slipped into his cell, and "slyly hid his own book among the boughs which
  he used to weave of palm branches," and went off to church himself "as if with a pure and clean
  conscience." When the service ended, "in the presence of all the brethren he brought his complaint to
  S. Isidore who was Presbyter of this desert before this same Paphnutius, and declared that his book
  had been stolen from his cell." Nobody knew what to think - no theft had ever been heard of in that
  desert before, or since. The accuser himself proposed the remedy: keep everyone in church and send
  chosen men to search the cells one by one. Three elders searched, "and at last found the book hidden
  in the cell of Paphnutius among the boughs of the palms ... just as the plotter had hidden it."

  Then, the tradition says, Paphnutius - "although he was perfectly clear in the sincerity of his
  conscience, yet like one who acknowledged the guilt of thieving, gave himself up entirely to make
  amends and humbly asked for a plan of repentance, as he was so careful of his shame and modesty (and
  feared) lest if he tried to remove the stain of the theft by words, he might further be branded as a
  liar, as no one would believe anything but what had been found out." He left the church "not cast
  down in mind but rather trusting to the judgment of God," wept at his prayers, fasted three times as
  often, and for almost a fortnight came early on Saturday and Sunday "not to receive the Holy
  Communion but to prostrate himself on the threshold of the Church and humbly ask for pardon."

  And then God, "Who is the witness of all secret things," would not let him be tried longer: the true
  thief, "possessed by a most fierce demon, he made known all the craft of his secret plot, and the
  same man who had conceived the accusation and the cheat betrayed it." No one could free him - not
  the saints of that desert, not Isidore himself, whose gift was such that "no one who was possessed
  was ever brought to his doors without being at once healed" - "for Christ was reserving this glory
  for the young Paphnutius, that the man should be cleansed only by the prayers of him against whom he
  had plotted." Piamun ends: "If then we want to attain to his height of virtue, we must lay the same
  foundation to begin with."
absent_detail: >-
  The accuser is never named. What Cassian witnessed was the telling, not the events; the possession
  and the reserved cure are the tradition's own claims. Nothing here places any of it in Gaul - what is
  Gaul's own is only that its monks were told, in the same sentence, "we must lay the same foundation."
modern_contrast: >-
  A modern reader expects the innocent man to defend himself, and reads his silence as either weakness
  or self-punishment. The tradition gives its own reason in the text: Paphnutius feared that words
  would only add "liar" to "thief," so he trusted the judgment of God and did the penance. And the
  exorcism at the end is careful on exactly the point Cassian's fathers warned about - the cure is
  reserved to the wronged man's prayers, and he never "professed himself" anything. Set beside Tours,
  where Martin reproved Brictio and bore with him, the same wound - a community wronged from within -
  is answered at Scete by the accused's silence before the community.
