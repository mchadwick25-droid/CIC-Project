text: >-
  The tradition tells - as Abbot Piamun told it to Cassian and Germanus, and as Cassian wrote it down
  for Gaul - of the patience of Paphnutius, when he was young.

  Paphnutius loved the desert of Scete so much that the other monks called him Bubalis. Even as a boy
  he was so serious and steady that the great men of that time admired him, and let him join the
  Elders' own order early, though he was younger than the rest. That honor bred trouble: one brother
  grew bitterly jealous of him, with an envy Piamun compares to the envy Joseph's own brothers felt.

  So this brother made a plan to ruin Paphnutius's good name. One Sunday, while Paphnutius was at
  church, the brother slipped into his cell and hid his own book among the palm branches Paphnutius
  used for his weaving. Then he went to church himself, acting as though his conscience were clean.
  When the service ended, he told Presbyter Isidore, in front of everyone, that his own book had been
  stolen from his cell. No one had ever heard of a theft in that desert before, and it never happened
  again - the whole community was stunned. The accuser himself proposed the remedy: keep everyone in
  church, and send chosen elders to search the cells one by one. Three elders searched, and found the
  book hidden in Paphnutius's own cell among the palm boughs, exactly where the plotter had hidden it.

  Paphnutius knew he was innocent. But he said nothing in his own defense: he judged that if he tried to
  clear his name in words, no one would believe him over what had already been found, and he would only
  be branded a liar on top of a thief. So he gave himself up entirely, asked to do penance, and left the
  church not cast down, but trusting God's own judgment. He wept at his prayers and fasted three times
  as often as before. For almost a fortnight, he came early every Saturday and Sunday - not to receive
  Communion, but to lie at the threshold of the church and beg for pardon.

  Then God, who witnesses every secret thing, let the trial end. The true thief became possessed by a
  demon, and in his torment he confessed the whole plot himself - the very man who had invented the
  accusation now betrayed it. No one could free him: not the saints of that desert, and not even
  Isidore, whose gift for healing the possessed had never once failed before. Christ had reserved that
  healing for the young Paphnutius alone - the accuser could be cleansed only by the prayers of the man
  he had wronged.

  Piamun closes with the lesson he drew from it: Paphnutius showed this character even as a boy, laying
  in his youth the foundation of the virtue he grew into. Anyone who wants to reach that height, Piamun
  says, must lay the same foundation to begin with.
absent_detail: >-
  The accuser is never named. What Cassian witnessed was the telling, not the events; the possession
  and the reserved cure are the tradition's own claims. Nothing here places any of it in Gaul - what is
  Gaul's own is only that its monks were told, in the same sentence, "we must lay the same foundation."
modern_contrast: >-
  A modern reader expects the innocent man to defend himself, and reads his silence as either weakness
  or self-punishment. The tradition gives its own reason in the text: Paphnutius feared that words
  would only add "liar" to "thief," so he trusted the judgment of God and did the penance. And the
  exorcism at the end is careful on exactly the point Cassian's fathers warned about - the cure is
  reserved to the wronged man's prayers, and he never "professed himself" anything. Set beside Tours,
  where Martin reproved Brictio and bore with him, the same wound - a community wronged from within -
  is answered at Scete by the accused's silence before the community.
