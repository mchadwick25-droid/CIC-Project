text: >-
  In his letter to the emperor Trajan, Pliny the Younger, governor of
  Bithynia-Pontus, records that he was uncertain how to handle accusations
  against Christians. He asked the emperor directly for guidance. He describes
  questioning those accused, and executing those who persisted in the name
  after repeated warning. Wanting to learn more about what these people
  actually did, he tells us he tortured two enslaved women, called ministrae.
  (Some read that term as a functional title, 'deaconesses,' though the
  reading is contested.) What he found, in his own words, was 'nothing except
  a superstition depraved and immoderate.' What his witnesses told him was
  this. They were accustomed to come together on a fixed day before daylight,
  and to sing responsively a song to Christ as God. They bound themselves with
  an oath - not to commit some crime, but that they would not commit theft,
  nor robbery, nor adultery, that they would not break faith, nor refuse to
  restore a deposit when asked for it. After that, their custom was to
  separate, and to assemble again to share a meal, 'common yet harmless.'
  Trajan's reply is also preserved. It tells Pliny he has followed the right
  course. Christians are not to be sought out. If accused and convicted, they
  are to be punished, with pardon for anyone who denies the name and proves it
  by act. And anonymous accusations are to be refused entirely, being 'of most
  evil precedent.'
telling: "Pliny the Younger was the Roman governor of Bithynia and Pontus. Writing to the emperor Trajan, he says he was unsure how to handle accusations against Christians, so he asked the emperor. He had already questioned those accused, and executed those who held to the name after repeated warnings.\n\nSome of the accused told him they had once been Christians but had given it up. They said they used to meet on a fixed day before dawn and sing a song in turns to Christ as God. They had bound themselves by an oath to commit no theft, robbery or adultery, and never to break faith or refuse to return a deposit. Then they parted, and later met again to share a meal of ordinary food, which they said was harmless. What that meal was, we cannot say for certain.\n\nTo learn the truth, he tortured two enslaved women who were called ministrae. It may be a title, deaconesses, though that reading is disputed. The women left no words of their own, and we have only what Pliny chose to record. What he found, in his own words, was \"nothing but a depraved superstition that went too far.\"\n\nTrajan's reply survives, and it says Pliny had acted rightly. Christians were not to be hunted out. Those accused and convicted were to be punished, and anyone who denied the name and proved it by act was to be pardoned. Anonymous accusations were to be refused as a very bad precedent."
absent_detail: "The two enslaved women who were tortured have no surviving words of their own - what survives is only what Pliny chose to record of what they, under torture, were made to say."
modern_contrast: >
  A modern reader often hears "interrogated under torture" and expects
  the confession to reveal something genuinely sinister, or reads
  "ministrae" as confirming a settled office of deaconess. This world's
  own record frames it differently: what Pliny found, in his own words,
  was "nothing except a superstition depraved and immoderate" - banal,
  not criminal - and the ministrae's own exact role remains genuinely
  unclear, not a confirmed title.
