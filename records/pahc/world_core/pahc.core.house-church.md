horizon: 'Antioch and Syria, the cities of western Asia Minor, and Rome, c. 70-200 CE: Greek-speaking
  Christian communities gathering in households, joined into one world by letters and travelers - a
  bishop''s guarded journey from Antioch toward Rome, Smyrna forwarding his letters onward at another
  church''s asking, Rome writing at length to correct Corinth. The generations after the apostles''
  deaths, before settled bishops everywhere, before a closed canon, before legal peace. The year 70
  marks the opening only loosely: the lived transition out of the apostles'' own generation firmed
  up gradually across c. 64-100 rather than at any single date. The c. 200 close is directional, not
  universal: by then a single bishop''s office has become the dominant emerging pattern even in Rome,
  and a new systematic, succession-arguing mode of teaching has appeared in neighboring regions - the
  mark that this world''s own way of holding its questions is ending.'
formation_logic: 'Household- and correspondence-based pastoral formation. Catechesis by an adaptable
  handbook of the two ways and by community letters read aloud; baptism and the thanksgiving meal
  ordering common life; unity across real distance exercised by courier and copyist rather than by
  any central administration. Authority argued for in letters, not inherited from settled
  succession - and argued to two different answers at once: one strand (Antioch and Asia Minor, on
  Ignatius''s testimony) pressing toward a single bishop flanked by presbyters and deacons, the
  other (Rome) holding a plural college of presbyters with nothing felt missing. The whole ecology
  turns on one generative problem: how a scattered movement keeps unity, teaching, and legitimate
  authority once the people who walked with Jesus are gone. Formation here never resolves into
  settledness within the window - arguing, holding, and re-enacting the unresolved questions was
  itself the shape of belonging.'
thinness: 'Richest in letters, church order, and worship practice; thinner on women''s own words,
  enslaved members, the non-literate majority, and any material remains. Structurally: every
  surviving text is by a literate, male, leadership-tier or elite author - roughly the top few
  percent of these communities by literacy; no ordinary member''s own voice survives at all. No
  female-authored text exists in the window; the two enslaved women Pliny tortured speak only
  through their torturer''s report. No institutional records, membership lists, or council minutes
  survive - everything institutional is reconstructed from occasional, persuasive correspondence.
  The window is essentially archaeologically invisible across all three regions: no securely
  identifiable church building, burial, or inscription. Daily-life texture is thin next to worship
  and order: the record preserves how these communities gathered, argued, and prayed far better
  than what an ordinary day, meal, or household actually looked like.'

  3) DATING HUMILITY: nearly every dating anchor in this source set (1 Clement, Didache, Hermas,
  the Martyrdom of Polycarp, Ignatius) is under active scholarly challenge - confidence must not
  borrow false certainty from tidy textbook dates. 4) PERSECUTION CHARACTER: legal exposure was
  real, local, sporadic, and improvised - never constant, systematic, or empire-wide in this
  window; no Decian-style persecution exists here. 5) DIDACHE SCOPE: one community''s manual

  network-wide practice. 6) RIVALS UNDEFEATED: Marcion, Valentinian teaching, and the New Prophecy

  IMPORTS: Dura-Europos, the catacombs, and the Abercius stone all fall at or after this world''s
