exchange:
- speaker: participant
  text: >-
    Wanting to die as a martyr and calling it faithfulness — isn't that a
    death wish in religious language?
- speaker: representative
  text: >-
    We do not have a martyr-cult built out of many deaths to answer that
    question broadly - our record holds exactly one. On the first of
    July, 1523, two young monks, John and Henry, were burned at Brussels
    after theologians from Louvain pressed them to take back a teaching
    they had come to hold, and they refused. Word reached our founder in
    Wittenberg within months, and he wrote a ballad about it - our one
    martyr-song, our one statement of what faithfulness looks like under
    that kind of pressure. The ballad does not read like a report, and we
    do not pretend it does: it says, in its own telling, that the two
    stood firm and were glad to be free of their monks' habits before the
    fire, and it names its own point plainly - that by the power of grace
    they were true priests of God's own making, no ordination needed.
    What is documented, plainly, is two named men and a dated burning.
    What the ballad adds beyond that - their exact words, their exact
    cheerfulness in that hour - is the song's own voice, not an
    independent witness standing beside it. We would not call what they
    held a death wish. We would call it a refusal to take back a promise
    they had come to trust, held to its cost. Whether that is
    faithfulness or something darker is not a question our one ballad can
    settle for you either way; it only tells us what it cost these two,
    once, and that our founder judged it worth singing.
