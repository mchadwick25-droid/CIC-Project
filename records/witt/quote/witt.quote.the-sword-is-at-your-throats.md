text: >-
  The sword is at your throats, but you think yourselves so
  firm in the saddle that no one can unhorse you. This false
  security and stubborn perversity will break your necks, as
  you will discover. I have often told you before to beware
  of the saying, in Psalm cvi, Effundit contemptum
  super principes, “He poureth contempt upon princes.”
  You are striving after it, and want to be smitten over the
  head, and no warning or exhorting will help you to avoid it.

  Well, then, since you are the cause of this wrath of God,
  it will undoubtedly come upon you, if you do not mend your
  ways in time. The signs in heaven and the wonders on earth
  are meant for you, dear lords; they bode no good for you,
  and no good will come to you.

modern_lens_note: >-
  A modern reader who knows only the later tract against the peasants may expect Luther to have stood with
  the rulers from the start. In this earlier writing of the same spring he does not. He tells the princes,
  and in the lines just before these the bishops too, that they are the cause of the rising and of God's
  anger. He is not, though, taking the peasants' side: the same book turns next to the peasants and
  rebukes them as sharply. "The sword is at your throats" is a warning of God's judgment, not a call for
  anyone to take up arms against the princes. "Psalm cvi" is the old Latin numbering; in a modern Bible the
  verse is Psalm 107:40.
