text: >-
  On the last day of October, 1517, a friar of the Augustinian order named Martin Luther, who taught
  theology at the young university in Wittenberg, sat down to write to a man very far above him in
  rank: Albrecht, Archbishop of Magdeburg and Mainz, Primate of the German church. He addressed him with
  every mark of deference the age required - "Most Reverend Father in Christ and Most Illustrious
  Prince" - and named himself, in the same breath, "the dregs of humanity," astonished at his own
  boldness in writing at all.

  The reason he gave was plain. Indulgences for the building of St. Peter's in Rome were being preached
  under the Archbishop's own name, and Luther did not attack the preaching itself, which he said he had
  not heard - he grieved instead over what the people who heard it now believed. They believed, he
  wrote, that a letter of indulgence made their salvation sure the moment it was purchased; that souls
  flew out of purgatory the instant a coin struck the bottom of the collection box; that no sin was too
  great to be absolved this way, "even, as they say - though the thing is impossible - if one had
  violated the Mother of God"; and that the letters freed a person from guilt as well as penalty. "O
  God, most good!" he wrote. "Thus souls committed to your care, good Father, are taught to their
  death." He asked the Archbishop, as a father would be asked, to look at the matter and withdraw the
  instruction the preachers were working from, before someone else did it in public and to the
  Archbishop's own shame. He closed asking the Lord to keep "your Most Reverend Fatherhood eternally,"
  signed himself simply "Brother Martin Luther," and added one line more: if it pleased the Most
  Reverend Father, he might look over "these my Disputations" - the ninety-five statements Luther had
  drawn up for university debate, sent along with the letter, on how doubtful a thing the preachers'
  confidence about indulgences really was.

  That is the act this library can show directly, dated and in Luther's own hand: a formal protest,
  addressed upward through the church's own channels, to a superior, asking for correction rather than
  announcing a break. What happened to the Theses after that - whether they were also nailed, that same
  day or another, to the door of Wittenberg's Castle Church, the way announcements for university
  debate were customarily posted - is a separate and much less certain question. The account of a
  door and a hammer that many people know today comes from a much later editor's own narrative telling,
  not from Luther's own words, and modern scholars have disputed whether it happened at all. The letter
  to Albrecht is not in question. The door is.
absent_detail: >-
  Luther's own letter says nothing about a door, a hammer, or a public posting of any kind - it is a
  private letter to one archbishop, enclosing the Theses for his own reading and for university debate.
  The door-posting narrative this library holds is a twentieth-century editor's own unsourced telling,
  not a period document, and this library has not independently read the modern scholarly argument
  (Iserloh) that disputes it - only verified that such an argument exists. No account here says how, or
  whether, the wider public first learned of the Theses' contents.
modern_contrast: >-
  A modern reader most often knows this story as "Luther nailed his 95 Theses to the church door" - a
  single dramatic image of defiance. What the letter itself actually shows is closer to the opposite
  gesture: a university teacher writing privately and deferentially to his own ecclesiastical superior,
  asking him to fix a problem quietly, before it became a public scandal. The famous hammer-and-door
  scene may or may not have happened alongside that letter; either way, it is not the act the letter
  itself records, and the letter's own tone - careful, respectful, addressed upward - is not the tone
  of a public act of rebellion.
