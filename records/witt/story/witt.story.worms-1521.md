text: >-
  This is how Luther himself told the story of Worms, years afterward, to the students gathered at his
  table.

  He had been cited to appear before the Diet, he said, carrying an imperial safe-conduct - and that
  safe-conduct was broken within a day of his leaving, when he was condemned at Worms and his books were
  burned, before he had even arrived. By the time he reached Erfurt, on the road, word had already
  spread that he was condemned; the herald who rode with him asked whether he still meant to go on.
  "Although I was somewhat astonished at the news," Luther told his students, "yet I answered the
  herald, and said, although in Worms there were as many devils as there are tiles on the houses, yet,
  God willing, I will go thither."

  Near Worms, at Oppenheim, Martin Bucer came to warn him off the city entirely - the Emperor's own
  confessor, Bucer said, had heard that Luther would be burned, and urged him to hide instead with a
  nearby gentleman. Luther went on anyway, in what he called "all simplicity," and was received into
  lodging by two men sent at the Elector's own command. A young Landgrave of Hesse, hearing he had come,
  told him plainly: "Sir, is your cause just and upright, then I beseech God to assist you."

  Then he was called before the Senate House, where the Emperor and the Electors themselves sat
  assembled. Dr. Eck asked him whether the books laid out on the table were truly his. "I believe they
  be mine," Luther answered; and when the titles were read aloud, "Yea, they are mine." Asked whether he
  would recant them, he answered that some were books of controversy, against his adversaries, and some
  were books of doctrine - and the doctrine he neither could nor would recant. If he had been too
  violent in the controversial ones, he said, he was willing to be corrected, and asked for time to
  consider. He was given one day and one night.

  What he actually said the next day, this library's own Table Talk text does not go on to record in
  full - only that he would not recant. The sentence most people today know this story by, "Here I
  stand, I cannot do otherwise, God help me," does not appear in Luther's own account as this library
  holds it. It reaches this library only secondhand, through a nineteenth-century writer's own
  translation, quoted by a hymn-book editor generations later - not from Luther's own retelling at his
  table.
absent_detail: >-
  This library's own text of Luther's retelling does not include the words "Here I stand, I cannot do
  otherwise" in any form at the moment of his final refusal - it breaks off after "one day and one
  night" without quoting his answer the next day in full. The famous sentence reaches this library only
  through a much later writer's translation, quoted inside a hymn-book editor's introduction, and its
  own authenticity is disputed by scholars this library has not independently read.
modern_contrast: >-
  A modern reader almost certainly knows Worms through one sentence - "Here I stand, I cannot do
  otherwise, so help me God" - treated as something close to a verified motto. What Luther's own later
  retelling, as this library holds it, actually gives at the comparable moment of decision is a
  different sentence entirely, about tiled roofs and devils, spoken not at the Diet itself but on the
  road toward it. The two sayings serve the same function in memory - defiance in the face of danger -
  but only one of them is this library's own directly attested wording from Luther's own mouth.
