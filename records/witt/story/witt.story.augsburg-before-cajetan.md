text: >-
  Years later, at his own table, Luther told his students how it had gone at Augsburg, in the summer of
  1518 - the first time Rome tried, in person, to make him take his words back.

  He had been summoned, he said, and Frederick, the Elector of Saxony, had arranged him a strong escort
  and a safe-conduct. He was warned not to trust the Italians. He waited three days in Augsburg before
  the Emperor's own safe-conduct arrived; in that time, an Italian came to him and led him to Cardinal
  Cajetan, urging him the whole way to recant - it would take only one word, the Italian said, Revoco,
  "I recant," and the Cardinal would send him home to his Elector in honor and the Pope's good favor.
  When Luther came before the Cardinal, he told his students, he knelt first on his knees, then all the
  way down to the ground, and stayed there until Cajetan three times bade him rise. The Cardinal was
  pleased by this - he hoped, Luther said, that it meant he would think better of things.

  The next day Luther came again, and would not recant anything. "What," the Cardinal said to him,
  "thinkest thou that the Pope careth for Germany? Or dost thou think that the Princes will raise arms
  and armies to maintain and defend thee? Oh, no; where wilt thou remain in safety?" And Luther told his
  students what he had answered: "Under Heaven."
absent_detail: >-
  This account is Luther's own later memory, told to students and recorded by them - not a transcript
  taken at Augsburg in 1518. It gives no exact date beyond "the 9th of July," no independent witness to
  the words exchanged, and no account of Cajetan's own side of the conversation beyond what Luther
  chose to remember and repeat.
modern_contrast: >-
  A modern reader may expect a formal hearing with recorded proceedings, the way a trial or a hearing
  would be documented today. What survives instead is a story - told once, years afterward, at a table
  among students, the same way any person remembers and retells the day they first stood up to someone
  far more powerful than themselves. The confrontation itself is not in serious doubt; the exact words
  are Luther's own memory of them, nothing more and nothing less.
