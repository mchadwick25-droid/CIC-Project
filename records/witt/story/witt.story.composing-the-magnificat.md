text: >-
  After Luther's excommunication was announced in the papal bull Exsurge Domine, a young prince named
  John Frederick - seventeen years old, the Elector's own nephew - wrote to Luther assuring him of his
  sympathy, and told him he had pleaded Luther's case with his own uncle. Luther wrote back that October,
  in 1520, moved by the young man's zeal "for the sacred truth of God," and by the end of the year had
  promised him something in return: an exposition, written for the young prince himself, of the
  Magnificat, Mary's own song of praise in the Gospel of Luke.

  The work went slowly. A letter from Spalatin to the Elector, dated the last day of December, reports
  that Luther "has also begun to expound the Magnificat and to dedicate it to the young lord." By late
  February, Luther himself wrote to Spalatin that he was busy on it, "as an answer, though a tardy one"
  to the prince's own gracious letter - and admitted the work kept being interrupted by what he called
  "the troublesome quarrels of many adversaries." The most serious interruption of all came next: his
  own citation to appear at the Diet of Worms, which stopped his writing entirely for a time.

  He did not leave the work unfinished before he left. On Easter Sunday, three days before his
  departure from Wittenberg for Worms - not knowing, so far as this library shows, whether he would
  return - Luther sent his young prince three quires of the exposition, as far as he had carried it.

modern_contrast: >-
  A modern reader might expect a work written "under danger" to be about danger, or written in a
  register of crisis. What Luther actually produced, in these same weeks, was patient devotional
  teaching for a teenager - proof, in this library's own account, that the movement's habit of forming
  the young through careful written instruction did not stop even when its own teacher's life was
  genuinely at risk.
