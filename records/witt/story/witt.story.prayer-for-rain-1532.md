text: >-
  In 1532, a great drought lay over Germany, and the corn in the fields, by this library's own account,
  had begun to wither. On the ninth of June that year, Luther called the whole assembly together into
  the church, and prayed aloud before them, with what the account calls "deep sighs."

  He did not pray only for rain. "O Lord," he said, "behold our prayers for thy promise sake; we have
  prayed, and our hearts have sighed, but the covetousness of the rich farmers doth hinder and hem in
  thy blessing." He named what he saw as the deeper trouble plainly: that the Gospel's own liberty had
  been taken by some as license - "they are unbridled, they think it free for them to live and do what
  they please; they now fear neither death nor hell, but say, 'I believe, therefore I shall be
  saved.'" He called such people "haughty spiteful Mammonists, and accursed covetous cut-throats, that
  suck out land and people," and asked that God would not let the whole town suffer for their sake alone.

  Then, the account says, he lifted his eyes and prayed again, pressing the same question a second time
  - if God gave no rain, would the ungodly not say that Christ himself had lied, since Christ had
  promised that whatever was asked in his name would be given? "I know, O Lord," Luther prayed, "that we
  do cry unto thee from our hearts, with yearning and sighing, why then dost thou not hear us?"

  The account closes with what the congregation itself is remembered to have experienced: that same day,
  within half an hour of the people leaving the church, it began to rain - gently, the record says, and
  steadily, for a fortnight afterward, "in a most miraculous manner."

  This is the one story in this whole library where the people being formed are not Luther alone, and
  not his students, but the gathered congregation itself - the whole assembly, called together and
  addressed, even though the account still reaches this library only through Luther's own recorded
  prayer, not through any one congregant's own voice.

modern_contrast: >-
  A modern reader may be tempted either to dismiss this story outright, as an unverifiable weather claim,
  or to treat the rain itself as the point. What the account itself actually dwells on longest is not
  the rain but the prayer's own content - a public rebuke of the town's own "rich farmers" and a worry
  that Christian liberty had curdled into "unbridled" self-interest. The tradition remembers this as
  answered prayer, and this library records that as a fact about what the community believed happened,
  not as an independently verified meteorological finding.
