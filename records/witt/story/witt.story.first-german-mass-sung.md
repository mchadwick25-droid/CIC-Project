text: 'This is what Johann Walter, Luther''s own collaborator in music, remembered many years later, writing
  in his old age, of the work behind the first German Mass at Wittenberg.

  Luther had written to the Elector of Saxony and to Duke Johannsen, Walter recalled, asking that he and
  the old musician Conrad Rupff be brought to Wittenberg to help decide how the church''s own musical
  tones should be set. Luther "kept me three weeks long at Wittenberg," Walter wrote, working out which
  tone should carry the Epistle and which the Gospel, settling at last on the Eighth Tone for the Epistle
  and the Sixth for the Gospel - "Our Lord Christ is a good Friend," Luther told him, by way of explanation,
  "and his words are full of love; so we will take the Sixth Tone for the Gospel." Luther set the notes
  himself over the Epistles and Gospels and the Words of Institution, Walter remembered, and sang them
  over for Walter''s own judgment. "I must needs stay to hear it," Walter wrote, "and take with me a copy
  of the Mass to Torgau and present it to His Grace the Elector from Doctor Luther" - the day, in Walter''s
  own words, "until the first German Mass was sung in the parish church."

  Walter remembered more besides the Mass itself: that Luther gave orders to restore Vespers where it
  had fallen out of use, with short choral hymns for the students; and that the poor charity-scholars
  of the town, who went collecting bread from door to door, were set to sing Latin hymns and responses
  as they went - "the most profitable songs for the common multitude," in Walter''s own recollection of
  Luther''s own judgment, "are the plain psalms and hymns," with the Latin kept for the learned and for
  students.

  Walter''s letter was written, by his own account, in his old age, remembering events some forty years
  past - and it survives in this library only because a later editor, Bacon, quoted it from an earlier
  printing by Rambach, which had itself taken it from Praetorius. It is the one place in this whole library
  where the account of Wittenberg''s worship comes from someone other than Luther himself.'
absent_detail: This library does not hold Walter's letter as a document in its own right - only as quoted
  inside Bacon's introduction, itself drawing on Rambach's 1813 printing of Praetorius. No exact date
  for the first German Mass's own singing is given beyond Walter's own "some forty years ago," and no
  vendored order of service confirms what else, if anything, that first German Mass contained.
modern_contrast: A modern reader might expect the story of a new form of worship to come with an eyewitness
  order of service, a program, or a precise date. What survives instead is one man's late-life memory
  of the working sessions behind it - the tone-by-tone decisions, the poor students singing for bread
  - filtered through three later hands before it reached this library. It is genuine testimony, but it
  is memory, not minutes.
