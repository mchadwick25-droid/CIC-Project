claim: >-
  On 31 October 1517, alongside or immediately following his letter to Archbishop Albrecht of Mainz, Luther
  himself, or someone acting for him, physically nailed the Ninety-Five Theses to the door of Wittenberg's
  Castle Church - the traditional "hammer and door" scene, told as this world's founding public act of
  defiance.
held_against:
- >-
  This library's own text for the posting narrative is not a period document at all. The Source Registry's
  own entry for the letter records that the posting account - "It was not night, but mid-day when the
  Theses were nailed up" (v1 473-474) - is "the editor's, 1915": a twentieth-century editorial narrative
  assertion introducing the volume, carrying no primary citation of its own, written roughly four centuries
  after the events it describes.
- >-
  Luther's own dated document from the same days says nothing of the kind. The letter to Albrecht, "the
  Vigil of All Saints, MDXVII" (31 October 1517), is directly attested, signed, and complete in this
  library's own text - and it is a private letter, addressed upward through the church's own channels to an
  ecclesiastical superior, enclosing the Theses for his own reading and for university debate. It mentions
  no door, no hammer, and no public act of any kind (Doc_09 witt-S01).
- >-
  The modern historiographical argument this library's "Contested" tag ultimately rests on is itself unread
  by this build. Erwin Iserloh's The Theses Were Not Posted: Luther between Reform and Reformation, which
  argues the posting is legend, is recorded in the Source Registry only as verified to exist, at confidence
  B, by a search of its reviews and citations - not as an argument this library has actually read (Source
  Registry row 76). "Contested" here therefore means, precisely: an unread secondary source is understood to
  dispute a modern editor's own unsourced narrative claim, not that two primary accounts stand in tension.
  That is a materially thinner evidentiary situation than "a documented scholarly dispute over a specific
  event" can sound like on its own, and this record states it directly rather than leaving a reader to infer
  it (a correction the Round 1 review of Doc_09 required of that document's own first drafting pass, carried
  forward here).
- >-
  The scene functions as this world's own founding image independent of what the evidence can show. It is
  the detail "a modern reader most often knows this story" by (witt.story.letter-to-albrecht-and-theses-circulation),
  a single dramatic image of public defiance - and it stands in real tension with what the letter itself
  actually records: a university teacher writing privately and deferentially, upward, asking a superior to
  correct a problem quietly, before it became a public scandal "to the Archbishop's own shame." Whichever
  way the posting question is eventually settled, the letter's own tone is not evidence for or against it,
  and this record does not treat it as though it were.
concedes: >-
  Not contested: that Luther wrote and dated the letter to Archbishop Albrecht on 31 October 1517, enclosing
  the Ninety-Five Theses for debate - a directly attested, signed, Tier 1 document in this library's own
  text, requiring no qualification; that the Theses did circulate afterward, in print, and became this
  world's own founding act of protest, however their first public form actually occurred; and that this
  world's own founding self-understanding is one of "loyal correction, not rebellion" (Doc_09 witt-S01's own
  formation-ecology connection to G1), a reading the letter itself supports regardless of how the door
  question resolves. Documented, all of it. What is not settled from this library: whether the Theses were
  also physically posted, on what date, by whom, or at all - a question this library can show is disputed in
  the field but cannot itself adjudicate, since neither side of that dispute is read here beyond the level of
  an existing, search-verified title.
