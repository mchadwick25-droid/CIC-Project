    text: >-
      In 1517, Martin Luther, an Augustinian friar and professor at the young
      university of Wittenberg, wrote to his archbishop against the sale of indulgences.
      That letter opened a movement. It set Scripture against a hierarchy that
      stood between people and God. It held that a person stands before God by
      a gift received through faith, not by works. Princes and city councils carried
      the reform into their lands, not bishops. This world's chief tool was the
      household catechism. It had three parts, to be said at rising, at table,
      and at night. A father or a schoolmaster was to examine each household every
      week. The commandments frightened a conscience. A promise spoken from outside
      it made the conscience sure. German hymns were sung inside a Mass that was
      kept. The story runs from the Ninety-Five Theses to a confession signed
      before the Emperor at Augsburg in 1530. It closes with a fuller book of
      confessions, gathered in 1580. The record is rich on the founding decade.
      It is thin on how any household or parish actually lived this, and no woman's
      own text survives.

      This world runs from 1517 to 1580. It opens with theses against the sale
      of indulgences, written in 1517. It closes with a fuller book of confessions,
      gathered in 1580 from documents written decades before. The record runs
      deepest to about 1531 and thins sharply after that. The founder's life ended
      in the mid-1540s. The years after reach us only through the 1580 book. This
      record does not describe those years as if it had lived them.
    grounded_in: [witt.core.witt]
  - text: >-
      On 31 October 1517, a friar named Martin Luther wrote to Albrecht, the Archbishop
      of Mainz. Luther taught theology at the young university in Wittenberg.
      Indulgences for building St. Peter's in Rome were being preached under the
      archbishop's name. Luther grieved over what hearers now believed. He asked
      the archbishop to withdraw the preachers' instructions. He enclosed ninety-five
      statements for debate. The letter asks for correction. It does not announce
      a break. The scene of a hammer and a church door comes from a much later
      editor. Scholars dispute it, and the letter never mentions it.
    grounded_in: [witt.story.letter-to-albrecht-and-theses-circulation, witt.contested.theses-door-posting]
  - text: >-
      Rome pressed him to take his words back. Years later, at his own table,
      Luther told students about Augsburg in 1518. A papal legate, Cardinal Cajetan,
      wanted one word from him: Revoco, I recant. A bull of June 1520 condemned
      forty-one of his statements. In 1521 he stood before the Emperor and the
      princes at Worms. By his own later account, he said he could not give up
      his teaching. He was willing to be corrected on his harsher polemics. The
      famous sentence 'Here I stand' is not in his own account as this record
      holds it. Scholars dispute its authenticity.
    grounded_in: [witt.story.augsburg-before-cajetan, witt.story.worms-1521, witt.force.papal-force-initiating]
  - text: >-
      The reform soon ran ahead of its founder. In March 1522, Luther left the
      Wartburg without his prince's leave and rode back to Wittenberg. His colleague
      Karlstadt had led a fast change in worship, and the city was in disorder.
      Luther preached eight days in a row. He told his congregation they had gone
      too fast. He saw little love among them. A faith without love, he said,
      is counterfeit. He gave them two words, must and free. Do not turn what
      is free into a must. By 1529 he turned the word liberty against pastors
      grown careless. The record keeps both uses.
    grounded_in: [witt.story.return-and-the-eight-sermons, witt.gravity.must-and-free]
  - text: >-
      The reform's chief tool was the household catechism of 1529. It has three
      parts: the Ten Commandments, the Creed, and the Lord's Prayer. A household
      was to say them at rising, at table, and at night. A father was to question
      his children and servants at least once a week. A child who could not say
      the words was to get no food or drink until he could. After each part came
      one question: what does this mean? Servants and masters each had their own
      words for confession. This is the program as written. The record does not
      show any household keeping it.
    grounded_in: [witt.gravity.household-catechism, witt.story.household-catechism-lesson-typical-practice]
  - text: >-
      The commandments, as this world read them, show a person what he cannot
      do. A terrified conscience then has nowhere to rest. The promises of God
      stand ready: a sinner is freely made right for Christ's sake, through faith.
      The sacraments carry that promise. Baptism is daily. Absolution is given
      as though from God himself. At the Supper, the words are given for you.
      The Mass was kept, and none came to the Supper without being examined. Christ's
      body and blood are held to be truly present, in and under the bread and
      wine. This world refuses to explain how.

  - text: >-
      German hymns were sung inside the Mass to teach the people. Latin stayed
      for those learning it. Johann Walter remembered, some forty years later,
      being kept three weeks at Wittenberg while he and Luther set the Epistle
      and Gospel to German tones. Wittenberg's first hymn-book came out in 1524.
      It held four hymns, and Paul Speratus wrote three of them. A tradition says
      a wanderer from Prussia once sang one under Luther's window. The record
      holds it only as tradition. It does not name the singer, and it does not
      say the singer was Speratus.

  - text: >-
      On 1 July 1523, two young monks, John and Henry, were burned at Brussels.
      Theologians from Louvain had pressed them to give up what they now taught.
      They refused. Within months, Luther wrote a ballad about them. It is the
      only martyr-song in this record. It is also the only case where the movement's
      teaching cost lives. The ballad says their refusal made them priests without
      any ordination. Its details, such as their last words, are the ballad's
      own telling. No other source confirms them. The record gives no family names
      or ages for either man.

  - text: >-
      In 1530 the Emperor called the Diet of Augsburg to plan a defense against
      the Turk and to hear the religious divisions. A confession of this world's
      teaching, drafted by Melanchthon, was read and signed. Luther is not among
      the nine signers in this record. They were an elector, princes, and the
      senates of two free cities. The document is closer to a treaty than a manifesto.
      It offers loyal address, not protest. Rome's theologians answered it in
      writing. This world replied in a second document, the Apology.

      This world broke plainly with the cities that read the Lord's Supper differently.
      The break came at Marburg in 1529. This world held that Christ's body and
      blood are truly present, in and under the bread and wine, and it rejected
      those who teach otherwise. The record holds that position and the fact of
      the break. It does not hold the argument. The Marburg Articles of October
      1529 are known, but this library does not have them. The name Zwingli does
      not appear in the texts searched. What the break felt like from this side
      has not come down to us.

      On who Jesus is, this world gives the answer its households said each week.
      Jesus Christ is truly God, born of the Father before time began, and truly
      man, born of the Virgin Mary. It treats this as received teaching, not as
      a question its own history had to settle. What it stresses is what he did.
      He bought back lost people at the cost of his own blood, and he alone stands
      as their Mediator. Faith in him is trust in a promise, not a work.
