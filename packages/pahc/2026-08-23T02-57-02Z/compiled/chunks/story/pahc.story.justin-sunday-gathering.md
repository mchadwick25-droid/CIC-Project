Justin describes, to the emperor and Senate, exactly what happens when his own community gathers on the day named for the sun: reading, a homily, common prayer, thanksgiving over bread and wine, and a collection for those in need.

Writing to the emperor and the Roman Senate in his own defense of Christian practice, Justin tells us plainly what "we" do. On the day named for the sun, he writes, those in the cities and the countryside gather together in one place. The memoirs of the apostles, or the writings of the prophets, are read for as long as time permits. Then the one presiding gives a discourse, exhorting the people to imitate these good things. Then all rise together and pray. Bread and wine and water are brought, and the president offers prayers and thanksgivings according to his ability, and the people assent, saying "Amen." Those present who have means, and are willing, give what they choose, and what is collected is deposited with the one presiding, who cares for orphans and widows, the sick, those in prison, and strangers sojourning among them - "in a word," Justin writes, he "takes care of all who are in need."
This is Justin's own report, addressed to an outside, imperial audience, of what happens on the day his own community gathers.
