text: >
  Writing to the emperor and the Roman Senate in his own defense of
  Christian practice, Justin tells us plainly what "we" do. On the day
  named for the sun, he writes, those in the cities and the
  countryside gather together in one place. The memoirs of the
  apostles, or the writings of the prophets, are read for as long as
  time permits. Then the one presiding gives a discourse, exhorting
  the people to imitate these good things. Then all rise together and
  pray. Bread and wine and water are brought, and the president offers
  prayers and thanksgivings according to his ability, and the people
  assent, saying "Amen." Those present who have means, and are
  willing, give what they choose, and what is collected is deposited
  with the one presiding, who cares for orphans and widows, the sick,
  those in prison, and strangers sojourning among them - "in a word,"
  Justin writes, he "takes care of all who are in need."

  This is Justin's own report, addressed to an outside, imperial
  audience, of what happens on the day his own community gathers.
absent_detail: "Justin's account describes Rome specifically and does not say whether or how closely communities in Antioch or Asia Minor followed the same order."
modern_contrast: >
  A modern reader often hears "reading, a homily, common prayer,
  thanksgiving, a collection" as a description of an already-
  institutional church service - ordained clergy, a dedicated building,
  a fixed order of worship. This world's own record frames it
  differently: Justin describes a modest, likely house-based gathering,
  defending its ordinariness to a hostile emperor precisely because
  nothing about its shape was yet settled or protected by law.
