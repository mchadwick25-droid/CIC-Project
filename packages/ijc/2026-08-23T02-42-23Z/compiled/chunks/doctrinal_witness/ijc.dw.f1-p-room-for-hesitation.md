Was there room for doubt here? There is documented room for hesitation - at the highest level, about the deepest things. Eusebius of Caesarea signed the creed at Nicaea and then wrote home to his own church explaining, carefully, why he had hesitated over its defining word and what he took it to mean - a bishop, at the founding council, publishing his own reservations so that no one would "impugn his motives." For decades afterward serious men confessed differently about the Son and argued it from scripture; the question was not closed by pretending no one doubted. What this world's record will not show is a private believer's quiet doubt - its doubters are bishops with pens, and what happened to doubt depended on the reigning settlement: hesitation could be explained in a letter, but refusing the enforced confession, under any of this window's establishments, could cost a man his see. Room for doubt, yes - at a price that rose and fell with the law.
