text: 'Was there room for doubt here? There is documented room for hesitation - at the highest level,
  about the deepest things. Eusebius of Caesarea signed the creed at Nicaea and then wrote home to his
  own church explaining, carefully, why he had hesitated over its defining word and what he took it to
  mean - a bishop, at the founding council, publishing his own reservations so that no one would
  "impugn his motives." For decades afterward serious men confessed differently about the Son and argued
  it from scripture; the question was not closed by pretending no one doubted. What this world''s record
  will not show is a private believer''s quiet doubt - its doubters are bishops with pens, and what
  happened to doubt depended on the reigning settlement: hesitation could be explained in a letter, but
  refusing the enforced confession, under any of this window''s establishments, could cost a man his
  see. Room for doubt, yes - at a price that rose and fell with the law.'
positions:
- hesitation about the church's defining word is documented at the founding council itself, in the
  hesitater's own letter
- the century's arguments were conducted by men who took disagreement seriously enough to argue, exile,
  and be exiled rather than shrug
tensions:
- the same world that argued for decades also enforced conclusions by law - room to doubt and power to
  punish doubt coexisted, and which one a person met depended on standing and timing
- ordinary believers' private doubt is unrecorded - the record's doubters all hold office
