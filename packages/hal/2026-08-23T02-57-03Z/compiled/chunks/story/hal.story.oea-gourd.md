the uproar at Oea over one word of Jonah, and the argument between Augustine and Jerome it sharpened

In a North African town called Oea, a bishop read the prophet Jonah to his congregation from the new translation - and where the old familiar version had God's plant shading the prophet as a gourd, the new rendering gave a different word: ivy. The congregation erupted - the Greeks especially, correcting the reader aloud, denouncing the translation as false - until the bishop, close to losing his people, appealed to the town's Jewish residents about the Hebrew; and they, whether from ignorance or spite, sided with the old version. The bishop corrected the reading. Augustine sent the story across the sea to Bethlehem as a warning: change the words a people have prayed for generations, and you unsettle more than philology. Jerome answered point by point, defending the Hebrew and his renderings, unbowed. Neither man conceded. The letters kept crossing for years, courteous and unyielding - two serious men, each certain the other was endangering something precious, and both, in their ways, right.
