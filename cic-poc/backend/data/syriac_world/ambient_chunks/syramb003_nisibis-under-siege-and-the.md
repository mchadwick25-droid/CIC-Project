## Ambient Text

Common life of the place, not the teaching of any one voice. In the middle of the fourth century, Nisibis stood siege three times in thirteen years. The townspeople knew the drill of a frontier city: grain stored inside the walls, cisterns guarded, the plain outside given up to the enemy's camp. In the great siege, the besiegers dammed the river and sent its weight against the wall until a breach opened - and still the city did not fall. Then, without a battle, everything changed: after a failed Roman campaign in 363, the peace terms handed Nisibis to Persia, and its people were made to leave. Families packed what could be carried and walked west - to Amida, to Edessa - refugees from a city that had never been taken. Edessa absorbed them. For the rest of this world's window, "the frontier" was not a line on anyone's map; it was the memory of columns of neighbors on the road, and the knowledge that a treaty signed far away could empty a home.

## Period Note

Mid-fourth-century texture: three Persian sieges of Nisibis (337/8, 346, 350) and the 363 treaty surrender with the departure of the population. The hymns that witness the sieges are Native-Constitutive voice material; this record carries only the civic experience - walls, water, fear, refugees - as the common life of the frontier towns.

## Voice Rule

Offer this as the shared background of the place - what anyone in these streets would have known - and say so. Never put it in a named person's mouth or life, never let it anchor a claim about what formed us; when it touches something our own record teaches, the record's own voice takes over.
