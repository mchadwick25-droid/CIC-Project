quick_meaning: 'For this world Christ — the Anointed One — is not Jesus''s surname but our confession:
  this is the one the whole scriptural story was moving toward, anointed by God to enact in his own person
  what priest, king, and prophet each enacted in shadow, and whose death and resurrection accomplish what
  the formation exists to make operative in the soul.'
world_meaning: 'We do not say "Jesus Christ" the way one says "John Smith." We confess "Jesus is the Christ"
  — and that confession is not the end of an inquiry but the beginning of one. The inquiry is: what does
  it mean that he is the Christ? And the answer is not a single proposition but the whole of what we believe
  about the human person, what has gone wrong with it, and what God is doing to restore it.


  Christ renders the Hebrew *Mashiach* — the Anointed One. In the tradition Israel handed us, anointing
  was never a ceremony of honor. It was a commission: the act by which God set a person apart for a vocation
  that carried the weight of God''s own purpose. Three figures received it, for three functions. The priest,
  anointed to stand between God and people, to bring offerings, to mediate a relationship that sin had
  strained. The king, anointed to govern God''s people as God''s representative, to embody the covenant''s
  justice and protection. The prophet, anointed to speak God''s own word into the people''s situation,
  to announce what God intended and to call the people back when they drifted.


  When we confess Jesus as the Christ, we confess that he enacts all three at once and in their fullness.
  He is the great High Priest, who mediates not by offering an animal but by offering himself, entering
  the human condition of death and reversing it from within. He is the King, whose reign is not territorial
  but reaches to the root of what we are, gathering the community that lives under his governance. He
  is the Prophet, who speaks God''s own word — not as a messenger carrying someone else''s message but
  as the Word himself, speaking directly.


  This is why confessing Christ is a formation act before it is a claim to be examined. To confess him
  is to place oneself in relation to the one who mediates, governs, and speaks at the center of everything
  we do. The catechumen is being formed toward the Christ. The one growing in illumination is growing
  in perception of the Christ who speaks through Scripture. The one growing into likeness is growing into
  the likeness of the Christ who is the image of God. The one growing in participation is participating
  in the life of the Christ. The confession is not background to our life together; it is that life''s
  organizing center, named.


  Since Nicaea our confession carries a further weight, and we carry it as both relief and burden. When
  we confess Christ we confess that the one who died and rose is not a creature raised to divine rank
  but the eternal Logos who entered human nature — the one who is of one substance with the Father. The
  relief is that the question was settled; the burden is that it had to be asked at all, and that we must
  go on holding what was settled rather than treating it as one more inherited fact. This matters in the
  most practical way: the Christ who forms the soul through Scripture, community, and the ordinary practices
  is not a great teacher, a holy example, or an exalted creature. He is the Logos made flesh — genuinely
  God who has entered genuinely human nature — and so the transformation he works in those who share his
  life is genuine participation in divine life, not proximity to an elevated creature. (What "of one substance"
  meant, and how it is read, is a matter under live contest; see below.)


  He is never one topic among our topics. Every Scripture we open is his address — the Anointed speaking
  through the text we have gathered to read. Every practice we keep is participation in his life. Every
  encounter is organized around the one already present, whose presence gives it its direction and its
  stakes.'

**Historical scope + Meaning.** The contest concerns what "consubstantial" (*homoousios*) meant to the Nicene bishops in 325 versus how it was received afterward, and it bears directly on how Athanasius's usage is read; it is temporally located after 325. The point of contest is not whether we confess Christ as of one substance with the Father — we do — but what that word carried in its own moment and how far its later sense may be read back into it. This is why the pre-Nicene and post-Nicene registers must be kept distinct: before Nicaea the precise ontological status of the Christ was genuinely open and under debate; only after 325 does the confession stand settled, and we hold that settlement as relief-and-burden rather than as a timeless given.
