quick_meaning: For this world death is first the soul's progressive separation from God — the consequence
  of sin's turning, in which a soul cut off from the source of its life loses what that source was sustaining;
  physical death is real and grievous but secondary to the spiritual death sin produces and the Logos's
  Incarnation reverses from within.
world_meaning: 'Death picks up exactly where sin leaves off. The soul has turned from God, who is the
  source of its life — and what happens next follows with a logic that needs no further cause.


  We do not sustain ourselves. Nothing does: to exist at all is to participate in the ground of being,
  which is God. The soul that stays turned toward that source keeps receiving what the source gives —
  life, coherence, the deepening of the image toward the likeness. The soul that turns away begins, by
  the turning itself, to lose what the turning cuts off. This is not a penalty dropped on us from outside.
  It is as natural as a cut flower losing its life, because the life was never the flower''s own. The
  soul that turns from God begins to undergo what we call death.


  This is spiritual death, and it is the first meaning of the word here. A soul separated from God is
  a soul dying inwardly: the image growing dim, the *nous* closing to the perception it was made for,
  the capacity for love and wisdom and communion becoming distorted and thin. To turn from Being is to
  drift toward non-being, because God alone is genuine being; the soul that turns from what truly is moves
  toward dissolution.


  This dying can run alongside full bodily health. A person can be alive, even thriving by every worldly
  measure, while the soul is in the condition of death — the image dimmed, the *nous* shut, its deep desire
  aimed at goods that keep a kind of life going but not the life it was made for. This is what it means
  to be "dead in sin" while still breathing and working and buying and selling. The body continues; the
  soul is cut off from its source.


  Physical death — the end of bodily life — is real, and we do not wave it away. The body dissolves, the
  soul is parted from it, the whole embodied person we knew is no longer here. This is a genuine catastrophe,
  not a tidy stage of growth. But here it is secondary: physical death is the temporal seal of what spiritual
  death has been enacting all along, the outward endpoint of the soul''s separation from the ground of
  its life.


  Our whole understanding of martyrdom rests on this order. The one who chooses death over denying Christ
  is choosing between two deaths. To deny Christ — to turn from the God whose image the soul bears — is
  spiritual death, the soul turning from its source. To be killed for refusing is only the body''s dissolution,
  the temporal limit of our mortality. Between them the martyr takes the bodily death and refuses the
  spiritual one. The choice is coherent only if death first means separation from God: the martyr is not
  choosing the worse thing but the lesser death to escape the greater. The body will dissolve; the soul
  stays turned toward God, toward the life he gives. The killing cannot sever that orientation. The denial
  would.'
distortion_risk: '**World Hearing:**

  Death here is first a theological and ontological condition, not a biological event. Spiritual death
  — the soul''s progressive separation from God — is what a turned-away soul undergoes, and it can be
  well underway while the person is biologically thriving. Physical death is the temporal expression and
  culmination of that separation, not a separate accident. The forensic frame fails by filing death under
  "punishment imposed by a judge" rather than "the natural consequence of the soul''s own turning" — and
  the difference decides everything: a penalty imposed must be satisfied to be reversed; a separation
  from the source of life is reversed by returning to that source, which is what the Incarnation and the
  whole arc accomplish.'

modern_hearing: '**Modern Hearing:**

  In modern usage death means biological cessation, full stop; even its metaphors (a dead relationship,
  dead hope) borrow from that. Where modern Christian talk gives death a theological weight, it usually
  follows the forensic frame: death is the penalty sin incurs, the sentence divine justice requires and
  Christ absorbs, reversed by a resurrection understood mainly as biological life restored and the penalty
  shown to be paid.'
