quick_meaning: For this world interpretation is not analytical method applied to a text — it is the formation
  discipline through which the soul encounters the Logos who speaks through Scripture, at the depth its
  formation has prepared it to receive; what changes through genuine interpretation is not first the reader's
  information but the reader's perception.
world_meaning: 'The word "interpretation" threatens to bring the wrong weight into this world. In common
  use, interpretation is what you do to a text that does not give up its meaning at once: you apply method,
  use tools, bring expertise, and produce a determination — "the text means this" — which you then pass
  on. The interpreter is the one who has done that work well, and the test is whether the determination
  is correct.


  That is the analytical-method hearing, and it misses what interpreting Scripture means among us at every
  point that matters. The interpreter here is not the one who has correctly settled what the text means.
  The interpreter is the one who has genuinely met what the text holds — who has received the Logos''s
  address through it at the depth their formation has prepared, and who has been changed by that meeting.


  Scripture is a living address, the Logos speaking now through the text to the soul that comes to it.
  The orientation is toward the Logos who is Christ; the allegorical discipline reaches the depths the
  formed nous can receive. Interpretation is the general name for what all of these are in their particular
  modes — the formation discipline through which the soul meets what Scripture holds at the depth its
  formation allows.


  So interpretation cannot be separated from the formation sequence. The catechumen beginning to hear
  Scripture in the assembly is already beginning to interpret — attending to the Logos''s address at the
  depth a catechumen can receive. The illumined student who perceives what a text holds at a depth not
  available before is interpreting more deeply: the same text, met by a nous whose capacity formation
  has opened further. The wise reader who perceives what years of formation have made perceptible is interpreting
  at a depth they could not have reached before. Formation and interpretive depth are one movement — formation
  opens the nous''s capacity, and that capacity is what interpretation requires.


  The teacher''s work is interpretation enacted in the student''s presence. The teacher does not settle
  the meaning and hand it over. The teacher meets the Logos through the text while the student watches,
  and that meeting creates the conditions for the student''s own meeting to begin or deepen. Interpretation
  is the school tradition''s primary formation practice because that tradition''s mode is the forming
  of the nous toward deeper perception, and that is exactly what guided encounter with Scripture develops.


  What interpretation produces is first in the interpreter, not only in what they say about the text.
  A person who has genuinely interpreted Scripture here has not mainly added to their store of information.
  They have been changed: their perception deepened, their orientation toward God made more specific,
  their desire trained toward the reality the text makes present. And that change is what makes deeper
  interpretation possible next time — the same cumulative spiral that runs through the whole formation
  sequence.


  But interpretation cut off from the other formation modes would stop being what this word names and
  collapse back into the analytical distortion it exists to refute. The reader who brings only language
  and technique — without the Eucharist''s enacted sharing in the Logos''s life, without fasting''s training
  of desire toward what Scripture holds rather than what the reader wants to find, without the household''s
  daily testing of whether the text''s transformation is actually at work in a life, without the community''s
  martyr-memory of what faithful witness costs — has the tools and not the formed perception this world''s
  interpretation requires. The other modes are not the preparation for interpretation, as if one climbed
  through them to arrive at interpretation as the destination. They are its ongoing condition. Remove
  any of them and what remains is not interpretation at a shallower depth. What remains is analysis.


  Interpretation is the school tradition''s primary formation mode, and so the complementary channel to
  what the sacraments and the martyr-witness give the whole community. It reaches those who can access
  the school''s modes — the literate, the taught, those with a teacher. The rest of the community meets
  Scripture through the assembly''s shared hearing and is formed through the Eucharist, the household,
  and the martyr-ideal that shapes our common identity. Interpretation at the school''s depth is the mode
  that requires formation to receive — and so it both builds on and deepens every other practice the interpreter
  already lives.'
distortion_risk: '**World Hearing:**

  Interpretation as a formation discipline — the practice through which the soul meets the Logos who speaks
  through Scripture at the depth formation has prepared. What changes is first the interpreter''s perception,
  not their information. This is why the same text, read by the same methods, yields different depths
  to different readers: the depth of the encounter depends on the depth of formation the reader brings.
  The reader formed through years of prayer, contemplation, and community life brings a different perceptual
  capacity than one with only linguistic and historical competence, however impressive. Interpretation
  is what the formed nous does with Scripture; the nous''s formation is the difference between shallow
  and deep encounter.'

modern_hearing: '**Modern Hearing:**

  Interpretation as analytical method applied to a text to determine its meaning. The interpreter is the
  expert who knows the languages, the contexts, the frameworks, and applies them to produce a determination;
  the more sophisticated the method, the more competent the interpreter. What changes is the interpreter''s
  knowledge — before, they did not know what the text meant; after, they do. Its popular echo: "interpreting
  Scripture" as figuring out what it really means, against misreadings. Both locate interpretation in
  the cognitive part of the encounter and test it by whether the result is correct.'
