quick_meaning: For this world, a *raza* is not a stand-in or illustration for the *shrara* (truth) it
  points to — it is bound to that truth and actually carries something of its hidden power, so that reading
  Scripture and creation by *raza* is closer to perceiving a real connection than decoding an arbitrary
  sign.
world_meaning: 'To read by raza is to hold that the world itself, and Scripture within it, is woven through
  with real connections that a properly formed eye can perceive — not connections invented by a clever
  interpreter, but connections that are already there, waiting to be seen. A raza is not a symbol in the
  modern sense of an agreed-upon stand-in, chosen more or less arbitrarily to represent something else.
  When Ephrem calls the Old Testament''s persons and events razê (plural) of Christ, he does not mean
  they merely foreshadow him the way a signpost foreshadows a destination it has no part in. He means
  the raza is bound to the shrara it signifies and carries within itself something of that shrara''s own
  hidden power. To perceive a raza rightly is to be brought, in some real measure, into contact with the
  truth it holds — a hidden power (hayla kasya) the raza itself carries, not a meaning attached to it
  from outside.


  Aphrahat works with the same underlying conviction — Old Testament figures and events read as razê,
  types, of Christ and the church''s own life — but his own usage is plainer and less theoretically elaborated
  than Ephrem''s. Where Ephrem builds an entire method of theological argument on the raza/shrara pairing,
  layering it across hymn after hymn, Aphrahat reaches for it as one exegetical tool among several, without
  developing it into a named, self-conscious hermeneutic. A participant should not hear Aphrahat''s use
  of typology and assume it carries the same theoretical weight Ephrem gives the same underlying move
  — the term functions in both voices, but at genuinely different depths.


  This way of reading extends beyond Scripture into creation itself: for Ephrem, the created world''s
  own furniture — light, water, oil, the vine — can function as raza, carrying the hidden power of the
  truth it discloses, not merely illustrating a point that could equally well be made another way. This
  is part of why so much of this world''s theological argument happens in hymn rather than treatise: a
  raza is something to be sung into and dwelt within, not merely stated and moved past.
