quick_meaning: The qyama is this world's own committed, celibate order of "sons and daughters of the covenant"
  — men and women who take a lifelong vow but remain resident among their own kin in town rather than
  withdrawing to the desert, making this world's asceticism a discipline practiced in the middle of ordinary
  community life rather than a flight from it.
world_meaning: 'To take the qyama is to stand for a promise that does not end: not a season of youth to
  be outgrown, but a lifelong undertaking, entered by both men (bnay qyama) and women (bnat qyama). This
  is a town''s own askesis, not the desert''s — the bar qyama and bat qyama live among their kin, unmarried,
  keeping the fast and the watch, present at the vigil and the reading, formed less by flight from the
  world than by refusal within it. Aphrahat, writing to his own community in Demonstration 6 — sometimes
  called "Aphrahat''s Rule" for its practical instruction — addresses this order as something already
  established and needing correction more than founding: a discipline in the middle of its life, not its
  first spring.


  The qyama''s connection to Ephrem runs differently, and this world''s own record requires holding two
  evidentiary layers apart rather than blending them. What is firsthand and contemporary is Aphrahat''s
  own testimony to the order''s existence, discipline, and communal shape. What is later is the specific
  claim that Ephrem himself organized or personally led the bnat qyama as women''s liturgical choirs performing
  his own hymns — a claim resting on Jacob of Serugh''s sixth-century panegyric and the sixth-century
  Syriac Vita Ephraemi, not on Ephrem''s own surviving corpus using the technical term in so many words.
  A participant asking about this world''s ascetic order should hear the qyama''s own vowed, communal,
  town-based character as firmly attested; a participant asking specifically whether Ephrem personally
  directed women''s choirs should hear that this is later tradition, not this world''s own contemporary
  self-testimony.
