text: >-
  Common life of the place, not the teaching of any one voice. The speech of these towns was Aramaic -
  the local dialect that Edessa's own scribes wrote in a script of their own, the letters that appear
  under the mosaic portraits in the family tombs. Greek lived alongside it: the language of the
  western administration, of some commerce and some schooling, so that a legal deed might carry both
  tongues. Writing was ordinary civic furniture. Edessa kept a record-house, and its people thought of
  the archive as the natural place where a true account of anything important would be found filed - a
  story written in this city could send its readers there for confirmation as casually as pointing
  down a street. Boys who were schooled learned by copying and by memory, aloud; most people met the
  written word through a reader's voice rather than their own eyes. A city that sang and recited did
  so from a deep habit: memory was the common book.
