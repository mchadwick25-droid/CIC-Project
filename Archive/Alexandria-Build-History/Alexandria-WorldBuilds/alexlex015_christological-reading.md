## Quick Meaning

Christological Reading is not a method but an orientation — the conviction that Scripture, at every level and in every passage, is the address of the Logos who is Christ, so that reading Scripture Christianly means reading it always in relation to the one who speaks through it and who is present in it from beginning to end.

---

## World Meaning

A distinction is needed before the content can be understood: Christological Reading is an orientation, not a method. The allegorical method (alexlex016) is a method — a set of interpretive moves applied to a text in order to reach its deeper meanings. Christological Reading is what governs and gives purpose to those moves. Without the orientation, the method is directionless. Without the method, the orientation has no way to reach the depths it is aimed at. But they are not the same thing, and the Scripture entry (alexlex014) must be held as the ground of both: Scripture is the Logos's living address, and both the orientation and the method are ways of attending to what the Logos is saying.

Philo of Alexandria interpreted the Jewish scriptures allegorically. He was brilliant, systematic, and deeply attentive to the text's depths. His allegorical readings uncovered layers of philosophical and spiritual meaning that the literal surface does not present. By any description of allegorical method, Philo was practicing it with great skill. And yet what Philo was doing and what Origen or Clement was doing are not the same thing. The difference is the orientation: Philo was reading the scriptures in relation to the divine Wisdom and the Logos as philosophical principles; the Alexandrian Christian interpreters were reading the same scriptures — now expanded to include the New Testament — in relation to the Logos who became flesh in Jesus Christ.

This is what makes Alexandrian biblical interpretation distinctively Christian rather than generically allegorical. The Logos who speaks through Scripture is the same Logos who is incarnate as Christ. This means that when an Alexandrian Christian interpreter reads Genesis or Exodus or the Psalms or Proverbs, they are reading the speech of one whose identity is known: the eternal Word who became human, who taught by the shores of Galilee, who was crucified and raised, who was confessed at Nicaea as of one substance with the Father. The depths they are reaching for in the allegorical reading are the Logos's own self-disclosure — and the Logos they are disclosing is Christ.

This is why the Song of Songs, in Origen's reading, becomes a meditation on the soul's union with the Logos. Not because the soul-and-Logos reading is forced onto a text that "really" means something else. But because the text's ground and goal is the Logos, and the soul's union with the Logos is what the entire formation ecology is aimed at, and a text whose author and subject is the Logos will, when read with formed perception, yield content whose deepest level concerns the relationship between the soul and the Logos. The Christological orientation is what makes this reading possible and what prevents it from being arbitrary: the reader is not finding anything they want in the text; they are finding the one who is always speaking through it.

Christological Reading is always-present in this ecology. It is not applied selectively to passages that seem to be about Christ. It is the orientation of every encounter with every scriptural text. A narrative about crossing the Red Sea is read as an event in which the God who delivers Israel from slavery is the same God who delivers humanity from the slavery of sin and death through baptism — not because the typological connection is invented but because both events are actions of the same Logos, and Scripture carries that connection whether or not any individual reader perceives it. The formed reader perceives it. The unformed reader sees only the narrative.

The Christological orientation also governs the relationship between the two Testaments. The Old Testament is not a collection of ancient Jewish texts that Christians have repurposed. In this world, the same Logos who is incarnate as Christ speaks throughout both Testaments, and the Old Testament is not primarily a preparation for Christ (as if it had its own separate meaning that Christ then fulfills) but the Logos's address through texts that the full incarnate identity of the Logos now illumines from within. Moses's encounter with God, Abraham's faith, the Psalms' prayers, Wisdom's call — these are encounters with, expressions of, and addresses by the same Logos whose identity the incarnation has now disclosed fully.

*[TC] The specific questions that are genuinely contested: How much of the Old Testament can be read as directly about Christ without distorting its historical meaning? What constrains Christological reading from becoming arbitrary? What is the relationship between the typological reading (Moses as a type of Christ) and the allegorical reading (the crossing of the Red Sea as the soul's journey)? These questions generated the Antiochene-Alexandrian debate about interpretive method, and they are not fully resolved. The broadly shared orientation — Scripture is read toward Christ because it is the Logos's address — is Widely Accepted. The specific practices and their scope and limits are more contested both within the tradition and in modern scholarship.*

## Ecological Function

Christological Reading is the interpretive orientation that governs how the entire Scripture entry's content is realized in practice. It is what distinguishes Alexandrian Christian interpretation from every other form of scriptural engagement in the ancient world.

**What Christological Reading anchors:**

- **The coherence of Scripture as a whole**: The Old Testament and the New Testament are not two separate collections of texts related by historical accident. They are two modes of the same Logos's address — the one whose incarnation the New Testament announces having spoken throughout the Old Testament. Christological Reading is what makes this conviction operational: it is the practice through which the unity of Scripture as the Logos's address is realized in interpretation.

- **The discipline of allegorical reading**: The allegorical method (alexlex016) requires the Christological orientation to be anything other than arbitrary. The interpreter who finds Christ in a passage through allegorical reading is not discovering a meaning they brought to the text. They are perceiving the presence of the one who is always speaking through the text — and that orientation is what the Christological Reading entry names. Allegory without Christological orientation is a reading technique. Allegorical reading oriented toward Christ is an expression of the conviction that Scripture is the Logos's address.

- **Theon's interpretive posture**: Theon reads every text with this orientation. When he reads a Psalm, he is reading the Logos's address and looking for what the Logos is saying about the soul's condition, its journey, its encounter with divine reality. When he reads a narrative in Genesis, he is reading the Logos's address and attending to what the events reveal about the Logos's own character and action. The Christological orientation is not a technique he applies; it is the assumption that organizes every encounter with the text.

- **The relationship to the Rule of Faith**: The Rule of Faith — the received apostolic deposit that governs what Scripture can mean — is partly a Christological constraint on interpretation. Interpretations that do not arrive at Christ, that do not cohere with the community's confession of who Christ is, have missed what Scripture is about. The Rule of Faith is not an external limitation on interpretation; it is the community's accumulated expression of what the Logos has made known about himself through Scripture.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing**: "Christological Reading" in modern hearing often sounds like eisegesis — reading a meaning into a text that is not actually there. The concern is that finding Christ in the Old Testament imposes a later Christian framework onto texts that had their own meaning in their own context, and that allegorical readings of, say, the Song of Songs as a meditation on the soul's union with Christ are creative theological fiction rather than actual interpretation of what the text says.

This concern is not entirely wrong — it names a real risk. And the Antiochene tradition within Christianity raised the same concern from inside: the Antiochenes worried that allegorical reading licenses arbitrary interpretation with no real constraint on what can be found in a text. The Alexandrian response is not to deny the risk but to name what constrains it: the Christological orientation is not an arbitrary orientation but a theologically grounded one (the Logos who speaks through Scripture is the same Logos who is Christ), the Rule of Faith governs what interpretations are coherent with the community's confession, and the formation of the interpreter's nous constrains what is perceived rather than invented.

**World Hearing**: Christological Reading is not what a reader does to a text. It is what the text does to a reader who has been formed to hear it. The Logos who speaks through Scripture is the Logos who is Christ, and the text whose ground and goal is the Logos will yield its deepest content to the reader who knows who is speaking. The formed reader does not impose Christ on a text that is really about something else; they perceive the presence of the one who has been speaking throughout, now disclosed more fully by the incarnation that allows his identity to be named. The historical meaning of the text is not erased — the Red Sea crossing really happened, the Psalms really express the anguish and hope of real human beings before God — but those events and expressions are events and expressions of the Logos, and perceiving that is perceiving more fully what actually occurred, not less.

## Key Sources

- **Origen**, *Commentary on John*, Books I–II — The most explicit development of the conviction that the Logos of John 1 speaks through the entire scriptural corpus. [Author Gravity note: Origen's systematic account dominates; the orientation itself is more widely attested.]
- **Origen**, *Commentary on the Song of Songs*, Prologue and Book I — The soul's union with the Logos as the depth-reading of the Song. The application of Christological Reading to a non-obvious text.
- **Clement of Alexandria**, *Stromata*, Books IV–V — Christological Reading as the framework within which Clement's allegorical practice operates: the Logos who speaks through Scripture is the teacher toward whom philosophy and Scripture both orient.
- **Athanasius**, *Letter to Marcellinus on the Psalms* — Every Psalm addresses the soul in its actual condition before God — and the God whom the Psalms address is the Logos now known as Christ.
- **1 Corinthians 10:1–4** (scriptural) — Paul's typological reading of the Exodus as a text about Christ. The scriptural authorization for the Christological Reading of Old Testament events.
- **Luke 24:27** (scriptural) — "Beginning with Moses and all the prophets, he interpreted to them the things about himself in all the scriptures." The scriptural grounding for reading the whole Old Testament as Christ's address.
