## The 1-Minute — ~170 words — the hallway version. Answers: what is it?

Most of us know Christianity through a single perspective: one tradition, one culture, one way of following Jesus. Church in Conversation is designed to widen that perspective.

We start with the early church, rebuilding documented Christian movements from their own letters, sermons, and arguments: a first-century house church, desert fathers and mothers in Egypt, the Syriac Church of the East. Later centuries are already underway. And you don't read about them. You sit at a table and talk with them, through a representative voice that speaks from inside that Christian tradition.

It's AI, locked inside each tradition's own source documents. It can't browse the internet, and it tells you plainly when the evidence is thin. It's a doorway to more conversations like these, where participants experience Jesus as others knew and worshiped Him.

Four traditions are alive today. Give me twenty minutes, and I'll introduce you.

## The 3-Minute — ~440 words — the coffee version. Adds: the experience, the trust question, the refusal, who it's for.

Most of us know Christianity through a single perspective: one tradition, one culture, one way of following Jesus. Twenty centuries of the church's lived faith — its courage, its arguments, its unresolved questions — sit locked in academic books most people will never open. Church in Conversation is designed to widen that perspective.

Here's what it actually is. Imagine asking a woman who hosted a church in her home around the year 110 what it cost her family to belong. Or asking a desert monk — a man who spent decades alone with his own thoughts — what to do with the thoughts you can't make stop. Or seating two of them at the same table, centuries apart, asking both how they know their teaching is true — and watching two genuinely different answers emerge. That's the experience: documented Christian movements, rebuilt from their own words, that you meet in conversation.

Now, everyone's first question is the right one: it's AI — how do I know it isn't making things up? Three answers. First, each voice is locked inside its own Christian tradition's source documents — it cannot browse the internet, and when its library doesn't cover something, it says so. Second, it shows its work: hover over any highlighted claim and you get a plain explanation; click, and you get the sources, the stories behind it, and how confident we are — down to the footnote. Third, we attack every voice with an adversarial test battery before any person meets it, and outside scholars review the work — and until that review is done, we tell every participant exactly that.

And it has a real, written constitution — forbidding persuasion, recruitment, manipulation, smoothed-over arguments, or pretended certainty. We measure whether each Christian movement is represented in an engaging and accurate way. What your experience means to you is yours to own and share.

It's for the curious and the searching, for pastors and classrooms — and especially for people wrestling with their faith, who need to discover that the house is so much bigger than the room they grew up in. Four traditions are alive now, nine more are in construction, real testers are sitting down with it as we speak, and scholarly review comes next. And it will always be free to any seeker — that's the model: churches and schools with budgets fund the access of everyone without one.

What I'd love from you is twenty minutes at the Table. Meet one of them. Then tell me plainly what you found.

## The 5-Minute — ~740 words — the whole thing. Adds: your story, the convictions, the measurement principle, the mission-access commitment, the full ask ladder.

I spent forty years in ministry — Bible translation in Papua New Guinea, twelve years with Compassion International, global training work in thirty languages. A couple of years ago, on a sabbatical, my son and I read the whole story of the church together — twenty centuries of it. And I couldn't shake two feelings: how much treasure is buried in that story, and how completely locked away it is. Most of us know Christianity through a single perspective: one tradition, one culture, one way of following Jesus.

Three things I love shaped the answer. The Bible Project, which makes serious scholarship accessible without dumbing it down. Colonial Williamsburg, where you don't read about the past — you stand inside it and it talks back. And honest history — the kind that names the church's failures alongside its beauty. Church in Conversation is my attempt to hold all three at once.

Here's what it is. We reconstruct historical Christian traditions — a particular community, in a particular time and place, with its own way of knowing Jesus — from their actual letters, sermons, rules of life, and arguments. Then you sit at a table and talk with a Representative: a voice built to speak from inside that tradition. Ask the woman who hosted a house church in the year 110 what it cost her family. Ask the desert mother what to do with the thoughts you can't stop. Seat the Syriac scholar and the Bethlehem translator together and ask them both how they know what's true — and watch two genuinely different voices answer.

Yes, it's AI — and eighteen months ago I wasn't a technology guy, so believe me when I say the governance came before the code. Each voice is sealed inside its tradition's source documents; it cannot browse the internet; when its sources are thin, it lowers its confidence out loud. Every claim you see is highlighted — hover for the plain explanation, click for the sources and the stories, down to the footnote. Before any person meets a voice, we attack it with an adversarial test battery — trying to catch it being out-of-period, overconfident, unsafe, or sloppy. And outside scholars review the work itself. Until they have, we tell every participant: this is a careful draft, not a vetted one. Saying plainly what isn't finished is part of the product.

Church in Conversation is committed to something specific: building a representative to speak for a Christian tradition, as a witness. It states plainly what that tradition believed, lived, and held to according to the primary sources. It's forbidden to argue you into believing the same thing. The system is built to reliably tell the documented story. It keeps the church's real tensions in view rather than settling them for you. Each historical Christian tradition is bound by the dates the sources identify. This is a doorway to sit with the Christian movements of history. It is not built to replace living community. Ask, and we'll gladly tell you what God is doing here. What we track is whatever helps us steward it faithfully. There are numbers we refuse to even collect, because some measurements bend a ministry toward pressures it should never feel.

Where it stands: four traditions are alive and conversing well. Nine more are in the construction pipeline. A structured pilot with real testers is running now; an expanded pilot and formal scholarly review come next; then public launch. We built it as a Colorado public benefit corporation for one reason: the mission is access. It will always be free to any seeker — and the churches, seminaries, and classrooms that have budgets will fund the access of everyone who doesn't. The more funding, the more open doors. That's the whole financial model, and it's in the charter, not just my heart.

So here's what I'd ask. First — before anything else — come and join us at the Table for twenty minutes; the conversation is a better argument than I am. Then, if it moves you: pray for it, push on it, tell me what's wrong with it. Open a door — a scholar who should review it, a pastor who should test it, a church that might partner. And if God stirs you toward the practical: it costs about a dollar a month to keep one seeker at the Table for a year, and the next gate — a hundred testers and our first scholarly review — costs about what a used car does. Every gift buys access for someone who couldn't buy it themselves.

That's Church in Conversation. Twenty centuries of the church, one table, and a chair pulled out for you.

## Delivery notes

• The doorway/house line is the hook in all three — it's yours (from the Letter to Friends) and it lands with every audience. Never cut it.

• The trust question ("it's AI — how do I know it isn't making things up?") is coming whether you raise it or not. Raising it yourself, first, is the credibility move.

• Every version ends by offering the demo, not asking for money. The Table sells itself; the packet handles the numbers.

• When the listener is a scholar, lead with confidence calibration and thin-evidence candor. When a pastor, lead with doorway-not-home. When a funder, add: "a dollar a month keeps one seeker at the Table for a year."

• The measurement line ("…how it impacts the kingdom belongs to our Lord.") is a protected asset — say it as written, don't paraphrase it.
