The Missionary Era · 1815-1906 · Jamaica, Demerara

The Caribbean Churches of Slavery and Emancipation

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

George Liele was a Black Baptist preacher from Georgia. His master, Henry Sharp, had freed him some years before he died. When the British left Georgia, Liele came to Jamaica as an indentured servant, to pay a debt to Colonel Kirkland. Phillippo dates his coming to 1782. Several members of his church followed. In 1813 the Baptist Missionary Society sent John Rowe to work with one of them, Moses Baker, near Falmouth. The Wesleyans had begun work on the island in 1789.

In 1843 a missionary named James Phillippo counted the churches of Jamaica. He found 226 places of worship. Native Baptists held eight or nine of them. They had fourteen ministers.

Samuel Sharpe was a Baptist. The missionary Henry Bleby talked with him in jail. Bleby writes that Sharpe learned from his Bible that whites had no more right to hold Black people in slavery than Black people had to hold whites. Sharpe used prayer meetings on the estates to spread his plan. Those who joined swore not to work again after the Christmas holidays unless they were free. The rising broke out a day or two after Christmas Day, 1831. Bleby writes that once it began, it was beyond Sharpe's control. Sharpe was executed at Montego Bay on 23 May 1832.

The planters blamed the missionaries for the rising. Bleby writes that three Baptist missionaries were tried or charged. A Moravian, Mr. Pfeiffer, faced a court-martial, and a Wesleyan, Mr. Box, was arrested. None of them was found guilty.

On 31 July 1838 the Baptist chapel at Falmouth filled for a night of prayer. The missionary William Knibb pointed to the clock. At the last stroke of midnight he cried, “The monster is dead: the negro is free.” Early next morning a crowd buried a chain, a whip and an iron collar in a coffin, as at a funeral. On 30 November 1838 Knibb wrote to Mr. Sturge about buying land for a village, which he called Birmingham.

In Demerara, now part of Guyana, enslaved people rose in revolt in August 1823. Many in the colony blamed John Smith, who served the London Missionary Society. The court sentenced him to death on 24 November 1823. His memoirist, Edwin Wallbridge, said the charge rested on lies. Smith died on 6 February 1824. The King lifted the sentence, but word of it reached the colony only on 30 March 1824.

In Its Own Record

Grew Out Of

  • The Baptists Inferential-Thin Major — In 1782 George Liele, a Black Baptist pastor, came from Georgia to Kingston. In 1813 the Baptist Missionary Society sent John Rowe to work with Moses Baker, one of his church. This rests on one account of 1843, one letter of 1793 and Liele's own narrative. No modern study was opened. Phillippo calls Liele a slave brought by his master. Liele's own narrative says he was freed and came as an indentured servant.
  • The Methodist Revival Inferential-Thin Major — The Wesleyans began work in Jamaica in 1789, under Dr. Coke, and set up a permanent station at Kingston. They worked the same island as the Baptists, another strand of this story. In 1832 a Wesleyan, Mr. Box, was arrested after the rising, as Bleby tells. This rests on two accounts: Phillippo, a Baptist missionary, in 1843, and Bleby in 1868. No modern study was opened.

In Contact With

  • The Moravian Church after 1815 Inferential-Thin — In 1831 slaves in Jamaica rose up. Clergy of several churches were then tried. Hamilton says all were cleared. Bleby names a Moravian, Pfeiffer, tried with Baptists and a Wesleyan. Both writers were missionaries.
