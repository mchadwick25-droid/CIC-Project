Chalcedonian Monasticism - Judean Desert & Gaza

The Judean desert, Gaza and Sinai, c. 450 to 614 CE — monks whose surviving letters record not only what a rule required, but what ordinary people actually asked and what they were told back.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:33

Read along

In the century after Chalcedon, monks settled in the ravines between Jerusalem and the Dead Sea, and in the villages around Gaza. They became almost a second population. In the laurae, hermits lived apart through the week and gathered on Sundays. Shared houses held hundreds. This tradition stands out clearly because its own papers survive. Cyril of Scythopolis wrote carefully dated lives of Euthymius, Sabas and the men who came after them. Two recluses near Gaza, Barsanuphius and John, left roughly 850 letters. They answered monks, married couples, sick people and local officials, one question at a time. Most of the Judean houses came to back Chalcedon. Euthymius took the council's side. His biographer says nearly all the desert monks would have followed him, had a rival monk named Theodosius not turned many of them against it. Near Gaza, Peter the Iberian became bishop for those who rejected the council. The empire pulled the monks hard into its fights over doctrine. One was the purge of teaching drawn from Origen in the 550s, which split their own houses. In 614 the Persian army sacked Jerusalem and tore through the desert houses. Those who lived wrote down what was lost.

Documented Stories

Sabas at Court, Aged Ninety-Two

The emperor Anastasius was pressing the eastern churches away from Chalcedon, and the patriarch of Jerusalem, Elias, sent the desert’s most respected abbot to plead the Palestinian case. Cyril of Scythopolis, who wrote Sabas’ life forty years later from the monks who had gone with him, describes the arrival: the palace guards, seeing a shabby old man among the delegation of abbots, tried to keep him out, and the emperor had him sent for. Anastasius, who liked him, offered him a thousand gold pieces for his monasteries; Sabas took them and, Cyril says, asked for something else besides — that the emperor remit the taxes of the province, ruined by Samaritan revolt and by the devastation of the countryside around Jerusalem, and that he leave Elias and the churches of Palestine alone. The emperor granted the first and, for a time, the second. Sabas also, Cyril reports, told Anastasius to his face that Chalcedon was the faith of the fathers and that the desert would not accept anything else, and the emperor let it pass. Sabas went home; Elias was deposed anyway in 516, and Sabas led ten thousand monks — Cyril’s number — to the new patriarch’s church to make him proclaim the council in public. He returned to court once more at ninety-one, under Justinian, with the same combination of requests: money for the poor, remission of taxes, hospitals, and the suppression of Origenism.

The New Laura Is Emptied

The Judean desert had been fighting over Origen for twenty years. The New Laura, a daughter house of Sabas’ Great Laura, had become the center of monks who read Origen and Evagrius on the pre-existence of souls and the final restoration of all things; the Great Laura and its allies regarded this as heresy and appealed to emperors and patriarchs against it. The quarrel included the murder of a monk, the beating of others, the expulsion of the anti-Origenists from their own houses, and a decade in which the Origenist party controlled the patriarchate of Jerusalem. In 553 Justinian’s council at Constantinople anathematized Origen and his followers. Cyril of Scythopolis records what happened next in his own community. In 555 the patriarch Eustochius obtained troops from the dux of Palestine and went to the New Laura; the monks who would not accept the council were driven out, ‘expelled from the whole province’, and the house was resettled with a hundred and twenty monks from orthodox communities. Cyril was one of them. He writes that he entered the New Laura on 21 February 555 and stayed until he moved to the Great Laura two years later, and it was there he wrote his Lives. He tells the story as a triumph of the faith. It is also the record of a monastic tradition using imperial soldiers to settle a theological dispute with its own brothers, told by a beneficiary. The expelled monks disappear from history.

‘I Carry Half Your Burden’

Barsanuphius was an Egyptian who had walled himself into a cell at the monastery of Seridos near Gaza around 520 and communicated with the world only through the abbot, who carried written questions in and written answers out. Among them is a long run of letters — more than fifty — to a monk named Andrew, old and ill, who wrote in fear: that his sickness was a punishment, that he could not keep the fasts, that his thoughts tormented him, that he was a burden to the brothers who tended him. The Old Man’s answers are short and patient. Andrew is to eat what the illness requires and not call it sin; he is to stop counting his failures; the brothers who serve him are earning their own reward. And then, in one letter, this: ‘I will carry half your burden, and God will carry the rest’ — and later, when Andrew writes again in despair, ‘I have said that I carry your burden; now I say that I carry all of it, on the condition that you carry the command not to grieve.’ Andrew’s side of the correspondence is preserved only in the summaries the compiler wrote above each answer, but it is enough to hear a sick old man being talked back from panic by someone who never saw his face. The desert this Christian tradition documents was not only heroic; it was also a place where someone answered the post.

Connections

In Its Own Record

It drew on Desert Monasticism, and its writers quoted the elders of Egypt by name. Its monks went back and forth to Sinai, where John Climacus later wrote the Ladder. Its services and teaching passed on into later Orthodox practice.

Direct descendants

  • gave rise to Melkite Arabic Christianity Dominant Modern Reconstruction Major — Mar Saba was one of the Judean desert houses founded before 614. Chalcedonian monks still lived there under Arab rule. Modern historians place John of Damascus among them. They trace the first Christian theology in Arabic to these monks. This rests on modern histories, not on a text from the time.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced Desert Monasticism Documented — Dorotheus of Gaza, teaching his monks in the sixth century, quotes the Egyptian desert elders by name. He cites Antony saying he no longer feared God, and he praises a saying of Abba Poemen. He also quotes the Book of the Elders, the collected sayings of the desert.

Opposed By

  • argued against Peter the Iberian Inferential-Thin — Peter the Iberian was made bishop by Theodosius, the monk set up against Juvenal and the council. His circle's books attack Chalcedon and those who defended it. But no text held here shows Peter writing or acting against the Judean desert monks by name, so this link is thin.

In Contact With

  • in contact with The Byzantine Imperial Church (Justinianic) Documented — Evagrius Scholasticus says the patriarch of Jerusalem sent leading monks from Judea to Constantinople. This was before the council of 553. Conon was from the monastery of Saba, and Rufus was from that of Theodosius. At court it was they who raised the questions about what Origen taught.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Hermits in scattered cells and monks in shared houses lived in the ravines of the Judean desert and the villages near Gaza. What they left behind is rare. Two recluses, Barsanuphius and John of Gaza, wrote roughly 850 letters. In them they gave answers to ordinary monks, married couples and sick people one question at a time. Most of these houses backed Chalcedon, yet the empire still pulled them into its fights over doctrine. The Persian army tore their houses apart when it sacked Jerusalem in 614.

Legacy

Much of what Eastern Orthodox monks still do draws on these houses. A book of services named after Sabas, the Typikon of Saint Sabas, was printed at Venice from 1515. Parts of it entered the Orthodox service books. John Climacus wrote the Ladder of Divine Ascent at Sinai, in the later 500s or about 600. The Greeks held him in great value, Philippe Labbe says, and Elias of Crete wrote explanations of the Ladder. Dorotheus of Gaza is still standard reading for the spiritual life. Two of the houses from this period still have monks living in them: Mar Saba in the Judean desert and Saint Catherine's at Sinai. They are among the oldest Christian houses anywhere to have been lived in without a break.

Experience It Today

  • Mar Saba, in the Kidron valley between Bethlehem and the Dead Sea — founded by Sabas in 483 and inhabited by monks almost continuously since

How Sure the Record Is

RICH - Cyril of Scythopolis's dated lives; Barsanuphius & John of Gaza's ~850 letters of direction to ordinary people

  • Cyril of Scythopolis, Lives of the Monks of Palestine (trans. R. M. Price, intro. J. Binns, 1991) (primary) — Dated, sober lives of Euthymius, Sabas and the Judean Desert founders; Cistercian Studies 114, in print.
  • Barsanuphius and John, Letters (trans. John Chryssavgis, FOTC 113-114, 2006-07) (primary) — The ~850 letters of spiritual direction to monks and ordinary laypeople in Gaza; first complete English translation (CUA Press), in print.
  • John Moschus, The Spiritual Meadow (trans. John Wortley, 1992) (primary) — Anecdotes gathered across Palestinian and Sinai monasticism right at the era's end (c.600); Cistercian Studies 139, in print; the anecdotal genre calls for caution as historical evidence.
  • Yizhar Hirschfeld, The Judean Desert Monasteries in the Byzantine Period (1992) (secondary) — Archaeological synthesis of the actual monasteries - diet, water, cells; complements the texts materially; Yale UP.
  • Jennifer Hevelone-Harper, Disciples of the Desert: Monks, Laity, and Spiritual Authority in Sixth-Century Gaza (2005) (secondary) — Modern study built directly on the Barsanuphius correspondence - spiritual direction of ordinary people; Johns Hopkins UP.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity, vol. 2, ch. 27, 'Asceticism and monasticism, I: Eastern' (Samuel Rubenson) (reference) — Reference-tradition chapter for eastern monasticism to c.600.
