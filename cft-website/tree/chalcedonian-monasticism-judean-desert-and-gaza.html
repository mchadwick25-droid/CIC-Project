The Age of Monks and Empires · c. 450-614 · Palestine, Sinai

Palestine (the historical region) · Sinai, today in Egypt

Chalcedonian Monasticism - Judean Desert & Gaza

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In the century after Chalcedon, monks settled in the ravines between Jerusalem and the Dead Sea, and in the villages around Gaza. They became almost a second population. In the laurae, hermits lived apart through the week and gathered on Sundays. Shared houses held hundreds. This tradition stands out clearly because its own papers survive. Cyril of Scythopolis wrote carefully dated lives of Euthymius, Sabas and the men who came after them. Two recluses near Gaza, Barsanuphius and John, left roughly 850 letters. They answered monks, married couples, sick people and local officials, one question at a time. Most of the Judean houses came to back Chalcedon. Euthymius took the council's side. His biographer says nearly all the desert monks would have followed him, had a rival monk named Theodosius not turned many of them against it. Near Gaza, Peter the Iberian became bishop for those who rejected the council. The empire pulled the monks hard into its fights over doctrine. One was the purge of teaching drawn from Origen in the 550s, which split their own houses. In 614 the Persian army sacked Jerusalem and tore through the desert houses. Those who lived wrote down what was lost.

In Its Own Record

Gave Rise To

  • Melkite Arabic Christianity Dominant Modern Reconstruction Major — Mar Saba was one of the Judean desert houses founded before 614. Chalcedonian monks still lived there under Arab rule. Modern historians place John of Damascus among them. They trace the first Christian theology in Arabic to these monks. This rests on modern histories, not on a text from the time.

Continues

  • Early Palestinian Ascetic Monasticism Inferential-Thin Major — Jerome's Life of Hilarion makes Hilarion the first monk in Palestine. John Moschus, writing around 600, mentions a monastery at Pharan. Neither text ties the later monks to the first ones, so this rests on place and sequence alone.

Influenced By

  • Desert Monasticism Documented — Dorotheus of Gaza, teaching his monks in the sixth century, quotes the Egyptian desert elders by name. He cites Antony saying he no longer feared God, and he praises a saying of Abba Poemen. He also quotes the Book of the Elders, the collected sayings of the desert.

In Contact With

  • The Byzantine Imperial Church (Justinianic) Documented — Evagrius says the patriarch of Jerusalem sent leading monks from Judea to Constantinople. This was before the council of 553. Conon was from the monastery of Saba, and Rufus was from that of Theodosius. At court it was they who raised the questions about what Origen taught.

Sources

  • Cyril of Scythopolis, Lives of the Monks of Palestine (trans. R. M. Price, intro. J. Binns, 1991) (primary) — Dated, sober lives of Euthymius, Sabas and the Judean Desert founders; Cistercian Studies 114, in print.
  • Barsanuphius and John, Letters (trans. John Chryssavgis, FOTC 113-114, 2006-07) (primary) — The ~850 letters of spiritual direction to monks and ordinary laypeople in Gaza; first complete English translation (CUA Press), in print.
  • John Moschus, The Spiritual Meadow (trans. John Wortley, 1992) (primary) — Anecdotes gathered across Palestinian and Sinai monasticism right at the era's end (c.600); Cistercian Studies 139, in print; the anecdotal genre calls for caution as historical evidence.
  • Yizhar Hirschfeld, The Judean Desert Monasteries in the Byzantine Period (1992) (secondary) — Archaeological synthesis of the actual monasteries - diet, water, cells; complements the texts materially; Yale UP.
  • Jennifer Hevelone-Harper, Disciples of the Desert: Monks, Laity, and Spiritual Authority in Sixth-Century Gaza (2005) (secondary) — Modern study built directly on the Barsanuphius correspondence - spiritual direction of ordinary people; Johns Hopkins UP.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity, vol. 2, ch. 27, 'Asceticism and monasticism, I: Eastern' (Samuel Rubenson) (reference) — Reference-tradition chapter for eastern monasticism to c.600.
