The Imperial Church Era · 4th c. · Aksum

Aksum, today in Ethiopia

Frumentius

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Aksumite Christianity (conversion era).

Known for
A Roman captive, taken as a boy when his ship's company was killed, who gathered Christians in the kingdom of Axum and was made its first bishop by Athanasius of Alexandria.
Also called
Abba Salama of Axum (Hartranft's note to Sozomen; "the Father of Peace" in the Dictionary of Christian Biography, quoted by Jackson)
Dates
Fourth century; the date of his consecration is disputed (see What is uncertain)
Place
Axum (Auxumis), in the kingdom of Aksum, in what is now Ethiopia
Role
Captive, royal official, missionary and first bishop of Axum

About This Person

Two small boys, Frumentius and Edesius, were found under a tree, going over their lessons, Rufinus says. Everyone else on their ship had just been killed. The people of that coast spared them out of pity and took them to their king.

Frumentius, from a family of Tyre, grew up a captive at that court and rose to share in ruling it. He gathered the Christian traders there to pray, then went to Alexandria to ask its bishop, Athanasius, for a bishop, and came back as that bishop. Rufinus calls the land India. But a later emperor's letter, kept by Athanasius, names its city Auxumis, that is Aksum, in what is now Ethiopia and Eritrea, and calls Frumentius its bishop.

Rufinus of Aquileia wrote the story down around 400, and the whole early life rests on him. In the times of Constantine, he writes, a philosopher from Tyre sailed there with two boys, his relatives, whom he was teaching. Homeward, the ship put in at a harbor for water. The people there had heard their treaty with Rome was broken, and by custom they killed every Roman they found.

The king made Edesius his cup-bearer, and gave Frumentius, whom he found sharp and wise, charge of his accounts and records. Dying, the king set them free, leaving his wife and small son as heirs. But the queen begged them, Frumentius above all, to stay and help her rule until her son grew up.

With the kingdom in his hands, Rufinus says, God stirred him to seek out Christians among the Roman traders. He urged them to set up meeting places to pray in, and gave them sites for buildings, so that a Christian seed might grow there.

When the prince came of age, they handed everything back and came back to the Roman world. Edesius went to his parents at Tyre. Rufinus names him as his source: "We learned that these things happened so, not from common talk, but from Edesius himself, who told it. He had been Frumentius's companion, and was later made a priest at Tyre."

Frumentius went to Alexandria instead, saying the Lord's work must not be hidden, and urged Athanasius to send a worthy man as bishop. Athanasius answered, Rufinus reports: "What other man like this could we find, with the Spirit of God in him as in you, who could carry it through in this way?" He made Frumentius bishop and sent him back.

The date is disputed. Rufinus gives no year, but sets it just after Athanasius became bishop, which was in 328. Athanasius's English editor says the story's timeline rules that out. He puts it no earlier than late 346, and says the emperor's letter reads more naturally if it was recent.

Back as bishop, Rufinus writes, it was said that signs like the apostles' were done through him, and countless people turned to the faith.

That tie to Alexandria was fought over. About 356, with Athanasius driven from his city, the emperor Constantius wrote to the rulers of Auxumis, demanding the same teaching in their churches as in the empire's. It ordered: "So send Frumentius the bishop to Egypt with all speed." There the emperor's bishop in Athanasius's place would judge him, and restore him only if found fit. The letter named who had made him: "This Frumentius was set in this rank by Athanasius, a man guilty of countless crimes." We do not know how the rulers answered, or what Frumentius did.

The church of Aksum kept taking its bishops from Alexandria. By 1681, a German scholar recorded, the Ethiopians counted ninety-five chief bishops in order from him. Three Greek church historians retold the story in the century after Rufinus, Socrates almost word for word, one editor says.

The Ethiopian church's book of saints, written down about a thousand years later, tells his story again. It says he preached the peace of Christ across Ethiopia, and was called Abba Salama for it, Abba meaning father. After he had greeted the people of Ethiopia, it says, he died in peace.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The church of Axum, which Rufinus of Aquileia says he began among the Roman merchants and the king's household, and over which Athanasius of Alexandria made him bishop.
  • Hiob Ludolf, writing in 1681 from an Ethiopian informant, reports that the Ethiopians counted ninety-five metropolitans, in order, from Frumentius to Simeon.

How they're remembered

  • Chester Hartranft, who translated Sozomen, notes that he was called the Abba Salama of Axum.
  • Blomfield Jackson, in a note to Theodoret, says the emperor Constantius later asked the prince of Axum to replace Frumentius with an Arian, Theophilus, without success. The chapter of Athanasius he cites does not name Theophilus.
  • The Ethiopian book of saints, put into Ge'ez in the fifteenth century by its editors' dating, keeps his day on 26 Hamle as Abba Salama, the Revealer of the Light, and says he was so called because he preached the peace of Christ.
  • Sozomen says he was revered as no less than an apostle. The Greek calendar of Nikodemos the Hagiorite keeps him on 30 November and calls him equal to the apostles, and the Roman Martyrology, in the Roman Breviary, names him first bishop of Ethiopia on 27 October.
  • John of Nikiu, a Coptic bishop writing in the late seventh century, names Afrudit, that is Frumentius, as a man of noble birth of India whom Athanasius made bishop. Mingana, the editor of the Syriac chroniclers, says they took the story from the three Greek historians rather than from Rufinus.

What Is Uncertain

  • Rufinus says he had the whole account from Edesius, Frumentius's companion, after Edesius became a presbyter at Tyre. So the story of his early life rests on that one chain. Socrates of Constantinople, Sozomen and Theodoret of Cyrrhus retell Rufinus, and Valesius, in A. C. Zenos's edition, says Socrates copied him almost word for word.
  • When he was consecrated is disputed. Rufinus places it soon after Athanasius became bishop, in 328. Valesius put it later, under Constantius. Archibald Robertson, who edited Athanasius in English, also puts it later, following Gwatkin: the dates in the story rule out the early year, and the emperor's letter fits a recent consecration.
  • The year of his death is not given in any held source.
  • "India" in these writers means Ethiopia, Zenos notes; the kingdom is Aksum, with its city of Axum.
  • No held source says how the rulers of Auxumis answered the emperor's letter, or what Frumentius did. The letter's date, about 356, is Robertson's dating of the Apology to Constantius (356 to 357). His table of dates gives the end of 346 as the earliest possible date for the consecration.
  • Theodoret differs from Rufinus on the killing: he has some of the crew drowned and the rest taken prisoner, where Rufinus has all but the two boys killed. Jackson, in a note, reads Rufinus's Roman way of prayer as the manner of the empire's citizens, not the customs of the church of Rome.
  • Philostorgius, in Photius's later summary, has an Arian envoy, Theophilus, come to the Auxumites. It names no Frumentius.
  • The Ethiopian book of saints, a late compilation, calls him Fremenatos, names the king Alameda, puts Frumentius's return under Abreha and Asbeha, has the two youths taught the work of war and says they brought up the king's children. None of this is in Rufinus.
  • A royal stone at Aksum, carved in Greek in the first half of the fourth century by Littmann's dating, names a king Aizanas and his brother Saiazanas. Littmann, following Nöldeke, matches the brother's name to Sazanas in the emperor's letter. That match is a modern reconstruction.
  • Rufinus opens by saying that no apostle's preaching had reached this further India, and that it first received the seeds of faith in the times of Constantine.
