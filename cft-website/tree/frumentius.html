The Imperial Church Era · 4th c. · Aksum

Aksum, today in Ethiopia

Frumentius

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Aksumite Christianity (conversion era).

Known for
A Roman captive, taken as a boy when his ship's company was killed, who gathered Christians in the kingdom of Axum and was made its first bishop by Athanasius of Alexandria.
Also called
Abba Salama of Axum (Hartranft's note to Sozomen; "the Father of Peace" in the Dictionary of Christian Biography, quoted by Jackson)
Dates
Fourth century; the date of his consecration is disputed (see What is uncertain)
Place
Axum (Auxumis), in the kingdom of Aksum, in what is now Ethiopia
Role
Captive, royal official, missionary and first bishop of Axum

About This Person

Two boys were found under a tree, studying their lessons, after everyone else on their ship had been killed. So Rufinus of Aquileia tells it in his Latin Church History. They were Frumentius and Edesius, young relatives of Meropius, a philosopher from Tyre, who had taken them to what Rufinus calls India. On the way home the ship put in for water. The local people had heard that their treaty with Rome was broken, and they killed all on board. They pitied the boys and took them to their king.

Rufinus says he had the story from Edesius himself, who later became a presbyter at Tyre. The Greek historians Socrates of Constantinople, Sozomen and Theodoret of Cyrrhus retell it after him. Valesius, in a note A. C. Zenos prints, says Socrates translated Rufinus almost word for word. Zenos adds that the writers meant Ethiopia by India.

The king made Edesius his cup-bearer and put Frumentius in charge of his accounts. When the king died, the queen asked the two to help rule until her son grew up. Frumentius sought out Christians among the Roman merchants. Rufinus says he urged them to set up places of prayer, and gave them sites.

When the prince came of age, the two went home. Frumentius went to Alexandria and asked its bishop, Athanasius of Alexandria, to send a bishop to the new Christians. Athanasius, Rufinus says, replied that they could find no better man than Frumentius himself. He consecrated him and sent him back.

A second witness does not depend on Rufinus. Athanasius later copied into his defense a letter from the emperor Constantius to the princes of Auxumis, the city of Axum. Athanasius calls him "Bishop of Auxumis." The emperor's letter speaks of "the Bishop Frumentius," and says he "was advanced to his present rank by Athanasius, a man who is guilty of ten thousand crimes."

Rufinus puts this soon after Athanasius became bishop. Valesius thought it came later, under Constantius. Archibald Robertson, who edited Athanasius in English, also puts it later, following Gwatkin. He says the dates in the story rule out the early year. The emperor's letter, he adds, makes most sense if Frumentius had been made bishop not long before.

He was called the Abba Salama of Axum, notes Chester Hartranft, who translated Sozomen. The Dictionary of Christian Biography, quoted by Blomfield Jackson, gives its meaning as "the Father of Peace."

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The church of Axum, which Rufinus of Aquileia says he began among the Roman merchants and the king's household, and over which Athanasius of Alexandria made him bishop.

How they're remembered

  • Chester Hartranft, who translated Sozomen, notes that he was called the Abba Salama of Axum.
  • Blomfield Jackson, in a note to Theodoret, says the emperor Constantius later asked the prince of Axum to replace Frumentius with an Arian, Theophilus, without success. The chapter of Athanasius he cites does not name Theophilus.

What Is Uncertain

  • Rufinus says he had the whole account from Edesius, Frumentius's companion, after Edesius became a presbyter at Tyre. So the story of his early life rests on that one chain. Socrates of Constantinople, Sozomen and Theodoret of Cyrrhus retell Rufinus, and Valesius, in A. C. Zenos's edition, says Socrates copied him almost word for word.
  • When he was consecrated is disputed. Rufinus places it soon after Athanasius became bishop, in 328. Valesius put it later, under Constantius. Archibald Robertson, who edited Athanasius in English, also puts it later, following Gwatkin: the dates in the story rule out the early year, and the emperor's letter fits a recent consecration.
  • The year of his death is not given in any held source.
  • "India" in these writers means Ethiopia, Zenos notes; the kingdom is Aksum, with its city of Axum.
