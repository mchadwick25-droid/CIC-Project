Rome, today in Italy · Latin Christendom

The Latin Papal Church (Gregorian Reform to Innocent III)

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In the middle of the eleventh century, a circle of reformers around Pope Leo IX set out to free the church from lay lords. These lords chose its bishops and sold its offices. The reformers ended by claiming for the papacy a power no bishop of Rome had held before. Gregory VII's Dictatus Papae of 1075 declared, among much else, that the pope may depose emperors. The Investiture Contest that followed ran for half a century. Its most famous scene came in 1077. The German king Henry IV stood barefoot for three days at the gate of Canossa, begging the pope's pardon. What followed was a working papal monarchy. Canon law was gathered into codes, papal legates went everywhere, and great councils met at the Lateran. Innocent III, pope from 1198 to 1216, judged between kings, approved Francis of Assisi's first friars and called crusades. In 1215 his Fourth Lateran Council made law for the whole Latin church. Its rules ran from how the eucharist is defined to a rule that every Christian confess once a year.

In Its Own Record

Gave Rise To

  • The Latin Church of Outremer Widely Accepted Major — At Clermont in 1095 Pope Urban II decreed that the journey to free the church of Jerusalem would count in place of all penance. After the crusade, Latin clergy filled the new sees in the East. The first Latin patriarch of Antioch had come as chaplain to the bishop of Le Puy. The see of Jerusalem went to the archbishop of Pisa.

Continued As

  • The Latin Papal Church (Avignon to Fifth Lateran) Documented Major — Gregory VII's Dictatus Papae of 1075 says that no one may judge the pope. In 1302 Boniface VIII, pope from 1294 to 1303, wrote in Unam sanctam that the highest spiritual power is judged by God alone. No human may judge it. The same see carried the same claim from Gregory to Boniface.

Influenced By

  • Carolingian Reform Christianity Widely Accepted — In the ninth century a forger in the Frankish lands made a collection of fake early papal letters, the False Decretals. In 1081 Pope Gregory VII quoted one of them, a letter said to be from Pope Julius, to argue that Rome could judge earthly rulers. His modern editor traced that letter to the forged collection.

Argued Against

  • The Byzantine Church after the Schism Documented — In July 1054 Cardinal Humbert and two other envoys of the pope cast the patriarch of Constantinople, Michael Cerularius, out of the church. Their written sentence calls him a patriarch in name only. It says the seeds of heresy are sown among his followers every day. Crusaders took Constantinople in 1204. Pope Innocent III then treated its church as his own. In 1205 he wrote that it had lately come back into unity with Rome.
  • The Humiliati Inferential-Thin — In 1184 Pope Lucius III condemned the Waldensians for claiming the right to preach. John Foxe, writing in the 1500s, reports a chronicler's claim that Lucius rejected the Humiliati with them. Foxe himself doubted another part of that story, about the friars approved in their place. This rests on that late report.
  • The Waldensians Documented Major — The inquisitor Bernard Gui, writing about 1320, tells the story. The archbishop of Lyon told Valdes's followers to stop preaching. They refused, so they were cut off from the church and driven from the city. Later a church council judged them heretics. A papal decree from the reign of Gregory IX names the 'Poor of Lyon' among the heretics it condemns.Lasting result: A church council at Rome met before the Lateran one. It judged Valdes's followers guilty of schism, and then it condemned them as heretics.
  • Cathars / Albigensians Documented Major — In 1179 a great church council at Rome condemned the heretics of Gascony and the lands around Albi and Toulouse, 'whom some call Cathars'. A later papal decree puts the Cathars first among the heretics it condemns. In 1209 the papacy went further and sent crusading armies against them.Lasting result: In 1179 the Third Lateran Council cut off the Cathars and all who defended or sheltered them. It barred anyone from housing them.
  • Joachimite Current Documented Major — In 1215 a great church council at Rome condemned a small book Joachim had written against Peter Lombard's teaching on the Trinity. In 1254 a friar's book built on Joachim's works caused alarm in Paris. A papal panel met at Anagni in 1255 to judge it, and the book was condemned.Lasting result: In 1215 the Fourth Lateran Council condemned and rejected a book by Joachim. In it he wrote against Peter Lombard on the Trinity.

Opposed By

  • The Reformed Cities - Zurich & Geneva Documented — In his Institutes, John Calvin of Geneva wrote against Pope Gregory VII by name. He said Gregory, once called Hildebrand, was the one who first won the popes the rank of emperors. He called him 'an impure and wicked man'. The popes after him, Calvin wrote, went on to rule the emperors.

Broke With

  • The Byzantine Church after the Schism Documented Major — Cardinal Humbert's own account says the papal legates laid their excommunication on the main altar of Hagia Sophia on 16 July 1054. He adds that Patriarch Michael then tried to bring them before a council there. This account of the break comes from the Latin side alone.

In Contact With

  • Cluniac Reform Monasticism Documented — In January 1077 Pope Gregory VII lifted his ban on King Henry IV at Canossa. Gregory's own report says the abbot of Cluny, Hugh, helped confirm the king's sworn promises. The record of the oath lists the abbot among those present on the king's side.
  • The Byzantine Church after the Schism Widely Accepted — At the Second Council of Lyon in 1274, the Greek side made a profession of faith. In it they accepted the full primacy of the Roman church. Its 1911 editor says Pope Clement IV first put the text to Emperor Michael VIII in 1267. Michael then offered it to Pope Gregory X at Lyon. The profession does not show how the Greek church received the union.
  • The Church of the East under the Mongols Documented — Rabban Sawma, a monk of the Church of the East, reached Rome as envoy of the Mongol king Arghun and of his own catholicos. He told the cardinals he carried letters 'concerning the matter of Jerusalem'. He then gave communion to the king of England, probably in Gascony. Back in Rome, he received it from the new pope, Nicholas IV.
  • The Carmelites Documented Major — Pope Innocent IV wrote to 'the prior and brethren, hermits of blessed Mary of Mount Carmel'. At their request two delegates had explained, corrected and softened their rule, and he confirmed the result. His letter is dated at Lyons in the fifth year of his reign, which was 1247.Lasting result: In 1247 the pope had the rule of the Carmelites made milder at their own request, and then he confirmed it.
  • The Franciscan Movement Documented Major — Francis's first Rule opens by saying he asked Pope Innocent III to grant and confirm this way of life. The pope, it says, granted and confirmed it for Francis and his brothers. Angelo Clareno copied the passage into his commentary on the Rule in the 1320s.Lasting result: Pope Innocent III granted and confirmed Francis's way of life for him and for all his brothers, present and future.
  • The Humiliati Inferential-Thin Major — Pope Innocent III approved the Humiliati around 1200, and many later popes confirmed them. The order then grew fast. In the province of Milan alone it soon had more than two hundred houses. This rests on two older accounts, from 1884 and 1910. No modern study of it has been consulted.Lasting result: In 1200 Pope Innocent III approved the Humiliati. They soon had 220 houses in just the province of Milan.
  • Maronite Christianity Dominant Modern Reconstruction — In 1215 the Maronite patriarch Jeremiah al-Amshitti went to Rome for the Fourth Lateran Council. A papal bull then set out the terms of the union. This rests on modern historians of the Maronites, not on the bull or the council's acts.
