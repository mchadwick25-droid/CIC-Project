Theodosius I of Alexandria

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Church of Alexandria and Miaphysite Egypt.

Known for
The patriarch of Alexandria who refused the council of Chalcedon, lived most of his patriarchate in exile, and gave his name to the Theodosians of Egypt.
Also called
Theodosius, patriarch of Alexandria, Pope Theodosius (in John of Ephesus)
Dates
Made patriarch on the death of Timothy III, in 535 by B. Evetts's heading and E. W. Brooks's notes; died at Constantinople nine months after Justinian, by John of Ephesus, which Brooks puts in 566. Evetts's heading gives 567
Place
Alexandria; Upper Egypt in his first exile; Constantinople and the fortress of Dercus in Thrace in his long exile, by John of Ephesus

Patriarch of Alexandria

About This Person

When the patriarch Timothy died, the new bishop kept watch over the body by night. By custom at Alexandria, the successor laid the dead man's right hand on his own head. Then he buried him and put on the pallium of Saint Mark. But before Theodosius could finish, the people and the monks learned what had been done. They drove him out. So says Liberatus of Carthage, a deacon on the side of Chalcedon who wrote while Theodosius still lived.

Theodosius I of Alexandria was patriarch of the church in Egypt that refused the council of Chalcedon. He spent most of his time as patriarch in exile. The Syriac chronicle under the name of Zachariah Rhetor, from his own side, calls him learned, kind and gentle, and a friend of Severus of Antioch. A rival, Gaianus, was set up in his place, though that chronicle puts Gaianus first. The empress Theodora had Theodosius restored. Liberatus says her chamberlain Narses did it by force and fire, and that the city split into Theodosians and Gaianites.

The History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria is a later history written in his own church. It tells how Justinian offered him the governorship of Egypt if he would accept the Tome of Leo. He refused. "The prince has no power except over my body," he says there, "but the Lord Jesus Christ, the true and great prince, has power over my soul and body together."

Summoned to Constantinople, he still refused Chalcedon. John of Ephesus, of his own party, says he and about three hundred bishops and clergy were held at Dercus, a fortress in Thrace. John often visited them there. He would not ordain outside his own see, John says, but by his mandate twelve bishops were made for Egypt. With his permission, Jacob Baradaeus made bishops at Alexandria.

Two of his letters survive in that chronicle. To Anthimus of Constantinople he wrote, "For I, who am feeble, judge that it is on account of my shortcomings that I endure all the troubles which befall me." He accepted, he said, the faith of Nicaea and the emperor Zeno's formula uniting the churches, which aimed to annul Chalcedon.

He died at Constantinople, John says, nine months after Justinian. E. W. Brooks, John's translator, puts that in 566. B. Evetts, who translated the History, gives 567. The History says believers took the name Theodosians "after his name".

Key Works

  • Letter to Severus of Antioch: a letter of communion and praise, given in English in the chronicle under the name of Zachariah Rhetor
  • Letter to Anthimus of Constantinople: a letter of communion setting out his faith: the creed of Nicaea, the formula of Zeno, and one nature of God the Word made flesh

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Liberatus says Alexandria was split into Theodosians and Gaianites. The History of the Patriarchs calls the believers of Egypt Theodosians after his name.
  • From exile, by John of Ephesus's account, he gave the mandate for twelve bishops for Egypt, permitted Jacob Baradaeus's work in Alexandria, and had the first charge of the new converts among the Nobadae.

How they're remembered

  • The Syriac chronicle under Zachariah's name calls him "a man of conspicuous faith and learned and kind and gentle", and a friend of Severus.
  • Chalcedonian writers remembered him as a heretic. Victor of Tunnuna calls him one, and Sophronius of Jerusalem and the Lateran council of 649 condemned him.

What Is Uncertain

  • Who was made patriarch first. The History of the Patriarchs says Theodosius, with Gaianus made two months later. Liberatus says Theodosius was ordained and then driven out during the funeral rite, and Gaianus held the see for 103 days. The chronicle under Zachariah's name has Gaianus there three months first. Victor of Tunnuna has both ordained on one day.
  • How he was restored. Liberatus says Theodora's chamberlain Narses did it by force and fire. The History of the Patriarchs tells of an inquiry and Gaianus's confession. Michael the Syrian has a general with six thousand men.
  • His witnesses. Liberatus and John of Ephesus wrote in his lifetime, Liberatus from the side of Chalcedon and John from his own. The History of the Patriarchs is a later Coptic history; Eusèbe Renaudot judged its account far from Liberatus's, whom he calls the most truthful historian.
  • His speeches before the emperor's envoys come only from the History of the Patriarchs.
  • Where his exile was. John of Ephesus says the fortress of Dercus in Thrace; Liberatus, six miles from Constantinople; Victor of Tunnuna, Sycae at Constantinople; Michael the Syrian, ten years in Theodora's palace. The Ethiopic Synaxarium gives twenty-eight years of exile in Upper Egypt. The History of the Patriarchs gives twenty-eight years "in banishment and otherwise", and four in Upper Egypt.
  • When he died. John of Ephesus says nine months after Justinian, which Brooks puts in August 566, noting that 'Dionysius' gives 19 June. Jean Garnier, after Victor, gives 566; Evetts's heading and R. Payne Smith give 567. Brooks thinks John's sentence on the death was added later by the author.
