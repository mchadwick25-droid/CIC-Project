Josephus

A person whose story no single tradition tells.

Outside witness

Also called Flavius Josephus

Josephus was a Jewish priest from Jerusalem who commanded in Galilee when the Jewish people rose against Rome, gave himself up to the Roman general Vespasian in 67, and wrote his people's history at Rome. A few lines in it tell of John the Baptist, of James the brother of Jesus, and of Jesus, and Christians copied them for centuries.

About This Person

In 67, when the Roman general Vespasian took Jotapata, a walled town in Galilee, in the north of Judea, Josephus hid in a cave with forty leading men. When the Romans found him on the third day and offered him his life, he prayed in secret, telling God he would surrender. "I give them my hands willingly, and I am content to live. And I declare openly that I do not go over to the Romans as a deserter from the Jewish people, but as a servant sent by you."

Josephus was a priest of the Temple in Jerusalem, from a priestly family, who commanded Jewish forces in Galilee when the Jewish people rose against Rome in 66. In his people's history, finished at Rome about 93, a few lines tell of John the Baptist, of James, the brother of Jesus, and of Jesus himself. Christians copied those lines for centuries as the word of a Jew outside their faith, and argued over the one on Jesus, which the Christian writers who quote it give in different words.

Back in the cave, after his prayer, the men with him held out a sword. He could die as general of the Jewish people, they said, or as a traitor to them. He argued that it was wrong to take one's own life, and they rushed at him and called him a coward. He proposed that they draw lots, each to be killed in turn by the next. He was left last with one other man, by chance or by God's providence, he writes, and persuaded him to live. Then he gave himself up to Vespasian and foretold that the general would be emperor. When that came true, he lived on at Rome under the emperors' favor.

At Rome he wrote the books that tell nearly everything known of his life. He wrote a history of the war in Greek, because others, he says, had written falsely of it, out of flattery to Rome or hatred of the Jewish people. Then came the Jewish Antiquities, his people's history in twenty books, to set their story before Greek-speaking readers. In his Life, a book on his own life, he defended himself against a rival historian who, he says, had written falsely of him, that he and the Galileans had begun the revolt against Rome.

In the Antiquities, Josephus tells of John, called the Baptist, a good man, he writes, who urged the Jewish people to deal justly with one another and to honor God. The ruler Herod, he says, feared that John's hold on the crowds might lead to a rising, so he had him imprisoned and killed.

In the same book, between a riot under Pilate, the Roman governor of Judea, and another disaster, the banishing of the Jewish people from Rome, stands a passage of about a hundred words on Jesus. Its key word, the Christ, means the Messiah, the anointed one the Jewish people awaited, and when it speaks of Greeks, it means those who were not Jewish. "About this time there lived Jesus, a wise man, if one ought to call him a man. For he did astonishing deeds, and taught people who gladly receive the truth. He won over many of the Jewish people, and many of the Greeks as well. This man was the Christ." The passage goes on: Pilate, at the urging of the leading men, had him crucified; he appeared alive on the third day; and the Christians, named after him, have not died out.

Further on, the high priest, the chief priest of the Temple, called a council of judges. He brought before them "the brother of Jesus who was called Christ, James by name", with some others, charged them with breaking the law, and had them stoned. The fairest-minded citizens protested that he had no right to call the court without the Roman governor's consent.

Origen, a Christian teacher of the third century, knew the lines on John and the phrase on James, and wrote that Josephus did not accept Jesus as the Christ. Early in the fourth century, Eusebius of Caesarea copied all three passages into his history of the church. He set the lines on John and Jesus, a Jew's own word, against forged records of Pilate's dealings with Jesus. Those records, he says, were written against Jesus and sent out in his own day with the emperor's approval.

Late in that century, another Christian writer, Jerome, quoted the passage in Latin, and his Latin says only that Jesus was believed to be the Christ.

At the close of his Life, Josephus called it the account of his whole life, and left others to judge his character by it, as they pleased.

Quotations are given in our modern English. The printed wording of each is below.

    The Greek is not held. The modern English is made from Whiston's English alone, and gives his "minister" as "servant sent by you".
  • In our modern English
    “About this time there lived Jesus, a wise man, if one ought to call him a man. For he did astonishing deeds, and taught people who gladly receive the truth. He won over many of the Jewish people, and many of the Greeks as well. This man was the Christ.”
    As printed
    “And there lived at that time Jesus, a wise man, if indeed it be proper to call him a man. For he was a doer of wonderful works, and a teacher of such men as receive the truth in gladness. And he attached to himself many of the Jews, and many also of the Greeks. He was the Christ.”
    Josephus, Jewish Antiquities 18.3, as Eusebius quotes it in Church History 1.11, tr. Arthur McGiffert
    The modern English follows the Greek of the passage as two later Christian books quote it, the Greek Illustrious Men printed with Jerome's Latin (lines 50993-51005 of that file) and the Greek Martyrdom of Donatus, Macarius and Theodore (lines 80255-80262). Whiston's English (lines 81405-81411 of the Josephus file) reads "Gentiles" where McGiffert's reads "Greeks", and "He was [the] Christ".

69 to 70 Freed by Vespasian, and the siege of Jerusalem

Josephus

  1. 69 Vespasian, made emperor, remembers the prediction and is troubled that Josephus is still in chains. At Titus's urging the chain is cut through. (Josephus, Jewish War, lines 117837-117879)
  2. 70 At the siege of Jerusalem, Titus sends him to speak to the defenders in their own language. He goes round the wall and begs them to spare themselves, their country and their Temple. (Josephus, Jewish War, lines 121492-121502)
  3. Date unknown He says he is often in danger of death: the Jewish people want him punished, and the Romans, whenever they are beaten, blame his treachery and ask the emperors to punish him as a traitor. (Josephus, Life, lines 5336-5345)His own account; both sides call him a traitor.
  4. After Jerusalem falls Titus lets him take what he wishes from the ruins. He asks for his family's liberty and the holy books, then the life of his brother and fifty friends, and has about 190 captive women and children he knew set free. He finds three friends crucified and tells Titus, who has them taken down; two die and one recovers. (Josephus, Life, lines 5349-5392)

Legacy and Today

How they're remembered

  • Origen, more than a century later, cites Josephus on James the brother of Jesus, and says Josephus did not accept Jesus as Christ.
  • Eusebius of Caesarea quotes the longer passage on Jesus in his Church History.
  • Jerome, late in the fourth century, gives the passage on Jesus in Latin in his notice of Josephus, and in his Latin Jesus was believed to be the Christ.
  • Sozomen, a church historian of the fifth century, writes that in these lines Josephus all but proclaims that Christ, by comparison of works, is God.
  • Michael the Syrian, a Syriac Orthodox patriarch of the twelfth century, gives the passage in his chronicle, in Jean-Baptiste Chabot's French, with the words that it is thought Jesus was the Messiah, but not on the witness of the chiefs of the people.

Open Questions

  • Origen says Josephus did not accept Jesus as Christ, which sits badly with the line "He was the Christ."
  • Origen also cites Josephus as saying the city fell because of what was done to James. That line is not in the Antiquities as we have it.
  • Josephus's prediction that Vespasian would rule is told by Josephus himself and also by the Roman writer Suetonius.
  • Why Josephus went to Galilee. His Life says the leading men of Jerusalem sent him and two other priests to persuade the rebels to lay down their arms and wait to see what the Romans would do. His War names him general of both Galilees. Both accounts are his own.
  • How the old copies word the key clause of the passage on Jesus. Eusebius and the Greek copies read that he was the Christ; Jerome's Latin, that he was believed to be the Christ; Michael the Syrian's chronicle, that it is thought he was the Messiah, but not on the witness of the chiefs of the people. Origen, who read the Antiquities, says Josephus did not accept Jesus as the Christ, and in the works held here quotes nothing of a passage on Jesus.
  • Jerome's notice also says Josephus openly acknowledges that Christ was slain by the Pharisees on account of the greatness of his miracles, that John the Baptist was truly a prophet, and that Jerusalem was destroyed because of the murder of James the apostle. None of the three is in the Antiquities as held here.
