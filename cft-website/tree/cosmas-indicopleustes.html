Alexandria, today in Egypt · Adulis

Cosmas Indicopleustes

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Aksumite Christianity.

Known for
A merchant turned monk whose Christian Topography sets out a world built like the tabernacle of Moses and reports churches with clergy in Ceylon, in India and across the East.
Dates
At Adulis at the start of the reign of the emperor Justin I, as McCrindle identifies him, some twenty-five years before he wrote, by his own account; J. W. McCrindle dates the visit to 525 and the writing to about 547, with Book X probably written about 535. Photius says only that he flourished under Justin. No source used here gives his birth or death
Place
Most probably Alexandria, in McCrindle's view; voyages on the Mediterranean, the Red Sea and the Persian Gulf; Adulis and Axum in Ethiopia
Role
Merchant, traveler and monk; writer on the shape of the world and recorder of the churches he saw

About This Person

Cosmas was sailing for Further India, he writes, when his ship came near the mouth of the ocean. A great flock of birds followed it, and everyone on board was afraid. Seasoned passengers and sailors cried to the pilot: "Steer the ship to port and make for the gulf, or we shall be swept along by the currents and be carried into the ocean and be lost."

Cosmas was a merchant on the Mediterranean, the Red Sea and the Persian Gulf. Later he became a monk and wrote his Christian Topography in Greek. His English translator, J. W. McCrindle, thinks he most probably came from Alexandria. He is called Indicopleustes, the Indian Navigator.

At Adulis, the port of the Ethiopian kingdom of Axum, Cosmas copied old Greek inscriptions for the governor. The king was then preparing a war across the gulf. McCrindle dates the visit to 525 and the writing to about 547.

Photius of Constantinople, a later patriarch, sums up the book's views: the earth is a flat oblong, and the sky a vault over it. Cosmas took the tabernacle of Moses as a copy of the whole world. He learned this, he says, from Scripture and from "that most divine man and great teacher Patricius," who had become Bishop Catholic of all Persia. McCrindle takes Patricius for Mar Aba, head of the Church of the East. He thinks Cosmas most probably belonged to that church, following the earlier scholar De La Croze. Yet Cosmas once calls Mary the Mother of God, a title McCrindle says that church found abhorrent.

In Ceylon, Cosmas says, there is "a Church of Christians, with clergy and a body of believers." At Calliana in India there is "a bishop, who is appointed from Persia."

"This I avouch to be the veritable fact," Cosmas writes of the gospel's spread, "from what I have seen and heard in the many places which I have visited."

Key Works

  • Christian Topography: twelve books in Greek, arguing from Scripture that the world is made like the tabernacle of Moses, with an eyewitness account of Adulis and Ethiopia and reports of Ceylon and the trade of India; Photius knew it as the Book of Christians

Legacy

What traces to them

  • His notes of churches with clergy in Ceylon, on the pepper coast of Male, at Calliana under a bishop appointed from Persia, and on the island of Dioscorides are, in J. Labourt's words, precious evidence for the spread of the church of Persia in his day.
  • He and his companion Menas copied the Greek inscriptions on the throne and tablet at Adulis for the governor, and kept a set of copies that he put in the book.

How they're remembered

  • Photius of Constantinople found the style poor and called him a teller of fables rather than a trustworthy authority.
  • McCrindle: Photius's condemning verdict "has not been endorsed by modern opinion".

What Is Uncertain

  • His church. McCrindle thinks he most probably belonged to the Church of the East, following De La Croze: he praises his teacher Patricius, leaves the followers of Nestorius out of his list of heresies, and follows that church's teachers in reading Scripture. McCrindle notes that Cosmas once calls Mary the Mother of God, a title that church refused.
  • Whether he reached India. He does not say whether the frightening voyage got there. McCrindle believes his account of Ceylon and India shows personal knowledge. J. H. Freese, translating Photius, says it is doubtful whether he deserves the title Indian Navigator.
  • His name. Isaac Voss and Fabricius thought he was called Cosmas because his book describes the kosmos. McCrindle says it must remain an open question.
  • Whether his teacher Patricius is Mar Aba I. McCrindle says so, from the list of the patriarchs of the Church of the East. The Latin of Montfaucon, the Benedictine who first edited the Greek, which McCrindle rejects, reads the passage as making Thomas of Edessa the head of the church of Persia.
  • The years. Cosmas gives only the start of Justin's reign, Justin I in McCrindle's note, and twenty-five years more or less. McCrindle gives 525 for the visit, and his notes report the traveler Salt's dates of 522 for the killing that set off the king's war and about 530 for the expedition.
