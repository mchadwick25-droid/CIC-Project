The Church of the East on the Silk Road

Persia to Tang China, c. 635 to 845 — one Syriac-speaking communion stretched the whole width of Asia, remembered now through a stone stele at Xi'an and manuscripts found in desert caves.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:18

Read along

A Syriac-speaking church whose bishops sat in Persia had, by the seventh century, sent monks the whole width of Asia: in 635 a missionary the Chinese records call Alopen reached the Tang capital of Chang'an and was received at the imperial court, and toleration for his religion was proclaimed three years later. What that mission looked like is known mostly through a handful of extraordinary survivals — above all a stone stele erected in 781, inscribed in both Chinese and Syriac, narrating a century and a half of what it calls the Luminous Religion in China, and caches of Christian manuscripts recovered at Dunhuang and Turfan. Between those bright points lie long silences, and much of what is said about the stretches in between is reconstruction rather than record. The reach closes visibly in 845, when an imperial edict aimed at foreign religions ordered clergy out of their monasteries and the Chinese church stops appearing in the sources. Though the same communion carried on in Persia and Central Asia.

Documented Stories

Which Religion Does God Approve?

Timothy came from Hazza in Adiabene and was bishop of Beth Beghash when the head of the Church of the East died. J.-B. Chabot, who edited the church’s synods, dates his enthronement at Seleucia to May 780, after eight months of bitter dispute. He lived, Mari ibn Sulayman says, under five caliphs: al-Mahdi, al-Hadi, Harun, al-Amin and al-Ma’mun. ‘Abdisho of Nisibis lists among his works a disputation with the caliph al-Mahdi, but its text is not among the sources used here. Amr ibn Matta says the caliph al-Hadi often called Timothy in to talk about religion. Amr also tells that Harun al-Rashid once asked him to say in few words which religion God approves. The one, Timothy answered, whose laws and commands agree with the reason God keeps in dealing with his creatures. The caliph said it was a full answer, and no objection could be raised to it. The same patriarch looked far beyond Baghdad. Thomas of Marga, writing about 840, tells how he made monks metropolitans of Gilan and Dailom, peoples no preacher had reached since the apostles. In their country, Timothy wrote to them, the Book of the Gospels could lie on the throne in place of the third bishop at an ordination. From Timothy’s letters Thomas also learned of a David, metropolitan of Beth Sinaye, which E. A. Wallis Budge glosses as China. In a letter Alphonse Mingana translates, Timothy names ‘the Indians, the Chinese, the Tibetans, the Turks’ among the provinces of his see. There was no king, Mari says, to whom he did not write. The record shows how a church lived on in a caliph’s capital: by answers given at court, by letters, and by monks sent out far beyond it.

What the Stone Says About Alopen

The stele set up at Chang’an in 781 is the community’s own history of itself. Its heading, in A. Wylie’s English, says it was ‘Composed by King-Tsing, A Priest of the Syrian Church.’ Lines of Syriac run down its sides. Henry Yule reads them as naming Adam, ‘Bishop and Pope’ of China, and Henri Cordier, in his notes to Yule, takes Adam and King-Tsing to be one man. The stone tells how, in the time of the emperor Taitsung, ‘the Most-virtuous Olopun, from the country of Syria’ came east. ‘Observing the azure clouds, he bore the true sacred books; beholding the direction of the winds, he braved difficulties and dangers.’ He reached Chang’an in 635. The emperor sent his prime minister to the western border to bring him in as a guest. The books were translated in the imperial library, and the emperor studied the teaching in his private rooms. In 638 he issued a decree, which the stone quotes: ‘Right principles have no invariable name, holy men have no invariable station’. The teaching, it says, ‘is beneficial to all creatures; it is advantageous to mankind.’ It was to be published through the empire, with a church in the capital for twenty-one priests. The stele then follows the church for a century and a half. It tells of the emperors’ portraits hung in its churches, of attacks by Buddhists in 699 and by ‘some low fellows’ in 713, and of a restoration. It ends with the alms of I-sz’, a man from a distant city who served at a Tang general’s side: ‘the naked came and were clothed; the sick were attended to and restored; the dead were buried in repose’.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

A Syriac-speaking church centered in Persia sent monks the whole width of Asia, and in 635 a missionary named Alopen reached the Tang capital of Chang'an and was received at the imperial court. What survives from that reach is a bilingual stone stele at Xi'an and manuscripts recovered from desert caves at Dunhuang and Turfan, testimony to a mission that a ninth-century edict expelling foreign clergy pushed out of visibility in China, though the same communion continued from its Mesopotamian heartland and would reach east again under the Mongols.

Legacy

In China itself nothing carried through. No community there can be traced across the gap between the ninth century and the arrival of much later missions, and what remains is archaeological — the stele, the Dunhuang and Turfan manuscripts, and Christian gravestones scattered across Central Asia. The communion that sent them did not end, though. The Church of the East continued from its Mesopotamian heartland and would reach east again under the Mongols. This is the furthest east any church of these centuries is known to have reached, and the clearest case of a Christianity that genuinely flourished and then left almost nothing behind but stone.

Sources

Doctrinal Floor Note

'Nestorian', the label most often attached to this church, was given to it by opponents rather than chosen by itself. Its own synods and its own patriarchs confess Christ as divine, even though its way of describing how divinity and humanity are joined in Christ differs from the formula agreed at Chalcedon in 451.

How Sure the Record Is

Uneven: spectacular fragments (Xi'an stele, Dunhuang/Turfan texts) over long silent stretches

  • Xi'an ('Nestorian') Stele, 781 CE — trans. in M. Nicolini-Zani, The Luminous Way to the East (OUP, 2022) (primary) — The community's own public self-presentation in Tang China (history, theology, imperial patronage). Newly annotated English translation in Nicolini-Zani; older full translation in P. Y. Saeki, The Nestorian Documents and Relics in China (1937), on archive.org.
  • Dunhuang and Turfan Christian ('Jingjiao') texts — trans. in Nicolini-Zani, The Luminous Way to the East (OUP, 2022) (primary) — The Chinese-language Christian texts themselves (sutras, hymns, catechetical documents) - spectacular surviving fragments. Nicolini-Zani gives the current richly annotated English translations of the Tang-era corpus.
  • Timothy I, Apology before the Caliph al-Mahdi (trans. A. Mingana, Woodbrooke Studies 2, 1928) (primary) — Patriarchal inside voice of the church that ran the Silk Road missions (Timothy I, patriarch from 780; the text is not among the sources used here); free online at tertullian.org. His letters also report missions to Turks and Tibetans; the critical edition (Heimgartner, CSCO) is Syriac with German translation only, with no complete English translation.
  • Wilhelm Baum & Dietmar W. Winkler, The Church of the East: A Concise History (RoutledgeCurzon, 2003) (secondary) — The standard one-volume English history of the whole tradition, including the Central Asian and Chinese expansion. Fills the long silent stretches with the institutional frame.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity, vol. 3, ch. 3: 'Beyond empire I: Eastern Christianities from the Persian to the Turkish conquest, 604-1071' (Igor Dorfmann-Lazarev) (reference) — Chapter title and author from the vol. 3 TOC (Noble & Smith, 2008). The era's reference-tradition anchor for the eastern churches beyond Byzantium.
