The High Medieval Era · 13th-14th c. · Rhineland, Low Countries

Rhineland, today in Germany · Low Countries

'Free Spirit' Heresy

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In the early fourteenth century the church declared that a sect existed. The decree Ad nostrum, issued at the Council of Vienne in 1312, listed eight errors said to be held by beguines and beghards in Germany. Their core claim was that a soul made perfect in this life can no longer sin. Such a soul needs neither the church nor the virtues any longer. One error spoke of people living 'in the spirit of liberty'. Interrogators then went looking for people who held them, and found them, often because the questions supplied the answers. Modern research, above all Robert Lerner's study of 1972, concluded that no organized sect of this kind ever existed. What existed was a wide spectrum of mystical piety, much of it among beguines and beghards. There was also a charge that could be laid against any part of it. The movement named here probably was not real; the charge and its victims certainly were.

People

No one is shown. Marguerite Porete's book is not in the Library. The others are known only from the records of their trials.

In Its Own Record

Grew Out Of

  • The Beguines Contested Major — Scholars disagree whether a Free Spirit sect grew out of the beguines. The Council of Vienne laid its eight errors on beguines and beghards in Germany. Robert Lerner's 1972 study found no organized sect, only a range of mystical piety, much of it among beguines, and a charge laid on it.

Opposed By

  • The Latin Papal Church (Avignon to Fifth Lateran) Documented Major — The Council of Vienne in 1311-12 condemned eight errors about the perfect soul. One said that people who live 'in the spirit of liberty' owe no obedience to any human. Robert Lerner's 1972 study argued that no organized sect ever held them. In 1367 a papal judge at Erfurt put all eight to a beghard, John Hartmann, whose answers Wilhelm Wattenbach judged sincere in 1887.Lasting result: The Council of Vienne in 1311-12 ruled as error the claim that those who live in the spirit of liberty owe no human obedience.

Sources

  • Marguerite Porete, The Mirror of Simple Souls (trans. Ellen Babinsky, Classics of Western Spirituality, 1993) (primary) — The one substantial text by a person condemned under what became the Free Spirit charge; she never claims the label, and whether the Mirror teaches what Ad nostrum condemned is precisely the question. The overlap with Beguine history is real: the suppression of beguines used this same charge.
  • The trial of Marguerite Porete and Guiard of Cressonessart (Paris, 1310), trans. in Sean L. Field, The Beguine, the Angel, and the Inquisitor (Notre Dame, 2012) (primary) — The inquisitorial process records that made her the emblematic case for the charge - documents from the persecuting side, not a neutral account.
  • Ad nostrum, Council of Vienne (1312), trans. in Tanner, Decrees of the Ecumenical Councils (1990) (primary) — The eight-error decree that legally defined 'the sect of the Free Spirit'; its errors were partly extracted, and distorted, from Porete's Mirror.
  • Interrogation records of accused beghards and beguines (e.g. John of Brunn; the Schweidnitz interrogations, 1332) (primary) — The 'movement' as it survives in the record: answers shaped by the questioners' own template. Partial translations exist, including in Lerner's own apparatus.
  • Robert E. Lerner, The Heresy of the Free Spirit in the Later Middle Ages (California, 1972) (secondary) — The consensus-setting study: no organized sect ever existed - a persecutors' construct laid over a wide spectrum of mystical piety.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 4, ch. 12 'Christians and heretics' (Biller); cf. ch. 19 'Mysticism and transcendence' (Hollywood) (reference)
