The Coptic Church under the Ottomans: the Patriarchate and the Archons

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In 1517 the Ottomans took Egypt. A list of the Coptic patriarchs, compiled by E. A. Wallis Budge and printed in 1928, runs on without a break through that year. It shows John XIII in office to 1524, and Gabriel VII from 1526 to 1569.

Volney traveled in Egypt and Syria from 1783 to 1785. He says the Copts had become the keepers of the registers of the lands and tribes. He says that at Cairo they served as the government's stewards, secretaries and tax collectors. He writes that the Turks they served despised them, and the peasants they pressed hated them. Volney was no friend of the Copts.

These lay leaders are called archons. One was Ibrahim al-Jawhari, chief clerk of the finance office. Butcher says he was so well liked by Muslim and Christian that he escaped a persecution under Hassan Pasha in the 1780s. Through his influence the churches and monasteries could be restored, and he gave much land and money to the church.

Edward Lane wrote his account of Egypt in the years 1833 to 1835. He reckoned the Copts at about 150,000. He writes that the Coptic patriarch judged small cases among his people in Cairo.

Rome built a Catholic line among the Copts. In 1741 the pope made a Copt named Athanasius a metropolitan.

He lived in Jerusalem. Butcher calls him the first with real power in Egypt. He named a priest as his vicar there. Butcher wrote in 1897 and did not favor Rome's claim.

The patriarch also sent the abun to Ethiopia. A decision of his reached Tigray, as Gobat's journal shows.

  • The Moravian Church at Herrnhut Widely Accepted — In 1752 the Moravian physician Frederick William Hooker went to Cairo, on the way to the Christians of Abyssinia. Watson's history says he met the Coptic patriarch, who gave him a letter in reply to one from Zinzendorf. La Trobe, in 1771, lists three Brethren at Cairo with the Copts as their special aim.
