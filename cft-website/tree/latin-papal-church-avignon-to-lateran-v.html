The Latin Papal Church (Avignon to Fifth Lateran)

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In 1309 Clement V settled the papal court at Avignon, and for nearly seventy years the popes governed the Latin church from the Rhône rather than the Tiber, building the largest Gothic palace in Europe and an administration to match. The return to Rome in 1377 produced something worse. A disputed election the following year left two popes, an attempt to fix it in 1409 left three, and the schism divided every kingdom, university and religious order in the West for a generation. It was ended not by a pope but by a council. Constance, which removed the rival claimants, elected Martin V in 1417, and declared that a general council holds its authority immediately from Christ. The decades after that were a contest over whether that was true, settled in the papacy's favor by the 1440s. The popes who followed rebuilt Rome, founded the Vatican Library, commissioned the Sistine Chapel and began the new St Peter's. The Fifth Lateran Council, which decreed a program of reform that changed very little, closed on 16 March 1517 — seven months before Luther's theses.

In Its Own Record

Continues

  • The Latin Papal Church (Gregorian Reform to Innocent III) Documented Major — Gregory VII's Dictatus Papae of 1075 says that no one may judge the pope. In 1302 Boniface VIII, pope from 1294 to 1303, wrote in Unam sanctam that the highest spiritual power is judged by God alone. No human may judge it. The same see carried the same claims across 1300.

Argued Against

  • Lollardy Documented — In 1409 Archbishop Thomas Arundel issued constitutions for England that ban unapproved writings of John Wickliff. They speak of a province 'defiled with a new and damnable name of Lollardy'. In 1415 the Council of Constance condemned forty-five articles of Wyclif.
  • The Hussite & Bohemian Brethren Movement Documented — The diary of Cardinal Fillastre records that on 6 July 1415 the Council of Constance condemned Jan Hus as a heretic. It stripped him of his orders and handed him to the secular court. The same session condemned Wyclif's errors.
  • The Beguines Documented — At the Council of Vienne in 1311-12, Pope Clement V condemned eight errors about the soul made perfect. The standard collection of church decrees files them as the errors of beguines and beghards. Antoninus of Florence wrote in the 1400s that these people had risen up in Germany.
  • 'Free Spirit' Heresy Documented — The Council of Vienne in 1311-12 condemned eight errors about the perfect soul. One said that people who live 'in the spirit of liberty' owe no obedience to any human. Robert Lerner's 1972 study argued that no organized sect ever held them.
  • Military-Religious Orders (Templars, Hospitallers) Documented — In March 1312 Pope Clement V issued the bull Vox in excelso, with the approval of a church council. It abolished the order of the Temple for ever. No one was to join it, wear its habit or pass himself off as a Templar, on pain of excommunication.

Opposed By

  • The Hussite & Bohemian Brethren Movement Documented — In De ecclesia (1413) Jan Hus argued that neither the pope nor the cardinals are the head of the universal church. He wrote that Christ alone is its head and the predestinate are its body.
