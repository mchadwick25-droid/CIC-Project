The Latin Papal Church (Avignon to Fifth Lateran)

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In 1309 Clement V settled the papal court at Avignon. For nearly seventy years the popes governed the Latin church from the Rhône rather than the Tiber. They built the largest Gothic palace in Europe, and an administration to match. The return to Rome in 1377 led to something worse. A disputed election the next year left two popes. An attempt to fix it in 1409 left three. For a generation the schism divided every kingdom, university and religious order in the West. It was ended not by a pope but by a council. The Council of Constance removed the rival claimants and elected Martin V in 1417. It also declared that a general council holds its authority directly from Christ. The decades after that were a contest over whether this was true. By the 1440s it was settled in the papacy's favor. The popes who followed rebuilt Rome. They founded the Vatican Library, commissioned the Sistine Chapel and began the new St Peter's. The Fifth Lateran Council decreed a program of reform that changed very little. It closed on 16 March 1517, seven months before Luther's theses.

In Its Own Record

Continues

  • The Latin Papal Church (Gregorian Reform to Innocent III) Documented Major — Gregory VII's Dictatus Papae of 1075 says that no one may judge the pope. In 1302 Boniface VIII, pope from 1294 to 1303, wrote in Unam sanctam that the highest spiritual power is judged by God alone. No human may judge it. The same see carried the same claim from Gregory to Boniface.

Continued As

  • The Tridentine Church Documented Major — In 1546 the Council of Trent enforced a penalty on unapproved religious books 'imposed in a canon of the last Council of Lateran'. In 1551 it renewed and enlarged a law that Pope Clement V had issued at the Council of Vienne. The same church carried its own laws on into the age of the Reformation.

Influenced

  • The Catholic Church of the Ancien Régime Documented — The French clergy appealed to the Council of Constance in their Articles, which Rome turned down in 1682. They held that its decrees on the power of general councils, made in its fourth and fifth sessions, still stand firm. A papal decree condemned the Articles in 1690.

Argued Against

  • Lollardy Documented Major — In 1409 Archbishop Thomas Arundel issued church rules for England. They ban any of John Wyclif's writings that the church has not cleared. They speak of a province 'defiled with a new and damnable name of Lollardy'. In 1415 the Council of Constance condemned forty-five articles of Wyclif.Lasting result: In July 1415 the Council of Constance sat to judge the errors of John Wyclif. It condemned forty-five of his articles.
  • The Hussite & Bohemian Brethren Movement Documented Major — The diary of Cardinal Fillastre records that on 6 July 1415 the Council of Constance condemned Jan Hus as a heretic. It stripped him of his orders and handed him to the secular court. The same session condemned Wyclif's errors.Lasting result: On 6 July 1415 the Council of Constance condemned Jan Hus as a heretic. It stripped him of his orders and handed him to the secular court.
  • The Beguines Documented Major — At the Council of Vienne in 1311-12, Pope Clement V condemned eight errors about the soul made perfect. The standard collection of church decrees files them as the errors of beguines and beghards. Antoninus of Florence wrote in the 1400s that these people had risen up in Germany.Lasting result: The Council of Vienne formally condemned eight errors. It said they were held by the beguines and beghards of Germany.
  • 'Free Spirit' Heresy Documented Major — The Council of Vienne in 1311-12 condemned eight errors about the perfect soul. One said that people who live 'in the spirit of liberty' owe no obedience to any human. Robert Lerner's 1972 study argued that no organized sect ever held them. In 1367 a papal judge at Erfurt put all eight to a beghard, John Hartmann, whose answers Wilhelm Wattenbach judged sincere in 1887.Lasting result: The Council of Vienne in 1311-12 ruled as error the claim that those who live in the spirit of liberty owe no human obedience.
  • Military-Religious Orders (Templars, Hospitallers) Documented Major — In March 1312 Pope Clement V issued the bull Vox in excelso, with the approval of a church council. It abolished the order of the Temple for ever. No one was to join it, wear its habit or pass himself off as a Templar, on pain of excommunication.Lasting result: In 1312 Pope Clement V did away with the Templars' order, habit and name for ever. The Council of Vienne approved.
  • Rhineland Mysticism & the Friends of God Documented Major — In 1326 Meister Eckhart answered charges of heresy at Cologne. On 27 March 1329 Pope John XXII condemned twenty-eight of his articles in a bull, In agro dominico. Seventeen were called heresy. By then Eckhart was dead, and he had taken back any errors put before the pope.Lasting result: In 1329 Pope John XXII condemned twenty-eight articles from Eckhart's teaching, and any of his books that held them.
  • The Waldensians in the Alps Documented Major — In 1487 Pope Innocent VIII sent his legate Albert de Capitaneis against the Waldenses. The bull called on rulers to take up arms against them. Troops raised at the legate's request then wiped out the Waldensians of the valley of Loyse in Dauphiné.Lasting result: A papal bull of 1487 ordered the Waldenses rooted out by force. In 1489 a court at Embrun judged those of Fraissinière heretics and ordered their goods seized.
  • Fraticelli (Spiritual Franciscan rigorists) Documented Major — In 1317 Pope John XXII struck at the people 'commonly called fraticelli' in his decree Sancta Romana. He ruled their sect void and wiped it out. In 1432 Pope Eugenius IV gave the friar John of Capistrano powers to root out the 'fraticelli de la opinione'.Lasting result: Pope John XXII's decree Sancta Romana of 1317 banned the fraticelli for good, and cut off from the church anyone who kept to their sect.
  • The Franciscan Order — Spirituals Crisis to the Great Division Documented Major — In December 1322 Pope John XXII ruled that the goods given to the Friars Minor no longer belonged to the Roman church. In 1328 he ordered the arrest of the minister general, Michael of Cesena, and William of Ockham, who had fled his court. Days later he removed Michael from office.Lasting result: In 1322 Pope John XXII gave up the Roman church's claim to own the friars' goods, and let no one run them in its name.
  • The Bosnian Church (Krstjani) Widely Accepted — About 1370 Pope Urban V wrote that many heretics lived in Bosnia. He told the archbishops of Split and Dubrovnik to cut off from the church anyone who sheltered them or traded with them. In 1451 Pope Nicholas V called the Bosnian lord Stephen a 'faithless Patarene'. An 1877 history says Roman writers always gave that name to the dissenters of Bosnia.
  • Armenian Christianity after the Kingdom (Sis to Etchmiadzin) Documented — In 1341 Pope Benedict XII sent the Armenians a long list of errors he said they held. A council of the Armenian church met in 1342 under the catholicos Mekhitar and King Constantine to answer it. An eighteenth-century editor adds that an earlier king, Leo, had asked the pope for help against the Saracens. He was told to renounce the errors first.

Opposed By

  • The Hussite & Bohemian Brethren Movement Documented — In De ecclesia (1413) Jan Hus argued that neither the pope nor the cardinals are the head of the universal church. He wrote that Christ alone is its head and the predestinate are its body.
  • The Franciscan Order — Spirituals Crisis to the Great Division Documented — On 14 January 1323 Bonagratia of Bergamo, speaking for the Order of Friars Minor, appealed against the pope's new ruling on the order's goods. He did so before John XXII and the cardinals at Avignon. In 1335 William of Ockham, a friar who had fled the papal court, argued that John had died a heretic.
  • Lutheran Wittenberg & Its Congregations Documented — On 31 October 1517 Martin Luther wrote to the archbishop of Mainz about papal indulgences being sold under his name. Luther's theses of the same day said the indulgence preachers were in error. The indulgences, he wrote, were nets that now 'fish for the riches of men.'

In Contact With

  • The Byzantine Church, Palaiologan Era Documented Major — At Florence in 1439 the Greek delegates signed one decree of union with the pope's council, written half in Latin and half in Greek. The pope asked whether Mark of Ephesus had signed. Told he had not, he said: 'Then we achieved nothing.'Lasting result: The council's decree let each church keep its own custom. Priests could use leavened or unleavened bread at the altar, as the East or the West did.
  • The Franciscan Order — Spirituals Crisis to the Great Division Documented Major — An Observant chronicle says that in 1517 Pope Leo X issued two bulls for the Franciscans. One joined all the reformed friars into a single body. The other set terms between the head of the whole order and the head of the Conventual friars. The chronicle's editors wrote in 1887 that Leo had made two separate orders.Lasting result: In 1517 Pope Leo X made the reformed friars one body under their own head, set apart from the Conventual friars.
  • The Hussite & Bohemian Brethren Movement Documented Major — In January 1434 the legates of the Council of Basel took Prague masters back into communion. The masters had agreed to stand by the Compacts. The Compacts were sealed at Jihlava in July 1436, and the Hussite war was over.Lasting result: In 1434 the Council of Basel's legates let Prague masters who had accepted the Compacts back into the church. They told all Christians to share worship with them.
  • Maronite Christianity — the Union Formalized Documented Major — In 1445 Elias, the Maronite bishop on Cyprus, sent his envoy Isaac to Pope Eugenius IV to profess the Roman faith. In 1516 the Maronite patriarch of Mount Lebanon sent men to pledge his obedience at the Fifth Lateran Council. The patriarch, Peter, wrote that popes before him, Eugenius among them, had sent his church gifts.Lasting result: In 1445 a bull of Pope Eugenius IV took the Maronites of Cyprus into union. It said no one was to call their bishop, priests or people heretics.
  • Observant Reform & Popular Preaching Documented Major — In 1415 the Council of Constance named a vicar general over the Observant friars of France, Burgundy and Touraine. In 1443 Pope Eugenius IV confirmed two vicars general for the Observants, one for each side of the Alps. The head of the Franciscans had named them at the pope's wish. In 1446 the pope ruled that the Observants would elect their own vicars general every three years. He said he was following rules of the Council of Constance and of Pope Martin V.Lasting result: In 1446 Pope Eugenius IV ruled that the Observants would choose their own leaders, and the head of the order had to confirm them.
