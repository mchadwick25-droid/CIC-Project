Schwenckfelders

Silesia and later Pennsylvania, from the 1540s — followers of a nobleman who suspended the Lord's Supper rather than fight over it, and kept a church of houses and handwritten books.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 0:52

Read along

Caspar Schwenckfeld was a Silesian nobleman who welcomed Luther's reform and then broke with it, concluding that outward means — preaching, sacraments, church order — could not do what only the inward work of Christ could do. In 1526 he urged that the Lord's Supper be suspended until Christians could agree what it was, a 'standing still' his followers kept for generations, and he taught a distinctive doctrine of the glorified, heavenly flesh of Christ that Lutherans and Reformed alike condemned. His followers never built a church in the ordinary sense. They met in houses, copied his writings out by hand, and were harried in Silesia for two centuries until a remnant emigrated to Pennsylvania in the 1730s.

Documented Stories

The Standing Still

Caspar Schwenckfeld had been the leading lay promoter of Luther’s reform in the duchy of Liegnitz since 1521, and by 1525 had concluded, from watching his neighbors take communion and go on living as before, that the Lutheran teaching on the real presence was producing no visible change of life — that people were eating the body of Christ and remaining, as he put it, hogs. He worked out with the Liegnitz preacher Valentin Crautwald a reading of the words of institution and rode to Wittenberg in December 1525 to lay it before Luther. Luther heard him for two days, told him he was in error, and wrote afterwards that Schwenckfeld had ‘swallowed the devil’. Schwenckfeld came home and, in April 1526, with the duke’s consent, persuaded the clergy of Liegnitz to suspend the celebration of the Lord’s Supper entirely — the Stillstand, the standing still — until, he wrote, ‘the Lord gives further understanding’ and Christians could partake worthily and in agreement. Luther broke with him; the duke, under pressure from the emperor, asked him to leave Silesia in 1529, and he spent the remaining thirty-two years of his life as a guest in the houses of sympathizers in Strasbourg, writing and forbidden the pulpit. His followers in Silesia kept the Stillstand: they read his books, did not take the Supper, and were hunted by Lutherans and Catholics alike until a remnant sailed for Pennsylvania in 1734. The Schwenkfelder Church there resumed communion only in 1877.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Caspar Schwenckfeld broke with Luther over a conviction that outward things — preaching, sacraments, church order — could never do what only Christ's inward work could accomplish. In 1526 he urged that the Lord's Supper be suspended until Christians could agree what it actually was, a 'standing still' his followers kept for generations, meeting in houses and copying his writings out by hand.

Legacy

A small Schwenkfelder Church still exists in south-eastern Pennsylvania, descended from the families who emigrated in the 1730s, and it keeps a heritage library and an annual thanksgiving marking their arrival. The movement's larger mark is as a witness: an early, articulate case that inward faith matters more than outward form, argued from inside the Reformation rather than against it.

Sources

Doctrinal Floor Note

They diverge on one point: Schwenckfeld taught that Christ's humanity was heavenly rather than ordinary flesh, and his followers suspended the sacraments. Neither position denies Christ's full divinity, which is why their standing relative to the historic creeds is genuinely arguable rather than settled.

How Sure the Record Is

Modest but real internal corpus

  • Corpus Schwenckfeldianorum (19 vols, 1907-61), incl. the Great Confession on Christ's glory (1540) (primary) — The 'celestial flesh' Christology and the Stillstand ('standing still') documented in his own words, across a 19-volume collected corpus. Mostly in Latin and German; English translations exist only as piecemeal selections.
  • The community's own texture: Silesian conventicle letters and devotional manuscripts, the Schwenkfelder hymn and prayer-book tradition, the 1734 Pennsylvania migration records and Gedaechtnistag (primary) — Evidence of the small fellowship's continuity across two centuries, held at the Schwenkfelder Library in Pennsburg.
  • The condemnations: Lutheran polemic and the Formula of Concord article against the 'Schwenkfeldians' (1577); Silesian suppression records; Jesuit mission pressure (1719-25) (primary) — Hostile sources — the majority churches' own filter on the community's memory. The 1719-25 material comes from a later period than the rest.
  • R. Emmet McLaughlin, Caspar Schwenckfeld, Reluctant Radical (Yale, 1986) (secondary) — The standard modern study.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 6, ch. 3 (McLaughlin, the radical Reformation) (reference) — The chapter's author is also the movement's standard biographer.
