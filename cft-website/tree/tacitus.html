About This Person

After a great fire in Rome, people believed that the emperor Nero had ordered it. The Roman historian Tacitus tells what Nero did to stop the talk. He "fastened the guilt" on "a class hated for their abominations, called Christians by the populace." Some were covered with the skins of beasts and torn by dogs. Others were nailed to crosses, or burned "to serve as a nightly illumination, when daylight had expired."

Tacitus was not a Christian. Tertullian of Carthage, who wrote to defend the Christians, knew him as Cornelius Tacitus. He calls him "most loquacious in falsehood," for a tale in his Histories about the Jews. In his Annals Tacitus explains the name. "Christus, from whom the name had its origin, suffered the extreme penalty during the reign of Tiberius at the hands of one of our procurators, Pontius Pilatus." He calls the faith "a most mischievous superstition." It was checked for a time, he says, and then broke out again in Judaea and in Rome.

Those who confessed were arrested first. On their information, he says, "an immense multitude" was convicted, less for the fire than for "hatred against mankind." Even for criminals who deserved the worst, he says, pity arose, because they seemed to die "to glut one man's cruelty."

Tacitus does not say where he learned about Christus. The historians Gibbon and, later, Schiller held that Tacitus was wrong here. In their view Nero's victims were the Jews as a whole. Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius of Caesarea in 1890, disagreed. Josephus, he noted, says nothing of any attack on the Jews under Nero.

Christian writers kept the account. Sulpicius Severus, born in Aquitania about 363, retold Nero's tortures in nearly the same words. "In this way," he wrote, "cruelty first began to be manifested against the Christians."

Key Works

  • Annals, Book 15, chapter 44: the fire of Rome, Nero's punishment of the Christians, and Christus under Pilate
  • Histories: cited by Tertullian of Carthage for what Tacitus said of the Jews; not held in the Library

Legacy

How they're remembered

  • Tertullian of Carthage, defending the Christians, calls him "most loquacious in falsehood" for his account of the Jews.
  • Sulpicius Severus, born in Aquitania about 363, retold Nero's tortures of the Christians in nearly the same words.
  • John Rolfe, who translated Suetonius, says Tacitus found no follower until Ammianus Marcellinus.

What Is Uncertain

  • Tacitus does not say where he learned about Christus.
  • Gibbon, and later Schiller, held that Nero's victims were the Jews as a whole, and that Tacitus was wrong to call them Christians. Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius of Caesarea in 1890, disagreed, noting that Josephus says nothing of an attack on the Jews under Nero.
  • The Library holds one chapter of the Annals. Nothing in it shows what Tacitus says elsewhere.
  • The English is the translation standardly identified as Church and Brodribb's of 1876. The file itself names no translator.
