Tacitus

A person whose story no single tradition tells.

Outside witness

Also called Cornelius Tacitus

Tacitus was a Roman historian who despised the Christians and named Christus, that is, Christ, as put to death under Pontius Pilate. In a few sentences, his Annals give the fullest account from outside the church of how the emperor Nero punished them after the great fire of Rome in 64.

About This Person

When daylight failed in the emperor Nero's gardens in Rome, Christians were set alight to light the dark. Some, covered in the skins of animals, were torn apart by dogs, and many were nailed to crosses. The Roman who tells it, the historian Tacitus, thought them guilty and despised them.

Writing a generation or more after the great fire of Rome in 64, Tacitus gave the fullest account from outside the church of how Nero punished the Christians. In it he named Christus, that is, Christ, from whom their name came, put to death under Pontius Pilate. His account runs to a few sentences, about 140 words of Latin, and the details of these deaths rest on him alone. Later, one Christian, Tertullian, answered him by name on another matter, and another, Sulpicius Severus, retold his scene in nearly his own words and called the dead innocent.

In the Annals, his year-by-year history of Rome, Tacitus tells how Rome turned after the fire to prayers and offerings to the gods. But nothing, not human effort, not the emperor's gifts, not the offerings, ended the shameful talk that someone had ordered the fire.

So, to kill the rumor, Nero put forward others as the guilty: people hated for their shameful acts, whom the common crowd called Christians. He punished them, Tacitus writes, with the most refined cruelty. Then Tacitus stops to explain the name, naming no source for it: "Christus, from whom that name comes, had been put to death in the reign of Tiberius by the governor Pontius Pilate." Their religion was a deadly superstition, he says with contempt, and though it was held back for a moment, it broke out again, in Judaea, where the evil began, and in Rome itself.

First those who confessed were seized. Then, on their information, a huge number were convicted, and he names none of them. They were convicted, he says, less for setting the fire than for hatred of the human race. Mockery was added to their deaths, the deaths told at the start, and Nero offered his own gardens for the show. He also put on chariot races, mixing with the crowd dressed as a charioteer, or standing in a chariot himself.

About 200, in Carthage, the Christian Tertullian answered Tacitus by name in his Apology, his defense of the Christians. His quarrel was with another of Tacitus's histories, which told where the Jewish people came from. That account of the Jewish people, Tertullian says, started the tale that the Christians, close to the Jewish people in religion, worshiped a donkey's head. Tertullian called Tacitus a liar, and a wordy one, and pointed to his own words against him: the same Tacitus tells that the Roman general who took Jerusalem went into the temple and found no image there. If the Jewish people worshiped an image, Tertullian says, the temple was the very place it would be shown.

About 400, Sulpicius Severus, a Christian writing a short history of the world, told the fire and the blame, and gave the same deaths in nearly Tacitus's own Latin words, though he names no source. Where Tacitus called the dead guilty, Sulpicius wrote: "So he turned the blame onto the Christians. And the cruellest tortures were used on the innocent."

Tacitus closed his own account this way: "So pity began to rise, even toward the guilty, who had earned the harshest punishment as a warning. It was as if they were being destroyed not for the public good, but for one man's cruelty."

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • About 400, Sulpicius Severus, a Christian writing a short history of the world, told the fire and the blame, and gave the same deaths in nearly Tacitus's own Latin words. He names no source, and he calls the dead innocent.
  • Later in the same history, Sulpicius calls the teaching of the Gnostics, found in Spain in his day, a deadly superstition. It was a secret teaching, he says, that came from the East and Egypt. His English translator and his Latin editor both point to Tacitus there, who used the same words, a deadly superstition, for the Christians' religion.

How they're remembered

  • Tertullian of Carthage, defending the Christians about 200, answered Tacitus by name for his account of the Jewish people. In To the Nations he calls him "most loquacious in falsehood", the wordiest of liars.
  • Richard Hooker quoted the Latin of the chapter's lines on Nero's blame and on Christus. His point was that "in the estimation of the rest of the world even Christ Jesus himself was execrable", that is, hated.
  • Thierry Ruinart, a monk who died in 1709, answered Henry Dodwell, whose study published at Oxford in 1684 argued that very few Christians died for their faith in the early persecutions. Ruinart put Nero's persecution first, and wrote that Dodwell did not dare deny what Tacitus reports.
  • William Whiston, who translated Josephus, set the chapter's lines on Nero's blame and on Christ first among the ancient citations he gathered beside Josephus.
  • In 1876, Robert Roberts and Charles Bradlaugh, an atheist, debated in public whether the Bible is divine. Roberts quoted Tacitus with Pliny the Younger to show a great number of believers in Christ in the first century. Bradlaugh called the quotation "liable to impeachment", since the church historian Eusebius does not quote it, but would not go to "absolute denial".

Open Questions

  • Tacitus does not say where he learned about Christus.
  • What Tacitus leaves open. He does not say what the Christians' shameful acts were, what the first ones arrested confessed, or who gave the order that people believed had set the fire. He names none of the dead.
  • Christus and the governor. The Latin says Christus was put to death "per procuratorem", by the procurator, the Roman governor. The 1876 English reads "at the hands of one of our procurators", and so adds "one of our".
  • The details of these deaths rest on Tacitus alone among the old sources the Library holds. Sulpicius Severus wrote more than three centuries later, in nearly Tacitus's words.
  • Sulpicius names no source. That he took the deaths from Tacitus rests on the wording. The held prints of Tacitus differ slightly at the crosses and the burning, so the words are nearly, not exactly, the same.
  • Sulpicius adds what Tacitus does not have: Nero away at Antium when the fire broke out, and Paul and Peter condemned at that time. He also says laws and edicts later made it unlawful to be a Christian.
  • Tertullian does not mention this chapter or the fire. His quarrel is with what Tacitus wrote of the Jewish people in his Histories, which the Library does not hold, so that account is known here only as Tertullian reports it.
  • What the verdict means. Tacitus says only that they were convicted less for setting the fire than for hatred of the human race. Thierry Ruinart took it to show that the Christians were judged worthy of death even before the fire.
  • Whether it is genuine. Among the writers the Library holds, only Charles Bradlaugh, in 1876, doubted it, and he would not deny it outright. Every other held writer uses it as Tacitus's.
  • The Latin is read differently in three places. The Library's note on its English says the Medicean manuscript, a handwritten copy of the Annals, reads chrestianos, corrected by a later hand. Mirbt and Schanz print Christianos. Mirbt prints convicti, convicted, where Ruinart reads conjuncti, joined. Mirbt has many nailed to crosses or burned with fire, where Ruinart has them nailed to crosses or set to be burned.
  • The Library holds one chapter of the Annals. Nothing in it shows what Tacitus says elsewhere.
  • The English is the translation standardly identified as Church and Brodribb's of 1876. The file itself names no translator.
