When daylight ended in Nero's gardens, people were burned to light the dark. Others, covered in animal skins, were torn apart by dogs or nailed to crosses, while Nero, dressed as a charioteer, mixed with the crowd. The Roman who tells it, the historian Tacitus, thought they were criminals.

Writing about fifty years after the great fire of Rome in 64, Tacitus gives the fullest account outside the church of what Nero did to the Christians. In it he names their founder, Christus, put to death under Pilate. He despised them and said so. Yet his one chapter became what later historians built on.

After the fire, no prayers or offerings to the gods could stop the belief that it had been set on an order. To kill the rumor, Nero put the guilt on people the crowd called Christians, hated, he says, for their shameful acts, and he had them tortured.

Tacitus stops to explain the name, and gives Christ in its Latin form. "Christus, from whom the name came, was put to death in the reign of Tiberius by one of our governors, Pontius Pilatus." A superstition, he calls it, and a deadly one. It began in Judaea, the Roman province around Jerusalem, and broke out even in Rome, where, he says, everything shameful in the world gathers.

First those who admitted it were seized, and then, on their word, a huge number more. They were convicted, he says, less of setting the city on fire than of hatred of the human race. Historians a century ago read that to mean the inquiry proved them Christians, not that they set the fire. Their deaths were made a show, as Nero held games at the chariot-racing ground.

Almost everything known of these deaths rests on this one chapter, and Tacitus never says where he learned of Christus.

About the year 200, Tertullian, defending the Christians, answered him by name over another of his histories. Tacitus had written there that the Jews honored an ass's head, a charge Tertullian thought had passed to the Christians. Yet, he wrote, "that same Cornelius Tacitus, who truly talks more lies than anyone," also says a Roman general found no image in the temple at Jerusalem.

About 400, Sulpicius Severus, a Christian writing a history of the world, told the same deaths in the same order. But where Tacitus called them criminals, he called them innocent.

Later historians made the chapter their main outside record of these deaths, and a Roman's witness that Christus was put to death under Pilate. In an 1876 debate, a speaker arguing that the Bible was not divine said the passage was open to challenge. The historians who weighed it treated it as Tacitus's own. In 1890, a translator of Eusebius's history of the early church rejected the view of some historians that the victims were Jews, not Christians.

Back in the gardens at night, Tacitus ends with his own judgment. "So even for criminals who deserved to be punished harshly, as an example to all, a feeling of pity arose. For it seemed they were being destroyed not for the public good, but to feed one man's cruelty."

Quotations and references

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “Christus, from whom the name came, was put to death in the reign of Tiberius by one of our governors, Pontius Pilatus.”
    As printed
    “Christus, from whom the name had its origin, suffered the extreme penalty during the reign of Tiberius at the hands of one of our procurators, Pontius Pilatus”
    Tacitus, Annals 15.44, in the translation standardly identified as Church and Brodribb
  • In our modern English
    “that same Cornelius Tacitus, who truly talks more lies than anyone,”
    As printed
    “At enim idem Cornelius Tacitus, sane ille mendaciorum loquacissimus”
    Tertullian, Apology 16, ed. Franz Oehler
  • In our modern English
    “So even for criminals who deserved to be punished harshly, as an example to all, a feeling of pity arose. For it seemed they were being destroyed not for the public good, but to feed one man's cruelty.”
    As printed
    “Hence, even for criminals who deserved extreme and exemplary punishment, there arose a feeling of compassion; for it was not, as it seemed, for the public good, but to glut one man's cruelty, that they were being destroyed.”
    Tacitus, Annals 15.44, in the translation standardly identified as Church and Brodribb

References

  • Annals 15.44, in the translation standardly identified as Church and Brodribb: the prayers and offerings after the fire, the belief that it was set on an order, Nero's blame, Christus under Pilate, the arrests and the verdict, the deaths in Nero's gardens and at the circus, and the closing judgment.
  • Eusebius of Caesarea, Church History 2.25, with Arthur McGiffert's notes of 1890: the fire of 64, the persecution Tacitus tells most fully, and Gibbon and Schiller on the victims as the Jews as a whole, which McGiffert rejects.
  • Schanz, Hosius and Krüger (1922), with Duchesne (1907) and McGiffert: the verdict read as proving the accused were Christians, not that they set the fire.
  • Tertullian, Apology 16, in Oehler's Latin, checked against To the Nations 1.11 and the Ante-Nicene English: Tacitus's account of the Jews and the ass's head in his Histories, and Pompey finding no image in the temple at Jerusalem.
  • Sulpicius Severus, Sacred History 2.29, in Alexander Roberts's English: the fire, the blame and the same deaths in the same order, inflicted on the innocent.
  • The Roberts and Bradlaugh debate of 1876: Bradlaugh's doubt of the quotation from Tacitus.
  • Charles Duke Yonge's preface to Ammianus Marcellinus: Tacitus's history ends at 96. That he wrote about fifty years after the fire is a modern reconstruction; his own text gives no date.

Key Works

  • Annals, Book 15, chapter 44: the fire of Rome, Nero's punishment of the Christians, and Christus under Pilate
  • Histories: cited by Tertullian of Carthage for what Tacitus said of the Jews; not held in the Library

Legacy

How they're remembered

  • Tertullian of Carthage, defending the Christians, calls him "most loquacious in falsehood" for his account of the Jews.
  • Sulpicius Severus, born in Aquitania about 363, retold Nero's tortures of the Christians in nearly the same words.
  • John Rolfe, who translated Suetonius, says Tacitus found no follower until Ammianus Marcellinus.

What Is Uncertain

  • Tacitus does not say where he learned about Christus.
  • Gibbon, and later Schiller, held that Nero's victims were the Jews as a whole, and that Tacitus was wrong to call them Christians. Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius of Caesarea in 1890, disagreed, noting that Josephus says nothing of an attack on the Jews under Nero.
  • The Library holds one chapter of the Annals. Nothing in it shows what Tacitus says elsewhere.
  • The English is the translation standardly identified as Church and Brodribb's of 1876. The file itself names no translator.
