The Early Medieval Era · born 720-730; died 790-800 · Friuli, Monte Cassino

Paul the Deacon

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Carolingian Reform Christianity.

Known for
Lombard monk and historian of the eighth century. A monk of Monte Cassino, he spent some years at Charlemagne's court, and wrote the History of the Langobards, the story of his own people, their kings and their church.
Also called
Paul Warnefrid, in O. M. Dalton's notes to Gregory of Tours, Paulus Diaconus
Dates
Eighth century. W. D. Foulke says he was born between 720 and 730, and that the precise date is unknown. He was a monk before 782, at Charlemagne's court in the 780s, and back in Benevento in the summer of 787, by Foulke. He died on 13 April, in a year that is unknown; Foulke cites Thomas Hodgkin for probably between 790 and 800
Place
Friuli, where he was born, probably at Cividale, by Foulke; Benevento, at the court of Duke Arichis and Adelperga; Monte Cassino, his monastery; the court of Charlemagne in France, with Metz
Role
Monk and deacon; historian of the Lombards

About This Person

A Lombard named Lopichis was carried off to the Avars as a boy. As a grown man he fled, with only a quiver, a bow and a little food. Then, his great-grandson writes, "a wolf came to him and became the companion of his journey and his guide." At last he reached Italy and the house where he was born. It had no roof and was full of brambles. Inside its walls grew a large ash tree, and he hung his quiver on it.

The great-grandson was Paul the Deacon, who tells this family story in his History of the Langobards, the Lombards. He was born in Friuli between 720 and 730, by the reckoning of W. D. Foulke, who translated the History in 1907. His father was Warnefrit and his mother Theudelinda, Paul writes. He became a monk, in all likelihood at Monte Cassino, Saint Benedict's house. The one sure point, Foulke says, is that it was before 782.

Around then Paul went to the court of Charlemagne, king of the Franks. Foulke says he taught Greek there. By the king's order, Foulke adds, he gathered a book of the church fathers' sermons, perhaps begun at court and finished at Monte Cassino. After a few years he went home to his monastery.

There he wrote the History of his people. He tells how Queen Theudelinda won gifts for the church, and how Pope Gregory the Great sent her his books. Through her, Paul says, King Agilulf held the Catholic faith. Foulke thinks Paul is probably mistaken in this. Paul also says that under King Rothari almost every city had two bishops, one Catholic and one Arian, which Foulke's notes call doubtful.

He tells how the Lombards sacked Monte Cassino by night, and how Petronax of Brescia brought monks back some hundred and ten years later.

Foulke says Paul died on the thirteenth of April, but the year is unknown. His History stops after the death of King Liutprand. Franks, he writes, carried off the bones of Benedict to their own country. Even so, Paul says, Benedict's "venerable mouth, sweeter than all nectar, and the eyes beholding ever heavenly things" remained "to us, although decayed."

Key Works

  • History of the Langobards: six books, from the Lombards' beginnings to the death of King Liutprand, written at Monte Cassino and left unfinished at his death. Held in W. D. Foulke's English of 1907
  • Roman History: a history in sixteen books, written for Adelperga, wife of the duke of Benevento, enlarging Eutropius. Foulke says it was a textbook for nearly a thousand years. Not held
  • Homiliary: a book of the church fathers' homilies for the church year, gathered by Charlemagne's order, Foulke says, possibly begun at court and finished at Monte Cassino. Not held

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Foulke says the homiliary has been in use for a thousand years in the whole Catholic church.
  • He also wrote a history of the bishops of Metz, at the request of Bishop Angilram, and he names it in the History.

How they're remembered

  • His pupil Hilderic's epitaph, as Foulke reports it, credits him with every good quality at one and the same time.
  • A poem at Charlemagne's court, Foulke says, calls him a Homer in Greek, a Virgil in verse and a Philo in Hebrew.
  • Foulke writes that no reproach has anywhere been made against him, and also that he was properly a compiler.

What Is Uncertain

  • His birth year. Ludwig Bethmann gives 730, earlier commentators 720, Georg Waitz, who completed Bethmann's edition of the History, 720 to 725, and Thomas Hodgkin about 725, by Foulke's note. Foulke says the precise date is unknown.
  • When and why he became a monk. Felix Dahn thinks it was a refuge after the Lombard kingdom fell in 774; Tamassia reads Paul's word exile as his stay at Charlemagne's court. Foulke says only that it was before 782.
  • Why he went to Charlemagne. The pages of Foulke's introduction that tell of his brother Arichis and the journey to France are missing from the held file. Paul's letter from the Moselle speaks of his prisoners, which Foulke thinks probably means his brother, though the meaning is not clear.
  • His year of death. Foulke gives the day, 13 April, and says the year is unknown.
  • Two of his claims. Foulke thinks Paul is probably mistaken that King Agilulf took the Catholic faith, and calls doubtful his report of a Catholic and an Arian bishop in almost every city under Rothari.
  • His family. Paul calls Leupchis his great-great-grandfather and Lopichis his great-grandfather. Foulke and Hodgkin think he left out some generations.
