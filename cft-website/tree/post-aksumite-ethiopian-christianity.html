Post-Aksumite Ethiopian Christianity

The Ethiopian highlands, c. 615 to 1137 — five near-silent centuries in which a church lost its empire, moved into the mountains, and simply kept going.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:22

Read along

Aksum had been one of the great powers of the late-antique world, minting its own coins, its kings Christian since the fourth century. Then the trade that sustained it moved away, the coinage stopped, and Ethiopian Christianity went quiet for something close to five hundred years — one of the least documented stretches in the whole history of any ancient church. What can be said with confidence is small but decisive. The church survived, its center shifted south into the highlands, and it went on receiving its chief bishop from the Coptic patriarch in Alexandria, a link that would last into the twentieth century. Later Ethiopian tradition fills the silence with the story of a queen remembered as Gudit, who is said to have destroyed churches around the year 960, but that account comes from much later and largely hostile sources and cannot be verified. When the record resumes under the Zagwe kings in the twelfth century, there is a functioning church with monasteries, a liturgy in Ge'ez and its own saints. Which tells us the intervening centuries were not empty, only silent.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Aksum had been a great Christian power, minting its own coins, but when the trade that sustained it moved away the coinage stopped and Ethiopian Christianity went nearly silent for close to five hundred years, one of the least documented stretches in the history of any ancient church. What can be said with confidence is that the church survived, moved its center into the highlands, and kept receiving its chief bishop from the Coptic patriarch in Alexandria, a link that lasted into the twentieth century, so that when a functioning church reappears under the Zagwe kings, it already has monasteries, a Ge'ez liturgy and its own saints.

Legacy

Everything later Ethiopian Christianity is rests on the fact that this church did not die. The same institution continues directly into the Zagwe period — the age of the rock-hewn churches of Lalibela — and on into the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church of today, one of the largest ancient churches in the world. The link with Alexandria that these centuries kept alive held until 1959, when the Ethiopian church finally received a patriarch of its own. And what was carried through the silence — worship in Ge'ez, a distinctive monasticism, a liturgical calendar unlike anyone else's — is still what marks Ethiopian Christianity out.

How Sure the Record Is
