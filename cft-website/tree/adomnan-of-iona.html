Adomnán of Iona

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Medieval Irish Christianity.

Known for
Abbot of Iona who wrote the Life of Columba, and a book on the holy places from what Arculf, a bishop from Gaul, told him. Bede says he took up the Roman Easter but could not bring the monks of Iona with him.
Also called
Adamnan, Adamnanus, Adomnanus, Adamanus
Dates
Born in 624 and abbot of Iona from 679, in Wentworth Huyshe's account. Huyshe says he seems to have stayed in Ireland until 704, then returned to Iona and died soon after. Bede says he died before the year came round after he kept the Roman Easter in Ireland and went home. The Annales Cambriae record his falling asleep in a year their editor, John Williams ab Ithel, sets at 704
Place
Donegal in Ireland by birth, in Huyshe's account. Iona, as monk and abbot. Northumbria, as envoy to King Aldfrith. Ireland, which Huyshe says he visited in 692 and 697
Role
Monk and abbot of Iona; writer of the Life of Columba and of a book on the holy places

About This Person

Ceolfrid, abbot of Wearmouth and Jarrow, once asked a guest why his head was shaved like that of the man Saint Peter had cursed. The guest was Adomnán of Iona, his people's envoy to King Aldfrith of Northumbria. He answered that "though I have Simon's tonsure, according to the custom of my country, yet I utterly detest and abhor the Simoniacal wickedness". Ceolfrid, who argued for the Roman tonsure, told the story in a letter that Bede copied into his History.

Wentworth Huyshe, who translated Adomnán's Life of Columba, says he was born in 624 in Donegal, of Columba's kin. By Huyshe's account he became a monk under his kinsman Seghine, abbot of Iona, and in 679 became abbot himself. Huyshe adds that the exiled Aldfrith was called his foster-son.

The Life is a saint's Life of prophecies, miracles and visions. Adomnán asks readers to attend "to the matter rather than to the words, which, as I think, seem uncultured and rude." He names his tellers. Abbot Failbeus, his predecessor, told him how King Oswald of Northumbria saw Columba in a vision before a battle. Adomnán writes that on a visit to "my friend, King Aldfrid," plague was still killing in England, yet none of his company fell ill.

Bede says Adomnán wrote a book on the holy places, from what Arculf, a bishop from Gaul, told him. A storm had driven Arculf onto Britain's west coast on his way home. In its preface, Adomnán says Arculf dictated the account to him. L. Cecil Jane, in notes to Bede, doubts that Adomnán wrote it.

In Aldfrith's kingdom, Bede says, learned men urged Adomnán not to keep an Easter against the custom of the whole Church. He changed his mind, but could not win his monks of Iona. In Ireland he brought many to the Roman Easter. He went home and died before the next year came round. Bede calls him "a good and wise man, and remarkably learned in Holy Scripture."

Newport White, who translated Patrick's writings, says Adomnán held a synod at Birr in the late 690s. A note in the Lebar Brecc, a late medieval Irish manuscript, counts Adomnán's rule among four rules of Ireland, "not to slay women".

At the close of his book on the holy places, Adomnán asks each reader to pray for Arculf. He asks them to pray for him too, the poor sinner who wrote it down.

Key Works

  • Life of Columba (Vita Columbae), in three books: a saint's Life of Columba, the founder of Iona. The first book tells his prophecies, the second his miracles, and the third his visions of angels and his death. Adomnán names many of his tellers. Wentworth Huyshe says the third book takes in an earlier book on Columba by Cummene the Fair, abbot of Iona
  • On the Holy Places (De Locis Sanctis), in three books: Jerusalem and other holy places as Arculf, a bishop from Gaul, described them. Adomnán's preface says Arculf stayed nine months in Jerusalem and dictated the account to him. Bede says Adomnán gave the book to King Aldfrith, and Bede drew passages from it into his History
  • Canons under his name (Canones Adomnani): short rulings on what meat may be eaten, such as sea creatures cast ashore and beasts that fall from a rock. F. W. H. Wasserschleben says a Saint-Germain manuscript calls them Adomnán's canons. He adds that d'Achery printed them with the chapters of Theodore of Canterbury

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Bede says Adomnán brought many in Ireland to the Roman Easter, almost all who were not under Iona's rule.
  • Bede says Iona's own monks took the Roman Easter after Adomnán's death, under Abbot Dunchad. They were taught by Egbert, an English priest who had long lived in Ireland. Bede's fifth book puts Egbert's coming in 716, and his third book gives 715.
  • Bede calls the book on the holy places useful to many readers, and copied passages from it into his History.
  • Newport White, who translated Patrick's writings, says Muirchu and Aedh of Sletty signed the acts of Adomnán's synod at Birr. Muirchu wrote a Life of Patrick, and White dates the synod 695 to 697.
  • A note in the Lebar Brecc, a late medieval Irish manuscript, counts Adomnán's rule, not to slay women, among four rules of Ireland. Whitley Stokes prints it. Wentworth Huyshe says Columba, and Adomnán after him, freed women from having to fight beside their husbands and sons.

How they're remembered

  • Bede calls him a good and wise man, remarkably learned in Holy Scripture, and a great lover of peace and unity.
  • Ceolfrid, abbot of Wearmouth and Jarrow, writes that he found wonderful wisdom, humility and religion in Adomnán's words and behavior. He counts him among those who were holy and worthy of God though they wore that tonsure.
  • Alcuin of York wrote verses for an altar of Patrick and other Irish saints. They name Adomnán among its famous fathers, teachers of morals and of life.
  • Wentworth Huyshe reports Dr. William Reeves's view that the Life of Columba is one of the most important pieces of hagiology in existence. He also reports George Cormack's remark that Adomnán "thought in Gaelic and wrote in Latin".

What Is Uncertain

  • His years and kin come from Wentworth Huyshe's introduction of 1905, which gives them without naming its source. Huyshe reports the view that the Life of Columba was written between Adomnán's visits to Ireland in 692 and 697. The Life is a saint's Life, and its wonders are Adomnán's report.
  • The book on the holy places. Bede credits it to Adomnán, and its preface names him as the one Arculf dictated to. L. Cecil Jane, in his notes to Bede, calls it doubtful that Adomnán really wrote the book Bede quotes.
  • Adomnán's rule. The Lebar Brecc note calls it cain Adamnan, which Whitley Stokes renders as Adamnan's rule. Wentworth Huyshe calls the Lebar Brecc a fifteenth-century manuscript. The held edition of the Antiphonary of Bangor says it was compiled about the end of the fourteenth century. The text of the rule is not among the sources used here. Newport White ties a synod at Birr to Adomnán, but no source used here says what the synod decreed.
  • The canons under his name. F. W. H. Wasserschleben says one manuscript names them Canones Adomnani, and that d'Achery had printed them as chapters of Theodore of Canterbury.
  • His Easter. Bede, who held to the Roman reckoning, tells his change of mind as a turn to the truth. Bede says God so ordered it that he died before Easter came round again. So, Bede adds, this lover of peace did not have to quarrel more seriously with those who would not follow him. Ceolfrid says he would also have changed his tonsure if his authority had reached that far.
  • The annals from the Book of Leinster, as Whitley Stokes prints them, say Adomnán brought the captives to Ireland. Stokes sets the entry at 693. No source used here says whose captives they were.
  • His name. He is a different man from Adamnan, the Irish monk of Coldingham whom Bede tells of in his fourth book.
