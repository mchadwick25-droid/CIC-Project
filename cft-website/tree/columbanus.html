The Age of Monks and Empires · c. 543-615 · Luxeuil, Bobbio

Columbanus

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Insular / Irish Monastic Christianity.

Known for
Irish monk who founded Luxeuil and other houses in Frankish Gaul and Bobbio in Lombard Italy, and wrote a monastic rule, a penitential and letters. Jonas of Bobbio says King Theuderic of Burgundy drove him out after he rebuked the king's way of life.
Also called
Columban, Columba, Columbanus Hibernus
Dates
Died at Bobbio on 21 November, after a single year there, Jonas of Bobbio says. Dana Munro gives the year as probably 615. Andrea Gallandi gives 615, after Charles Le Cointe and Jean Mabillon. No early source used here gives his birth year. Gallandi reports that he is said to have been born about the middle of the sixth century. William Dudley Foulke, in his notes to Paul the Deacon, gives about 543, citing Thomas Hodgkin
Place
Leinster in Ireland by birth, Jonas says; the monastery of Bangor; Anegray, Luxeuil and Fontaines in the Vosges; Bregenz among the Swabians; Bobbio in the Apennines
Role
Monk; abbot and founder of Luxeuil and Bobbio; writer of a monastic rule, a penitential, sermons, letters and poems

About This Person

Columbanus was writing to his monks from near the Bretons when a messenger came in. A ship was being made ready, the messenger said, to carry him back to his own country against his will. If he were thrown into the sea like Jonah, he wrote, they should pray that someone would row their Jonah back to land.

Columbanus was an Irish monk who founded monasteries in Frankish Gaul and Lombard Italy. Jonas, a monk of Bobbio, wrote his Life after his death. He names companions of the saint who told him its stories. It is a saint's Life, full of wonders, and it takes his side against his enemies.

Jonas says he was born in Leinster and entered Bangor, the monastery of Abbot Comgall. After many years he longed to live among strangers, and sailed with twelve companions.

In the Vosges wilderness he built houses at Anegray, Luxeuil and Fontaines, and wrote a rule for them, which survives. Each day, it says, a monk must fast, pray, work and read. His Penitential holds that different sins need different penances, as doctors treat wounds and sicknesses in different ways.

Jonas says he rebuked King Theuderic of Burgundy for keeping concubines. When Brunhild, the king's grandmother, brought him the sons of these unions, he refused to bless them. Jonas calls her a second Jezebel. His Irish reckoning of Easter had given offense in Gaul, James Barmby notes. Columbanus wrote to Pope Gregory the Great to defend it. "Error is manifestly ancient," he wrote, in Barmby's English, "but truth which reproves it is ever more ancient still."

About twenty years after he came, Jonas says, the king's men took him to Nantes to ship him home. The ship ran aground until his goods were put ashore, which Jonas reads as God's will that he stay. He preached among the Swabians near Bregenz, then crossed into Italy, where Agilulf, king of the Lombards, received him. The Lombard historian Paul the Deacon tells the same. Jonas says he died at his new monastery of Bobbio after a single year. Jonas's translator, Dana Munro, gives the year as probably 615.

Writing to a council of Gallic bishops, he had asked to live in silence in the Vosges, by the bones of seventeen dead brothers. "Let Gaul, I pray, hold us together," he wrote, in modern words, "whom the kingdom of heaven will hold, if our merits are good."

Key Works

  • Rule for monks: ten chapters on obedience, silence, food, poverty, chastity, the psalms and discretion; it asks a monk to fast, pray, work and read each day
  • Penitential: a scale of penances for monks, clergy and lay people, which holds that different sins need different cures; F. W. H. Wasserschleben holds it was written on the Continent
  • Letters: six letters under his name. Two went to Pope Boniface IV, and one each to Pope Gregory the Great, a council of Gallic bishops and a disciple. Another went to his monks as he waited to be shipped home. In several he names himself Columba, a dove

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Jonas of Bobbio names houses founded under his rule. Donatus, bishop of Besançon, whom Columbanus raised from the font, founded one, and his brother Ramelen another. The brothers Ado and Dado, whom he blessed as children, founded two more. Dana Munro identifies Ado's as Jouarre; Dado's stood on the little river Rebais.
  • Eustasius, his pupil and later abbot of Luxeuil, defended his Rule against the monk Agrestinus before a council at Mâcon. The account is from Jonas's Life of Eustasius, which Giovanni Domenico Mansi quotes. Mansi dates the council to 627; Johann Albert Fabricius reports Mabillon's 624 and Charles Le Cointe's 625.
  • Laurence, who followed Augustine of Canterbury as archbishop, wrote with his fellow bishops to the Irish, in a letter Bede gives. They had learned from Abbot Columbanus in Gaul, they say, that the Irish in no way differ from the Britons in their behavior.

How they're remembered

  • Jonas of Bobbio: "If anyone wishes to learn of his activity, let him seek it in the saint's writings", in Dana Munro's English.
  • Paul the Deacon, the Lombard historian, says he came into Italy from Luxeuil and was kindly received by the king of the Langobards. He built Bobbio, Paul says, and a great community of monks grew up there.
  • Andrea Gallandi judges his sermons most worthy of reading, full of piety joined to equal eloquence. He calls the letters written with burning zeal and Christian freedom.

What Is Uncertain

  • His life comes mainly from Jonas of Bobbio, a monk of the house Columbanus founded. Jonas wrote after his death. In his preface he says many who saw the deeds were still alive to tell them. He learned more, he says, from Attala and Eustasius, who followed Columbanus as abbots of Bobbio and Luxeuil. Dana Munro, Jonas's translator, says Jonas came to Bobbio three years after Columbanus died. William Dudley Foulke, citing Hodgkin, dates the Life about 640. The Life is a saint's Life, full of miracles, and it takes his side. It calls Brunhild a second Jezebel and has the family of Theuderic destroyed as Columbanus foretold. The Chronicle called Fredegar's tells the quarrel with Theuderic and Brunhild. Bruno Krusch notes that the chapter is for the most part copied word for word from Jonas.
  • His age when he left Ireland. Jonas's text gives his twentieth year. Dana Munro notes that the manuscripts differ and thinks the thirtieth more likely; Andrea Gallandi gives the thirtieth.
  • The king who received him in Gaul. Jonas names King Sigibert as ruler of Austrasia and Burgundy. Dana Munro notes that Sigibert died in 575 and was king only of Austrasia. Andrea Gallandi places Luxeuil in the kingdom of Burgundy under King Guntram. He reports 590 for Columbanus's coming there, from Le Cointe and Mabillon, and adds that the time is not plainly settled.
  • His age at death. His poem to Fedolius says he had reached eighteen Olympiads. James Ussher, as Andrea Gallandi reports, reckons seventy-two years if an Olympiad is four years, or ninety if five. Mabillon, as Gallandi quotes him, and Melchior Goldast, in his notes to the poems, take the five-year Olympiad and ninety years. Gallandi prefers seventy-two.
  • The year of his quarrel with Theuderic. The Chronicle called Fredegar's puts it in Theuderic's fourteenth year, which Bruno Krusch reckons as 608.
  • The dates of his letters. The Benedictine editors of Gregory's letters, as James Barmby reports, date the letter to Gregory to 598 or 599. Mabillon, as Gallandi reports, dates the letter to the Gallic bishops to 602, by its twelve years in the woods. A note in Mansi's tenth volume says it is not clear whether the twelve years count from his coming to Gaul or to Luxeuil. Gallandi dates the letter to his monks to 610, and Mabillon, as Gallandi quotes him, places it at Nantes. He dates the letter to Pope Boniface IV on the Three Chapters to 615.
  • Whether Gregory answered. James Barmby says no reply is extant and probably none was sent. In his later letter to Boniface IV, Columbanus says Satan twice hindered the bearers of his letters to Gregory.
  • His Penitential. F. W. H. Wasserschleben says it was written on the Continent, in Burgundy or northern Italy. Patrick Fleming first published it in 1667, he says, from a manuscript of Bobbio. Jonas says Columbanus wrote a learned work against the Arians at Milan; Johann Albert Fabricius lists it among his lost works.
