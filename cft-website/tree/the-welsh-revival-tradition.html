The Welsh Revival Tradition

Wales, 1735 to 1815 — a revival that happened in Welsh, met in small groups to tell the truth about itself, and left the country a body of hymns it still sings.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:06

Read along

The Welsh revival began independently of England and in its own language: in 1735 Howell Harris, a schoolmaster from Trefeca, and Daniel Rowland, a curate at Llangeitho, were converted within months of each other and began preaching to whoever would gather. What grew was not only a wave of feeling but a structure. The seiat, or experience meeting, a small society where members told one another honestly how things stood with their souls, described in a handbook written by the movement's poet, William Williams Pantycelyn. Williams gave Welsh Christianity a hymn corpus that became a national possession, and so, more quietly, did Ann Griffiths, a farmer's daughter who composed her hymns aloud and never wrote them down. They survive because her maid Ruth Evans held them in memory and Ruth's husband set them on paper after Ann died at twenty-nine.

People and Stories

Voices in the Record

  • Howell Harris — the Trefeca layman whose relentless itinerant preaching and enormous diaries drove the revival's first decades
  • Daniel Rowland — curate of Llangeitho, whose communion seasons drew people on foot from across Wales
  • William Williams Pantycelyn — the movement's hymn-writer and the theorist of its experience meetings; his 'Guide me, O thou great Jehovah' is sung far beyond Wales
  • Ann Griffiths — composed her hymns orally; they reached the page only because her maid Ruth Evans remembered them and Ruth's husband John Hughes wrote them down
  • Thomas Charles — organized the revival's Sunday schools and was among the founders of the British and Foreign Bible Society in 1804

Documented Stories

The Breach of 1750

By 1749 Howell Harris had been the driving force of the Welsh revival for fourteen years, riding the country with an energy his diaries record hour by hour, organizing the societies, and quarreling with everyone. He had also begun to preach in a way that alarmed his colleagues, speaking of the blood of God and the death of God in a manner that sounded to Daniel Rowland and the other clergy like the old heresy that the Father suffered. And he had met Sidney Griffith, the wife of a Caernarvonshire squire, who had left her drunken husband and whom Harris believed to have the gift of prophecy; she traveled with him, he consulted her about his decisions, and he defended her against his wife's misery and his friends' warnings. At the Association at Llanidloes in May 1750 the two parties failed to agree, and Harris was in effect excluded; the societies divided into Harris's people and Rowland's people, and most went with Rowland. Harris withdrew to his family home at Trefeca and gathered there a community he called the Family, more than a hundred people living in common, farming, printing, weaving and keeping hours of prayer under his rule, an experiment nearer to Herrnhut than to anything else in Wales. In 1762, when a new awakening broke out at Rowland's Llangeitho, the two men were reconciled and Harris rode the country again until his death in 1773, on his own terms and to smaller crowds.

Mary Jones Walks to Bala

Mary Jones was born in 1784 at Llanfihangel-y-Pennant, a hamlet under Cader Idris in Merioneth, the daughter of a weaver who died when she was small. She learned to read at one of the circulating schools that had taught Welsh peasants their letters for two generations, and at the Sunday school Thomas Charles of Bala had organized, and she wanted a Bible of her own. Welsh Bibles were scarce and dear; the last printing had sold out and the Society for Promoting Christian Knowledge, which had paid for it, was slow to pay for another. She saved for six years, and in 1800, aged fifteen, walked the twenty-five miles to Bala, most of it barefoot to spare her shoes, to buy one from Charles, who was known to have received a small consignment. He had promised them all. He gave her one, or three, the accounts differ. Two years later, in London, at a committee of the Religious Tract Society, Charles pressed the need for cheap Welsh Bibles, and told of the girl who had walked to Bala; a Baptist minister present, Joseph Hughes, is recorded as asking why not a society for Bibles for Wales, and if for Wales, why not for the kingdom, and if for the kingdom, why not for the world. The British and Foreign Bible Society was founded in March 1804, and its first Welsh Bible appeared in 1806. Mary Jones married, kept bees, lived to be eighty, and is buried at Bryn-crug.

Connections

In Its Own Record

A sister movement to English Methodism. It began at the same time, kept in touch with its leaders, and ran in its own language. Its hymns and habits of revival may lie behind the Welsh revival of 1904-05, but how far is hard to say.

In Contact With

  • in contact with The Methodist Revival Documented — Howell Harris, a leader of the Welsh revival, first met George Whitefield at Cardiff in March 1739. In 1746 an English visitor sat in on a meeting of the Welsh leaders. He wrote in his diary what they told him. God had raised up Harris and Daniel Rowland, they said, at the same time as Whitefield and the Wesleys.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

The Welsh revival ran in its own language from 1735, when Howell Harris and Daniel Rowland began preaching and organizing converts into the seiat, a small meeting where members told each other honestly how things stood with their souls. Its hymn writer William Williams Pantycelyn gave Welsh Christianity a national songbook, and so, more quietly, did Ann Griffiths, a farmer's daughter whose hymns survive only because her maid Ruth Evans memorized them and Ruth's husband wrote them down after Ann died at twenty-nine.

Legacy

It became a church. The Calvinistic Methodists ordained their own ministers in 1811 and are now the Presbyterian Church of Wales. The revival's wider effect made Nonconformity the ordinary religion of Wales for a century and a half. That meant the religion of the chapels, not of the established church. Its Sunday schools taught a great part of the nation to read Welsh. That is one reason the language came through the age of mines and mills alive. The singing it created may run on to the revival of 1904-05 and into Welsh choral music. That is a likely link, not a proven one.

How Sure the Record Is

Rich for its size, though much of it is in Welsh and reaches English readers only in translation; ordinary and women's voices are prominent, including Ann Griffiths.

  • Howell Harris's Trevecka diaries (c. 300 ms. volumes, National Library of Wales) and autobiographical accounts (primary) — One of the century's densest self-records of an itinerant preacher; largely unpublished.
  • Williams Pantycelyn: the hymn corpus (from Aleluia, 1744), Theomemphus (1764), The Experience Meeting (Drws y Society Profiad; Eng. trans. Bethan Lloyd-Jones, 1973) (primary) — Hymnody as actually used, plus the movement's own manual for its small-group structure. Available in English translation.
  • Ann Griffiths: hymns and letters (d. 1805) - composed orally, held in Ruth Evans's memory, transcribed posthumously by John Hughes (primary) — A rare woman's voice from the revival. It reached us only through memory and a later transcription by someone else, so two layers of transmission stand between her and the page.
  • The seiadau (experience meetings) records and rules, Daniel Rowland's Llangeitho communion seasons, the Trevecka letters (converts' correspondence) (primary) — The revival's lived organizational core, comparable to Methodism's class meetings. Records are archival, in Welsh.
  • Derec Llwyd Morgan, The Great Awakening in Wales (Epworth, 1988), with Geraint Tudur, Howell Harris (2000) and Eryn White's seiadau scholarship (secondary)
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 7: nearest coverage in 'Great Britain and Ireland' (Clark) and Ward's awakenings chapter (reference)
