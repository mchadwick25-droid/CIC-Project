Constantinople, today in Turkey

Eutyches

A person whose story no single tradition tells.

Known for
A priest and head of a monastery at Constantinople whose teaching of one nature in Christ after the union was condemned in 448, upheld at Ephesus in 449 and condemned again at Chalcedon in 451.
Also called
Eutyches the archimandrite
Dates
Condemned at a synod under Flavian of Constantinople that S. G. F. Perry and Charles Lett Feltoe date to November 448; restored at Ephesus in 449. He told Leo of Rome that he had spent "seventy years of life in continence and all chastity". No source used here gives his birth or death
Place
Constantinople; Ephesus
Role
Priest and archimandrite

About This Person

Eutyches, the head of a monastery in Constantinople, stood at last before a meeting of bishops. He had said he was ill and old. Pressed to say what Christ is, as God and as man, he answered: "I confess that our Lord was from two natures before the union. But after the union I confess one nature." The union was the moment God the Word became man in the Virgin's womb.

For that sentence he was condemned in 448, restored the next year, and condemned again at Chalcedon in 451. He had fought the teaching of Nestorius, which divided Christ, by holding to one nature. Yet both sides of the coming split would curse his name.

An abbot of many years in the emperor's city, Eutyches wrote that from childhood he had wanted only a quiet life.

A bishop, Eusebius of Dorylaeum, charged Eutyches before Flavian, bishop of Constantinople. Asked to say two natures and curse all who denied them, he refused. He called Christ's body the body of God, yet a human body taken from the Virgin. But his judges recorded that he denied it shared our nature. Nor would he curse the fathers who, he said, had rejected the words "two natures". Flavian's meeting stripped him of his priesthood, his monastery and his place in the church.

Eutyches wrote to Leo, bishop of Rome, that a faction had readied his sentence before the hearing and forced other abbots to sign. He told Leo he had appealed at the trial. Flavian told Leo: "This he certainly never did." Leo, answering in a long letter to Flavian called the Tome, judged Eutyches very unwary and exceedingly ignorant. The letter taught one Christ in two natures, each keeping its own character.

The emperor called a council at Ephesus, under Dioscorus, bishop of Alexandria; its Syriac record says Eutyches's petition moved the emperor to call it. He told the emperor his trial record was false. To the council he wrote that he had been handed to a crowd ready to drag him through the city as a heretic. Flavian, he wrote, had shown him no pity: "He had no regard for my gray hairs. I have grown old with them in wars against heretics, and in confessing the true faith."

That record was read aloud. A bishop who had judged him denied a sentence the record gave as his own: that denying two natures after the union meant mixing God and man. He then said he did not remember. The council shouted that his accuser should be burned alive, and cursed all who said two natures. Its minutes, kept by the side that restored him, say he declared himself right-believing. He was restored, and Flavian deposed, charged with changing the faith.

A new emperor then called a council at Chalcedon. It received Leo's letter against Eutyches's teaching. Egypt's bishops condemned those who said Christ's flesh came from heaven, but would not name Eutyches. The council demanded they curse him and sign Leo's letter. They asked to wait for their new archbishop. So writes Evagrius, on Chalcedon's side, from its records some 140 years later.

The bishops at Chalcedon cried that Leo's opponents were Eutychians. A later writer on their side called the churches that refused the council followers of Dioscorus and Eutyches. Yet those churches were not his followers. They kept what he had defended: one Christ against Nestorius. They rejected his account of Christ's body: in their own chronicle, he would not confess it shared our nature. About 475, Timothy Aelurus returned from exile as bishop of Alexandria. The prefect, that chronicle says, was thought to be a Eutychian, so the people demanded in church that Timothy curse Nestorius and Eutyches. He did so at once, aloud, and was cleared of suspicion.

After 451 the record goes quiet; no source we have tells where or when he died. What was left was his name, cursed by both sides, even by churches still holding the one nature after the union that the council at Ephesus had cheered.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The Council of Chalcedon's definition receives Leo's letter to Flavian "for the removal of the false doctrines of Eutyches".
  • Later councils name Eutyches beside Nestorius in their lists of condemned teachers.
  • The Syriac chronicle says Peter Mongus of Alexandria, about 482, accepted the emperor Zeno's Henotikon, a letter of union that anathematized Nestorius and Eutyches.
  • The Life of Severus says Severus of Antioch wrote a discourse and many letters against the heresies of Eutyches, and that eastern monks anathematized the heresies of Nestorius, Eutyches and Apollinaris. Severus's own letters speak of the heresy of Eutyches.
  • Philoxenus of Mabbug lists Eutyches with Mani and Marcion among those who deny the Incarnation of the Word.
  • A book printed in English in 1912 by Ormanian, a former Armenian patriarch, says the Armenian Church officially and solemnly anathematizes his name, with those of Arius, Macedon and Nestorius.
  • Canon 95 of the council of 692 names Eutyches among the heretics whom converts must anathematize.

How they're remembered

  • Leo of Rome: "very unwary and exceedingly ignorant"; "the ignorant old man".
  • The Syriac chronicle known as that of Zachariah of Mitylene: he "affirmed an inaccurate dogma".
  • Michael the Syrian, about 700 years later, repeats the chronicle's charge that his teaching denied the reality of the body God the Word took from the Virgin.

At Major Councils

  • Council of Chalcedon, 451: Not present; the monk whose condemnation and restoration lay behind the council; his teaching condemned

What Is Uncertain

  • His words at his trial come through the minutes of the synod that condemned him. He said the record was false, and at Ephesus in 449 Basil of Seleucia denied a sentence written as his, then said he did not remember.
  • Whether he appealed to Rome is disputed. He told Leo he lodged an appeal; Flavian wrote to Leo that "this he certainly never did".
  • Leo and Flavian were his opponents, and the acts of 449 come from the council that restored him.
  • That the court eunuch Chrysaphius was his godson rests here on Liberatus of Carthage, a later Latin writer held only in a rough scan, and on Charles Lett Feltoe's introduction to Leo's letters.
  • The date of Flavian's synod, November 448, is an editors' date. No source used here gives his birth, his death, or the name of his monastery.
  • That he was in exile after Chalcedon rests on Feltoe's summary of a letter of Leo that the English edition does not translate, which suggests he be banished to a still remoter place.
  • The Syriac chronicle cited here comes from the side that rejected Chalcedon; Hamilton and Brooks place its compiler in Mesopotamia, probably at Amida. It faults his teaching, though it says he was received at Ephesus after he confessed the true faith.
  • Leo wrote to him on June 1, 448, that his concern pleased Leo, since Nestorius's teaching was said to be reviving. Leo praised his concern, not his teaching. Feltoe's note that Eutyches had accused Domnus and Theodoret is Quesnel's opinion, an editor's.
  • The Syriac chronicle, which is hostile to his teaching, says soldiers of the palace visited him and were his friends, and that he relied on them when he said he was sick and old. No other held source says so.
  • The Syriac heading of his petition says it moved the emperor to call the council of 449. The emperors' own letter calling it blames Flavian for raising a question of the faith with him.
  • The chronicle calls the petition he gave the council at Ephesus a libel of recantation. The council's own minutes record only that he declared himself orthodox. Evagrius says the acts of 448 were confirmed when his complaint was heard.
  • Nestorius, writing in exile and hostile to Eutyches and to the emperor, says the emperor had accusers suborned to say the records of the trial at Constantinople were false.
  • The chronicle charges him with a teaching that used the image of air turning to rain, snow or ice. Its text is corrupt there and the translators supply some words; they are the chronicle's account of his teaching, not his words.
  • In his letter to Leo he asked not to be expelled after seventy years of continence, and so suffer shipwreck at the very end of life. The seventy years are his own claim.
  • The Greek of his answer at the trial survives in Evagrius's quotation of the acts, in a damaged print with a garbled line before its last word. Evagrius's English, Perry's English and Leo's Tome give the same words.
  • Hefele, a historian of the councils, doubts the text of canon 95 of the council of 692, which names Eutyches among the heretics converts must anathematize.
