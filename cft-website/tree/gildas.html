Gildas

A person. The tradition that tells their story: British and Welsh Christianity.

Known for
A British churchman of the sixth century whose long Latin letter, The Ruin of Britain, condemns the kings and clergy of his country.
Also called

About This Person

For ten years or more, Gildas says, he held back. "I have kept silence, I confess, with infinite sorrow of heart," he writes, "for the past ten years or even longer." At last he wrote a long letter in Latin to the rulers and clergy of Britain, "more in tears than in denunciation," as he puts it.

That letter, The Ruin of Britain, is almost all that survives from this British churchman of the sixth century. Hugh Williams, who put it into English, says it is "in no way a history". He calls Gildas "a preacher, a revivalist".

Gildas was born in the year of the siege of Badon Hill, almost the last great British victory over the Saxons. He writes of "the forty-fourth year, with one month now elapsed". Bede took the years from the coming of the Saxons. Williams follows the scholar Ussher, who counts them from Badon, so Gildas wrote some forty-three years after his birth. Williams puts that birth about 500, a date no early source gives. He dates the book a few years after 540, and before 547, while a king it attacks, Maglocunus, was alive. The Welsh annals put that king's death in a great plague, in Williams's year 547.

"Kings Britain has, but they are as her tyrants," Gildas writes. He names five, and calls Maglocunus "the island dragon", "last in my writing, first in wickedness". Then he turns on the clergy: "Priests Britain has, but foolish ones." He spares "a very few", the church's "only real children", who he says hold up his own weakness.

Readers abroad soon knew his name. The Irish saint Columbanus wrote to Pope Gregory the Great about bishops whom "the writer Giltas" calls pests. Williams dates the letter between 595 and 600. Bede later called Gildas the Britons' "own historian".

Two Lives tell much more, but they are late. Williams dates the core of the Breton Life, by a monk of Ruys, to the ninth century. Caradoc of Llancarfan wrote the Welsh Life in the twelfth. Williams calls its Glastonbury chapters "a piece of literary fiction". The Breton Life says that Gildas, dying, asked to be laid in a boat on his stone pillow and pushed out to sea.

Alcuin of York, writing in the 790s by Williams's dates, called him the wisest of the Britons. He told his readers to learn from Gildas why the Britons lost their land.

Key Works

  • The Ruin of Britain (De Excidio Britanniae): a long letter in Latin, part history and mostly rebuke, against five British kings and the clergy, which Williams calls a sermon
  • Fragments of lost letters: short sayings quoted under his name in early Irish collections of church law
  • A penitential (De Paenitentia): rules of penance for monks, printed under his name; Williams thinks some parts are later than Gildas

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Columbanus, writing to Pope Gregory the Great between 595 and 600 by Williams's dating, asks about bishops whom "the writer Giltas" calls pests, and says Gildas answered a question from Vennianus "most elegantly".
  • Bede called him the Britons' "own historian" and drew on his account of Badon. Williams says the habit of reading him as a historian began with Bede.

How they're remembered

  • Alcuin of York, in letters Williams dates to 793 and 797, called him the wisest of the Britons and told his readers to learn from his book why the Britons lost their land.
  • Williams: "he is a preacher, a revivalist", and the book "has been mistakenly read as history".

What Is Uncertain

  • His own book tells almost nothing of his life: not his monastery, teacher, family or birthplace. Those come from two Lives written long after him. Williams dates the core of the Breton Life, by a monk of Ruys, to the ninth century, with later additions. Caradoc of Llancarfan wrote the Welsh Life in the twelfth century, and Williams calls its Glastonbury chapters "a piece of literary fiction".
  • The sentence on Badon and his birth. Bede took the forty-four years to run from the coming of the Saxons. M. de la Borderie followed Bede and put Badon in 493. Ussher, whom Williams follows, read Gildas as born in Badon's year and writing forty-three years and a month later. Mommsen proposed an emendation that gives the same sense.
  • His dates. Williams's birth about 500 is an estimate, and he says the Welsh annals' 516 for Badon cannot be right. The annals Williams lists differ on his death: 570, 569, 571 and 562. Wasserschleben says he died in 583 at Bangor in Wales, giving no source.
  • The Lorica, a prayer printed under his name. In his opening note Williams calls it probably genuine. After weighing the manuscripts he concludes it is by an unknown South Welsh writer of the late seventh century, "nor by him".
  • Where he wrote. The Breton Life has him write in Brittany, ten years after leaving Britain. Williams thinks the writer misread Gildas's "ten years" of silence, and that Gildas wrote in Britain.
  • Where he died. The Breton Life has him drift to sea at his death and his body found at Croes-dy and buried at Ruys. Caradoc has him buried at Glastonbury.
  • Whether he was a monk. His book speaks of true monks and, in Taylor's rendering, of "our own order". Williams and Thomas Taylor read him as a monk.
