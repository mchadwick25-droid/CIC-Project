John III of Samannud

About This Person

In Holy Week, the patriarch John III of Samannud was held at the house of Samad, the governor of a castle. The Amir Abd al-Aziz, governor of Egypt, had handed him over until he paid a hundred thousand denarii. John answered calmly that God's law did not command him to save anything for himself. Samad ordered a brass pan of burning coals brought for his feet, until he promised to pay.

That night, the History of the Patriarchs says, the Amir's wife fell gravely ill. She sent her eunuch to Samad: "Take heed that thou do no harm to that man of God." At cock-crow Samad told the Amir he had not put the patriarch to the torture.

Samad came down to fifty thousand. "The only things that I can dispose of are my garments which are upon my body," John answered. Samad kept lowering his price, to ten thousand in the end. "I will not promise what I cannot perform," John said. He was freed on the Thursday of Holy Week, and he washed the feet of the people.

John followed Agathon as patriarch of Alexandria. The History is a later Arabic history of the Coptic church. B. Evetts, who translated it, dates John's patriarchate 677 to 686. The writer of John's life says he was with him at the end, "for he was his spiritual son", but gives no name.

Theodore, the Chalcedonian governor of Alexandria who had harassed Agathon, died of dropsy, the History says. The Chalcedonians held to the council of Chalcedon, which John's church refused. Theodore's son became governor and was like a son to John.

John rebuilt the church of Saint Mark in Alexandria in three years. He fed the poor through a famine of three years. Dying, he was carried to the altar of Saint Mark's, where he stood and said the whole prayer of thanksgiving. He died on the first of the Coptic month Kiahk, after nine years.

To Samad he had said: "My body is in thy hands, but my soul and body are both in the hands of my Lord Jesus Christ."

Legacy

What traces to them

  • He rebuilt the church of Saint Mark in Alexandria in three years, the History says, and was buried in it.
  • In his time the people of Agharwah and of the Xoite nome, who had been Chalcedonians, joined his church, the History says.
  • He chose Isaac, a monk of Saint Macarius, made him his partner in governing the church, and named him to follow him.

How they're remembered

  • The History calls him a saint and "a virgin in soul and body". It says none could look on his face, as with Moses.
  • The Ethiopic Synaxarium, a book of saints' days, keeps him on the 1st of Takhshash in E. A. Wallis Budge's English. It names churches built in his time, and the famine in which he fed the poor.
  • The book under the name of Abu Salih says the governor Abd al-Aziz had a church founded for "the patriarch Jonas". Its translator's note reads Jonas as John.

What Is Uncertain

  • His life rests on the History of the Patriarchs, a later Arabic history of the Coptic church. Its writer says he was John's spiritual son and was with him at the end, but gives no name.
  • The sum. The History has the Amir demand a hundred thousand denarii. In B. Evetts's English, John answers that he has not a hundred thousand drachmae of it. Eusèbe Renaudot, a Catholic scholar writing in Latin in 1713, gives the answer as not even a hundred drachmas.
  • Theodore's son. The History says the son of Theodore, the Chalcedonian governor of Alexandria, followed him and loved John like a son. Renaudot adds that he was a Jacobite, which Evetts's English does not say.
  • When his successor was chosen. Evetts heads Isaac's life from 686, and Renaudot puts John's death in 686. R. H. Charles puts the election in 690.
  • The order after him. Michael the Syrian, a twelfth-century patriarch of the Syriac Orthodox church, puts Simon next and then Isaac. The History puts Isaac first.
  • Whom he named. After his death some bishops chose George, a deacon of Sakha. They planned to tell the Amir that John had named him. The Amir found from the documents that he had named Isaac, the History says.
