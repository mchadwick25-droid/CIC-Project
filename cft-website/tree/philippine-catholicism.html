Philippine Catholicism

The Philippines, 1565 to 1650 — the first century of a Catholicism that learned to speak Tagalog, printed in baybayin, and ended up being sung at home.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:01

Read along

Spanish rule in the Philippines began in 1565, and with it came friars — Augustinians first, then Franciscans, Jesuits, Dominicans and Recollects. Who baptized, gathered scattered settlements into parishes and built stone churches at their centers. What grew there was not simply transplanted Spanish religion. The first book printed in the islands, the Doctrina Christiana of 1593, appeared in Spanish and in Tagalog written both in Latin letters and in the local baybayin script, and lay confraternities took on much of the work of devotion. Out of that vernacular habit came the tradition the islands are still known for — the pasyon, the Passion of Christ told as a long verse epic and chanted in ordinary houses through Holy Week — though the surviving pasyon texts date from the century after this one.

Documented Stories

The Governor’s License and the Chinese Printer

On 20 June 1593 Governor Gómez Pérez Dasmariñas wrote to Philip II that he had licensed the printing, in Manila, of two Doctrinas Christianas, one in Tagalog and one in Chinese, because of the great need for them, although printing was forbidden without royal permission; he set the price at four reals and asked the king's approval after the fact. The books were made by xylography, whole pages cut into wooden blocks in the Chinese manner, and the Tagalog one printed the catechism three times over: in Spanish, in Tagalog in Roman letters, and in Tagalog in the baybayin syllabary that Filipinos already used. The printer, according to the Dominican chronicler Diego Aduarte, was a Chinese Christian of the Parián named Juan de Vera, a merchant of means who, Aduarte says, took up the craft not for profit but out of devotion, so that the books the friars needed could be made; he kept an oratory in his house and was buried in the Dominican church. Aduarte adds that de Vera also made the type for the first movable-type printing in the islands a decade later. The single surviving copy of the Tagalog Doctrina is in the Library of Congress. Whatever the friars intended by teaching in baybayin, the effect was that the first printed words in a Philippine language were the Our Father, the Hail Mary and the Ten Commandments in the converts' own script.

The Archbishop and the Massacre of 1603

In May 1603 three Chinese officials arrived in Manila and asked to inspect a mountain of gold said to lie at Cavite; the Spaniards, who had no such mountain, concluded that they were scouting an invasion. Archbishop Miguel de Benavides, a Dominican who had spent years in the Chinese mission and spoke the language, warned the governor that the twenty thousand or more Chinese of the Parián would rise, and urged that their numbers be cut. The city began to arm, Chinese were forbidden weapons, and the Chinese, hearing that the Spaniards planned to kill them first, rose on the night of 3 October. Over the following weeks Spanish, Japanese and Filipino forces killed, by the governor's own reckoning, some fifteen thousand Chinese; other reports say twenty-three thousand. Benavides wrote afterwards that the Chinese had brought it on themselves and that God had preserved the city, and the Dominican fathers who had preached to them in Binondo and the Parián buried their converts alongside the rest. Within three years the Chinese quarter had refilled, the Dominicans' Chinese mission resumed, and the Chinese Christian settlement at Binondo, founded in 1594 for converts married to Filipino women, went on to become the parish from which Manila's Chinese Christianity descends. The massacre was repeated in 1639 and 1662. The letters of Benavides and of Governor Acuña both survive, and neither reads as regret.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

The first book printed in the islands, the Doctrina Christiana of 1593, appeared in Spanish and in Tagalog written in both Latin letters and the local baybayin script. Out of that vernacular habit grew the pasyon, the Passion of Christ told as a long verse epic and chanted in ordinary homes through Holy Week, a practice still kept today.

Legacy

It became one of the largest Catholic populations on earth — the Philippines is today the largest Christian country in Asia — and its devotional culture of patronal fiestas, confraternities, the Black Nazarene procession in Manila and the chanted pasyon at Holy Week descends directly from this period. Four of the churches begun in these centuries are now a UNESCO World Heritage site, and Filipino Catholics have since become one of the world's largest migrant Christian populations, carrying those devotions everywhere they go.

Experience It Today

  • The Baroque Churches of the Philippines — four churches of the Spanish period, including San Agustín in Manila, inscribed by UNESCO in 1993
  • San Agustín Church, Intramuros, Manila — completed in 1607 and the oldest stone church in the country

How Sure the Record Is

Moderate-to-rich: the pasyon (vernacular passion epic chanted in homes - inside voice by definition), confraternity records, rich devotional documentation

  • The Doctrina Christiana (1593) - the first Philippine imprint, Tagalog and Spanish, in Latin script and baybayin (primary) — Catechesis in the converts' own script from the very first year. A facsimile of the Library of Congress copy has been digitized.
  • The mission record: Chirino's Relacion de las Islas Filipinas (1604), the orders' chronicles (Plasencia's customs accounts); translations in Blair & Robertson, The Philippine Islands (primary) — The fullest early descriptions of Filipino Christian life — but written by the missionaries themselves, not the converts.
  • The pasyon and confraternity layer: cofradia records and parish devotional documentation; the pasyon/pabasa tradition of home chanting (primary) — The earliest surviving pasyon text is Aquino de Belen's, from 1704 — later than this period. The account here rests on the broader devotional practice of the Spanish era, not on that specific text.
  • Phelan, The Hispanization of the Philippines (1959), with Rafael, Contracting Colonialism (1988) (secondary) — A classic framing work, plus the current standard on translation and conversion.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 6 (reference) — This volume has no chapter dedicated to the Philippines; the closest is the mission cluster in Part V, chapters 27-30.
