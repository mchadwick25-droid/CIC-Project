The Chinese Church - Indigenous & Suffering

China, 1920s to 2010 — house churches carrying forward the pattern of the earliest Chinese Christianity, from the Little Flock's Nee-then-Lee line onward, its record thinnest and least safe to document exactly where its growth was greatest.

Nee's The Spiritual Man appeared in 1928, and Wang Mingdao published his own account of fifty years in 1950. The traveling preacher John Sung kept diaries that his family later put together. After 1950 the Christian Manifesto and the Three-Self Patriotic Movement brought Protestant church life under the state. Wang Mingdao published 'We, Because of Faith' in 1955. In November 1998 house-church leaders published a joint Confession of Faith. No living person and no specific community is named here. What is known is thinnest, and least safe to make public, exactly where the growth was greatest. That is a fact about the church itself, not only about the available sources.

Documented Stories

‘I Am Peter’

Wang Mingdao had led the Christian Tabernacle in Beijing since 1937 as an independent congregation, and after 1950 refused to join the Three-Self Patriotic Movement on the ground that a church under the state's direction was not the church. In June 1955 he published a pamphlet, We, Because of Faith, saying so; on the night of 8 August 1955 he and his wife Liu Jingwen were arrested, and the Tabernacle was closed. In prison he was interrogated for months, and in September 1956, broken, he signed a confession admitting counter-revolutionary crimes and promising to join the Three-Self, and was released. His own account, written after 1980, tells what followed. He could not eat or sleep; he walked the streets of Beijing repeating that he was Peter, who had denied his Lord, and Judas; and after some months he went to the authorities, withdrew the confession, and said that he would rather be in prison than live with what he had signed. In April 1958 he and his wife were arrested again. He was sentenced to life, she to fifteen years; he was held in prisons and labor camps in Shanxi and elsewhere until January 1980, when he was released at seventy-nine, nearly blind and deaf, refusing the release papers because they implied guilt. He lived eleven more years in Shanghai and died in 1991. His account of 1956 is the reason the story is known, and he wrote it, he said, so that no one would think him a hero.

Watchman Nee’s Last Note

Watchman Nee had founded the assemblies known as the Little Flock in the 1920s on the principle of one church in each city, without denominations or foreign control, and by 1949 they numbered some seventy thousand members, the largest indigenous Protestant body in China; his books were read far beyond it. He had also run a pharmaceutical company, which made him a capitalist in the new state's eyes. In April 1952 he was arrested in Manchuria and held without trial. In January 1956 the authorities convened accusation meetings at which Nee was denounced, and the church's own leaders, under pressure, joined the denunciations; the published charges included counter-revolutionary activity, espionage, embezzlement, and licentious conduct with women of the church. He was sentenced in June 1956 to fifteen years. His wife Charity was allowed rare visits until her death in 1971; he was moved in 1972, his sentence over and not ended, to a labor farm in Anhui, and died there on 30 May 1972. His family, collecting his ashes, were given a slip of paper found under his pillow, on which he had written, in his grandniece's account, that Christ is the Son of God who died for the redemption of sinners and rose after three days, that this is the greatest fact in the universe, and that he died for his belief in Christ. The Little Flock survived underground and abroad; the charges against his conduct are denied by his followers and treated as unproven by historians.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

From the 1910s Chinese Christians began founding churches of their own, free of the foreign missions. Wei Enbo's True Jesus Church dates from 1917, Watchman Nee's Little Flock from 1922, and the Jesus Family from 1927. Their leaders wrote a great deal and paid for it. Nee was imprisoned from 1952 to 1972, and Wang Mingdao from 1955 to 1980. In the decades that followed, much of the church went on meeting in homes. It was led by people with no institutional standing. That pattern, congregations meeting quietly under pressure, echoes the house churches of Christianity's first two centuries.

Legacy

By 2010 it was a very large church by any count, though how large was genuinely contested. A Pew study published later, in 2011, estimated about 67 million Christians in China. Official Three-Self figures and the movements' own claims fell elsewhere, and the spread between them is itself worth knowing. The founder corpora were read far outside China. The longest inheritance may be the shape rather than the size: congregations meeting in homes, led by people with no institutional standing, carrying on through decades in which the alternative was prison. That pattern is the map's strongest modern echo of the house-churches of the first two centuries.

Sources

On the Nicene Creed

Founder-defined branches — especially the Little Flock's line from Watchman Nee to Witness Lee — are best judged on their own published texts: Nee's own writings, and Lee's Recovery Version and local-churches teaching. The Christian Research Institute reversed its own earlier classification of this tradition in a 2009 reassessment, worth naming honestly, and how each of these bodies stood doctrinally remains a genuinely open, body-by-body question. The clearest trinitarian, creed-close voice in this family is the house-church movement's own Confession of Faith from November 1998.
