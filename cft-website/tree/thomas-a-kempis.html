The Late Medieval Era · 1406-1471 · Mount St Agnes near Zwolle, and Deventer

Thomas à Kempis

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Devotio Moderna / Brethren of the Common Life.

Known for
Canon of Mount St Agnes near Zwolle who copied books for his house. He wrote its Chronicle and the Lives of the founders of the New Devotion. Johannes Busch, a canon of Windesheim, names him as the author of the Imitation of Christ, which others have given to other men.
Also called
Thomas Hemerken of Kempen, Thomas Hemerken, Thomas of Kempen, Thomas a Kempis, Thomas Kempis
Dates
Born about 1380, says William Benham, who translated the Imitation in 1886; his later translators Vincent Scully and William Duthoit give 1380. The notice of his death places him in his ninety-second year. He came to Mount St Agnes in 1399, his own Chronicle says. John Mason Neale, an English historian of the Dutch church writing in the 1850s, gives 1400. His Chronicle records that he was clothed as a canon on the feast of Corpus Christi, 1406. He died in 1471, on the feast of St James the Less, after Compline, the notice says.
Place
Kempen, in the diocese of Cologne, where he was born, his Chronicle says. Deventer, where he went to school. Mount St Agnes, outside Zwolle, from 1399. Lunenkerc in Frisia, the Chronicle's spelling, in exile from 1429. Bethany, where his brother John died, his Chronicle says. William Duthoit places the convent near Arnhem.
Role
Canon regular of Mount St Agnes. Copyist. Chronicler of his house and biographer of the movement's founders. Twice sub-prior, the prior's deputy, and in Vincent Scully's account for many years in charge of the novices

About This Person

"Whoever follows me does not walk in darkness," says the Lord. So opens the Imitation of Christ, a fifteenth-century book that says these words call us to imitate Jesus's life and ways. It puts a changed heart above argument: "I would rather feel sorrow for sin than know how to define it." And it turns inward: "Learn to scorn outward things and to give yourself to inward things. And you will see the kingdom of God come within you."

The book is credited to Thomas à Kempis, a canon who spent almost all his life in one small house, Mount St Agnes, near Zwolle. There he copied books, among them the house's Bible, and wrote its Chronicle and the Lives of the New Devotion's founders. Johannes Busch, a canon of his own order and time, names him as the book's author. Early copies named John Gerson, a Paris theologian, and scholars still disagree.

The book calls the cross the "royal way", the only road to life and inner peace. That way is dying to self each day: denying oneself, and willingly bearing the pain of body or trouble of spirit that comes.

Born about 1380 in Kempen, near Cologne, Thomas went to school at Deventer. Florens Radewijns, who led the Brothers of the Common Life there, took him in, placed him in the school, gave him books and found him lodging. He lived among the Brothers, who shared all they had and spent their days copying books. In 1399 he came to Mount St Agnes and asked to stay, and there he became a canon, living in community under the Rule of St Augustine.

When a dispute over the bishop of Utrecht brought a pope's ban on church services, the canons kept it. In 1429, for keeping it, they were insulted and driven out, and the community spent three years in Frisia. Thomas died in 1471, in his ninety-second year.

The book's call crossed the lines that later divided the church. Ignatius of Loyola, founder of the Jesuits, knew it as "the little Gerson". He told a fellow Jesuit that after finding it he wanted no other book of devotion, and read one chapter a day. Two centuries later, reading it, the young John Wesley "began to see that true religion was seated in the heart". His Methodist Conference advised its preachers to read it, among other books, between four and five in the morning. The Quaker John Woolman, though told Thomas was a Roman Catholic, judged him "a man of a true Christian spirit".

Key Works

  • The Chronicle of Mount St Agnes: the house's history year by year, from Geert Grote's choice of the site to 1471. He says he drew on what he saw, what the elders told him and what others wrote. Another brother carried it on to 1477 and wrote the notice of his death. J. P. Arthur translated it in 1906
  • The Lives of the founders of the New Devotion: the Lives of Geert Grote, Florens Radewijns and their followers, written as talks between an elder brother and a novice, J. P. Arthur says. Thomas wrote them to leave a memorial of the brothers for those who come after. Arthur translated them in 1905
  • The Imitation of Christ: four books of devotion, credited to him but disputed. Johannes Busch names him as its author. William Atkynson's English version of 1504 names John Gerson, chancellor of Paris, and William Benham argues for John Gersen, an abbot of Vercelli

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Another brother of Mount St Agnes carried his Chronicle on from 1471 to 1477, J. P. Arthur says, and wrote the notice of his death into it.
  • Ignatius of Loyola told Consalvius de Cámara, who wrote it down in Spanish in 1555, that at Manresa he first saw the Gerconzito, the little Gerson. After it he wanted no other devotional book. The Jesuit editors of his Spiritual Exercises identify it as the Imitation of Christ.
  • From the second week on, the Spiritual Exercises of Ignatius advise reading devout little books at times. They name the Imitation of Christ, the Gospels and the lives of the saints. The Spanish edition printed at Burgos in 1516 gives the book to John Gerson.
  • Oliver Manare, a Jesuit from 1551, answered questions about Ignatius. He writes in Latin that Ignatius usually kept no books on the table of his inner room but the New Testament and Thomas à Kempis. Ignatius used to call Thomas à Kempis, Manare says, the partridge of spiritual books.
  • William Atkynson put the first three books into English for Margaret, Countess of Richmond and Derby, John Ingram says. She was the mother of King Henry the Seventh. Wynkyn de Worde printed it in 1504.
  • Reviewing his life in his Journal, John Wesley wrote that at about twenty-two he read the Imitation as "Kempis's Christian Pattern". He began to see "that true religion was seated in the heart". He was "very angry at Kempis for being too strict", yet often found "much sensible comfort in reading him".

How they're remembered

  • The brother who wrote his death notice calls him "wondrous comfortable to the troubled and the tempted". He says the thought of the Lord's passion filled Thomas's heart with love.
  • Johannes Busch calls him, in Latin, a man of proven life.
  • William Benham, who doubted that Thomas wrote the Imitation, says its author "has produced the most popular religious work in Christendom".
  • John Mason Neale says the Imitation has, next to Scripture, "probably been read more widely than any other in the Western Church".

What Is Uncertain

  • Who wrote the Imitation of Christ. Johannes Busch, a canon of Windesheim in Thomas's day, names him as its author, in the story of the dream of 1424. Thomas's own Chronicle tells that dream without naming the dreamer. A copy in his hand is dated 1441, and John Mason Neale quotes its closing note. Neale holds that this note is the only ground for giving him the book. The same reason, he says, might make him the author of the Bible.
  • Other claimants to the Imitation. William Atkynson's English version of 1504 calls it the book of John Gerson, chancellor of Paris. William Benham, who translated it in 1886, argues for John Gersen, an abbot of Vercelli, though he grants that the ground grows somewhat uncertain. J. P. Arthur, who translated the Lives in 1905, saw a manuscript of the first book from about 1423 that gives it to a Carthusian monk. John Ingram reports that Francis Lee, an English translator of Thomas in the early 1700s, asked whether Walter Hilton wrote it. Hilton was the English author of the Scale of Perfection.
  • Scholars on Thomas's side. John Ingram, who edited the early English versions in 1893, is convinced the book is Thomas's, and says Gersen "appears to be an apocryphal person". Michael Joseph Pohl, who edited Thomas's Latin works from 1902, is persuaded that Gersen never existed. Arthur holds that Thomas wrote at least the first part in the early years of his priesthood.
  • Who sent him to Mount St Agnes. In his own Chronicle Thomas came to Zwolle for the indulgences of 1399, went on to Mount St Agnes and asked to stay. The brother who wrote his death notice says Florens Radewijns sent him, at twenty, to his own brother the prior.
  • The year he came. His Chronicle puts it in 1399, and William Benham says he entered as a novice that year. John Mason Neale gives 1400.
  • When he was born. No line in his own writings gives the year, but his Chronicle makes him sixty-seven in 1448. The notice of his death puts him in his ninety-second year in 1471. William Benham gives about 1380, and Vincent Scully and William Duthoit give 1380. John Ingram reports a seventeenth-century note in a Dublin manuscript that gives 1380.
  • The day he died. The notice of his death gives the feast of St James the Less, after Compline. The seventeenth-century note in the Dublin manuscript that John Ingram reports gives 25 July.
  • When he became a priest. His Chronicle does not record his ordination. The notice says he had been a priest for above fifty-seven years in 1471. William Duthoit, who translated his prayers in 1904, gives 1413.
  • His parents. His Chronicle names them John and Gertrude and gives nothing more. William Benham says his father was a laboring man and his mother the village schoolmistress. Duthoit calls them humble, and says his father is believed to have worked in metal. Neither gives a source.
  • The Bible he copied. The notice says he wrote the whole Bible the house used. Benham says it took fifteen years, and that one Rosweide saw it at Cologne with a closing note dated 1439. John Mason Neale says it was begun in 1417 and finished in 1459.
  • Which telling of the 1424 dream came first. Karl Grube, who edited Busch's Latin in 1886, notes that the Chronicle of Mount St Agnes borrowed this passage. The Chronicle's version leaves the dreamer unnamed, and Busch names him as Thomas.
  • His Life of Lydwine of Schiedam. Vincent Scully, who translated it in 1912, says Thomas edited the Life already published by John Brugman and kept Brugman's language almost throughout. Its prologue is signed by Brother N., a poor pilgrim. J. P. Arthur says the name may easily refer to Thomas, and that a copy of the Life exists in his own hand.
