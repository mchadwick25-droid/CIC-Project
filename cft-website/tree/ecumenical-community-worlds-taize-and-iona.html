Ecumenical Community Traditions - Taize & Iona

France and Scotland, 1938/1940 to 2010 — Taizé and Iona, ecumenical communities built as deliberate heirs of older monastic lines, Iona's community set literally on Columba's own island.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 0:58

Read along

Two communities, founded two years apart, built a shared life of work and prayer. George MacLeod founded the Iona Community in 1938. He set it to rebuilding the abbey on Columba's own island, a work that took until 1965. He held that worship, labor and politics belong together. Roger Schutz began the community at Taizé in France in 1940. Its Rule was written in 1954. From the 1970s young people came in large numbers. A Council of Youth met in 1974, and European meetings were held every year from 1978. Both communities are ecumenical by design. Their members come from many churches, with none of the usual boundaries between them. Brother Roger was murdered on 16 August 2005, and the community carried on under Brother Alois.

Documented Stories

The Evening of 16 August 2005

Roger Schutz had come to Taizé in Burgundy in 1940, alone, to shelter refugees, and had founded there in 1949 a community of brothers, Protestant and Catholic; by the 1970s young people were coming in thousands each week to pray three times a day and sing the community's short repeated chants, and Brother Roger sat at the end of each evening prayer to receive them one by one. On the evening of 16 August 2005 some 2,500 young people were in the church. During the prayer a Romanian woman of thirty-six walked up the aisle, took a knife from her bag, and stabbed Brother Roger three times in the neck; he was ninety and died within minutes, while the brothers carried him out and the prayer, after a silence, continued. She was found by the court to be mentally ill and unfit to stand trial, and committed to a hospital. The prayer the next day was held as usual. Brother Roger had written a letter eight years before naming Brother Alois, a German Catholic, as his successor, and the community confirmed it within two days. The funeral on 23 August, in the church, was led by Cardinal Kasper, with Protestant and Orthodox leaders present; the prayers asked God's forgiveness for the woman who had killed him, by name.

The Craftsmen and the Ministers on Iona

George MacLeod, a decorated officer of the first war turned pacifist, and since 1930 minister of Govan Old, a shipyard parish, had concluded that the Church of Scotland had lost the working class because its ministers had never worked with their hands. In the summer of 1938 he took to Iona, where Columba had landed in 563 and the abbey's monastic buildings lay in ruins, a party of unemployed craftsmen from Govan, and of young ministers, to live together in huts, worship morning and evening in the abbey, and rebuild the ruins. The craftsmen were paid; the ministers worked as laborers under them. MacLeod told that when timber for the refectory roof could not be got, a cargo of Canadian timber was washed ashore after a storm, which he regarded as an answer to prayer and his critics as a good story. The Iona Community that grew from those summers was a dispersed order under a rule of daily prayer, accounting for money and time, and work for peace and justice, whose members served in the housing schemes and industrial parishes of Scotland; the rebuilding was finished in 1965. Its membership was, from 1938, men only, and MacLeod, in most things ahead of his church, held that its purpose, the ministry to working men, required it; women were not admitted as members until 1969, two years after he retired, when the community changed its rule over his objection. Its songs and liturgies since have been written by both.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

George MacLeod founded the Iona Community in 1938 and set it to rebuilding the abbey on Columba's own island, a project that took until 1965. Roger Schutz began the community at Taizé in France two years later, in 1940, and from the 1970s young people started arriving by the thousands, for meetings held every year from 1978. When Brother Roger was murdered in 2005, the community simply continued under Brother Alois.

Legacy

Both communities were still going in 2010, and both took visitors. Taizé's European meetings were held every year from 1978. Iona's Wild Goose material, much of it by John Bell, found its way into hymnals across the English-speaking world. Their passage through the death of a founder is itself part of what they left. Taizé carried on under Brother Alois, so the community outlived the man who began it.

Doctrinal Floor Note

The person-defined question here is cleanly resolved rather than open. This was a completed founder-to-successor transition, not a movement still centered on one living founder's unique authority. By 2010 both Taizé and the Iona Community had continued institutionally past their founders.
