Constantinople, today in Turkey · Nitria

Evagrius Ponticus

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Desert Monasticism.

Also called Evagrius of Ibora, Evagrius the Monk (in the Philokalia)

A deacon of Constantinople who became a monk of the Egyptian desert, and wrote of the eight tempting thoughts and of prayer.

Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition→Desert Monasticism→no descendant recorded

About This Person

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 4:30

Read along

One night in Constantinople, the deacon Evagrius saw in a vision that soldiers had seized him and chained him among forty prisoners. A friend offered to free him, but first made him swear on the gospel to leave the city. Palladius says the friend was an angel in a friend's shape. He woke and reasoned with himself. "Even if the oath was sworn in a trance, I still swore it." He sailed.

That oath set Evagrius Ponticus on the road to the Egyptian desert. There he became a monk, named eight tempting thoughts that trouble a monk, and taught that prayer is the mind's own work. Long after his death, councils named him with Origen, a third-century teacher of Alexandria, among the condemned. Yet monks in the Latin West and the Syriac East went on using what he taught.

He was born in Pontus, on the Black Sea. Gregory of Nazianzus made him a deacon, and in 381 left him with the new bishop of the great city, since Evagrius argued so well against every heresy.

A married woman of the highest rank fell in love with him, and he with her. He prayed for something to stand in the way, yet could not break away. Palladius, his disciple and our main witness, says Evagrius told him of this years later. Sozomen, a church historian a generation on, tells it harder: her husband was plotting his death. Palladius says only that Evagrius imagined the husband had acted. The vision came, and he sailed for Jerusalem.

There Melania, a Roman lady, took him in. He slipped back into his old ways and fell ill, and the doctors gave up. Melania asked what his thoughts were, and he confessed everything. She made him promise before the Lord to live as a monk, and said she would pray for him. Within days he was well, and he went to Egypt.

He spent two years at Nitria, then fourteen at the Cells, a settlement of hermits' huts deeper in the desert. Palladius says he ate a pound of bread, and a pint of oil lasted him three months. Socrates, another historian, says the city debater learned the philosophy of deeds from the old monks.

Out of that life came his Praktikos, which names eight tempting thoughts: gluttony, lust, love of money, sadness, anger, acedia, vainglory and pride. Acedia is a listless boredom the monks called the noonday demon. "Whether these thoughts are able to disturb the soul or not is not up to us; but whether they linger or not, and whether they arouse passions or not; that is up to us." On prayer he writes that no law makes a monk work or fast all the time, but a law tells him to pray without ceasing, since the mind is made for prayer. A second book set verses of Scripture against each of the eight, as Christ answered his tempter.

Gennadius, a priest of Marseilles writing eighty years on, calls him the first to teach the eight, "or among the first at least". He put that second book into Latin himself. John Cassian gave eight books of his Institutes to the same eight, for the monks of Gaul. Cassian never names Evagrius. In the East, in 840, the monk and bishop Thomas of Marga quoted "the holy Evagrius" in Syriac on that law to pray continually.

In 415 Jerome ranked Evagrius with heretics for a book that promised freedom from passion, a calm the passions no longer stir. "In that state the mind is stirred no more. To put it simply, it becomes either a stone or a god." Later councils, from 680 to 787, listed Evagrius with Origen and Didymus as condemned at Constantinople in 553. A nineteenth-century historian of the councils notes that the surviving acts of 553 do not name him at all.

Shortly before he died, Evagrius told his friends that for three years fleshly desire had not troubled him, after so long a life of ceaseless prayer. He died at fifty-four, Palladius writes, after receiving communion in church at the feast of Epiphany.

Key Works

  • Praktikos: a hundred short chapters on the ascetic life, the eight tempting thoughts and freedom from passion (held in Luke Dysinger's English)
  • Antirrhetikos: verses of Scripture set against each tempting thought, in eight parts, as Socrates of Constantinople describes it; Gennadius of Marseilles put it into Latin; not held
  • The Gnostic: fifty chapters for the learned, which Socrates quotes; not held

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Gennadius of Marseilles says he was the first to mention the eight principal sins, "or among the first at least to teach these", and translated his work against them into Latin.
  • Palladius of Helenopolis, author of the Lausiac History, was his disciple, as Socrates of Constantinople says.
  • The Philokalia, the Greek collection on prayer compiled by Macarius of Corinth and Nikodemos the Hagiorite, lists works of his in its contents under the name of Evagrius the Monk.
  • John Cassian, in the Institutes written for Castor in Gaul, sets out eight principal faults: the same eight as the Praktikos, with dejection and anger in the other order. He does not name Evagrius.
  • Thomas of Marga, writing in Syriac in 840, quotes "the holy Evagrius" that monks are not commanded to watch and fast at all times, but that they should pray continually.
  • Jerome says his work is widely read, in the East in Greek and in the West in a Latin translation made by his disciple Rufinus.
  • Palladius calls him his teacher, and John of Lycopolis took Palladius for one of Evagrius's company.

How they're remembered

  • Socrates of Constantinople copied passages from his books and wrote, "Whoever shall read these productions will be convinced of their excellence."
  • Jerome of Strido, writing in 415 against Pelagius, ranked him with the heretics and said his teaching on freedom from passion makes the mind "either a stone or a God."
  • Evagrius Scholasticus, writing in the sixth century, reports that the emperor Justinian condemned Origen, Didymus and Evagrius.
  • The Sixth Council in 680-681, the Council in Trullo in 692 and the Second Council of Nicaea in 787 each name Evagrius with Origen and Didymus; the council of 692 charges them with bringing back Greek myths and the circlings of certain bodies and souls.
  • Cyril of Scythopolis, in the Life of Sabas as printed in Surius's Latin, says the monk Nonnus held the errors of Didymus, Evagrius and Origen, who talked nonsense about pre-existence.
  • Victor of Tunnuna records that in 565 Justinian sent into exile Eutychius of Constantinople, who had condemned the Three Chapters and Evagrius the hermit and deacon and Didymus, whose praises Victor had set out earlier.

Open Questions

  • His life is known mainly from his disciple Palladius, who writes in praise of him and tells it as he heard it from Evagrius himself.
  • The route from Constantinople differs. Palladius and Sozomen take him to Jerusalem first. Socrates of Constantinople says he went with Gregory of Nazianzus into Egypt.
  • Sozomen says the woman's husband plotted his death. Palladius says only that Evagrius imagined her husband had acted.
  • W. K. Lowther Clarke, who translated Palladius, notes that most of his works survive only in fragments in Greek and Latin, "having been suppressed for their Origenistic tendency," and that the chapter on him is missing from some copies of Palladius.
  • Evagrius Scholasticus names an "Evagrius" with Origen and Didymus in a dispute over Origen's teaching. He does not say which Evagrius. That it is this one is a reading of the passage, not his statement.
  • Which Gregory ordained him deacon. Socrates, Sozomen and Clarke's text of Palladius give Gregory of Nazianzus; the older text of Palladius, which the 1782 Philokalia follows, gives Gregory of Nyssa. Sozomen alone calls him archdeacon, and Palladius alone says Basil of Caesarea made him a reader.
  • Gennadius of Marseilles says he lived to old age. Palladius, who was present at his death, says fifty-four.
  • Cassian lists the same eight faults as Evagrius and never names him. That Cassian took them from Evagrius is a modern view that no held text states.
  • Whether the Fifth Council of 553 itself condemned him is contested. The councils of 680, 692 and 787 say it did, but Karl Joseph von Hefele finds that the names of Evagrius and Didymus do not stand in the surviving acts of that synod.
  • Cyril of Scythopolis and Victor of Tunnuna name an "Evagrius" beside Origen or Didymus; that he is this Evagrius is read from the company he keeps, as with Evagrius Scholasticus.
