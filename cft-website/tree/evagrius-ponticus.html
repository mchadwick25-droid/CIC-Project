The Imperial Church Era · c. 345-399 · Constantinople, Nitria

Constantinople, today in Turkey · Nitria

Evagrius Ponticus

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Desert Monasticism.

Known for
A deacon of Constantinople who became a monk of the Egyptian desert, and wrote of the eight tempting thoughts and of prayer.
Also called
Evagrius of Ibora, Evagrius the Monk (in the Philokalia)
Dates
About 345 to 399 or 400. Palladius of Helenopolis says he died at fifty-four. Ernest Cushing Richardson's note to Gennadius gives 345 to 399; W. K. Lowther Clarke's note to Palladius puts the death in 399 or 400.
Place
Ibora in Pontus; Constantinople; Nitria and Cellia in Egypt
Role
Monk and writer on the ascetic life

About This Person

In Constantinople, a young deacon honoured across the city fell in love with a woman of the highest rank, and she loved him in return. One night, in a vision, soldiers seized the deacon and chained him among forty prisoners. An angel, in the form of a friend, made him swear on the gospel to leave the city. He woke and said, "Even if the oath was in a trance, nevertheless I did take it." He took ship for Jerusalem. He later told the story to his disciple, Palladius of Helenopolis, who wrote it down in praise of him.

The deacon was Evagrius Ponticus, from Ibora in Pontus, the son of a country bishop. Palladius says Basil of Caesarea made him a reader and Gregory of Nazianzus a deacon. At the council of Constantinople in 381, Gregory left him with the new bishop as a skilled debater.

Palladius and Sozomen take him to Jerusalem. Socrates of Constantinople has him go into Egypt with Gregory. In Jerusalem, Palladius says, Melania the Elder took him in. He wavered and fell ill for six months. When Melania asked what was on his mind, he confessed, and she made him promise to live as a monk. He recovered and went to Nitria in Egypt, then to Cellia. He lived there fourteen years on bread and a little oil, earning his food as a copyist.

In his Praktikos, in Luke Dysinger's English, he names eight tempting thoughts, among them gluttony and pride. "The demon of acedia, which is also called the noonday demon, is the most burdensome of all the demons," he writes. It makes the sun seem to stop, "so that the day appears to be fifty hours long." Gennadius of Marseilles, who put him into Latin, says he was the first to mention the eight, "or among the first at least to teach these." Socrates quotes his warning against defining God. "Let then what is inexplicable be adored in silence."

Jerome of Strido, writing in 415, ranked him among the heretics and mocked his teaching on freedom from passion. A mind in that state, Jerome says, "becomes either a stone or a God." Evagrius died at fifty-four, Palladius says, after taking communion at Epiphany. W. K. Lowther Clarke, who translated Palladius, notes that his works were later suppressed for their "Origenistic tendency."

"Whoever shall read these productions," Socrates wrote of his books, "will be convinced of their excellence."

Key Works

  • Praktikos: a hundred short chapters on the ascetic life, the eight tempting thoughts and freedom from passion (held in Luke Dysinger's English)
  • Antirrhetikos: verses of Scripture set against each tempting thought, in eight parts, as Socrates of Constantinople describes it; Gennadius of Marseilles put it into Latin; not held
  • The Gnostic: fifty chapters for the learned, which Socrates quotes; not held

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Gennadius of Marseilles says he was the first to mention the eight principal sins, "or among the first at least to teach these", and translated his work against them into Latin.
  • Palladius of Helenopolis, author of the Lausiac History, was his disciple, as Socrates of Constantinople says.
  • The Philokalia, the Greek collection on prayer compiled by Macarius of Corinth and Nikodemos the Hagiorite, lists works of his in its contents under the name of Evagrius the Monk.

How they're remembered

  • Socrates of Constantinople copied passages from his books and wrote, "Whoever shall read these productions will be convinced of their excellence."
  • Jerome of Strido, writing in 415 against Pelagius, ranked him with the heretics and said his teaching on freedom from passion makes the mind "either a stone or a God."
  • Evagrius Scholasticus, writing in the sixth century, reports that the emperor Justinian condemned Origen, Didymus and Evagrius.

What Is Uncertain

  • His life is known mainly from his disciple Palladius, who writes in praise of him and tells it as he heard it from Evagrius himself.
  • The route from Constantinople differs. Palladius and Sozomen take him to Jerusalem first. Socrates of Constantinople says he went with Gregory of Nazianzus into Egypt.
  • Sozomen says the woman's husband plotted his death. Palladius says only that Evagrius imagined her husband had acted.
  • W. K. Lowther Clarke, who translated Palladius, notes that most of his works survive only in fragments in Greek and Latin, "having been suppressed for their Origenistic tendency," and that the chapter on him is missing from some copies of Palladius.
  • Evagrius Scholasticus names an "Evagrius" with Origen and Didymus in a dispute over Origen's teaching. He does not say which Evagrius. That it is this one is a reading of the passage, not his statement.
