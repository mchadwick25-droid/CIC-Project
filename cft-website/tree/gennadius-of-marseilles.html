About This Person

After the people of Alexandria killed their bishop, Proterius, Timothy Aelurus was made bishop in his place by a single bishop, willingly or not. To defend himself, Gennadius of Marseilles writes, Timothy sent the emperor Leo a very persuasive book. It claimed that Pope Leo, the Council of Chalcedon and all the Western bishops were Nestorians at heart. At the brothers' request, Gennadius put the book into Latin "for learning's sake", and "prefixed a caveat".

Gennadius was a priest of Marseilles, in Gaul, late in the fifth century. Little more is known of his life. He carried on the Lives of Illustrious Men by Jerome, a list of Christian writers. Ernest Cushing Richardson, who translated both into English, judges most of Gennadius's part was written about 480. Gennadius also put Greek works into Latin, among them those of the monk Evagrius, a disciple of Macarius the Egyptian.

Richardson finds in him glimpses of a point of view that he calls semi-Pelagian. Gennadius thinks Prosper of Aquitaine wrote an unsigned book against works of John Cassian. He says Prosper "brands as injurious" works "which the church of God finds salutary". Some editions add a line on Augustine of Hippo. Because he said so much, it runs, Solomon's saying came true of him, "In the multitude of words there wanteth not sin." The line drew much comment on his bias, Richardson says, though it almost certainly does not go back to Gennadius.

"I Gennadius, a presbyter of Marseilles, have written eight books Against all heresies," begins a notice at the end of the work. It also names a letter on his creed sent to Gelasius, bishop of Rome. Richardson thinks this notice is a later hand's. He and Blomfield Jackson, Theodoret's translator in the same volume, give 496 for his death.

Of Faustus of Riez he wrote, "There are still other works by him, but as I have not read, I do not care to mention them."

Key Works

  • Lives of Illustrious Men (De viris illustribus): notices of Christian writers after Jerome, from James of Nisibis to the end of the fifth century, with what each wrote and what Gennadius had read of it
  • Book of Church Dogmas (De ecclesiasticis dogmatibus): a short statement of the faith in numbered chapters, printed among the doubtful works of Augustine; Walafrid Strabo and later writers give it to Gennadius, and other manuscripts to other authors

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Ernest Cushing Richardson calls the joint work of Jerome and Gennadius unique and indispensable for early Christian writing, and of prime value for the years after Eusebius's Church History ends.
  • Later editors still lean on his notices: Theodor Mommsen on the writings of Prosper of Aquitaine, and Blomfield Jackson on the works of Theodoret of Cyrus.

How they're remembered

  • Alcuin cites the Book of Church Dogmas on the Holy Spirit as the work of Gennadius of Marseilles, whom he calls a bishop.
  • The theologians of Louvain, as an unsigned note in Migne's edition of Augustine reports, placed him in the party of the Gauls against whom Prosper and Hilary wrote to Augustine and the second Council of Orange met. They also warned of grave errors in his catalog.

What Is Uncertain

  • His own life is thin in the sources. Little is known of it beyond his work. The notice in the first person that closes the Lives, which names him a presbyter of Marseilles and lists his books, is in few manuscripts, and Ernest Cushing Richardson thinks it is by a later hand.
  • His years. The year 496 for his death is given in a note by Richardson and in Blomfield Jackson's prolegomena to Theodoret, in the same English volume, with no reason given in the lines used here. Theodor Mommsen and the Acta Sanctorum place him at the end of the fifth century.
  • When the Lives were written. Richardson reports that some date them 492 to 495. He holds with Ebert, a historian of Latin Christian writing, that most of the work was written about 480, since it speaks of Timothy Aelurus, who died in 477, as still living. He thinks the last chapters were added within a few years, by Gennadius or more probably by two other hands.
  • His view on grace. Richardson finds in him glimpses of a point of view he calls semi-Pelagian. Some editions add a line saying that Augustine's many words bore out Solomon's saying on sin; Richardson says it drew much comment on his bias and almost certainly is not what Gennadius wrote.
  • The Timothy translation. The sentence saying he translated Timothy Aelurus's book and set a caveat before it is missing from Migne's and Herding's editions, Richardson notes.
  • The Book of Church Dogmas. Walafrid Strabo names Gennadius, priest of Marseilles, as its author. The unsigned note in Migne's Augustine reports that Ratramnus of Corbie gave it to a Gennadius of Constantinople, and that one manuscript gives it to a Faustus.
  • Priest or bishop. His own notice calls him a presbyter. Alcuin and some manuscript titles call him bishop of Marseilles; the note in Migne's Augustine says Platina called him bishop wrongly.
  • His name. He is a different man from Gennadius, patriarch of Constantinople, whose notice stands in the same Lives, and from the much later Gennadios Scholarios.
