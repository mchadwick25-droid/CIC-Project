John Moschus

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Chalcedonian Monasticism - Judean Desert & Gaza.

Known for
Monk of Palestine who traveled with his disciple Sophronius, later patriarch of Jerusalem, and gathered the sayings and deeds of monks of his time, seen on their travels or told to them, into the Spiritual Meadow, which he dedicated to him.
Also called
Joannes Moschus, Joannes Eucrata, Joannes Eviratus
Dates
No ancient line used here gives a year of his life. The old notice set before the Meadow ties his travels to the reigns of the emperors Tiberius II and Maurice. Daniel Papebroch reckons the return of his body to Palestine in 620, and Johann Albert Fabricius reports 620 for his death from John Bolland. Aloysius Lipomanus says the time is not known for certain and guesses the book was written about 600
Place
The monastery of Saint Theodosius; the desert by the Jordan; the New Laura of Saint Sabas; travels to Antioch, Alexandria and Rome, where he died
Role
Priest and monk, as the old notice calls him; gatherer of monks' stories

About This Person

One noon in Alexandria, John Moschus and his friend Sophronius, later patriarch of Jerusalem, went to study with the philosopher Stephen. He was asleep, so they waited at the Tetrapylon, where three blind men told how each had lost his sight. The third had once stripped a richly dressed corpse in its tomb. The dead man sat up, he said, and tore out his eyes. Sophronius nodded, and they left. "Truly, Abba John, let us study no more today," he said, in modern words. "We have been edified enough."

Moschus, a monk of Palestine, tells this in the Spiritual Meadow, his book of stories about monks of his time, written for Sophronius.

An old notice of his life was set before the Meadow. Daniel Papebroch, a Bollandist scholar, says Photius of Constantinople copied much of it. It says Moschus became a monk at the monastery of Saint Theodosius. He lived long in the desert by the Jordan, then at the New Laura of Saint Sabas. After the emperor Maurice was murdered and the Persians pressed in, he went to Antioch and then Alexandria. Hearing that the holy places had been taken, he sailed for Rome with Sophronius.

There, the notice says, he saw his death coming, wrote the Meadow and gave it to Sophronius. He asked to be buried on Mount Sinai, or if barbarians barred the way, at Saint Theodosius. Raiders barred the road to Sinai, so Sophronius buried him at Saint Theodosius. Papebroch reckons that year as 620.

Johann Albert Fabricius says the Meadow often attacks the followers of Severus of Antioch, who rejected the Council of Chalcedon. It has its share of fabulous tales, he adds. John of Damascus and the Second Council of Nicaea, in 787, cited it as Sophronius's book.

In the dedication, Moschus says he picked the fairest flowers from these lives and wove a crown from a meadow that never fades. "I offer it to you, and through you to all," he writes, in modern words.

Key Works

  • The Spiritual Meadow (Pratum spirituale; in Greek, Leimon): short chapters of the sayings and deeds of monks, many told in the first person, often with Sophronius at his side. Papebroch counts 219 chapters in his day; Photius of Constantinople, as Papebroch quotes him, found up to 342 in some copies

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The Second Council of Nicaea, in 787, heard a monk read out a whole chapter of the Meadow under Sophronius's name. In it a demon tells a hermit on the Mount of Olives to stop venerating his icon of Mary holding Christ, and the abbot Theodore, then at the Laura of Pharan, tells him it would be better to enter every brothel in the city than refuse to venerate Christ and his mother.
  • John the Deacon, writing the Life of Gregory the Great, put one of its stories, told by Abba John the Persian, into Latin, as Aloysius Lipomanus notes.
  • Daniel Papebroch thinks the book first spread under Sophronius's name because Sophronius carried it from Rome to Palestine with his master's body and made it known.

How they're remembered

  • John of Damascus quoted it in his defense of icons as the work of Sophronius.
  • Johann Albert Fabricius calls it a very well-known book, not without fabulous tales.
  • Aloysius Lipomanus, bishop of Verona, who printed it in Latin when it had been printed only in Italian, wrote that it seemed to have sprung from the delights of Paradise.

What Is Uncertain

  • His own life is thin in the sources. It rests on the old notice set before the Meadow, which Daniel Papebroch prints in Latin and says Photius partly copied, and on what the Meadow itself tells. The notice gives no author or date. Photius's own notice of the book is not among the sources used here and is known through Papebroch and Lipomanus.
  • Whose book. John of Damascus, the Second Council of Nicaea and Nicephorus Callistus, as Fabricius quotes him, give it to Sophronius. Lipomanus answers that John wrote it, and that it carries Sophronius's name because it is addressed to him, because he walks through it, or because he may have helped to write it. Papebroch adds that Sophronius first made it known.
  • His years. Papebroch reckons from the notice that Moschus was sent to Egypt early in the reign of Tiberius, which he dates to the end of 579, and left Palestine after Maurice's murder, about the end of 602. He puts the arrival in Rome at the end of Pope Deusdedit's time or the start of Boniface's, and the return of the body to Palestine in 620. Fabricius found 620 for his death in John Bolland.
  • The disciple. The notice says the disciple who buried him spent the rest of his life at Saint Theodosius. Daniel Papebroch, who holds with almost all Greek tradition that the disciple was Sophronius, later patriarch of Jerusalem, reads that as most of the rest of his life.
  • Where he wrote. The notice says he wrote down what he saw and heard as he traveled, and at Rome too. Papebroch thinks the book was finished at Rome.
  • His names. The old notice calls him John, priest and monk, surnamed Moschus. Migne's heading for the Greek title calls him John Eucrata, and Lipomanus's Latin calls him John Eviratus. Lipomanus says Photius gives the name Moschus, and thinks the Latin translator may have turned his surname into Eviratus.
  • No English translation of the Meadow is among the sources used here. The brief tells it from the Latin version printed beside the Greek.
