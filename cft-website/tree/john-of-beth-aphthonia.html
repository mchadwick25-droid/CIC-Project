John of Beth Aphthonia

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Syriac Orthodox (West Syriac) Christianity.

Known for
Writer of a Life of Severus of Antioch, written in Greek at the request of Dometios and kept in a Syriac translation, whose heading calls him John, superior of the monastery of Beith-Aphthonia. M.-A. Kugener keeps him apart from the archimandrite John bar Aphthonia of Kenneshre, while J.-B. Chabot, R. Duval and E. W. Brooks give the Life to the archimandrite.
Also called
John of Beth-Aphthonia
Dates
No source used here gives the years of the writer of the Life. The archimandrite John bar Aphthonia died on 4 November, in a Syriac chronicle extract that M.-A. Kugener prints in the same entry as Severus's death on 8 February of the year 849 of the Greeks, which E. W. Brooks gives as 538; Elias of Nisibis puts bar Aphthonia's death in the next year of that count. The chronicle under Zachariah's name dates the expulsion that brought his monks to Kenneshre from the year three to the year nine, which its translators read as 525 to 531
Place
Beith-Aphthonia, by the heading of the Life; Peter the Iberian's monastery near Gaza, where the writer says he knew John the Canopite. The archimandrite John bar Aphthonia was a native of Edessa and settled the monks of Saint Thomas of Seleucia at Kenneshre on the Euphrates, a house Michael the Syrian calls the monastery of Bar Aphtonia
Role
Monastic superior; biographer of Severus of Antioch

About This Person

A man named Dometios asked for a Life of Severus of Antioch, since the wise and eloquent had left this world. The writer protested, in words told here in modern English. Asking him was like hiring a worker in lead to make a royal crown. He wrote it all the same, in Greek, and it survives in a Syriac translation, which M.-A. Kugener put into French.

The heading names the writer as John, superior of the monastery of Beith Aphthonia. He calls himself small and not learned in letters.

Who this John was is disputed. One manuscript, which holds a short extract, gives the Life to the archimandrite John bar Aphthonia, and Kugener calls that false. The Life itself tells of John bar Aphthonia's death as another man's. J.-B. Chabot and Rubens Duval write of the Life as John bar Aphthonia's, and E. W. Brooks names him its author.

John bar Aphthonia was a learned man of Edessa, the Syriac chronicle under Zachariah's name says. When the monks of Saint Thomas at Seleucia were driven from their house, he settled them at Kenneshre on the Euphrates. A hymn heading calls him its first abbot. Michael the Syrian calls this house the monastery of Bar Aphthonia. John bar Aphthonia sat with the bishops who refused the Council of Chalcedon at debates in Constantinople. He wrote a record of them, which the chronicles mention but do not give. In hymns he wrote that Severus fought with his hand as well as his tongue, shooting arrows of ink and pen.

Near death, the Life says, Severus wrote a long letter in his own hand to the archimandrite John bar Aphthonia. Through him it went to all the monks of the East. John lived fifteen days after it.

Michael the Syrian says Jacob of Edessa later became a monk at the monastery of Aphthonia and studied the Psalms there in Greek. Bar Hebraeus calls it the Kenneshre monastery.

Key Works

  • Life of Severus: written in Greek for Dometios, who later became a bishop, and translated into Syriac by the abbot Mar Sergios; it tells Severus's life from his birth at Sozopolis to his last letter and his death, and the writer says he has written no eulogy. Kugener edits it from a Berlin manuscript he dates probably to the ninth century and a London one that William Wright dates to the eleventh
  • Hymns on Severus, by John bar Aphthonia: three of the four hymns on Severus in Severus's own hymn book, the Octoechus, which a heading gives to John bar Aphthonia, the first abbot of Kenneshre; they survive in the abbot Paul's Syriac version as revised by Jacob of Edessa, in a manuscript of 675
  • Record of the debates at Constantinople, by John bar Aphthonia: his written account of the talks that the bishops who refused Chalcedon held in the capital for more than a year, at the emperor's command, which the chronicle under Zachariah's name and Michael the Syrian say he wrote; no copy is among the sources used here

Legacy

What traces to them

  • A pottery fragment from the Monastery of Epiphanius at Thebes, in Egypt, holds a passage from a Life of Apa Severus, which W. E. Crum identifies as John of Beth-Aphthonia's. Crum says another extract, Rylands 99, shows the work was familiar in Egypt, and notes a further fragment in the Munich Library.
  • The Syriac chronicle under the name of Dionysius of Tell-Mahre and Michael the Syrian copied passages of the Life into their chronicles, Kugener says.
  • John bar Aphthonia's house at Kenneshre became a place of Greek learning. Bar Hebraeus says Thomas of Harkel was taught Greek as a boy in the Kenneshre monastery, and Michael the Syrian and Bar Hebraeus say Jacob of Edessa studied Greek at the monastery of Aphthonia, which Bar Hebraeus also calls the Kenneshre monastery.

How they're remembered

  • Severus of Antioch, in a letter to Andrew the reader and notary, answers a question about "the letter addressed to father John the son of Aphthonia".
  • A line in a Syriac chronicle extract names John Bar Aphthonia among the famous men of his time; Kugener takes it for a note later slipped into the text.
  • Elias of Nisibis, in his chronicle, calls John son of Aphthonia a teacher of the Jacobites.

What Is Uncertain

  • Which John wrote the Life. The heading names John, superior of the monastery of Beith-Aphthonia, and the writer calls himself small and not learned in letters. One manuscript of about the eleventh century, which holds a short extract, gives the Life to John bar Aphthonia, and Kugener calls that attribution false. The Life tells of the death of the archimandrite John bar Aphthonia as another man's. J. S. Assemani, in his Bibliotheca Orientalis, names two Johns as abbots of the Kenneshre house. J.-B. Chabot and R. Duval say a fragment in Michael the Syrian probably comes from the Life written by John bar Aphthonia, and E. W. Brooks calls the writer John, archimandrite of Kenneshre. Kugener promised an introduction on these questions, and it is not among the sources used here.
  • Beth Aphthonia and Kenneshre. That the writer's Beith-Aphthonia is the Kenneshre house rests on Michael the Syrian's name for it, the monastery of Bar Aphthonia, called of Kenneshre, and on E. W. Brooks's note on John of Ephesus, who names two bishops from the monastery of Aphthonia, Dimet made metropolitan of Laodicea and Sergius made bishop in Charrhae. The heading of the Life gives no place for Beith-Aphthonia.
  • When the archimandrite died. The chronicle extract Kugener prints gives 4 November, in the entry for Severus's death in the year 849 of the Greeks, which Brooks gives as 538. Michael the Syrian says he died in the same year as John son of Cyriacus. Elias of Nisibis puts his death in the next year of that count, the year after Severus's. The Life puts it fifteen days after Severus's last letter, before Severus died.
  • His earlier work. The chronicle under Zachariah's name calls the archimandrite "an ex-pleader (?)", and its translators doubt the word suits the passage. Michael the Syrian calls him John the Rhetor.
  • Dometios. The Life says he later became a bishop, and no source used here gives his see.
  • The translator. The closing note of the Life names the abbot Mar Sergios, son of Charia, and Kugener marks the second name with a query.
