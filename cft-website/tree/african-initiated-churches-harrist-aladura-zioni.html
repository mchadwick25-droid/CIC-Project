About This Tradition

Audio Brief 2:07

Read along

From the 1880s onward Africans began founding churches of their own rather than staying inside the missions. Nehemiah Tile left the Methodists to form the Thembu National Church in the 1880s, and in November 1892 Mangena Mokone founded the Ethiopian Church in South Africa. Later waves went further. William Wadé Harris walked through the Ivory Coast and the Gold Coast between 1913 and 1915 preaching and baptizing, and the churches that followed him bear his name. In Nigeria the Aladura churches grew from the 1920s and 1930s — the Cherubim and Seraphim, founded in 1925 by Moses Orimolade and Christianah Abiodun; the Christ Apostolic Church around Joseph Babalola's revival of 1930; the Celestial Church of Christ from 1947. In the Congo, Simon Kimbangu's ministry began on 18 March 1921 and ended within months before a colonial military court. In 1972 Martial Sinda studied Kimbanguism beside Matsouanism, the movement around André Matswa in Congo-Brazzaville. In South Africa, Engenas Lekganyane founded the Zion Christian Church in 1924. In Uganda, Reuben Spartas Mukasa broke with the Anglicans in January 1929. In 1946 he visited Alexandria and was made the patriarch's trustee, and from then his church was linked to the Greek Orthodox Patriarchate there. In Zimbabwe, the apostolics of Johane Masowe set aside the Bible as a written text. Matthew Engelke studied them in 2007. In Northern Rhodesia, now Zambia, Alice Lenshina's Lumpa Church ended in conflict and a ban in 1964. Each of these carried its founder's legacy forward differently, which is why this map takes them one at a time.

Documented Stories

Kimbangu before the Military Court

Simon Kimbangu was a catechist of the Baptist Missionary Society at Nkamba in the Belgian Congo, and on 6 April 1921, according to the movement's own account, he healed a sick woman in the name of Christ after years of resisting a call heard in dreams. Within weeks Nkamba was full, and Kimbangu was preaching, healing, and condemning fetishes; workers left the plantations to hear him, and the Baptist missionaries, uneasy, did not disown him. The administration saw a mass movement of Africans under an African and a threat to labor, and in June sent troops to arrest him; he escaped, and on 12 September 1921 walked back into Nkamba and gave himself up. He was tried at Thysville on 3 October before a conseil de guerre, a military court, on charges of sedition and hostility to whites, without counsel, in a single day; the judgment sentenced him to a hundred and twenty lashes and death. Missionaries protested, and King Albert commuted the sentence to life imprisonment. Kimbangu was held in prison at Elisabethville, two thousand kilometers from home, for thirty years, until his death on 12 October 1951, never allowed to see his church; his wife Muilu Marie led the movement in secret, his followers were deported by the tens of thousands to camps across the colony, and the church they built, recognized only in 1959, grew into one of the largest in Africa. The trial record is among the church's founding documents, though the state wrote it.

Christianah Abiodun’s Trance, and What Became of Her

Christianah Abiodun Akinsowon was the fifteen-year-old daughter of an Anglican family in Lagos when, on 18 June 1925, watching the Corpus Christi procession, she fell into a trance in which, by her account, she was taken to heaven and shown things she was to tell; she lay unconscious for days and her family sent for Moses Orimolade Tunolase, a lame itinerant Yoruba preacher known for prayer and healing. He prayed and she revived, and the two of them began to hold prayer meetings that drew Christians who wanted what the mission churches did not offer: visions, healing, prophecy, and prayer against witchcraft. In September 1925 the society took the name Cherubim and Seraphim, given, Abiodun said, in a vision. It grew fast, on her visions and his prayer, and within four years it had branches across Yorubaland and the white garments and processions that mark the Aladura churches. Then it divided. In 1929 Abiodun, now nineteen and a leader in her own right, and Orimolade, old and ill, fell out over authority; the elders sided with Orimolade, and Abiodun's followers were excluded and formed their own society, the Cherubim and Seraphim Society, under her, while Orimolade's became the Eternal Sacred Order. He died in 1933. She led her branch for sixty-five years, was called Captain Abiodun, and died in 1994; the movement's histories name Orimolade as founder and her as co-founder, in that order, and her own account, and Peel's study, show that the church began in her trance.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Many of these churches began when African Christians left the mission churches. Scholars still debate how much they owed to Pentecostal teaching. African founders led them, and they were fully the work of neither.

Descended from

  • grew out of Nineteenth-Century Methodism Dominant Modern Reconstruction Major — Nehemiah Tile worked for the Methodist mission among the Thembu people from the early 1870s. He broke with his mission leaders. In 1884 he founded the Thembu National Church. Historians call it the first church in South Africa that Africans ran on their own.
  • grew out of The Anglican Communion in the Twentieth Century Dominant Modern Reconstruction Major — Reuben Spartas Mukasa grew up in the Anglican church of Uganda. Later he turned to the African Orthodox Church. In 1946 he went to Alexandria, where the patriarch made him trustee of the church in Uganda.

Direct descendants

  • gave rise to Global South Pentecostal Christianity Widely Accepted Major — Josiah Akindayomi joined the Cherubim and Seraphim, one of Nigeria's prayer churches, in 1931. So says the church he went on to found. He left in 1952 and began the Redeemed Christian Church of God. Allan Anderson puts a second Nigerian church halfway between the prayer churches and the Pentecostals.
  • gave rise to African Diaspora & Reverse-Mission Churches in Europe Dominant Modern Reconstruction Major — Emmanuel Adejobi led a Nigerian prayer church, the Church of the Lord. He opened its first branch outside Africa in London. A study of his life dates it to 12 April 1964. It calls it the first West African church in Europe.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

William Wadé Harris walked through the Ivory Coast and the Gold Coast between 1913 and 1915, preaching and baptizing, and the churches that trace to him took his name. Others rose on their own ground: Simon Kimbangu's ministry in Congo began in March 1921 and ended within months in a colonial court, while Engenas Lekganyane founded the Zion Christian Church in South Africa in 1924. Each carried its founder's legacy forward differently, from Nigeria's Aladura congregations to the ZCC's annual pilgrimage at Moria.

Legacy

By 2010 these were established churches, several of them very large. The Zion Christian Church's pilgrimage to Moria was central to its life, and the Kimbanguist church was the first African-initiated church admitted to the World Council of Churches, in 1969. Their congregations traveled with African migration and were planted across Europe, a chapter this map tells in a separate entry. They also changed the scholarship: Bengt Sundkler's Bantu Prophets in South Africa (1948) and J. D. Y. Peel's Aladura (1968) made these churches a standard subject rather than a footnote to mission history.

Sources

On the Nicene Creed

Each branch is assessed on its own terms rather than as one group. The Harrist and Aladura mainstreams are creedally conventional by their own church constitutions. The Kimbanguist church's own teaching shifted over time and is described here by its dated texts. A moderate official doctrine was stated in 2005. A 2008 assembly revoked it in favor of a different teaching. That teaching held that Kimbangu himself was the Holy Spirit incarnate, and his three sons incarnations of the Trinity. In 1969 it had been the first African-initiated church admitted to the World Council of Churches. The Zion Christian Church published little about its own beliefs, by its own choice. No confession of its own is on record to cite, so this stays an open question rather than a settled one. Everything here describes a dated text, never the private faith of any member.
