Latin American Base Communities

Brazil and Central America, 1960s to 1990s — small communities of the poor who read and discussed scripture together and published their own commentary, as at Solentiname, growing up alongside the Pentecostal revival reaching the same poor.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:15

Read along

From the 1960s, in Brazil and then across Latin America, small groups of poor Catholics began meeting to read the Bible together, often led by a lay coordinator or a catechist, and usually reading the gospel against their own week. The bishops gave the practice their backing at Medellín in 1968, a meeting told in the entry on Latin American Catholicism. Ernesto Cardenal published four volumes of the conversations his peasant neighbors at Solentiname had about the Sunday gospel, beginning in 1975 — one of the few records anywhere of poor people doing theology in their own words, printed as they said them. Gustavo Gutiérrez had named the theology in 1971. Many of these communities' people were killed: Rutilio Grande in 1977, Óscar Romero at the altar in March 1980, four American churchwomen later that year, six Jesuits of the UCA in 1989. Rome's doctrinal office issued two instructions on liberation theology, in 1984 and 1986.

Documented Stories

The End of Solentiname

Ernesto Cardenal, a poet and priest formed under Thomas Merton, had founded in 1966 on an island in the Solentiname archipelago of Lake Nicaragua a contemplative community that became a parish of fishermen and peasant families who met on Sundays to read the gospel and talk about it, without a sermon; their talk, recorded and published from 1975 as El Evangelio en Solentiname, became one of the few places where the poor of Latin America could be read doing theology in their own words. They read the gospel against Somoza. The community had painters, a library and a school, and by 1977 it had young men who had concluded that the kingdom the gospel talked about required the dictatorship's end, and who had joined the Sandinista Front with Cardenal's knowledge and, in the end, his blessing. On 13 October 1977 a column that included Solentiname's youth attacked the National Guard barracks at San Carlos across the lake; the attack failed and several of the island's young men were killed or captured. The Guard came to Solentiname within days. The houses, school, library and church were burned or looted, the community dispersed; Cardenal, in Costa Rica, was charged in absentia. He returned in 1979 as the revolution's minister of culture, was rebuked by John Paul II on the tarmac at Managua in 1983, and suspended from the priesthood in 1984. The island's church was rebuilt; the community, as it had been, was not.

The Road to El Paisnal

Rutilio Grande, a Jesuit born in the village of El Paisnal, had since 1972 led a pastoral team in the sugar-cane parish of Aguilares, north of San Salvador, that trained peasant catechists, formed base communities, and preached that the campesinos were children of God with a right to the land they worked and to organize. In February 1977, at Apopa, he preached that a foreigner who came to El Salvador preaching the gospel would be expelled, and that the Bible itself would be stopped at the border as subversive. On the afternoon of 12 March 1977 he drove from Aguilares toward El Paisnal to say the evening Mass, with Manuel Solórzano, seventy-two, Nelson Rutilio Lemus, sixteen, and three children in the jeep; in the cane fields gunmen fired from both sides, and Grande, Solórzano and Lemus were killed; the children were spared. Óscar Romero, installed as archbishop three weeks before as the safe candidate, drove to Aguilares that night and stood over the bodies in the church. Over the following days he announced that on Sunday 20 March there would be a single Mass in the archdiocese, in the cathedral square, and no other; a hundred thousand came, and he preached that the church would not be silent. He never attended another government function.

In Its Own Record

Pentecostal churches grew fast among the same poor communities in Latin America at the same time as the base communities, a pairing worth noting side by side. The base communities themselves contracted in the 1990s. But the pastoral practice they pioneered, and the communities' own heirs, continued inside Latin American church life. The bishops' meetings at Medellín in 1968 and Puebla in 1979 are told in the entry on Latin American Catholicism, the parent of this one.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

From the 1960s, poor Catholics across Latin America began meeting in small groups to read the Bible against their own week, a practice the bishops backed at Medellín in 1968. At Solentiname in Nicaragua, Ernesto Cardenal published four volumes of what his peasant neighbors actually said about the Sunday gospel, one of the few places anywhere poor people's theology survives in their own words. Many of these communities' people were killed for it, among them Archbishop Óscar Romero, shot at the altar in March 1980.

Legacy

The base communities contracted in the 1990s, and the movement was no longer what it had been. But the tradition lived on. Its pastoral practice, its small reading groups and the communities' own heirs continued inside Latin American church life. The Solentiname volumes went into several languages. Pentecostal churches grew fast among exactly the same poor at exactly the same time, and any account of one that leaves out the other is not describing what happened.
