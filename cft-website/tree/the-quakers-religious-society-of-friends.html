The Quakers (Religious Society of Friends)

England and the Atlantic world, 1650s to 1815 — a movement that sat in silence waiting for God to speak, refused to swear an oath or take off its hat to any man, and went to prison for both.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:07

Read along

In the turmoil of the English Civil War years, a shoemaker's apprentice named George Fox began telling anyone who would listen that Christ could teach his people himself. That every person carried an inward light, that no paid clergy or consecrated building was needed, and that a meeting could sit in silence until someone was moved to speak. Friends refused oaths, hat-honor and titles, refused to fight, and let women preach on the same footing as men. Thousands of them were fined, jailed and transported for it, and they kept a running written record of their own sufferings as they went. By the eighteenth century the movement had settled into a quieter, disciplined life of meetings and plain dress, and turned outward. The New Jersey tailor John Woolman spent decades traveling among Friends, pressing them to free the people they held as slaves, until the American yearly meetings made slaveholding a bar to membership.

Documented Stories

James Nayler Rides into Bristol

James Nayler, a Yorkshire farmer who had served in the parliamentary army, was by 1656 the best-known Quaker preacher in London and, to many, the movement's real leader. On 24 October 1656 he rode into Bristol in pouring rain on a horse led by a man with his head bare, while a small group of women, Martha Simmonds and Hannah Stranger among them, walked before him through the mud singing ‘Holy, holy, holy, Lord God of Sabaoth’ and spreading garments in the way. He was arrested at once. Parliament took the case itself and debated it for ten days in December; the motion to put him to death failed by 96 votes to 82. The sentence was that he be pilloried and whipped through the streets of London, 310 lashes, his tongue bored through with a hot iron and his forehead branded B for blasphemer, and then carried to Bristol to ride into the city again facing his horse's tail and be whipped there, and be kept in solitary confinement at Bridewell at hard labor. He was released in 1659, broken in health. Fox, who had shunned him, received him coldly; Nayler knelt to him. In October 1660, robbed and beaten on the road home to Yorkshire, he died at a Friend's house near King's Ripton, and his last words were written down: ‘There is a spirit which I feel, that delights to do no evil, nor to revenge any wrong.’

Germantown, 1688: A Protest the Meetings Set Aside

On 18 April 1688 four men of the Germantown meeting near Philadelphia, Francis Daniel Pastorius, Garret Hendericks and the brothers Derick and Abraham op den Graeff, signed a paper addressed to the monthly meeting at Dublin, later Abington. It is written in Pastorius's English and it argues from the Golden Rule: that the Friends who had fled Europe for liberty of conscience were now holding men and women in bondage against their will; that there was no more liberty to make slaves of Africans than of Europeans, ‘though they are black’; that the trade separated husbands from wives and children from parents, which was adultery in all but name; that Friends who would not fight would nevertheless have to fight if their slaves rose; and that people in Europe hearing that Quakers kept slaves would think the worse of the Quakers. The monthly meeting found the matter ‘so weighty’ that it sent it to the quarterly meeting at Philadelphia, which found it ‘of too great a weight’ and sent it to the yearly meeting at Burlington in September, which minuted that it was not proper for the meeting ‘to give a positive judgment in the case, it having so general a relation to many other parts’, and did nothing. Philadelphia Yearly Meeting first advised against buying slaves in 1696, forbade the trade to members in 1758, and made slaveholding a ground for disownment in 1776, eighty-eight years after Germantown.

The Bill of Sale John Woolman Wrote, and the One He Refused

John Woolman was about twenty-two and keeping a shop for a merchant at Mount Holly, New Jersey, when, as he tells it in his Journal, his employer sold a Black woman to an elderly Friend and told him to write the bill of sale. ‘The thing was sudden,’ he wrote; he was employed and the buyer was a member of the Society, and he wrote it, but ‘I said before my master and the Friend that I believed slave-keeping to be a practice inconsistent with the Christian religion.’ It weighed on him. Not long afterwards a young man of the meeting asked him to write a will conveying a slave to his children, and Woolman declined, told him why, and the young man went away and, Woolman says, later freed her. From these two small transactions came thirty years of work: journeys on foot through the slaveholding meetings of Maryland, Virginia and North Carolina from 1746, paying the enslaved people who waited on him in Friends' houses and refusing hospitality bought with their labor; the essay Some Considerations on the Keeping of Negroes, which Philadelphia Yearly Meeting printed in 1754; and the patient visiting of slaveholding Friends one by one, sitting with them, as he put it, in the love of Christ. He wore undyed clothes because dyes were made by slaves, and died of smallpox at York in England in 1772, visiting the meetings there. The Journal was published two years later.

Connections

In Its Own Record

The Quakers grew out of the most radical wing of the Puritan upheaval in England. Many of the first Friends came from Independent and Baptist churches. Later, their campaigning did much to end slavery.

Descended from

  • grew out of Puritanism Documented Major — George Fox's Journal says James Nayler was put out of an Independent church when he took up Fox's message. Fox also won many from 'a company of shattered Baptists' in Nottinghamshire. William Penn told the Quakers' rise as the next step after the Puritan groups of the English Reformation.

Direct descendants

  • continued as Nineteenth-Century Quakerism — the Separations Widely Accepted Major — The London Yearly Meeting of the Quakers sent a letter to its local meetings almost every year from 1675. In 1805 it was still weighing 'the present state of our religious society'. The same body came into the nineteenth century whole, and then split.
  • gave rise to Shakers Documented Major — The Shakers' confession of 1810 says James and Jane Wardley had belonged to the Quakers and then separated from them. Their society began near Manchester about 1747 and later took Ann Lee as its Mother. The source is the Shakers' own account, written sixty years on.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Quakers built their worship around silence, waiting until someone felt moved by the Spirit to speak, and refused any oath or hat-honor to social superiors — convictions that filled English jails with them in the seventeenth century. Women preached on the same footing as men from the movement's earliest days, and it was a Quaker tailor, John Woolman, who spent decades traveling from meeting to meeting until Friends in America made owning slaves grounds for exclusion.

Legacy

The Religious Society of Friends is a living body on both sides of the Atlantic, though it divided in the nineteenth century into unprogrammed, evangelical and pastoral branches that persist to this day. Its wider mark is out of all proportion to its size. Quaker campaigning was central to abolition in Britain and America, Quaker refusal to swear or to fight helped push conscience into English law, and Pennsylvania began as a Quaker experiment in governing without an established church. The plain meeting house, the silent meeting and the practice of testing a decision until the whole gathering can consent are all still recognizably theirs.

Experience It Today

  • Swarthmoor Hall, Ulverston, Cumbria — Margaret Fell's house, the early movement's headquarters, open to visitors with its gardens and café

Doctrinal Floor Note

A carefully-stated case rather than a simple one. The earliest Friends affirm Christ's divinity in plain terms, but they reject the practice of signing up to a creed at all — which is an argument about the form of confession rather than a disagreement about its content. Later Quakerism spread out considerably, so the answer depends on which century and which body of Friends is being asked about.
