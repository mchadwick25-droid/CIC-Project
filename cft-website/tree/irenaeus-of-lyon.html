Irenaeus of Lyon

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Post-Apostolic Household-Church Christianity.

Also called Saint Irenaeus, Irenæus

Bishop of Lyon in Roman Gaul who had heard Polycarp of Smyrna as a boy. About 180 he wrote five books Against Heresies, the fullest answer we still have to the followers of Valentinus. Against their claim to a secret teaching he set three open things: the faith received at baptism, the churches the apostles founded, with their bishops counted one after another, and four Gospels.

About This Person

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 5:02

Read along

"It is as if a skilled artist had carefully made a fine portrait of a king from choice small stones. Then someone broke up the man's likeness. He moved those stones, fitted them together again, and made the shape of a dog or a fox, and a poor one at that."

The portrait is a mosaic, a picture made of small colored stones, and he insists the fox is still the king. That, wrote Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon in Roman Gaul, today's France, is what the followers of Valentinus did with Scripture. Valentinus had taught at Rome a generation earlier, and his followers claimed, Irenaeus says, that their secret teaching went deeper than the apostles'.

As a boy Irenaeus had heard the old bishop Polycarp of Smyrna, and he became bishop himself after a persecution in 177. His five books against these teachers, written about 180, are the fullest answer we still have. Against their secret teaching he set three open things: the faith received at baptism, the churches the apostles founded, and four Gospels.

In the year 177 a persecution struck the Christians of Lyon and Vienne, on the river Rhône. Their bishop, Pothinus, more than ninety years old, the survivors wrote, was beaten and died in prison. From prison they asked Irenaeus, one of the elders who led the church with its bishop, to carry their letters to Rome. Others were put to death, and their burned remains were swept into the Rhône, so that, the persecutors said, they could not hope to rise again.

Irenaeus became bishop after Pothinus, Eusebius says, and most of what we know of his life comes from that historian, writing more than a century later. The new bishop read his opponents' books. They taught, he says, that the world's maker was a lesser power below a perfect, hidden God. He called their system madness and blasphemy against Christ, and likened them to wolves in sheep's clothing.

His first answer was the faith received from the apostles, in one God the maker, in Christ Jesus who became flesh, suffered and rose, and in the Holy Spirit. A Christian received this short summary at baptism, he says, as a rule to measure by. "The church has received this preaching and this faith, as I said. Though it is scattered through the whole world, it guards them with care, as if it lived in one house. It believes them alike, as if it had one soul and one heart. It preaches them, teaches them and hands them on in harmony, as if it had one mouth." From Germany and Spain to Egypt and Libya, he says, the churches hand on no other.

His second answer anyone could check. If the apostles had secret teachings, he argued, they would have given them to the men they left in charge of the churches. Those bishops could be counted, one after another, to his own day. He counted Rome's, down to the bishop of his own time, in "the twelfth place from the apostles". Historians still argue whether Rome then had a single bishop.

His third answer was four Gospels, by Matthew, Mark, Luke and John, for as there are four winds, he says, the church has four pillars. The followers of Valentinus claimed more, and Marcion fewer.

Florinus, a man Irenaeus had seen as a boy at Polycarp's side, had become an elder at Rome. He seemed, Eusebius says, to teach that God was the author of evil, and he was being drawn toward Valentinus. Irenaeus wrote to him that the elders before them, who had known the apostles, never handed on such teachings. "I remember that time more clearly than recent years. For what boys learn grows with the mind and becomes one with it. So I can describe the very place where the blessed Polycarp sat as he taught." He could describe, he wrote, how Polycarp came and went, how he lived and looked, and his talks to the people. "And I can tell how he spoke of his time with John and the others who had seen the Lord."

Later writers drew on his book for generations: Hippolytus called him "the blessed elder", and Tertullian "a very exact inquirer". About two centuries on, Epiphanius copied his opening account of the Valentinians word for word, and that copy is how much of its Greek survives. For eighteen centuries the Valentinians were known almost only through opponents such as him, until books of their own were found in Egypt in 1945.

A Christian who holds the rule received at baptism, Irenaeus wrote, will know the stones of the broken mosaic, but will not take the fox for the king. He puts each one back in its place, and the false picture falls apart.

Quotations are given in our modern English. The printed wording of each is below.

Timeline

Dates
He gives no year of his own. Eusebius dates the persecution at Lyon to the seventeenth year of the emperor Antoninus Verus, which Arthur McGiffert reckons as 177, under Marcus Aurelius. Eusebius's Chronicle places it after 167, grouped with Polycarp's martyrdom, and McGiffert holds that the grouping does not date it. He became bishop after Pothinus, and McGiffert thinks not at once but not long after. He wrote the third book of Against Heresies while Eleutherius was bishop of Rome, between 174 and 189 in McGiffert's dates; about 180 is the usual date. His last trace is the quarrel over Easter under Victor of Rome, toward the close of the second century
Place
As a boy, lower Asia, the Roman province of Asia, where Polycarp was bishop of Smyrna; then Lyon, in Roman Gaul, on the Rhone, today in France. He says he lived among the Celts
Role
Elder and then bishop of the church at Lyon; writer against teachings he held false

Key Works

  • Against Heresies: five books, by Eusebius's title the Refutation and Overthrow of the Knowledge Falsely So-called, against the followers of Valentinus and related teachers. By Photius's summary, the first sets out Valentinus's teaching and others' from Simon Magus to Tatian, the second refutes them, the third brings the testimony of Scripture, the fourth answers their difficulties, and the fifth draws on the Lord's parables and the apostles' letters. The third also counts the bishops of the apostles' churches and argues for four Gospels. It survives whole only in an old Latin translation; Epiphanius kept most of the first book in Greek
  • Letter to Florinus, On Monarchy, or That God is not the Author of Evil: to an elder at Rome whom he had seen as a boy at Polycarp's side, and who seemed to teach that God was the author of evil. It survives only in the passages Eusebius quotes, among them his memory of the place where Polycarp sat and taught
  • Letter to Victor of Rome on the Easter quarrel: written in the name of the Christians of Gaul over whom he presided. It held that Easter should be kept on the Lord's day, yet urged Victor not to cut off whole churches that kept an ancient custom. Eusebius quotes it

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • About two centuries later Epiphanius of Salamis copied his opening account of the Valentinians, from the preface to the eleventh chapter of the first book, word for word into his own book against heresies. That copy is how most of the first book survives in Greek.
  • Eusebius quotes him often as an authority for the second century. He copies his list of Rome's bishops, his account of the four Gospels, and his letters to Florinus and to Victor.
  • A. Cleveland Coxe, the American editor of his English, says Tertullian takes over the first book against the Gnostics in his own Against the Valentinians.
  • For eighteen centuries the Valentinians were known almost only through opponents such as him, until books of their own were found in Egypt in 1945.

How they're remembered

  • Hippolytus calls him "the blessed presbyter Irenæus", and Tertullian names him among those who answered the heretics as "that very exact inquirer into all doctrines".
  • Epiphanius calls him the elder Irenaeus, who like a noble athlete wrestled down their whole foolish system.
  • Eusebius says Irenaeus was well named, for he became a peacemaker in the quarrel over Easter; his name, McGiffert notes, comes from the Greek word for peace.
  • Theodoret of Cyrrhus lists him among his witnesses as one who enjoyed the teaching of Polycarp and became a light of the western Gauls.

Open Questions

  • His life. Most of what is known of it comes from Eusebius, writing more than a century later, and from the few words he gives about himself. No early source says where or when he was born. Arthur McGiffert, following Lipsius, puts his birth in Asia Minor between 120 and 130; Jerome's editor Ernest Cushing Richardson gives 140 to 145; Photius's editor J. H. Freese gives about 120 to 140.
  • Smyrna, and the way to Gaul. He says he saw Polycarp as a boy in lower Asia; that he grew up at Smyrna is inferred from Polycarp's see. No early source says when or why he went to Gaul. Gregory of Tours, about four centuries later, says Polycarp sent him; A. Cleveland Coxe says he joined Pothinus at Lyon as his fellow pupil under Polycarp, and gives no source.
  • The year 177 and the martyrs' letters. In their letter to Eleutherius at Rome, which Eusebius quotes, the imprisoned martyrs asked him to carry it. Their letter to the churches of Asia and Phrygia does not say he saw them die. Valesius thought he wrote that letter, and Coxe thinks so too; McGiffert finds that in the highest degree improbable. The letters also took a side on the new prophecy of Montanus in Phrygia, and which side is disputed: McGiffert sets out the common view that they favored it and argues, with Salmon, that they opposed it.
  • The account of his opponents. What the Valentinians taught is told here as he tells it, and his is an opponent's witness. He says he wrote above all against the disciples of Ptolemaeus, a bud from the school of Valentinus. The many women led astray in the district of the Rhone he lays to the followers of Marcus, one teacher among those he treats.
  • Rome's bishops. Whether Rome then had a single bishop at its head, historians still argue. The clause in his Rome passage that the English renders as Rome's pre-eminent authority is a famous crux: its translators say both the Latin's text and meaning are disputed and its Greek cannot be known, and Coxe adds his own polemic.
  • The letter to Florinus has no sure date. Lightfoot made it one of his earliest writings; Lipsius, followed by McGiffert, puts it under Victor, after Against Heresies.
  • His death. The early record is silent after the Easter quarrel. Jerome is the first to call him a martyr, in a later commentary on Isaiah, and Gregory of Tours gives the first full account. McGiffert holds the silence of Tertullian, Hippolytus, Eusebius and the earlier writers to be conclusive that he was not a martyr, and thinks he died, at the latest, soon after the beginning of the third century. Freese says he is said to have been martyred under Severus, but this is not regarded as certain.
  • Hippolytus as his pupil. Photius, in the ninth century, calls Hippolytus the pupil of Irenaeus and his book a synopsis of Irenaeus's lectures; the English translators of Against Heresies call him the author's pupil too.
  • The modern English. The mosaic and the one house are rendered from the Greek Epiphanius kept, not from the Latin, which is not held. The brief's small stones follow the Greek; the English made from the Latin has precious jewels. The words to Florinus are rendered from McGiffert's English, since Eusebius's Greek of that book is not held, and the middle of his sentence, on Polycarp's comings and goings, his way of life, his looks and his talks to the people, is told in the narrator's words. Hippolytus's presbyter is rendered elder.
