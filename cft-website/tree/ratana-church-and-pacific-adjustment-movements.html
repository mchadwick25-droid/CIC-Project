About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:09

Read along

In November 1918 Tahupōtiki Wiremu Rātana had a vision in Aotearoa New Zealand. A healing ministry grew up around him from that year. In 1925 the movement became a church of its own, Te Haahi Rātana. Rātana himself was known as the mangai, the mouthpiece. That role appears in the church's own creed, prayers and hymns, alongside the faithful angels. The movement was political as well as religious from early on. A group took a Treaty of Waitangi petition to London in 1924. An alliance with the Labour Party followed a meeting with Michael Joseph Savage in 1936. By 1943 the movement held all four Māori seats in Parliament. Rātana died in 1939, and the church went on under the tumuaki, the leaders who followed him. Other Pacific movements of a like shape belong beside it. Among them is Silas Eto's Christian Fellowship Church in the Solomon Islands, from around 1960.

Documented Stories

The Petition Nobody Would Receive

Tahupōtiki Wiremu Rātana had been healing the sick at his farm near Whanganui since his vision of November 1918, and by 1924 the settlement around him, Rātana Pā, drew thousands. He had begun to say that the spiritual work was only half of it: that the Treaty of Waitangi of 1840 had never been made law, and that the King who had signed it should be asked to keep it. A petition was drawn up asking for the Treaty's ratification and for redress of confiscated lands, and it gathered more than thirty thousand signatures, a large part of the adult Māori population. In April 1924 Rātana sailed with a party of about forty, including a brass band, for England. The New Zealand government had advised London that the petition was a domestic matter, and the Colonial Office followed that advice: George V did not receive him, and the petition was not accepted. The party went on to Geneva to put it to the League of Nations, which declined to hear it. They traveled on to Japan, where Rātana was received with courtesy by public figures and preached, and then home. The journey had cost the movement heavily and had achieved nothing that could be written down. Within the year Rātana had established the movement as a church, Te Haahi Rātana, and had turned the political work toward the Māori parliamentary seats, which his followers would win one by one until, in 1943, they held all four.

A Potato, a Watch, a Tiki and a Feather

The Labour Party had won government in New Zealand in November 1935, and its leader Michael Joseph Savage, a Catholic of Irish-Australian origin, was the first prime minister the movement's people thought might listen. Rātana had already had one of his followers, Eruera Tirikatene, elected to Parliament in 1932. On 22 April 1936 he came to Parliament in Wellington with a party of his people and, in a meeting retold at Rātana Pā every January for decades afterward, placed four things before Savage. A potato: the ordinary food of the people, which without land they could not grow, a sign of their loss. A broken gold watch: the Treaty, its promises broken, which needed mending by the law. A greenstone tiki: the mana of the Māori people, their standing and their spirit. And a huia feather, the plume of chieftainship, which he told Savage he could wear if he took up the other three. Savage accepted all four. An alliance followed: Rātana candidates stood as Labour, the church's people voted Labour, and Labour carried Māori grievances into its program. By 1943 the four Māori seats were all held by Rātana members, and the alliance held for most of the century. The four gifts were not lost. The tiki and feather were kept by Savage's family and later returned to the movement. The potato and the watch survived in memory and in the annual retelling.

In Its Own Record

These movements grew out of Pacific mission Christianity, led by local people. Tahupōtiki Wiremu Rātana had a vision on 8 November 1918. His church, Te Haahi Rātana, was set up in 1925. A like pattern shows up elsewhere in the Pacific. The best-known case is Silas Eto's Christian Fellowship Church in the Solomon Islands. It began around 1960.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

After a vision in 1918, Tahupōtiki Wiremu Rātana founded Te Haahi Rātana in Aotearoa New Zealand in 1925. The church calls him the mangai, the mouthpiece, and the title runs through its own creed and hymns. The movement became a political force as well as a religious one. It took a Treaty of Waitangi petition to London in 1924. By 1943 it held all four Māori seats in Parliament. Similar movements arose elsewhere in the Pacific. One is Silas Eto's Christian Fellowship Church in the Solomon Islands, which began around 1960.

Legacy

In 2010 Te Haahi Rātana was a living church and still a force in New Zealand public life. The annual gathering at Rātana Pā was a fixture of the political calendar. Both sides still referred to the alliance made in 1936. The movement gave Māori Christianity an institutional form that was not a mission's, at a time when almost nothing else was on offer. The questions this map records about it — what the mangai's mediating role means, put to the church's own prayers rather than to its members — are held here as questions, in the terms the church's own texts set.
