The Global Church Era · c. 1975-c. 2010 · N. America, then global

N. America, today in United States and beyond

The Megachurch & Seeker Movement

North America and then worldwide, c. 1975 to 2010 — evangelical pragmatism built into small-group systems and a worship-music culture, Hillsong among them, that carried the megachurch model around the globe.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:16

Read along

From the mid-1970s a particular way of building a church spread out of American suburbs and then around the world. Willow Creek Community Church was founded in 1975 on the premise that the Sunday service should be designed for the person who does not go to church, with the actual formation happening in small groups during the week. Saddleback followed in 1980, and Rick Warren's The Purpose Driven Church (1995) put the method into a book other pastors could work from. Behind both lay the church-growth school, and Donald McGavran's Understanding Church Growth (1970). The Willow Creek Association, from 1992, exported the model by conference and manual. The other great carrier was music: Hillsong, founded in Australia in 1983, became a global publisher of congregational songs, and licensing data shows how widely they were sung. The movement studied itself unusually hard — Willow Creek's own REVEAL research from 2007 questioned whether its programs were forming anyone.

Documented Stories

The Wake-Up Call

Willow Creek Community Church, in the Chicago suburbs, had since 1975 built its method on a premise: design the weekend service for the person who does not go to church, and grow believers through programs, small groups and volunteering during the week. By 2004 it had twenty thousand attenders and an association of more than ten thousand churches using its model. That year its executive pastor Greg Hawkins commissioned a survey to measure whether the method produced spiritual growth, expecting it to. The results, from some six thousand of its own people and then from thirty other churches, were not what anyone expected. Participation in church activities did not predict growth beyond a point; the people who reported the deepest love of God and neighbor were those who had learned to feed themselves through scripture and prayer, and a quarter of the most mature said they were stalled or considering leaving, because the church had not taught them how. The findings were published in 2007 as Reveal: Where Are You? At the Leadership Summit in August 2007 Bill Hybels called the results 'the wake-up call of my adult life' and said, in words widely quoted afterwards, that they had made a mistake, that they should have taught people to become self-feeders. The church redesigned its formation program. Critics of the seeker model treated the study as a confession; its authors treated it as the method doing what it had always claimed to do, which was measure.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

From 1975, when Willow Creek Community Church designed its Sunday service for people who don't go to church, a model built on small groups and contemporary worship spread from American suburbs worldwide, carried in part by songs written by Hillsong, founded in Australia in 1983, and by 2010 sung far beyond anyone's knowledge of where they came from. In 2007 Willow Creek's own REVEAL study turned that same rigor on itself, asking whether its programs were forming anyone spiritually at all.

Legacy

By 2010 the model was everywhere. For many people it was simply what a church looked like: a stage, a band, a sermon in plain speech, and a small-group system underneath. Its worship music was sung in congregations that had no idea where it came from. By then the Hartford Institute's research studied it as a serious subject rather than a curiosity. Kate Bowler's Blessed (2013) later did the same. In 2021 Lester Ruth and Lim Swee Hong published a history of contemporary praise and worship.
