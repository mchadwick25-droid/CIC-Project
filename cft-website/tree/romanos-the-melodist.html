Romanos the Melodist

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Byzantine Imperial Church (Justinianic).

Known for
A deacon from Syria, remembered as a writer of kontakia, long sung poems for the church's feasts, whose gift a late notice says came in a dream of the Mother of God.
Also called
Sanctus Romanus, veterum melodorum princeps (Pitra's title), Romanus
Dates
The notice places his coming to Constantinople in the time of an emperor Anastasius, and Jean-Baptiste Pitra leaves open whether the first or the second is meant. The later Greek book of saints revised by Nikodemos the Hagiorite also names the emperor Anastasius. No source used here gives a year of birth or death
Place
From Syria; from Emesa, by the later Greek book of saints that Nikodemos the Hagiorite revised; a deacon of the church at Berytus, now Beirut; at Constantinople, at a church of the Mother of God and the shrine of Blachernae
Role
Deacon; hymn writer

About This Person

One Christmas night, a book of saints says, the Mother of God came to Romanos the Melodist in his sleep. She handed him a roll of paper and told him to take it and eat it. He woke full of wonder, went up into the pulpit and began to sing of the Virgin giving birth that day.

The story comes from one short notice in a Menologion, a book of saints for the church year. Cardinal Jean-Baptiste Pitra, who printed it in 1888, calls it the Menologion of the emperor Constantine Porphyrogenitus. Migne's edition of Theodore the Studite gives the same Vatican manuscript to the emperor Basil. Pitra says almost nothing more is recorded of Romanos anywhere.

The notice says Romanos came from Syria and was a deacon of the church at Berytus, now Beirut. He came to Constantinople in the time of the emperor Anastasius. He lived at a church of the Mother of God and kept night vigils at the shrine of Blachernae. He wrote about a thousand kontakia, long sung poems for the feasts, and then died. A later Greek book of saints, revised by Nikodemos the Hagiorite, names his home city as Emesa.

Pitra asks which Anastasius is meant, the first or the second, and looks for clues in one of the poems. He says it is not at all clear that Romanos lived under the second, who reigned from 713 to 716.

Pitra printed three poems from manuscripts on the island of Patmos: on Stephen the first martyr, on Joseph the chaste, and on the ten virgins. He set a Latin prose version beside the Greek, and the words here are from it. In two, Pitra's headings say, the first letters of the verses name the author as humble Romanos.

Each verse of the poem on the ten virgins ends with the plea of those left outside the bridal chamber. In Pitra's Latin it is "Aperi nobis": open to us.

Key Works

  • Kontakion on Stephen the first martyr: printed by Pitra from the Patmos manuscripts, with a Latin prose version; his heading says the first letters of the verses name the author as humble Romanos
  • Kontakion on Joseph the chaste: printed by Pitra, who says Romanos wrote two poems on Joseph; its refrain, in Pitra's Latin, is that the Lord alone is great
  • Kontakion on the ten virgins: printed by Pitra; each verse ends with the plea of those left outside the bridal chamber, "Aperi nobis", open to us

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Pitra printed three of the poems in 1888 from manuscripts of the monastery of Saint John on Patmos, sent to him from the island.

How they're remembered

  • Pitra titles his edition "Sanctus Romanus veterum melodorum princeps": Saint Romanos, prince of the old melodists.
  • The Menologion notice says he wrote about a thousand kontakia, and Pitra notes that the roll in the dream gave the kontakion its name.

What Is Uncertain

  • His life rests mainly on one short notice in a Menologion, a book of saints for the church year. Pitra calls it the Menologion of the emperor Constantine Porphyrogenitus, who reigned from 944 to 959 in Samuel Cross's dates. Migne's edition of Theodore the Studite gives the same Vatican manuscript, number 1613, to the emperor Basil. The Bollandists cite a Menologion made by order of the emperor Basil Porphyrogenitus. It is a saint's story, and Pitra says almost nothing more is recorded of Romanos anywhere.
  • Which emperor Anastasius the notice means. Pitra asks whether it is the first or the second, looks for clues in the poem on the ten virgins, and sets out the earthquakes and wars of both reigns. He says it is not at all clear that Romanos lived under Anastasius the Second, who reigned from 713 to 716. A Greek book of saints revised by Nikodemos the Hagiorite, much later than the Menologion and in the same tradition, names the emperor Anastasius and gives his home city as Emesa.
  • The poems are quoted here only in Pitra's Latin prose version, not in Romanos's Greek.
