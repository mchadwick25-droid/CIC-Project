Armenian Christianity after Avarayr

Armenia, 451 to 554 CE — a church that fought a battle for the right to stay Christian in the same year the empire held the council it would spend a century declining to accept.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:09

Read along

In 451, while the Roman world's bishops met at Chalcedon, Armenia's nobles met the Persian army at Avarayr rather than accept an imposed return to Zoroastrian worship. They lost the battle and their commander, Vardan Mamikonian, but the resistance went on until Persia conceded the right to remain Christian in 484. And the Armenian church came to read that defeat as a covenant sealed in martyrdom rather than as a loss. Absorbed in that struggle, Armenia had sent no bishops to Chalcedon. When it did take up the council's formula it judged it too close to dividing Christ, and at the second council of Dvin in 554 the church formally set itself outside Chalcedon. By then it had a Bible and a liturgy in an alphabet barely fifty years old, and a body of historical writing — Elishe's and Lazar's accounts of the war. That fixed the story it has told about itself ever since.

Documented Stories

The Priests Who Were Marched East

The Armenian nobles lost at Avarayr in 451, but the Persian king Yazdgerd II did not get what he wanted, which was an Armenia that worshipped fire. In the years after the battle he had the surviving leaders — the nobles who had fought and the clergy who had preached the war as a covenant — brought to his court and then sent on, in chains, into the eastern provinces of the empire, toward Nishapur and beyond. Elishe, who says he was a witness to the war, devotes the last part of his History to what happened to the priests. Ghevond, the presbyter who had spoken for the church before the battle, the bishop Sahak of Rshtunik, the presbyters Mushe, Arshen, Samuel, Abraham and the rest were held for years, interrogated by the Magi, offered honors for apostasy, and finally, in the summer of 454, taken out to a remote place and executed at night. Elishe gives them long speeches; he also gives the detail that the king’s men chose the site so that the Armenians would never find the graves, and that a Persian officer, moved by what he saw, later told the Armenian hostages where it was. What the two accounts share is the claim that the Armenian church’s identity was made in a Persian prison as much as on a battlefield — by clergy who would not trade the faith for their lives, and by a people that decided to remember them rather than the defeat.

Vasak Siwni, the Man the Tradition Needed to Blame

Vasak, prince of Siwnik, was marzpan — the Persian-appointed governor — of Armenia when the nobles rose against the order to convert to Zoroastrianism. He had at first joined the resistance; when it became clear that Byzantium would send no help and that the revolt could not win, he went over to the Persian side, took hostages from the rebel families, and worked to bring the country back under the king’s authority without a general massacre. At Avarayr he was on the Persian side of the field. Elishe, writing the church’s account of the war, made him the villain of the whole story: an apostate who ‘loved the world more than God’, whose treachery caused the defeat, and whose punishment followed — summoned to the Persian court, accused by the very nobles he had betrayed, stripped of his honors before the king, and left to die in prison, his body eaten, Elishe says, by worms while he still lived. Lazar’s version is drier and lets the political calculation show. Modern historians have noted that Vasak’s policy of accommodation was the one Armenia actually adopted after 484, when Vahan Mamikonian negotiated the right to remain Christian under Persian rule, and that Vasak’s crime in the tradition’s eyes was less what he did than that he did it too soon and without a martyr’s death to redeem it. The Armenian church has kept him as its type of the traitor for fifteen centuries.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 451, while Roman bishops argued over Christ's natures at Chalcedon, Armenia's nobles fought and lost a battle at Avarayr rather than accept a forced return to Zoroastrian worship. The church came to read that defeat as a covenant sealed in martyrdom, and a century later, at the second council of Dvin in 554, it formally set itself outside Chalcedon's formula.

Legacy

The Armenian Apostolic Church that came out of this period is still the church of Armenia and of Armenians everywhere, and the same institution continued through the medieval Bagratid centuries into the present. Avarayr is still kept as a feast and Vardan and his companions are still counted among its saints. Its stone-vaulted, domed churches became a distinctive architectural tradition studied well beyond the Caucasus, and the alphabet devised to carry its Bible remains the alphabet of the Armenian language.

Experience It Today

  • Etchmiadzin Cathedral, Vagharshapat, Armenia — the mother church of the Armenian Apostolic Church, whose core was rebuilt in 483-4 by Vahan Mamikonian

  • Elishe (Ełishē), History of Vardan and the Armenian War (trans. R. W. Thomson, 1982) (primary) — The classic inside account of Avarayr (451) and its martyr-theology of covenant; Harvard Armenian Texts and Studies 5; standard annotated English translation, online at archive.org.
  • Łazar P'arpec'i, History of the Armenians (trans. R. W. Thomson, 1991) (primary) — The other fifth-century narrative of Avarayr and the 480s Vahan revolt, more sober than Elishe; Thomson's English translation is standard.
  • Girk' T'łt'oc' (Book of Letters) (primary) — The Armenian church's own dossier of doctrinal correspondence, incl. its sixth-century rejection of Chalcedon (Dvin councils); Classical Armenian editions (Tiflis 1901, Jerusalem 1994) - no full English translation; access via excerpts in Garsoian/Sarkissian (transmission flag).
  • Karekin Sarkissian, The Council of Chalcedon and the Armenian Church (1965) (secondary) — Accessible standard account (by an insider-scholar) of why and how the Armenian church came to reject Chalcedon; older but still the usual English entry point.
  • Nina Garsoian, L'Eglise armenienne et le grand schisme d'Orient (CSCO 574, 1999) (secondary) — The definitive scholarly treatment of the Armenian schism with translations of key Book of Letters documents; French only - a real access barrier.
