Venantius Fortunatus

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Merovingian Gallic Christianity.

Known for
Poet from northern Italy who came to Gaul for Martin of Tours, settled at Poitiers beside Radegund's monastery of the Holy Cross, and wrote verse for kings and bishops, Lives of saints, and hymns of the Cross.
Also called
Venantius Honorius Clementianus Fortunatus, Fortunatus
Dates
No ancient source used here gives any year of his life. Born about 530 and died about 600 in W. D. Foulke's note to Paul the Deacon; M. A. Luchi reports the belief that he was born about 530, and puts his death early in the seventh century; Hugh Williams says he died about 609. Bruno Krusch puts his coming to Gaul in 565; Friedrich Leo's index places it between 561 and 567
Place
Born near Ceneda and Treviso in northern Italy; trained at Ravenna; Poitiers
Role
Poet; priest, and bishop of Poitiers in Paul the Deacon's account

About This Person

Venantius Fortunatus and his friend Felix of Treviso suffered a grievous disease of the eyes. Paul the Deacon, the Lombard historian, tells what they did at Ravenna. They went to the church of Saints Paul and John. There a lamp burned in a window beside an altar of Martin of Tours. They touched their eyes with its oil, and at once the disease was gone. Fortunatus's poem says Martin once gave the light back to Felix and to him.

Fortunatus was a poet from northern Italy. Paul says he was born near Ceneda and Treviso, and trained at Ravenna. For love of Martin he left Italy for the saint's tomb at Tours, a little before the Lombards invaded. The German scholar Bruno Krusch puts his coming to Gaul in 565.

He stayed at Poitiers, Paul says. Radegund, once the wife of King Chlothar the First, lived there in her monastery of the Holy Cross. Fortunatus wrote poems to her and to its abbess, Agnes. He called Agnes a mother in honor and a sister in sweet love, and said his love was of the spirit, with Christ as witness.

He wrote verse for kings and bishops, and Lives of saints. Gregory of Tours, who calls him his venerable fellow servant, says he told Martin's life in four books of verse. O. M. Dalton, Gregory's English translator, says that when Radegund's house received a relic of the Cross, Fortunatus marked it with hymns. One begins "Vexilla Regis prodeunt", in plain words, the banners of the King go forward.

Paul says he was ordained at Poitiers, first priest and then bishop. Baudonivia, a nun of Holy Cross, also calls him bishop. Dalton notes that Gregory never calls him bishop. Dalton thinks Fortunatus became bishop only after Gregory died.

Paul came to his grave to pray. At the request of Aper, the abbot, he wrote an epitaph: "Famous in talent, quick to perceive and in speech ever gentle."

Key Works

  • Life of Saint Martin: the life of Martin of Tours in four books of verse, as Gregory of Tours and Paul the Deacon both say; its fourth book names his birthplace and the healing of his eyes
  • Poems: verse for kings, bishops and friends, poems to Radegund and Agnes, and the hymns of the Cross, among them Pange lingua and Vexilla Regis prodeunt, given here in Latin only
  • Lives of saints in prose: among them Hilary of Poitiers, Germanus of Paris, Marcellus of Paris and Radegund; Paul the Deacon says he wrote the deeds of many saints, part in prose and part in verse

Legacy

What traces to them

  • His Life of Radegund is the first book of her Life; Baudonivia, a nun of Holy Cross, wrote the second to tell what he passed over.
  • O. M. Dalton says the arrival of the relic of the Cross at Radegund's monastery was celebrated by Fortunatus in several hymns, the most famous beginning Vexilla Regis prodeunt.

How they're remembered

  • Paul the Deacon calls him second to none of the poets in soft and fluent speech, and wrote his epitaph at Poitiers at the request of the abbot Aper.
  • Gregory of Tours calls him his venerable fellow servant, and in his book on Martin's miracles wishes that Fortunatus were there to write them.
  • An epitaph printed in Alcuin's works in Migne calls him an ornament of the Church who wrote many songs of the saints in verse.

What Is Uncertain

  • Whether and when he was bishop. Paul the Deacon says he was ordained at Poitiers, first priest and then bishop, and Baudonivia calls him bishop. Gregory of Tours calls him a priest. O. M. Dalton, Gregory's English translator, thinks he was consecrated only after Gregory's death, citing his own Introduction, which is not among the sources used here; M. A. Luchi gives the same reason and says the year is hard to fix. No source used here dates the bishopric. An unsigned note in Migne answers earlier scholars who struck him from the list of the bishops of Poitiers.
  • His years. No ancient source used here gives any year of his birth, his coming to Gaul or his death. The years on this card are W. D. Foulke's, M. A. Luchi's, Hugh Williams's, Bruno Krusch's and Friedrich Leo's. Paul the Deacon says only that he left Italy a little before the Lombards invaded it.
  • The healing of his eyes at Ravenna is told three ways. Paul the Deacon puts it at the church of Saint Paul and Saint John, at a lamp in a window by an altar of Martin. Gregory of Tours, who had it from Fortunatus, puts the lamp beneath a painted image of Martin. Fortunatus's own poem says Martin gave the light back to Felix of Treviso and to him. W. D. Foulke, citing R. Jacobi's study of Paul's sources, says Paul took the story from Fortunatus's poem.
  • His hymns. No English translation of them is among the sources used here; the Latin is in Friedrich Leo's edition and, under his name, in Migne's volume of Alcuin. The count of 600 hymns is W. D. Foulke's, with no source used here behind it.
  • Some Lives under his name. Bruno Krusch denies him the Life of Medard, and says the Life of Remigius is wrongly given to him; others gave the Life of Marcellus to another Fortunatus, which Krusch rejects.
  • Fortunatus of Grado, Fortunatus of Naples and Fortunatus of Fano are other men.
