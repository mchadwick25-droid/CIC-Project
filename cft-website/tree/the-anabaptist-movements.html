The Anabaptist Movements

Swiss lands, Moravia and the Low Countries, 1525 to 1650 — believers who baptized one another as adults and were hunted for it by every other church of their century.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:03

Read along

In January 1525 a handful of men in Zurich baptized one another as adults. They were impatient with how slowly their own city's reform was moving, and they were hunted for it. Anabaptists held that baptism belonged to those who could confess faith for themselves. The church, they said, was a free body of believers, not a whole society. Most of them also held that a Christian could neither swear oaths nor take up the sword. Catholic, Lutheran and Reformed rulers alike saw them as a threat to order. By one early count more than two thousand were put to death, above all in Catholic lands. The survivors regrouped. In Moravia, a band that pooled its goods in 1528 grew into the Hutterite Brethren. In the Low Countries, a former priest named Menno Simons gathered congregations, and their descendants still carry his name.

People and Stories

Voices in the Record

  • Conrad Grebel — Zurich radical, among the first to be re-baptized in January 1525
  • Felix Manz — drowned in the Limmat at Zurich in 1527, the movement's first martyr at Protestant hands
  • Michael Sattler — former Benedictine prior, principal author of the Schleitheim Articles of 1527 and executed that same year
  • Menno Simons — Dutch priest who left his parish in 1536 and gathered the scattered congregations that took his name
  • Anneken Jans — executed at Rotterdam in 1539, whose testament written for her infant son survives in the movement's own martyr book

Documented Stories

Dirk Willems Turns Back on the Ice

Dirk Willems had been rebaptized at Rotterdam, had let others be rebaptized in his house at Asperen in Holland, and was under sentence when he escaped from the town’s prison by a rope of knotted rags and ran across the moat, which was frozen. The Martyrs Mirror, the great Dutch Mennonite compilation of 1660, gives the story and the court record. A thief-catcher — a guard set to pursue him — followed onto the ice, which had borne the starved prisoner and did not bear the guard; he broke through. Willems, on the far bank, heard him and turned back and pulled him out. The guard, the account says, wanted to let him go; the burgomaster standing on the bank called out that he should remember his oath, and the guard arrested the man who had saved him. The sentence of the Asperen court, dated 16 May 1569, is printed: Dirk Willems, having ‘persisted obstinately in his opinion’, rebaptized and having permitted rebaptism in his house, is to be executed by fire. The Martyrs Mirror adds that the wind carried the flames away from him so that he died slowly, crying out seventy times ‘O my Lord, my God’, until the bailiff, sickened, ordered him despatched. The Mennonites and Amish have kept the story and the engraving of it — the man on the ice reaching down — as the picture of what their faith requires, which is not to escape.

The Kingdom at Münster

Münster had elected an Anabaptist-leaning council in February 1534 under the preacher Bernhard Rothmann, and within weeks Dutch followers of the prophet Melchior Hoffman’s successor Jan Matthijs had arrived and taken it over. Those who would not be rebaptized were driven out of the gates into the snow; property was declared common and money abolished; books other than the Bible were burned. The prince-bishop besieged the city. Matthijs, who had declared it the New Jerusalem, rode out against the besiegers on Easter Day with a handful of men, believing himself invulnerable, and was killed. Jan van Leiden, a tailor from Leiden, took his place, ran naked through the streets in prophecy, dissolved the council, introduced polygamy — he took sixteen wives, and beheaded one in the market square himself — and in September had himself crowned king of the whole world, with a court and a throne. In June 1535 the city was betrayed to the besiegers and most of its defenders killed. Jan van Leiden and two lieutenants were tortured with hot tongs before the crowd in January 1536, and their bodies hung in cages from the tower of St Lambert’s, where the cages still are. Menno Simons, who left his priesthood the following year, wrote against ‘the abomination of Münster’ for the rest of his life; the peaceful Anabaptists who became the Mennonites were hunted for two generations as if they had been there. The tradition has never been allowed to forget it, and has not tried to.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Lutheran, Reformed and Catholic churches all condemned them. Catholic lands hunted them hardest. Later free churches such as the Baptists shared some of their beliefs. How much they owed them is still argued.

Descended from

  • grew out of The Reformed Cities - Zurich & Geneva Documented Major — In February 1525 a man tried in Zurich said Felix Manz had baptized him. In 1527 Zwingli wrote of the Baptists: 'They have gone out from us.' This is the Swiss branch only. Historians hold that Anabaptists in South Germany, Moravia and the Netherlands had other roots too.

Direct descendants

  • gave rise to The Hutterite Brethren Documented Major — In 1528 Jakob Widman led some two hundred people out of the Anabaptist church at Nikolsburg. They put all their goods together and moved on to Austerlitz. The Hutterite chronicle tells this as the start of the community.
  • continued as The Mennonites and the Amish Documented Major — The Dutch and Swiss Anabaptist churches carried on after 1650, many of them under the name of Menno Simons. At Dort in 1659 Thieleman van Braght called the martyrs "my slain fellow brethren and sisters". A Mennonite manual of 1890 still used the Dordrecht Confession of 1632.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced

  • influenced The Baptists Contested Major — Scholars disagree about how much the English Baptists owed the Anabaptists. John Smyth's church in exile baptized its own members anew in Amsterdam. Then Smyth asked to join the Mennonite church there. He called his own new baptism an error. Thomas Helwys and his party kept theirs.Lasting result: The question split Smyth's church in Amsterdam in about 1609. His party sought to join the Mennonites. Helwys's party kept apart and later founded the General Baptists.
  • influenced Radical Pietism & the Brethren (Dunkers, Ephrata) Documented — The Ephrata chronicle says its first members were "mostly descended from the Mennonites." They kept the plain Mennonite way of life. In 1748 Ephrata printed the Mennonite book of martyrs in German. But the Brethren writer Martin Brumbaugh said his church was not a branch of the Mennonites.

Argued Against

  • argued against Lutheran Wittenberg & Its Congregations Documented — The Hutterite chronicle names Luther and Zwingli. It says they laid bare the faults of the popes, but then leaned on worldly rulers and human help. This is the view of one Anabaptist group, in a chronicle kept over many years.

Opposed By

  • argued against Lutheran Wittenberg & Its Congregations Documented — In 1530 the Lutheran side named and condemned the Anabaptists in the Augsburg Confession. It rejects their view that children should not be baptized. It also rejects their rule that a Christian may not serve as a judge or ruler.
  • argued against The Reformed Cities - Zurich & Geneva Documented Major — In January 1525 Zurich's council told Conrad Grebel and Felix Manz to stop arguing about baptism. Two years later it sentenced Manz to be drowned for baptizing adults again. That same year Zwingli wrote that the Baptists had split and betrayed the church.Lasting result: Zurich's council made a second baptism a crime punished by drowning. On 5 January 1527 it sentenced Felix Manz to die that way.
  • argued against The Tridentine Church Documented Major — In March 1547 the Council of Trent condemned anyone who said that those baptized as babies must be baptized again as adults. The canon does not name the Anabaptists. A Mennonite martyr book quotes a writer who says they were hunted 'first especially in popedom.' It says about six hundred died at Ensisheim alone.Lasting result: In March 1547 the Council of Trent condemned all who taught that a child baptized at birth must be baptized again.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In January 1525, a small group in Zurich baptized one another as adults. Lutheran, Reformed and Catholic leaders alike condemned them for it. What survives from inside the movement is rare and direct. Trial papers survive, and so do prison hymns and a book of martyrs. That book, the Martyrs Mirror, is still read aloud in Amish and Mennonite homes.

Legacy

The movement survives directly. Mennonites, Amish and Hutterites descend from it, and the later Brethren drew on its beliefs. Its free-church idea also spread far beyond it. Joining a church is a free choice made as an adult, and the church should not be an arm of the state. That became one of the most widely shared Protestant ideas in the world. Its martyr book, the Martyrs Mirror of 1660, is still printed and still read aloud in Amish and Mennonite homes.

Experience It Today

  • The Täuferhöhle near Bäretswil, Switzerland — the cave in the hills outside Zurich where hunted Anabaptists met to worship, reachable on a short marked walk
  • The Menno Simons monument at Witmarsum, Friesland — freely accessible, on the site where he is said to have preached to his congregation

Sources

On the Nicene Creed

The mainstream of the movement held the historic creeds' teaching about God and Christ, though Menno Simons and the Dutch wing taught a distinctive doctrine of Christ's heavenly flesh that their neighbors disputed. The era's anti-Trinitarian groups are a separate story, told elsewhere on this map.

How Sure the Record Is

RICH in ordinary inside voice: court testimonies, prison hymns, the Martyrs Mirror, Hutterite Chronicle

  • The confessions and leaders' corpus: Schleitheim Articles (1527) with Sattler's trial record (The Legacy of Michael Sattler, ed./trans. Yoder, 1973); Menno Simons, The Complete Writings (trans. Verduin, ed. Wenger, 1956); Dirk Philips, Enchiridion (primary) — Swiss and Dutch wings speak in their own words.
  • The court testimonies: Taeuferakten series (Quellen zur Geschichte der Taeufer); Sources of South German/Austrian Anabaptism; Harder, Sources of Swiss Anabaptism (primary) — Hostile, coerced evidence: interrogations conducted under threat and torture, recorded by the persecuting authorities. Ordinary believers speak only through the persecutor's pen.
  • Martyrs Mirror (van Braght, 1660; Eng. trans. Sohm) with the Ausbund prison hymns (from 1564) (primary) — A 1660 Dutch Mennonite compilation, not a contemporaneous archive, but it embeds authentic prison letters, including Anneken Jans's 1539 testament — a rare quantity of women's voices.
  • The Chronicle of the Hutterian Brethren, vol. 1 (trans./ed. the Hutterian Brethren, Plough, Rifton, 1987), with the Hutterite community ordinances (primary) — Inside institutional memory covering 1528-1665, including the only account of the first believers' baptism. It's a self-vindicating genre, written to defend the community's own memory.
  • Roth & Stayer (eds.), A Companion to Anabaptism and Spiritualism, 1521-1700 (Brill, 2007) (secondary) — The current standard reference on the movement's diverse, multiple origins ('polygenesis').
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 6, ch. 3 (McLaughlin, the radical Reformation) (reference)
