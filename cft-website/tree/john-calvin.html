Geneva, today in Switzerland

John Calvin

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Reformed Cities - Zurich & Geneva.

Also called Jean Calvin, Calvinus

French exile and author of the Institutes, who was held at Geneva in 1536 by William Farel's curse, was voted out in 1538 and came back in 1541 to give the city a written order for its church, with pastors, teachers, deacons and elders; the French and Scottish churches took up the same offices, and his name became the label for the family of Reformed churches.

About This Person

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 4:17

Read along

In August 1536 a young French scholar, already the author of a book that defended his persecuted fellow believers, stopped at Geneva for one night, on his way to Strasbourg. The city's preacher, William Farel, begged him to stay, then did something more frightening. The scholar told it himself, twenty years later: "When he saw that asking got him nowhere, he went as far as a curse. He called on God to curse my quiet life if I held back from helping in so great a need. Struck by that terror, I gave up the journey I had begun."

The scholar was John Calvin. His book was the Institutes, a book of instruction in the Christian faith. He stayed, and gave Geneva a written plan for its church, with four kinds of office. Churches in France and Scotland built theirs the same way, and his name became the label for a family of churches begun at Zurich before him.

Born in France in 1509, he says he left it to hide in some quiet corner. But believers burned there were being slandered as rebels, so he wrote a short handbook to the French king to clear their name.

In 1538 Geneva's citizens voted him and Farel out for defying a ban on their preaching. In 1540 Geneva's councils wrote begging him back. He went back in 1541, but would take up his post only once the city swore to keep a catechism, a book of questions and answers on the faith. It also swore to keep a church discipline, a system for correcting members' lives.

His rules set pastors to preach, baptize and give communion, the shared bread and wine of worship. Teachers taught sound teaching, and deacons cared for the poor and sick. Twelve elders, laymen from the city's councils, watched over people's lives and met weekly with the pastors as the consistory, a church court that questioned and warned. But the councils kept the power to punish. Who could bar people from communion stayed in dispute between them until 1555. In 1547, while that dispute went on, the city beheaded a man for speaking against God, for acts against the city's government, and for threatening the pastors.

By then the much larger Institutes taught that God chooses beforehand who will be saved. In Calvin's words, "Some are ordained beforehand to eternal life, others to eternal damnation," to life with God, or to being lost forever. In 1551, as Geneva's pastors recorded it, a doctor of medicine, Jérôme Bolsec, rose at their weekly Bible meeting. He said this made God a tyrant. He was jailed, and when Geneva asked other Swiss churches, Bern's pastors wrote that some said he was not so bad a man, and hoped for agreement. Geneva still sent him away for good.

In 1553 the city council burned Michael Servetus alive for books against infant baptism and against the teaching that God is three persons in one. It had first asked the advice of four Swiss churches. Calvin had written that if Servetus came, he would not leave alive, and during the trial he hoped for a death sentence, though a less cruel one.

Others built the same offices with no friendly city behind them. In 1559, when going to Geneva could mean death, French churches met secretly to adopt a statement of belief and a rulebook with pastors, elders and deacons. In 1562 Scotland's church ruled to give baptism and communion, marry and bury as Geneva's church did.

Gravely ill in 1564, Calvin spoke to the city's pastors, and one of them wrote it down: "I have lived here amid extraordinary battles. In mockery, I was greeted in the evening outside my door with fifty or sixty gunshots. Think how that could shake a poor, timid scholar, as I am and always have been, I confess." He died that May.

Timeline

Dates
Born at Noyon in Picardy on 10 July 1509, as B. J. Kidd's note gives it; Beza's history of 1580 says only that he was a native of Noyon. He died on 27 May 1564. He was held at Geneva in August 1536, voted out in April 1538, called back by the city's councils in October 1540, and back in Geneva in September 1541. The preface that tells the scene at Geneva was written in 1557, about twenty-one years later. The Institutes dedication is dated 1 August 1536 in the 1559 text; the first edition's letter was dated 23 August 1535 and the book was printed in March 1536, as Kidd's and Beveridge's notes give it
Place
Noyon; Basel; Geneva; Strasbourg
Role
Pastor and Bible lecturer at Geneva; author of the Institutes; drafter, with his colleagues, of the Church Ordinances of 1541

Key Works

  • Institutes of the Christian Religion: begun at Basel as a short handbook addressed to the French king, to clear the name of the believers burned in France; it grew from six chapters in 1536 to seventeen in 1539 and into the large work of 1559, in which he teaches that God chooses beforehand who will be saved
  • Church Ordinances of Geneva, 1541: the city's written rules for its church, drafted by Calvin and his colleagues with six councilors; they set four kinds of office, pastors, teachers, elders and deacons, and a weekly meeting of elders and pastors; the councils amended the draft, kept the last word on punishment, and the General Council passed it on 20 November 1541
  • Geneva Catechism: a book of questions and answers on the faith, which he says he wrote in haste on his return from Strasbourg, after the city swore to keep a catechism and a discipline; it opens by asking what is the chief end of man

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • In September 1555 believers meeting in a Paris house chose a minister and set up a consistory of elders and deacons. The church history of 1580 says this followed the example of the early church; its modern editors tie it to the order Calvin gave the church at Strasbourg.
  • On 26 May 1559, with going to Geneva a crime that could cost a man his life under the edict of 1557, deputies of the French churches met secretly at Paris and wrote a statement of belief and a church discipline. B. J. Kidd counts ministers asked or sent from Geneva at nineteen in 1559, twelve in 1560 and ninety in 1561.
  • In December 1562 the General Assembly of the Church of Scotland ordered one uniform way of giving the sacraments and of marrying and burying, according to the church of Geneva. John Knox, by his own history, went to Frankfurt at Calvin's command, came back to Geneva and then went on to Scotland.
  • The Edinburgh Book of Common Order of 1611 still prints the catechism that Calvin made.
  • His name became the label for the whole family of churches: in 1672 a synod of the Greek church at Jerusalem met against the Calvinists, and a Dutch teacher at Utrecht, in lessons printed in 1662, said his church held with Calvin but he was not its head.
  • On 5 June 1559 Geneva opened its Academy, with Theodore Beza named Rector; B. J. Kidd reckons its students at twelve hundred junior and three hundred senior by 1564.

How they're remembered

  • The church history of 1580 under Beza's name calls the Institutes an incomparable book.
  • Richard Hooker, writing against those who wanted Geneva's discipline in England, said about thirty years after Calvin's death that his books were almost the standard for judging doctrine and discipline, and that the French churches and the church of Scotland were cast in the mold Calvin had made.

Open Questions

  • The scene at Geneva in 1536 rests on Calvin's own account in the preface to his Psalms commentary, written in 1557. His letter of 13 October 1536 says only that he was detained at Geneva by the brethren until they drew from him a promise to return. Calvin says war had closed the direct road to Strasbourg and that he meant to stay one night; Bonnet's note says he was going back to Basel by way of Geneva. The brief follows Calvin.
  • His conversion. He calls it a sudden conversion. B. J. Kidd, following Williston Walker, dates it late in 1532 or early in 1533, and writes that how far it took him toward his later teaching was perhaps as doubtful to Calvin as to us.
  • The Church Ordinances of 1541. The text in the Geneva archive is a draft with additions in another hand and a few words in Calvin's own; the official text differs from it. The councils amended it and would not let the ministers see the changes. The official text adds that the ministers have no civil jurisdiction and must report to the council wherever punishment is needed.
  • The quarrel with Bolsec rests on the Company of Pastors' own register, the city's sentence and a letter of Beza to Bullinger, which is the record of Geneva's side. Bern's pastors replied that some said Bolsec was not so bad a man. Calvin's 1552 treatise calls him a wandering vagabond sent by Satan. B. J. Kidd's summary of how the dispute fared with other churches is an editor's, and the council vote of November 1552 is not held as a primary line. Sebastian Castellio's writings against predestination are not held.
  • The Latin of the 1559 Institutes is not held. The teaching on predestination is rendered from Beveridge's English of 1845.
  • The death of Servetus. The sentence names books against the Trinity and against the baptism of infants. Calvin wrote in 1546 that if Servetus came he would not let him leave alive, and in August 1553 that he hoped for a death sentence but wished the punishment made less severe. The city council had first asked the advice of the churches of Bern, Basel, Zurich and Schaffhausen.
  • Who wrote the French statement of belief of 1559. Philip Schaff says it was prepared by Calvin and his pupil De Chandieu; B. J. Kidd says it was not Calvin's but the work of Antoine de la Roche Chandieu.
  • Whether the Paris congregation copied Geneva. The church history of 1580 names the early church as its model; its editors name Calvin's order at Strasbourg.
  • The farewell of 28 April 1564 is a minister's record. Pinant wrote it down as nearly as memory could keep it, and a second account confirms it. Kidd's French has fifty or sixty gunshots; Bonnet's English has forty or fifty.
  • Verdicts by B. J. Kidd that Library notes carry as the editor's, not as sources: that the Ordinances powerfully influenced all but Lutheran Protestantism, that after 1555 Calvin's party proceeded by banishment and executions against its opponents, and that the Academy made Geneva the intellectual and missionary center of the Reformed. Hooker's judgment of Calvin comes from a critic of the discipline, written about thirty years after his death.
