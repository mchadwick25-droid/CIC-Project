The Age of Monks and Empires · c. 480-c. 575 · Ravenna, Vivarium

Cassiodorus

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Roman Church in the Age of Gregory the Great.

Known for
A Roman statesman in the service of the Gothic kings of Italy who wrote their letters, then founded the monastery of Vivarium and wrote for its monks a guide to sacred and secular reading.
Also called
Cassiodorus Senator, Magnus Aurelius Cassiodorus Senator (Hodgkin's form), "the Senator" (in Jordanes)
Dates
Consul in the year Theodor Mommsen numbers 514, by his own chronicle; praetorian prefect from the year Thomas Hodgkin gives as 533. He wrote on spelling in his ninety-third year, by his own word. Hodgkin, following Trithemius, puts his birth about 480 and his death in 575
Place
Italy, at the Gothic court in Ravenna and at Rome; later Vivarium, near Scyllacium (Squillace) in the far south, where Hodgkin says his family came from
Role
Statesman; founder of the monastery of Vivarium; writer

About This Person

In his ninety-third year, Cassiodorus says, his monks came to him with a complaint. What good was it, they asked, to study the old books and the ones he had added? They did not know how to spell what they read. Thomas Hodgkin, who put his letters into English, tells it. So the old man wrote them a book on spelling.

Cassiodorus was a Roman statesman in Italy under the Gothic kings, and later the founder of a monastery. Hodgkin and Theodor Mommsen say Senator was part of his own name. A fragment drawn from a lost tract of his own, Hodgkin says, tells how he praised Theoderic, king of the Goths. He spoke so well that the king made him quaestor. He rose to consul, master of the offices and praetorian prefect. In his own chronicle he notes that in his consulship the clergy and people of the Roman church were united again. Mommsen dates that year 514.

He wrote the letters of the Gothic kings for them, and later gathered them in twelve books, the Variae. In the preface he says an author is told to keep his writings nine years, "but I have not as many hours, hardly as many moments." He also wrote a History of the Goths in twelve books, now lost. Jordanes condensed it, he says, from a three days' reading.

With Pope Agapetus, Cassiodorus tried to set up a Christian school at Rome, with paid teachers, as at Alexandria and Nisibis. War stopped it, he says. He then withdrew to Scyllacium in the far south, where his family came from, Hodgkin says. There he built a monastery named for its fishponds, Vivarium, and a retreat for hermits on the hill above.

For his monks he wrote a guide to sacred and secular reading, the Institutions. In it he praises the copyist: "With his fingers he gives life to men and arms them against the wiles of the devil." He made them lamps that fed their own flame, with a sundial and a water-clock. Hodgkin finds no mention of Benedict of Nursia in all his writings, and doubts he was ever formally abbot.

No early source gives the years of his birth or death. Hodgkin, following a writer who died in 1516, puts them near 480 and 575.

Hodgkin calls him "a well-read scholar, and a devout Christian; but he was apt to crouch before the possessors of power."

Key Works

  • Variae: twelve books of the letters he wrote for the Gothic kings and, as prefect, in his own name
  • Institutions of Divine and Secular Learning (Institutiones): a guide for the monks of Vivarium to the reading of Scripture and its commentators, and to the liberal arts
  • Exposition of the Psalms (Expositio Psalmorum): a commentary on the Psalms, which by his own word he began at the time of his turn to the religious life

Legacy

What traces to them

  • At Vivarium he set his monks to copy and bind books, and made them lamps that fed their own flame, a sundial and a water-clock for the work, as he describes in the Institutions.
  • With Pope Agapetus he planned a Christian school at Rome on the models of Alexandria and Nisibis; war stopped it, he says.
  • Jordanes says he condensed the twelve books of the Senator's History of the Goths, which is lost, into his own short work.

How they're remembered

  • Hodgkin calls him "a statesman of considerable sagacity and of unblemished honour, a well-read scholar, and a devout Christian; but he was apt to crouch before the possessors of power however unworthy".
  • Alcuin of York lists Cassiodorus among the authors in the library of York.

What Is Uncertain

  • His years. No early source gives his birth or death. Hodgkin's 480 and 575 rest on Trithemius, a writer who died in 1516, whom Hodgkin calls "a very late and somewhat unsatisfactory source". Hugh Williams gives his death as 570, and Philipp Strauch as 563. The one fixed point is his own "ninety-third year", which no source dates.
  • His name. Mommsen and Hodgkin hold that Senator was his own name, not a title. Paul the Deacon, two centuries later, took it as a rank.
  • Whether he was abbot or a Benedictine. He names two abbots, Chalcedonius and Gerontius, in the Institutions. Hodgkin thinks he probably never formally held the office, though he allows that Trithemius, who says he was elected abbot, may preserve a true tradition. Hodgkin finds no mention of Benedict of Nursia in his writings.
  • His History of the Goths is lost. Jordanes says he condensed it after a three days' reading, which Hodgkin calls false.
  • Much of his life is told in his own letters, some written in the king's name in praise of himself, as Hodgkin notes.
  • Hodgkin's English of the Variae is condensed. Most letters are summaries; the preface to Book I and the letter on Scyllacium are given in full.
