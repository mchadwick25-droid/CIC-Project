The Ethiopian Monastic Flowering

The Ethiopian highlands, c. 1270 to 1517 — an independent Christian kingdom whose monks argued with its emperors, and often won.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:20

Read along

In 1270 the Solomonic dynasty took the Ethiopian throne. It claimed descent from Solomon and the Queen of Sheba. A wave of monks came with it and carried churches deep into newly conquered lands. Two networks led the way: the houses Tekle Haymanot built in Shewa, and the northern houses of the followers of Ewostatewos. The bond between crown and cloister was contested throughout. The Ewostathians kept Saturday as well as Sunday as a Sabbath. The lives of their own saints say they were persecuted for it for generations. Then, at a council in 1450, the emperor Zara Yaqob gave way. His court chronicle says he ordered the old Sabbath kept as holy as Sunday. Zara Yaqob wrote theology himself. He reordered the church and pressed devotion to Mary on it. When the monks of Estifanos refused to bow down before Mary and the cross, he persecuted them. All of it was written down in Ge'ez: royal chronicles, saints' lives, hymns to Mary, and the Kebra Nagast. That book gave the dynasty its Solomonic charter.

Voices in the Record

  • Tekle Haymanot — the monastic founder whose Shewan network of houses carried the church across the expanding kingdom
  • Ewostatewos — whose insistence on keeping both Saturday and Sunday as Sabbaths made his followers a persecuted movement for a century, until the church conceded
  • Zara Yaqob — emperor, theological author and church reformer, and the persecutor of the monks who would not conform
  • Estifanos — leader of the monks who refused the emperor's required observances, and who died in confinement for it

Documented Stories

Estifanos Will Not Bow to the King’s Cross

Zara Yaqob was the most theologically active of the Solomonic emperors, and he required of his subjects a set of observances — prostration before the icons of Mary, the keeping of thirty-two Marian feasts, the wearing of an amulet with the names of the Trinity, and public veneration of the cross he had set up — enforced by officials and by his own writings. The monks who followed Estifanos of Gwendagwende in Tigray refused. Their own account, the Acts of Estifanos, written by a disciple, describes the confrontation: Estifanos was brought before the emperor and asked why he would not prostrate himself to the images and the royal cross, and answered that he prostrated to God alone and honored the king as king, and that the emperor’s ordinances added to the faith of the apostles. He was flogged, his nose and ears were cut, and he was imprisoned; the Acts say he died in confinement in the province of Hadiya. His followers were hunted for a generation, called ‘enemies of Mary’ and forbidden the sacraments; some were burned, and the emperor’s own book, the Mashafa Berhan, denounces them. Zara Yaqob is remembered by the Ethiopian church as its great reformer and by the Stephanite record as its persecutor; the two accounts describe the same king, and the church that made him a saint also preserved the acts of the monk he mutilated.

The Council of Debre Mitmaq Concedes the Sabbath

Ewostatewos had taught in the early fourteenth century that the church should keep the Sabbath of the Old Testament as well as the Lord’s Day, and had been driven into exile for it, dying in Armenia around 1352. His followers in the north held to the practice, were excluded from ordination by the Egyptian metropolitans, and ordained no one for decades rather than give it up, living, their gadl says, as a church without priests. Zara Yaqob, who wanted the kingdom’s monks united behind his reforms, decided the question. In 1450 he summoned a council to Debre Mitmaq in Shewa: the two Egyptian bishops then in the country, Mikael and Gabriel; the abbots of the great houses of Debre Libanos and the Ewostathian houses; and the court. The royal chronicle and the emperor’s own writings record the outcome. The bishops were persuaded — the chronicle implies commanded — to accept the observance of both Sabbaths, and the Ewostathians were readmitted and their monks ordained; the emperor sealed it by having the ruling written into his Mashafa Berhan, with the argument that the apostles had kept both days. The Ethiopian church has kept both ever since, one of the practices that most distinguishes it from every other Christian body. The council is the same emperor’s work as the persecution of Estifanos: a king deciding what the church would believe, in one case against dissenters and in the other for them.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 1270 the Solomonic dynasty took the Ethiopian throne, and a wave of monks came with it. Tekle Haymanot and the followers of Ewostatewos led them, and they carried churches deep into newly won lands. The Ewostathians kept Saturday as well as Sunday as a Sabbath. The lives of their own saints say they were persecuted for it for generations. Then, at a council in 1450, the emperor Zara Yaqob gave way. His court chronicle says he ordered the old Sabbath kept as holy as Sunday. The same emperor wrote books of theology. He persecuted the monks of Estifanos when they refused to bow before Mary and the cross. It was all written down in Ge'ez: royal chronicles, saints' lives, hymns to Mary, and the Kebra Nagast. That book gave the dynasty its claim to come down from Solomon and Sheba.

Legacy

This is a living church. The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church still keeps the outcome of the Sabbath argument. It still sings the hymns to Mary that Zara Yaqob's reign promoted, and it still worships in Ge'ez. Its great monasteries still trace themselves to the founders of these centuries. The manuscripts of the period include painted gospel books, gadl lives of the saints, and collections of hymns. Together they form one of the largest bodies of medieval Christian writing made outside Europe. The tradition came through the wars and upheavals of the next century. It lives on in today's church of tens of millions of members.

How Sure the Record Is

RICH: royal chronicles, saints' lives in quantity (royal-sponsorship gravity needs care)

  • The royal chronicles: The Glorious Victories of Amda Seyon (trans. Huntingford, Oxford, 1965) and the chronicle of Zara Yaqob (ed./French trans. Perruchon, 1893) (primary)
  • Kebra Nagast (The Glory of Kings) (trans. Budge, 1922; free online) (primary) — The Solomonic dynasty's own charter myth. Its dating is a live scholarly argument: the text as it survives is an early-14th-century compilation claiming much older authority.
  • The gadl literature of the era's own movements: Gadla Ewostatewos and Gadla Estifanos (Getatchew Haile, The Ge'ez Acts of Abba Estifanos of Gwendagwende, CSCO, 2006) (primary) — The Estifanosite acts are the persecuted dissenters' own memory of refusing royal-cult conformity under Zara Yaqob — a real counterweight to the royally-sponsored sources that dominate elsewhere.
  • Zara Yaqob's own writings: Mashafa Berhan (Book of Light) and Mashafa Milad (primary) — A reforming king writing theology and enforcing it — an inside voice for the royal reform, but a hostile source for the movements it condemned, including the Estifanosites.
  • Taddesse Tamrat, Church and State in Ethiopia, 1270-1527 (Oxford, 1972) (secondary) — Still the standard account for exactly this window, written by an Ethiopian historian; current French scholarship (Derat) supplements it.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 5, 'Church and nation: the Ethiopian Orthodox Tawahedo church (from the thirteenth to the twentieth century)' (Crummey) (reference) — A direct fit — this period is the chapter's opening ground.
