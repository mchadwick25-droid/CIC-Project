The High Medieval Era · 1108-1246 · Paris and beyond

Paris, today in France and beyond

Canons Regular / Victorine School

Paris and the abbey of Saint-Victor, 1108 to 1246 — clergy under a rule who would not choose between the cloister and the classroom, and worked out a theology of contemplation in the middle of a university town.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:04

Read along

Canons regular were clergy who lived in community under a rule, usually the Rule of St Augustine. But unlike monks, they kept working in the world: serving churches, running hospitals, teaching. The most influential of their houses was the abbey of Saint-Victor on the left bank of Paris. There the master William of Champeaux withdrew from the schools in 1108. His successors built a way of learning that refused to choose between the classroom and the cloister. Hugh of St Victor argued in the Didascalicon that every art and science, rightly ordered, leads toward wisdom. Richard of St Victor mapped the stages of contemplation more carefully than anyone in the Latin West had yet done. Adam of St Victor wrote the sequences the abbey sang. The school's line ran down to Thomas Gallus, who died in 1246. The abbey itself no longer stands.

People and Stories

Voices in the Record

  • William of Champeaux — the Paris master who withdrew to Saint-Victor in 1108 and began the school there
  • Hugh of St Victor — the school's greatest teacher, whose Didascalicon ordered all learning toward wisdom
  • Richard of St Victor — his successor, author of the West's most careful account of the stages of contemplation
  • Adam of St Victor — poet of the liturgical sequences the abbey sang, the school's ordinary, singing face
  • Norbert of Xanten — founder in 1120 of Prémontré, the reformed canons who carried this way of life across northern Europe
  • Thomas Gallus — the last major Victorine thinker, who died in 1246

Documented Stories

How Saint-Victor Began, According to the Man Who Humiliated Its Founder

The only narrative of the founding of Saint-Victor comes from the man who caused it, and it is not generous. Abelard, writing his Story of My Calamities twenty years later, says that as a young student in Paris he attached himself to William of Champeaux, archdeacon and master of the cathedral school, ‘then supreme in the teaching of dialectic’, and set out to refute him in public. William held the realist position that a universal — ‘humanity’ — is wholly and identically present in every individual. Abelard pressed him in disputation until, ‘by the clearest arguments’, William was forced to abandon the position and adopt a weaker one; after which, Abelard says, his lectures ‘fell into such contempt’ that students left him, and his standing was ruined. In 1108 William resigned his archdeaconry and his chair and withdrew, with a few disciples, to a ruined chapel dedicated to St Victor on the left bank, to live as canons regular under the Rule of Augustine and, he intended, to teach no more. He taught anyway; Abelard followed him there to resume the attack, and complains that William used his influence to have him driven from every school he opened. The house William founded in flight became within a generation the most influential school of theology in Paris under Hugh and Richard, with a tradition of learning ordered to contemplation that was, in part, the alternative to the disputatious culture that had broken its founder.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced

  • influenced The English Mystics and Devout Documented — The English author of The Cloud of Unknowing also put Denis's Mystical Theology into English. He wrote that he had 'much followed' the Abbot of Saint Victor's reading of it. His 1924 editor says this was Thomas, abbot at Vercelli, a Victorine canon who died in 1246. He also shows the Cloud taking much from Richard of St Victor.
  • influenced The Franciscan Movement Widely Accepted — Bonaventure, the Franciscan friar and teacher, wrote a Journey of the Mind into God. It turns on a pattern of three that Richard of St Victor had used: outside us, within us and above us. In 1924 Justin McCann, who edited The Cloud of Unknowing, called Bonaventure 'a disciple of Richard' in his teaching on prayer.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Canons regular lived under a rule like monks but kept working in the world — serving churches, running hospitals, teaching — and the abbey of Saint-Victor in Paris became the movement's greatest school after William of Champeaux withdrew there in 1108. Hugh of St Victor argued that every art and science, rightly ordered, leads toward wisdom, while Richard of St Victor mapped the stages of contemplation more carefully than anyone before him in the Latin West. Adam of St Victor supplied the abbey's own sung liturgy, the sequences that outlasted the school itself.

Legacy

The abbey is gone, but the way of life is not. Canons regular still exist, and Norbert's Premonstratensians remain an order today. The Victorines' real bequest was intellectual. Hugh taught that the arts and sciences belong inside the search for God. Richard mapped contemplation stage by stage. Nearly everyone who wrote on prayer in the later Middle Ages read them and borrowed from them. Bonaventure followed Richard closely, and so did the English mystics of the fourteenth century. And the abbey's sequences stayed in the liturgy long after the school that wrote them had gone quiet.
