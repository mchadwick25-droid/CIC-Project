The Age of Monks and Empires · c. 500-525; dead by 556 · Rome

Rome, today in Italy

Dionysius Exiguus

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Early Benedictine / Italian Monasticism.

Known for
Monk at Rome who put the church's canons into Latin from Greek copies and gathered the rulings of past popes. His Easter tables number the years from the Incarnation of Christ instead of from the emperor Diocletian.
Also called
Dionysius monachus, Dionysius abbas Romanus, Denys le Petit
Dates
No source used here gives the year of his birth or death. Cassiodorus writes of him as dead in his Institutions. William Cave, whose notice Migne reprints, says he died before 556, the year Cave gives for that book. The Easter rules printed with his letters take the consulship of Probus the Younger as their example year and count it as 525. Bede, as Migne's testimonia quote him, says his cycles begin with the year 532. Henry Percival dates his translation of the canons about 500
Place
Scythia by birth, Cassiodorus says; Rome, where Bede calls him an abbot of the city
Role
Monk, called abbot in some sources; translator from Greek; collector of church canons and papal rulings; maker of Easter tables

About This Person

Cassiodorus remembered a monk who could take any Greek book and run through it in Latin without a stumble. He could read Latin books back again in Greek. You would think, Cassiodorus says, that the words pouring from his mouth had already been written down.

That monk was Dionysius Exiguus, who worked at Rome in the early 500s. Cassiodorus says he was a Scythian by birth but wholly Roman in his ways. The two studied logic together. Dionysius never set himself above the lowest servants, Cassiodorus writes, though he was fit to speak with kings.

Bishop Stephen of Salona asked him to put the church's canons, or council rulings, into Latin from Greek copies. Dionysius set the canons called the Apostles' first, and noted that many did not readily accept them. Cassiodorus says the Roman church used his canons widely, and he told his own monks to read them often.

For Julian, a Roman priest, Dionysius gathered the rulings of past popes. He also put Gregory of Nyssa's book on the making of man into Latin for the priest Eugippius. Some points in it could rightly be faulted, he warned. He had set them out as a translator, he said, and had not approved them as a censor.

At the urging of Bishop Petronius, Dionysius set out the rule for Easter. He wrote that he followed the bishops of Nicaea and their nineteen-year cycle, which Cyril of Alexandria had kept. Louis Duchesne says Nicaea made no such ruling, and that Dionysius drew it from a later letter from Alexandria. Cyril had counted his years from the emperor Diocletian, but Dionysius would not tie his tables to the memory of a wicked persecutor. He numbered the years from the Incarnation instead. That way, he wrote, the start of our hope would be better known, and the Redeemer's passion would shine more clearly.

Later writers differ over his part in the count of years from Christ. William Cave says Dionysius was the first to reckon years this way. Johann Wilhelm Janus agrees, but notes that some credit Bede. Charles Plummer says Bede was the first chronicler to date events from Christ's birth, and so brought the Dionysian era into Western Europe.

Cassiodorus wrote after his death that Dionysius used to pray with them. He asked Dionysius to plead for them, so that those once upheld by his prayers might be helped by his merits.

Key Works

  • Collection of church canons: the canons of the councils in Latin, made from Greek copies at the request of Bishop Stephen of Salona. It sets first the canons called the Apostles', which he says many did not readily accept. Then come the canons of Nicaea and of the councils before and after it up to the council of Constantinople. Those of Chalcedon follow, and last the Latin canons of Sardica and of Africa
  • Collection of papal rulings: the decrees of past bishops of Rome, gathered for Julian, priest of the church of Saint Anastasia. William Cave says it runs from Siricius to Anastasius II
  • Two letters on the reckoning of Easter, with Easter tables: to Bishop Petronius, and to Bonifatius, first of the notaries, and Bonus, the second. He followed the nineteen-year cycle he credits to the bishops of Nicaea. He set first the last cycle of Cyril of Alexandria, of which six years remained. Then he added five cycles of his own, numbering the years from the Incarnation

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Cassiodorus says the Roman church used his canons widely in his day, and he told the monks he wrote for to read them often.
  • Abbot Ceolfrid wrote a letter that Bede gives in his Ecclesiastical History. In it he says that after Cyril of Alexandria, Dionysius added as many cycles again, reaching down to Ceolfrid's own time.
  • Theodor Mommsen says the Easter table of Dionysius was received in Italy by common consent.
  • A treatise on the course of the moon, printed among Alcuin's works, quotes his letter to Bonifatius and Bonus.
  • Louis Duchesne writes that Pope Hadrian sent the collection of Dionysius to Charlemagne.

How they're remembered

  • Bede's On the Reckoning of Time is quoted in Migne's testimonia. There Bede calls him the venerable abbot of the city of Rome, learned in Greek and Latin. He repeats Dionysius's own reason for not counting from Diocletian.
  • Charles Plummer says Bede was the first chronicler to date events from Christ's birth, and so brought the Dionysian era into Western Europe. Papal documents did not use it until the eleventh century, Plummer adds.
  • Thomas Hodgkin says Dionysius fixed the era of Christ's birth wrongly, as it now appears. His reckoning, Hodgkin adds, was accepted by all the nations of Christendom.

What Is Uncertain

  • Later writers differ over his part in the count of years from Christ. His own letter says only that he numbered his tables from the Incarnation. William Cave says Dionysius was the first to use it for reckoning years. Johann Wilhelm Janus holds the same, but notes that others think the era came later, by Bede's authority. Janus also reports Jean Hardouin's view that the era was in use across the Christian world early in the fifth century, before Dionysius. Janus argues against it. Charles Plummer credits Bede with bringing it into Western Europe. Ernest Brehaut says Isidore of Seville shows no sign of knowing the era, though he knew the Easter table.
  • The Nicene rule. Dionysius says the bishops of Nicaea fixed the nineteen-year cycle. Louis Duchesne, after Bruno Krusch, says Nicaea made no such ruling. Duchesne says Dionysius drew it from the letter of Proterius of Alexandria to Pope Leo. With Krusch, he calls it a pious fraud put to the service of a sound reckoning.
  • His life rests almost wholly on Cassiodorus, who knew him and wrote of him after his death. Johann Wilhelm Janus, in a letter printed in Migne's volume, says Cassiodorus was nearly the only one of his time to keep his memory. All later writers on his life followed him, Janus says. Cassiodorus adds that bad men set some things under his name to excuse their own errors.
  • His years. No source used here gives his birth or death. William Cave says he died before 556. Robert Bellarmine, as Migne's testimonia quote him, places him under King Theodoric and his successors. The Easter rules printed with his letters, which a manuscript title gives to Dionysius, take 525 as their example year. Johann Wilhelm Janus notes that Bede and others reworked them for later years.
  • Monk or abbot. Cassiodorus calls him a monk in the body of his chapter, and abbot in its heading. Bede and Cave call him abbot. Johann Wilhelm Janus reports that his monastery is unknown, and that Antoine Pagi denied him the rank, saying Bede first gave it. Janus answers that Felix, abbot of Gillitanum, called him abbot of the city of Rome in 616, and takes the title as one of honor.
  • His translations. William Cave lists Latin versions of Proclus of Constantinople under his name. He reports that Jean Garnier gave the sermon on the Mother of God to Marius Mercator instead. Dionysius presents his Latin version of the Alexandrian council's letter against Nestorius as the first and as his own. Louis Duchesne, after Friedrich Maassen, says it is copied from that of Marius Mercator.
  • His name. He is a different man from Dionysius of Alexandria and Dionysius of Rome, the bishops, and from Dionysius the Areopagite. No source used here ties him to the writings under the Areopagite's name.
