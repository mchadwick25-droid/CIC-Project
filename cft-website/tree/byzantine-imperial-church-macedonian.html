The Byzantine Imperial Church (Macedonian Era)

Constantinople, 843 to 1054 — icons back on the walls, missionaries sent to the Slavs, and an argument with Rome that ended with two churches excommunicating each other inside Hagia Sophia.

About This Tradition

With the icons restored in 843, the Byzantine church entered two centuries of confidence. It recovered its learning and sent missions that converted Bulgaria, Moravia and eventually Rus'. Photius is the age's emblem: a layman raised straight to the patriarchate, an enormous reader who summarized 279 books, by his own count, many of which survive nowhere else, and the man who put in its sharpest form the Eastern objection to the Latin West's addition of one clause, the Filioque, to the Creed. That argument, along with disputes over papal authority and over who had jurisdiction among the newly converted Slavs, hardened across the following century and a half, until in 1054 a papal delegation and the patriarch of Constantinople excommunicated one another inside Hagia Sophia. The conventional date for a division that neither side at the time understood as final.

Documented Stories

A Hundred and Six Questions from Bulgaria

Boris of Bulgaria had been baptized in 864 with the emperor Michael III as godfather, had put down a pagan revolt of his nobles by killing fifty-two of them with their families, and had then, finding the Greek clergy unwilling to give him his own patriarch, turned to Rome. The questionnaire he sent Nicholas I does not survive, but Nicholas’ reply does, and from it the questions can be reconstructed. They are the questions of a people trying to learn what a Christian life would actually require. What should be done with a man who has slept with his sister-in-law? Must they stop working on Sundays and feast days? May a man have two wives? What of those who fled the country before baptism and want to return? Nicholas answers each — one hundred and six answers — with a patience that surprised his own clergy, and with a consistent line: customs that do no harm may stay, sins must go, and force is not to be used. On the question of whether a thief or robber should be tortured to extract a confession, his answer became a text in canon law: ‘this neither divine nor human law permits’, since a confession must be voluntary and a man might confess to what he had not done to end the pain. Boris used the answers, and the Greek reaction to them, to play the two churches against each other, and got his autonomous archbishopric from Constantinople in 870.

The Emperor Turned Away at the Door on Christmas Day

Byzantine church law permitted a second marriage with penance and a third barely; a fourth was forbidden, and Leo VI himself had legislated to say so. His mistress Zoe Karbonopsina bore one in 905, and the patriarch Nicholas Mystikos agreed to baptize the child, the future Constantine VII, on condition that Zoe leave the palace. Instead Leo married her, and had a palace priest crown her empress. On Christmas Day 906 Leo came in procession to Hagia Sophia and Nicholas met him at the imperial door and would not let him in. Nicholas was, on his own account, trying to protect the law and offer the emperor a penitential way back. Leo appealed to Rome and the eastern patriarchs, all of whom allowed a dispensation, and in February 907 he deposed Nicholas, exiled him to a monastery, and installed a patriarch who admitted him. Nicholas’ letters survive, more than a hundred and sixty of them, and they show a man who did not think he had been wrong and did not think the emperor had either — he calls the marriage a sin and Leo a son. He was restored after Leo’s death, presided over a council that condemned fourth marriages while accepting Constantine VII’s legitimacy, and served as regent for the boy whose birth had caused it all. The imperial church of the Macedonian age is often described as the emperor’s creature; on Christmas morning 906 it was not.

Connections

In Its Own Record

The church of the restored icons. It saw Photius twice made patriarch, the Photian schism, and the first round of the Filioque dispute. It sent missions to Bulgaria in 864 and, with Cyril and Methodius, to the Slavs. It ends with the break of 1054.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced Iconophile Byzantine Monasticism Documented — Photius, patriarch from 858, read and praised the work of two iconophile writers. One was the patriarch Nicephorus, the other Sergius the Confessor. His notes on Sergius speak of the lawless and vile acts of Constantine V. His church kept a feast each year for the victory of the icons.

Influenced

  • influenced Athonite & Comnenian Byzantine Monasticism Dominant Modern Reconstruction — Robert Curzon was an English visitor to Athos in the 1830s. He cites an account from 1701: the Great Lavra 'was founded by Nicephorus Phocas', the emperor. That report is late and secondhand. But a modern study by Rosemary Morris, from 1995, tells how Phocas paid for the house that the monk Athanasios built.

Argued Against

  • argued against Paulicians Dominant Modern Reconstruction — After 843 the empress Theodora had the Paulicians hunted, and the patriarch Photius wrote against them. Peter of Sicily, sent to Tephrike by Basil I, wrote a book against them to warn the new church in Bulgaria. In 970 John Tzimiskes moved Paulicians from Asia to Plovdiv.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

With icons restored in 843, the Byzantine church sent missions that converted Bulgaria, Moravia and eventually Rus', while in Constantinople the patriarch Photius — a layman raised straight to the office, and a reader who summarized 279 books, by his own count, many of them since lost — sharpened the Eastern case against the Latin church's addition of the Filioque to the Creed. That dispute, and others over jurisdiction among the newly converted Slavs, hardened for a century until 1054, when a papal delegation and the patriarch excommunicated each other inside Hagia Sophia.

Legacy

The same church continued as the Byzantine church after the schism and, through it, into the Orthodox churches of today. Two of its acts shaped the map permanently. The mission to the Slavs, which made most of eastern Europe Orthodox and Slavonic-speaking, and the rupture of 1054, whose mutual excommunications were only lifted in 1965, when Pope Paul VI and Patriarch Athenagoras revoked them together. Photius's Bibliotheca preserves summaries of 279 books, by his own count. A great many of them survive in no other form.
