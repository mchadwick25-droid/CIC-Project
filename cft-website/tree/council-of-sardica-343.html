The Imperial Church Era · 343 · Sardica in Illyricum, Bulgaria

Council of Sardica

Major council

Known for
The council the emperors Constans and Constantius called at Sardica to settle the case of Athanasius of Alexandria, which split into a Western council that cleared him and an Eastern one that condemned his defenders.
Dates
Socrates of Constantinople and Sozomen give the consulship of Rufinus and Eusebius, which their editors take as 347. Charles Joseph Hefele, from the Easter letters of Athanasius, their preface and the Historia Acephala, dates the opening to the autumn of 343, perhaps running into 344. Archibald Robertson puts it in 343, the index to the Easter letters giving the Egyptian year 342 to 343; Mansi, as Hefele reports, placed it in 344
Rivers
Greek East, Latin West
Called by
The emperors Constans and Constantius jointly, at Constans's request (Athanasius, History of the Arians 15; Socrates of Constantinople II.20)

About This Council

At Sardica, the accusers of Athanasius of Alexandria came to the church where Ossius of Cordoba was staying. Ossius later told the emperor Constantius his offer. If they would not speak before the whole council, they could speak "before me alone." If Athanasius was guilty, the bishops would reject him. If he was innocent and they still shunned him, Ossius would take him home to Spain. Athanasius agreed. They refused.

Bishops of the East had deposed him. Julius, bishop of Rome, had received him into communion, as he had Marcellus of Ancyra. Constans, emperor of the West, pressed his brother Constantius, and the two called a council at Sardica.

Socrates of Constantinople and Sozomen date it by a consulship that their editors take as 347. Charles Joseph Hefele, the historian of the councils, works from Athanasius's Easter letters and puts its start late in 343.

Most of the record comes from one side. Athanasius kept the Western letters, and Ossius's, in his own books. The Eastern bishops' letter survives in Latin, in papers gathered by Hilary of Poitiers, who held to Nicaea.

Athanasius says about 170 bishops came. The Eastern letter speaks of eighty from the East, and Hefele puts the West at about ninety-five.

The Eastern bishops would not sit with men they had already condemned. Their letter says they found the two men sharing the sacrament with Ossius. It says Marcellus would "set bounds to the perpetual, eternal, and timeless kingdom of our Lord Christ." The West would not shut them out, so the Easterners left. Socrates says they met on their own at Philippopolis in Thrace.

Each side then condemned the other. The Westerners cleared both men. They deposed their accusers, among them Valens of Mursa and Ursacius of Singidunum. They kept the creed of Nicaea. Under Ossius they also made rules for appeals. A bishop who lost his case could write to Julius, to "honour the memory of Peter the Apostle." The Easterners deposed Julius and Ossius, Sozomen says.

Socrates says East and West shared communion only up to a mountain called Soucis, "which divides the Illyrians from the Thracians." Constans threatened his brother with war, and Athanasius went home in 346, by the index to his Easter letters. Later, Pope Zosimus quoted a Sardica rule to the bishops of Africa as a rule of Nicaea. They could not find it in their copies.

Why They Met

  • Eastern bishops had deposed Athanasius of Alexandria, Marcellus of Ancyra, Paul of Constantinople and Asclepas of Gaza (Sozomen III.11; Athanasius, Defence against the Arians).
  • Julius, bishop of Rome, had looked into the case and received Athanasius into communion. The Western bishops later said he had the testimony of eighty bishops (Western synodal letter, in Athanasius, Defence against the Arians 37).
  • The question was whether the Eastern sentences stood. The Eastern bishops held that men their synods had condemned could not sit in council. The Western bishops held that Julius had cleared them and that they were ready to answer (Sozomen III.11; the Eastern letter, as Hefele reports it).
  • Behind the case lay the creed of Nicaea. The Western bishops held to it; the Eastern bishops issued a creed without the word consubstantial (Socrates of Constantinople II.20; Sozomen III.11).
  • Constans, emperor of the West, wrote to his brother Constantius, and the two agreed to call a council (Athanasius, History of the Arians 15; Socrates II.20).

Who Came

  • About 170 bishops from East and West, says Athanasius of Alexandria, who was there and was the man on trial.
  • Eighty from the East, by the Eastern bishops' own letter. Seventy-three sign it; Hefele adds three known to be present and reaches seventy-six, the number Socrates and Sozomen give.
  • About 300 from the West, say Socrates of Constantinople and Sozomen; 250 in all, says Theodoret of Cyrrhus. Hefele thinks Socrates counted bishops who signed the decrees later.
  • Hefele reckons about ninety-four to ninety-seven Western bishops, from Athanasius's figure and the surviving lists; Robertson reaches ninety-seven. Athanasius gives a list of names that includes later signers.
  • Julius of Rome sent two presbyters, Archidamus and Philoxenus. The Eastern bishops brought two imperial officials, Musonianus and Hesychius, Athanasius says.

What They Decided

  • The Western bishops cleared Athanasius, Marcellus of Ancyra and Asclepas of Gaza and told their churches to receive them (Western synodal letters, in Athanasius, Defence against the Arians 36-50; Sozomen III.12).
  • They deposed Theodore of Heraclea, Narcissus of Neronias, Acacius of Caesarea, Stephen of Antioch, Ursacius, Valens, Menophantus of Ephesus and George of Laodicea (Athanasius, Defence against the Arians 36).
  • They kept the creed of Nicaea. A fuller statement drafted by Ossius and Protogenes, Hefele thinks, was not adopted but found its way into the record (Socrates II.20; Hefele).
  • Under Ossius they made about twenty canons, in Greek and Latin. The first forbids a bishop to move from a small city to a larger one. The third lets a condemned bishop's case be referred to Julius of Rome, to 'honour the memory of Peter the Apostle' (the canons, npnf214).
  • The Eastern bishops confirmed their sentences on Athanasius, Paul, Marcellus and Asclepas. They deposed Julius of Rome, Ossius, Maximus of Trier, Protogenes of Sardica and Gaudentius of Naissus, and forbade communion with them. Their creed, Hefele says, is the fourth formula of Antioch (Sozomen III.11; the Eastern letter in Hilary's dossier).

What Came of It

  • Socrates of Constantinople says the Western church was now severed from the Eastern, and that communion stopped at the mountain called Soucis, between the Illyrians and the Thracians.
  • Socrates gives a letter of Constans threatening Constantius with war unless Athanasius and Paul were restored. Constantius then wrote to recall Athanasius, who reached Alexandria in October 346, by the index to his Easter letters.
  • The canons were later received by the Greek church through the Council in Trullo, and in many Western collections they followed the canons of Nicaea without a break, Henry Percival says.
  • Pope Zosimus, in 417 or 418, quoted the fifth canon of Sardica to the African bishops as a canon of Nicaea. They could not find it in their copies, Hefele says, as Percival quotes him.
  • Constantius refused to acknowledge its decrees, and no later authority called it a general council, Hefele says.

Traditions It Touched

Gathered

  • Athanasius of Alexandria: His case was the council's main business; the Western bishops cleared him.
  • Imperial and Juridical Christianity: Constans and Constantius called the council, and Constans later threatened war to have its decision carried out.

Condemned

  • Latin Christianity in the Danubian Provinces: The Western bishops deposed Valens of Mursa and Ursacius of Singidunum, bishops of these provinces.

Refused

  • Homoian ('Arian') Christianity: The Eastern bishops withdrew and issued a creed without the word consubstantial; Valens and Ursacius, who were with them, later led the Homoian church of the West.

Whose Account We Have

  • The canons of Sardica (acts): Held in Greek and Latin versions in English translation; they name Ossius as their proposer.
  • The Western synodal letters, in Athanasius, Defence against the Arians 36-50 (acts): Kept by Athanasius, the man the council cleared. On his side.
  • The Eastern bishops' letter, in the dossier of Hilary of Poitiers (CSEL 65, Series A IV 1) (acts): The Eastern side in its own words, in a Latin version; the Greek is lost. Kept by Hilary, a committed Nicene. An English passage is quoted in A. C. Zenos's notes to Socrates.
  • Athanasius of Alexandria, History of the Arians 15-17 and 44, with the letter of Ossius to Constantius (participant): Written about a dozen years later by the accused, against Constantius's party. Ossius's letter survives only here.
  • Socrates of Constantinople II.20-23; Sozomen III.11-13; Theodoret of Cyrrhus II.6-8 (later narrative): Church historians of the next century, on the Nicene side. Socrates and Sozomen give the wrong year, Hefele says, and depend on each other for it.
  • Charles Joseph Hefele, History of the Councils II; Henry Percival's notes; Archibald Robertson's prolegomena (modern reconstruction): Supply the date, the numbers on each side and the reading of the Eastern letter.

What Is Uncertain

  • Most of the record comes from one side. The Western letters and Ossius's letter survive in Athanasius's own books. The Eastern letter survives only in a Latin version kept by Hilary of Poitiers, a committed Nicene.
  • The date is disputed. Socrates and Sozomen give 347. Hefele dates it to the autumn of 343, perhaps into 344; Robertson to 343; Mansi, as Hefele reports, to 344. Percival heads it 343 or 344.
  • The numbers differ widely. Athanasius gives about 170 in one place and more than 300 assenting in another; Socrates and Sozomen about 300 Westerners and 76 Easterners; Theodoret 250; the Eastern letter eighty. Hefele reckons about ninety-four to ninety-seven Westerners.
  • Where the Eastern bishops met is told differently. Socrates says they withdrew to Philippopolis. Sozomen says they gathered there first and then came to Sardica. Their letter says it was written from Sardica, which Hefele reports Tillemont and Ceillier took for a lie.
  • Socrates says the Eastern bishops rejected consubstantial and brought in the word 'unlike'. Hefele says their creed has no such word and condemns the Anomoean teaching.
  • Whether the Western council issued a creed of its own is disputed. Athanasius, as Hefele reads him, says it refused to make one; Theodoret gives one; Hefele thinks it was a draft by Ossius and Protogenes that was not adopted.
  • Whether Ossius presided for the bishop of Rome is disputed. Hefele thinks he may have had a commission from the pope. Van Espen, quoted by Percival, finds no trace of such a legation.
  • Why Constantius gave way is told differently. Socrates gives Constans's threat of war. Hefele points also to the disgrace of Stephen of Antioch, which made Constantius trust the Eastern party less.
