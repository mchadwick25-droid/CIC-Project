Eadburga of Thanet

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Anglo-Saxon Christianity.

Known for
An English abbess of the eighth century who sent books and vestments to Boniface of Mainz in Germany. He asked her to copy the Epistles of Saint Peter in gold. She taught Leoba the art of verse.
Also called
Eadburga, abbess, Eadburge (in one of Edward Kylie's headings), Eadburg (Charles Plummer)
Dates
Eighth century. Edward Kylie dates the letters to her from about 717 to the 740s. The Bollandists, who take her to be Bugga, give her death day as 27 December; the year cannot be read in the scan used here. No source used here gives her birth
Place
Thanet, in Edward Kylie's headings and introduction; the letters themselves do not name her house
Role
Abbess; teacher of Leoba; supplier of books to the Boniface mission

About This Person

Boniface of Mainz, the English missionary in Germany, wrote to an abbess with a request. He asked her "to copy in gold the Epistles of my lord, Saint Peter". The Scriptures would then be honored, he wrote, "when the preacher holds them before the eyes of the heathen." She had often eased his distress already, he said, with gifts of books and vestments.

The abbess was Eadburga, who lived in the eighth century. Edward Kylie, who put Boniface's letters into English in 1911, heads the letters to her as letters to the abbess of Thanet. He dates this one to 735. None of her own letters is in his book. She is known from what others wrote to her and about her.

Boniface wrote to her over many years. About 717, by Kylie's date, he sent her an account she had asked for. A man in the convent of Abbess Milburga at Wenlock had died and come back to life. Boniface wrote down the vision the man told him. Later he thanked her for books of Scripture, which had consoled "an exile in Germany". In a hard time he asked for her prayers. The snares of false brethren, he wrote, "surpass the malice of the heathen."

Leoba, a young woman of Boniface's circle, learned verse from her. She sent Boniface four lines of her own. "The art I have learned from the teaching of Eadburga," Leoba wrote, "who continues without ceasing to search into the divine law." Kylie says Leoba had been sent to Thanet, where Eadburga taught her the art of verse. Lul, Boniface's deacon, wrote to Eadburga too. He sent her a silver stylus, storax and cinnamon, and asked for her prayers.

The Bollandists, editors of the Acta Sanctorum, report a belief that she was an abbess in Kent also called Bugga. Kylie keeps the two women apart. His Bugga is the daughter of another abbess, Eangyth, and her own name was Heaburg.

Boniface opened one letter to her this way: "To the Abbess Eadburga, beloved sister, now long bound to him in the kinship of spiritual love."

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Boniface thanked her for gifts of books and vestments. He asked her to copy in gold the Epistles of Saint Peter, to honor the Scriptures before the heathen.
  • Leoba wrote that she had learned the art of verse from the teaching of Eadburga.
  • Kylie writes that Boniface trusted the gold letters of Saint Peter to her skilled hands. He adds that English convents sent him books, vestments and clothing.
  • Lul, Boniface's deacon, sent her a silver stylus, storax and cinnamon, and asked for her prayers and a letter.

How they're remembered

  • Boniface opened one letter to her: "To the Abbess Eadburga, beloved sister, now long bound to him in the kinship of spiritual love."
  • Leoba remembered her as a woman "who continues without ceasing to search into the divine law".
  • Charles Plummer, in his edition of Bede, cites the gold letters to her as a sign that nuns as well as monks worked as copyists.

What Is Uncertain

  • None of her own letters is in Kylie's book. She is known from letters written to her and from Leoba's letter.
  • Her house. Kylie heads the letters to her as letters to the abbess of Thanet, and says Leoba was sent to her there. The letters themselves name no place.
  • Whether she was also Bugga. The Bollandists, editors of the Acta Sanctorum, report a belief that Eadburga, also called Bugga, was an abbess in Kent. Kylie keeps Eadburga and Bugga apart.
  • A church at Thanet. A note in Migne's edition of Alcuin's poems guesses that a poem on an apse of Saint Peter is about the church at Thanet. It states that Eadburga built that church, and gives no source.
  • Her years. Kylie dates the letters to her from about 717 to the 740s. The Bollandists give her death day as 27 December, the day Bregwin of Canterbury gives for an abbess Bugga. That rests on their view that she was Bugga.
  • Who sent the silver stylus. Kylie, Charles Plummer and Louis Duchesne give the letter to Lul. A note in Patrologia Latina 103 gives it to Boniface.
