The Roman Church in the Age of Gregory the Great

Rome in 590 had a plague, no working government and the Lombards outside the walls; the man who ended up running it had been trying to be a monk.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 2:01

Read along

The Rome Gregory was born into around 540 had been besieged, taken, retaken and emptied within living memory. The Gothic wars and Justinian's reconquest had wrecked the city and the countryside that fed it. From 568 the Lombards held most of the peninsula. The imperial government sat at Ravenna and had little left to spend on Rome. Gregory was city prefect and gave it up. He turned the family house on the Caelian into a monastery, and was sent to Constantinople as the pope's representative. He was elected bishop in 590, during a plague, and spent fourteen years doing a job that had quietly become something else. The letters show what it was. He ran the papal estates in Sicily as the city's food supply and audited the men who managed them. He paid soldiers and bargained with Lombard dukes when the exarch would not. He ransomed prisoners. He told bishops in Gaul, Spain, Africa and Illyricum what he thought they were doing wrong, at length. He refused the title of universal bishop when Constantinople's patriarch used it. He already called himself servant of the servants of God, a title Pope Damasus and Augustine had used before him. In 596 he sent a party of his own monks to Kent. Then he wrote to them again and again about what to do with pagan temples, and how far to insist on Roman custom. The Pastoral Rule, written at the start of all this, is the book underneath the rest. It argues that the government of souls is the art of arts, and that different people must be told the same truth in different ways. And it holds that to want the office is already to be unfit for it.

Documented Stories

The Procession of 590

Gregory of Tours had the story from a deacon of his own church who was in Rome that winter, and he put it in the last book of his History of the Franks. The Tiber had flooded in November 589, destroying the granaries; the plague followed; Pope Pelagius II died of it in February. The clergy and people elected Gregory, the deacon who had been the pope’s envoy to Constantinople and was now living as a monk in his family’s house on the Caelian, and while the election went to the emperor for confirmation Gregory preached to the city. He then organized what came to be called the sevenfold litany. On the appointed morning the city set out from seven churches in seven processions — the clergy, the laymen, the monks, the nuns, the married women, the widows, the children and the poor — each led by priests, all converging on the basilica of St Mary Major, singing Kyrie eleison through streets full of the dying. The deacon told Gregory of Tours that within the space of an hour, while the people were still processing and praying, eighty of them fell dead in the streets. The later legend of an angel sheathing a sword over Hadrian’s tomb belongs to the Middle Ages; no one who was there mentions it. What they recorded was a city walking through its own plague toward a church, and a bishop-elect who thought that was the right thing to ask of it.

Give the Synagogue Back

The letter is addressed to Januarius, bishop of Caralis — Cagliari, in Sardinia — and it opens with a complaint. ‘The Jews who have come hither from your city have complained to us that Peter, who has been brought by the will of God from their superstition to the worship of Christian faith, having taken with him certain disorderly persons, on the day after his baptism, that is on the Lord’s day of the very Paschal festival, with grave scandal and without your consent, had taken possession of their synagogue in Caralis’, and placed in it an image of the Mother of God, a cross, and the white baptismal robe he had worn out of the font. He then tells the bishop what to do: remove the image and the cross ‘with fitting reverence’, and ‘restore what has been violently taken away’ — because, though the law forbids Jews to build new synagogues, it allows them to keep their old ones without disturbance. And he anticipates the objection. If Peter or his helpers say they did it ‘in zeal for the faith, in order that a necessity of being converted might thereby be imposed on the Jews’, they are to be told that ‘moderation should rather be used’, since those brought to the font by force do not come as believers. Two centuries after Ambrose defended the burning of a synagogue at Callinicum and refused to let the emperor punish it, the bishop of Rome ordered one restored.

Do Not Destroy the Temples

Mellitus was traveling through Gaul with reinforcements for the mission Gregory had sent to the English five years before when a second letter overtook him. ‘I have long been considering with myself about the case of the Angli; to wit, that the temples of idols in that nation should not be destroyed, but that the idols themselves that are in them should be.’ Let holy water be sprinkled in the temples, altars built and relics placed, ‘since, if these same temples are well built, it is needful that they should be transferred from the worship of idols to the service of the true God’ — so that the people, seeing their familiar places kept, ‘may have recourse with the more familiarity to the places they have been accustomed to’. The same for the feasts: since the English are used to slaughtering oxen for their gods, let them keep a festival on the dedication day or a martyr’s feast, build booths of branches round the converted temples, and kill the animals for a meal to God’s praise instead. ‘For it is undoubtedly impossible to cut away everything at once from hard hearts, since one who strives to ascend to the highest place must needs rise by steps or paces, and not by leaps.’ Bede copied the second letter into his history, and it became the Western church’s standard theory of how a pagan people is to be converted — by re-use rather than erasure.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Gregory the Great was a monk and wrote the only life of Benedict there is. But his own work was a bishop's: the Pastoral Rule is a manual for bishops, and the Register is the paperwork of a government. His letters also tell us about the early English church, the Frankish church of Gaul and the imperial church at Constantinople.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced Early Benedictine / Italian Monasticism Documented — Gregory the Great tells the life of Benedict in his Dialogues. He praises the rule Benedict wrote for his monks as 'excellent for discretion'. Gregory says he had his stories from four of the saint's disciples. One was Constantinus, the next abbot after him.
  • influenced Desert Monasticism Inferential-Thin — Cassian heard Abbot Serapion of Scetis list eight principal faults, ending with pride. Gregory the Great later made pride 'the queen of sins' and set seven principal sins beneath her. Gregory names no source, so the tie rests on the likeness of the two lists.

Influenced

  • influenced Carolingian Reform Christianity Widely Accepted — A Carolingian list sets out what clergy must learn. It tells canons to know 'the pastoral book', which its editor says is Gregory the Great's. James Barmby, who translated Gregory, goes further. He says that in 813, councils called by Charlemagne made all bishops study it.
  • influenced Late Anglo-Saxon Christianity Inferential-Thin — King Alfred had Gregory the Great's Pastoral Rule put into West Saxon English. He said he meant to send a copy to each bishop in his kingdom. We have this from James Barmby, Gregory's translator, writing in 1895. He points to an 1871 edition of Alfred's English text. No modern study of it has been consulted.

Argued Against

  • argued against The Byzantine Imperial Church (Justinianic) Documented — In June 595 Gregory the Great wrote to Emperor Maurice. He said John, the patriarch of Constantinople, was trying to be called universal bishop. Gregory calls the title a new name. He says John took it against the Gospel and the canons.
  • argued against Donatism Documented — Pope Gregory the Great wrote to Columbus, a Numidian bishop, in the early 590s. He had heard that the Donatists were spreading and baptizing many people a second time. He ordered a council of bishops to look into it.

Opposed By

  • argued against Insular / Irish Monastic Christianity Documented — Around 600 the Irish monk Columbanus wrote from Luxeuil to Pope Gregory the Great. He stood up for the Irish date of Easter and asked why Gregory kept a dark Easter. Irish scholars, he said, had never taken up the Easter tables of Victorius. No reply from Gregory survives.

In Contact With

  • in contact with The Byzantine Imperial Church (Justinianic) Documented — Before he was pope, Gregory the Great lived at Constantinople as the pope's envoy. In his Morals on Job, he recalls a debate on the resurrection with Eutychius, the city's bishop. It went on until Emperor Tiberius heard them both. Paul the Deacon tells the same story in the late 700s.
  • in contact with Merovingian Gallic Christianity Documented — Gregory the Great wrote often to Gaul. He answered Queen Brunhild's letters, and warned four leading bishops that holy orders there were being bought. Gregory of Tours recorded the pope's election in 590, as his own deacon reported it on his return from Rome.
  • in contact with Nicene Visigothic Iberia Documented — Gregory the Great first met Leander of Seville at Constantinople, and the two kept writing to each other. In one letter Gregory said that Leander's letters had made him love King Reccared. Later he wrote to the king himself. He praised the conversion of the whole Gothic nation.
  • in contact with Latin African Christianity under Byzantium Documented Major — Gregory the Great wrote to all the bishops of Numidia, answering a request they had sent to his predecessor. He let them keep their old customs for choosing a senior bishop. But he barred bishops who had come over from the Donatists from that rank.Lasting result: Pope Gregory the Great let the Numidian bishops keep their old way to pick a head bishop, but not one who had been a Donatist.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Gregory the Great became bishop of Rome in 590, during a plague, in a city whose Roman government had already collapsed under the Gothic wars and the Lombard invasions. He ran the papal estates in Sicily as the city's food supply, negotiated directly with Lombard dukes when the imperial governor at Ravenna would not, and in 596 sent his own monks to Kent to begin a mission whose questions and his answers still survive in his letters.

Legacy

The Pastoral Rule became the standard book on the bishop's office in the West, and it stayed in use for a thousand years. Carolingian councils told all bishops to study it. Hincmar of Rheims reports that new bishops were handed a copy at their consecration. Alfred had it translated into English, meaning, he said, to send a copy to every bishop in his kingdom. The mission to Kent is why there is an English church in communion with Rome at all. It is also why the early English church has a Roman half. Then there is the working model of the bishop of Rome as the practical government of central Italy. He fed the city from church estates, dealt with invaders and paid troops. That is where the temporal power of the papacy really begins, whatever later documents claimed for it.

How Sure the Record Is

Very good. Gregory the Great's Book of Pastoral Rule (about seventy thousand words) and the Register of his letters (a hundred and sixty thousand) both survive — the Register especially valuable because it is a working archive rather than a literary work: grain shipments, episcopal discipline, the ransom of captives, instructions to a mission, and the daily correspondence of a man administering a city the empire had largely stopped administering.
