The Transylvanian Unitarian Church, 1660 to 1906

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

At the Diet of Warsaw in 1658, the Catholics brought a formal charge against the Polish Unitarians, the English Unitarian Thomas Rees wrote in 1818. A decree then banned their public worship on pain of death. It told them to leave Poland within three years, unless they joined the Catholic, Lutheran or Calvinist church.

József Ferencz, a Unitarian pastor at Kolozsvár, dates the ban to 1659. Rees says a harsher decree of 20 July 1660 told them to leave at once. Ferencz says the diet of 1660 set 10 July as the last day to turn Catholic or leave.

Rees says about four hundred went to Transylvania and Hungary. Ferencz says about 380 set out for Hungary. At the border, robbers took all they had.

Some settled in Hungary. Thirty to forty families went on to Transylvania. Their brothers in the faith took them in with love.

Most of them settled at Kolozsvár. There they founded a separate Polish church under the Transylvanian bishop.

They still worshipped in Polish around 1792. Their last pastor was Izsák Száknovics. Later they merged with the Hungarian congregation.

Pál Augustinovics was a descendant of the exiles from Poland. In 1837 he left his whole estate to the church.

Bishop Dániel Beké served until 1661. János Járai held the seat from April to June of that year. The seat then stood empty for two years, because the wars made church meetings impossible. Boldizsár Koncz was bishop from 1663 to 1684.

In 1691 Transylvania ceased to be a separate state and came under the Austrian emperor. In 1693 the Unitarians had to give up their college in the Óvár, the old castle quarter of Kolozsvár. It had been theirs since 1566.

The church's account of 1907, compiled under Bishop Ferencz, says the Jesuits fell upon the college. It says the emperor's army helped them.

A fire in 1697 burned down part of the market church and its buildings. The church restored them, partly with its own people's gifts. Pál Dimény, the head teacher, also collected 9,000 thalers in the Low Countries (Ferencz writes "Belgium and Holland"), from Remonstrant and "collegian" brothers.

On 30 March 1716 an army commander named Steinville took the market church by force. On 2 April Bishop György Mártonffi, a Catholic, led worship in it. The bishop's house, the school, the suburban church of Saint Peter and all the property that went with them were taken next.

More churches were lost, among them Torda in 1721 and Brassó in 1777. Under Charles III and Maria Theresa the Unitarians were slowly shut out of every public office. Their equal rights in religion were denied, and their writing was halted.

Mihály Szentábrahámi Lombárd led the rebuilding. His contemporaries said he was the eyes, heart and mouth of the Unitarians. In 1718, with nine students, he opened the church's third college. He was bishop from 1737 to 1758.

In 1791 László Zsuki gave the church all the capital he had gathered, and named the Kolozsvár college in particular. The college building of 1806 rose from his gift. The new Kolozsvár church, beside the college, was opened on 14 December 1796.

In 1848 the diet at Pozsony made the Unitarian faith a lawful religion. Until then, says the account of 1907, it had that standing only in Transylvania. Now it held it in all of Hungary.

Ferencz calls this a worthy mark of respect for 300 years of struggle.

After Bishop Sándor Székely died in 1852, the government in Vienna would not let the church fill his seat. The seat stayed empty for nine years, and the government threatened to close the church's schools. In 1858 English Unitarians came to its aid with 13,000 forints. On 2 July 1861 the synod at Torda elected János Kriza bishop.

In 1868 the church marked 300 years, and the diet voted it a grant of 5,000 forints a year. On 13 June 1869 it held its first public service in Budapest, in a hall that the Reformed brothers lent. The census of 1869 counted 53,539 Unitarians in Transylvania and 788 in Hungary.

Ferencz says English Unitarians first wrote to Kolozsvár in 1821, and the church wrote back. The account of 1907 gives the year 1822. In 1830 and 1847 young men preparing to teach went in person to London.

In 1831 Sándor Bölöni Farkas toured North America. He was the first to make the Hungarian church known to the Americans.

In 1868 the Americans sent a greeting for the church's 300th year. Dall, their missionary in India, also came to Kolozsvár.

Ferencz says Dall won American help to translate Channing's works into Hungarian. He says it was given. After a meeting at Budapest in June 1873, Hale and Morison, two Americans, raised money for two chairs at the Kolozsvár college. The chairs were to be named for Channing and Priestley.

In 1875 Ferencz wrote that the Unitarian faith differs from other forms of Christianity above all in denying the Trinity. He wrote that it holds neither Christ nor the Holy Spirit equal to God.

He calls Jesus the Christ or Messiah, God's holy son, and the greatest of the prophets. He is, "so to speak", a perfect man. He writes that the one God alone is to be worshipped. He adds that the church does not claim to be infallible.
