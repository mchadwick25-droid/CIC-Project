Procopius of Caesarea

A person whose story no single tradition tells.

Outside witness

Known for
The historian of Justinian's reign, whose Wars, Buildings and Secret History report the church of his day from a layman's distance, and who refused to set down the points Christians argued over.
Dates
Born late in the fifth century, by H. B. Dewing's account; chosen as Belisarius's adviser in 527, Dewing's year for the event Procopius tells. The Palestine Pilgrims' Text Society's preface says he is supposed to have died in 565
Place
Born at Caesarea in Palestine; worked at Constantinople and on campaign in the East, Africa and Italy
Role
Historian; legal adviser and secretary to the general Belisarius

About This Person

Two envoys came from Constantinople to the chief priest of Rome, as Procopius of Caesarea calls the pope. They came about a point of faith that Christians quarreled over. He knew the points well, he writes, but would not record them. "I consider it a sort of insane folly to investigate the nature of God," he explains. Of God he will say only that "He is altogether good and has all things in His power."

Procopius was born at Caesarea in Palestine, late in the fifth century, says H. B. Dewing, his English translator. Procopius trained in law and went to Constantinople. He says himself that he was chosen as adviser to the general Belisarius in the war with Persia. He followed Belisarius to Africa and Italy, and saw much of what he tells.

Dewing notes the calm tone of the History of the Wars. It tells how Belisarius suspected Silverius, the chief priest of Rome, of plotting with the Goths. He sent him away to Greece and put Vigilius in his place. The Buildings praises Justinian's churches. Procopius writes that the dome of Hagia Sophia seemed "suspended from heaven by the fabled golden chain." Dewing says the Buildings is written in the style of a fawning flatterer, but is still a storehouse of facts.

The Secret History attacks the emperor, the empress Theodora and Belisarius. Dewing finds its slander too extreme to believe. The unnamed English translator of 1896 thinks it exaggerates but gives a faithful picture of the court. Some have doubted that Procopius wrote it. Dewing says it is now believed highly probable that he did. The 1896 translator notes that the first writers to name him as its author lived centuries later.

Dewing says Procopius writes at times as a Christian and at times like a man steeped in the old religion of Greece. The 1896 translator thinks he was a skeptic who used Christian forms under an orthodox emperor.

Key Works

  • History of the Wars: eight books on Justinian's wars with Persia, the Vandals and the Goths, written as an eyewitness, with the Nika riot and the plague along the way
  • On the Buildings: six books of praise for Justinian's buildings, beginning with Hagia Sophia and the churches of Constantinople
  • Secret History (Anecdota): a private attack on Justinian, Theodora, Belisarius and Antonina, published after Justinian's death, by Dewing's account

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The Wars tell church matters as part of the war: the envoys to Rome on a point of faith, and Belisarius sending Silverius of Rome to Greece and putting Vigilius in his place.
  • The Buildings opens with Hagia Sophia, rebuilt after the Nika riot burned the church, and is, in Dewing's words, "a storehouse of information concerning the internal administration of the empire".

How they're remembered

  • Dewing: "Procopius is known to posterity as the historian of the eventful reign of Justinian".
  • The unnamed translator of the 1896 Secret History calls him "the first and most talented of Byzantine historians".

What Is Uncertain

  • His religion. Dewing says he "writes at times as a Christian, and at times as one imbued with the ideas of the ancient religion of Greece". The 1896 translator says his own writings do not decide it and thinks he was a skeptic who "affected the forms and language of Christianity". Outside the church marks him as a historian who reports on the church and, by his own word, would not set down the points of faith in dispute. It makes no claim about his faith.
  • Who wrote the Secret History. Dewing says serious doubts were once held, and that it is now believed "highly probable at least" to be his. The 1896 translator says direct proofs are wanting, since the first writers to give it to him, the Suda ("Suidas") and Nicephorus Callistus, lived centuries later, but judges it the work of a court official of the time.
  • How far the Secret History can be trusted. Dewing: "the very extravagance of the calumny makes it impossible to be believed", though it has "very few contradictions in statements of fact". The 1896 translator: details may be exaggerated, but it gives on the whole "a faithful picture of the Byzantine Court".
  • The Buildings is court praise. Dewing calls it "written in the empty style of the fawning flatterer".
  • His birth. Dewing says late in the fifth century; the 1896 translator, towards the beginning of the sixth; the Palestine Pilgrims' Text Society's preface, early in the sixth or at the end of the fifth.
  • His later career. The 1896 translator says Justinian made him a senator and Prefect of Byzantium in 562, and that he died shortly before or after 565; the Palestine Pilgrims' Text Society's preface gives the same prefecture and says he "is supposed to have died in 565". Dewing says his work went on till near the end of Justinian's life, and that he seems to have outlived Belisarius.
