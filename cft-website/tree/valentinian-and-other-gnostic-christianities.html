Valentinian & other Gnostic Christianities

At Rome and in Egypt, the teacher Valentinus and his students told of a highest God, the Father of all, above a lesser maker of this world. They taught that the Savior came to make that Father known.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:36

Read along

Valentinus was a teacher from Alexandria who came to Rome around 136. Tertullian says he hoped to become a bishop. When a confessor, someone who had suffered for the faith, was chosen instead, he broke with the church. His students Ptolemy, Heracleon and Theodotus carried his name. He and these students taught that the highest God did not make the visible world. A lesser craftsman made it. Human beings carry within them a spark of the divine that has fallen into matter. Christ came to bring the knowledge that wakes it up. They, and related currents around teachers like Basilides, mostly did not build rival churches. They sat in ordinary congregations. They offered their teaching as the deeper meaning, open to those ready for it. That is precisely what alarmed bishops like Irenaeus. Around 180 he wrote Against Heresies, in five volumes, to drag the system into the open and refute it. For eighteen centuries, these groups were known almost entirely through such opponents. Then, in 1945, farmers digging near Nag Hammadi in Upper Egypt found a sealed jar. Inside were papyrus books, or codices, in Coptic. Among them was the Gospel of Truth. For the first time since antiquity, these currents had their own voice.

Voices in the Record

  • Valentinus — Alexandrian teacher, later in Rome, the most influential figure of these currents and possibly the author of the Gospel of Truth
  • Ptolemy — a Valentinian teacher whose Letter to Flora, a patient explanation of how to read the Law of Moses, survives nearly complete
  • Heracleon — wrote the earliest known commentary on the Gospel of John, preserved in fragments quoted by Origen
  • Basilides — an Alexandrian teacher of a related but distinct system, known only through hostile summaries
  • Irenaeus of Lyons — their most thorough opponent, whose refutation preserved much of what they taught

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Valentinus, an Alexandrian teacher who came to Rome around 136, taught that a divine spark lies trapped within human beings and that Christ came to awaken it - a teaching offered quietly inside ordinary congregations rather than through a rival church. For eighteen centuries this movement was known only through the bishops who wrote against it, until 1945, when a buried jar near Nag Hammadi in Egypt turned out to hold their own writings, including the Gospel of Truth.

Legacy

No community survives, and none claims descent. Their trace is double. First, the shape of what became orthodox Christianity was set partly by the labor of answering them. The insistence that the one God made matter and called it good, that the Son took real flesh and rose in a real body, and that the faith is a public rule handed down openly rather than a secret reserved for initiates, were all pressed into their enduring form in this argument. Second, since 1945 the Nag Hammadi texts have been read, translated and fought over continuously, and have reshaped how modern readers picture the diversity of the earliest Christian centuries.

Experience It Today

  • The Coptic Museum, Cairo — holds the Nag Hammadi codices along with the world's largest collection of Coptic Christian art, and is open to the public

Open Questions

  • When Valentinus was at Rome. Irenaeus says he came in the time of Bishop Hyginus, flourished under Pius and stayed until Anicetus. Tertullian says he and Marcion were believers in the church of Rome under Bishop Eleutherus before they were expelled.
  • Why he broke with the church. Only Tertullian, an opponent, gives a reason: Valentinus had hoped to become a bishop, and another man won the place by the claim his suffering for the faith had given him. Tertullian names no city, and says such men are often roused by ambition to revenge.
  • Where he came from. Epiphanius, late in the 4th century, heard that some said he was born on the coast of Egypt and schooled in Greek learning at Alexandria, and he calls the report uncertain. He also says Valentinus left the faith in Cyprus, while Irenaeus says he stayed at Rome until Anicetus.
  • His teacher. Clement says the followers of Valentinus claimed he had heard Theudas, a pupil of Paul, and that the followers of Basilides claimed Glaucias, the interpreter of Peter.
  • What is his and what is his school's. Irenaeus gives a short account of Valentinus' own teaching and a much longer one of Ptolemy's school. Tertullian says Valentinus kept the powers within God, and Ptolemy made them persons outside God.
  • The Gospel of Truth. Irenaeus says those from Valentinus called a recent writing of theirs the Gospel of Truth. The old sources held here do not say who wrote it.
  • Teaching in private. Irenaeus and Tertullian, both opponents, say the Valentinians first won hearers in the churches and told them their deeper teaching only later, in private.
  • Their own words. The words of Valentinus, Ptolemy and Heracleon held here survive only as their opponents copied them: Clement, Epiphanius and Origen.
