The Enlightenment & Awakening Era · 1650-1906 · China

Chinese Catholics Under Edict and Treaty

About This Movement

The Jesuit mission of the Ming years went on at the Qing court. The Jesuits served the emperor as astronomers and interpreters. The Catholic Encyclopedia of 1908 says they stood high in favor. In March 1692, it adds, a note from the president of the Board of Rites said it seemed unfair to ban the Europeans' religion. An imperial decree approved the note. Copies went to all the provincial governors.

The Rites quarrel asked what a Chinese Christian might do for Confucius and the dead. In 1645 missionaries in China sent questions to Rome. The Holy Office answered them on 23 March 1656. It let Chinese Christians join the rite in the Confucius hall. It seemed, the ruling says, to be worship that is only civil and political.

In 1658 Rome named two French priests, Pallu and Lambert de la Motte, to lead the missions of China. The seminary they asked for was given a site in Paris in 1663 and opened in 1664. The encyclopedia calls it the beginning of the Paris Foreign Missions Society.

Propaganda wrote to them in 1659. It asked what could be more absurd than to carry France, Spain or Italy into China.

Rome later turned against the rites.

Father Ricci was the Jesuits' first leader in China. He was tolerant of the Chinese rites, the encyclopedia says. Rome, not China, decided the question. Maigrot was a vicar apostolic in Fujian and a priest of the Paris Foreign Missions. In 1693 he condemned the rites in a mandate. A bull of Benedict XIV settled the matter on 11 July 1742. It was, the encyclopedia says, "a terrible blow to the missions in China".

In 1723 officials in Fujian asked the emperor to send the Europeans out of the provinces. Europeans at the court could stay.

On 16 October 1724 the Jesuit de Mailla wrote from Peking. He said the holy faith was entirely banned in China. Churches were pulled down or turned to common uses. An order of 1723 complained of girls who were kept from marriage and called virgins.

The church went on in the provinces. In 1726 Propaganda ruled that Chinese-born priests must swear the same oath as the Europeans. In 1736 an edict of the Ch'ien-lung emperor forbade the preaching of Christian doctrine on pain of death. A persecution in Fujian in 1746 killed Bishop Sanz and four Spanish Dominicans.

In 1755 Propaganda warned the missionary at Peking for letting Mass be said in Chinese on feast days. It forbade the practice for the future. In 1766 the Holy Office was told that Christians in China were few and could not avoid their non-Christian neighbors' tables. It allowed them to eat food set before them, even if offered to idols, outside any temple and with no sign of worship.

In 1811 a decree ordered a search for foreign preachers. In 1844 a treaty was signed at Whampoa. On 20 February 1846 the emperor ordered that the old Christian buildings be restored.

Treaties in 1858 and 1860 gave missions special privileges. These were the Treaty of Tientsin and the Peking Convention. Anger followed in 1870. A Holy Office instruction of the 1860s dealt with trouble in Hunan. Non-Christians, it says, were angry at a treaty and drew crosses on the roads so that Christians had to step on them. At Tientsin on 21 June 1870, twenty-one people died, among them a Lazarist and a Chinese priest named Hu.

The Boxer rising of 1900 brought "sad days for the missions". The encyclopedia says five bishops were among the dead.

  • Mission-Born Christianity in Ming China Widely Accepted Major — In 1656 the Holy Office answered questions that missionaries in China had sent to Rome in 1645. The Catholic Encyclopedia of 1908 tells the Ming mission, the Rites quarrel and the later church as one story. No text says in so many words that one church became the other.
