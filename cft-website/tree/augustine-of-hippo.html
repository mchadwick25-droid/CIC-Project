The Imperial Church Era · 386-430 · Hippo Regius, Milan

Hippo Regius, today in Algeria · Milan, today in Italy

Augustine of Hippo

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity.

Known for
Teacher of rhetoric who became priest and then bishop of Hippo in Roman North Africa. He wrote the Confessions and the City of God and taught that God's grace comes first, a teaching the Western churches carried on and divided over.
Also called
Aurelius Augustinus
Dates
Possidius says he lived seventy-six years, almost forty of them as priest or bishop. H. T. Weiskotten reckons his birth at 13 November 354 from Augustine's own words on his birthday. Prosper's chronicle, as Mommsen prints it, dates his death to 28 August 430. Weiskotten's note gives 391 both for his ordination as priest and for his consecration as bishop beside Valerius, and has him succeed Valerius in 396 or 397; the dominant modern reconstruction puts his becoming bishop at about 395 to 396. The Confessions are dated 397 to 400 in the Library's works list. The City of God was begun after Rome fell to the Goths, which Dods's note dates to 410
Place
Born at Tagaste in the African province; taught at Tagaste, Carthage, Rome and Milan; priest and bishop at Hippo Regius, in modern Algeria
Role
Teacher of rhetoric; priest and bishop of Hippo; writer

About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Milan, where the emperor then held court. A young teacher is preparing a speech in praise of the emperor, in which, he says, he would tell many lies. In the street he passes a beggar, well fed and joking, and sighs: the beggar already has the happiness he is straining after.

The teacher was Augustine. He taught rhetoric, the art of persuasive public speaking. In a garden in Milan, he says, a child's voice sent him to the Bible, and he turned to Christ. He became bishop, the leader of the church, in Hippo, a port town in Roman North Africa. There he wrote the Confessions, his life told as a prayer to God, and the City of God, on those who love God above themselves. He taught that grace, God's free and unearned gift, comes first: no one even begins to believe unless God gives it. The Western churches carried that teaching on, and it both shaped and divided them.

Born in Roman Africa, he taught at Carthage and Rome, then Milan; in his own words, he sold the skill of talking to win. At Milan he heard its bishop, Ambrose, preach. There, in 386, came the moment he tells in his Confessions. Torn and weeping, he threw himself down under a fig tree. Then, he writes: "And look, I hear a voice from the house next door, singing and repeating it again and again, like a boy's or a girl's voice, I do not know which: 'Take it and read, take it and read.'" He opened Paul's letters at the first lines he saw: not drunken revels or quarrels, but put on the Lord Jesus Christ. He told his mother, Monica, who rejoiced, and at Easter Ambrose baptized him, washing him in water to join the church.

Back in Africa, he became bishop of Hippo. Early in those years, a bishop of Milan sent him questions on Paul's letter to the Romans. Answering them, he changed his mind about free will, a person's own power to choose good or evil. Near the end of his life, calling Paul the apostle, he looked back: "In answering this question, I worked hard for the free choice of the human will. But God's grace won. I could only come to see that the apostle spoke the plainest truth: 'Who sets you apart? What do you have that you did not receive?'"

Beside his church stood the Donatists, a rival African church, for a century the larger one. It held that bishops who had handed over the Bible in persecution had lost the church. At first, Augustine wrote in about 408, he held that no one should be forced into the church; argument alone should win. But his fellow bishops pointed to his own town, brought back by fear of the emperors' laws. He came to back those laws to force the Donatists back, citing Jesus's parable: compel them to come in.

In the same years Pelagius, a monk at Rome, held that God had already given human nature the power to do good. Augustine answered that free will and good teaching are not enough: God's Spirit must give the love of the good. His friend Possidius, who later wrote his life to honor him, tells it from the winning side. He says meetings of African bishops moved two bishops of Rome, the leaders of the church there, to condemn Pelagius's followers. The emperor's laws then punished them as heretics, false teachers cut off from the church.

He died in 430, at seventy-six, while the Vandals, a people from beyond the empire's frontier, besieged Hippo. Possidius says he told them to keep the church's library.

Those books carried his teaching on. In 529 a meeting of bishops at Orange, in what is now France, set out rulings on grace drawn largely from his writings, as their modern editor shows. A thousand years later Martin Luther sent an opponent to his book On the Spirit and the Letter. Its letter is the law that commands; its spirit is God's Spirit, giving the love to keep it. John Calvin said he could build a whole volume on God's choice and grace from Augustine's words alone. Both stood against Rome. Yet inside the church united with Rome, the Catholic bishop Cornelius Jansen wrote a book called Augustinus, and Rome condemned it.

Near the end of his account, Possidius recalled a poet's lines: whoever reads the words aloud gives the writer his voice.

Key Works

  • Confessions: thirteen books addressed to God, which he says praise God and stir the human mind and heart toward him; books one to ten are about himself and the last three about Scripture. They tell the turn in the garden at Milan
  • City of God: twenty-two books begun after Rome fell to the Goths, when worshippers of the old gods blamed the Christians; it holds that two loves made two cities, love of self the earthly city and love of God the heavenly
  • On the Spirit and the Letter: against the Pelagians: free will and teaching are not enough, and God gives the Holy Spirit, who forms in a person a delight in and love of the good. Luther's editor dates it to 412

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Of the twenty-five canons of the council of Orange in 529, its editor Friedrich Maassen finds that nine and eleven to twenty-five repeat word for word sentences Prosper drew from Augustine's works, and that the rest follow passages of his writings very closely. The chapters were confirmed by Boniface II. B. B. Warfield calls the result a guarded and somewhat weakened Augustinianism.
  • In 1521 Luther told his opponent Emser he would teach him better than any teacher he had had, except Augustine, if Emser had read his On the Spirit and the Letter; on letter and spirit in Scripture, he says, none of the others will teach him aright.
  • Calvin says Augustine was for a time of another opinion but, as he came to know Scripture better, retracted it as false, and that he could build a whole volume out of Augustine's words alone.
  • Cornelius Jansen, bishop of Ypres, gave twenty years to a book called Augustinus on Augustine's teaching on grace. Urban VIII condemned the book by a bull of 6 March 1642, and in 1653 Innocent X condemned the Five Propositions.
  • Possidius added a list of his works so that readers could copy them from the library of the church of Hippo, and says no student could read them all.

How they're remembered

  • Possidius says he left monasteries and the church's library, and that in his books the faithful may always find him living.
  • Prosper called him excellent in every way and says he kept defending the grace of Christ to the end.
  • Isidore of Seville wrote that he outdid the other writers he names in mind and learning, and wrote so much that no one could copy or even read it all.
  • Facundus of Hermiane, an African bishop writing to Justinian, called him the wisest and most famous teacher of the churches of Christ.
  • Philip Schaff says his anti-Pelagian works had a powerful influence on the Reformers of the sixteenth century and on the Jansenists of the seventeenth.

At Major Councils

  • Conference of Carthage, 411: One of the seven Catholic speakers; wrote the summary of the conference

What Is Uncertain

  • Most of his life comes from two voices. His own Confessions were written about ten years after the garden in Milan, as a bishop praying to God. Possidius, his friend and fellow bishop for almost forty years, wrote his Life to honor him and was on the winning side of every quarrel he tells. Prosper was Augustine's defender. J. M. Neale wrote in sympathy with the Jansenists.
  • His parents' faith. Possidius calls them honorable Christian parents; Augustine says his father was only a catechumen, and that recently. The brief leaves his parents out.
  • The year he became bishop. Weiskotten's note says 391, the same year it gives for his ordination as priest. The dominant modern reconstruction is about 395 to 396.
  • How long he fought the Pelagians. Possidius says almost ten years; Prosper has him still answering Julian at his death. The two counts may measure different things, so the brief gives no number.
  • The dates of the Confessions and the City of God. No held ancient source gives a year for either. The Confessions are dated 397 to 400 in the Library's works list, and the year 410 for the fall of Rome is Dods's note.
  • Orange. That its canons drew on Augustine is the finding of the editors, Maassen and Warfield, not a statement of the council, which says its chapters came from the apostolic see. Warfield calls the result a weakened Augustinianism. The canons themselves were not read canon by canon here.
  • In the garden scene, Knoll's Latin text prints a voice from the divine house, from one manuscript, S; all the other manuscripts and earlier editors read the neighboring house, as Pilkington's English does. The rendering follows the neighboring house.
  • In Letter 93 the town he names as brought back by fear of the emperors' laws is his own town. The letter does not name it, so the brief says only his own town.
  • His anti-Pelagian treatises are held here in English only; their Latin was not found in the Library.
  • The date of birth rests on Weiskotten's note, which reckons it from Augustine's De beata vita; that work is not held here.
