The Mainline Century — Liberal Protestantism & Neo-Orthodoxy

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:14

Read along

This is the world the mainline Protestant seminaries built and the mainline denominations taught. Its reach-back runs to Schleiermacher and to the historical-critical century — the Briggs heresy trial of 1891 to 1893 in America, Harnack's What Is Christianity? in 1900 — and its institutional face arrived with the Federal Council of Churches in 1908. The public rupture came in the 1920s: Harry Emerson Fosdick preached 'Shall the Fundamentalists Win?' on 21 May 1922; J. Gresham Machen answered with Christianity and Liberalism in 1923; and the Auburn Affirmation, released on 9 January 1924 with about 150 signatures and eventually some 1,300, answered from the other side. Then the ground moved again from inside: Karl Barth's Romans commentary of 1919, rewritten in 1922, opened the neo-orthodox turn against the liberal theology that had formed the field, and Reinhold Niebuhr, Rudolf Bultmann and Paul Tillich each reshaped the field in turn.

Documented Stories

Shall the Fundamentalists Win?

Harry Emerson Fosdick was a Baptist, a professor at Union Theological Seminary, and since 1918 the regular preacher at First Presbyterian Church in New York, an arrangement the congregation had made and the presbytery tolerated. On 21 May 1922 he preached a sermon he called 'Shall the Fundamentalists Win?', pleading for tolerance of ministers who did not hold the virgin birth, the inerrancy of scripture or a literal second coming as tests of fellowship, and saying that a church which drove them out would lose the coming generation. The congregation's publicist, Ivy Lee retitled it and mailed it to some 130,000 Protestant ministers. Clarence Macartney of Philadelphia answered with 'Shall Unbelief Win?', and the 1923 General Assembly, meeting in Indianapolis, directed the Presbytery of New York to see that the preaching at First Church conformed to the Westminster Confession, reaffirming five doctrines as essential. The presbytery exonerated Fosdick; a complaint went up again. The 1924 Assembly, at Grand Rapids, chose not to try him but to invite him to become a Presbyterian and so come under its discipline. He declined: he would not, he said, subscribe to a creed as a condition of preaching, and he resigned the pulpit, preaching his last sermon there on 1 March 1925 to a church filled beyond the doors. John D. Rockefeller Jr., a member of the congregation, then built him a church of his own on Riverside Drive, opened in 1930, where he preached until 1946 without a creed in the door.

The Inaugural That Went to Trial

Charles Augustus Briggs had taught Hebrew at Union Theological Seminary in New York since 1874 and was among the first American scholars to adopt the German historical criticism of the Old Testament. On 20 January 1891, inaugurated to a new chair of biblical theology, he delivered an address on 'The Authority of Holy Scripture' in which he said that the Bible contained errors, that Moses had not written the Pentateuch nor Isaiah the whole of Isaiah, that the church had turned the Bible into an idol as it had once turned the church and reason into idols, and that God speaks through reason and the church as well as through scripture. He said it with some heat. The Presbyterian General Assembly, which held a veto over Union's appointments under an agreement of 1870, refused to confirm him that May. The Presbytery of New York tried him for heresy and acquitted him in 1892; the prosecuting committee appealed, and in 1893 the Assembly at Washington, sitting as the church's highest court, found him guilty of teaching doctrines contrary to the Westminster standards and suspended him from the ministry. Union's directors had already voted to sever the seminary's relationship with the Assembly rather than surrender its chair, and did so in 1892; Briggs kept teaching there until his death in 1913. He was ordained an Episcopal priest in 1899. The conservative party had won the case and lost the seminary, which is roughly the shape the next century took.

Connections

In Its Own Record

This tradition reaches back to Schleiermacher and the historical-critical scholarship of the nineteenth century, including the Briggs heresy trial of 1891–93 and Harnack's work around 1900, and took institutional shape with the Federal Council of Churches in 1908. The fundamentalist-modernist controversy — Fosdick's sermon in 1922, Machen's reply in 1923, and the Auburn Affirmation in 1924 — stands in tension with fundamentalism as its opposite pole. From within liberal theology, Barth's turn to neo-orthodoxy (1919/1922) was carried further by Niebuhr, Bultmann, and Tillich; Barth's line also runs into the Confessing Church and the Barmen Declaration. The National Council of Churches, formed in 1950, and the World Council of Churches are this tradition's institutional and conciliar homes. Membership peaked in the mid-1960s and then declined, and this current hands off around 1990 to the current called progressive Christianity.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Harry Emerson Fosdick's 1922 sermon 'Shall the Fundamentalists Win?' and J. Gresham Machen's reply the following year opened a rupture that the Auburn Affirmation, eventually signed by some 1,300 mainline clergy, answered from the liberal side. From inside that same Christian tradition, Karl Barth's Romans commentary opened a neo-orthodox turn that Reinhold Niebuhr, Rudolf Bultmann and Paul Tillich each carried further, while the seminaries that trained the denominations' clergy watched membership peak in the mid-1960s before its long decline began.

Legacy

The mainline churches were alive in 2010. What closed around 1990 was this one century of theirs. Their numbers peaked in the mid-1960s and fell after that. From around 1990 the people who carried their questions often called their faith progressive. They left large bodies behind: the National Council of Churches from 1950, a home in the World Council of Churches, and many seminaries. Those seminaries trained the clergy and wrote much of the century's Bible scholarship. Barth's line ran into the Confessing Church, whose Barmen Declaration he drafted. And the argument with fundamentalism, fought out in dated texts on both sides, was never settled.

Sources

On the Nicene Creed

Where this tradition reaches past 1990, it's best judged body by body, not as one label — the same approach used for Progressive Christianity, which this current hands off to around 1990. The record spans confessional reaffirmation, like the Barmen Declaration of 1934, and non-realist reinterpretation, and each body and text needs its own accounting rather than one judgment covering the whole current.
