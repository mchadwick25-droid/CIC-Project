The Mainline Century — Liberal Protestantism & Neo-Orthodoxy

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

This is the world the mainline Protestant seminaries built and the mainline denominations taught. Its reach-back runs to Schleiermacher and to the historical-critical century — the Briggs heresy trial of 1891 to 1893 in America, Harnack's What Is Christianity? in 1900 — and its institutional face arrived with the Federal Council of Churches in 1908. The public rupture came in the 1920s: Harry Emerson Fosdick preached 'Shall the Fundamentalists Win?' on 21 May 1922; J. Gresham Machen answered with Christianity and Liberalism in 1923; and the Auburn Affirmation, released on 9 January 1924 with about 150 signatures and eventually some 1,300, answered from the other side. Then the ground moved again from inside: Karl Barth's Romans commentary of 1919, rewritten in 1922, opened the neo-orthodox turn against the liberal theology that had formed the field, and Reinhold Niebuhr, Rudolf Bultmann and Paul Tillich each reshaped the field in turn.

In Its Own Record

Gave Rise To

  • Progressive Christianity (a broad label, not one movement) Dominant Modern Reconstruction Major — From about 1990, many people in the old mainline churches called their faith progressive. Historians such as Gary Dorrien trace their ideas back to the liberal faith of those same churches in the years before.

Influenced By

  • The Social Gospel Widely Accepted — In May 1908, 32 church bodies formed the Federal Council of Churches. That December it adopted the Social Creed of the Churches. The creed made the Social Gospel's concern for working people a shared program. Historians of the movement trace its stream on into the mainline churches.
  • Charismatic Renewal & the Jesus Movement Widely Accepted — In 1969 a Presbyterian report found a small but growing charismatic movement in America's main churches. In 1970 it told pastors and church boards how to live with it.

Influenced

  • The Confessing Church Dominant Modern Reconstruction — Karl Barth's book on Romans, first out in 1919, turned many Protestants away from liberal theology. In May 1934 a church synod at Barmen, in Germany, took up a statement Barth had drafted. It said the church must obey one Word alone, Jesus Christ.

Argued Against

  • The Social Gospel Dominant Modern Reconstruction — In 1932 Reinhold Niebuhr published Moral Man and Immoral Society. Historians read it as a sharp attack on the Social Gospel's hope that reform would bring in God's kingdom. This reading rests on modern histories.

Broke With

  • Fundamentalism Widely Accepted Major — In the 1920s conservatives and liberals fought hard over doctrine, most of all in the Presbyterian church. In 1924 more than a thousand of its ministers signed a statement. They would not let five doctrines become a test for new ministers. In 1936 the church's top court upheld the guilty verdicts on Machen and other conservative ministers. They then formed a church of their own.

Sources

  • Harry Emerson Fosdick, 'Shall the Fundamentalists Win?' (preached 21 May 1922); the Auburn Affirmation (released 9 January 1924) (primary) — The Auburn Affirmation had about 150 initial signers and roughly 1,300 eventually. These are the rupture's texts from the liberal side.
  • Karl Barth, Der Römerbrief (1919; 2nd ed. 1922) (primary) — The neo-orthodox turn's opening text — a break with liberalism made from inside it.
  • Rudolf Bultmann, 'New Testament and Mythology' (1941); Reinhold Niebuhr, Moral Man and Immoral Society (1932) and The Nature and Destiny of Man (1941–43); Paul Tillich, Systematic Theology (1951–63) (primary) — Bultmann's date is 1941; the Niebuhr and Tillich dates come from standard reference works.
  • The historical-critical reach-back: Adolf von Harnack, What Is Christianity? (1900); the Briggs heresy trial record (1891–93) (primary) — This traces the deeper background to this century's main story; dates come from standard reference works.
  • Federal Council of Churches records (from 1908) and National Council of Churches records (from 1950); denominational archives (primary) — This is the institutional half of this story, kept alongside the academic half deliberately, since the seminaries were the denominations' own teaching offices.
  • Gary Dorrien, The Making of American Liberal Theology (Westminster John Knox, 3 vols., 2001, 2003, 2006) (secondary) — The standard history of this tradition.
  • Bradley J. Longfield, The Presbyterian Controversy (Oxford, 1991) (secondary) — This studies the institutional rupture from both sides.
