English Old Dissent — Presbyterians and Congregationalists

England and Wales, 1662 to 1815 — two thousand ministers ejected in a single day, who built their own schools, invented the English hymn, and quietly divided over the Trinity.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:36

Read along

On St Bartholomew's Day 1662, about two thousand ministers were put out of the Church of England. They could not accept the restored Prayer Book, or the rule that only bishops could ordain. From then on, Presbyterians and Congregationalists were churches of their own, not parties inside one church. Barred from the universities, they built their own schools. These dissenting academies taught math, science and modern languages when Oxford and Cambridge mostly did not. They trained a striking share of the nation's scientists, factory owners and reformers. Their other great gift was sung. Isaac Watts, a Congregational minister, broke the rule that only the Psalms could be sung in worship, and wrote hymns instead. That is why English-speaking Christians sing 'When I Survey the Wondrous Cross' and 'Joy to the World'. The tradition also had a fault line. At Salters' Hall in London in 1719, most of the ministers would not make others sign a statement on the Trinity. Over the next century many Presbyterian churches moved away from the Trinity. They went first toward Arian and then toward Unitarian views. The Congregationalists kept the old faith. Some of their leaders, like Watts and Doddridge, took up the revival of the 1730s and 1740s.

People and Stories

  • Isaac Watts — Congregational minister who effectively created the English hymn
  • Philip Doddridge — Northampton minister and academy tutor, hymn-writer, and author of The Rise and Progress of Religion in the Soul (1745)
  • Joseph Priestley — chemist, Dissenting minister and academy tutor, whose Unitarian convictions and radical politics eventually drove him out of England

Documented Stories

The Farewell Sermons

The Act of Uniformity of 1662 required every minister holding a living in the Church of England to declare, before St Bartholomew's Day, 24 August, his unfeigned assent and consent to everything in the revised Book of Common Prayer, to have episcopal ordination, and to renounce the Covenant. The date, the day of the massacre of the Huguenots ninety years before, was chosen so that those who refused would lose the year's tithes. Between the Restoration and that day about two thousand ministers, a fifth of the parish clergy of England and Wales, left or were put out, most of them on the day itself. On Sunday 17 August, the last before the deadline, they preached their farewell sermons. In London Edmund Calamy preached at Aldermanbury, Thomas Watson at St Stephen Walbrook, William Bates at St Dunstan-in-the-West, Thomas Manton at Covent Garden, to churches so crowded that people stood in the streets; Richard Baxter, who had already left Kidderminster, had preached his at Blackfriars in May. The sermons were on parting, on holding fast, on the church being where Christ was and not where the buildings were, and they told their hearers to remain in the parish churches and hear the new men if they preached the gospel. Shorthand writers took them down and they were printed within weeks, without license, as the Farewell Sermons, and reprinted for a century in Dissenting households as the founding document of a separation none of the preachers had wanted.

‘Richard, Thou Art an Old Knave’

Richard Baxter had been ejected in 1662, imprisoned in 1669 and 1675, and had gone on preaching and writing in London through it all; by 1685 his books, The Saints' Everlasting Rest and A Call to the Unconverted among them, were in more English households than any except the Bible and Bunyan. Baxter's Paraphrase on the New Testament, published that winter, was found to contain, in its notes on the Pharisees and on the persecutors of the apostles, reflections on the bishops of the Church of England, and he was charged with seditious libel. The trial at the Guildhall on 30 May 1685 was presided over by George Jeffreys, Lord Chief Justice, whose conduct of it was written down by those present and printed after the Revolution. Jeffreys would not let Baxter's counsel finish a sentence, called Baxter an old knave, said he had written books enough to load a cart, every one as full of sedition as an egg is full of meat, and, when Baxter tried to speak, ‘Richard, Richard, dost thou think we'll hear thee poison the court?’ The jury convicted without leaving the box. He was fined five hundred marks and imprisoned until it was paid, which he could not do, and stayed in the King's Bench prison for eighteen months until the king, seeking Dissenting support against the bishops, remitted the fine. He came out, preached again, saw the Toleration Act, and died in 1691.

Church and King Burn Priestley’s House

Joseph Priestley, discoverer of oxygen, Fellow of the Royal Society, minister of the New Meeting in Birmingham and the most prominent Unitarian in England, had spent the 1780s arguing in print that the established church was corrupt, that the Test Acts should be repealed, and that the French Revolution was the beginning of better things. On 14 July 1791 a dinner was held at a Birmingham hotel to mark the second anniversary of the fall of the Bastille; Priestley did not attend, having been warned. A crowd gathered outside shouting for church and king, and when the diners had gone it went first to the New Meeting and burned it, then to the Old Meeting, and then, in the night, a mile out of town to Priestley's house at Fair Hill, where his family had fled before them; the crowd broke in, drank the cellar, and burned the house with its library, the laboratory and twenty years of manuscripts and scientific notes. Priestley watched the glow from a neighbor's house and fled to London the next day. For three more days the rioters burned the houses and chapels of Dissenters and reformers, the magistrates, some of them Anglican clergy, standing by, until troops arrived on the 17th. He preached in Hackney, found himself shunned by the Royal Society and by former friends, and in 1794 sailed for Pennsylvania, where he died at Northumberland in 1804, still a minister.

Connections

In Its Own Record

About two thousand ministers were put out of the Church of England in 1662. They built a lasting tradition. It gave England its own schools, the dissenting academies. It also gave Isaac Watts, who changed how English churches sang. In 1719 its ministers met at Salters' Hall in London. After that, many Presbyterian churches drifted away from the Trinity. They moved first toward Arian and then toward Unitarian views. The Congregationalists kept the old faith, and some of their leaders took up the new revival. Watts helped print Jonathan Edwards's story of the awakening in New England. Philip Doddridge asked John Wesley to teach his students. With the Quakers and the Baptists, it makes up the wider family of English Dissent. Baptist ministers sat at Salters' Hall too.

Descended from

  • grew out of Puritanism Documented Major — Richard Baxter grew up in a home that neighbors mocked as Puritan. In 1662 he was silenced with nearly two thousand other ministers. His memoir shows them going on as Nonconformists, and some kept preaching until they were put in prison.

Direct descendants

  • gave rise to English Unitarianism Documented Major — Joseph Priestley trained and served among the English Dissenters. He writes that he and the other tutors at his academy were all Arians. At Leeds he became a Socinian. In London he spent his Sundays at Lindsey's chapel, and sometimes led its worship.

Broke With

  • broke with The Restoration and Georgian Church of England Documented Major — The Act of Uniformity of 1662 set a deadline: St Bartholomew's Day. A minister who had not accepted the Prayer Book and a bishop's ordination by then would lose his post. Richard Baxter wrote that when the day came, about 1,800 or 2,000 ministers were cast out.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced The Evangelical Awakening in New England Documented — Two London ministers, Isaac Watts and John Guyse, read Jonathan Edwards's first short report of the Northampton revival. They told its story to large crowds. Then they printed his full account in London in 1737, with their own preface.

Influenced

  • influenced The Evangelical Awakening in New England Documented — Early in 1742, at the height of the revival in Northampton, Sarah Edwards heard a friend read a hymn by Isaac Watts. She wrote that it moved her so strongly she leaped from her chair. Words from another of his hymns had moved her too.

In Contact With

  • in contact with The Methodist Revival Documented — In September 1745 John Wesley called on Philip Doddridge at his academy in Northampton, England. It was the hour when Doddridge taught the young men in his care, and he asked Wesley to take his place.
  • in contact with The Baptists Documented — In 1719 Dissenting ministers met at Salters' Hall in London over a dispute about tests of faith. A pamphlet of that year says one speaker asked if they had come to be contradicted by 'Anabaptist teachers'. Some of the other side, it explains, were Baptists.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

About two thousand ministers were put out of the Church of England on a single day in 1662, and the Presbyterian and Congregationalist traditions that grew from the ejection built their own dissenting academies, teaching mathematics and modern languages when Oxford and Cambridge largely didn't. Isaac Watts, one of their ministers, broke the old rule that only the Psalms could be sung in worship and wrote hymns instead, while a 1719 meeting at Salters' Hall split the tradition quietly: many English Presbyterian congregations drifted over the following century toward Arian and then Unitarian belief, while the Congregationalists stayed orthodox.

Legacy

Both halves continue. English Congregationalism ran on into the United Reformed Church and the Congregational Federation, carrying the gathered-church principle into the modern world. The Presbyterian congregations that had moved toward Unitarianism became the core of British Unitarianism. The academies' effect on English intellectual and industrial life was out of all proportion to Dissent's numbers, and the long campaign to remove the legal disabilities Dissenters lived under shaped a century of British politics. Watts's and Doddridge's hymns are sung every week in churches that have never heard of Old Dissent.

Experience It Today

  • Bunhill Fields, London — the Nonconformists' burial ground, where Bunyan, Isaac Watts, Daniel Defoe and William Blake are buried; open as a public garden

Sources

On the Nicene Creed

No question arises about the tradition itself, which held the historic creeds. But one strand of it moved: at Salters' Hall in 1719 a majority of London's Dissenting ministers declined to require ministers to subscribe to a trinitarian formula, and over the following century many English Presbyterian congregations drifted toward Arian and then Unitarian belief. That drift is named here rather than hidden — it is where the later British Unitarian churches came from.

How Sure the Record Is
