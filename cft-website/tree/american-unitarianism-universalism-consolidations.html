About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:39

Read along

Two movements had grown up apart in New England. In this century both grew into organized churches. By 1815 the liberal clergy of Boston's Congregational churches were already being called Unitarians. Many complaints were made that the label slandered them. In 1819 William Ellery Channing preached at an ordination in Baltimore. His sermon gave these liberals a platform. It also spoke out against the Trinity. In 1825 they founded the American Unitarian Association. The Universalists taught that God would in the end save everyone. They built their conventions on a track of their own. Within a generation the Unitarian side produced a third thing it had not looked for. Ralph Waldo Emerson left the ministry. In 1838 he told Harvard's divinity students to seek religion in their own souls and in nature. They were not to look for it in a revelation fixed in the past. The Transcendentalists took that further than most of their elders wanted to go. The two movements did not treat creeds alike. The Unitarians bound no one to a creed. The Universalists kept a short one, the Winchester Profession of 1803. In 1870 they made it a test of fellowship. Both had a strong practical bent. They worked hard for schools, prisons and asylums, and to end slavery.

Documented Stories

The Divinity School Address

Ralph Waldo Emerson had resigned his Boston pulpit in 1832 because he could no longer administer the Lord's Supper in good conscience, and by 1838 was a lecturer and the author of Nature, suspected by the Unitarian clergy who had trained him. The seven seniors of Harvard's Divinity School invited him to give their graduation address, and on the evening of 15 July 1838, in the chapel of Divinity Hall, before students and faculty, he gave it. He told them that religion was born in the soul and could not be received at second hand; that historical Christianity had dwelt on the person of Jesus with a noxious exaggeration, making him a demigod; that the miracle Jesus spoke of was the blowing clover and the falling rain; that preaching had become so formal that a snowstorm outside the window was more real than the preacher; and that they should preach not tradition but the soul's own acquaintance with God. The faculty were appalled. Andrews Norton, the school's great biblical scholar, answered a year later in an address called The Latest Form of Infidelity, arguing that a Christianity without the evidence of miracles was no Christianity, and Emerson's doctrine German pantheism. The pamphlet war ran for three years, Emerson said nothing more, and Harvard did not invite him back for nearly thirty years. The students who had invited him mostly became ministers; and the denomination that had made him found, in the following century, that it had become his.

A Bible and a Knife

William and Ellen Craft had escaped from Georgia in December 1848, Ellen, who was light-skinned, traveling as a young white planter with her arm in a sling so that she need not sign, and William as his slave. They settled in Boston, where both joined Theodore Parker's congregation, the Twenty-Eighth Congregational Society, and heard the most famous radical preacher in New England. In September 1850 the Fugitive Slave Act made every citizen liable to assist in recapturing escaped slaves, and in October two agents of the Crafts' owner arrived in Boston with warrants. The Vigilance Committee, which Parker led, hid Ellen in Parker's own house and William in the home of Lewis Hayden, a Black abolitionist who had mined it with gunpowder; the agents were harassed through the streets and finally, after Parker told them their lives could not be guaranteed, left the city. On 7 November 1850 Parker married the Crafts, since their slave marriage had no legal standing. In his own account he put a Bible in William's hand for the salvation of his soul, and then a bowie knife for the defense of his wife's liberty, to be used, if it came to it, as a Christian might; the Crafts sailed for England and did not return until after the war. Parker wrote to President Fillmore that he had done it and would do it again. Indicted in 1854 over the attempt to rescue Anthony Burns, he published his undelivered defense as a book.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

The Universalists built their movement on a single conviction, that God would in the end save everyone, while the Unitarians organized around William Ellery Channing's 1819 sermon and an explicit rejection of the Trinity. A generation later, Ralph Waldo Emerson pushed further still, leaving the pulpit to tell Harvard's divinity students in 1838 to seek religion in intuition and nature rather than historical revelation.

Legacy

Both bodies survived, and in 1961 merged into the Unitarian Universalist Association, still a functioning denomination in North America. Their wider mark is out of proportion to their numbers. The Transcendentalists' essays became a permanent part of American literature, and the causes the movement threw itself into — abolition, public education, humane treatment of prisoners and the mentally ill. Long outlived the theological arguments that produced them.

Doctrinal Floor Note

Two different questions here, and they get different answers. The Unitarian side placed itself outside the Nicene creed's confession of the Trinity by its own explicit teaching — that is the movement's own account of itself, not an outsider's charge. The Universalist side is a separate case: its defining claim was about the scope of salvation rather than about the doctrine of God, and much of it would sit comfortably inside Nicene bounds. A third strain, the Transcendentalists, moved past the question of Christian doctrine altogether.
