The High Medieval Era · c. 1190-1310 · Low Countries, Rhineland

Low Countries · Rhineland, today in Germany

The Beguines

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

From the late twelfth century, women in the towns of the Low Countries and the Rhineland began to live religious lives without joining any order. They took no lifelong vows, kept no rule and lived under no enclosure. They kept their own property or worked, often in cloth. Some lived alone, others in walled courtyard communities called beguinages. They prayed, and nursed the sick and the dying. Some of them wrote, and that is what makes them extraordinary on any map of medieval Christianity. Hadewijch wrote visions and letters. Mechthild of Magdeburg wrote The Flowing Light of the Godhead, and Marguerite Porete wrote The Mirror of Simple Souls. These are among the very few medieval accounts of a religious life told from the inside, by the women living it. Because they fitted no category, they drew suspicion. Porete was burned in Paris in 1310. In 1312 the Council of Vienne condemned errors it laid on beguines and beghards.

In Its Own Record

Gave Rise To

  • 'Free Spirit' Heresy Contested Major — Scholars disagree whether a Free Spirit sect grew out of the beguines. The Council of Vienne laid its eight errors on beguines and beghards in Germany. Robert Lerner's 1972 study found no organized sect, only a range of mystical piety, much of it among beguines, and a charge laid on it.

Opposed By

  • The Latin Papal Church (Avignon to Fifth Lateran) Documented Major — At the Council of Vienne in 1311-12, Pope Clement V condemned eight errors about the soul made perfect. The standard collection of church decrees files them as the errors of beguines and beghards. Antoninus of Florence wrote in the 1400s that these people had risen up in Germany.Lasting result: The Council of Vienne formally condemned eight errors. It said they were held by the beguines and beghards of Germany.

Sources

  • Hadewijch, The Complete Works (trans. Mother Columba Hart, Classics of Western Spirituality, 1980) (primary) — Visions, letters, and poems in Middle Dutch - a beguine directing other women's formation, in her own voice.
  • Mechthild of Magdeburg, The Flowing Light of the Godhead (trans. Frank Tobin, Classics of Western Spirituality, 1998) (primary) — Begun as a beguine, finished at Helfta; includes her defense of writing as an unlettered woman.
  • Marguerite Porete, The Mirror of Simple Souls (trans. Ellen Babinsky, Classics of Western Spirituality, 1993) (primary) — The movement's boldest theology, and its martyrdom — Porete was burned in 1310 — carried in the fate of one book. Her theology of the soul's freedom also overlaps with the Free Spirit heresy of the same period.
  • Jacques de Vitry, Life of Marie d'Oignies, in Mary of Oignies: Mother of Salvation (ed. Mulder-Bakker, Brepols, 2006) (primary) — A sympathetic outside view from a churchman, and the account that won the movement its first toleration — written, though, in the idealizing frame of a saint's life.
  • Walter Simons, Cities of Ladies: Beguine Communities in the Medieval Low Countries, 1200-1565 (Penn, 2001) (secondary) — The standard social history - courtyards, labor, and rents behind the mystics.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 4, ch. 20 'On the margins of religious life... beguines and beghards' (Simons); cf. ch. 19 'Mysticism and transcendence' (Hollywood) (reference) — The volume's co-editor writes on this exact movement.
