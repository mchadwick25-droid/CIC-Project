The Early Medieval Era · Elipandus c. 717-809; Felix died c. 818 · Toledo, Urgell

Elipandus of Toledo and Felix of Urgell

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Mozarabic Christianity.

Known for
Two bishops of Spain, Elipandus archbishop of Toledo and Felix bishop of Urgell, who taught that Christ in his humanity is the adoptive Son of God. The council of Frankfurt condemned the teaching in 794 as the heresy of both. Felix submitted at Aachen in 799. Elipandus, Alcuin says in a letter Froben dates to 800, still held it.
Also called
Elipandus Toletanus; Helipandus; Eliphandus, in Pope Hadrian I's letter, Felix Urgellitanus or Orgellitanus
Dates
Late eighth century. Heterius and Beatus date Elipandus's letter to the abbot Fidelis to October of the Spanish era 823, which a note in J.-P. Migne's volume reckons as 785. Elipandus says in a letter to Felix that he had entered his eighty-second year; the letter carries no year. Froben, Alcuin's editor, reckons his birth to 717, and with Madrisi places his death in 809 or the year after, and says Felix is believed to have died about 818. The Frankish annals place Felix's first condemnation in 792
Place
Toledo, Elipandus's see; Urgell, on a ridge of the Pyrenees, Felix's see, by the Frankish annals; Regensburg, Rome and Aachen, where Felix was heard; Frankfurt, where the council condemned the teaching; Lyon, where Froben says Felix was kept after 799
Role
Bishops: Elipandus, archbishop of Toledo; Felix, bishop of Urgell. Condemned as Adoptionists by their opponents

About This Person

At the end of one July, Elipandus, archbishop of Toledo, received a letter from his friend Felix. He wrote back that it came to him as if fallen from heaven, and that he thanked God with arms stretched out. He was now, he said, in worn-out old age, and had entered his eighty-second year. He still held the teaching for which both men had been condemned.

Felix was bishop of Urgell, a city the Frankish annals place on a ridge of the Pyrenees. In 794 the council of Frankfurt condemned the teaching as the heresy of them both. The sources tell the two men together, so they share one profile. Much of their story comes from the men who fought them.

Elipandus set out his view in a letter to Alcuin of York. Christ is adoptive, he wrote, only in the form of a servant, his humanity, and not in the glory or substance of his divine nature. As God, the Son is Son by nature, not by grace. Elipandus also cited the Toledo prayers for the mass of the Lord's Supper. As he quoted them, they spoke of the passion of the adoptive man.

His opponents heard two sons in this. Pope Hadrian I likened it to Nestorius, who confessed the Son of God a mere man. Alcuin wrote that Felix divided Christ into a proper son and an adoptive one. Elipandus called Alcuin a new Arius.

The Frankish annals say Felix was condemned at Regensburg in 792 and renounced his teaching before Pope Hadrian at Rome. In 799 he came to the palace at Aachen, where Alcuin says he was heard, proved wrong, and returned to the peace of the church. Felix then wrote to his clergy that he came back with no pretense this time, as there had been once, which God knows.

Froben, Alcuin's editor, reports from a book of Agobard of Lyon that Felix still taught some people his errors, and held to them until he died. In a letter Froben dates to 800, Alcuin says Elipandus still held his view. No source of their own time gives the year either man died.

Elipandus had defended his friend to Alcuin. He had known Felix from early youth, he wrote, as a man of the highest charity, chaste and of good conduct. Yet Alcuin, he wrote, now hunted him in the mountains and the caves.

Key Works

  • Letters of Elipandus: four letters, against Migetius, to Charlemagne, to Alcuin and to Felix, held in Latin in Patrologia Latina 96. His letter to the abbot Fidelis survives only as Heterius and Beatus quote it. A letter of the bishops of Spain to the bishops of Gaul, which Froben gives to Elipandus and his fellow bishops, is in Patrologia Latina 101
  • Confession of faith, by Felix: written to the clergy of Urgell after he submitted at Aachen in 799, renouncing the adoption of the flesh. In Patrologia Latina 96
  • Felix's book to Alcuin: known only from the passages Alcuin quotes in his seven books against Felix, in Patrologia Latina 100-101

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The council of Frankfurt in 794, in its first canon, condemned the impious and wicked heresy of Elipandus and Felix, and ordered it rooted out of the church.
  • Alcuin wrote seven books against Felix and four against Elipandus.
  • Ardo's Life of Benedict of Aniane says Benedict fought the teaching of Felix, which had spread through almost all that province, and won back bishops from it.
  • Paulus Alvarus of Córdoba, in the ninth century, recalls the time when the plague of Elipandus laid waste his province.

How they're remembered

  • Alcuin, writing to Leidrad of Lyon, calls Elipandus a man heavy with years and long famous for a religious life, and says Elipandus called him a forger, a heretic and a persecutor of Christians.
  • Elipandus called Beatus of Liébana Antiphrasius, as if his name, blessed, meant its opposite; Charlemagne's reply, as Nicolás Antonio quotes it, records the name.
  • Marius Férotin, who edited the Mozarabic sacramentary in 1912, holds that the old Toledo prayers mean by adoption the Son's taking of human nature, and that there is nothing in them but what is orthodox.

What Is Uncertain

  • Much of what is held comes from their opponents: Alcuin, Pope Hadrian, Beatus and Heterius, the council of Frankfurt and the Frankish annals. Elipandus's letters and Felix's confession are their own words.
  • What adoptive meant. Elipandus says Christ is adoptive only in the form of a servant, not in the substance of his divinity, and that he is one person in two natures. Hadrian and Alcuin read the teaching as two sons and liken it to Nestorius.
  • The Toledo prayers. Elipandus cites the mass of the Lord's Supper for the passion of the adoptive man. Marius Férotin's manuscript reads instead the adoption of his birth, and he notes that Elipandus did not quote it as his codex has it.
  • Who taught whom. The Frankish annals say Elipandus asked Felix by letter and Felix answered adoptive. A late Life of Beatus has Felix teach Elipandus as a boy. Jean Mabillon names Dungal as calling Felix the master of the error.
  • Whether Felix's submission held. Felix writes that he returned without pretense, as he had not some time before, and repents of an oath he once broke. Froben says Alcuin did not doubt him afterward. Agobard of Lyon, in a book that is not held, says Felix still taught some people his errors and held to them to the end; Froben reports it. Ado of Vienne, through Nicolás Antonio, says he died unrepentant.
  • Whether Elipandus repented. Only forged chronicles say so. Nicolás Antonio and Froben reject them, and Alcuin, in a letter Froben dates to 800, says he still held his view.
  • The years of both men. Elipandus's eighty-second year depends on when his letter to Felix was written, which Madrisi, the editor of Paulinus of Aquileia, put in 792 and Froben after 799.
  • Whether the setting under Muslim rule shaped the teaching. Nicolás Antonio puts the error down to the ignorance of a people under Saracen rule, and adds that the two bishops were perhaps touched by the Muslims around them. No held text of their time says so.
