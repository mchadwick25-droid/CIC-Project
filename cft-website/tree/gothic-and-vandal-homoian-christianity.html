The Age of Monks and Empires · 451-c. 680 · Iberia, Italy, North Africa

Gothic, Vandal & Lombard Homoian Christianity

Iberia, Italy and North Africa, 451 to c. 680 CE — the churches of the post-Roman kingdoms, with their own Gothic Bible and their own bishops, on the losing side of the argument that fixed the Creed.

Homoian ('Arian') Christianity→Gothic, Vandal & Lombard Homoian Christianity→no descendant recorded

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:26

Read along

Most of the peoples who took over the western Roman provinces — Goths, Vandals, Burgundians, and later the Lombards — were Christians before they arrived, but of a kind the Nicene churches did not recognize. Homoian, holding that the Son is like the Father rather than of one being with him. Their tradition went back to Ulfila, the fourth-century missionary bishop who devised an alphabet for Gothic and translated most of the Bible into it, and it worked as a real church, with clergy, liturgy, scripture in the vernacular, and arguments of its own. In Vandal Africa it was the persecuting party, exiling and maiming Nicene bishops in a campaign its victims documented in detail. In Ostrogothic Italy it lived alongside the Catholic church in separate buildings, some of which still stand in Ravenna. Where it ended, it ended two ways. In Africa and Ostrogothic Italy it was destroyed by Byzantine reconquest. In Spain and among the Lombards it ended by conversion. The Visigothic king Reccared brought his kingdom over at the third council of Toledo in 589, and the last Arian Lombard kings gave way in the seventh century.

Documented Stories

The Confessors of Tipasa Speak Without Tongues

In 484 King Huneric ordered the Nicene churches of Africa closed and their clergy exiled, and sent Homoian bishops to the towns. Victor of Vita, a Nicene bishop who lived through it and wrote within a year or two, records what happened at Tipasa on the Mauretanian coast. Most of the Catholic population took ship for Spain rather than accept the new bishop; those who could not leave met in a private house and celebrated the liturgy anyway. When the Arian bishop reported this to Carthage, Huneric sent a count with orders that the whole congregation should have their tongues cut out by the root and their right hands cut off in the forum. It was done. And then, Victor says, they went on speaking ‘as they had before’, and some of them made their way to Constantinople, where anyone could hear them. That last claim is what makes the story unusual. Procopius, the secular historian, says he saw and heard them himself in the capital; the emperor Justinian mentions them in a law; a philosopher, Aeneas of Gaza, says he examined their mouths. The page’s concern is narrower: a Homoian king, the heir of a tradition that had itself been persecuted under the Nicene emperors, using the full cruelty of the state to enforce his church on a town, and a Nicene population that fled, and, when it could not flee, met in a house to pray.

‘We Cannot Command Religion’

Theodoric ruled Italy for thirty-three years as an Arian king over a Catholic population, and his correspondence, drafted by the senator Cassiodorus and collected in the Variae, is the nearest thing to a Homoian ruler speaking for himself. Two letters concern the Jews. The Jews of Genoa had asked leave to reroof their synagogue; Theodoric granted it — they might repair, not enlarge — and added a sentence that has been quoted ever since: ‘We cannot command religion, since no one is forced to believe against his will’ (religionem imperare non possumus, quia nemo cogitur ut credat invitus). To the Jews of Milan, whose synagogue Catholics had attacked, he wrote that they should have the law’s protection like everyone else, while reminding them that they were pursuing earthly security and neglecting eternal. He said the same about Catholics: his letter to the Roman Senate declares that he has never interfered with the Catholic faith and expects the same restraint toward his own. The end of the reign was darker — the execution of Boethius and Symmachus, the imprisonment of Pope John I — and after his death Justinian’s armies destroyed the Gothic kingdom and its church; the Arian mosaics of Sant’Apollinare Nuovo were re-cut to remove the Gothic court. But the sentence about religion survived in the letters of a barbarian king’s chancery, written by an Arian for a Catholic audience, and it was said earlier than anyone on the Catholic side said it.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Descended from Ulfila's missionary church, this tradition covers the churches of the post-Roman barbarian kingdoms, including the Lombard Arian church that lasted from 568 to about 680.

Descended from

  • grew out of Homoian ('Arian') Christianity Documented Major — Ulfila, bishop of the Goths, took part in the council at Constantinople in 360 that confirmed the Homoian creed. In 427 or 428 the Homoian bishop Maximinus came to Africa with the Goths. He named the creed of Ariminum as his faith. Socrates of Constantinople wrote in the fifth century. He says many Goths took up this faith for the sake of the emperor Valens. Many still held it in his day.

Direct descendants

No direct descendant is recorded. Its other links are influence, argument and contact.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Most of the peoples who took over the western Roman provinces, Goths, Vandals and later the Lombards, were already Christian, but held that the Son is like the Father rather than of one being with him, a creed going back to the missionary bishop Ulfila who translated the Bible into Gothic. In Vandal Africa this church exiled and maimed Nicene bishops; in Ostrogothic Italy it worshipped alongside them in separate buildings, until conquest and conversion ended it in Spain and among the Lombards by the seventh century.

Legacy

Nothing continues this tradition today: it ended in conversion, and its buildings were reconsecrated rather than destroyed. What it left is physical and textual. The Gothic Bible, surviving mainly in the silver-and-purple Codex Argenteus at Uppsala, is by far the largest body of text in any early Germanic language and the foundation of Germanic philology. Historians of English and German still work from it. In Ravenna the Arian Baptistery and Theodoric's palace church survive with their mosaics, including a panel where the Gothic court figures were later scrubbed out of the picture. And the crossing of Visigothic Spain at Toledo in 589 set the shape of Iberian Christianity for the next century.

Experience It Today

  • The Arian Baptistery in Ravenna, Italy — built for Theodoric's Gothic church around 500 and still standing with its mosaic dome, part of a UNESCO World Heritage site

Sources

Doctrinal Floor Note

These churches held the Homoian creed agreed at Rimini and Constantinople in 359 and 360: the Son is like the Father, but not described as sharing the Father's very being in the way the Nicene Creed says. That places them outside the Nicene definition the rest of this map works from, and how they should be treated here is an open question that has not been settled.

How Sure the Record Is

Real corpus; Vandal persecution of Nicenes documented by its victims

  • The Gothic Bible of Ulfila (Codex Argenteus etc.; ed. Streitberg) (primary) — The tradition's founding artifact - scripture in Gothic; substantial NT portions survive; texts freely online (Wulfila Project); the Skeireins John-commentary fragments add a rare slice of Gothic exegesis.
  • Auxentius of Durostorum, Letter on the life and creed of Ulfila (trans. in P. Heather & J. Matthews, The Goths in the Fourth Century, TTH 11, 1991) (primary) — The Homoian church's own memory of its apostle, with his creed - preserved in the Dissertatio Maximini; affordable English translation with commentary.
  • Victor of Vita, History of the Vandal Persecution (trans. John Moorhead, TTH 10, 1992) (primary) — Documented by its victims: Nicene account of Vandal Homoian coercion in Africa (429-489); hostile witness - read against the grain for Homoian church life; affordable TTH translation.
  • Acts of the Third Council of Toledo (589) (primary) — The tradition's endpoint: Reccared and the Gothic bishops formally abandon Homoian Christianity; Latin in Vives, Concilios visigoticos (1963); English excerpts in sourcebooks - no standard complete English translation.
  • Robin Whelan, Being Christian in Vandal Africa: The Politics of Orthodoxy in the Post-Imperial West (2018) (secondary) — The key modern corrective - reconstructs Homoian Christianity as a functioning church with clergy, texts and arguments, not a caricature; UC Press.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity, vol. 2, ch. 2, 'Germanic and Celtic Christianities' (Knut Schaferdiek) (reference) — Reference-tradition chapter by the leading specialist on Gothic (Homoian) Christianity.
