About This Person

On the night of his wedding, Evagrius Scholasticus writes, all Antioch was feasting at public cost. About the third hour of the night, an earthquake struck. It threw down most of the buildings and every part of the great church except its dome.

Evagrius was a lawyer, or scholasticus, and a church historian, born at Epiphania on the Orontes. His English translator, E. Walford, puts his birth about 536. He is a different man from the monk Evagrius Ponticus.

As a schoolboy, he says, his parents took him to Apamea after the Persian king Chosroes had burned Antioch. Bishop Thomas carried the wood of the cross around the church, and a fire followed him, "blazing but not consuming".

The plague came two years later, he writes, and he caught it as a schoolboy. Over the years it took several of his children, his wife and many of his kin. Nearly two years before writing this, he lost a daughter and her son. He wondered why such things did not happen to heathens with many children. He told no one, he says, yet Simeon the Stylite the Younger wrote telling him to give up such thoughts, as displeasing to God.

He served Gregory, patriarch of Antioch. A banker accused Gregory of intercourse with his own sister, who was married to another man. Evagrius went with him to Constantinople as his adviser, and Gregory was cleared. Evagrius married four months after Gregory came home. Writings, most of them in Gregory's name, won him the ranks of quaestor and prefect.

He wrote for the side that held to the Council of Chalcedon, and he calls Nestorius "that God-assaulting tongue". Photius, a later patriarch of Constantinople, judged him more trustworthy in doctrine than other historians.

He closed his book in the twelfth year of the emperor Maurice. "If any matter has been overlooked by me or has been treated without sufficient accuracy, let no one blame me," he wrote.

Key Works

  • Ecclesiastical History: six books, from the rise of the quarrel over Nestorius to the twelfth year of Maurice; it tells the councils, the emperors, the Persian wars, the plague and the patriarch Gregory of Antioch, with passages on his own life
  • A volume of memorials, letters, decrees, orations and disputations: which he says he compiled, most of the memorials written in the name of Gregory of Antioch; Walford says it is lost

Legacy

What traces to them

  • His history carries the story of the church on from where Socrates of Constantinople and Theodoret of Cyrrhus end, Photius says, down to the twelfth year of Maurice.
  • Walford says Nicephorus Callistus used it in writing his own history.
  • Wilhelm Arndt and Bruno Krusch, editing Gregory of Tours, match stories in Gregory's books to stories in Evagrius.

How they're remembered

  • Photius, patriarch of Constantinople in the 800s: "The style is not without charm, although somewhat diffuse; in matters of doctrine he is certainly more trustworthy than other historians."
  • Walford finds his style affected and his telling of wonders credulous, and says this should in no way harm his name for honesty.

What Is Uncertain

  • His years. Walford puts his birth about 536; Evagrius gives no year of his own birth, only that he was a schoolboy at the time of the Apamea display and in his fifty-eighth year when he wrote of the plague. Walford's page headings put the display in 540 and his wedding in 589; Evagrius dates the wedding by the year 637 of the era of Antioch. No source used here gives his death.
  • His side. He writes for the Council of Chalcedon and calls Nestorius "that God-assaulting tongue" and Eutyches "impious". He served Gregory of Antioch and wrote in his name, so his account of Gregory is that of his adviser.
  • His family. Evagrius says the plague took several of his children, his wife and many of his kin, and later a daughter and her son. Walford calls the wife his first wife, since Evagrius married again; Evagrius's own words say "my wife". No names are given.
  • The charge against Gregory. Evagrius says a banker accused Gregory of intercourse with his own sister, who was married to another man. Walford calls it a charge of incest. Evagrius gives no year for the hearing at Constantinople.
  • Gregory's years. Walford's headings put Gregory's election in 571 and his death in 594. Andrea Gallandi, in a notice reprinted by Migne, gives 570 to 593.
