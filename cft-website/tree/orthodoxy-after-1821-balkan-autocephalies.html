Orthodoxy after 1821 — the National Churches

Greece, the Balkans and Constantinople, 1821 to 1906 — Orthodoxy under one Ottoman roof breaking into national churches, each new nation claiming a church of its own.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:52

Read along

Under Ottoman rule, the Orthodox of the Balkans were governed as one religious community. That community answered to the Patriarch of Constantinople. The Greek revolt of 1821 broke this order apart. Weeks after the rising began, Patriarch Gregory V was hanged at the gate of his own home. As each new nation won its freedom, its church claimed the right to govern itself. Greece declared its church free in 1833, and Constantinople formally accepted it in 1850. Bulgaria won a decree from the sultan in 1870 that set up a national church, the exarchate. Serbia won its own church in 1879 and Romania in 1885. In 1872 a council at Constantinople condemned phyletism, which means building the church around a nation rather than a place. Even so, national churches kept on spreading. The century did not simply break one church apart. It made a family of churches. Each had its own synod, its own language of worship and its own claim on a people.

The call for a national church was older. Ivanov says the monk Paisii, writing in 1762, named the lack of a national hierarchy and national schools. He listed them among the causes of his people's spiritual ruin. The exarchate of 1870 was a national church.

Not every new Orthodox church grew out of the Ottoman patriarchate. Fortescue, a Catholic writer, dates a self-governing church of the Vlachs of Hungary to 1864. He calls it Hermannstadt. Today the town is Sibiu.

Documented Stories

The Regency Closes the Monasteries

When the Greek revolt ended, the church in the liberated territory had no lawful head, since the Patriarch of Constantinople was the Sultan's subject and had anathematized the rising. King Otto, a Bavarian prince of seventeen, arrived in 1833 with a Bavarian regency, whose Protestant jurist Georg von Maurer drew up with the Greek cleric Theoklitos Pharmakidis a settlement on the Bavarian model. On 23 July 1833 a royal decree declared the Orthodox Church of the Kingdom of Greece autocephalous, independent of every foreign authority, and governed by a Holy Synod appointed by the king, with a royal commissioner at every session and no act valid without the crown's approval. Constantinople was not consulted. Konstantinos Oikonomos, the ablest theologian in the kingdom, denounced it as schism and was ignored. Then the regency turned to the monasteries. Decrees of 1833 dissolved every men's monastery with fewer than six monks and all but three of the women's houses, and took their lands and property for a national fund that was to pay for schools and clergy and mostly did not. Of some 524 monasteries, 412 were closed; the monks were sent to the survivors or into the world, and much of the treasure sold. The people, who had hidden in those monasteries from the Turks, did not forget it. Constantinople recognized the Church of Greece in 1850, and the pattern the regency set, a national church under a state, was followed by every Balkan nation after.

The Epiphany Liturgy of 1872

The Bulgarians had asked for a generation for bishops who spoke their language, and in March 1870 the Sultan, glad to divide his Orthodox subjects, had issued a firman creating a Bulgarian Exarchate. The Patriarchate of Constantinople refused to recognize it and forbade the Bulgarian bishops in the capital to officiate. On 6 January 1872, the feast of Epiphany, three of them celebrated the liturgy in the Bulgarian church of St Stephen on the Golden Horn before a crowd that filled the streets, in open defiance; the patriarch suspended them and the Porte briefly exiled them. The crowd had them back within days. In February the Bulgarian assembly elected Antim, metropolitan of Vidin, as exarch; on 11 May 1872 he celebrated the liturgy in the same church and read a proclamation declaring the Bulgarian church independent, and the patriarch declared him deposed. In September a council at Constantinople, with the patriarchs of Alexandria and Antioch, condemned phyletism, the organization of the church by race or nation within the same territory, as a heresy foreign to the church's tradition, and declared the Exarchate and all who adhered to it schismatic. The Bulgarians called it the Greek council. The schism lasted seventy-three years, through the making of Bulgaria, two Balkan wars and two world wars, and was lifted in 1945; the condemnation of phyletism stands, and every Orthodox church founded since on a nation has had to explain why it does not apply to itself.

Connections

In Its Own Record

The hanging of Patriarch Gregory V in 1821 opened the break-up of a single Ottoman-era Orthodox community into national churches: Greek autocephaly (declared 1833, recognized by the Tomos of 1850), the Bulgarian Exarchate (1870), and recognition for Serbia (1879) and Romania (1885). A council in 1872 condemned phyletism, organizing churches by nationality rather than place, even as the practice went on spreading.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Weeks after the Greek revolt broke out in 1821, the Patriarch of Constantinople was hanged at his own gate. By the end of the century, Greece, Bulgaria, Serbia and Romania had each won a church that ran itself. In 1872 a council at Constantinople condemned the practice of building churches by nation rather than by place. It kept happening anyway.

Legacy

These are the churches that make up Orthodoxy today: the Church of Greece and the Bulgarian, Serbian and Romanian patriarchates all date their modern independence to this century, and the pattern they set — a self-governing church per nation — became the normal shape of the Orthodox world. So did the argument the 1872 council started. Wherever Orthodox jurisdictions overlap on the same ground, as they do throughout the diaspora, the question of whether a church is defined by place or by people comes straight back.
