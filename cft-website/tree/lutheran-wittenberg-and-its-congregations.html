The Reformation Era · 1517-1580 · Saxony, then N. Europe

Saxony, today in Germany and beyond

Lutheran Wittenberg & Its Congregations

Meet Nikolaus on Church in Conversation

About This Movement

Wittenberg was a small Saxon university town when one of its theology professors, an Augustinian friar named Martin Luther, wrote 95 theses against the sale of indulgences and sent them to the archbishop of Mainz on 31 October 1517. Tradition also holds that he nailed a copy to the door of the Castle Church, the university's usual notice board for an academic debate — but that detail comes only from an account written decades later, after Luther's death, and historians still disagree over whether it happened. Within a few years a movement had grown around him that reorganized worship, emptied monasteries, and put the Bible, a catechism and a hymnbook into ordinary German households. What that looked like from the pew is documented unusually well. From 1527-28 the Saxon churches sent visitors out to examine parish after parish, and their reports are frank about how little many villagers actually knew. The movement settled its own arguments in writing — the Augsburg Confession of 1530, then the Book of Concord of 1580. And the churches shaped by it spread north and east across Germany and Scandinavia, where they remain.

In Its Own Record

Influenced By

  • Antiochene Exegetical Christianity (Chrysostom-centered) Documented — The Augsburg Confession of 1530 calls on Chrysostom as a witness to how the early church shared Communion. Melanchthon wrote a defense of the Confession, known as the Apology. In it he quotes a saying of Chrysostom. It runs: contrition in the heart, confession in the mouth, humility in the work.
  • Rhineland Mysticism & the Friends of God Documented — Martin Luther printed the Theologia Deutsch at Wittenberg, part of it in 1516 and a fuller text in 1518. In his preface he wrote that, next to the Bible and Augustine, no book had taught him more of God, Christ and man. In 1521 he cited Tauler on purgatory.
  • The Hussite & Bohemian Brethren Movement Documented — In his 1520 letter to the German nobility, Luther wrote that he had not yet found any errors in Hus's writings. He said Hus's books and teaching were unjustly condemned, and he urged union with the Bohemians.

Influenced

  • The Confessional Lutheran Revival & the Emigrations Documented — In 1911 Henry Eyster Jacobs edited the Book of Concord. He did it for the General Council, a body of American Lutherans. The book holds the Lutheran statements of faith from the 1500s, such as Luther's catechisms. In his preface he saw a 'widespread and all but general return' to them.

Argued Against

  • The Anabaptist Movements Documented — Lutheran leaders condemned the Anabaptists by name in the Augsburg Confession of 1530. The confession rejects their refusal of infant baptism and their ban on Christians holding public office.
  • The Reformed Cities - Zurich & Geneva Documented — The Lutheran Formula of Concord (1577) has a chapter on the Lord's Supper that names the 'Zwinglian teachers'. It says they separated from the Augsburg Confession when it was presented. The chapter goes on to condemn the 'Sacramentarians'.

Opposed By

  • The Anabaptist Movements Documented — The Hutterite chronicle names Luther and Zwingli. It says they exposed the papacy's abuses but then leaned on worldly rulers and human help. This is one Anabaptist group's view, in a chronicle kept over several generations.

In Contact With

  • The Reformed Cities - Zurich & Geneva Documented — In October 1529 Zwingli traveled to Marburg for a colloquy with Luther and Melanchthon on the Lord's Supper. Two Zurich writers record it: Zwingli himself, who says he preached there, and his friend Myconius.

Sources

  • Luther's Works (American Edition, 55 vols + new series, Concordia/Fortress), incl. the Small and Large Catechisms (1529) and the hymns as used texts (primary)
  • The Saxon visitation records (from 1527-28, with Melanchthon's Instructions for the Visitors) and church ordinances in Sehling, Die evangelischen Kirchenordnungen des XVI. Jahrhunderts (primary) — Shows what villagers actually knew. Mostly untranslated German archival material. Historians disagree on how to read it: Strauss's 'failure' reading (Luther's House of Learning, 1978) versus critics like Scribner and Kittelson; the debate is part of what the record means.
  • Argula von Grumbach, A Woman's Voice in the Reformation (ed./trans. Matheson, T&T Clark, 1995); Katharina Schuetz Zell, Church Mother (ed./trans. McKee, Chicago, 2006) (primary) — Women's voices in their own published words; Zell writes from Strasbourg, within the wider evangelical world.
  • The confessional documents: Augsburg Confession (1530), Book of Concord (1580; ed. Kolb & Wengert, Fortress, 2000), read against parish registers the ordinances mandated (primary)
  • Susan Karant-Nunn, The Reformation of Ritual (Routledge, 1997), with Strauss (1978) as classic provocation (secondary) — A lived-practice view of parish religion.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 6, ch. 1 'Martin Luther, reformer' (Hendrix), with ch. 2 (Brady) and ch. 4 (Lehmann) (reference)
