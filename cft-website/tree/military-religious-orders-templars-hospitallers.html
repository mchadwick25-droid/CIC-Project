The High Medieval Era · 1120-1291 · Latin East, Iberia

Military-Religious Orders (Templars, Hospitallers)

The Latin East and Iberia, 1120 to 1291 — men who took monastic vows of poverty, chastity and obedience and then went to war, defended in their own time by the most admired churchman in Europe.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:15

Read along

In the decades after the First Crusade, two kinds of institution fused into something the church had never authorized before. The Hospitallers began as a hospital in Jerusalem caring for pilgrims. They called the sick 'our lords the sick', and only later took on a military role. The Templars began as a small band of knights guarding the pilgrim roads. They took monastic vows and were given a rule at the Council of Troyes in 1129. Bernard of Clairvaux wrote their defense, In Praise of the New Knighthood. He argued that a man who killed in this cause was not a homicide but, in his own coined word, a malicide: a killer of evil. Both orders grew enormously rich. They ran castles, estates and banking networks from the Latin East across Europe, and lost their reason for existing when Acre fell in 1291. The Templars were arrested in France in 1307 and made to confess under torture. In 1312 Pope Clement V abolished their order for ever.

Documented Stories

White Bread for Our Lords the Sick

The Hospitallers began as a hospital, and in 1182, six years before Jerusalem fell, their master Roger des Moulins had the house’s customs for the sick written down as statutes. The document survives, and it is precise. Each patient in the great hall is to have his own bed, with a mattress, a sheet, a coverlet and — the clause that is quoted — ‘a little cloak of sheepskin and boots for going to the latrines’. Four physicians are to be employed who can examine urine, diagnose, and prescribe; the house is to buy the sick whatever the physicians order. The sick are to be fed white bread, not the brown the brothers ate, and given meat three times a week; on the days when they cannot eat meat, chicken. Infants abandoned at the hospital are to be taken in and wet-nurses hired for them. Women in childbirth have their own ward with cradles beside the beds, ‘so that the child may lie alone and not be endangered by its mother’. The statutes call the patients, throughout, ‘our lords the sick’, and the brothers their servants. Pilgrims’ reports from the 1170s put the number of beds at up to two thousand. The same house had by then been fighting for forty years, its brothers-at-arms garrisoning the castle of Krak des Chevaliers; the sick were nursed by an order that killed. The statutes do not discuss the tension. They discuss the bread.

Jacques de Molay Takes Back His Confession

The Templars had been arrested across France on a single morning in October 1307 on the orders of Philip IV, who owed them money, and interrogated by royal officers and inquisitors under torture. Jacques de Molay, the master, confessed within two weeks to denying Christ and spitting on the cross at his reception, and repeated it before the university of Paris; he retracted before papal envoys, was told he would be treated as a relapsed heretic, and confessed again. On 18 March 1314 Molay and three other senior brothers were brought onto a scaffold in front of Notre-Dame to hear a commission of cardinals sentence them to perpetual imprisonment. The continuator of the chronicle of Nangis, who was in Paris, records what happened: as the sentence was read, Molay and Geoffroi de Charney, preceptor of Normandy, stood up and declared to the crowd that they had lied, that the order was innocent and holy, and that their only crime had been to confess falsely to save their lives. The cardinals, taken aback, remanded them; the king, hearing of it the same day, ordered them burned that evening as relapsed heretics, on a small island in the Seine, without further trial. The chronicler says they went to the fire calmly and that the people took their ashes as relics. What the record shows is a man who broke under torture twice and, at the end, with nothing to gain, said so.

In Its Own Record

These orders fused monastic vows with arms, something the church had never authorized before, and Bernard of Clairvaux wrote their defense, In Praise of the New Knighthood. The Hospitallers continued after the fall of Acre in 1291, moving their base to Rhodes in 1310 and remaining there into the sixteenth century.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

The Hospitallers began as a hospital in Jerusalem caring for pilgrims. They called the sick 'our lords the sick', and only later took on a military role. The Templars began as knights guarding the pilgrim roads and were given a monastic rule at the Council of Troyes in 1129. Bernard of Clairvaux defended this joining of vows and arms in his In Praise of the New Knighthood. He argued that a knight who killed in this cause was not a homicide but a 'malicide', a killer of evil. Both orders grew rich running castles, estates and banking networks. After Acre fell in 1291, the Templars were arrested and tortured into confession. In 1312 Pope Clement V abolished their order.

Legacy

One of the two orders is still going. The Hospitallers moved to Rhodes, then Malta, and continue today as the Sovereign Military Order of Malta, a hospitaller body with a legal personality of its own, alongside Protestant orders of St John in Britain and Germany out of which St John Ambulance grew — the medical vocation outlived the military one by many centuries. The Templars did not survive, and their destruction left a very long afterlife in legend and conspiracy writing that has almost nothing to do with the historical order. What both left physically is architecture. Castles across the eastern Mediterranean and Iberia, and round churches in Europe built in imitation of the Holy Sepulchre, of which London's Temple Church is the best known.

Experience It Today

  • London, England — the Temple Church, consecrated in 1185 as the Templars' English headquarters, with its round nave and its floor of knightly effigies

Sources

On the Nicene Creed

No creedal question — these orders held the ordinary faith of the Latin church. The hard question they raise is holy violence, and it is a real one.

How Sure the Record Is

Rich institutionally; interior voice thin

  • The Rule of the Templars (trans. J. M. Upton-Ward, Boydell, 1992) (primary) — Primitive Rule, hierarchical statutes, penances - the fused monastic-military life in regulated detail, down to horses and mess discipline.
  • Bernard of Clairvaux, In Praise of the New Knighthood (De laude novae militiae) (trans. Greenia, Cistercian Fathers 19) (primary) — The apologia for the fused monastic-military life; the era's theology of holy violence stated plainly.
  • Malcolm Barber & Keith Bate, The Templars: Selected Sources (Manchester Medieval Sources, 2002) (primary) — Foundation documents, papal bulls, charters, letters from the East, and trial documents. The trial testimony was extracted under torture (1307-12) and should be read as coerced, not free confession.
  • The Hospitallers' Rule and statutes (trans. E. J. King, The Rule, Statutes and Customs of the Hospitallers, 1934) (primary) — The order's own legislation, with its 'lords the sick' care obligation - the charitable half of the fusion. This is an older translation.
  • Malcolm Barber, The New Knighthood: A History of the Order of the Temple (CUP, 1994) (secondary) — Standard history, foundation to suppression.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 4, ch. 4 (McGuire) + ch. 11 'Christendom and Islam' (Nirenberg) (reference) — No single chapter is dedicated to the military orders specifically; coverage is split across these two chapters.
