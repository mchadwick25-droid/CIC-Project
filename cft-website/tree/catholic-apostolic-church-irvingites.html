Britain, today in United Kingdom

Catholic Apostolic Church (Irvingites)

Britain, 1832 to 1901 — a church that expected the world to end, restored twelve apostles to meet it, and then quietly ran out of clergy when the last of them died.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:15

Read along

In 1830 and 1831, people in the fashionable London congregation of the Scottish preacher Edward Irving began speaking in tongues and prophesying — something almost nobody in Britain had expected to see again. And Irving was deposed by his presbytery for his teaching and died in 1834, before the movement that bears his name had really taken shape. What emerged was a church built on the conviction that Christ was about to return and had restored the apostolate to prepare for it. Twelve men were called as apostles, and around them grew a fourfold ministry, church buildings on a near-cathedral scale, and one of the richest liturgies composed in the nineteenth century. Because the apostles were held to be irreplaceable, no successors were ordained. As they died, the church's ministry simply ran out, and when the last of them died in February 1901 its congregations entered what they called the time of silence. Continuing to worship, with nobody left who could ordain anyone.

Voices in the Record

  • Edward Irving — the Scottish preacher whose London congregation was where it began, deposed in 1833 and dead in 1834; the movement is named for him although he was never one of its apostles
  • Mary Campbell and Margaret Macdonald — the young women in western Scotland whose 1830 prophecies and tongues touched the whole thing off, and whose words survive only in other people's accounts
  • John Bate Cardale — the London lawyer called as the first of the twelve apostles, and the principal author of the church's liturgy
  • Henry Drummond — banker, politician and patron, on whose Albury estate the prophetic conferences met
  • Francis Valentine Woodhouse — the last surviving apostle, who died on 3 February 1901, after which no more ordinations were possible

Documented Stories

The Voice in the Church at Annan

Edward Irving had been removed from his Regent Square church in London in 1832 for allowing tongues and prophesying in the services, and had taken his congregation to a hall in Newman Street. The Church of Scotland then moved against his doctrine. He had taught that Christ took human nature as it is after the Fall, kept sinless only by the Spirit, and the presbytery of Annan, which had licensed him as a young man, was directed to try him. The trial was held in Annan church on 13 March 1833 before a crowd that filled it to the doors, from eleven in the morning until after dark by candlelight. Irving defended himself for hours. The presbytery found him guilty and the moderator rose to pronounce deposition from the ministry. At that moment David Dow, an elder from Irving's London congregation, cried out in a voice the witnesses called unearthly: ‘Arise, depart! Arise, depart! Flee ye out, flee ye out of her! Ye cannot pray. How can ye pray? How can ye pray to Christ whom ye deny?’ Irving, who held that such utterances were the Spirit's, took his hat, said he would obey the voice of God, and walked out through the crowd into the dark, followed by many of them. He was deposed in his absence and died in Glasgow in December 1834 at forty-two. The church that took his name had by then called its first apostle, and Irving was never one.

Baxter Recants

Robert Baxter, a Doncaster barrister and Anglican, came to Irving's congregation in the autumn of 1831, when the tongues and prophecies had been heard there for months, and within weeks was himself speaking in the power, as they called it, uttering messages the congregation received as the Spirit's. Through the winter of 1831–32 he became the most prominent of the prophets. His utterances declared that the church was to be prepared for the Lord's coming in 1,260 days, by July 1835; that a new order of apostles and prophets was being raised; and that he himself was to leave his profession and go out. He obeyed in part, and doubted. By the spring of 1832 he had noticed that some utterances contradicted others and that a prophecy about his own family had failed; he concluded that a lying spirit had spoken through him, as through the prophets of Ahab, and told Irving so. Irving would not accept it. In 1833 Baxter published his Narrative of Facts, Characterizing the Supernatural Manifestations in Members of Mr. Irving's Congregation, a sober account of what he had said and felt and why he now believed it delusion, the fullest inside description of the movement's prophetic origins. The congregation dismissed him as a fallen prophet; the church that formed around the apostles called from those same utterances went on to build, out of them, one of the century's great liturgies. Both facts are in the record.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Between 1830 and 1831, members of Edward Irving's fashionable London congregation began speaking in tongues and prophesying, convinced that Christ's return was near, and the church that grew out of it restored twelve apostles to prepare for it. Because the apostles were held to be irreplaceable, no successors were ever ordained, and as they died one by one the church's ministry simply ran out. When the last apostle died in February 1901, its congregations entered what they called the time of silence, continuing to worship with no one left who could ordain anyone.

Legacy

The liturgy outlived the church that wrote it. Scholars of worship studied it long after the congregations fell silent, and it drew on Eastern, Roman and Anglican sources in a combination nobody else had attempted. Its buildings survive and are still in use by other congregations — the great church in Gordon Square, London, is Grade I listed. And a German branch that broke away in 1863 precisely because it wanted to keep calling new apostles became the New Apostolic Church, which today has millions of members, mostly in Africa and Europe. The original body itself has no ministry left and, by its own theology, cannot acquire one.

Experience It Today

  • The Church of Christ the King, Gordon Square, London — built 1850-54 as the Catholic Apostolic Church's central church, Grade I listed and still in use for worship

Sources

On the Nicene Creed

The Catholic Apostolic Church was trinitarian and creedally conventional; there is no question at all about its doctrine. What is genuinely open is a different question — whether a body whose entire existence rested on twelve restored apostles counts as a tradition in its own right or as a movement defined by an office. It answered that question about itself more plainly than most: when the last apostle died in 1901 it ordained no successors and let its ministry lapse, yet its congregations went on worshipping for another half-century.

How Sure the Record Is

Real liturgical and institutional record

  • Edward Irving's writings, incl. the christological works, with the 1830 London and 1833 Annan trial records (primary) — Irving died in 1834 and was never himself one of the twelve apostles the church later restored - worth keeping distinct from the movement's founding narrative. The trial records are adversarial legal material, not the movement's own account.
  • The prophetic origin record: the 1830-31 Scottish manifestations (Mary Campbell, Margaret Macdonald); the Newman Street prophesyings; Robert Baxter, Narrative of Facts (1833) (primary) — The women's voices at the movement's origin survive only as transmitted through others' accounts, a real filter. Baxter's Narrative of Facts is a former prophet's recantation, written after he left the movement and turned critic.
  • The church's own liturgy and testimony: The Liturgy and Other Divine Offices (1842-); the Great Testimony (1837-38); Cardale, Readings upon the Liturgy (primary) — This liturgy is among the century's richest liturgical creations, and shows the church in its own most characteristic register.
  • The institutional record: Gordon Square and Albury records, angel/elder/deacon registers, the sevenfold London churches; the post-1901 'time of silence' record (primary) — This documents a church continuing to record its own life even after the apostolic office that had defined it lapsed.
  • Columba Graham Flegg, Gathered Under Apostles (OUP, 1992), with Tim Grass, Edward Irving: The Lord's Watchman (Paternoster, 2011) (secondary) — Flegg's is the standard study; he has his own connection to the tradition, which is worth noting.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 8 (chapter assignment hedged): nearest Bebbington's voluntary-religion and Wolffe's UK-identities chapters (reference) — No chapter in this reference volume is dedicated to this movement; these are the nearest topical fits.
