Origen

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition.

Known for
Teacher and Bible scholar at Alexandria and Caesarea, who wrote commentaries on Scripture and a book of doctrine that was argued over for centuries.

About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Around the year two hundred, in Alexandria, Origen's father was arrested for his faith. Origen was a boy, and the church historian Eusebius says he was seized by such a longing for martyrdom that he rushed toward danger. His mother hid his clothes to keep him home, so he wrote to his father in prison. Eusebius kept one sentence of that letter: "Take heed not to change your mind on our account."

His father died a martyr, and the state took the family's property. Origen was not yet seventeen. In his eighteenth year he took charge of teaching the faith in Alexandria, because everyone else had fled. Jerome says he was so devoted to the Scriptures that he learned Hebrew, against the habit of his time and his people. He then set the Hebrew text beside the Greek translations, in a work called the Hexapla.

He taught that Scripture, like a person, has body, soul and spirit. In On First Principles he says the plain story builds up simple believers, its soul those who have advanced, and its spiritual law the perfect. The book also taught that the Son's generation is as eternal as brightness from the sun, and that souls existed before birth. We read that book mostly in Rufinus's Latin, and Jerome said Rufinus changed it. Origen also wrote eight books in answer to Celsus, who had attacked Christians in a book called the True Discourse.

But on a journey through Palestine, the bishops of Caesarea and Jerusalem ordained Origen a priest. Jerome says this so enraged his own bishop, Demetrius, that he wrote everywhere to ruin Origen's name. In the year 231 Origen left Alexandria for Caesarea. He later wrote that he had come through a storm and been brought out of Egypt. About twenty years later, under the emperor Decius, he was stretched in the stocks. Jerome says he died at Tyre, aged sixty-nine.

Gregory of Nazianzus later sent a friend a book of passages from Origen, which he and Basil of Caesarea are thought to have gathered. The church historian Socrates, who defended Origen, says Athanasius often cited him in support of his own faith. Three centuries after his death, a council at Constantinople named him among condemned heretics, though how far it went is still argued.

Key Works

  • On First Principles: his teaching on God, the Son, souls and Scripture
  • Against Celsus: eight books answering a pagan attack on Christians
  • The Hexapla: the Hebrew text set beside the Greek translations; the church historian Eusebius of Caesarea says Origen left us copies of it. Jerome says Origen searched out three more translations, which Jerome himself had from Origen's library

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Eusebius of Caesarea tells of two brothers, Gregory the Wonderworker and Athenodorus, who came to Origen at Caesarea full of zeal for Greek and Roman learning. He gave them a love of philosophy and turned them to the study of divinity. After five years with him, both became bishops in Pontus.
  • A letter Origen wrote to that Gregory survives in the Philocalia. It urges him to take from Greek learning whatever serves Christianity, as geometry and grammar serve philosophy.
  • Gregory of Nazianzus, sending the Philocalia to a friend, called it a selection of passages useful to students of literature.

How they're remembered

  • The church historian Socrates of Constantinople, who defended Origen, names four men who attacked him: Methodius of Olympus, Eustathius of Antioch, Apollinaris and Theophilus of Alexandria. He says each blamed a different point, and none charged Origen with unsound teaching on the Trinity.
  • Socrates of Constantinople also says Theophilus of Alexandria wrote to the bishops condemning Origen's books, to hide his real aim, which was to move against John Chrysostom.
  • Epiphanius of Cyprus charged, and Jerome repeated, that Origen taught even the devil would one day repent.
  • The Fifth Council at Constantinople, in 553, named Origen in its eleventh anathema, beside Arius and others. The historian Evagrius Scholasticus says the council also issued special anathemas against him. Whether it did is still disputed.

At Major Councils

  • Second Council of Constantinople, 553: Long dead; named in the eleventh anathema

What Is Uncertain

  • We read On First Principles mostly in Rufinus of Aquileia's Latin, and Jerome said Rufinus changed it.
  • Jerome says Origen was ordained in Palestine on a journey, which so enraged his bishop Demetrius that he wrote everywhere to ruin Origen's name. Eusebius of Caesarea orders it differently: Demetrius wrote against Origen first, out of jealousy, and the ordination came after.
  • Eusebius of Caesarea is our witness that Origen took a saying of Jesus too literally and made himself a eunuch. He adds that Demetrius at first admired the act, then wrote of it to bishops everywhere as most foolish. Jerome says only that Demetrius was wildly enraged at Origen after his ordination.
  • Eusebius of Caesarea says Origen died in the time of the emperor Trebonianus Gallus, at sixty-nine. Jerome adds that he died at Tyre and was buried there.
