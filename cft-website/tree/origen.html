Origen

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition.

Origen taught the Christian faith at Alexandria, in Egypt, and then at Caesarea, in Palestine, and spent his life asking what Scripture means beneath its plain words. He set the Hebrew text beside the Greek translations, and was tortured under the emperor Decius.

About This Person

At Alexandria, in Egypt, Origen's father taught him Scripture every day. Eusebius of Caesarea, who tells most of Origen's life about two generations after his death, from letters and from people who knew him, says the boy was not content with the plain, obvious meaning. He kept asking what the inspired words really meant, until his father scolded him to his face, for appearance's sake, not to search past his age or past the plain meaning. Yet in private the father thanked God for such a son, and they say he often stood by the sleeping boy, uncovered his chest and kissed it.

That question, what Scripture means beneath its plain words, became Origen's life's work. He taught the faith at Alexandria and then at Caesarea, on the coast of Palestine. He set the Hebrew text beside the Greek translations, and bishops and scholars after him read Scripture through his books and fought over them for centuries.

Demetrius, the bishop of Alexandria, entrusted to him alone the teaching of the faith to those who came to learn it. Eusebius says Origen took too literally Jesus' saying that some men have made themselves eunuchs for the sake of the kingdom of heaven, and castrated himself. He did it to keep the saying, and to give unbelievers no ground for scandal, since he taught women as well as men. Learning of it, Demetrius admired his daring and urged him to keep teaching.

Origen learned Hebrew, got the Hebrew Scriptures from the Jewish people, and searched out other Greek translations besides the Seventy, the old Greek translation which the churches read. He set them side by side in columns with the Hebrew. He kept the churches' Greek, he explained in a letter to a fellow scholar, so that he would not be found passing a forgery to the churches. Yet he learned the Jewish readings, so that in arguments with Jewish people he would not quote what their copies lacked.

In a book called On First Principles, he wrote of why readers go wrong. The unbelieving Jewish people, he said, followed the bare letter of the prophecies and did not receive Jesus. Heretics, teachers he judged false, reading of God's anger and jealousy, took the God of the Jewish Scriptures for an imperfect, lesser maker of the world, and said Jesus had proclaimed a more perfect God above him. Simple believers imagined things of God they would not believe of the most savage and unjust man. "All these I have spoken of hold false and impious opinions about God, or ignorant ones. The only reason, it seems, is that Scripture is not understood on its spiritual side, but is taken in its bare, literal sense."

As a human being is body, soul and spirit, he taught, so is Scripture: its body, the plain sense, helps simple believers, its soul those further on, and its spirit the perfect. Yet the Word of God, he wrote, had set stumbling blocks in it, things impossible if read by the letter, so that readers would look for a meaning worthy of God.

Porphyry, a pagan philosopher who had met Origen when Porphyry was young and later wrote against the Christians, attacked this way of reading. He tied it to the secret rites of Greek religion. Origen, Porphyry wrote, used the books of Chaeremon, a Stoic philosopher, and of Cornutus. "From these writers he learned the figurative way of explaining the Greek mystery rites, and he applied it to the Jewish scriptures."

Years later, on a church errand to Greece, Origen passed through Palestine and was ordained at Caesarea as a presbyter, an elder, a priest of the church. Here the old sources disagree. Eusebius says Demetrius, seeing him grow famous, had already written to bishops everywhere that his castration as a youth had been most foolish. After the ordination, with nothing else to say against him, Demetrius accused him bitterly, and those who had ordained him too. Jerome, writing over a century later, says the ordination itself so enraged Demetrius that he wrote everywhere against him.

Origen left Alexandria for Caesarea. In his book on the Gospel of John, he wrote: "We came out of the storm; we were brought out of Egypt. The God who led his people out from there set us free."

At Caesarea two brothers came to study with him, and he found them keen on Greek and Roman learning. He gave them a love of philosophy and turned them to the study of God, and after five years both became bishops in Pontus.

The emperor Decius, Eusebius says, persecuted the churches out of hatred for Philip, the emperor before him. For the word of Christ, Origen endured chains, tortures of the body, the iron collar and the dungeon. For many days his feet were stretched four spaces in the stocks, a wooden frame that held a prisoner's feet apart, and he was threatened with fire. His judge did all he could not to let him die. Origen died in the time of the emperor Gallus, at sixty-nine.

Pamphilus of Caesarea wrote a Defense of him, answering from Origen's own words those who said his books differed from the church's teaching. In the century after Origen's death, the church historian Socrates says, Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nazianzus got his books and drew from them the right reading of Scripture. When the Arians, who taught that the Son of God had a beginning, quoted Origen, he adds, the two showed that the Arians misunderstood him.

Gregory also sent a friend the Philocalia of Origen, a book of passages chosen from his writings for students. Its first chapter is on how Scripture is to be read and understood, and why, taken literally, it sometimes seems impossible.

About three centuries after his death, a council of bishops at Constantinople named him, with his writings, among the heretics.

Dates
No old source used here gives a calendar year for his life. Eusebius sets the persecution in which his father died in the tenth year of the emperor Severus, when Origen was not quite seventeen. He sets the move to Caesarea in the tenth year of the emperor Alexander. He says Origen was tortured under Decius and died in the time of Gallus, aged sixty-nine. The years in the translators' notes are their own reckoning, and the third century comes from the modern dating of those reigns.

Key Works

  • On First Principles: his teaching on God, Christ, souls and how Scripture is to be read. Eusebius lists it among the works he wrote at Alexandria. Most of it is read in Rufinus of Aquileia's Latin, and Rufinus says he left out or reworded what he found on the Trinity that disagreed with Origen's teaching elsewhere. The part on reading Scripture also survives in Origen's Greek, kept in the Philocalia
  • Against Celsus: eight books answering True Discourse, which Celsus the Epicurean had written against the Christians. Eusebius says he wrote them when he was over sixty
  • The Hexapla: the Hebrew text set beside the Greek translations; the church historian Eusebius of Caesarea says Origen left us copies of it. Jerome says Origen searched out three more translations, which Jerome himself had from Origen's library

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Eusebius of Caesarea tells of two brothers, Gregory and Athenodorus, who came to Origen at Caesarea full of zeal for Greek and Roman learning. He gave them a love of philosophy and turned them to the study of divinity. After five years with him, both became bishops in Pontus.
  • Jerome says Gregory gave a speech of thanks to Origen before a large gathering, with Origen present. The speech says Origen had his pupils read all the philosophers and poets, rejecting none but those who deny God and providence.
  • A letter from Origen to a Gregory he calls his son survives in the Philocalia. It urges him to take from Greek philosophy what can prepare for Christianity, as geometry and grammar serve philosophy.
  • Dionysius, one of his pupils, took charge of the teaching at Alexandria after Heraclas and later became bishop there, Eusebius says.
  • Pamphilus of Caesarea collected a library of his works and copied most of them by hand, and Jerome says they were still kept in the library at Caesarea. Eusebius gathered as many of his letters as he could find and arranged them in books.
  • Pamphilus, with Eusebius, wrote a Defense of Origen. Photius says it was in six books, five written by Pamphilus in prison and the sixth by Eusebius after Pamphilus's death. Its first book answers his accusers from Origen's own words, above all on On First Principles, the books they said differed most from the church's teaching.
  • Athanasius of Alexandria, defending the Council of Nicaea, called on Origen as a hardworking witness that the Word of God always existed with the Father.
  • The church historian Socrates says Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nazianzus got Origen's books and drew from them the right reading of Scripture. When the Arians, who taught that the Son of God had a beginning, quoted Origen, the two showed that the Arians did not understand him.
  • Gregory of Nazianzus, sending the Philocalia to a friend, called it a selection of passages useful to students of literature.
  • Jerome, attacked because his new Latin translation of the Old Testament seemed to censure the Seventy, the old Greek translation, answered that Origen himself had marked where the Seventy fell short of the Hebrew.

How they're remembered

  • Porphyry, a pagan philosopher who met Origen when Porphyry was young and later wrote against the Christians, said Origen took the Greeks' figurative way of explaining their mystery rites and applied it to the Jewish scriptures. Eusebius says Porphyry spoke truly of his hard work and learning.
  • Methodius of Olympus wrote On the Resurrection against him. By Photius's extracts, Methodius charged that Origen taught the body was given to the soul as a fetter after the fall, and that the soul had lived without a body before.
  • The church historian Socrates of Constantinople, who defended Origen, names four men who attacked him: Methodius of Olympus, Eustathius of Antioch, Apollinaris and Theophilus of Alexandria. He says each blamed a different point, and none charged Origen with unsound teaching on the Trinity.
  • Socrates of Constantinople also says Theophilus of Alexandria wrote to the bishops condemning Origen's books, to hide his real aim, which was to move against John Chrysostom.
  • Epiphanius of Cyprus charged, and Jerome repeated, that Origen taught even the devil would one day repent.
  • Epiphanius wrote against Origen more than a century after his death. He says that in Alexandria Origen was made to put incense on an altar fire and was cast out by the confessors and the church. Unable to bear the mockery, Epiphanius adds, he left for Palestine.
  • About three centuries after his death, a council at Constantinople named Origen in its eleventh anathema, a formal curse, beside Arius and others, with their impious writings.
  • The church historian Evagrius Scholasticus says that monks petitioned the council against Origen's teachings. He says the council sent the emperor Justinian a report after crying out against Origen and those who shared his errors, and attached chapters setting out what Origen's followers had been taught to hold.
  • The History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria, a much later Egyptian chronicle, says Demetrius cut him off for writing unlawful books of magic, and that he then wrote treatises full of blasphemies.

Open Questions

  • The witnesses. Most of his life rests on Eusebius of Caesarea, who wrote about two generations after his death, from letters and from people who knew him. Eusebius was a defender: he says he and Pamphilus wrote their Defense of Origen because of faultfinders. Jerome wrote over a century after Origen's death, and Photius, who summarizes Pamphilus's lost account, about six centuries after.
  • Jerome says Origen was ordained in Palestine on a journey, which so enraged his bishop Demetrius that he wrote everywhere to ruin Origen's name. Eusebius of Caesarea orders it differently: Demetrius wrote against Origen first, seeing him prosper, and the ordination came after.
  • Photius, summarizing Pamphilus, says Origen was ordained against the church's rule, as he was leaving for Athens without his bishop's permission. A gathering of bishops and some presbyters then ruled that he must not live or teach in Alexandria but could keep his priesthood, and later Demetrius and some Egyptian bishops took his priesthood from him too. No earlier source used here tells of this gathering. Jerome says Origen made the journey with a church letter.
  • Eusebius of Caesarea is our witness that Origen took a saying of Jesus too literally and made himself a eunuch. He adds that Demetrius at first admired the act, then wrote of it to bishops everywhere as most foolish. Jerome says only that Demetrius was wildly enraged at Origen after his ordination.
  • In his Commentary on Matthew, Origen writes that some took the saying on eunuchs in a bodily sense and did it to themselves, out of fear of God but without understanding, and brought reproach on themselves. He names no one, himself included.
  • In his Commentary on John, Origen says he came out of the storm in Alexandria and that an enemy attacked him in new writings. He does not say who the enemy was.
  • Eusebius of Caesarea says Origen died in the time of the emperor Gallus, at sixty-nine. Jerome adds that he died at Tyre and was buried there. Photius says Pamphilus and others wrote that he died a martyr at Caesarea under Decius. Photius himself thinks the account of Tyre truer, unless the letters Origen is said to have written after the persecution are forged.
  • We read On First Principles mostly in Rufinus of Aquileia's Latin. Rufinus says in his preface that wherever he found anything on the Trinity contrary to what Origen taught elsewhere, he left it out or set it down in words that agree with the rule of faith. Jerome, who made his own translation, says Rufinus cut out what gave offense, while he himself set the books down just as they stood in the Greek.
  • The simple believers. In On First Principles, the third group who go wrong about God are, in Crombie's English, the simpler among those who profess to belong to the church. In Lewis's English, they are the less sophisticated among those who have left the church.
  • He does not say every passage has three senses. He writes that some passages have no bodily, plain sense at all, and that the plain sense alone helps a great many simple believers.
  • Where the way of reading came from. Origen points to Paul, who said the story of Hagar and Sarah contains an allegory. Porphyry says Origen learned it from Greek writers. Porphyry also said Origen had been raised a pagan Greek and went over to the Christians; Eusebius calls this false, since his parents taught him Christ.
  • Whether Origen's name stood in the council's eleventh anathema from the first is a question raised by modern scholars, not by the old sources used here.
  • The quotations. Origen's Greek of On First Principles and of Book VI of his Commentary on John is not held, so their modern English follows the printed English translations. Porphyry's Greek is held only in a damaged scan, so the English printed with his words is McGiffert's.
