Dionysius of Tel Mahre

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Syriac Orthodox Christianity under Islam.

Also called Denys de Tell Mahrê, Dionysius Telmaharensis

Patriarch of the Syriac Orthodox church from 818 to 845, who pleaded his church's cause before the caliph al-Ma'mun and his emirs. He wrote a history of the church down to his own times, which later chroniclers kept in part.

About This Person

In Baghdad, in March 829, the caliph al-Ma'mun let the patriarch Dionysius of Tel Mahre come to him alone in his garden. Lazarus, a deposed bishop of Baghdad, cited the caliph's decree that any ten men of a faith could choose a leader. The Christians' fathers, Dionysius answered, had handed many cities to the Muslims on promises that their laws would stand. A law, he said, cannot be kept without a lawgiver, and the church's leadership was one of those laws. Al-Ma'mun ruled that the church could depose a sinner. It could not shut him out of church and prayer, for sinners above all must pray.

Bar Hebraeus tells this in his thirteenth-century Ecclesiastical Chronicle.

He led the Syriac Orthodox church as patriarch of Antioch from 818 until his death in 845. That church refused the Council of Chalcedon and lived under the caliphs. He was a monk of Kenneshre. In words Bar Hebraeus quotes, he tells how two monks seized him like a criminal. He pleaded, weeping, that he was unfit. The bishops made him deacon on a Friday, priest on Saturday, and patriarch on Sunday.

The emir Abdallah, son of Tahir, had gone down to Egypt, and his brother tore down new church buildings in Edessa. Dionysius sailed after the emir, and a storm drove him into Tanis. The emir scolded him for coming instead of writing, then wrote to his brother forbidding attacks on the churches. Dionysius won the people of Tanis a tax graded by wealth, in place of five dinars a head.

Later al-Ma'mun sent him, with Joseph, patriarch of Egypt, to talk rebel Christians of Lower Egypt into peace. The general Afshin refused terms, burned their villages, killed many, and shipped the rest away as captives. Dionysius told the caliph they were being crushed.

He wrote a history at the request of John, metropolitan of Dara. Michael the Syrian, a twelfth-century patriarch, keeps its preface. There Dionysius protests that John overlooked his old age and the contempt of waiting at princes' doors. He says Theophilus of Edessa, a Chalcedonian, dropped every story that named anyone of Dionysius's church.

The history closed, Bar Hebraeus says, with words to John about the crushing taxes on Christians. He could not sleep, and wept over his life as he watched the church's children suffer. Death was the one release left to him, he wrote, and he sought it as a good thing.

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Michael the Syrian copies from it. A marginal note in his chronicle says that a letter of Peter, patriarch of Antioch, is written out in full in Dionysius's book. Michael gives extracts of it.
  • Michael quotes Dionysius naming a source for one story: the histories of Daniel, son of Samuel, of the Tur Abdin. Daniel was his grandfather on his mother's side. Dionysius then gives his own view of the story.
  • Bar Hebraeus quotes his report on the Coptic church of Egypt. Dionysius found its people chaste, and they gave him every honor due to their own pope. He also found the study of Scripture neglected. No one reached the office of bishop, he wrote, without two or three hundred gold coins.
  • Bar Hebraeus also quotes his account of the pyramids. Dionysius judged them solid tombs of ancient kings, and not the granaries of Joseph that some had thought.
  • Bar Hebraeus says Dionysius ordained a hundred bishops. When Daniel of Tagrit died, Dionysius chose and ordained Thomas there as maphrian, the first bishop after the patriarch in all the East.
  • Bar Hebraeus's Nomocanon, his book of church law, cites Dionysius with Jacob of Edessa. It gives their rule on kinship through the sponsors at a wedding.

How they're remembered

  • Bar Hebraeus, closing his account of Dionysius's last words, calls him that blessed man.
  • A heading at the start of Michael the Syrian's chronicle names Dionysius of Tel Mahre among the writers it was compiled from. J.-B. Chabot, who translated Michael, holds that the heading is not Michael's own.

Open Questions

  • How we know him. The scene with al-Ma'mun is told by Bar Hebraeus, who was born in 1226 by his editors' account. He does not mark it as a quotation from Dionysius. He does quote Dionysius's own words on his election, on Egypt and in his last letter. Renaudot, in his history of the patriarchs of Alexandria, also has him come to Egypt and spend many days with the patriarch Jacob.
  • How much of his history survives. Michael keeps its preface and passages of it. J.-B. Chabot says that one passage in Michael's tenth book, taken from Dionysius, is the only chapter of Dionysius's chronicle whose text we have.
  • Which chronicle is his. Joseph Assemani found at Scete a shorter chronicle ending about 775 and took it for Dionysius's. Bar Hebraeus's editors follow him, and say it is addressed to George, a country bishop of Amida, and others, not to John of Dara. W. Wright, in his edition of Joshua the Stylite, also gives this chronicle to Dionysius. He says Dionysius took Joshua's short chronicle into his own larger work. In 1895 Chabot published part of it as Dionysius's. By 1899, in his Michael, he calls its author Pseudo-Denys. J. K. Fotheringham, in 1923, says it was once given to Dionysius in error.
  • Dates. The years 818 and 845 are the editors' reckoning of Bar Hebraeus's years of the Greeks. Bar Hebraeus sets the first voyage in the year 1136 of the Greeks, when the emir Abdallah, son of Tahir, went down to Egypt. The editors give that year as 823. For the second journey, with al-Ma'mun, he gives no year of its own, only the same year as the events before it.
  • The rebels of Lower Egypt. Bar Hebraeus calls them Byamites and has Afshin burn their villages. Eutychius of Alexandria has al-Mu'tasim lead the army, and says the rebels refused their tax. Neither he nor Makrizi, as Renaudot reports him, says Dionysius was there. Bar Hebraeus's editors give other names for the rebels from Renaudot and Le Quien.
  • His own side. In his preface Dionysius charges Theophilus of Edessa, a Chalcedonian, with leaving out every story about his church. He writes as a patriarch of the church that refused Chalcedon.
