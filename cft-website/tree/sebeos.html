About This Person

When the emperor Heraclius brought the Holy Cross back to Jerusalem, the crowd was so moved that no one could sing the hymns. An Armenian history tells it so. There was weeping and sighing, it says, and a great flame in every heart, from the king and his princes to the people of the city. Heraclius set the Cross back in its place.

The history is known as the History of Heraclius, by Bishop Sebeos. Frédéric Macler put it into French in 1904. He calls Sebeos the only Armenian writer of the seventh century who tells of the first Arab invasions of Armenia. Macler says he wrote in the third quarter of that century, and saw most of what he tells.

Stephen of Taron, an Armenian historian of the tenth century, lists the History of Heraclius as written by Bishop Sebeos. The printed editions place three books under his name. Macler says the first is plainly not his. The second copies Moses of Khoren and also Stephen, whose work, Macler notes, came three centuries after Sebeos. Macler translates only the third, which he takes to be the whole of Sebeos's history. It runs to 661, when the caliph Muawiya came to the throne.

After the Greeks crossed the Jordan to fight the Arabs, Sebeos writes, a terror sent by the Lord seized their army. Macler takes him to see the Arab invasion as God's punishment of the Greeks, for not keeping one faith with the Armenians. Sebeos never says so outright, Macler adds.

For the first Arab wars, Sebeos says who his witnesses were. We learned these things, he writes, from prisoners who came from Arabia and had seen them. Of the conquests he says he will tell the evils that came in our time. A burning, deadly wind blew on us, he writes, and burned the fine young trees of the gardens. And we had deserved it, for we sinned against the Lord.

Near the end, Sebeos says he speaks in vain. He sets out his words by the feeble thought of his mind, he writes, and not by the dignity of learning.

Key Works

  • History of Heraclius: the third book of the history printed under his name, running from the late fifth century to 661. It is held in Frédéric Macler's French of 1904, made from K. Patkanian's Armenian edition of 1879. The two printed editions rest on one manuscript at Etchmiadzin

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Frédéric Macler says Sebeos named no written sources and that his book is itself a source, from which later writers drew.
  • Stephen of Taron, in the tenth century, lists the History of Heraclius among the Armenian histories. Macler says Giragos of Gandzak and Tchamtchean, in the preface to his history, cite it too.
  • Georg Hoffmann and Oskar Braun, editing Syriac texts, cite Sebeos for the debate on the faith held at the court of the Persian king Khosrau Parvez after Jerusalem and Alexandria fell.

How they're remembered

  • Macler calls the work above all a priest's, in its faults as in its virtues.
  • Macler writes that Sebeos tells events haphazardly, as they come to his memory, without discussing them, in the manner of the Byzantine writers.
  • In 1917 Macler wrote that Sebeos has undeniable historical value, though he gives no dates.

What Is Uncertain

  • The history credited to him. Stephen of Taron, about three centuries later, lists it as written by Bishop Sebeos, and the printed editions carry that title. Frédéric Macler says the first of the three books printed under his name is plainly not his. The second copies Moses of Khoren and Stephen of Taron. In 1917 Macler listed another copy, manuscript 52 of the Mekhitarist library at Vienna, with extracts of Moses of Khoren and Stephen of Taron.
  • Macler takes the third book alone to be Sebeos's history, and translates only that. He does not argue the point, and no source used here argues against it.
  • His see, his family and his years. Macler names no see and gives no birth or death.
  • A bishop who refuses communion with the emperor Constans II at Dvin appears in the history unnamed. Macler does not say he is the author.
  • The chronology. Macler finds the dates of the Persian wars very obscure, and in 1917 he wrote that Sebeos gives no dates.
  • Macler's reading that Sebeos saw the Arab invasion as God's punishment of the Greeks is his own. He says Sebeos never states it outright.
