Avitus of Vienne

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Merovingian Gallic Christianity.

Known for
Bishop of Vienne under the Burgundian kings, who argued the Catholic faith against the Arian church of King Gundobad, wrote to Clovis of the Franks at his baptism, and signed first at the council of Epaone in 517.

About This Person

King Gundobad of the Burgundians came to believe that the Son and the Holy Spirit are equal to the Father. He asked the bishop of Vienne to anoint him in secret. Gregory of Tours, writing later in the same century, gives the bishop's answer. The king should not fear his people, he said, but lead them. "For thou art the head of the people; the people is not thy head." Gregory says the king never confessed it in public.

That bishop was Avitus of Vienne, in Gaul. An old Life of him says he followed his father Isicius as bishop. His editor, Rudolf Peiper, puts his birth about 460. Gundobad's church was Arian, and the Life says Avitus fought that teaching with great labor.

When Clovis, king of the Franks, was baptized, Avitus wrote to him in Latin. He had not been there in body, he said, but had shared the joy. In choosing for yourself, he told the king, you judge for everyone, and your faith is our victory. Peiper dates the letter to 496 or 497. The year and place of the baptism are disputed. O. M. Dalton, Gregory's English translator, follows those who hold Reims in 496, against Bruno Krusch's Tours in 507 or 508.

At Gundobad's request, Gregory says, Avitus wrote against the teaching of Eutyches. Agobard of Lyon, writing three centuries later, says he argued with the king in person and by letter. Once Gundobad was lost in his unbelief, Agobard says, Avitus turned his son and heir Sigismund to the Catholic faith. Peiper tentatively dates Avitus's letter to the pope about that conversion between 513 and 516, while Gundobad still lived. In 517 Avitus signed first at the council of Epaone.

His Life says he died under the emperor Anastasius, and gives no year. Peiper corrects the emperor to Justin.

Gregory wrote of his letters that "as they once quelled heresy, so now they edify the Church of God."

Key Works

  • Letters (Epistulae): letters to Clovis, Gundobad and Sigismund, to the popes of Rome and the bishop of Constantinople, and some written in Sigismund's name to the emperor; Gregory of Tours counts nine books of them
  • Against the heresy of Eutyches (Contra Eutychianam haeresim), in two books: written at King Gundobad's request, in Gregory of Tours's account, against Eutyches, who in O. M. Dalton's note recognized only the divine nature in Christ; Peiper dates them to 512 or 513
  • Poems, in six books: five books in heroic verse, from the origin of the world to the crossing of the Red Sea, and a sixth in praise of virginity for his sister Fuscina, as Isidore of Seville lists them

Legacy

What traces to them

  • He called the bishops of his province to the council of Epaone in 517, writing that the pope of Rome had sent him sharp letters because councils had been left unheld. He signed its canons first, and one of them refused to put the churches of heretics to holy use.
  • In 501, in Peiper's date, he wrote to the Roman senators Faustus and Symmachus about the wrong done to Pope Symmachus. If the pope of Rome is called in doubt, he warned, the whole order of bishops will seem to waver.
  • His letter to Clovis is one of the three early witnesses that O. M. Dalton weighs when he dates the Frankish king's baptism.

How they're remembered

  • Agobard of Lyon, writing in the ninth century, calls him Catholic in faith, most eloquent, and most learned in the letters of the world as well.
  • His epitaph, which Peiper prints, says no orator and no poet was his equal.
  • The martyrology of Ado of Vienne keeps his day on the Nones of February, and says his faith and teaching defended Gaul from the Arian heresy in King Gundobad's time.

What Is Uncertain

  • His years. No source used here gives the year he died. His Life says it was while Anastasius was still emperor; Rudolf Peiper corrects this to Justin and calls the Life's emperor an error. Peiper's index puts his death before 533, when Julian signs as bishop of Vienne at the council of Orléans. It also puts it after the end of 525 or the start of 526, when he preached at the dedication of a church at Geneva. That homily's date is Binding's, and Peiper's preface puts all the Geneva letters and homilies between 513 and 516. Peiper's birth year, about 460, is an estimate that follows Binding and Jahn. No source used here gives the year he became bishop; his Life puts it in the time of the emperor Zeno.
  • The scene with Gundobad. It is known from Gregory of Tours, a Catholic bishop writing later in the same century, who calls the king's refusal madness. Peiper prints Gregory's chapter first among the remains of Avitus's dialogues with the king.
  • Clovis's baptism. Peiper dates the letter to 496 or 497. O. M. Dalton holds with Kurth, d'Arbois de Jubainville and Levillain for Reims at Christmas 496; Bruno Krusch argued for the church of Saint Martin at Tours in 507 or 508. Dalton notes that none of the three early witnesses names the place. Avitus says only that the king was reborn on the day the world received its Lord.
  • Sigismund's conversion. Agobard of Lyon says Avitus turned Sigismund to the Catholic faith once Gundobad was lost in his unbelief, and the Life says he taught him after an exile. Peiper dates Avitus's letter to the pope of Rome about Sigismund's conversion, with a query, to between 513 and 516, while Gundobad was alive. The chronicle of Marius of Avenches, which Peiper cites, has Sigismund building the monastery of Agaune in 515 and Gundobad dying in 516.
  • The Life. Peiper says its second to fourth chapters are copied from the Life of his brother Apollinaris. Peiper thinks it older than Ado of Vienne and written in Gregory of Tours's time; Binding thought it worthless and copied from Ado. The Life says Avitus grieved over Sigismund's killing. Peiper gives that grief to a namesake, the abbot Avitus of Micy near Orléans, whom Gregory of Tours shows pleading with King Chlodomer for Sigismund's life.
  • Faustus. Ado of Vienne says Avitus wrote against Faustus, bishop of Riez. Louis Duchesne says Avitus took the Faustus he answered to be Faustus the Manichaean, and held the bishop of Riez in the highest regard.
  • A debate at Lyon. A Collation of the bishops before King Gundobad, with Avitus as the bishops' speaker against the Arians, is known only from the manuscript papers of Hieronymus Vignier, printed by d'Achery. Peiper says neither Maassen nor Thiel found a manuscript of it. Peiper prints it in his appendix and thinks it keeps some likeness of a meeting held in 499.
