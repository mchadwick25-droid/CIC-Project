The Roman Church in the Third Century

About This Movement

Natalius was a confessor, one who had stood trial for the faith and not given way. Yet, early in the 200s, he let a group teaching that Christ was only a man make him their paid bishop. A writer of his day says he ignored warning visions, so holy angels whipped him all night. He put on sackcloth and ashes and threw himself down before Zephyrinus, bishop of Rome, rolling at the feet of clergy and people. He was barely taken back into communion, the church's shared bread and cup.

In the first half of the 200s, the Christians of Rome were a large, mixed church. It held slaves and women of rank, and fed more than fifteen hundred widows and poor, under a bishop the people chose. They argued in public over how to speak of Father and Son, whether the church could take back grave sinners, and whom a woman of rank might marry, until a stricter party broke away in 251. Almost all that is told of its first decades comes from Hippolytus, a writer of that time. He withstood two of Rome's bishops, but the church sided with them.

Zephyrinus's right hand was Callistus, who had been a slave. When Zephyrinus died, Callistus became bishop and put Sabellius, who taught that Father and Son were one and the same, out of the church. Hippolytus says Callistus did it because he feared Hippolytus, and to clear himself of the charge that he shared Sabellius's view. In his words, as his enemy reports them: "I will not profess belief in two Gods, Father and Son, but in one." Hippolytus spoke of one God but two persons, and Callistus, he says, called that two Gods.

Hippolytus says Callistus let people off for sins of the body, saying he forgave them all. People troubled in conscience came to his church, along with people other groups had refused, and some Hippolytus's side had thrown out. Callistus pointed, his enemy says, to the weeds in Christ's parable, left to grow with the wheat.

He let a woman of rank, who would lose that rank by a lawful marriage, take a companion, slave or free, and count him her husband. Hippolytus charged that such women took drugs to avoid or end a pregnancy, not wanting a child by a slave, and he called the rule a teaching of adultery and murder.

Some years after Callistus, as a later historian tells it, all the brothers and sisters met to choose a bishop by vote, and set Fabian on the bishop's seat. In 250 the emperor Decius forced Christians to sacrifice to the gods. The same historian traces it to Decius's hatred of Philip, the emperor before him, who was reported to be a Christian. Fabian died a martyr, leaving Rome without a bishop. Some gave way from terror or fear of men, and became known as the fallen. Rome's clergy wrote of them to Carthage's clergy: "They are separated from us, but we have not abandoned them. We urged them, and urge them still, to repent, if by some means they can receive pardon from the One who is able to grant it. If we abandoned them, they would grow worse."

Cornelius, made bishop in 251, counted his church: forty-six elders, seven deacons, and more than a hundred lesser clergy. Novatian, an elder, held that the fallen had no more hope, and with a stricter party and confessors at his side he was made a second bishop, set up against the first. That year sixty bishops met at Rome, declared Novatian's party outsiders, and resolved to heal the fallen through repentance.

That year, the confessors who had sided with Novatian came back, saying they had been deceived. Cornelius wrote how the people met them: "A great crowd of the brothers and sisters came running together. All of them had one voice, giving thanks to God. They showed the joy of their hearts in tears. They embraced them as if they had been set free from prison that very day."

Connections

Descended from

  • continues Post-Apostolic Household-Church Christianity Widely Accepted Major — Irenaeus lists the bishops of Rome down to Eleutherius in the 180s. Hippolytus shows Zephyrinus at the head of the same church about a generation on. But Peter Lampe argues that Rome had no single bishop in charge until about 189 to 199.

Direct descendants

  • gave rise to Novatianism Documented Major — Eusebius of Caesarea says Novatus, a priest at Rome, led those who held that the lapsed had no hope of salvation. Editors take him to be Novatian. A synod at Rome around 251 declared him and his followers strangers to the church.

Influence, argument and contact

Argued Against

  • argued against Modalist Monarchianism Documented — Dionysius was bishop of Rome in the 260s. He wrote against the Sabellians. Athanasius of Alexandria later quoted him. Earlier, Hippolytus says, the bishop of Rome, Callistus, cut Sabellius off from the church. But Hippolytus also blamed Callistus for backing the same views. So he was a foe of the man he writes about.

Opposed By

  • argued against Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity Documented — In 255 Cyprian and a council of African bishops wrote to Stephen of Rome. Those coming over from heretics, they said, must be baptized. Stephen replied that nothing new should be done, only hands laid on in penance. Cyprian sent his reply to Pompey and called it Stephen's error.

In Contact With

  • in contact with The Church of Antioch in the Third Century Documented — A council of bishops at Antioch put Paul of Samosata out of office. They wrote of it to Dionysius, bishop of Rome, and to Maximus of Alexandria.
