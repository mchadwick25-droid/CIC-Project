The Imperial Church Era · c. 340-410 · Aquileia and Concordia, at the head of the Adriatic

Aquileia · Concordia, today in Italy

The Church of Aquileia

A commentary written to explain the local baptismal creed is the only reason anyone knows the Western churches did not all say the same words at the font.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:44

Read along

Aquileia stood at the head of the Adriatic, where the roads from Italy, the Danube and the Balkans met. In the fourth century it was among the largest cities of the empire, and its church was among the more consequential in the West. Its basilica floor mosaics from the 310s are still there. In the 370s a group of young ascetics gathered in the city, and Jerome later remembered them as a chorus of the blessed. He was one of them. So was Rufinus of Concordia. Thirty years later, the friendship between those two ended in the bitterest literary quarrel the Latin church produced. In 381 Ambrose came up from Milan to a council at Aquileia. The record names the city's own bishop, Valerian, first. Ambrose led the questioning. The council condemned two Homoian bishops of the Danube provinces, Palladius and Secundianus. Around 404 Rufinus, home in Italy after decades in Egypt and Palestine, sat down and wrote out the creed his own church used at baptism. He explained it line by line for people about to say it. That is why anyone knows two things about the Aquileian creed. It called the Father invisible and impassible, where Rome's did not. And it already had the clause about the descent into hell, which Rufinus points out, a little pointedly, that the Roman creed does not have. Rufinus's own words show that the Western churches did not all say the same words at the font.

Documented Stories

Rufinus Translates Origen While Reggio Burns Across the Strait

Rufinus had come home to Aquileia in 399 after nearly thirty years in Egypt and on the Mount of Olives, and spent a decade there translating Origen, Eusebius and the desert literature into Latin for a church that could no longer read Greek, and writing out his own church’s baptismal creed with a commentary. When Alaric’s Goths came down into Italy he fled south with the Roman refugees — Melania the Younger’s household among them — first to the monastery at Pinetum near Rome and then to Sicily. There, in the preface to his translation of Origen’s homilies on Numbers, he describes the circumstances: he was working ‘in sight of the burning of Rhegium’ across the strait, in a house crowded with fugitives, without proper books, at the urging of his patron Ursacius, who wanted the whole of Origen on the Pentateuch in Latin before either of them died. Jerome, in Bethlehem, learned of it while writing his commentary on Ezekiel, and put the news into the preface: ‘The scorpion lies buried between Enceladus and Porphyrion’ — the giants under Etna — and the many-headed hydra had at last stopped hissing. The tradition’s greatest translator died doing the work; its greatest scholar could not stop insulting him; and the Latin church has read Origen in Rufinus’ Latin ever since, including the books Jerome said should not be read at all.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition Widely Accepted — Rufinus put Origen into Latin. The editors of his works say he did much of this at Aquileia. In his preface to First Principles, written in 397, he says he is carrying on a task Jerome began. A preface of 410 recalls his promise to put Origen's work on the Law of Moses into Latin for his people.
  • influenced The Church of Roman Palestine before Constantine Documented — Chromatius, bishop of Aquileia, asked Rufinus to put the Church History of Eusebius of Caesarea into Latin. Rufinus did so around 401. He cut the tenth book down and added two books of his own.
  • influenced Cappadocian Christianity Documented — Gennadius of Marseilles wrote about Rufinus around 480. He calls him a priest of the church at Aquileia. Rufinus, he says, opened up much Greek writing to Latin readers. Among the works that Rufinus put into Latin, he lists books by Basil of Caesarea and by Gregory of Nazianzus as well.
  • influenced Apocryphal and Pseudepigraphal Literature in Christian Transmission Documented — Rufinus translated the Recognitions, a Greek work then read as by Clement of Rome, into Latin. In his preface he says the task had been laid on him to put Clement into 'our language'. Gennadius later lists it among Rufinus's translations.

Influenced

  • influenced Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity Widely Accepted — W. H. Fremantle wrote the introduction to Rufinus's works in English in 1892. He says Jerome and Rufinus were part of a group of young ascetics at Aquileia in the early 370s. Rufinus recalled being baptized in a monastery by Chromatius, Jovinus and Eusebius. In 374 Jerome wrote to the same three men from the desert.

Argued Against

  • argued against Homoian ('Arian') Christianity Documented Major — The Council of Aquileia met in 381. There the city's own bishop, Valerian, spoke out against Palladius, who was a Homoian bishop. He said that whoever defends Arius is an Arian. And whoever will not condemn the blasphemies of Arius, he added, is a blasphemer as well. The council condemned both Palladius and Secundianus.Lasting result: At Aquileia the bishops condemned Palladius. They found him unworthy to be a priest, and said a Catholic must be made bishop in his place.

In Contact With

  • in contact with Ambrosian Milan (standalone) Documented — Ambrose of Milan and Valerian of Aquileia both sat at the Council of Aquileia. It opened on 3 September 381. Ambrose says the bishops of the West met there on the emperor's order. The Acts show him as he put questions to Palladius.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Around 404, Rufinus of Concordia sat down in his home church at Aquileia and wrote out the creed used there at baptism. He explained it line by line for people about to say it. He also noted, a little pointedly, that this creed already had the clause about Christ's descent into hell, which Rome's did not. Rufinus's own words show that the Western churches did not all say the same words at the font. In the 370s, Rufinus had belonged to a circle of young ascetics at Aquileia that also included Jerome. Later their friendship broke apart in the bitterest literary quarrel the Latin church produced.

Legacy

The Apostles' Creed that the Latin West came to say is not just the Roman text. Every later version carries the descent into hell, and the first Western creed for baptism known to carry it is this one. Rufinus notes that the Roman creed lacks it. Still, a creed drawn up at Sirmium in 359 already had a form of it. The clause reached the accepted Western text from the creeds of the provinces, not from Rome. Rufinus' commentary is also one of the first Latin works to explain a creed in full. Other writers had explained it briefly before him, he says. A century later, Gennadius judged it so good that others counted for nothing beside it. Rufinus' translations also brought much of Greek Christianity to the Latin Middle Ages, often as the only text that survives. He translated Origen, Eusebius of Caesarea, the Clementine Recognitions, Basil of Caesarea, and the Sentences of Sextus.

How Sure the Record Is

One work, and an unusually valuable one. Rufinus' Commentary on the Apostles' Creed - about twenty thousand words - survives, and it expounds the baptismal creed of Aquileia clause by clause while noting where that creed differs from Rome's. Nothing else from the Aquileian church survives directly: Chromatius' sermons are lost, Fortunatianus' gospel commentary was lost until 2012, and the acts of the Council of Aquileia in 381 arrive through Ambrose's own record rather than this church's own.
