Benjamin I of Alexandria

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Coptic Christianity under Early Islam.

Patriarch of the Coptic church of Alexandria who hid in Upper Egypt from the Chalcedonian patriarch Cyrus. He came back after the Arab conquest. The History says he came under a safe-conduct from 'Amr and won back many who had left his church.

About This Person

'Amr, the Arab commander, had taken Alexandria. A Christian duke named Sanutius told him about the patriarch of the Copts, a fugitive from the emperor's men. 'Amr wrote to the provinces of Egypt that the patriarch could come out safely and govern his church. When Benjamin came before him, 'Amr told his friends he had never seen a man of God like this man.

So says the History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria, a later Arabic history of Benjamin's own church. B. Evetts, the History's translator, heads his life 622 to 661. Benjamin I of Alexandria led the Egyptian church that refused the council of Chalcedon. He came from a rich family at Barshut and became a monk under an old man named Theonas. The patriarch Andronicus made him a priest and named him his successor.

The emperor Heraclius sent Cyrus, bishop of Phasis, to Egypt as prefect and patriarch, to bring the Egyptians over to Chalcedon. The History says an angel told Benjamin to flee and to warn his bishops to hide. He walked by night to the desert monasteries of Wadi Habib, then hid in Upper Egypt. Heraclius had Benjamin's brother Mennas tortured and drowned for refusing to call Chalcedon good.

R. H. Charles thinks John, the Coptic bishop of Nikiu whom he translated, was born about the time of the conquest. John says Benjamin came back in the thirteenth year after his flight. People said, John writes, that the Muslims won because of Heraclius's persecution through Cyrus.

Michael the Syrian, a twelfth-century patriarch of the Syriac Orthodox church, tells it differently. He found in histories that Benjamin promised the Arabs Alexandria if they would drive out Cyrus and give him back the churches.

Back in his see, the History says, Benjamin won back by gentleness most of those Heraclius had led astray. His spiritual son Agathon tells how he consecrated a church of Saint Macarius in Wadi Habib. Some of Benjamin's rules for its sanctuary end in curses. "Think not, my brethren, that I have written these anathemas for this generation," he told those around him. Eusèbe Renaudot, who wrote a Latin history of these patriarchs in 1713, calls their line heretical. He calls the account a fable of later Copts.

Benjamin died after thirty-nine years as patriarch. Agathon, who succeeded him, remembered him as one "who understood the interpretation of the scriptures, and dwelt in the desert, and grasped many mysteries."

  • Rules for the sanctuary of the church of Saint Macarius: a short list of canons for the new church in Wadi Habib, kept in the account Agathon gives in the History of the Patriarchs. Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Agathon, his successor, served the hidden faithful in Alexandria for ten years disguised as a carpenter. When Benjamin returned, the History says, he took Agathon as his son in governing the church.
  • Agathon's own life in the History opens by saying that Benjamin, back from banishment, restored what Heraclius had overthrown.
  • By his prayers, the History says, the rebuilding of the monasteries of Wadi Habib and Al-Muna began. A note in B. T. A. Evetts's 1895 English Abu Salih says those monasteries were restored in Benjamin's time.
  • Michael the Syrian writes that from Benjamin's return to his own day, the Chalcedonians could not prosper in Alexandria and Egypt. The Orthodox, he says, held the churches and monasteries.
  • The History's life of Alexander II, patriarch from 705, tells how he went round Upper Egypt begging alms. People said they had not seen a patriarch there since the time of the Father Benjamin.

How they're remembered

  • The Arabic Synaxarium of the Coptic church, in René Basset's French, keeps his death on the eighth of Toubeh. It keeps the consecration of the sanctuary at the monastery of Macarius on the same day.
  • The Ethiopic Synaxarium, in E. A. Wallis Budge's English, keeps both on the eighth of Ter. It greets him as one "so learned in the Scriptures that he was made Archbishop of Alexandria."
  • Eusèbe Renaudot reports that the Coptic calendar joins his memory and the consecration of the church of Saint Macarius. He gives the day as 13 January.

Open Questions

  • How he came back. The History of the Patriarchs, a later history of his own church, and John of Nikiu have him return after the conquest. In the History he comes on 'Amr's safe-conduct. Michael the Syrian found in histories that Benjamin promised the Arabs Alexandria if they drove out Cyrus and gave him back the churches.
  • Whether the Copts helped the Arabs. Eusèbe Renaudot thinks they did, and reads the History's duke Sanutius as a go-between for the peace between 'Amr and Benjamin. Renaudot writes as an opponent, and calls the line of these patriarchs heretical.
  • The church of Saint Macarius. The account of its consecration, with Benjamin's vision and his rules, comes from Agathon's telling within the History. Renaudot calls it a fable made up by later Copts to give their new monastery standing. He finds nothing in it worth noting except the consecration and its rite.
  • How long he hid. The History has an angel foretell ten years of trouble. John of Nikiu says he returned in the thirteenth year after his flight. Basset's synaxarium counts thirteen years, ten under Heraclius and three under the Arabs. Budge's Ethiopic has him come out in the tenth year of Heraclius, with a query, and the third of the Muslims.
  • His years as patriarch. Evetts's heading gives 622 to 661. Basset's synaxarium has 630 to 648 in parentheses, though its text counts thirty-nine years. A note in B. T. A. Evetts's 1895 Abu Salih gives 622 to 660 with a question mark. Renaudot rejects the count of the Chronological Canon that Abulbircat gives, which would put the death in 648. That is a decade earlier than the historian Elmacinus and others give, Renaudot says.
  • Who ruled Egypt with Heraclius. The History names Cyrus, whom it calls the Colchian; Evetts's note makes him Cyrus, bishop of Phasis. Abu Salih's book names George, son of Mennas, as the Mukaukis.
  • His last illness. The History says he was sick in his feet for two years; the Ethiopic Synaxarium says twenty.
  • His birthplace. The History spells it Barshut; Basset's synaxarium, Farchout; the Ethiopic Synaxarium, Barset.
