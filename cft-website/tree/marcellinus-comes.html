Marcellinus Comes

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Byzantine Imperial Church (Justinianic).

Known for
A Latin chronicler at Constantinople whose chronicle of the Eastern empire, from 379 to 534, tells church riots and quarrels in the capital from the side he calls orthodox.
Also called
Marcellinus vir clarissimus comes, Marcellinus Illyricianus
Dates
His chronicle ran first to 518 and later to 534, in Theodor Mommsen's years for the consulships his preface names. Mommsen judges that he first issued it under the emperor Justin, before 527. A continuation to 548 is by another hand, in Mommsen's judgment
Place
Constantinople; by Cassiodorus's account, from Illyricum
Role
Chronicler; by Cassiodorus's report, a cancellarius, a court clerk, of Justinian before he became emperor

About This Person

On a Sunday in Constantinople, two men named Marinus and Plato stood in a church pulpit and changed the hymn of the Trinity. Marcellinus Comes says they did it at the order of the emperor Anastasius. Many of those he calls orthodox kept singing the old words and shouted the new singers down. They were struck down in the church, he writes, and taken to prison, where they died.

Marcellinus tells what came next in his Latin chronicle. More were killed the next day in the court of the church of Saint Theodore. Then crowds filled the forum of Constantine. They threw down the emperor's statues and called for a man named Areobindus to rule. On the third day Anastasius sent them home with what Marcellinus calls his usual false oaths. He promised them everything and did nothing. Theodor Mommsen, who edited the chronicle, puts this in 512.

Marcellinus wrote at Constantinople, but Latin was his own tongue, Mommsen says. Cassiodorus, a Latin writer of the same age, calls him Marcellinus of Illyricum. He says Marcellinus is reported to have served Justinian as a court clerk before Justinian became emperor.

In his preface Marcellinus calls himself a count and a man of high rank. He says he followed the Eastern empire only. His book carries on the chronicle of Eusebius of Caesarea and Jerome. Mommsen dates it from 379 to 518. Later Marcellinus added sixteen more years, up to 534. He calls it "meum rusticum opus", my rustic work.

Mommsen says hatred of heretics breathes from every page, above all against the followers of Nestorius. He also notes the writer's warm support for Justinian. One of the two families of manuscripts runs on to 548. Mommsen judges that part to be by another hand. Cassiodorus does not know it, and the preface does not mention it.

In his guide to Christian reading, Cassiodorus names Marcellinus right after Eusebius and Jerome among the writers of chronicles.

Key Works

  • Chronicle (Chronicon): a year-by-year Latin chronicle of the Eastern empire from 379 to 534, dated by indictions and consuls, carrying on the chronicle of Eusebius of Caesarea and Jerome

Legacy

What traces to them

  • All the manuscripts come down from two early copies of nearly the author's own time, Mommsen says. One ends at 534, where the preface says it ends; the other runs on to 548, where it breaks off.
  • Cassiodorus names it after the chronicles of Eusebius of Caesarea and Jerome in his guide to Christian reading, and also names four books by Marcellinus on Constantinople and Jerusalem.

How they're remembered

  • Mommsen: "orthodoxum odium quaevis pagina apud Marcellinum spirat", hatred of heretics breathes from every page, above all against the followers of Nestorius.
  • Mommsen notes the writer's zeal for Justinian, which Cassiodorus also reports.

What Is Uncertain

  • His life rests on Cassiodorus's short notice and the chronicle itself. Cassiodorus gives the court post as a report ("fertur"). The four books on Constantinople and Jerusalem that Cassiodorus names are not held.
  • The year 512 for the hymn riot, and every year of our reckoning, are Mommsen's. Marcellinus dates by indictions and consuls.
  • Mommsen reads Cassiodorus's words "meliore condicione devotus" to mean that Marcellinus left public life and became a cleric, before 527. This is Mommsen's reading.
  • The continuation from 534 to 548 is in one of the two families of manuscripts, and breaks off in 548. Mommsen gives it to another writer, since Cassiodorus does not know it and the preface does not mention it, and he thinks it was probably written in the East.
