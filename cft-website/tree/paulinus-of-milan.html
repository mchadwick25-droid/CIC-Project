Milan, today in Italy · Carthage, today in Tunisia

Paulinus of Milan

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Ambrosian Milan (standalone).

Known for
Secretary in the household of Ambrose of Milan, who wrote the Life of Ambrose at the request of Augustine of Hippo, and who is taken to be the deacon Paulinus who accused Caelestius at Carthage.
Also called
Paulinus, deacon of Milan
Dates
Flourished between about 375 and 422, by Sister Mary Simplicia Kaniecka's reckoning; wrote the Life in Africa between 412 and 422, by her dating; his petition to Zosimus of Rome is dated 8 November 417 by its editor Otto Günther
Place
Milan; later Africa, where he was at Carthage
Role
Secretary to Ambrose, deacon and biographer

About This Person

A few days before Ambrose of Milan took to his bed, he was dictating the forty-third Psalm. Paulinus of Milan was taking it down. Suddenly, Paulinus writes, "a fire in the shape of a small shield covered his head," and entered his mouth. The bishop's face became like snow. Paulinus froze and could not write until the vision had passed.

Paulinus tells this in his Life of Ambrose, written at the request of Augustine of Hippo. He had lived in Ambrose's household. Later he moved to Africa, "in which we now live and write." Sister Mary Simplicia Kaniecka, who translated the Life in 1928, says little else is known of him.

Paulinus drew on men who served Ambrose before him, and on Ambrose's sister Marcellina. He begged readers to trust him, "since indeed it is better to say nothing at all than to set forth something false." Kaniecka calls it a saint's life, written to build up the reader, and says it pays no heed to dates.

At Carthage in 412, by B. B. Warfield's date, a deacon named Paulinus brought a written charge against Caelestius, a follower of Pelagius. Marius Mercator, a Latin writer against the Pelagians, calls the accuser a deacon of Ambrose. Kaniecka takes him to be the author of the Life. Augustine quotes the council's record. Caelestius said a presbyter at Rome had denied that sin passes down from Adam, and others said the same. "Tell us their names," Paulinus pressed. "Is not one priest enough for you?" Caelestius answered. The council condemned him.

In 417 Zosimus, bishop of Rome, heard Caelestius again and called Paulinus to Rome. Paulinus sent a short petition instead, which survives in Latin. In it he thanks Zosimus for ordering Caelestius to condemn what Paulinus had charged. What he had always wanted for Caelestius, he writes, was his correction.

He closed the Life by asking Augustine to pray for "the most humble man and sinner Paulinus."

Key Works

  • Life of Ambrose (Vita Sancti Ambrosii): the bishop's life from birth to death and the wonders after it, written in Africa at Augustine's request from his own memory, Ambrose's sister Marcellina and men who served Ambrose
  • Petition against Caelestius, given to Zosimus of Rome: a short Latin letter explaining why he did not answer the summons to Rome, and thanking Zosimus for ordering Caelestius to condemn the charges; kept in the Collectio Avellana

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Sister Mary Simplicia Kaniecka says the Life "contains many interesting details of St. Ambrose's private life which have not come down to us in any other work."
  • Kaniecka says he took the Life of Martin by Sulpicius Severus as his chief model.
  • Augustine of Hippo says Zosimus required Caelestius to condemn "all the objectionable points which had been alleged against him by the deacon Paulinus."

How they're remembered

  • Kaniecka writes, "The integrity of the writer cannot be questioned."
  • She also says that, judged as biography, "the Vita is something of a disappointment," since it pays no heed to dates and touches Ambrose's public work only in passing.
  • Of the petition to Zosimus she says it "does not give evidence of any great theological depth or learning."

What Is Uncertain

  • Kaniecka calls the Life hagiography, written to edify. Its wonders, such as the fire on Ambrose's head as he dictated, are told as Paulinus saw them or heard them from others.
  • That the accuser of Caelestius is the author of the Life is an inference. Marius Mercator, a Latin writer against the Pelagians, calls the accuser a deacon of Ambrose of Milan. The Life puts its author in Ambrose's household and later in Africa. Kaniecka takes them to be one man. A note in the English edition of Augustine's On Original Sin says Mercator makes him the author of the Life, but the Latin of Mercator held here names him only as Ambrose's deacon.
  • His orders are unclear. Kaniecka says the Life shows him in minor orders under Ambrose, that Mercator and Augustine call him a deacon, and that Isidore of Seville makes him a priest.
  • Kaniecka dates the Life by John's term as praetorian prefect, 412 to 413 or 422, and settles on the years 412 to 422.
  • The Carthage hearing is dated 412 by B. B. Warfield and by the note in the English edition of Augustine. Kaniecka's own note gives 416 for the synod, though she calls Paulinus the accuser "of five years before" when Zosimus summoned him in 417.
  • Otto Günther dates the petition to 8 November 417. Kaniecka reports that some historians date it 8 November 416, and that Baronius, in his church annals, puts it a year later.
  • Kaniecka writes, "Of the life of Paulinus we know very little." The sources used here do not give his birth, his family or his death.
