The Enlightenment & Awakening Era · 1658-1806 · Jaffna, Puttalam, Kandy, Goa

Catholics of Dutch Ceylon

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

The Dutch took Ceylon's coast and banished the Catholic priests. They seized the churches and convents. At Jaffna the Dutch put several Catholics to death. The Dutch minister Baldeus says they had plotted against the government. He admits that one of them, the Jesuit Caldeiro, was not in the plot. Baldeus says he knew of it and did not report it.

A proclamation of 19 September 1658 forbade anyone to hide a Catholic priest, on pain of death. One of 11 January 1715 banned Catholic meetings. One of 8 August 1715 forbade baptism by Catholic priests. The 1848 Catholic sketch says the Dutch used force. It adds that the Protestant writers it cites deny this. Many Catholics took the new creed, partly out of fear and partly to win office. Others kept the old faith and met in their houses at night. Some fled to the inland kingdom of Kandy, whose king let them build churches. He later ordered the churches pulled down.

Late in the seventeenth century the priest Joseph Vaz of the Oratory of St Philip Neri at Goa came, disguised as a beggar. A Dutch chief persecuted him and his flock, and he had to leave the district. He went inland and spent nearly two years in prison at Kandy. Freed, he made Kandy his base. From there he visited Catholics in Dutch lands and gave them the sacraments in private, going from house to house. The bishop of Cochin named him vicar general. The Goa Oratory made him superior of the mission and sent more priests. He died at Kandy on 17 January 1711.

By 1717 the sketch counts about 70,000 converts since Vaz began. It counts over 15,000 communicants in the Jaffna district alone, and 15 large and over 400 small churches. Even then no priest dared to live in Dutch lands. They came by night in disguise. In 1743 a Kandyan king banned the faith and razed its churches. A Dutch governor, Baron Van Imhoff, wrote to his successor that the Romish priests did the Company much injury. They were, he wrote, warm in their opposition and closely united among themselves.

The Dutch gave up Ceylon to the British in 1796. Their laws stayed on the books until the regulation of 27 May 1806 took off the restraints.
