The 1904-07 Revival Family (Wales; Mukti India; Pyongyang Korea)

Wales, Mukti in India, and Pyongyang in Korea, 1904 to 1910 — three revivals that broke out independently of each other and of Azusa Street, recorded almost verbatim by the journalists and missionaries who were there.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:11

Read along

Three revivals broke out within a few years of each other in three parts of the world, and none of them descended from the others or from Azusa Street. In Wales the revival ran through 1904 and 1905 and was reported almost daily in the Western Mail by a correspondent writing as 'Awstin', with Evan Roberts as its public face; W. T. Stead published The Revival in the West in 1905. At Pandita Ramabai's Mukti mission in India it came in 1905, and Minnie Abrams, who worked there, described what happened in The Baptism of the Holy Ghost and Fire the following year. In Korea it began in January 1907, at a Bible conference at the Presbyterian seminary in Pyongyang, on ground that Korean colporteurs and foreign missionaries had been working since the 1880s. Three separate beginnings, recorded almost verbatim as they happened, which is why this map keeps them together rather than tracing them to a single source.

Documented Stories

The Elder Confesses at Pyongyang

The Presbyterian missionaries of Pyongyang had held a winter Bible class for the men of the province every January since the 1890s, and in January 1907, in the Central Church, some fifteen hundred came, in a country made bitter by the war of 1904–05 and the Japanese protectorate. On the night of 14 January, after the sermon, the missionary Graham Lee called for prayer and the whole assembly began to pray aloud at once; then men began to stand and confess sins publicly, weeping, and the confessions went on past midnight. The next evening Kil Sŏn-ju, an elder of the Central Church, the most respected Korean Christian in the city, rose and confessed that a friend who was dying had entrusted him with the settlement of his estate for the widow, and that he had kept a part of the money for himself; he named the sum and said he would repay it the next day, and did. After him the confessions could not be stopped: hatreds between Christians, adultery, theft, and, the missionaries recorded with discomfort, hatred of the missionaries themselves and of the Japanese. The revival spread through the schools and churches of the north that spring; the dawn prayer meeting and praying aloud together became the Korean church's marks from that winter. Kil was ordained that September as one of the first seven Korean Presbyterian ministers, and in 1919 signed the Declaration of Independence and went to prison for it.

Evan Roberts Falls Silent

Evan Roberts, a collier and ministerial candidate at Loughor, went home in October 1904, after months of prayer and visions, to speak to the young people of his chapel, and within weeks the meetings he led, with no sermon, only prayer, testimony and singing, had drawn the whole of industrial South Wales. The pits and the pubs emptied; the churches counted a hundred thousand converts by the spring of 1905. Roberts was twenty-six and the object of an attention no one could have borne. Through 1905 the strain showed: he refused to appear when he felt the Spirit did not lead, was attacked in the press as a hypnotist, and suffered periods of exhaustion. In February 1906, during a mission at Neath, he stopped speaking altogether for seven days, in obedience, he said, to a command, while the meetings went on around him. When he emerged he accepted the invitation of Jessie Penn-Lewis, an English Keswick writer who had befriended him, to rest at her home in Leicester, and he did not come back. He stayed in her household for years, contributed to War on the Saints (1913), which taught that much of what had passed for the Spirit in the revival had been demonic deception, and withdrew from Wales; he returned to Cardiff in the 1930s, lived quietly, and died in 1951. The converts had filled the chapels for a generation and some had emptied them again, and historians have never agreed what happened to Roberts or to it.

Connections

In Its Own Record

These revivals arose independently of the Azusa Street revival happening around the same time, rather than descending from it — revival broke out in more than one place at once. Korea's revival grew out of earlier missionary and indigenous groundwork: Horace Allen's arrival in 1884, Underwood and Appenzeller in 1885, the Nevius method adopted in 1890, and Sŏ Sang-yun's earlier work distributing scripture.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Revival broke out almost at once in three unconnected places -- Wales in 1904, the Mukti mission in India in 1905, and a Bible conference in Pyongyang in 1907 -- and none of the three descended from the others or from Azusa Street. Journalists and missionaries recorded each one nearly as it happened, from the Western Mail's daily reports on Wales to Minnie Abrams's account of Mukti, leaving three separate beginnings rather than one single source.

Legacy

The wave subsided around 1910; the churches did not. Korea's church in particular grew out of this decade and the decades just before it — Horace Allen's arrival in 1884, Underwood and Appenzeller in 1885, the Nevius method adopted in 1890 — and it went on growing long after the revival wave had passed. The three revivals together are the standing argument that revival is polygenetic: it broke out in unconnected places at nearly the same time, which is a harder and more honest fact than any single point of origin. The journalism, the mission presses and the Korean church's own histories they generated are the record.
