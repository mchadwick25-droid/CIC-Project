The Early Medieval Era · reached Chang-an 635 · Syria, Chang-an

Alopen

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Church of the East on the Silk Road.

Known for
The man from the far west who, by the Xi'an stele, brought the Church of the East's sacred books to the Tang capital, Chang-an, in 635. The books were translated in the imperial library, and the emperor ordered the religion spread. A decree of 638 ordered a monastery built in the capital for twenty-one priests.
Also called
Olopun (A. Wylie's English of the Xi'an stele), Olopan (Henry Yule), Olopen, Olopuen (Joseph Assemani), A-lo-pen (E. A. Wallis Budge)
Dates
Reached Chang-an in 635, by A. Wylie's English of the stele and by Henry Yule. Joseph Assemani gives 636. The stele names the decree of 638 and the honor he received under the next emperor, Kautsung. No source used here gives his birth or death
Place
Syria, as A. Wylie renders the stele's Ta-ts'in, which Henry Yule says Chinese writers used of the Roman Empire, here apparently Syria; Chang-an, the Tang capital
Role
Missionary of the Church of the East to China

About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In 1625, workmen digging outside Xi'an, in China, came upon a great stone with a cross carved at its top and strange writing beside the Chinese. It told of Christians who had taught in that city a thousand years before, with the favor of the Tang emperors.

The stone named the man who first brought the books of their church, the Church of the East. We know him as Alopen, and his is the earliest Christian mission to China that the record describes. In the year 635 he reached Chang-an, the Tang capital — today's Xi'an — coming from the far west. The emperor Taizong sent his chief minister to meet him outside the capital and bring him to the palace.

The books were translated in the imperial library, and the emperor studied the teaching and ordered it spread. In 638 his decree, quoted on the stone, said of this faith: "It helps every creature, and it does people good. Let it spread freely through the empire." Officials built a monastery in the capital for twenty-one priests, and a foreign faith now had the emperor's open license.

Under the next emperor, the stone says, its monasteries filled a hundred cities. In the years after, Buddhists spoke out against it and others mocked it, but its priests held it together. In 781 they set up the stone, about 146 years after he came, and told his story: his church looking back, in words that also praise the emperors. It pictures his journey: "Watching the blue clouds, he carried the true sacred books. Watching the way the winds blew, he faced hardships and dangers." That is the stone's way of saying the road was long and hard, and he kept the books with him.

After 781, Chinese history keeps only slight traces of the church. One is an edict of 845 that sent more than three thousand foreign teachers, Christians and others, back to ordinary life. The stone lay in the ground until 1625. Then a Chinese Christian official sent a copy to the Jesuit missionaries, and they circulated it through the empire as proof that Christianity had come to China early. So a church the world had forgotten was known again.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The stele says the next emperor, Kautsung, had churches built in every province, and that every city was full of churches.
  • The stele says the church kept its ground against the Buddhists in 699 and against slanders in 713, and that a later emperor restored its buildings.
  • S. Wells Williams reports that in 845 an edict of the emperor Wu-tsung sent the priests of the sect from Ta Tsin, no fewer than three thousand, back to private life; the edict as James Legge quotes it counts in that number the teachers of the Mu-hu-pi, another foreign religion.

How they're remembered

  • The stele's closing verse begins: "When the pure, bright Illustrious Religion / Was introduced to our Tang dynasty, / The Scriptures were translated, and churches built".
  • E. A. Wallis Budge's index calls him the introducer of Christianity into China.
  • T. J. Lamy, as J.-B. Chabot quotes him, calls him a Syrian priest who brought Nestorianism to China.

What Is Uncertain

  • Everything the sources used here say of him goes back to one witness, the Xi'an stele of 781, set up by his own church nearly a century and a half after he came. Its Chinese text names King-Tsing, a priest, as its author. The Tang court's own record of the 638 decree is not among the sources used here. No words of his own are held, and nothing of his death.
  • The year he came. A. Wylie's English of the stele, Henry Yule and Budge's appendix give 635, and T. J. Lamy, as J.-B. Chabot quotes him, puts the church and its twenty-one priests in 635. Joseph Assemani gives 636, and so do the notes of J. B. Abbeloos and T. J. Lamy to Bar Hebraeus, and Budge's own introduction. W. A. Wigram, in 1929, says about 630.
  • His name. Henry Yule thinks Olopan probably a Chinese form of Rabban, meaning monk. F. Hirth suggests Ruben. Assemani guesses the Syriac Jaballaha. No source used here gives his Syriac name from a Syriac text.
  • His title under Kautsung. Wylie renders it great conservator of doctrine for the preservation of the State. Athanasius Kircher's Latin, as Assemani gives it, has bishop of the great law that governs China. P. Y. Saeki, as Budge gives him, has Great Patron and Spiritual Lord of the Empire.
  • Whose portrait hung in the church. Wylie and Yule say the emperor's. Assemani's Latin summary can be read as Alopen's.
  • Who translated the books. The stele says only that they were translated in the imperial library. S. Wells Williams reads it as Alopen's work, in part or in whole.
  • Who sent him, and his rank. No source used here says. Monk is Budge's word, and Yule reads it in his name. Bishop is Assemani's word, and priest Lamy's.
  • The stone. Henry Yule says workmen found it by chance in 1625. S. Wells Williams says the Jesuits and their opponents argued warmly over whether it is genuine, and that the weight of evidence leaves no doubt. He quotes E. C. Bridgman: a hundred Chinese students would each read some parts differently.
