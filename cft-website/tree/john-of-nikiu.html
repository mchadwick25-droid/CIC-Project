The Early Medieval Era · 7th c.; bishop by 686 · Nikiu, Alexandria

Nikiu · Alexandria, today in Egypt

John of Nikiu

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Coptic Christianity under Early Islam.

Known for
Coptic bishop of Nikiu whose Chronicle, from the creation to the Arab conquest of Egypt, survives only in Ethiopic. It is the Coptic church's own account of the conquest. The History of the Patriarchs tells that he was given charge of the monasteries and then deposed.
Also called
John, bishop of Niciu (History of the Patriarchs), John the rector, bishop of Nikius (the Chronicle's closing words)
Dates
Seventh century. R. H. Charles thinks he was born about the time of the Arab conquest of Egypt. The History of the Patriarchs places him at the death of the patriarch John III and among the bishops who met to choose his successor. B. Evetts, its English translator, heads the life of the next patriarch, Isaac, 686 to 689. Charles gives 690 for that election and 696 for his charge of the monasteries. No source used here gives his death
Place
Nikiu, which the Chronicle calls Nakius and Absai. B. Evetts thinks the modern village of Ibshadi, east of the Rosetta branch of the Nile, is probably on its site. Alexandria, where the bishops met
Role
Bishop; overseer of the monasteries; recorder of the Arab conquest of Egypt

About This Person

When the Muslim army took the town of Nakius, in Egypt, it found no soldiers to stop it, a Coptic chronicle says. The army put to the sword all it found in the streets and in the churches, men, women and infants, "and they showed mercy to none." Soon after, the chronicler writes: "Let us now cease, for it is impossible to recount the iniquities".

The chronicle is by John of Nikiu, a later Coptic bishop of that same town. It survives only in an Ethiopic version made in 1602 from an Arabic one now lost. R. H. Charles, who put it into English in 1916, says it was first written in Greek. He thinks John was born about the time of the Arab conquest.

A John, bishop of Nikiu, appears in the History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria, a later Arabic history. R. H. Charles takes him for the chronicler. This John was among the bishops at the deathbed of the patriarch John III, and met with them to choose the next. The patriarch Simon later gave him charge of the monasteries, since he knew the monks' life and rules.

Then some monks carried a virgin off from her convent. John had the guilty monk beaten, and ten days later the monk died. The bishops deposed John, the History says, for going beyond what humanity required of him. He was silent while they did it. Then he cried out that the Lord would make them, in his words, "strangers to your sees".

Charles dates the election after John III to 690. B. Evetts, who put the History into English, heads the life of the patriarch then chosen, Isaac, 686 to 689.

In his Chronicle, John writes that people blamed the conquest on the emperor Heraclius, and on his persecution of the Orthodox through the patriarch Cyrus. Of the Arab general 'Amr he says both that he "had no mercy on the Egyptians" and that "he took none of the property of the Churches".

In his preface he calls himself "wanting in eloquence beyond all authors." He adds, "I have been honest".

Key Works

  • Chronicle: a history from the creation to the Arab conquest of Egypt, in one hundred and twenty-two chapters. R. H. Charles, following Hermann Zotenberg, says it was written in Greek, perhaps with some chapters in Coptic. It was put into Arabic, now lost, and from Arabic into Ethiopic in 1602. Held in Charles's English of 1916

Legacy

What traces to them

  • R. H. Charles quotes A. J. Butler: the finding of John's manuscript has made it possible "to write a history of the Arab conquest of Egypt".
  • Charles writes that his Chronicle, even in its broken state, is of immense value to historians of Egypt.
  • Its account of the conquest tells of Benjamin I of Alexandria's return to the city, in the thirteenth year after his flight. The History of the Patriarchs tells of his return too.
  • R. H. Charles reports from the manuscript that the Abyssinian general Athanasius and the wife of King Jacob had it put into Ethiopic in 1602.

How they're remembered

  • The History of the Patriarchs calls him "this holy bishop" and says his words against the bishops who deposed him came true within days.

What Is Uncertain

  • The Chronicle reaches us at many removes: from Greek, through a lost Arabic version, into the Ethiopic of 1602, then into Charles's English. Charles says the question of Greek or Coptic still needs to be threshed out.
  • Whether the deposed bishop wrote the Chronicle. The History of the Patriarchs does not say its John of Niciu wrote one. The Chronicle calls its author John, administrator and bishop of Nakius, and Charles takes them as one man.
  • His dates. Charles gives 690 for the election after John III and 696 for the charge of the monasteries. B. Evetts's English of the History heads Isaac's life 686 to 689, and Eusèbe Renaudot sets John III's death in 686. Charles's sources for 690 and 696 are not among those used here.
  • Why he was deposed. The History says he went beyond what humanity required of him when the monk he had beaten died. Charles says he abused his powers.
  • How he took it. The History says he was silent while they deposed him, then cursed them. Renaudot says he bore it impatiently.
  • Charles calls him rector of the bishops of Upper Egypt. At the election, the History names Gregory, bishop of Al-Kais, as presiding.
  • Thirty years are missing from the Chronicle, from 610 to 640, Charles says, so its account of the Persian wars is lost.
  • The words blaming Heraclius and Cyrus are what "every one said", in John's report. They are not given as his own judgment.
