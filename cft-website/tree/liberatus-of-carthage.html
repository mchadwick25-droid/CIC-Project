Liberatus of Carthage

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Latin African Christianity under Byzantium.

Known for
Deacon of Carthage under Justinian who wrote the Breviarium. It is a short history of the quarrels over Nestorius and Eutyches, down to Justinian's book against the Three Chapters. Bossuet names the Three Chapters as Theodore of Mopsuestia, Theodoret's writings against Cyril and the letter of Ibas of Edessa. Liberatus wrote from the side of the African church that defended them.
Also called
Liberatus Carthaginensis diaconus, Liberatus archidiaconus Ecclesiae Carthaginensis
Dates
No source used here gives the year of his birth or death. Otto Günther dates Pope Agapetus's letter that names him to 9 September 535. Theodor Mommsen dates the Breviarium between 555 and 567, since it tells of the death of Pope Vigilius. He adds that Liberatus wrote while Theodosius, once bishop of Alexandria, and Apollinaris of Alexandria were alive. Jean Garnier says he wrote under Justinian
Place
Carthage, in Roman Africa; Rome, where he says he was when Pope John wrote to the emperor; Euchaida, if Garnier is right that he shared his bishop's exile there
Role
Deacon of the church of Carthage, called archdeacon in the title of his book; envoy of Bishop Reparatus to Rome; writer of the Breviarium

About This Person

The emperor Justinian sent two bishops to Rome to consult Pope John, called Mercurius. They came to oppose Cyrus and Eulogius, envoys of the monks called the Acoemetae. These two denied that Mary is truly and properly the Mother of God. Liberatus of Carthage writes that he was in Rome when the pope upheld this teaching by letter.

Liberatus was a deacon of the church of Carthage under Justinian. Pope Agapetus, writing to Reparatus, bishop of Carthage, says two bishops and Liberatus the deacon came to speak for Reparatus. Otto Günther dates the letter to 535. His life is known almost only from this letter and his book.

His book is the Breviarium, a short history of the quarrels over Nestorius and Eutyches. He wrote it, he says, worn out by the hardships of a journey. He wrote to answer the followers of both heresies, who do not tell the truth about their own leaders.

Liberatus blames a later fight on Theodore, bishop of Caesarea in Cappadocia. Theodore was a fierce defender of Origen, he says. Grieved when Origen was condemned, he urged the emperor to condemn Theodore of Mopsuestia and a letter of Ibas of Edessa. These were two of the Three Chapters. So Justinian wrote a book against the Three Chapters, Liberatus says, for our sins. Jean Garnier, whose edition Migne reprints, counts him among the African defenders of the Three Chapters.

The African chronicler Victor of Tunnuna writes that Reparatus would not condemn the Three Chapters, and died in exile at Euchaida. Garnier takes the journey in Liberatus's preface to be this exile, which Liberatus shared until his bishop died.

At the close he writes that bishops who agreed to condemn them were rewarded with gifts, and those who refused were deposed and exiled. Some hid in flight and found a happy end in their hardship. All this is known to everyone, he says, so he thinks he should now be silent.

Key Works

  • Breviarium (Breviarium causae Nestorianorum et Eutychianorum): twenty-four short chapters. They begin with Paul of Samosata and Apollinaris, whom Liberatus names as the sources of the two heresies. They go on through Nestorius, the councils of Ephesus and Chalcedon and the bishops of Alexandria. They end with Theodore of Caesarea and Justinian's book against the Three Chapters

Legacy

What traces to them

  • A. C. Zenos, in the English Socrates, says the Breviarium certainly used the church history of Socrates of Constantinople. Chester Hartranft, in the same volume, says Liberatus used the Tripartite History. Garnier says the church history Liberatus names was the Latin one Epiphanius made at Cassiodorus's urging.
  • Theodor Mommsen notes that he took passages from Prosper's chronicle under the name of Lucentius. Otto Günther notes that Liberatus used a summary in his chapters 15, 16 and 18. Günther prints it in the second appendix to the Collectio Avellana.

How they're remembered

  • Garnier says some value the Breviarium highly for its brevity and the range of its matter. Others fault it for too little care with the documents, too much trust in men not wholly honest, and zeal for his own party. He was caught in schism, Garnier says, through his stubborn defense of the Three Chapters.
  • Garnier finds his style plain almost beyond belief. He says the range of his matter makes up for it, since the sources he drew on are now largely lost.
  • Theodor Mommsen says Liberatus and Victor of Tunnuna were of the same time, land and party. He calls them fierce defenders of the true faith.

What Is Uncertain

  • His life is known almost only from one letter of Pope Agapetus and his own book, the Breviarium. No source used here gives his birth, his death or his family. Victor of Tunnuna, who tells of Bishop Reparatus's exile, does not name him in the lines used here.
  • Deacon or archdeacon. Pope Agapetus calls him a deacon, and so does the heading of the Breviarium in Migne's volume. The title of the Breviarium itself calls him archdeacon of the church of Carthage, of its sixth district.
  • His time in Rome. He says he was in Rome when Pope John wrote to the emperor. Agapetus's letter names him with the bishops Gaius and Petrus. Garnier reasons that either Liberatus's words are false, which he can hardly believe, or he came to Rome twice. Garnier says the envoys named in Agapetus's letter left Africa only after Pope John had died. Garnier holds that he came first to tell John of Reparatus's ordination, then to greet Agapetus and carry the business of the African council.
  • The journey of his preface. Liberatus says only that he wrote worn out by the hardships of a journey. Garnier takes it to be Reparatus's exile at Euchaida and says Liberatus did not leave until his bishop had died. Garnier also notes that the wording of the sentence may be corrupt.
  • When he wrote. Mommsen dates the Breviarium between 555 and 567. Garnier dates it after the deaths of Vigilius and of Primosus, Reparatus's successor at Carthage. He dates it before the death of Theodosius of Alexandria, who Liberatus says lives to this day.
  • His fairness. Garnier finds him partial to Nestorius, or at least to Theodoret of Cyrus, and less fair to Cyril of Alexandria and the emperor Theodosius. He says the end of the book tries to lead the reader to see the fifth council as a political gathering. Its bishops, on this view, flattered the emperor. Garnier reports that Cardinal Baronius accused him of trusting writers who favored Nestorius. Liberatus reports that Theodore of Caesarea cried out in public that he and Pelagius deserved to be burned alive. Of this, Garnier says the credit rests with an author who writes in anger.
  • Liberatus and Victor of Tunnuna. Mommsen says both, embittered against Pope Vigilius by a hatred he calls just, give a letter of his that some think forged. He holds that Victor did not draw on the Breviarium, though the two often agree.
  • Who is meant. The last chapter names no African bishop. It speaks only of bishops rewarded, deposed, exiled or hiding. Garnier's note says Liberatus means his own Africans. Reparatus of Carthage was deposed and exiled to Euchaida. Primasius, primate of Byzacena, was sent back to his see with gifts after he condemned the Three Chapters. Verecundus and Victor of Tunnuna were moved from prison to prison and shut in monasteries. Victor of Tunnuna says that Firmus, primate of Numidia, was won over by the emperor's gifts.
  • His name. He is a different man from Liberatus the deacon who signs the Eleventh Council of Toledo for Sinduittus, bishop of Segovia.
