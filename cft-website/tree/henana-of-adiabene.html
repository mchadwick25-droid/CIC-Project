Henana of Adiabene

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Church of the East under Persia.

Known for
Master of the school of Nisibis, the school of the Church of the East in the Persian empire, in the late 500s. The teaching he was condemned for is known only through his opponents, who say he left the reading of Scripture taught by Theodore of Mopsuestia; about three hundred students walked out in protest against him.
Also called
Hnana, Hannana, Hananus Adiabenus

Place
Adiabene by origin; Nisibis
Role
Master of the school of Nisibis; interpreter of Scripture

About This Person

About three hundred students walked out of the school of Nisibis, the Chronicle of Seert says. They carried Gospels and crosses on black veils, with censers, and sang the hymns of the Rogations. They were protesting against their master, Henana of Adiabene.

Henana led the school of the Church of the East at Nisibis, in the Persian empire. Addai Scher, who edited the chronicle, reckons he became its master in 572. The teaching he was condemned for is known only through his opponents, though a pupil's praise of the man survives.

The chronicle, which writes against him, says he explained Scripture against Theodore of Mopsuestia, whom his church called the Interpreter. Abdisho of Nisibis, listing Henana's books, says many were rejected because of Theodore.

In the chronicle's telling, Gregory of Kashkar, metropolitan of Nisibis, condemned Henana's books. Sabrisho, head of the church, took Henana's side. Gregory shook the dust off his sandals at the gate and left. The students followed. But Timothy I, a later head of the church, says Sabrisho condemned Henana's writings.

The sharpest charges rest on Babai the Great alone, abbot on Mount Izla. In his Life of the martyr George of Izla, a monk says Henana taught that fate rules everything and denied the resurrection of the body. Babai calls him a Chaldean, which Oskar Braun reads as astrology, and a follower of Origen. Braun calls Babai passionate and speaks of his overstatements. J. Labourt calls Babai Henana's antagonist. He cannot say if the charge about fate is false, but finds it plausible.

Henana's pupil Barhadbeshabba wrote the Cause of the Foundation of the Schools while his master lived, Scher says. Scher thinks the pupil probably hid the truth to praise his master.

Barhadbeshabba calls him gentle, merciful and patient, a man who does not seek his own glory. He says his master worked day and night to read and explain the Scriptures. He prays that God will lengthen his days.

Key Works

  • Commentaries on Scripture: on Genesis, Job, the Psalms, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, the Song of Songs, the Twelve Prophets, Mark and the letters of Paul, with treatises on the faith, the sacraments and church feasts, as Abdisho of Nisibis lists them. Addai Scher says only his treatises on the Rogations and on Golden Friday survive; they are not among the sources used here

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Ignazio Guidi says new rules were added to the statutes of the school of Nisibis in 590, when Simeon was metropolitan of Nisibis and Henana was master of the school.
  • The Chronicle of Seert names Aha, Isaiah of Tahal and Meskena 'Arbaya as disciples of Henana who would not leave with Gregory's friends.

How they're remembered

  • His pupil Barhadbeshabba calls him a man adorned with humility and with every gift that interpreting Scripture needs.
  • Mari ibn Sulayman says he had eight hundred students.
  • Babai the Great, his opponent, calls him Henana the heretic, the Chaldean and the follower of Origen.

What Is Uncertain

  • The teaching he was condemned for is known only through his opponents. Two later witnesses agree that he left Theodore of Mopsuestia's reading of Scripture: the Chronicle of Seert, which writes against him, and Abdisho of Nisibis, who says many of his books were rejected because of Theodore. His pupil Barhadbeshabba says nothing of this; Addai Scher thinks the pupil probably hid the truth to praise his master.
  • The charges of fatalism and astrology, of following Origen, of denying the resurrection of the body, and of sharing with the Severians the teaching of one nature and one hypostasis in Christ rest on Babai the Great alone, in his Life of the martyr George of Izla. Oskar Braun calls Babai passionate and speaks of his exaggerations and contradictions. J. Labourt calls Babai Henana's antagonist; he cannot say whether the charge of fatalism is slander, though he finds it not implausible.
  • The synods. The synods that Chabot, their editor, dates to 585, 596 and 605 defend Theodore's writings but never name Henana, as Braun notes. Chabot and Braun read a canon of the synod of 585 as aimed at him, and Labourt reads canons of 585 and 596 the same way. Braun adds that the defense of Theodore is aimed above all at the Monophysite party.
  • Sabrisho's part. The Chronicle of Seert says the catholicos Sabrisho welcomed Henana's letter and would not confirm the Fathers' sentence against him. Timothy I, a later head of the church, as Abdisho of Nisibis quotes him, says Sabrisho condemned Henana's writings. Labourt reads Sabrisho's synod of 596 as against Henana's party, and J.-B. Chabot says Henana's works were condemned more than once by the patriarchs.
  • His years. Scher reckons he became master in 572; Chabot says about 570. Scher's notes put his death under the catholicos Sabrisho, who died in 604, and elsewhere say he was still alive in 610. Babai, as Chabot reads him, writes of him as already dead.
  • The walk-out. The Chronicle of Seert says the students left with Gregory of Kashkar, metropolitan of Nisibis, and against Henana; Scher says Barhadbeshabba was among them.
