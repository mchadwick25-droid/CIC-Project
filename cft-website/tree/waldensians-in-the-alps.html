The Waldensians in the Alps

The Cottian Alps and beyond, 1300 to 1517 — an outlawed church of scattered households, held together by preachers who walked to them.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:10

Read along

A century and more after they were condemned, the Waldensians had become something no medieval dissent was supposed to be. They were a lasting, organized church, kept out of sight. In public its members lived as ordinary Catholics of their parish. In private they were something else. They lived in the high valleys of the Cottian Alps, between Piedmont and Dauphiné. There were scattered groups too in Provence, in Calabria and Apulia, and in Germany and Bohemia. What held the church together were the barbes, the 'uncles'. These were preachers who went from place to place in pairs. They knew long parts of the Bible by heart in their own tongue. They carried small books copied by hand, and they made a round of the households. People knew they existed, and they were attacked again and again. In 1487 Innocent VIII called a crusade against them. The campaign that followed tore through valleys on both sides of the Alps. They survived it.

Documented Stories

The Cave of the Val Louise

Innocent VIII’s bull of April 1487 called the faithful to take up arms against the Waldensians of the Alpine valleys, granting the indulgences of a crusade and the goods of the heretics to those who took them. The archdeacon Alberto Cattaneo was made commissioner, and in the spring of 1488 an army raised in Dauphiné under the royal lieutenant Hugues de la Palud moved up the valleys on the French side while a Savoyard force attacked the Piedmontese valleys of Angrogna and Pragelato from the east. According to the testimony gathered twenty years later, when the survivors’ heirs sued for the restoration of their confiscated property and the king’s commissioners took evidence from the valley, the inhabitants of Val Louise took their families, their animals and their stores up to a large cave on the mountainside — the Balme Chapelue, ‘the bearded cave’, above the hamlet of Aiguefreide — where they had sheltered from raids before. La Palud’s men reached the ledge and piled brushwood at the entrance. The testimony says that those who came out were killed and those who stayed inside suffocated; later accounts give the dead as a few hundred, including women and children. The inquiry of 1501–1509, conducted by the king’s own court at Grenoble, found the whole crusade in Dauphiné to have been irregular and ordered the property restored to the survivors; Cattaneo was censured. The Waldensians of the Piedmontese valleys, who had fought, were still there to meet the Reformers in 1532.

In Its Own Record

These were scattered communities. The barbes, lay preachers who went on foot, linked them together. They lived through Pope Innocent VIII's call to crusade in 1487, and the Cattaneo crusade that came after it. This thread leads to the Synod of Chanforan in 1532. There the Waldensians formally joined the Reformation.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

A century after they were condemned, the Waldensians had become something no medieval dissent was supposed to be. They were a lasting, organized church, kept out of sight. In public its members lived as Catholics of their parish, and in private as something else. The barbes held it together. They were preachers who went from place to place in pairs. They knew long parts of the Bible by heart in their own tongue. They carried small books copied by hand on a round of scattered households across the Alps. They survived a crusade Pope Innocent VIII called against them in 1487. In 1532 they joined the Protestant Reformation outright.

Legacy

They are one of the very few medieval dissenting movements still here. In 1532 the Waldensians of the Alps met at Chanforan in the Angrogna valley and joined the Protestant Reformation. The church that came out of it still exists as the Waldensian Evangelical Church. It is based in the same valleys. The valleys keep the memory alive in place names, in caves and hidden meeting places, and in museums the church runs.
