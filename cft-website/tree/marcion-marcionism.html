Marcion / Marcionism

From Rome to Persia, churches followed the teacher Marcion of Pontus. They held that Jesus came from a good Father, above the God who made the world, and they read one Gospel, from Luke, with ten letters of Paul.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 4:50

Read along

In 250, in the stadium at Smyrna, a presbyter, or church elder, named Pionius stretched himself on a stake and let the soldier drive in the nails. He had refused to sacrifice to the gods, saying he feared the living God. Beside him they raised Metrodorus, a presbyter of the rival Marcionites. Both faced east, and the wood was heaped around them.

The Marcionites were the churches that followed Marcion after Rome's church cast him out, in the middle of the second century. For about three hundred years, from Rome to Persia and down to the villages of Syria, they gathered with their own presbyters, teachers and martyrs. Their Scripture was two books, a Gospel cut down from Luke's and ten letters of Paul. They held one belief above all: Jesus came from a good Father, above the God who made the world, and Jesus abolished that God's law and prophets, the Jewish Scriptures. Everything this story tells of them comes from their enemies. Tertullian, a writer at Carthage, in North Africa, says Marcion was cast out for his restless curiosity, which spread to others. Irenaeus, a bishop writing within a generation, says Marcion cut from both books every passage that called the maker of the world the Father of Jesus.

Late in the second century, a writer from the Roman province of Asia named Rhodon found Marcion's followers divided over how many first sources, or beginnings of all things, there are. Apelles, one of their teachers, held there was only one, one God, where Marcion held two. Rhodon, who argued with him, set down his words: "He said there was no need at all to examine a person's teaching, and that each should keep to what he believed. For those who trusted in the one who was crucified would be saved, he said, if only they were found doing good works."

About 207, Tertullian reported that their churches baptized only virgins, widows, the unmarried and the divorced, a rule he said condemned marriage. Their good God, he said, punished no one, and only cast the sinner away out of sight. An accused Christian was told to sprinkle incense from a box to the gods, and whoever did it denied Christ and went free. So he asked his Marcionite reader, who feared no punishment: "Why, in times of persecution, do you not save your life at once by denying, when the incense box is held out? 'Never,' you say, 'never!'"

In the persecution that began under the emperor Diocletian, Asclepius was burned at Caesarea in Palestine, the historian Eusebius's own city. He was said to be, Eusebius writes, a bishop of the Marcionites, eager for religion as he understood it, but without true knowledge.

When the persecutions ended, the emperor Constantine issued an edict, a law addressed by name to the Marcionites and other heretics, those who, their enemies said, chose error. Their teaching, it said, made the healthy soul sick. It forbade their meetings, even in private houses, and gave their houses of prayer to the catholic Church.

Yet their meetings went on. At Jerusalem in the fourth century, explaining the creed's "one Holy Catholic Church" to those about to be baptized, Cyril named the Marcionites first among the heretics whose meetings were also called churches. He told them to ask in an unfamiliar city for the Catholic Church, the church over all the world.

In the fifth century Theodoret was bishop of Cyrrhus, in northern Syria. There John Chrysostom had once charged an earlier bishop to drive Marcion's teaching out of the district, offering the aid of the imperial edicts. Theodoret worked to bring them to his own faith, that Christ was, as he wrote, not only good but just. He was stoned many times by the heretics he fought. After twenty-five years as bishop, he wrote: "I led eight villages of Marcion's followers, and the country around them, into the way of truth."

Documented Stories

The Marcionites Who Died Beside the Catholics

Almost everything about the Marcionite congregations comes from writers who wanted them gone. But Eusebius, working from earlier documents, twice notes something his sources could have left out. In the account of the martyrs of Smyrna he appends to Polycarp’s story, he records that Metrodorus, ‘who appears to have been a proselyte of the Marcionitic sect’, was burned alive in the same city with the presbyter Pionius. Eusebius sets this in Polycarp’s time; the Acts of Pionius date it under Decius and call Metrodorus a presbyter of the Marcionites. And describing the persecution under Valerian in Palestine, he names three men executed at Caesarea for provoking the judge by their confession, and adds: ‘They relate that besides these, in the same persecution and the same city, a certain woman endured a similar conflict. But it is reported that she belonged to the sect of Marcion.’ The anonymous writer against the Montanists whom Eusebius quotes elsewhere was aware that the Marcionites claimed ‘a multitude of martyrs for Christ’, and had to argue that this did not prove them right. What the notices show is a movement that lived the same life as the churches it had left — assemblies, presbyters, women who confessed before a governor — and paid the same price, while the two sides refused each other communion to the end. Marcion had taught that the God of the Old Testament was not the Father of Jesus; his followers died confessing Jesus all the same. The catholic historians recorded it in a subordinate clause, and it is to their credit that they recorded it at all.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Marcion was a wealthy shipowner from Sinope. He concluded that the strict God of the Hebrew Scriptures, who gave the law, could not be the same being as the merciful Father whom Jesus revealed. The Roman church expelled him and gave back his money. He then built a rival network of churches with its own edited Scripture: a version of Luke and ten letters of Paul, which he had stripped of what he judged to be Jewish corruption.

Legacy

Epiphanius still found Marcionite churches in Rome and Italy late in the fourth century, and they lingered considerably longer in Syria and the East, but nothing descends from them. What Marcion left behind is what he provoked. The confession of one God, maker of heaven and earth, whose Son truly took flesh, was hammered into its sharpest early form in argument against him.

Their baptism became a question for other churches. A letter Cyprian of Carthage answered held that those who came from Marcion did not need to be baptized, since they seemed already baptized in the name of Jesus Christ. Cyprian answered that Marcion did not hold the same Father, the Creator, and so his baptism could not give remission of sins.

Open Questions

  • The year of the break with Rome. No old source used here gives one. Tertullian says Marcion was expelled more than once, and that he went with the two hundred sesterces he had brought into the church.
  • Apelles. Rhodon, who spoke with him when Apelles was an old man, counts him among Marcion's divided followers. Tertullian says Apelles left Marcion's continence for a woman, withdrew to Alexandria, and came back no longer a Marcionite. Both tie him to a maiden, Philumene: Rhodon says she was possessed by a demon, and Tertullian calls her a prostitute. These are enemies' charges.
  • How many first sources. Rhodon says Apelles held one, Potitus and Basilicus two, as Marcion did, and Syneros three. Asked how he knew there was one, Apelles answered that the prophecies refuted themselves. Pressed, he swore that he did not know how there is one unbegotten God, but believed it, and Rhodon says he laughed at him.
  • Their rites. Tertullian says their God still used the Creator's water to wash his people, oil to anoint them, honey and milk to feed them as children, and bread to represent his own body. Epiphanius, writing late in the fourth century, says they held their rites with learners not yet baptized looking on, used water in them, fasted on the Sabbath so as not to keep the rest of the God of the Jews, and let women baptize. He adds that, as he had heard from many, they allowed up to three washings or more.
  • The marriage rule. Tertullian reads it as a condemnation of marriage. Clement of Alexandria, another opponent, says the Marcionites would not marry because they did not wish to fill the world the Creator made. Hippolytus says Marcion called marriage destruction, thinking to grieve the maker by abstaining.
  • Their prayer. Ephrem, at Edessa, reports their claim to pray more than Daniel, and to pray the whole day.
  • Martyrs. An anonymous writer against the Montanists, of the late second century, says the Marcionites claimed a "multitude of martyrs for Christ", and denies that this proves them right. Eusebius tells of a woman at Caesarea, under Valerian, who died after three men were given to wild beasts, and says it was reported that she belonged to the sect of Marcion.
  • Metrodorus. The Acts of Pionius, written by those who were present, call him a presbyter of the sect of the Marcionites, and set Pionius on the right and Metrodorus on the left, both facing east. They date the deaths to a day in March, under the governor of Asia, Julius Proculus Quintilianus. Eusebius says only that Metrodorus appears to have been a proselyte of the Marcionite sect, and sets the deaths in Polycarp's time.
  • Asclepius. Eusebius says he died on the same pyre as Peter, an ascetic also called Apselamus, from the village of Anea, whom the judge and those around him begged many times to spare himself.
  • After the edict. Eusebius says that some of those the heretics had deceived, frightened by the emperor's threats, "disguising their real sentiments, crept secretly into the Church."
  • A charge about the dead. John Chrysostom tells, expecting laughter, that when a learner died among them, a living man hidden under the dead man's couch answered for him that he wished to be baptized, and they baptized him in the dead man's place. It is an enemy's mockery.
  • Theodoret's counts. In one letter he says he led eight villages of Marcionists into the truth; in another, that he rescued more than a thousand souls from the plague of Marcion; in a third, that he converted more than ten thousand of them and brought them to baptism. He also says his district held eight hundred parish churches.
  • A teacher of the early third century. Hippolytus says that in his own day a follower of Marcion, Prepon, an Assyrian, set out his teaching in a work addressed to Bardesanes.

On the Nicene Creed

Marcion taught two distinct gods — the creator of the Hebrew Scriptures and a higher Father revealed by Jesus — and a Christ who only appeared to have a body. Both directly contradict the creed's confession of one God, maker of all things, and a Son truly made flesh.
