About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:22

Read along

In 1084 Bruno of Cologne, who had been a leading master of the schools at Reims, gave up his career, took six companions into a high, hard valley in the Alps above Grenoble, and built a life with almost nothing in it: each man in his own cell with a small garden, meeting the others only for the night office, part of the Mass, and a weekly walk. Guigo I, the fifth prior, wrote the customs down around 1127 — cell, silence, and a strict cap on how many could live in any one house — and Guigo II's Ladder of Monks, a short account of reading, meditating, praying and resting in God, became one of the most widely used descriptions of prayer in the Western church. Deliberately, the order produced almost no public figures. The one great exception was Hugh, prior of the English charterhouse at Witham and then bishop of Lincoln, whose chaplain wrote him up in unusual detail. The Grande Chartreuse is still a working monastery, and the order has kept substantially the same shape ever since. A traditional saying has it that the Carthusians were never reformed because they were never deformed.

Documented Stories

Hugh of Lincoln Faces the Mob at Stamford

In the spring of 1190, as men gathered for the Third Crusade, attacks on Jews broke out across England, culminating in the deaths of the whole Jewish community of York in the castle keep. Adam of Eynsham, Hugh’s chaplain and biographer, records what happened in the diocese of Lincoln. At Stamford and at Northampton, and at Lincoln itself, crowds assembled to do what York had done. Hugh went to them. Adam says he faced the armed mobs without an escort, ‘not fearing to expose himself to danger’, rebuked them, and by ‘the authority of his words’ dispersed them; the Jews of Lincoln, sheltered in the castle, survived. Adam also records the sequel that he thought most telling: when Hugh died in 1200, the Jews of Lincoln followed his funeral procession weeping, and called him a faithful servant of the great God. Hugh was a Carthusian — a monk of the strictest and most silent order in the church, brought from the Grande Chartreuse to be prior of Henry II’s foundation at Witham and then made a bishop against his will — and the Life shows the same man refusing to let a royal forester bully a tenant, excommunicating the king’s officials, and picking a bit of bone from the arm of Mary Magdalene’s relic at Fécamp with his teeth. His protection of the Jews was of a piece with the rest: a habit of standing between power and whoever it was crushing.

The Bishop Who Would Not Pay for the King’s War

In December 1197 Archbishop Hubert Walter, as justiciar, summoned the bishops and barons to Oxford and asked, in the king’s name, that each provide three hundred knights to serve for a year in Normandy against Philip of France. Adam of Eynsham was there and describes the scene. The bishops, one by one, consented. Hugh of Lincoln, asked in his turn, said that the church of Lincoln owed the king military service within England, and that he would not burden his church with a duty it had never borne, and that he had learned this obligation was not owed. There was silence; then Herbert of Salisbury, emboldened, said the same. The archbishop, ‘greatly disturbed’, dismissed the assembly and reported to the king, who ordered the two bishops’ property seized. Salisbury’s was. Lincoln’s officers, Adam says, refused to touch the property of a man they revered, and Hugh went to Normandy himself, found the king at Roche d’Andely hearing Mass, and — when Richard, furious, would not give him the kiss of peace — took the king’s cloak in his hand and shook him until he laughed and gave it. Historians have treated the refusal at Oxford as the earliest recorded instance in England of a subject declining a royal demand for aid on the ground of custom — a Carthusian monk who thought silence a virtue, saying no in a council of the realm, and the record of what he said kept by the man who stood behind him.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 1084 Bruno of Cologne left a career teaching at Reims to build a life of near-total silence in the Alps above Grenoble, each monk alone in his own cell with a small garden. Guigo II's short treatise, the Ladder of Monks, describes reading, meditating, praying and resting in God, and became one of the most widely used accounts of prayer in the Western church. The order deliberately produced almost no public figures, and has kept substantially the same shape for nine hundred years.

Legacy

The order is still living and still small, which is exactly what it intended. Its longest reach is Guigo II's Ladder of Monks. Its four steps of reading, meditating, praying and resting in God were put into English in the fourteenth century. The author of The Cloud of Unknowing seems to have known them. The pattern is still taught today, often by people who have never heard of the Carthusians. The charterhouse plan, a great cloister ringed by single cells and gardens, became a familiar building type across Europe. Several former charterhouses now serve as museums and galleries. The monks of the Grande Chartreuse still make a liqueur, which keeps the name in everyday use long after most people forgot what it belonged to.

How Sure the Record Is

Moderate: strong formation texts (Guigo I & II); deliberately few personalities - which IS the formation logic

  • Guigo II, The Ladder of Monks and Twelve Meditations (trans. Colledge & Walsh, 1978; Cistercian Studies 48) (primary) — The lectio divina ladder - the tradition's most influential formation text.
  • Guigo I, Meditations (trans. A. Gordon Mursell, Cistercian Studies 155, 1995) (primary) — Founder-generation interior voice: aphoristic self-scrutiny, no system.
  • Guigo I, Consuetudines Cartusiae (Customs, c. 1127) (primary) — The order's foundational legislation: cell, silence, and capped community size. Available only in Latin and French (Sources Chretiennes 313); no complete English translation exists.
  • Adam of Eynsham, Magna Vita Sancti Hugonis (Life of Hugh of Lincoln) (trans. Douie & Farmer, Oxford Medieval Texts, 2 vols) (primary) — Lived Carthusian texture through the order's one very public member, written by his own chaplain.
  • Gordon Mursell, The Theology of the Carthusian Life in the Writings of St. Bruno and Guigo I (Analecta Cartusiana 127, 1988) (secondary) — The thinness of English scholarship mirrors the order's deliberate silence.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 4, ch. 4 'Monastic and religious orders, c.1100-c.1350' (B. P. McGuire) (reference)
