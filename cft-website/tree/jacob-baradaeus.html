Tella · Constantinople, today in Turkey

Jacob Baradaeus

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Syriac Orthodox (West Syriac) Christianity.

Known for
The bishop of Edessa who, made bishop at the empress Theodora's order, traveled the eastern empire in secret ordaining bishops and clergy for the party that refused the council of Chalcedon, and after whom its people were called Jacobites.
Also called
James (in John of Ephesus), James Burd'ana, Jacques Baradée, Jacob Zanzalus (in later Greek and Latin writers)
Dates
Made bishop in the sixteenth year of Justinian, which E. W. Brooks gives as 542; died on the thirtieth of Tammuz, July, in the Seleucid year 889 by Michael the Syrian, which Brooks gives as 30 July 578
Place
Born at Tella, by John of Ephesus; a monk of Fsiltha; fifteen years in a cell at Constantinople; bishop for Edessa; died at the monastery of Cassianus on the border of Egypt
Role
Bishop of Edessa; ordaining bishop of the anti-Chalcedonian church

About This Person

Horsemen were hunting a bishop across the roads of the empire. Often they met him on the way, and did not know him. "Have you heard any secret news of that deceiver James anywhere there?" they asked. He named a place well behind him. "If you set out," he told them, "perhaps you will catch him." John of Ephesus, whom James made a bishop, tells the story.

Jacob Baradaeus, whom John calls James, was the bishop who built up the church that rejected the council of Chalcedon in Syria and far beyond. Born at Tella, John says, he trained in the monastery of Fsiltha. He went up to Constantinople, where the empress Theodora gave him a house. He lived alone in a cell for about fifteen years. He cut one thick, ugly cloak in two and made his clothing from the halves. From this, says E. W. Brooks, who edited John, came his nickname, Burd'ana, the man of the patchwork garment.

Bishops of his party had grown few. Harith bar Gabala, king of the Saracens, asked Theodora to have two or three made. In the sixteenth year of Justinian, John says, James was made bishop for Edessa, and Theodore for Hirtha of the Saracens.

Then James went on foot through Syria, Armenia, Cappadocia, Asia and the islands, as far as Alexandria. He would finish his work in a district in a night and a day, then move thirty or forty miles on. The emperor paid gold to anyone who promised to catch him. John counts twenty-seven bishops he ordained, and two patriarchs. If anyone says he ordained a hundred thousand clergy, John adds, "I do not think he will depart from the truth."

In old age he was drawn into a bitter quarrel over Paul of Antioch, a patriarch he had made. John calls him "a simple man", swayed by the violent men around him. Michael the Syrian, a twelfth-century patriarch of the same church, says those who stood with the old man were called Jacobites. Brooks thinks the name arose from that quarrel.

He died on the road to Alexandria, at the monastery of Cassianus on the border of Egypt, by John's first account. John also gives a report that their ship sank, and says, "which of these stories is true, God knows." Michael gives the day as the thirtieth of Tammuz, which is July, and Brooks the year as 578.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • John of Ephesus counts twenty-seven bishops and archbishops he ordained, besides two patriarchs, and many clergy, many of them in John's own presence. One of them was John himself, made bishop of Ephesus.
  • Michael the Syrian says those who stood with the old man in the quarrel over Paul were called Jacobites. Brooks thinks the name probably arose from that quarrel, set against the Paulites.

How they're remembered

  • John of Ephesus: if anyone says a hundred thousand were ordained by him, "I do not think he will depart from the truth."
  • Michael the Syrian: he ordained up to a hundred thousand bishops, priests or deacons, by what was found in his papers.

What Is Uncertain

  • Most of what is known comes from John of Ephesus, who wrote his Life in 566 by Brooks's year and was made a bishop by him. Brooks calls Jacob John's hero, and places John on the other side in the quarrel of 575. Michael the Syrian says John did not break with Jacob's party but rebuked both sides.
  • A longer Life of James, printed as John's, is later. Brooks, following Kleyn, holds it spurious; its colophon gives it to Theodosius of Fsiltha in the Seleucid year 1052, which Brooks gives as 741. Its parents and its eighty-seven bishops are not John's.
  • His birthplace. John says Tella. Michael the Syrian and Bar Hebraeus make him a priest of Fsiltha from the village of Gamoua on Mount Izla.
  • The years. John gives the sixteenth year of Justinian. Brooks makes it 542; Joseph Assemani, after the chronicle he calls Dionysius's, and Hefele make it 541. The sources give no year of birth. Elias of Nisibis puts his death ten years earlier than Michael and Bar Hebraeus.
  • How he died. John tells it two ways in his Church History. In one, four of the party died one after another at Cassianus within twelve days. In the other, the ship may have sunk, or they reached the monastery and four died there within three days. "Which of these stories is true, God knows," he says. He also rejects a rumor that Paul's men stoned him.
  • His nickname. Brooks explains Burd'ana as "the man of the patchwork garment", from his cloak. A later manuscript of the spurious Life compares the cloak to a pack-saddle.
  • The escape from his pursuers. Brooks notes that the same story is told of Simeon of Beth Arsham.
  • The name Jacobites. Michael the Syrian ties it to the quarrel over Paul, and Brooks thinks so too. The spurious Life gives the cry "We are of the faith of James". Bar Hebraeus says it came from the priests he multiplied.
