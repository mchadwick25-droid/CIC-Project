The Reformation Era · 1560-1610 · Lombardy, model for the Catholic world

Lombardy, today in Italy

Tridentine Parish Renewal - Borromean Milan

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

The Council of Trent decided what Catholic reform ought to mean; Milan under Archbishop Charles Borromeo showed what it looked like in a parish. From 1565 Borromeo set about redesigning a whole archdiocese around the question of what a parish priest was actually for — founding seminaries to train them, holding provincial councils and diocesan synods, walking his own visitations through remote alpine valleys, and issuing minutely detailed instructions on everything from the fabric of a church building to the enclosed confessional box, which his diocese did much to standardize. He also mobilized the laity, through Schools of Christian Doctrine in which ordinary men and women taught catechism to children and through confraternities that organized charity. When plague struck Milan in 1576 he stayed in the city, and his reputation as a pastor was made.

Influenced By

  • The Tridentine Church Documented Major — Charles Borromeo wrote that he called Milan's first provincial council because the Council of Trent had ordered it. The council met in 1565. Its decree on seminaries quotes Trent's order. Then it taxes church incomes across the province to pay for one.Lasting result: Milan's first provincial council did what Trent told it to do. It taxed church incomes in the province to found and pay for a seminary.
  • Ambrosian Milan (standalone) Inferential-Thin — Borromeo's biographer says Milan had always kept the Ambrosian rite. Borromeo revised its Missal and let no one depart from it, because it was so old. Augustine says Ambrose's church began to sing hymns 'after the manner of the Eastern Church.' How much of the later rite goes back to Ambrose is unclear.

Influenced

  • The Tridentine Church Widely Accepted — Francisco Peña wrote in 1610, from the hearings held to make Borromeo a saint. He says bishops in many lands followed his example and his councils' decrees. Many came from beyond the Alps to learn from him. His biographer says he hoped other bishops would copy his seminaries.

Sources

  • Acta Ecclesiae Mediolanensis (1582) - Borromeo's provincial councils, diocesan synods, and instructions (incl. Instructiones fabricae, 1577) (primary) — The model diocese's own legislation, later adopted across the Catholic world. Available only in Latin and Italian.
  • The Milan visitation records - Borromeo's and successors' parish visitations across the archdiocese (primary) — A Catholic counterpart to the Lutheran Saxon visitation records, showing ordinary parish life. Largely archival material in Italian.
  • Lay formation records: Schools of Christian Doctrine rules and catechist rosters (laymen and laywomen teaching), confraternity statutes and registers (primary) — Shows the renewal as ordinary people's weekly practice.
  • Giussano's Life of St Charles Borromeo (1610; Eng. trans. 1884) and the canonization-process testimony (1604-10) (primary) — Written for a hagiographic purpose; the canonization testimony is structured, guided evidence rather than a free-standing account.
  • Wietse de Boer, The Conquest of the Soul (Brill, 2001), with Headley & Tomaro (eds.), San Carlo Borromeo (1988) (secondary) — The current standard work on how Borromeo's discipline was actually lived.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 6, ch. 9 'Redefining Catholicism: Trent and beyond' (Bireley), with ch. 12 (Ditchfield) (reference)
