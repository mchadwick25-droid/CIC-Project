Tridentine Parish Renewal - Borromean Milan

Milan and Lombardy, 1560 to 1610 — an archbishop who took the Council of Trent's decrees and worked out, parish by parish, what they would actually change.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:05

Read along

The Council of Trent decided what Catholic reform ought to mean; Milan under Archbishop Charles Borromeo showed what it looked like in a parish. From 1565 Borromeo set about redesigning a whole archdiocese around the question of what a parish priest was actually for — founding seminaries to train them, holding provincial councils and diocesan synods, walking his own visitations through remote alpine valleys, and issuing minutely detailed instructions on everything from the fabric of a church building to the enclosed confessional box, which his diocese did much to standardize. He also mobilized the laity, through Schools of Christian Doctrine in which ordinary men and women taught catechism to children and through confraternities that organized charity. When plague struck Milan in 1576 he stayed in the city, and his reputation as a pastor was made.

Documented Stories

Borromeo Walks Barefoot Through the Plague

The plague entered Milan in August 1576, and by October the Spanish governor had withdrawn to Vigevano and most of the nobility to their estates. Charles Borromeo stayed. He turned the archbishop’s palace into a supply depot and sold or pawned its furnishings, the tapestries and his own bed, to buy food; he organized the lazaretto outside the Porta Orientale, where the sick were sent, with priests and Capuchins to serve it, and visited it himself; he had altars set up at street corners so that the quarantined could hear Mass from their windows; he arranged for the confessors to hear confessions through the windows of infected houses; and he had the clergy sign a promise to stay, which most did — his household priests who died are named. In October he led three penitential processions through the city, barefoot, with a rope round his neck as a sign of guilt, carrying the relic of the Holy Nail from the cathedral. The city council, which had quarreled with him for a decade over jurisdiction, asked him afterwards to remain as its protector. Some seventeen thousand Milanese died, of about a hundred and twenty thousand; the plague was afterwards called the plague of San Carlo. His canonization in 1610 rested largely on this year, and the model of the Tridentine bishop — resident, visiting, in the streets — was built on it.

The Humiliati Try to Shoot the Archbishop at Prayer

The Humiliati had begun in the twelfth century as penitent wool-workers and by the sixteenth had dwindled to some hundred and seventy members in richly endowed houses across Lombardy, their provosts living on the revenues. Pius V made Borromeo their protector in 1567; he imposed enclosure, common life and the surrender of private income, and deposed provosts who refused. Four of them met at Brera in 1569 and, according to the trial records, raised money among the houses to have him killed. They found a member of the order, a priest named Girolamo Donato, called Farina, who agreed for forty gold scudi. On the evening of 26 October, as Borromeo knelt at vespers in the chapel of his palace, singing with his household the antiphon ‘Let not your heart be troubled’, Farina fired an arquebus at his back from a few paces. The ball struck his rochet and fell to the floor, leaving a bruise; whether the charge was weak or the weapon loaded with shot is disputed, and the tradition called it a miracle. Borromeo forbade the household to pursue the assassin and finished the office. Farina was caught in Piedmont; he and the three surviving provosts were tried by a papal commission and hanged in 1570, the fourth having died in prison. Pius V suppressed the Humiliati entirely in February 1571 and gave their houses to other orders. An order founded on humility ended by hiring a murder, and the reformer whose zeal provoked it inherited its property.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Archbishop Charles Borromeo took the Council of Trent's decrees and worked out what they meant for one ordinary parish: founding seminaries, walking visitations through remote alpine valleys, and issuing instructions detailed enough to help standardize the enclosed confessional box still found in Catholic churches. When plague struck Milan in 1576, he stayed in the city rather than flee it.

Legacy

Borromeo's Milan became a model. His book of rules for Milan, the Acta Ecclesiae Mediolanensis, first came out in 1582. It gathered the decrees of his councils, and bishops in many lands used it as a working handbook. The parish it describes lasted into the twentieth century. It had a trained priest who lived among his people, regular lessons in the faith, the confessional and the bishop's visit. Catholics still call on Charles Borromeo as patron of bishops and of men training to be priests. His diocese still keeps the Ambrosian rite, which is far older than he is.

How Sure the Record Is

Rich institutional + visitation ordinary-texture

  • Acta Ecclesiae Mediolanensis (1582) - Borromeo's provincial councils, diocesan synods, and instructions (incl. Instructiones fabricae, 1577) (primary) — The model diocese's own legislation, later adopted across the Catholic world. Available only in Latin and Italian.
  • The Milan visitation records - Borromeo's and successors' parish visitations across the archdiocese (primary) — A Catholic counterpart to the Lutheran Saxon visitation records, showing ordinary parish life. Largely archival material in Italian.
  • Lay formation records: Schools of Christian Doctrine rules and catechist rosters (laymen and laywomen teaching), confraternity statutes and registers (primary) — Shows the renewal as ordinary people's weekly practice.
  • Giussano's Life of St Charles Borromeo (1610; Eng. trans. 1884) and the canonization-process testimony (1604-10) (primary) — Written for a hagiographic purpose; the canonization testimony is structured, guided evidence rather than a free-standing account.
  • Wietse de Boer, The Conquest of the Soul (Brill, 2001), with Headley & Tomaro (eds.), San Carlo Borromeo (1988) (secondary) — The current standard work on how Borromeo's discipline was actually lived.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 6, ch. 9 'Redefining Catholicism: Trent and beyond' (Bireley), with ch. 12 (Ditchfield) (reference)
