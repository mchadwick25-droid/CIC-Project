The Imperial Church Era · c. 325-450 · Jerusalem

Jerusalem Liturgical-Pilgrimage Christianity

About This Movement

Constantine's builders uncovered what they took to be the tomb of Christ, and in the 330s they raised a great church complex over it. From then on Jerusalem was something no other city was. Here the events of the gospel could be marked on the day and at the spot they were believed to have happened. Its clergy built a liturgy out of exactly that. Palm Sunday became an actual procession down the Mount of Olives with branches in hand. Good Friday brought the veneration of the Cross, and Easter an all-night vigil at the tomb. The crowds who came to see it wrote home about what they saw. One of those visitors was a woman from the Latin west named Egeria. In the early 380s she kept a detailed travel diary of exactly what was done and when. The manuscript disappeared for centuries. It was found again at Arezzo, in Italy, in 1883, and it remains the single most valuable eyewitness account of early Christian worship anywhere. What makes this community hard to pin down is the very thing that made it distinctive. It was always part settled and part passing through: resident clergy and monks, local Christians, and a constant flow of pilgrims from every corner of the Christian world.

In Its Own Record

Continues

  • The Church of Roman Palestine before Constantine Inferential-Thin Major — In the library at Aelia, as Jerusalem was then called, Eusebius saw letters that Bishop Alexander had gathered. He names the next bishops of the city down to Hermon. He says their chair was kept there until his own time. Later, Constantine wrote to Macarius, its bishop, about building the Church of the Savior. So the see of Jerusalem carried on. But the earlier church was the church of the whole province, not of Jerusalem alone. To call the later pilgrim church the same community is only an inference.

Influenced

  • Georgian Christianity Inferential-Thin — A lectionary sets out the Bible readings for the year. One kept in Georgian manuscripts follows the worship of Jerusalem from the fifth to the eighth century. Michel Tarchnischvili edited it in 1959–60. This rests on that one edition.

In Contact With

  • Imperial and Juridical Christianity Documented — Eusebius copies a letter from Constantine to Macarius, bishop of Jerusalem. It is about building a church at the site of Christ's tomb. The emperor writes that he has no greater care than to adorn that spot with a splendid building.
  • Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity Documented — Jerome tells how Paula, his patron, worshipped at the tomb of the Resurrection in Jerusalem. Yet he told a friend that heaven was as easy to reach from Britain as from Jerusalem.
  • Early Palestinian Ascetic Monasticism Documented — Jerome says Hilarion, the first monk of Palestine, went to Jerusalem for just one day, so as not to neglect the holy places. In the 380s the pilgrim Egeria watched monks and virgins come to the tomb church before dawn. They came for the daily round of prayer.
