The Ruthenian Greek Catholic Church — Polotsk to Galicia

Belarus, Ukraine and Austrian Galicia, 1815 to 1906 — an Eastern church in communion with Rome dissolved by decree in one empire and turned into a nation's backbone in the other.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:12

Read along

Millions of Belarusians and Ukrainians worshipped in the Byzantine rite while in communion with Rome. The arrangement dated from a union of 1596. In the Russian Empire it was ended by decree. At the Synod of Polotsk in February 1839, bishops led by Joseph Semashko declared their church reunited with Russian Orthodoxy. Roughly a million and a half people were moved with a stroke of the pen. The remaining eparchy, Chełm, followed in 1875, and in some villages soldiers broke the resistance by force. What survived did so under Austrian rule in Galicia. There the Greek Catholic Church became more than a church. It was the backbone of Ukrainian national life. Its priests were the teachers, editors and co-op organizers of a people who had almost no other leaders. At the turn of the century Andrey Sheptytsky took the see of Lviv as its metropolitan. His long tenure would carry the church through all that the next fifty years brought.

Documented Stories

Pratulin, 24 January 1874

After the Synod of Polotsk of 1839, the eparchy of Chelm in Russian Poland was the last Uniate diocese in the empire, some 250,000 people, most of them Ukrainian-speaking peasants who had kept the union for nearly three centuries. In the early 1870s the government, having brought in a compliant bishop, ordered the removal of the Latin practices the parishes had absorbed, as a prelude to reunion with Orthodoxy, and where parishes resisted, sent troops. In January 1874 the parish of Pratulin, a village on the Bug, learned that its priest had been removed and an Orthodox one appointed. On 24 January the villagers, men, women and children, gathered in the churchyard around their church and would not give up the keys; soldiers came and ordered them to disperse. The accounts, from the survivors and the officer's own report, describe the people kneeling, singing hymns, and saying that they would die rather than surrender the church; the soldiers were ordered to fire and did, and then to charge. Thirteen men were killed, among them Wincenty Lewoniuk, aged twenty-five, and some thirty wounded; the survivors were flogged, fined or exiled. In 1875 the eparchy was declared reunited with Orthodoxy by petition, and the Uniates who refused lived for thirty years as an underground church, baptizing and marrying in secret, until the toleration edict of 1905, when some 200,000 declared themselves Roman Catholic, the only legal alternative. The thirteen of Pratulin were beatified in 1996.

The Almanac the Church Banned

Markiyan Shashkevych, Ivan Vahylevych and Yakiv Holovatsky were students at the Greek Catholic seminary in Lviv in the 1830s, in a church whose clergy were the only educated class the Ukrainian peasants of Galicia had, and whose written language no peasant could read. The three, who called themselves the Ruthenian Triad, had begun collecting the folk songs of the villages and writing poems in the spoken language, and in 1837 they had an almanac printed at Buda, where the censorship was lighter: Rusalka Dnistrovaia, the Nymph of the Dniester, a hundred and thirty pages of songs and verse in the vernacular. The censor in Lviv, a Greek Catholic priest, banned it in Galicia: the language was vulgar, the orthography a novelty, and the whole an incitement to the peasantry. Eight hundred of the thousand copies were impounded and kept in the police stores for eleven years. The metropolitan's consistory reprimanded the three; Shashkevych was ordained in 1838 and sent to poor mountain parishes, where he went on writing, and died of consumption in 1843 at thirty-one. Vahylevych left the church; Holovatsky became a professor. The impounded copies were released in 1848, when the revolution briefly freed the press, and the almanac became the founding book of modern Ukrainian literature in Galicia. The church that had banned it produced, within two generations, the priests who edited the newspapers, ran the reading rooms and led the national movement, and by the century's end it claimed Shashkevych as its own.

Connections

In Its Own Record

The Synod of Polotsk in 1839 dissolved the union with Rome across the Russian Empire, transferring roughly a million and a half people to Russian Orthodoxy; the Chełm eparchy followed in 1875. The church survived only under Austrian rule in Galicia, where it became the institutional engine of Ukrainian national life, especially under Metropolitan Andrey Sheptytsky from 1900. It stood in natural tension with the Russian Orthodox Church, which absorbed the dissolved dioceses.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

At the Synod of Polotsk in 1839, bishops declared their church reunited with Russian Orthodoxy and roughly a million and a half Eastern-rite Catholics were transferred by decree; a further eparchy followed in 1875, some of it by force. Under Austrian rule in Galicia the church survived instead, its priests doubling as the schoolteachers, editors and cooperative organizers of a Ukrainian people who had almost no other leadership of their own.

Legacy

The Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church is the largest Eastern Catholic church in the world today. All it survived later rested on the base it built in Galicia in this century: Soviet liquidation in 1946, four decades underground, and its return to the open in 1989. In the lands where the union was ended by decree, it never came back. The church's habit of carrying a national culture as well as a liturgy took shape here. It is still one of the most distinctive things about it.
