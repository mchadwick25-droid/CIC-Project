The Early Medieval Era · c. 884-1045 · Armenia (Ani, Vaspurakan)

Bagratid Armenian Christianity

Armenia, c. 884 to 1045 — a restored kingdom that raised the cathedral of Ani, and produced in one monk's book of laments the prayer book Armenians kept by the bed for the next thousand years.

Armenian Christianity before the Bagratids→Bagratid Armenian Christianity→Armenian Christianity in Cilicia

About This Movement

After two centuries under Arab governors, an Armenian kingdom was restored under the Bagratid dynasty in the 880s. With it came a burst of church building and writing. The catholicos, the head of the Armenian church, moved his seat to the royal capital of Ani. Its cathedral, finished around 1001 by the architect Trdat, still stands roofless on the plain. Trdat may also have been called to Constantinople to rebuild the dome of Hagia Sophia after an earthquake brought it down. The period's most honored voice, though, was not an architect but a monk. Gregory of Narek wrote a book of ninety-five prayers, called the Narek after his monastery. Malachia Ormanian was once Armenian patriarch of Constantinople. In a book put into English in 1912, he wrote that it was used for private prayer. It was, he said, 'regarded as a potent talisman against all kinds of dangers.' The kingdom ended in 1045, when Byzantium annexed Ani. The Seljuk Turks took the city in 1064.

Documented Stories

The King Who Would Not Eat Without the Poor

Stepanos of Taron, called Asoghik, wrote his Universal History at the end of the tenth century, within living memory of Ashot III, and his account of the king is one of the few in Armenian historiography that dwells on private conduct rather than war. Ashot, he says, was called ‘the Merciful’ — Oghormats — and earned the name. He built hospices and hospitals for the sick, endowed monasteries, and paid the expenses of the poor from his own treasury; and he made it his rule never to eat unless the destitute ate with him. Asoghik describes the royal table at Ani with the blind, the lame and the leprous seated at it, the king serving them with his own hands before he touched his food. Matthew of Edessa, a century later, repeats the tradition and adds that Ashot fed the poor from the same dishes he used himself. The same king fortified Ani with the double walls that still stand, moved the catholicos there, and negotiated with the Byzantine emperor John Tzimiskes when a Greek army came through Armenia in 974 — Ashot met him with eighty thousand men and the two parted as allies. His son Gagik built the cathedral. The tradition of the merciful king is hagiographic in tone and cannot be checked, but it was the tradition an Armenian church under Muslim overlords and Byzantine pressure chose to remember about the founder of its capital: not the walls, but who sat at the table inside them.

The Book Called Narek

Gregory was raised from infancy in the convent of Narek, in the land of the Rushtunians, and in time became its abbot. So writes Michael Chamich, a monk of the Armenian order at Venice, in his History of Armenia. Chamich tells how the book came to be. Gregory long prayed to see, with his own eyes, the Virgin holding the Son of God, and at last was granted the sight. Then, ‘Filled with the Holy Ghost, he composed that wonderful book of prayers called Narek, the like of which has never till this time appeared in the world.’ Giovanni de Serpos, writing at Venice in 1786, counts ninety-five prayers in it and calls them a masterpiece in every line. In his day, he says, the book was still used in the church’s services. Two of Gregory’s prayers to the Holy Spirit stand in the Armenian Liturgy. In the first, the people assembled before God pray, ‘we implore Thee with out-stretched arms and deep groans.’ Malachia Ormanian, a former Armenian patriarch of Constantinople, wrote that the Narek was used for private prayer; his book was put into English in 1912. Its style, he says, is ‘florid and sublime’, and it ‘is regarded as a potent talisman against all kinds of dangers.’ The book still carries the name of the house that raised him.

Descended from

  • continues Armenian Christianity before the Bagratids Widely Accepted Major — In Armenia the office of catholicos ran on with no break as the Bagratid kingdom rose in the 880s. Samuel of Ani's chronicle shows a Bagratid prince, Smbat, as he chose a catholicos around 855. Gevorg II held that office from the late 870s into the 890s.

Direct descendants

  • continued as Armenian Christianity in Cilicia Documented Major — Matthew of Edessa says Khatchig, head of the Armenian church, died in exile in 1065. He grieved for the lost crown of the Bagratid kings and for the high office handed down from Saint Gregory the Illuminator. When Peter held that office, Matthew adds, it had rich lands given by the Armenian kings. Later Matthew shows Basil in the same office, leaving Ani with his bishops in the early 1100s.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

After two centuries under Arab governors, an Armenian kingdom rose again under the Bagratid kings in the 880s. It built the cathedral at Ani, finished in 1001 by the architect Trdat. Trdat may also have rebuilt the dome of Hagia Sophia in Constantinople after it fell. The kingdom's most honored voice was the monk Gregory of Narek. He wrote a book of prayers named after his monastery, the Narek. A modern Armenian patriarch calls its style florid and sublime. He says Armenians pray with it in private. They treat it as a strong charm against every kind of danger.

Legacy

The church itself continued: the same institution carried on into the Armenian kingdom of Cilicia and runs unbroken to the Armenian Apostolic Church of today. Gregory of Narek's book of prayers stayed in use. Giovanni de Serpos found it used in the church's services in 1786. Malachia Ormanian, in a book put into English in 1912, found it used in private prayer. Ani itself was abandoned and is now a ruin field on the Turkish side of the closed Armenian border, inscribed as a UNESCO World Heritage Site in 2016. Its cathedral and churches are among the most important surviving works of medieval Armenian architecture.

Experience It Today

  • The Cathedral of Ani, Kars province, Turkey — completed around 1001 and still standing in the ruined Bagratid capital, a UNESCO World Heritage Site

Doctrinal Floor Note

The Armenian church does not accept the Council of Chalcedon's formula of 451 and belongs to the family usually called miaphysite, or Oriental Orthodox. That is a disagreement about how to describe the joining of divinity and humanity in Christ, not about the Nicene Creed, which this church confesses in full.
