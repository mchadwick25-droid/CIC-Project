About This Person

Under the emperor Heraclius, a priest of Alexandria went about the city at night in a layman's clothes. He comforted the faithful who were in hiding and gave them the sacrament. By day he carried a basket of carpenter's tools on his shoulder. He passed for a carpenter, so that his opponents would not stop him at the doors of the faithful. He kept this up for ten years, until the Muslims came.

So says the History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria, in its life of the patriarch Benjamin. The History is a later Arabic history of the Coptic church. The priest was Agathon of Alexandria, a native of Mareotis. When Benjamin came back to his see, he took Agathon as his son in governing the church. Agathon later followed him as patriarch. B. Evetts, who translated the History, heads his life 661 to 677.

As patriarch, Agathon bought captives whom Muslim raiders had brought from Sicily, and set them free, the History says.

Then Theodore, a Chalcedonian, paid Yazid, son of Muawiyah, the Muslim ruler at Damascus, for power over Alexandria and Mareotis. The Chalcedonians held to the council of Chalcedon, which Agathon's church refused. Theodore ordered that anyone who saw "the pope of the Theodosians" going out, by night or by day, might stone him to death. The Theodosians were the History's name for Agathon's own church. Agathon stayed hidden in his cell and prayed for Theodore, as the Gospel bids.

Agathon died on the 16th of the Coptic month Babah, after seventeen years, the History says. The Ethiopic Synaxarium, a book of saints' days, gives nineteen. His body was laid with Benjamin's.

Benjamin's life in the History holds an account told in Agathon's voice. It tells how Benjamin consecrated a church of Saint Macarius in the desert. Eusèbe Renaudot was a Catholic who wrote a Latin history of these patriarchs in 1713. He calls their line heretical, and that account a fable under Agathon's name. Early in the account, its writer calls himself the sinner Agathon. "I was the son of the Father Benjamin," he says, "and knew much of his virtues through my intimacy with him."

Key Works

  • Account of the consecration of the church of Saint Macarius: told in his voice inside the History's life of Benjamin I. Benjamin's vision and his rules for the church come through it. Eusèbe Renaudot called it a fable under Agathon's name

Legacy

What traces to them

  • A vision named John of Samannud to him as his successor, the History says. Some asked Agathon to make John a bishop of Upper Egypt, but God was keeping him for the throne.
  • The History dates a church dedicated to Saint Macarius to his days, without saying who built it. The brothers grew so many that they built cells near the Marsh.
  • He ordained Simon I of Alexandria, the later patriarch, a priest, says the History's life of Simon.

How they're remembered

  • The Arabic Synaxarium, the Coptic church's book of saints' days, keeps him on the 16th of Babah in René Basset's French. It adds that he was bound when he was made patriarch.
  • The Ethiopic Synaxarium, in E. A. Wallis Budge's English, keeps him on the 16th of Tekemt. It says he died of overwork, after nineteen years.
  • A book on the churches of Egypt, under the name of Abu Salih, tells a story of him at al-Ushmunain. A woodcutter, sent by the governor, struck a tree the patriarch stood under. The axe flew back into the woodcutter's face.

What Is Uncertain

  • His life rests on the History of the Patriarchs, a later Arabic history of the Coptic church. It does not name the writer of his life.
  • The account told in his voice in Benjamin's life. Eusèbe Renaudot, a Catholic scholar writing in 1713, calls the Coptic patriarchs heretics. He calls the account a fable under Agathon's name, and judges Benjamin's canons in it the work of later Copts.
  • His years. The History and the Arabic Synaxarium give seventeen. The Ethiopic Synaxarium gives nineteen, and so does Renaudot's reading of the History. Renaudot reports sixteen from the historian Makrizi.
  • The governor's name. The History's text has Theodore, and B. Evetts notes that some manuscripts have Theodosius. Both synaxaria have Theodosius.
  • The fleet. The History says Theodore's demands cost him seven thousand denarii. Basset's synaxarium has seven thousand dinars a year, and Budge's seven hundred.
  • The captives. The History adds that those he freed were Gaianites and Barsanuphians, sects outside his church.
  • His consecration. Both synaxaria say he was bound when he was made patriarch. The History does not say so.
