Messianic Judaism

Israel and North America, 1960s to 2010 — a modern return of Christianity's oldest contested question, deliberately echoing the map's own first-century Nazoraean entry, and requiring double care because its sensitivities run in both Jewish and Christian directions at once.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:22

Read along

Jewish believers in Jesus are as old as the church, but the modern congregational movement belongs to the later twentieth century. From the 1960s and 1970s, Jewish believers began forming congregations that kept Jewish practice — Sabbath, festivals, Hebrew liturgy — rather than joining gentile churches. The Hebrew Christian Alliance of America, founded in 1915, renamed itself the Messianic Jewish Alliance of America in 1975, and the change of name was also a change of self-description. The Union of Messianic Jewish Congregations followed in 1979 and set out its position in a document called 'Defining Messianic Judaism' in 2005. Jews for Jesus, founded in 1973, took a more evangelistic shape. The movement's own writers have argued about what it is: David Stern's Messianic Jewish Manifesto appeared in 1988 and his Complete Jewish Bible in 1998, and Mark Kinzer's Postmissionary Messianic Judaism in 2005. It was argued about from outside in two directions at once, by Jewish communal bodies and in Christian debates over supersessionism.

Documented Stories

The Vote at Messiah College

The Hebrew Christian Alliance of America had been founded in 1915 as a fellowship of Jewish converts in Presbyterian, Baptist and other churches; its name said what it was, Christians who happened to be Hebrews. By the early 1970s a younger generation, touched by the Jesus movement and by the Six-Day War, had begun forming congregations rather than joining churches, keeping the Sabbath and festivals, and calling themselves Messianic Jews, a phrase that put the noun first. Manny Brotman, Joe Finkelstein and others of that generation had been pressing the Alliance to change its name since 1973. At the annual conference in June 1975, held at Messiah College in Grantham, Pennsylvania, the motion was put and carried by the required two-thirds, and the Hebrew Christian Alliance of America became the Messianic Jewish Alliance of America. The older members who opposed it argued that the new name would separate them from the church, mislead the Jewish community into thinking they were a Jewish sect, and lead the young into legalism; several left. The younger argued that Hebrew Christian had meant assimilation and that Jews who followed Jesus were still Jews and should live as such. The Union of Messianic Jewish Congregations followed in 1979, and Jews for Jesus, founded in 1973, kept the evangelistic emphasis the congregations had left. The vote was a change of self-description, and its opponents were right that it would be contested from both sides, which it was in the decades that followed.

Beresford v. Minister of the Interior

Israel's Law of Return gives every Jew the right to citizenship, and its 1970 amendment defines a Jew as one born to a Jewish mother or converted, ‘and who is not a member of another religion’. Gary and Shirley Beresford, South African Jews who belonged to a Messianic congregation, applied for citizenship in 1986 as Jews; the Ministry of the Interior refused them as Christians, and they petitioned the Supreme Court. Their argument was that they had not converted to anything: they kept the commandments and belonged to no church, and their belief about the Messiah was a Jewish belief about a Jewish question, held by the first followers of Jesus. On 25 December 1989 the court, in a judgment written by Justice Menachem Elon, rejected the petition. The law's phrase, he wrote, was to be read as the Jewish people understood it, and for two thousand years the Jewish people had held that a Jew who accepted Jesus as Messiah had left Judaism for Christianity, whatever practices he kept, and their sincerity did not change that. The Beresfords were refused; a second petition on other grounds was refused in 1992. The ruling governed the movement's standing in Israel in the decades that followed. Messianic Jews called the judgment the proof that they were judged as Jews and excluded as such. The rabbinate and the court called it the boundary the people had always kept. Both readings are in the opinion.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Messianic Judaism raises a question as old as the church: can a person be fully Jewish and follow Jesus? The first Jewish believers argued over it. In the book of Acts, the believers in Jerusalem were "all zealous of the law". They heard that Paul taught Jews "to forsake Moses". This movement brought the question back in living form.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

David Stern's Complete Jewish Bible, published in 1998, and the Union of Messianic Jewish Congregations' 2005 statement "Defining Messianic Judaism" are among the clearest attempts to say what the movement actually is.

Legacy

By 2010 it was a living movement, with congregations in Israel and North America. For its size, it had written a great deal about what it is. Its Bible translation and its prayers for worship were used well beyond it. On this map, the question it kept open matters more than what it built. Can a person be fully Jewish and follow Jesus? The first Jewish believers argued over that, and this movement put it back in living form. Counting it was hard. The congregations' own figures and outside counts differed, and nothing independent settled it.

Doctrinal Floor Note

Mainstream confessions here are trinitarian, so there's no live doctrinal question for those bodies. As always, each is assessed on its own specific, dated confession: the Union of Messianic Jewish Congregations (founded 1979) states it in "Defining Messianic Judaism" (2005), and the Messianic Jewish Alliance of America in its own statement of faith. These are readings of dated, published statements.
