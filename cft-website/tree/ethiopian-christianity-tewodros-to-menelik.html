About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:50

Read along

For a century Ethiopia's emperors had been figureheads while regional lords ruled. Then in 1855 a soldier from the provinces took the throne as Tewodros II. He set out to remake the empire, and its church too. He seized church lands and tried to bring the clergy under his hand. His quarrel with Britain ended in 1868, when a British army stormed Magdala and he took his own life. Yohannes IV took the throne a few years later. He settled an argument that had split Ethiopian scholars for two centuries. At the Council of Boru Meda in 1878 he ruled for the Tewahedo teaching on how God and man are united in Christ. He then made it the one teaching of the empire. Menelik II did what no other African ruler managed in that century. At Adwa in 1896 he crushed an invading Italian army and kept his country free. Ethiopia entered the twentieth century as an ancient Christian state that had never been ruled by Europeans. Its church still took its head, the abun, from the Coptic patriarch in Egypt.

Missionaries kept coming. Donald Crummey wrote about them in a study of the missions from 1830 to 1868. It is called Priests and Politicians.

A Catholic source of 1907 says Tewodros made Catholics suffer. It says Yohannes brought them twenty years of trouble.

It says Menelik, crowned in March 1889, restored calm to the missions. This is a Catholic account, not the church's own.

Documented Stories

The Missionaries Who Cast the Emperor's Mortar

Tewodros II had admitted Protestant missionaries on condition that they bring skills rather than doctrine, and the Basel mission men had settled at Gafat near his capital as gunsmiths and builders, teaching the Bible to the Beta Israel with the emperor's leave. In October 1863 Henry Stern, a British missionary to the Jews, was received by the emperor; a misunderstanding with an interpreter, and the emperor's knowledge that Stern's book described his mother as a seller of a purgative, ended with Stern's two servants beaten to death before him and Stern flogged and imprisoned. The British consul was chained in 1864 when a letter Tewodros had sent to Queen Victoria went unanswered; the envoy sent to negotiate was chained in his turn in 1866. Tewodros, whose empire was dissolving in revolt, moved the prisoners and the missionaries to his fortress of Magdala and set them to casting cannon; they built him a seven-ton mortar he named Sevastopol and dragged over the mountains. The British expedition under Napier landed in January 1868 with thirteen thousand troops and forty-four elephants, marched four hundred miles inland, and broke his army below Magdala on Good Friday, 10 April. Tewodros released the prisoners, and on Easter Monday, 13 April, as the redcoats stormed the gate, shot himself with a pistol Queen Victoria had sent him in better days. The missionaries went home; the British took the manuscripts of Magdala's church, which are in London; the Sevastopol mortar, never fired, lies where it was left.

The Baptism of Wollo

Yohannes IV convened the council of Boru Meda in Wollo in May 1878 to end the christological disputes that had divided the Ethiopian church since the seventeenth century, and with the Egyptian metropolitan at his side ruled for the Tewahedo teaching and forbade the rest. He did not stop at the church. The same assembly issued a proclamation that all subjects of the empire must be Christian: Muslims were to be baptized within three years, followers of traditional religions within five; those who refused would lose their lands, and Muslims who would not convert were free to leave. Wollo, where the council sat, was the test. Its population had been Muslim for centuries; its two great chiefs, Mohammed Ali and Abba Watew, were baptized that year, Mohammed Ali as Mikael with the emperor as godfather, and their followers with them, in mass baptisms in the rivers. Mosques were closed or turned into churches, Islamic teachers expelled, churches built by forced labor; the chronicle records the numbers baptized with satisfaction. Many Wollo Muslims went through the water and went on praying as before; others resisted and were killed; thousands emigrated to the Sudan and the Ottoman coast. Yohannes was killed fighting the Mahdists at Metemma in 1889; Menelik let the policy lapse, leaving Ras Mikael, who had been Mohammed Ali, to rule his Muslim province as a Christian lord. Ethiopian Islam remembers Boru Meda as its persecution; the Ethiopian church remembers it as the council that gave it one faith.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Tewodros II seized church lands and tried to bring the clergy under his own control, a quarrel that ended with a British army at Magdala and his suicide in 1868. A decade later Yohannes IV settled a two-century theological argument over how Christ's divinity and humanity are united at the Council of Boru Meda, even as the church that ruling bound together still had to receive its own head, the abun, from the Coptic patriarch in Egypt.

Legacy

The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church is one of the largest of the ancient Oriental Orthodox churches, and both things this century settled are still in place: the Tewahedo doctrine ruled at Boru Meda, and the independence Adwa secured. The one arrangement that did change came later — the church received its first Ethiopian bishops in 1929 and its own patriarch in 1959, ending sixteen centuries of taking its head from Alexandria. Adwa itself became a byword far beyond Ethiopia, a rallying point for Pan-Africanism and, in the Caribbean and the Americas, for movements that looked to Ethiopia as the image of an Africa that had never been conquered.
