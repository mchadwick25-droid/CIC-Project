The Imperial Church Era · c. 300-451 · Persian Mesopotamia

Persian Church of the East (early)

Persian Mesopotamia, c. 300 to 451 — a church that never had a Christian emperor of its own, and was persecuted at home for the conversion of the one across the border.

Syriac Christianity (Edessa/Nisibis)→Persian Church of the East (early)→The Church of the East under Persia

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:32

Read along

East of the Roman frontier, in the Persian Empire, lived Christians who had never had a Christian emperor and never would. When Constantine turned Christian, it made things worse for them, not better. It let the Sassanian court read them as a Roman fifth column. Under Shapur II, a long persecution began about 340. It killed clergy and lay people in numbers this church remembered in a whole literature of martyr acts. Among the dead was the church's own head, the catholicos Shemon bar Sabbae. He was put to death in the 340s after he would not collect a punitive double tax from Christians to fund the war against Rome. When toleration came at last, the church set up its own order by design. A synod at Seleucia-Ctesiphon in 410 gave it a legal structure and a recognized head. A further synod in 424 said its bishops would answer to no power outside Persia. Its worship was in Syriac, and it shared the learning of Edessa. Persian students there read Theodore of Mopsuestia's commentaries once they were put into Syriac. The church's later teachers made his Antiochene way of reading their own. And it was already becoming the church that would carry Christianity along the trade roads to India, Central Asia, and China.

People and Stories

Voices in the Record

  • Shemon bar Sabbae — catholicos executed in the 340s after refusing to collect a punitive tax on his own people for Persia's war with Rome
  • Aphrahat, called the Persian Sage — whose Demonstrations, written in the 330s and 340s, are the earliest substantial witness to this church's own thinking
  • Marutha of Maypherqat — bishop and envoy, whose diplomacy helped make the synod of 410 possible
  • Isaac — the catholicos who presided at that synod, which gave the church its legal shape under Persian rule

Documented Stories

The Old Tutor Who Had Bowed to the Sun

Symeon bar Sabba‘e, bishop of Seleucia-Ctesiphon, was arrested on charges of treasonous sympathy with Rome and brought in chains to the king, before whom — Sozomen reports — he refused to prostrate himself as he once had, saying that he had bowed to the king before as a subject but would not bow now as a prisoner brought in to deny his God. On the way to prison he passed an old man named Usthazanes sitting at the palace gate. Usthazanes had been the tutor of Shapur’s childhood, was head of the royal eunuchs, and had been a Christian until the persecution began, when he had bowed to the sun to save himself. He rose and greeted the bishop; Symeon turned his face away and walked on. Usthazanes, the account says, broke down weeping, took off his white court garments and put on black, and sat at the gate lamenting that if his friend would not look at him, how would he stand before God. Shapur, who loved him, tried to talk him round and could not, and had him beheaded — granting his last request that a herald proclaim that Usthazanes died not for any crime against the king but because he was a Christian, so that the Christians who had followed his example in apostasy would know he had repented. Symeon was executed the next day with a hundred clergy.

Tarbula, the Bishop’s Sister

Sozomen, following the Syriac Acts, says that Tarbula, Symeon’s sister, a consecrated virgin, was arrested with her sister and her maid when the queen fell ill, on a charge of having caused the illness by sorcery in revenge for the bishop. Sozomen writes that ‘the Jews’ brought the accusation, and that the queen, who leaned toward Judaism, believed it — a claim that appears in this literature repeatedly and that modern historians treat as the martyr-genre’s stock explanation rather than a record. Tarbula was reportedly offered her life by a Magian judge who desired her, and refused. The three were then executed in a manner meant to work as a counter-charm: their bodies were sawn in half and the halves hung on stakes on either side of a road, and the queen was carried between them to break the spell. The account is short and terrible, and it belongs to a genre — the Persian martyr acts — that shaped the Church of the East’s memory of itself for a thousand years: a minority under a Zoroastrian crown, suspected of loyalty to Rome, its women accused of magic, its clergy killed at Easter. The Jewish framing is part of that inheritance too, and the Church of the East, like the Greek and Latin churches, carried it forward without examination. Tarbula’s name and the manner of her death survive as the sources record them; the charge against ‘the Jews’ is a genre convention, not a corroborated fact.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

This church lived east of the Roman frontier, in the Persian Empire, and Constantine's conversion of Rome made its position worse rather than better, since it let the Persian court treat Christians as a Roman foothold. Under Shapur II, the catholicos Shemon bar Sabbae was executed in the 340s after refusing to collect a punitive tax on Christians to help fund the war against Rome. When toleration eventually came, a synod at Seleucia-Ctesiphon in 410 gave the church a legal structure and a recognized head, and a further synod in 424 declared its bishops answerable to no authority outside Persia.

Legacy

This church did not disappear. It became the Church of the East, which for centuries had the widest geographical reach of any Christian body on earth, with bishops in India, Central Asia, and Tang-dynasty China. Its descendants are living communities today — the Assyrian Church of the East and the Ancient Church of the East, along with the Chaldean Catholic Church and the Syro-Malabar Church of Kerala. And its liturgy, including the ancient eucharistic prayer of Addai and Mari, is still in use. Its Syriac scholarship also became a bridge for learning far beyond the church. Translators from this Christian tradition put Greek philosophy and medicine into Syriac and then Arabic, which is much of how that inheritance reached the medieval world.
