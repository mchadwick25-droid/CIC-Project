The Imperial Church Era · c. 300-451 · Persian Mesopotamia

Persian Church of the East (early)

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

East of the Roman frontier, in the Persian Empire, lived Christians who had never had a Christian emperor and never would. When Constantine turned Christian, it made things worse for them, not better. It let the Sassanian court read them as a Roman fifth column. Under Shapur II, a long persecution began about 340. It killed clergy and lay people in numbers this church remembered in a whole literature of martyr acts. Among the dead was the church's own head, the catholicos Shemon bar Sabbae. He was put to death in the 340s after he would not collect a punitive double tax from Christians to fund the war against Rome. When toleration came at last, the church set up its own order by design. A synod at Seleucia-Ctesiphon in 410 gave it a legal structure and a recognized head. A further synod in 424 said its bishops would answer to no power outside Persia. Its worship was in Syriac, and it shared the learning of Edessa. Persian students there read Theodore of Mopsuestia's commentaries once they were put into Syriac. The church's later teachers made his Antiochene way of reading their own. And it was already becoming the church that would carry Christianity along the trade roads to India, Central Asia, and China.

In Its Own Record

Continues

  • Syriac Christianity (Edessa/Nisibis) Documented Major — The Chronicle of Edessa names Jacob and Vologesh as bishops of Nisibis before 363. That year the city passed to Persia, and Ephrem moved to Edessa. In 410 the Persian church held a synod. Osee of Nisibis signed it. The synod made him the second of its chief bishops.

Continued As

  • The Church of the East under Persia Documented Major — In 424 the Persian bishops met under their catholicos, Dadisho. The acts of the synod call Isaac and Yahbalaha 'our masters and fathers'. These two catholicoi had led the synods of 410 and 420. The same synod ruled that Eastern bishops could not go over their head to the Western patriarchs.

In Contact With

  • Imperial and Juridical Christianity Documented — In 410 a synod met at Seleucia-Ctesiphon. Its acts call Bishop Marutha of Maipherqat the go-between for peace between East and West. The Persian bishops then agreed to follow the western forms of worship that Isaac and Marutha had taught them.
