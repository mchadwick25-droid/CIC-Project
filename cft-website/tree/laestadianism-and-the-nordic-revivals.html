The Missionary Era · 1844-1906 · Lapland, Finland, Norway, Sweden

Laestadianism & the Nordic Revivals

Lapland and the Nordic north, 1844 to 1906 — a revival among Sami and Finnish parishes where ordinary believers forgave each other's sins out loud.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:07

Read along

Lars Levi Laestadius was a Swedish Lutheran pastor and a serious botanist, of partly Sami descent, serving parishes in Lapland that were being destroyed by alcohol — and in 1844 a conversation with a young Sami woman he called Mary changed his preaching entirely. What followed spread through the Sami and Finnish north: fierce sermons in the local languages, an end to drinking, and intense emotional expression in meetings that startled outsiders. Its most distinctive practice is confession and spoken absolution between ordinary believers — forgiveness declared face to face by a neighbor rather than pronounced from a pulpit. It was never only his: the same century brought Paavo Ruotsalainen's awakening in Finland and, in Sweden, the low-church revivals of Carl Olof Rosenius and P. P. Waldenström — a wider family of Nordic revivals, not just one man's movement.

Documented Stories

Kautokeino, 8 November 1852

Laestadius's preaching from 1844 had spread among the reindeer-herding Sami of the Swedish and Norwegian north as a revolt against the liquor trade and the merchants who lived by it, and in Finnmark it took, among some, an ecstatic form that Laestadius himself, across the border, did not control. In 1851 a group of the awakened in Kautokeino disrupted services, called the pastor and the merchant servants of Satan, and were arrested and fined; several were imprisoned. On 8 November 1852 some thirty-five of them, men and women, led by Aslak Hætta and Mons Somby, came to the village, killed the merchant and the sheriff, burned the merchant's house, and flogged the pastor with birch rods, calling on him to repent, before Sami from the neighboring settlement overpowered them. The trial at Alta sentenced five to death and many to prison; the crown commuted three, and on 14 October 1854 Aslak Hætta and Mons Somby were beheaded at Alta. Their heads were sent to the University of Christiania and were returned for burial only in 1997. The Norwegian authorities blamed Laestadius, who denied that his teaching sanctioned violence and whose sermons do not; the movement, which spread widely in the following decades under lay leaders who insisted on obedience to the authorities, has carried Kautokeino ever since as the thing it must explain, and Sami memory has carried it as the thing the Norwegians must. Both accounts are documented and the text gives both.

Raattamaa Finds the Keys

Juhani Raattamaa was a catechist and schoolmaster in the Finnish-speaking villages of the upper Torne valley when Laestadius's preaching reached them, and he became the preacher's chief lay assistant. Laestadius's sermons produced conviction of sin on a scale the valley had not seen, and the movement's problem by the late 1840s was not repentance but what came after it: the awakened, terrified by the law, could find no way to assurance, and some fell into despair. Raattamaa, by his own later account, went back to Luther. In the Church Postil and the Small Catechism he found the doctrine of the keys, that Christ had given his church the authority to forgive sins on earth, and that the absolution spoken by a Christian to a penitent was Christ's own word and to be believed as such. At a meeting in the early 1850s, the tradition gives the place variously, a woman in anguish over her sins could not be comforted by any preaching, and Raattamaa, laying his hands on her, told her that her sins were forgiven in the name and blood of Jesus. She believed it and was at peace. He told Laestadius, who approved, and the practice became the movement's center: confession to another believer and spoken absolution, by a preacher or a neighbor, in the name and blood of Jesus, in every meeting since. Raattamaa led the movement from Laestadius's death in 1861 until his own in 1899, and its later branches all keep the keys.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Laestadianism is often counted as a late branch of Pietism in the Lutheran north. It is marked by spoken absolution between believers, and its communities are still alive today. The Swedish low-church revivals are counted alongside it, as part of the same wider Nordic revival. Carl Olof Rosenius led one, and the mission society he helped inspire formed in 1856. P. P. Waldenström led another, and the Mission Covenant Church came out of it in 1878.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Lars Levi Laestadius, a Swedish Lutheran pastor and botanist of partly Sami descent, had his preaching changed entirely by an 1844 conversation with a young Sami woman he called Mary, and the revival that followed carried fierce, local-language preaching and an end to drinking through Sami and Finnish parishes. Its most distinctive practice is confession and absolution spoken face to face between ordinary believers, rather than pronounced from a pulpit. The same century's low-church revivals in Sweden, led by Carl Olof Rosenius and P. P. Waldenström, are counted as part of the same Nordic movement.

Legacy

Laestadian groups still meet across the far north of Finland, Sweden and Norway. They meet in the upper Midwest of the United States too, among the heirs of those who left. Today they form a number of branches, and most hold to the old ways. Few revivals in Europe have won so many indigenous people, and Sami is still a tongue of its preaching. The Swedish revivals left their own bodies as well. One is the mission society Rosenius helped inspire. Another is the Mission Covenant Church. Its cousin in America took the name Evangelical Covenant Church.

How Sure the Record Is

Moderate: sermons, movement periodicals, later oral histories; the Sami dimension adds real marginalized-voice value

  • Laestadius's own corpus: sermons/postillas; Darhushjonet; Ens Ropandes Rost i Oknen (1852-54); Fragments of Lappish Mythology (1840s) (primary) — The sermons survive through transcription and copying, so treat the text as mediated rather than a direct transcript. Fragments of Lappish Mythology is Laestadius's own account of Sami religion - his own partly-Sami background complicates that positioning, worth keeping in mind. Only partial English translation exists.
  • The Sami dimension: Milla Clementsdotter ('Lapp Mary' of Asele, 1844) - known through Laestadius's own account; Juhani Raattamaa's letters and the lay-reader network; the Kautokeino uprising trials (1852) (primary) — Milla Clementsdotter's own voice survives only through Laestadius's account of her, a real filter on the record. The Kautokeino trial records are hostile legal material, from a state court condemning the Sami revivalists it tried.
  • The lived practice record: spoken absolution and liikutuksia in sermons, movement periodicals, and later collected oral histories; firstborn-network village-meeting records (primary) — The oral histories in this collection were recorded well after the events they describe, so treat them as retrospective. Most sources are in Finnish or Swedish and not easy to access in translation.
  • The wider Nordic revivals: Paavo Ruotsalainen's letters and the herannaisyys meeting culture; C. O. Rosenius's Pietisten (1842-) and the Swedish lasare networks (primary) — Ruotsalainen's letters are a lay peasant leader's own record - thin, but genuine. Each national strand of this wider revival should be kept distinct from the others.
  • Juha Pentikainen's Laestadius scholarship (incl. the Fragments critical edition), with Warren Hepokoski's Laestadian-movement surveys (secondary) — The English-language scholarship on this movement is notably thin.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 8 (chapter assignment hedged): nearest Thompson's popular-religion and Bebbington's voluntary-religion chapters (reference) — This reference volume has no dedicated Nordic or Scandinavian chapter; these are the nearest topical fits.
