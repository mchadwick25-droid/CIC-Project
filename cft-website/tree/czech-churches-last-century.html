About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:13

Read along

A century before Martin Luther, Bohemia already had its own reform-minded national church — the Utraquists, who insisted that the laity receive both bread and wine at communion, and the Unity of the Brethren who grew alongside them. Surviving as a tolerated, functioning body long after the Hussite wars that gave it birth. Its final century opens with real legal standing, the 1609 Letter of Majesty guaranteeing religious freedom, and closes in catastrophe: the 1620 Battle of White Mountain crushed Bohemian Protestant resistance, and by 1627-28 the Habsburgs had outlawed the church outright, forcing its bishop, the scholar and educator Jan Amos Comenius, into permanent exile along with much of his flock. In between, the church produced lasting work — the Bohemian Confession of 1575 and the Kralice Bible, the first complete Czech translation from the original languages — leaving behind a body of literature that outlasted the institution itself.

Documented Stories

Twenty-Seven on the Old Town Square

On 21 June 1621, on a scaffold built against the Old Town Hall in Prague, twenty-seven leaders of the Bohemian revolt were executed by the Habsburg government in a single morning: three lords, seven knights and seventeen burghers, most of them Utraquists or Brethren, one a Catholic. The executioner Jan Mydlář worked for four hours with four swords. Among the dead were Václav Budovec, the Brethren nobleman who had drafted the Letter of Majesty of 1609; Kryštof Harant, a traveler and composer; and Jan Jesenius, rector of the university and physician, whose tongue was cut out before he was beheaded and quartered. Twelve of the heads were hung in iron cages on the Old Town bridge tower, where they stayed for ten years. The condemned had been attended in prison by ministers of their own churches, and the accounts written by those ministers and by the chronicler Pavel Skála describe men going to the block singing psalms and reciting the Creed, and Budovec telling his companions that they were going to a better banquet. The confiscations that followed transferred perhaps half the land of Bohemia to new owners; in 1624 non-Catholic clergy were expelled, and by the ordinance of 1627 every noble had to be Catholic or leave. The Brethren's account of it all, the History of the Bohemian Persecution, was printed at Leiden in 1632 and in English in 1650, and became the exiles' book of martyrs.

The Bequest of the Dying Mother

The Peace of Westphalia in 1648 restored religious liberty in much of the Empire but expressly not in the Habsburg hereditary lands, and the Bohemian exiles, who had waited thirty years for the peace, understood that they would not be going home. In 1650 Jan Amos Comenius, then at Leszno in Poland and the last consecrated bishop of the Unity of the Brethren, wrote a short book in Czech called the Bequest of the Dying Mother, the Unity of Brethren. The Unity speaks in it as a mother making her will. She confesses her own faults and blames no one else for her death; she leaves to the other churches her love of Scripture and her discipline, to the Roman Church the charge to stop persecuting, and to the Czech nation her Kralice Bible, her hymnbook and her schools, with the prayer that her people not lose their mother tongue. ‘I believe before God,’ Comenius wrote, ‘that when this storm of wrath — which our sins have brought on us — has subsided, thou wilt, oh Bohemian people, again obtain the control over thy destiny.’ The exile churches kept the Unity's episcopal succession alive in Poland and Prussia, and Comenius lived another twenty years, seeing Leszno burned and his manuscripts lost in 1656. The Bequest was read aloud again in Prague in 1918, when the sentence about the rule of the nation's affairs was taken as fulfilled.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

The Utraquists insisted that ordinary laypeople receive both bread and wine at communion, a demand that outlived the Hussite wars that produced it and won real legal standing in the 1609 Letter of Majesty. The 1620 Battle of White Mountain crushed that settlement, and by 1627-28 the Habsburgs had outlawed the church outright, sending its last bishop, Jan Amos Comenius, into exile with much of his flock — leaving behind the Kralice Bible, the first complete Czech translation made directly from the original languages.

Legacy

Its story didn't end with the 1627-28 suppression. A remnant of the Unity of the Brethren survived underground for nearly a century in Bohemia and Moravia, and in 1722 their descendants found refuge on a German nobleman's estate at Herrnhut — becoming the seed of the modern Moravian Church, one of Protestantism's most influential missionary movements.

Experience It Today

  • Herrnhut, Germany — the Moravian Church's founding town, still a living community and a UNESCO World Heritage Site
  • The Comenius Mausoleum, Naarden, Netherlands — his tomb and museum, open to the public
