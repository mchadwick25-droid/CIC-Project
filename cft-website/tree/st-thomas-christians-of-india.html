About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:11

Read along

On the Malabar coast of India there was, long before any European arrived, a Christian community that traced itself to the apostle Thomas, worshipped in Syriac, and received its bishops from the patriarch of the Church of the East in Mesopotamia — an Indian church inside an Asian communion, on nobody's European line at all. What it left of itself in this period is mostly not writing but objects and documents drawn up by others: granite crosses carved with inscriptions in Pahlavi, the language of Persia, and above all a set of copper plates issued around 849 in which a local ruler grants land and trading privileges to a church at Kollam and names the Christians as a recognized community with standing among their neighbors. Almost nothing survives in which these Christians describe themselves in their own words before about 1500. Which makes them at once one of the oldest continuously existing Christian communities in the world and one of the hardest to hear.

Documented Stories

Who Signed the Kollam Plates

The copper plates of Kollam are the oldest surviving document of Christianity in India, and they are not a Christian document at all. They are a royal grant, engraved in Old Malayalam in the Vatteluttu script, by which Ayyan Adikal, governor of Venad under the Chera king Sthanu Ravi, conveyed land, tenant families, and a bundle of rights to Tarisappalli — the church at Kollam founded by Mar Sapir Iso, a Persian Christian merchant-bishop remembered in the tradition with a companion, Mar Proth. The rights are specific: exemption from certain taxes, the right to collect duties in the market, jurisdiction over the tenants, the services of carpenters, and the protection of the Christians and their property by two guilds of merchants and the local militia. Then come the witnesses, and this is the scene the plates preserve. Eleven signed in Kufic Arabic, with Muslim names; ten in Pahlavi, the Middle Persian of Zoroastrian and Persian-Christian merchants; four in Judaeo-Persian, Hebrew letters writing Persian, with Jewish names. Together they represented the trading community of the port guaranteeing a Hindu grant to a Christian church. Nothing in the plates says what the Christians of Kollam believed or how they worshipped; everything in them says how they stood in their city — as one recognized community among several, with standing enough to receive land from a king and neighbors enough to witness it. The tradition that would later tell Portuguese visitors it had always been here had, in the ninth century, a deed.

A Malabar manuscript of 1301 names Jacob, metropolitan of 'the holy see of the Apostle St. Thomas'. At Kollam in the fourteenth century, the papal envoy Marignolli found the pepper gardens owned by 'the Christians of St. Thomas'. Few texts survive from the centuries between, so the line rests on place, name and office.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

On India's Malabar coast, a Christian community that traced itself to the apostle Thomas worshipped in Syriac and received its bishops from the patriarch of the Church of the East in Mesopotamia. What survives from this period is not writing in their own words but objects made by and for them — granite crosses carved with Persian inscriptions, and a set of copper plates from around 849 recording a local ruler's grant of land and standing to the church at Kollam.

Legacy

The community never stopped existing. Its descendants are the St Thomas Christians of Kerala. Today divided among several churches, after Portuguese pressure in the sixteenth century and the ruptures that followed, but continuous with this one, and still using Syriac liturgical traditions. The objects survive as well: the ninth-century copper plates are preserved in Kerala, and the Pahlavi-inscribed stone crosses still stand in churches at Kottayam and on St Thomas Mount in Chennai.

Experience It Today

  • St Mary's Knanaya Church (Valiyapally), Kottayam, Kerala — home to two ancient granite 'Persian crosses' carved with Pahlavi inscriptions

  • Quilon (Tharisappalli) copper plates, c. 849 CE (primary) — Royal grant of land and privileges to the Tharisappalli church community under Mar Sapir Iso, discussed in recent scholarship (e.g. the Tarisappalli Copperplates study on academia.edu). Specialist editions only; no single standard English reader edition.
  • Ishoyahb III, letter to Shem'on of Rev Ardashir on the lapse of the Indian episcopate (c. 650s) (primary) — Church of the East patriarchal correspondence attesting India's place in, and friction with, the Persian hierarchy.
  • Pahlavi-inscribed 'Persian crosses' (St Thomas Mount, Kottayam, etc.) (primary)
  • Cosmas Indicopleustes, Christian Topography (trans.
