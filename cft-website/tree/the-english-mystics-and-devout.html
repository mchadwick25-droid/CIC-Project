The Late Medieval Era · c. 1330-1440 · England

England, today in United Kingdom

The English Mystics and Devout

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Across a single century, a scattering of English men and women began writing about the inner life of prayer. They wrote in their own language rather than in Latin. Richard Rolle, the Yorkshire hermit, wrote the Fire of Love. Walter Hilton wrote the Scale of Perfection. An author whose name is lost wrote The Cloud of Unknowing. All of them wrote for recluses and devout lay people rather than for scholars. Two of the voices are women's. Julian of Norwich was an anchoress, enclosed in a cell beside a Norwich parish church. She spent decades revising an account of sixteen revelations she had received during a near-fatal illness. In the end she reached the line she is still known for: that all shall be well. Margery Kempe was a brewer's wife from Bishop's Lynn, a mother of fourteen who could not write. She dictated to scribes a book about her pilgrimages, her uncontrollable public weeping and the heresy examinations she survived. It is the closest thing the period leaves us to an ordinary believer's own life story. Carthusian monks copied The Cloud and Hilton's Scale and kept them safe.

In Its Own Record

Influenced By

  • Cistercian Monasticism Documented — Richard Rolle, the Yorkshire hermit who died in 1349, quotes 'S. Bernard' on fasting and keeping watch. Walter Hilton's Scale of Perfection quotes Bernard too. In 1914 Evelyn Underhill named Bernard as one of the three masters who shaped Rolle most.
  • Canons Regular / Victorine School Documented — The English author of The Cloud of Unknowing also put Denis's Mystical Theology into English. He wrote that he had 'much followed' the Abbot of Saint Victor's reading of it. His 1924 editor says this was Thomas, abbot at Vercelli, a Victorine canon who died in 1246. He also shows the Cloud taking much from Richard of St Victor.
  • Carthusian Life Inferential-Thin — The Cloud of Unknowing, written in fourteenth-century England, names reading, thinking and praying as a beginner's tools. Its author says another man described them better in another book. His 1924 editor thinks that book is probably the Ladder of Guigo II, prior of the Grande Chartreuse. The author names no one, so this rests on the editor's judgment.

Influenced

  • Recusant English Catholicism Documented — In 1629 Augustine Baker, an English monk at Cambrai, wrote a guide to The Cloud of Unknowing for the English nuns there. He said his copy came from one that English Carthusians took abroad when they left England under Henry VIII. In 1670 Serenus Cressy, who had become a monk with the English at Douai, printed Julian's book.

Sources

  • Julian of Norwich, Showings (trans. Colledge & Walsh, Classics of Western Spirituality, 1978; Short and Long Texts) (primary) — A named woman's first-person theological writing, revised over a lifetime, in the vernacular. A modern critical alternative exists: Watson & Jenkins, The Writings of Julian of Norwich (2006).
  • The Book of Margery Kempe (trans. Barry Windeatt, Penguin Classics, 1985) (primary) — An illiterate townswoman's dictated life: pilgrimage, weeping, heresy examinations survived - among the richest surviving accounts of an ordinary medieval believer's life. It was dictated to two scribes, and the priest-scribe's shaping hand is part of the text.
  • The teaching corpus: Richard Rolle, The Fire of Love; Walter Hilton, The Scale of Perfection; The Cloud of Unknowing (primary) — The movement's formation texts, written for anchoresses, recluses, and devout laity. Editions: Penguin (trans. Wolters); Hilton in Classics of Western Spirituality (trans. Clark & Dorward, 1991).
  • The Carthusian transmission layer: the unique Kempe MS (BL Add. 61823, Mount Grace annotations), the Amherst MS of Julian's Short Text, The Chastising of God's Children; plus Arundel's Constitutions (1409) as counter-pressure (primary) — Physical evidence that Carthusians copied and preserved these texts, set against the era's censorship of vernacular theology.
  • Bernard McGinn, The Varieties of Vernacular Mysticism 1350-1550 (Presence of God vol. 5, 2012) (secondary) — Current standard frame; Nicholas Watson's censorship scholarship is the key work on the Arundel context.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 4, ch. 19 'Mysticism and transcendence' (Hollywood) (reference) — Coverage of the English vernacular mystics specifically within this chapter is inferred rather than confirmed page by page.
