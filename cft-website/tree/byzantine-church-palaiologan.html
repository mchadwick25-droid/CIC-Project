The Byzantine Church, Palaiologan Era

Constantinople, 1300 to 1453 — the church of a dying empire, which in the end chose its theology over its survival.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:21

Read along

In its last century and a half, the Byzantine church governed the shrinking remnant of an empire. Even so, it did some of its most lasting work. Synods at Constantinople in 1341, 1347 and 1351 ruled for the hesychast monks. They made Gregory Palamas's theology the standard of Orthodoxy. Then came the harder question. The Ottomans were closing in. The emperor and the patriarch went to Italy, and in 1439 they signed a union with Rome at the Council of Florence. They did it in exchange for military help, which never came in enough strength. Mark of Ephesus refused to sign. At home the union was rejected by monks, by much of the clergy and by the city. When it was at last proclaimed in Hagia Sophia in December 1452, it met open hostility. Five months later, on 29 May 1453, Constantinople fell. The same era produced the Palaiologan revival in art: the mosaics of the Chora church and the painted churches of Mystras. They are some of the finest work Byzantium ever did, made while the state around it was collapsing.

People and Stories

Voices in the Record

  • Mark of Ephesus — the one Greek bishop at Florence who refused to sign the union, and became Orthodoxy's symbol of that refusal
  • Bessarion — a Greek archbishop who accepted the union, remained in Italy as a cardinal, and whose library helped carry Greek learning west
  • Gennadios Scholarios — opponent of the union, and the first patriarch of Constantinople under Ottoman rule

Documented Stories

The Pope Asks Whether Mark Has Signed

Sylvester Syropoulos was a senior official of the patriarchate who went to Italy with the Greek delegation and wrote his memoirs of the council afterwards from the anti-unionist side, with a bureaucrat’s attention to who said what in which room. The emperor John VIII, who needed the West’s help against the Turks, had pressed the Greek bishops to accept the Latin doctrines of the procession of the Spirit, purgatory, unleavened bread and papal primacy; the patriarch Joseph had died in Florence in June, leaving a note in favor of union that Syropoulos suspects was written for him; the bishops, worn down, exiled from their sees by the Turks and living on papal subsidies that had stopped arriving, signed the decree Laetentur caeli on 5 July. Mark Eugenikos, metropolitan of Ephesus, who had led the Greek argument through the whole council, did not, and the emperor had shielded him from being compelled. Syropoulos reports that when the signed decree was brought to the pope, Eugenius looked through the names, asked whether Mark had signed, and on hearing that he had not, said: ‘Then we have accomplished nothing.’ The decree was proclaimed in the cathedral of Florence on 6 July in Latin and Greek. Mark went home to Constantinople and was greeted, Syropoulos says, by crowds who called the others traitors; several signatories publicly repented within months. Mark died in 1444 and the Orthodox church canonized him; Eugenius’ remark, if he made it, was accurate.

‘Better the Turkish Turban Than the Latin Mitre’

With the Ottoman army preparing to besiege the city, the emperor Constantine XI agreed at last to what his brother had signed at Florence thirteen years before. On 12 December 1452 the union was proclaimed in Hagia Sophia by Cardinal Isidore of Kiev, himself a Greek who had signed at Florence and become a Roman cardinal, with the pope and the absent patriarch commemorated in the liturgy. Doukas, a Greek in Genoese service who wrote the history of the fall within a few years, describes the reaction: the people, ‘as though the church had been defiled’, would not enter Hagia Sophia afterwards, treating it ‘like a Jewish synagogue or a pagan temple’; the monks and nuns of the city cursed the unionists; the theologian Gennadios Scholarios, from his cell in the Pantokrator monastery, posted a written warning on his door that those who accepted the union had lost the faith and the city. And Doukas gives the grand duke Loukas Notaras, the emperor’s chief minister, the sentence: ‘It would be better to see the turban of the Turks ruling in the midst of the city than the Latin mitre.’ Gennadios, whose warning Doukas quotes, was made patriarch by the sultan in January 1454, and the Orthodox church under the Ottomans began with a man who had said the fall was preferable. Doukas, who favored the union, records all of it as folly; the church he blamed has kept the sentence as its own.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

With the Ottomans closing in, the Byzantine emperor and patriarch signed a union with Rome at the Council of Florence in 1439. They did it in exchange for military help, which never came in enough strength. Mark of Ephesus alone among the Greek bishops present refused to sign. At home the union was rejected by monks, clergy and the city itself. It was finally proclaimed in Hagia Sophia, and five months later, on 29 May 1453, Constantinople fell. The same dying empire produced some of Byzantium's finest art. Among it are the mosaics of the Chora church and the painted churches of Mystras.

Legacy

The church outlived the empire. Soon after the conquest the sultan had Gennadios Scholarios made patriarch. For four centuries the church led the Orthodox under Ottoman rule. Its line runs on to the patriarch in Istanbul today. The Greeks never truly took up the union of Florence. It is still a sore memory between Orthodox and Catholics. Some scholars left for Italy, Bessarion among them. They took Greek books and Greek learning with them into the Renaissance.

Experience It Today
