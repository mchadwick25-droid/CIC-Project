The Byzantine Church, Palaiologan Era

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In its last century and a half, the Byzantine church governed the shrinking remnant of an empire. Even so, it did some of its most lasting work. Synods at Constantinople in 1341, 1347 and 1351 ruled for the hesychast monks. They made Gregory Palamas's theology the standard of Orthodoxy. Then came the harder question. The Ottomans were closing in. The emperor and the patriarch went to Italy, and in 1439 they signed a union with Rome at the Council of Florence. They did it in exchange for military help, which never came in enough strength. Mark of Ephesus refused to sign. At home the union was rejected by monks, by much of the clergy and by the city. When it was at last proclaimed in Hagia Sophia in December 1452, it met open hostility. Five months later, on 29 May 1453, Constantinople fell. The same era produced the Palaiologan revival in art: the mosaics of the Chora church and the painted churches of Mystras. They are some of the finest work Byzantium ever did, made while the state around it was collapsing.

In 1324 its synod settled a dispute by a decision made long before at Nymphaion, under Patriarch Arsenios. The patriarchs' register also copies a ruling of his from 1256, found in the church's old books.

Continued As

  • Orthodoxy under the Ottomans - Parish, Millet & Neomartyrs Documented Major — After the conquest of 1453 the sultan told the Christians to make a patriarch in their own way, since the old one had died. The bishops chose George Scholarios, still a layman, and named him Gennadios. A Greek chronicle under the name of George Sphrantzes tells the story.
