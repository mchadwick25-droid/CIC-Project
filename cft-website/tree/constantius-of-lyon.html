About This Person

Bishop Patiens of Lyon built a new church, and verses were written for its walls beside the altar. Constantius of Lyon wrote some of them. Wilhelm Levison, who edited Constantius's work, reports this from the letters of Sidonius Apollinaris, bishop of Clermont.

Constantius lived at Lyon in Gaul in the later fifth century, and little else is known of his own life. Levison cannot say whether he was a cleric or a layman given to the religious life. Constantius had urged Sidonius to publish his letters, and Sidonius dedicated them to him. About 473, by Levison's date, Constantius helped make peace in a quarrel at Clermont. Sidonius praised him as a man grave with age and venerable in religion.

Patiens, Constantius writes, ordered him again and again to set down the Life of Germanus, bishop of Auxerre. So much time had passed, he says, that what was known of it could scarcely be gathered. He sent it to Patiens and to Censurius, a later bishop of Auxerre. When Censurius ordered it sent beyond a close circle, it made him, Constantius says, the accuser and the betrayer of his own fault. Levison dates the Life about 480 or a little later.

Levison judges Constantius wrote less as a biographer than as a writer of saints' Lives. Wonders of little weight fill much of the book, as in the Life of Martin of Tours by Sulpicius Severus, which Constantius knew. With so few other witnesses, Levison holds that the book should be used with care and must not be scorned. Bede copied large parts of it almost word for word into his History of the English.

At its close Constantius asks the reader's pardon for his rough words and for the book's length. Then he writes, in modern words, "And yet I call God, who knows what is hidden, to witness that I have left out many deeds of my lord Germanus that were known and proved."

Key Works

  • Life of Germanus, bishop of Auxerre (Vita Germani): written at the order of Bishop Patiens of Lyon and sent with letters to Patiens and to Censurius, bishop of Auxerre; it tells Germanus's life as a lawyer, governor and bishop, his journeys to Britain against the Pelagians, and his death at Ravenna

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Bede copied large parts of the Life almost word for word into his History of the English, Wilhelm Levison shows.
  • Levison finds the Life used or imitated by Ennodius of Pavia in his Life of Epiphanius, by Venantius Fortunatus in his Life of Radegund and by Adomnán of Iona, who set Germanus's dangers at sea beside Columba's.

How they're remembered

  • Sidonius Apollinaris dedicated his collected letters to him. As Levison reports, he praised him as a man grave with age and venerable in religion, devoted to reading Scripture and outstanding in eloquence.
  • Isidore of Seville, in his book on famous men, writes that Constantius the bishop composed the Life of Germanus.

What Is Uncertain

  • His own life is thin in the sources. No source used here gives his family, his birth or his death, and the sources disagree on his orders. What is known comes from his two dedicatory letters and from Sidonius Apollinaris's letters to and about him. Those letters are not among the sources used here and are known only through Wilhelm Levison's summary and quotations.
  • Whether he was a cleric. Levison cannot say whether he was a cleric or a layman given to the religious life. The headings of some manuscripts of an interpolated second Life of Germanus call him a priest. W. J. Rees and an unsigned dissertation in Migne's Marius Mercator also call him a priest. Isidore of Seville calls him a bishop, which Levison counts an error. Levison suggests the error may come from the copy Isidore used, since a Spanish manuscript also names him bishop, or from the word sinner after his name, a word bishops of that age often used.
  • His years. The date about 480 or a little later for the Life is Levison's reckoning. It rests on the long time Constantius says had passed since Germanus's death and on Patiens, bishop of Lyon from 449, who died some time before 494. The year about 473 for his peacemaking at Clermont is also Levison's date.
  • How far the Life can be trusted. Levison says Constantius wrote less as a biographer than as a writer of saints' Lives. Wonders of little weight fill much of the book, as in Sulpicius Severus's Life of Martin of Tours, and more than fifteen years of events are packed into a short span. Levison knows no other witness to the victory over the Saxons and Picts known as the Alleluia victory. Yet with so few witnesses, he holds, the book must not be scorned.
  • Who sent Germanus to Britain. Prosper of Aquitaine says Pope Celestine sent him. Constantius names no pope and says a synod of Gallic bishops chose him. Levison holds that any account of how the two fit together can only be a guess.
  • Whether Gregory of Tours knew the Life. In his introduction to Gregory's Miracles, Bruno Krusch says Gregory copied a whole chapter of his Miracles of Julian almost word for word from a Life of Germanus, which he cites in Duru's edition. In the additions at the end of the same volume he reverses this, and holds that the writer of that Life, which he calls the Life commonly given to Constantius, drew on Gregory. Wilhelm Levison holds that Gregory did not know Constantius's Life, since Gregory has Germanus die at Rome, where the Life has Ravenna. Levison also says the text in common use was a much-interpolated second Life, and Duru's edition reprints that second Life.
