About This Person

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 3:00

Read along

Hermas, a Christian in Rome, begins his book by saying that the one who raised him sold him to a woman named Rhoda. Years later he saw her bathing in the Tiber, drew her out, and thought how happy he would be with such a wife. Then, he writes, she appeared to him from the heavens to accuse him of that one desire.

He wrote in the second century, and all he tells of himself comes from his visions. In them the Church, the whole Christian people seen as a woman, and then an angel dressed as a shepherd, called Rome's Christians to repent while there was still time. To repent was to turn from sin and live rightly again. A Christian who sinned after baptism, the washing that made a person a Christian, would have one more chance, and only one. His book, the Shepherd, was read for centuries, quoted as Scripture by some and shut out by others.

He writes that the Church, as an old woman, gave him a book that said a day had been fixed. Those who repented with all their heart before it would be forgiven, but after it a believer who sinned would not be saved. Later he saw her as a tower being built, where stones set aside could still be used if their owners repented before it was finished. Rich believers were round stones that had to be cut square, and she turned this on Hermas himself: "When you were rich, you were of no use. But now you are useful, and fit for life."

Then, he says, an angel sent to teach repentance came to him dressed as a shepherd, and gave the book its name. Hermas asked him: "Sir, I have heard some teachers say this. There is no other repentance than the one when we went down into the water, and our past sins were forgiven." The angel agreed that the baptized ought to sin no more. But the Lord, knowing human weakness, had granted one repentance to those already called, and someone who kept sinning and repenting gained nothing: "with difficulty will he live."

Some churches read it aloud. Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon, quoted it with the words "the Scripture declared", and a teacher in Alexandria carried on its second repentance. An old list of the church's books found it too recent to stand with the Scriptures: "So it ought indeed to be read. But it cannot be read out to the people in church."

Then its question became a fight. When a bishop forgave Christians who had committed adultery and shown their repentance, Tertullian, in Carthage, scorned the book as "the Shepherd of adulterers". By the late fourth century it was kept for people preparing to become Christians, and the Latin West had almost forgotten it.

Near the end of his book, Hermas says he makes God's great works known to everyone. He hopes that all who have sinned before may hear these words, repent, and receive life again.

Quotations are given in our modern English. The printed wording of each is below.

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • The Shepherd taught that a Christian who sins after "that great and holy calling" may repent once.

How they're remembered

  • Irenaeus of Lyon quoted it as Scripture. Clement of Alexandria and Origen used it as inspired.
  • Tertullian of Carthage called it "the only one which favours adulterers."
  • The Muratorian Canon says it should be read, but not in church to the people. Eusebius of Caesarea placed it among the spurious books. Jerome of Strido found it almost unknown among the Latins.

Open Questions

  • What the Shepherd tells of Hermas comes from inside its visions, and no other source confirms it.
  • The Muratorian Canon makes him the brother of Pius, bishop of Rome, which puts the book in the middle of the second century. Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius of Caesarea, says most scholars accepted this. Zahn dated it between 97 and 100, to an unknown Hermas living when Clement of Rome did.
  • That he was the Hermas whom Paul greets in Romans goes back to Origen. McGiffert calls it "a mere guess."
  • Cleveland Coxe, in his notes to the American edition, asks whether the words on one repentance can be genuine, since they sound like the strictness the book opposes. Part of the sentence before them is found in only one manuscript.
  • In the text F. Crombie translated, the man who raised Hermas sold him to a woman named Rhoda. The Vatican Latin and the Leipzig Greek copy say instead that he sold a young woman.
