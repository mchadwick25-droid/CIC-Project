Kievan Rus' Christianity, 1054-1240

Kyiv, Novgorod and Vladimir-Suzdal, 1054 to 1240 — a young church growing its own saints, chronicles and monasteries out of a hillside cave, until the Mongols ended it in a single season.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:00

Read along

In the two centuries after Yaroslav the Wise died in 1054, the Christianity that had arrived in Rus' only two generations earlier put down roots of its own. Its center was the Caves Monastery at Kyiv, begun by a hermit named Anthony in a hillside cave above the Dnieper and turned into a community by Theodosius, its third abbot, who gave it a Byzantine rule and a distinctive emphasis on humility, manual work and care for the poor. Out of that monastery came bishops, chronicles, and a book of stories about its own monks. The church was formally a metropolitanate of Constantinople, usually headed by a Greek, but native metropolitans appeared and a native literature and a native calendar of saints grew up around the princely dynasty. It ended abruptly: the Mongols sacked Kyiv in 1240.

  • Boris and Gleb — the murdered princes who became the first saints canonized in Rus', venerated for refusing to fight back

Documented Stories

The Boy Who Wanted to Bake the Bread

Nestor, a monk of the Caves who joined the community shortly after Theodosius’ death in 1074, wrote the first Life of a Rus’ monk, and its opening is a family quarrel. Nestor says the boy went out to the fields with the slaves to work, in worn clothes, and that his mother — ‘strong of body, like a man, and with a man’s voice’ — beat him for shaming the family. Then he took to baking prosphora, the loaves for the Eucharist, and selling them, giving the profit to the poor and keeping the work because, he told her, it was work for Christ’s body. She beat him for that too; he went to another town and did it there; she brought him back in chains and locked him up. At twenty-three he heard the words ‘whoever does not leave father and mother’, walked to Kyiv, was refused by the established monasteries because he was poor, and was received by the hermit Anthony in his cave above the Dnieper. His mother followed him, found him after four years, and — Nestor says — was persuaded by him to enter a convent in Kyiv herself, where she died. The monastery Theodosius made out of Anthony’s cave gave Rus’ monasticism its shape: a rule from Constantinople, an insistence on manual labor and coarse food, an open door for the poor and for princes.

Isaac and the Demons Who Made Him Dance

The Kyiv Caves Paterik, a thirteenth-century compilation of the monastery’s own stories about its monks, does not only tell of successes. Isaac had been a merchant, sold everything, come to Anthony’s cave and asked for the hardest life: a cell so small he could not stretch his legs, a hair shirt over a raw goatskin, one loaf every other day. After seven years, the Paterik says, a light filled the cell and two beautiful young men told him Christ was coming; he bowed to the figure that entered — and it was a demon, and the demons seized him, made him dance to their pipes until he collapsed, and left him. Theodosius took him into the monastery and for two years fed him with his own hands, turned him, washed him; Isaac lay ‘like a log’ and had to be taught to walk and to eat again. When he recovered he refused the cave, worked in the kitchen for the brothers, played the fool in the town, gathered the children of Kyiv around him and dressed them in monks’ habits, and was beaten by the cellarer for it. The Paterik tells it as a lesson on pride; it is also the monastery’s honest record that the ascetic program could break a man, that the community’s response was to nurse him, and that the holiness he found afterwards did not look like the one he had wanted.

In Its Own Record

A continuation of Rus' Christianity's first generations, centered on the Kyiv Caves Monastery, with native-born metropolitans appearing alongside the usual Greek ones sent from Constantinople. It ended with the Mongol sack of Kyiv in 1240.

Descended from

  • continues Kievan Rus' Christianity Inferential-Thin Major — Before he became head of the church in Kyiv in 1051, the priest Ilarion dug a cave above the Dnieper. Mouravieff, a Russian church historian writing in 1838, calls that cave the seed of the Caves Monastery and of every religious house in Russia. Herberstein retold the Rus' annals in 1549. He says Russia has kept the faith of Christ since Vladimir's baptism. The claim of one unbroken church rests on these two later accounts.

Direct descendants

  • gave rise to Muscovite Monastic Christianity Documented Major — A ruling of the patriarch's council in Constantinople, from the 1300s, says Kyiv had long held the cathedral of the chief bishop of Rus'. When Kyiv fell into ruin, the bishops moved their seat to Vladimir. Herberstein, an envoy to Moscow in the 1500s, wrote that the seat had since moved on to Moscow.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In the two centuries after Yaroslav the Wise died in 1054, the Kyiv Caves Monastery grew from a hermit's hillside cave — dug by Anthony — into a community under its abbot Theodosius, who gave it a Byzantine rule built on humility, manual work and care for the poor. Out of that monastery came bishops, chronicles and a native literature, even though the church was formally headed by Greek metropolitans sent from Constantinople. It ended abruptly when the Mongols sacked Kyiv in 1240.

Legacy

The Caves Monastery survived the sack and is still there. Today it is the Kyiv Pechersk Lavra, with underground passages full of relics, and one of the great monasteries of the Orthodox world. In our own century it is also claimed by rival Ukrainian churches. The writings this church left, above all the Primary Chronicle, are the founding record of East Slavic history. Boris and Gleb, princes honored as saints because they did not fight back, set a pattern of holiness of their own among the Slavs. Who inherits Kyiv's legacy — Kyiv, Moscow, or both — is a live political question to this day. The century of Mongol rule after 1240 left few records. What ran through it was the see of the metropolitan. It moved north to Vladimir and then to Moscow, and Moscow later claimed Kyiv's legacy as its own.

Experience It Today
