Pelagius

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Pelagianism.

Known for
A monk at Rome who taught that God gave human nature the power to live without sin, and whose teaching on grace and free will was condemned in Africa and at Rome in 418.
Dates
At Rome in the early 400s; to Africa in 411 and on to Palestine; cleared by the Synod of Diospolis late in 415; declared orthodox by Zosimus of Rome in September 417, then condemned by him in 418 (B. B. Warfield's dates)
Place
Rome; Africa; Palestine
Role
Monk and teacher

About This Person

Late in 415, by B. B. Warfield's date, fourteen bishops met at Diospolis in Palestine to judge a monk named Pelagius. His accusers, two bishops from Gaul, were absent, one of them ill. A charge was read that he had written, "A man is able, if he likes, to be without sin." Pelagius answered that this power "has been given to him by God." Denying other words laid against him, he said of those who held them, "I anathematize them as fools, not as heretics, for there is no dogma." The bishops declared him in communion with the Catholic Church.

Augustine of Hippo quoted the synod's record in a book against him. Jerome of Strido, to whom Augustine appealed, wrote against him too. Apart from his letter and his answers at the synod, most of his story comes from their side. Warfield, who edited Augustine's books in English, takes Augustine's side.

Pelagius lived at Rome in the early 400s. Augustine first called him "a holy man, as I am told." At Rome, he says, a bishop quoted to Pelagius a prayer from Augustine's Confessions, "Give what Thou commandest." Pelagius could not bear it and nearly quarreled.

About 413 he wrote from the East to Demetrias, a Roman noblewoman vowed to Christ as a virgin. In the Latin letter he says he always begins by showing what human nature can do. It does no good, he explains, to call people to what they think impossible. We tell God his commands are too hard, and "we are men." "Oh, blind madness!" he answers, in Warfield's English. Habit makes doing good hard, he adds, until sin seems to have "the force of nature."

Warfield says Pelagius and his follower Caelestius fled the Goths from Rome to Africa in 411. After Diospolis, the African bishops condemned Pelagius again, and Innocent, bishop of Rome, agreed. Innocent's successor Zosimus at first declared Pelagius orthodox, in 417. In 418 the emperor banished the Pelagians from Rome, and Zosimus condemned him.

In a letter to Innocent, Pelagius pointed to his letter to Demetrias. There "they will find that we so commend the nature of man as always to add the help of God's grace." It almost persuaded Augustine. He decided that by grace Pelagius meant help from outside, such as teaching and Christ's example.

Gennadius of Marseilles says that before he was proclaimed a heretic, Pelagius wrote "works of practical value for students."

Key Works

  • Letter to Demetrias: his counsel to a Roman noblewoman vowed to Christ as a virgin, written from the East about 413; long printed among Jerome's works, it survives whole in Latin
  • Answers at the Synod of Diospolis: his replies to the charges, kept in the synod's record, which Augustine of Hippo quotes in On the Proceedings of Pelagius
  • Letter and statement of faith to Innocent of Rome: sent to Rome in 417 to win Rome to his side, as Warfield tells it; Warfield says that on its arrival Zosimus, Innocent's successor, declared Pelagius and Caelestius orthodox and rebuked the Africans; known here from the passages Augustine quotes in On the Grace of Christ and Warfield quotes in his essay

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The Pelagians were named after him. B. B. Warfield says a council of about two hundred bishops at Carthage condemned their teaching in nine canons on 1 May 418.
  • Augustine of Hippo wrote a long run of books against him and his followers, among them On Nature and Grace, On the Proceedings of Pelagius and On the Grace of Christ.
  • Jerome of Strido wrote his Dialogue against the Pelagians at Bethlehem. W. H. Fremantle, who edited Jerome in English, says Jerome "does not scruple to brand Pelagius as a heretic."

How they're remembered

  • Before the quarrel, Augustine called him "a holy man, as I am told, who has made no small progress in the Christian life."
  • Gennadius of Marseilles calls him "the heresiarch," but says that before he was proclaimed a heretic he wrote three books on belief in the Trinity and a book of selections from Scripture on the Christian life.
  • B. B. Warfield, writing on Augustine's side, says Pelagius escaped condemnation at Diospolis "only by a course of most ingenious disingenuousness."

What Is Uncertain

  • Apart from his letter to Demetrias and his answers at Diospolis, his story comes through Augustine of Hippo and Jerome of Strido. Augustine appealed to Jerome, who wrote against him too. It also comes through modern editors who take Augustine's side, such as B. B. Warfield.
  • The synod of Diospolis declared him in communion with the Catholic Church. Augustine accepts that the bishops judged rightly on what they heard, but holds that Pelagius condemned views that were his own. Pelagius, in a letter Augustine quotes and doubts is his, says fourteen bishops approved his statement that a man is able to be without sin, "and easily to keep the commandments of God, if he wishes."
  • What he meant by grace is disputed. Pelagius told Innocent that he always adds "the help of God's grace." Augustine judged that he meant teaching, the forgiveness of past sins and the example of Christ, and no help within each act of the will.
  • Peter Holmes, who translated Augustine, says Augustine, Prosper of Aquitaine and Gennadius call him a Briton, and that Jerome ties him to the Scots near Britain. Holmes calls it "quite uncertain" what part of Britain he came from. The English of Gennadius held here does not name his country.
  • When he came to Rome is told two ways. Warfield and Holmes place him there from about the start of the fifth century. W. H. Fremantle, in his edition of Jerome, says he came to Rome in 409.
  • Warfield dates Diospolis to December 415. Fremantle gives 415 in his tables and 416 in his note to Jerome's Dialogue.
  • Warfield dates the Letter to Demetrias to 413. The note printed before it in Migne's Latin edition gives 413 or 414.
  • The sources used here do not give his birth, his death or where he died.
