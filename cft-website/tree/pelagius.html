Pelagius

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Pelagianism.

A monk at Rome who taught that God gave human nature the power to live without sin, and whose teaching on grace and free will was condemned in Africa and at Rome in 418.

About This Person

About 413, Pelagius wrote to Demetrias, a young Roman noblewoman who had vowed herself to Christ and given up marriage. God made human nature good and free to choose, he told her, and her birth and wealth were her family's. "But spiritual riches no one can give you except yourself. In these, then, you are rightly praised. In these you are rightly set above others. They cannot exist except from you and in you."

Pelagius, a monk from Britain who taught at Rome, held that God made human nature able, by God's gift, to choose the good and to live without sin. Bishops in Palestine cleared him in 415. But Augustine of Hippo answered him book by book, and in 418 bishops in North Africa and the bishop of Rome condemned him over grace, God's help freely given. The teaching that bears his name was ruled out of the church.

He lived long at Rome, where Caelestius became his follower. About 411 he crossed to Africa, then sailed on to Palestine. Apart from Pelagius's own letter and answers, his story comes to us mostly through Augustine and others who wrote against him.

In December 415, two bishops from Gaul charged him in writing but could not come. Fourteen Greek-speaking bishops heard him through an interpreter, at Diospolis. Some charges were Caelestius's views: that Adam's sin harmed only Adam, and newborn babies are as Adam was before he sinned. Pelagius said these were not his, and condemned them. Yet a man can be without sin, he said, and keep God's commandments if he wishes. The bishops declared him a member in good standing of the church. Augustine, reading the record, granted that the judges were right on what they heard, but to him Pelagius did not appear cleared.

Augustine reports, as something said, that followers of Pelagius had beaten the monks and nuns in Jerome's care at Bethlehem, killed a deacon and burned their houses. Early in 417, at the African bishops' urging, Innocent, the bishop of Rome, condemned the Pelagians, as his followers came to be called.

Pelagius wrote to Innocent that he always added the help of God's grace. The next bishop of Rome, Zosimus, had letters from him read aloud. In September 417 he asked the Africans, "Is there any passage in which God's grace or help has been left out?" Some who heard them, he wrote, could barely hold back their tears that men of such complete faith had been slandered. For a few months, Rome itself took his side.

Writing with a fellow bishop, Augustine warned Demetrias's mother against Pelagius's letter. "'These good things can only be in you': that was very well and very truly said. That is clearly food. But when he says 'only from you', that is wholly poison." Goodness might live in her, they meant, but did not come from her.

On 30 April 418 the emperor ordered Caelestius and Pelagius driven from Rome, and anyone spreading their teaching tried and, if convicted, exiled. Pelagius was then in the East. The next day some two hundred African bishops met at Carthage. God's grace, they ruled, does not only forgive past sins, and does not only show us what to do: it helps us do it. Rome, under Zosimus, condemned him too, Augustine says. That year Augustine judged that Pelagius made grace mean teaching, forgiveness and Christ's example, and never help to the will itself.

The sentence held. By 429, a chronicler says, a Pelagian was spreading the teaching in Britain's churches, and Germanus of Auxerre was sent against it. About 430 John Cassian, a monk in Gaul, charged the bishop of Constantinople with the Pelagians' teaching. Both, he wrote, made Christ a mere man who lived without sin, so others could too. In 431 the council at Ephesus wrote to Rome upholding the sentence on the Pelagians, Pelagius among them.

The sources we have do not say where or when Pelagius died. But his letter to Demetrias outlived him. It long passed as Jerome's, and was printed among the works of Jerome, who had written against the Pelagians.

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • The Pelagians were named after him. B. B. Warfield says a council of about two hundred bishops at Carthage condemned their teaching in nine canons on 1 May 418.
  • Augustine of Hippo wrote a long run of books against him and his followers, among them On Nature and Grace, On the Proceedings of Pelagius and On the Grace of Christ.
  • Jerome of Strido wrote his Dialogue against the Pelagians at Bethlehem. W. H. Fremantle, who edited Jerome in English, says Jerome "does not scruple to brand Pelagius as a heretic."
  • Augustine answered Pelagius's book on nature without naming him. He says he left the name out to keep on friendly terms with him. Two men he calls Pelagius's former disciples, Timasius and Jacobus, had put the book in his hands.
  • A letter from Rome to the Irish churches, kept in Bede's History, says that the poison of the Pelagian heresy "again springs up among you" and that the heresy had been "abolished these two hundred years".
  • Bede, writing in 731, calls him "a Briton" who spread "far and near" the infection of his doctrine against the assistance of God's grace.
  • Vincent of Lérins, in 434, asks who before "that profane Pelagius" gave free will so much strength "as to deny the necessity of God's grace to aid it towards good in every single act".
  • Faustus of Riez, about 474, begins the list of chapters of his book On Grace with the view of Pelagius, who denied grace, which he says must first be destroyed.
  • Constantius of Lyon, about 480, in his Life of Germanus, sets two sides of a public dispute against each other: on one side divine authority, on the other human presumption; on one side Pelagius as author, on the other Christ. Bede copies the passage.

How they're remembered

  • Before the quarrel, Augustine called him "a holy man, as I am told, who has made no small progress in the Christian life."
  • Gennadius of Marseilles calls him "the heresiarch," but says that before he was proclaimed a heretic he wrote three books on belief in the Trinity and a book of selections from Scripture on the Christian life.
  • B. B. Warfield, writing on Augustine's side, says Pelagius escaped condemnation at Diospolis "only by a course of most ingenious disingenuousness."

Open Questions

  • Apart from his letter to Demetrias and his answers at Diospolis, his story comes through Augustine of Hippo and Jerome of Strido. Augustine appealed to Jerome, who wrote against him too. It also comes through modern editors who take Augustine's side, such as B. B. Warfield.
  • The synod of Diospolis declared him in communion with the Catholic Church. Augustine accepts that the bishops judged rightly on what they heard, but holds that Pelagius condemned views that were his own. Pelagius, in a letter Augustine quotes and doubts is his, says fourteen bishops approved his statement that a man is able to be without sin, "and easily to keep the commandments of God, if he wishes."
  • What he meant by grace is disputed. Pelagius told Innocent that he always adds "the help of God's grace." Augustine judged that he meant teaching, the forgiveness of past sins and the example of Christ, and no help within each act of the will.
  • Peter Holmes, who translated Augustine, says Augustine, Prosper of Aquitaine and Gennadius call him a Briton, and that Jerome ties him to the Scots near Britain. Holmes calls it "quite uncertain" what part of Britain he came from. The English of Gennadius held here does not name his country.
  • When he came to Rome is told two ways. Warfield and Holmes place him there from about the start of the fifth century. W. H. Fremantle, in his edition of Jerome, says he came to Rome in 409.
  • Warfield dates Diospolis to December 415. Fremantle gives 415 in his tables and 416 in his note to Jerome's Dialogue.
  • Warfield dates the Letter to Demetrias to 413. The note printed before it in Migne's Latin edition gives 413 or 414.
  • The sources used here do not give his birth, his death or where he died.
  • Augustine says he first learned Pelagius's name, with great praise of him, while Pelagius lived at Rome. He says he glimpsed him once or twice in Africa, while busy preparing for the conference with the Donatists, and that Pelagius hurried away across the sea. In On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins he says he has lately read some very brief expository notes by Pelagius on the epistles of Paul. Those notes are not held here.
  • Augustine says that at Diospolis Pelagius produced even the private letters Augustine and his colleagues had written to him and read them at the trial. Asked about the passage where he wrote that all men are ruled by their own will, Pelagius answered, in the record Augustine quotes: "God is its helper whenever it chooses good."
  • Zosimus, in his letter of 21 September 417 to the African bishops, calls Pelagius a layman who strove toward good fruit by long service to God, and says he sees him slandered by the writings of Heros and Lazarus, the two bishops from Gaul who charged him at Diospolis.
  • The letter to Demetrias says it was written at the request, or rather the command, of her mother. The Migne editors say it was ascribed to other writers of his party, first to Julian, as Bede thought, and also to Caelestius. They hold it to be Pelagius's own, on the strength of Augustine's On the Grace of Christ, where Pelagius, writing to Innocent, names it as his.
  • Marius Mercator, writing against the teaching of Caelestius, says that a Syrian named Rufinus first brought the teaching to Rome in the time of Pope Anastasius and misled Pelagius, a British monk, into it. No other source held here says so.
  • Jerome's Commentary on Jeremiah is not held here. W. H. Fremantle's English summary of it has Jerome say that the devil "now barks through the hound of Albion (Pelagius), who is like a mountain of fat". The name in brackets is Fremantle's; Jerome's words as summarized do not name him.
  • After Zosimus condemned him, B. B. Warfield says, eighteen Italian bishops, with Julian of Eclanum at their head, refused to sign Zosimus's paper and were deposed, though several later recanted and were restored.
