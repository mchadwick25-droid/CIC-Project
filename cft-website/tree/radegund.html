Radegund

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Merovingian Gallic Christianity.

Known for
A Thuringian princess taken captive and married by the Frankish king Lothar, who left him to become a nun and founded the monastery of Holy Cross at Poitiers.
Also called
Radegundis, Radegunde, Saint Radegund of Poitiers
Dates
Died in the twelfth year of King Childebert, on the thirteenth of August by Baudonivia's Life, a year the editors give as 587
Place
Born a princess of the Thuringians; queen of the Franks; consecrated at Noyon; founded her monastery at Poitiers, where she died
Role
Queen; nun and founder of the monastery of Holy Cross at Poitiers

About This Person

Radegund came to Bishop Medard at Noyon and begged him to make her a nun. The king's nobles dragged the bishop back from the altar. So she went into the sacristy, put on a nun's dress and walked up to the altar. If he put off consecrating her, she told him, and feared a man more than God, the shepherd would answer for a sheep's soul. Medard laid his hand on her and made her a deacon. Her friend Venantius Fortunatus, the poet, tells it in his Life of her.

Radegund was a Thuringian princess who became a Frankish queen. Gregory of Tours says her uncle killed her father, King Berthar. When the Franks won Thuringia, King Lothar took her captive and married her. Later he had her brother killed. Then, Fortunatus writes, she came from the king to Medard.

At Poitiers she built a monastery for women, founded and enriched by Lothar, she says. She made Agnes its abbess, obeyed her, and took for the house the Rule of Caesarius of Arles. Gregory says she went to Arles to get it. Bruno Krusch, who edited her two Lives, later agreed with the scholar Arnold that Gregory was wrong.

She sent clerics to the East for wood of the True Cross. Gregory writes that Maroveus, the bishop of Poitiers, would not receive it. He rode off to a country estate. Her house came to be called Holy Cross, Krusch says.

Her letter to the bishops survives in Gregory's History. "I turned of my own will to the Rule of religion under Christ's guidance," she writes.

Three poems in her voice are printed with Fortunatus's works. Friedrich Leo, who edited them, and Krusch treat them as his, written in her name.

Gregory was at her burial. About two hundred nuns stood round the bier, he says. The abbess showed him the book she used to read, and the spindles she spun with through long fasts and tears.

Key Works

  • Letter to the bishops: her letter, kept in Gregory of Tours's History, setting out the founding of her monastery, its Rule and its abbess, and asking the bishops to protect it

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Her monastery at Poitiers took the name of Holy Cross, Bruno Krusch says. Gregory of Tours tells how she sent to the East for wood of the True Cross.
  • She gave her community the Rule that Caesarius of Arles wrote for his sister Caesaria, as her letter says.
  • Venantius Fortunatus stayed at Poitiers because of her, he writes, and wrote poems to her and her Life.

How they're remembered

  • Two Lives were written of her: one by Fortunatus, her friend, after her death, and one by Baudonivia, a nun of her house, after 600, as Krusch dates them.
  • Gregory of Tours was at her burial and tells of it in his History and his book on the confessors.

What Is Uncertain

  • Her own words. Her letter in Gregory's History is given as hers, and Dalton and Krusch take it so. Three poems in her voice, among them a lament for Thuringia, are printed with Fortunatus's works; Friedrich Leo, Bruno Krusch, M. A. Luchi and O. M. Dalton all treat them as Fortunatus's, written in her name.
  • Whether she went to Arles. Gregory says she went there with the abbess for the Rule. Krusch followed him in 1888, but in 1896 judged, after Arnold, that Gregory was wrong; Malnory, as Krusch reports him, kept to Gregory.
  • When she wrote the letter. Sirmond placed it at the founding of her community and linked the bishops' answer to the council of Tours of 567. Friedrich Maassen notes that the link rests on the names of the signers.
  • Her years. None of these sources gives a year of birth or of her capture. The year 587 for her death is the editors' reckoning from Gregory's year of King Childebert.
  • The scenes of her Lives come from friends and nuns of her house. Krusch says Baudonivia copied some passages word for word from the Life of Caesarius.
