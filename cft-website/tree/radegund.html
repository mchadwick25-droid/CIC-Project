Radegund

Also called Radegundis, Radegunde, Saint Radegund of Poitiers

About This Person

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 2:45

Read along

In the sixth century, a queen who had become a nun wrote to the bishops of Gaul about the years after her death. "Because I could not come in person," wrote Radegund, "I bow down through this letter in my place, as if fallen at your feet."

Radegund had come to the Franks as a captive. By the end of her life she had founded a house for women at Poitiers, in what is now France. She gave it a written Rule and a relic of the Cross, and wrote this letter to guard it after she was gone.

She had been born a princess of the Thuringians. When the Franks destroyed her people's kingdom, she was taken as spoil and married to Chlothar, who later had her brother killed. She left the court for Medard, bishop of Noyon, and begged him to consecrate her to God.

Her friend the poet Fortunatus tells what followed. Medard hesitated because she was a married woman, and the king's nobles dragged him from the altar. She put on a nun's dress and charged him. "If you put off consecrating me, and fear a man more than God, then, shepherd, may the soul of your sheep be demanded from your hand." Medard consecrated her a deaconess.

Her letter tells what she built. With King Chlothar's backing, she founded the monastery and gave it everything the king had given her. The Rule was one that Caesarius, bishop of Arles, had made for his sister's convent, and under it a nun stayed inside the walls for life. Women came from the bishops' own dioceses to live by it.

With the letters of King Sigibert, she sent to the emperor in the East for a piece of the wood of the Cross. The house came to be called Holy Cross. Gregory, a bishop who knew her, says the bishop of Poitiers would not receive the relic. So Sigibert sent the bishop of Tours, who brought it in with psalms, candles and incense.

Her letter asked the bishops to guard her sisters from any tyrant. It made a second request as well: if anyone, even the city's own bishop, tried after her death to break the Rule or replace the abbess, they must stop him. Gregory later copied the whole letter into his History, which is how we still have it.

When she died in 587, Gregory buried her, and about two hundred nuns stood around her body.

Soon after, forty or more nuns walked out in revolt. The abbess read Radegund's letter aloud and sent copies to the bishops nearby. Before the bishops, the abbess said she had brought in no new custom, only what was done under the lady Radegund.

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Her monastery at Poitiers took the name of Holy Cross, Bruno Krusch says. Gregory of Tours tells how she sent to the East for wood of the True Cross.
  • She gave her community the Rule that Caesarius of Arles wrote for his sister Caesaria, as her letter says.
  • Venantius Fortunatus stayed near her by her wish, he writes, and wrote poems to her and her Life.

How they're remembered

  • Two Lives were written of her: one by Fortunatus, her friend, after her death, and one by Baudonivia, a nun of her house, after 600, as Krusch dates them.
  • Gregory of Tours was at her burial and tells of it in his History and his book on the confessors.

Open Questions

  • Her own words. Her letter in Gregory's History is given as hers, and Dalton and Krusch take it so. Three poems in her voice, among them a lament for Thuringia, are printed with Fortunatus's works; Friedrich Leo, Bruno Krusch, M. A. Luchi and O. M. Dalton all treat them as Fortunatus's, written in her name.
  • Whether she went to Arles. Gregory says she went there with the abbess for the Rule. Krusch followed him in 1888, but in 1896 judged, after Arnold, that Gregory was wrong; Malnory, as Krusch reports him, kept to Gregory.
  • When she wrote the letter. Sirmond placed it at the founding of her community and linked the bishops' answer to the council of Tours of 567. Friedrich Maassen notes that the link rests on the names of the signers.
  • Her years. None of these sources gives a year of birth or of her capture. The year 587 for her death is the editors' reckoning from Gregory's year of King Childebert.
  • The scenes of her Lives come from friends and nuns of her house. Krusch says Baudonivia copied some passages word for word from the Life of Caesarius.
