The Waldensians

Lyon and the Alpine valleys, from about 1173 — a merchant who gave away everything to preach, condemned not for what he believed but for preaching without permission, and a movement that outlived everyone who condemned it.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:04

Read along

Around 1173 a rich merchant of Lyon named Valdes gave away his property. He had parts of Scripture put into the language people actually spoke, and he began preaching in the streets. His confession of faith was wholly orthodox. What he would not do was stop preaching without a license, and in 1184 the bull Ad abolendam condemned him and his followers for exactly that. Driven out of the cities, the movement held on in the Alpine valleys between France and Italy. It lasted through three and a half centuries of on-and-off persecution, with its own traveling ministers and its own books. In 1532 its leaders met the Reformers at Chanforan and threw in with the Reformed churches. So a single line of protest runs from twelfth-century Lyon into the Reformation, though scholars disagree about how unbroken it was.

People and Stories

Voices in the Record

  • Valdes of Lyon — the merchant who gave away his wealth, commissioned Scripture in the vernacular, and would not stop preaching
  • Durand of Osca — a first-generation Waldensian whose theological defense of the movement survives, and who later reconciled with Rome
  • Bernard Gui — the Dominican inquisitor whose handbook describes Waldensian practice from the outside, and whose registers preserve the words of ordinary believers recorded nowhere else

Documented Stories

Walter Map Makes Fun of the Poor Men of Lyon

Walter Map, an archdeacon and a clerk of Henry II of England, was at the Lateran Council of 1179 and describes in his book of court gossip what he saw. ‘We saw at the council Waldensians, simple and unlettered people, named after their leader Valdes, a citizen of Lyon on the Rhône, who presented to the lord pope a book written in the French tongue containing the text and a gloss of the Psalter and of many books of both Testaments.’ Map was told to examine them. He says he took them aside before an audience of learned men and asked whether they believed in God the Father; yes; the Son; yes; the Holy Spirit; yes; and in the Mother of Christ? Yes — and the room, he writes, ‘broke out in laughter’, because they had not understood that the question was a trap. He describes them as going about two by two, barefoot, in wool, owning nothing, ‘following naked the naked Christ’, and adds that they were now beginning humbly ‘because they cannot get a foothold; if we let them in they will throw us out.’ Map’s sneer preserves the only eyewitness description of the first Waldensians, and it records — meaning to mock — that they had a Bible in French, went barefoot, believed everything the church believed, and were turned away.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Around 1173 a rich merchant of Lyon named Valdes gave away his property. He had parts of Scripture put into the language people actually spoke, and he began preaching in the streets. His confession of faith was wholly orthodox. He was condemned in 1184 only for preaching without a license. Driven from the cities, the movement held on in the Alpine valleys between France and Italy. It lived through three and a half centuries of persecution there, with its own traveling ministers and its own books. In 1532 its leaders met the Reformers at Chanforan and joined the Reformed churches. That carried a line of protest from twelfth-century Lyon into the Reformation. Scholars disagree, though, about how unbroken that line was.

Legacy

The Waldensian church is still here. It joined the Reformed churches at Chanforan in 1532, and it lives on as the Waldensian Evangelical Church, based in the same valleys of Piedmont. It is one of the very few medieval movements condemned by Rome that never died out. It never had to be pieced together from its enemies' files. Its long habit of Scripture in the people's own tongue, and of preaching by lay people, came before the Reformation and then joined it.

Sources

Doctrinal Floor Note

No plain-reading doctrinal question - discipline, not doctrine, was the charge

How Sure the Record Is

Hostile-source problem early (inquisition registers), genuine internal literature later

  • Valdes's Profession of Faith (1180) and the early Waldensian dossier, trans. in Wakefield & Evans, Heresies of the High Middle Ages (Columbia, 1969; repr. 1991) (primary) — The founder's own orthodox confession - evidence the charge was discipline, unauthorized preaching, not doctrine.
  • Durand of Osca, Liber Antiheresis (c. 1190s; ed. Selge, 1967) (primary) — The earliest inside theological text, from the first generation; its author later reconciled with Rome, so how representative it is remains debated. Available only in Latin; no English translation exists.
  • Bernard Gui, Inquisitor's Guide, Waldensian section (trans. Janet Shirley, 2006), plus 13th-c. inquisition registers (primary) — Written by the persecutors: how they described Waldensian practice. The depositions preserve ordinary believers' voices that no inside text records.
  • Euan Cameron, Waldenses: Rejections of Holy Church in Medieval Europe (Blackwell, 2000) (secondary) — Source-critical standard; warns against reading later Waldensian identity back into the 1170s.
  • Gabriel Audisio, The Waldensian Dissent: Persecution and Survival, c.1170-c.1570 (CUP, 1999) (secondary) — Covers the survival arc down to the 1532 union with the Reformed tradition.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 4, ch. 12 'Christians and heretics' (Biller); cf. ch. 3 (Kienzle) (reference) — Biller is the leading Waldensian specialist.
