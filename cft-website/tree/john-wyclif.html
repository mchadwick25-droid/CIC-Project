The Late Medieval Era · c. 1360-1384 · Oxford and Lutterworth, England

England, today in United Kingdom

John Wyclif

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Lollardy.

Known for
Oxford doctor of theology and parish priest of Lutterworth. His teaching was denounced in papal letters of 1377 and condemned by a London synod in 1382. He denied that the bread of the Mass ceases to be bread. His followers were called Lollards, and his circle made the English Bible that bears his name.
Also called
John Wycliffe, John de Wycliffe, Johannes Wyclif, Wiclif, Wicleff, Wickliff
Dates
Born in 1324, his biographers said, as Robert Vaughan, who edited his tracts in 1845, reports. Josiah Forshall and Frederic Madden, the 1850 editors of the Wycliffite Bible, report the same. Walter Shirley, who in 1858 edited the Fasciculi Zizaniorum, a Carmelite collection hostile to Wyclif, calls the year a vague conjecture. Master of Balliol College by April 1361, Shirley says. Struck by paralysis on 29 December, Shirley and Vaughan say, he died on the last day of December 1384, as Forshall and Madden date it.
Place
Born near Richmond, in Yorkshire, by Vaughan's account. Oxford, where he was master of Balliol College. Lutterworth in Leicestershire, his parish to the end, after earlier livings at Fylingham and Ludgershall. Bruges, as a royal envoy in 1374. London, for the hearing at St Paul's in 1377.
Role
Oxford scholar and doctor of theology; master of Balliol College; parish priest of Lutterworth; royal envoy; writer in Latin and English

About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In fourteenth-century England, friars said it was heresy to write God's law in English for lay people, everyone who was not clergy. An English tract that goes under John Wyclif's name answers them: "So Christ and his apostles taught the people in the tongue the people knew best. Why should people not do the same now?"

For him, God's law belonged to everyone, in their own tongue, and Scripture was the test of the church's wealth and power. His circle made the first whole Bible in English. A hostile Leicester chronicler of his own day says Wyclif put the Gospel into English himself, for lay people and women who could read. In the chronicler's words, "And so the Gospel's pearl is scattered and trampled by pigs." The Bible's editors of 1850 thought the New Testament was probably his own work, but a later scholar found no real proof, and thought his students made it at his wish.

He taught at Oxford, and from 1374 was priest of Lutterworth, in the English Midlands. He wanted the clergy as poor as the apostles and told the pope to leave worldly lordship to the secular power.

The church taught that at the priest's words the bread of the Mass changed in substance, leaving only its appearance. In 1381 Wyclif held that Christ's body truly is that bread, yet it is still bread. Oxford's ban on his teaching was read out while he sat in the master's chair, arguing the opposite.

In 1382 a London council condemned twenty-four of his conclusions as an earthquake shook the city. Banished from Oxford, he went on writing at Lutterworth until he died there after a stroke, at the end of 1384.

His followers, called Lollards in scorn, carried it on. About 1408 Archbishop Arundel forbade anyone to translate Scripture into English, or read a translation from Wyclif's time, without a bishop's leave. The Bible was copied by hand, not yet printed, and scarce, so a family might own a single gospel. In 1521 the bishop's records show John Grosar examined for an English gospel book, passed to him by a priest, which he lent on. At Iver, old Durdant sent a boy outside, so he could not hear and tell, before reciting passages of Paul and the Gospels to his family.

Long after, in 1526, William Tyndale took up the same work from the Greek and printed it, writing that he had no one's English to follow.

Earlier, in 1415, the Council of Constance declared Wyclif a heretic and ordered his bones dug up. Jan Hus of Prague had built his own book on the Church from Wyclif's. He told that council: "Yet in hope I would wish my soul to be where John Wyclif's soul is."

Quotations are given in our modern English. The printed wording of each is below.

Quotations and references

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “So Christ and his apostles taught the people in the tongue the people knew best. Why should people not do the same now?”
    As printed
    “& j^us crist & his apostlis taujtew fe puple in fat tuwge fat was moost knoAvuw to J^e puple ; why shuldew not mew do non so ?”
    De Officio Pastorali, chapter 15, in The English Works of Wyclif Hitherto Unprinted, ed. F. D. Matthew
    The printed words are the scan's, which garbles the Middle English; read, they are: & þus crist & his apostlis tauȝten þe puple in þat tunge þat was moost knowun to þe puple; why shulden not men do now so? Men in the question means people in general. Authorship of the English tracts rests on editors: Matthew calls this one undoubtedly genuine (2866-2867), and Shirley says an accurate list of his English tracts cannot yet be made (fasciculi-zizaniorum_shirley1858.txt 2612-2616); the brief says the tract goes under his name.
  • In our modern English
    “And so the Gospel's pearl is scattered and trampled by pigs.”
    As printed
    “et sic evangelica margarita spargitur et a porcis conculeatur”
    Chronicon of Henry Knighton, volume 2, ed. Joseph Rawson Lumby
    A hostile witness. A manuscript title names Henry Knighton; Forshall and Madden date the passage only probably before 1400 (wycliffite-bible_forshall-madden1850.txt 870-871), so the brief calls him a chronicler of his own day. The scan reads conculeatur for conculcatur, trampled.
  • In our modern English
    “Yet in hope I would wish my soul to be where John Wyclif's soul is.”
    As printed
    “vellem tamen in spe, quod anima mea esset ibi, ubi est anima Joannis W.”
    Peter of Mladoňovice, account of Hus's hearing at Constance, 7 June 1415, in Documenta Mag. Joannis Hus, ed. František Palacký
    The record abbreviates the name, "Joannis W."; the brief gives the full name, which the passage names just before ("Joannis Wiclef", 15242-15243). Hus says he first spoke these words twelve years before (15243-15245).

Key Works

  • Trialogus: a Latin dialogue. In it he calls the London synod of 1382 the friars' earthquake council, and argues that the bread of the sacrament remains. Gotthard Lechler, who edited it in 1869, holds it was not finished before 1382
  • On the Pastoral Office (De Officio Pastorali): an English tract that F. D. Matthew, who printed it in 1880, calls undoubtedly genuine. The friars say it is heresy to write God's law in English, Wyclif writes. Christ and his apostles, he answers, taught the people in the tongue best known to them
  • Letter to Pope Urban VI: his excuse, in 1384, for not answering the pope's summons to Rome. In the Latin he counsels the pope to leave worldly lordship to the secular power. Thomas Arnold, who edited his Select English Works in 1871, calls the English form a heightened version, probably made by his followers

Legacy

What traces to them

  • His followers were named Lollards, Vaughan writes, by clergy who wished to add to the scorn cast on them.
  • Forshall and Madden write that between 1382 and 1526 the versions of the Wycliffite Bible spread a great amount of scriptural truth.
  • Jan Hus of Prague, in a sermon of probably 1408, hoped his soul would be where Wyclif's soul rests. Herbert Workman and R. Martin Pope report it in their 1904 edition of his letters.
  • David Schaff, who translated Hus's book on the Church in 1915, says Hus took paragraph after paragraph from Wyclif's treatise on the same subject.
  • In May 1415 the Council of Constance declared him a heretic who died in heresy. It ordered his bones dug up, if they could be told from other bodies. In 1418 Pope Martin V condemned his articles again in the bull Inter cunctas.

How they're remembered

  • William Thorpe, a priest, was examined in 1407 by Thomas Arundel, archbishop of Canterbury. He told Arundel that Wyclif "was holden of full many men, the greatest Clerk" they then knew. Alfred Pollard, who printed the Examination in modern spelling in 1903, glosses "Clerk" as "Divine", a scholar of theology.
  • The Leicester chronicle under Henry Knighton's name, hostile to him, still calls him the most eminent doctor of theology in those days. In philosophy, it says, he was reckoned second to none.
  • Thomas Walsingham, a monk of St Albans hostile to him, calls him the devil's instrument and the church's enemy. He tells his stroke as God's judgment.
  • Vaughan says his teaching on the sole authority of Scripture earned him the name Gospel Doctor. Gotthard Lechler reports a manuscript of the Trialogus that calls him the gospel doctor of the English nation.
  • John Foxe told the story in his book of the martyrs, in Latin in 1559 and in English in 1563, his 1837 editors note. He says the bones were burned forty-one years after Wyclif's death. Yet the word of God, Foxe writes, "they could not burn".
  • Walter Shirley writes, "If considered only as the father of English prose, the great reformer might claim more reverential treatment at our hands."

What Is Uncertain

  • Whose Bible it was. Forshall and Madden thought the New Testament probably his, from its style. An early note gives the first Old Testament translation to Nicholas Hereford, his helper. That copy breaks off in Baruch. The editors guess Hereford stopped when summoned to the synod of 1382. They think another hand finished it, not improbably Wyclif's. They call it undeniable that John Purvey, who lived with Wyclif in his later years, made the later revision. Alfred Pollard, in 1903, finds the Purvey case lamentably weak. He says Hereford's books ran as far as Daniel. Historians confess, he writes, that there is substantially no evidence the rest was Wyclif's. He thinks other disciples at Oxford more likely finished it. The Knighton chronicle, hostile, says Wyclif himself turned the Gospel into English.
  • How he was struck. Walter Shirley says the stroke came as he heard Mass in his parish church. Vaughan reports only that he is said to have been giving out the bread. Thomas Walsingham, hostile, says God struck him as, it was said, he meant to preach against Saint Thomas of Canterbury on his feast. Walsingham adds, from those present, that he died in despair.
  • When he was born. Vaughan and the 1850 editors give 1324 as his biographers' year. Shirley traces it to a vague conjecture of an earlier biographer, Lewis. F. D. Matthew, in 1880, calls the date and place alike uncertain and places Wyclif's birth about 1320. Shirley thinks Wyclif's own writings suggest a greater age.
  • When he received Lutterworth. Shirley, citing the patent roll for 7 April, and the 1850 editors give April 1374, before Bruges. Vaughan puts it about November 1375.
  • Whether he had a part in the rising of 1381. The Knighton chronicle calls John Ball, a priest among the rebels, his forerunner. The Carmelite collection says Ball confessed at St Albans to two years as Wyclif's disciple. Walsingham says many blamed the bishops' slackness toward Wyclif and his followers. Shirley says Ball confessed, or was said to have confessed. Vaughan rejects the charge that Wyclif was an agent of the rising.
  • Who wrote the Leicester chronicle. A manuscript title names Henry Knighton, of Leicester Abbey. Shirley calls it the so-called Knighton and pseudo-Knighton. He says its chronicler did not understand the Oxford hearings he reports.
  • The date and aim of the St Paul's hearing. Vaughan says the hearing was set for 19 February 1377. Shirley says Wyclif appeared on 23 February. The 1850 editors say he came to answer charges of false doctrine. Shirley holds the true aim was to humble John of Gaunt, duke of Lancaster.
  • When the papal letters were issued. Shirley says Pope Gregory XI issued the bulls on 30 May 1377. The 1850 editors date them 11 June. Vaughan says only that they were sent in June.
  • Whether he faced the synod of 1382. Shirley says he was never summoned before it. The 1850 editors report Knighton's claim that he appeared and gave a profession on the sacrament. They doubt it, since the archbishop's register does not name him.
  • His early Oxford years. Vaughan and the 1850 editors place him at Queen's College and Merton, and make him warden of Canterbury Hall from 1365. Shirley finds no record of his entry at Queen's. He gives the Merton fellow and the Canterbury Hall warden to another man of the same name.
  • When and how his bones were burned. The Council of Constance ordered them dug up in 1415. Foxe says they were burned forty-one years after his burial at Lutterworth, and the ashes thrown into the river. His heading for the decree gives A.D. 1425. In Foxe's own English, the council's decree orders the bones dug up and thrown far from any church burial. It says nothing of burning.
