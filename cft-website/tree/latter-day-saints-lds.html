The Missionary Era · 1830-1906 · N. America

N. America, today in United States

Latter-day Saints (LDS)

New York to the Great Basin, 1830 to 1906 — a new scripture, a church driven west across a continent, and a desert territory built very largely from scratch.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:18

Read along

In 1830 a young man in western New York named Joseph Smith published the Book of Mormon — which he said he had translated from golden plates shown to him by an angel — and on 6 April that year organized a church around it. Its members were driven out of Ohio, then Missouri, then Illinois, where a mob killed Smith in the Carthage jail in 1844. Under Brigham Young most of them crossed the plains from 1847 and settled the Salt Lake valley, laying out towns, irrigation systems and eventually a territory largely of their own making. The nineteenth-century church practiced plural marriage openly from 1852, which brought decades of federal prosecution before its president formally renounced the practice in 1890 and Utah entered the Union six years later. Its members documented themselves relentlessly: the Relief Society's minute book, Eliza R. Snow's poems and trail diaries, and Emmeline B. Wells's newspaper make up one of the fullest records any nineteenth-century American religious community left behind.

Documented Stories

Haun's Mill

The Latter-day Saints had come to Missouri from 1831 to build their Zion and had been driven from county to county; by 1838 they were concentrated in Caldwell County, and the old settlers, alarmed by their numbers and their bloc voting, had formed militias. Skirmishes through October 1838, some begun by the Saints' own Danites, led Governor Lilburn Boggs to order on 27 October that the Mormons 'must be treated as enemies, and must be exterminated or driven from the State if necessary for the public peace'. Haun's Mill was a settlement of some thirty families on Shoal Creek, whose people, against Joseph Smith's advice to gather to Far West, had stayed and agreed a truce with the local militia. On the afternoon of 30 October some 240 militiamen rode in. The men and boys ran to the blacksmith's shop; the militia fired through the gaps in its log walls and then went inside. Seventeen Saints were killed and thirteen wounded. Among the dead were a boy of ten, Sardius Smith, shot in the head by a militiaman who said afterwards that nits make lice, and an old man hacked with a corn-knife after he surrendered. The women, who had fled to the woods, came back in the dark and, with no men left to dig, put the bodies down a dry well and covered them. No one was ever prosecuted. Two days later the Saints' leaders surrendered at Far West, and the church left Missouri that winter for Illinois.

The Upper Room of the Red Brick Store

In the spring of 1842 Sarah Kimball and her seamstress proposed a society of Nauvoo women to sew shirts for the temple workmen, and Eliza R. Snow drafted a constitution and took it to Joseph Smith. He said the women should have something better: he would organize them after the pattern of the priesthood. On Thursday 17 March 1842 twenty women met with him, John Taylor and Willard Richards in the upper room of Smith's red brick store. Emma Smith, his wife, was elected president; Snow was appointed secretary and began the minute book that afternoon. Smith told them, according to her record, that the society was to relieve the poor and to save souls, and, in the sentences the society has quoted since, that he turned the key to them in the name of the Lord, and that knowledge and intelligence would flow down from that time. The Female Relief Society of Nauvoo grew to over a thousand members in two years and heard Smith preach to it six times; Emma used it in 1844 to oppose plural marriage, which her husband was then practicing and denying, and it was suspended after his death and not revived until 1867, in Utah, under Snow. The minute book, in Snow's hand, was kept, hidden, copied, and finally published in full by the church in 2016. It is the founding record of the largest women's organization in the Latter-day Saint world and the place where the argument about women and priesthood began.

Mountain Meadows

In the summer of 1857 the Latter-day Saints in Utah were preparing for war. President Buchanan had sent an army to install a non-Mormon governor, Brigham Young had declared martial law, and the memory of Missouri and of Carthage was preached from the stands. The Baker–Fancher train, some hundred and forty Arkansas emigrants bound for California, camped in early September at Mountain Meadows in the south-west. What happened is known from the participants' later confessions, the federal investigations and the church's own study of its archives. Local militia leaders, Isaac Haight and John D. Lee among them, decided to destroy the train, recruited Paiute allies, and attacked on 7 September; when the emigrants held out for four days, Lee approached under a white flag, promised safe passage if they surrendered their arms, and walked them out in a column, the men last, each beside a militiaman. At a signal the militiamen shot the men, and the others were killed by militiamen and Paiutes. About a hundred and twenty died. Seventeen children under seven were spared and later returned to Arkansas. The killers blamed the Paiutes and swore silence. A letter from Young ordering the train left alone arrived two days late. Lee alone was tried, convicted in 1876, and executed by firing squad at the meadows in 1877, saying he had been made a scapegoat. In 2007 the church expressed profound regret for the part its members and local leaders had played, and in 2008 published the study.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 1830, Joseph Smith published the Book of Mormon, which he said he had translated from golden plates shown to him by an angel, and organized a church around it in western New York. Driven out of Ohio, Missouri, and Illinois, where a mob killed Smith in 1844, his followers crossed the plains under Brigham Young from 1847 and built the Salt Lake settlements largely from scratch. The nineteenth-century church practiced plural marriage openly from 1852 until its president renounced it in 1890, a history its members documented in extraordinary detail, from the Relief Society's minute book to Eliza R. Snow's trail diaries.

Legacy

It is a large and growing worldwide church, headquartered in Salt Lake City, with millions of members on every continent and one of the most extensive volunteer missionary programs of any religious body. Its genealogical archives, built for its own theological reasons, have become a resource used by family historians of every background in the world. And its settlement pattern is still legible on the ground: grid-plan towns, irrigation canals and ward meetinghouses across the American Intermountain West.

Experience It Today

Sources

On the Nicene Creed

Latter-day Saints hold scriptures beyond the Bible — the Book of Mormon (1830) and the Doctrine and Covenants — and teach about God in terms that differ plainly from the historic creeds. That is the movement's own account of itself, stated here as description rather than as a judgment of it or its members. A second and separate question is how far the church is defined by Joseph Smith's own person and revelations.

How Sure the Record Is

Rich own record

  • The founding texts: the Book of Mormon (1830), Doctrine and Covenants, Pearl of Great Price (primary) — The founding texts, in the movement's own words — a factual description of its confession, not a value judgment on it.
  • Joseph Smith's own record: the Joseph Smith Papers (church-published critical edition, 2008-); the First Vision accounts (1832/1835/1838 versions); History of the Church (primary) — The First Vision accounts vary across their 1832, 1835 and 1838 versions.
  • The members' record, women's voices leading: the Nauvoo Relief Society Minute Book (1842); Eliza R. Snow's poems, discourses, and trail diaries; Emmeline B. Wells's Woman's Exponent (1872-); pioneer trail diaries (primary) — The movement's ordinary and women's self-record at depth. Relief Society minutes published in the church's own critical edition.
  • E. D. Howe, Mormonism Unvailed (1834, with the Hurlbut affidavits); John C. Bennett's expose (1842); anti-polygamy literature and federal prosecution records (1870s-80s) (primary) — A hostile, ex-member source; its affidavits were collected under legally coercive circumstances.
  • Richard Bushman, Joseph Smith: Rough Stone Rolling (Knopf, 2005), with Jan Shipps, Mormonism: The Story of a New Religious Tradition (Illinois, 1985) (secondary) — A believing scholar's account, paired with a sympathetic outside view.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 8, ch. 22 'Christian America' and 'Christian Canada' (Noll) (reference)
