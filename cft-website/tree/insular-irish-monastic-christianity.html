The Age of Monks and Empires · c. 450-664 · Ireland, Iona, Northumbria

Ireland · Iona, today in United Kingdom and beyond

Insular / Irish Monastic Christianity

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Ireland was never Roman, and the church that took root there in the fifth century was built to match. It grew up around monasteries and their abbots, not city bishops, and monastic families and founder-saints were the anchors of whole regions. Two short letters by Patrick survive: one a defense of his own life, the other a furious protest because his converts had been taken as slaves. They are the only voices from the beginning that we can be sure are genuine, and they are startlingly personal. Two things this church made were taken up by the wider church. One was peregrinatio, leaving home for good for God's sake. It sent Columba to Iona in 563, and Columbanus into Gaul and Italy, where he founded Luxeuil and Bobbio and argued with Frankish kings. The other was the penitentials, handbooks that set out the penance for each kind of sin. Its own way of reckoning Easter, and its own style of tonsure, in time clashed with Roman practice. At Whitby in 664 the Northumbrian king ruled against the Irish reckoning.

In Its Own Record

Gave Rise To

  • Anglo-Saxon Christianity Documented Major — Bede reports that King Oswald asked the Irish for a bishop, and they sent him Aidan, a monk of Iona, in 635. Bede adds that the English were taught the rules of monastic life by their Irish masters. The Roman mission of 597 was a second, separate source of English Christianity.

Continued As

  • Medieval Irish Christianity Documented Major — After the Synod of Whitby in 664, Bishop Colman led his Irish monks out of Lindisfarne. Bede says he built a house for them on a small island off the west coast of Ireland. About 703 Adamnan, abbot of Columba's monastery on Iona, preached in Ireland. In his Life of Columba he names the abbots who came before him.

Influenced By

  • Desert Monasticism Inferential-Thin — An Irish monk on the Continent wrote in the eighth century, mixing fact with legend. He traced the Irish daily prayers back to Antony, Paul, Macarius and other early hermits. Warren edited the Bangor Antiphonary in 1895. He judged this one late account sound at its core but wrong in detail. He held that any Eastern features reached Ireland through Gaul.
  • Gallic Monastic-Ascetic Christianity Inferential-Thin — One Irish account of the eighth century names Cassian and the monks of Lérins in the chain that reached Ireland. Beyond that, there is only Patrick's wish, in his Confession, to visit the brothers in Gaul. Warren edited the Bangor Antiphonary in 1895. He held that Ireland's Eastern features came by way of Gaul.
  • British and Welsh Christianity Documented — Around 600 the Irish monk Columbanus wrote a letter to Pope Gregory. In it he cited Gildas, the British monk and writer. He said that a writer named Vennianus had asked Gildas about monks who left their monastery. Gildas, he said, had given him a most elegant reply.

Influenced

  • Merovingian Gallic Christianity Documented — Writing around 640, Jonas of Bobbio tells how Columbanus sailed from Ireland and set up Luxeuil in Gaul. Local nobles then set up houses for monks under his rule. Among them were Donatus, bishop of Besançon, and Dado at Rebais. His book of penances, which he wrote on the Continent, set the model for later Frankish ones.
  • Ecumenical Community Traditions - Taize & Iona Inferential-Thin — Bede, writing in the eighth century, says Columba's successors still held the island of Iona. In 1938 George MacLeod founded the Iona Community there and set about rebuilding its abbey. No source shows what it drew from Columba's monks, so this rests on place alone.

Argued Against

  • The Roman Church in the Age of Gregory the Great Documented — Around 600 the Irish monk Columbanus wrote from Luxeuil to Pope Gregory. He stood up for the Irish date of Easter and asked why Gregory kept a dark Easter. Irish scholars, he said, had never taken up the Easter tables of Victorius. No reply from Gregory survives.

Opposed By

  • Anglo-Saxon Christianity Documented Major — At Whitby in 664, Bede says, the English priest Wilfrid argued against the Easter kept by Bishop Colman and his Irish clergy. He said Rome, Italy, Gaul, Africa, Asia and Egypt kept the Roman date, and he called the other side obstinate. King Oswy then chose to follow Peter, who held the keys of heaven.Lasting result: In 664 the king and all at the debate decided to follow the Catholic Easter. Bishop Colman went back to Scotland with those who would not.

Sources

  • Patrick, Confessio and Epistola ad milites Corotici (ed. Bieler; multiple translations; confessio.ie) (primary) — The only two certainly authentic voices from fifth-century Ireland; Royal Irish Academy's confessio.ie gives text, translations and manuscript images free online.
  • Columbanus, Letters, Rules and Penitential (ed./trans. G. S. M. Walker, Sancti Columbani Opera, 1957) (primary) — The Irish peregrinus's own words c.590-615, incl. his monastic rules; Latin with facing English (Scriptores Latini Hiberniae 2); reprinted, and texts online via CELT.
  • Adomnan, Life of St Columba (trans. Richard Sharpe, 1995) (primary) — Iona's own account of its founder (d. 597), written c.700 - close but retrospective, and needs careful critical reading as hagiography; excellent annotated Penguin translation.
  • The Irish Penitentials (ed./trans. Ludwig Bieler, 1963) (primary) — Finnian, Cumméan and kin - the tariffed-penance documents that show pastoral practice on the ground; Latin/English (Scriptores Latini Hiberniae 5); library access.
  • T. M. Charles-Edwards, Early Christian Ireland (2000) (secondary) — The standard scholarly synthesis for the whole window incl. Iona and the paschal controversy to 664; Cambridge UP paperback.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity, vol. 2, ch. 2, 'Germanic and Celtic Christianities' (Knut Schaferdiek) (reference) — Reference-tradition chapter covering Celtic (incl. Irish/British) Christianity to c.600.
