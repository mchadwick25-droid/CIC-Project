Insular / Irish Monastic Christianity

Ireland, Iona and Northumbria, c. 450 to 664 CE — a Christianity that grew up outside the Roman Empire, organized itself around abbots instead of cities, and then sent its monks back across Europe as voluntary exiles.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:18

Read along

Ireland was never Roman, and the church that took root there in the fifth century was built to match. It grew up around monasteries and their abbots, not city bishops, and monastic families and founder-saints were the anchors of whole regions. Two short letters by Patrick survive: one a defense of his own life, the other a furious protest because his converts had been taken as slaves. They are the only voices from the beginning that we can be sure are genuine, and they are startlingly personal. Two things this church made were taken up by the wider church. One was peregrinatio, leaving home for good for God's sake. It sent Columba to Iona in 563, and Columbanus into Gaul and Italy, where he founded Luxeuil and Bobbio and argued with Frankish kings. The other was the penitentials, handbooks that set out the penance for each kind of sin. Its own way of reckoning Easter, and its own style of tonsure, in time clashed with Roman practice. At Whitby in 664 the Northumbrian king ruled against the Irish reckoning.

Documented Stories

Patrick Excommunicates a Warlord

Patrick’s Letter to the Soldiers of Coroticus is one of two things he wrote that survive, and it was written in anger within days of the event. He had baptized a large group of converts — ‘the day after they were anointed with chrism, still in their white garments, the fragrance still on their foreheads’ — and Coroticus’ raiders, whom Patrick identifies as fellow Christians and fellow Romans, fell on them, killed the men, and took the women and boys to sell to the Picts and the Irish. Patrick had sent a priest he had trained from boyhood with a letter asking for the captives back; the soldiers laughed at him. So Patrick wrote to everyone. He declares Coroticus and his men excommunicate — no one is to eat or drink with them or take their alms until they repent and free ‘the servants of God and the baptised handmaids of Christ’. He defends himself against the charge, which he plainly expects, that a man of low birth and poor Latin has no standing to judge a king: ‘I am a bishop, appointed by God in Ireland’, and it was God who took him, a captive at sixteen, and sent him back to the people who had enslaved him. He asks that the letter be read aloud everywhere, ‘even in the presence of Coroticus himself’. It is the earliest document of the Irish church, and it is a bishop taking the side of Irish slaves against a Christian king.

Columbanus Refuses to Bless the Bastards

Columbanus had been in Burgundy twenty years, had founded Luxeuil and two other houses, and had a reputation for saying what he thought to kings when Brunhild, the old queen who ruled through her grandson Theuderic II, sent for him. Jonas of Bobbio, who joined the community three years after Columbanus’ death and wrote from the monks who had been there, tells the scene. Brunhild brought out Theuderic’s four sons by his concubines and asked the abbot to bless them. He refused: they were ‘born of adultery’ and would never be kings. Brunhild, Jonas says, was enraged — she preferred concubines to a queen who might displace her — and the court began to harass the monastery, forbidding the monks to leave the enclosure and the people to give them alms. Columbanus went to the king’s villa at Époisses to protest, refused to enter, refused the food sent out to him — the dishes, Jonas says, shattered — and rebuked Theuderic for his concubines. The king came to Luxeuil and tried to enter the monks’ quarters; Columbanus barred him; Theuderic said he would not make him a martyr, only send him back where he came from. Soldiers took Columbanus to Nantes for a ship to Ireland. What the record shows without the vindication is a foreign monk who would not let a royal household set the terms of its own marriages, and a church in Gaul that mostly did.

Colmán Takes the Bones Home

Bede describes the synod at Whitby as a debate: Colmán, bishop of Lindisfarne, arguing for the Irish Easter from the authority of Columba and of John the apostle; Wilfrid arguing for the Roman date from Peter and the universal church; and King Oswiu ruling that he would not contradict the keeper of heaven’s keys. Colmán, ‘seeing his doctrine rejected and his party despised’, gave up his bishopric rather than change the practice he had received from his elders, and went home. He took with him the Irish monks of Lindisfarne, and also about thirty of the English who had been trained there and chose the Irish side; and he took, Bede says, some of the bones of Aidan, the founder of Lindisfarne, leaving the rest in the church. At Iona he found the community divided over the same question, and went on west to found a monastery on Inishbofin off the Mayo coast for his mixed community — and then, when the Irish and English monks quarreled over the harvest (the Irish went wandering in summer and came back to eat what the English had reaped), a second house on the mainland for the English alone. Bede, who thought Colmán wrong about Easter, praises without reserve the poverty and simplicity of his rule at Lindisfarne, where the bishop kept no wealth and the king came with five or six attendants and left after prayers.

Connections

In Its Own Record

A thinly documented line from Gallic asceticism, and through Gaul from that of the East. One of the two roots of Anglo-Saxon Christianity.

Direct descendants

  • gave rise to Anglo-Saxon Christianity Documented Major — Bede reports that King Oswald asked the Irish for a bishop, and they sent him Aidan, a monk of Iona, in 635. Bede adds that the English were taught the rules of monastic life by their Irish masters. The Roman mission of 597 was a second, separate source of English Christianity.
  • continued as Medieval Irish Christianity Documented Major — After the Synod of Whitby in 664, Bishop Colman led his Irish monks out of Lindisfarne. Bede says he built a house for them on a small island off the west coast of Ireland. About 703 Adamnan, abbot of Columba's monastery on Iona, preached in Ireland. In his Life of Columba he names the abbots who came before him.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced Desert Monasticism Inferential-Thin — An Irish monk on the Continent wrote in the eighth century, mixing fact with legend. He traced the Irish daily prayers back to Antony, Paul, Macarius and other early hermits. Warren edited the Bangor Antiphonary in 1895. He judged this one late account sound at its core but wrong in detail. He held that any Eastern features reached Ireland through Gaul.
  • influenced Gallic Monastic-Ascetic Christianity Inferential-Thin — One Irish account of the eighth century names Cassian and the monks of Lérins in the chain that reached Ireland. Beyond that, there is only Patrick's wish, in his Confession, to visit the brothers in Gaul. Warren edited the Bangor Antiphonary in 1895. He held that Ireland's Eastern features came by way of Gaul.
  • influenced British and Welsh Christianity Documented — Around 600 the Irish monk Columbanus wrote a letter to Pope Gregory the Great. In it he cited Gildas, the British monk and writer. He said that a writer named Vennianus had asked Gildas about monks who left their monastery. Gildas, he said, had given him a most elegant reply.

Influenced

  • influenced Merovingian Gallic Christianity Documented — Writing around 640, Jonas of Bobbio tells how Columbanus sailed from Ireland and set up Luxeuil in Gaul. Local nobles then set up houses for monks under his rule. Among them were Donatus, bishop of Besançon, and Dado at Rebais. His book of penances, which he wrote on the Continent, set the model for later Frankish ones.
  • influenced Ecumenical Community Traditions - Taize & Iona Inferential-Thin — Bede, writing in the eighth century, says Columba's successors still held the island of Iona. In 1938 George MacLeod founded the Iona Community there and set about rebuilding its abbey. No source shows what it drew from Columba's monks, so this rests on place alone.

Argued Against

  • argued against The Roman Church in the Age of Gregory the Great Documented — Around 600 the Irish monk Columbanus wrote from Luxeuil to Pope Gregory the Great. He stood up for the Irish date of Easter and asked why Gregory kept a dark Easter. Irish scholars, he said, had never taken up the Easter tables of Victorius. No reply from Gregory survives.

Opposed By

  • argued against Anglo-Saxon Christianity Documented Major — At Whitby in 664, Bede says, the English priest Wilfrid argued against the Easter kept by Bishop Colman and his Irish clergy. He said Rome, Italy, Gaul, Africa, Asia and Egypt kept the Roman date, and he called the other side obstinate. King Oswy then chose to follow Peter, who held the keys of heaven.Lasting result: In 664 the king and all at the debate decided to follow the Catholic Easter. Bishop Colman went back to Scotland with those who would not.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Ireland was never part of the Roman Empire, and its church grew up organized around monasteries and abbots rather than city bishops. Patrick's own two surviving letters, a defense of his life and a protest against the enslavement of his converts, are strikingly personal, and the practice of permanent voluntary exile it produced sent Columba to Iona and Columbanus deep into Gaul and Italy.

Legacy

Irish monks left their mark far beyond Ireland. Columbanus founded Luxeuil in Gaul and Bobbio in Italy, and nobles in Gaul set up many more houses under his rule. While on the Continent he also wrote a handbook of penances, and it set the model for a long line of Frankish ones. Monks trained at Iona carried the faith into Northumbria and beyond. Irish scribes made the insular style of manuscript, which reaches its height in the Book of Kells. The high crosses and round towers of Irish monastic sites rank among the best-known Christian landmarks in the world. People still come to Iona, Clonmacnoise and Glendalough, and on Iona a religious community lives and works once more.

Experience It Today

  • Iona Abbey, Isle of Iona, Scotland — on the site Columba founded in 563; the medieval abbey church is restored and in use, and the island is still a place of pilgrimage

How Sure the Record Is

Moderate: Patrick's own two writings; Columbanus's rules; penitentials; later hagiography needs discipline

  • Patrick, Confessio and Epistola ad milites Corotici (ed. Bieler; multiple translations; confessio.ie) (primary) — The only two certainly authentic voices from fifth-century Ireland; Royal Irish Academy's confessio.ie gives text, translations and manuscript images free online.
  • Columbanus, Letters, Rules and Penitential (ed./trans. G. S. M. Walker, Sancti Columbani Opera, 1957) (primary) — The Irish peregrinus's own words c.590-615, incl. his monastic rules; Latin with facing English (Scriptores Latini Hiberniae 2); reprinted, and texts online via CELT.
  • Adomnan, Life of St Columba (trans. Richard Sharpe, 1995) (primary) — Iona's own account of its founder (d. 597), written c.700 - close but retrospective, and needs careful critical reading as hagiography; excellent annotated Penguin translation.
  • The Irish Penitentials (ed./trans. Ludwig Bieler, 1963) (primary) — Finnian, Cumméan and kin - the tariffed-penance documents that show pastoral practice on the ground; Latin/English (Scriptores Latini Hiberniae 5); library access.
  • T. M. Charles-Edwards, Early Christian Ireland (2000) (secondary) — The standard scholarly synthesis for the whole window incl. Iona and the paschal controversy to 664; Cambridge UP paperback.
