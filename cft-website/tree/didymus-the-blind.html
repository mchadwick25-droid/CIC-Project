Didymus the Blind

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition.

Known for
A blind teacher of the church's school at Alexandria, who answered the Arians, admired Origen, and taught Jerome of Strido and Rufinus of Aquileia.
Also called
Didymus of Alexandria
Dates
Died aged 85, by the account of Palladius of Helenopolis. Jerome of Strido, writing his Lives of Illustrious Men, says he was still living and "has already passed his eighty-third year."

About This Person

Antony the hermit once came down from the desert to Alexandria to help its bishop, Athanasius, against the Arians. A blind scholar of Alexandria named Didymus came to see him. Jerome of Strido tells the story and places it in his own childhood. They talked of Scripture, then Antony asked whether Didymus missed his eyes. At first Didymus was ashamed to answer. Asked a third time, he said it was a great grief to him. Antony told him a wise man should not grieve for "a faculty which he shares with ants and flies and gnats." Socrates of Constantinople, Sozomen and Rufinus of Aquileia tell the same saying, each listing different animals.

Didymus, called the Blind, taught in that city. Palladius of Helenopolis visited him four times over ten years. Didymus told him he had lost his sight at four. He had never learned to read, Palladius says. Rufinus, who heard him speak, says he listened while others read. When the readers fell asleep, he went over it all again, like a clean animal chewing the cud.

Rufinus says he became teacher of the church's school at Alexandria, with Athanasius's approval. Socrates says he was seen as "the great bulwark of the true faith" against the Arians. Jerome put his book on the Holy Spirit into Latin.

Didymus admired Origen. Socrates says he wrote on Origen's book First Principles. Those who attack Origen, Didymus wrote, cannot grasp "the profound wisdom of that extraordinary man." In the sixth century, the historian Evagrius Scholasticus reports, the emperor Justinian condemned Didymus along with Origen.

Jerome had studied with Didymus at Alexandria. In the later quarrel over Origen, he was blamed for calling him master. He answered that a pupil may call an old and learned man master. He had much to thank him for, Jerome wrote, "for what I did not know I learned from him, and what I knew already I did not forget."

Key Works

  • On the Trinity, three books: Socrates of Constantinople names three books on the Trinity; the Library holds Mingarelli's 1769 edition, printed from one manuscript as Didymus's. Its Greek column did not survive the scan, and his Latin translation is usable
  • On the Holy Spirit: which Jerome of Strido says he translated into Latin; Jerome's Latin is not held in this Library
  • Commentaries on Origen's First Principles: which Socrates says defended Origen; not held

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Rufinus of Aquileia says he became teacher of the church's school at Alexandria, approved by Athanasius, and that he himself heard him speak.
  • Jerome of Strido studied with him at Alexandria and called him master: "for what I did not know I learned from him, and what I knew already I did not forget."

How they're remembered

  • Socrates of Constantinople says he was then regarded as "the great bulwark of the true faith" against the Arians.
  • Sozomen says many came to Alexandria "for the express purpose of hearing, or, at least, of seeing him."
  • Evagrius Scholasticus, writing in the sixth century, reports that the emperor Justinian condemned Origen, Didymus and Evagrius.

What Is Uncertain

  • The story of Antony and Didymus is told by four writers with differences. Jerome of Strido has Antony speak of "ants and flies and gnats", Socrates of Constantinople of "gnats and flies", Sozomen of "rats, mice, and the lowest animals", and Rufinus of Aquileia of mice, flies and lizards. Sozomen gives it as what "is related", and Socrates as what "is said".
  • His years are not fixed. Palladius says he died at 85. Ernest Cushing Richardson's note to Jerome gives about 311 to 396. Blomfield Jackson's note to Theodoret has him flourishing from about 309 to 399.
  • His praise of Origen is known from Socrates, who quotes it. The commentaries Socrates names are not held.
  • Sozomen reports, as what "is said", that he learned his letters from carved tablets felt with his fingers.
  • Evagrius Scholasticus names the three condemned men but gives no year in the passage.
