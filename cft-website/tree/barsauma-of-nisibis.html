Barsauma of Nisibis

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Church of the East under Persia.

Known for
Bishop of Nisibis on the Persian frontier, who held a synod at Beth Lapat against the catholicos Babai, then retracted it in letters to Babai's successor Acacius of Seleucia-Ctesiphon, and who welcomed Narsai of Nisibis and helped him found the school of Nisibis.
Also called
Bar Sauma, Barsumas
Dates
At the school of Edessa still in 449 and expelled in 457, by Jean-Baptiste Chabot's note; bishop through King Peroz, by Chabot, or from 435, by the later writers Addai Scher cites; the synod of Beth Lapat in April 484 and the reconciliation in August 485, by Chabot's reckoning. Chabot says his death year is unknown and came after 491
Place
Edessa; Nisibis; Beth Lapat; Beth Edrai
Role
Bishop of Nisibis

About This Person

Barsauma, bishop of Nisibis, wrote to the catholicos Acacius of Seleucia-Ctesiphon to take back what he had done. Satan had stirred up a quarrel between him and Babai, the catholicos before Acacius, he confessed. Bishops whom Babai meant to judge had come to him, and he had given way to them. They held a synod at Beth Lapat and made canons out of human passion. Now he called that synod contrary to Christianity.

Nisibis was a Persian city on the Roman frontier. Jean-Baptiste Chabot, who edited the synods of the Church of the East, dates Beth Lapat to April 484. He says Barsauma was first a slave of Mare of Qardu and studied at the school of Edessa. Expelled from Edessa in 457, Chabot says, he became bishop through the favor of King Peroz. Addai Scher, another editor, cites Elias and Ebedjesu of Nisibis for an earlier date, 435.

In his second letter, he told Acacius why he could not come. Drought had lasted two years, he wrote, and tribes subject to Persia had raided Roman villages. He persuaded a Roman general to come to Nisibis to meet the Persian governor, and the raiders struck while they feasted. The Romans blamed Barsauma.

Later writers told his story against him. Bar Hebraeus, a Syriac Orthodox chronicler of the 1200s, says Barsauma accused Babai to Peroz as a Roman spy, and Peroz had Babai killed. Then, he says, Barsauma led Persian soldiers against those who refused his teaching, and he repeats a report that 7,700 died. Chabot calls Bar Hebraeus very inaccurate here. Mari ibn Sulayman, a writer of the Church of the East in the 1100s, also tells of the accusation. He passes on a report that Barsauma soon regretted it.

Mari says Barsauma married a nun and made marriage lawful for bishops and priests. Bar Hebraeus calls the woman a concubine. Barhadbeshabba 'Arbaia, a historian of Barsauma's own church, praises his quick mind. He tells how Barsauma welcomed Narsai of Nisibis, then a teacher at the school of Edessa, and helped him found a school at Nisibis.

Chabot dates his death only to after 491. Bar Hebraeus gives two reports. In one, nuns of Tur Abdin killed him with the keys of their cells. In the other, his tomb was shown at Nisibis.

In his third letter, Barsauma told Acacius that Nisibis was troubled and tossed like a stormy sea.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The compiler of the Synodicon orientale left the synod of Beth Lapat out of the synods of the Fathers, because Barsauma himself had annulled it; the compiler kept his letters of retraction instead.
  • R. H. Connolly, translating Narsai, writes that the founding of the school of Nisibis by Narsai and his friend Barsauma, bishop of the city, began the formation of "what is now known as the Nestorian Church".

How they're remembered

  • Barhadbeshabba 'Arbaia, of his own church, praises his quick mind; the Cause of the Foundation of the Schools, by Barhadbeshabba, bishop of Halwan, says he "composa lui aussi beaucoup d'homélies".
  • Bar Hebraeus, a hostile Syriac Orthodox chronicler, calls him blasphemous and charges him with Babai's death and with killing those who refused his teaching.
  • Jean-Baptiste Chabot regrets the loss of the rest of the letters "du fougueux évêque de Nisibe", the fiery bishop of Nisibis.

What Is Uncertain

  • Much of his story comes from writers of other churches or centuries later. Bar Hebraeus, a Syriac Orthodox chronicler of the 1200s, is hostile, and Chabot calls him very inaccurate on Barsauma. Mari ibn Sulayman wrote in the 1100s. Barhadbeshabba 'Arbaia, of Barsauma's own church, is friendly. His own six letters are the nearest witness.
  • When he became bishop is disputed. Chabot puts it after his expulsion from Edessa in 457; Abbeloos and Lamy, editing Bar Hebraeus, cite Simeon of Beth Arsham for the same. Addai Scher cites Elias and Ebedjesu of Nisibis for 435, and Abbeloos and Lamy note a Vatican manuscript with that date.
  • That he caused Babai's death rests on later writers. Bar Hebraeus says so, and Chabot says Bar Hebraeus took the story, without naming it, from the letter of Maruta of Tagrit, written from oral tradition more than a century and a half later. Mari ibn Sulayman tells of the accusation and a report that Barsauma soon regretted it. 'Amr says Barsauma sent Babai's letter to the king unopened and the physician Gabriel twisted it. Barsauma's own letters say only that Satan stirred up a quarrel between them.
  • His marriage is told two ways. Mari says he married a nun and made marriage lawful for bishops and priests. Bar Hebraeus calls her a concubine kept in his cell.
  • That he spread his teaching by force with the king's troops is Chabot's reading of the later writers, and Chabot hedges his remark that the king made him governor of the city. The count of 7,700 dead is a report Bar Hebraeus repeats.
  • The quarrel with Narsai over Barsauma's wife is told by Barhadbeshabba 'Arbaia, and later by Mari and Bar Hebraeus. All say the two were reconciled.
  • Chabot says the year of his death is unknown, after 491. Bar Hebraeus gives two reports: that nuns of Tur Abdin killed him with the keys of their cells, or that his tomb was shown in the church of Mar Jacob at Nisibis.
  • Not the archimandrite Barsauma at Ephesus in 449 and Chalcedon, nor Barsauma of Susa.
