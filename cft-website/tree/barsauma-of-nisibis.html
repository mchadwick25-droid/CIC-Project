Barsauma of Nisibis

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Church of the East under Persia.

Known for
Bishop of Nisibis on the Persian frontier, who held a synod at Beth Lapat against the catholicos Babowai, then retracted it in letters to Babowai's successor Acacius of Seleucia-Ctesiphon, and who welcomed Narsai of Nisibis and helped him found the school of Nisibis.
Also called
Bar Sauma, Barsumas
Dates
At the school of Edessa still in 449 and expelled in 457, by Jean-Baptiste Chabot's note; bishop through King Peroz, by Chabot, or from 435, by the later writers Addai Scher cites; the synod of Beth Lapat in April 484 and the reconciliation in August 485, by Chabot's reckoning. Chabot says his death year is unknown and came after 491
Place
Edessa; Nisibis; Beth Lapat; Beth Edrai
Role

About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

The Church of the East gathered its synods, its councils of bishops, into one book. Its compiler, about three centuries later, left one out on purpose. Barsauma had held it at Beth Lapat, and he himself had said it was held "out of ill will and human passion."

Barsauma was bishop of Nisibis, a city on the border between the Roman and Persian empires, in the late 400s. His church, the church of the Persian empire, had governed itself for sixty years; its synods now fixed its teaching and rules. Barsauma and his side stood for two things: ministers free to marry, and the Greek Bible teacher Theodore of Mopsuestia as the church's sure guide. In 484 he led a synod against the catholicos, the head of his church, and later took it back in writing. Yet his church made both causes its law, while neighboring churches remembered him as the man who set it apart.

At Nisibis he welcomed the teacher Narsai and asked him to found a school, which counts them both as its founders. By his own account, "Satan stirred up a quarrel" between him and the head of the church, Babowai. At Beth Lapat, in April 484, he and the bishops on his side drew up a written case against Babowai and made rules on ministers' marriage. That year the Persian king put Babowai to death. A later historian of Barsauma's own church says Barsauma passed Babowai's letter to the king and then regretted it. Writers of the rival Syriac church say he had him killed. His own letters speak only of the quarrel.

His marriage is told two ways. The same historian says the king later ordered him to take a wife, and he married a nun, a woman vowed to a single life. A rival writer, later still, says instead that he kept a woman and called her his wife. Another charges he made the marriage rule so he would not be blamed for her.

The year after the synod, with Acacius as the new head of the church, Barsauma took back everything done at Beth Lapat, its rules included. His letters to Acacius, the nearest witness to his life, show a frontier pressed by drought, raiding tribes and a Roman army. He asked Acacius to cut his city's rebels off from the church, "like the doctor who amputates," and to threaten to report them to the king.

A synod early the next year, which he stayed away from but accepted in advance, confessed Christ fully God and fully human, the two never mixed. It sent wandering men in monks' black, whom it called frauds, back to the monasteries. It barred bishops from stopping their ministers marrying, and expected new assistant ministers to take a wife.

From 491, he and Acacius were cutting each other off from the church.

In 497 a synod let every minister, from the head of the church down, marry one wife openly. It accepted Beth Lapat "but only on this point."

The Armenian bishops refused the rules that Acacius and Barsauma had set up. Writers of the rival Syriac church drew on a letter written from old men's memories about a century and a half later. They said he told the Persian king his Christians would never be loyal until their faith differed from the Romans'. With the king's soldiers, they said, he killed thousands who refused.

More than a century after Beth Lapat, a synod made Theodore's writings required study for every teacher in the church. It quoted Beth Lapat's ruling on Theodore, and called the synod left out of the book "the divine assembly." It named him with the title Mar, which means my lord. To those bishops he was "the blessed Mar Barsauma."

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The compiler of the Synodicon orientale left the synod of Beth Lapat out of the synods of the Fathers, because Barsauma himself had annulled it; the compiler kept his letters of retraction instead.
  • R. H. Connolly, translating Narsai, writes that the founding of the school of Nisibis by Narsai and his friend Barsauma, bishop of the city, began the formation of "what is now known as the Nestorian Church".

How they're remembered

  • Barhadbeshabba 'Arbaia, of his own church, praises his quick mind; the Cause of the Foundation of the Schools, by Barhadbeshabba, bishop of Halwan, says he "composa lui aussi beaucoup d'homélies".
  • Bar Hebraeus, a hostile Syriac Orthodox chronicler, calls him blasphemous and charges him with Babowai's death and with killing those who refused his teaching.
  • Jean-Baptiste Chabot regrets the loss of the rest of the letters "du fougueux évêque de Nisibe", the fiery bishop of Nisibis.

What Is Uncertain

  • Much of his story comes from writers of other churches or centuries later. Bar Hebraeus, a Syriac Orthodox chronicler of the 1200s, is hostile, and Chabot calls him very inaccurate on Barsauma. Mari ibn Sulayman wrote in the 1100s. Barhadbeshabba 'Arbaia, of Barsauma's own church, is friendly. His own six letters are the nearest witness.
  • When he became bishop is disputed. Chabot puts it after his expulsion from Edessa in 457; Abbeloos and Lamy, editing Bar Hebraeus, cite Simeon of Beth Arsham for the same. Addai Scher cites Elias and Ebedjesu of Nisibis for 435, and Abbeloos and Lamy note a Vatican manuscript with that date.
  • That he caused Babowai's death rests on later writers. Bar Hebraeus says so, and Chabot says Bar Hebraeus took the story, without naming it, from the letter of Maruta of Tagrit, written from oral tradition more than a century and a half later. Mari ibn Sulayman tells of the accusation and a report that Barsauma soon regretted it. 'Amr says Barsauma sent Babowai's letter to the king unopened and the physician Gabriel twisted it. Barsauma's own letters say only that Satan stirred up a quarrel between them.
  • His marriage is told two ways. Mari says he married a nun and made marriage lawful for bishops and priests. Bar Hebraeus calls her a concubine kept in his cell.
  • That he spread his teaching by force with the king's troops is Chabot's reading of the later writers, and Chabot hedges his remark that the king made him governor of the city. The count of 7,700 dead is a report Bar Hebraeus repeats.
  • The quarrel with Narsai over Barsauma's wife is told by Barhadbeshabba 'Arbaia, and later by Mari and Bar Hebraeus. All say the two were reconciled.
  • Chabot says the year of his death is unknown, after 491. Bar Hebraeus gives two reports: that nuns of Tur Abdin killed him with the keys of their cells, or that his tomb was shown in the church of Mar Jacob at Nisibis.
  • Not the archimandrite Barsauma at Ephesus in 449 and Chalcedon, nor Barsauma of Susa.
