The Social Gospel

Urban, industrial North America, c. 1886 to 1918 — the rupture's social-reform side, from Gladden's Applied Christianity and Rauschenbusch's Hell's Kitchen years to the Social Creed of 1908, ending with Rauschenbusch's death and the war's blow to the movement's optimism, its stream absorbed into the mainline's own agencies.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:03

Read along

In 1886 Washington Gladden published Applied Christianity, and in the same year Walter Rauschenbusch took a pastorate on the edge of Hell's Kitchen in New York, where what he saw of tenement life reorganized his theology. He and others formed the Brotherhood of the Kingdom in 1892. His Christianity and the Social Crisis appeared in 1907 and went through thirteen printings in five years, and A Theology for the Social Gospel followed in 1917. Charles Sheldon's novel In His Steps (1896) put the same instinct into a form anyone could use: asking, before each decision, what Jesus would do. In 1908 the newly formed Federal Council of Churches adopted the Social Creed of the Churches — the movement's clearest institutional mark, and the ancestor of the denominational social statements that came after it.

Documented Stories

The Children's Funerals

Walter Rauschenbusch was twenty-four when he became pastor of the Second German Baptist Church on West 45th Street in New York in 1886, a congregation of working immigrants on the edge of the district called Hell's Kitchen. He had come to save souls, he later said, and found himself burying children: the tenements had among the highest infant mortality in the city, and he conducted the funerals. 'Oh, the children's funerals!' he wrote afterwards; 'they gripped my heart.' He began to ask why the children died, and the answer led him to wages, housing and the men who owned them. In 1888 an illness left him almost entirely deaf, so that for the rest of his life he followed conversation by lip-reading and an ear trumpet, and preached without hearing his own voice. In 1892 he and a few friends formed the Brotherhood of the Kingdom, meeting each summer to think through what Jesus's phrase 'the kingdom of God' meant for a city. He left the pastorate in 1897 to teach at Rochester Theological Seminary. On sabbatical in Germany in 1907 he finished a manuscript he had been writing for years, Christianity and the Social Crisis, arguing that the prophets and Jesus had spoken about society and that the church had forgotten it; he expected, he said, that publishing it would end his career. Instead it went through thirteen printings in five years and made him the most read theologian in America.

The Newspaper Jesus Would Edit

Charles Sheldon, pastor of Central Congregational Church in Topeka, Kansas, had written In His Steps (1896) as a serial to be read aloud at his Sunday evening services, a chapter a week, the story of a congregation that pledges to ask before every decision what Jesus would do. It had sold in the millions, largely because a defective copyright let any publisher print it. In 1900 the owner of the Topeka Daily Capital offered Sheldon the editorship for one week on the novel's terms. From 13 to 20 March 1900 Sheldon ran the paper. He refused advertisements for liquor, tobacco, patent medicines; he dropped the prize-fight reports, the society gossip and the crime detail; he put the famine then killing millions in India on the front page every day; he printed the Sunday edition on Saturday evening so that no one would have to work on Sunday; and he required, and got, a sober newsroom. Subscriptions for the week came in from across the United States and Britain, and the print run rose from about eleven thousand to more than three hundred and sixty thousand. Newspapermen mocked it as dull, and some of it was. When the week ended the Capital returned to its usual practice, and Sheldon to his pulpit and to Tennesseetown, Topeka's Black neighborhood, where he had founded a kindergarten. He said afterwards that he had not expected to change journalism, only to show that the question could be asked of any job.

In Its Own Record

This was the social-reform wing of the same era, beginning with Washington Gladden in 1886, Rauschenbusch's pastorate in Hell's Kitchen from that same year, and Charles Sheldon's novel in 1896. Its core period ran from 1907, when Christianity and the Social Crisis appeared, to Rauschenbusch's death in 1918. The Federal Council of Churches' 1908 Social Creed anchors it to the mainline Protestant tradition that later absorbed its stream into its own social agencies. Reinhold Niebuhr's 1932 critique challenged the movement's optimism from within that same broader current.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 1886 Washington Gladden published Applied Christianity the same year Walter Rauschenbusch took a pastorate on the edge of New York's Hell's Kitchen, an experience that turned his theology toward social reform; his Christianity and the Social Crisis (1907) went through thirteen printings in five years. Charles Sheldon's 1896 novel In His Steps put the same impulse into a question anyone could ask before acting — what would Jesus do — while the Federal Council of Churches adopted the movement's own Social Creed in 1908. Rauschenbusch's death in 1918 and the war's blow to reform-minded optimism effectively ended the movement, though its stream flowed on into the mainline denominations' own social agencies.

Legacy

The movement itself ended. Rauschenbusch died in 1918, and the war took the confidence out of a theology that expected the kingdom to arrive through reform. Reinhold Niebuhr's Moral Man and Immoral Society (1932) later made the criticism stick. But the stream did not stop. It was absorbed into the mainline churches' own social agencies, and the mainline century that followed took up its cause. The Social Creed of 1908 stands behind the church social statements that came after it. And In His Steps left something odder and longer-lasting. The question 'what would Jesus do' was still being asked a hundred years after the novel that put it there.

Doctrinal Floor Note

The Social Gospel's defining claim was about the Kingdom's social scope — how far Christian faith obligates believers toward social reform — not a dispute over the doctrine of God. 'What would Jesus do?' is treated here as a devotional reading practice within the movement, not as a separate movement of its own.
