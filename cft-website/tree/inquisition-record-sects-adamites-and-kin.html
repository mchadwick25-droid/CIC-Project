The Late Medieval Era · 14th-15th c. · Central Europe

Inquisition-Record 'Sects' (Adamites and kin)

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Some of the 'sects' in the medieval record are known only from the documents that condemned them. Take the Bohemian Adamites of 1421. They are described as a naked band on an island in southern Bohemia, who shared everything and waited for the end of the world. They appear in the chronicles of people who despised them, and in the account of a later outside observer. The Taborites destroyed them, so no voice from inside survives at all. The wider 'heresy of the Free Spirit' works in much the same way. A list of errors condemned at the Council of Vienne passed among inquisitors as a template. Much of what later questioning 'found' matches that template closely. Since Robert Lerner's work in 1972, historians have concluded that a good deal of it was made by the questioning rather than found by it. Some of these people were certainly real, and some of what they were accused of may well have been true. But the evidence is one-sided. It was forced out of people, and shaped in advance by the questions.

In Its Own Record

Opposed By

  • Taborite Radicalism Documented — The Hussite writer Laurence of Březová tells of a band of men and women cast out of Tabor for the Picard heresy. They settled on an island near Veselí. He says Jan Žižka, the blind captain of the Taborites, sent their articles to Prague after they were killed. Lützow's 1914 history says Žižka wiped them out almost at once.
  • The Latin Papal Church (Avignon to Fifth Lateran) Documented — In December 1367 at Erfurt, a beghard called John Hartmann was questioned by Walter Kerling, named by the pope's authority to try heresy. Kerling put to him the first article of Ad nostrum, a decree of the Council of Vienne. In 1887 Wilhelm Wattenbach noted that the questions kept to the decree point by point. Such answers, he wrote, were often forced. But he judged that Hartmann's answers went far beyond the questions, and that the man seemed to believe them.

Sources

  • The Bohemian 'Adamites' of 1421: accounts in Lawrence of Brezova's chronicle and later in Aeneas Sylvius; partial English via Fudge's Hussite-crusade volume (primary) — Hostile, and doubly filtered: a community exterminated by Zizka's own Taborites — radicals describing the radicals they killed. No inside voice survives.
  • Free Spirit-pattern interrogations: John of Bruenn's deposition; the Schweidnitz beguine interrogations (1332) (primary) — A hostile, coerced source: answers shaped by the questioners' own template for what heresy should look like — the same pattern seen in other Free Spirit cases.
  • The heresiological template itself: Ad nostrum (Vienne, 1312; trans. in Tanner, Decrees of the Ecumenical Councils, 1990) and circulating inquisitors' error-lists (primary) — A hostile construction: the template document that could generate a 'sect' on paper, whether or not one existed in practice.
  • Robert E. Lerner, The Heresy of the Free Spirit in the Later Middle Ages (California, 1972) (secondary) — The scholarly consensus: such "sects" are substantially constructs of the persecutors' own template. For the Adamite case, modern Hussite scholarship (Kaminsky; Fudge) weighs how much reality lay under the atrocity stories.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 4, ch. 23 'Repression and power' (Arnold); cf. ch. 12 'Christians and heretics' (Biller) (reference) — A close fit with the repression chapter: the absence of any inside voice is the central fact these sources show, since no self-standing account from within these groups survives.
