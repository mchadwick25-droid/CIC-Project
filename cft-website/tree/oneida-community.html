Oneida Community

Upstate New York, 1848 to 1881 — a community that believed sin was already over, abolished marriage among themselves, and finally voted itself into a cutlery company.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:00

Read along

John Humphrey Noyes taught that a Christian could be wholly free of sin in this life, and in 1848 he gathered a community at Oneida in upstate New York to live as though the kingdom had already arrived. They held property in common, replaced marriage with what they called complex marriage — in which every member was in principle married to every other. Submitted to a formal system of group criticism, and from 1869 ran a selective breeding program they named stirpiculture. They also worked hard and paid their way, manufacturing animal traps, silk thread and eventually tableware. Outside campaigning and internal strain finished it. Noyes left for Canada in 1879, and in 1880 the community voted itself out of existence, reorganizing on New Year's Day 1881 as a joint-stock company.

Documented Stories

Tirzah Miller's Diary

Tirzah Miller was born in 1843 into the Oneida Community, the daughter of John Humphrey Noyes's sister, and grew up in the Mansion House, where property, work, meals and, after childhood, sexual relations were held in common under Noyes's doctrine that exclusive love was selfishness. She edited the community's paper and was among the women chosen for stirpiculture, the program begun in 1869 in which committees, and above all Noyes, decided which men and women should have children together. Her diary, kept privately and published only in 2000, is the fullest inside account of what that meant. It records her belief in the community and her uncle, her pride in her work, and the practice by which the young were introduced to sexual life by the old, which she does not question. It records, too, Noyes directing her to men she did not love and away from men she did: her passion for Edward Inslee, a musician, which Noyes judged exclusive and ordered ended, and which ended when Inslee left the community in 1877; her pregnancies as assignments; her sessions of mutual criticism; and her exhaustion. She bore three children in the experiment. When the community dissolved in 1880 she married a member and lived until 1902 in Kenwood, among the same people, now in separate houses. Her diary does not renounce anything. It shows, from inside, a woman who believed and was used, and her editor has let it say both.

The Founder Crosses to Canada

By 1879 the Oneida Community was prosperous, manufacturing traps, silk and spoons, and under attack. A campaign led by John Mears, a Hamilton College professor, had gathered the clergy of central New York to demand that the state act against a community whose marriage system, they said, was licensed adultery and whose initiation of girls by its older men, Noyes first among them, was the corruption of minors; inside the community younger men had begun to question Noyes's authority. In the night of 22–23 June 1879, warned that a warrant for his arrest was being prepared, Noyes, sixty-seven and nearly deaf, left the Mansion House and went by train to the Canadian side of Niagara Falls. He did not come back. In August, from Canada, he wrote to the community proposing that complex marriage be given up, not as wrong but as no longer expedient, and on 28 August 1879 the community announced that it had ended; within a year most of the adults had paired off in conventional marriages, and the children were assigned to parents. The economic community held longer, and then it too was voted away: on 1 January 1881 the Oneida Community became the Oneida Community, Limited, a joint-stock company whose shares were divided among the members by years of service and property brought in. Noyes lived at the Falls until his death in 1886. The company made silverware for a century. The community's own paper printed the announcement of August 1879 without mentioning the warrant.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 1848, John Humphrey Noyes gathered a community at Oneida in upstate New York to live as though sin were already conquered, replacing marriage with what they called complex marriage and submitting to a formal system of group criticism. They also worked hard at manufacturing, making animal traps, silk thread, and eventually tableware, to pay their way. Under outside pressure Noyes left for Canada in 1879, and in 1881 the community voted itself out of existence and became a joint-stock company instead.

Legacy

As a religious body it left nothing — no successor community, no continuing church, no doctrinal descendants. As a business it left a great deal. Oneida Limited became one of the best-known tableware manufacturers in the world, and its flatware sat on ordinary tables for generations without the faintest hint of where it came from. The Mansion House the community built still stands and is a National Historic Landmark, run as a museum and inn.

Experience It Today

  • Oneida Community Mansion House, Oneida, New York — the community's own building, now a museum with period rooms, guided tours and lodging

Sources

On the Nicene Creed

Oneida diverged from historic Christian teaching on two counts, both stated in its founder's own published writings: John Humphrey Noyes taught that his followers were already free of sin, and he reorganized marriage and sexual life around that claim under his own personal authority. The community also settled the question of its own continuity — in 1881 it dissolved itself and became a joint-stock company.
