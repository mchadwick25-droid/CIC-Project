The Age of Monks and Empires · 6th or early 7th c. · Sinai

Sinai, today in Egypt

John Climacus

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Chalcedonian Monasticism - Judean Desert & Gaza.

Known for
Monk and abbot of Mount Sinai who wrote the Ladder, a guide to the monk's life in thirty steps. He wrote it at the request of John, abbot of Raithu. Philippe Labbe says he was called Climacus after the book.
Also called
Joannes Climacus, Joannes Sinaita, Joannes Scholasticus
Dates
No source used here gives a firm year of his birth or death. Philippe Labbe says he lived about 580. He also argues that he died between 560 and 565, if a Sinai monk's tale, as Antonius Magister retells it, is true. The Bollandists find a death about 580, or at least near the end of the sixth century, likely. James Barmby reports that he is said to have died early in the seventh century. Labbe gives 30 March as the day he died, from the Greek Menaea and the Roman Martyrology. The Bollandists say the Greeks keep his memory on that day
Place
Mount Sinai. Daniel of Raithu says he came there at sixteen, lived alone at Tholas, five miles from the church, and was made head of the monastery. Daniel cannot say where he was born
Role
Monk, hermit and abbot of the monastery on Mount Sinai; writer of the Ladder

About This Person

John, abbot of the monastery of Raithu, wrote to the abbot of Mount Sinai. He asked him to write down what he had seen on that mountain, as Moses once did. Such a book, he wrote, would carry those who strive for heaven up as if by the steps of a ladder. Jacob, only a keeper of sheep, had seen a ladder. A teacher of a flock that can reason, he argued, could show everyone the way to God.

The abbot answered that only the fear of casting off obedience made him take on a task beyond his strength. From his book, the Ladder, he was called John Climacus, from the Greek word for a ladder. Philippe Labbe, writing in the 1600s, places him late in the 500s.

His life comes mainly from a short saint's Life by Daniel, a monk of Raithu, near the Red Sea. At sixteen John gave himself to God on Sinai, under a teacher. When his teacher died, he went to live alone at a place called Tholas, five miles from the church, and stayed forty years.

Daniel tells how John, dozing in his cell, saw a man who rebuked him for sleeping safely while his disciple Moses was in danger. John rose and prayed. That evening Moses said a huge rock had nearly crushed him in his sleep. Later the monks made John abbot by force.

The Ladder climbs in thirty steps, from leaving the world to faith, hope and love. Christ was baptized at about thirty, John writes at its end, and so took the thirtieth step, since God is love.

An unnamed monk of Sinai tells that John, as he died, promised his brother George that George would follow him within the year. Labbe reports a form of this tale tying George's death to that of Peter, patriarch of Jerusalem. If so, he argues, John died between 560 and 565. Yet he says hardly anything about the year is certain. Gregory the Great wrote to a John, abbot of Mount Sinai, in a letter its editors, Paul Ewald and Ludwig Hartmann, date to 600. Gregory's English translator, James Barmby, takes this John to be Climacus, still alive then.

In the 1400s, Thomas à Kempis remembered John Ketel, a cook in the House of Florentius at Deventer. "Sometimes he read the book of John Climacus", Thomas writes. Ketel explained it so well, Thomas says, that wise men marveled.

Key Works

  • The Ladder (Scala Paradisi): thirty steps of the monk's life, from leaving the world to faith, hope and love, written at the request of John, abbot of Raithu. At its end John ties the thirty steps to Christ's age at his baptism
  • Book to the Shepherd (Liber ad Pastorem): a short book on the work of an abbot, which likens him to a shepherd, a ship's master, a doctor and a teacher

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Philippe Labbe says the Greeks held him in great value. The Questions of Athanasius, Elias of Crete, Theodore the Studite, Philip the Solitary and John Zonaras show it, he says. Elias of Crete, he says, wrote explanations of the Ladder.
  • Labbe reports that the Ladder is said to have been first put into Latin in 1295 by Angelo Clareno. He adds that it was printed at Venice in 1590 in the everyday Greek of the time. Franciscan chroniclers of the 1300s and 1400s, quoted by Livarius Oliger, credit the translation to Angelo of Cingoli, whom Oliger identifies as Angelo Clareno. Bernardino of Aquila says the book was held in great repute among his Observant friars.
  • Migne reprints Matthew Rader's Greek and Latin edition of his works. It carries the Life by Daniel of Raithu and the Greek notes on the Ladder.

How they're remembered

  • Thomas à Kempis writes of John Ketel, a cook in the House of Florentius: "Sometimes he read the book of John Climacus". Thomas names the book "On perfect renunciation of the world and mortification of the Will". Ketel "expounded therefrom sentiments full of spiritual truth", Thomas says, so that wise men marveled at how well he understood the book.
  • Cardinal Baronius, as Rader and the Bollandists report him, says a divine likeness of his soul is seen far more clearly in his writings.
  • Jean Gerson found things in the Ladder to fault. Rader answers that nothing in the whole of Climacus is false or against the church's decrees, though much is misunderstood.

What Is Uncertain

  • His life comes mainly from the short Life by Daniel, a monk of Raithu. The Bollandists say Daniel wrote it soon after John's death, while John, abbot of Raithu, still lived. It is a saint's Life, with a vision and a cure by prayer. Daniel says he cannot tell for sure where John was born or how he was raised. More tales come from an unnamed monk of Sinai, printed after the Life. Matthew Rader and the Bollandists call him a man of John's own time.
  • His years. Philippe Labbe places him about 580. He reports the reckoning of Antonius Magister, a French translator of the Ladder: born about 525, a monk at sixteen, dead in 605 or 606. Labbe says the sums do not add up. He then turns to the tale as Antonius Magister retells it, which brings in the death of Peter, patriarch of Jerusalem. From Peter's years in Theophanes, Evagrius and Nicephorus, he reckons that if the tale is true John died between 560 and 565. The tale as Rader and the Bollandists print it does not name Peter. He concludes that hardly anything certain can be said of the year of his death. Still less, he says, can be said of his entry into the monastery, his time in the desert or when he became abbot. The Bollandists judge Labbe's 580 likely, or at least the end of the sixth century. James Barmby reports that he is said to have been elected abbot at seventy-five and to have died early in the seventh century. Matthew Rader says Robert Bellarmine was right to place him in 530.
  • Pope Gregory the Great's letter to John, abbot of Mount Sinai. Gregory sent fifteen cloaks, thirty rachanae, and fifteen beds for a house for the aged built there by one Isaurus, in Barmby's English. Barmby says rachanae may mean woolen garments, or blankets. Paul Ewald and Ludwig Hartmann date the letter 1 September 600 and point to Rader's edition for Climacus's life. Barmby says the John addressed is "No doubt" Climacus. Labbe's reckoning would put John's death at least thirty-five years before the letter.
  • His early years on Sinai. Rader's Latin of Daniel has him take up the life alone as he began his twentieth year. Rader also prints a short summary of the Life. It says he went to Tholas nineteen years after he came to Sinai. So does the Menologium of the emperor Basil, as the Bollandists give it. The Sinai monk says Abbot Martyrius made him a monk at twenty; Rader takes Daniel's sixteen as his entry as a novice.
  • His age at death. Labbe says the Greek Menaea make him nearly a hundred when he died, if they are to be trusted.
  • His brother George. In the Sinai monk's tale, John told George he would not finish the year without following him. Rader's Latin and Greek say George died within ten months; the Bollandists' Latin reads ten years. John Moschus tells of a George, abbot on Sinai, who wrote to Peter, patriarch of Jerusalem, that they would both die in six months. Labbe links Moschus's account to the tale. Rader doubts that Moschus's George was John's brother.
  • The time of his life. Johannes Trithemius placed him under the sons of Constantine. Rader, Labbe and the Bollandists reject this, since John wrote after Sabas, who died in 532.
  • Namesakes. He is a different man from John, abbot of Raithu, who asked for the Ladder; from John Moschus; from John the Faster, patriarch of Constantinople, whose Penitential stands in the same Migne volume; and from John the Sabaite, who in the Sinai monk's tale washed his feet. Labbe reports another John, abbot of Sinai, whom manuscripts name as author of the story of Barlaam and Josaphat. Rader holds that Climacus did not write it.
