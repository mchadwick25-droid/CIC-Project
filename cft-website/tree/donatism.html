The Imperial Church Era · c. 311-439 CE · Roman North Africa (Carthage and Numidia)

Donatism

Meet Fidelis on Church in Conversation

About This Movement

Caecilian was elected bishop of Carthage in 311 or 312. The sources support both years, and neither can be ruled out. He was consecrated by Felix of Aptungi. Felix was accused of handing over the Scriptures to be burned in the recent persecution. A rival party would not accept a bishop ordained by the hand of such a traditor. They made Majorinus bishop beside him instead, not in his place, and from Majorinus's successor Donatus the movement took its name. The doctrine at stake was narrow and absolute. A sacrament's validity rises and falls on the giver's own unbroken purity. So a tainted ordination gives nothing, and everyone it touches must be washed again by a hand that can be trusted. This was never a fight over the Trinity or whether Christ was God, for both sides held the same Nicene faith. The quarrel was over who could validly give what the church gives. For a century, across much of Roman Africa, this was simply the larger of the two churches. It had its own bishops, its own basilicas, and its own councils, and it fought its rival for the same towns.

The empire did not stay neutral. In 347, imperial commissioners came to force the two churches into one communion, and they met resistance in Numidia. A bishop named Marculus was seized, paraded through several towns, and thrown from a cliff called Novapetra. His church honored him and others killed with him as martyrs, and its own account of his death survives. A rural movement called the circumcellions appears in Optatus's account of these same years. An imperial law of 412 fined them as a class of their own, so they were a real, legally distinct group. But almost everything said about what they did comes from opponents, who had reason to make them look like a mob. The record will support that they existed and had a name. It will not cleanly support a picture of their character.

This church denied that any emperor had the standing to say which church was the true one. Yet it turned to imperial power three times when that served its case. It appealed to Constantine in 313 and to Julian in 361. In the 390s it turned the same machinery it condemned everywhere else against its own breakaway bishops, the Maximianists. In 411 the emperor finally ordered the whole dispute settled at law. Two hundred and seventy-nine of its bishops met roughly two hundred and eighty-six from the rival church before an imperial judge at Carthage. They refused the chairs he offered them and argued for three days. The verdict went against them, and the laws that followed took their churches by name. The sources say little about this church under the Vandals, but it was still there under Byzantine rule. In 535 Justinian's law for Africa barred Donatists from holding services. The last surviving word on it comes from Pope Gregory the Great. His letters of the 590s pressed for an inquiry into Donatist rebaptism in Numidia.

In Its Own Record

Influenced By

  • Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity Documented — Cyprian of Carthage was a leading African authority in the 250s. Augustine tells the Donatists that they keep quoting Cyprian's letters, his opinion and his council. He asks why they use Cyprian's authority to defend their schism.

Influenced

  • Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity Documented — Tyconius was a Donatist writer of about 380. In On Christian Doctrine, Augustine calls him a Donatist and sets out the seven rules of his Book of Rules. He says they give real help in opening the hidden sense of Scripture.

Argued Against

  • Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity Widely Accepted — Optatus quotes Parmenian, the Donatist bishop of Carthage, saying that a stained minister cannot wash away sins in baptism. Optatus also says the Donatists re-baptized the faithful. The Donatists applied this rule to Catholic clergy.
  • Imperial and Juridical Christianity Widely Accepted — Optatus says that agents of Emperor Constans, Paul and Macarius, came to Donatus. Donatus burst out: 'What has the Emperor to do with the Church?' Optatus opposed Donatus, so this is a hostile report.

Opposed By

  • Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity Documented — Augustine, bishop of Hippo, wrote a long book to the Donatists after the 411 conference at Carthage between their bishops and his. He lists it in his Retractations. Bishop Optatus of Milevis had earlier written against the Donatist bishop Parmenian.
  • Imperial and Juridical Christianity Documented Major — Emperor Constantine granted the Donatists judges, who heard their case at Rome with Miltiades, the city's bishop. Optatus, a Catholic bishop, reports that a Donatist bishop then appealed. Constantine replied: 'Oh, mad daring of their fury!'Lasting result: Constantine gave leave for a council of nineteen bishops to meet at Rome. It found Donatus guilty and cleared his rival, Caecilian.
  • The Byzantine Imperial Church (Justinianic) Documented Major — In 535 the emperor Justinian made a law for Africa, which his armies had won back. It named the Donatists along with Arians, Jews and pagans, and barred them from holding services. In the 590s Pope Gregory the Great wrote to the emperor Maurice. He praised the orders Maurice had given against the Donatists.Lasting result: Justinian banned Donatists, Arians, Jews and pagans from having places to meet. Nor could they hold rites as the church does.
  • The Roman Church in the Age of Gregory the Great Documented — Pope Gregory the Great wrote to Columbus, a Numidian bishop, in the early 590s. He had heard that the Donatists were spreading and baptizing many people a second time. He ordered a council of bishops to look into it.

Broke With

  • Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity Documented Major — Optatus of Milevis was a Catholic bishop. He says the split began when a rival party made Majorinus bishop at Carthage. Yet Caecilian was bishop there by then. Optatus also copies a plea that the Donatist bishops sent to Constantine. In it they say that disputes had come up between them and the rest of the bishops of Africa.

Sources

  • Augustine, On Baptism, Against the Donatists (primary) — Seven books answering the doctrine at its most systematic — the fullest surviving reconstruction of Donatist sacramental logic, built in order to refute it.
  • Optatus of Milevis, Against the Donatists (primary) — The earliest sustained Catholic answer to the schism, written roughly five decades after the founding events it narrates; its own appended dossier of documents is the richest surviving source for the conflict.
  • Augustine, Answer to the Letters of Petilian (primary) — Three books quoting a Donatist bishop's own letters clause by clause in order to answer them — the fullest surviving Donatist voice, reaching us because it was chosen for answerability.
  • Tyconius, The Book of Rules (primary) — The one Donatist theological work that survives entire, by a writer this communion's own council condemned — and the book outlived the party that expelled him.
  • The Passion of Marculus (tradition) — A hagiographic account of the death of Marculus in the Macarian persecution of 347–348; the visionary and miraculous details are carried as this tradition's own testimony, not as verified events.
  • Gesta Collationis Carthaginiensis anno 411 (Acts of the Conference of Carthage) (primary) — The transcript of the great trial at Carthage, taken down by imperial notaries — the one place in the whole surviving record where the Donatist bishops argue their own standing in their own recorded words.
  • Codex Theodosianus, Book 16 (primary) — The imperial legal record, including the 412 fine naming the circumcellions by name and the 405 Edict of Unity that outlawed this communion's own rebaptism.
