The Imperial Church Era · c. 382–420 CE · Rome & Bethlehem

Rome, today in Italy · Bethlehem, West Bank

Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity

Meet Albina on Church in Conversation

About This Movement

In the early 380s, Jerome arrived in Rome. There he joined a circle of ascetic women, serious about Scripture, who already met around the widow Marcella on the Aventine. Paula, another widow, and her daughters Blaesilla and Eustochium belonged to the same circle. All of them took Jerome as their teacher, a caustic, brilliant scholar with a gift for making enemies. In 384 Blaesilla took up the severe fasting Jerome taught, and within four months she was dead. At her funeral, Jerome reports, people in the crowd muttered that the monks should be stoned or thrown in the Tiber. In his letter of comfort to the grieving Paula, he admits that he wrote the whole letter in tears.

Within the year Pope Damasus, Jerome's protector, was also dead, and his position in Rome had become impossible. Paula and Eustochium sold their estates, sailed east, and founded monasteries at Bethlehem — a house for men, houses for women, a hostel for pilgrims — with Jerome installed alongside to work. What came out of it was the Vulgate: a Latin Bible rendered book by book from the Hebrew rather than the Greek everyone was used to, against fierce criticism — in one North African town, a congregation nearly rioted when its bishop read the new wording of a single word in Jonah, and Augustine wrote across the sea to warn Jerome what unsettling a people's prayed-over text could cost.

Not one word that any of these women wrote in her own hand has survived. What is known of them comes only through Jerome's letters, or through others who describe letters now lost. In the community's last years, an attack tied to the Pelagian dispute set fire to the Bethlehem monasteries. Eustochium and her niece Paula sent Rome an account of it. Writing to the bishop of Jerusalem, Pope Innocent praised the "wonderful clemency" with which they had left the attacker's name untold.

In Its Own Record

Influenced By

  • Desert Monasticism Documented — Jerome wrote that the Roman ascetic Marcella learned of Antony and of Pachomius's monasteries from Athanasius and other Alexandrian exiles in Rome. He also described Paula going into the monks' cells at Nitria in Egypt. In Letter 22 he says Antony made the hermit life famous.
  • Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition Documented — In his preface to Job, written in 392, Jerome defends his new translation by pointing to Origen. Origen had marked every Old Testament book with obeli and asterisks to show where the old Greek text fell short.
  • Cappadocian Christianity Documented — Jerome, writing in the 390s, calls Gregory of Nazianzus his instructor in the Scriptures. He recalls asking Gregory to explain a phrase in Luke, and Gregory joked his way out of answering.
  • Latin Christianity in the Danubian Provinces Documented — Jerome lists Victorinus of Pettau in his catalog of Christian writers and judges his works noble in thought but weak in style. In Against Helvidius he cites Victorinus as an authority on the Lord's brothers.
  • The Church of Aquileia Widely Accepted — The translation's editors say Jerome and Rufinus belonged to a company of young ascetics at Aquileia in the early 370s. Rufinus recalled being baptized in a monastery by Chromatius, Jovinus and Eusebius. In 374 Jerome wrote to the same three men from the desert.
  • Tertullian's Voice Documented — Jerome found Tertullian 'packed with meaning' but rough in style. He wrote that Origen should sometimes be read for his learning, 'just as we read Tertullian', taking the good and leaving the bad.
  • The Church of Roman Palestine before Constantine Documented — Jerome says Pamphilus copied most of Origen's works by hand, and that the copies were still in the library at Caesarea. He owned twenty-five volumes in Pamphilus's hand. Writing against Rufinus, he says he found Eusebius's Apology for Origen in that library.
  • The Anatolian Church in the Third Century Documented — In his catalog of Christian writers, Jerome lists the works of Methodius of Olympus, among them the Banquet of the ten virgins. He names the book, but whether he drew on it in his own writing is not shown.
  • Early Palestinian Ascetic Monasticism Documented — Jerome wrote a Life of Hilarion of Gaza at Bethlehem around 390. He says Epiphanius of Salamis knew Hilarion well and had already praised him in a letter. Jerome presents his own book as taking up that work.

Influenced

  • The Tridentine Church Widely Accepted Major — In 1546 the Council of Trent ruled that the old Latin Vulgate was authentic. It was to be the text for public reading and preaching. Its English translator of 1848 says the council chose the Vulgate as Jerome's version. Augustine had said Jerome's work was a translation from the Hebrew.Lasting result: The Council of Trent made the old Latin Vulgate the authentic text for public lectures, debates, sermons and teaching. No one could reject it.

Argued Against

  • Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition Documented — In Letter 84 Jerome calls Origen's doctrines poisonous and says they do violence to the Holy Scriptures. He adds that his own commentaries show he has always opposed them.
  • Ebionite / Nazoraean Current Documented — In a letter to Augustine of 404, Jerome names the Ebionites and a sect he calls the Nazarenes. He says the Nazarenes want to be both Jews and Christians, and so are neither.
  • Homoian ('Arian') Christianity Documented — Jerome wrote his Dialogue against the Luciferians in about 379. In it he blames the Homoian leaders Valens and Ursacius for what was done at the council of Ariminum. There, he says, the Nicene faith was condemned, and the whole world groaned to find itself Arian.

Opposed By

  • Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity Documented — Augustine urged Jerome to translate from the Greek Septuagint, not from the Hebrew. He feared the Latin and Greek churches would end up reading different Bibles. Even after Jerome died, he still held that no translator should be put above the Seventy.
  • Pelagianism Inferential-Thin — In 416 or 417, Jerome's monasteries at Bethlehem were attacked and burned, and his deacon was killed. Augustine wrote that followers of Pelagius were said to have done it. Pope Innocent noted that the women who told him of it had not named the man behind it, though he saw no doubt about who was guilty. So the charge rests on report, not on a named act.

Broke With

  • The Church of Aquileia Documented Major — Jerome and Rufinus had once been close friends. Around 400 they broke for good over Rufinus's Latin translation of Origen. Each wrote a defense against the other. Rufinus said their friendship had been mended only with difficulty. Jerome complained that an old friend he had made peace with now attacked him.

In Contact With

  • Jerusalem Liturgical-Pilgrimage Christianity Documented — Jerome tells how Paula, his patron, worshipped at the tomb of the Resurrection in Jerusalem. Yet he told a friend that heaven was as easy to reach from Britain as from Jerusalem.
  • Imperial and Juridical Christianity Documented — Writing from the East to Damasus of Rome, Jerome said he kept communion only with 'the chair of Peter'. He later recalled helping Damasus with his church letters, answering questions sent by councils of East and West.
  • Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity Documented — Jerome and Augustine wrote to each other across the sea for years. Augustine told Jerome of a bishop whose congregation rose up over the new wording of a line in Jonah. Jerome later wrote that he was resolved to love and respect Augustine and to defend his opinions as his own.

Sources

  • Jerome, Letters (primary) — The correspondence in which Paula, Eustochium and Marcella's questions, funding and insistence are visible on nearly every page; the New Advent collection indexes the surviving letters.
  • Jerome, Life of Paulus (Life of Paul the Hermit) (primary) — Written in 374 or 375 during Jerome's own stay in the Syrian desert, before the Bethlehem years.
  • Palladius of Galatia, Historia Lausiaca, chs. 36 and 41 (primary) — The principal hostile independent near-contemporary witness to this circle — including the one outside account of the Paula-Jerome partnership, which contradicts Jerome's own.
  • J.N.D. Kelly, Jerome: His Life, Writings, and Controversies (secondary) — Harper & Row, 1975 — the standard modern biography, drawn almost entirely from Jerome's own prolific correspondence.
