The Imperial Church Era · c. 382–420 CE · Rome & Bethlehem

Rome, today in Italy · Bethlehem, West Bank

Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity

Conversations with Albina are coming soon to Church in Conversation.

About This Movement

In the early 380s, Jerome arrived in Rome. There he joined a circle of ascetic women, serious about Scripture, who already met around the widow Marcella on the Aventine. Paula, another widow, and her daughters Blaesilla and Eustochium belonged to the same circle. All of them took Jerome as their teacher, a caustic, brilliant scholar with a gift for making enemies. In 384 Blaesilla took up the severe fasting Jerome taught, and within four months she was dead. At her funeral, Jerome reports, people in the crowd muttered that the monks should be stoned or thrown in the Tiber. In his letter of comfort to the grieving Paula, he admits that he wrote the whole letter in tears.

Within the year Pope Damasus, Jerome's protector, was also dead, and his position in Rome had become impossible. Paula and Eustochium sold their estates, sailed east, and founded monasteries at Bethlehem — a house for men, houses for women, a hostel for pilgrims — with Jerome installed alongside to work. What came out of it was the Vulgate: a Latin Bible rendered book by book from the Hebrew rather than the Greek everyone was used to, against fierce criticism — in one North African town, a congregation nearly rioted when its bishop read the new wording of a single word in Jonah, and Augustine wrote across the sea to warn Jerome what unsettling a people's prayed-over text could cost.

Not one word that any of these women wrote in her own hand has survived. What is known of them comes only through Jerome's letters, or through others who describe letters now lost. In the community's last years, an attack tied to the Pelagian dispute set fire to the Bethlehem monasteries. Eustochium and her niece Paula sent Rome an account of it. Writing to the bishop of Jerusalem, Pope Innocent praised the "wonderful clemency" with which they had left the attacker's name untold.

Voices in the Record

  • Jerome was the scholar and translator at the center of it all, and his Latin Bible became the West's standard text for a thousand years. The letters, the memorials, the record of what the women around him said and did — all of it passed through his own hand before it reached us.
  • Paula was a Roman widow who sold her fortune to fund the move to Bethlehem and then built and governed its monasteries and pilgrim hostel for nineteen years, until her death. The one independent witness to describe her partnership with Jerome, the historian Palladius of Helenopolis, says the opposite of Jerome's own account: that Jerome held her back out of jealousy rather than let her surpass him.
  • Eustochium was consecrated to virginity as a teenage girl in Rome, the dedicatee of one of Jerome's most famous letters, and led the Bethlehem women's community for the rest of her life after her mother's death. Palladius of Helenopolis later put the size of her community at fifty women; no other source confirms the number.
  • Marcella's household on the Aventine was gathering ascetic, scripturally serious women years before Jerome ever reached Rome, and he took his place inside her circle, not the other way around. By his own account, clergy still brought her their hardest scriptural questions long after he had left the city — though the only record of that standing is the letter he wrote about her after she died.
  • Rufinus of Aquileia had been Jerome's friend since their student days and translated Origen's own work into Latin. Their bitter public quarrel over Origen's legacy split the wider ascetic network in two, and how much of it was really about doctrine rather than about the two men themselves is a question neither side's own account can settle.
  • Every one of these women demonstrably wrote — Marcella's questions survive by implication in Jerome's dozens of answers, and Eustochium's report of the 416 attack reached a pope who wrote back. None of their own letters were kept. The single text that carries two of their names, a letter to Marcella signed by Paula and Eustochium, is itself judged by scholars to be Jerome's own composition in their names, not theirs.
  • Fabiola was a Roman noblewoman who left a husband whose vices the whole neighborhood knew, refusing to name them even to defend herself, then remarried while he still lived — a step this circle's own teaching held to be exactly as much a fault for a woman as it would have been for a man. When her second husband died she did public penance in sackcloth before all Rome and was restored to communion; afterward she founded what Jerome calls Rome's first hospital, carrying the sick in from the streets herself, and helped found a hostel for travelers at Portus. She never lived at Bethlehem, and everything known of her — the hospital and its 'first' claim included — rests on the single letter Jerome wrote after her death.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Descends scripturally from Alexandria's Origen, the same legacy this circle later fractured over with Rufinus; Jerome served as Damasus of Rome's own secretary before Bethlehem began.

Descended from

No direct parent is recorded.

Direct descendants

No direct descendant is recorded. Its other links are influence, argument and contact.

Broke With

  • broke with The Church of Aquileia Documented Major — Jerome and Rufinus had once been close friends. Around 400 they broke for good over Rufinus's Latin translation of Origen. Each wrote a defense against the other. Rufinus said their friendship had been mended only with difficulty. Jerome complained that an old friend he had made peace with now attacked him.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced Desert Monasticism Documented — Jerome says Marcella, a Roman ascetic, heard of Antony from Athanasius of Alexandria. He and other men in exile from Alexandria told her in Rome. They told her too of the houses for monks that Pachomius set up. Jerome tells how Paula went into the monks' cells at Nitria in Egypt. In Letter 22 he says Antony made the hermit life famous.
  • influenced Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition Documented — In 392 Jerome wrote a preface to his new version of Job. In it he defends his work by pointing to Origen. Origen had marked every Old Testament book with signs called obeli and asterisks. They showed where the old Greek text fell short.
  • influenced Cappadocian Christianity Documented — Jerome, writing in the 390s, calls Gregory of Nazianzus his teacher in the Scriptures. He recalls asking Gregory to explain a phrase in Luke. Gregory made a joke and got out of answering.
  • influenced Latin Christianity in the Danubian Provinces Documented — Jerome lists Victorinus of Pettau in his catalog of Christian writers. He judges his works noble in thought but weak in style. In Against Helvidius he calls on Victorinus as a witness on the Lord's brothers.
  • influenced The Church of Aquileia Widely Accepted — W. H. Fremantle wrote the introduction to Rufinus's works in English in 1892. He says Jerome and Rufinus were part of a group of young ascetics at Aquileia in the early 370s. Rufinus recalled being baptized in a monastery by Chromatius, Jovinus and Eusebius. In 374 Jerome wrote to the same three men from the desert.
  • influenced Tertullian Documented — Jerome found Tertullian 'packed with meaning' but rough in style. He wrote that Origen should sometimes be read for his learning, 'just as we read Tertullian', taking the good and leaving the bad.
  • influenced The Church of Roman Palestine before Constantine Documented — Jerome says Pamphilus copied most of Origen's works by hand. The copies were still kept at Caesarea, in its library. Jerome owned twenty-five books in Pamphilus's own hand. In his book against Rufinus, he says he found the Apology for Origen by Eusebius of Caesarea in that same library.
  • influenced The Anatolian Church in the Third Century Documented — In his catalog of Christian writers, Jerome lists the works of Methodius of Olympus, among them the Banquet of the ten virgins. He names the book, but whether he drew on it in his own writing is not shown.
  • influenced Early Palestinian Ascetic Monasticism Documented — Jerome wrote a Life of Hilarion of Gaza at Bethlehem around 390. He says Epiphanius of Salamis knew Hilarion well and had already praised him in a letter. Jerome presents his own book as taking up that work.

Influenced

  • influenced The Tridentine Church Widely Accepted — In 1546 the Council of Trent ruled that the old Latin Vulgate was authentic. It was to be the text for public reading and preaching. Its English translator of 1848 says the council chose the Vulgate as Jerome's version. Augustine had said Jerome's work was a translation from the Hebrew.

Argued Against

  • argued against Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition Documented — In Letter 84 Jerome calls Origen's teachings poisonous. He says they do violence to the Holy Scriptures. He adds that his own commentaries show he has always opposed them.
  • argued against Ebionite / Nazoraean Current Documented — In a letter to Augustine of 404, Jerome names the Ebionites and a sect he calls the Nazarenes. He says the Nazarenes want to be both Jews and Christians, and so are neither.
  • argued against Homoian ('Arian') Christianity Documented — Jerome wrote his Dialogue against the Luciferians in about 379. In it he blames the Homoian leaders Valens and Ursacius for what was done at the council of Ariminum. There, he says, the Nicene faith was condemned, and the whole world groaned to find itself Arian.
  • argued against The Luciferians Documented — In about 379 Jerome wrote a dialogue against the followers of Lucifer. In it a Luciferian named Helladius debates a churchman. Jerome calls Lucifer a good shepherd. But he took a few sheep away, Jerome says, and left the rest of the flock to the wolves.
  • argued against Jovinian Documented — In 393 Jerome answered Jovinian's book in two books. He says the brothers at Rome sent him the work and asked him to crush it. He quotes Jovinian's points, among them that baptized virgins, widows and wives are of equal merit.

Opposed By

  • argued against Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity Documented — Augustine urged Jerome to translate from the Greek Septuagint, not from the Hebrew. He feared the Latin and Greek churches would end up reading different Bibles. Even after Jerome died, he still held that no translator should be put above the Seventy.
  • argued against Pelagianism Inferential-Thin — In 416 or 417, Jerome's monasteries at Bethlehem were attacked and burned, and his deacon was killed. Augustine wrote that followers of Pelagius were said to have done it. Pope Innocent noted that the women who told him of it had not named the man behind it. Yet he saw no doubt about who was guilty. So the charge rests on report, not on a named act.

In Contact With

  • in contact with Jerusalem Liturgical-Pilgrimage Christianity Documented — Jerome tells how Paula, his patron, worshipped at the tomb of the Resurrection in Jerusalem. Yet he told a friend that heaven was as easy to reach from Britain as from Jerusalem.
  • in contact with Imperial and Juridical Christianity Documented — Writing from the East to Damasus of Rome, Jerome said he kept communion only with 'the chair of Peter'. He later recalled helping Damasus with his church letters, answering questions sent by councils of East and West.
  • in contact with Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity Documented — Jerome and Augustine wrote to each other across the sea for years. Augustine told Jerome of a bishop whose congregation rose up over the new wording of a line in Jonah. Jerome later wrote that he was resolved to love and respect Augustine and to defend his opinions as his own.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

A circle of wealthy Roman women funded and led a community at Bethlehem where Jerome translated the Bible into Latin word by word from the Hebrew - work now known as the Vulgate - though nearly everything known of the women themselves survives only through his own letters.

Legacy

The Vulgate became the Bible of the Latin West for over a thousand years. Medieval theology argued from it, and later translators worked from it or against it. In 1546 the Council of Trent declared it the authentic text. Its rise was not immediate or uncontested. Augustine quoted Jerome's Hebrew version at times, but he never gave it the old version's authority. Writing after Jerome's death, he granted that even the Jews judged the work faithful. Yet, he wrote, 'the churches of Christ judge that no one should be preferred to the authority' of the Seventy translators. In the 590s, Pope Gregory the Great still drew on both the new Latin version and the old.

Its principle mattered as much as its content. Jerome insisted on going back to the Hebrew instead of resting on the Greek. Scholars of the Renaissance and Reformation later made the same kind of appeal to the original languages. In the end, they turned it on the Vulgate itself.

The circle left a second, quieter pattern too — wealthy women funding, hosting, and directing serious biblical scholarship — which recurs constantly in later Christian history. The caves beneath the Church of the Nativity in Bethlehem, where tradition places Jerome's study, are still shown to pilgrims.

Experience It Today

  • Church of the Nativity and the Pilgrimage Route, Bethlehem — the UNESCO World Heritage site whose underground caves include the one long shown as Jerome's study

Sources

Doctrinal Floor Note

Nothing here is in question on the Creed. Jerome was a combative defender of Nicene orthodoxy, and the circle's controversies were about asceticism, Origen's legacy, and the right basis for translating Scripture.

How Sure the Record Is

Richest written record among the Built & Live traditions — but nearly all of it is one man's own hand. Even the one independent witness against him doesn't agree with itself: the same report of Jerome holding Paula back survives in two languages, and they diverge — a single guarded line in the Greek hardens, in a later Syriac translation, into a flat claim that she died young to escape his 'envy and evil-eyedness.'

  • Jerome, Letters (primary) — The correspondence in which Paula, Eustochium and Marcella's questions, funding and insistence are visible on nearly every page; the New Advent collection indexes the surviving letters.
  • Jerome, Life of Paulus (Life of Paul the Hermit) (primary) — Written in 374 or 375 during Jerome's own stay in the Syrian desert, before the Bethlehem years.
  • Palladius of Galatia, Historia Lausiaca, chs. 36 and 41 (primary) — The principal hostile independent near-contemporary witness to this circle — including the one outside account of the Paula-Jerome partnership, which contradicts Jerome's own.
  • J.N.D. Kelly, Jerome: His Life, Writings, and Controversies (secondary) — Harper & Row, 1975 — the standard modern biography, drawn almost entirely from Jerome's own prolific correspondence.
