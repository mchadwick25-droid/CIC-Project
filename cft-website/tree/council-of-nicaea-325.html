The Imperial Church Era · 325 · Nicaea, in Bithynia

Nicaea, today in Turkey

Council of Nicaea

Major council

Known for
The council Constantine called at Nicaea in 325, which condemned Arius, set out the creed that calls the Son of one being with the Father, fixed one day for Easter and made twenty canons.
Dates
325. Socrates of Constantinople gives the 20th of May, from the date on the record of the synod; the copy of the creed read at Chalcedon carries the 19th of June. Charles Joseph Hefele reconstructs an opening on 20 May, the creed on 19 June and a close on 25 August, the last from a manuscript Baronius reported
Rivers
Greek East, Africa, Latin West, Syriac East, Non-Nicene
Called by
The emperor Constantine, by letters inviting bishops from all quarters (Eusebius of Caesarea, Life of Constantine III.6; the council's own letter)

About This Council

The bishops sat in rows on each side of the largest hall in the palace at Nicaea. The emperor Constantine walked in, robed in purple and gold. Eusebius of Caesarea says Constantine did not sit until the bishops "had beckoned to him."

In Alexandria, Bishop Alexander had put his presbyter Arius out of the church. The quarrel spread, and in 325 Constantine called bishops "from all quarters" to settle it.

The council's minutes are lost. Its creed, its rules and its letter to Egypt survive, with accounts by three men who were there. Eusebius admired the emperor and was slow to sign. Eustathius of Antioch and Athanasius of Alexandria, then Alexander's deacon, were Arius's foes. Arius's own letter to Eusebius of Nicomedia survives. Theodoret of Cyrrhus, who wrote against his party, kept it. "We are persecuted," Arius wrote, "because we say that the Son has a beginning." The council's letter says he taught "that there was a time when he was not."

The witnesses count the bishops differently. Eusebius says more than 250, Eustathius about 270, and Athanasius "three hundred more or less." A later generation, Archibald Robertson notes, settled on 318. Most came from the Greek East, and Eusebius says a Persian bishop came too. Ossius came from Spain and Caecilian from Carthage. Two presbyters stood in for the aged bishop of Rome.

In Athanasius's telling, the bishops offered words from Scripture about the Son. Arius's friends accepted each one, but were caught "whispering to each other and winking with their eyes." Eustathius says a statement from Arius's party was torn up "in the sight of all." Eusebius of Caesarea read out his church's creed. The emperor approved it, he says, but asked for one word to be added. The Son was to be called homoousios, of one being with the Father.

The council's letter says two bishops shared Arius's sentence. Constantine sent Arius away. Socrates of Constantinople says Eusebius of Nicomedia and Theognis of Nicaea were exiled too. The council also set one day for Easter everywhere. It limited the power of Melitius of Lycopolis.

A Syriac text says Marutha of Maipherqat translated the council's rules for Isaac, head of the church in Persia. A Persian synod accepted them in 410. In Carthage, nearly a century after Nicaea, bishops still checked the copy of its rules that Caecilian had brought home.

Why They Met

  • In Alexandria, Bishop Alexander put his presbyter Arius and his followers out of the church. Eusebius of Nicomedia and others wrote asking that the sentence be lifted (Socrates of Constantinople I.6).
  • Constantine first sent Ossius of Cordoba with a letter to Alexander and Arius urging peace. Eusebius of Caesarea says the letter failed, and the emperor then called a general council (Socrates I.7; Eusebius, Life of Constantine III.5-6).
  • The question was what the church should say about the Son. The council's letter says Arius taught that the Son was made from things that are not and that there was a time when he was not. Arius's own letter to Eusebius of Nicomedia, kept by Theodoret of Cyrrhus, says he and his friends were persecuted for saying the Son has a beginning (synodal letter, npnf214; Theodoret I.4, npnf203).
  • On one side stood Alexander, with Eustathius of Antioch and Ossius; on the other, Arius's friends, among them Eusebius of Nicomedia, Theognis of Nicaea and Maris of Chalcedon (Socrates I.8; Archibald Robertson, who edited Athanasius, on the parties).
  • Two other questions came before it: the day of Easter, and the split in Egypt left by Melitius of Lycopolis (synodal letter, npnf214).

Who Came

  • More than 250 bishops, says Eusebius of Caesarea, who was there, with presbyters and deacons beyond counting.
  • About 270, says Eustathius of Antioch, who was there, in a passage kept by Theodoret of Cyrrhus. He adds that he did not try hard to count.
  • Three hundred more or less, says Athanasius of Alexandria, who was there as Alexander's deacon. Constantine's letter to the Alexandrians, in Socrates of Constantinople, says more than three hundred.
  • 318 in Socrates and Theodoret of Cyrrhus; about 320 in Sozomen. Archibald Robertson says the figure 318 was adopted by a later generation, perhaps for its meaning; Hefele notes that Ambrose of Milan compared it with Abraham's 318 servants.
  • The subscription lists survive in Latin, Greek, Coptic, Syriac, Arabic and Armenian. Their editors, Gelzer, Hilgenfeld and Cuntz, say they name about 220 bishops and were not copied from the original signatures.
  • Most came from the Greek East. Eusebius names a Persian bishop and a Scythian. From the West came Ossius of Cordoba, Caecilian of Carthage and a few others, with two presbyters for the bishop of Rome.

What They Decided

  • A creed was set out calling the Son begotten, not made, and of one substance (homoousios) with the Father, with anathemas against those who say there was a time when he was not (the creed, from the acts of Ephesus and Chalcedon and Eusebius of Caesarea's letter).
  • Arius and his teaching were anathematized. Secundus of Ptolemais and Theonas of Marmarica received the same sentence (synodal letter).
  • Melitius of Lycopolis kept the bare title of bishop in his own city but could not ordain. Clergy he had ordained were received after a new laying on of hands, below those appointed by Alexander (synodal letter).
  • Easter was to be kept on one day everywhere, as at Rome and Alexandria. Constantine's letter on it speaks of the Jews in hostile terms (synodal letter; Constantine's letter in Eusebius, Life of Constantine III.18-20).
  • Twenty canons were made. Among them, the sixth kept the old rights of Alexandria over Egypt, Libya and Pentapolis, as Rome had over its region, and of Antioch; the eighth set terms for receiving the Cathari (the canons, npnf214).

What Came of It

  • Constantine sent Arius into exile and ordered his books burned, Sozomen says. Socrates of Constantinople says Eusebius of Nicomedia and Theognis of Nicaea were exiled soon after, and were restored to their churches after writing a recantation.
  • Archibald Robertson says the fight over the council's results went on for nearly half a century.
  • Its creed was read again at the councils of Ephesus and Chalcedon, whose acts preserve it.
  • A Syriac text carried under the name of Marutha of Maipherqat says he translated the canons of the 318 from Greek for Isaac, catholicos of Seleucia-Ctesiphon. Oscar Braun, who translated the text, reports that a Persian synod in 410 accepted the canons.
  • Under Pope Zosimus, about ninety years later, the bishops of Africa checked a disputed canon against the copy Caecilian of Carthage had brought home from Nicaea.

Traditions It Touched

Gathered

  • Constantine the Great: Constantine called the bishops to Nicaea and sat with them.
  • Imperial and Juridical Christianity: The emperor called the council and enforced its sentences with exile.
  • Athanasius of Alexandria: Athanasius came as Alexander's deacon and later wrote in defence of the creed.

Condemned

  • Homoian ('Arian') Christianity: The council condemned Arius and the teaching that there was a time when the Son was not.

Affected

  • The Melitian Schism: The council let Melitius keep the bare title of bishop but no power to ordain.
  • Novatianism: Its eighth canon set terms for receiving clergy of the Cathari, the Novatians, back into the church.
  • Persian Church of the East (early): A Persian synod under the catholicos Isaac in 410 accepted the Nicene canons in a Syriac version, as Oscar Braun reports from its acts.

Whose Account We Have

  • The creed, the twenty canons and the synodal letter to Egypt (acts): The minutes are lost; Henry Percival, who edited the council records, says no acts of the first two councils have come down. The creed survives in the acts of Ephesus and Chalcedon and in Eusebius of Caesarea's letter; the synodal letter in Socrates, Theodoret and Gelasius.
  • The subscription lists (acts): Held in the Latin recensions of the edition by Gelzer, Hilgenfeld and Cuntz. They list about 220 names; the editors say the names were not taken from the original signatures.
  • Eusebius of Caesarea, Letter to his church, and Life of Constantine III.6-14 (participant): An eyewitness who held back from signing for a time and wrote to explain himself. The Life praises the emperor throughout.
  • Eustathius of Antioch, in Theodoret of Cyrrhus I.8 (participant): An eyewitness on Alexander's side, hostile to Arius's friends, whom he calls the Ariomaniacs.
  • Athanasius of Alexandria, Defence of the Nicene Definition (De decretis) 3 and 19-20 (participant): Present as Alexander's deacon. Written decades later, against those who attacked the creed.
  • Socrates of Constantinople I.5-14; Sozomen I.17-25; Theodoret of Cyrrhus I.6-11 (later narrative): Church historians of the next century, all on the Nicene side. Socrates follows Eusebius but changes his count.
  • Marutha of Maipherqat (attributed), De sancta Nicaena synodo (later narrative): The Syriac tradition's account and canons of Nicaea, in Oscar Braun's German translation. Braun discusses whether Marutha wrote it.
  • Charles Joseph Hefele, History of the Councils I; Archibald Robertson's prolegomena to Athanasius; Henry Percival's notes (modern reconstruction): Supply the dates, the order of events and the reading of the counts.

What Is Uncertain

  • The council's minutes are lost. What it decided comes from its creed, canons and letter to Egypt. How it argued comes from three eyewitnesses, Eusebius of Caesarea, Eustathius of Antioch and Athanasius of Alexandria, and from historians of the next century, all on the Nicene side.
  • Arius's teaching reaches us through the council's letter and through his own letter to Eusebius of Nicomedia, which survives only because his opponent Theodoret of Cyrrhus kept it.
  • The number of bishops is disputed. Eusebius gives more than 250, Eustathius about 270, Athanasius three hundred more or less, and later writers 318. The surviving lists name about 220. Hefele thinks numbers changed over the months.
  • The order of events is a reconstruction. Archibald Robertson places the whispering scene before Eusebius of Caesarea's creed; Montfaucon and Gwatkin, as he reports, place it after. He says there is no real certainty.
  • Whose statement was torn up is not certain. Eustathius says only Eusebius's; Theodoret takes him to mean Eusebius of Nicomedia. Robertson notes it may have been produced against him, not by him.
  • Who presided is disputed. Hefele holds that Ossius presided for the bishop of Rome, relying on Gelasius of Cyzicus. Henry Percival finds no evidence of it, and Robertson leaves it open.
  • How many refused to sign is told differently. The synodal letter names two. Socrates says five held out at first. Why Eusebius of Nicomedia and Theognis were exiled also differs between Socrates and Constantine's own letter.
  • The dates are Hefele's reconstruction. Socrates gives the 20th of May from the record; the copy of the creed read at Chalcedon gives the 19th of June. Athanasius says the fathers put no date on their creed.
