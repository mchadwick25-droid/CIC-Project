The Imperial Church Era · c. 360-430 · Laodicea, Syria, Asia Minor

Apollinarian Christianity

An ally of Athanasius of Alexandria who pressed the unity of Christ so hard that he left no room for a human mind in him - and drew the answer that what is not assumed is not healed.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:12

Read along

Apollinaris of Laodicea was on the right side of the Arian controversy. He was a friend of Athanasius, a defender of Nicaea, and by all accounts a formidable scholar. The position that took his name came from pressing the unity of Christ harder than anyone else dared. If Christ is one, Apollinaris reasoned, then there cannot be two minds competing in him. So the divine Word must take the place a human rational soul would hold. Christ has a human body and its animating life, but not a human mind. This view was condemned at a council in Rome in the 370s, and again by name at Constantinople in 381. Gregory of Nazianzus wrote the reply that stuck, in a letter to a priest named Cledonius: what is not assumed is not healed. If Christ did not take a human mind, then the human mind, the part that most needs it, is not saved. Apollinaris' followers kept his works in circulation under other men's names, which is why some of them survive at all.

Documented Stories

The Bible Rewritten as Homer

In 362 the emperor Julian issued a law forbidding Christians to teach grammar and rhetoric: a man who did not believe in the gods, he argued, had no business expounding Homer and Hesiod. The effect, as Socrates says, was to shut Christian children out of the whole education of the ancient world, which was built on those texts. Apollinaris the elder, a grammarian and presbyter of Laodicea, and his son Apollinaris, a rhetorician and reader in the same church, answered the law by rewriting the curriculum. The father, Socrates writes, ‘translated the Pentateuch into heroic verse’ — the Books of Moses as a Homeric epic in twenty-four books — and turned the historical books into ‘comedies, tragedies and odes’ in every classical meter; the son ‘put the Gospels and the apostolic writings into dialogues in the manner of Plato’. Sozomen adds that the elder’s paraphrase of the Psalms in hexameters was so good that it was still being read, and it is the one piece of the enterprise that survives, a metrical Psalter of about five thousand lines. Julian died within the year and the law with him, and Socrates observes that the works ‘are now of no more use than if they had never been written’, since Christians went back to Homer. But for a year or two the whole apparatus of a classical education existed in Christian dress, made by the man who would shortly be condemned for pressing the unity of Christ too far.

Excommunicated for a Friendship with a Sophist

Before there was an Apollinarian heresy there was, by Socrates’ account, an Apollinarian friendship. The elder Apollinaris taught grammar and the younger rhetoric in Laodicea, and both were close to Epiphanius, the leading pagan sophist of the city. Bishop Theodotus, ‘fearing that such communication should pervert their principles and lead them into paganism’, forbade the association; they ignored him. His successor George tried again, failed, ‘and excommunicated them’. Sozomen gives the occasion: Epiphanius had recited his hymn to Dionysus, and the Apollinares had been in the audience, and the bishop held that a presbyter and a reader had no business hearing it. Socrates then says something he immediately half-withdraws — that the younger Apollinaris, ‘regarding this severe procedure as an act of injustice’, turned his rhetorical skill to inventing a new doctrine; but ‘some affirm’ it was rather George’s own inconsistency, Homoian one day and Arian the next, that drove them from him. The two were eventually restored and Apollinaris the younger became bishop of the Nicene congregation in Laodicea, Athanasius’ friend and ally, and the author of the Christology that would be condemned by name in 381. What it documents is the pressure on Christian scholars in a classical city — the same pressure Julian would later apply from the other side — and a church willing to excommunicate two of its most learned men for going to a poetry reading.

In Its Own Record

This was not an Arian position at all. Apollinaris had stood with Athanasius of Alexandria against Arianism, and he came to his view by pressing the unity of Christ too hard. The question he raised, how much of a human being Christ became, ran on into the councils of Ephesus and Chalcedon.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Apollinaris of Laodicea was an ally of Athanasius of Alexandria and a defender of Nicaea. He pressed the unity of Christ so hard that he left no room in him for a human mind. The divine Word, he argued, took the place a human rational soul would hold. Gregory of Nazianzus gave the reply that stuck, in a letter to the priest Cledonius: what is not assumed is not healed. So a Christ without a human mind could not save the human mind. Apollinaris' teaching was condemned at Rome in the 370s and by name at Constantinople in 381. His own writings survived mostly because his followers kept passing them around under other men's names.

Legacy

Gregory of Nazianzus's principle, what is not assumed is not healed, became a load-bearing rule of Christian thought. It is still quoted in debates that have nothing to do with Apollinaris. The quarrel also shaped the next hundred years. At Ephesus in 431 the bishops heard a letter from Cyril of Alexandria. It said the Word took flesh "animated by a rational soul." At Chalcedon in 451 their definition called Christ truly man, with a rational soul and a body.

Doctrinal Floor Note

Apollinaris held that in Christ the divine Word took the place of the human rational soul - so that Christ had a human body and animal soul but not a human mind. Gregory of Nazianzus' answer is the classic one and states the ground exactly: what is not assumed is not healed. A Christ who did not take a human mind did not save the human mind. Condemned at Rome in 377 and by name in canon 1 of Constantinople I in 381.

How Sure the Record Is

Through opponents. Apollinaris was a prolific writer and very little of his own work survives under his name; some circulated under other names to escape condemnation. In this corpus he is present through Gregory of Nazianzus' letters to Cledonius and the conciliar record.
