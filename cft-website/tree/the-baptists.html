The Enlightenment & Awakening Era · 1609-1815 · England, then America

England, today in United Kingdom and beyond

The Baptists

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In 1609 an exiled English congregation in Amsterdam decided that baptism belonged to believers who could profess faith for themselves. Its pastor, John Smyth, baptized himself and then the rest. That was the start of the English Baptists. Two streams grew up. General Baptists held that Christ died for all. Particular Baptists held, with Calvin, that God had chosen who would be saved. Both formed gathered churches: bodies of ordinary people who made a covenant together and chose their own ministers. They disciplined their own members and wrote it all down in minute books, week after week. They were persecuted hard under the Restoration. John Bunyan spent twelve years in Bedford jail for preaching without a license and wrote Grace Abounding there. They became one of the strongest voices in English for freedom of conscience. In America they grew fast late in the eighteenth century. The awakening may have fed them many converts, though the numbers are not known. The movement turned outward in 1792. That year a shoemaker named William Carey persuaded a small group of ministers to found a mission society. Then he sailed for India himself.

In Its Own Record

Grew Out Of

  • Puritanism Widely Accepted Major — John Smyth gave up the orders he had taken from the bishop of Lincoln. In 1911 W. J. McGlothlin wrote that some Puritans left the English church to found their own churches, and that Smyth's flock at Gainsborough was one. In Amsterdam around 1609 it became the first English Baptist church.

Gave Rise To

  • The Black Church in America Widely Accepted Major — George Liele was baptized in a Baptist church in Georgia, and its white members called him to preach. David George recalled that "Brother Palmer formed us into a church" at Silver Bluff. Historians treat such Black Baptist congregations as one root of the Black Church.
  • The Missionary Movement's Native Churches (Serampore India; Sierra Leone & the Niger; Pacific Islands; China Coast & CIM) Inferential-Thin Major — In 1792 Baptist ministers at Kettering formed a society to spread the gospel among the heathen. It sent William Carey and John Thomas to India, with a letter to Indian believers Thomas had known. No record from those first years shows the churches that grew from the mission, so the tie is thin.
  • The Caribbean Churches of Slavery and Emancipation Inferential-Thin Major — In 1782 George Liele, a Black Baptist pastor, came from Georgia to Kingston. In 1813 the Baptist Missionary Society sent John Rowe to work with Moses Baker, one of his church. This rests on one account of 1843, one letter of 1793 and Liele's own narrative. No modern study was opened. Phillippo calls Liele a slave brought by his master. Liele's own narrative says he was freed and came as an indentured servant.

Continued As

  • Nineteenth-Century Baptists Widely Accepted Major — In America many Baptist churches kept their old confession of faith through the century. W. J. McGlothlin, a Baptist historian, listed new printings of the Philadelphia Confession in 1813, 1831 and 1850. In 1911 he called it still widely used. In England the old confessions fell out of use, but the two old wings of English Baptists slowly drew closer.

Influenced By

  • The Anabaptist Movements Contested Major — Scholars disagree about how much the English Baptists owed the Anabaptists. John Smyth's church in exile baptized its own members anew in Amsterdam. Then Smyth asked to join the Mennonite church there. He called his own new baptism an error. Thomas Helwys and his party kept theirs.Lasting result: The question split Smyth's church in Amsterdam in about 1609. His party sought to join the Mennonites. Helwys's party kept apart and later founded the General Baptists.
  • The Evangelical Awakening in New England Documented — In 1792 the Baptist William Carey pleaded for a mission to the heathen. He praised David Brainerd's work among the Indians from about 1743. He named Brainerd, with Paul and Elliot of New England, as a model for missionaries.

Opposed By

  • The Restoration and Georgian Church of England Documented Major — John Bunyan of Bedford was charged before the justices. They said he stayed away from the parish church and held unlawful meetings. An Act of Parliament in 1664 banned any religious meeting of more than four people outside Church of England ways. A Baptist historian wrote in 1909 that for three years the General Baptists could hold no open assembly.Lasting result: A law of 1664 banned any religious meeting of more than four people that did not follow the ways of the Church of England.

In Contact With

  • English Old Dissent — Presbyterians and Congregationalists Documented — In 1719 Dissenting ministers met at Salters' Hall in London over a dispute about tests of faith. A pamphlet of that year says one speaker asked if they had come to be contradicted by 'Anabaptist teachers'. Some of the other side, it explains, were Baptists.

Sources

  • The confessional record: First London Confession (1644/46), Second London Confession (1677/1689), Standard Confession (1660), Orthodox Creed (1678), with local church covenants (primary) — The two wings' self-statements; the documents any independent-origins argument runs on. Whether Baptists descend in part from the continental Anabaptists is itself a genuinely contested question among historians.
  • Bunyan: Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners (1666) and The Pilgrim's Progress (1678) (primary)
  • Church minute books and association records: Broadmead (Bristol) records, Fenstanton/Warboys/Hexham church books (Underhill's editions), association circular letters, Philadelphia Baptist Association minutes (1707-) (primary) — Women members' testimony and cases are visible throughout, though filtered through the church's own institutional minutes.
  • Women's and Black voices: Agnes Beaumont, The Narrative of the Persecutions of Agnes Beaumont (1674); George Liele's letters and account (1791; congregations from c. 1773-75) and David George's autobiographical narrative (1793) (primary) — A Bedford congregation woman's own persecution story; enslaved and formerly-enslaved preachers' own words.
  • Michael Watts, The Dissenters, vol. 1 (OUP, 1978), with David Bebbington, Baptists Through the Centuries (Baylor, 2010) (secondary)
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 7: the 'Great Britain and Ireland' chapter (Clark), Ward's awakenings chapter, and Noll for America (reference)
