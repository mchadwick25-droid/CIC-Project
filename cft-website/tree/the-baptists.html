England, today in United Kingdom and beyond

The Baptists

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In 1609 an exiled English congregation in Amsterdam concluded that baptism belonged to believers who could profess faith for themselves, and its pastor John Smyth baptized himself and then the rest — the beginning of the English Baptists. Two streams developed, General Baptists holding that Christ died for all and Particular Baptists holding to a Calvinist election, and both organized as gathered churches. Bodies of ordinary people who covenanted together, chose their own ministers, disciplined their own members and wrote it all down in minute books, week after week. They were persecuted hard under the Restoration — John Bunyan spent twelve years in Bedford gaol for preaching without a license and wrote Grace Abounding there. And they became one of the strongest voices in English for liberty of conscience. In America they grew explosively out of the awakening, and the movement's outward turn came in 1792, when a Northamptonshire shoemaker named William Carey persuaded a small association of ministers to found a missionary society and then sailed for India himself.

In Its Own Record

Grew Out Of

  • Puritanism Widely Accepted Major — John Smyth gave up the orders he had taken from the bishop of Lincoln. In 1911 W. J. McGlothlin wrote that some Puritans left the English church to found their own churches, and that Smyth's flock at Gainsborough was one. In Amsterdam around 1609 it became the first English Baptist church.

Gave Rise To

  • The Black Church in America Widely Accepted Major — George Liele was baptized in a Baptist church in Georgia, and its white members called him to preach. David George recalled that "Brother Palmer formed us into a church" at Silver Bluff. Historians treat such Black Baptist congregations as one root of the Black Church.

Influenced By

  • The Anabaptist Movements Contested Major — Scholars disagree about how much the English Baptists owed the Anabaptists. John Smyth's church in exile baptized its own members anew in Amsterdam. Then Smyth asked to join the Mennonite church there. He called his own new baptism an error. Thomas Helwys and his party kept theirs.Lasting result: The question split Smyth's church in Amsterdam in about 1609. His party sought to join the Mennonites. Helwys's party kept apart and later founded the General Baptists.

Sources

  • The confessional record: First London Confession (1644/46), Second London Confession (1677/1689), Standard Confession (1660), Orthodox Creed (1678), with local church covenants (primary) — The two wings' self-statements; the documents any independent-origins argument runs on. Whether Baptists descend in part from the continental Anabaptists is itself a genuinely contested question among historians.
  • Bunyan: Grace Abounding to the Chief of Sinners (1666) and The Pilgrim's Progress (1678) (primary)
  • Church minute books and association records: Broadmead (Bristol) records, Fenstanton/Warboys/Hexham church books (Underhill's editions), association circular letters, Philadelphia Baptist Association minutes (1707-) (primary) — Women members' testimony and cases are visible throughout, though filtered through the church's own institutional minutes.
  • Women's and Black voices: Agnes Beaumont, The Narrative of the Persecutions of Agnes Beaumont (1674); George Liele's letters and account (1791; congregations from c. 1773-75) and David George's autobiographical narrative (1793) (primary) — A Bedford congregation woman's own persecution story; enslaved and formerly-enslaved preachers' own words.
  • Michael Watts, The Dissenters, vol. 1 (OUP, 1978), with David Bebbington, Baptists Through the Centuries (Baylor, 2010) (secondary)
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 7: the 'Great Britain and Ireland' chapter (Clark), Ward's awakenings chapter, and Noll for America (reference)
