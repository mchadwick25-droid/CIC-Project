The Baptists

England and then America, 1609 to 1815 — congregations of ordinary people who baptized believers only, governed themselves in writing week by week, and went to prison for preaching without a license.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:30

Read along

In 1609 an exiled English congregation in Amsterdam decided that baptism belonged to believers who could profess faith for themselves. Its pastor, John Smyth, baptized himself and then the rest. That was the start of the English Baptists. Two streams grew up. General Baptists held that Christ died for all. Particular Baptists held, with Calvin, that God had chosen who would be saved. Both formed gathered churches: bodies of ordinary people who made a covenant together and chose their own ministers. They disciplined their own members and wrote it all down in minute books, week after week. They were persecuted hard under the Restoration. John Bunyan spent twelve years in Bedford jail for preaching without a license and wrote Grace Abounding there. They became one of the strongest voices in English for freedom of conscience. In America they grew fast late in the eighteenth century. The awakening may have fed them many converts, though the numbers are not known. The movement turned outward in 1792. That year a shoemaker named William Carey persuaded a small group of ministers to found a mission society. Then he sailed for India himself.

People and Stories

Voices in the Record

  • John Smyth — pastor of the exiled English congregation in Amsterdam that took the first step in 1609
  • Thomas Helwys — brought the first Baptist church back to England in 1612 and wrote one of the earliest English pleas for complete liberty of conscience, extending it to Jews and Muslims
  • John Bunyan — Bedford tinker and preacher, imprisoned twelve years, author of Grace Abounding and The Pilgrim's Progress
  • Agnes Beaumont — a young woman of Bunyan's congregation who wrote her own account of the scandal and persecution she faced
  • George Liele and David George — formerly enslaved preachers who founded some of the first Black Baptist congregations in America and then carried the movement to Jamaica and Sierra Leone
  • William Carey — Northamptonshire shoemaker whose 1792 appeal founded the Baptist Missionary Society; he spent the rest of his life in India

Documented Stories

‘The King Is a Mortal Man, and Not God’

Thomas Helwys had financed the flight of John Smyth's separatist congregation from Gainsborough to Amsterdam in 1608 and had been baptized there by Smyth in 1609, when the congregation concluded that baptism belonged to believers. When Smyth moved toward joining the Dutch Mennonites, Helwys and about ten others broke with him and, deciding that flight itself had been wrong, came back to England in 1612 and settled at Spitalfields outside London, the first Baptist church on English soil. That year Helwys published A Short Declaration of the Mystery of Iniquity, which argued that the king's authority was over bodies and goods and not over souls, and that the magistrate had no power to punish anyone, ‘be they heretics, Turks, Jews, or whatsoever’, for their religion, since religion was between God and each person. He sent a copy to James I with an inscription in his own hand: ‘The king is a mortal man, and not God, therefore hath no power over the immortal souls of his subjects, to make laws and ordinances for them, and to set spiritual lords over them.’ He was in Newgate prison within the year and was dead by 1616, in his forties. His congregation, led by John Murton, survived, kept writing on liberty of conscience from prison, and by 1626 had five sister churches. It is the first claim in English for full religious liberty for everyone, made by a man with perhaps a dozen followers to a king who did not read it.

Agnes Beaumont Rides behind Mr Bunyan

Agnes Beaumont was twenty-one, unmarried, and keeping house for her widowed father at Edworth in Bedfordshire, and a member of John Bunyan's congregation at Bedford, against the wishes of her father, who had been turned against Bunyan by a lawyer of the neighborhood. In February 1674 she wanted to go to a meeting at Gamlingay and had no horse; her brother's man failed her, and when Bunyan rode by on his way to preach she begged a place behind him, and he, reluctantly, took her up. When she came home that night he had bolted the doors, and told her through the window that she should never come in again unless she promised to leave the meetings; she spent the night in the barn, praying, and the next day gave way, promised not to go without his leave, and was let in. Two nights later her father woke with a pain in his chest and died in her arms before she could fetch help. The lawyer, Feery, who had wanted to marry her, told the neighborhood that she had poisoned him with Bunyan's connivance, and a coroner's jury was summoned; the men who examined the body found nothing, the jury cleared her. She wrote it all down within a year or two in a manuscript that circulated among Bedfordshire Baptists for eighty years before it was printed. It is one of the few accounts of what belonging to a gathered church cost a young woman in her own household.

George Liele Indentures Himself to Reach Jamaica

George Liele had been born enslaved in Virginia about 1750 and taken to Georgia by his owner Henry Sharp, a Baptist deacon. Converted in 1773 under a white preacher and baptized, he was licensed by the church to preach to the enslaved on the plantations of the Savannah River, and gathered congregations at Silver Bluff in South Carolina and in Savannah, the first Black Baptist churches in America; Sharp, a Loyalist, freed him before the war took Sharp's life. When the British evacuated Savannah in 1782, Liele had no money to get his wife and four children out, and indentured himself to a British officer, Colonel Moses Kirkland, to pay their passage to Jamaica. He worked off the debt in Kingston in two years, and in 1784 began preaching there, to enslaved people and free people of color, with the permission of the governor and under the suspicion of the planters. By 1791 he had baptized some four hundred, and was writing to the Baptists of London to describe it: the covenant his church kept, the rule that no member might be baptized without the owner's consent, the chapel they were building at Kingston. In 1794 he was charged with preaching sedition, held in irons and tried, and acquitted; he was later imprisoned for the chapel's debt. His church became the root of the Native Baptists of Jamaica, from whom Sam Sharpe came; David George, of the Silver Bluff church, carried the same tradition to Nova Scotia and Sierra Leone.

Connections

In Its Own Record

The Baptists grew out of Puritan and Separatist ferment in England. In 1609 John Smyth wrote that his people had moved from Puritanism to Separatism, and then to the baptism of believers. They seem to have reached that belief largely on their own. Historians are divided on how much the Anabaptists shaped it. Soon after, Smyth and his followers asked to join a Mennonite church in Amsterdam. From the Baptists came William Carey's mission and the Black Baptist churches. Those churches in turn became one of the founding traditions of the Black Church in America.

  • gave rise to The Missionary Movement's Native Churches (Serampore India; Sierra Leone & the Niger; Pacific Islands; China Coast & CIM) Inferential-Thin Major — In 1792 Baptist ministers at Kettering formed a society to spread the gospel among the heathen.   • continued as Nineteenth-Century Baptists Widely Accepted Major — In America many Baptist churches kept their old confession of faith through the century. W. J. In England the old confessions fell out of use, but the two old wings of English Baptists slowly drew closer.
  • gave rise to The Caribbean Churches of Slavery and Emancipation Inferential-Thin Major — In 1782 George Liele, a Black Baptist pastor, came from Georgia to Kingston. No modern study was opened. Phillippo calls Liele a slave brought by his master. Liele's own narrative says he was freed and came as an indentured servant.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 1609 an exiled English congregation in Amsterdam concluded that baptism belonged only to believers who could profess faith for themselves, and its pastor John Smyth baptized himself and then the rest. The churches that grew from that governed themselves in writing, week by week, in minute books and covenants — John Bunyan wrote Grace Abounding during twelve years in Bedford gaol for preaching without a license — and in 1792 a shoemaker named William Carey persuaded a small association of ministers to found a missionary society before sailing for India himself.

Legacy

Baptists are now one of the largest Protestant families in the world. The tradition held that no ruler has power over what people believe. That idea passed into the founding of Rhode Island under Roger Williams. Through many years of Baptist pleas to the state, it also shaped how the American constitution treats religion. Carey's was not the first Protestant mission. Carey himself named the Danes at Tranquebar and the Moravians before him. But his society sent Carey and John Thomas to India, and Baptist work abroad grew from there. The Black Baptist churches begun by Liele, George and those who came after became a pillar of Black life in America. They were also the ground from which the civil rights movement later grew.
