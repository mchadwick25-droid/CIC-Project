The Early Church Era · c. 180-400 · Edessa and Osrhoene

Edessa, today in Turkey · Osrhoene

Bardaisan and the Bardaisanites

Edessa's first Christian philosopher, whose hymns were good enough that Ephraim had to write better ones - and whose orthodoxy has been argued over ever since.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:04

Read along

Bardaisan of Edessa was a courtier, an archer of some reputation, a philosopher, and by his own lights a Christian. He wrote against Marcion, and he was baptized in the Edessene church. He and his school made hymns. Ephraim later accused those hymns of sweetening poison for the simple, and he answered them with hymns of his own. What Bardaisan actually taught is hard to recover. The Book of the Laws of Countries was written by his pupil Philip. It shows him arguing that human freedom is real, against the fatalism of the astrologers. That is a thoroughly Christian argument. Ephraim wrote a century and a half later in the same city. He presents Bardaisan as a heretic with a cosmology of primordial beings and a flawed doctrine of the body's resurrection. Both cannot be the whole truth, and the sources that would settle it are gone.

Documented Stories

Avida Will Not Ask, So Bardaisan Answers Anyway

The Book of the Laws of Countries is the one surviving text in which Bardaisan speaks, and it is a dialogue his pupil Philip wrote down in Syriac. It opens with a scene. A group of friends have come to visit Shemashgram, and a young man named Avida — who has been arguing that if God is one and good he would have made human beings incapable of doing wrong — is sitting apart, refusing to ask Bardaisan the question directly because, as one of the others says, he wants to seem to have found the answer himself. Bardaisan, arriving, gently draws him out: a question asked to learn is a good thing; a question asked to win is not. What follows is the earliest sustained Christian argument for free will in any language. Bardaisan grants that nature governs birth, growth, hunger and death, and that the stars may shape fortune; but he argues that conduct is free, because the same stars shine on the Seres, the Brahmins, the Persians, the Britons and the Christians, and each people keeps its own laws regardless — and the Christians, ‘wherever they are’, keep one law under every constellation. The dialogue ends without Avida’s conversion recorded. Eusebius admired it enough to translate a long passage into Greek; Ephrem, a century later, thought its author a heretic. Both were reading the same man, who argued that God had made human beings free in order that they might be judged.

In Its Own Record

Bardaisan was Edessa's best-known Christian thinker before Ephraim. A century and a half later, Ephraim fought his followers. He was a near neighbor of Edessa's Syriac church, not a stranger to it.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced

  • influenced Syriac Christianity (Edessa/Nisibis) Documented — Ephraim complained that Bardaisan 'fashioned hymns, and joined them with tunes'. Sozomen and Theodoret wrote in the 400s. They say Harmonius, the son of Bardaisan, wrote songs. Ephraim answered them with orthodox songs to the same music.

Argued Against

  • argued against Marcion / Marcionism Documented — Eusebius of Caesarea wrote early in the 300s. He says Bardaisan wrote dialogues in Syriac against the followers of Marcion. He adds that Bardaisan's pupils put them into Greek.

Opposed By

  • argued against Syriac Christianity (Edessa/Nisibis) Documented — Ephraim the Syrian wrote Prose Refutations against Mani, Marcion and Bardaisan. In them he names Bardaisan. He attacks what Bardaisan taught on the soul, on space and on matter.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Bardaisan of Edessa was a courtier, philosopher, and baptized member of the Edessene church who wrote against Marcion and argued that human freedom is real against the fatalism of the astrologers. His hymns were popular enough that, a century and a half later, Ephraim the Syrian felt he had to write better ones, even while accusing Bardaisan of a heretical cosmology of primordial beings. Which picture is true is a question the surviving evidence has never settled.

Legacy

Syriac hymnody as a form owes something to both sides of this argument: Bardaisan and his son Harmonius set the pattern, and Ephraim's decision to answer them in the same form gave the Syriac church the madrasha tradition that shaped its worship for centuries. The Book of the Laws of Countries remains one of the earliest Syriac prose works of any kind.

Sources

On the Nicene Creed

The creedal question is genuinely open. Ephraim treats Bardaisan as a heretic and links him to Valentinus; the Chronicle of Edessa's own notes call him a promulgator of Valentinus' doctrine. But he was a baptized member of the Edessene church who wrote against Marcion, and how far his cosmology - of primordial entities, of fate and the stars - actually crosses the creedal line is disputed by every generation that has looked at it. The question remains open, not answered.

How Sure the Record Is

Through opponents and one disputed survival. The Book of the Laws of Countries preserves his voice at second hand, written by his pupil Philip; everything else comes through Ephraim's Prose Refutations, which quote him at length in order to answer him.
