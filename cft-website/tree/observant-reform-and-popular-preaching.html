Observant Reform & Popular Preaching

Italy and Spain, c. 1368 to 1517 — friars who went back to the strict rule, and then preached it to whole cities standing in the square.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:28

Read along

The Observance began as an argument inside the religious orders. Its friars said the Rule should be kept as it was written. It should not be kept as years of exceptions had relaxed it. It started small, with Paoluccio Trinci's handful of friars at Brugliano in 1368. Then it spread across the Franciscans, Dominicans, Augustinians and Benedictines alike. In the end, reformed houses formed rival provinces beside the unreformed ones. Its public face was preaching. Bernardino of Siena drew whole towns into the square to hear him for weeks on end. In 1427 he preached forty-five sermons at Siena. A listener in the crowd took them down as he spoke, with his asides and the crowd's reactions. It is about as close as anyone gets to hearing a medieval sermon, not just reading one. John of Capistrano did the same across central Europe. The movement's dark side belongs in the same story. Capistrano also drove the persecution of Jewish communities. In 1447 the pope made him enforcer of the papal laws restricting Jews and Muslims in Italy. The most notorious case was at Breslau in 1453, where Jews were burned.

Documented Stories

Bernardino Tells the Women of Siena to Stop Talking

Benedetto di maestro Bartolomeo was a Sienese cimatore, a finisher of woollen cloth, who had taught himself a shorthand, and in the late summer of 1427 he went every morning before dawn to the Piazza del Campo, where Bernardino of Siena was preaching a cycle of sermons from a wooden pulpit against the Palazzo Pubblico, and took down what he heard. He then wrote it out in full — forty-five sermons in the Sienese dialect, with the preacher’s digressions, his imitations of voices, his repeated ‘Oh, oh!’, and the crowd’s reactions where Benedetto noted them. Bernardino preached on usury, on sodomy, on witchcraft, on the vanities that he asked the city to bring to a bonfire, on the name of Jesus painted on a tablet he held up, and on the duties of husbands and wives. He stopped, more than once, to address the women, who sat on one side of the square behind a rope: ‘Women, keep quiet over there, or I will stop — you, I mean, over there, chattering.’ He also told them, in the sermon on witches, to denounce any woman who practiced charms, and he boasted of having had one burned in Rome. The whole cycle is the movement’s program delivered to ordinary people: a friar in the town square, reforming a city by talking to it for six weeks, and a working man deciding the words were worth keeping.

Capistrano at Breslau

John of Capistrano arrived in Breslau in February 1453 at the invitation of the city council, at the end of two years of preaching tours through Austria, Bohemia and Silesia that had drawn crowds no building could hold and had produced bonfires of vanities, conversions of Hussites, and repeated demands that towns expel their Jews. In Breslau the pretext came in May: a convert reported that a Jew had bought a consecrated host from a peasant and abused it. The city arrested the entire Jewish community — over three hundred people. Capistrano, as papal legate and inquisitor, conducted the interrogations, with torture; the confessions, in the form the trial record preserves, describe the host bleeding and the Jews attempting to burn it. On 2 July forty-one Jews were burned alive in the Salzring; the rest were expelled from Breslau, their children under seven taken to be baptized, and their property confiscated; King Ladislaus banished the Jews from Silesia. Capistrano wrote to the pope reporting the case as a triumph over the enemies of Christ. He went on to Poland, then to Hungary, where in 1456 he preached the crusade that relieved Belgrade from the Turks, and died that year; he was canonized in 1690. The Observant reform’s greatest preacher and the Breslau burning are one biography. The Franciscan chronicles that record his sermons record the trial in the same tone, and the movement that revived the Rule of St Francis in the fifteenth century did this with the same energy.

In Its Own Record

A reform of the friars' orders that began inside the Franciscan order in 1368 and spread to other orders. In Spain it worked against the background of the converso crisis, from the pogroms of 1391 to the expulsion of 1492.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

The Observance began small. In 1368 Paoluccio Trinci and a handful of friars kept the Rule strictly at Brugliano. Then it spread across the Franciscans, Dominicans, Augustinians and Benedictines alike. Its public face was preaching to great crowds. In 1427 Bernardino of Siena gave forty-five sermons, and a listener in the crowd took them down as he spoke, asides and all. It is about as close as anyone gets to hearing a medieval sermon, not just reading one. What it left is mixed. It left the low-interest pawn-banks called monti di pietà, which still trade today. But John of Capistrano also drove the persecution of Jewish communities. In 1447 the pope made him enforcer of the papal laws restricting Jews and Muslims in Italy. The most notorious case was at Breslau in 1453, where Jews were burned.

Legacy

The Observance won. When Leo X divided the Franciscans in 1517, the Observants took the order's leadership. By the 1560s the Capuchins stood beside them. The Council of Trent then let other friars own land and buildings, but not these two. The same pattern of reformed against unreformed shaped the Dominicans and Augustinians too. Two smaller things passed into ordinary use and outlived the movement. One is the monti di pietà, low-interest town pawn-banks that Observant friars promoted from the 1460s as an answer to ruinous lending. The one founded at Siena in 1472 still trades as a bank today. The other is the radiant IHS monogram that Bernardino held up at the end of his sermons. It became a fixture of Catholic devotional art.

Experience It Today

  • Basilica of San Bernardino, L'Aquila — built for Bernardino of Siena in the decades after his death, reopened in 2015 after earthquake repairs

Sources

Doctrinal Floor Note

No creedal question. Nor does the movement stand or fall with a single figure: it had many founders across several orders, and its claim rested on keeping the Rule strictly rather than on any leader's person. Savonarola, the one Observant whose following really did gather around himself, is the exception rather than the pattern.

How Sure the Record Is

Rich: Bernardino's sermon-cycles taken down as spoken - near-transcripts of mass lay formation

  • Bernardino of Siena, the 1427 Siena sermon cycle (reportatio of Benedetto di maestro Bartolomeo; standard ed. Delcorno, 1989, Italian) (primary) — Forty-five vernacular sermons taken down as they were spoken, audience reactions included - close to a live recording of a medieval sermon. English translation exists only in selections.
  • John of Capistrano: letters, treatises, and eyewitness accounts of the 1451-56 central-European preaching tours (primary) — Mass preaching at Vienna, Nuremberg, and Breslau. This includes the movement's darkest episode: at Breslau in 1453, Capistrano led the trial and burning of Jews on host-desecration charges. Those trial records are testimony extracted under coercion by the persecuting side, not neutral evidence. Largely untranslated (Acta Sanctorum; Hofer).
  • The Observance as structure: Observant constitutions and statutes (e.g. the Martinian constitutions, 1430), vicariate records, Bernardino's Latin model-sermon collections (primary) — The movement's institutional self-organization across Franciscan, Dominican, Augustinian, and Benedictine families. Latin editions only.
  • The reform's women in their own words: Colette of Corbie's Constitutions for the Poor Clares; Caterina Vigri of Bologna, The Seven Spiritual Weapons (primary) — The Observance's female branch as authors, not subjects - a woman legislating a reform, and an Observant Poor Clare's own formation book.
  • James Mixson & Bert Roest (eds.), A Companion to Observant Reform in the Late Middle Ages and Beyond (Brill, 2015) (secondary) — Current standard, cross-order by design. Treats Savonarola within the Observant frame - on his own he would not fit a movement defined by a single person, since the Observance was always broader than any one figure.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 4, ch. 29 'Observant reform in religious orders' (Roest); cf. ch. 30 'Public purity and discipline' (Rusconi) (reference) — A dedicated chapter by the Companion's co-editor - a direct fit.
  • Bullarium Franciscanum, nova series, vol. I (1431-1455), ed. Ulric Hüntemann (Quaracchi, 1929) (primary) — Papal letters of Bernardino's and Capistrano's years, in Latin. Among them is Nicholas V's letter of June 1447, which renews the decrees restricting Jews and Muslims in Italy and makes Capistrano their enforcer.
