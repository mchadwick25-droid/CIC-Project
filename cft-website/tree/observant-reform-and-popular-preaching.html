The Late Medieval Era · c. 1368-1517 · Italy, Spain

Observant Reform & Popular Preaching

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

The Observance began as an argument inside the religious orders. Its friars said the Rule should be kept as it was written. It should not be kept as years of exceptions had relaxed it. It started small, with Paoluccio Trinci's handful of friars at Brugliano in 1368. Then it spread across the Franciscans, Dominicans, Augustinians and Benedictines alike. In the end, reformed houses formed rival provinces beside the unreformed ones. Its public face was preaching. Bernardino of Siena drew whole towns into the square to hear him for weeks on end. In 1427 he preached forty-five sermons at Siena. A listener in the crowd took them down as he spoke, with his asides and the crowd's reactions. It is about as close as anyone gets to hearing a medieval sermon, not just reading one. John of Capistrano did the same across central Europe. The movement's dark side belongs in the same story. Capistrano also drove the persecution of Jewish communities. In 1447 the pope made him enforcer of the papal laws restricting Jews and Muslims in Italy. The most notorious case was at Breslau in 1453, where Jews were burned.

In Its Own Record

Grew Out Of

  • The Franciscan Order — Spirituals Crisis to the Great Division Documented Major — In 1438 the pope confirmed a letter from William of Casale, head of the whole order. It made one of his friars, Bernardino of Siena, vicar of the order's houses in Italy set up 'under the holy name of observance'. The historian Henry Charles Lea traced these strict houses to 1368. That year the head of the order asked for a hermitage at Brugliano for Paoluccio Trinci and his men.

Influenced By

  • The Franciscan Movement Documented — In 1440 Pope Eugenius IV wrote of a hermitage in Spain where Franciscan friars lived. He said they lived under the observance of the Rule of blessed Francis. In 1438 he had confirmed Bernardino of Siena as head of the order's Observant houses in Italy. That letter says the houses were set up 'under the holy name of observance'.

In Contact With

  • The Latin Papal Church (Avignon to Fifth Lateran) Documented Major — In 1415 the Council of Constance named a vicar general over the Observant friars of France, Burgundy and Touraine. In 1443 Pope Eugenius IV confirmed two vicars general for the Observants, one for each side of the Alps. The head of the Franciscans had named them at the pope's wish. In 1446 the pope ruled that the Observants would elect their own vicars general every three years. He said he was following rules of the Council of Constance and of Pope Martin V.Lasting result: In 1446 Pope Eugenius IV ruled that the Observants would choose their own leaders, and the head of the order had to confirm them.

Sources

  • Bernardino of Siena, the 1427 Siena sermon cycle (reportatio of Benedetto di maestro Bartolomeo; standard ed. Delcorno, 1989, Italian) (primary) — Forty-five vernacular sermons taken down as they were spoken, audience reactions included - close to a live recording of a medieval sermon. English translation exists only in selections.
  • John of Capistrano: letters, treatises, and eyewitness accounts of the 1451-56 central-European preaching tours (primary) — Mass preaching at Vienna, Nuremberg, and Breslau. This includes the movement's darkest episode: at Breslau in 1453, Capistrano led the trial and burning of Jews on host-desecration charges. Those trial records are testimony extracted under coercion by the persecuting side, not neutral evidence. Largely untranslated (Acta Sanctorum; Hofer).
  • The Observance as structure: Observant constitutions and statutes (e.g. the Martinian constitutions, 1430), vicariate records, Bernardino's Latin model-sermon collections (primary) — The movement's institutional self-organization across Franciscan, Dominican, Augustinian, and Benedictine families. Latin editions only.
  • The reform's women in their own words: Colette of Corbie's Constitutions for the Poor Clares; Caterina Vigri of Bologna, The Seven Spiritual Weapons (primary) — The Observance's female branch as authors, not subjects - a woman legislating a reform, and an Observant Poor Clare's own formation book.
  • James Mixson & Bert Roest (eds.), A Companion to Observant Reform in the Late Middle Ages and Beyond (Brill, 2015) (secondary) — Current standard, cross-order by design. Treats Savonarola within the Observant frame - on his own he would not fit a movement defined by a single person, since the Observance was always broader than any one figure.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 4, ch. 29 'Observant reform in religious orders' (Roest); cf. ch. 30 'Public purity and discipline' (Rusconi) (reference) — A dedicated chapter by the Companion's co-editor - a direct fit.
  • Bullarium Franciscanum, nova series, vol. I (1431-1455), ed. Ulric Hüntemann (Quaracchi, 1929) (primary) — Papal letters of Bernardino's and Capistrano's years, in Latin. Among them is Nicholas V's letter of June 1447, which renews the decrees restricting Jews and Muslims in Italy and makes Capistrano their enforcer.
