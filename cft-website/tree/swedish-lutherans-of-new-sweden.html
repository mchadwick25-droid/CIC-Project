The Swedish Lutherans of New Sweden

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In 1638 Swedish settlers built Fort Christina on the Delaware River. Their pastor, Reorus Torkillus, came from East Gothland in Sweden. The colony stayed small, and its churches were built of logs.

Swedish rule ended in 1655, and one Swedish pastor, Lars Lock, stayed on. In 1677 the people called a second pastor, Jacob Fabritius, who preached in Dutch. By 1691 he was too old to serve. Then, Acrelius writes, an old man read sermons out of a book in one church, and Charles Springer read in another. "The people waited, but no clergyman came."

Springer had a strange road to the river. He was seized in London, shipped to America and sold as a servant in Virginia. Once free, he joined his own people on the Delaware. In 1693 he wrote for them to Sweden. They asked for "two Swedish ministers", for Bibles, and for two hundred catechisms. They were farmers, they said, who lived "according to the laudable old Swedish customs". They were ready to seal their faith "with our own blood".

The letter reached King Charles XI. In 1697 three pastors arrived with a commission from the king and the archbishop. The people built two brick and stone churches. Trinity Church at Christina was consecrated in 1699, and Gloria Dei at Wicaco in 1700.

In the 1740s Moravian preachers came among the Swedes. In 1744 one of them, Bruzelius, was set to preach at Raccoon church. His opponents held the key, so his friends broke in through a window. A panel of twenty-five men later ruled against him. Moravian ministers were then barred from the Swedish and English churches.

Acrelius came as provost in 1749 and went home in 1756. He urged the people to keep the Swedish language and teach it to the young. Yet the English tongue was gaining, and some services were already held in English.
