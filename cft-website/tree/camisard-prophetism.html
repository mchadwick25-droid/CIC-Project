Camisard Prophetism

The Cévennes, 1700 to 1710 — a peasant war fought by people who had no pastors left, and who took their orders from prophets, some of them children.

The Huguenots→Camisard Prophetism→no descendant recorded

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:07

Read along

After Louis XIV outlawed Protestantism in 1685, the Protestants of the Cévennes were left with no pastors, no churches and no legal existence. And out of that vacuum came prophecy. From 1688 ordinary people, many of them children and young women, began falling into trance and speaking in what they took to be the voice of the Spirit. The first widely reported was a shepherd girl, Isabeau Vincent, who preached in her sleep. In 1702 the movement turned to armed revolt. Bands of peasants known as Camisards fought royal troops through the mountains for two years, led by men like Jean Cavalier, a baker's apprentice of twenty, with prophets traveling alongside the fighters and directing them. The rising was broken by 1704 and the last resistance ended by 1710; some of the prophets escaped to London, where they caused a fresh sensation as the 'French Prophets'.

People and Stories

Voices in the Record

  • Isabeau Vincent — the shepherd girl whose prophesying from 1688 was the first case to be widely reported
  • Jean Cavalier — a baker's apprentice who became the revolt's most capable commander before negotiating terms in 1704
  • Abraham Mazel and Élie Marion — Camisard leaders who wrote their own memoirs; Marion carried the prophecy to London
  • Pierre Laporte, called Rolland — Camisard captain, killed in 1704; the museum of the whole period stands in the house where he was born

Documented Stories

The Night at Pont-de-Montvert

The abbé François de Langlade du Chaila, archpriest of the Cévennes and inspector of the missions charged with converting the Protestants, lived at Pont-de-Montvert on the slopes of Mont Lozère, and in July 1702 was holding in his house a party of young Protestants caught trying to leave the kingdom, whom he had tortured to make them name their guide. Abraham Mazel, a wool-carder of Falguières who had received the Spirit, later deposed in London that he had seen in a vision black oxen eating a garden and had been told to drive them out. On the night of 24 July some fifty men from the mountain assemblies, singing the psalms, came down to the house, demanded the prisoners, broke in when refused, freed them, and set fire to the building; du Chaila let himself down from a window on knotted sheets, broke his leg, and was found in the garden. He asked for his life. He was stabbed, according to the depositions, by one man after another, each naming a relative the abbé had sent to the galleys or the gallows, fifty-two wounds in all. Esprit Séguier, a prophet who had led the party, was taken on 12 August; asked at his trial whether he expected mercy, he answered, the tradition says, ‘My soul is a garden full of shade and fountains’; his right hand was cut off and he was burned alive at Pont-de-Montvert on the spot where the abbé died.

Bunhill Fields, 25 May 1708

Élie Marion, Durand Fage and Jean Cavalier of Sauve reached London in 1706 and began to prophesy there as they had in the Cévennes, in trances, with agitations of the body and a voice that spoke for the Spirit; the French Reformed churches of London disowned them, English enthusiasts joined them, and a former Presbyterian gentleman, John Lacy, became their chief English prophet and their publisher. In 1707 Marion, Fage and Daudé were tried for sedition and blasphemy at the Guildhall, fined and set in the pillory at Charing Cross, where the crowd pelted them. Then Thomas Emes, a physician and one of the believers, died on 22 December 1707, and the Spirit, speaking through Lacy and others, declared that he would rise from the dead. The day was fixed, 25 May 1708, and the place, his grave in Bunhill Fields, the Dissenters' burying ground, and the prophets published it. On the day a crowd estimated at twenty thousand filled the fields and the streets around; the government posted the train-bands in case of riot. The prophets did not appear. Emes did not rise. Lacy explained afterwards that the unbelief of the crowd had prevented the miracle, and the movement, which had counted several hundred followers, lost most of them within the year and went on in small circles in England and Germany for decades; its remnants in Manchester were among the people from whom Ann Lee's Shakers came.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

After Protestantism was outlawed in France in 1685, ordinary people in the Cévennes — many of them children and young women with no pastors left to guide them — began falling into trance and speaking as they believed the Spirit directed. A shepherd girl named Isabeau Vincent was the first widely reported case, and by 1702 the movement had become an armed revolt, led in the field by a twenty-year-old baker's apprentice, Jean Cavalier, with prophets traveling alongside the fighters to direct them.

Legacy

The Camisards left no church of their own. French Protestantism rebuilt itself without them, in a wholly different key, sober and hidden. What the prophets left was a wave that carried. The exiles in London published sworn accounts of what they had seen, and drew crowds and scorn. For a generation they shaped the debate in England and Europe over religious enthusiasm. Faint echoes may have reached later revivals: in 1739 John Wesley went to hear a woman 'commonly called' a French prophet. In the Cévennes the memory was never allowed to fade. Every September an assembly still gathers at the Musée du Désert to mark it.

How Sure the Record Is

Persecution-born ecstatic prophecy, documented in trials and memoirs

  • Le Theatre sacre des Cevennes (London, 1707; ed. Misson; Eng. as A Cry from the Desart) - sworn depositions of exiled Camisard prophets (Marion, Fage, Cavalier) (primary) — The movement's own voices, in exile; the collection was assembled to defend the French Prophets in London, so it's an advocacy compilation.
  • Memoirs: Jean Cavalier, Memoirs of the Wars of the Cevennes (1726); Abraham Mazel's and Elie Marion's memoires; the child- and women-prophets record (Isabeau Vincent, 1688, via pastoral and hostile observers) (primary) — Participant memoirs, written some years after the events; the material on Vincent comes through outside observers, not her own words.
  • The persecution record: intendants' interrogation and trial records, abjuration registers, galley rolls (Tournier's Les galeres de France series), execution records (primary) — The movement's shape here is reconstructed from its persecutors' own records; testimony was given under torture and threat.
  • W. Gregory Monahan, Let God Arise: The War and Rebellion of the Camisards (OUP, 2014), with Lionel Laborie, Enlightening Enthusiasm (Manchester, 2015) (secondary) — Laborie covers the movement's afterlife among exiles in London.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 7, 'Continental Protestant Europe' (Lehmann), with the toleration chapter (reference) — The nearest chapter covering the Huguenot aftermath.
