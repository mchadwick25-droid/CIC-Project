The Enlightenment & Awakening Era · 1700-1710 · Cevennes

Cevennes, today in France

Camisard Prophetism

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

After Louis XIV outlawed Protestantism in 1685, the Protestants of the Cévennes were left with no pastors, no churches and no legal existence. And out of that vacuum came prophecy. From 1688 ordinary people, many of them children and young women, began falling into trance and speaking in what they took to be the voice of the Spirit. The first widely reported was a shepherd girl, Isabeau Vincent, who preached in her sleep. In 1702 the movement turned to armed revolt. Bands of peasants known as Camisards fought royal troops through the mountains for two years, led by men like Jean Cavalier, a baker's apprentice of twenty, with prophets traveling alongside the fighters and directing them. The rising was broken by 1704 and the last resistance ended by 1710; some of the prophets escaped to London, where they caused a fresh sensation as the 'French Prophets'.

In Its Own Record

Grew Out Of

  • The Huguenots Documented Major — In their manifesto of 1703, the armed Protestants of the Cévennes said their country had turned Reformed at the Reformation and stayed so ever since. They had taken up arms, they wrote, to keep the faith they had long professed, not to bring in a new one.

Influenced

  • Shakers Documented — The Shakers' own confession of 1810 says a few French Prophets reached England about 1706. It says James and Jane Wardley, whose society near Manchester later took Ann Lee as its Mother, received 'the spirit of the French prophets.' This rests on the Shakers' own account, written about sixty years after the Wardleys began.

Opposed By

  • The Church of the Desert Documented Major — Antoine Court called his first synod in the Cévennes for 21 August 1715. Its preachers included the last remnants of the Camisard bands. Court wrote that the synod voted to forbid women to preach and to reject the revelations then in vogue among them.Lasting result: In 1715 the first synod of the Cévennes preachers banned women from preaching and threw out the revelations then in vogue.

In Contact With

  • The Methodist Revival Inferential-Thin — In January 1739 John Wesley went to hear a young woman 'commonly called' a French prophet, to test her spirit. His editor names her as probably Mary Plewit and calls the French prophets refugee Camisards. The tie to the Camisard rising, thirty years on, rests on that name alone.

Sources

  • Le Theatre sacre des Cevennes (London, 1707; ed. Misson; Eng. as A Cry from the Desart) - sworn depositions of exiled Camisard prophets (Marion, Fage, Cavalier) (primary) — The movement's own voices, in exile; the collection was assembled to defend the French Prophets in London, so it's an advocacy compilation.
  • Memoirs: Jean Cavalier, Memoirs of the Wars of the Cevennes (1726); Abraham Mazel's and Elie Marion's memoires; the child- and women-prophets record (Isabeau Vincent, 1688, via pastoral and hostile observers) (primary) — Participant memoirs, written some years after the events; the material on Vincent comes through outside observers, not her own words.
  • The persecution record: intendants' interrogation and trial records, abjuration registers, galley rolls (Tournier's Les galeres de France series), execution records (primary) — The movement's shape here is reconstructed from its persecutors' own records; testimony was given under torture and threat.
  • W. Gregory Monahan, Let God Arise: The War and Rebellion of the Camisards (OUP, 2014), with Lionel Laborie, Enlightening Enthusiasm (Manchester, 2015) (secondary) — Laborie covers the movement's afterlife among exiles in London.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 7, 'Continental Protestant Europe' (Lehmann), with the toleration chapter (reference) — The nearest chapter covering the Huguenot aftermath.
