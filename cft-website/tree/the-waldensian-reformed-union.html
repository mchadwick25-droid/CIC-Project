The Reformation Era · 1532-1655+ · Alpine valleys

The Waldensian-Reformed Union

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

The Waldensians had already survived three centuries of suppression as an underground movement of lay preachers when, in 1532, their representatives met at Chanforan in the Alpine valleys of Piedmont and agreed to join the young Reformed churches. And to pay for a new French translation of the Bible, which appeared in 1535 as the Olivétan Bible. It was an unusual graft: an old dissenting tradition became a Reformed church with ministers, confessions and printed books, without letting go of its own memory of the centuries before. That memory was tested repeatedly, most brutally in the Piedmont massacre of Easter 1655, which drew protests from across Protestant Europe and a famous sonnet from John Milton.

In Its Own Record

Continues

  • The Waldensians in the Alps Contested Major — In 1532 the Waldensian churches of the Alpine valleys chose to hold their worship in the open. Their confession of that year says its teaching was handed down from father to son. Scholars disagree whether this shows an unbroken line back to the old Waldensians, or a later reading of the past.

Influenced By

  • The Reformed Cities - Zurich & Geneva Documented — A man of Angrogne, caught standing guard, said the ministers met at Chanforan included a red-bearded 'M. Farel'. The pastor Pierre Gilles, writing in 1644, calls them pastors who kept coming from Switzerland. In 1535 the valleys paid to print a French Bible.

Sources

  • The Synod of Chanforan decisions (1532) and the Olivetan Bible (1535) - the union's concrete deliverable, financed by the Waldensian communities (primary) — The Chanforan decisions survive only through later copies and transcriptions, not an original protocol.
  • The valleys' own historians: Lentolo's manuscript history (1560s), Gilles, Histoire ecclesiastique des eglises vaudoises (1644), Leger, Histoire generale (1669) (primary) — Insider history carrying the medieval memory forward into Reformed identity. Léger wrote as an advocate after 1655 and included contested, possibly backdated 'ancient' documents — the act of memory-keeping is itself part of the evidence. Available only in French and Italian.
  • Samuel Morland, The History of the Evangelical Churches of the Valleys of Piemont (1658) - the 1655 Piedmont Easter massacre dossier with survivor depositions (primary) — Written by a sympathetic outsider — Cromwell's envoy — from a Protestant-international advocacy standpoint. Covers the massacre and its aftermath from 1655 onward.
  • Euan Cameron, The Reformation of the Heretics: The Waldenses of the Alps 1480-1580 (Oxford, 1984), with Gabriel Audisio, The Waldensian Dissent (CUP, 1999) (secondary) — Cameron's book covers exactly this transition into union with the Reformed churches.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 6 (reference) — This volume has no chapter dedicated to the Waldensians; the closest are chapter 8 (Benedict) and chapter 15 (Gregory).
