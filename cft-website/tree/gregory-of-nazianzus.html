About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In 379 Gregory came to Constantinople, where the Arians held the churches. He lodged with a relative, and part of the house became a chapel that he named Anastasia, for the faith that was to rise again. There he preached the five orations that earned him the name of the Theologian. They defend the Trinity against those who blurred the three Persons together and those who split the one substance. The emperor Theodosius gave him the cathedral, and the council of 381 made him archbishop of the city. When its president, Meletius of Antioch, died, Gregory took the chair. Egyptian bishops objected, citing a rule from Nicaea against moving a bishop from one see to another, so he gave up the see and went home.

His closest friend was Basil of Caesarea, whom he had known as a boy and met again as a student in Athens. In his funeral speech for Basil, Gregory says he refused to haze the newcomer, and that this was the kindling spark of their friendship. Of their years together he wrote: "We seemed to have one soul, inhabiting two bodies." Gregory's father, the bishop of Nazianzus, ordained him a priest against his will, and Gregory fled to Basil in Pontus before returning. Years later Basil made him bishop of Sasima, a miserable little village, almost by force. Gregory was angry. Another bishop held the village with troops, and his father begged him to stay home, so he likely never served there.

He wrote two letters to the priest Cledonius against Apollinarius, who denied that Christ had a human mind. The councils of Ephesus and Chalcedon later adopted them, and Jerome, who heard him preach, called him his teacher in the Scriptures. Gregory wrote: "For that which He has not assumed He has not healed; but that which is united to His Godhead is also saved."

Key Works

  • The Five Theological Orations: Sermons defending the Trinity, preached in Constantinople.
  • Two Letters to Cledonius: Letters against Apollinarius on whether Christ had a human mind.
  • Funeral Oration on Basil: His speech on his friend, with the story of their student days.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Gennadius of Marseilles, calling Gregory of Nazianzus that most eloquent man, reports that Rufinus of Aquileia put his works into Latin along with those of Basil of Caesarea.
  • With Basil of Caesarea he gathered the Philocalia, a book of choice passages from Origen. About Easter in 382 he sent a copy to a friend as a book useful to students of literature.
  • In 553 the emperor Justinian's letter to the council at Constantinople named Gregory the Theologian among the holy Fathers it followed.

How they're remembered

  • Theodoret remembered him among the great teachers of the imperial city, as Gregory of Nazianzus, shining friend and supporter of the truth.
  • Jerome called him a most eloquent man and his instructor in the Scriptures, and counted thirty thousand lines of his writing. He remembered asking Gregory of Nazianzus about a hard verse, and Gregory teasing that he would explain it in church, where the applause would force Jerome to understand.

At Major Councils

  • First Council of Constantinople, 381: Bishop of the city and for a time the council's president; resigned

What Is Uncertain

  • Whether he ever took up Sasima is unclear. It seems he did not, with a rival bishop's troops holding the village and his father pleading with him to stay. His own letters pull two ways. In one he says he was appointed to Sasima, though for a short time he governed Nazianzus out of respect for his father. In another he says he was originally appointed to Nazianzus, "and not to Sasima as some would falsely persuade you".
