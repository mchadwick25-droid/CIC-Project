About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In 380 the Arians had held the churches of Constantinople, the eastern capital, for forty years. Those who still kept to the creed of Nicaea were led by Gregory of Nazianzus, from the church of Cappadocian Christianity. Many bishops had called him there from Asia Minor. They met in a little house of prayer. The church historian Sozomen says it was named Anastasia, the Resurrection, because, he believes, the faith of Nicaea, buried in the city, rose again through Gregory's sermons.

His closest friend was Basil of Caesarea. Yet when Basil made him bishop of Sasima, a village that a rival bishop also claimed, Gregory refused to fight for it. "Why should I fight over piglets and chickens, and ones that are not my own," he wrote to Basil, "as if I were fighting for souls and church canons?"

In Constantinople he preached the Trinity. He would keep, he said, "equally far from the merging of Sabellius and from the division of Arius," the one fusing God together and the other cutting Him into unequal parts. In November 380 the emperor Theodosius ordered the Arian bishop to accept Nicaea or leave the churches, and he left. When some bishops objected to Gregory's election, he gave up the see. In his farewell he said the city's other churches were full again, and he gave the credit to the grace that was with him.

Apollinarius held that Christ had no human mind. Against him Gregory wrote to the priest Cledonius that whatever Christ did not take on, He did not heal. Whatever is joined to His divine nature, Gregory added, is also saved. Three centuries later the church argued over whether Christ had a human will, and Pope Agatho cited Gregory to show that He did.

Jerome called him "my teacher in the Scriptures." In 553 the emperor Justinian told a council at Constantinople that he followed the holy Fathers, and among them he named Gregory the Theologian.

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “Why should I fight over piglets and chickens, and ones that are not my own,”
    As printed
    “Why should I fight for sucking pigs and fowls, and those not my own,”
    Gregory of Nazianzus, Letters 48-50 to Basil, tr. Browne and Swallow
  • In our modern English
    “as if I were fighting for souls and church canons?”
    As printed
    “as though for souls and canons?”
    Gregory of Nazianzus, Letters 48-50 to Basil, tr. Browne and Swallow
    "Canons" may mean church rules or a bishop's canonical rights of jurisdiction, which were at stake in the quarrel. Both readings are kept.
  • In our modern English
    “equally far from the merging of Sabellius and from the division of Arius,”
    As printed
    “equally far from the confusion of Sabellius and from the division of Arius,”
    Gregory of Nazianzus, Oration 39.11, tr. Browne and Swallow
    "Confusion" here means fusing Father, Son and Spirit into one, not bewilderment. "Division" means splitting God into unequal parts.
  • In our modern English
    “my teacher in the Scriptures,”
    As printed
    “my instructor in the Scriptures,”
    Jerome, Lives of Illustrious Men 117, tr. Ernest Cushing Richardson

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Gennadius of Marseilles, calling Gregory of Nazianzus that most eloquent man, reports that Rufinus of Aquileia put his works into Latin along with those of Basil of Caesarea.
  • With Basil of Caesarea he gathered the Philocalia, a book of choice passages from Origen. About Easter in 382 he sent a copy to a friend as a book useful to students of literature.
  • In 553 the emperor Justinian's letter to the council at Constantinople named Gregory the Theologian among the holy Fathers it followed.

How they're remembered

  • Theodoret remembered him among the great teachers of the imperial city, as Gregory of Nazianzus, shining friend and supporter of the truth.
  • Jerome called him a most eloquent man and his instructor in the Scriptures, and counted thirty thousand lines of his writing. He remembered asking Gregory of Nazianzus about a hard verse, and Gregory teasing that he would explain it in church, where the applause would force Jerome to understand.

At Major Councils

  • First Council of Constantinople, 381: Bishop of the city and for a time the council's president; resigned

What Is Uncertain

  • Whether he ever took up Sasima is unclear. It seems he did not, with a rival bishop's troops holding the village and his father pleading with him to stay. His own letters pull two ways. In one he says he was appointed to Sasima, though for a short time he governed Nazianzus out of respect for his father. In another he says he was originally appointed to Nazianzus, "and not to Sasima as some would falsely persuade you".
