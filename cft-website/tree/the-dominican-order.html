The Dominican Order

Toulouse, Paris and Bologna, from 1216 — an order built backwards from a single purpose, and the rare medieval institution that put its whole reason for existing into its own name.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:04

Read along

Dominic de Guzmán was a Spanish cathedral canon who, traveling through Languedoc, concluded that the church was losing the argument with dissenters because its own preachers arrived on horseback with baggage while their opponents walked and fasted. His answer was an order built backwards from a purpose: friars trained in theology, living poor and mobile, whose stated reason for existing was preaching. Approved in 1216, the Order of Preachers wrote itself a constitution of unusual sophistication — elected superiors, representative chapters, and a clause stating that the rule did not bind under sin, so that it could never get in the way of study — and planted itself immediately in the new universities at Paris and Bologna. Its convents of women, in the German lands especially, became one of the very few settings in medieval Europe where women wrote theology and were read.

Documented Stories

Dominic Sells His Books

Three early witnesses tell this story. Jordan of Saxony, in his Libellus on the beginnings of the order, says that while Dominic was studying at Palencia, a severe famine struck almost all of Spain. Moved by the needs of the poor, Dominic sold his books, which he greatly needed, and all his household goods, and gave the money to the poor. His example, Jordan says, moved other theologians and masters to give alms more freely. At the inquest held at Bologna for Dominic’s canonization, two friars also told the story under oath. Brother John of Spain had heard it from Dominic’s fellow canons and from many other men worthy of belief. In his telling, the famine was so bad that many of the poor were dying, and Dominic sold his books and everything he had to feed them. Brother Stephen, prior of the friars’ province of Lombardy, had heard it from great men worthy of belief before he ever saw Dominic. He adds two details. The books were glossed in Dominic’s own hand. And Dominic said he would not study on dead skins while men were dying of hunger. Some men of great standing followed his example, Stephen said, and from then on they began to preach with him.

The Inquisitor on the Road to Milan

Peter of Verona was a Dominican preacher, the son, by the order’s account, of Cathar parents, who had spent twenty years disputing with heretics in Lombardy and had been appointed inquisitor for Milan and Como by Innocent IV in 1251. Milan was full of Cathars with powerful protectors, and Peter had begun prosecuting them. On the road from Como to Milan in April 1252 he and a companion, Brother Dominic, were ambushed by a hired killer named Carino, who split Peter’s skull with a billhook and stabbed the companion. The order’s tradition says Peter began to recite the Creed as he died, or wrote its first word in his own blood. The killer’s employers were traced to the Cathar nobles of Milan and Giussano; Carino himself later repented and died a Dominican lay brother. Innocent IV canonized Peter on 25 March 1253, eleven months after the death, and issued in the following year the bull Ad extirpanda authorizing the use of torture by the inquisition in Italy. Peter became the patron of inquisitors, painted in every Dominican church with a cleaver in his head, and the murder was used to justify the machinery it had provoked. The order that had been founded to win the argument by preaching and poverty here documents itself doing something else: prosecuting, dying for it, and treating the death as a warrant. Both the preaching and the prosecuting were Dominican, and Peter of Verona did both.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced

  • influenced The Carmelites Inferential-Thin — In 1247 the pope sent two men to revise the Carmelite rule. They were Hugh, a cardinal, and William, bishop of Tortosa. The editor of Teresa's Foundations says both were Dominicans. Their letter calls the Carmelites friars, while the pope's own letter still calls them hermits. Neither letter says that the Dominicans shaped the order. So this tie is thin.

Argued Against

  • argued against Cathars / Albigensians Documented — Bernard Gui signed himself in 1309 as a friar of the Order of Preachers and inquisitor of heresy in France. His handbook for inquisitors sets out the errors of 'the Manichaeans of modern times'. That was his name for the Cathars.

In Contact With

  • in contact with The Franciscan Movement Widely Accepted — A Franciscan book, the Mirror of Perfection, tells how Francis and Dominic met in Rome before the cardinal bishop of Ostia. Its date is disputed. In it Dominic wishes that their two orders could become one. In 1888 the historian Henry Charles Lea called them rival orders.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Dominic de Guzmán, a Spanish cathedral canon, concluded that the church was losing arguments with dissenters because its preachers traveled with baggage while their opponents walked and fasted — so he built an order backwards from that single purpose: friars trained in theology, poor and mobile, planted immediately in the new universities at Paris and Bologna. Its 1216 constitution even ruled that the order's own rule did not bind under sin, so it could never get in the way of study. The order's convents of women, especially in the German lands, became one of the few places in medieval Europe where women wrote theology and were read.

Legacy

The Dominicans are still here, and much of what they built outlasted the century. The constitutional machinery of elected superiors and representative chapters, a permanent commitment to study as itself a form of religious life, and the university theology that Albert the Great and Thomas Aquinas produced inside the order and that still anchors Catholic thought. Their German convents fed the mystical writing associated with Meister Eckhart, Johannes Tauler and Henry Suso. The order also supplied the church with much of its inquisitorial personnel, which belongs to the same inheritance and is not separable from it. The rosary's spread through Europe was carried largely by Dominicans, though the traditional story that Dominic himself received it is legend rather than history.

Experience It Today

  • Bologna, Italy — the Basilica of San Domenico, where Dominic is buried in a shrine carved by Nicola Pisano, Niccolò dell'Arca and the young Michelangelo
