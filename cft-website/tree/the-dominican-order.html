The High Medieval Era · 1216-1300 · Toulouse, Paris, Bologna

Toulouse · Paris, today in France and beyond

The Dominican Order

About This Movement

Dominic de Guzmán was a Spanish cathedral canon who, traveling through Languedoc, concluded that the church was losing the argument with dissenters because its own preachers arrived on horseback with baggage while their opponents walked and fasted. His answer was an order built backwards from a purpose: friars trained in theology, living poor and mobile, whose stated reason for existing was preaching. Approved in 1216, the Order of Preachers wrote itself a constitution of unusual sophistication — elected superiors, representative chapters, and a clause stating that the rule did not bind under sin, so that it could never get in the way of study — and planted itself immediately in the new universities at Paris and Bologna. Its convents of women, in the German lands especially, became one of the very few settings in medieval Europe where women wrote theology and were read.

In Its Own Record

Gave Rise To

  • Rhineland Mysticism & the Friends of God Documented Major — In 1326 Meister Eckhart answered his accusers as Brother Eckhart of the Order of Preachers. Henry Suso's Life names his spiritual daughter, Elsbeth Stagel, as a Dominican nun at Töss. She drew his teaching out of him and wrote it down.

Influenced

  • The Carmelites Inferential-Thin — In 1247 the pope sent two men to revise the Carmelite rule. They were Hugh, a cardinal, and William, bishop of Tortosa. The editor of Teresa's Foundations says both were Dominicans. Their letter calls the Carmelites friars, while the pope's own letter still calls them hermits. Neither letter says that the Dominicans shaped the order. So this tie is thin.

Argued Against

  • Cathars / Albigensians Documented — Bernard Gui signed himself in 1309 as a friar of the Order of Preachers and inquisitor of heresy in France. His handbook for inquisitors sets out the errors of 'the Manichaeans of modern times'. That was his name for the Cathars.

In Contact With

  • The Franciscan Movement Widely Accepted — A Franciscan book, the Mirror of Perfection, tells how Francis and Dominic met in Rome before the cardinal bishop of Ostia. Its date is disputed. In it Dominic wishes that their two orders could become one. In 1888 the historian Henry Charles Lea called them rival orders.

Sources

  • Simon Tugwell (ed.), Early Dominicans: Selected Writings (Classics of Western Spirituality, 1982) (primary) — Jordan of Saxony's Libellus, the Nine Ways of Prayer, Humbert of Romans' Treatise on the Formation of Preachers, and the early constitutions in an appendix.
  • The canonization process of St Dominic (Bologna and Toulouse, 1233), in F. C. Lehner, Saint Dominic: Biographical Documents (1964) (primary) — Friars and lay witnesses under oath on what Dominic actually did.
  • Jordan of Saxony, letters to Diana d'Andalo (trans. Gerald Vann, To Heaven with Diana!, 1960) (primary) — The master general's correspondence with the Bologna nuns - the order's women's side and its interior register.
  • Gerard de Frachet, Vitae Fratrum (Lives of the Brethren, c. 1260) (primary) — Communal self-portrait in anecdote: dormitory, road, deathbed - ordinary texture. English translation: Conway, 1924.
  • M. Michele Mulchahey, 'First the Bow is Bent in Study': Dominican Education before 1350 (PIMS, 1998) (secondary) — The formation system as system.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 4, ch. 4 (McGuire); cf. ch. 9 'The Word and its diffusion' (Jansen) (reference) — Preaching is the order's reason for existing.
