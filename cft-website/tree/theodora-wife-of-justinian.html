The Age of Monks and Empires · empress 527-548 · Constantinople

Constantinople, today in Turkey

Theodora, wife of Justinian

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Byzantine Imperial Church (Justinianic).

Known for
The empress, wife of Justinian, who by John of Ephesus's account sheltered the leaders and monks of the party that refused the Council of Chalcedon, and gave the order for James, bishop for Edessa, and Theodore, bishop for Hirtha of the Saracens.
Also called
Theodora the queen, the empress Theodora
Dates
Married to Justinian before he became emperor, by John of Ephesus's account; empress from his accession, in 527 by H. B. Dewing's year. She died in the twentieth year of Justinian's reign, by Michael the Syrian's count; E. W. Brooks gives 548. Her birth year is in no held source
Place
Constantinople. Procopius of Caesarea makes her a child of Byzantium; Michael the Syrian says she was from Daman, near Callinicus
Role
Empress; patron of the party that refused Chalcedon

About This Person

Mare, the exiled bishop of Amida, sent his deacon Stephen from Petra to plead at court. John of Ephesus says God led Stephen to "Theodora who came from the brothel." She was then a patrician, married to Justinian, the emperor's nephew. She begged her husband with tears to ask his uncle for relief. An order came, and the exiles moved to Alexandria.

John wrote from the side she sheltered. R. Payne Smith, who put John's Church History into English, notes that John belonged to the party that refused the Council of Chalcedon. Procopius of Caesarea wrote as her enemy in his Secret History. He says her father kept the bears for the Green faction at Byzantium, and that she went on the stage as a girl. The rest he tells as scandal. H. B. Dewing, who translated his Wars, says we are not forced to believe it. The twelfth-century Michael the Syrian, a patriarch of John's church, made her a priest's daughter from Daman, near Callinicus, raised in chastity.

In the Wars, Procopius gives Theodora a speech in the Nika riot of 532, as the court weighed flight by sea. She ends by approving an ancient saying, "royalty is a good burial-shroud." The words are Procopius's own telling. Dewing notes his love of sounding phrases in speeches.

Evagrius of Epiphania, a later historian on Chalcedon's side, says Justinian upheld the council and Theodora backed the single nature of Christ. Evagrius offers two reasons: true conviction, or an agreement between them. Procopius's Secret History calls it a plan to divide the Christians. Evagrius adds that she persuaded Justinian to send for Severus of Antioch.

John tells what she did for his side. When Justinian raged at Anthimus, who had laid down his office as patriarch of Constantinople, she hid him in her palace for years. She gathered no fewer than five hundred persecuted monks into the palace of Hormisdas. In the sixteenth year of Justinian's reign, the Saracen king Hereth Bar Gabala asked her for bishops. She gave the order, and James was made bishop for Edessa and Theodore for Hirtha of the Saracens.

Michael the Syrian says she died in the twentieth year of Justinian's reign. E. W. Brooks, John's English translator, gives 548. John says she left the emperor a charge to care for her monks.

John calls her the Christ-loving Theodora, "perhaps appointed queen by God to be a support for the persecuted."

Legacy

What traces to them

  • John of Ephesus says she gathered no fewer than five hundred persecuted monks into the palace of Hormisdas in Constantinople, and that after her death the community was kept by her charge to the emperor.
  • At the request of the Saracen king Hereth Bar Gabala, in the sixteenth year of Justinian's reign, she gave the order by which James was made bishop for Edessa and Theodore for Hirtha of the Saracens, John says.
  • She hid Anthimus of Constantinople in her palace for years, John says, and Evagrius of Epiphania says she persuaded Justinian to send for Severus of Antioch.

How they're remembered

  • John of Ephesus calls her the Christ-loving Theodora, "who was perhaps appointed queen by God to be a support for the persecuted".
  • A Syriac chronicle printed under the name of Zachariah Rhetor says the emperor received Severus of Antioch "incited thereto by Theodora the queen, who was devoted to Severus". E. W. Brooks says that part is a later compiler's, since Zachariah's own history ended in 491.
  • Evagrius of Epiphania, on Chalcedon's side: Justinian upheld the council and Theodora "those who maintained the single nature"; "Our people she treated with the warmest kindness".
  • Procopius of Caesarea, in the Secret History, paints her in the blackest colors; H. B. Dewing calls the work's account of her early years one "we are not forced to regard as true".

What Is Uncertain

  • Her witnesses take sides. John of Ephesus wrote from the party she sheltered and belonged, R. Payne Smith notes, to the party that refused Chalcedon. Michael the Syrian, a twelfth-century patriarch of John's church, wrote later from the same side. Procopius of Caesarea wrote the Secret History as her enemy. Evagrius of Epiphania wrote later, from Chalcedon's side.
  • Her origins. Procopius's Secret History makes her the daughter of Acacius, bear-keeper of the Green faction at Byzantium, and an actress and harlot as a girl. John of Ephesus, of the party she sheltered, also calls her "Theodora who came from the brothel". Michael the Syrian, in the twelfth century, makes her a priest's daughter from Daman, near Callinicus, raised in chastity; in another place he says Justinian married her at Mabboug. J.-B. Chabot, Michael's French translator, judges the Mabboug story "inventé par les Monophysites". Nicephorus Callistus, a later Greek church historian, says Cyprus was her homeland. Chabot gives the same to Evagrius, but the held Evagrius has no such line.
  • The Nika speech in Procopius's Wars is Procopius's composition, not a record of her own words. H. B. Dewing notes that in the speeches he is fond of sounding phrases.
  • Why the emperor and empress backed different sides. Evagrius gives two possible reasons, real conviction or a mutual understanding. Procopius's Secret History says the disagreement was "purposely arranged" to divide their subjects.
  • How long Anthimus stayed hidden. John's Lives say about ten years, until her death; E. W. Brooks notes that she lived twelve years after his deposition, and that John's Church History gives seven.
