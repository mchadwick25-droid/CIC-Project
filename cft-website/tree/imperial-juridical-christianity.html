The Imperial Church Era · c. 312-451 · Rome, Constantinople, Milan

Rome, today in Italy · Constantinople, today in Turkey and beyond

Imperial and Juridical Christianity

Conversations with Marius are coming soon to Church in Conversation.

About This Movement

For nearly three centuries the church had been an illegal association. Then, between Constantine's toleration in 313 and Theodosius's legislation at the century's end, it became first protected, then privileged, then the empire's official religion — and had to work out, very fast, what to do with that. Bishops found themselves hearing civil lawsuits, administering public building funds, and traveling to councils at imperial expense; emperors found themselves summoning those councils and expecting the results to hold.

Three answers competed over where final authority in the church now lay, and this Christian tradition's own window closes without settling which was right. Rome traced its authority to Peter. Constantinople claimed rank because it sat beside the throne. The Council of Chalcedon extended that claim in 451, over the protest of Rome's own delegates. The same council also received a letter Leo of Rome had written two years before. It took that letter as the last word on Christ's two natures. Ambrose of Milan staked a third, quite different claim: the emperor stood inside the church, not over it. He said those exact words in 386, from inside his own basilica. Soldiers of a Homoian imperial court were besieging it, because the court wanted the building for its own worship. He would not give it up, and his congregation stayed inside with him. Augustine, then living in Milan, later wrote that they were 'prepared to die with their bishop'. He also wrote that at this time Milan's people began to sing hymns and psalms in the Eastern way. That custom, he says, lasted and spread to almost all congregations elsewhere. Four years later Ambrose proved the same claim again, against Theodosius himself. He refused the emperor the sacrament until he did public penance for a massacre at Thessalonica.

The hardest evidence against this tradition is not what was done to it, but what it did. In 366, a disputed election to the bishopric of Rome left the city so violent that a hundred and thirty-seven people were killed in a single day — the same Damasus whose reign then built the city's great martyr shrines. The same sacramental refusal that later humbled an emperor over Thessalonica had already been used once before, two years earlier, for the opposite end: protecting a bishop who had incited a mob to burn a synagogue at Callinicum from having to pay for it, and leaving the burned community no recourse at all.

In Its Own Record

Influenced By

  • The Church of Roman Palestine before Constantine Documented — Eusebius of Caesarea says he drew on a library at Aelia, or Jerusalem, for his Church History. Bishop Alexander had founded it. The letters kept there had lasted to his own time.
  • The Latin Apologists Inferential-Thin — Jerome says that Lactantius, a Latin apologist, was tutor to Crispus, the son of Constantine. He did this in Gaul, when he was very old. Jerome does not say how far his case for the faith shaped the court.

Influenced

  • Homoian ('Arian') Christianity Documented — The emperor Constantius II called the bishops to two councils in 359. He hoped to bring them all to one creed. A council at Constantinople in 360 confirmed the creed read at Ariminum, and Ulfila of the Goths signed it. In 427 or 428 the Homoian bishop Maximinus still named that creed as his faith.

Argued Against

  • Anomoean (Eunomian) Christianity Documented Major — A law of 19 July 381 forbade the Eunomians and Arians to build churches. That year the Council of Constantinople met at the call of Theodosius. Its first canon condemned the Eunomians by name. A law of 383 also banned their meetings.Lasting result: An imperial law of July 381 forbade Eunomians to build churches in town or country. It seized any land where they did.
  • Pneumatomachian ('Macedonian') Christianity Documented Major — In 381 the Council of Constantinople, in its first canon, condemned by name the Semi-Arians, who were also called Pneumatomachi. A law of 383 banned their meetings too, as well as those of Eunomians, Arians and Macedonians. In 451 the Council of Chalcedon said the creed of 381 set out the faith on the Holy Spirit. It was aimed at those who sought to destroy the Spirit's sovereignty.Lasting result: Theodosius had the bishops meet at Constantinople. In their first canon they condemned a group by name. It was the Semi-Arians, who were also known as the Pneumatomachi.
  • Apollinarian Christianity Documented Major — The Council of Constantinople met in 381. Its first canon condemned the Apollinarians by name. A law of March 388 barred them from the walls of cities. It also cut them off from the company of honest citizens and from the communion of the saints.Lasting result: A law of March 388 drove the Apollinarians from the cities and stripped their bishops of the title of bishop.
  • The Gallic Nicene Episcopate Widely Accepted — Hilary of Poitiers was a bishop in Gaul. He says he was sent into exile after Saturninus won over the emperor. He adds that bishops who stood against the creed forced on the churches were driven out too.
  • Homoian ('Arian') Christianity Documented Major — The Council of Constantinople met in 381 at the call of the emperor Theodosius. Its first canon names the Arians, or Eudoxians, among the heresies it rejects. Laws of 381 and 383 also name the Arians. They bar heretics from building churches or from meeting.Lasting result: In July 383 an imperial law said the Arians could not meet or gather any crowd. Eunomians and other groups were banned as well.
  • Donatism Documented Major — Emperor Constantine gave the Donatists judges. They heard the case at Rome with Miltiades, its bishop. Optatus, a Catholic bishop, says one of the Donatist bishops then made an appeal. Constantine wrote back: 'Oh, mad daring of their fury!'Lasting result: Constantine gave leave for a council of nineteen bishops to meet at Rome. It found Donatus guilty and cleared his rival, Caecilian.
  • Montanism (the New Prophecy) Documented Major — Constantine sent an edict to the heretics, kept in Eusebius of Caesarea's Life of Constantine. It names 'ye who are called Cataphrygians', a common name for the Montanists. It forbids their meetings. It takes away the houses where they met.Lasting result: Constantine's law said the Montanists could not meet, not even in a private house. It urged them to join the catholic Church instead.
  • Novatianism Documented Major — In 325 the Council of Nicaea set terms, in its eighth canon, for receiving 'those who call themselves Cathari'. These were the Novatianists. Their clergy had first to promise in writing to follow the teaching of the Catholic Church.Lasting result: The Council of Nicaea let Novatianist clergy keep their rank. First they had to promise in writing to follow the teaching of the Catholic Church.
  • Ebionite / Nazoraean Current Documented — Eusebius of Caesarea wrote his Church History under Constantine, and it was done around 324. In it he says the evil demon won the Ebionites over to his own ends. The name fit them, he writes, for they held 'poor and mean opinions concerning Christ'.
  • Antiochene Exegetical Christianity (Chrysostom-centered) Documented Major — Two synods condemned John Chrysostom. Then the emperor barred him from his own church. Two months later, as Socrates of Constantinople writes, 'a decree of the emperor sent him into exile'.Lasting result: After synods condemned John Chrysostom, his followers left the church. They met on their own and were known as the Johannites.
  • Priscillianist Asceticism Documented Major — Sulpicius Severus says Spanish bishops got an order from the emperor Gratian that drove the heretics out. Later Maximus, who had seized the throne, heard the case at Trier. He decreed that Priscillian and his friends be put to death.Lasting result: Once the case was reported to the palace, the emperor decreed that Priscillian and his friends be put to death.
  • The Melitian Schism Documented Major — The Council of Nicaea wrote to the church of Alexandria about Melitius in 325. It let him stay in his own city with the bare title of bishop. It forbade him to ordain or name anyone for ordination.Lasting result: In 325 the Council of Nicaea left Melitius the name of bishop but took away his right to ordain.
  • The Luciferians Inferential-Thin — The Luciferian petition of 383 or 384 says that the clergy of Damasus, bishop of Rome, broke up a night vigil held by the priest Macarius. It says they dragged him over the stones. This rests on the petition alone; no account from Damasus's side is in hand.
  • Jovinian Inferential-Thin — Pammachius brought Jovinian's book to Siricius, bishop of Rome, and a synod at Rome condemned it about 390. Siricius then sent word of the sentence to Milan. His own letter is not held here; both facts come from the editors of Jerome and Ambrose.

Opposed By

  • The Gallic Nicene Episcopate Widely Accepted — In On the Councils, Hilary writes to the Eastern bishops. He says Arian bishops misled the emperor into forcing a form of faith on the churches. Hilary stood against the emperor's creed.
  • Donatism Widely Accepted — Optatus says two agents of Emperor Constans, Paul and Macarius, came to Donatus. Donatus burst out: 'What has the Emperor to do with the Church?' Optatus was Donatus's foe, so this is a hostile report.
  • Ambrosian Milan (standalone) Documented Major — In 388 Theodosius ordered a bishop to rebuild a burned synagogue at Callinicum. Ambrose wrote to him against it. He would not go up to the altar until the emperor promised to drop the case. In 390 came the massacre at Thessalonica. Ambrose then wrote that he dared not offer the sacrifice with Theodosius there.Lasting result: Ambrose got Theodosius to promise two things. He would change his edict on the burned synagogue, and he would end the whole inquiry.

Broke With

  • The Church of Alexandria and Miaphysite Egypt Documented Major — On 13 October 451 the Council of Chalcedon told Dioscorus of Alexandria that he had been deposed. A chronicle from the Egyptian side, written in the sixth century, tells what came next. Clergy, monks and people went off to their monasteries. They broke communion with the bishop put in his place.
  • The Luciferians Documented Major — After 362 Lucifer's followers broke with the main church. It had taken back bishops who gave way. Socrates of Constantinople says a new sect arose and still stood apart in his day. The group's own petition rejects the name Luciferians. Augustine says they cut themselves off from the unity of the church.

In Contact With

  • Cappadocian Christianity Documented Major — Sozomen says Theodosius put Gregory of Nazianzus in charge of the churches of Constantinople. In July 381 the emperor made a law. It named the bishops whose communion marked a Catholic church. Helladius of Caesarea and Gregory of Nyssa were two of them. Socrates of Constantinople says the council of 381 gave these two men charge of the church in Pontus.Lasting result: A law of July 381 named Helladius of Caesarea, Gregory of Nyssa and others. It said all churches must go to bishops in communion with them.
  • Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity Documented — Writing from the East to Damasus of Rome, Jerome said he kept communion only with 'the chair of Peter'. He later recalled helping Damasus with his church letters, answering questions sent by councils of East and West.
  • Antiochene Exegetical Christianity (Chrysostom-centered) Documented Major — John Chrysostom was made bishop of Constantinople. Socrates says it was the emperor's court that chose him. The chamberlain Eutropius told Theophilus of Alexandria to consecrate John or face trial. Back in 381 the emperor had made a law. It named the bishops whose communion marked a Catholic church, and Diodore of Tarsus was one of them.Lasting result: A law of July 381 named Diodore of Tarsus and others. It said that every church must go to bishops in communion with them.
  • Gallic Monastic-Ascetic Christianity Documented — Martin of Tours was at the emperor Maximus's court at Trier during the Priscillian affair. Sulpicius Severus says he begged Maximus not to shed the blood of the accused. He called it an unheard-of outrage for a secular ruler to judge a church case.
  • Persian Church of the East (early) Documented — In 410 a synod met at Seleucia-Ctesiphon. Its acts call Bishop Marutha of Maipherqat the go-between for peace between East and West. The Persian bishops then agreed to follow the western forms of worship that Isaac and Marutha had taught them.
  • Novatianism Documented — Constantine called Acesius, a bishop of the Novatians, to the Council of Nicaea. Acesius told him the council had laid down nothing new. Yet he kept his church apart. Socrates of Constantinople had the story from Auxanon, a Novatian priest. As a young man, Auxanon went to Nicaea with Acesius.
  • Jerusalem Liturgical-Pilgrimage Christianity Documented — Eusebius of Caesarea copies a letter from Constantine to Macarius, bishop of Jerusalem. It is about building a church at the site of Christ's tomb. The emperor writes that he has no greater care than to adorn that spot with a splendid building.
  • The Luciferians Documented Major — In 383 or 384 two Luciferian priests, Marcellinus and Faustinus, sent a petition to the emperors. The emperors wrote back to their prefect Cynegius. Their letter called the two men priests full of faith.Lasting result: The emperors told their prefect to shield two bishops, Gregory and Heraclida, and priests like them. No one was to harm them.

Sources

  • Eusebius, Life of Constantine (primary) — The most admiring contemporary account of Constantine's reign, by the bishop who wrote the first history of the church.
  • Eusebius, Church History (primary) — Covers the church's transition from illegal association to imperial favor through Eusebius's own lifetime.
  • Ambrose of Milan, Letter 51 (primary) — Ambrose's letter to Theodosius after the massacre at Thessalonica in 390, refusing to offer the sacrifice in the emperor's presence until he did penance.
  • Ambrose of Milan, Letters 40 and 41 (primary) — The Callinicum affair, 388: Ambrose's argument against making a bishop rebuild a synagogue his own mob had burned, and his account of refusing Theodosius the sacrament until the order was withdrawn.
  • Socrates Scholasticus, Ecclesiastical History IV.29 (primary) — The contested election of Damasus as bishop of Rome in 366, and the violence it cost the city.
  • Leo the Great, Letter 28 ("The Tome") (primary) — Leo's 449 letter to Flavian of Constantinople on the two natures of Christ, read aloud and received as settling the question at Chalcedon in 451.
  • Peter Brown, The Rise of Western Christendom: Triumph and Diversity, A.D. 200–1000 (secondary) — Blackwell, 1996 (2nd ed. 2003) — a standard account of how imperial favor reshaped church institutions and authority.
