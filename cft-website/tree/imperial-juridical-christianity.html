About This Movement

For nearly three centuries the church had been an illegal association. Then, between Constantine's toleration in 313 and Theodosius's legislation at the century's end, it became first protected, then privileged, then the empire's official religion — and had to work out, very fast, what to do with that. Bishops found themselves hearing civil lawsuits, administering public building funds, and traveling to councils at imperial expense; emperors found themselves summoning those councils and expecting the results to hold.

Three answers competed for where final authority over the church now actually lay, and this Christian tradition's own window closes without settling which was right. Rome traced its authority to Peter; Constantinople claimed rank because it sat beside the throne — a claim the Council of Chalcedon extended in 451 over the protest of Rome's own delegates, at the same council that received, as the last word on Christ's two natures, a letter Leo of Rome had written two years before. Ambrose of Milan staked a third claim entirely: that the emperor stood inside the church, not over it. He said those exact words from inside his own basilica in 386, besieged by the soldiers of a Homoian imperial court that wanted the building for its own worship. He would not give it up, and his congregation stayed inside with him — prepared, an eyewitness later wrote, to die with their bishop. It was there, waiting through the night, that Milan's people first sang hymns the way the Eastern churches did, a custom that, by that eyewitness's account, never stopped and spread to churches across the world. Four years later Ambrose proved the same claim again, against Theodosius himself, refusing the emperor the sacrament until he did public penance for a massacre at Thessalonica.

The hardest evidence against this tradition is not what was done to it, but what it did. In 366, a disputed election to the bishopric of Rome left the city so violent that a hundred and thirty-seven people were killed in a single day — the same Damasus whose reign then built the city's great martyr shrines. The same sacramental refusal that later humbled an emperor over Thessalonica had already been used once before, two years earlier, for the opposite end: protecting a bishop who had incited a mob to burn a synagogue at Callinicum from having to pay for it, and leaving the burned community no recourse at all.

In Its Own Record

Influenced By

  • The Church of Roman Palestine before Constantine Documented — Eusebius says he drew material for his Church History from the library at Aelia (Jerusalem) that Bishop Alexander founded. The letters in it were kept until his own time.
  • The Latin Apologists Inferential-Thin — Jerome reports that Lactantius, a Latin apologist, tutored Crispus, Constantine's son, in Gaul in his old age. No source here shows how far his argument shaped the court.

Argued Against

  • Anomoean (Eunomian) Christianity Documented — A law of 19 July 381 forbade Eunomians and Arians to build churches. Canon 1 of the Council of Constantinople, held that year at Theodosius's summons, condemned the Eunomians by name. A law of 383 also banned their assemblies.
  • Pneumatomachian ('Macedonian') Christianity Documented — Canon 1 of the Council of Constantinople (381) anathematized the Semi-Arians, called Pneumatomachi, by name. A law of 383 also banned assemblies of Eunomians, Arians, Macedonians and Pneumatomachi.
  • Apollinarian Christianity Documented — Canon 1 of the Council of Constantinople (381) anathematized the Apollinarians by name. A law of March 388 barred them from city walls, from the company of honest citizens and from the communion of the saints.
  • The Gallic Nicene Episcopate Widely Accepted — Hilary of Poitiers, a bishop in Gaul, was exiled after Saturninus won over the emperor. Hilary adds that bishops who opposed the imposed creed were driven into exile.
  • Homoian ('Arian') Christianity Documented — The Council of Constantinople met in 381 at the emperor Theodosius's summons. Its first canon names the Arians, or Eudoxians, among the heresies it rejects. Imperial laws of 381 and 383 also name the Arians when they bar heretics from building churches or holding assemblies.
  • Donatism Documented — Emperor Constantine granted the Donatists judges, who heard their case at Rome with Miltiades, the city's bishop. Optatus, a Catholic bishop, reports that a Donatist bishop then appealed. Constantine replied: 'Oh, mad daring of their fury!'

Opposed By

  • The Gallic Nicene Episcopate Widely Accepted — In On the Councils, Hilary tells the Eastern bishops that Arian bishops misled the emperor into imposing a form of faith on the churches. Hilary opposed that imperial creed.
  • Donatism Widely Accepted — Optatus says that agents of Emperor Constans, Paul and Macarius, came to Donatus. Donatus burst out: 'What has the Emperor to do with the Church?' Optatus opposed Donatus, so this is a hostile report.

In Contact With

  • Persian Church of the East (early) Documented — The acts of the 410 Synod of Seleucia-Ctesiphon call Bishop Marutha of Maipherqat the mediator of peace between East and West. The Persian bishops then agreed to follow the western forms of worship that Isaac and Marutha had taught them.

Sources

  • Eusebius, Life of Constantine (primary) — The most admiring contemporary account of Constantine's reign, by the bishop who wrote the first history of the church.
  • Eusebius, Church History (primary) — Covers the church's transition from illegal association to imperial favor through Eusebius's own lifetime.
  • Ambrose of Milan, Letter 51 (primary) — Ambrose's letter to Theodosius after the massacre at Thessalonica in 390, refusing to offer the sacrifice in the emperor's presence until he did penance.
  • Ambrose of Milan, Letters 40 and 41 (primary) — The Callinicum affair, 388: Ambrose's argument against making a bishop rebuild a synagogue his own mob had burned, and his account of refusing Theodosius the sacrament until the order was withdrawn.
  • Socrates Scholasticus, Ecclesiastical History IV.29 (primary) — The contested election of Damasus as bishop of Rome in 366, and the violence it cost the city.
  • Leo the Great, Letter 28 ("The Tome") (primary) — Leo's 449 letter to Flavian of Constantinople on the two natures of Christ, read aloud and received as settling the question at Chalcedon in 451.
  • Peter Brown, The Rise of Western Christendom: Triumph and Diversity, A.D. 200–1000 (secondary) — Blackwell, 1996 (2nd ed. 2003) — a standard account of how imperial favor reshaped church institutions and authority.
