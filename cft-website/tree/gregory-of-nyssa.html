About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In 379, after his brother Basil died, Gregory came to her monastery and found his sister Macrina dying. She told him it was their parents' prayers that had lifted him up, since he had little of his own to explain such success.

Macrina had turned him around once before. As a young man Gregory gave up reading the sacred books to the people and taught rhetoric. His friend Gregory of Nazianzus asked in a letter why he would rather be called a professor of rhetoric than of Christianity. But it was his sister who persuaded him to join the family's monastery. In 372 Basil made him bishop of Nyssa over his protest. Later an imperial official, whom Basil calls a friend of heretics, had Gregory summoned on charges about church money and his ordination. Basil answered that if any church rule had been broken, the blame lay with those who ordained him, not with a man forced to serve. In 376 Gregory was banished, until the emperor Valens died in 378.

He took up Basil's fight with Eunomius, who argued that a gulf separated the Father, who is unbegotten, from the Son, who is begotten. Gregory defended the Son's eternal generation, and said that in this life we can never know God's name. In 381 he brought a treatise against Eunomius to the council at Constantinople, and the emperor named him one of the bishops by whose communion the Catholic church would be known.

In his dialogue on the soul, where Macrina speaks, those who have parted with evil are united with God, who will be all in all. Some read that as hope for the restoration of everyone. Yet some of his sermons speak of eternal fire with no hint of it, so readers argue over his meaning.

A later council made one of his letters part of church law, and the second council of Nicaea called him the Father of Fathers. Because we were made changeable, he wrote in the Great Catechism, we can keep moving toward the good, and there "the advance has no check."

Key Works

  • The Great Catechism: A reasoned statement of the faith, opening with the Trinity.
  • On the Soul and the Resurrection: A dialogue in which his sister Macrina answers his questions about death.
  • Life of Macrina: His account of his sister's life, holiness and death.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Theodoret copied a passage from his sermon on Abraham into a collection of witnesses from the Fathers.
  • In 553 the emperor Justinian's letter to the council at Constantinople named him among the holy Fathers whose writings on the true faith it followed.
  • In 692 the council in Trullo set its seal on the canons of Gregory, Bishop of Nyssa, and his canonical letter to Letoius, bishop of Melitene, stands among them.

How they're remembered

  • Theodoret counted him and Basil of Caesarea among the foremost champions of piety in Cappadocia. He wrote that the brothers taught their own age and left their teaching in their writings as a goodly heritage.
  • Jerome heard Gregory of Nyssa read a work against Eunomius aloud, together with Gregory of Nazianzus.

At Major Councils

  • First Council of Constantinople, 381: Present; gave the funeral oration for Meletius

What Is Uncertain

  • Whether Gregory of Nyssa hoped that all would at last be restored is still argued. Those who doubt it point to his sermons On the Poor, On the Beatitudes and Against Usurers, which speak of eternal punishment. Others answer that a sermon meant to stir its hearers weighs less than a careful dialogue.
  • A later tradition says he married a woman named Theosebeia. Nicephorus Callistus first tells it, in the thirteenth century. It seems to rest on two passages: a letter of Gregory of Nazianzus that calls a Theosebeia a priest's yokefellow, and Gregory of Nyssa's own complaint in On Virginity that he is cut off from virginity. None of his writings speaks of her as his wife.
