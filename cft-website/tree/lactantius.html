The Early Church Era · from 303 · Nicomedia, Gaul

Lactantius

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Latin Apologists.

Also called Firmianus, Lucius Caecilius Firmianus Lactantius (the fullest form in the book titles of some copies of the Divine Institutes)

Lactantius was teaching Latin public speaking at Nicomedia, the city where the emperor Diocletian kept his palace, when the Great Persecution began in 303. He answered it with the Divine Institutes, seven books that argue the faith to educated pagans and hold that religion cannot be forced.

About This Person

"I myself saw in Bithynia a governor astonishingly elated with joy, as if he had conquered some nation of barbarians. It was because one man, who had held out for two years with great courage, seemed at last to give way." Lactantius wrote this during the persecution of the Christians, among governors who boasted that they had shed no blood. The judges, he said, knew it was a contest, and tortured to win.

Lactantius was teaching Latin public speaking at Nicomedia in Bithynia, in what is now north-west Turkey, the city where the emperor Diocletian kept his palace, when the Great Persecution began in 303. He answered with the Divine Institutes, seven books arguing the faith to educated pagans from their own philosophers and historians. The books hold that religion cannot be forced, since it lives only in the will. He is the last on the tree of the Latin Apologists, who made the case for the faith in Latin.

Most of his life outside his books rests on Jerome, a Christian writer later that century, who says he came from Africa. He was called to Bithynia to teach, he says, and was teaching when "the temple of God was overthrown." The imperial order leveled churches, burned Scriptures, stripped high-ranking Christians of their honors, and took freedom from Christian servants who would not give up the faith. Its aim, Lactantius writes, was to make Christians worship the gods "by force and tortures."

Then two men published books against the Christians "in my presence, and to my grief," he says. One, as he tells it, a philosopher who praised poverty, wrote three books saying a philosopher's duty was to call people back to the gods. The other, a judge who had pressed for the persecution, wrote two books to the Christians, to prove their Scripture contradicted itself.

He set out to answer them and all like them. Of the Christian writers before him, he found Tertullian learned in every kind of literature, but rough and very obscure. Cyprian, the most renowned, had answered a pagan with Scripture the man thought false. A beginner needs milk before solid food, Lactantius says, and must be refuted by his own authorities.

He took his title from the lawyers' handbooks of the civil law, called Institutes, written to settle quarrels. He called his Divine, a full course on hope, life, salvation, immortality and God. The pagan gods, he argued, had once been men. The philosophers refuted one another, and there could be no religion without wisdom, nor wisdom without religion.

Religion cannot be compelled, he wrote, so it must be argued with words rather than blows. The pagans said their rites must be defended. He agreed religion must be defended with all one's strength: "For religion must be defended not by killing but by dying, not by cruelty but by endurance, not by crime but by faithfulness."

A book on how the persecutors died survives in one copy under the name Lucius Caecilius, and whether it is Lactantius's is disputed. It tells how a dream directed the emperor Constantine to mark his soldiers' shields with the sign of Christ. In extreme old age, Lactantius taught Constantine's son Crispus in Gaul.

Jerome wished Lactantius could have built up the faith as easily as he tore down other people's. Yet he asked who could stop him, Jerome, from reading the Institutes, written against the pagans with much ability.

Lucifer of Cagliari, a bishop, wrote to Constantine's son, the emperor Constantius, who backed the Arian teaching that there was a time when the Son of God did not exist. You say this teaching must be defended, Lucifer told him, and you defend it with the sword, but we know religion is defended not by killing but by dying for God. Lucifer had taken over Lactantius's sentences without naming him, recast to address the emperor.

Jerome called Lactantius the most eloquent man of his time, yet so poor in this life that he often lacked even what he needed.

Quotations and references

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “I myself saw in Bithynia a governor astonishingly elated with joy, as if he had conquered some nation of barbarians. It was because one man, who had held out for two years with great courage, seemed at last to give way.”
    As printed
    “uidi ego in Bithynia praesidem gaudio mirabiliter elatum tamquam barbarorum gentem aliquam subegisset, quod unus qui per biennium magna uirtute restiterat, postremo cedere uisus esset”
    Lactantius, Divine Institutes V.11.15, Latin ed. Samuel Brandt, English tr. William Fletcher
    Rendered from Brandt's Latin, not Fletcher's English. Uidi ego is I myself saw. Fletcher's præfect is the brief's governor (praesidem, the governor of a province), and his wonderfully is the brief's astonishingly (mirabiliter). It was because renders quod. A second scan of Brandt's edition reads the same Latin (lines 33611-33614 of that file).
  • In our modern English
    “the temple of God was overthrown.”
    As printed
    “dei templum euerteretur”
    Lactantius, Divine Institutes V.2.2, Latin ed. Samuel Brandt, English tr. William Fletcher
    Rendered from Brandt's Latin, which agrees with Fletcher's English word for word. Lactantius does not say whether he means the church building at Nicomedia or the church at large.
  • In our modern English
    “by force and tortures.”
    As printed
    “ui atque tormentis”
    Lactantius, Divine Institutes V.19.6 (chapter 20 in the English edition), Latin ed. Samuel Brandt, English tr. William Fletcher
  • In our modern English
    “in my presence, and to my grief,”
    As printed
    “praesente me ac dolente”
    Lactantius, Divine Institutes V.4.1, Latin ed. Samuel Brandt, English tr. William Fletcher
    Rendered from Brandt's Latin, which agrees with Fletcher's English word for word.
  • In our modern English
    “For religion must be defended not by killing but by dying, not by cruelty but by endurance, not by crime but by faithfulness.”
    As printed
    “defendenda enim religio est non occidendo sed moriendo, non saeuitia sed patientia, non scelere sed fide”
    Lactantius, Divine Institutes V.19.22 (chapter 20 in the English edition), Latin ed. Samuel Brandt, English tr. William Fletcher
    Rendered from Brandt's Latin, not Fletcher's English. Fletcher's putting to death is the brief's killing (occidendo). Patientia is endurance under suffering. Fides here is keeping faith, as a soldier keeps faith with his king in the lines that follow, so the brief has faithfulness for Fletcher's good faith, and crime for his guilt (scelere). A second scan of Brandt's edition reads the same Latin.

Timeline

Dates
No old source gives the year of his birth or of his death. He was teaching in Bithynia when the persecution began, in the nineteenth year of the emperor Diocletian as Eusebius counts it, so in 303. His own books were written during and after the persecution, which they describe, and name no year. Jerome puts his teaching of Crispus in his extreme old age.
Place
From Africa, Jerome says, who tells of a verse account of his journey from Africa to Nicomedia. Nicomedia was a Greek-speaking city in Bithynia, a Roman province on the Black Sea in north-west Asia Minor, now north-west Turkey. The emperor Diocletian had his palace there, and Lactantius taught Latin public speaking there. Later Gaul, where Jerome says he taught Crispus; Jerome names no city.
Role
Teacher of Latin public speaking at Nicomedia; Christian writer in Latin

Key Works

  • Divine Institutes: seven books arguing the faith to educated pagans from their own philosophers and historians. He took the title from the lawyers' handbooks of the civil law, called Institutes, written to settle quarrels; his are on hope, life, salvation, immortality and God. Book I argues that the pagan gods had once been men, and calls the Sibyls, prophetesses whose verses the pagans kept, as witnesses against them. Book II traces the origin of error, and Book III sets the philosophers against one another. Book IV joins true wisdom and religion, holding that there is no religion without wisdom, nor wisdom without religion. Book V turns to the persecution and holds that religion cannot be forced, and must be defended by dying, not by killing. Book VI is on true worship. Book VII holds that the world lasts six thousand years, and that righteousness then reigns on earth for a thousand. Some copies open with an address to the emperor Constantine, and whether Lactantius wrote it is disputed
  • On the Workmanship of God: a short book on the making of man, addressed to Demetrianus, which Jerome calls On the work of God or the creation of man. It opens with Lactantius writing that he is disturbed, and in the greatest necessities
  • On the Deaths of the Persecutors: a book of disputed authorship, which survives in one copy under the name Lucius Caecilius. It is addressed to Donatus, who had been tortured nine times and was freed from a jail where he had lain six years. It tells how the emperors who persecuted the Christians died, so that later generations might learn from it. It tells of the fall of the church at Nicomedia, the edicts, and Galerius's disease and his edict letting Christians be Christians again. It tells of Constantine's dream before battle, the edict of Milan posted at Nicomedia, and the killing of the persecutors' families, among them a boy of eight and a girl of seven. It counts ten years and about four months from the overthrow of the church to its restoration. The Library holds it in English, in Lord Hailes' translation, and its chapter 48, the edict of Milan, in Latin

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Lucifer of Cagliari, a bishop, wrote to the emperor Constantius, Constantine's son, who backed the Arian teaching that there was a time when the Son of God did not exist. He took over Lactantius's sentences on defending religion by dying, not by killing, without naming him, and turned them on the emperor.
  • Augustine says Lactantius brought in the Sibylline testimonies bit by bit as his argument needed them, and that he himself set them down in one connected series in the City of God.
  • In On Christian Teaching, Augustine counts Lactantius among the faithful who came out of Egypt loaded with its gold, his image for pagan learning taken into Christian use.
  • The book on the persecutors' deaths keeps the text of two imperial orders: Galerius's edict letting Christians be Christians again, and the edict of Milan, which gave Christians and all others the freedom to follow the religion each chose, and whose Latin survives there.
  • Calvin ends one argument with Lactantius, that no religion is genuine that is not in accordance with truth, and tells readers to read Lactantius and Eusebius on images.
  • In a letter to the parish priest of Cernex, Calvin calls Lactantius an ancient doctor of the Church, meaning one of its early teachers. He writes that Lactantius and Augustine show that in their time the world's troubles were blamed on the Gospel, because it had done away with the pagan rites.

How they're remembered

  • Jerome says Lactantius has a flow of eloquence worthy of Tully, meaning Cicero, and wishes he could have built up the faith as easily as he tore down that of others.
  • Jerome writes that in Lactantius's books one finds a summary of Cicero's dialogues.
  • Jerome charges that Lactantius, in his books and above all in his letters to Demetrian, denies the Holy Spirit a being of his own. He takes the passages on the Spirit, Jerome says, to mean the Father or the Son. Yet Jerome asks who can forbid him to read the Institutes, in which Lactantius wrote against the pagans with much ability.
  • Jerome's Chronicle calls him the most eloquent man of his time and says he was so poor in this life that he often lacked even the necessities.
  • Jerome counts him among those who held that after the resurrection the Lord would reign in the flesh with the saints.
  • A list of books received and not received, which its heading credits to Pope Gelasius and a second copy to Pope Hormisdas, sets the little works of Lactantius among the books not received.

Open Questions

  • Most of his life outside his own books rests on one witness, Jerome, who wrote later in the same century. Lactantius himself says only that he was called to Bithynia to teach, and names no one who called him.
  • Where he was born. Jerome says he came from Africa, and no old source names the place of his birth.
  • Where he wrote his Banquet. The English of Jerome's notice and one Latin print say he wrote it as a young man in Africa; the other Latin print lacks the words in Africa. Both have the journey from Africa to Nicomedia.
  • His name. Jerome calls him Firmianus, known also as Lactantius. Some copies of the Divine Institutes call him Caecilius Firmianus Lactantius in their book titles, some with the first name Lucius. The one copy of the book on the persecutors' deaths names its author Lucius Caecilius.
  • When and where he became a Christian is not known. He was one when the persecution began, since he writes that the attacks on the Christians were published in his presence and to his grief.
  • Whether he saw the church fall. He writes only that he was teaching in Bithynia when the temple of God was overthrown, and does not say he watched it. The scene of the church at Nicomedia leveled is told in the book on the persecutors' deaths.
  • Who wrote the book on the persecutors' deaths is disputed. It survives in one copy, which names its author Lucius Caecilius. Jerome lists among Lactantius's books one On persecution, and no old source says it is this book.
  • The cause of the palace fire at Nicomedia. The book on the persecutors' deaths says Galerius's agents set it, and the blame was laid on the Christians. Eusebius says it broke out he knows not how, and was laid on the Christians through a false suspicion. Constantine, in the speech Eusebius keeps, says lightning destroyed the palace and the emperor's chamber, and that eyewitnesses, he among them, declare it.
  • The address to Constantine. Some copies of the Divine Institutes open with an address to the emperor Constantine, and others lack it. Whether Lactantius wrote it is disputed.
  • The two writers he answers. The philosopher who praised poverty and the judge who pressed for the persecution are not named in the Divine Institutes.
  • When he taught Crispus. Jerome puts it in his extreme old age, and gives no year.
  • How, when and where he died is not known.
