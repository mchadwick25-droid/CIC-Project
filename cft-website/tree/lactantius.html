The Early Church Era · c. 303-325 · Nicomedia, Gaul

Lactantius

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Latin Apologists.

Known for
A teacher of Latin rhetoric from Africa who was at Nicomedia when the Great Persecution began in 303. He answered it with the Divine Institutes, a handbook of the true religion argued to educated pagans from their own poets and philosophers, which holds that religion cannot be forced. On the Deaths of the Persecutors, which most scholars hold his, tells how the persecuting emperors died. Jerome says that late in life he taught Constantine's son Crispus in Gaul.
Also called
Firmianus, L. Caecilius Firmianus
Dates
No birth year and no death year is held. Jerome says he was summoned to Nicomedia in Diocletian's reign; Paul Monceaux reckons he arrived about 290, at about forty. He was teaching in Bithynia when the church at Nicomedia fell, on the day the editors convert to February 23, 303. Schanz holds that he had left Bithynia by 305 or 306, and Monceaux that he left in late 305 or 306. Monceaux says he conceived the Divine Institutes about the start of the persecution and wrote them between early 307 and early 311, and puts On the Deaths of the Persecutors about 314. Jerome places the teaching of Crispus in his extreme old age; Crispus was made Caesar on March 1, 317, and Monceaux puts the teaching about 316, Schanz about 317. William Fletcher's notice says he is believed to have died at Trier (Treves) about 325; Monceaux says nothing at all is known of his last years or the date of his death
Place
From Africa, by Jerome, who calls him a pupil of Arnobius and says he wrote a Banquet as a young man in Africa and an Itinerary, in hexameters, of a journey from Africa to Nicomedia; Nicomedia in Bithynia, the Greek-speaking city that Diocletian made his eastern capital, where he taught Latin rhetoric; Gaul, where Jerome says he taught Crispus. Monceaux holds that he was almost surely born in Africa, probably in the region of Cirta and Mascula; William Fletcher reports an older view that he came from Firmum on the Adriatic, which Monceaux rejects. Jerome says only Gaul, not Trier
Role
Teacher of Latin rhetoric at Nicomedia; Christian writer in Latin

About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

At first light, as Lactantius's own account tells it, officials forced the doors of the church at Nicomedia, burned its Scriptures and looted the rest. The emperor Diocletian, watching from his palace, feared fire would spread, so guards with axes leveled the church within hours. Lactantius, a teacher of Latin speech from Africa, was teaching in Bithynia, the province around Nicomedia.

It was 303, and the Great Persecution had begun, the attack on Christians that Diocletian and his junior emperor Galerius started there. Lactantius answered with the Divine Institutes, a handbook of the true religion. It argued the faith to educated pagans from their own poets and philosophers, and held that religion cannot be forced. A second book told how the persecutors died. Late in life, Jerome says, he taught Constantine's son Crispus in Gaul.

Jerome, our witness for his life, writing some seventy years later, says he was summoned in Diocletian's reign to teach rhetoric, the art of public speaking, in Latin. But Nicomedia spoke Greek, pupils were few, and he turned to writing. When he became a Christian is not known.

The day after the church fell, an edict, an emperor's public order, stripped Christians of rank and laid them open to torture. Fire broke out in the palace. Lactantius blames agents of Galerius. Eusebius of Caesarea says he does not know how it began, and Constantine, who was in the city, later said lightning. Priests and servants were killed, some burned in crowds, some drowned with millstones around their necks.

While the church lay in ruins, two men read out books against the faith before Lactantius. One was a philosopher who preached poverty but dined like a lord, the other a judge who had urged the persecution. He wrote: "Spurred on by their proud ungodliness, by my awareness of the truth itself, and, as I believe, by God, I took up this task."

Of the Latin defenders before him, Tertullian, he judged, had been obscure, and Cyprian had answered a pagan with Scripture the pagan thought false. A newcomer, he says, needs milk before solid food and must be refuted from his own authorities.

His seven books argue that the gods were once men, the philosophers contradict each other, and wisdom and religion belong together. True worship is a pure mind, not blood or incense, and the world will run as it is for six thousand years. He argues from Cicero, Rome's great orator, and from the Sibyls, pagan prophetesses whose verses the Romans revered. Amid the persecution he wrote, "Religion must be defended not by killing but by dying, not by cruelty but by patient endurance, not by crime but by faithfulness."

As Lactantius tells it, Galerius, dying of a rotting disease, ordered in 311 that Christians might be Christians again. Two years later the edict of Milan returned their churches. Lactantius copied the text of both into his book.

That book, On the Deaths of the Persecutors, was written around 314 for a friend tortured nine times, so later ages might learn how God dealt with persecutors. It ends with the victor killing their families, a boy of eight and a girl of seven among them. Thus, Lactantius says, the wicked received what their deeds had earned. Most scholars hold it his; some have doubted.

How and when Lactantius died is not known.

Jerome praised an eloquence like Cicero's, but wished Lactantius had been as ready to teach Christian doctrine as to pull down pagan belief. He charged that Lactantius denied the Holy Spirit was a person, yet asked who could forbid reading the Institutes. Augustine of Hippo took the Sibyl's verses Lactantius had quoted and set them out together in the City of God. His book on the persecutors keeps the edict of Milan's Latin, a modern editor notes. A modern historian calls it the most exact account of those years, and a partisan one.

Jerome wrote of the man, "He was the most eloquent man of his time, but so poor in this life that he often lacked even the necessities."

Quotations are given in our modern English. The printed wording of each is below.

Quotations and references

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “Spurred on by their proud ungodliness, by my awareness of the truth itself, and, as I believe, by God, I took up this task.”
    As printed
    “being incited both by the arrogant impiety of these, and by the consciousness of truth itself, and (as I think) by God, I have undertaken this office”
    Lactantius, Divine Institutes V.4, English tr. William Fletcher, Latin ed. Samuel Brandt
    Rendered from Brandt's Latin, not Fletcher's English. The brief ends the quotation at this task; Lactantius's sentence goes on, in Fletcher's English, that with all the strength of my mind I might refute the accusers of righteousness. Fletcher's arrogant impiety of these is the brief's proud ungodliness (superba inpietate), and his this office is the brief's this task (munus). Brandt's apparatus reports that earlier editors thought a word was lost after deo; the printed text reads as quoted. A second scan of Brandt's edition reads the same Latin apart from scan faults.
  • In our modern English
    “Religion must be defended not by killing but by dying, not by cruelty but by patient endurance, not by crime but by faithfulness.”
    As printed
    “For religion is to be defended, not by putting to death, but by dying; not by cruelty, but by patient endurance; not by guilt, but by good faith”
    Lactantius, Divine Institutes V.19 (chapter 20 in the English edition), English tr. William Fletcher, Latin ed. Samuel Brandt
    Rendered from Brandt's Latin, not Fletcher's English. Fletcher's putting to death is the brief's killing (occidendo), his guilt and good faith are the brief's crime (scelere) and faithfulness (fide). A second scan of Brandt's edition reads the same Latin.
  • In our modern English
    “He was the most eloquent man of his time, but so poor in this life that he often lacked even the necessities.”
    As printed
    “uir omnium suo tempore eloquentissimus, sed adeo in hac uita pauper, ut plerumque etiam necessariis indiguerit”
    Jerome, Chronicle, the entry for the year Crispus was made Caesar, as Prosper copies it in Brandt's collection of ancient witnesses to Lactantius (Latin)
    No English translation of the Chronicle is held, so the brief renders the Latin. The printed wording is Prosper's copy of Jerome's entry, which leaves out Latinis litteris (taught Crispus in Latin letters) before the quoted words; the words quoted are the same as in Jerome's own entry in Fotheringham's edition (lines 44984-44986 of that file), where a margin number and the word olymp stand beside the last line and eloquentissimus breaks at the line end. Brandt's collection also prints Jerome's own entry (lines 11241-11243) with the same words and a stray letter before tempore.

Key Works

  • Divine Institutes: seven books, a handbook of the true religion argued to educated pagans: the gods who were once men, the philosophers who contradict one another, true wisdom and religion, justice, true worship, and the happy life and the end of the world after six thousand years and a thousand-year reign. Book V says religion cannot be forced and must be defended by dying, not by killing. In it he says he trained young men in oratory not to virtue but to cunning wickedness, that he was never eloquent because he never engaged in public speaking, and that he saw in Bithynia a governor elated because one Christian who had held out for two years seemed at last to yield. He says Tertullian was obscure and Cyprian answered a pagan with Scripture the pagan thought false, and that a newcomer must be refuted by his own authorities. Some copies open with an address to Constantine
  • On the Deaths of the Persecutors: addressed to Donatus, who had been tortured nine times and was freed from a jail where he had lain six years. It tells the fall of the church at Nicomedia, the edict, the palace fires, the man who tore the edict down and was burned alive, Galerius's disease and the edict of 311, the edict of Milan posted at Nicomedia, Constantine's dream before the battle at Rome, and the deaths of the emperors who persecuted, ending with the killing of their families, among them a boy of eight and a girl of seven. It counts ten years and about four months from the overthrow of the church to its restoration. It survives in one manuscript, and the Library holds it in English only, in Lord Hailes' translation
  • On the Workmanship of God: a short book on the making of man, addressed to Demetrianus, which Jerome calls On the work of God or the creation of man. It opens by saying how disturbed he is and in the greatest necessities. Monceaux dates it to the last months of 305, a little before the Divine Institutes, and reads its opening as the cry of a man who had just lost his school, which is his inference

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Augustine says Lactantius used the Sibylline testimonies, bringing them in bit by bit as his argument needed them, and that he himself set them down in one connected series in the City of God.
  • His book on the persecutors keeps the edict of Milan: A. C. McGiffert notes that the greater part of the edict is extant in its original Latin in chapter 48 of Lactantius's book, and that the Greek translation in Eusebius's Church History is still less accurate.
  • Paul Monceaux says the pamphlet rests on the most exact and complete account we have of those years, and that coins confirm many facts that Lactantius alone reports; he also calls it very partisan, with every persecutor a bad prince and every one punished.
  • Monceaux says he is rarely named in early Christian writing and only from the end of the fourth century, that his direct influence on his own time seems almost nil, and that he was widely read in the Middle Ages and the Renaissance.
  • Calvin ends one argument with Lactantius, that no religion is genuine that is not in accordance with truth, and tells readers to read Lactantius and Eusebius on images.

How they're remembered

  • Jerome says Lactantius has a flow of eloquence worthy of Tully, meaning Cicero, and wishes he had been as ready to teach Christian doctrines as to pull down those of others.
  • Jerome charges that Lactantius, in his books and particularly in his letters to Demetrian, altogether denies the subsistence of the Holy Spirit, yet asks who can forbid him to read the Institutes, in which Lactantius wrote against the Gentiles with much ability.
  • Jerome's Chronicle calls him the most eloquent man of his time and says he was so poor in this life that he often lacked even the necessities.
  • Jerome counts him among those who held that after the resurrection the Lord would reign in the flesh with the saints.
  • A list later credited to Pope Gelasius sets the little works of Lactantius among the books not received; whether Gelasius wrote the list is disputed.

What Is Uncertain

  • His life is Jerome's, and Jerome wrote some seventy to eighty years after the events: the Chronicle about 380 and the Lives of Illustrious Men in 392. The books are his own. No single witness carries the whole life. Jerome says he was summoned to Nicomedia in Diocletian's reign and names no one who summoned him; Lactantius himself writes only that he was called thither.
  • Where he was born. Jerome has him from Africa. Monceaux holds it almost sure that he was born there, but this is his reconstruction. Fletcher reports an older view that he came from Firmum, which Monceaux rejects.
  • His name. Jerome gives Firmianus, known also as Lactantius. Monceaux reads the full name, L. Caecilius Firmianus who is also Lactantius, from two African inscriptions and takes the second name for a nickname. The one manuscript of On the Deaths of the Persecutors names its author L. Cecilius.
  • When he became a Christian. Schanz says it is not known whether in Africa or at Nicomedia. Monceaux holds it almost sure that it was at Nicomedia, between 290 and 303, and says he was certainly a Christian in February 303, since he tells that he grieved to hear pagans attack Christianity. Fletcher guessed a link between his poverty and his conversion; Coxe's note says he sees no force in the suggestion.
  • Whether he saw the church fall. The account of its fall is in On the Deaths of the Persecutors. In the Divine Institutes he says only that he was teaching in Bithynia at the time the temple of God was overthrown. Monceaux writes that he saw the church destroyed there and the first edict posted.
  • Who wrote On the Deaths of the Persecutors. The one manuscript names the author L. Cecilius, and Jerome lists a book of Lactantius On persecution. Monceaux, after weighing the dispute, judges the case for his authorship more likely and says scholars lean more and more that way; Schanz lists it as Jerome's De persecutione, whose authenticity he says is disputed. Most scholars hold it his, and some have doubted it.
  • The cause of the palace fire at Nicomedia. Lactantius says Galerius's agents set it. Eusebius says only that a fire broke out in the palace, he knows not how, and that it was laid by a false suspicion on the Christians. Constantine, years later in the speech Eusebius preserves, says lightning destroyed the palace and the emperor's chamber, and that eyewitnesses, he among them, declare it. McGiffert, an editor, thinks the case against the Christians pretty strong and links the lightning to this fire.
  • The address to Constantine. Some copies of the Divine Institutes open with an address to Constantine. Samuel Brandt, in 1890, set the addresses aside as later additions; Monceaux, in 1905, calls them a forger's work; Schanz, in 1922, reports that Brandt later gave up that view and holds them Lactantius's own, from a second edition. Whether Lactantius wrote them is disputed.
  • The two writers he answers. The philosopher who preached poverty and dined well, and who wrote three books against the Christians, is unnamed in the Divine Institutes; Brandt's apparatus says the name is unknown and notes that Baronius thought Porphyry was meant, which Holstenius refuted. The judge who urged the persecution and wrote two books to the Christians is not named there either. The editors identify him with Hierocles, whom On the Deaths of the Persecutors names as the author and adviser of the persecution; that identification is the editors' and Monceaux's.
  • When he taught Crispus. Monceaux puts it about 316, with Crispus about sixteen; Schanz says he was called to Gaul about 317, with Crispus born about 307. Both place it late, after 315.
  • How and when he died is not known. Fletcher's Trier or Treves, about 325, is an editor's belief. The death in 325 printed with Jerome's notice in the English translation is the translation editor's note, not Jerome's. The name the Christian Cicero is a later editors' name, not an ancient title.
  • Judgments that belong to editors and later writers, not to Lactantius. McGiffert thinks the man who tore down the edict deserved death; Monceaux sketches Lactantius as a timid Christian without a calling to martyrdom. Each is that writer's own view.
