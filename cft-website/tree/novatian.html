Novatian

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Novatianism.

About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In 250, under the emperor Decius, many Christians gave way in persecution, offering sacrifice to the gods or buying a certificate to avoid it. Rome's church had no bishop, and one of its elders, Novatian, wrote for its clergy to Carthage; Cyprian, the bishop there, names him the writer.

The fallen must wait in tears for a bishop and a council, he wrote, though the dying who repented might be helped. "Let them knock at the doors, by all means, but certainly not break them down."

Novatian was a leading elder at Rome, and his book On the Trinity set out what the church believed of Father, Son and Spirit. Later he was made a rival bishop of Rome, set up against the first. His church would not take the fallen back to communion, the bread and cup the church shares, and left their pardon to God. That church lasted for centuries.

The book follows the rule of truth, the church's short summary of belief. It sets out God the Father, maker of all, and the Son, truly man and truly God. Father and Son are two, not one person under two names. Then it turns to the Spirit. Its date is uncertain.

In 251 Cornelius was chosen bishop, and a stricter party, with confessors jailed for the faith who had not given way, set Novatian against him. Almost all we know of him comes from the men who fought him, above all his rival Cornelius.

In the persecution, Cornelius charged, Novatian had shut himself away and refused to help the brothers. He had sworn terrible oaths never to seek the office. Then, Cornelius says, he fetched three simple bishops from a corner of Italy, and, with them drunk and sick, forced them to lay hands on him to make him bishop. An American historian who edited the record calls the refusal and the drunkenness slander.

Novatian said he was pushed into it against his will. Dionysius of Alexandria wrote to him: "If you were drawn in unwillingly, as you say, you will prove it by withdrawing willingly. One ought to suffer anything at all rather than tear apart the church of God."

At Rome sixty bishops declared Novatian outside the church. Cornelius made a graver charge: at the communion table, Novatian held each person's hands and made him swear, by Christ's body and blood, never to go back to Cornelius. Cyprian answered that Novatian's rule told the fallen to weep day and night, and then die outside the church.

Novatian sent bishops of his own to many cities. His followers called themselves the Katharoi, Greek for "the pure", and baptized a second time those who joined them.

In 325 the emperor Constantine called Acesius, a bishop of Novatian's church, to Nicaea, the great council of bishops. Acesius accepted the creed and the Easter date, saying nothing new had been decided. Asked why he stayed apart, he gave the old rule from Decius's day. Those who commit a sin unto death after baptism, a grave sin such as denying Christ, should be urged to repent. They must look to God for pardon, not the priest.

Constantine answered: "Set up a ladder, Acesius, and climb to heaven alone." Socrates of Constantinople, a historian kind to Novatian's church, had this from an old man who went to Nicaea as a youth. Socrates gives no reading; a later historian took it as blame. The council's eighth canon set terms for taking in their clergy: they must accept the twice married, and the fallen after a set time of repentance.

Novatian's church flourished at Rome, Socrates says, until the 420s, when a bishop of Rome took their churches and drove their bishop into private houses.

How Novatian died is uncertain. Socrates, writing almost two centuries later, says he died a martyr in a later persecution. No other early writer confirms it.

Quotations and references

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “Let them knock at the doors, by all means, but certainly not break them down.”
    As printed
    “Let them indeed knock at the doors, but assuredly let them not break them down”
    Letter of the Roman clergy to Cyprian (Cyprian, Epistle 30), Ante-Nicene Fathers translation
    The modern wording follows Hartel's Latin. The English prints "indeed" and "assuredly" for the Latin sane and non utique, which the modern wording gives as "by all means" and "certainly not"; both readings kept.
  • In our modern English
    “If you were drawn in unwillingly, as you say, you will prove it by withdrawing willingly. One ought to suffer anything at all rather than tear apart the church of God.”
    As printed
    “If, as thou sayest, thou hast been led on unwillingly, thou wilt prove this if thou retirest willingly. For it were better to suffer everything, rather than divide the Church of God.”
    Eusebius, Church History VI.45, Dionysius to Novatus, tr. McGiffert
    The modern wording follows the Greek, which the one held print gives in a scan too damaged to print here; it garbles the two words for unwilling and willing, whose sense Rufinus's Latin and both English prints confirm. "One ought" follows ἔδει. Salmond's English (Ante-Nicene Fathers, vol. 6) reads "If you were carried on against your will, as you say, you will show that such has been the case by your voluntary retirement", and "it would have been but dutiful to have suffered any kind of ill, so as to avoid rending the Church of God". Both English prints address Novatus. Schwartz's Greek addresses Novatian (Νοουατιανῷ), and his notes give forms of Novatus from other manuscripts; Salmond's note says Jerome gives Novatianus. Both readings kept.
  • In our modern English
    “the pure”
    As printed
    “pure”
    Eusebius, Church History VI.43, tr. McGiffert, note on the name Cathari
    Eusebius says they took the name in the pride of their imagination; the name is the one they gave themselves.
  • In our modern English
    “Set up a ladder, Acesius, and climb to heaven alone.”
    As printed
    “Place a ladder, Acesius, and climb alone into heaven.”
    Socrates, Church History I.10, tr. Zenos
    The modern wording follows Socrates's Greek. Sozomen's retelling in the same English edition reads "take a ladder and ascend alone to heaven". Both readings kept.

Key Works

  • On the Trinity: the rule of faith on Father, Son and Spirit, which McGiffert calls "both able and orthodox"
  • On the Jewish Meats: a letter on the food laws, which Cleveland Coxe thinks was written during the persecution of Decius

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The Novatian churches, which spread east and west and lasted, McGiffert says, into the sixth century.
  • Canon 8 of the Council of Nicaea set the terms on which clergy of the Cathari, as it calls them, could come over to the Catholic church: they had to promise in writing to follow its teaching, and to communicate with the twice married and with the lapsed who had a time of penance and restoration fixed.
  • Socrates says the Novatians flourished at Rome, with many churches and large congregations, until Celestine, bishop of Rome, took their churches and made Rusticula, their bishop, hold his meetings in private houses.
  • Cyprian complains that Novatian was sending his new apostles through very many cities to set up bishops of his own, and says the Novatians baptize again those they win away from the church.
  • Cyprian turned back Novatian's envoys at Carthage, Maximus the presbyter and Augendus the deacon among them, and wrote to Cornelius that he had done so.
  • Jerome lists his writings, among them On the Passover, On the Sabbath, On circumcision, On the priesthood, On prayer, On the food of the Jews, On zeal and On Attalus. Harnack says all but two of these have vanished without a trace.

How they're remembered

  • Socrates of Constantinople tells of the Novatian bishop Acesius at the Council of Nicaea, and of Constantine's answer, "Place a ladder, Acesius, and climb alone into heaven."
  • Cyprian of Carthage, writing of him, said that "he who is not in the Church of Christ is not a Christian."
  • Dionysius of Alexandria wrote to him that a martyrdom suffered to prevent a division of the church would be no less glorious than one suffered to refuse the worship of idols, and that to him it seemed greater.

At Major Councils

  • Councils of Carthage and Rome on the lapsed, 251: Rival bishop at Rome; he and his followers were condemned at Rome

What Is Uncertain

  • Much of what is known of him comes from his enemies, Cornelius, bishop of Rome, and those on his side, says Robert Wallis, who translated his works. Lardner, quoted by Cleveland Coxe, observed that not one line of his own defense survives.
  • Cornelius says Novatian refused to come out of his room to help in the persecution. McGiffert calls this a calumny. Neander suggested that he had withdrawn to live as an ascetic.
  • Cornelius says he wanted the bishopric all along, though he swore he did not. McGiffert sees no reason to doubt Novatian's word, and thinks he may well have been urged against his will.
  • Cornelius says he was baptized on his sickbed. McGiffert accepts this.
  • Socrates says he died a martyr under Valerian. Valesius disputed this. He held that the Novatians' own book on his martyrdom was full of fables, and that it made him only a confessor.
  • Socrates had the Acesius story from Auxanon, an aged Novatian presbyter who went to Nicaea with Acesius, and says no other writer records it. Sozomen tells it after him. Some, among them Baronius, took Socrates for a Novatian. Valesius, followed by most later writers, held him only impartial.
  • When he wrote On the Trinity is not known. Cleveland Coxe reports that Cave dates it about 257, and Wallis puts it after the heresy of Sabellius, which he gives as 256. Harnack says no one knows how long before the persecution of Decius it was written, but that one must stay with the 240s. McGiffert, reporting Closs, hears Cornelius's phrase "this dogmatist, this defender of the doctrine of the Church" as a jab at the book, which would put it before 251.
  • Jerome names Novatian as the author of On the Trinity and calls it a sort of epitome of Tertullian's work. Harnack says that claim is not borne out. Jerome says many wrongly ascribed the book to Cyprian, and Wallis says it was formerly attributed by some to Tertullian and by others to Cyprian. Harnack says it and On the Jewish Meats survived under Tertullian's name.
  • Harnack says On the Jewish Meats was written by Novatian as a schismatic bishop, long parted from his Roman congregation, and that a persecution most likely drove him from Rome. Coxe takes it to have been written during the Decian persecution.
  • Cornelius says his ordination as presbyter was resisted by all the clergy and many of the laity, because he had been baptized on a sickbed. McGiffert says ordinary opinion held such baptism a bar to the presbyterate, and takes the fact that he was ordained in spite of it as a sign of an exceptionally pious and zealous man.
  • Cornelius says Novatian had three simple Italian bishops shut in with him, and by the tenth hour, with them drunk and sick, forced them to lay hands on him. McGiffert accepts that three Italian bishops ordained him, and that one afterward repented, but calls the charge of deceit and drunkenness baseless calumny.
  • Cornelius says Novatian made each person swear at the communion table, by the body and blood of Christ, never to go back to Cornelius. Only Cornelius reports this.
  • Cyprian says Cornelius was made bishop by sixteen co-bishops. His charge from the other side is that Novatian tried by bribery to be made bishop by the hands of deserters.
  • What his rule held is told four ways. Eusebius says Novatian acted as if the fallen had no hope of salvation. Socrates, who drew on Novatian's letters, says he told the churches not to admit them to the sacred mysteries, but to urge them to repent and leave their pardon to God. Cyprian says the rule told them to mourn and weep day and night and then die outside the church. Ambrose says the Novatians held all sins equal in gravity, after the fashion of the Stoics.
  • Dionysius of Alexandria, writing about 257, charges Novatian with impious teaching concerning God. McGiffert says he was not at all heretical in doctrine. Acesius accepted the creed at Nicaea, and Sozomen says the Novatians held the same opinions about the Godhead as the Catholic church.
  • McGiffert says that, according to the tradition of his followers as well as Socrates, he died a martyr under Valerian. Harnack reports the same, says it is not known for certain when he died, and says the martyr acts current in the sixth century were forged. Coxe says Socrates's word, amid the silence of all others, is not enough to guarantee it.
  • Socrates calls Acesius a bishop of the Novatians and gives no reading of the emperor's words. Sozomen places Acesius at Constantinople, says the emperor esteemed him for his virtuous life, and takes the words as blame, since Acesius, being but a man, fancied himself exempt from sin.
  • How long his church lasted is told two ways. McGiffert says it lasted until the sixth century. The editor of Ambrose's On Repentance says it grew weaker in the fifth century and faded away as a separate body.
