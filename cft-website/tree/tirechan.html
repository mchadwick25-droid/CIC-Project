Tirechan

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Medieval Irish Christianity.

Known for

Role

About This Person

An Irish bishop, writing down the churches of Saint Patrick, stopped to say what moved him. His heart, he wrote in Latin, thought of love for Patrick. He saw deserters, arch-robbers and warriors of Ireland who hated Patrick's paruchia, the churches under his authority, because they had taken what was his. They feared, he said, that if Patrick's heir claimed that paruchia, he could win back almost the whole island.

The bishop was Tirechan. His notes on Patrick are kept in the Book of Armagh, which Ferdomnach, the scribe of Armagh, copied between 807 and 846. Newport White, who translated parts of them in 1920, dates the work no earlier than 664, possibly about 670. Whitley Stokes, who printed the Latin in 1887, takes the latest plagues it mentions to be those of 683, "apparently".

Tirechan tells little of himself. A heading says he wrote from the mouth or the book of Bishop Ultan, whose pupil he was. Tirechan says Ultan fostered him, and that he found four names of Patrick written in a book kept by Ultan. White makes Ultan bishop of Ardbraccan in Meath, and Tirechan a native of Tirawley, in County Mayo.

His book follows Patrick from place to place, naming the churches he founded. White says its purpose was to record what belonged to Armagh, against the claims of rival churches. In it Patrick fasts forty days on Mount Egli, "observing the discipline of Moses, and of Elijah, and of Christ." Two daughters of King Loiguire, Ethne the fair and Fedelm the golden-haired, find the bishops at a well at dawn. "Who is God?" the elder asks. "And where is God?"

Of a magician's stone near Tara he writes, in White's English, "I have seen it with mine own eyes."

Louis Duchesne, the French church historian, saw in it little but local traditions, much embellished over two centuries of telling, with Armagh's interests at stake. To White, the book gives the impression of "an honest author who had no gift of composition".

Key Works

  • Collections on Saint Patrick (also called his Memoir): Latin notes in two books, kept in the Book of Armagh. They follow Patrick through Meath, then across the Shannon into Connaught and Ulster, and break off at Cashel. Lists of churches and clergy alternate with stories. Whitley Stokes prints the Latin; White translates passages

Legacy

What traces to them

  • White says the book in Ultan's keeping, from which Tirechan drew, would be the earliest attempt at an account of Patrick known to us.
  • White says Tirechan's work embodies the traditions of Meath and of Connaught.
  • John O'Donovan, editor of the Annals of the Four Masters, uses Tirechan's notes to fix old boundaries, such as the plain of Eilne.

How they're remembered

  • White writes that the book leaves "the impression of an honest author who had no gift of composition". He warns that Tirechan's silence is not always safe proof that something did not happen.
  • White finds Tirechan's account of King Loiguire more credible than Muirchu's, and more creditable to the king.
  • Louis Duchesne, the French church historian, finds in it little but local traditions. He says two centuries of telling had much embellished them, since they touched the national apostle and the interests of Armagh.

What Is Uncertain

  • Tirechan tells little of himself. A heading in the Book of Armagh says he wrote from the mouth or book of Bishop Ultan, whose pupil he was. Tirechan himself says Ultan fostered him.
  • His date. White gives no earlier than 664, possibly about 670, and reports Bury's 664 to 668. Stokes reads the latest plagues Tirechan mentions as those of 683, with "apparently".
  • Ultan's death. White gives 657 and Louis Duchesne 656.
  • Where his work ends. White, after Dr. John Gwynn, gives the note on Pope Celestine sending Patrick to Ferdomnach, the scribe, not to Tirechan. White thinks the note on Patrick's burial at Saul is probably Ferdomnach's too. Duchesne will not credit Tirechan with the last part of the text.
  • Why it breaks off at Cashel. White suggests the author's death, lost leaves, or no further record of Munster.
  • The church that seized Patrick's places. Tirechan says the community of "Clono" holds many of them by force since the latest plagues. Stokes suggests Clonmacnois, with a question mark.
