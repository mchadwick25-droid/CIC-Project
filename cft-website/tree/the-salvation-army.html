The Salvation Army

East London and then the world, 1865 to 1906 — a mission that turned itself into an army, put women in command from the start, and kept the books on every soul and every soup bowl.

Nineteenth-Century Methodism→The Salvation Army→no descendant recorded

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:09

Read along

William Booth, a Methodist preacher who could not settle inside a denomination, gave up his pulpit in 1861, and by 1865 was preaching in the streets of London's East End to people the churches were not reaching and did not particularly want. In 1878 his Christian Mission renamed itself an Army — with a General, ranks, uniforms, flags and brass bands to drown out the hecklers. And its officers were commissioned regardless of sex from the beginning, on Catherine Booth's insistence and the published argument she had made for it in 1859. It fed and housed as much as it preached: In Darkest England (1890) laid out a whole scheme of shelters, labor yards, rescue homes and emigration, and the Army counted everything it did, soul by soul and meal by meal. Mobs attacked it, magistrates fined it, and by 1906 it was working on every inhabited continent.

Documented Stories

The Purchase of Eliza Armstrong

In 1885 a bill to raise the age of consent from thirteen to sixteen had failed in Parliament three times, and W. T. Stead, editor of the Pall Mall Gazette, agreed with Bramwell Booth, the Salvation Army's chief of staff, and Josephine Butler to force it through by demonstration. Rebecca Jarrett, a former brothel-keeper converted in the Army's rescue home at Whitechapel, was sent to buy a girl. She bought Eliza Armstrong, thirteen, from her mother in Marylebone for five pounds, for what the mother was told was domestic service; the girl was examined by a midwife, taken to a brothel room where Stead entered briefly and left, and then, by the Army, to France. On 6 July Stead began publishing The Maiden Tribute of Modern Babylon, four days of reporting on the trade in children. The Army collected 393,000 signatures for the bill in seventeen days and carried the petition to Parliament in a procession; the Criminal Law Amendment Act passed on 14 August with the age at sixteen. Then the law turned on the demonstrators. Eliza's father had not consented, and in October Stead, Jarrett, Bramwell Booth, the midwife Louisa Mourez and Sampson Jacques were tried at the Old Bailey for abduction. Booth was acquitted; Stead served three months; Mourez six months' hard labor; Jarrett six months. The Army stood by her and she worked in its rescue homes until her death in 1928. Eliza Armstrong was returned to her mother.

The Skeleton Army at Hastings

The Salvation Army's method, from 1878, was to march: a brass band, a flag, uniformed men and women processing through the streets to an open-air meeting outside the public houses, singing hymns to music-hall tunes. The publicans, the brewers and the young men who drank with them answered in kind. From 1881, first at Weston-super-Mare and Exeter and then across the south, Skeleton Armies formed, with their own banners and bands and a tactic of surrounding the Salvationists' processions with noise, mud, dead cats, flour, rotten eggs and fists. Magistrates in several towns prosecuted the Salvationists for obstruction rather than the Skeletons for assault, and the Home Office's own returns for 1882 recorded 669 Salvationists assaulted, 251 of them women, and 56 Army buildings attacked. In Sheffield in January 1882 a procession led by William Booth was stoned through the streets and a young officer, a former wrestler, was carried in bleeding. At Hastings, where the Skeleton Army was among the most organized, a Salvationist named Susannah Beaty was knocked down and kicked during an attack on a procession in 1882 and died of her injuries; the Army counted her its first martyr and named a memorial in The War Cry. The riots went on into 1884 at Worthing, where the military were called out, and faded in the later 1880s as the courts began to convict the attackers and the towns tired of them. The Army had kept the counts and printed them.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

William Booth gave up his Methodist pulpit in 1861, and by 1865 he was preaching in the streets of London's East End. By 1878 his Christian Mission had renamed itself an Army, with ranks, uniforms, and brass bands to drown out hecklers. Catherine Booth had already argued in print, in 1859, that women had the right to preach, and the Army commissioned women as officers from the start. It counted everything it did, soul by soul and meal by meal. In 1890 it published its scheme for shelters and labor as In Darkest England.

Legacy

It is still here and still working, one of the largest charitable and religious organizations in the world, active in well over a hundred countries and running shelters, disaster relief, rehabilitation programs and thrift shops that many people use without ever thinking of it as a church. Its brass bands and its Christmas kettles became part of the ordinary furniture of public life in a dozen countries, and its model of organized, uniformed, professionally administered Christian social work was copied widely.

Sources

Doctrinal Floor Note

The Salvation Army does not baptize or celebrate communion. That is a matter of practice rather than of belief — what it confesses is entirely conventional Christian doctrine — and it is worth saying plainly rather than leaving a visitor to guess.
