Clement of Alexandria

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition.

Also called Titus Flavius Clement

Clement was a Christian teacher and a presbyter, an elder of the church, at Alexandria in Egypt, late in the second century and into the third. He wrote that Greek philosophy may have been given to the Greeks to bring them to Christ, as the Law of Moses brought the Hebrews.

About This Person

Clement of Alexandria went looking for teachers, he wrote, and he found them in Greece, in southern Italy and in the East. The last of them was the first of all in ability. "I came upon the last, who was the first in power. I tracked him down where he was hidden in Egypt, and I found rest."

Alexandria in Egypt became Clement's own city, late in the second century and into the third. There he taught, and he served as a presbyter, an elder of the church. He answered Christians who, he wrote, feared Greek philosophy, and his answer was that it might have been God's gift to lead the Greeks to Christ. Eusebius of Caesarea, writing about a century later, says Origen was among his pupils. About two centuries after Clement, Jerome, charged with defiling the church by quoting pagan writers, pointed to Clement's books.

In Eusebius's account, which holds most of what is told of his life, Clement was trained in the Scriptures at Alexandria under his teacher and then took over from him the teaching of the faith to learners there.

Many believers, he wrote, feared philosophy as children fear masks, afraid it would lead them astray. Clement also wrote against the followers of Valentinus, a teacher he opposed, who had taught under the emperor Hadrian, by Clement's reckoning. They left faith, Clement wrote, to ordinary believers like himself, "us, the simple," and claimed knowledge for themselves as men saved by their very nature. He copied words from Valentinus's own letters into his book in order to answer them.

His answer to the believers who feared philosophy took the word Paul uses in his letter to the Galatians for the guide who leads a child, and gave it to philosophy. He set philosophy beside the law that God gave the Hebrews through Moses. "Perhaps philosophy too was given to the Greeks directly and in the first place, until the Lord should call the Greeks. For it was a guide to bring the Greek mind to Christ, as the Law brought the Hebrews." By philosophy he meant whatever each Greek school had said well. But he would never call divine the human reasoning that men had mangled and falsified. He called his book the Miscellanies: notes, he said, patched together, passing from one thing to another.

Eusebius writes that under the emperor Severus the churches were persecuted. Every day, Clement wrote, he and his fellow believers saw martyrs burned, impaled and beheaded before their own eyes. Some asked, he wrote, why Christians were persecuted and put to death if God cared for them. He answered that Christ had foretold it, and that Christians suffered unjustly, for doing right. Christ's word to flee when persecuted, he taught, was meant to keep believers from bringing harm on themselves or on the persecutor, and anyone who gave himself up to the judge shared the guilt of his own death.

Alexander, a bishop, wrote from prison to the church at Antioch and sent the letter by Clement, "the blessed presbyter." Later, writing to Origen, Alexander counted two blessed men among those who had gone on ahead, one of them "the holy Clement," his master and helper. Through them, he wrote, he had come to know Origen.

Jerome's critic had asked him why he stained the whiteness of the church with the filth of the pagans by quoting their books. Jerome answered with a roll of Christian writers who had done the same. Among them he named Clement, in his judgment the most learned of all, and he added that Origen imitated him in ten books of Miscellanies that set Christian and philosophers' views side by side. Of Clement's books Jerome asked, "What in them is unlearned? Or rather, what in them does not come from the heart of philosophy?"

Key Works

  • Exhortation to the Greeks: a call to the Greeks to leave their idols for the Word, Christ, whom he calls the New Song
  • The Instructor: three books on Christ, the Word, as the Instructor who leads the children, with rules for behavior and conduct
  • Miscellanies (Stromata): eight books of notes, patched together, he said, passing from one thing to another. Eusebius says they treat Scripture, quote the Greek writers wherever they seemed useful, and answer false teachers

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Eusebius of Caesarea says Origen was among his pupils, and Jerome that Origen is known to have been his disciple.
  • Jerome says Origen, imitating his example, wrote ten books of Miscellanies that compare the views of Christians and of philosophers.
  • His Stromata keep words from the letters of Valentinus.
  • Eusebius copied passages of his Exhortation to the Greeks on the pagan mysteries and on human sacrifice into his Preparation for the Gospel, calling him "the admirable Clement".
  • About two centuries after him, Jerome answered a critic who charged him with defiling the church by quoting pagan books. He named Christian writers who had done the same, Clement among them.

How they're remembered

  • Alexander of Jerusalem called him "the blessed presbyter, a man virtuous and approved."
  • Writing later to Origen, Alexander named "the holy Clement, my master and benefactor" among the blessed men who had gone the way before.
  • Jerome called him "in my judgment the most learned of men", and asked what in his books did not come from the heart of philosophy.
  • Jerome's Chronicle counts him and Pantaenus, a Stoic philosopher, among those held most eloquent in arguing for the faith.
  • Epiphanius of Salamis counts him with Irenaeus and Hippolytus among those who wonderfully refuted the followers of Valentinus.
  • Theodoret of Cyrrhus counts him, as Clement the author of the Miscellanies, among those who fought for the truth against Basilides and against Valentinus.
  • Photius of Constantinople, about 650 years later, found the Instructor "entirely free from idle and blasphemous opinions" and the Miscellanies unsound "in some parts". He charged the Outlines with "impious and legendary fables", and was not sure Clement wrote them.

Open Questions

  • The witnesses. Clement's own books say little of his life. Most of what is told of it comes from Eusebius of Caesarea, writing about a century later. The only words about him from his own time are two letters of the bishop Alexander, which Eusebius keeps. Jerome wrote about two centuries later.
  • The hidden teacher. Clement names his teachers in Greece, southern Italy and the East only by where they came from, and gives no name for the last, whom he found hidden in Egypt. Eusebius thinks it was Pantaenus, since Clement names Pantaenus as his teacher in another book, the Outlines (Hypotyposes), which the Library does not hold.
  • Taking over the teaching. Eusebius says Clement succeeded Pantaenus in charge of the teaching of the faith to learners at Alexandria, which he elsewhere calls a school. Jerome says Clement led the church's school after the death of his master; Eusebius says nothing of a death.
  • Origen as a pupil. The English of Eusebius says Origen was one of his pupils "while still a boy". The manuscripts of Eusebius's Greek differ on those words, and the Greek edition held leaves them out. That Origen was among his pupils stands in every reading.
  • Leaving Alexandria. Eusebius says that when persecution threatened, no one was left at Alexandria to teach the faith, since all had been driven away. He does not name Clement, and no old source says whether or when Clement left the city.
  • Birth and death. No old source gives the year or place of his birth or of his death. Epiphanius says some called him an Alexandrian and others an Athenian. Alexander's letter to Origen, which counts him among the blessed who had gone before, is the last word of him.
  • The persecution. Eusebius says the churches were persecuted under Severus, and gives no reason. Clement writes of martyrs burned, impaled and beheaded every day, but no source ties his words to that persecution, and none ties his rule against giving oneself up to the judge to his own life.
  • The groups he answered. The believers who feared Greek philosophy, and those who wanted "bare faith alone", speak only in Clement's account. His charge that the followers of Valentinus claimed to be saved by nature is also reported by Irenaeus of Lyon, who says they held they would be saved because they are spiritual by nature.
  • Alexander. His letter from prison gives no reason for the prison and no place. Eusebius says Alexander was first a bishop in Cappadocia and later bishop at Jerusalem.
  • The Outlines. Photius, who charged them with fables, doubted they were Clement's own: "either he or some one else under his name".
