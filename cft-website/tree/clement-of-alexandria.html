About This Person

Clement of Alexandria learned from teachers across the Greek world. In the first book of his Stromata, or Miscellanies, he remembers them. One taught in Greece and one in the Greek south of Italy, and others in the East. The last was the greatest. "Having tracked him out concealed in Egypt," Clement wrote, "I found rest." He called that teacher "the Sicilian bee," gathering from the meadow of the prophets and apostles. Eusebius of Caesarea thought this teacher was Pantaenus, head of the Christian school at Alexandria, whom Clement names as his teacher in another book.

Eusebius notes that he shared his name with Clement of Rome, the early bishop. Jerome of Strido, in his Lives of Illustrious Men, says Clement was a presbyter of the church at Alexandria. He led its school after Pantaenus died, and Origen was his pupil.

Little more of his life is known. Eusebius quotes a letter that Alexander of Jerusalem wrote from prison to the church of Antioch, and Clement carried it there. Alexander calls him "the blessed presbyter, a man virtuous and approved." Eusebius also writes that when persecution came to Alexandria, all the teachers there "were driven away." Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius, reads Clement into that line and dates his leaving to the year 202.

He wrote an Exhortation to the Greeks and an Instructor on how a Christian should live. His Stromata he called "Gnostic Notes on the True Philosophy." There he suggests that philosophy was given to the Greeks as the law was given to the Hebrews, to bring them to Christ. "Philosophy, therefore, was a preparation," he wrote, "paving the way for him who is perfected in Christ."

Clement used the word "gnostic," meaning one who knows, for the mature Christian. For him, knowledge was built on faith. The first saving change, he wrote, is from heathenism to faith, "and the second, that from faith to knowledge." The followers of Valentinus, a teacher who had broken with the church at Rome, divided people differently. They left faith to "the simple," Clement says, and kept knowledge for themselves, as people saved by their very nature. To answer them, he quoted their founder's own letters, and so he kept some of Valentinus's words.

Clement said he wrote his notes "against old age, as a remedy against forgetfulness," to keep what his teachers had given him.

Key Works

  • Exhortation to the Greeks: a call to leave the old gods
  • The Instructor: three books on how a Christian should live
  • Stromata: "Gnostic Notes on the True Philosophy," on faith, knowledge and Greek learning

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Jerome of Strido, in his Lives of Illustrious Men, says Origen was his pupil.
  • His Stromata keep words from the letters of Valentinus.

How they're remembered

  • Alexander of Jerusalem called him "the blessed presbyter, a man virtuous and approved."
  • Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius of Caesarea, notes that he was long honored as a saint, and that Pope Clement the Eighth removed his name from the list because his teaching was suspected.

What Is Uncertain

  • Little of his life is known beyond his own books, a few chapters of Eusebius of Caesarea and one of Jerome of Strido.
  • That the "Sicilian bee" he found in Egypt was Pantaenus is Eusebius's judgment. Eusebius says Clement names Pantaenus as his teacher in another book, the Hypotyposes.
  • Eusebius says all who taught at Alexandria were driven away when persecution came. Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius, reads Clement into that line and dates his leaving to 202. Clement is not named there.
  • McGiffert says nothing certain is known of the place and time of his birth, or of his death.
