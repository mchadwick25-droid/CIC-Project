About This Person

Soldiers had arrested the bishop of Alexandria and were holding him at Taposiris. A countryman on his way to a wedding feast heard the news and told the guests. They, Dionysius wrote, "as if on a preconcerted signal, arose with one impulse," and burst in. Dionysius thought the guests were robbers and offered them his clothes. When he understood, he begged them to leave him, and even to cut off his head. They dragged him away by his hands and feet.

Dionysius of Alexandria tells this himself, of the persecution under the emperor Decius, in a letter Eusebius of Caesarea kept. He wrote it to answer Germanus, a bishop who accused him of running away, so it is his own defence. Dionysius had studied under Origen of Alexandria and led its school. He became bishop about 247. Eusebius calls him the great bishop of the Alexandrians.

When Novatian set himself up at Rome as a rival bishop, Dionysius wrote to him. It was "better to suffer everything," he urged, "rather than divide the Church of God." When plague struck Alexandria, he wrote, "The most of our brethren were unsparing in their exceeding love." The heathen, he says, "deserted those who began to be sick." He writes as a bishop praising his own people.

He also asked who wrote the Revelation of John. "I could not venture to reject the book, as many brethren hold it in high esteem," he wrote. He granted that a holy man named John wrote it. But he compared its thought and words with the Gospel and letter of John, and he could not accept that this John was the apostle. Among his reasons was its Greek. Its writer, he said, "uses barbarous idioms."

In letters against Sabellius's followers he stressed that the Son was distinct from the Father. Some churchmen went to Rome and accused him of teaching that the Son was made. Dionysius of Rome wrote to him, and he answered in four books, which ended the matter, says Archibald Robertson, an editor of Athanasius. A century later the Arians claimed him as their own. Athanasius of Alexandria wrote a defence of Dionysius to answer them.

Eusebius says he died in the twelfth year of the emperor Gallienus, after seventeen years as bishop. He had been asked to a synod at Antioch about Paul of Samosata, but he was too old and weak to go.

Key Works

  • Letters: on his flight under Decius, the plague, the lapsed, rebaptism and Novatian, kept in Eusebius's Church History, Books 6 and 7
  • On Promises: against Nepos, with his study of who wrote the Revelation of John
  • Refutation and Defence: four books answering Dionysius of Rome

Legacy

How they're remembered

  • Eusebius calls him the great bishop of the Alexandrians.
  • Athanasius of Alexandria wrote a defence of him a century later, when the Arians claimed him.
  • Basil of Caesarea, in a letter, charged him with sowing the seeds of the Anomoean heresy, Blomfield Jackson notes. Jackson adds that Lumper put the charge down to Basil's imperfect knowledge of his writings, and that elsewhere Basil calls him "the Great."

At Major Councils

  • Councils of Carthage and Rome on the lapsed, 251: Not present; wrote to Novatian urging him to step down

What Is Uncertain

  • The story of his flight under Decius is his own, written to answer Germanus, a bishop who accused him of running away. McGiffert says Germanus's see is not known.
  • On the plague he writes as a bishop praising his own people.
  • He judged that the apostle did not write the Revelation, from its thought, words and style beside the Gospel and letter of John, and from its Greek. He still would not reject the book. Before him, he says, some had rejected it altogether.
  • Archibald Robertson, an editor of Athanasius, says his four books to Dionysius of Rome ended that dispute.
  • Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius, says the word Dionysius used for those with him in his flight is read as children, pupils or servants.
