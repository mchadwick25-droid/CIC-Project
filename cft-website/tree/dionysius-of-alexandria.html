About This Person

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 4:09

Soldiers had seized Dionysius, the bishop of Alexandria, and were holding him in a small town near the city. A countryman on his way to a wedding feast told the guests, who ran shouting to the house to carry him off, and the soldiers fled. "Then I understood why they had come. I cried out, begging and pleading with them to go away and leave us alone. If they wanted to do a kindness, I asked them to act before the men leading me away, and cut off my head themselves."

A pupil of Origen, Dionysius ran the church's school at Alexandria, where people were taught the faith. From about 247 he led its church as bishop, through two persecutions and a plague. His letters, which Eusebius of Caesarea copied into his history, are our eyewitness record of those years.

About 250, the emperor Decius set out to make Christians give up their faith and return to the old gods. Dionysius stayed at home four days while an officer hunted him. Then God told him to go, he says, and he left, but was caught at sunset, and after the guests carried him off, he hid in the Libyan desert. He told all this to answer a fellow bishop who accused him of running away, so the story rests on his own word.

In one book, Dionysius turned to the Revelation, the last book of the Bible. He would not reject it, but he set its rough Greek beside the polished Greek of the Gospel and letter of John. "That he was called John, and that this book is the writing of a John, I will not dispute. For I agree it is the work of someone holy and inspired by God. But I would not easily grant that this was the apostle, the son of Zebedee, the brother of James." That is John, one of the twelve, and he guessed it was another John.

In the churches of Libya, some bishops taught that the Father and the Son were one and the same. To show the Son was not the Father, Dionysius used blunt images. As his opponents later cited them, the Son was as different from the Father as a vine from its farmer, or a boat from its builder. Some of his own church, Athanasius of Alexandria says, went over his head to the bishop of Rome. The bishop wrote against both errors and told Dionysius what was said against him. Dionysius answered in four books that there was never a time when God was not Father, and that the Son comes from him like light from an eternal light.

When plague came, about 263, many Christians, he wrote, nursed the sick without fear and caught it themselves, and he counted their deaths as dying for the faith. "So even this kind of death, which came from great devotion and strong faith, seemed to fall nothing short of martyrdom." The pagans, he wrote, pushed their sick into the streets half dead. He died, old and still bishop, about 264 or 265.

Nearly a century later came the followers of Arius, who taught that there was once a time when the Son did not exist. They quoted the vine and the boat against Nicaea's creed. Dionysius had written two generations before Arius, against the opposite error, yet his words were now read as support for theirs. Athanasius, bishop of the same city, wrote a book to answer them. Dionysius did write those words, he granted, but his four books showed his real faith, and to claim him was a slander. Basil of Caesarea compared him to a woodman who pulls a crooked sapling so hard the other way that he bends it wrong on the other side. Whether the Arians had any ground, later readers disagree.

For Athanasius, the proof was how Dionysius ended: no bishops ever condemned him or drove him out, and he never left the church. He died, Athanasius wrote, with honor inside it, and his memory was kept among the fathers.

Quotations and references

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “Then I understood why they had come. I cried out, begging and pleading with them to go away and leave us alone. If they wanted to do a kindness, I asked them to act before the men leading me away, and cut off my head themselves.”
    As printed
    “Then I understood why they were come, and I cried out, beseeching and entreating them to depart and leave us alone. And I requested them, if they desired to benefit me in any way, to anticipate those who were carrying me off, and cut off my head themselves.”
    Eusebius, Church History VI.40.8, quoting Dionysius's letter to Germanus, tr. Arthur Cushman McGiffert
    Salmond's English of the same letter (Extant Fragments and Letters, lines 10873-10877) reads "if they wished to do us any good, they might anticipate those who led me captive, and strike off my head." The modern "do a kindness" and "the men leading me away" follow the Greek, which is legible at those words in the one Greek print held, a damaged scan of Schwartz's edition; "why they had come" follows the sense of both English prints, because the Greek is broken there.
  • In our modern English
    “That he was called John, and that this book is the writing of a John, I will not dispute. For I agree it is the work of someone holy and inspired by God. But I would not easily grant that this was the apostle, the son of Zebedee, the brother of James.”
    As printed
    “That this person was called John, therefore, and that this was the writing of a John, I do not deny. And I admit further, that it was also the work of some holy and inspired man. But I could not so easily admit that this was the apostle, the son of Zebedee, the brother of James,”
    Dionysius, On Promises, Book 2, in Eusebius, Church History VII.25.7, tr. S. D. F. Salmond
    McGiffert's English (Church History VII.25.7, lines 41453-41457) reads "the work of one John" and "I agree also that it is the work of a holy and inspired man". The modern "the writing of a John" follows Salmond and the Greek, which has no word for "one"; "I agree" follows McGiffert and the Greek. "I will not dispute" follows the Greek verb, which is future, "I will not speak against", where both prints say "I do not deny"; "inspired by God" renders the one Greek word the prints give as "inspired".
  • In our modern English
    “So even this kind of death, which came from great devotion and strong faith, seemed to fall nothing short of martyrdom.”
    As printed
    “so that this form of death, through the great piety and strong faith it exhibited, seemed to lack nothing of martyrdom.”
    Eusebius, Church History VII.22.8, quoting Dionysius's plague letter, tr. McGiffert
    Salmond's English (Extant Fragments and Letters, lines 11516-11521) reads "so that this very form of death, in virtue of the distinguished piety and the steadfast faith which were exhibited in it, appeared to come in nothing beneath martyrdom itself." The modern "even" follows the Greek, which has a word for "even" there; McGiffert leaves it out, and Salmond gives it as "this very form"; "devotion" renders the Greek word the prints give as "piety".

Legacy

How they're remembered

  • Eusebius calls him the great bishop of the Alexandrians.
  • Athanasius of Alexandria wrote a defense of him a century later, when the Arians claimed him.
  • Basil of Caesarea, in a letter, charged him with sowing the seeds of the Anomoean heresy, Blomfield Jackson notes. Jackson adds that Lumper put the charge down to Basil's imperfect knowledge of his writings, and that elsewhere Basil calls him "the Great."
  • Athanasius says no bishops ever condemned him or drove him out, that he never left the church of his own will, and that he died honorably within it, his memory kept among the fathers.
  • Basil of Caesarea compares him to a woodman who pulls a crooked sapling so far the other way that he drags it awry on the other side.

At Major Councils

  • Councils of Carthage and Rome on the lapsed, 251: Not present; wrote to Novatian urging him to step down

What Is Uncertain

  • The story of his flight under Decius is his own, written to answer Germanus, a bishop who accused him of running away. McGiffert says Germanus's see is not known.
  • On the plague he writes as a bishop praising his own people.
  • He judged that the apostle did not write the Revelation, from its thought, words and style beside the Gospel and letter of John, and from its Greek. He still would not reject the book. Before him, he says, some had rejected it altogether.
  • Archibald Robertson, an editor of Athanasius, says his four books to Dionysius of Rome ended that dispute.
  • Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius, says the word Dionysius used for those with him in his flight is read as children, pupils or servants.
  • He says the man sent to search for him "was smitten with blindness" and did not find the house. McGiffert reads this as the officer not thinking to look there, since he supposed Dionysius would be elsewhere. No source says whether the wedding guests who carried him off were Christians; in a later letter he calls them certain persons from Mareotis.
  • He guessed that the writer of the Revelation was some other John of Asia, since, as they say, there are two monuments at Ephesus, each bearing the name of John.
  • What he says of the pagans in the plague, that they deserted their sick and cast them out half dead, is his own claim about his neighbors, and the held sources give no pagan account.
  • What his words on the Son meant is disputed. The vine and farmer and boat and builder images are the words as his opponents cite them. Athanasius grants he wrote them, says he wrote them for the occasion and the person concerned, about Christ's human nature, and says his four books show his real faith; in those books Dionysius calls the images examples given cursorily and less adequate. Archibald Robertson says Athanasius, not the Arians, had the right to claim him, but that the Arians had good ground at first sight, and calls the defense about Christ's human nature scarcely tenable. Rufinus, about 397, says that the books he wrote against Sabellius have things inserted in them, so that the Arians try to shelter under his authority. Jerome, answering Rufinus about 402, reports him as saying that in the four books against Sabellius Dionysius lapses into the dogma of Arius, but only to show that heretics had corrupted such writings, and says himself that before Arius arose things were said incautiously which cannot be defended against a malevolent criticism. Basil, about a century after Dionysius, thought he first gave men the seeds of the later error, out of zeal against Sabellius and not from bad intent.
