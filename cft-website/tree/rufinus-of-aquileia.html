Aquileia, today in Italy

Rufinus of Aquileia

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Church of Aquileia.

Known for
Latin monk and priest who put Origen of Alexandria, the Church History of Eusebius of Caesarea and other Greek works into Latin, and whose quarrel with Jerome of Strido over Origen ended an old friendship.
Also called
Tyrannius Rufinus, Rufinus of Concordia
Dates
Born about 344 or 345, by W. H. Fremantle's reckoning; back with his family in Italy after nearly thirty years away, by his own account in a letter Fremantle dates to 400; in Sicily in 410, when he wrote his preface to Origen on Numbers; died there in 410, by the notes to Fremantle's edition of Jerome
Place
Aquileia; Egypt; Jerusalem; Rome; Sicily
Role
Monk, priest and translator

About This Person

Jerome heard, in 374, that his friend Rufinus was traveling among the monks in the far parts of Egypt. He wrote to draw him to Syria, and ended: "A friendship that can end was never real." Within thirty years, a book Rufinus translated had ended theirs.

Rufinus of Aquileia, a monk from northern Italy, spent nearly thirty years among the monks of Egypt and at Jerusalem. Then he came home and put Greek Christian books into Latin. One was First Principles, by Origen, a Greek Bible scholar of the 200s, much admired and by now much attacked. The other was Eusebius's history of the church, which Rufinus carried on in two books of his own. First Principles and many of Origen's other works survive chiefly in his reworded Latin, his editors note. And the preface to First Principles broke his friendship with Jerome.

He spent eight years in Egypt, he writes, learning from the desert monks. When the emperor turned on the monks there for their creed, he says, he was jailed and exiled. At Jerusalem, in the 390s, a bishop from Cyprus charged that Origen taught souls had sinned in heaven before birth, that the devil would be saved, and that this flesh would not rise. Jerome took his side, Rufinus the other, but they made peace before Rufinus sailed home.

At home he argued in a short book that heretics had falsified Origen's writings. Then he translated First Principles, Origen's book on God and creation. Its preface praised, without naming him, a friend who had translated Origen before and pared away what jarred with the faith. He said he followed that method. "Where I found anything in his books against the truth about the Trinity, which he teaches soundly elsewhere, I left it out as not his own. Or I set it down to agree with the rule of faith, which we find him holding to again and again." The rule of faith is the church's summary of its belief.

Jerome's friends at Rome sent him the translation, saying it was falsely translated. Jerome took the preface as aimed at him. In the Greek, Jerome later wrote, Origen ranked the Son below the Father, the Spirit below the Son. He charged that Rufinus had made the Trinity sound to put readers off guard against Origen's other teachings. He made his own literal translation: "In the books On First Principles, I simply set down what stood in the Greek. I did not want the reader to believe what I translated. I wanted him not to believe what you had translated before."

Rufinus told the bishop of Rome that he was no champion of Origen, and had cut only what he believed others added. He affirmed that this very flesh would rise, and the devil burn forever. But the bishop of Rome wrote to the bishop of Jerusalem that Origen meant to undermine the faith, and that the emperors had banned his books. Rufinus was wholly apart from Rome and must answer to God, he wrote, and he neither knew nor wished to know where Rufinus lived. Each wrote a full defense against the other, and the friendship did not recover.

When the Goths broke through into Italy, Chromatius, bishop of Aquileia, asked Rufinus to translate the Church History. Chromatius hoped it would help listeners forget their sufferings a little. He did.

The few Greek fragments of First Principles show, his editors note, that he altered Origen's wording. His History became the Latin West's Eusebius through the Middle Ages, an editor judges. The Greek historian Socrates followed it, then rewrote his first two books, trusting more a bishop who had suffered in those events.

Rufinus's last work came in Sicily, about 410 by his editor's date. The barbarian had set fire to Rhegium, just across the strait. On quiet nights, he wrote, as a comfort in their troubles, he gathered Origen's sermons on Numbers into Latin. He hoped to add Deuteronomy, he said, if God gave him back his eyesight. He died soon after, his editors judge.

  • In our modern English
    “Where I found anything in his books against the truth about the Trinity, which he teaches soundly elsewhere, I left it out as not his own. Or I set it down to agree with the rule of faith, which we find him holding to again and again.”
    As printed
    “Wherever therefore I have found in his books anything contrary to the truth concerning the Trinity which he has in other places spoken of in a strictly orthodox sense, I have either omitted it as a foreign and not genuine expression or set it down in terms agreeing with the rule of faith which we find him constantly assenting to.”
    Rufinus, preface to On First Principles, tr. W. H. Fremantle
    The Latin is not held. A second English print of 1893, in Jerome's letters (Letter 80, lines 18367-18372), differs in the second half: it says he omitted the passage as garbled and misleading, or substituted the view he found Origen frequently asserting, and has no rule of faith.
  • In our modern English

    As printed
    “I expressed in Latin just what I found in the Greek text of the books Περὶ ᾽Αρχῶν, not wishing the reader to believe what was in my translation, but wishing him not to believe what was in yours.”
    Jerome, Apology against Rufinus I.7, tr. W. H. Fremantle

References

  • Jerome, Letter 3, to Rufinus the Monk, written from Antioch in 374 by the editors' heading: he has heard that Rufinus is going through the remotest parts of Egypt visiting the monks, and sends the letter in the hope that it will bring him to Jerome. The brief quotes the last line on friendship.
  • W. H. Fremantle, Prolegomena on Rufinus: Rufinus and Jerome close friends in youth; estranged over Origen in Palestine and reconciled before Rufinus left; the preface to On First Principles meant to show Jerome as his forerunner; Jerome's Apology closed the controversy, Rufinus not replying and Jerome not relenting; Origen's works known through his translations; his Numbers translation his last work, and his death soon after in Sicily.
  • Rufinus, Apology to Apronianus II: six years in Egypt and again, after an interval, two more, among the desert monks.
  • Rufinus, Apology to Anastasius: persecuted by the heretics while at Alexandria, he underwent imprisonment and exile; home to his family after nearly thirty years away; he cut not a few passages he suspected Origen had not written; he is no defender or champion of Origen; he believes it will be this very flesh that rises, and that the devil and his angels will receive the eternal fire.
  • Rufinus, preface to On First Principles, to Macarius, in two English prints: the unnamed friend who translated Origen's homilies before him and pared away what jarred with the faith; the short treatise showing heretics had falsified Origen's books; the method for the passages on the Trinity. The Latin is not held.
  • Jerome, Letter 81, to Rufinus (399 by Fremantle): he recognizes the preface as Rufinus's and says it was aimed at him.
  • Jerome, Apology against Rufinus I.6 and I.7, to Pammachius and Marcella (402 by Fremantle): the friends at Rome writing that the books were falsely translated; the charge that the change on the Trinity would leave the reader unwarned of the rest; his own literal translation. The Latin of I.7 is in Migne.
  • Anastasius, bishop of Rome, letter to John of Jerusalem about Rufinus (401 by Fremantle): Anastasius's view that Origen's object was to disintegrate the faith of the apostles; the emperors' decree against reading Origen; Rufinus to bring his conscience to the bar of the divine majesty; Rufinus wholly apart from Rome, whose place of living Anastasius neither knows nor wishes to know.
  • Rufinus, preface to the Church History, Books 10 and 11, in Latin, and Fremantle's English: Chromatius's request as the Goths broke into Italy, the hope that listeners would forget their sufferings to some extent, and the two books added.
  • Socrates of Constantinople, Church History II.1: his first two books followed Rufinus; after reading Athanasius he judged more credit due to him who had suffered and to eyewitnesses, and revised them.
  • A. C. McGiffert, Prolegomena to Eusebius: the version of Rufinus had a large circulation and became in the Western Church a substitute for the original throughout the Middle Ages.
  • Introductory Note to the Works of Origen, Ante-Nicene Fathers vol. 4: On First Principles comes to us chiefly in Rufinus's Latin; the few Greek fragments show him altering many of Origen's expressions.
  • Rufinus, preface to Origen's Homilies on Numbers, in Latin (Baehrens) and Fremantle's English: the Barbarian within sight, Rhegium on fire, the narrow sea between Italy and Sicily, work on quiet nights as a solace from their troubles, only the comments on Deuteronomy wanting, to be added if God restores his eyesight. Fremantle dates it 410 from the burning of Rhegium.
  • Socrates of Constantinople, Church History IV.24: the emperor Valens's edict that the orthodox be persecuted in Alexandria and the rest of Egypt, and the assault on the monks there; Rufinus declares he was a witness and one of the sufferers.
  • Epiphanius of Salamis, Letter 51, 4 and 5, in Jerome's Latin: the charges that Origen taught that souls were once angels who sinned in heaven and were confined in bodies as a penalty, that the devil will return to his former dignity in the kingdom of heaven, and that he denies the resurrection of this flesh; his prayer that God free Rufinus from the heresy of Origen. Fremantle, introduction to the Apology to Apronianus: in the disputes over Origen Jerome sided with Epiphanius and Rufinus with John of Jerusalem.
  • Jerome, Letter 124, to Avitus, 2, dated 409 or 410 by the editors' heading: Origen places the Son second to the Father and the Holy Spirit third, and calls the Son inferior to the Father and the Spirit inferior to the Son.
  • World census, The Church of Aquileia: his home tradition.

Key Works

  • Commentary on the Apostles' Creed: the creed as he was taught it at his baptism in Aquileia, clause by clause, with the old tradition that the apostles framed it together
  • Church History, Books 10 and 11: his Latin Eusebius, with two books of his own added, from Constantine to the death of Theodosius, written at the request of Chromatius of Aquileia
  • Apology to Apronianus, against Jerome: two books in answer to Jerome's letter to Pammachius, defending his faith and his way of translating Origen

Legacy

What traces to them

  • W. H. Fremantle, who translated him, says many works of Origen, On First Principles among them, are known chiefly through his Latin, since they are "lost in their native Greek".
  • Gennadius of Marseilles credits him with Latin versions of Basil of Caesarea, Gregory of Nazianzus, the Recognitions of Clement of Rome, the Sentences of Xystus and of Evagrius, and the Church History of Eusebius.
  • Socrates of Constantinople followed his Church History for his own first two books, then revised them, finding that Rufinus "has erred in respect to chronology."
  • His Commentary on the Creed follows the creed of Aquileia, and notes that "He descended into Hell" was not in the creed of the Roman church.
  • Gennadius adds that in this way Rufinus "opened to the Latin speaking church the greater part of the Greek literature".
  • In his Church History Rufinus says he had the story of Frumentius, who brought the faith to a king's court in what he calls India, from Edesius, Frumentius's companion and later a priest at Tyre. Socrates of Constantinople repeats that Rufinus says so.
  • Socrates says that in revising his first two books he still used the testimony of Rufinus "where it is evident that he could not be mistaken."
  • The introduction to Origen in the Ante-Nicene Fathers says that only small portions of Jerome's own new translation of On First Principles have reached our day.
  • Gennadius says Rufinus wrote on Jacob's blessing on the patriarchs in a threefold sense, "the historical, moral and mystical".
  • W. H. Fremantle says Rufinus also wrote a History of the Monks of the East, partly from personal knowledge, partly from what he had heard or read of them.
  • Palladius of Helenopolis says that Melania and Rufinus received at their own charges those who visited Jerusalem in pursuance of a vow, bishops and monks and virgins.

How they're remembered

  • Palladius of Helenopolis, who took his side against Jerome: "A more learned man or a kinder than he was not to be found among men."
  • Gennadius says Rufinus "was not the least among the doctors of the church and had a fine talent for elegant translation from Greek into Latin."
  • Augustine of Hippo, writing to Jerome, asked "where is the friend who may not be feared as possibly a future enemy, if the breach that we deplore could arise between Jerome and Rufinus?"
  • Epiphanius of Salamis, in a letter Jerome put into Latin, prayed that God would free "especially the presbyter Rufinus, from the heresy of Origen."

What Is Uncertain

  • Much of his life is told by Rufinus himself and by Jerome of Strido, once his close friend and later his foe. Each is a partisan on the other. Palladius of Helenopolis and Gennadius of Marseilles, who praise him, took his side.
  • Fremantle puts his birth about 344 or 345. Ernest Cushing Richardson's note to Gennadius gives 345. No early witness in the sources used here gives the year.
  • Jerome, writing to him in 399 by Fremantle's date, speaks of his native country. Fremantle's edition of Jerome names it as Concordia, near Aquileia.
  • Rufinus says he cut only passages he believed heretics had put into Origen's books, as Jerome himself had done. Jerome says he left heretical views standing, so that a reader "would take in these heretical views without warning".
  • Rufinus translated the Defence of Origen as the work of the martyr Pamphilus. Jerome says it was the first of six books by Eusebius of Caesarea. Fremantle quotes Lightfoot's view that it was the joint work of both.
  • Anastasius, bishop of Rome, wrote that Rufinus "must bring his conscience to the bar of the divine majesty." Fremantle finds no reason to think the bishop passed sentence on him or summoned him to Rome.
  • His imprisonment and exile in Egypt rest on his own word. Socrates of Constantinople says only that Rufinus called himself a witness, and one of the sufferers, when the monks of Egypt were attacked.
  • Jerome's line that "the scorpion lies beneath the ground" does not name him. The notes to Fremantle's edition of Jerome take the scorpion for Rufinus and date his death to 410, in Sicily.
  • Rufinus says he followed Jerome's way of translating Origen. Jerome charges that Rufinus changed the passages on the Trinity and left the rest, so that readers would not be on guard. Fremantle says of the preface that "the use made of his previous action can hardly be justified", and the introduction to Origen in the Ante-Nicene Fathers says that, from the few Greek fragments, Rufinus "was justly chargeable with altering many of Origen's expressions".
  • Fremantle dates the preface to On First Principles to 397 and says it was published early in 398. He puts the Church History probably in 401, from Rufinus's words about Alaric invading Italy. The brief gives no year for either.
  • Rufinus says he was baptized about thirty years before at the hands of Chromatius, Jovinus and Eusebius, all now bishops. He says one of them was then a presbyter, the second an archdeacon and the third a deacon, and that the third was his teacher in the creed.
  • Rufinus says he stayed in Egypt six years, and two more after an interval, where Didymus lived. Fremantle calls him a pupil of Didymus, then head of the catechetical school.
  • Rufinus says that in the Church History he left out the tenth book of Eusebius because it was taken up with praise of particular bishops, and added what belonged to history to the ninth book. He says he compiled his own two books "partly from the traditions of the former generation, partly from facts within my own memory", and added them to the earlier books "like the two fishes to the loaves".
  • In his Apology to Anastasius, Rufinus says: "I am not a defender or a champion of Origen".
  • Anastasius's letter about Rufinus answers John of Jerusalem, who had asked his advice about him.
  • Rufinus says the brethren were constantly adjuring him to translate On First Principles, and that he yielded to Macarius. Fremantle says Urseius and Macarius asked him to translate various Greek books, among them that one.
  • Rufinus, in the preface to Origen on Numbers, says he had joined Pinianus and his company in their flight, for the delight of their chaste conversation. Fremantle says the party was starting out by way of Sicily and Africa and that Rufinus joined their religious company.
  • Whether Origen taught what his accusers charge is disputed. Epiphanius of Salamis, in a letter Jerome put into Latin, charged that souls had sinned in heaven before birth, that the devil would be restored, and that this flesh would not rise. Jerome, in a letter his editors date to 409 or 410, says that in the Greek Origen ranked the Son below the Father and the Spirit below the Son. Rufinus says heretics falsified Origen's books.
  • A note in the translation of Jerome's Letter 124 says that another of Jerome's charges there, that Origen said Christ was made God's Son, not born, is not borne out by the existing fragments of First Principles. The brief does not use that charge.
