The Age of Monks and Empires · c. 521-597 · Ireland, Iona

Ireland · Iona, today in United Kingdom

Columba

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Insular / Irish Monastic Christianity.

Known for
Irish monk who sailed to Britain on pilgrimage for Christ, founded the monastery on Iona, and in Bede's account turned the northern Picts to the faith.
Also called
Colum Cille, Columb-Kille, Columkille
Dates
Sailed to Britain in his forty-second year, two years after the battle of Cooldrevny, in Adomnán's words: 563 in Wentworth Huyshe's reckoning, 565 in Bede's. Huyshe gives his years as 521 to 597; Bede says he died at seventy-seven
Place
Ireland by birth; Iona, from which he went among the Picts
Role
Monk; founder and abbot of Iona

About This Person

On his last Saturday, Columba went with his attendant Diormit to bless the granary of his monastery on Iona. He said his monks would have enough grain for the year. Then he told Diormit in secret that this day was his Sabbath, his rest. That night he would go the way of his fathers. On the way back, the white horse that carried the milk pails laid its head against his breast, shedding tears. Diormit tried to drive it away. "Let him alone, let him alone, for he loves me," Columba said.

The scene comes from Adomnán, a later abbot of Iona. His translator, Wentworth Huyshe, says Adomnán was born twenty-seven years after Columba died. Adomnán wrote from older writings and from what faithful old men told him. His book is a saint's Life, full of wonders.

Columba was an Irish monk and abbot. Adomnán says he came of noble parents, Fedilmith and Aethne. In his forty-second year, two years after a battle at Cooldrevny, he sailed from Ireland to Britain to make a pilgrimage for Christ. Huyshe puts the voyage in 563. Bede of Jarrow gives 565. Huyshe and later Irish tradition tie the battle to his leaving. Adomnán names it only to date the voyage.

Adomnán tells how Brude, king of the Picts, kept his fortress gates shut against him. Columba made the sign of the cross on the doors and knocked, and the bolts flew back. Bede says Columba turned the northern Picts to the faith by his preaching and example, and they gave him the island for a monastery.

Adomnán says he could not let an hour pass without prayer, reading, writing or manual work. Bede adds that he had founded the Field of Oaks in Ireland. Many houses in Britain and Ireland grew from it and from Iona. The island was ruled by its abbot, a priest, and even the bishops were under him, after Columba's example. Bede, who followed the Roman Easter, says Columba's heirs kept uncertain rules for it.

That last evening, Adomnán says, Columba was copying the Psalms. He stopped at a verse and left the rest for Baithene to write. In the night he gave his last words for the monks. Only Diormit heard them: "that ye have mutual and unfeigned charity among yourselves, with peace." He died before the altar, his hand raised to bless them. Bede says he was seventy-seven.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Bede says many monasteries in Britain and Ireland had their beginning through his disciples from Iona and from the house he built in Ireland, the Field of Oaks, and that the Iona house was the chief of them all.
  • Bede says the abbot of Iona, a priest, ruled the whole province, even its bishops, after the example of Columba, who was a priest and monk and not a bishop.

How they're remembered

  • Bede calls him a famous priest and abbot, a monk by habit and life, and says he left successors known for their love of God and their keeping of the monastic rule.
  • Adomnán says his name reached beyond Ireland and Britain to Spain, Gaul, Italy and Rome itself.
  • At the synod Bede places at Streaneshalch, Whitby, the Irish party called him their most reverend Father Columba; Wilfrid, the priest who spoke for the Roman side, granted he might be a holy man and asked whether he could be preferred to Peter.

What Is Uncertain

  • His life comes from Adomnán, abbot of Iona, whose Life Wentworth Huyshe places between 679 and 704; Huyshe says Adomnán was born twenty-seven years after Columba died. Adomnán says he wrote from older writings and from what faithful old men told him. The Life is a book of prophecies, wonders and visions, and his sayings in it are Adomnán's report.
  • The year he came to Britain. Adomnán gives his forty-second year, two years after the battle of Cooldrevny; Huyshe dates the battle to 561 and the voyage to 563. Bede gives 565, in the ninth year of Bridius, king of the Picts. The Welsh annals, the Annales Cambriae, put his going to Britain in their year 118, which their editor, John Williams ab Ithel, sets at 562.
  • His years and age. Huyshe gives 521 to 597. Bede says he died at seventy-seven, about thirty-two years after he came to Britain. The Middle Irish Life in the Book of Lismore gives seventy-seven years and forty-five in Scotland. An Old Irish Life that Huyshe quotes says he was forty-two when he went and thirty-four years in Alba. Whitley Stokes reports that the birthday the Book of Lismore Life gives, a Thursday, 7 December, points to 517 or 523, but Bishop William Reeves thinks 521 most likely. The Annales Cambriae record his birth and death in years their editor sets at 521 and 595.
  • Why he left Ireland. Huyshe's note says the battle of Cooldrevny was the cause of his exile. His introduction gives the battle as the cause "according to tradition", and calls the tradition that it had something to do with his leaving a constant one. It counts the tales around his exile, the penance among them, as legends. It calls the Old Irish Life's account of a pilgrimage long resolved "perfectly simple and probable". Adomnán names the battle only to date the voyage. He does tell of a synod at Teltown that excommunicated Columba, wrongly as it later proved. William Reeves, as Huyshe reports him, thinks it possibly met in 561, over the battle.
  • Who gave him Iona. Bede says the Picts gave him the island. Huyshe says the Irish annals name Conall, son of Comgill, king of the Irish of Dalriada in Britain; the Irish annals Huyshe cites are not among the sources used here.
  • The day of his death. Adomnán puts it in the night after a Saturday, soon after a day in May when he blessed the island. The Book of Lismore Life says the night of Pentecost Sunday. Huyshe gives his feast as 9 June.
  • Later tradition credits him with poems and hymns. For the Latin hymn Altus Prosator, Huyshe reports the Marquess of Bute's view that the ascription rests on an apparently unbroken tradition and seems open to no serious doubt. The sources used here give only Huyshe's English of some stanzas and poems.
