The Early Medieval Era · 751-888 · Francia

Carolingian Reform Christianity

Francia, 751 to 888 — an empire that treated the correcting of the church's books, schools and worship as an act of government, and left behind the handwriting we still read by.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:16

Read along

When Pippin was anointed king in 751 and his son Charlemagne built an empire across western Europe, they treated the condition of the church as a matter of government: schools attached to cathedrals and monasteries, corrected copies of Scripture and the liturgy, one monastic rule for the whole realm, and decrees ordering that every priest be able to explain the Creed and the Lord's Prayer to his people in words they could understand. Scholars were imported to run it, Alcuin of York above all, and the clear round script their scriptoria developed — Carolingian minuscule — is the ancestor of the letters you are reading now. Almost everything that survives, though, is what people were told rather than what they thought. The reform is documented from the top down, and its ordinary interior life has to be inferred from its own instructions. One striking exception is a lay voice, Dhuoda, a noblewoman writing a handbook of faith and conduct for her absent son in the 840s. The imperial framework that carried the reform broke apart after 888.

Documented Stories

Verden, the Saxon Law, and Alcuin’s Doubts

The Royal Frankish Annals, the court’s own record, state it in a sentence. In 782 the Saxons destroyed a Frankish army in the Süntel hills, and Charlemagne came to the place where the Aller flows into the Weser. There the Saxons handed over to him the men behind the revolt, four thousand five hundred of them, to be put to death, and so it was done. The revised version of the annals adds that they were all beheaded in one day, at a place called Verden. The Capitulary for the Saxon Regions, from about these years, set out the terms of the conquest. It gave death to anyone who burned a church, killed a bishop or priest, or burned the dead in the pagan way. It gave death, too, to a Saxon who hid unbaptized, scorned to come to baptism and chose to stay a pagan. The same law made every Saxon, noble, free or half-free, give a tenth of his goods and his labor to the churches. Alcuin of York, the reform’s leading scholar, saw it otherwise. The index to his works in the Patrologia Latina sums up what his letters say. Faith is a matter of the will, to which no one can be forced. The Saxons were not to be harassed with tithes and harsh punishments, and the demand for tithes is judged to have hindered their conversion. The letters’ own words are not in the Library’s copy. Both kinds of text are the reform’s: the law it enforced, and the objection its own scholar raised.

Dhuoda Writes to the Son She Has Been Made to Give Up

Dhuoda was married to Bernard of Septimania, one of the great lords in the civil war among Charlemagne’s grandsons. In 841, Nithard says, Bernard sent their son William, then fifteen, to Charles the Bald. He told the boy to commend himself to the king, if Charles would grant him Bernard’s holdings in Burgundy. Their second son, born that year, was taken from her before he was baptized and carried off to his father. She did not know his name. Left alone at Uzès, she wrote a book for William and had it copied and sent to him. She calls it a handbook — Liber manualis. She writes, she says, ‘though I am unworthy and frail’, because she cannot be with him, and a mother’s words may do what her presence cannot. She tells William to love God and to honor his father in all things — even, she implies, this thing. He is to be loyal to the king, to read, and to pray for his father’s dead kin, whom she lists by name. She quotes Scripture again and again, with lines of the poet Prudentius and the grammarian Donatus, and she includes acrostic verses spelling out her name and his. She asks him to teach his little brother when he is old enough, and to have her epitaph, which she composes, carved on her grave. Bernard was condemned and beheaded for rebellion in 844. William rose to avenge his father, an older editor’s note in the Patrologia Latina says. Édouard Bondurand, the book’s editor, writes that he was put to death as a rebel in 850. The younger son, Bernard, grew up, Bondurand adds, thinking only of avenging his father and his brother. The book shows, from inside a Frankish household, what the reform’s program of Christian formation looked like when a mother had to do it alone by post.

Connections

In Its Own Record

A reform of the old Frankish church, led in part by English churchmen. Cluny later kept the Rule of Benedict that the reform had pressed on the monasteries of the realm.

Descended from

  • continues Merovingian Gallic Christianity Widely Accepted Major — In 742 Boniface wrote that Carloman wanted a synod to repair the Frankish church. It had been trampled, he said, for sixty or seventy years. In 751 the bishops of Gaul anointed Pippin king. Historians treat it as the same Frankish church, reformed but not replaced when the new kings took power.

Direct descendants

  • gave rise to Scandinavian Conversion Christianity Documented Major — In 823, the Frankish royal annals report, Archbishop Ebo of Reims went to preach among the Danes. He went with the emperor's backing and the pope's authority, and he baptized many. In 826 the Danish king Harald was baptized at Mainz with his wife and a great crowd of Danes. From 829 Anskar, a monk of Corbie, preached in Sweden.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced Early Benedictine / Italian Monasticism Documented Major — In 802 Charlemagne gathered abbots and monks at Aachen. There they read the Rule of Saint Benedict together. He then ordered that anything done in monasteries against the rule be put right by it. Sixty years before, in 742, Carloman's council had told monks and nuns to live by the same rule.Lasting result: In 802 Charlemagne gave an order about monasteries. Anything done in them against the Rule of Saint Benedict was to be put right by that rule.
  • influenced The Roman Church in the Age of Gregory the Great Widely Accepted — A Carolingian list sets out what clergy must learn. It tells canons to know 'the pastoral book', which its editor says is Gregory the Great's. James Barmby, who translated Gregory, goes further. He says that in 813, councils called by Charlemagne made all bishops study it.
  • influenced Anglo-Saxon Christianity Documented Major — English churchmen helped lead the Frankish reform from the start. In 742 Carloman, a ruler of the Franks, set the English missionary Boniface as archbishop over the bishops of his realm. Alcuin of York later wrote that he had come to Francia to help King Charles in the Catholic faith.Lasting result: In 742 Carloman, on the advice of Boniface and his fellow bishops, ordered a church synod each year. Its aim was to restore church law and correct Christian practice.

Influenced

  • influenced Cluniac Reform Monasticism Inferential-Thin — From 742 the Frankish reformers ordered monks and nuns to live by the Rule of Benedict. Cluny, founded around 910, was a house of Benedictine monks. The tie rests on that shared Rule, not on any named founder who passed from one to the other.
  • influenced The Latin Papal Church (Gregorian Reform to Innocent III) Widely Accepted — In the ninth century a forger in the Frankish lands made a collection of fake early papal letters, the False Decretals. In 1081 Pope Gregory VII quoted one of them, a letter said to be from Pope Julius, to argue that Rome could judge earthly rulers. His modern editor traced that letter to the forged collection.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

When Pippin was anointed king in 751 and Charlemagne built an empire across western Europe, they treated the state of the church as a matter of government, correcting the texts of Scripture and the liturgy and ordering that every priest be able to explain the Creed to his people in words they understood. Almost everything that survives records what people were told rather than what they thought, though one noblewoman, Dhuoda, wrote a handbook of faith for her absent son in the 840s that lets a lay voice through.

Legacy

Its church law ran on into the papal reform of the eleventh century. In 1081 Pope Gregory VII still quoted papal letters forged in the Frankish lands two centuries before. Through that reform, such texts passed into the law of the Latin church for the rest of the Middle Ages. Its most lasting gifts are practical. Carolingian minuscule, the clear round script of its copyists, became the model for Renaissance humanists, and so stands behind modern printed type. Its corrected texts of the Bible and the Roman liturgy stayed standard for centuries. And its cathedral and monastic schools were the seed-bed of the medieval universities. The chapel Charlemagne built at Aachen still stands and is still in use.

Experience It Today

  • The Palatine Chapel, Aachen Cathedral, Germany — Charlemagne's own chapel, consecrated in 805 and Germany's first UNESCO World Heritage Site

  • Dhuoda, Handbook for William (trans. Carol Neel, CUA Press, 1991) (primary) — A lay noblewoman's own religious counsel to her son (843) - a genuine ordinary-lay voice inside the reform world, though hers is an aristocratic voice, not a non-elite one.
  • Rosamond McKitterick, The Frankish Church and the Carolingian Reforms, 789-895 (Royal Historical Society, 1977) (secondary) — The standard study of the reform as a pastoral program, arguing it reached further down than 'policy tradition' skeptics allow.
