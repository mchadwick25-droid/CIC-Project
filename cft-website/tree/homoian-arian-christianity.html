Homoian ('Arian') Christianity

The Roman Empire, and then the Gothic and Vandal kingdoms, fourth to sixth centuries — for a while the imperial church itself, and the tradition that gave the Germanic peoples their first Bible.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:51

Read along

For long stretches of the fourth century, the church the emperors backed was not the one that won in the end. The Homoians — from the Greek homoios, "like" — taught that the Son is like the Father according to the Scriptures. They refused to go further and call him of one being with the Father. Scripture never used that word, they said, and no council should force it on anyone. They did not call themselves Arians; that label came from their opponents. One of their own bishops put it the other way round: Arius had followed the bishops, not the bishops Arius. The emperors Constantius II and later Valens backed this position, and the councils of 359 and 360 adopted it. It stayed the imperial church's official position long enough for Jerome to write that the world groaned to find itself Arian. Its most lasting work was done outside the empire. Ulfila, made a bishop around 341, spent his life among the Goths. He made an alphabet for their language and translated the Bible into it. The Goths became Christians in this form, and the Vandals, Burgundians, and Lombards held it too. Jordanes, a sixth-century historian of the Goths, says they passed it on to "all people of their speech." Homoian kingdoms ruled Italy, Spain, and North Africa for generations after the empire itself had turned Nicene. The Visigoths of Spain went over to the Nicene creed at Toledo in 589. The Lombards of Italy were the last to follow, two generations later.

Documented Stories

The Moses of the Goths

Ulfila had been consecrated bishop ‘of the Christians in the Gothic land’ around 341 by Eusebius of Nicomedia, at a time when the imperial church was Homoian; he was himself of Cappadocian captive stock on his mother’s side, spoke Gothic, Greek and Latin, and had been sent home to a people that had received Christianity from prisoners taken in third-century raids. Within seven years the Gothic ruler — Philostorgius, the Arian historian who is the main source, calls him ‘the impious’ — turned on the Christians. Philostorgius describes it: he ‘crossed over into the Roman territory with a great multitude’, and the emperor Constantius received them and settled them ‘in the parts of Moesia’ near Nicopolis, at the foot of the Haemus mountains, ‘where they lived a pastoral life’; and Constantius, Philostorgius says, ‘was wont to speak of him as the Moses of our time’. There Ulfila spent the remaining thirty-odd years of his life, presiding over a community of refugee Goths, devising an alphabet for their language out of Greek and runic letters, and translating the Bible into it — all of it, Philostorgius adds, except the books of Kings, ‘which contain the history of wars, since the Gothic nation was fond of war and needed the bridle rather than the spur’. The empire’s official heresy came to the barbarians through a refugee bishop who had led his flock out of a persecution, and it was the form in which most of the Germanic peoples first knew Christ.

‘I, Ulfila, Bishop and Confessor, Have Always So Believed’

Auxentius, bishop of Durostorum on the lower Danube, had been raised by Ulfila from boyhood, and after the old man’s death wrote a letter about his life and teaching that survives only because a Homoian bishop of the next generation copied it into the margins of a manuscript of Ambrose — a book of the enemy, annotated by the losing side. In 383 the emperor Theodosius summoned bishops of all parties to Constantinople for a conference of the sects, and Ulfila, now about seventy and forty years a bishop, made the journey. Auxentius says that before he died he ‘left in his testament’ a statement of faith for his people, and gives its words: ‘I, Ulfila, bishop and confessor, have always so believed, and in this, the one true faith, I make my journey to my Lord: I believe in one God the Father, alone unbegotten and invisible, and in his only-begotten Son, our Lord and God, the designer and maker of all creation, having none like him … and in one Holy Spirit, an illuminating and sanctifying power … neither God nor Lord, but the faithful minister of Christ.’ It is the Homoian faith in its own words: the Son subordinate but divine and creator; the Spirit a minister, not God; the Nicene homoousion refused as unscriptural. Almost none survive. The Gothic Bible does, in one purple codex at Uppsala. The creed of the man who made it was preserved by his opponents’ carelessness and his disciple’s love.

Descended from

  • grew out of Latin Christianity in the Danubian Provinces Widely Accepted Major — Athanasius of Alexandria names Ursacius of Singidunum and Valens of Mursa as leaders of the Arian party. Both were bishops in the lands along the Danube. Today scholars see them as two of the men who did most to shape the Homoian settlement.
  • grew out of The Church of Antioch in the Third Century Documented Major — Arius wrote to Eusebius of Nicomedia as 'my fellow-Lucianist'. Lucian of Antioch had taught them both. Philostorgius lists Lucian's pupils. Among them were Leontius, who was later bishop of Antioch, and one Eudoxius. Both these reports come from Arius's own side.

Direct descendants

  • gave rise to Gothic, Vandal & Lombard Homoian Christianity Documented Major — Ulfila, bishop of the Goths, took part in the council at Constantinople in 360 that confirmed the Homoian creed. In 427 or 428 the Homoian bishop Maximinus came to Africa with the Goths. He named the creed of Ariminum as his faith. Socrates of Constantinople wrote in the fifth century. He says many Goths took up this faith for the sake of the emperor Valens. Many still held it in his day.

Broke With

  • broke with Anomoean (Eunomian) Christianity Documented Major — In 360 a Homoian council at Constantinople deposed Aetius. Its letter says he was cast out of the church. Theodoret says Eunomius later split from Eudoxius and set up a group of his own. Socrates of Constantinople and Philostorgius place the final break under Valens. Philostorgius wrote from the side of Eunomius.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced Imperial and Juridical Christianity Documented — The emperor Constantius II called the bishops to two councils in 359. He hoped to bring them all to one creed. A council at Constantinople in 360 confirmed the creed read at Ariminum, and Ulfila of the Goths signed it. In 427 or 428 the Homoian bishop Maximinus still named that creed as his faith.

Argued Against

  • argued against Cappadocian Christianity Widely Accepted — Around 375 the emperor's vicar in Pontus ordered Gregory of Nyssa to stand trial. The charge was that he had mishandled church money. Basil of Caesarea then wrote that his foes 'have expelled my brother from Nyssa' and put a rival in his place. The editors of Gregory of Nazianzus's letters, in 1894, read those foes as the party of Valens, which opposed Nicaea.

Opposed By

  • argued against The Gallic Nicene Episcopate Documented Major — Phoebadius of Agen wrote a Book Against the Arians. Hilary of Poitiers wrote On the Councils to the bishops of Gaul and Britain. He praises them for condemning the creed sent from Sirmium. He also says they had refused communion to Saturninus of Arles.Lasting result: The bishops of Gaul refused communion to Saturninus. They condemned the creed sent to them from Sirmium as soon as it came.
  • argued against Imperial and Juridical Christianity Documented Major — The Council of Constantinople met in 381 at the call of the emperor Theodosius. Its first canon names the Arians, or Eudoxians, among the heresies it rejects. Laws of 381 and 383 also name the Arians. They bar heretics from building churches or from meeting.Lasting result: In July 383 an imperial law said the Arians could not meet or gather any crowd. Eunomians and other groups were banned as well.
  • argued against The Church of Aquileia Documented Major — The Council of Aquileia met in 381. There the city's own bishop, Valerian, spoke out against Palladius, who was a Homoian bishop. He said that whoever defends Arius is an Arian. And whoever will not condemn the blasphemies of Arius, he added, is a blasphemer as well. The council condemned both Palladius and Secundianus.Lasting result: At Aquileia the bishops condemned Palladius. They found him unworthy to be a priest, and said a Catholic must be made bishop in his place.
  • argued against Ambrosian Milan (standalone) Documented — At Aquileia in 381, Ambrose led the questioning of Palladius, a Homoian bishop. Palladius would not condemn a letter of Arius, so Ambrose had it read out. In Milan in 386 he preached against Auxentius, a rival bishop who was also Homoian. He declared him cast out.
  • argued against Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity Documented — Jerome wrote his Dialogue against the Luciferians in about 379. In it he blames the Homoian leaders Valens and Ursacius for what was done at the council of Ariminum. There, he says, the Nicene faith was condemned, and the whole world groaned to find itself Arian.
  • argued against The Luciferians Documented — In exile from 355 to 361, Lucifer of Cagliari wrote pamphlets to the emperor Constantius II. He called the bishops of the emperor's party false bishops of his sect, the Arians. He asked how Christians could ever meet with them.
  • argued against Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition Documented — Athanasius of Alexandria wrote a Defence Against the Arians. In it he quotes the letter of the council held at Sardica. That letter names the leaders of the party against him, Ursacius and Valens among them.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

The Homoians taught that the Son is 'like' the Father according to the Scriptures. But they refused to say he was of one being with the Father. No council, they held, should force a word that Scripture itself never used. The emperors Constantius II and Valens backed them, and for long stretches of the fourth century this was the imperial church's official position. It lasted long enough for Jerome to write that the world groaned to find itself Arian. Its bishop Ulfila spent his life among the Goths, made an alphabet for their language, and translated the Bible into it. The Goths became Christians in this form, and the Vandals, Burgundians, and Lombards held it too. Homoian kingdoms ruled Italy, Spain, and North Africa for generations after the empire itself had turned Nicene.

Legacy

Its own churches are gone. Every Homoian kingdom in time came over to the Nicene creed, and no community today carries the tradition forward. What it left behind is less direct. Large parts of Ulfila's Gothic Bible survive, and scholars of the Germanic languages still edit and study it. Its finest copy is kept in Uppsala. It was written in silver and gold letters on purple parchment in the fifth or sixth century. Its buildings survive too. Ravenna was the capital of Theoderic, an Arian king. Its Arian Baptistery and Sant'Apollinare Nuovo still stand, with mosaics among the finest anywhere. And the Nicene Creed itself partly carries the shape of the argument with them. In 381 the Council of Constantinople confirmed the faith of Nicaea, which calls the Son of one being with the Father. In the same canon it condemned the Homoians by name, as "the Arians or Eudoxians."

Experience It Today

  • The Arian Baptistery, Ravenna — built for Homoian worship under Theoderic, part of the UNESCO-listed Early Christian Monuments of Ravenna
  • The Codex Argenteus, Uppsala University Library — the "Silver Bible" of Ulfila's Gothic translation, on permanent display

On the Nicene Creed

This is the tradition the Nicene Creed was written against. Its teachers would say the Son is "like" the Father, but not that he is "true God from true God, of one being with the Father" — the very clauses the creed added in order to rule that position out. This map draws its boundary at that creed, so this tradition falls outside it. That is a statement about doctrine and nothing else: for long stretches of the fourth century these were the churches of the empire, and their leaders included some of the most effective missionaries and translators in Christian history.
