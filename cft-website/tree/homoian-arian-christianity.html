The Imperial Church Era · 4th-6th c. · Empire-wide; then Gothic & Vandal kingdoms

Homoian ('Arian') Christianity

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

For long stretches of the fourth century, the church the emperors backed was not the one that won in the end. The Homoians — from the Greek homoios, "like" — taught that the Son is like the Father according to the Scriptures. They refused to go further and call him of one being with the Father. Scripture never used that word, they said, and no council should force it on anyone. They did not call themselves Arians; that label came from their opponents. One of their own bishops put it the other way round: Arius had followed the bishops, not the bishops Arius. The emperors Constantius II and later Valens backed this position, and the councils of 359 and 360 adopted it. It stayed the imperial church's official position long enough for Jerome to write that the world groaned to find itself Arian. Its most lasting work was done outside the empire. Ulfila, made a bishop around 341, spent his life among the Goths. He made an alphabet for their language and translated the Bible into it. The Goths became Christians in this form, and the Vandals, Burgundians, and Lombards held it too. Jordanes, a sixth-century historian of the Goths, says they passed it on to "all people of their speech." Homoian kingdoms ruled Italy, Spain, and North Africa for generations after the empire itself had turned Nicene. The Visigoths of Spain went over to the Nicene creed at Toledo in 589. The Lombards of Italy were the last to follow, two generations later.

In Its Own Record

Grew Out Of

  • Latin Christianity in the Danubian Provinces Widely Accepted Major — Athanasius lists Ursacius of Singidunum and Valens of Mursa, bishops in the Danubian provinces, among the leaders of the Arian party. Modern scholars count them among the chief architects of the Homoian settlement.
  • The Church of Antioch in the Third Century Documented Major — Arius wrote to Eusebius of Nicomedia as 'my fellow-Lucianist', naming their teacher Lucian of Antioch. Philostorgius lists Lucian's disciples, among them Leontius, later bishop of Antioch, and a Eudoxius. Both reports come from Arius's own side.

Gave Rise To

  • Gothic, Vandal & Lombard Homoian Christianity Documented Major — Ulfila, bishop of the Goths, took part in the council at Constantinople in 360 that confirmed the Homoian creed. In 427 or 428 the Homoian bishop Maximinus came to Africa with the Goths. He named the creed of Ariminum as his faith. Socrates, writing in the fifth century, says many Goths took up this faith for the sake of the emperor Valens, and that many still held it in his day.

Influenced By

  • Imperial and Juridical Christianity Documented — The emperor Constantius II called the bishops to two councils in 359. He hoped to bring them all to one creed. A council at Constantinople in 360 confirmed the creed read at Ariminum, and Ulfila of the Goths signed it. In 427 or 428 the Homoian bishop Maximinus still named that creed as his faith.

Argued Against

  • Cappadocian Christianity Widely Accepted — Around 375 the emperor's vicar in Pontus ordered Gregory of Nyssa to stand trial. The charge was that he had mishandled church money. Basil then wrote that his foes 'have expelled my brother from Nyssa' and put a rival in his place. The editors of Gregory of Nazianzus's letters, in 1894, read those foes as the party of Valens, which opposed Nicaea.

Opposed By

  • The Gallic Nicene Episcopate Documented Major — Phoebadius of Agen wrote a Book Against the Arians. Hilary of Poitiers, writing On the Councils to the bishops of Gaul and Britain, praises them for condemning the creed sent from Sirmium. He also says they had refused communion to Saturninus of Arles.Lasting result: The bishops of Gaul refused communion to Saturninus and condemned the creed sent to them from Sirmium as soon as it arrived.
  • Imperial and Juridical Christianity Documented Major — The Council of Constantinople met in 381 at the emperor Theodosius's summons. Its first canon names the Arians, or Eudoxians, among the heresies it rejects. Imperial laws of 381 and 383 also name the Arians when they bar heretics from building churches or holding assemblies.Lasting result: An imperial law of July 383 banned Arians, along with Eunomians and other groups, from meeting or gathering any crowd.
  • The Church of Aquileia Documented Major — The Council of Aquileia met in 381. There the city's own bishop, Valerian, spoke out against Palladius, who was a Homoian bishop. He said that whoever defends Arius is an Arian. And whoever will not condemn the blasphemies of Arius, he added, is a blasphemer as well. The council condemned both Palladius and Secundianus.Lasting result: At Aquileia the bishops condemned Palladius, judged him unworthy of the priesthood and ordered a Catholic bishop ordained in his place.
  • Ambrosian Milan (standalone) Documented — At Aquileia in 381, Ambrose led the questioning of Palladius, a Homoian bishop. Palladius would not condemn a letter of Arius, so Ambrose had it read out. In Milan in 386 he preached against Auxentius, a rival bishop who was also Homoian. He declared him cast out.
  • Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity Documented — Jerome wrote his Dialogue against the Luciferians in about 379. In it he blames the Homoian leaders Valens and Ursacius for what was done at the council of Ariminum. There, he says, the Nicene faith was condemned, and the whole world groaned to find itself Arian.

Broke With

  • Anomoean (Eunomian) Christianity Documented Major — In 360 a Homoian council at Constantinople deposed Aetius. Its letter says he was cast out of the church. Theodoret says Eunomius later split from Eudoxius and set up a group of his own. Socrates and Philostorgius place the final break under Valens. Philostorgius wrote from the side of Eunomius.
