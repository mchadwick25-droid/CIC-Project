Tertullian

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Latin Apologists.

Also called Tertullian of Carthage, Tertullianus, Septimius Tertullianus, Tertullianus Afer

Tertullian of Carthage was the foremost early writer of the faith in Latin, around the year 200. He defended the Christians to Rome's governors and claimed the Scriptures for the churches the apostles founded; later in life he joined the New Prophecy, a strict movement of prophets, and wrote against the bishops.

About This Person

At Carthage, in Roman Africa, Tertullian wrote to the governors who judged Christians for Rome. If they could not look into the charges openly, from the judge's seat, he wrote: "Let the truth reach your ears at least by the hidden road of silent writing." Of the truth he added: "For now, she longs for one thing: not to be condemned unknown."

Tertullian was the foremost early writer of the faith in Latin, around the year 200. He defended the Christians to Rome's governors, claimed the Scriptures for the churches the apostles founded, and said in Latin how God is one and yet three, and the Latin writers after him read him closely. Yet later in life he joined a strict movement, the New Prophecy, and wrote against the bishops.

Those lines open his Apology, a defense of Christians on trial. The governors, he argued, condemned the very name of Christian without asking what Christians did. He ended it in defiance: the more often Rome cut them down, the more they grew, for the blood of Christians is seed.

Rival teachers used the Scriptures too, for teachings the churches rejected. Marcion, as Tertullian tells it in another book, taught that beside the Creator there was another god, who was only good. Valentinus told of a swarm of divine beings, and said the Creator came from the sin of one of them. Marcion cut the Scriptures down to suit himself, and Valentinus kept them whole but bent their meaning. In that book he answered them with a plea from the law courts, one that throws a case out before it is argued. Before anyone argued from Scripture, he said, it must be settled whose the Scriptures were. They belonged to the churches the apostles founded, such as Rome, which still kept what the apostles taught. Then he spoke as an owner speaks to trespassers: "By what right, Marcion, do you cut my timber? By whose leave, Valentinus, do you turn my springs aside?" He went on: "This property is mine. I have held it long; I held it first." And he claimed: "I am the heir of the apostles."

The New Prophecy had begun in Phrygia, in today's Turkey. Its prophets said that the Holy Spirit, the Helper Jesus promised, was speaking anew through them, and they asked a stricter life of Christians, with no second marriage after a husband or wife had died. Tertullian wrote that his acceptance and defense of the Spirit had set his side apart from the others, whom he called the unspiritual. Jerome, who wrote his life, says the envy and insults of the clergy of Rome drove him to the New Prophecy.

After his turn he wrote Against Praxeas. By Tertullian's account, Praxeas taught at Rome that the Father himself was born and suffered, so that Father and Son were one and the same. Tertullian held that Father, Son and Spirit are distinct but not divided, and he showed it with things from nature: "The root and the tree are two things, but joined. The spring and the river are two forms, but undivided. The sun and the ray are two forms, but they hold together." Of the Spirit he went on: "The Spirit is third from God and the Son, as the fruit from the tree is third from the root." In the same book he put it in two words. God is one substance, one in what God is, and Father, Son and Spirit are three persons, each distinct. The Latin word for the Trinity, trinitas, stands in this book too.

When a bishop announced that he would forgive adultery once the sinner had repented, Tertullian answered him in On Modesty. The church would forgive sins, he wrote, but it would be the church of the Spirit, through a spiritual man, and not the church that is a number of bishops. Jerome lists the book among those he wrote directly against the church.

A generation later, Cyprian, the bishop of Carthage, never let a day pass without reading him. Jerome heard it from an old man in Italy who had met Cyprian's secretary, and Cyprian, the secretary said, would often ask, "Give me the master," meaning Tertullian.

About two centuries after Tertullian, Augustine wrote that many of his works, written with great eloquence, were still read. Yet he listed the Tertullianists among the heresies, the teachings he counted outside the true faith. Tertullian became a heretic, Augustine wrote, because he went over to the prophets he had once argued against, and began to condemn second marriage as if it were sexual sin. Later he broke from the prophets too and gathered meetings of his own. His followers at Carthage dwindled. While Augustine was there, the very few who were left crossed into the Catholic church, the main body, and handed over their church building.

People and Stories

Documented Stories

The Soldier Who Would Not Wear the Crown

Tertullian opens On the Military Crown with the event that prompted it. A donative was being distributed to the troops on the accession of new emperors, and the soldiers came forward one by one, crowned with laurel, to receive it. One came with the wreath in his hand. Asked why he was dressed differently, he said he could not do as the others did, and when pressed said the reason: he was a Christian. He was stripped of his cloak and boots, handed over his sword, and was put in prison to await execution. What interests Tertullian is not the soldier but the reaction of the church. Many Christians, he reports, condemned the man as rash and headstrong — ‘eager to die’, provoking trouble for a peaceful congregation over ‘a mere matter of dress’, when nothing in Scripture forbade a crown. Tertullian, already leaning toward the New Prophecy, took the soldier’s side with everything he had: the man had been ‘more steadfast than his brethren, who thought they could serve two masters’, and the whole book is an argument that a Christian’s ordinary habits — not swearing by the emperor’s genius, not wearing what idol-festivals wore — were themselves a confession. The soldier is unnamed, his fate unrecorded. The argument he started, over how far a Christian can go along with the customs of the state before it becomes denial, never closed.

‘You Wretches, If You Want to Die…’

Writing an open letter to Scapula, the proconsul of Africa, in 212, Tertullian tried to make him see how persecution looked from the other side, and told him what had happened to a predecessor in Asia. Arrius Antoninus, governor there, was ‘vigorously persecuting’, and in response ‘all the Christians of the city’ came to his tribunal in a body and offered themselves. He had a few led off to execution and said to the rest: ‘You wretches, if you want to die, you have cliffs to leap from and ropes to hang by.’ Tertullian tells it with approval — his point is that if the same happened in Carthage, Scapula would have to decimate the city, and would find himself executing men and women of every rank, ‘perhaps even persons of your own household’. He does not stop to ask whether the Asian Christians were right to do it, and the church of Smyrna, a generation earlier, had explicitly said they were not. The episode is real enough to have entered the record independently, and it captures an early Christian temper that later centuries found hard to look at: a willingness to court death that could shade into demanding it, and an eagerness to see the courts overwhelmed. Tertullian, who never faced a tribunal himself, was its most eloquent defender.

Key Works

  • Apology: a defense of the Christians addressed to the governors who sat as judges for Rome: the charge is only the name, the crowd blames the Christians for every flood, drought, famine and plague, and the more they are cut down, the more they grow
  • The Prescription Against Heretics: a plea of the kind the law courts call a demurrer, a formal objection that throws a case out before it is argued: those who teach against the apostles may not argue from Scripture at all, because the Scriptures belong to the churches that received them from the apostles, such as Ephesus and Rome
  • Against Praxeas: written after his turn to the New Prophecy, against a teacher at Rome who, by Tertullian's account, said the Father himself was born and suffered; he answers that God is one substance and three persons, distinct but not divided, and the Latin words trinitas, persona and substantia stand in his text

Connections

Tradition

The Latin Apologists

Connections on the Map

Influenced By

  • Montanism (the New Prophecy) Documented — Tertullian of Carthage took up the New Prophecy's claims. He quotes a saying of the Paraclete given 'by the mouth of the prophetess Prisca'. In Against Praxeas he writes that his side withdrew from the 'carnally-minded' over the Paraclete.

Influenced

  • The Latin Apologists Documented — Lactantius wrote the Divine Institutes. In it he says Tertullian had already argued the same cause in full. He did so in his book called the Apology.
  • Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity Documented — Jerome says Cyprian read Tertullian every day and asked for him as 'the master'. Jerome had this from an old man who had been Cyprian's secretary. Augustine later said that many of Tertullian's works were still read, and that they were very well written.
  • Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity Documented — Jerome found Tertullian 'packed with meaning' but rough in style. He wrote that Origen should sometimes be read for his learning, 'just as we read Tertullian', taking the good and leaving the bad.

Argued Against

  • Modalist Monarchianism Documented — In Against Praxeas, Tertullian names Praxeas. He charges that Praxeas 'crucified the Father'. He also answers those who think that if the Trinity has an order, its unity is split.
  • Marcion / Marcionism Documented — Tertullian wrote five books Against Marcion. In them he attacks Marcion by name, charging that 'the heretic of Pontus introduces two Gods'.
  • Valentinian & other Gnostic Christianities Documented — Tertullian wrote a book Against the Valentinians. He traces the school back to its founder. He says Valentinus had hoped to become a bishop. When a confessor was chosen instead, Valentinus broke with the church.

  • Augustine says his followers, the Tertullianists, dwindled to a last remnant at Carthage; the very few left crossed to the Catholic church and handed over their basilica, which he calls still well known.

How they're remembered

  • Jerome places him first of the Latin writers in his book after Victor and Apollonius, and calls him a man of keen and vigorous character.
  • Lactantius says he was skilled in literature of every kind but had little readiness in eloquence, was not polished and was very obscure, so that he did not find sufficient renown; elsewhere Lactantius says he fully pleaded the same cause in the treatise entitled the Apology.
  • Augustine says many of his little works, most eloquently written, are read.
  • Vincent of Lérins, in 434, gives him by far the first place among the Latins, as Origen has among the Greeks, then calls him more eloquent than faithful, quotes Hilary that by his subsequent error he detracted from the authority of his approved writings, and says he was a great trial in the Church.

Open Questions

  • Nearly all that is known of his life comes from his own books and from Jerome's short life of him, written about 170 years later. Eusebius and Lactantius wrote about a century after him, and Augustine and Vincent about two centuries. No source says when or why he was converted.
  • Trained in the law. Eusebius calls him a man well versed in the laws of the Romans and one of those especially distinguished in Rome. His books use the language of the law courts: the Prescription pleads like a lawyer, and speaks as an owner does of timber, springs and heirs. No old source says he practiced as an advocate, and that is not settled.
  • The governors. The Apology speaks to the rulers of the Roman Empire who sat as judges for Rome, not to the emperors.
  • Why he turned to the New Prophecy. Jerome, who wrote his life, says that after middle life he lapsed to the doctrine of Montanus, driven by the envy and abuse of the clergy of the Roman church. Augustine gives no reason for the turn. He says Tertullian became a heretic because, going over to the Phrygians whom he had earlier refuted, he began to condemn second marriage as fornication against the apostle's teaching, and later broke from them too and spread his own gatherings.
  • From whom he withdrew. In Against Praxeas he says his side withdrew from the carnally-minded, whom the Latin calls psychici, on its acknowledgment and maintenance of the Paraclete, the Helper whom Jesus promised and whom the New Prophecy's prophets said was speaking through them; unspiritual is the plain English for psychici. The old sources do not say whether this was a break with the church at Carthage or only with the bishop of Rome. Augustine says he later broke from the New Prophecy too.
  • His office. That he was a presbyter rests only on Jerome, who says he was one until middle life.
  • Foremost. The rank is Widely Accepted and rests on the old witnesses together: Vincent says that as Origen holds by far the first place among the Greeks, so does Tertullian among the Latins, and Augustine says many of his works are most eloquently written and are read. Jerome's Latin, primus post Victorem et Apollonium Latinorum ponitur, places him first of the Latin writers in his book after Victor and Apollonius, a place in his list; the English translation renders it now regarded as chief.
  • The Latin words. Trinitas, persona and substantia all stand in his Against Praxeas. That he coined them, or first put them to work in Latin, is a modern claim, and no held old or later witness says it. The Greek word for the Trinity is older: Theophilus of Antioch uses it in To Autolycus II.15. No held source says the Council of Nicaea used his words.
  • Praxeas and the bishops. What is known of Praxeas comes from Tertullian's own hostile account. The old sources held here do not name the bishop of Rome who, in that account, withdrew his letter of peace, or the bishop whose edict On Modesty answers; both are disputed.
  • When the books were written. The years proposed for the Prescription, the turn, Against Praxeas and On Modesty rest on modern reconstruction and are left out. Jerome lists On Modesty among the volumes he says Tertullian wrote directly against the church, and On Modesty itself cites the new prophets, which places it after his turn. Jerome says many of his small works are not extant.
  • Cyprian's words. The report that Cyprian read him daily and said, Give me the master, reaches us through Jerome, from the old man Paul of Concordia, who had met Cyprian's aged secretary at Rome. Jerome's Latin says so; the English translation of Jerome wrongly makes Paul himself the secretary, while Heurtley, quoting Kaye, reads it as the Latin does and gives the words as Give me my master. No held source says that Cyprian and Tertullian ever met.
  • Novatian. Jerome calls Novatian's On the Trinity a sort of epitome of Tertullian's work, and says many mistakenly ascribe it to Cyprian. That is Jerome's judgment, and the card gives no other.
  • His father. Jerome's Latin says patre centurione proconsulari, a centurion on the governor's staff, and his Chronicle says the same; the English translation of the Lives reads son of a proconsul or Centurion, which is a mistranslation, and Heurtley's quotation of Kaye reads the son of a proconsular centurion.
  • Vincent's verdict. The English reads more eloquent by far than faithful, but its editor notes that Baluze reads fidelior and others read felicior, so the reading is disputed. Hilary's judgment reaches us here only through Vincent in English. The decree wrongly called Gelasian lists Opuscula Tertulliani among the books not received, beside the History of Eusebius and the works of Lactantius; its date and its author have long been disputed.
  • The quoted Latin. The Latin of the Apology and the Prescription is held only in Oehler's 1853 edition in a damaged scan, which has lost words and garbled others; no other held print confirms them, so these quotations are printed in English alone, except one short line of the Prescription that the scan prints cleanly, I am the heir of the apostles, which carries the Latin. For Against Praxeas the Latin of the root and the tree is held in Kroymann's print, which Oehler's agrees with; Kroymann's scan misreads one word of the sentence on the Spirit, which Oehler's print confirms.
