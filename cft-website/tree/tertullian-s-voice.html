Tertullian

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Latin Apologists.

Also called Tertullian of Carthage, Tertullianus, Septimius Tertullianus, Tertullianus Afer

Convert and writer of Carthage, the foremost early writer of the faith in Latin. In the Apology he defended the Christians before Rome's governors; in the Prescription he claimed the Scriptures for the churches the apostles founded; in Against Praxeas he put the Latin words trinitas, persona and substantia to work for God as one substance in three persons. He later took up the strict New Prophecy, and Augustine counted his followers, the Tertullianists, among the heretics.

About This Person

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 4:25

Read along

In about 197, Tertullian wrote to the governors who sat in judgment for Rome. If they dared not look into the charges openly, he said, "Then let the truth reach your ears at least by the hidden road of silent writing." He pleaded: "One thing only she asks for now: not to be condemned unknown."

Tertullian of Carthage, in Roman Africa, was a convert trained in Roman law, as Eusebius says, who became the foremost early writer of the faith in Latin. He defended the Christians before Rome's courts, claimed the Scriptures for the apostles' churches, and put to work Latin words for God as one substance in three persons. Then he turned to a strict movement of prophets, the New Prophecy, and two centuries later Augustine counted his followers, the Tertullianists, among the heretics.

That book was his Apology, a defense of the Christians. The governors, he argued, hated and judged the name of Christian without learning what Christians did, while crowds blamed them for every flood, drought, famine or plague. He closed defiant: the more you cut us down, the more we grow; "the blood of Christians is seed."

Against teachers who built new doctrines on Scripture, he borrowed a lawyer's plea that throws a case out before it is argued. Their arguments should not be heard until it was settled whose the Scriptures were. The faith, he said, passed from Christ to the apostles, then to their churches, such as Ephesus and Rome, which anyone could visit. "By what right, Marcion, do you cut my timber? By whose leave, Valentinus, do you turn my springs aside?" He went on, "This property is mine. I have held it long; I held it first." And he ended, "I am the heir of the apostles."

The New Prophecy came from Phrygia, in today's Turkey, and its prophets said the Paraclete, the Helper Jesus promised, the Holy Spirit, was speaking anew through them. They asked for stricter fasts, no marrying again after a spouse had died, and no flight from persecution. His defense of the Paraclete, he writes, set him apart from those he called the unspiritual. Why he turned, two later writers disagree. Jerome blames the envy and insults of the clergy at Rome. Augustine says he joined prophets he had once argued against, then broke from them too and gathered his own followers.

After his turn, he wrote against Praxeas, who, by Tertullian's account, taught at Rome that the Father himself was born and suffered. At creation, he said, God commands the light and the Word, the Son, makes it, so the two are distinct. "I have already said how you should take the word 'another'. It names a person, not a substance. It marks a distinction, not a division. I hold everywhere one substance in three who are joined together." Substance is what God is, one and undivided; person is who is distinct: Father, Son and Spirit. His text holds the Latin words trinitas, persona and substantia, which, later writers say, became the Latin church's words for God.

A bishop offered to forgive adultery after repentance. In what is probably his last surviving book, Tertullian replied that only the church of the Spirit could forgive it, "not the church which consists of a number of bishops."

Novatian wrote a book on the Trinity that Jerome took for a summary of Tertullian's, though a modern historian doubts it. Most of Tertullian's life rests on his own books and on Jerome. Jerome heard, at two removes, that Cyprian, the next great bishop of Carthage, read him daily and would ask, "Give me the master." A century on, Lactantius said his Apology had already pleaded the Christians' cause in full.

His followers at Carthage bore his name down to Augustine's day, when the last few joined the Catholic church, the main body, and gave up their building. Augustine named Tertullian a heretic for going over to the New Prophecy.

Tertullian, Jerome says, is said to have lived to a great old age. No one records when or how he died.

Timeline

Dates
No source gives his birth or his death. Jerome says only that he flourished chiefly under the emperors Severus and Antoninus Caracalla and is said to have lived to a decrepit old age. The note to the English Eusebius gives the common view of a birth about 160, and Nöldechen's conclusion of a birth about 150 and a death about 230; the note to the English Jerome gives a birth about 160 and a death about 245. Monceaux dates the Apology to 197, the Prescription to about 200, the turn to the New Prophecy to 207 to 212, the final break with the church to the start of 213, Against Praxeas to after 213, and On Modesty to between 217 and 222, probably the last of his surviving books. McGiffert says almost all scholars place the Apology in 197 to 204 and holds 197 practically established; older views give 198 (Mosheim) and 204 (Kaye)
Place
Carthage, in the Roman province of Africa, today in Tunisia (Jerome: of the city of Carthage in the province of Africa); Eusebius calls him one of those especially distinguished in Rome
Role
Writer in Latin at Carthage; convert, versed in Roman law by Eusebius's account; later a follower of the New Prophecy

People and Stories

Documented Stories

The Soldier Who Would Not Wear the Crown

Tertullian opens On the Military Crown with the event that prompted it. A donative was being distributed to the troops on the accession of new emperors, and the soldiers came forward one by one, crowned with laurel, to receive it. One came with the wreath in his hand. Asked why he was dressed differently, he said he could not do as the others did, and when pressed said the reason: he was a Christian. He was stripped of his cloak and boots, handed over his sword, and was put in prison to await execution. What interests Tertullian is not the soldier but the reaction of the church. Many Christians, he reports, condemned the man as rash and headstrong — ‘eager to die’, provoking trouble for a peaceful congregation over ‘a mere matter of dress’, when nothing in Scripture forbade a crown. Tertullian, already leaning toward the New Prophecy, took the soldier’s side with everything he had: the man had been ‘more steadfast than his brethren, who thought they could serve two masters’, and the whole book is an argument that a Christian’s ordinary habits — not swearing by the emperor’s genius, not wearing what idol-festivals wore — were themselves a confession. The soldier is unnamed, his fate unrecorded. The argument he started, over how far a Christian can go along with the customs of the state before it becomes denial, never closed.

‘You Wretches, If You Want to Die…’

Writing an open letter to Scapula, the proconsul of Africa, in 212, Tertullian tried to make him see how persecution looked from the other side, and told him what had happened to a predecessor in Asia. Arrius Antoninus, governor there, was ‘vigorously persecuting’, and in response ‘all the Christians of the city’ came to his tribunal in a body and offered themselves. He had a few led off to execution and said to the rest: ‘You wretches, if you want to die, you have cliffs to leap from and ropes to hang by.’ Tertullian tells it with approval — his point is that if the same happened in Carthage, Scapula would have to decimate the city, and would find himself executing men and women of every rank, ‘perhaps even persons of your own household’. He does not stop to ask whether the Asian Christians were right to do it, and the church of Smyrna, a generation earlier, had explicitly said they were not. The episode is real enough to have entered the record independently, and it captures an early Christian temper that later centuries found hard to look at: a willingness to court death that could shade into demanding it, and an eagerness to see the courts overwhelmed. Tertullian, who never faced a tribunal himself, was its most eloquent defender.

Key Works

  • Apology: a defense of the Christians addressed to the governors who sat as judges for Rome: the charge is only the name, the crowd blames the Christians for every flood, drought, famine and plague, and the more they are cut down, the more they grow
  • The Prescription Against Heretics: a plea of the kind the law courts call a demurrer, a formal objection that throws a case out before it is argued: those who teach against the apostles may not argue from Scripture at all, because the Scriptures belong to the churches that received them from the apostles, such as Ephesus and Rome
  • Against Praxeas: written after his turn to the New Prophecy, against a teacher at Rome who, by Tertullian's account, said the Father himself was born and suffered; he answers that God is one substance and three persons, distinct but not divided, and the Latin words trinitas, persona and substantia stand in his text

Tradition

The Latin Apologists

Connections on the Map

Influenced By

  • Montanism (the New Prophecy) Documented — Tertullian of Carthage took up the New Prophecy's claims. He quotes a saying of the Paraclete given 'by the mouth of the prophetess Prisca'. In Against Praxeas he writes that his side withdrew from the 'carnally-minded' over the Paraclete.

Influenced

  • The Latin Apologists Documented — Lactantius wrote the Divine Institutes. In it he says Tertullian had already argued the same cause in full. He did so in his book called the Apology.
  • Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity Documented — Jerome says Cyprian read Tertullian every day and asked for him as 'the master'. Jerome had this from an old man who had been Cyprian's secretary. Augustine later said that many of Tertullian's works were still read, and that they were very well written.
  • Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity Documented — Jerome found Tertullian 'packed with meaning' but rough in style. He wrote that Origen should sometimes be read for his learning, 'just as we read Tertullian', taking the good and leaving the bad.

Argued Against

  • Modalist Monarchianism Documented — In Against Praxeas, Tertullian names Praxeas. He charges that Praxeas 'crucified the Father'. He also answers those who think that if the Trinity has an order, its unity is split.
  • Marcion / Marcionism Documented — Tertullian wrote five books Against Marcion. In them he attacks Marcion by name, charging that 'the heretic of Pontus introduces two Gods'.
  • Valentinian & other Gnostic Christianities Documented — Tertullian wrote a book Against the Valentinians. He traces the school back to its founder. He says Valentinus had hoped to become a bishop. When a confessor was chosen instead, Valentinus broke with the church.

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Jerome heard, through two people, that Cyprian never let a day pass without reading him and would say to his secretary, Give me the master. Benson calls Cyprian his devoted yet discriminating disciple and prints a table of Cyprian's verbal debts to him in the treatise on the Lord's Prayer.
  • Jerome says Novatian's book On the Trinity is a sort of epitome of the work of Tertullian; Harnack finds that not borne out.
  • The Latin words he put to work in Against Praxeas, trinitas, persona and substantia, became the Latin church's words for God, as the census and later editors say; Sanday credits him with coining terms and marking out the main lines of Latin theology.
  • Augustine says his followers, the Tertullianists, dwindled to a last remnant at Carthage; the very few left crossed to the Catholic church and handed over their basilica, which he calls still well known.

How they're remembered

  • Lactantius says he was skilled in literature of every kind but had little readiness in eloquence, was not polished and was very obscure, so that he did not find sufficient renown; elsewhere Lactantius says he fully pleaded the same cause in the treatise entitled the Apology.
  • Augustine says many of his little works, most eloquently written, are read.
  • Vincent of Lérins, in 434, gives him by far the first place among the Latins, as Origen has among the Greeks, then calls him more eloquent than faithful, quotes Hilary that by his subsequent error he detracted from the authority of his approved writings, and says he was a great trial in the Church.
  • Jerome calls him a man of keen and vigorous character.

Open Questions

  • Nearly all that is known of his life comes from his own books and from Jerome's short life of him, written about 170 years later. Eusebius and Lactantius wrote about a century after him, and Augustine and Vincent about two centuries. No source says when or why he was converted; McGiffert's guess of between 180 and 190 is an editor's.
  • Trained in the law. Eusebius calls him a man well versed in the laws of the Romans, but his translator's note says this knowledge is not very conspicuous in his writings. McGiffert says he became a lawyer and rhetorician in Rome and very likely practiced; Sanday calls him primarily a lawyer. Monceaux holds his legal training and his fondness for the language of the courts beyond question, but says that identifying him with the jurist Tertullian quoted in the Digest is only a hypothesis, that the name was common, and that the jurist may be a relative. Whether he practiced as an advocate is not settled.
  • Why he turned to the New Prophecy. Jerome says he was driven to the doctrine of Montanus by the envy and abuse of the clergy of the Roman church. Augustine says he went over to the Phrygians whom he had earlier refuted, began to condemn second marriage as fornication against the apostle's teaching, and later broke from them too and spread his own gatherings.
  • From whom he withdrew. He says his side withdrew from the carnally-minded on its acknowledgment of the Paraclete. The editor Coxe reads this as perhaps only a withdrawal from the communion of the bishop of Rome, not a final break with his own bishop and the church of North Africa; Monceaux reads a break with the church at Carthage and dates it to the start of 213; Augustine says he later broke from the New Prophecy too.
  • His office. That he was a presbyter rests only on Jerome, who says he was one until middle life; Monceaux accepts it and thinks he was ordained about 200, since he speaks as a simple believer in the Apology of 197; McGiffert's note says it cannot be told whether he served at Rome or at Carthage.
  • First in Latin. Jerome places him first among the Latin writers after Victor and Apollonius, and the census calls him the first major Christian author to write in Latin. Coxe calls him the founder of Latin Christianity and dates the Octavius of Minucius Felix to about 210, as his contemporary; Wallis, in the same volume, says the Octavius's date is keenly disputed and may be about 166, which would make it older.
  • The Latin words. The census says he invented trinitas, persona, substantia and sacramentum; the held sources show the first three in his text, and Sanday credits him with coining terms. Coxe notes that Theophilus of Antioch used the Greek word for a triad, the earliest use of the word Trinity he knows, so the Greek word is older. Kaye thought the Athanasian Creed was composed by one who had Against Praxeas always in mind; that is an editor's view. No held source says the Council of Nicaea used his words.
  • Praxeas and the bishop's edict. What is known of Praxeas comes from Tertullian's own hostile account. The English edition's editor says the bishop of Rome who withdrew his letter of peace was probably Victor; Monceaux says the edict that On Modesty answers was Callistus's, and which bishop issued it is disputed.
  • What is probably his last surviving book. Monceaux dates On Modesty to 217 to 222 and calls it very probably the last of the surviving works; Jerome says many of his small works are not extant, and Eusebius's translator says a few of his works were written in Greek.
  • Cyprian's words. The report that Cyprian read him daily and said, Give me the master, reaches us through Jerome, from the old man Paul of Concordia, who had met Cyprian's aged secretary at Rome. Jerome's Latin says so; the English translation of Jerome wrongly makes Paul himself the secretary, while Heurtley, quoting Kaye, reads it as the Latin does and gives the words as Give me my master.
  • Novatian. Jerome calls Novatian's On the Trinity a sort of epitome of Tertullian's work, and says many mistakenly ascribe it to Cyprian; Harnack says the claim that it epitomizes a work of Tertullian, which can only be Against Praxeas, is not borne out.
  • His father. Jerome's Latin says patre centurione proconsulari, a centurion on the governor's staff, and his Chronicle says the same; the English translation of the Lives reads son of a proconsul or Centurion, which is a mistranslation, and Heurtley's quotation of Kaye reads the son of a proconsular centurion.
  • Vincent's verdict. The English reads more eloquent by far than faithful, but its editor notes that Baluze reads fidelior and others read felicior, so the reading is disputed. Hilary's judgment reaches us here only through Vincent in English. The decree wrongly called Gelasian lists Opuscula Tertulliani among the books not received, beside the History of Eusebius and the works of Lactantius; Mansi puts it at about 496 and says its author has long been disputed.
  • The Passion of Perpetua and Felicitas. It was handed down with his works; Coxe thinks Tertullian was the editor of the story, not its author, which is an editor's guess.
  • The quoted Latin. The Latin of the Apology and the Prescription is held only in Oehler's 1853 edition in a damaged scan, which has lost words and garbled others; no other held print confirms them, so these quotations are printed in English alone, except two short lines the scan prints cleanly, the blood of Christians is seed and I am the heir of the apostles, which carry the Latin. For Against Praxeas the two held Latin prints differ: Kroymann reads ubique teneo with etsi added in brackets, and Oehler reads ubique teneam with no etsi.
