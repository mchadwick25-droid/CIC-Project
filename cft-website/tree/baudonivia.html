About This Person

Radegund's body was carried beneath the wall of her monastery at Poitiers. She had ruled that no living nun should leave it. So the whole community stood on the wall and wept, Baudonivia writes. In place of the psalm they gave tears, and groans in place of the alleluia.

Baudonivia was a nun of Holy Cross at Poitiers, the house Queen Radegund founded. She wrote the second book of Radegund's Life, for the abbess Dedimia and the community. The sources give nothing of her family, age or death. This account rests on her book. The German scholar Bruno Krusch dates it soon after 600, from a bishop of Autun she names.

Venantius Fortunatus had already written Radegund's Life. Baudonivia will not repeat, she says, what the apostolic man Fortunatus, the bishop, wrote. She will tell what he passed over for length. Krusch thinks the nuns missed two things in his book, the coming of the Cross and Radegund's death.

She calls herself the least of all, less learned and more devoted. Krusch notes most of her prologue is copied from Fortunatus's prologues to his Lives of Hilary of Poitiers and Marcellus of Paris. One phrase says Radegund raised her from the cradle as her own household servant. Fortunatus first wrote it of Bishop Pascentius of Poitiers, as raised by Hilary. There, Krusch says, it means Hilary's monastery raised Pascentius.

What Helena did in the East when she sought out the Cross, Baudonivia writes, Radegund did in Gaul. When the relic reached Poitiers, she says, the bishop and all the people wished to receive it. But the enemy of mankind worked through his agents to keep it out. Gregory of Tours tells it otherwise. Bishop Maroveus, he says, rode off to a country estate rather than receive it.

She tells the death in the voice of the whole house. On Wednesday morning, the thirteenth of August, she writes, Radegund's eyes were closed, and ours were darkened.

Key Works

  • Life of Radegund, Book II: written for the abbess Dedimia and the community of Holy Cross, to tell what Fortunatus passed over for length: Radegund's daily life in the monastery, the coming of the relic of the Cross, her death and burial, and miracles after it

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Her book is the second of the two books of Radegund's Life, after Fortunatus's, in the manuscripts Bruno Krusch edits and in Migne.
  • The abbess Dedimia is known only from her, Krusch notes.
  • An unsigned note in Migne cites her, as Fortunatus's contemporary, against scholars who struck him from the list of the bishops of Poitiers.

How they're remembered

  • Hildebert, bishop of Le Mans, rewrote Radegund's Life. He says he followed Fortunatus and added some miracles that Fortunatus had not touched and the nun had recorded.
  • Bruno Krusch judges her style very rough and says she took freely from the saints' Lives she had read; he adds that the reader will pardon good Baudonivia, who confesses her weakness.

What Is Uncertain

  • Her own life is thin in the sources. No source used here gives her family, her age, how she came to the house or her death, so this profile rests on her book. O. M. Dalton calls her a contemporary of Radegund who was in the monastery during Radegund's life.
  • Whether Radegund raised her. Her prologue says Radegund raised her from the cradle as her own household servant. Bruno Krusch notes that most of the prologue is copied from Fortunatus's prologues to his Lives of Hilary of Poitiers and Marcellus of Paris. This phrase comes from the Hilary prologue, where it is said of Pascentius, bishop of Poitiers; Krusch notes that there it means Hilary's monastery raised him, and that those who read it literally erred. So the phrase alone does not show that Radegund raised her.
  • The bishop in her story of the Cross. She says the bishop of the place and all the people wished to receive the relic devoutly, and the enemy of mankind worked through his agents to keep it out; she names neither the bishop nor the emperor. Gregory of Tours names the bishop, Maroveus, and says he rode off to a country estate rather than receive it; Bruno Krusch's note follows Gregory, and O. M. Dalton reads her as saying the devil seduced Maroveus and the people. Justin is Krusch's and Dalton's supplement for the emperor. The empress Sophia and the gold triptych are Dalton's words, not hers.
  • The year of Radegund's death. Baudonivia gives a Wednesday, the thirteenth of August, and Gregory of Tours the thirteenth day of the sixth month. The year 587 is Bruno Krusch's.
  • The date of her book. Soon after 600 is Krusch's reasoning, from Leifastus, a bishop of Autun she names, and from people she says are still living.
  • What is hers. Krusch says she copied passages from the Life of Caesarius of Arles, among them Radegund's vision of a young man a year before her death. Dalton credits her with the vision of the tribune Domolenus on the day of Radegund's death; it is in Fortunatus's Life.
  • Her name. One manuscript gives the book without her name, as written by one of the holy nuns who lived with Radegund.
