The Moravian Church at Herrnhut

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In 1722 a handful of families slipped over the Bohemian border into Saxony. They were the remnant of the Unity of the Brethren, the Czech reform church. It had survived in hiding for nearly a century after the Habsburgs outlawed it. Count Nikolaus von Zinzendorf let them settle on his estate, and they built a village called Herrnhut. Years of quarreling followed, among the refugees and the other seekers who joined them. Then on 13 August 1727 they shared the Lord's Supper, and they always dated their renewal from that day. What followed was remarkable for a settlement of a few hundred people. They kept a prayer watch in shifts around the clock. They sorted the church into 'choirs' by age and state of life. They wrote up a diary every single day, and thousands of ordinary members set down their own life stories. From 1732 they sent missionaries to the enslaved people of St Thomas, and to Greenland and Suriname. Others went to the Native congregations of Pennsylvania and New York. They were not the first, for Lutherans trained at Halle had reached India in 1706.

The Brethren tried the Cape early. George Schmidt landed there on 9 July 1737. He taught Khoikhoi children to read, and he baptized seven people. The Brethren had to leave in 1745. In 1786 some of them met a woman Schmidt had baptized, and she still prized the Dutch Bible he had given her. The Herrnhut synod of 1789 renewed the plan. Three missionaries reached the Cape on 21 November 1792.

The Brethren also looked to North Africa. La Trobe wrote in 1771 that three Brethren lived at Cairo, with the Copts of Egypt and Abyssinia as their special aim. In 1752 Dr. Frederick William Hooker went to Cairo, on the way to the Christians of Abyssinia. Watson's history says he met the Coptic patriarch, who gave him a letter in reply to one from Count Zinzendorf. In Algiers, Brother Richter went in 1740 to the Christian slaves. He died of the plague. Another Brother stayed there from 1744 to 1748.

Not every Czech Protestant had left. In 1781 the emperor Joseph II issued a Patent of Toleration. It freed the Lutherans and Calvinists. It left out even the Unity of the Brethren. Count Lützow wrote in 1920 that many Bohemians had secretly stayed Protestant. They were glad of the Patent. The Toleration churches after 1781 are not told here.

In Its Own Record

Grew Out Of

  • The Czech Churches' Last Century (Utraquists and Unitas) Documented Major — The Moravian Church's own history of 1771 says a hidden 'seed of the Brethren' lived on near Fulnek, where Comenius had been pastor. In 1722 the Neisser brothers left with their families, led by Christian David. In 1735 Daniel Jablonsky, a bishop of the old Unity since 1699, made David Nitschmann a bishop.

Continued As

  • The Moravian Church after 1815 Widely Accepted Major — The Moravian Church kept the same missions after 1815. A report of 1790 lists its missions in Greenland, Labrador and the West Indies. Hamilton's 1901 history follows those fields on past 1815.

Influenced By

  • German Pietism - Spener, Francke, Halle Documented — Count Zinzendorf, who let the refugees settle at Herrnhut, had Spener as a godfather and went to school under Francke at Halle. He said the Herrnhut community first grew from 'Spener's idea of planting little churches in the great church'. His colleague August Spangenberg recorded both in his life of the count.

Influenced

  • The Methodist Revival Documented — In March 1738 the Moravian Peter Böhler convinced John Wesley, by Wesley's own account, 'of the want of that faith whereby alone we are saved'. That summer Wesley traveled to Herrnhut, 'to see the place where the Christians live'.
  • The Missionary Movement's Native Churches (Serampore India; Sierra Leone & the Niger; Pacific Islands; China Coast & CIM) Documented — William Carey's 1792 'Enquiry' led to the founding of the Baptist Missionary Society, and Carey went on to lead its mission at Serampore in India. In the 'Enquiry' he wrote that 'none of the moderns have equalled the Moravian Brethren' in mission work. He held up their missions in Greenland and Labrador as proof that the work could be done.
  • The Reveil & Inner-Mission Awakening Dominant Modern Reconstruction — The historian Timothy Stunt finds Moravian roots in the awakening that broke out in Geneva after 1815. This rests on his book.

Opposed By

  • The Swedish Lutherans of New Sweden Documented Major — Acrelius tells how Moravian preachers came among the Delaware Swedes in the 1740s. In 1744 the Swedish party locked one of them, Bruzelius, out of Raccoon church. A panel of twenty-five men at Gloucester then ruled against him.Lasting result: In 1744 the wardens who took his side were put out of office. Moravian ministers were then barred from the Swedish and English churches.

In Contact With

  • The Coptic Church under the Ottomans: the Patriarchate and the Archons Widely Accepted — In 1752 the Moravian physician Frederick William Hooker went to Cairo, on the way to the Christians of Abyssinia. Watson's history says he met the Coptic patriarch, who gave him a letter in reply to one from Zinzendorf. La Trobe, in 1771, lists three Brethren at Cairo with the Copts as their special aim.
  • Southern African Mission Churches Widely Accepted — The Moravians' own report of 1790 tells Schmidt's landing at the Cape in 1737 and his seven baptisms. AF-5 opens with this mission among the churches of southern Africa. A Moravian history of 1901 tells Schmidt's work at the southern end of Africa. No study read says the Moravians shaped the other churches it tells.

Sources

  • The Herrnhut congregational record: Gemein-Diarium (daily diary), Brotherly Agreement (12 May 1727), the 13 August 1727 communion account, choir records, the Losungen (printed annually since 1731) (primary) — A community writing itself down daily. The Unity Archives are largely untranslated.
  • The Lebenslaeufe: thousands of member memoirs, incl. women's (selection in Faull, Moravian Women's Memoirs, Syracuse, 1997) and formerly-enslaved members' (Rebecca Protten's letters and memoir materials) (primary) — An unusually deep record of ordinary voices.
  • Zinzendorf's corpus: discourses, litanies, hymns (primary) — Zinzendorf's own writings, kept distinct from the congregation's communal record. The excesses of the Sifting Time (1743-50) are named and corrected in the community's own records.
  • Mission diaries: St Thomas (from 1732; Friedrich Martin's diaries; the 1739 enslaved converts' petition-letters), Greenland, Shekomeko/Bethlehem Native congregations; Oldendorp's Historie (1777) (primary) — The converts' words survive only as recorded by missionaries, within contexts of slavery and dispossession.
  • Craig Atwood, Community of the Cross (Penn State, 2004), with Jon Sensbach, Rebecca's Revival (Harvard, 2005) (secondary) — Sensbach's book recovers Rebecca Protten's story.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 7, 'Evangelical awakenings in the North Atlantic world' (Ward), with 'Continental Protestant Europe' (Lehmann) (reference) — Both are a direct fit for this movement.
  • Benjamin La Trobe, A Succinct View of the Missions Established among the Heathens by the Church of the Brethren (London, 1771) (primary) — A Moravian's own account of the missions, including Cairo and Algiers.
  • Periodical Accounts relating to the Missions of the Church of the United Brethren, vol. 1 (London, 1790) (primary) — The Brethren's own reports, including Schmidt's Cape mission and its renewal in 1792.
  • Andrew Watson, The American Mission in Egypt, 1854 to 1896 (Pittsburgh: United Presbyterian Board of Publication, 1898) (secondary) — By a missionary of the American mission. It tells the Moravian physician Hooker's visit to Cairo in 1752.
  • Count Francis Lützow, Bohemia: An Historical Sketch (London: J. M. Dent, Everyman's Library, 1920) (secondary) — On the Patent of Toleration of 1781 and the Protestants who had stayed.
