Aidan of Lindisfarne

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Anglo-Saxon Christianity.

Known for
Monk of Iona whom the Irish sent as bishop to King Oswald of Northumbria. He set his see on Lindisfarne and gave what he was given to the poor and to ransom people wrongly sold as slaves. He kept the Irish reckoning of Easter, which Bede faults.
Also called
Aedan, Aidanus, Edanus
Dates
No source used here gives the year of his birth. Bede says he died on 31 August, twelve days after King Oswin of Deira, in the seventeenth year of his time as bishop. Bede's annals put his death in 651. The Bollandists put the founding of the see of Lindisfarne in 635
Place
Iona, which Bede calls Hii, where he was a monk. Lindisfarne in Northumbria, his see. A royal estate near the royal city named after Queen Bebba, where he died; L. Cecil Jane identifies that city as Bamborough
Role
Monk of Iona; first bishop of Lindisfarne

About This Person

Bede passes on a report of a council among the elders of the Scots, as he calls the Irish. King Oswald of Northumbria had asked them for a bishop, but the first man they sent came home calling the English stubborn and barbarous. Aidan, a monk of Iona, told him he had been too severe, and had not first given his hearers "the milk of more easy doctrine". The elders made Aidan a bishop and sent him.

He came to the English from Iona in the 630s. Oswald made Lindisfarne, an island at high tide, the seat of Aidan's bishopric. Aidan spoke little English, so the king, who had learned the Scots' tongue in exile, interpreted for him. Nearly all we know of Aidan comes from Bede, who wrote some eighty years after his death, learning from those who knew him.

Bede says Aidan taught no otherwise than he lived. He walked through town and country, urging people to believe and to give alms. Gifts from the rich went to the poor, or to ransom people wrongly sold as slaves. Many of those he freed he taught and made priests.

King Oswin of Deira gave him a fine horse, and Aidan gave it to a beggar. When the king complained, he answered, "Is that foal of a mare more dear to you than the Son of God?" Oswin later knelt for his pardon, and Aidan wept and told his priest the king would not live long. Oswin was killed soon after, and Aidan died twelve days later, on 31 August 651, Bede says.

Aidan kept Easter Sunday by his country's custom, between the fourteenth and twentieth day of the moon. Bede, who followed the Roman Easter, says he detests this. Charles Plummer, who edited Bede in 1896, calls it "sad" that Bede breaks off his praise of Aidan to say so. Yet Bede grants that Aidan always kept it on a Sunday. While Aidan lived, he adds, all bore the difference patiently.

An unnamed Lindisfarne monk wrote a saint's Life of Cuthbert of Lindisfarne. He says the young shepherd Cuthbert saw a soul carried to heaven, and days later heard that Aidan had died that hour. Bede's Life of Cuthbert says he learned it the next morning.

Bede praises Aidan's love of peace and his care for the afflicted and the poor. "These things I much love and admire in the aforesaid bishop," he writes.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Bede says that from Aidan's coming, many Scots, as Bede calls the Irish, came into Britain to preach in Oswald's lands. Churches were built, and the king gave lands for monasteries. Most of the preachers were monks.
  • Bede says the bishop of Lindisfarne lived there with his clergy, and the abbot with his monks under the bishop's care. He explains that Aidan, its first bishop, was a monk and brought monks there.
  • Bede says many religious men and women followed his example and fasted on Wednesdays and Fridays until the ninth hour. They kept this all year, except in the fifty days after Easter.
  • Eata, one of the twelve English boys Aidan took to teach, became abbot of Melrose and later bishop of Lindisfarne. Bede calls Chad, Cedd's brother, one of Aidan's disciples, who travelled on foot to preach.
  • Bede says Aidan called Hild of Whitby, whom Bede calls Hilda, home, and she received land on the north side of the Wear. He and other religious men often visited and taught her. Bede also says Aidan consecrated Heiu, said to be the first nun in Northumbria.

How they're remembered

  • Bede praises his love of peace and charity, his humility, and his authority in rebuking the proud and powerful. He praises his tenderness to the afflicted and the poor, and says he much loves and admires these things in him.
  • Bede says that while Aidan lived, all men bore the difference over Easter patiently. Even Bishop Honorius of Canterbury and Bishop Felix of the East Angles held him in veneration.
  • Charles Plummer calls Bede's account of him a "beautiful sketch of the sweet and saintly character of Aidan". He finds it sad that Bede stops in the middle to fault his Easter.
  • Alcuin of York, in his poem on the church of York, retells in verse how Aidan blessed King Oswald's right hand at an Easter meal. He calls him Edanus.
  • The Roman Breviary, in the Marquess of Bute's English, keeps his feast on 31 August with lessons from Bede. Its martyrology says Cuthbert of Lindisfarne, then a shepherd lad, saw his soul carried up into heaven.

What Is Uncertain

  • Nearly all that is known of him comes from Bede's Ecclesiastical History. It describes Britain as it stood in 731, some eighty years after Aidan's death. Bede says he wrote as near as he could learn from those who knew him. He gives the council that chose Aidan as a report.
  • His Easter. Bede says Aidan kept Easter Sunday between the fourteenth and twentieth day of the moon, by the custom of the northern Irish and the Picts. Bede, who held to the Roman reckoning, says he detests it. He grants that Aidan always kept it on a Sunday, and could not go against the custom of those who sent him. Charles Plummer thinks the question had a place in Bede's mind far beyond its real importance.
  • His years as bishop. In one chapter Bede says he died after he had been bishop sixteen years, in the seventeenth year of his office. Later he says Aidan was bishop seventeen years. Bede's annals give 651 for his death. No source used here gives the year of his birth or his age.
  • The wonders. Bede tells of oil that stilled a storm, which the priest Cynemund heard from the priest Utta who used it. He tells of a fire turned back from the royal city at Aidan's prayer, which he says many told. He also tells of the post Aidan died against, which fire twice could not burn.
  • Cuthbert's vision. An unnamed monk of Lindisfarne, in a saint's Life of Cuthbert of Lindisfarne, says the shepherds heard of Aidan's death some days later. The Bollandists date that Life about 700. Bede's own prose Life of Cuthbert says Cuthbert learned it the next morning.
  • Bede does not name the first man sent to Oswald, who came home with no success.
  • His name. He is a different man from Aedan, king of the Scots in Britain, whom Bede and Adomnán of Iona name. He is also not the Aidan who was a disciple of David of Menevia in W. J. Rees's Lives of the Cambro-British Saints. The Welsh annals, the Annales Cambriae, do not name him.
