The Reformation Era · 1599-1665 · Malabar Coast

Malabar Coast, today in India

St. Thomas Christians under Portuguese Pressure - Diamper & the Coonan Cross

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

The Christians of the Malabar coast traced their church to the apostle Thomas. For centuries they had worshipped in Syriac and taken their bishops from the Church of the East. The Portuguese found this puzzling and then intolerable. In 1599 Archbishop Aleixo de Menezes held a synod at Diamper. It placed the community under Latin rule, revised its liturgy, and ordered its Syriac books corrected or burned. Half a century of resentment came to a head on 3 January 1653. At Mattancherry a crowd swore they would no longer obey the Jesuits or the Jesuit archbishop, Francisco Garcia. Tradition says they tied ropes to a stone cross as they swore. This is the Coonan Cross Oath, named for a cross said to have bent under the strain.

In Its Own Record

Gave Rise To

  • Northeast India Revivals & the Mar Thoma Reform Widely Accepted Major — In 1787 'all the Church people of Malankara' signed a paper recalling their forefathers' oath at Mattancherry. In 1869 a priest of the Great Church at Kottayam wrote that a bishop from Antioch won back 'the now existing Syrian churches' after 1685. The Mar Thoma reform grew inside this church. In 1847 the Patriarch of Antioch wrote about the reforming bishop Matthew. His predecessor, he said, had sent a bishop to keep 'all the flock of Mar Thomas' from him. The hill churches of the northeast are not part of this line.
  • The Malankara Churches — Orthodox & Jacobite Widely Accepted Major — In 1869 Edavalikel Philipos, a priest at Kottayam, wrote that those who left Rome under a bishop from Antioch were called the New Party. The Malankara Orthodox Church traces its own start to that break with Rome.

Continues

  • St Thomas Christians, 1300-1517 Documented Major — In 1504 bishops sent by the patriarch wrote from Malabar about its Christians, their towns and their churches. In 1599 the archbishop who called the Synod of Diamper wrote that the pope had told him to bar any bishop coming from Babylon. That, he said, had been the custom until then.

Continued As

  • The St Thomas Christians — Two Obediences Documented Major — The Angamaly paper of 1787 calls the men of the oath its forefathers. An English chaplain says Mar Thomas the Great led the revolt from Rome. The community's own history says he was consecrated in 1665.

Opposed By

  • The Tridentine Church Documented Major — In 1599 the Archbishop of Goa, Aleixo de Menezes, held a synod at Diamper and bound it to the Council of Trent. Its decrees called the community's Syriac books the work of Nestorian heretics. They forbade anyone to read them and ordered them destroyed.Lasting result: The synod said many Syriac books came from Nestorian heretics. It forbade all Christians to read them and ordered them destroyed.

Broke With

  • The Society of Jesus Documented Major — In 1653 the Malabar Christians swore they would never again accept the Jesuit archbishop, Francisco Garcia. A Carmelite envoy sent by Rome wrote down the oath in 1666. The community's own paper of 1787 says their forefathers swore to have nothing more to do with the Jesuits.

In Contact With

  • The Church of the East — the Schism of 1552 Documented Major — Until 1599 the Malabar church named the patriarch of the Church of the East at every Mass. A Portuguese book of 1606 tells of priests at Diamper who mourned 'our Patriarch of Babylon, who governed us so many years.' They called him Mar Simon.Lasting result: The Synod of Diamper banned the Malabar church from naming or obeying the Patriarch of Babylon. The penalty was to be cut off from the church.

Sources

  • The Synod of Diamper acta (1599): Eng. trans. Michael Geddes (1694); critical ed. Scaria Zacharia, The Acts and Decrees of the Synod of Diamper 1599 (Kottayam, 1994) (primary) — Hostile to the tradition it absorbed: the decrees Latinize the community and condemn its East Syriac books, many of which were burned or corrected under the synod's own orders — which is why the earlier record is so thin. The community's practices survive mainly as a list of things condemned.
  • Gouvea, Jornada of Archbishop Menezes (1606; Eng. trans. Malekandathil, 2003) (primary) — The fullest contemporary description of the community, but written by its conqueror's own chronicler.
  • The community's own layer: surviving Kerala Syriac manuscripts (HMML digitization), the East Syriac liturgical and canonical tradition, palm-leaf (ola) records, transmitted memory of the copper-plate privileges (primary) — The inside voice, fragmentary because the synod burned so many of its books — that destruction is itself part of the evidence. Largely uncataloged and untranslated.
  • The Coonan Cross oath (1653): community tradition of the oath's wording, contemporary Carmelite and Jesuit reports of the revolt, correspondence around Mar Thoma I's consecration (primary) — The oath's text is preserved only through tradition, in variant forms, and the surviving reports come from pens sympathetic to the mission side. This event marks the origin of today's division among Kerala's churches.
  • Frykenberg, Christianity in India (OUP, 2008), relevant chapters, with Istvan Perczel's Kerala Syriac manuscript studies (secondary) — Perczel's manuscript discoveries have directly revised the earlier picture of how thin the sources are.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 5; vol. 6, ch. 30 (Zupanov & Hsia) (reference) — Volume 5 has no chapter dedicated to the St Thomas Christians; the closest is chapter 22 (O'Mahony, on Syriac Christianity, though it focuses on the modern period). Zupanov is a specialist on the Malabar missions.
