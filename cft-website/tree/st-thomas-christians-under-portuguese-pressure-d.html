About This Tradition

Audio Brief 0:55

Read along

The Christians of the Malabar coast traced their church to the apostle Thomas. For centuries they had worshipped in Syriac and taken their bishops from the Church of the East. The Portuguese found this puzzling and then intolerable. In 1599 Archbishop Aleixo de Menezes held a synod at Diamper. It placed the community under Latin rule, revised its liturgy, and ordered its Syriac books corrected or burned. Half a century of resentment came to a head on 3 January 1653. At Mattancherry a crowd swore they would no longer obey the Jesuits or the Jesuit archbishop, Francisco Garcia. Tradition says they tied ropes to a stone cross as they swore. This is the Coonan Cross Oath, named for a cross said to have bent under the strain.

Documented Stories

The Bishop Who Never Arrived, and the Oath at the Bent Cross

In 1652 a Syrian bishop calling himself Ignatius Ahatallah reached Mylapore on the Coromandel coast, claiming that he had been sent as patriarch of the Christians of India. Who had sent him is still argued: he had made a Catholic profession at Rome twenty years earlier, had since served the Syrian and the Coptic patriarchs, and carried letters whose authority the Portuguese refused to recognize. Two Malabar deacons on pilgrimage at Mylapore met him and carried his letter home, and the community received it with joy. The Portuguese put him on a ship for Goa. When it called at Cochin the Thomas Christians, said to have gathered in thousands, asked to see him and were refused; the ship sailed on, and word spread through the churches that he had been drowned in the harbor, or burned by the Inquisition at Goa. On 3 January 1653 a crowd gathered at the stone cross outside the church at Mattancherry and swore, hands on ropes tied to the cross, that they would no longer obey the Jesuit archbishop Francisco Garcia or any Jesuit. In May, at Alangad, twelve priests laid hands on Archdeacon Thomas and named him Mar Thoma, citing a letter attributed to Ahatallah that authorized it. Ahatallah himself was shipped on toward Lisbon and disappears from the record; the likeliest account has him dying in Europe in 1654 without reaching Rome.

Diamper: The Synod That Burned the Books

Archbishop Aleixo de Menezes of Goa arrived in Malabar early in 1599 with soldiers, money and a determination that the Thomas Christians should have no bishop but one Rome sent. Through the spring he toured their churches, confirming and ordaining, on the reasoning that priests who owed their orders to him would owe him obedience. The Archdeacon George, who had governed the community since the death of its last East Syrian bishop, resisted, was threatened with excommunication and with Portuguese pressure on the pepper trade, and submitted. The synod met at Udayamperur, which the Portuguese called Diamper, from 20 to 26 June 1599, with some 150 priests and more than 600 lay representatives. Its decrees, read in Portuguese and Malayalam, declared the patriarch of the East a heretic, condemned the prayers for Nestorius and Theodore, imposed Latin discipline on clerical marriage, fasting and the sacraments, and ordered a list of Syriac books — the Nomocanon, the Book of the Fathers, the homilies of Narsai among them — to be burned or corrected. Menezes then made a second circuit collecting manuscripts, and many were burned at the churches where they were found. The chronicler Gouvea, who wrote the archbishop's official account, presents this as rescue. The modern editor of the acts has shown that the printed decrees include material the assembly never voted on. Much of what is known about the community's worship before 1599 is what the synod condemned.

In Its Own Record

It carries on the older church of the St Thomas Christians. The split that came after the 1653 oath still shapes the churches of Kerala. Its heirs are the Syro-Malabar Catholics and the Malankara and Mar Thoma churches.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

The Christians of the Malabar coast traced their church to the apostle Thomas. For centuries they had worshipped in Syriac under bishops from the Church of the East. Then a 1599 synod placed them under Latin jurisdiction and ordered their Syriac books corrected or burned. Resentment broke open in 1653, when a crowd at Mattancherry swore they would no longer obey the Jesuits. Tradition says they tied ropes to a stone cross as they swore. The Coonan Cross Oath is named for a cross said to have bent under the strain.

Legacy

The division made in 1653 is still the shape of Christianity in Kerala. Those who returned to Rome became the Syro-Malabar Catholic Church, now one of the largest Eastern Catholic churches in the world. Those who did not are the ancestors of the Malankara Orthodox, Jacobite, Mar Thoma and related churches. All of them still worship in liturgies descended from the Syriac tradition Diamper set out to correct, and the bent cross at Mattancherry is still there.

How Sure the Record Is

Moderate to rich for this period: synod acts, missionary records (written by a hostile hand), and community tradition - richer than the thin early sources for the earlier Thomas Christian period.

  • The Synod of Diamper acta (1599): Eng. trans. Michael Geddes (1694); critical ed. Scaria Zacharia, The Acts and Decrees of the Synod of Diamper 1599 (Kottayam, 1994) (primary) — Hostile to the tradition it absorbed: the decrees Latinize the community and condemn its East Syriac books, many of which were burned or corrected under the synod's own orders — which is why the earlier record is so thin. The community's practices survive mainly as a list of things condemned.
  • Gouvea, Jornada of Archbishop Menezes (1606; Eng. trans. Malekandathil, 2003) (primary) — The fullest contemporary description of the community, but written by its conqueror's own chronicler.
  • The community's own layer: surviving Kerala Syriac manuscripts (HMML digitization), the East Syriac liturgical and canonical tradition, palm-leaf (ola) records, transmitted memory of the copper-plate privileges (primary) — The inside voice, fragmentary because the synod burned so many of its books — that destruction is itself part of the evidence. Largely uncataloged and untranslated.
  • The Coonan Cross oath (1653): community tradition of the oath's wording, contemporary Carmelite and Jesuit reports of the revolt, correspondence around Mar Thoma I's consecration (primary) — The oath's text is preserved only through tradition, in variant forms, and the surviving reports come from pens sympathetic to the mission side. This event marks the origin of today's division among Kerala's churches.
  • Frykenberg, Christianity in India (OUP, 2008), relevant chapters, with Istvan Perczel's Kerala Syriac manuscript studies (secondary) — Perczel's manuscript discoveries have directly revised the earlier picture of how thin the sources are.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 5; vol. 6, ch. 30 (Zupanov & Hsia) (reference) — Volume 5 has no chapter dedicated to the St Thomas Christians; the closest is chapter 22 (O'Mahony, on Syriac Christianity, though it focuses on the modern period). Zupanov is a specialist on the Malabar missions.
