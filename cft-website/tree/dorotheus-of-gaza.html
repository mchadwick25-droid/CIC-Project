Dorotheus of Gaza

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Chalcedonian Monasticism - Judean Desert & Gaza.

Known for
Monk and archimandrite near Gaza, disciple of the abbot John, the disciple of Barsanuphius, whose twenty-four Teachings for monks pass on the counsel of the old men on obedience, humility and love of neighbor.
Also called
Abbas Dorotheus, Dorotheus archimandrita
Dates
Born about 540, in François Nau's date. Andrea Gallandi reports that the Bollandists place him at the end of the sixth century and says the year of his death is not clearly known; J.-P. Migne heads Gallandi's notice with the year 596. No ancient source used here gives his years
Place
The community of the abbot Seridus, which a Greek book of saints revised by Nikodemos the Hagiorite puts near Gaza and François Nau places near Thabatha
Role
Monk and archimandrite; teacher of monks

About This Person

While Dorotheus of Gaza lived with the abbot Seridus, the attendant of the abbot John of Gaza fell sick. Seridus sent Dorotheus to serve John. Each evening, as Dorotheus left, the old man gave him a saying of the Fathers. Before each, he asked God to keep their love for one another. One night the saying was to flee these human things and be saved. Another night, it was that if we bear one another's burdens, we fulfill the law of Christ. He kept them all, he says, to guard his life. He served John nine years.

He tells this in his Teachings. Elsewhere in them he calls John the disciple of blessed Barsanuphius. The scholar Andrea Gallandi calls Dorotheus a Palestinian archimandrite, the head of a monastery. The Bollandist scholars, Gallandi reports, place him late in the sixth century.

His example of cutting off one's own will is the blessed Dositheus. A Greek letter printed before the Teachings calls Dositheus his first disciple. Dositheus came, Dorotheus says, from a soft and pampered life, taught nothing of divine things. By obedience alone, he reached the height of perfection in a short time.

Once a leading man of Gaza heard the monks talking of humility, and asked how this could be. Dorotheus asked where he stood among his fellow citizens. As a noble, the man said. At Caesarea he would rank a little below its nobles, and at Antioch he would be a peasant. Before the king at Byzantium, he would be a poor beggar. Holy men are the same, Dorotheus said, and the nearer they come to God, the lower they think themselves.

A later Greek book of saints, revised by Nikodemos the Hagiorite, says he founded his own monastery after John died. The scholar François Nau instead makes him a later head of Seridus's monastery.

Gallandi calls his twenty-four Teachings an outstanding work, most worth reading, and says Theodore the Studite approved them highly.

Key Works

  • Teachings (Doctrinae), twenty-four in number: talks to monks on giving up one's own will, humility, patience with insult and love of neighbor, told through his own years under the abbot Seridus and the abbot John; Gallandi calls them an outstanding work, most worth reading
  • Questions of Dorotheus to Barsanuphius and John, with their answers: the set of questions he put to the two old men, printed with the Teachings

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Andrea Gallandi says Theodore the Studite approved the Teachings highly and counted their author among the foremost monks.
  • Sayings of his were also kept in the third person, as "Aiebat abbas Dorotheus", the abbot Dorotheus used to say, in the brief sayings printed as Teaching 19.

How they're remembered

  • An unnamed writer of a letter printed before the Teachings, in a passage found only in the Latin, calls him "sublimis in contemplatione, sublimior in humilitate", high in contemplation and higher in humility.
  • The Greek book of saints revised by Nikodemos the Hagiorite remembers him on 13 August, with the abbot Seridus and Dositheus.

What Is Uncertain

  • His own monastery. A Greek book of saints revised by Nikodemos the Hagiorite says he founded a monastery of his own after the abbot John died; the scanned Greek there is rough. François Nau names Barsanuphius, Seridus and at last Dorotheus as heads in turn of Seridus's monastery. Andrea Gallandi calls him an archimandrite, the head of a monastery, without saying how he came to head one.
  • His first monastery. Gallandi takes the house of the abbot Seridus to be where he first became a monk, from Dorotheus's own mentions of Seridus. Dorotheus says only that he was with the abbot Seridus.
  • Which John. In the passage of his nine years' service the old man is the abbot John, with no epithet. Elsewhere in the Teachings Dorotheus calls the abbot John the disciple of blessed Barsanuphius, and the heading of the Questions and Gallandi's notice call John the Prophet.
  • His years. Gallandi reports the Bollandists, Fabricius and Cave for the end of the sixth century, and sets aside Oudin, who places him in the seventh. François Nau gives his birth about 540 without giving his reasons in the lines used here. Migne's heading gives 596.
  • The Life of Dositheus. Gallandi says the disciple who wrote it reports that Dorotheus came to know the two old men when he entered Seridus's monastery. The Life itself is not among the sources used here, so nothing more of Dositheus is told here than Dorotheus's own lines and the Greek of a letter printed before the Teachings, which calls Dositheus his first disciple.
  • His name. A preface Migne prints without an author's name says there were two men named Dorotheus, one defending the errors of Severus of Antioch. Theodore the Studite separates the condemned Dorotheus from the one he accepts. In another letter Theodore also curses a Dositheus with the condemned men; no line used here ties him to Dorotheus's Dositheus.
  • The language. No English of Dorotheus is among the sources used here, and the Greek in Migne's volume is hard to read in the scan. Every detail here follows the Latin Migne prints, which Gallandi gives to Hilarion of Verona, as revised by Fronto Ducaeus.
