Cyril of Alexandria

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Church of Alexandria and Miaphysite Egypt.

Known for
Bishop of Alexandria who opposed Nestorius of Constantinople over calling Mary the Mother of God, and whose letter the Council of Ephesus of 431 judged to agree with the creed of Nicaea.
Also called
Cyril, Cyrillus
Dates
Became bishop on the third day after the death of Theophilus, 15 October, in a year Socrates of Constantinople gives by its consuls and the notes to A. C. Zenos's edition give as 412; died in 444, by Blomfield Jackson, who quotes Canon Venables for "June 9, or 27, 444"
Place
Alexandria; Ephesus
Role
Bishop of Alexandria

About This Person

At Ephesus in June 431, a council heard a letter read. Cyril, bishop of Alexandria, had written it to Nestorius, bishop of Constantinople. Cyril asked the bishops whether he had written "rightly and blamelessly," in line with the creed of Nicaea. One by one they said he had. Then Nestorius's reply was read and judged, and the acts record that they cried out together, "we all anathematize Nestorius as a heretic."

Socrates of Constantinople, a church historian of those years, tells of his rise. He was a nephew of Theophilus, bishop of Alexandria. When Theophilus died in 412, a party wanted the archdeacon Timothy, and the army commander backed him. Three days later Cyril held the see, "with greater power than Theophilus had ever exercised." He at once shut the churches of the Novatians, a separate Christian body in the city.

Socrates says that after Jews killed Christians in a night attack, Cyril led a crowd to the synagogues and drove the Jews from the city. Monks from Nitria came to fight for him, and one of them stoned Orestes, the prefect. That monk died under torture, and Cyril praised him as a martyr. Then, in March 415, a Christian crowd murdered Hypatia, a philosopher who often met Orestes. Socrates does not say Cyril ordered it. He says it brought great shame on Cyril and on the whole church of Alexandria. A. C. Zenos, who edited Socrates in English, links this verdict to Socrates's closeness to the Novatians.

At Constantinople, Socrates says, a priest close to Nestorius preached, "Let no one call Mary Theotocos," Mother of God. Nestorius backed him. Cyril wrote that the holy Fathers "ventured to call the holy Virgin, the Mother of God." They did not mean that the Word began in her. They meant that of her was born the body to which the Word was united.

Socrates says Cyril's party at Ephesus did not wait for John, bishop of Antioch. Nestorius's party met apart and deposed Cyril. When John came, he blamed Cyril for all the confusion. Nestorius, Cyril's enemy, later charged him with bribery, "the gold of iniquity." In 433 Cyril made peace with John. His letter begins, "Let the heavens rejoice, and let the earth be glad."

At Chalcedon in 451, bishops heard his letters again, and one by Leo of Rome. They cried, "Everlasting be the memory of Cyril. Leo and Cyril taught the same thing."

Key Works

  • Second Letter to Nestorius: the letter read at Ephesus in 431, which says the Word united to himself "flesh animated by a rational soul" and that the Fathers called the Virgin the Mother of God
  • Third Letter to Nestorius, with the Twelve Anathematisms: twelve condemnations, the first of anyone who will not confess that "the Holy Virgin is the Mother of God"
  • Five Tomes against Nestorius: five books answering a book of Nestorius's words, passage by passage, beginning with his attack on the word Mother of God

Legacy

What traces to them

  • At Chalcedon in 451 his letter to Nestorius and his letter to John of Antioch were read, and Aëtius, archdeacon of Constantinople, said the first had been "confirmed by the subscription of all" at Ephesus.
  • His letter to John of Antioch, which begins "Let the heavens rejoice", sealed the peace of 433. Blomfield Jackson says both sides accepted a formula which Theodoret of Cyrrhus "himself had drawn up at Ephesus two years before".
  • Henry Percival, who edited the English volume of the councils, says the Fifth Council condemned Theodoret "because he wrote against these very Anathemas".

How they're remembered

  • The bishops at Chalcedon, after the reading of Leo of Rome's letter: "Everlasting be the memory of Cyril. Leo and Cyril taught the same thing."
  • Socrates of Constantinople, on the murder of Hypatia: it brought "not the least opprobrium, not only upon Cyril", "but also upon the whole Alexandrian church".
  • Nestorius, his opponent, in the Bazaar of Heracleides, accuses him of winning by "the gold of iniquity".
  • A letter on his death, "ascribed to Theodoret" by the Fifth Council, begins "At last and with difficulty the villain has gone." Its authorship is doubted; see What is uncertain.

At Major Councils

  • Council of Ephesus, 431: Led the council that deposed Nestorius; deposed in turn by John's assembly; made peace with John in 433

What Is Uncertain

  • Much of his story comes through Socrates of Constantinople, who blames Cyril for the shame of the murder of the philosopher Hypatia. A. C. Zenos, who edited Socrates in English, says this "severe censure" is more easily accounted for by Socrates's closeness to the Novatians, since Cyril was their declared enemy. Socrates does not say Cyril ordered the murder.
  • His part in Hypatia's death is weighed differently. The notes to Zenos's edition report that the historians Walch, Gibbon and Milman lean to holding him guilty. They quote William Bright: "Cyril was no party to this hideous deed, but it was the work of men whose passions he had originally called out."
  • Nestorius, in his own defense, says Cyril won at Ephesus by bribery. G. R. Driver and Leonard Hodgson, who translated it, say the other evidence is in letters of Acacius of Beroea and of Epiphanius, Cyril's own archdeacon. Those letters are not among the sources used here.
  • Socrates says Cyril's party deposed Nestorius before John of Antioch arrived, and that Nestorius's party then deposed Cyril. Nestorius's own account has the council that met with John depose Cyril and Memnon of Ephesus.
  • Whether Ephesus received the Twelve Anathematisms is disputed. Henry Percival says "the Acts do not recite that this letter of Cyril's was read, nor do they state that the Anathemas were received", though the extract he prints has a bracket saying the letter was read. He argues that the council's critics cannot consistently deny it adopted them, and reports that Bishop Hefele first thought the letter merely read, then changed his mind. Percival adds that no one disputes that Chalcedon received them twenty years later.
  • The letter on his death "ascribed to Theodoret" is contested. Blomfield Jackson's note reports that Baronius rejected it and that Garnerius thought its bitterness no argument against Theodoret's authorship. Jackson quotes Canon Bright, who rejects it, and Canon Venables, who would be glad to reject it with Tillemont, Newman and Bright, but says "A painful suspicion of their genuineness, however, still lingers", and that Neander leans to accepting it.
  • The years 412 and 415 are given by the notes to Zenos's edition from Socrates's consuls. The year 433 for the peace is Blomfield Jackson's. Socrates gives no year for his death.
