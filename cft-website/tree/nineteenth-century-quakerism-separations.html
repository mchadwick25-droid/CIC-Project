Nineteenth-Century Quakerism — the Separations

North America and Britain, 1815 to 1906 — a people known for silence dividing three ways over what the silence was for, while leading the fights against slavery and for women's rights.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:12

Read along

The Society of Friends came into the nineteenth century intact and left it in pieces. In 1827-28 the American yearly meetings tore apart over Elias Hicks, a Long Island farmer and traveling minister who held that the Inward Light, not the Bible or a doctrine of atonement, was the heart of the faith. Those who followed him and those who opposed him became separate bodies meeting in separate houses. Within twenty years the evangelical side divided again, between followers of the English banker-minister Joseph John Gurney and the conservative John Wilbur. Out of the Gurneyite stream came something Friends had never had — revival meetings, planned worship and paid pastors. So that by the century's end some Quaker meetings still sat in silence while others sang hymns and listened to a sermon. Through all of it Friends kept up the work they were best known for: abolition, prison reform, and the women's rights movement several of them helped to launch.

Documented Stories

The Clerk’s Table

Elias Hicks was seventy-eight in 1827, a Long Island farmer who had traveled in the ministry for fifty years, and whose preaching that the Inward Light was the sole authority, above the Scriptures, had for a decade divided American Friends between his hearers, mostly rural, and the wealthy urban elders who held him a deist. Friends had no votes and no creed; disputes were settled by the clerk's discernment of the sense of the meeting. In April 1827 Philadelphia Yearly Meeting, the largest, could not agree on a clerk: the Orthodox elders kept the table, the Hicksite majority protested, and after days of contention the Hicksites withdrew to hold their own yearly meeting at Green Street in October, claiming to be the true body and calling the others seceders, as the others called them. New York, Baltimore and Indiana divided the following year. At Ohio Yearly Meeting at Mount Pleasant in September 1828 the two parties arrived each intending to be the meeting: the Orthodox clerk Elisha Bates took the table, the Hicksites put forward their own, and in the press of bodies the table was overturned and broken, benches were thrown, and Friends who had never raised their voices in a meeting-house shouted and shoved until the Hicksites withdrew. Each side minuted the other's disorder. Property and schools were divided by lawsuit, families were split, and the two bodies did not reunite until 1955. Hicks died in 1830, having said that he had never wished to divide anyone.

Behind the Bar at Freemasons’ Hall

Lucretia Mott had been a recorded minister among Friends since 1821, had gone with the Hicksites in 1827, and was by 1840 a founder of the Philadelphia Female Anti-Slavery Society and of the American Anti-Slavery Society, which had divided that spring over admitting women. The British and Foreign Anti-Slavery Society had called a World's Convention in London for June 1840, and the American delegations included women, Mott among them; the British organizers had not expected this and would not have it. On the opening day, 12 June, the convention debated whether the women's credentials should be accepted; Wendell Phillips moved that they should, English clergymen replied that it was contrary to Scripture and custom, and the motion was lost by a large majority. The women were seated behind a bar, or a rail, at the side of the hall, where they could hear and not speak; William Lloyd Garrison, arriving late, sat with them in silence for the whole convention in protest. Mott went to the sessions, argued with the delegates in the intervals, and preached on the Sunday in a Unitarian chapel. Among the women behind the bar was Elizabeth Cady Stanton, twenty-four, who wrote later that she and Mott, walking arm in arm through the streets afterwards, resolved to hold a convention on the rights of women when they got home. It took eight years; it met at Seneca Falls in July 1848, in a Wesleyan chapel, and Mott was its senior figure.

Connections

In Its Own Record

The Hicksite separation of 1827–28 was the defining split of the century for the Society of Friends. It was followed by a further division between the evangelical Gurneyite and conservative Wilburite wings, and by a pastoral-system revival that introduced planned worship and paid ministers to part of the movement.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Friends split first in 1827-28 over Elias Hicks' teaching that the Inward Light, not the Bible or a doctrine of atonement, stood at the heart of the faith, then split again between evangelical and conservative wings. Out of the evangelical stream came something Quakers had never had before: revival meetings, planned worship, and paid pastors, so that by the century's end some meetings sat in silence while others sang hymns and listened to sermons.

Legacy

All three streams are still here: the pastoral, evangelical Friends who are now the majority of Quakers worldwide — most of them in Kenya and Latin America. The unprogrammed liberal meetings, and the small conservative bodies that kept the older ways. Friends' public work outlasted their divisions. The peace testimony survived the separations intact, the relief agencies that grew from it were jointly awarded the Nobel Peace Prize in 1947, and the Quaker practice of seeking the sense of a meeting rather than counting votes has been borrowed far outside the Society.
