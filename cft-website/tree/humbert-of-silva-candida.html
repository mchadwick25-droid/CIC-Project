Silva Candida · Constantinople, today in Turkey

Humbert of Silva Candida

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Latin Papal Church (Gregorian Reform to Innocent III).

Known for
Cardinal bishop and first-named envoy of Pope Leo IX to Constantinople in 1054. With Peter of Amalfi and Frederick the chancellor, he laid the sentence excommunicating Patriarch Michael Keroularios on the altar of Hagia Sophia.
Also called
Cardinal Humbert of Silva Candida, Humbertus
Dates
Sent to Constantinople in January 1054, Cornelius Will says. The sentence was laid on a Saturday in July, which Will reads as 16 July. The synod answered on 20 July, by Will's reckoning. Signed Pope Nicholas II's decree on papal elections in 1059. No held source gives his birth or death
Place
Bishop of Silva Candida. Envoy at Constantinople, where the envoys stayed at the palace called Pigi
Role
Cardinal bishop; papal envoy

About This Person

On a Saturday in July, the clergy of Hagia Sophia in Constantinople were ready for Mass. Three envoys from Rome came in and laid a sentence of excommunication on the high altar. They went out, shook the dust from their feet, and cried out that God should see and judge.

Their own account names Humbert, cardinal bishop of Silva Candida, first. Pope Leo IX had sent him with Peter, archbishop of Amalfi, and Frederick, the chancellor. Cornelius Will, who edited it, believes Humbert wrote it. Will reads its date as 16 July 1054, and Carl Mirbt gives 13 July.

In 1053, Will says, Leo, archbishop of Ohrid, wrote against the Latins at the urging of Michael Keroularios, patriarch of Constantinople. Will dates the embassy to January 1054, and Pope Leo's death to 19 April. Wibert, Pope Leo's biographer, says Humbert wrote a Dialogue in the city, against the Greek charges.

A monk of the Studios monastery, Nicetas, had written a book against the Latins, on unleavened bread at Mass, the Sabbath and married priests. On 24 June, the account says, Nicetas cursed his own book before the emperor, and it was burned.

The envoys' account says the patriarch avoided them. Michael wrote that they came to him but would not greet him, bow, or sit below his bishops. Their sentence likens his party to the heretics of old. They sell the gift of God, it says, and they have cut from the creed the Spirit's going forth from the Son. It curses Michael, Leo of Ohrid and their followers, unless they repent.

The edict of Michael's synod calls the envoys men from the darkness of the West, who burst into the city like a wild boar. The Greeks will not corrupt the creed, it says, for they hold that the Spirit proceeds from the Father. The pope never sent the envoys, the edict adds. They came on the advice of Argyrus, the Greek governor in Italy, and forged the pope's letters. On 20 July, by Will's reading, the synod cursed the paper and its makers.

The envoys' account says Michael falsified the Greek version of their sentence to turn the crowd against them. Will takes their side and says the edict heaps lie on lie.

Will wrote that the sentence of 1054 made the split between the churches complete, and that it has lasted.

Key Works

  • Brief account of what the envoys of the Roman see did in the royal city: a record of the 1054 embassy, told in the third person. Cornelius Will and Carl Mirbt print it under Humbert's name, and Will believes he wrote it. Sigebert of Gembloux and Ekkehard say Humbert wrote these things down
  • Sentence of excommunication against Michael Keroularios: laid on the altar of Hagia Sophia in the names of Humbert, Peter of Amalfi and Frederick the chancellor. It likens the patriarch's party to the heretics of old and curses him, Leo of Ohrid and their followers
  • Dialogue between a Roman and a man of Constantinople: an answer to the Greek charges against the Latins. Wibert, Pope Leo's biographer, says Humbert wrote it in Latin at Constantinople in 1054, and the emperor had it put into Greek. Will reports that the church historian A. F. Gfrörer thought it was brought from Rome

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The edict of Michael's synod says the envoys' paper was not burned. It was kept in the archive of the patriarch's record keeper, to the lasting shame of all who blaspheme God so.
  • Michael Keroularios wrote twice to Peter, patriarch of Antioch, after the envoys had gone. Will says he repeated the old charges against the Roman church and added new ones.
  • In 1059 he signed Pope Nicholas II's decree on how popes were to be chosen, as bishop of Silva Candida.

How they're remembered

  • Sigebert of Gembloux and Ekkehard, in their chronicles, say Humbert wrote down these events and refuted the writings of Nicetas and of Leo of Ohrid.
  • Cornelius Will calls the envoys' account the source of most of the eleventh century's own tellings of the split. He holds that its truth cannot be shaken.
  • Will wrote that through the sentence of 1054 the separation of the churches was made complete, and that it has lasted.
  • Carl Mirbt's collection of papal sources prints the account and the sentence under a heading that calls it the break between Rome and Byzantium.

What Is Uncertain

  • Most of the story comes from the envoys' own account. The Greek side is known from the edict of Michael's synod. The two disagree, and Cornelius Will, who edited both, takes the Roman side.
  • Who forged what. The edict says the pope never sent the envoys. They came on the advice of Argyrus, the Greek governor in Italy, it says, and their letters bore false seals. The envoys' account says Michael falsified the Greek version of their sentence. Will calls the edict's charge a lie.
  • Why they never met. The envoys' account says the patriarch avoided them. Michael wrote to Peter of Antioch that they would not greet him, bow, or sit below his metropolitans.
  • Who wrote the account. It speaks of the envoys in the third person. Will gives it to Humbert as head of the embassy, and Sigebert and Ekkehard say Humbert wrote these things down.
  • Who wrote the reply to Nicetas. Wibert, Pope Leo's biographer, gives it to Frederick the chancellor, and Will says others followed him. Will gives it to Humbert, since it points back to his earlier answer to Michael and Leo of Ohrid.
  • The day of the sentence. The account gives a Saturday, the seventeenth day before the Kalends of August, which Will reads as 16 July 1054. Carl Mirbt's heading gives 13 July.
  • The year. Some copies of the account give the eleventh year of Emperor Constantine Monomachos, and Will reads the twelfth. Will says many put the embassy in 1053, but Pope Leo's letter that the envoys carried is dated January 1054. No held source gives the day the envoys arrived.
  • The emperor's letter in the edict, blaming the interpreters and the men of Argyrus. Will notes that the envoys' account does not mention it. He thinks the emperor wrote it under pressure from the riot the patriarch had stirred up.
  • His life outside the embassy. No held source gives his birth, his death or his other writings, such as his books against the simoniacs.
