The Imperial Church Era · 386-407 · Antioch, Constantinople

Antioch · Constantinople, today in Turkey

John Chrysostom

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Antiochene Exegetical Christianity (Chrysostom-centered).

Known for
Preacher at Antioch and bishop of Constantinople, who spoke plainly about wealth and the poor and died in exile.
Role
Preacher and bishop of Constantinople

About This Person

In 399 the bishop of Constantinople, the eastern capital, was John, a preacher from Antioch. Not long before, the emperor's chief eunuch, Eutropius, had a law passed that stripped refugees of the right to shelter in churches. Then he fell from favor and fled into the cathedral. As he lay trembling under the altar, John climbed into the pulpit and preached against him. Socrates of Constantinople says some thought John added insult to cruelty. John said he spoke to soften the people's hearts toward him, for the church, like a mother, had hidden her enemy under her cloak.

He preached plainly about the poor, and his blunt words to the powerful led to his exile. At Antioch John had trained under Diodore, who kept to the literal sense of Scripture. In a sermon on Matthew, he told his listeners that if they wanted to honor the body of Christ, they must not neglect Him when He is naked.

In 398 Eutropius made Theophilus of Alexandria ordain him. Palladius, who wrote John's life and took his side, says he moved the bishop's extravagant spending to the hospital.

Socrates says his rebukes of men in office turned the tide against him. It is said he was as blunt with the Gothic general Gainas, who asked for a city church for the Arians. They taught that the Son of God had a beginning. Socrates says Theophilus held a grudge, since his favorite had lost the see to John. Monks Theophilus had driven out of Egypt, in a quarrel over whether God has a human body, came to John for help. Socrates says that when Theophilus heard, falsely, that John had given them the Eucharist, he resolved to unseat him.

He and his bishops, meeting at the Oak near Chalcedon, cited John to appear. His accusers charged that he had sold church property, insulted the clergy, struck a man and eaten greedily alone. When John refused them as his enemies, they deposed him, Socrates says, for refusing. The people rose, and he was recalled.

Public games were held at a silver statue of the empress Eudoxia beside the cathedral. John mocked those who allowed them, and she took this as aimed at her. A second council condemned him for taking back his see without a council's leave, and in 404 the emperor sent him into exile. Palladius says soldiers hurried him toward Pityus, hoping he would die. He died on the way in 407, with his usual words, "Glory to God for all things."

In 438 Bishop Proclus carried John's body back to Constantinople. By this, Socrates says, he brought back to the church's communion the followers who had left it over John's deposition.

Key Works

  • Homilies on Matthew: verse-by-verse sermons, including Homily 50 on honoring Christ in the poor
  • Homilies on the Statues: twenty-one sermons from the weeks of fear in Antioch
  • On the Priesthood: written, the church historian Socrates of Constantinople says, while he was a deacon at Antioch

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Socrates of Constantinople says his sermons reached readers two ways: some John Chrysostom published himself, and others were taken down in shorthand as he spoke.
  • Palladius, a bishop who took his side, wrote a dialogue on his life and fall, and tells the journey into exile.
  • After his deposition John Chrysostom's followers, called Johannites by their opponents, left the church and held their own services in the public baths and elsewhere. They came back, Socrates says, only when Proclus of Constantinople brought John's body home on the twenty-seventh of January, 438. He laid it in the Church of the Apostles.
  • Socrates of Constantinople says that on the day John was led into exile some of the Johannites set fire to the church, and the fire spread to the senate-house.

How they're remembered

  • Honored by Palladius, who defends him throughout his dialogue, and by the crowds who kept watch all night to stop his removal from the church.
  • Disputed in his lifetime: Severian of Gabala preached that John Chrysostom's haughtiness was reason enough to depose him. Sisinnius, the Novatian bishop, wrote against John's saying 'Approach, although you may have repented a thousand times.' Socrates of Constantinople, who tells of both, writes that John's zeal for strictness sat oddly with that sermon.
  • Socrates of Constantinople notes that Theophilus of Alexandria condemned Origen about two hundred years after Origen's death, while John Chrysostom was restored to communion in the thirty-fifth year after his deposition.

What Is Uncertain

  • Socrates of Constantinople reports that the sermon over Eutropius, the emperor's chief eunuch, struck some as cruel. In the sermon itself John Chrysostom says he was blamed for taking Eutropius into the sanctuary, and that he described the man's fall to move the people to mercy. Socrates also passes on that John was said to be stern and severe, and that a friend of his youth found him more prone to irritability than to modesty.
  • Socrates of Constantinople says Eutropius made Theophilus of Alexandria ordain John by collecting the memorials of charges laid against Theophilus, showing them to him, and bidding him choose between ordaining John and standing trial on those charges.
  • John Chrysostom ate alone, and Socrates of Constantinople says no one knew why for certain. Palladius says he drank no wine, had a disordered stomach, and sometimes put off his meals while absorbed in prayer and study. Socrates also says people gave other reasons, and that the habit helped his enemies.
  • Socrates of Constantinople says John Chrysostom died in Comana. Palladius says the soldiers lodged him at a martyr's shrine five or six miles outside the town and that he died there.
  • Socrates of Constantinople says Theophilus of Alexandria resolved to unseat John when he heard, falsely, that John had admitted the Egyptian monks to the mysteries, and that his letters condemning the books of Origen hid his real motive, which was revenge on Isidore and Dioscorus and the removal of John. Socrates also says that at the Oak no mention was made of Origen.
  • The charges laid against John at the Oak are known from the synod's own minutes, which Photius, writing more than four centuries later, summarizes in his Library, Codex 59. Photius calls the synod unlawfully summoned and John a holy man. Socrates of Constantinople calls many of the charges ridiculous. Nothing held proves any of them.
  • Socrates of Constantinople says the bishops at the Oak deposed John for refusing the summons and gave no other cause. The minutes, as Photius gives them, say several of the counts were examined, beginning with the first and second, and then the forty-five members present voted unanimously to deprive him.
  • Socrates of Constantinople says the Oak followed a public sermon of John against women in general, which the people took as aimed at the empress Eudoxia. She complained to the emperor, and the emperor authorized Theophilus of Alexandria to call a synod against John.
  • Socrates of Constantinople says that at the second council John answered that the rule used against him was an Arian canon, framed at the council at Antioch against Athanasius, and that the bishops would not listen to his defense.
  • Modern editors, not the ancient writers, call Diodore's way of reading Scripture a system or a school. The translator Zenos, in a note to Socrates VI.3, says his literal and historical principle 'became the corner-stone of the Antiochian system of interpretation' under his pupils Theodore and Theodoret. M. B. Riddle, in his introductory essay to the Homilies on Matthew, says Diodore 'is reckoned the leader of the so-called Antiochian school of exegetes.' These are modern judgments.
  • The year of the Synod of the Oak is a modern date. Socrates gives no year for it. Freese, the translator of Photius, dates it 403 in a note that cites Hefele, and Moore, the translator of Palladius, puts it at 403 in his table of dates. The same table puts the removal to Pityus in June 407. Palladius himself gives no year for the march. These are modern judgments.
  • C. Marriott, the Oxford editor of the Homilies on Matthew, judges in his preface that they were 'undoubtedly delivered at Antioch' and 'probably in the latter part of the time during which he preached as a Presbyter.' That is a modern judgment about the whole series.
  • The Dialogue on the Life of Chrysostom is anonymous as it stands. Moore, its translator, writes in his introduction that it 'is generally attributed to Palladius, Bishop of Helenopolis,' and that nothing except the title suggests the speaker is Palladius. He also notes that Palladius was a common name and that one scholar, Bigot, has proposed another Palladius as its author. That the writer of the Dialogue and John's companion Palladius of Helenopolis are one man is the modern translator's attribution, not the ancient text's own statement.
  • The year of the Homilies on the Statues is a modern date. The Benedictine editors, following Tillemont, date the riot and the sermons to 387, and Schaff gives Lent 387. Baronius, Petavius and Valesius put them in 388, and the old historians Sozomen and Theodoret tell the riot as after the war against Maximus. These are modern judgments.
