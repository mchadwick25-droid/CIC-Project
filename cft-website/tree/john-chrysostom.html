The Imperial Church Era · 386-407 · Antioch, Constantinople

Antioch · Constantinople, today in Turkey

John Chrysostom

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Antiochene Exegetical Christianity (Chrysostom-centered).

Preacher at Antioch and bishop of Constantinople, who spoke plainly about wealth and the poor and died in exile.

About This Person

The emperor's chief chamberlain, Eutropius, had a law passed that stripped people who fled into churches of the right to shelter there. Soon after, in 399, he fell from favor and himself fled into the cathedral of Constantinople, the emperor's city. As he lay trembling under the altar, the bishop, John Chrysostom, climbed into the pulpit and preached against him. Some thought he was attacking a man already brought low. John said he spoke to soften the people's hearts, for the church had sheltered its enemy: "When everyone ignored him in his desolation, she alone, like a loving mother, hid him behind her curtains. She stood against the emperor's anger, the people's fury and unbearable hatred."

He preached plainly about the poor, and his blunt words to the powerful led to his exile. His sermons were read for centuries, and the followers who left the church over his fall came back only when his body came home.

John was born at Antioch, in Syria, and as a priest there for twelve years he preached on what the rich owed the poor. They decked the altar, where the bread of Communion lies, which Christians call Christ's body, with gold cups and silk. In one sermon he told them to honor Christ in the poor first: "Do you want to honor Christ's body? Do not neglect him when he is naked. While you honor him here with silk clothes, do not neglect him outside, dying of cold and nakedness." He published some of his sermons, and shorthand writers took down others as he spoke. Both kinds were kept and read.

In 398 the court chose him as bishop of Constantinople, and Eutropius made Theophilus, bishop of Alexandria, ordain him. As bishop he cut the bishop's own spending and gave the money to a hospital. He corrected his clergy sharply over how they lived and put some of them out of the church, and many came to hate him.

When a Gothic general asked for a church inside the city for the Arians, who taught that the Son of God had a beginning, John spoke against it before the emperor. Socrates, a church historian of the city writing a generation later, says Theophilus also held a grudge, since his own choice for bishop had lost out to John, and that he resolved to unseat him.

Theophilus and his bishops met at a place called the Oak, outside Chalcedon, and summoned John. His accusers charged that he had sold church property, insulted the clergy, struck a man and eaten greedily alone. John refused to come before judges he called his enemies, and asked for a council of the whole church. Socrates says they removed him from office only for refusing to come. A later bishop of Constantinople summed up the meeting's own written record, and they tell it differently: some charges were heard, and then all forty-five bishops voted to remove him. The emperor ordered him into exile, and John gave himself up, but he was soon called back.

Public games were held at a silver statue of the empress Eudoxia, just across the street from the cathedral. John mocked those who allowed them, calling the games an insult to the church, and the empress took this as aimed at her and sought another council. It condemned him for taking back his place as bishop, after his removal, without a council's leave. Since two councils had condemned him, the emperor barred him from the church, and John was silenced. His followers kept Easter in the public baths, and from then on they met apart from the church. In 404 he was taken into exile by force.

He was first exiled to Armenia. Palladius, who wrote John's life and took his side, says bishops in Syria, seeing the church of Antioch still looking to John, had him moved to Pityus, a more distant place on the Black Sea. In his telling, the soldiers hurried John on, saying they had been told his death would bring them promotion. Near Comana he asked for white clothes, took Communion and prayed the words he always used: "Glory to God for all things." He died there, in September 407.

In 438 the bishop of Constantinople had the emperor's leave to bring John's body home from Comana. He carried it through the city in procession and laid it in the Church of the Apostles, and those who had left the church over John's fall came back.

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Socrates of Constantinople says his sermons reached readers two ways: some John Chrysostom published himself, and others were taken down in shorthand as he spoke.
  • Palladius of Helenopolis says that as bishop he also built more hospitals, and put two devout priests, doctors and cooks over them.
  • Palladius of Helenopolis, a bishop who stood by him, wrote a dialogue on his life and fall, and tells the journey into exile.
  • After his deposition John Chrysostom's followers, called Johannites by their opponents, left the church and held their own services in the public baths and elsewhere. They came back, Socrates says, only when Proclus of Constantinople brought John's body home on the twenty-seventh of January, 438. He laid it in the Church of the Apostles.
  • Socrates of Constantinople says that on the day John was led into exile some of the Johannites set fire to the church, and the fire spread to the senate-house.
  • Sozomen says that in exile in Armenia he bought back many captives from the Isaurian robbers and returned them to their families, and that people from Antioch and the rest of Syria often came to see him.
  • John Cassian, whom he made a deacon, held him up to the people of Constantinople, against their bishop Nestorius, as the teacher to remember and follow.

How they're remembered

  • Honored by Palladius of Helenopolis, who defends him throughout his dialogue, and by the crowds who kept watch all night to stop his removal from the church.
  • Sozomen says his boldness pleased the people but grieved the wealthy and the powerful, who were guilty of most of the wrongs he spoke against.
  • Socrates of Constantinople says his own clergy turned against him when he set out to correct how they lived. His deacon Serapion made it worse, telling him before all the clergy that he would never govern them unless he drove them all with a rod, and John then put many of them out of the church.
  • Disputed in his lifetime: Severian of Gabala preached that John Chrysostom's haughtiness was reason enough to depose him. Sisinnius, the Novatian bishop, wrote against John's saying 'Approach, although you may have repented a thousand times.' Socrates of Constantinople, who tells of both, writes that John's zeal for strictness sat oddly with that sermon.
  • Socrates of Constantinople notes that Theophilus of Alexandria condemned Origen about two hundred years after Origen's death, while John Chrysostom was restored to communion in the thirty-fifth year after his death.
  • Theodoret says the bishops of the West would not share Communion with the bishops of the East until John's name was put back among the dead bishops named in the church's prayers, and that at Antioch Bishop Alexander first put it back.
  • Theodoret says that when the body came home, the emperor laid his head against the coffin and prayed for his parents and for pardon on those who had sinned in ignorance.

Open Questions

  • The name Chrysostom means golden-mouthed. No held writer of his lifetime gives it to him: it stands in the title of the Dialogue on his life and in the chapter headings of Sozomen's history, and Sozomen says some gave the same name to another bishop, Antiochus of Ptolemais, for his fine voice.
  • Socrates of Constantinople says he studied rhetoric under Libanius and then trained under Diodore, later bishop of Tarsus, who kept to the literal sense of Scripture and avoided hidden meanings. Sozomen adds that he had been expected to become a lawyer.
  • Sozomen says the general of the East took him from Antioch in secret and handed him to the emperor's officers, before the people of Antioch knew, since they might have rioted rather than let him go.
  • Socrates of Constantinople reports that the sermon over Eutropius, the emperor's chief eunuch, struck some as cruel. In the sermon itself John Chrysostom says he was blamed for taking Eutropius into the sanctuary, and that he described the man's fall to move the people to mercy. Socrates also passes on that John was said to be stern and severe, and that a friend of his youth found him more prone to irritability than to modesty.
  • Socrates of Constantinople says Eutropius made Theophilus of Alexandria ordain John by collecting the memorials of charges laid against Theophilus, showing them to him, and bidding him choose between ordaining John and standing trial on those charges.
  • The Gothic general who asked for a church for the Arians was Gainas. Socrates of Constantinople gives John's rebuke only as it is said. Sozomen says John went to the palace with the bishops in the city, spoke at length before the emperor and Gainas, and showed the law of Theodosius that forbade those outside the church's faith to hold a church within the walls.
  • John Chrysostom ate alone, and Socrates of Constantinople says no one knew why for certain. Palladius of Helenopolis, who knew him, says he drank no wine, had a disordered stomach, and sometimes put off his meals while absorbed in prayer and study. Socrates also says people gave other reasons, and that the habit helped his enemies.
  • Socrates of Constantinople says John Chrysostom died in Comana. Palladius of Helenopolis says the soldiers lodged him at a martyr's shrine five or six miles outside the town and that he died there.
  • Monks whom Theophilus of Alexandria had driven out of Egypt, in a quarrel over whether God has a body and the form of a man, came to John at Constantinople for help. Socrates of Constantinople says Theophilus himself taught that God has no body, and that John received the monks with honor but put off their Communion until their case was examined.
  • Socrates of Constantinople says Theophilus of Alexandria resolved to unseat John when he heard, falsely, that John had admitted the Egyptian monks to the mysteries, and that his letters condemning the books of Origen hid his real motive, which was revenge on Isidore and Dioscorus and the removal of John. Socrates also says that at the Oak no mention was made of Origen.
  • The charges laid against John at the Oak are known from the synod's own minutes, which Photius, writing more than four centuries later, summarizes in his Library, Codex 59. Photius calls the synod unlawfully summoned and John a holy man. Socrates of Constantinople calls many of the charges ridiculous. Nothing held proves any of them.
  • Socrates of Constantinople says the bishops at the Oak deposed John for refusing the summons and gave no other cause. The minutes, as Photius gives them, say several of the counts were examined, beginning with the first and second, and then the forty-five members present voted unanimously to deprive him.
  • After the Oak John was soon called back, and the old sources differ on why. Socrates of Constantinople says the people's anger, made worse by a sermon of Severian of Gabala against him, moved the emperor to recall him. Theodoret says an earthquake in the night terrified the empress Eudoxia, and envoys were sent at daybreak to bring him back.
  • Socrates of Constantinople says the Oak followed a public sermon of John against women in general, which the people took as aimed at the empress Eudoxia. She complained to the emperor, and the emperor authorized Theophilus of Alexandria to call a synod against John.
  • The Dialogue on his life says his enemies charged him with treason for calling the empress Jezebel, the queen in the Bible who killed the Lord's prophets.
  • Socrates of Constantinople says that at the second council John answered that the rule used against him was an Arian canon, framed at the council at Antioch against Athanasius, and that the bishops would not listen to his defense.
  • Socrates of Constantinople says some of John's followers set the fire on the day he was led into exile. Sozomen says each side blamed the other: his enemies said his followers set it in revenge, and his followers said their enemies set it to burn them in the church.
  • His first place of exile was Cucusus in Armenia. Marcellinus, writing in the sixth century, says rival bishops sent him there against the will of the emperor Arcadius. Socrates of Constantinople gives the exile as the emperor's decree.
  • Palladius of Helenopolis says bishops of Syria had John moved to Pityus, a desolate spot on the shore of the Black Sea, and that the journey took three months. Sozomen says an imperial edict sent him to a more distant exile after the envoys whom Innocent, bishop of Rome, sent to ask the emperors for a council were sent away in disgrace.
