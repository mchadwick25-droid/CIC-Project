The Imperial Church Era · 386-407 · Antioch, Constantinople

Antioch · Constantinople, today in Turkey

John Chrysostom

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Antiochene Exegetical Christianity (Chrysostom-centered).

Known for
Preacher at Antioch and bishop of Constantinople, who spoke plainly about wealth and the poor and died in exile.
Role
Preacher and bishop of Constantinople

About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In 399, the emperor's chief eunuch, Eutropius, fled into the cathedral of Constantinople, though he had just stripped refugees of the right to shelter there. As he lay trembling under the altar table, Bishop John climbed into the pulpit and preached against him. The church historian Socrates says some thought John added insult to cruelty. John himself said he spoke to soften the people's hearts toward him, for the church, like a mother, had hidden her enemy under her cloak.

Years before, a tax riot in Antioch tore down the emperor's statues. John, by then a priest, preached to the frightened crowds, comforting them and urging them to change their lives, until Flavian, the city's old bishop, won the emperor's pardon.

In February of 398 John had been made bishop, the court's own choice, and Eutropius had made Theophilus of Alexandria ordain him. Palladius, who stood by John to the end, says he found the bishop's expenses extravagant and moved that money to the hospital. John told the rich: "Wouldest thou do honor to Christ’s body? Neglect Him not when naked."

But Theophilus held a grudge, because his own favorite, the Alexandrian priest Isidore, had lost the see to John. Socrates says Theophilus used a quarrel over Origen's books against him. In 403 the bishops at the Oak cited John four times. He refused them as his enemies, and they deposed him for refusing. The people rose, and he was recalled. After a clash with the empress Eudoxia, a second council silenced him. Socrates notes the irony: the bishops condemned John under a canon first framed at Antioch against Athanasius of Alexandria, and so approved Athanasius's own deposition. In 404 an imperial decree sent John to Cucusus in Armenia. Palladius says he spent a year there while a great famine struck Armenia, and that John fed the poor more with words than with grain. Then his enemies had him moved on. In 407 a harsher order sent him toward Pityus on the Black Sea. He died on the road, Palladius says, hurried along by soldiers who hoped he would not survive.

In 438 Bishop Proclus of Constantinople carried John's body back to the city in procession, and John's followers returned to the church.

Key Works

  • Homilies on Matthew: verse-by-verse sermons, including Homily 50 on honoring Christ in the poor
  • Homilies on the Statues: twenty-one sermons from the weeks of fear in Antioch in 387
  • On the Priesthood: written, the church historian Socrates of Constantinople says, while he was a deacon at Antioch

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Socrates of Constantinople says his sermons reached readers two ways: some John Chrysostom published himself, and others were taken down in shorthand as he spoke.
  • Palladius of Helenopolis, a bishop who stood by him, wrote a dialogue on his life and fall, and tells the journey into exile.
  • After his deposition John Chrysostom's followers, called Johannites by their opponents, left the church and held their own services in the public baths and elsewhere. They came back, Socrates says, only when Proclus of Constantinople brought John's body home on the twenty-seventh of January, 438. He laid it in the Church of the Apostles.

How they're remembered

  • Honored by Palladius of Helenopolis, who defends him throughout his dialogue, and by the crowds who kept watch all night to stop his removal from the church.
  • Disputed in his lifetime: Severian of Gabala preached that John Chrysostom's haughtiness was reason enough to depose him. Sisinnius, the Novatian bishop, wrote against John's saying 'Approach, although you may have repented a thousand times.' Socrates of Constantinople, who tells of both, writes that John's zeal for strictness sat oddly with that sermon.
  • Socrates of Constantinople notes that Theophilus of Alexandria condemned Origen about two hundred years after Origen's death, while John Chrysostom was restored to communion in the thirty-fifth year after his deposition.

What Is Uncertain

  • Socrates of Constantinople reports that the sermon over Eutropius, the emperor's chief eunuch, struck some as cruel. In the sermon itself John Chrysostom says he was blamed for taking Eutropius into the sanctuary, and that he described the man's fall to move the people to mercy. Socrates also passes on that John was said to be stern and severe, and that a friend of his youth found him more prone to irritability than to modesty.
  • John Chrysostom ate alone, and Socrates of Constantinople says no one knew why for certain. Palladius of Helenopolis, who knew him, says he drank no wine, had a disordered stomach, and sometimes put off his meals while absorbed in prayer and study. Socrates also says people gave other reasons, and that the habit helped his enemies.
  • Socrates of Constantinople says John Chrysostom died in Comana. Palladius of Helenopolis says the soldiers lodged him at a martyr's shrine five or six miles outside the town and that he died there.
