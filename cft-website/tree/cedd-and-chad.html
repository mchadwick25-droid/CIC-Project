Cedd and Chad

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Anglo-Saxon Christianity.

Also called Ceadda (Chad, the form of Bede's Latin), Cedda (a form later writers use for either brother)

Two English brothers whom Bede tells together, through Lastingham, the monastery Cedd founded and Chad ruled after him. Cedd was bishop of the East Saxons and stood with the Irish party at the synod of Whitby in 664. Chad was made bishop of York, then of the Mercians at Lichfield.

About This Person

Cedd chose a site for a monastery, on land given by King Ethelwald of the Deiri. Bede says its crags and distant hills looked more like haunts of robbers and wild beasts than homes. First Cedd meant to cleanse it by prayer and fasting. Through Lent he fasted each day but Sunday until evening, and then ate only "a little bread, one hen's egg, and a little milk mixed with water."

The place was Lastingham, in the north of England. Cedd and Chad were brothers, two of four who all became priests, which Bede calls "a rare thing to be met with." Bede tells their lives together, as he learned them from the monks of the one house they shared.

King Oswy sent Cedd to preach to the East Saxons, and Finan of Lindisfarne made him their bishop. He built churches and gathered monks at Ithancestir and at Tilbury, on the Thames. At the synod of Whitby in 664 he stood with the Irish party on Easter. Bede says he was also "a most careful interpreter for both parties." Afterward he accepted what Bede calls "the Catholic observance of Easter." He fell sick and died at Lastingham in a time of plague.

He left Lastingham to Chad, a disciple of Aidan of Lindisfarne. Oswy then sent Chad to be made bishop of York, while Wilfrid of Ripon was overseas. Chad went about on foot, Bede says, "after the manner of the apostles." Theodore of Tarsus came as the new archbishop. When he told Chad he had not been properly consecrated, Chad offered to resign, "for I never thought myself worthy of it." Bede says Theodore completed his ordination instead. Stephen, who wrote Wilfrid's Life and takes his side, says Theodore ordered Chad removed from another man's see. Even so, Stephen calls him a true and most gentle servant of God.

At Theodore's request, Chad became bishop of the Mercians, at Lichfield. When Chad would not ride, Theodore lifted him onto the horse with his own hands. He died there during a plague, on the second of March.

Trumbert, one of Bede's teachers, had been a monk under Chad. He said that when a strong wind rose, Chad prayed for mercy for all mankind. If the wind grew stronger, "he closed his book, and prostrating himself on the ground, prayed still more earnestly."

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Bede says he learned how Cedd and Chad lived and died from the monks of Lastingham, the monastery they built.
  • Bede names a second informant on Chad: Trumbert, one of his own teachers, who had been a monk under Chad.
  • Lichfield, Bede says, remained the see of the bishops of the Mercians after Chad. His tomb there was a wooden house, and people took its dust to give in water to the sick.
  • The Roman Breviary keeps Chad's feast on 2 March, and the Bollandists give Cedd's on 7 January.

How they're remembered

  • Stephen, who wrote the Life of Wilfrid and takes Wilfrid's side, calls Chad a most religious servant of God and an admirable teacher, though he says Chad was ordained against the canons.
  • Egbert, an English monk who had lived in Ireland with Chad in their youth, told of a man who saw the soul of Cedd come down with angels to take Chad's soul to heaven. Bede says he does not know whether Egbert meant himself or another.
  • Later writers often confuse the brothers. Henry of Huntingdon makes Chad succeed Cedd as bishop of the East Saxons, and the Bollandists note that the two names are often mixed up.

Open Questions

  • Chad's first consecration. Bede says Wini of the West Saxons consecrated him with two British bishops, and that Theodore later completed his ordination. Stephen, Wilfrid's biographer, says Chad was put into another man's see against the canons, that Theodore ordered him removed, and that Chad did penance before he was ordained in full for Lichfield.
  • The year of Cedd's death. Bede gives none. He dates the synod of Whitby to 664, and says Cedd died later at Lastingham in a time of plague. Charles Plummer and the Bollandists give 664. A chapter heading in J. A. Giles's English gives 659, which cannot stand beside Bede's own account of Whitby.
  • The year Chad was made bishop of York. Bede's own summary of years gives 664, and a heading in J. A. Giles's English gives 665.
  • The year of Chad's death. Bede gives the day, 2 March, and says he ruled the Mercians two and a half years. The Bollandists give 672.
  • Which brother was older. No held source says. The Bollandists call Cedd the elder, without a witness.
  • Cedd's see. Bede calls him bishop of the East Saxons and names no city as his see.
  • Two slips in J. A. Giles's English. Bede's teacher is Trumbert in the Latin, where Giles has Trumhere. Bede says the British bishops kept Easter contrary to the canonical custom, where Giles has according to it.
