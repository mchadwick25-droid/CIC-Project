Braulio of Zaragoza

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Nicene Visigothic Iberia.

Also called Braulio of Saragossa, Braulio Caesaraugustanus

Bishop of Zaragoza in Visigothic Spain, friend and editor of Isidore of Seville, and a letter writer. He wrote to Pope Honorius for the bishops of Spain in 638.

About This Person

In seventh-century Visigothic Spain, Braulio, bishop of Zaragoza, was losing his sight when King Chindaswinth sent for his archdeacon, Eugenius. A part of his soul was being taken from him, he wrote, and he begged the king not to part them. The king answered that he trusted this was God's will, and that Eugenius had been born in the city that now needed a bishop. Braulio sent him. The bond between them had burst, he wrote, rather than been cut.

Ildefonsus of Toledo, a later bishop there, tells of a cleric of the royal church named Eugenius. He fled to Zaragoza to live as a monk, until the king's force brought him back to be bishop of Toledo. Ildefonsus does not name Braulio.

Ildefonsus says Braulio followed his brother John as bishop of Zaragoza and served about twenty years, under four kings. He signed at three councils of Toledo in the 630s.

He was the friend and editor of Isidore of Seville. In Ernest Brehaut's English, Braulio says that Isidore left the Etymologies unfinished. Braulio divided it into twenty books, "since he wrote it at my request."

In 638 Braulio wrote to Pope Honorius for the bishops of a council of Toledo. The pope had urged them to act more firmly against the faithless. The council had just praised King Chintila, who would let no one who was not Catholic live in his kingdom. The council's canon on the Jews made every later king swear to keep it so. Braulio defended the bishops' slower way. They would bend by Christian coaxing, he wrote, those whom hard discipline could hardly move. The pope's rebuke had called them dumb dogs that cannot bark. That did not fit them at all, Braulio answered.

Braulio once teased a priest named Taio, who answered with insults. Braulio could bite back if he wished, he wrote. He would rather follow Christ, who gave his back to the whips and did not curse in return.

Key Works

  • Letters: among them letters to Isidore of Seville, to Kings Chindaswinth and Reccesvinth, and to the priest Taio. One, to Pope Honorius, he wrote for the bishops of Spain
  • Life of Saint Aemilian: the Life of a monk named Aemilian, sent to his brother Fronimian, a priest. Ildefonsus of Toledo says it keeps Braulio's own memory and makes the holy man's virtue known
  • List of Isidore's works: his notice of Isidore of Seville and his books. In it he says he divided the Etymologies into twenty books, since Isidore wrote it at his request

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Isidore of Seville sent him the Etymologies, which Braulio says Isidore wrote at his request. Braulio divided the work into twenty books.
  • Ildefonsus of Toledo gives him a chapter in his book of famous church writers.
  • Ildefonsus's book tells of a Toledo cleric named Eugenius who became a monk at Zaragoza and then bishop of Toledo, Eugenius the second. He may be the archdeacon Braulio gave up to King Chindaswinth.

How they're remembered

  • Nicolás Antonio, in his Bibliotheca Hispana vetus, quotes a chronicle he gives to Isidore, bishop of Pax. Theodor Mommsen, who edits it, says it runs to 754 and calls the name false.
  • The chronicle says Braulio shone at a council of Toledo under King Sisenand, which Mommsen identifies as the fourth. Rome later marveled at his eloquence through a letter, it adds.
  • At a later council under King Chintila, which Antonio takes as the sixth, the chronicle says he poured devout teaching into Christian minds. It adds that the church still read his short works.

Open Questions

  • His life is known from his own letters, from Ildefonsus's short chapter on him and from the acts of the councils he signed.
  • Whether his archdeacon Eugenius is the Eugenius whom Ildefonsus makes bishop of Toledo. Braulio's letters and the king's do not name the city. Ildefonsus tells of a Toledo cleric named Eugenius who became a monk at Zaragoza and was brought back by the king's force. The match rests on these two accounts.
  • Whom Pope Honorius meant by the faithless. Braulio's reply speaks of the faithless. It also denies a rumor that a ruling of the Roman prince let baptized Jews return to what it calls their superstition. The pope's letter is not held.
  • His age and death. His letter to the king speaks of his age and his failing sight. No held source gives the year of his death. Theodor Mommsen, from Ildefonsus's list of kings, puts it under Chindaswinth, between 641 and 652. His name is missing from the acts of the seventh council of Toledo, Antonio notes, and by the eighth, Taio was bishop of Zaragoza.
  • Whether Braulio wrote the notice of Isidore's works. A note printed with Nicolás Antonio's notice says Gregorius Mayansius, in his Life of Antonio, denied it. The note keeps it as his, as all the old manuscripts its writer had seen and nearly all bibliographers agree.
