The Imperial Church Era · c. 291-371 · Gaza, Cyprus

Hilarion of Gaza

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Early Palestinian Ascetic Monasticism.

Known for
Antony's disciple, remembered as the first monk of Palestine, who brought the Egyptian desert life to the coast near Gaza.
Dates
About 291 to about 371 (371 is Chester Hartranft's reckoning; Jerome says he died in his eightieth year)
Place
Thabatha, near Gaza, and later Cyprus
Role
Hermit and founder of Palestinian monasticism

About This Person

A woman named Aristaenete came to a hermit in the desert near Gaza, her three sons near death. She had just been to see Antony in Egypt. Now she begged this monk to come to the city and heal them. He answered that he never left his cell. She would not go until he promised to come after sunset. He made the sign of the cross over each boy, and by Jerome's account they recovered within the hour.

The monk was Hilarion. Jerome of Strido wrote his life about 390, and it is the main record. W. H. Fremantle's English edition of Jerome warns that the Life "contains, amidst much that is legendary, some statements which attach it to genuine history." Jerome tells it as the life of a saint.

Hilarion was born at Thabatha, near Gaza, late in the third century. He studied at Alexandria, then went to see Antony in the desert, and stayed about two months. When he came home his parents had died. He gave away his share and settled in the coastal scrub to live as Antony did. Jerome calls him the first monk in Palestine. "It was he who originated this mode of life and devotion," he writes, and sets him beside his master. "The Lord Jesus had in Egypt the aged Antony: in Palestine He had the youthful Hilarion."

As his fame grew, crowds came for healing, and Hilarion grieved at it. "I have returned again to the world," he said, "and have received my reward in my lifetime." He left Palestine and moved from place to place, through Egypt, Sicily and Dalmatia, and died on Cyprus at about eighty.

Sozomen, the church historian, had a family reason to remember Hilarion. He says Hilarion cured a man named Alaphion near Gaza "by simply calling on the name of Christ." It was said, Sozomen writes, that the cure made Christians of Alaphion's household and his own grandfather's.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Monastic life on the Palestinian coast, which Jerome credits him with originating: "It was he who originated this mode of life and devotion."
  • The conversion of families near Gaza, among them that of Sozomen's own grandfather, which Sozomen traces, as a thing that "is said", to Hilarion's cure of Alaphion.

How they're remembered

  • Jerome of Strido wrote his Life about 390 and set him beside Antony: "The Lord Jesus had in Egypt the aged Antony: in Palestine He had the youthful Hilarion."
  • Epiphanius of Salamis, who knew him, had already "set forth his praises in a short but widely circulated letter," by Jerome's account.

What Is Uncertain

  • Hilarion left no writing that is held here. His life is known chiefly from Jerome's Life, a work of praise. W. H. Fremantle's English edition of Jerome warns that it "contains, amidst much that is legendary, some statements which attach it to genuine history." The same edition calls the theory that it was "a sort of religious romance" one that "seems destitute of foundation."
  • Sozomen, whose own family came from the region, gives a separate notice of Hilarion and of the cure of Alaphion. He reports the conversions as what "is said".
  • His dates rest on the Life and on Chester Hartranft's reckoning in his introduction to Sozomen, "Hilarion passed away about 371". Jerome says he died in his eightieth year, having served Christ "nearly seventy years."
