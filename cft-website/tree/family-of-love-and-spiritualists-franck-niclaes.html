Family of Love & Spiritualists (Franck, Niclaes)

The Rhineland, the Low Countries and England, c. 1540 to 1600 — spiritualists who held that the true church was invisible, and a secret fellowship whose members outwardly conformed to everything.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 0:53

Read along

Some of the Reformation's most radical figures decided that no visible church was worth joining at all. Sebastian Franck, a former priest who became a printer and chronicler, held that the true church had been hidden since the apostles. God's word, he said, spoke inwardly to people of every nation. Hendrik Niclaes, a merchant in the Low Countries, went further and founded a fellowship of his own. This was the Family of Love, a secret group whose members outwardly obeyed whatever church their country required. They kept their real beliefs to themselves. Familists were hunted by royal proclamation in England. And because they blended in so well, historians still argue about how many there were and how long they lasted.

Documented Stories

Familists in the Yeomen of the Guard

Hendrik Niclaes’ Family of Love had reached England in the 1550s through Dutch immigrants and Cambridgeshire villages, and its books, translated and printed in the 1570s, taught that the true Christian was ‘godded with God’ in love, that Scripture was to be read spiritually, and that the Family’s members should conform outwardly to whatever church the magistrate required, since ceremonies were indifferent and persecution pointless. Puritan writers denounced it — John Rogers’s Displaying of an Horrible Sect in 1578 printed confessions extracted from Familists in Surrey — and on 3 October 1580 the queen proclaimed against the ‘damnable sect’ whose members ‘deny anything upon oath’ and ordered its books burned and its members imprisoned. The Privy Council’s letters then record an embarrassment. Several Yeomen of the Guard — the sovereign’s ceremonial bodyguard — were reported to be Familists; in the examinations that followed, the men admitted reading Niclaes’ books and meeting together, and also showed that they attended their parish churches, took communion, and had sworn the oath of supremacy. The Council could find nothing to charge them with under the law, since the law punished non-conformity and they had conformed; two were briefly imprisoned and released, and the rest kept their posts. Christopher Marsh’s study of the Cambridgeshire Familists found the same pattern in the parish records: known members serving as churchwardens, making orthodox wills, buried in the churchyard. The proclamation named a sect that could not be found because it had been built not to be.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Sebastian Franck decided that the true church had been hidden from sight since the apostles. To him, God's word spoke inwardly to people of every nation, not through any church. Hendrik Niclaes went further and built a secret group, the Family of Love. Its members outwardly obeyed whatever church their land required, but kept their real beliefs to themselves. They hid them so well that England hunted them by royal proclamation.

Legacy

Neither group lasted as an institution. Franck's writings, though, were reprinted long after his death. They found readers who wanted a Christian faith without a church to house it. His Paradoxa came out again in 1559, and an English version of another of his books in 1640. They may have reached later mystics and spiritualists, but that path is hard to trace. The Familists' habit of outward conformity hides their influence almost completely. That is exactly what they wanted. Their books were printed abroad in English for English readers, and one was reprinted in London as late as 1652. Whether they fed the next century's case for toleration cannot be shown.

Sources

Doctrinal Floor Note

These writers read the creeds' content symbolically and inwardly rather than as statements about outward, historical realities, which raises a real question about how far they held the historic faith in the sense the creeds intended. A second question hangs over the Family of Love in particular: how far it was a tradition and how far it was one man, Hendrik Niclaes. The evidence cuts both ways — English Familist households continued for decades after his death, but the movement was built around him as an inspired prophet — and neither question has a settled answer here.

How Sure the Record Is

Real texts; communities deliberately invisible

  • Sebastian Franck: Chronica (1531), Letter to Campanus (in Williams & Mergal, Spiritual and Anabaptist Writers, LCC, 1957), 280 Paradoxes (trans. Furcha) (primary)
  • Hendrik Niclaes's own writings: Evangelium Regni, Terra Pacis, epistles and ordinances (English prints of the 1570s) (primary) — Hendrik Niclaes's own prophetic self-presentation — elected 'godded man,' his writings styled as scripture. This is the founder-shaped side of the movement's own evidence.
  • The schism record: Hendrik Jansen van Barrefelt ('Hiel') and his separate following after breaking with Niclaes (c. 1573); the Plantin circle's quiet adherence (primary) — Authority here was contested and the movement survived the contest — evidence that cuts both ways: a founder-shaped rupture, but also a fellowship that outlived it.
  • The English Familists: the 1580 proclamation, member examinations and recantations, Rogers's The Displaying of an horrible secte (1578); against these, wills, parish offices, and outward conformity of known Familist families persisting decades after Niclaes's death (Marsh's evidence base) (primary) — Both hostile (the polemic and the proclamation) and coerced (the examinations and recantations). The wills and parish-conformity records are the counter-evidence, shaped by the community's own structure: it was a community without its prophet, invisible by design, the same pattern seen in other communities that survived by staying unseen.
  • Alastair Hamilton, The Family of Love (1981), with Christopher Marsh, The Family of Love in English Society, 1550-1630 (CUP, 1994) (secondary) — Standard continental and English studies. Marsh is the community-evidence base.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 6, ch. 3 (McLaughlin, the radical Reformation) (reference)
