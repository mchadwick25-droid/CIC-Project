Dioscorus of Alexandria

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Church of Alexandria and Miaphysite Egypt.

Known for
Bishop of Alexandria after Cyril who presided at the council of Ephesus in 449 that deposed Flavian of Constantinople, and was himself deposed at Chalcedon in 451, by the council's letter for disobedience and for refusing its summons three times; the churches that rejected Chalcedon kept his name.
Also called
Dioscorus, Dioscorus the Confessor
Dates
Leo of Rome wrote to him as bishop in 445, by Charles Lett Feltoe's account; deposed on 13 October 451, by the council's letter to him; died in exile at Gangra in 454, by Feltoe and by François Nau, who gives 4 September. The chronicle known as that of Zachariah of Mitylene gives him eight years and three months as bishop. No source used here gives his birth
Place
Alexandria; Ephesus; Chalcedon; Gangra in Paphlagonia
Role
Bishop of Alexandria

About This Person

At the Council of Chalcedon in 451, the minutes of Eutyches's trial were read. Dioscorus, bishop of Alexandria, broke in. The acts record him saying, "I receive 'the of two;' 'the two' I do not receive." He added, "I am forced to be impudent, but the matter is one which touches my soul."

He had followed Cyril of Alexandria as bishop. Charles Lett Feltoe, who translated Leo of Rome's letters, says Leo wrote to him in 445 to lay down church rules.

In 449, Feltoe says, Dioscorus presided at a council at Ephesus by the emperor's order. Its minutes, in S. G. F. Perry's English, give his sentence on Flavian, bishop of Constantinople, and Eusebius of Dorylaeum. He judged them "alien from all the Honor of the Priesthood and of the Episcopate." Flavian answered, "I repudiate you. I protest." Leo later called that meeting one "not of judges but of robbers."

Evagrius of Epiphania, a historian born some eighty years later, gives the charges from the council's own acts. By that record, Chalcedon convicted Dioscorus of hiding Leo's letter, and of having bishops sign a blank paper to depose Flavian. Called three times, he did not come, and the council wrote that he was deposed on the thirteenth of October. Evagrius says he was sent to live at Gangra in Paphlagonia.

The Syriac chronicle known as that of Zachariah of Mitylene, from the side that rejected Chalcedon, tells it another way. It says he was deposed for teaching that Christ became incarnate from two natures, and for refusing two after the union. Asked to sign and regain his throne, he said, "Sooner would Dioscorus see his own hand cut off, and the blood falling on the paper, than do such a thing as that." In Egypt, it says, priests, monks and many of the people broke with his successor.

A History of him goes under the name of his disciple Theopistus. Its editor, François Nau, does not vouch for many details. In it Dioscorus tells the empress Pulcheria she may take his throne of wood, but not the one the Messiah has prepared for him in heaven. Nau and Feltoe date his death in exile to 454.

The History of the Patriarchs of the Coptic Church says, "He preserved the orthodox faith, which persists in the see of the evangelist Saint Mark to this day and for ever."

Key Works

  • Sentence on Flavian and Eusebius, Ephesus 449: his judgment at the council he presided, in the minutes in S. G. F. Perry's English of the Syriac acts
  • Words at Chalcedon, Session I: "I receive 'the of two;' 'the two' I do not receive", in the extracts of the acts in the English council volume

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Feltoe writes that the "still surviving 'Jacobite' schism originated with the deposition of Dioscorus".
  • In Egypt, the chronicle known as that of Zachariah of Mitylene says, priests, monks and many of the people broke with Proterius, his successor, and "wrote his name in the book of life".
  • Zachariah says Timothy Aelurus later brought his bones back to Alexandria in a silver coffin.

How they're remembered

  • The History of the Patriarchs of the Coptic Church: "He preserved the orthodox faith, which persists in the see of the evangelist Saint Mark to this day and for ever".
  • The bishops at Chalcedon, writing to Leo of Rome: he "purposed excommunication" against Leo.
  • Leo of Rome, to the empress Pulcheria: the council of 449 was a meeting "not of judges but of robbers".

At Major Councils

  • Council of Chalcedon, 451: Accused for his conduct at Ephesus in 449; deposed and exiled

What Is Uncertain

  • His trial is told two ways. Evagrius of Epiphania, whose History runs to 594, and the acts of Chalcedon give the charges: hiding Leo's letter, a blank paper signed for Flavian's deposition, and refusing three summonses. The Syriac chronicle known as that of Zachariah of Mitylene, from the side that rejected Chalcedon, says he was deposed for his teaching, and that Anatolius of Constantinople said he was not deposed for the faith.
  • The History of him under the name of his disciple Theopistus is a panegyric. Its editor, François Nau, does not vouch for many of its details, dates its present form after 512, and reports that Assemani held it a later fiction. Nau also says Theopistus did not invent every speech.
  • The year of his death, 454, is given by Feltoe and Nau. B. Evetts's heading in the History of the Patriarchs gives his see as 444 to 458, and that History says no account of his life after his banishment was found.
  • Nau's introduction, as printed, has him succeed Cyril in 446; Feltoe has Leo writing to him in 445.
  • That Ephesus in 449 deposed Flavian on a blank paper is the charge Evagrius reports; the minutes in Perry's English give his sentence and the bishops' votes.
