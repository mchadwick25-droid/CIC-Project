The Ukrainian Greek Catholic Underground

Ukraine, 1946 to 1989 — the world's largest banned church, consecrating clergy in secret and enduring bishops sent to the Gulag until it emerged still intact in 1989.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:22

Read along

In March 1946 a synod was convened at Lviv that dissolved the Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church into the Russian Orthodox Church. Every one of the church's bishops had already been arrested, and none took part. In communist Czechoslovakia the Greek Catholic Church was liquidated between 1948 and 1950. A 'sobor' met at Prešov on 28 April 1950, in an operation called 'Action P'. For forty-three years the Ukrainian church was the largest banned church in the world. It had priests ordained in secret, liturgies in apartments and forests, and bishops in the Gulag. Metropolitan Andrey Sheptytsky's pastoral letters — among them one from 1942 headed 'Thou Shalt Not Kill' — went on being read through the underground years, and his successor Josyf Slipyj spent from 1945 to 1963 in the camps before his release. From 1984 an initiative group circulated a Chronicle of the Catholic Church in Ukraine in samizdat. In 1989 the church was legalized, and it came out with its hierarchy, its parishes and its people intact — which is not what the state had been counting on.

Documented Stories

A Council Without Bishops

In April 1945 the Soviet authorities arrested every bishop of the Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church, Metropolitan Josyf Slipyj among them. The following month an 'Initiative Group for the Reunion of the Greek Catholic Church with the Orthodox Church' announced itself, led by Havryil Kostelnyk, a respected Lviv priest, theologian and long-standing critic of Latin influence in his church, who had lost a son in the war. The group worked under the direction of the state security service, whose files, opened after 1991, record the arrangements. Priests who refused to join were arrested; some 800 signed. To give the gathering a semblance of episcopal presence, two members of the group, Mykhailo Melnyk and Antonii Pelvetsky, were secretly received into Orthodoxy and consecrated bishops in Kyiv in February 1946. On 8 to 10 March 1946, in St George's Cathedral in Lviv, 216 priests and 19 laypeople, with no bishop of their own church present, voted to annul the Union of Brest of 1596 and to join the Russian Orthodox Church. The church that had existed for three and a half centuries was declared not to exist. On 20 September 1948, after celebrating the liturgy at the Transfiguration Church in Lviv, Kostelnyk was shot dead in the street by a young man who then killed himself. The Soviet authorities blamed Ukrainian nationalists and the Vatican; the underground church suspected the security service of removing a man who knew too much. The question had not been settled by 2010.

Eighteen Years, Then a Train to Rome

Josyf Slipyj was arrested in April 1945, tried in secret and sentenced to eight years; when those ended he was sentenced again, and again, and by 1962 had spent seventeen years in camps and exile from Siberia to Mordovia, ordaining priests in secret when he could. In December 1962 Norman Cousins, the editor of the Saturday Review, visited Khrushchev as an unofficial go-between during the thaw after the Cuban missile crisis; he had been asked by John XXIII's circle to raise one specific matter, the metropolitan's release. Khrushchev agreed, on terms: Slipyj was to leave the Soviet Union and never return, and the release was to be presented as a humanitarian gesture, not a concession. On 26 January 1963 Slipyj was told he was free. Monsignor Jan Willebrands of the Secretariat for Christian Unity traveled to Moscow to collect him, and the two men went by train to Vienna, then on to Rome, arriving on 9 February. Slipyj had been permitted to bring a small suitcase; in it, among other things, was soil from the graves of his people in the camps. John XXIII received him at once. He was seventy, and he would live twenty-one more years in Rome, never returning to Lviv, holding together a banned church from outside and pressing at the Council and after it, without success, for the recognition of a Ukrainian patriarchate. His body was returned to St George's Cathedral in Lviv in 1992, when there was again a church to receive it.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In March 1946, a synod at Lviv dissolved the Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church into the Russian Orthodox Church -- with every one of its own bishops already under arrest and none present to agree to it. For the next forty-three years priests were ordained in secret and liturgies held in apartments and forests, while Metropolitan Josyf Slipyj spent from 1945 to 1963 in the camps. When the church was finally legalized in 1989, it came back with its hierarchy, parishes and people still intact.

Legacy

Legalization in 1989 ended the underground, and by 2010 the church that emerged was the largest of the Eastern Catholic churches. Its survival became a standing counter-example to the assumption that a church without buildings, seminaries, publishing or legal existence will simply fade — it did none of those things for four decades and came back whole. The record it left is unusual in having both halves: the confessors' own letters and testimony, and the archives of the agencies that hunted them, which could be read side by side once the archives opened after 1991.
