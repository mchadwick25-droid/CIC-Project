The High Medieval Era · c. 1000-1245 · Georgia

The Georgian Golden Age

Georgia, c. 1000 to 1245 — a mountain kingdom at its height, with an academy at Gelati teaching philosophy beside the liturgy, ended not by decay but by the Mongols.

Georgian Revival — Tao-Klarjeti and Athos→The Georgian Golden Age→no descendant recorded

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 0:55

Read along

For two and a half centuries Georgia was a real power in the Caucasus, and its church rose with it. David IV, called the Builder, broke the Seljuk hold at the battle of Didgori in 1121, and in 1106 had begun the monastery of Gelati, which was also an academy: philosophy, astronomy, rhetoric and Greek taught alongside the liturgy, with the Neoplatonist Ioane Petritsi among its scholars. Under his great-granddaughter Tamar the kingdom reached its widest extent, its churches were covered in frescoes, and its language was given a literature to match. The age ended not in decline but in conquest — after the Mongol invasions Georgia became a tributary state, and the death of Queen Rusudan in 1245 closes the age.

Documented Stories

David the Builder and the Muslims of Tbilisi

Tbilisi had been the seat of a Muslim emirate since the eighth century when David IV took it in 1122, a year after his victory over the Seljuk coalition at Didgori. The Georgian royal chronicle, the Life of David, records the conquest briefly and spends its energy on the king’s piety — the monastery-academy at Gelati, the penitential hymns he wrote, his habit of carrying the Gospels and reading them on campaign. Ibn al-Azraq al-Fariqi, an Arab historian from Mayyafariqin, visited Tbilisi in 1154 in the reign of David’s grandson and wrote down what the city’s Muslims told him of David’s arrangements: that he had forbidden pigs to be slaughtered in the Muslim quarters, that he had exempted Muslims from the tax on baths and from the poll tax that Christians and Jews paid, that he had built a guest-house for Muslim scholars and poets and attended Friday prayer himself, sitting to hear the sermon, and that he had had a Christian who insulted the Prophet punished. Al-Fariqi says the Muslims of Tbilisi considered themselves better treated under the Georgian kings than they had been under the emirs. Later Georgian tradition made David a saint; later Muslim tradition remembered him as a just infidel. The record also shows that David sacked the city when he took it and that the Muslim population declined steadily under his successors; what al-Fariqi describes is a policy of the conqueror, not a settlement, and it lasted a generation.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

David IV, called the Builder, broke the Seljuk hold on Georgia at the battle of Didgori in 1121 and founded the monastery of Gelati in 1106 as an academy where philosophy, astronomy and rhetoric were taught alongside the liturgy, with the Neoplatonist Ioane Petritsi among its scholars. Under his great-granddaughter Tamar the kingdom reached its widest extent, with frescoed churches and a literature of its own. The age ended not through decline but through conquest, closing with the Mongol submission and Queen Rusudan's death in 1245.

Legacy

The Georgian Orthodox Church is still the national church of Georgia, in a line running straight through this period. Gelati survives, with its twelfth-century mosaic of the Virgin and centuries of wall painting, and David the Builder is buried at its gate. What the age produced in Georgian — the chronicles, the hymnody, the translations made at Gelati, and beside them Rustaveli's great secular poem — remains the foundation of the national literature. The hymn traditionally attributed to King Demetre I, 'Thou Art a Vineyard', is still sung in Georgian churches and concert halls today.

Experience It Today

How Sure the Record Is

Moderate: chronicles, typika, hymnography, hagiography (Gelati academy)

  • Kartlis Tskhovreba (the Georgian Chronicles): Robert W. Thomson, Rewriting Caucasian History (Clarendon, 1996) (primary) — Annotated English translation of the medieval corpus via the 13th-century Armenian adaptation, which reaches only to c. 1125 - it does not cover the later Golden Age. A complete English translation of the Georgian corpus (ed. S. Jones, Tbilisi, 2014) also exists.
  • Tamara Grdzelidze (trans.), Georgian Monks on Mount Athos: Two Eleventh-Century Lives of the Hegoumenoi of Iviron (Bennett & Bloom, 2009) (primary) — The hagiography in the movement's own voice: George the Hagiorite's Life of John and Euthymios, and the Life of George.
  • Typikon of Gregory Pakourianos for Petritsoni/Bachkovo (1083), in Byzantine Monastic Foundation Documents (Dumbarton Oaks, 2000; free online) (primary) — A Georgian grandee's monastic foundation, with its Georgians-only clause, available in English.
  • Hymnography and the Gelati world: 'Shen khar venakhi' ('Thou art a Vineyard', attrib. King Demetre I); Gelati academy translations (Ioane Petritsi); Gelati mosaics (primary) — Available mainly in specialist and Georgian-language editions; some attributions are traditional rather than firmly documented.
  • Antony Eastmond, Royal Imagery in Medieval Georgia (Penn State, 1998) (secondary) — Standard English analysis of royal-sanctity ideology (Davit the Builder to Tamar) through monuments - material culture doing work the chronicles cannot.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 5, ch. 1 'The Byzantine commonwealth 1000-1500' (Shepard) (reference) — No chapter is dedicated specifically to Georgia; this chapter is the nearest anchor, though its coverage of Georgia specifically has not been confirmed page by page.
