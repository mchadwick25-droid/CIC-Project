Oneness Pentecostalism

North America and then worldwide, 1913 to 2010 — a Pentecostal current inseparable from Azusa Street's own aftermath, distinguished by the Jesus'-name baptismal practice and modalist confession its own named bodies stated in their own published statements of faith.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:34

Read along

In April 1913, at the Arroyo Seco camp meeting, R. E. McAlister preached on baptism. He noted that the baptisms in the book of Acts are done in the name of Jesus. The next year Frank Ewart and Glenn Cook baptized each other on that basis. The question tore through the young Pentecostal movement. In October 1916 the Assemblies of God adopted its Statement of Fundamental Truths, a plainly trinitarian creed. The ministers who could not sign it left. Several bodies followed. The oldest is the Pentecostal Assemblies of the World, Oneness from 1916, and G. T. Haywood was among its leaders. For decades it had Black and white members together, while other Pentecostal bodies split by race. Its own telling includes the splits of 1924 and the part race played in the merger of 1945. The United Pentecostal Church International was formed by a merger vote on 25 September 1945. It printed its Articles of Faith in a Manual each year. In Mexico the Apostolic revival began in 1914. Romanita Carbajal de Valenzuela had become a Pentecostal in Los Angeles. That year she went home to Villa Aldama in Chihuahua. The Iglesia Apostólica de la Fe en Cristo Jesús grew from it.

Documented Stories

St Louis, October 1916

The new-issue teaching, that baptism was in the name of Jesus Christ and that the one God was Jesus, had spread through the movement since the Arroyo Seco camp meeting of 1913, and by 1915 a large part of the Assemblies of God, founded in 1914 with a declaration against creeds and for the Bible alone, had accepted it, including for a time its chairman. The General Council of 1915 had resolved that the question should not divide. It divided. The fourth General Council met at the Bethel Chapel in St Louis from 1 to 7 October 1916, and the trinitarian party, led by J. Roswell Flower and E. N. Bell, brought forward a Statement of Fundamental Truths in sixteen articles, of which the longest by far set out the Trinity in language drawn from the creeds the council had renounced two years before, and required the baptismal formula of Matthew 28. The Oneness ministers protested that the council was doing what it had promised never to do; the council adopted the Statement and required all ministers to endorse it. One hundred and fifty-six of the five hundred and eighty-five ministers on the roll refused and were removed or withdrew, among them G. T. Haywood of Indianapolis and Frank Ewart. Within two years they had merged into the Pentecostal Assemblies of the World. Both sides kept the record; the Assemblies of God's Statement remained its confession, and Ewart's history of 1947 is the other half.

The White Ministers Leave the Pentecostal Assemblies of the World

The Pentecostal Assemblies of the World was led from Indianapolis by Garfield Thomas Haywood, a Black former foundry worker whose Christ Temple drew thousands of both races, whose hymns, I See a Crimson Stream of Blood among them, were the movement's theology in its most read form, and whose ordination was accepted by white ministers, most of them from the South. It was the only Pentecostal body in the United States in which Black and white ministers sat on one board and met in one convention; the conventions were held in the North so that Black ministers could attend. The arrangement did not hold. White ministers complained that they could not hold conventions in the South under a Black chairman, that white candidates would not be ordained by Black hands, and that growth in the South required white leadership; a compromise of 1922 did not satisfy them. In 1924 the majority of the white ministers withdrew and formed bodies of their own, chief among them the Pentecostal Ministerial Alliance, on the same doctrine and without the Black members; the Pentecostal Assemblies of the World continued under Haywood, presiding bishop from 1925 until his death in 1931, as a largely Black body with a white minority that stayed. In September 1945 the two white Oneness bodies merged at St Louis to form the United Pentecostal Church, the largest Oneness denomination; the racial question had been settled by separate existence. Both bodies tell the story in their histories, at different lengths.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Oneness Pentecostalism grew out of the wider Pentecostal movement that came after the Azusa Street revival. Two other churches share those roots. One is the True Jesus Church, founded in Beijing in 1917. The other is La Luz del Mundo in Mexico. Its founder came to faith among Oneness believers there in the 1920s.

Descended from

  • grew out of Azusa Street & Early Pentecostalism Widely Accepted Major — In 1914 Pentecostal ministers from many missions and churches formed a general council. By 1916 they were arguing over the words to say at baptism. The council told them not to fight over it. Historians trace the Oneness movement to this quarrel.

Direct descendants

  • gave rise to The Way International; Unification Church; La Luz del Mundo Dominant Modern Reconstruction Major — Scholars trace La Luz del Mundo to Oneness Pentecostal believers in northern Mexico. Its founder, Eusebio Joaquín González, met them through his wife. He was baptized in late 1925. The church does not call itself Pentecostal.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In April 1913, at the Arroyo Seco camp meeting, R. E. McAlister preached that the baptisms recorded in Acts were performed in the name of Jesus alone, and the following year Frank Ewart and Glenn Cook baptized each other on that basis. When the Assemblies of God adopted an explicitly trinitarian statement of faith in 1916, the ministers who could not sign it left and formed their own bodies, among them the interracial Pentecostal Assemblies of the World, long led by G. T. Haywood. Baptism in the name of Jesus alone was what a visitor to these churches noticed first.

Legacy

By 2010 these were living churches with congregations worldwide. Their mark, baptism in the name of Jesus, was what a visitor noticed first. Their size was hard to fix. The figures in use were the bodies' own, with no outside count to check them. Two other churches share these roots. The True Jesus Church was founded in Beijing in 1917. The founder of La Luz del Mundo in Mexico came to faith among Oneness believers.

Sources

On the Nicene Creed

The formation record is well documented: the April 1913 Arroyo Seco camp meeting and a baptismal sermon preached there, the rebaptisms that followed in 1914, and the Assemblies of God's 1916 Statement of Fundamental Truths written specifically to exclude this position. Oneness theology holds that Father, Son, and Spirit are one person under three titles or manifestations, rather than three persons in one being — which the Nicene Creed's own language was written to affirm.
