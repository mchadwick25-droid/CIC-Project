British and Welsh Christianity

Wales, western Britain, Cornwall and Brittany, c. 451 to 768 CE — the old British church, pushed west by Saxon settlement, which declined to recognize Rome's missionary and kept its own Easter for another century and a half.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:18

Read along

Britain had been Christian under Roman rule, and when the province fell away and Anglo-Saxon settlers took the east, that church did not vanish — it survived in the west: in Wales, Cornwall, the north-west, and across the sea in Brittany, which British emigrants gave its name. Its own record is thin and largely furious. Gildas, writing around the 540s, delivered a book-length denunciation of British kings and clergy for the sins he believed had brought the Saxon catastrophe on them. Its life ran through monastic schools and founder-saints whose names still sit on Welsh parishes — David, Illtud, Teilo, Cadoc — and through inscribed memorial stones that are among the few hard traces of the period. When Augustine of Canterbury arrived from Rome and met British bishops around 603, they declined to accept his authority or to join his mission to the Saxons who had displaced them; they were not at the synod of Whitby in 664 either. The Welsh church came round to the Roman dating of Easter on its own, around 768.

Documented Stories

The Hermit’s Test at Augustine’s Oak

Bede, who had the story from the English side a century later, says Augustine of Canterbury met the bishops of the Britons twice. At the first conference, under an oak on the border of the West Saxons, he asked them to adopt the Roman Easter, the Roman baptism and to join his mission to the English; they refused to abandon their customs without consulting their own people; a blind Englishman was, Bede says, healed by Augustine’s prayers and not by theirs; and a second meeting was fixed. Before it the Britons — seven bishops and many learned men, chiefly from the monastery of Bangor-is-Coed, where Bede says two thousand monks lived by their own labor — went to a hermit and asked whether they should give up their traditions at Augustine’s preaching. He said: if he is a man of God, follow him. How shall we know? ‘If he is meek and lowly of heart, he bears the yoke of Christ. Arrange that he arrives first at the meeting place; if he rises to greet you when you come, hear him; if he does not rise, despise him.’ Augustine, seated, did not rise. Read from the other side, it is the record of a conquered people’s church declining to evangelize its conquerors, told by the conquerors’ historian, who kept the hermit’s test and the unrisen chair in the story anyway.

Gildas Names the Dragon of the Island

Gildas’ On the Ruin of Britain is a sermon addressed to a whole people, arguing that the Saxon conquest was God’s judgment on British sin, and its central section addresses five living kings by name. The longest indictment is of Maglocunus — Maelgwn of Gwynedd — ‘dragon of the island’, ‘first in evil, mightier than many both in power and in malice’. Gildas gives his career as an accusation. As a young man he had killed his uncle the king with his own troops. He had then repented, publicly and dramatically, entering a monastery and taking the vow — Gildas says the whole island rejoiced — and had left it again, ‘like a dog returning to its vomit’, to seize the throne. He had put away his wife and murdered his nephew in order to marry the nephew’s widow. And he presided, Gildas says, over a court where bards sang his praises ‘with foaming mouths’ instead of the praise of God. Then the preacher turns pastoral: Maelgwn had had the best teacher in Britain — Gildas means Illtud — and knew better; he was still alive and could still repent; God had spared him this long for that. Maelgwn died of the plague around 547, and later Welsh tradition made him a patron of saints. Gildas’ chapter is the reason the tradition’s own founding century is known as much for its kings’ crimes as for its monasteries.

Connections

In Its Own Record

The older church of Roman Britain, pushed west when the Anglo-Saxons invaded. Like the Irish church, it kept its own customs, which the Roman mission in Kent called errors. The Irish monk Columbanus cited its writer Gildas. It met Augustine's mission around 603 and refused to submit to him, and it kept its own Easter long after Whitby (664).

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced

  • influenced Insular / Irish Monastic Christianity Documented — Around 600 the Irish monk Columbanus wrote a letter to Pope Gregory the Great. In it he cited Gildas, the British monk and writer. He said that a writer named Vennianus had asked Gildas about monks who left their monastery. Gildas, he said, had given him a most elegant reply.
  • influenced Anglo-Saxon Christianity Documented — In his history of the English church, Bede cites Gildas as the Britons' own historian for their sins after the Romans left. Bede then adds a charge of his own: that the Britons never preached the faith to the English.

Opposed By

  • argued against Anglo-Saxon Christianity Documented — Laurentius, who followed Augustine, wrote to the British clergy and urged them into Catholic unity. Later, Bede reports, Aldhelm wrote a book against the way they fixed the date of Easter. Bede himself complained about the Britons. They would not share the faith with the English, he said, yet still held to their errors.

In Contact With

  • in contact with Anglo-Saxon Christianity Documented — Bede tells of two meetings around 603 between Augustine of Canterbury and the British bishops. The first was at Augustine's Oak. Pope Gregory the Great had put the British bishops under Augustine, but at the second meeting they would not take him as their archbishop. Bede wrote this more than a century later.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

When Anglo-Saxon settlers took eastern Britain, the older British church survived in the west, in Wales, Cornwall and Brittany, running through monastic schools and founder-saints like David, Illtud and Teilo. Its monk Gildas delivered a furious denunciation of British kings around the 540s, and its bishops refused to recognize Augustine of Canterbury's authority in 603, keeping their own dating of Easter until the Welsh church adopted Rome's calendar on its own around 768.

Legacy

There is no separately continuing British church. The Welsh church carried on under its own bishops after 768, and was gradually drawn into the wider Western church. What it left is written across the landscape. The llan- place names of Wales mark its churchyards, and its founder-saints are still the dedications of Welsh and Cornish parishes. Its inscribed memorial stones survive in numbers. Gildas's account, for all its fury, reached Bede, who cited him as the Britons' own historian. And Brittany, settled from Britain in these centuries, kept both a Celtic language and a cluster of British saints' cults that are still there.

Experience It Today
