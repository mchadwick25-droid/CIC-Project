The Age of Monks and Empires · c. 451-768 · Wales, W. Britain, Cornwall, Brittany

Wales · W. Britain, today in United Kingdom and beyond

British and Welsh Christianity

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Britain had been Christian under Roman rule, and when the province fell away and Anglo-Saxon settlers took the east, that church did not vanish — it survived in the west: in Wales, Cornwall, the north-west, and across the sea in Brittany, which British emigrants gave its name. Its own record is thin and largely furious. Gildas, writing around the 540s, delivered a book-length denunciation of British kings and clergy for the sins he believed had brought the Saxon catastrophe on them. Its life ran through monastic schools and founder-saints whose names still sit on Welsh parishes — David, Illtud, Teilo, Cadoc — and through inscribed memorial stones that are among the few hard traces of the period. When Augustine of Canterbury arrived from Rome and met British bishops around 603, they declined to accept his authority or to join his mission to the Saxons who had displaced them; they were not at the synod of Whitby in 664 either. The Welsh church came round to the Roman dating of Easter on its own, around 768.

In Its Own Record

Continued As

  • Medieval Welsh Christianity Documented — The Welsh annals record that in 768 the Britons changed their Easter, guided by Elbodug, a man of God. The same annals record his death in 809 as archbishop in Gwynedd. The annals were compiled in the tenth century from older entries.

Influenced

  • Insular / Irish Monastic Christianity Documented — Around 600 the Irish monk Columbanus wrote a letter to Pope Gregory. In it he cited Gildas, the British monk and writer. He said that a writer named Vennianus had asked Gildas about monks who left their monastery. Gildas, he said, had given him a most elegant reply.
  • Anglo-Saxon Christianity Documented — In his history of the English church, Bede cites Gildas as the Britons' own historian for their sins after the Romans left. Bede then adds a charge of his own: that the Britons never preached the faith to the English.

Opposed By

  • Anglo-Saxon Christianity Documented — Laurentius, who followed Augustine, wrote to the British clergy and urged them into Catholic unity. Later, Bede reports, Aldhelm wrote a book against the way they fixed the date of Easter. Bede himself complained about the Britons. They would not share the faith with the English, he said, yet still held to their errors.

In Contact With

  • Anglo-Saxon Christianity Documented — Bede tells of two meetings around 603 between Augustine of Canterbury and the British bishops. The first was at Augustine's Oak. Pope Gregory had put the British bishops under Augustine, but at the second meeting they would not take him as their archbishop. Bede wrote this more than a century later.
