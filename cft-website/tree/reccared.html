The Age of Monks and Empires · king 586-601 · Toledo

Reccared

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Nicene Visigothic Iberia.

Known for
King of the Visigoths in Spain who became Catholic early in his reign and set his written confession of faith before the bishops at the Third Council of Toledo, where the Gothic bishops renounced the teaching of Arius.
Also called
Recaredus, Reccaredus, Rechared, Recared
Dates
Took the throne on the death of his father Leovigild in Isidore of Seville's era 624, which Theodor Mommsen, Bruno Krusch, O. M. Dalton and Edmund Gardner give as 586. He died after fifteen years; Isidore dates his son Liuva's accession to the era 639, 601 in Mommsen's dates. The council's acts date it to 8 May, era 627, in his fourth year, which James Barmby and Henry Percival give as 589. No source used here gives his birth year
Place
Toledo, his royal city, where he died; Narbonese Gaul, where he fought the Franks in his father's time
Role
King of the Visigoths in Spain

About This Person

King Reccared came among the bishops gathered at Toledo and prayed with them, the council's acts say. Not many days after his father's death, he reminded them, they had learned he had joined the Catholic faith. All he had to say of his faith was written in a book, to be read before them. The king offered it, and a notary read it out in a clear voice.

Reccared was king of the Visigoths in Spain, the son of King Leovigild. The acts date this Third Council of Toledo to the eighth of May in his fourth year. Their Latin is told here in modern words.

The book speaks as the king, though O. M. Dalton, translating Gregory of Tours, says Bishop Leander of Seville composed the speech. God had given him many peoples to rule, it says, yet he remembered he was mortal. It confesses Father, Son and Spirit as one substance.

God stirred the king, burning with faith, to call back a people who served error under the name of religion. The whole Gothic people was there, it says. A great host of Sueves, brought under his kingdom, had also been called back. He offered these noble peoples to God through the bishops' hands, as a holy sacrifice. It condemns Arius and all his teaching. Then Reccared signed, holding this faith in his heart, he wrote, and affirming it with his mouth.

All the witnesses here write from the Catholic side. John of Biclaro, a chronicler of the time, had been exiled by Leovigild. He says Reccared took the throne in peace. In the tenth month of his first year, John says, Reccared became Catholic. Gregory of Tours, a Frankish bishop, tells instead of a debate between the bishops of both sides. Pope Gregory the Great credits Leander's counsel and the merits of Reccared's brother Hermenegild, whom Gregory calls a martyr.

Gregory wrote to Reccared, "I cannot express in words, most excellent son, how much I am delighted with thy work and thy life." Jews offered him money over a law against them, Gregory had heard. Reccared "preferred innocence to gold".

Isidore of Seville, Leander's brother, wrote that Leovigild was irreligious and quick to war, and his son pious in faith and famed for peace. At the end Reccared made a public confession of penance, and died peacefully at Toledo after reigning fifteen years.

Key Works

  • Book of faith (tomus) read at the Third Council of Toledo, with his signature: a confession in the king's voice, read out by a notary: the Father, Son and Holy Spirit of one substance, the Spirit proceeding from the Father and the Son, the four councils received, Arius condemned, and the Goths and Sueves offered to God
  • Edict confirming the council: signed Flavius Recaredus, ordering that no one despise or pass over what the council decided

Legacy

What traces to them

  • John of Biclaro says he brought the Arian clergy over by wise talk, by reason rather than command, and called the Goths and Sueves back to the unity of the church. Isidore of Seville says he called the Gothic people back to the right faith at the start of his reign.
  • At the council, Henry Percival's English of its acts reads, the bishops enacted with the king's advice that the creed of Constantinople be recited in all the churches of Spain and Gallaecia after the form of the Eastern church.
  • John says he gave back what his predecessors had seized and founded and endowed churches and monasteries.

How they're remembered

  • John of Biclaro likens him at the council to Constantine at Nicaea and the emperor Marcian at Chalcedon.
  • Isidore of Seville: his father was irreligious and quick to war, the son pious in faith and famed for peace; he was gentle and kind, and drew even the wicked to love him.
  • Pope Gregory the Great calls the Goths' conversion through him a new miracle in our days.

At Major Councils

  • Third Council of Toledo, 589: King; called the council, offered his book of faith and confirmed the canons by edict

What Is Uncertain

  • Every witness used here writes from the Catholic side. John of Biclaro, a Goth, had suffered exile under Leovigild for the Catholic faith, as Isidore and Mommsen report; Isidore was Leander's brother. No account from the Arian side is used here.
  • Whose words the book of faith is. The acts set it in the king's voice and say a notary read it aloud. O. M. Dalton says Leander of Seville composed his speech at the council, citing Labbe's Concilia.
  • How and when he became Catholic. Reccared told the council it was not many days after his father's death. John of Biclaro gives the tenth month of his first year. Gregory of Tours tells of a debate between the bishops of both sides, after which the king received chrism. Fredegar, a later Frankish chronicler, says he was first baptized in secret, then had the Arian books burned at Toledo and all the Goths baptized. Gregory the Great credits Leander's counsel and Hermenegild's merits. James Barmby, Edmund Gardner and O. M. Dalton give 587.
  • Whether Leovigild entrusted him to Leander. Gregory the Great says so. O. M. Dalton reports Altamira's view that no record of the time proves it.
  • His brother Hermenegild's end. John of Biclaro says Sisbert killed him at Tarragona. Gregory of Tours says his father killed him. Gregory the Great says Leovigild had him killed in prison for refusing the Arian communion; Dalton calls that account fictitious.
  • The opposition. John of Biclaro names the Arian bishop Sunna and one Segga (Siuma and Segga in Mommsen's text), and later Bishop Uldila and Queen Gosuintha, his father's widow, as plotters against him. Gregory of Tours says Reccared recognized Gosuintha as his mother.
  • A letter in his name to Gregory the Great survives in the Spanish canon collection. James Barmby says its genuineness is considered doubtful, and that it may be a forgery based on Gregory's letter to Reccared; he adds that its rough Latin is what Reccared's might well have been. Ludwig M. Hartmann, who edited Gregory's letters after Paul Ewald's death, reports that Gams judged it false and Mommsen agreed. Hartmann answers that the priest Probinus carried it, beyond doubt, and thinks it badly preserved and corrupt in many places.
  • His queen. Baddo signs the council's acts after him as glorious queen. Isidore says the mother of his son Liuva was of humble birth, and does not name her.
  • The years. Isidore gives eras and John gives regnal years; every year in AD is a modern reckoning. A note signed Perez in Migne's Isidore gives 600 for his death and 601 for Liuva's accession.
