The Old Catholic Churches — Utrecht to the Union

The Netherlands, Germany and Switzerland, 1815 to 1906 — Catholics who could not accept the pope's 1870 infallibility, and an old Dutch church that had bishops to spare.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:09

Read along

There had been a small church at Utrecht out of communion with Rome since an eighteenth-century quarrel over Jansenism left it electing its own archbishops. It stayed a Dutch curiosity until 1870, when the First Vatican Council defined papal infallibility and a body of German, Swiss and Austrian Catholics — professors, priests and laypeople, with the church historian Ignaz von Dollinger at their head — refused to accept it. Excommunicated, they needed bishops, and Utrecht had them: Joseph Hubert Reinkens was consecrated for the German Old Catholics in 1873 by a bishop of that line. In 1889 five bishops signed the Declaration of Utrecht, binding these churches into a union that professed the faith of the undivided church of the first thousand years and rejected the papal claims of 1870. And then went on to drop compulsory celibacy for clergy and to put the liturgy into the language of the people.

Documented Stories

'As a Christian, as a Theologian, as a Historian, as a Citizen'

Ignaz von Döllinger had taught church history at Munich for nearly half a century and had been the most respected Catholic scholar in Germany. He had opposed the definition of infallibility before the council, under the name Janus, on the ground that history did not support it, and after 18 July 1870 the archbishop of Munich required him to submit. On 28 March 1871 Döllinger sent his answer, and published it. He could not accept the doctrine, he wrote, as a Christian, because it contradicted Scripture; as a theologian, because the whole tradition stood against it; as a historian, because he knew the record and it did not contain it; and as a citizen, because it would set the church against every modern state. He asked for a hearing and was refused. On 17 April 1871 he was excommunicated. The professors and priests who shared his refusal met at Munich in September 1871 and organized the Old Catholic movement, and he presided at the meeting. But he would not join the church they founded or attend its worship; he held that he had been unjustly excluded from the Catholic Church and remained a member of it in his own understanding, saying Mass for no one, receiving no sacrament, for nineteen years. He died in January 1890, aged ninety, still excommunicate, and was buried without the church's rites by a Catholic priest who defied his archbishop to do it. The movement he would not join keeps his letter as its charter.

The Bonn Conferences

The Old Catholics, professing the faith of the undivided church, had to decide with whom they were in communion, and Döllinger proposed to find out. He invited theologians of the churches to Bonn in September 1874, and some forty came: Old Catholics, Anglicans including Canon Liddon of St Paul's, Orthodox including the Russian archpriest Janyshev, and Lutheran observers. Döllinger presided, in German and English, and drove the meeting through a list of propositions: that the Vulgate had no authority over the originals, that the eucharist was a sacrifice in the sense of the ancient liturgies, that invocation of saints was not obligatory, that the Filioque had been inserted into the creed unlawfully; and the assembly accepted them, with reservations recorded. The second conference in August 1875, with more Orthodox present, took up the Filioque itself and, after days in which Döllinger expounded the fathers from memory, agreed a set of six theses on the procession of the Spirit from the Father through the Son that both East and West could sign. The Anglicans and the Orthodox went home; the Anglican evangelicals attacked Liddon, the Russian Synod did nothing, and a third conference was never held. The Old Catholics established intercommunion with the Church of England in 1931 by an agreement on the principle the conferences had stated, that each church recognized the other's catholicity without requiring acceptance of all its opinions. The 1875 theses on the Filioque are still cited in the dialogues.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

When German, Swiss and Austrian Catholics refused the pope's new claim to infallibility in 1870, an old Dutch church at Utrecht, out of communion with Rome since an eighteenth-century dispute but still holding valid bishops, consecrated one of their own. The five bishops who signed the 1889 Declaration of Utrecht professed the faith of the undivided church of the first thousand years, then dropped required celibacy for priests and put the liturgy into the language of the people.

Legacy

The Union of Utrecht is still there. It is a small family of national churches in the Netherlands, Germany, Switzerland, Austria, Poland and the Czech lands. Its most lasting work turned out to be ecumenical. In 1874 and 1875 Dollinger called meetings at Bonn that brought Old Catholic, Anglican and Orthodox theologians to one table. Their theses were a first attempt to set down the faith all three held in common. In 1931 the Old Catholics and the Church of England entered full communion by the Bonn Agreement.
