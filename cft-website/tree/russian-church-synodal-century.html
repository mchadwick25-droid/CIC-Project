The Missionary Era · 1815-1906 · Russia

The Russian Church — the Synodal Century

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

For this whole century the Russian Orthodox Church had no patriarch. It was governed by a Holy Synod whose chief lay officer, the ober-procurator, answered to the tsar — an arrangement that produced both an immense institution and a cramped one. Metropolitan Filaret of Moscow gave Russia its standard catechism and pressed for decades for a Bible in modern Russian, finished in 1876 over long resistance. Missions went out to the Altai peoples, to Alaska and to Japan. Monasteries and their elders drew crowds, and a parish priest, John of Kronstadt, became the most sought-after confessor in the empire. But under Konstantin Pobedonostsev, ober-procurator for a quarter-century, the church was also the instrument of a policy of religious uniformity that pressed hard on Old Believers, Eastern Catholics and evangelicals alike. When toleration was finally decreed in 1905, the church immediately began preparing the council that would give it back its own head.

The church also governed Georgia. In 1811 the Georgian catholicate ended. Ioselian, a Georgian historian in Malan's English, says the head of the Georgian clergy then took the title "Member of the Synod, and Exarch of Georgia". The first to hold it was Archbishop Balaam. Ioselian calls him a Georgian of a princely family. A Georgian appeal calls him a Russian. In 1814 the emperor gave Balaam the exarchate of Imereti as well. In December 1818 one exarchy was set up for all Georgia. The same appeal says that Georgia's church lost its rights against canon law and that its property was seized. The Georgian church's later story is told in the entry on the restored Georgian church.

Missions went east too. Blackmore put Mouravieff's history into English in 1842. In its preface he wrote of the "marked success" of the missions in Siberia.

In Its Own Record

Gave Rise To

  • The Optina Elders & Russian Ascetic Renewal Documented Major — Optina was a monastery of the Russian Church in the diocese of Kaluga. An Optina book of 1847 says the monk Leonid moved into its hermitage in 1829. The abbot gave him the care of the brothers' souls, and soon nobles, merchants and common people came to him.
  • Japanese Christianity — the Hidden Christians’ Emergence & the Meiji Church Dominant Modern Reconstruction Major — In 1861 the Russian Church sent the monk Nikolai to Hakodate in Japan. He built a Japanese Orthodox church, served mostly by Japanese priests, one of three kinds of Christian church in Meiji Japan. This rests on modern histories.
  • The Alaskan Mission — Herman’s Decades & Veniaminov Documented Major — In 1842 an English chaplain in Russia wrote that the Russian church had just set up a new diocese in the Aleutian Islands. Its first bishop was 'Veniamineff', a missionary who had long worked there. The same book lists a new see for the Aleutians, Kamchatka and Russian America.

Continues

  • The Russian Church — Nikon to the Holy Synod Documented Major — The Holy Synod that Peter the Great set up in 1721 still ran the church a century later. An account of the church in 1830 says the Synod still worked under Peter's law of 1721, the Spiritual Regulation. Andrei Mouravieff, who wrote his history in 1838, was an official of the same Synod.
  • The Georgian Church — Between Persia, the Ottomans and Russia Widely Accepted Major — The Georgian church kept its catholicos until 1811. Then the title ended, and the head of the Georgian clergy became Exarch of Georgia and a member of the Russian Synod. A Georgian appeal to the patriarch of Constantinople calls this the loss of the church's rights. The Georgian church went on as an exarchate inside the Russian church.

Continued As

  • The Russian Orthodox Church — Council, Patriarchate and Reunion Widely Accepted Major — On 4 November 1917 a council of the Russian Orthodox Church ruled that the patriarchate was restored. A patriarch would again lead the church. In December the council ruled that he would chair its Holy Synod.
  • The Georgian Orthodox Church Restored Widely Accepted Major — The Georgian church that the Russian Synod governed through an exarch from 1811 is the church that restored its own head in 1917. It is the exarchate line, not the Russian church as a whole, that carries on here. By 1906 it was pressing for its old rights, in an appeal printed by Dowling.

Influenced By

  • The Kollyvades & Greek Renewal Widely Accepted — In 1782 the Kollyvades printed the Philokalia, a Greek book on prayer, and Paisius Velichkovsky put it into Slavonic. A book of 1847 from Optina says the Synod's press in Moscow first printed it. Then, through the work of Philaret of Moscow, it was printed there again.

Argued Against

  • Russian Evangelicals — Stundists to the Baptist Union Dominant Modern Reconstruction — Orthodox clergy wrote books against the Stundists by name. Conybeare's 1921 survey cites a priest's 1889 study of Stundism in South Russia. He also cites a missionary's book, a Refutation of the Errors of Stundism, printed at Kiev in 1890. This rests on his report of the two books.
  • The Ruthenian Greek Catholic Church — Polotsk to Galicia Documented Major — In 1839 the Holy Synod printed an account of the Greek Catholics of its western lands. It called their union with Rome 'the schism'. The Synod received their bishops, clergy and people into the Russian Church, and the emperor confirmed it that March.Lasting result: In 1839 the Russian Holy Synod took the Greek Catholic bishops, priests and people of Russia's western lands into its church. The emperor confirmed it.
  • The Old Believers After 1815 Documented — Notes to an English history of the Russian Church, printed in 1842, call the Old Believers 'the Schism'. They count 19,833 Russian dissenters who joined the state church in 1839. Conybeare says Nicholas I tried to force the Old Believers into the state church from 1827. He says Russian Orthodox writers kept denying that the Belaya Krinitsa orders were valid.

Broke With

  • Russian Evangelicals — Stundists to the Baptist Union Dominant Modern Reconstruction Major — The first Stundists still took communion in Orthodox churches, F. C. Conybeare wrote in 1921. Only about 1870 did they form a separate sect, and they baptized again anyone who joined them from Orthodoxy. In 1880 the historian Julian Pelesz listed the Stundists among the new sects that had left the Russian church.

In Contact With

  • The Old Catholic Churches — Utrecht to the Union Documented — Russian Orthodox members sat at the Bonn conference Döllinger called in 1874. Among them was the head of the church's academy at St Petersburg. On one article, the Greek and Russian members held back their consent until they could look into it further.
  • The Church of the East & the Chaldean Consolidation Widely Accepted Major — Wigram writes that in 1896 the part of the church living in Persia submitted to a Russian Orthodox mission. They hoped it would bring them protection.Lasting result: In 1896, Wigram says, the church in Persia put itself under a Russian mission.
