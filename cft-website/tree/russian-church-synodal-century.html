The Russian Church — the Synodal Century

Russia, 1815 to 1906 — a church with no patriarch, run by a lay official of the tsar, that produced a Bible, a missionary empire and a famous parish priest while waiting a century to govern itself again.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 2:19

Read along

For this whole century the Russian Orthodox Church had no patriarch. It was governed by a Holy Synod whose chief lay officer, the ober-procurator, answered to the tsar — an arrangement that produced both an immense institution and a cramped one. Metropolitan Filaret of Moscow gave Russia its standard catechism and pressed for decades for a Bible in modern Russian, finished in 1876 over long resistance. Missions went out to the Altai peoples, to Alaska and to Japan. Monasteries and their elders drew crowds, and a parish priest, John of Kronstadt, became the most sought-after confessor in the empire. But under Konstantin Pobedonostsev, ober-procurator for a quarter-century, the church was also the instrument of a policy of religious uniformity that pressed hard on Old Believers, Eastern Catholics and evangelicals alike. When toleration was finally decreed in 1905, the church immediately began preparing the council that would give it back its own head.

The church also governed Georgia. In 1811 the Georgian catholicate ended. Ioselian, a Georgian historian in Malan's English, says the head of the Georgian clergy then took the title "Member of the Synod, and Exarch of Georgia". The first to hold it was Archbishop Balaam. Ioselian calls him a Georgian of a princely family. A Georgian appeal calls him a Russian. In 1814 the emperor gave Balaam the exarchate of Imereti as well. In December 1818 one exarchy was set up for all Georgia. The same appeal says that Georgia's church lost its rights against canon law and that its property was seized. The Georgian church's later story is told in the entry on the restored Georgian church.

Missions went east too. Blackmore put Mouravieff's history into English in 1842. In its preface he wrote of the "marked success" of the missions in Siberia.

People and Stories

Voices in the Record

  • Filaret (Drozdov) of Moscow — metropolitan for nearly half a century, author of the church's catechism and champion of the Russian Bible
  • Makary (Glukharev) — founder of the Altai mission, who translated Scripture into the languages of Siberian peoples
  • Nikolai (Kasatkin) of Japan — sent to Hakodate in 1861, he built a Japanese Orthodox church staffed by Japanese clergy
  • John of Kronstadt — the parish priest whose eucharistic preaching and charity drew the whole empire to a naval town
  • Konstantin Pobedonostsev — the lay ober-procurator whose long tenure defined the era's policy of religious uniformity
  • Balaam — the first exarch of Georgia, named when the Georgian catholicate ended; sources disagree on whether he was Georgian or Russian

Documented Stories

The Pentateuch Burned at the Brickworks

The Russian Bible Society, founded in 1813 under the patronage of Alexander I and his minister Golitsyn, had by 1823 printed the New Testament in Russian, a language in which no Scripture had ever been printed, since the church read ninth-century Slavonic, and had begun the Old Testament with a Pentateuch supervised by Filaret Drozdov, the young archbishop of Moscow. Its enemies gathered. Admiral Shishkov held that a Bible in the vulgar tongue would breed heresy and revolution; the archimandrite Photius denounced the Society as a Masonic plot; and Metropolitan Seraphim, its president, turned against it. Golitsyn fell in 1824. In 1825 the printed sheets of the Pentateuch, tens of thousands of copies, were taken to the brickworks of the Alexander Nevsky Lavra and burned in the kilns, on the Synod's authority, and in 1826 Nicholas I suspended the Society. Filaret, who had been made metropolitan of Moscow in 1826, said nothing in public and went on. He published his catechism, taught two generations of bishops, and in 1856 moved in the Synod that the translation be resumed; the Synod agreed in 1858. The New Testament appeared in 1862, the Psalter in 1868, and the whole Bible, the Synodal translation, in 1876, nine years after Filaret's death. It remains the Bible of Russian-speaking Christians of every confession. The men who burned the Pentateuch had held that the people could not be trusted with it; the people bought the Synodal Bible by the hundred thousand.

The House of Industry at Kronstadt

John Sergiev had been a priest at Kronstadt, the naval fortress off St Petersburg, since 1855, and had made himself known first in its slums: he went into the cellars where the dockers and the drunk lived, gave them what he had, and came home, his wife recorded, without his boots. The clergy thought him eccentric and the town thought him a fool. In the 1870s his reputation as a confessor whose prayers healed drew the sick from across the empire, and with them money, thousands of roubles a day in the end, which he gave away by nightfall. He had concluded that giving was not enough, and in 1872 he began to appeal for a house of industry where the poor could work for wages, learn a trade, eat and sleep. On 12 October 1882 the House of Industry opened, built by public subscription and his own gifts: workshops that paid piece rates, a free school, a canteen that fed eight hundred a day for a few kopecks or nothing, a night shelter, a dispensary and a church. It housed thousands over the following decades and was copied in other towns. He went on saying the liturgy daily to a packed cathedral, hearing general confessions of thousands at once because he could not hear them one by one, and dying in 1908 the most famous priest in Russia and a monarchist whom the revolution's historians disliked. The House stood until 1917.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Governed under Metropolitan Filaret of Moscow and, later, the state-appointed ober-procurator Konstantin Pobedonostsev. Sent missions to the Altai peoples and to Japan (from 1861, under Nikolai), and reached the 1905 toleration edict that opened the way to the council that restored the patriarchate. The Optina elders' renewal movement grew within this church, and Alaska was one of its missions. It stood in tension with the separate Old Believer Belokrinitsa hierarchy, founded in 1846, and with the evangelical Stundist movement that broke from it. After 1811 it also governed the Georgian church through an exarch.

Descended from

  • continues The Russian Church — Nikon to the Holy Synod Documented Major — The Holy Synod that Peter the Great set up in 1721 still ran the church a century later. An account of the church in 1830 says the Synod still worked under Peter's law of 1721, the Spiritual Regulation. Andrei Mouravieff, who wrote his history in 1838, was an official of the same Synod.
  • continues The Georgian Church — Between Persia, the Ottomans and Russia Widely Accepted Major — The Georgian church kept its catholicos until 1811. Then the title ended, and the head of the Georgian clergy became Exarch of Georgia and a member of the Russian Synod. A Georgian appeal to the patriarch of Constantinople calls this the loss of the church's rights. The Georgian church went on as an exarchate inside the Russian church.

Direct descendants

  • gave rise to The Optina Elders & Russian Ascetic Renewal Documented Major — Optina was a monastery of the Russian Church in the diocese of Kaluga. An Optina book of 1847 says the monk Leonid moved into its hermitage in 1829. The abbot gave him the care of the brothers' souls, and soon nobles, merchants and common people came to him.
  • gave rise to Japanese Christianity — the Hidden Christians’ Emergence & the Meiji Church Dominant Modern Reconstruction Major — In 1861 the Russian Church sent the monk Nikolai to Hakodate in Japan. He built a Japanese Orthodox church, served mostly by Japanese priests, one of three kinds of Christian church in Meiji Japan. This rests on modern histories.
  • gave rise to The Alaskan Mission — Herman’s Decades & Veniaminov Documented Major — In 1842 an English chaplain in Russia wrote that the Russian church had just set up a new diocese in the Aleutian Islands. Its first bishop was 'Veniamineff', a missionary who had long worked there. The same book lists a new see for the Aleutians, Kamchatka and Russian America.
  • continued as The Russian Orthodox Church — Council, Patriarchate and Reunion Widely Accepted Major — On 4 November 1917 a council of the Russian Orthodox Church ruled that the patriarchate was restored. A patriarch would again lead the church. In December the council ruled that he would chair its Holy Synod.
  • continued as The Georgian Orthodox Church Restored Widely Accepted Major — The Georgian church that the Russian Synod governed through an exarch from 1811 is the church that restored its own head in 1917. It is the exarchate line, not the Russian church as a whole, that carries on here. By 1906 it was pressing for its old rights, in an appeal printed by Dowling.

Broke With

  • broke with Russian Evangelicals — Stundists to the Baptist Union Dominant Modern Reconstruction Major — The first Stundists still took communion in Orthodox churches, F. C. Conybeare wrote in 1921. Only about 1870 did they form a separate sect, and they baptized again anyone who joined them from Orthodoxy. In 1880 the historian Julian Pelesz listed the Stundists among the new sects that had left the Russian church.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced The Kollyvades & Greek Renewal Widely Accepted — In 1782 the Kollyvades printed the Philokalia, a Greek book on prayer, and Paisius Velichkovsky put it into Slavonic. A book of 1847 from Optina says the Synod's press in Moscow first printed it. Then, through the work of Philaret of Moscow, it was printed there again.

  • argued against Russian Evangelicals — Stundists to the Baptist Union Dominant Modern Reconstruction — Orthodox clergy wrote books against the Stundists by name. This rests on his report of the two books.
  • argued against The Ruthenian Greek Catholic Church — Polotsk to Galicia Documented Major — In 1839 the Holy Synod printed an account of the Greek Catholics of its western lands. It called their union with Rome 'the schism'. The Synod received their bishops, clergy and people into the Russian Church, and the emperor confirmed it that March.Lasting result: In 1839 the Russian Holy Synod took the Greek Catholic bishops, priests and people of Russia's western lands into its church.   • argued against The Old Believers After 1815 Documented — Notes to an English history of the Russian Church, printed in 1842, call the Old Believers 'the Schism'. Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

For a century Russia's church had no patriarch of its own, governed instead by a lay official answering to the tsar. Metropolitan Filaret spent decades pushing through a Russian-language Bible against long resistance, finished in 1876, while later the ober-procurator Konstantin Pobedonostsev used the same church to enforce religious uniformity on Old Believers, Eastern Catholics and evangelicals alike.

Legacy

The Bible Filaret fought for is still the Russian Orthodox Church's standard translation, and the missionary churches begun in this period — in Japan, in Alaska — are still there. Its deepest legacy is the council it spent the century waiting for. The preparatory work of 1905-06 became the Moscow Council of 1917-18, which restored the patriarchate just as the revolution arrived that would try to destroy it.
