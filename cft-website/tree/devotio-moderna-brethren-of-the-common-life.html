The Late Medieval Era · c. 1380-1517 · Low Countries

Devotio Moderna / Brethren of the Common Life

The Low Countries, c. 1380 to 1517 — devout townspeople who lived in common without taking vows, kept notebooks of what they read, and produced the most copied book of the Middle Ages after the Bible.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:31

Read along

It began with Geert Grote, a wealthy and worldly scholar of Deventer. He gave up his career and opened his own house to a group of devout women. Then he began preaching repentance in the towns of the IJssel valley. After his death in 1384 his followers organized. The Brothers and Sisters of the Common Life lived together and paid their way by copying books and other ordinary work. They took no vows and did not leave ordinary society. Beside them stood a stricter branch, the canons of Windesheim. Its houses kept their own chronicles. Johannes Busch wrote the chronicle of Windesheim, and Thomas à Kempis that of Mount St Agnes. What held them together was less a doctrine than a method: a plain, inward and orderly devotion. They had the habit of keeping a rapiarium, a personal notebook of gathered passages to return to. One such gathering became the Imitation of Christ. It is credited to Thomas à Kempis of Mount St Agnes near Zwolle, though its authorship is disputed. The houses also took in schoolboys and shaped the Latin schools of towns like Deventer and Zwolle. That is how the movement's way of shaping lives reached far more people than ever joined it.

Documented Stories

Geert Grote Gives His House Away

Grote had been a well-connected scholar with two prebends and a taste for astrology when, in 1372 or 1374, an illness and the influence of a Carthusian friend turned him round. He resigned his benefices, spent three years at the charterhouse of Monnikhuizen, and came back to Deventer to preach. Before he left, on 20 September 1374, he executed a deed for the large house his parents had left him on the Bagijnenstraat. The house was to be a dwelling for ‘poor persons who wish to serve God’ — women, in practice — under the oversight of the city magistrates, not of any religious order; the residents were to be lay, take no vows, wear no habit, and be free to leave; they were not to beg but to live on the house’s endowment and their own work; and Grote kept for himself the use of two rooms and the right to be buried in the garden. He added a set of rules: hours for prayer and reading, simple food, obedience to a mistress chosen from among them, and a ban on receiving anyone who wanted the house as a convent. The women who moved in became the first Sisters of the Common Life, and the ‘Master Geert’s House’ was the model for dozens more across the Low Countries. Grote died of plague in 1384 at forty-three, having been forbidden to preach the year before for attacking the clergy’s concubines.

Salome Sticken Refuses to Be Called Holy

The sisters of Diepenveen, the women’s house of the Windesheim congregation near Deventer, kept a book of the lives of their own members, written by the sisters themselves in Middle Dutch, and the longest entry is for Salome Sticken, prioress from 1412 to 1446. It records that she had come as a young woman from Master Geert’s House at Deventer, where she had been noticed for taking the dirtiest work; that as prioress she went on doing it, carrying water and scouring pots with the lay sisters; that she was severe with any sister who showed learning or lineage, and once told a noblewoman who wished to enter that the convent had enough ladies and needed washerwomen; and that she wept when she was praised. They also record that she wrote, at the request of a house of sisters elsewhere, a ‘Way of Life for Sisters’, which survives: it prescribes the day in detail — rising, prayer, work in the kitchen and the garden, reading at meals, examination of conscience at night — and its recurring counsel is to take the lowest place and to do small things attentively. She went blind in old age, resigned, and was tended by the sisters she had trained. The Diepenveen book names some sixty women in this way, by their work and their faults and their deaths; it is the women’s side of a movement whose men wrote the Imitation of Christ, and it is written in the same register.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced Rhineland Mysticism & the Friends of God Documented — Johannes Busch kept the chronicle of the Windesheim canons. He tells how their prior, at the council of Constance, gladly visited the cell of the friar who wrote the Clock of Eternal Wisdom. The chronicle's 1886 editor names that friar as Henry Suso. A 1902 editor of Thomas à Kempis lists Suso among the writers Thomas took sayings from.
  • influenced The Carthusians — The Later Medieval Charterhouses Documented — Thomas à Kempis tells how the Carthusian prior of Monnikhuizen, an old friend, urged Geert Grote to change his life. Grote then went to the Carthusians at Monnikhuizen, near Arnhem, and they gave him a cell as a guest.

Influenced

  • influenced The Society of Jesus Documented — Johannes Busch, a canon of Windesheim, wrote that his brother canon Thomas a Kempis made the Imitation of Christ. Ignatius of Loyola's Spiritual Exercises tell those who make them to read it from the second week on. A Jesuit who knew Ignatius said he kept just two books on his table: the New Testament and Thomas a Kempis.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Geert Grote gave up a rich career to gather devout men and women into shared homes. They lived in common without ever taking vows, and paid their way by copying books by hand. Each kept a private notebook of passages to return to, called a rapiarium. That habit produced the Imitation of Christ, the most copied Christian book after the Bible. It is credited to Thomas à Kempis, though its authorship is disputed. Their schools shaped Erasmus. Ignatius of Loyola, who founded the Jesuits, kept the Imitation on his desk. His Spiritual Exercises tell those who make them to read it.

Legacy

The Imitation of Christ never stopped being read. After the Bible it is the most copied and printed Christian book of the era. It is still in print in dozens of languages. The movement's schools shaped people who then went in opposite directions. Erasmus was educated at Deventer. Luther may have spent a school year at Magdeburg in a house linked with the Brethren, but the tie is unsure. A century after the Imitation was written, Ignatius of Loyola kept it on his desk. His Spiritual Exercises tell those who make them to read it. The houses themselves did not outlast the century they helped bring about. Most closed during the Reformation.

How Sure the Record Is

RICH: customaries, brothers' and sisters' lives, rapiaria, the Imitation of Christ

  • Devotio Moderna: Basic Writings (ed./trans. John Van Engen, Classics of Western Spirituality, 1988) (primary) — Geert Grote's own writings (incl. Resolutions and Intentions - a layman's self-rule), lives of the Brothers and Sisters, customary material, from Latin and Middle Dutch - founder's voice and communal texture in one volume.
  • Thomas a Kempis, The Imitation of Christ (c. 1418-27) (primary) — The movement's mass-formation engine - the era's most copied book after the Bible, grown out of the rapiarium practice. Countless editions exist, with no single standard translation.
  • The sisters' houses literature: vitae of the Sisters of the Common Life at Deventer (Master Geert's House) and Diepenveen (primary) — Collective women's memory in the movement's own genre; partly available in Van Engen's volume, though the fuller corpus survives only in Middle Dutch.
  • Gerard Zerbolt of Zutphen, The Spiritual Ascents, plus the rapiarium practice (primary) — The movement's method text - interiorized, graded devotion for people in ordinary life - and its distinctive excerpt-book genre.
  • John Van Engen, Sisters and Brothers of the Common Life (Penn, 2008) (secondary) — Current standard, by the corpus's own translator.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 4, ch. 26 'Empowerment through reading, writing and example: the Devotio Moderna' (Goudriaan) (reference) — A dedicated chapter, one of the strongest reference fits for this movement. Its influence on Luther and the Jesuits reaches beyond 1500, but the movement's own historical window closes around that year.
