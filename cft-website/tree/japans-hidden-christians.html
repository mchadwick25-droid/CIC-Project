The Enlightenment & Awakening Era · 1650-1865 · Japan

Japan's Hidden Christians

About This Movement

In 1614 Japan banned Christianity, and the entry before this one tells how it began. Marnas wrote the history of the church's return in 1896. He says that in 1666 the government at Edo set up an inquiry in every town and village to learn what each family believed. Once a year, he adds, every Japanese had to tread on a cross laid on the ground, or on an image of the Virgin and the saints.

This was the e-fumi, the treading of the image. Marnas says its inventors meant it to expose the surviving Christians or force them to give up the faith.

Almost everywhere the hidden Christians kept the same order, Marnas says. Most villages had two chiefs. The chief of prayer led the Sunday prayers and went to the dying.

The baptizer gave baptism and trained a pupil to take his place. Marnas credits these offices, oral teaching and a few kept books and images for the survival of the faith.

In 1865 the missionaries studied the papers the villagers had kept.

They included prayer books, written baptism formulas and Christian calendars.

One treatise, Contrition, was dated 1603 and written wholly in Japanese. The baptizer Domingo brought it to the missionaries. It had escaped a search at Urakami eight years before. Then twenty-eight Christians were jailed and most of their books were burned. On 17 March 1865, at about half past twelve, twelve to fifteen people stood at the door of the church of the Twenty-Six Martyrs in Nagasaki.

Three women knelt beside Father Petitjean. One said, with her hand on her heart, that the hearts of all of them were the same as his. Then she asked, "Sancta Maria no gozowa doko?", "Where is the image of Holy Mary?" (our rendering). Within weeks the missionaries counted about 3,800 Christians.

About 1,300 lived at Urakami and 2,500 in the hills and on the nearby islands.
