About This Person

One day in Jerusalem, John Rufus walked the road from Siloam up the valley with a notable of the city. Below the mountain he saw a large old monastery falling into ruin. His companion smiled and said it had been the monastery of Juvenal, bishop of Jerusalem. It became a desert, he said, when the council of Chalcedon met. John answered that Juvenal had been the companion of Judas, as Dioscorus of Alexandria had said.

John tells this in his Plerophories, a book of testimonies against Chalcedon. François Nau, its French editor, draws John's life from the chapters where he speaks of himself. By Nau's account John was an Arab of southern Palestine, probably from Ascalon, and studied law at Beirut. Peter the Fuller, patriarch of Antioch, whose close aide he had been, made him a priest there.

When Peter the Fuller was driven out, John went to Palestine. There, Nau says, he came to know Peter the Iberian, the anti-Chalcedonian bishop of Maiuma near Gaza. In his Life of Severus, Zachariah Rhetor says that after Peter died the altar was kept for John, called Rufus. The book's heading calls John bishop of Maiuma.

Nau says John wrote in Greek soon after 512, while Severus was patriarch of Antioch. It survives in Syriac, in 89 short chapters of visions and wonders, most of them told by Peter the Iberian. In one chapter John says he checked a story with people who had seen it. Nau calls the book a new kind of collection of sayings, written to fight the council and its defenders.

A Greek Life of Peter the Iberian from about 500, held in Syriac, names no author. Its German editor, Richard Raabe, asks whether the author of the Plerophories wrote it too, and leaves the question open.

The book's first chapter begins with a story Peter the Iberian used to tell, and John calls Peter his father and bishop.

Key Works

  • Plerophories (Testimonies and Revelations against the Council of Chalcedon): 89 short chapters of visions, wonders and prophecies, most of them told by Peter the Iberian, written in Greek soon after 512 by Nau's account and surviving in Syriac

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Nau: the Plerophories were used by the writer of the History of Dioscorus by his disciple Theopistus, and Michael the Syrian summed up nearly all their chapters in his chronicle.
  • Nau: the book shows how the anti-Chalcedonians, then in power between 512 and 518, saw the council of Chalcedon.

How they're remembered

  • The heading of the Syriac text, in Nau's French, names the author as one of Peter the Iberian's disciples, the priest "Jean de Beit-Rufin, d'Antioche", bishop of "Maiouma de Gaza".
  • Zachariah Rhetor, in his Life of Severus, says he had studied law at Beirut with Theodore and left a name there for chastity and piety.

What Is Uncertain

  • His life rests on François Nau's reading of the few chapters where he speaks of himself, and on Zachariah Rhetor's Life of Severus. Nau works out the dates of his ordination and of the book from the book itself.
  • Nau says he succeeded Peter the Iberian as bishop of Maiuma. Zachariah says only that the altar was kept for him after Peter died, while Theodore and John the Canopite led the monastery.
  • The Life of Peter the Iberian names no author. Richard Raabe, its German editor, says the name was already unknown in the time of Evagrius of Epiphania, and leaves open whether the author of the Plerophories is the same man.
  • The Plerophories are partisan. Nau calls them a new kind of collection of sayings, written to fight the council of Chalcedon and its defenders, and says their sources are oral, with a written witness very rarely cited.
