Cyril and Methodius

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Cyrillo-Methodian Slavic Christianity.

Also called Constantine the Philosopher (Cyril's name until he took the name Cyril in Rome, by the Italian Legend)

Two brothers of Thessalonica who taught the Slavs of Moravia the faith in their own language. Pope John VIII praised the Slavonic letters found by Constantine and, in 880, allowed the Mass to be sung in Slavonic, with the Gospel read first in Latin.

About This Person

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 3:14

Read along

The emperor asked a tired scholar to go to the Slavs. He would go gladly, the scholar said, "if they have letters for their language." Without them, he asked, "who can write words on water, and who wants to be called a heretic?"

The scholar was Constantine, and the Slavs had no letters, so he made them. With his brother Methodius he put the Gospel, the Psalms, the readings from the apostles and chosen services into Slavic. When Latin clergy said God could be praised only in Hebrew, Greek and Latin, Constantine answered that every people may praise God in its own language. Their work began in Moravia, in what is now the Czech Republic. After Methodius died, a Life of their disciple Clement says, the younger disciples were sold as slaves, and the teachers were chained, stripped and driven out. Clement, Naum and Angelarius, it says, carried the letters to Bulgaria.

That Life was written in Bulgaria a generation or more later. In it Prince Boris thanks God for sending them, and Clement becomes "the first bishop of the Bulgarian tongue." Writing in Kiev more than two centuries later, the chroniclers said this Slavic writing was found among the Rus.

It had begun in the 860s, when Rastislav, prince of Moravia, told the emperor that his people had teachers from Italy, Greece and Germany, but none in their own language. The emperor chose the two brothers because they came from Thessalonica, a Greek city where Slavic was spoken too.

Most of what we know comes from two Lives written by their own followers. They survive only in copies made centuries later. In Venice, his Life says, Constantine asked the Latin clergy: "Does God's rain not fall on all alike?"

In Rome, the Lives tell, Constantine fell ill, became a monk named Cyril, and died in 869. His last words to Methodius were: "Look, brother, we were partners plowing a single furrow. And I fall in the field, having finished my day." Methodius loved the monks' mountain, but Constantine asked him not to give up his teaching for its sake.

Methodius went back as a bishop, and his Life says Frankish bishops held him prisoner for two and a half years. A Salzburg writer of his day complained that this "Greek" with "newly invented Slavic letters" had partly cheapened, in the people's eyes, the Latin Masses and Gospels. Pope John VIII repeated a ban on Slavonic Mass in 879, but in 880 he allowed it, with the Gospel read first in Latin. He wrote: "For he who made the three chief languages, Hebrew, Greek and Latin, also himself created all the others, for his praise and glory."

Methodius died in 885. His Life says his disciples held the funeral in Latin, Greek and Slavonic, and crowds came, rich and poor, free and slave.

Timeline

Dates
Ninth century. No source used here gives either brother's year of birth. Johann Ginzel reckons their arrival in Moravia to 863. Cyril died in Rome on 14 February, by the Italian Legend; Ginzel accepts 868 for the year, and Ernst Dümmler and S. H. Cross give 869. Methodius died on 6 April, in the year 6393 from the creation, by the Life of Methodius; Ginzel works this out as 885
Place
Thessalonica, their home; Cherson, where the Italian Legend says Constantine found the relics of Clement of Rome; Moravia, their mission; Rome, where Cyril died and was buried in the basilica of Saint Clement; Pannonia, the see Methodius was made bishop for, by the Life of Methodius
Role
Brothers and missionaries: Constantine (Cyril), priest, scholar and finder of the Slavonic letters; Methodius, monk, then bishop and archbishop for the Slavs

Key Works

  • The Slavonic letters: which Pope John VIII calls the Slavonic letters found by Constantine the Philosopher. S. H. Cross says Constantine made the Glagolitic letters, not the Cyrillic; Count Lützow says his alphabet became known as Cyrillic
  • Slavonic translations: the Gospel, the Apostle, the Psalter and chosen services, by the two brothers, as the Life of Methodius says; it adds that Methodius later put the rest of the Scriptures into Slavonic in six months

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • The Russian Primary Chronicle says the writing made for Moravia exists also in Rus and among the Danube Bulgarians.
  • The Life of Clement of Ohrid names Gorazd, Clement, Naum, Angelarius and Sava as the brothers' chief disciples.
  • The Life of Methodius says he named Gorazd, a man of the Moravians' own country, learned in Latin books, to follow him.

How they're remembered

  • In S. H. Cross's English of the Russian Primary Chronicle, "The Slavs rejoiced to hear the greatness of God extolled in their native tongue."
  • Ginzel reports that in 1380 Bishop John of Olomouc ordered their feast kept on 9 March, as Moravia's chief fathers, apostles and patrons.
  • Augustin Theiner dedicated his Vatican documents of 1863 to Joseph George Strossmayer, bishop of Bosnia or Diakovo, and wrote that the work kept the thousandth-year feast of Saints Cyril and Methodius, which the Church celebrated that year.

Open Questions

  • Which account to trust. Ginzel trusts the Italian Legend, which the Bollandists, and Ginzel after them, think very probably the work of Gauderich of Velletri, a bishop of the brothers' time. Of the Life of Methodius, Dümmler says a pupil wrote it soon after Methodius died, and Wilhelm Wattenbach rates it highly. Ginzel, who writes against the Greek schism, calls it a Greek schismatic's concoction.
  • Who made them bishops. The Italian Legend says Pope Hadrian II made both brothers bishops. The Life of Methodius says Pope Nicholas received them and ordained Methodius priest, and that he was made bishop later, for the Slavs of Pannonia.
  • How long they stayed in Moravia. The Italian Legend says four and a half years; the Life of Methodius says three. Ginzel follows the Legend and calls the Life wrong.
  • The year Cyril died. Ginzel accepts 868; Dümmler and Cross give 869.
  • The day Methodius died. The Life gives 6 April, in the year Ginzel works out as 885. Ginzel reports that Wattenbach thought a death on so high a feast suspicious, and defends the date.
  • His prison. The Life of Methodius says the enemy of the Moravian king and all the bishops sent him to Swabia and held him two and a half years. Count Lützow says the German governors seized him and handed him as a prisoner to the German bishops. Only the Life gives the place and the length.
  • The six-month Bible. Cross says it is generally regarded as apocryphal.
  • The Salzburg account against Methodius is hostile. Ginzel and Bartholomäus Kopitar date it to 873; Ginzel reports that Joseph Assemani and Wattenbach give 871.
