Colonial Latin American Devotional Catholicism

New Spain and Peru, about 1600 to 1810 — the centuries when Catholicism stopped being a mission and became the ordinary religion of a continent, held together by lay confraternities and their own saints.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 6:59

Read along

Once the first missionary generation had passed, Catholicism in Spanish America settled into being the ordinary religion of whole societies, and its real institutions were local: the cofradías, lay confraternities that maintained an altar or an image, buried their members and paid for their funerals, organized by trade, by parish and often by race — with indigenous confraternities and Black brotherhoods running their own devotional life. Its saints were homegrown: Rose of Lima, canonized in 1671 as the first saint of the Americas, and Martín de Porres, the mixed-race Dominican lay brother of the same city. Its convents produced writers of the first rank, among them Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz in Mexico, who defended a woman's right to study, and Úrsula de Jesús in Lima, an enslaved woman later freed, whose spiritual diary survives in her own dictated words. Above all it produced Our Lady of Guadalupe, whose shrine at Tepeyac became the devotional center of Mexico and eventually a symbol of Mexican identity itself.

Portuguese America had its own brotherhoods. In Bahia a sermon of 1633 is headed as preached to the brotherhood of the Blacks of a sugar mill, on the feast of St John the Evangelist. It is by the Jesuit António Vieira, in his Maria Rosa Mystica of 1686. In the same book he tells the enslaved workers of a sugar mill that no labor in the world is more like the Cross and Passion of Christ than theirs (a rendering). In 1711 André João Antonil wrote of the mills. He says some masters keep enslaved people for a long time in the cane fields or the mill without baptism. He tells masters not to object when the enslaved choose their own kings. He tells them to let the enslaved keep their morning feasts of Our Lady of the Rosary, of St Benedict and of the patron of the mill chapel. In 1741 Benedict XIV wrote to the bishops of Brazil, Paraguay and the Río de la Plata. His bull Immensa pastorum principis condemned those who enslaved or sold Indians, even baptized ones.

Saint-Domingue, the French colony that became Haiti, is seen here through one long witness. It is the Description of Moreau de Saint-Méry, printed in Philadelphia in 1797. It shows the colony as it stood on 18 October 1789. Moreau was at Le Cap in 1783, and in 1788 the Navy minister gave him a drafting task for the colonies. He writes from the colonists' side, so the picture of the Black people in it is his, not theirs.

He says the parishes of the North first had Capuchins as pastors. In 1704 the Capuchins could not supply enough men and gave up the mission. The Jesuits took it over and kept it until their order was expelled from the colony, at the end of 1763. Secular priests held it until 1768, when the Capuchins came back. In the West, the Dominicans served the parishes.

Moreau judges the Africans of the colony harshly. He says they could not sort religious ideas in their minds, so that all their belief lay in outward signs. In church, he says, they mumbled prayers they knew badly, or slept. Yet he reports that new arrivals from Africa pressed to be baptized. Black people born in the colony claimed to stand far above them because of their baptism, and the newcomers were taunted as "horses". Adults were baptized on two Saturdays of the year, often with no preparation. He says they held a godparent in more respect than their own father and mother.

At Le Cap, after the parish Mass on Sundays, Black worshippers met in the church. They prayed and sang hymns, led by some old men and women. A low Mass followed, which Moreau calls la messe des nègres, the Mass of the Black people. He credits the Jesuit Pierre Boutin, who reached Le Cap in 1705, with teaching them each evening, with the baptism of adults at Easter and Pentecost, and with that Mass.

Guatemala gave this world a brotherhood that ran a hospital. It was called the Bethlemites. Pedro de Betancur founded it and took the name Pedro de San José.

Two books tell his story, and both are hagiography, written to honor him. One is a Life printed at Rome in 1683, by Francisco Antonio de Montalvo. The other is the order's own history of 1723, by Joseph García de la Concepción, a Discalced Franciscan who wrote it for the order.

Montalvo says Pedro was born on Tenerife. He sailed for the Indies in 1650 and reached Guatemala City in 1651. He died in April 1667.

Montalvo writes of the Hospital of Our Lady of Bethlehem. He also writes of a Bethlemite Company that Pedro was to found. It was to shelter the sick, the poor and the abandoned. Members of the Third Order of St Francis, he says, moved over to the new house of hospitality.

Montalvo's Life is full of wonders. In one, he says, the Virgin let Pedro know how welcome his service was, by a favor no less rare than miraculous. Of one such wonder Montalvo says he offers it only as human report, because the Sacred Congregation of Rites is the body that weighs miracles.

García says the brothers sent their constitutions to Brother Antonio de la Cruz at Madrid. He was to go to Rome and seek apostolic confirmation. Antonio went to the Roman Curia and won papal confirmation of the constitutions. García prints the bull of Clement X for it.

García also prints a bull of Innocent XI. It closes with the date 26 March 1687, in the eleventh year of that pope's reign. A later memorial recalls the pope's grace in raising the Company to a regular community under the Rule of St Augustine.

Documented Stories

Úrsula de Jesús Falls Down a Well

Úrsula de Jesús was born enslaved in Lima in 1604 and grew up in the household of a Spanish lady and then, from the age of about thirteen, in the convent of Santa Clara as the personal servant of a nun. In 1642, sent to recover a skirt that had fallen down the convent's well, she leaned out over the shaft on a plank that broke, hung above the drop, and was not killed; she took it as God's warning and changed her life. In 1645 a nun of the house, Rafaela de Esquivel, paid the price of her freedom. Úrsula did not leave. She stayed as a donada, a free servant who took simple vows, ranked below the nuns and above the slaves, and did the same work as before. From 1650 her confessors, hearing what she told them, ordered her to keep a spiritual diary, which she dictated to nuns who wrote it down and partly wrote herself. It records visions of souls in purgatory who came to her for prayers: nuns of the convent, priests, and Black and mulatto women, servants like herself, who told her that God had a place for them too, and asked whether Black people could be saved, and were told that they could. It records also her exhaustion, her resentment of nuns who treated her as a slave still, and her doubts. It is the only text of its kind by an African-descended woman in colonial Spanish America.

‘I, the Worst of All’

Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz had lived in the Hieronymite convent of San Jerónimo in Mexico City since 1669, in a cell with a library said to hold four thousand volumes, writing poems, plays and theology for the viceregal court. In 1690 the bishop of Puebla published, under the name Sor Filotea, a theological critique she had written of a Jesuit sermon, together with a letter advising her to give up profane letters. Her Answer to Sor Filotea, dated 1 March 1691, defended a woman's right to study and to write, and told the story of her own life as a proof of it. The archbishop of Mexico, Francisco de Aguiar y Seijas, was known to avoid the sight of women; her old confessor Antonio Núñez de Miranda, whom she had dismissed, was received back in 1693. In 1694 she renewed her vows in writing, signed a general confession and a petition to the tribunal of God, and wrote in the convent's book of professions a renunciation of study, signing it ‘I, the worst of all’, in her blood. Her library and instruments were sold and the money given to the archbishop for the poor. In 1695 an epidemic entered the convent; she nursed the sisters, caught the sickness, and died on 17 April, aged forty-six. What she thought in those last two years is not recorded, the documents allow both a conversion and a silencing, and do not exclude both at once.

The Extirpators and the Book They Left

Francisco de Ávila was parish priest of San Damián in the province of Huarochirí, in the mountains above Lima, when in 1608 he discovered, or announced, that they were still worshipping the huacas, the mountains and stones and mummified ancestors of the old religion, alongside the Mass he said for them. He gathered evidence, seized the mummies and the objects, and on 20 December 1609, in the main square of Lima before the viceroy and the archbishop, preached a sermon in Quechua and Spanish while the mummies and idols were burned and a native religious specialist, Hernando Paucar, was whipped and sentenced to exile. The archbishop created a new office, visitor of idolatry, and gave it to Ávila. For the next sixty years, in waves, the visitors of the archdiocese of Lima went from village to village with notaries and interpreters, took confessions, burned ancestors, whipped and shaved the specialists, sent the obstinate to a prison at Lima called the House of Santa Cruz, and forbade the dances; the Jesuit Pablo José de Arriaga wrote the manual for it in 1621. The extirpation is documented in its own trial records, thousands of pages in which Andean Christians describe, under compulsion, what they believed. Ávila, to prepare his own campaign, had also had someone in his parish write down in Quechua the myths and rites of Huarochirí, and that manuscript survived in Madrid to be the fullest account of an Andean religion in an Andean language, and the extirpators' unintended monument.

Connections

In Its Own Record

This is the settled, everyday form of the mission faith that came before it in Spanish America. The missions Junípero Serra began in California from 1769 belong to this same world of devotion in New Spain. Much later, the base communities of the twentieth century grew among people raised in this piety. How much they drew on it is a matter of judgment.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Ordinary religious life across colonial Spanish America ran through the cofradías — lay confraternities, often organized by trade, parish, or race, that maintained a saint's image, buried their own members, and paid for their funerals. The era produced homegrown saints like Rose of Lima and Martín de Porres, convent writers including Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz and the formerly enslaved Úrsula de Jesús, whose dictated spiritual diary survives in her own words, and the shrine of Our Lady of Guadalupe at Tepeyac, which became the devotional heart of Mexico.

Legacy

This is a living tradition, not a closed one. Latin America holds the largest Catholic population on earth. The shape the faith took in these centuries is still how most of its people practice it. That means the shrine, the patron saint's feast, the confraternity and the image carried in procession. Guadalupe's basilica in Mexico City is the most visited shrine to Mary in the world. The base communities of the twentieth century began reading Scripture from below. They grew among people formed by this piety, though how far they built on it is a matter of judgment, not record.

Experience It Today

  • The Basilica of Our Lady of Guadalupe, Mexico City — the shrine at Tepeyac, the most visited Marian pilgrimage site in the world
