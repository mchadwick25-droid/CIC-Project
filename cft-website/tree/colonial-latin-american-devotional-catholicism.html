The Enlightenment & Awakening Era · c. 1600-1810 · New Spain, Peru, Brazil

Colonial Latin American Devotional Catholicism

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Once the first missionary generation had passed, Catholicism in Spanish America settled into being the ordinary religion of whole societies, and its real institutions were local: the cofradías, lay confraternities that maintained an altar or an image, buried their members and paid for their funerals, organized by trade, by parish and often by race — with indigenous confraternities and Black brotherhoods running their own devotional life. Its saints were homegrown: Rose of Lima, canonized in 1671 as the first saint of the Americas, and Martín de Porres, the mixed-race Dominican lay brother of the same city. Its convents produced writers of the first rank, among them Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz in Mexico, who defended a woman's right to study, and Úrsula de Jesús in Lima, an enslaved woman later freed, whose spiritual diary survives in her own dictated words. Above all it produced Our Lady of Guadalupe, whose shrine at Tepeyac became the devotional center of Mexico and eventually a symbol of Mexican identity itself.

Portuguese America had its own brotherhoods. In Bahia a sermon of 1633 is headed as preached to the brotherhood of the Blacks of a sugar mill, on the feast of St John the Evangelist. It is by the Jesuit António Vieira, in his Maria Rosa Mystica of 1686. In the same book he tells the enslaved workers of a sugar mill that no labor in the world is more like the Cross and Passion of Christ than theirs (a rendering). In 1711 André João Antonil wrote of the mills. He says some masters keep enslaved people for a long time in the cane fields or the mill without baptism. He tells masters not to object when the enslaved choose their own kings. He tells them to let the enslaved keep their morning feasts of Our Lady of the Rosary, of St Benedict and of the patron of the mill chapel. In 1741 Benedict XIV wrote to the bishops of Brazil, Paraguay and the Río de la Plata. His bull Immensa pastorum principis condemned those who enslaved or sold Indians, even baptized ones.

Saint-Domingue, the French colony that became Haiti, is seen here through one long witness. It is the Description of Moreau de Saint-Méry, printed in Philadelphia in 1797. It shows the colony as it stood on 18 October 1789. Moreau was at Le Cap in 1783, and in 1788 the Navy minister gave him a drafting task for the colonies. He writes from the colonists' side, so the picture of the Black people in it is his, not theirs.

He says the parishes of the North first had Capuchins as pastors. In 1704 the Capuchins could not supply enough men and gave up the mission. The Jesuits took it over and kept it until their order was expelled from the colony, at the end of 1763. Secular priests held it until 1768, when the Capuchins came back. In the West, the Dominicans served the parishes.

Moreau judges the Africans of the colony harshly. He says they could not sort religious ideas in their minds, so that all their belief lay in outward signs. In church, he says, they mumbled prayers they knew badly, or slept. Yet he reports that new arrivals from Africa pressed to be baptized. Black people born in the colony claimed to stand far above them because of their baptism, and the newcomers were taunted as "horses". Adults were baptized on two Saturdays of the year, often with no preparation. He says they held a godparent in more respect than their own father and mother.

At Le Cap, after the parish Mass on Sundays, Black worshippers met in the church. They prayed and sang hymns, led by some old men and women. A low Mass followed, which Moreau calls la messe des nègres, the Mass of the Black people. He credits the Jesuit Pierre Boutin, who reached Le Cap in 1705, with teaching them each evening, with the baptism of adults at Easter and Pentecost, and with that Mass.

Guatemala gave this world a brotherhood that ran a hospital. It was called the Bethlemites. Pedro de Betancur founded it and took the name Pedro de San José.

Two books tell his story, and both are hagiography, written to honor him. One is a Life printed at Rome in 1683, by Francisco Antonio de Montalvo. The other is the order's own history of 1723, by Joseph García de la Concepción, a Discalced Franciscan who wrote it for the order.

Montalvo says Pedro was born on Tenerife. He sailed for the Indies in 1650 and reached Guatemala City in 1651. He died in April 1667.

Montalvo writes of the Hospital of Our Lady of Bethlehem. He also writes of a Bethlemite Company that Pedro was to found. It was to shelter the sick, the poor and the abandoned. Members of the Third Order of St Francis, he says, moved over to the new house of hospitality.

Montalvo's Life is full of wonders. In one, he says, the Virgin let Pedro know how welcome his service was, by a favor no less rare than miraculous. Of one such wonder Montalvo says he offers it only as human report, because the Sacred Congregation of Rites is the body that weighs miracles.

García says the brothers sent their constitutions to Brother Antonio de la Cruz at Madrid. He was to go to Rome and seek apostolic confirmation. Antonio went to the Roman Curia and won papal confirmation of the constitutions. García prints the bull of Clement X for it.

García also prints a bull of Innocent XI. It closes with the date 26 March 1687, in the eleventh year of that pope's reign. A later memorial recalls the pope's grace in raising the Company to a regular community under the Rule of St Augustine.

In Its Own Record

Continues

  • Mission-Born Christianity in New Spain and the Andes Inferential-Thin Major — Motolinia tells of the first friars baptizing many people in Mexico from 1524. A century later, the friar Thomas Gage saw Indian towns in Guatemala run their own groups for the saints. The tie rests on these two accounts, a century apart.

Continued As

  • Nineteenth-Century Latin American Catholicism Inferential-Thin Major — In 1895 a priest in Guadalajara reprinted a book on the Guadalupe image written in the 1600s. He wrote that Mexican love for Our Lady of Guadalupe grew warmer in his own day. That shows one colonial devotion living on in the new Mexico, but for the rest of the region the evidence is thin.

Sources

  • The cofradia books: confraternity records across New Spain and Peru, incl. indigenous confraternities' books and Black brotherhoods' records (Afro-Peruvian and Afro-Brazilian rosary confraternities) (primary) — A lay-run devotional economy that was the ordinary institutional life of the piety; the records themselves were kept under clerical oversight.
  • Convent writings, women's: Sor Juana, Respuesta a Sor Filotea (1691; trans. Arenal & Powell); Madre Maria de San Jose's autobiography (ed. Myers & Powell); Ursula de Jesus, The Souls of Purgatory (ed./trans. van Deusen, UNM, 2004) (primary) — An enslaved-then-freed convent servant's dictated spiritual diary, begun in 1650 - an enslaved woman's own religious voice, though filtered through the confessor who took her dictation. The convent's racial hierarchy is visible within it.
  • The canonization processes: Rose of Lima (canonized 1671), Martin de Porres (process testimony from the 1660s, ordinary witnesses incl. Afro-Peruvians), Mariana de Quito (primary) — Canonization-process testimony is structured and led by the questioners, but it remains the era's densest record of ordinary devotees speaking for themselves.
  • The devotional-practice layer: the Guadalupan corpus (Nican Mopohua, published 1649 in Huei tlamahuicoltica; its dating and authorship are still debated), shrine and pilgrimage records, novena imprints; Inquisition and extirpation records that policed popular devotion (primary) — The records that policed popular devotion come from a hostile administrative source.
