Marius of Avenches

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Merovingian Gallic Christianity.

Known for
Bishop of Avenches who carried on the Chronicle of Prosper of Aquitaine from 455 to 581, with close notice of Burgundy, Agaune and the Valais.
Also called
Marius Aventicensis, Marius Aventicensis seu Lausannensis, Marius d'Avenches
Dates
No ancient source used here gives his birth or death year. He signed the acts of the council of Mâcon, held in King Guntram's twenty-fourth year, which Friedrich Maassen gives as 585. Theodor Mommsen reckons from his epitaph and from a church he dedicated that he became bishop in 574 and died in 594. That makes him sixty-three or sixty-four at death, born in 530 or 531. Conon of Lausanne puts the start of his rule in 581. A later hand in the annals of Lausanne puts his death in 601. Mommsen rejects both. Andrea Gallandi, after Pierre-François Chifflet, gives his death as 31 December 596
Place
Avenches, in the Burgundian lands; the see later moved to Lausanne, where Andrea Gallandi says he was buried. Conon of Lausanne heard that he came from the diocese of Autun, which Mommsen treats as doubtful
Role
Bishop of Avenches; chronicler

About This Person

In the year Theodor Mommsen counts as 563, Marius of Avenches writes, the mountain of Tauredunum in the Valais suddenly fell. It buried a nearby fort and villages with everyone in them, and shook the whole lake until the water broke over both shores. The flood swept away old villages with their people and cattle. It carried off the bridge at Geneva with its mills and men, then burst into the city and killed many.

Marius was bishop of Avenches, in the Burgundian lands, in the later 500s. His Chronicle carries on that of Prosper of Aquitaine from 455 to 581.

At the council of Mâcon, in King Guntram's twenty-fourth year, Marius signed as bishop of the church of Avenches. His epitaph comes down through Conon, provost of Lausanne, writing in 1235. It says he was of noble birth and a cleric from his early years. He made sacred vessels for the church and worked his lands with his own hands, it says. He fed the poor and ate sparingly himself.

From the epitaph and a church Marius dedicated, Mommsen reckons that he became bishop in 574 and died in 594. Conon gives 581 for his becoming bishop, and a later hand in the annals of Lausanne puts his death in 601. Mommsen rejects both. Andrea Gallandi, after Pierre-François Chifflet, gives his death as 596.

The bishop's seat later moved from Avenches to Lausanne. Mommsen thinks this came after Marius. Gallandi reports Charles Le Cointe's view that Marius moved it himself.

Marius wrote most about Agaune and the Valais, Mommsen says. He records that King Sigismund of Burgundy built the monastery at Agaune, and that its monks tried one night to kill their bishop, Agricola. A single manuscript keeps the Chronicle, which Mommsen says went unused in the Middle Ages.

A Lausanne cathedral book of the dead from about 1510 marked his day, Mommsen notes, with a procession to the church of Saint Marius.

Key Works

  • Chronicle: year-by-year notes from 455 to 581, carrying on Prosper of Aquitaine and dated by the consuls. It tells of the Burgundian and Frankish kings, the wars in Italy, plague and floods, and above all Agaune and the Valais. It survives in a single manuscript of the tenth century, in which his part ends "usque hic Marius aepiscopus"

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Theodor Mommsen thinks Marius and his contemporary Gregory of Tours drew on the same Gallic annals, and prints Gregory's parallel passages beside the Chronicle.
  • Bruno Krusch thinks the annals behind the Chronicle called Fredegar's may have been written at Avenches, where Marius wrote. Both tell of the duke Theudefrid and of the Lombards breaking into Burgundy.
  • Pierre-François Chifflet first printed the Chronicle in 1636. Andrea Gallandi reports his view that Gregory of Tours's History is poor in dates. Anyone who set the Chronicle beside it, Chifflet held, would find great light shed on Gregory.
  • Gregory of Tours tells the same fall of Tauredunum in his History of the Franks, and O. M. Dalton's note points to Marius's account of it.

How they're remembered

  • His epitaph, which Conon of Lausanne gives, calls him a good judge, a model for priests and the glory of bishops.
  • Andrea Gallandi says tradition counts him among the saints, with his day on the day of his death. He was buried at Lausanne in the church of Saint Thyrsus, which later took the name of Saint Marius.
  • Theodor Mommsen notes a book of the dead from Lausanne cathedral, written about 1510. It marks a procession on his day to the church of Saint Marius.

What Is Uncertain

  • His years are disputed. Theodor Mommsen reckons 574 for his consecration and 594 for his death. He works from the epitaph and from the fourteenth year of his rule, when Conon says he dedicated a church at Paterniacum. Conon gives 581 for the start of his rule, and a later hand in the Lausanne annals gives 601 for his death. Mommsen thinks both come from an error about the year King Guntram died. Andrea Gallandi, after Chifflet, gives about 576 for his consecration and 596 for his death, and Charles Le Cointe about 532 for his birth.
  • His life comes almost wholly from late witnesses. His signature at Mâcon is from his own time. The epitaph and the years of his life come through Conon, provost of Lausanne, writing in 1235. Mommsen thinks the epitaph was copied from the tomb, though Conon may have taken it from the Lausanne cartulary.
  • His birthplace. Conon heard from trustworthy elders that he was born of noble family in the diocese of Autun. Mommsen calls the report of his origin from Autun doubtful. Gallandi, after Le Cointe, gives his birth among the Aedui, the people of the diocese of Autun.
  • When the see moved from Avenches to Lausanne. Mommsen says no one knows. Since Marius signs at Mâcon only for Avenches, Mommsen thinks it moved after him. He leaves open whether Marius wrote at Avenches or at Lausanne. Gallandi reports Le Cointe's view, after Claude Robert, that Marius moved it himself under King Childebert.
  • The years of the Chronicle. Marius dates by consuls, and the years shown in the margin are Mommsen's. Mommsen finds his reckoning of the consulships of Justin the Younger and Tiberius his own and quite wrong. Mommsen thinks the Chronicle was first written up to 567 and finished to 581 when Marius was already bishop.
  • Agaune. Marius says King Sigismund built the monastery. O. M. Dalton, in his notes to Gregory of Tours, says Sigismund restored it.
  • His name. He is a different man from the earlier Latin writer Marius Mercator.
