Prudentius of Troyes

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Carolingian Reform Christianity.

Known for
Spanish-born bishop of Troyes who kept the annals now called the Annals of Saint-Bertin until his death in 861. He took the side of double predestination against Hincmar of Reims.
Also called
Galindo
Dates
Kept the annals from 835, after a first writer who stopped in the middle of that year, G. H. Pertz says. Died in 861, by the annals' entry for that year. At councils at Paris in 846 and at Soissons in 853, says Nicolás Antonio, a Spanish scholar writing after 1650. No held source gives his birth

Role
Bishop of Troyes; annalist

About This Person

Wenilo, archbishop of Sens, sent Prudentius of Troyes nineteen chapters from a book by John Scotus Eriugena, and asked him to answer each error. Prudentius found the poison of Pelagius in them, he wrote, and at times the madness of Origen. He trembled, he confessed, to see old errors, long asleep with their authors, sprouting again in his own day.

Prudentius was bishop of Troyes in Frankish Gaul, and he died in 861. On his death, the annals he had kept name him Galindo, called Prudentius, a Spaniard by birth. In his own verses for a Gospel book, Prudentius calls himself born in Hesperia and brought to the Celts. Nicolás Antonio, a Spanish scholar writing after 1650, takes Hesperia to be Spain.

He kept the annals of the Frankish kings' deeds, now called the Annals of Saint-Bertin, from the 830s until his death. G. H. Pertz, in Waitz's edition, gives the abbé Lebeuf's signs of his hand. Prudentius calls Epiphany the Apparition of the Lord, by the custom of his homeland. He notes eclipses, comets, floods and the ravages of wolves, and he credits good and bad alike to God.

The great quarrel of his day was over predestination. In 849, the annals record, the monk Gottschalk was flogged in public and made to burn his own books. Prudentius took the side Hincmar of Reims opposed. He taught, Antonio says, that God freely chose some for life, and by a justice past searching out destined others for punishment. Under 859 he wrote that Pope Nicholas the First had confirmed a double predestination. Hincmar complained to Egilo, archbishop of Sens, that he had heard this from no one else and read it nowhere.

After Prudentius died, Hincmar took over the annals, Pertz says. The entry for 861 says Prudentius had once resisted Gottschalk, but later turned in bitterness into a fierce defender of the heresy. Antonio calls this a slander, and blames an unnamed annalist on Hincmar's side. He says Prudentius never opposed Gottschalk.

Troyes, Antonio adds, names him a saint in its lists of bishops.

Key Works

  • Annals of Saint-Bertin, from the 830s to 861: the annals of the deeds of the Frankish kings, which Hincmar says Prudentius composed and the king held. Georg Waitz, who edited them, says some have held them to be official annals and others have doubted it
  • On Predestination, against John Scotus: his answer to nineteen chapters taken from the book of John Scotus Eriugena, written at the request of Wenilo, archbishop of Sens
  • Letter to Hincmar and Pardulus: a letter on predestination to Hincmar of Reims and Pardulus of Laon, which Antonio dates to 849

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Hincmar took over the annals after Prudentius's death, G. H. Pertz says. Hincmar carried them on almost to the end of his own life, Georg Waitz says.
  • His entry under 859, that Pope Nicholas the First had confirmed a double predestination, made Hincmar write to Egilo of Sens. Hincmar asked that the pope be told, so that no scandal should come to the church.
  • Antonio says he sent a letter, his Tractoria, to the synod of Sens. In it he held that Aeneas, chosen bishop of Paris, must sign four statements first.

How they're remembered

  • The annals' notice of his death, which G. H. Pertz, an earlier editor, gives to Hincmar, his opponent in the predestination quarrel, calls him learned. It says he turned in bitterness into a fierce defender of Gottschalk's heresy.
  • Nicolás Antonio calls the annal on him a slander. He gives it to an unnamed annalist on Hincmar's side, and says Prudentius never opposed Gottschalk.
  • A note in a manuscript of his treatise, printed in Patrologia Latina 115, warns that he did not think rightly on some teachings. Nicolaus Camusat of Troyes answers that it came perhaps from someone who could not follow him.
  • Antonio says the church of Troyes calls him a saint in its lists of bishops.

What Is Uncertain

  • Whether he ever opposed the monk Gottschalk. The annals' notice of his death says he first resisted him and then took his side. Nicolás Antonio says he never opposed him.
  • Most of what is told of his life comes through Hincmar, his opponent, and the annals. Some comes through defenders who answered them, such as Nicolás Antonio.
  • The entry on Gottschalk under 849. G. H. Pertz says learned men judge that Prudentius could not have written it as it stands, and that Hincmar changed it. A note from Bouquet's edition, which Waitz prints, gives the abbé Lebeuf's guess at the first wording.
  • His name. The annals' notice of his death calls him Galindo, surnamed Prudentius. Antonio says a count Galindo of Aragon shows the name is Spanish. He asks what stops Prudentius being of the same family.
