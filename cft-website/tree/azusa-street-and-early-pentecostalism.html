Azusa Street & Early Pentecostalism

Los Angeles, 1906 to the 1920s — a revival born out of Holiness and Black church worship that became the parent of nearly every movement in this era's family, documented as it happened in its own newspaper, The Apostolic Faith.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:10

Read along

The revival on Azusa Street in Los Angeles began in April 1906, and it came out of two streams already running — the Holiness movement, and Black church worship. What made it travel was that the mission printed its own newspaper. The Apostolic Faith ran to thirteen issues between 1906 and 1908, went out free from Los Angeles, and was read by people who then started meetings of their own on the strength of it. William J. Seymour led the mission and set down how it was to be ordered in The Doctrines and Discipline of the Azusa Street Apostolic Faith Mission in 1915. The city's press mocked it from the first weeks — the Los Angeles Times ran 'Weird Babel of Tongues' on 18 April 1906 — and participants answered with accounts of their own, Frank Bartleman's among the best known. The mission itself declined after 1909, and Seymour died in 1922. By then what began there was long past its own address.

Documented Stories

Parham Comes to Azusa Street

William Seymour had learned the doctrine of Spirit baptism with tongues in Houston in 1905, sitting outside the door of Charles Fox Parham's Bible school because a Black man was not allowed inside, and had carried it to Los Angeles in 1906, where from April the mission in a former stable on Azusa Street drew Black and white, Mexicans and Armenians to services that ran day and night. Seymour wrote to Parham as his father in the gospel and asked him to come. Parham arrived in October 1906 expecting to correct and lead. What he found, by his own later account, was a scene he could not bear: Black and white kneeling together at the altar, women laying hands on men, and what he described, in the language of the segregated South, as a darky camp meeting with white people imitating Negro worship. He preached against it from the mission's pulpit, and the mission's elders, Seymour among them, asked him to leave. He set up rival meetings, drew off a few hundred, and went home to Kansas denouncing Azusa Street in his own paper for the rest of his life; a morals charge against him in Texas in 1907, never brought to trial, ended his wider influence. The revival did not need him. The missionaries it sent out from 1906 carried Seymour's reading, that the Spirit had washed away the color line, which Parham never accepted and which the white Pentecostal bodies founded after 1914 did not keep.

The Split of 1911 and the Clause of 1915

By 1911 the Azusa Street mission had shrunk from the crowds of 1906 to a congregation of a few hundred, and Seymour was often away preaching. In February 1911 William Durham, a Chicago leader who taught that sanctification was given complete in Christ's finished work, against the Holiness doctrine Seymour preached, came to Azusa Street and was given the pulpit in Seymour's absence. The crowds came back, and the finished-work message, which most white Pentecostals would adopt, went out from the building. Seymour returned in May, and with his trustees padlocked the doors against Durham, who took most of the congregation with him to a mission on Seventh Street and denounced Seymour until his death the following year. Seymour was left with a small, mostly Black congregation and the building. In 1915 he published The Doctrines and Discipline of the Azusa Street Apostolic Faith Mission, a church order drawn largely from the Methodist Discipline, and wrote into it a clause that the mission's bishop and its trustees must be people of color, so that, as the text says, the work would not be taken from them again as it had been in the years past; he added that this was not from prejudice but to keep the mission from division. He died in 1922 and his wife Jennie led the congregation until the building was lost in 1931 and demolished. The white bodies that descended from the revival, the Assemblies of God among them, were organized without him.

Connections

In Its Own Record

It grew out of the Holiness movement and Black church worship. Many of this era's Pentecostal churches trace their line back to it. Others, such as Africa's prayer churches, began with their own leaders.

Descended from

  • grew out of The Holiness Movement Widely Accepted Major — In September 1906 William Seymour wrote that a mission in Los Angeles had locked him out. Its people then called in the head of the Holiness Association to rule on what he taught. A month later his paper said four Holiness preachers had received the new baptism.

Direct descendants

  • gave rise to Global South Pentecostal Christianity Contested Major — Scholars disagree on how far Pentecostal churches in the Global South go back to Azusa Street. In 1906 its paper said six missionaries had left for Africa. But Allan Anderson finds churches in Lagos that stressed gifts of the Spirit from 1918, led by Africans.
  • gave rise to Oneness Pentecostalism Widely Accepted Major — In 1914 Pentecostal ministers from many missions and churches formed a general council. By 1916 they were arguing over the words to say at baptism. The council told them not to fight over it. Historians trace the Oneness movement to this quarrel.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced The Black Church in America Dominant Modern Reconstruction — The Azusa Street revival began in Los Angeles in 1906. Its leader, William J. Seymour, was a Black Holiness preacher. Historians trace much of its worship, its songs and its testimony to the Black church.

Influenced

  • influenced African-Initiated Churches (Harrist; Aladura; Zionist/ZCC; Kimbanguist) Contested — Scholars do not agree on how much Africa's Zionist and prayer churches drew from Pentecostal teaching. Many of them were begun by African prophets and local leaders. They were not begun by missionaries. The debate goes on.
  • influenced The Black Church in the Twentieth Century Widely Accepted — In early 1907 Charles H. Mason, a Black preacher from Memphis, came to the Azusa Street mission in Los Angeles. The mission's paper printed his account of receiving the Spirit there. Later that year he wrote that he had been 'put out' for it.
  • influenced Charismatic Renewal & the Jesus Movement Widely Accepted — A Presbyterian report of 1969 said Pentecostal faith was in its second time of fast growth. Now it was growing in the old churches too, as the charismatic renewal.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

The revival broke out on Azusa Street in Los Angeles in April 1906. It grew from two streams already flowing together, Holiness worship and Black church worship. It spread not by hierarchy but by print. The mission's own newspaper, The Apostolic Faith, went out free and started meetings in places its founders never visited. Local papers mocked it as a "Weird Babel of Tongues." William Seymour kept leading the mission until his death in 1922. Many Pentecostal churches of the twentieth century traced their line back to what happened on that street.

Legacy

The revival's own episode closed in the 1920s, but the tradition it started did not. Many movements in this era's Pentecostal family run back through here. Scholars still argue over how far the Global South churches and Africa's independent churches do. New church bodies formed fast. The Church of God in Christ turned Pentecostal in 1907, and the Assemblies of God was formed in 1914. In 1913 a quarrel over the doctrine of God broke out inside the same young movement. It gave rise to Oneness Pentecostalism. What traveled furthest was not a doctrine but a way of meeting: testimony, song, and the expectation that anyone present might speak.
