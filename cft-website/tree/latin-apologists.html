The Latin Apologists

A Carthaginian convert defended Christians against Roman slander in Latin, and others made the same case at Rome, at Sicca, and at Nicomedia.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 4:55

Read along

After his friend Octavius died, Minucius Felix, a pleader, or court lawyer, wrote down a day from their friendship in his dialogue the Octavius. On a court holiday at the grape harvest, the two of them and Caecilius walked the shore at Ostia, near Rome. Caecilius saw an image of the god Serapis and raised a hand to his mouth, kissing it toward the god. Octavius, a Christian, scolded Minucius for letting Caecilius bow to stones smeared with oil, and the three sat on the breakwater to argue it out.

Minucius was one of the Latin Apologists, Christians of Rome and Roman Africa. From around 200 to the emperor Diocletian's reign, they defended their faith in Latin to the educated who despised it. An apology was a defense speech for the accused, and theirs answered the charges out of the pagans' own books.

The old religion is ancient and useful, Caecilius holds, and the Christians are "A people that lurks and shuns the light, silent in public, chattering in corners." By common report, he says, newcomers kill an infant hidden in flour, and their feasts end in incest when a dog tied to the lamp puts out the light.

Octavius calls them fables no one looked into. He and others like him, Octavius says, once believed them too. As lawyers they had defended the worst criminals. But sometimes they tortured Christians who confessed, to make them deny it. Christians, he says, keep one marriage, eat sober meals, and love one another as brothers, because they do not know how to hate. No temple holds God, who is unseen and everywhere, and the offering he wants is a good mind. Beaten with the philosophers' own weapons, Caecilius yields.

At Carthage around 200, Tertullian met the same charges in his Apology to the governors who judged Christians. He said torture, used on others to make them confess, was turned around on Christians, to make them deny what they were. Christians prayed for the emperors, he wrote, and gave each month to bury the poor and keep orphans, a love that branded them in the eyes of people who hated one another. Jerome, a Christian scholar writing later, says the Roman clergy's envy and insults drove Tertullian to the New Prophecy. It held that the Holy Spirit now gave a stricter rule.

In Diocletian's reign Arnobius taught rhetoric, the art of public speaking, at Sicca in Africa. The story of how he became a Christian rests on Jerome alone, who wrote about eighty years later that dreams drove him toward belief, and the bishop would not take him in. "He could not win from the bishop the faith he had always attacked. So he labored by lamplight over most brilliant books against his old religion. At last, as if with hostages for his devotion, he won the treaty." His seven books were his pledge, and he was let in. In them he owns his past: "Whenever I had seen a stone made slick and smeared with olive oil, I fawned on it as if a power were present in it. I spoke to it, and begged favors from a block that felt nothing."

Arnobius's pupil, Jerome says, was Lactantius, called to teach rhetoric at Nicomedia, a Greek city in Bithynia. He was teaching there when, Lactantius says, the emperor's officers burned the Christians' Scriptures and leveled their church, on a day picked to end Christianity. Next day an order laid every Christian, whatever his rank, open to torture. Tertullian's Apology, Lactantius granted, had pleaded the cause in full, but answering accusers was one thing and teaching the whole faith another, and he took on the second.

Later, asked why he quoted pagan books, Jerome named all four together. Their books, he said, were full of the pagans' own learning.

Of the writers known to him, Lactantius wrote, Minucius Felix had stood well among the pleaders, and his Octavius showed how fit a champion of truth he might have been, had he given himself wholly to it.

Voices in the Record

  • Tertullian of Carthage - a convert and trained rhetorician who wrote his Apology at Carthage around 200 and grew more rigorist and Montanist-aligned in his later years
  • Minucius Felix - a Roman advocate whose dialogue gives the pagan side its full case, and whose own date nobody can fix within a century
  • Arnobius of Sicca - a teacher of rhetoric who wrote seven books against the gods, by one account to convince his bishop that he meant it
  • Commodianus - who wrote catechism in acrostic verse that does not scan, and whose date is not known
  • Lactantius - Arnobius' pupil on Jerome's account, who wrote the fullest of the Latin defenses while the persecution was still running and afterwards tutored the son of the emperor who ended it

Documented Stories

The Morning the Church at Nicomedia Came Down

Lactantius was teaching Latin rhetoric at Nicomedia when, at first light on the feast of the Terminalia, the prefect arrived at the city’s church with officers and soldiers. It stood on rising ground in full view of the palace, and Lactantius says Diocletian and Galerius watched from a height, arguing about whether to burn it. Fire might spread to the houses; so the Praetorians came with axes and leveled it ‘in a few hours’. The doors were forced, the Scriptures found and burned, the building plundered. The next day the edict was posted: Christians stripped of legal standing, their books surrendered, their assemblies forbidden. A man — Lactantius gives no name; Eusebius says he was of high rank — tore the edict down, mocking it aloud as a proclamation of victories over Goths and Sarmatians; he was arrested, ‘not only tortured, but burnt alive’, and, the text says, bore it with patience. Lactantius lost his post. He wrote the Divine Institutes over the following years to make the Christian case to exactly the educated class that had watched the church come down, and in old age wrote On the Deaths of the Persecutors to record how each emperor who signed the edict died. It is a partisan book, unsparing about its enemies’ agonies; but for the first day of the persecution it is the only eyewitness.

Connections

In Its Own Record

This is the Latin counterpart to the Greek apologists of the second century, a generation later and never as coherent a body. It includes Tertullian, whose defense of the Christians, the Apology, Lactantius later said had pleaded their cause in full.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

A Carthaginian convert named Tertullian defended his faith in Latin, answering the charges against Christians in his Apology to the governors who judged them. Others made the same case: Minucius Felix, in a dialogue in which a pagan friend states the case against them, and, a century after Tertullian, Lactantius, who wrote his own defense in the middle of persecution.

Legacy

Lactantius named the Latin defenders before him and judged them. Minucius Felix, he wrote, might have been a fit champion of truth had he given himself wholly to it; Tertullian was skilled in literature of every kind but had little readiness in eloquence; and Tertullian's Apology had pleaded the cause in full, but only by answering accusers, where Lactantius set out to teach the whole faith. Eusebius, writing in Greek, quoted Tertullian's Apology from a Greek translation and called him well versed in the laws of the Romans. Jerome named Tertullian, Minucius, Arnobius and Lactantius together to defend a Christian's use of pagan books, and judged their style: Tertullian rugged and uncouth, Lactantius with a flow of eloquence worthy of Cicero, Arnobius lengthy and unequal. A later Latin list of books the church was not to receive, carried under the name of Gelasius, set the works of Tertullian, Lactantius, Commodian and Arnobius among the apocrypha. And Lactantius' Latin was good enough that the Renaissance called him the Christian Cicero and printed him before it printed most of the Fathers - the Divine Institutes was among the first books off a press in Italy, in 1465.

Sources

Doctrinal Floor Note

Not in question for the corpus as a whole. Tertullian's own later years aligned with the New Prophecy (Montanism) out of Phrygia - a real, disclosed complication, but not a floor question: the census's own Montanism entry states that, by Tertullian's account and Hippolytus's for most of them, the New Prophecy "held the same doctrine as the churches that condemned it," the dispute being one of authority and discipline, not of the Creed's own commitments.

How Sure the Record Is

Substantial evidence survives for this tradition. Minucius Felix's Octavius (about twenty-four thousand words), Commodian's Instructiones (about fifteen thousand), and Arnobius' seven books Against the Heathen (a hundred and forty thousand) survive whole. Lactantius' writings survive at even greater length: the Divine Institutes alone runs to two hundred and forty thousand words, and with the Anger of God, the Workmanship of God, and other fragments, his surviving work totals just under half a million words. Tertullian's own corpus of thirty-two works also survives — the Apology, the five books Against Marcion (his longest single work, about a hundred and eighty-four thousand words), Against Praxeas, the Prescription Against Heretics, and the rest — along with the Passion of Perpetua and Felicitas, transmitted alongside his writings. In all, well over a million words survive from these five authors together. Three of the five are barely datable: Minucius Felix within a century, Commodian within three centuries; Tertullian's own time, the reigns of Severus and Caracalla by Jerome's account, is the best anchored of the set.
