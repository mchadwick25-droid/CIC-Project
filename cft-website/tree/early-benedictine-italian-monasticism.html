The Age of Monks and Empires · c. 500-604 · Italy

Early Benedictine / Italian Monasticism

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Benedict of Nursia left Rome for a cave at Subiaco. Around 529 he ended up founding a monastery on the hill of Monte Cassino. Italy was being torn apart, first by the Gothic wars and then by the Lombard invasions. What he left behind was a rule: seventy-three short chapters on prayer, work, sleep, food, guests, discipline and the election of an abbot. Much of it, most scholars now think, was drawn from an older and harsher text, the anonymous Rule of the Master. Benedict cut it down and softened it, with an eye for what ordinary people could actually keep up. A generation later Gregory the Great, a monk before he was a pope, wrote the only account we have of Benedict's life. He also governed the Italian church through more than 850 letters, which show the same world from the other side. They tell of famine, plague, refugees, Lombard armies, and the daily running of a church trying to hold itself together.

In Its Own Record

Influenced By

  • Desert Monasticism Widely Accepted — Benedict's Rule tells monks to read 'the Lives of the Fathers' when they meet in the evening. Its last chapter names the Fathers' Lives and Basil of Caesarea's Rule as models for monks. Its editor, Cardinal Gasquet, takes these to be the Lives of the Desert Fathers.
  • Gallic Monastic-Ascetic Christianity Widely Accepted — Benedict's Rule tells monks to read the 'Collationes' when they meet in the evening. It also names the 'Institutes' as models. These are the titles of Cassian's two books. Gennadius says Cassian set up two houses for monks at Marseilles.

Influenced

  • The Roman Church in the Age of Gregory the Great Documented — Gregory the Great tells the life of Benedict in his Dialogues. He praises the rule Benedict wrote for his monks as 'excellent for discretion'. Gregory says he had his stories from four of the saint's disciples. One was Constantinus, the next abbot after him.
  • Carolingian Reform Christianity Documented Major — In 802 Charlemagne gathered abbots and monks at Aachen. There they read the Rule of Saint Benedict together. He then ordered that anything done in monasteries against the rule be put right by it. Sixty years before, in 742, Carloman's council had told monks and nuns to live by the same rule.Lasting result: In 802 Charlemagne gave an order about monasteries. Anything done in them against the Rule of Saint Benedict was to be put right by that rule.
  • Cluniac Reform Monasticism Inferential-Thin — Oswald Hunter Blair was a Benedictine editor of the Rule. In 1907 he drew on the later customs of Cluny to show how it was lived. Cardinal Gasquet, another Benedictine editor, calls the Rule practically the only rule for monks in the West from the ninth to the twelfth century. He does not name Cluny. This rests on these two editors, not on Cluny's own texts. No modern study of it has been consulted.
  • Cistercian Monasticism Inferential-Thin — The monks of Cîteaux set out to keep the Rule of St Benedict more plainly. The tie rests on a 1907 editor of the Rule, not on the early Cistercians' own words. Oswald Hunter Blair notes only that early Cistercian statutes, like the Rule, set times when monks might talk.

Sources

  • The Rule of St Benedict (RB 1980, ed. Timothy Fry) (primary) — The movement's constitutive text; RB 1980 gives Latin, English, and commentary and is the standard study edition; countless cheap translations also exist.
  • The Rule of the Master (trans. Luke Eberle, 1977) (primary) — The longer rule Benedict abbreviated and humanized - essential comparison text for what is distinctive in RB; Cistercian Studies 6.
  • Gregory the Great, Dialogues, Book II (Life of Benedict) (trans. O. Zimmerman, FOTC 39) (primary) — The only narrative of Benedict's life, written c.593; hagiographic, so it needs careful, critical reading (plus the Francis Clark authorship debate, now mostly rejected but worth noting).
  • Gregory the Great, Letters (trans. John R. C. Martyn, 3 vols, 2004) (primary) — The 850+ surviving letters - day-to-day governance of the Italian church 590-604; complete English translation (PIMS); expensive but in research libraries.
  • R. A. Markus, Gregory the Great and His World (1997) (secondary) — Standard modern portrait of Gregory's Italy - the world early Benedictine monasticism inhabited; Cambridge UP paperback.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity, vol. 2, ch. 28, 'Asceticism and monasticism, II: Western' (Marilyn Dunn) (reference) — Reference-tradition chapter for western monasticism incl. Benedict and the Master.
