The Age of Monks and Empires · c. 500-604 · Italy

Early Benedictine / Italian Monasticism

Italy, c. 500 to 604 CE — a short, practical rule for a single household of monks, written while the peninsula was being fought over, that ended up organizing Western monastic life for fifteen centuries.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:12

Read along

Benedict of Nursia left Rome for a cave at Subiaco. Around 529 he ended up founding a monastery on the hill of Monte Cassino. Italy was being torn apart, first by the Gothic wars and then by the Lombard invasions. What he left behind was a rule: seventy-three short chapters on prayer, work, sleep, food, guests, discipline and the election of an abbot. Much of it, most scholars now think, was drawn from an older and harsher text, the anonymous Rule of the Master. Benedict cut it down and softened it, with an eye for what ordinary people could actually keep up. A generation later Gregory the Great, a monk before he was a pope, wrote the only account we have of Benedict's life. He also governed the Italian church through more than 850 letters, which show the same world from the other side. They tell of famine, plague, refugees, Lombard armies, and the daily running of a church trying to hold itself together.

Documented Stories

The Monks of Vicovaro Poison the Cup

Gregory the Great tells the story in the second book of his Dialogues, written about fifty years after Benedict’s death from what the old monks of Subiaco and Monte Cassino remembered. He warned them, Gregory says, that ‘his way of life would not suit theirs’; they insisted; he came. Within a short time they hated him. His rule was strict, they had been used to doing as they pleased, and ‘they saw that under him they could no longer do what was unlawful’. They put poison in his wine. When the glass jug was brought to him at table for the customary blessing, he made the sign of the cross over it and it shattered ‘as if a stone had struck it’. Benedict, Gregory writes, understood at once what had been in it, called the brothers together, and said, without anger: ‘God forgive you, brethren. Why have you plotted this against me? Did I not tell you that my ways would not agree with yours? Go and find an abbot to your liking, for after this you cannot keep me.’ He went back to his cave. The miracle is the genre’s; what the story preserves is the memory of a reform that failed, of a community that preferred murder to discipline, and of a man who left rather than force them — and then wrote a rule whose first chapter is about the kinds of monks who will not be ruled.

Scholastica’s Storm

His sister Scholastica had been consecrated to God from childhood and lived with a community of women a few miles from Monte Cassino; once a year Benedict came down to meet her at a house on the monastery’s land, since neither would enter the other’s enclosure. That year they spent the day, Gregory says, ‘in the praises of God and spiritual talk’, and ate together as evening came on. Scholastica asked him not to leave, but to stay the night talking ‘of the joys of heaven’. Benedict refused, ‘saying that he might not by any means tarry all night out of his Abbey’. Scholastica put her hands together on the table, laid her head on them, and prayed; when she lifted her head there was thunder and lightning and rain so heavy that no one could set foot outside. Benedict grew sad and complained of his sister, saying: ‘God forgive you, what have you done?’ She answered: ‘I desired you to stay, and you would not hear me, I have desired our good Lord, and he hath vouchsafed to grant my petition.’ So they stayed up all night talking. Three days later, from his cell, he saw her soul leave her body in the form of a dove, and had her buried in the tomb he had prepared for himself. Gregory’s comment is the point he wants made: ‘of right she did more which loved more.’ It is the one story in which the author of the Rule is overruled, and the tradition kept it.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced Desert Monasticism Widely Accepted — Benedict's Rule tells monks to read 'the Lives of the Fathers' when they meet in the evening. Its last chapter names the Fathers' Lives and Basil of Caesarea's Rule as models for monks. Its editor, Cardinal Gasquet, takes these to be the Lives of the Desert Fathers.
  • influenced Gallic Monastic-Ascetic Christianity Widely Accepted — Benedict's Rule tells monks to read the 'Collationes' when they meet in the evening. It also names the 'Institutes' as models. These are the titles of Cassian's two books. Gennadius says Cassian set up two houses for monks at Marseilles.

Influenced

  • influenced The Roman Church in the Age of Gregory the Great Documented — Gregory the Great tells the life of Benedict in his Dialogues. He praises the rule Benedict wrote for his monks as 'excellent for discretion'. Gregory says he had his stories from four of the saint's disciples. One was Constantinus, the next abbot after him.
  • influenced Carolingian Reform Christianity Documented Major — In 802 Charlemagne gathered abbots and monks at Aachen. There they read the Rule of Saint Benedict together. He then ordered that anything done in monasteries against the rule be put right by it. Sixty years before, in 742, Carloman's council had told monks and nuns to live by the same rule.Lasting result: In 802 Charlemagne gave an order about monasteries. Anything done in them against the Rule of Saint Benedict was to be put right by that rule.
  • influenced Cluniac Reform Monasticism Inferential-Thin — Oswald Hunter Blair was a Benedictine editor of the Rule. In 1907 he drew on the later customs of Cluny to show how it was lived. Cardinal Gasquet, another Benedictine editor, calls the Rule practically the only rule for monks in the West from the ninth to the twelfth century. He does not name Cluny. This rests on these two editors, not on Cluny's own texts. No modern study of it has been consulted.
  • influenced Cistercian Monasticism Inferential-Thin — The monks of Cîteaux set out to keep the Rule of St Benedict more plainly. The tie rests on a 1907 editor of the Rule, not on the early Cistercians' own words. Oswald Hunter Blair notes only that early Cistercian statutes, like the Rule, set times when monks might talk.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Benedict of Nursia left Rome for a cave at Subiaco, and around 529 he founded a monastery at Monte Cassino. Italy was being torn apart by the Gothic wars and then the Lombard invasions. His Rule ran to seventy-three short chapters on prayer, work, food and the election of an abbot. Most scholars think it cut down and softened an older, harsher text, with an eye for what ordinary people could actually keep up.

Legacy

The Rule became the standard for monks in the West. Carolingian reformers made it the norm across the Frankish empire. It stands behind the Cluniacs, the Cistercians and the Benedictine congregations that keep it today. Its language of stability, obedience and moderation reached far beyond the cloister. So did the way it built the day around fixed hours of prayer, and both passed into the West's habits of work and time. Monte Cassino itself was destroyed again and again, and rebuilt every time. Paul the Deacon tells how the Lombards sacked it. The abbey's own medieval chronicle tells of a Saracen attack. An earthquake later brought it down, and Allied bombing in 1944 is reported to have destroyed it. Monks live there now.

Experience It Today

  • The Abbey of Monte Cassino, Italy — Benedict's own foundation of around 529, destroyed and rebuilt many times over and again a working monastery

How Sure the Record Is

Moderate-to-rich: the Rule, Gregory the Great's 850+ letters; Dialogues need tier care

  • The Rule of St Benedict (RB 1980, ed. Timothy Fry) (primary) — The movement's constitutive text; RB 1980 gives Latin, English, and commentary and is the standard study edition; countless cheap translations also exist.
  • The Rule of the Master (trans. Luke Eberle, 1977) (primary) — The longer rule Benedict abbreviated and humanized - essential comparison text for what is distinctive in RB; Cistercian Studies 6.
  • Gregory the Great, Dialogues, Book II (Life of Benedict) (trans. O. Zimmerman, FOTC 39) (primary) — The only narrative of Benedict's life, written c.593; hagiographic, so it needs careful, critical reading (plus the Francis Clark authorship debate, now mostly rejected but worth noting).
  • Gregory the Great, Letters (trans. John R. C. Martyn, 3 vols, 2004) (primary) — The 850+ surviving letters - day-to-day governance of the Italian church 590-604; complete English translation (PIMS); expensive but in research libraries.
  • R. A. Markus, Gregory the Great and His World (1997) (secondary) — Standard modern portrait of Gregory's Italy - the world early Benedictine monasticism inhabited; Cambridge UP paperback.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity, vol. 2, ch. 28, 'Asceticism and monasticism, II: Western' (Marilyn Dunn) (reference) — Reference-tradition chapter for western monasticism incl. Benedict and the Master.
