Suetonius

A person whose story no single tradition tells.

Outside witness

Also called Gaius Suetonius Tranquillus

A Roman biographer of the emperors, who notes that Claudius expelled Jews stirred up by "Chrestus" and that Nero punished the Christians.

About This Person

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 2:52

Read along

In his life of the emperor Claudius, the Roman scholar Suetonius lists what Claudius did. He freed the people of old Troy from tax, and he let German envoys sit with the senators at the theater. Between the two comes one sentence: "He drove from Rome the Jews who were constantly rioting at the urging of Chrestus."

Suetonius wrote his lives of twelve of Rome's rulers around 120, decades after Claudius, and the Christians get this line and one other: under Nero, they were punished. Yet these are among the very few notices of the first Christians at Rome from outside the church, and readers still argue over who Chrestus was.

Suetonius was a close friend of Pliny the Younger, and served for a time as secretary to the emperor Hadrian, until Hadrian dismissed him.

The Claudius line gives no year and no source. In the New Testament, the Acts of the Apostles tells of a Jew named Aquila and his wife, whom the apostle Paul met at Corinth. They had come from Italy "because Claudius had commanded all the Jews to depart from Rome," and historians read the two as one event. But Suetonius never says who Chrestus was, a name in use among Greeks and Romans and close in sound to Christus, the Latin for Christ. Editors read it as Christ, placed in the wrong city and reign, though one allows a Jew of that name.

The Nero line sits in another list, between rules for food shops and a ban on chariot drivers' pranks. It reads: "Punishments were inflicted on the Christians, a kind of people given to a new and harmful superstition." Superstition was his word of contempt for their religion. He names no charge and no kind of punishment, and he counts this among Nero's acts that were "beyond criticism." Elsewhere he blames Nero for the great fire, but never ties it to the Christians. Another Roman, Tacitus, tells in full of Christians put to death under Nero.

Historians still weigh both lines, and his English editors noted "how little" even this well-informed Roman knew. Long before, Christian writers had complained that Romans said their name wrong. Around 200, Tertullian, defending the Christians, told them so. "And even when you wrongly say 'Chrestianus', since you do not even know the name for certain, it is formed from sweetness or kindness." The Greek chrestos means kind, or good. Tertullian never names Suetonius; editors set the two side by side.

Seven Latin words, between old Troy's tax and the Germans' theater seats, from a man not writing about Christians at all, still read word by word for one name.

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “He drove from Rome the Jews who were constantly rioting at the urging of Chrestus.”
    As printed
    “Iudaeos impulsore Chresto assidue tumultuantis Roma expulit.”
    Suetonius, Lives of the Caesars, Claudius 25.4, Latin, with tr. John C. Rolfe (1914)
    The Latin has no word for "since", "all" or "one". Rolfe adds "since"; the Thomson and Forester English adds "all" and "one".
  • In our modern English
    “because Claudius had commanded all the Jews to depart from Rome,”
    As printed
    “because Claudius had commanded all the Jews to depart from Rome.”
    Acts 18:2, World English Bible (British Edition)
  • In our modern English
    “Punishments were inflicted on the Christians, a kind of people given to a new and harmful superstition.”
    As printed
    “afflicti suppliciis Christiani, genus hominum superstitionis novae ac maleficae”
    Suetonius, Lives of the Caesars, Nero 16.2, Latin, with tr. John C. Rolfe (1914)
    For maleficae Rolfe has "mischievous" and the Thomson and Forester English "impious"; their note takes the word to suggest witchcraft. The modern English says "harmful".
  • In our modern English
    “beyond criticism.”
    As printed
    “I have brought together these acts of his, some of which are beyond criticism,”
    Suetonius, Lives of the Caesars, Nero 19.3, tr. John C. Rolfe (1914)
  • In our modern English
    “how little”
    As printed
    “How little even the well-informed Suetonius knew of the real facts”
    Lives of the Twelve Caesars, tr. Alexander Thomson, revised by T. Forester (1909), note 335
  • In our modern English
    “And even when you wrongly say 'Chrestianus', since you do not even know the name for certain, it is formed from sweetness or kindness.”
    As printed
    “Yes, and even when it is wrongly pronounced by you “Chrestianus” (for you do not even know accurately the name you hate), it comes from sweetness and benignity.”
    Tertullian, Apology 3, in the Ante-Nicene Fathers, vol. 3
    The modern English is made from Tertullian's Latin in Franz Oehler's edition (1853). The Ante-Nicene English has "the name you hate" where the Latin says only "the name".

Legacy and Today

How they're remembered

  • John Rolfe, his translator, says Tertullian of Carthage drew on Suetonius's work about the public shows. Rolfe adds that the influence of Suetonius is seen in the form of the Life of Ambrose by Paulinus, Ambrose's secretary.

Open Questions

  • Rolfe and the notes to Thomson and Forester's edition both read "Chrestus" as Christ. Rolfe calls it another form of Christus and thinks Suetonius probably got the date wrong, though a Jew of that name may be meant. The Thomson and Forester notes say Suetonius moved Christ to Rome and into the reign of Claudius.
  • Thomson and Forester translate "one Chrestus." Rolfe notes that the Latin has no word for "one" here, and takes that as a sign that Christ is meant.
  • The Thomson and Forester notes cite Luke, in Acts 18:2, for the expulsion of the Jews by Claudius. They add that Dio Cassius says he only forbade their meetings.
  • Rolfe reports two dates for his birth: 77, from Mommsen, and 69, from Macé, which Rolfe thinks somewhat more probable. The Thomson and Forester preface puts it late in the reign of Vespasian.
  • Suetonius blames Nero for the great fire, but his notice of the Christians does not mention it.
