Suetonius

A person whose story no single tradition tells.

Outside witness

Also called Gaius Suetonius Tranquillus, Tranquillus (the name Jerome uses for him)

Suetonius was a Roman scholar who wrote the lives of Rome's rulers from Julius Caesar to Domitian. In them, two short lines tell of Jewish people driven from Rome under Claudius for rioting at the urging of Chrestus, and of punishments laid on the Christians under Nero.

About This Person

In his life of the emperor Claudius, who ruled in the first century, the Roman writer Suetonius sets down the emperor's acts one after another. Claudius freed the people of old Troy from tax forever, because, he says, they founded the Roman race, and he let German envoys sit with the senators at the theater. Between those two comes one sentence: "He drove out of Rome the Jewish people who kept rioting at the urging of Chrestus."

Suetonius was a Roman scholar who wrote the lives of Rome's rulers from Julius Caesar to Domitian. This line, and one more under Nero, is all he says of the first Christians, if this one is about them at all. Each is one item in a list of an emperor's acts, and only the second names the Christians. Later, Christians complained, without naming him, that outsiders got their name wrong, calling them "Chrestians" and Christ "Chrestus", the name in his first line. A Christian chronicle set that line down almost in his words and joined it to a story in the New Testament.

His father, in his own telling, was an army officer, and Suetonius remembers being a young man under the emperor Domitian. His lives end with that emperor, so he wrote after Domitian's death, a generation or more after both events. He later ran the emperor Hadrian's letters.

He tells the expulsion in that one sentence, with no year, number or source. The Acts of the Apostles, in the New Testament, also tells of Claudius ordering the Jewish people out of Rome. At Corinth, the apostle Paul met a Jewish man and his wife lately come from Italy because of that order. Suetonius names Chrestus without saying who he was or where. The name is close in sound to Christus, the Latin form of Christ, and it was also a real name.

In his life of Nero, Suetonius lists measures by which, he says, abuses were severely punished and put down, and new rules made. Spending was limited, and taverns were barred from selling cooked food except beans and greens. Then comes the second line: "Punishments were laid on the Christians, a kind of people holding a new and harmful superstition." Superstition was his word for a religion he despised. The list runs straight on to the chariot drivers, who were stopped from roaming the streets and cheating people for fun.

He names no charge, number or kind of punishment, and he files these among Nero's acts that deserve no blame, some even praise, before he turns to Nero's crimes. In another chapter he says Nero himself set Rome on fire, and there he names no Christians. Another Roman, Tacitus, tells at length of Christians put to death under Nero.

About 200, at Carthage in North Africa, the Christian writer Tertullian had read Suetonius and names him on other matters. In his appeal to the pagans, without naming Suetonius, he says the name "Christian" comes from anointing; in plain words, Christ means "the anointed one", for the Jewish people anointed their priests and kings with holy oil. Then he turns to them: "You say it wrong, and call us 'Chrestians', for you are not even clearly sure of the name itself. Even so, it rings of sweetness or goodness." In Greek, chrestos means good or kind.

About 725, Bede, in his chronicle of the world, set down the Claudius line almost in Suetonius's words, without Chrestus. He put it in Claudius's ninth year and added that Luke also reports it, joining it to the Acts.

That is all Suetonius set down: seven Latin words between old Troy's tax and the German envoys' seats at the theater, and nine between the taverns' beans and greens and the chariot drivers.

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “He drove out of Rome the Jewish people who kept rioting at the urging of Chrestus.”
    As printed
    “Iudaeos impulsore Chresto assidue tumultuantis Roma expulit.”
    Suetonius, Lives of the Caesars, Claudius 25.4, Latin, with tr. John C. Rolfe (1914)
    The Latin has no word for "since", "all" or "one". Rolfe adds "since"; the Thomson and Forester English adds "all" and "one".

    Suetonius, Lives of the Caesars, Nero 16.2, Latin, with tr. John C. Rolfe (1914)
    For maleficae Rolfe has "mischievous" and the Thomson and Forester English "impious". The modern English says "harmful". The Latin has no verb with Nero as its subject: punishments were laid on the Christians, under him.

  • Lives of the Caesars: twelve rulers from Julius Caesar to Domitian, with the notices in the lives of Claudius and Nero

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • About 725, Bede set down the Claudius line in his chronicle of the world almost in Suetonius's words, without Chrestus. He put it in Claudius's ninth year and added that Luke also reports it, joining it to the Acts of the Apostles.
  • Jerome wrote short lives of Christian writers, up to the fourteenth year of the emperor Theodosius. He says he was asked to follow the example of Tranquillus, and to do for Christian writers what Suetonius had done for the men of letters among the pagans.
  • In the preface to his chronicle, Jerome says he gathered much for the years from Troy to Constantine from Tranquillus and other historians.

How they're remembered

  • About 200, Tertullian of Carthage names Suetonius twice. He sends his readers to him on which games were founded for which gods, and cites him for the report that Nero never dreamed, except with difficulty at the very end of his life. He never cites the line on the Christians.
  • Tertullian complained that pagans called Christians Chrestians, and Lactantius, later, that the ignorant called Christ Chrestus. Neither names Suetonius.
  • Thierry Ruinart, a monk of Saint-Germain-des-Prés who died in 1709, used both lines against Henry Dodwell's dissertation on the fewness of martyrs, printed at Oxford in 1684. Ruinart read the Jewish people whom Claudius drove out as including Christians. Because Suetonius lists the punishment among Nero's measures, Ruinart judged that Nero must have issued a written order against the Christians. Suetonius names no such order.

Open Questions

  • Who Chrestus was. Suetonius does not say who Chrestus was, or where he was. The name is close in sound to Christus, the Latin form of Christ. Lactantius says the ignorant call Christ Chrestus by changing one letter, and Tertullian says pagans call Christians Chrestians. Neither of them names Suetonius.
  • Chrestus as a name. It was also a real name. Eusebius copies a letter from the emperor Constantine to Chrestus, bishop of Syracuse.
  • All the Jewish people. The Latin of the Claudius line says only Jewish people. It has no word for all, and none for one before Chrestus. The Acts of the Apostles says Claudius ordered all the Jewish people to leave Rome.
  • Driven out, or barred from meeting. Suetonius and Acts both say the Jewish people left Rome. Dio, a later Roman historian, is held here only in a badly damaged print of his Greek. As far as it can be read, he says Claudius did not drive the Jewish people out, but ordered them not to gather.
  • The year. Neither Suetonius nor Acts gives a year for the expulsion. Bede, writing about 725, puts it in the ninth year of Claudius's reign.
  • The same event. Neither Suetonius nor Acts says that it tells of the same order as the other. Bede joins them, adding that Luke also reports it.
  • The punishment under Nero. Suetonius names no charge, no number, no kind of punishment and no law. He files it among Nero's acts that, he says, deserve no blame, and some even praise.
  • Suetonius blames Nero for the great fire, but his notice of the Christians does not mention it.
  • Superstition. Suetonius calls the Christians' faith a new and harmful superstition, his word for a religion he despised. In his life of Claudius, the Druids' rite in Gaul is a religion.
  • Suetonius names the Christians only once, in his life of Nero.
  • The dismissal. A life of Hadrian written long after says that Hadrian replaced Suetonius, Septicius Clarus, the commander of his guard, and many others. The reason it gives is that they had behaved toward his wife Sabina, without his leave, more familiarly than the respect owed to the court allowed. No other old source tells of it.
