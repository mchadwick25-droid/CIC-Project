The Melkite Greek Catholic Split

Damascus, Aleppo and Mount Lebanon, from 1650 — one Arabic-speaking Byzantine-rite church that elected two patriarchs and never became one church again.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:56

Read along

When the Patriarch of Antioch died in 1724, the Christians of Damascus elected Cyril VI Tanas, who favored union with Rome. Constantinople refused to accept him and consecrated a rival, Sylvester, a Greek monk from Cyprus. The Antiochian patriarchate never reunited. What followed was the permanent division of one Arabic-speaking, Byzantine-rite community into two churches — one in communion with Rome, recognized by the pope in 1729, and one with Constantinople — sharing the same liturgy, the same language and frequently the same families. The Catholic side, based at Damascus, Aleppo and the monasteries of Mount Lebanon, was pressed hard by Ottoman authorities acting on Orthodox complaints, and was not granted a recognized civil standing of its own until well into the nineteenth century.

The split had a long run-up. In 1654 Patriarch Macarius of Antioch was in the Cossack lands. His journey took him to Kyiv and on to Moscow. His son Paul, an archdeacon of Aleppo, wrote the journey down.

Before 1724 some Orthodox bishops of Syria had tried to reach reunion with Rome. Fortescue, a Catholic writer, tells it. Euthymius, bishop of Tyre and Sidon, approached the Holy See. So did the patriarchs Athanasius IV and Cyril of Berrhoea. They hoped to receive the pallium. Rome found their professions of faith too weak. In 1722 a synod at Constantinople sent the Antiochene bishops a warning letter. It listed Latin heresies. No Orthodox account of these approaches to Rome was read.

Documented Stories

Patriarch Sylvester Comes to Aleppo

Aleppo in the 1720s had the largest and richest Melkite community in Syria, and for a generation its merchants and notables, taught by Jesuit, Capuchin and Franciscan missionaries and led by their own bishop, had been Catholic in conviction while remaining in the one Byzantine-rite church of Antioch, which had not yet divided. The double election of 1724, Cyril Tanas at Damascus for the Catholic party and Sylvester, a monk from Cyprus, at Constantinople for the Orthodox, made the division real, and Sylvester came to Aleppo in 1725 with an Ottoman firman as the lawful patriarch to bring the city into line. The Aleppo chronicles, Catholic and Orthodox, tell what followed differently and agree on its shape. Sylvester demanded that the clergy commemorate him and not the pope, replaced priests who would not, and, according to one account, gave a dinner in a fast season at which meat was served, to see who ate and so declare himself Orthodox and who refused and so betray himself. He then had the leading Catholic notables denounced to the Ottoman governor as rebels against their lawful head; several were imprisoned, fined heavily, or bastinadoed, and others fled to the Maronite mountain or to Europe. The persecution went on intermittently for three years. In 1728 the Catholic party obtained a counter-firman through the French consul, the community's Catholic majority reasserted itself, and Sylvester left Aleppo for Constantinople, where he complained that the Franks had stolen his church.

The see of Antioch split for good, and the Melkite Greek Catholic Church began. It is still alive today. Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

When the Patriarch of Antioch died in 1724, Damascus elected Cyril VI Tanas, who favored union with Rome, but Constantinople refused to accept him and consecrated a rival instead — permanently splitting one Arabic-speaking, Byzantine-rite community into two churches that share the same liturgy, language, and often the same families to this day. Rome recognized Cyril's line in 1729, and the resulting Melkite Greek Catholic church was pressed hard by Ottoman authorities for generations before gaining any recognized civil standing of its own.

Legacy

Both churches are alive. The Melkite Greek Catholic Church has a patriarch of Antioch today. It is found across the Middle East and in large communities abroad, in the Americas, Europe and Australia. It worships in Arabic in the Byzantine rite, in communion with Rome. The Greek Orthodox Patriarchate of Antioch lives on beside it. The split gave the Christian East one more case of a familiar pattern. An Eastern-rite church in union with Rome stands beside the Orthodox church it came from, with all the everyday strain that brings. Through Aleppo's presses and schools, the Melkites became major carriers of Arabic Christian writing.
