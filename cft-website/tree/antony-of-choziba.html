The Early Medieval Era · 7th c. · Choziba, Jericho

Antony of Choziba

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Chalcedonian Monasticism - Judean Desert & Gaza.

Known for
Monk of Choziba and disciple of George of Choziba, whose Life he wrote in Greek. It tells of the Persian attack on the holy city and of monastic life at Choziba. The Bollandist heading also gives him the Miracles of the Mother of God at Choziba.
Dates
Seventh century. He lived through the Persian invasion and wrote after George's death. No source used here gives a year for his birth, his arrival at Choziba or his death
Place
His father's house, which he does not name; the monastery of Choziba; the guesthouse at Jericho, where he was cellarer after the invasion; the holy city, where George then lived
Role
Monk and cellarer; recorder of his teacher George and of the monastery of Choziba

About This Person

A young man left his father's house in secret, with a single companion and none of his family knowing. The two meant to go to the monks of Raithou. Saracens were raiding the public roads, so on the advice of holy fathers they went to the monastery of Choziba instead. There they were received and soon given the monk's tonsure. Later his companion slipped away to Raithou, and he was left grieving.

He tells it himself. He is Antony of Choziba, a monk who lived through the Persian attack on Jerusalem. He wrote the Life of his teacher, George of Choziba, in Greek. The Bollandists edited it in 1888, with a Latin translation. In the Life, George calls him his child Antony, and when dying he asks where Antony is.

When Antony wanted to follow his companion, George took him aside one Sunday. It is not the place they look for in a monk, he said, but the way of life. And not father or mother, kin or friends, but the will. On that great and fearful day, no one would stand by us but each man's deeds.

Antony tells the Persian invasion through George. The Persians besieged the holy city, and some fled to Arabia. In a ravine the soldiers killed abba Stephen the Syrian, an old man of a hundred or more. Seeing George poor, weak and modest, they let him go.

After the invasion the monks settled in the guesthouse at Jericho, and Antony was made cellarer, the keeper of stores. There, he writes, dealing with worldly people and women, he was fiercely tempted. He went in secret to George in the holy city and told him his struggle. George prayed over him, Antony says, and by word and deed healed him and kept him near.

The Bollandists' volume also prints, under his name, miracles of the Mother of God at Choziba. At the end he says he is not wise, nor able in speech. Yet God, rich and generous, did not disdain to pour on him, useless and foolish, this small drop of blessing.

Key Works

  • Life of George of Choziba: the life of his teacher, told in large part from what he saw and from what George told him. In Greek, edited by the Bollandists in 1888, with a Latin translation
  • Miracles of the Mother of God at Choziba: stories of the monastery's church, which the Bollandist heading gives to Antony as author. Printed after the Life in the same volume

Legacy

What traces to them

  • His Life is the one full held account of George of Choziba; Nikodemos the Hagiorite's Synaxaristes gives George a short notice. It is also one of the held accounts of the Persian invasion as the desert monks lived it.

How they're remembered

  • The Bollandist editors find that the Life plainly bears the marks of sincerity, and note the Graeco-barbarous words in Antony's writing.
  • Antony himself closes the Life by asking the fathers to pray for him, a sinner and the least of them.

What Is Uncertain

  • Everything the held sources say of him comes from the Life and from the Miracles printed under his name. No other held source names him.
  • His name appears in the Life where George speaks to him, and in the Bollandist headings.
  • The Miracles of the Mother of God at Choziba are his by the Bollandist heading.
  • When he came to Choziba. He set out while Saracens were raiding the roads, and after the Persian invasion he was cellarer at Jericho. The held text does not fix whether he arrived before or after the invasion.
  • Whether he saw the Persians in the ravine himself. He tells that part of George.
  • The Greek of the held copy is badly damaged. Some passages are clear only in the Latin translation printed with it.
