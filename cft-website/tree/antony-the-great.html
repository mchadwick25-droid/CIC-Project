The Imperial Church Era · 251-356 · Egypt

Antony the Great

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Desert Monasticism.

Known for
The Egyptian hermit whose withdrawal to the desert, and the Life written of him, drew monks after him and spread monasticism east and west.
Also called
Antony of Egypt, Antony the Abbot
Dates
Born 251, died 356, in Ernest Cushing Richardson's note to Jerome; the Life gives his age at death as "near a hundred and five"
Place
Coma, near Heraclea in Egypt (Sozomen), and the Egyptian desert
Role
Hermit and father of the desert monks

About This Person

Less than six months after his parents died, a young Egyptian named Antony walked into church. He had been pondering how the first disciples left everything to follow Christ. Just then he heard the Gospel being read: "If thou wouldest be perfect, go and sell that thou hast and give to the poor." Antony took it as spoken to him. He gave away the family land, three hundred acres, and sold the rest. Keeping a little for his sister, he set out to live as an ascetic near his village.

The church has long read the Life of Antony, which tells this, as the work of Athanasius of Alexandria. Archibald Robertson, who edited it, says the authorship is still disputed. He holds that "the external evidence for the Vita is too strong to allow us to set it aside as spurious." In the text he prints, the writer was Antony's attendant and "poured water on his hands." Robertson notes that some manuscripts make the attendant someone else. The book is a work of praise, and Robertson grants there is "idealisation, panegyric" in it.

Ernest Cushing Richardson, in a note to Jerome, gives Antony's years as 251 to 356. He spent almost all of them in the Egyptian desert. He learned from an old hermit near his village, then withdrew step by step. He went to the tombs, then to a deserted fort, and at last to a mountain deep in the inner desert. There he fought the demons the Life describes at length. Others began to follow him, so that, in its words, "the desert was colonised by monks."

Under the persecution of Maximinus he came to Alexandria and served the confessors in the prisons, longing to be martyred himself. Years later he came again to denounce the Arians, "saying that their heresy was the last of all." When the emperor Constantine and his sons wrote to him, Antony was unmoved. "Do not be astonished if an emperor writes to us," he told the monks, "for he is a man."

Sozomen says Antony came from Coma, near Heraclea. Jerome of Strido says he sent seven letters in Coptic to monasteries. He died at about a hundred and five. He asked two companions to bury him secretly, so that no one would make a shrine of his body. The Life closes on what it says his fame proved, that God "maketh His own known everywhere."

Key Works

  • Seven Letters: letters in Coptic to various monasteries, which Jerome of Strido says were translated into Greek; none is held in this Library

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Monks who settled the Egyptian desert in his lifetime, so that the Life says "the desert was colonised by monks."
  • A pattern of withdrawal that spread west. In his Confessions, Augustine of Hippo tells how two court officials read the Life and resolved to leave the court and serve God, and how hearing of Antony moved him.

How they're remembered

  • The Life of Antony, long read as the work of Athanasius of Alexandria, carried his name across the Latin West.
  • Jerome of Strido modelled his own Life of Hilarion on it, and Robertson notes it "kindled the flame of monastic aspirations" at Rome and across the West.

What Is Uncertain

  • Antony's life is known chiefly from the Life of Antony, a work of praise. Archibald Robertson, its editor, grants there is "idealisation, panegyric" in it.
  • The Life's writer says he was Antony's attendant and "poured water on his hands." Robertson notes that some manuscripts, Greek and Latin, make the attendant someone else, and that the text "requires further sifting."
  • Who wrote the Life is disputed. The church has long given it to Athanasius of Alexandria. Robertson holds the dispute open but judges "the external evidence for the Vita is too strong to allow us to set it aside as spurious." He also records that Gwatkin went so far as to pronounce that Antony "never existed," and calls that far ahead of the evidence.
  • Jerome says Antony's seven Coptic letters were translated into Greek. None is held in this Library, so Antony's own voice cannot be checked here against the Life.
