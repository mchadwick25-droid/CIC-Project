The Imperial Church Era · 251-356 · Egypt

Antony the Great

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Desert Monasticism.

Also called Antony of Egypt, Antony the Abbot

The Egyptian hermit whose withdrawal to the desert, and the Life written of him, drew monks after him and spread monasticism east and west.

Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition→Desert Monasticism→no descendant recorded

About This Person

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 2:41

Read along

A young man named Antony, in a village in Egypt, walked into church thinking of the apostles who left everything to follow Jesus. Inside, he heard the Gospel tell a man to sell what he had and give to the poor, and he took it as spoken to him. He gave his family's fields to the villagers and sold the rest for the poor.

He went ever further into the Egyptian desert to live by prayer, work with his hands, and fasting. At last he walled himself into a ruined fort for nearly twenty years. When many wanted to live as he did, his friends forced the door. He persuaded many to take up that life and directed them as a father, until the desert became a city of monks.

One book carried that life far from Egypt. Most of what we know comes from it, a Life written soon after his death, which the church has long credited to Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria. It was written for monks in other lands, as a pattern for their own life, and it was soon put into Latin. Preaching later in the same century, Gregory of Nazianzus called it the laws of the monastic life, set out as a story.

The Life tells how crowds came to him for healing, and how he moved three days further into the desert, to a mountain. There he lived on into the time of the emperor Constantine and his sons, who wrote to him. The Arians taught that the Son of God was created. When they claimed he agreed with them, he came down to Alexandria and taught that the Son was not created.

A historian writing nearly a century later says his disciples flourished in Egypt, Libya, Palestine, Syria and Arabia. And in Augustine's own account, two officials of the emperor's court at Trier found the Life in a monks' cottage and read it. That very hour they resolved to leave the court and serve God. Pontitianus, a fellow official who was there that day, told the story to Augustine in Milan. Augustine never met Antony, but in a garden there, in tears, he remembered him. Then Augustine opened Paul's letters and read, and his doubt was gone. Later he wrote of it in a book addressed to God.

"For I had heard this of Antony. He had come in by chance during the Gospel reading, and was urged on by it. It was as though the words read were spoken to him. 'Go, sell all that you have, give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven. And come, follow me.' By such a divine word he was at once turned to you."

Quotations and references

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “For I had heard this of Antony. He had come in by chance during the Gospel reading, and was urged on by it. It was as though the words read were spoken to him. 'Go, sell all that you have, give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven. And come, follow me.' By such a divine word he was at once turned to you.”
    As printed
    “audieram enim de Antonio, quod ex euangelica lectione, cui forte superuenerat, admonitus fuerit, tamquam sibi diceretur quod legebatur: uade, uende omnia, quae habes, da pauperibus et habebis thesaurum in caelis; et ueni, sequere me, et tali oraculo confestim ad te esse conuersum.”
    Augustine, Confessions VIII.12.29, Latin, CSEL 33
    "Urged on" renders admonitus, which means warned or prompted. The Pilkington English (npnf101, lines 13117-13124) reads "he received the admonition as if what was read were addressed to him". "Divine word" renders oraculum, which Pilkington gives as "oracle"; "you" is God, to whom the Confessions are addressed. The footnotes and page head between lines 13473 and 13501 are left out.

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Monks who settled the Egyptian desert in his lifetime, so that the Life says "the desert was colonised by monks."
  • A pattern of withdrawal that spread west. In his Confessions, Augustine of Hippo tells how two court officials read the Life and resolved to leave the court and serve God, and how hearing of Antony moved him.

How they're remembered

  • The Life of Antony, long read as the work of Athanasius of Alexandria, carried his name across the Latin West.
  • Jerome of Strido modeled his own Life of Hilarion on it, and Robertson notes it "kindled the flame of monastic aspirations" at Rome and across the West.

Open Questions

  • Antony's life is known chiefly from the Life of Antony, a work of praise. Archibald Robertson, its editor, grants there is "idealisation, panegyric" in it.
  • The Life's writer says he was Antony's attendant and "poured water on his hands." Robertson notes that some manuscripts, Greek and Latin, make the attendant someone else, and that the text "requires further sifting."
  • Who wrote the Life is disputed. The church has long given it to Athanasius of Alexandria. Robertson holds the dispute open but judges "the external evidence for the Vita is too strong to allow us to set it aside as spurious." He also records that Gwatkin went so far as to pronounce that Antony "never existed," and calls that far ahead of the evidence.
  • Jerome says Antony's seven Coptic letters were translated into Greek. None is held in this Library, so Antony's own voice cannot be checked here against the Life.
