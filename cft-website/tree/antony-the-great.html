The Imperial Church Era · 251-356 · Egypt

Antony the Great

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Desert Monasticism.

Known for
The Egyptian hermit whose withdrawal to the desert, and the Life written of him, drew monks after him and spread monasticism east and west.
Also called
Antony of Egypt, Antony the Abbot
Dates
Born 251, died 356, in Ernest Cushing Richardson's note to Jerome; the Life gives his age at death as "near a hundred and five"
Place
Coma, near Heraclea in Egypt (Sozomen), and the Egyptian desert
Role
Hermit and father of the desert monks

In Egypt, a young man walked to church thinking about the apostles, who had left everything to follow Jesus. Inside, he heard the Gospel's call to sell what you have and give to the poor, and Antony took it as spoken to him. That choice led him into the life of Egypt's desert monks, and he lived on into the time of the emperor Constantine and his sons.

His wealthy Christian parents had died months before, when he was about eighteen or twenty, leaving him the farm. After giving the fields to the villagers and selling the rest for the poor, he learned from nearby hermits, working with his hands and eating bread and salt once a day.

Most of what we know comes from one book, a Life written soon after his death, which the church has long credited to Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria. It tells how he went further, to a deserted fort across the river, where he walled up the door, and bread was let down to him twice a year. After about twenty years, because many wanted to live as he did, people who knew him forced the door. He came out, it says, the same as before, and so many followed him that "the desert became a city of monks."

Crowds seeking healing drove him three days into the desert, to a mountain with a spring. Yet when Arians claimed he agreed with them, he came down to Alexandria and taught that the Son of God was not created. Near a hundred and five, he asked to be buried in secret, so that no one could keep his body in a house, as Egyptians kept their holy dead.

Written for monks abroad as a pattern to follow, the Life was soon read in Latin. It asks how a man hidden on a mountain came to be heard of in Spain, Gaul, Rome and Africa. At Trier, two court officials read it in a monks' cottage and left the court that day to serve God.

A generation after Antony died, in Milan, an official who had been at Trier told Augustine what happened there. Soon after, weeping in a garden, Augustine remembered Antony and that same Gospel reading. Writing to God in his Confessions, he recalled: "For I had heard this of Antony. He had come in by chance during the Gospel reading, and was urged on by it. It was as though the words read were spoken to him. 'Go, sell all that you have, give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven. And come, follow me.' By such a divine word he was at once turned to you."

There in the garden, Augustine opened Paul's letters and read, and his doubt was gone.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Monks who settled the Egyptian desert in his lifetime, so that the Life says "the desert was colonised by monks."
  • A pattern of withdrawal that spread west. In his Confessions, Augustine of Hippo tells how two court officials read the Life and resolved to leave the court and serve God, and how hearing of Antony moved him.

How they're remembered

  • The Life of Antony, long read as the work of Athanasius of Alexandria, carried his name across the Latin West.
  • Jerome of Strido modeled his own Life of Hilarion on it, and Robertson notes it "kindled the flame of monastic aspirations" at Rome and across the West.

What Is Uncertain

  • Antony's life is known chiefly from the Life of Antony, a work of praise. Archibald Robertson, its editor, grants there is "idealisation, panegyric" in it.
  • The Life's writer says he was Antony's attendant and "poured water on his hands." Robertson notes that some manuscripts, Greek and Latin, make the attendant someone else, and that the text "requires further sifting."
  • Who wrote the Life is disputed. The church has long given it to Athanasius of Alexandria. Robertson holds the dispute open but judges "the external evidence for the Vita is too strong to allow us to set it aside as spurious." He also records that Gwatkin went so far as to pronounce that Antony "never existed," and calls that far ahead of the evidence.
  • Jerome says Antony's seven Coptic letters were translated into Greek. None is held in this Library, so Antony's own voice cannot be checked here against the Life.
