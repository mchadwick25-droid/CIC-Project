Ruricius of Limoges

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Merovingian Gallic Christianity.

Known for
Gallic nobleman drawn to the religious life by Faustus of Riez, later bishop of Limoges, whose two books of letters to bishops, friends and family survive with letters written to him.
Also called
Ruricius Lemovicensis
Dates
Bishop of Limoges from 485, by August Engelbrecht's reckoning from a letter of Faustus. Engelbrecht dates his marriage before 468 and his turn to the religious life before 479, and, with Bruno Krusch, puts his death not long after 507. No ancient source used here gives the year of his birth, his election or his death
Place
Gourdon, near Cahors, where Engelbrecht says he lived as a layman; Limoges, in Gaul, as its bishop
Role
Bishop of Limoges; letter writer

About This Person

Bishops of Gaul met at Agde under Caesarius of Arles, in 506, and Ruricius of Limoges did not come. Caesarius wrote that he had been deeply troubled by the absence. Ruricius's letter had gone to Agde with a deacon of Verus, bishop of Tours, but Caesarius did not recall receiving it. Ruricius should, he wrote, have sent someone to sign in his place. Yet Caesarius held nothing against him.

Ruricius answered, in modern words, "Infirmity did this, not will." Caesarius himself, he wrote, had seen him worn out at Bordeaux, and in winter, when he was usually stronger.

Ruricius was bishop of Limoges in Gaul. August Engelbrecht, who edited his letters with those of Faustus of Riez, dates his election to 485 from one of Faustus's letters. Venantius Fortunatus wrote an epitaph for him and for his grandson, also bishop of Limoges. It joins the two to the Anicii, a great house of Rome. Sidonius Apollinaris wrote the poem for his wedding to Iberia.

Faustus, Engelbrecht says, drew him to the religious life. Ruricius's first letter to Faustus says he had long known him by fame. "My soul has clung to you, outstanding teacher," he writes, in modern words, borrowing a psalm. He asks that Faustus's letters be harsher food, fit for a sinner's sickness, in place of the sweet dishes sent so far. From exile Faustus rejoiced that Ruricius had turned the prow of salvation toward the harbor of religion.

Two books of his letters survive. Several speak of his poor health. Engelbrecht thinks he died not long after 507, when he was too frail to reach a council at Toulouse. Fortunatus says each Ruricius founded a church in his time, one to Augustine and one to Peter.

Limoges was a small city. Ruricius wrote to Caesarius, in modern words, "It is far better and far more distinguished for a city to become known by its bishop than a bishop by his city."

Key Works

  • Letters (Epistulae), in two books: letters to Faustus of Riez, Sidonius Apollinaris, Caesarius of Arles and other bishops, to friends and to his sons and kin, printed with letters written to him by Faustus, Caesarius, Sedatus of Nîmes and others

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Venantius Fortunatus, in an epitaph for the two bishops named Ruricius, grandfather and grandson, says each founded a church at Limoges, one to Augustine and one to Peter. Engelbrecht takes the elder to be the founder of Augustine's.
  • August Engelbrecht edits his letters from a ninth-century manuscript at St Gall, which also keeps the letters Faustus of Riez wrote to him and to others.

How they're remembered

  • Faustus of Riez, writing from exile, calls Ruricius's turn to the religious life the first gift God gave him there.
  • Venantius Fortunatus joins the two Ruricii to the Anicii of Rome and says they sent their wealth ahead to heaven by giving it to the poor.

What Is Uncertain

  • His life comes almost wholly from his letters and those written to him, as gathered by August Engelbrecht, and his years are Engelbrecht's reckoning. The year 485 for his election rests on Engelbrecht's reading of a letter Faustus wrote soon after returning from exile, to Ruricius newly made bishop, and on his guess that Faustus came home in 485, when King Euric died, if Euric had exiled him.
  • The council he missed. Caesarius's letter names Agde. Its acts date the council to the twenty-second year of King Alaric, and some copies add the consul Messala. August Engelbrecht reckons this as 506. The Spanish era printed with the acts, 644, does not fit that year. Engelbrecht and an unsigned prolegomenon in Migne's Caesarius tie Ruricius's answer, that infirmity kept him away, to that council; Jacques Basnage, as that prolegomenon reports, held that he stayed away on purpose, which it calls invented. Ruricius also missed the council of Toulouse planned for the next year, as Sedatus of Nîmes's letter shows.
  • His death. Engelbrecht, with Bruno Krusch, thinks he died not long after 507, since he was then too frail to travel. No line used here gives the year.
  • Exile. Some scholars, Engelbrecht reports, held that Ruricius was exiled as bishop, from a phrase in one of his letters. Engelbrecht rejects this, since no other letter mentions an exile.
  • Which Ruricius. His grandson, also named Ruricius, was later bishop of Limoges too, by Venantius Fortunatus's epitaph. The index to Fortunatus's works in the Monumenta edition names the grandson as the Ruricius who signs at the councils of Clermont in 535 and Orléans in 541, and through his archdeacon at Orléans in 549, which Friedrich Maassen's edition prints.
