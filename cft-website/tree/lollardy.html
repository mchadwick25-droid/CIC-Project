The Late Medieval Era · c. 1380-1520 · England

England, today in United Kingdom

Lollardy

England, c. 1380 to 1520 — artisans and household readers who wanted the Bible in English, and got a century and a half of heresy trials for it.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:26

Read along

Lollardy grew out of the teaching of John Wycliffe, an Oxford theologian. He attacked the church's wealth and the pope's authority. He denied transubstantiation, the belief that the bread and wine become Christ's body and blood. And he said the Bible should be open to all in English. But the movement soon became much wider than him. Most of its followers were ordinary people. They were weavers, smiths, tailors, wives and widows who owned or shared the Bible in English. They met in one another's houses to read it aloud. They doubted images, pilgrimage and the priest's power over the sacrament. The response was severe. In 1401 burning for heresy became law in England. In 1409 Archbishop Arundel's rules banned any English Bible made without a license. Sir John Oldcastle's failed rising in 1414 made Lollardy treason as well as heresy. After that the movement went underground. It lived on in scattered households, linked to each other, into the 1520s. Almost everything known about them comes from the records kept by those who tried them. To convict someone, you first had to write down what they said.

Documented Stories

William Thorpe Argues with the Archbishop

Thorpe had been a priest of Wycliffite views for more than twenty years, imprisoned before and released, when he was arrested for preaching in St Chad’s, Shrewsbury, in April 1407 and brought before Thomas Arundel, archbishop of Canterbury, at Saltwood in August. His Testimony, written afterwards and circulated in manuscript, gives the examination as dialogue. Arundel is not a caricature in it: he argues, loses his temper, recovers, offers Thorpe the chance to submit and be sent to preach with a license. Thorpe refuses the terms — he will not swear on a book, since an oath by a creature is idolatry; he will not accept that the bread on the altar ceases to be bread; he will not condemn those who decline to pay tithes to unworthy priests, or agree that pilgrimage with its bagpipes and taverns is devotion, or that images should be worshipped. He describes the archbishop’s clerks mocking him, and the archbishop asking him whether he thought himself wiser than the whole church. He also describes Arundel saying, wearily, that he had been too soft with such men in the past. The Testimony survives in one English and one Latin manuscript and was printed in 1530 by the reformers, who claimed him. It is a partisan text and it gives its author the last word in every exchange; it is also the only place a fifteenth-century English heretic tells, in his own voice and at length, what it was like to stand in the room.

What Hawisia Moone Was Made to Say

Between 1428 and 1431 Bishop William Alnwick of Norwich tried some sixty people from the villages of south Norfolk for heresy, and his court-book survives. Her abjuration is in English, in the first person, and it begins by admitting that she had ‘been right homely and privy’ with the heretics — she names them, William White, Hugh Pye, and the rest — had received them in her house, ‘kept and concealed them’, and been ‘taught, learned and informed’ by them. Then it lists what she was taught: that the sacrament of baptism in water is of no avail if the parents are Christian; that confession should be made to God alone; that no priest has power to make Christ’s body, and that the bread after the words remains ‘material bread’; that marriage is consent between a man and a woman without a priest; that every good man and woman is a priest; that images should not be worshipped nor pilgrimages made, and that money spent on them were better given to the poor; that it is lawful to eat meat in Lent; and that the pope is Antichrist. The same articles, in nearly the same words, appear in a dozen other abjurations from the same court — the clerks had a template. What can be known is that her house was a school, that she named her teachers, and that she went home flogged rather than burned.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Lollardy began with John Wycliffe, a scholar at Oxford. He attacked the wealth of the church and the power of the pope. His teaching grew into a movement carried mainly by ordinary people. Weavers, smiths, tailors, wives and widows met in each other's houses to read the Bible aloud in English. In 1401 England made burning the law for heresy. After a failed rising in 1414, the movement was driven underground. Yet the trial records made to convict Lollards ended up keeping some of the fullest accounts of what ordinary believers of the time thought. Around two hundred and fifty handwritten copies of the Wycliffite Bible still survive. That is more than of any other Middle English text.

Legacy

Historians still argue over whether Lollardy fed straight into the English Reformation. What is not in doubt is that Lollard households were still on trial in the 1520s. In 1521 the bishop of Lincoln questioned many around Amersham in Buckinghamshire. Some had learned from Wyclif's Wicket, a short English tract on the sacrament. The Protestant writer John Foxe said this was before Luther's name was heard there. The movement's most solid survival is its books. Around two hundred and fifty handwritten copies of the Wycliffite Bible still exist, more than of any other Middle English text. It is a strange sort of monument for a movement its enemies thought they had stamped out.

Sources

Doctrinal Floor Note

No creedal question: Lollards disputed the church's wealth, its authority and its sacramental teaching, not the Creed. Nor is this simply one man's following — the movement outlasted Wycliffe by well over a century, and what the trial records preserve are ordinary believers' own scripture-centered convictions rather than an Oxford theologian's system.

How Sure the Record Is

Hostile trial records preserving ordinary artisans' and women's stated beliefs, plus real internal texts

  • John Wyclif's own corpus: Trialogus (trans. Stephen E. Lahey, CUP, 2013), with the Latin works in the Wyclif Society editions (primary) — The academic source the movement outgrew — kept distinct here from the movement's own later texts, since Lollardy demonstrably became more than Wyclif's own teaching.
  • Anne Hudson (ed.), Selections from English Wycliffite Writings (CUP, 1978; repr. Toronto MART, 1997) (primary) — Twenty-seven texts of 1385-1425 by the movement itself - Twelve Conclusions, Bible tracts, polemic, doctrine.
  • English Wycliffite Sermons (ed. Hudson & Gradon, 5 vols, Oxford, 1983-96) (primary) — The movement's formation machinery: a complete vernacular preaching corpus for the liturgical year.
  • Norman Tanner (ed.), Heresy Trials in the Diocese of Norwich, 1428-31 (Camden 4th ser. 20, 1977) (primary) — These are the persecutors' own records, including abjurations extracted under coercion — hostile evidence, but evidence that still preserves ordinary artisans' and women's stated beliefs (Hawisia Moone's recorded confession is a digest of lay Lollard belief). Foxe's later martyrology is a partisan, later account.
  • Anne Hudson, The Premature Reformation (Oxford, 1988), with Shannon McSheffrey, Gender and Heresy (1995) (secondary) — The field's standard, plus the contested question of women's actual place in Lollard communities.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 4, ch. 28 'Wycliffism and Lollardy' (Ghosh) (reference) — A dedicated chapter, a direct fit for the 1380-1520 period covered here. The contested question of Lollardy's influence on the English Reformation belongs to the Reformation period itself.
