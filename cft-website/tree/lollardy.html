The Late Medieval Era · c. 1380-1520 · England

England, today in United Kingdom

Lollardy

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Lollardy grew out of the teaching of John Wycliffe, an Oxford theologian. He attacked the church's wealth and the pope's authority. He denied transubstantiation, the belief that the bread and wine become Christ's body and blood. And he said the Bible should be open to all in English. But the movement soon became much wider than him. Most of its followers were ordinary people. They were weavers, smiths, tailors, wives and widows who owned or shared the Bible in English. They met in one another's houses to read it aloud. They doubted images, pilgrimage and the priest's power over the sacrament. The response was severe. In 1401 burning for heresy became law in England. In 1409 Archbishop Arundel's rules banned any English Bible made without a license. Sir John Oldcastle's failed rising in 1414 made Lollardy treason as well as heresy. After that the movement went underground. It lived on in scattered households, linked to each other, into the 1520s. Almost everything known about them comes from the records kept by those who tried them. To convict someone, you first had to write down what they said.

In Its Own Record

Influenced

  • The Hussite & Bohemian Brethren Movement Widely Accepted — David Schaff put Hus's On the Church into English. He reports that Hus took paragraph after paragraph from Wyclif's book on the same subject. On 6 July 1415 the Council of Constance condemned Wyclif's errors. It sentenced Hus the same day. Wyclif's teaching began Lollardy. But this shows his influence on Hus, not that English Lollard groups shaped the Bohemians.
  • The English Reformation & Elizabethan Settlement Documented — In 1521 the bishop of Lincoln tried households around Amersham who had learned from Wyclif's Wicket, an English tract. The Protestant writer John Foxe said this came before Luther's name was heard there. Historians still disagree how far the Lollards fed the English Reformation.

Opposed By

  • The Latin Papal Church (Avignon to Fifth Lateran) Documented Major — In 1409 Archbishop Thomas Arundel issued church rules for England. They ban any of John Wyclif's writings that the church has not cleared. They speak of a province 'defiled with a new and damnable name of Lollardy'. In 1415 the Council of Constance condemned forty-five articles of Wyclif.Lasting result: In July 1415 the Council of Constance sat to judge the errors of John Wyclif. It condemned forty-five of his articles.

Sources

  • John Wyclif's own corpus: Trialogus (trans. Stephen E. Lahey, CUP, 2013), with the Latin works in the Wyclif Society editions (primary) — The academic source the movement outgrew — kept distinct here from the movement's own later texts, since Lollardy demonstrably became more than Wyclif's own teaching.
  • Anne Hudson (ed.), Selections from English Wycliffite Writings (CUP, 1978; repr. Toronto MART, 1997) (primary) — Twenty-seven texts of 1385-1425 by the movement itself - Twelve Conclusions, Bible tracts, polemic, doctrine.
  • English Wycliffite Sermons (ed. Hudson & Gradon, 5 vols, Oxford, 1983-96) (primary) — The movement's formation machinery: a complete vernacular preaching corpus for the liturgical year.
  • Norman Tanner (ed.), Heresy Trials in the Diocese of Norwich, 1428-31 (Camden 4th ser. 20, 1977) (primary) — These are the persecutors' own records, including abjurations extracted under coercion — hostile evidence, but evidence that still preserves ordinary artisans' and women's stated beliefs (Hawisia Moone's recorded confession is a digest of lay Lollard belief). Foxe's later martyrology is a partisan, later account.
  • Anne Hudson, The Premature Reformation (Oxford, 1988), with Shannon McSheffrey, Gender and Heresy (1995) (secondary) — The field's standard, plus the contested question of women's actual place in Lollard communities.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 4, ch. 28 'Wycliffism and Lollardy' (Ghosh) (reference) — A dedicated chapter, a direct fit for the 1380-1520 period covered here. The contested question of Lollardy's influence on the English Reformation belongs to the Reformation period itself.
