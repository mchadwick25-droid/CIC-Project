Methodius of Olympus

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Anatolian Church in the Third Century.

Lycian bishop and martyr who argued against Origen that the same body rises, and who praised the life of chastity.

About This Person

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 2:17

Read along

Around the year 300, Methodius was a bishop in Lycia, in the south of Asia Minor. He belonged to the Anatolian church of the third century. Origen had been dead some fifty years, and his books still had critics and defenders. Methodius wrote against him, and two of Origen's admirers wrote about what he did.

Socrates of Constantinople says Methodius attacked Origen in many places and then, as if taking it all back, praised him in a dialogue. Eusebius of Caesarea, in a defense of Origen that Jerome quotes, asked how Methodius dared to attack Origen now, after all he had said of his teaching. Both men admired Origen, so neither is a neutral witness.

Little else about Methodius is certain. Jerome says he was bishop of Olympus and later of Tyre. Methodius wrote a Banquet of the Ten Virgins, in which ten women in turn praise the chaste life.

His chief quarrel with Origen was over the body. Origen had taught, Photius of Constantinople reports, that the soul was given a body as a chain after its fall. In On the Resurrection, Methodius answered that the body is no chain, and that a person is soul and body joined. He pictures sin as a wild fig tree grown into a temple's stones. Death tears the tree out so the temple can stand again. Then the flesh is raised, he writes, "with the same parts, uninjured and immortal, while sin is utterly and entirely destroyed." Decades later, Epiphanius of Salamis wrote against Origen and copied Methodius word for word instead of arguing it himself.

Methodius also answered Porphyry, who had written against the Christians. That reply is lost, but Jerome counted it among the answers that had met Porphyry well. A century and more later, Theodoret of Cyrrhus kept a passage from his sermon on the martyrs. He named him among the bishops and martyrs whose words witness to the church's faith in Christ. Jerome says Methodius died a martyr at Chalcis.

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Epiphanius of Cyprus made great use of him in his own refutations of Origen.
  • Most of what he wrote has been lost. His books against Porphyry of Tyre survive only in slight fragments, and his commentaries are lost. The longer pieces of On the Resurrection come to us through Epiphanius and Photius.
  • Theodoret copied a passage from his sermon on the martyrs into a set of testimonies from earlier bishops. In it Methodius of Olympus says martyrdom is so precious that Christ himself honored it.
  • The complete Banquet was first printed at Rome in 1656, from a manuscript in the Vatican.

How they're remembered

  • Remembered chiefly as Origen's opponent. The church historian Socrates of Constantinople names him first among the four who reviled Origen.
  • Honored as a martyr. Jerome says he was crowned with martyrdom at Chalcis, and Theodoret calls him the holy Methodius, bishop and martyr.

Open Questions

  • Jerome knew two dates for his death. The later one, around 312, fits his writing against Origen after Origen's death, and the earlier one, under Decius, does not. Some place Chalcis in Syria rather than Greece, because Syria lay nearer his diocese. Jerome makes him bishop of Olympus and later of Tyre, while an editor's note to Theodoret's Eranistes says Olympus and then Patara. Epiphanius also calls him Eubulius, which may only be the pen name he used in his Banquet.
  • Socrates of Constantinople tells of his change of heart, and Socrates admired Origen. He names the dialogue in which Methodius of Olympus praised him: Xenon. Eusebius of Caesarea, as Jerome quotes him, asks how Methodius dared to write against Origen now. Jerome reads this as the same charge, but Eusebius's words do not themselves say that Methodius praised Origen.
