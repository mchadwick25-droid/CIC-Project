Methodius of Olympus

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Anatolian Church in the Third Century.

Also called Eubulius (the second name Epiphanius gives him)

Methodius of Olympus was a bishop in Lycia, in the south of Asia Minor. He wrote the Banquet, in which ten young women praise the unmarried life given to God, and a dialogue against Origen arguing that the same body will rise.

About This Person

Methodius of Olympus, a bishop who died a martyr at the end of the last persecution, Jerome says, wrote a book called the Banquet. In it a young woman and two friends climb a rough, steep path to a garden where ten guests will meet. A tall woman in a robe white as snow embraces them like a mother, and her name means Virtue. They feast under a wide, shady tree, and she asks each guest to praise the virgin life.

Methodius was bishop of Olympus, a city in Lycia, in the south of Asia Minor. In the Banquet, ten young women each praise chastity, the unmarried life given to God. In a dialogue, On the Resurrection, he argued against Origen, the great teacher of Alexandria and Caesarea, that the body is no chain on the soul and that the same body will rise. A later bishop copied his words into his own case against Origen.

The Banquet is a story within a story, in which one friend begs another to repeat the speeches. One guest says praising virginity does not abolish marriage, which is still God's work. "The moon may be greater than the stars, but its light does not put out the light of the other stars." Virtue, speaking last, says it is no chastity to keep the body pure but not the tongue, the eyes, the hands or the mind.

The dialogue, as a later reader summed it up centuries on, asks what the body is to the soul. Origen, Methodius wrote, held that the soul lived without a body before the fall, the first sin, and was given the body afterwards as a chain. At the resurrection, Origen held, the same flesh does not return, only each person's shape, stamped on a different, spiritual body. Methodius could not bear, he says, those who turn bones and flesh into symbols, so that the dead rise without flesh.

If the body were a chain to hold back evil, he answered, it had failed, since Cain committed murder in this body. A human being, he held, is soul and body joined in one. In his telling, a craftsman finds that an envious man has spoiled a beautiful statue he made, so he melts it down and casts it again. "For the statue cannot be lost while it is in the same craftsman's hands, even if it is melted down into its material again. It can be restored. But the blemishes and the damage can be lost, for they melt away. They cannot be restored." So, he wrote, God, seeing his finest work spoiled by envy, dissolves the body in death and remakes it in the resurrection.

Eusebius of Caesarea, in a book he wrote in Origen's defense, asked how Methodius dared to write against Origen now. Jerome, who reports the question, says Methodius was crowned with martyrdom at Chalcis in Greece, in that last persecution, though others, he adds, placed it under earlier emperors, Decius and Valerian.

Later in the fourth century, Epiphanius of Salamis wrote against Origen and chose to rest his case on On the Resurrection, setting out its words on the rising of the same body word for word.

More than a century after Methodius, the church historian Socrates, who defended Origen, put him first among four men who attacked Origen. "Worthless people, who cannot be noticed on their own, want to be seen by finding fault with their betters. Methodius was the first to fall into this." Socrates adds that Methodius, after railing against Origen in many places, took it all back, as it were, and praised him in a dialogue he called the Xenon. Socrates alone tells of this change of heart.

At the Banquet's close, Virtue tells the guests to rise and stand under the tree. One guest begins a hymn, and the others answer her, each keeping herself for Christ, the Bridegroom.

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “The moon may be greater than the stars, but its light does not put out the light of the other stars.”
    As printed
    “although the moon may be greater than the stars, the light of the other stars is not destroyed by the moonlight.”
    Methodius, The Banquet of the Ten Virgins, Discourse II.1, Theophila's speech, tr. William R. Clark, Ante-Nicene Fathers vol. 6
    The Greek of the Banquet is not held, so the modern English follows Clark's English alone. The chapter heading gives the plain sense: Marriage Not Abolished by the Commendation of Virginity (lines 30269-30270).
  • In our modern English
    “For the statue cannot be lost while it is in the same craftsman's hands, even if it is melted down into its material again. It can be restored. But the blemishes and the damage can be lost, for they melt away. They cannot be restored.”
    As printed
    “For it is impossible for an image under the hands of the original artist to be lost, even if it be melted down again, for it may be restored; but it is possible for blemishes and injuries to be put off, for they melt away and cannot be restored”
    Methodius, On the Resurrection I, as Epiphanius copies it, Panarion 64.34, ed. Karl Holl; English tr. William R. Clark, Ante-Nicene Fathers vol. 6
    The Greek is printed as the damaged scan reads it; it reads κἂν πάλιν εἰς ὕλην λυθῇ and ἀποκατασταθῆναι, and Oehler's print of the same passage confirms the reading (epiphanius_panarion-haer-48-69, lines 22929-22933). Holl's notes record that two manuscripts lack ὄντι and one reads χυθῇ, which leaves the sense unchanged (lines 35747-35750). The modern English follows the Greek: statue for ἄγαλμα, where Clark prints image, and material for ὕλη, since the statue is of gold or other material (Clark, lines 35424-35425); the same craftsman for τῷ αὐτῷ τεχνίτῃ, where Clark prints the original artist.
  • In our modern English
    “Worthless people, who cannot be noticed on their own, want to be seen by finding fault with their betters. Methodius was the first to fall into this.”
    As printed
    “Worthless characters, and such as are destitute of ability to attain eminence themselves, often seek to get into notice by decrying those who excel them. And first Methodius, bishop of a city in Lycia named Olympus, labored under this malady”
    Socrates of Constantinople, Church History VI.13, tr. A. C. Zenos, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers series 2 vol. 2; Greek as Hans Lietzmann prints it, and ed. Robert Hussey
    The Greek is printed as the scan reads it; for dp’ it reads ἀφ᾽, as Hussey's print of Socrates has it (socrates_historia-ecclesiastica-tomus2, line 12878), which is damaged further on. The modern English follows the Greek: wish to be seen for δείκνυσθαι βούλονται, and the first to fall into this for τοῦτο πέπονθε πρῶτος, where Zenos prints often seek to get into notice and first labored under this malady.

Key Works

  • The Banquet of the Ten Virgins: A dialogue in which ten women praise chastity in a garden. One friend asks another to repeat the speeches given at a feast held by Virtue under a wide, shady tree. One speaker says the praise of virginity does not do away with marriage, and Virtue, the host, says chastity keeps the tongue, the eyes, the hands and the mind pure, not the body alone. It ends with a hymn to Christ, the Bridegroom.
  • On the Resurrection: Argues against Origen that the body is no fetter and rises again. A person is soul and body joined, and God dissolves the body in death and remakes it, as a craftsman melts down a spoiled statue and casts it again. Long passages survive in Greek in Epiphanius, and a later summary by Photius.
  • On Free Will: A treatise Jerome lists as his, also against Origen.

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Epiphanius of Salamis, writing against Origen later in the fourth century, chose to rest his case on what the blessed Methodius had said in On the Resurrection, and set it out word for word. He called him a learned man who fought hard for the truth.
  • Theodoret of Cyrrhus copied a passage from his sermon on the martyrs into a set of testimonies from earlier bishops on how God became man. In it Methodius says martyrdom is so precious that Christ himself honored it, to crown the manhood he had come down into.
  • Photius read On the Resurrection centuries later and summed it up, and his summary keeps what Methodius said of Origen's teaching on the body.
  • A later Syriac chronicle retells his story of Phidias, a sculptor who poured oil before his ivory statue so that it would last, from the work he addressed to Aglaophon on the resurrection.
  • Three fragments of his work against Porphyry are held, from a later collection, the Parallels of John of Damascus. John of Damascus, writing on the use of images in worship, cited him too.

How they're remembered

  • Remembered as Origen's opponent. The church historian Socrates of Constantinople, who defended Origen, names him first among the four who reviled Origen.
  • Honored as a martyr. Jerome says he was crowned with martyrdom at Chalcis in Greece, and Theodoret calls him the holy Methodius, bishop and martyr. Photius calls him Saint Methodius, Bishop and Martyr.
  • Jerome set him among the church's writers, praised the polished and logical style of his books against Porphyry, and called On the Resurrection an excellent work.
  • Theodoret named him with Ignatius, Irenaeus and Hippolytus among the bishops and martyrs whose words witness to the faith, and among the teachers of the church whose teaching he followed.

Open Questions

  • His death. Jerome gives two times: at the end of the recent persecution, or, as others affirm, under Decius and Valerian, earlier emperors. An editor of Jerome notes that the words on Decius and Valerian are missing from the oldest manuscript, at Verona. Where he died rests on Jerome alone, and no held source tells how he died.
  • His sees. Jerome makes him bishop of Olympus in Lycia and afterwards of Tyre; Socrates names Olympus only. John of Damascus, writing much later, calls him bishop of Patara, also in Lycia.
  • His second name. Epiphanius calls him Methodius, also called Eubulius. Euboulios is also the friend who asks for the story in the Banquet; the sources do not say why he bore the name.
  • Socrates of Constantinople tells of his change of heart, and Socrates defended Origen. He names the dialogue in which Methodius of Olympus praised him: Xenon, a work the Library does not hold. Eusebius of Caesarea, as Jerome quotes him, asks how Methodius dared to write against Origen now. Jerome says Eusebius makes the same complaint against Methodius that Rufinus, Jerome's opponent, makes against Jerome himself, but Eusebius's words do not themselves say that Methodius praised Origen.
  • Eusebius's question. Jerome, who reports it, calls Eusebius an Arian, a follower of Arius, who taught that there was a time when the Son was not. Pamphilus of Caesarea had written a defense of Origen before Eusebius wrote his.
  • Origen's side. Origen's teaching on the body is told as Methodius gave it. The soul lived without a body before the fall, the first sin, and was given the body afterwards as a fetter. At the resurrection only each person's form rises, on another, spiritual body. Origen's own book On First Principles, held only in English from the Latin of Rufinus, a later translator, says it is the body that rises, our own, as a spiritual body. Rufinus says he left out or changed passages on the Trinity, and made unclear sentences plainer from Origen's other works.
  • The summary. What the dialogue On the Resurrection is about, and its account of Origen, come from the summary Photius made centuries later. Methodius's own Greek survives only in the long passages Epiphanius copied, and the Greek of the Banquet is not held.
  • Socrates's reason. He argues that the four who attacked Origen, each on one point, never charged him with unsound views on the Trinity, and so bear witness to his soundness.
  • Jerome also says he answered Porphyry, a philosopher of his own time who wrote books against the Christians and their Scriptures, in a work of ten thousand lines. Jerome names him again, with Eusebius and Apollinaris, among those who answered Porphyry's attack on the book of Daniel.
  • The Banquet's other speakers give more of its teaching. The first says the plant of virginity was sent down from heaven late, after the human race had moved by stages from marrying sisters, to one wife, to restraint, to virginity. Thekla, a guest in the book, is given the larger crown; the book does not say who she is. In a closing talk, a soul that feels desire and holds its course is called better, as the pilot who saves his ship in a storm is better than one who sails in a calm.
  • On the Resurrection has a second image beside the statue: sin is like a fig tree grown into the stones of a temple, torn out by the roots so that the temple can stand again. Methodius also quotes Justin of Neapolis, a man near the apostles in time, on the resurrection.
