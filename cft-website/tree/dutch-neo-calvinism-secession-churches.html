The Missionary Era · 1834-1906 · Netherlands; N. America

Netherlands · N. America, today in United States

Dutch Neo-Calvinism & the Secession Churches

The Netherlands and the American Midwest, 1834 to 1906 — Calvinists who walked out of the national church twice, and then built a university, a newspaper and a governing party.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:25

Read along

In 1834 a village pastor in Groningen, Hendrik de Cock, was suspended for preaching the old Reformed doctrines against what he took to be the liberalism of the national church, and his congregation walked out with him. The Afscheiding that followed was a secession of mostly poor and rural people, who were fined and had soldiers billeted on them for holding illegal services. Many emigrated, and in 1857 their American cousins founded what became the Christian Reformed Church in North America. Fifty years after de Cock, Abraham Kuyper — journalist, politician, theologian — led a second and larger secession, the Doleantie of 1886, and in 1892 brought most of the two streams together. Kuyper's idea was always bigger than a church. That Christ's claim covers every sphere of life, each with its own proper authority, so believers should build their own schools, university, newspaper and political party rather than either withdraw from society or dissolve into it. He founded the Free University in Amsterdam in 1880 and served as prime minister of the Netherlands from 1901 to 1905.

Documented Stories

Ulrum, October 1834

Hendrik de Cock came to Ulrum, a village in Groningen, in 1829 as a conventional minister of the Netherlands Reformed Church, and was converted by his own parishioners, who read the old Reformed writers and asked why he did not preach what the confessions said. He began to. Parents from other parishes brought their children to Ulrum for baptism, and he baptized them; in 1833 he published a pamphlet calling two neighboring ministers wolves in the sheepfold. He was suspended in December 1833 and deposed in 1834. On 13 October de Cock and the consistory signed the Act of Secession or Return, declaring that they separated from a church that had departed from the confessions and returned to the church of the fathers; most of the congregation signed after them. The state invoked the Napoleonic code against unlicensed assemblies over twenty. The seceders were fined for every meeting and troops were billeted on them: some hundred and fifty soldiers in Ulrum alone that winter, in one-room houses, fed and lodged at the family's cost until the meetings stopped. De Cock was imprisoned for three months for preaching. The meetings did not stop. Congregations seceded across the provinces and were fined and billeted in turn until the government tired of it in the 1840s; thousands emigrated to Michigan and Iowa in 1846–47, founding the churches that became the Christian Reformed Church. De Cock died in 1842 at forty-one; the Act of Secession hangs in the churches that descend from it.

The Panel-Sawing

Abraham Kuyper had spent a decade turning the orthodox party in the Netherlands Reformed Church into a movement, with a newspaper, a political party and a Free University; in Amsterdam, where he was an elder, the orthodox consistory refused to admit candidates who would not confess the divinity of Christ, and in 1885 it changed the property rules so that the buildings would go with the consistory if the higher boards deposed it. The regional board suspended eighty elders and deacons, Kuyper among them, on 4 January 1886. The suspended majority did not accept it. On the night of 5–6 January, learning that the minority intended to take possession of the consistory room in the Nieuwe Kerk, where the archives and property registers were kept, Kuyper's men went to the church, found the door locked, and sawed the panels out of it to get in; they then held the room, with the papers, against the minority and the police for weeks. The city called it the paneelzagerij, the panel-sawing, and the liberal press made the most of a party of pious elders committing burglary in a church. The synod deposed the eighty in December 1886, and they and their followers, some two hundred thousand by 1889, declared themselves the Grieving Churches, the Doleantie; the courts gave the buildings to the synod. In 1892 the Doleantie and most of the Secession churches united as the Reformed Churches in the Netherlands; in 1901 Kuyper was prime minister. The saw was kept.

In Its Own Record

Abraham Kuyper led a second secession, the Doleantie, in 1886, founded the Free University in 1880, and brought the two streams together in the 1892 union that formed the Gereformeerde Kerken in Nederland.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

When Hendrik de Cock's congregation walked out of the Dutch national church in 1834, the state fined them and billeted soldiers on them for holding illegal services. Fifty years later Abraham Kuyper led a second, larger secession on a bigger idea, that Christ's claim covers every sphere of life, each with its own proper authority, and built a university, a newspaper and a political party to carry it out rather than withdraw from society or dissolve into it.

Legacy

The churches are still here: the Dutch Reformed families at home, most of them since 2004 inside the Protestant Church in the Netherlands, and in North America the Christian Reformed Church with its schools and colleges. Kuyper's larger idea traveled furthest. Sphere sovereignty and a Christian worldview applied to scholarship, politics and the arts became a standing tradition in Reformed thought and shaped a great deal of evangelical higher education in North America. It also traveled in a direction Kuyper's heirs still argue about, supplying vocabulary that South African theologians later adapted in defense of apartheid.
