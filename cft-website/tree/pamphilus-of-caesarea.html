Pamphilus of Caesarea

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition.

About This Person

Eusebius of Caesarea wrote of how his friend Pamphilus gave what he could to poor book lovers: "He handed out the holy Scriptures as well, and most readily, to keep and not only to read. He gave them to men, and to women too, when he saw they were devoted to reading. So he kept many books prepared, to give freely to anyone who wished whenever need called for it."

Pamphilus of Caesarea was a presbyter, an elder and priest of the church, at Caesarea, a port on the coast of Roman Palestine. He built a library for his church and, Jerome says, copied most of Origen's works with his own hand. Origen, the Bible scholar who had taught at Alexandria and then at Caesarea, had died in the century before. In the persecution Pamphilus was tortured, held two years in prison and put to death about 310. Eusebius says the two friends wrote a Defense of Origen together, and the books Pamphilus kept were read for generations after him.

Eusebius, whose pages hold nearly all of his life, says he was devoted above all to the Scriptures. Jerome says he searched the world for books, buying all of Origen's at great cost.

Then the emperors' orders reached Palestine: tear down the churches, burn the Scriptures, then make everyone sacrifice to the Roman gods. In the persecution's fifth year, the governor tested Pamphilus's learning in public speaking and philosophy, then tried to force him to sacrifice. He refused and ignored his threats. In anger the governor had his sides scraped in long torture and put him in prison with the confessors, Christians who had held to the faith under trial.

Eusebius says he and Pamphilus wrote the Defense with care, because of those who looked for faults. Photius of Constantinople, a scholar five centuries later, says Pamphilus wrote most of it in prison. It answered charges that Origen taught that the soul lived before the body, and that he made Christ either a mere man or one whose deeds only seemed to happen. The Defense set out Origen's own words with replies between. It was written to confessors condemned to forced labor in the mines of Palestine. The Defense says that readers of pagan books, and even of heretics' books, whose teaching the church condemns, are usually pardoned. But Origen's accusers think that they alone have the skill to test what they read, keeping the good like a money-changer who tests each coin and throws out the false. "They think the command to become approved money-changers is wholly shut out for those who read Origen's books. Such money-changers know how to keep what is good and hold back from every kind of evil. If anyone is only seen reading his books, at once they drench him in the shame of the heretics."

After two whole years in prison, Pamphilus and his companions came before a new governor. He knew they had held firm under earlier tortures, and asked only whether they would now obey. Each gave the same answer, and he sentenced them to death, as he had others before them. Pamphilus died with eleven others, the only presbyter among them. Eusebius took Pamphilus's name as a surname.

Eusebius sent readers to the library's catalog to learn which of Origen's works survived, and Jerome used the library in his day. Rufinus of Aquileia put the Defense's first book, the only one that survives, into Latin under Pamphilus's name. Some ninety years after Pamphilus died, Jerome complained that readers ignored bishops who had since condemned Origen as a heretic, because they thought a martyr had praised him.

Jerome had also found twenty-five volumes of Origen's commentaries on the twelve prophets, copied in Pamphilus's hand, guarding them, he says, like a king's riches. "If it is a joy to own one letter from a martyr, how much greater to own so many thousand lines! It seems to me he marked them with the traces of his own blood."

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “He handed out the holy Scriptures as well, and most readily, to keep and not only to read. He gave them to men, and to women too, when he saw they were devoted to reading. So he kept many books prepared, to give freely to anyone who wished whenever need called for it.”
    As printed
    “He would not only lend them copies of the Holy Scriptures to read, but would give them most readily, and that not only to men, but to women also if he saw that they were given to reading. He therefore kept a store of manuscripts, so that he might be able to give them to those who wished for them whenever occasion demanded.”
    Eusebius, Life of Pamphilus III, as Jerome quotes it in the Apology against Rufinus I.9, in Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, second series, vol. 3
    Eusebius's Greek is lost. The story's English is made from the Latin that Jerome quotes, held only in a scan with letter faults, so the printed English stands alone; it agrees with the Latin in sense. The Latin says to keep, where the English says lend and give.
  • In our modern English
    “They think the command to become approved money-changers is wholly shut out for those who read Origen's books. Such money-changers know how to keep what is good and hold back from every kind of evil. If anyone is only seen reading his books, at once they drench him in the shame of the heretics.”
    As printed
    “ab his vero, qui Origenis libros legunt, istud penitus exclusum putant esse mandatum, quo probabiles jubentur effici trapezitae, scientes quod bonum est retinere, ab omni autem se specie mala abstinere: sed tantum modo si legere libros illius visus fuerit, statim ab his haereticorum perfundetur infamia”
    The Defense of Origen, Book I, preface to the confessors, in Rufinus's Latin, ed. Lommatzsch
    No English of the Defense is held, so the story's English is made from this Latin. The Migne printing of the same Latin agrees, and reads si quis, if anyone, which the story's English follows. The Defense says this; whose words they are, Pamphilus's or Eusebius's, is disputed.
  • In our modern English
    “If it is a joy to own one letter from a martyr, how much greater to own so many thousand lines! It seems to me he marked them with the traces of his own blood.”
    As printed
    “And if it is such joy to have one epistle of a martyr how much more to have so many thousand lines which seem to me to be traced in his blood.”
    Jerome, Lives of Illustrious Men 75, in Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, second series, vol. 3
    The story's English is made from Jerome's Latin, which the Library holds only in a scan with letter faults, so the printed English stands alone. The Latin says Pamphilus seems to him to have marked them with the traces of his own blood. The Greek of Sophronius beside it, read from a scan with faults, ends with the traces of his own suffering.

Timeline

Dates
No old source gives the year of his birth. Eusebius dates the start of the persecution in Palestine to April in the nineteenth year of the emperor Diocletian, which is 303. He puts Pamphilus in prison in its fifth year, after the Nones of November, so late in 307 or soon after. Pamphilus died after two whole years in prison, on the fourteenth day before the Kalends of March, a day in February, so about 310. Jerome says he died in the persecution of Maximinus. Jerome's Lives of Illustrious Men, which tells of his copying, is dated to the fourteenth year of the emperor Theodosius, so 392 or 393

Key Works

  • Defense of Origen (Apologia pro Origene): a book answering the charges made against Origen, written in the persecution to confessors, Christians who had held to the faith under trial, condemned to the mines of Palestine. It sets out Origen's own words, under his name, with the defender's replies between, and says Origen searched the Scriptures as far as his strength allowed and did not settle them as fixed doctrine. Photius says it had six books. Only the first survives, in the Latin of Rufinus of Aquileia, which the Library holds in two printings. Eusebius says he and Pamphilus did the work jointly; later writers differ on whose it is.
  • The library at Caesarea: a collection of sacred books for the church: the works of Origen and of other church writers, Eusebius says. Eusebius gave its catalog in his Life of Pamphilus, now lost, so that readers could learn which of Origen's works survived. Jerome says Pamphilus searched the whole world for books, bought every book Origen wrote at great cost, and gave them to the church at Caesarea.
  • Copies of Origen in his own hand: Jerome says he copied the greater part of Origen's works with his own hand, and that these were still kept in the library at Caesarea. Jerome also writes that he found twenty-five volumes of Origen's commentaries on the twelve prophets in Pamphilus's hand, which he guarded with joy. Photius says only that Pamphilus is said to have copied most of Origen's commentaries on Scripture.

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Eusebius sent readers to the library's catalog to learn which of Origen's works survived.
  • Jerome used the library. He writes that the Hebrew Gospel of Matthew was kept there, and that he found the six volumes of Eusebius's Defense of Origen there.
  • Jerome writes that the library was afterwards partly destroyed, and that two bishops of Caesarea, Acacius and later Euzoius, tried to restore it in parchment volumes. The second recovered many works and left an inventory.
  • Jerome says the churches of Palestine read the copies of the Greek Old Testament that Origen prepared and that Eusebius and Pamphilus published.
  • Rufinus of Aquileia put the first book of the Defense into Latin under Pamphilus's name, and it is all that survives of the work. About ninety years after Pamphilus died, Jerome complained that readers gave no heed to the bishops who condemned Origen as a heretic, one whose teaching the church condemns, since they thought a martyr had praised him.
  • Eusebius took Pamphilus's name as a surname. Jerome says it came from his friendship for the martyr, and Photius that Eusebius was an ardent admirer of Pamphilus.
  • Isidore of Seville, about three centuries later, says Pamphilus was the first among Christians to try to equal Pisistratus, the founder of a famous pagan library, in zeal for a sacred library, and that he had nearly thirty thousand volumes.

How they're remembered

  • Eusebius calls him his dearest friend, a man thrice dear to him, and the most illustrious of the martyrs of his time. In the longer form of his account, held only in a damaged Greek scan, he calls him my master.
  • Eusebius calls him a most eloquent man, of truly philosophical life, and says he set up a school at Caesarea.
  • Eusebius writes that he shared his possessions with the needy. The longer form says he sold what came to him from his forebears, gave it all to the naked, the maimed and the poor, and lived without possessions.
  • Eusebius's lost Life of Pamphilus, as Jerome quotes it, says he gave what he could to poor lovers of books, and gave the holy Scriptures freely to keep, to women as well as men.
  • Eusebius writes that on the day Pamphilus and the others with him died, the chief gate of the kingdom of heaven was opened to them.
  • Socrates of Constantinople, a historian of the fifth century, calls Pamphilus and Eusebius trustworthy witnesses for Origen, in their joint life of him and defense of him.
  • A Syriac chronicle written centuries later says he taught Eusebius, and Theophanes, in the ninth century, lists him among the martyrs as a presbyter of Caesarea.
  • Photius names Pamphilus and Pierius as holy teachers, honored above others as famed for martyrdom, though he says he does not accept all their views.

Open Questions

  • His life rests on one friend. Nearly all of it comes from Eusebius, who admired him, in the Church History and the Martyrs of Palestine. Eusebius's Life of Pamphilus in three books is lost. Its one surviving passage is held only in Jerome's Latin, where Jerome quotes it against Pamphilus's authorship of the Defense.
  • Who wrote the Defense of Origen. Eusebius writes that he and Pamphilus did the work jointly. Jerome, in 392 or 393, listed it under Pamphilus's name. Rufinus put its first book into Latin as Pamphilus's, though he called it the book Pamphilus is said to have written. In his Letter 84, Jerome doubted that it was Pamphilus's, since no other work of his could be shown, and suggested it was by Didymus or someone else. He added that if Pamphilus wrote it, he wrote it before he became a martyr, and was counted worthy of martyrdom to wash away his one fault. Rufinus answered that Jerome no longer spared even the martyrs.
  • Jerome's later charge. About ten years after his catalog, by his own count, Jerome said the Defense was a work of Eusebius in six books, and called Eusebius the chief of the Arians. He said he had found the six volumes in the library at Caesarea, and that Rufinus had changed a few statements about the Son of God and the Holy Spirit. He admitted he had once listed it under Pamphilus's name.
  • The other old witnesses on the Defense. Socrates calls it their joint defense, and also speaks of the books Pamphilus wrote in defense of Origen. Photius says it had six books, five written by Pamphilus in prison with Eusebius, and the sixth by Eusebius alone after Pamphilus's death. Jerome's own counts differ: in his Apology against Rufinus he says Rufinus translated the first of Eusebius's six books, and in Letter 84 that the book holds nearly the first thousand lines of Eusebius's sixth book.
  • Whether Eusebius was in prison with him. Photius says Pamphilus wrote the Defense in prison in the company of Eusebius. Eusebius never says he was imprisoned. He says only that the two did the work jointly.
  • Friend or teacher. Eusebius calls Pamphilus his dearest friend. Jerome calls Pamphilus the patron of Eusebius. That Pamphilus taught Eusebius is said only in a Syriac chronicle written centuries later.
  • The year of his death. No old source gives the year. Eusebius's own counts, the fifth year of the persecution and then two whole years in prison, end in February about 310. The exact year depends on how the years of the persecution are counted, and the old sources do not settle it.
  • Where he studied. Photius, more than five centuries later, says Pierius, head of the school of Christian teaching at Alexandria, was his teacher. The longer form of Eusebius's account says he was schooled at Berytus before he turned to the sacred writings, and names no teacher.
  • His birthplace and family. That he came from Berytus, of a noble family, and was distinguished in public office there, stands only in the longer form of Eusebius's account, held in damaged Greek with no other print. The Syriac of the longer form is not held. The shorter account, in English, does not give it.
  • The manner of his death. The shorter account says only that the governor punished Pamphilus and his companions like the others before them. The longer form in Greek says a capital sentence, and Photius says by the sword.
  • How much he copied. Jerome says the greater part of Origen's works, and Photius says he is said to have copied most of Origen's commentaries on Scripture. In Jerome's Latin, manu ejus says only in his hand, in a sentence about Pamphilus.
  • The end of Jerome's passage on the volumes. His Latin says Pamphilus seems to him to have marked them with the traces of his own blood. The English of the Library's Jerome volume has lines that seem traced in his blood. The Greek of Sophronius printed beside the Latin ends with the traces of his own suffering, read from a scan with letter faults.
  • Whose command the money-changers are. The Defense calls it only a command, to become approved money-changers who keep what is good. Origen, in his Commentary on John, calls it the command of Jesus, beside Paul's teaching to abstain from every form of evil. Dionysius of Alexandria, as Eusebius quotes him, calls it the apostolic voice.
  • Another book and another apology. Photius describes an anonymous defense of Origen in five volumes, which relies chiefly on Pamphilus and Eusebius. He judges that it mostly supports the charges against Origen, and lists fifteen charges it calls slanders, among them that souls pass from one body into another and that there is no eternal punishment. He describes the Defense of Pamphilus and Eusebius next, as a separate work.
  • Other writings. Gennadius lists among Rufinus's Latin translations a work of Pamphilus the Martyr Against the mathematicians, meaning astrologers. Nothing held says whether it is a lost work or another name for the Defense. An Exposition of the Chapters of the Acts of the Apostles is printed under his name in the Ante-Nicene Fathers, whose notice says others give it to Euthalius; it is not counted here.
  • Copies of the Bible. Notes in some Bible manuscripts, reported by editors, say Pamphilus and Eusebius corrected the copies. The manuscripts are not held, so the notes are not counted here.
  • The text of the Defense. The Greek is lost. Book I survives only in Rufinus's Latin, held in two rough scans that agree with each other, and no English of it is held. Jerome charged Rufinus with changing passages on the Son and the Holy Spirit. The passage quoted in the story is not on those subjects.
