Caesarea Maritima, today in Israel

Pamphilus of Caesarea

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition.

Known for
Presbyter of the church at Caesarea in Palestine who gathered a library of sacred books for it and, Jerome says, copied most of Origen's works with his own hand. Jailed in the Great Persecution, he worked from prison with Eusebius on a Defense of Origen, and was put to death about 310.
Also called
Pamphilus the Martyr, Pamphilus the presbyter
Dates
No birth year is known. Arrested late in 307 (between November 307 and April 308, by A. C. McGiffert's reckoning) and held two whole years; put to death in February, which McGiffert's note on Eusebius's Martyrs of Palestine dates to 310 and the common reckoning to 309. Jerome's catalog of writers, which lists the Defense under his name, is dated 392 or 393; Rufinus's Latin of its first book, 397
Place
Caesarea, a port on the coast of Roman Palestine, where he was presbyter, set up a school, gathered his library, was imprisoned and was put to death. Eusebius's longer account of the martyrdom, held only in a damaged Greek scan, has him come from Berytus, today's Beirut. A report as late as Photius has him study at Alexandria under Pierius; McGiffert thinks his teacher more probably Achillas
Role
Presbyter of Caesarea; library builder and copyist of Origen's books; martyr

About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

About eighty years after Pamphilus of Caesarea died, the scholar Jerome found twenty-five volumes of Origen's commentaries, his books explaining Scripture, copied, he wrote, in Pamphilus's own hand. "If it is a joy to have one letter of a martyr," he wrote, "how much more to have so many thousand lines! He seems to me to have marked them with the traces of his own blood."

Pamphilus was a presbyter, one of the elders who served the church beside its bishop, in Caesarea, a port in Roman Palestine. Origen, the great Bible scholar of the century before, had taught there. Pamphilus gathered a library of sacred books for his church, and Jerome says he copied most of Origen's books himself. Arrested in the last and greatest persecution of Christians by Rome's emperors, he worked from prison with his pupil and friend Eusebius on a book defending Origen. He was put to death for his faith about 310.

Eusebius writes that he gave his inheritance to the poor; Jerome, that he bought every book Origen had written, at great cost, for the church.

In a Life of him, now lost but quoted by Jerome, Eusebius wrote: "Who among those eager to learn was not a friend of Pamphilus? If he saw any of them lacking what they needed to live, he gave freely what he could. He gave the holy Scriptures too, most readily, not only to read but to keep. He gave them not only to men, but also to women he saw devoted to reading. So he kept many books ready, to give to those who wanted them whenever need arose."

Late in 307, the governor of Palestine ordered him to offer sacrifice to the Roman gods. When he refused, the governor had his sides scraped in long torture and jailed him for two years.

Eusebius writes that the two worked on this Defense of Origen "jointly", Pamphilus from prison. The Defense speaks of the ideas that came to Origen from the Scriptures. "When he explains these things, he often adds and declares that he is not pronouncing them as a final verdict, or settling them as fixed doctrine. He is searching as far as his strength allows, and examining the sense of the Scriptures. He does not claim to have grasped it wholly and perfectly."

In February of 309 or 310, a new governor condemned the prisoners who still would not obey. Twelve died in all. Pamphilus, the only presbyter among them, was put to death by the sword.

About 392, Jerome listed the Defense under Pamphilus's name. In 397 Rufinus of Aquileia put its first book, all that survives, into Latin as Pamphilus's, though he called it the book Pamphilus "is said to have written". Around 400, Jerome began to doubt it was Pamphilus's, and Rufinus answered that Jerome no longer spared even the martyrs. Jerome then named Eusebius as the author, and charged that Rufinus had put a martyr's name on it so that readers would trust its praise of Origen. His proof was Eusebius's own Life, which says Pamphilus wrote nothing but letters to friends.

Almost all we know of Pamphilus rests on that same Eusebius, who had called the Defense their joint work. Five centuries later, a scholar in Constantinople who read all six books said Pamphilus wrote five in prison, working with Eusebius, and Eusebius the sixth alone. Modern editors read it as joint work.

The library outlived him. Eusebius drew much of his history from it, his English editor judged, and Jerome worked from it. The Defense became a weapon in the quarrel over Origen. Jerome complained that readers ignored the bishops who condemned Origen, "since they think a martyr praised him." The book defended the good name of a teacher half a century dead.

And at Caesarea lay the books themselves, many thousand lines in Pamphilus's hand, which Jerome had once said seemed marked with his blood.

Key Works

  • Defense of Origen (Apologia pro Origene): written in prison, addressed to Christians condemned to the mines of Palestine; its first argument is that Origen searched the Scriptures as an inquirer and did not lay down doctrine. Of its six books only the first survives, in Rufinus's Latin of 397, which the Library holds in two printings. Eusebius says he and Pamphilus did the work jointly; Jerome, Rufinus, Photius and the editors differ on whose it is.
  • The library at Caesarea: a collection of sacred books for the church, which McGiffert says was rich in copies of Scripture, commentaries on it and Origen's works. Eusebius gave a catalog of it in his Life of Pamphilus, now lost, so that readers could learn which of Origen's works survive. Jerome says Pamphilus bought every book Origen wrote, at great cost, and gave them to the church, and that he used the library himself.
  • Copies of Origen in his own hand: Jerome says he copied the greater part of Origen's works with his own hand, and wrote, about 392, that he had found twenty-five volumes of Origen's commentaries on the twelve prophets in Pamphilus's hand, which he guarded with joy. Photius says only that Pamphilus is said to have copied most of Origen's commentaries on Scripture.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Eusebius sent readers to the library's catalog for Origen's works, and A. C. McGiffert judges that Eusebius owes a large part of the materials of his history to it.
  • Jerome used the library: he writes that the Hebrew Gospel of Matthew was kept there, and that he found the six volumes of Eusebius's Defense of Origen there.
  • Jerome writes that the library was afterwards partly destroyed, and that two bishops of Caesarea, Acacius and later Euzoius, tried to restore it in parchment volumes; the second left an inventory.
  • Rufinus put the first book of the Defense into Latin in 397 under Pamphilus's name, and it is all that survives of the work. About 401 Jerome complained that readers gave no heed to the bishops who condemned Origen, since they thought a martyr had praised him.
  • Eusebius took his name and called himself Eusebius Pamphili; Jerome says the surname came from his friendship for the martyr, and McGiffert says Eusebius took it after Pamphilus's death.

How they're remembered

  • Eusebius calls him his dearest friend, a man thrice dear to him, and the most illustrious of the martyrs of that time; in the longer form of his account he calls him my master, a phrase that stands in a damaged Greek scan.
  • Eusebius writes that he shared his possessions with the needy; the longer form says he sold what came to him from his forebears and gave it all to the naked, the maimed and the poor, and lived without possessions.
  • Eusebius's Life of Pamphilus, as Jerome quotes it, says he gave the holy Scriptures freely, not only to read but to keep, to women as well as men, and kept many books ready to give away.
  • Socrates, a historian of the fifth century, calls Pamphilus and Eusebius trustworthy witnesses for Origen in their joint life of him and defense of him.
  • McGiffert writes that Pamphilus is chiefly celebrated for the library he collected at Caesarea.

What Is Uncertain

  • His life rests on one friend. Nearly all of it comes from Eusebius, who admired him, in the Church History and the Martyrs of Palestine. Eusebius's Life of Pamphilus in three books is lost; its one surviving passage is held only in Jerome's Latin and English, where Jerome quotes it as evidence against Pamphilus's authorship of the Defense.
  • Who wrote the Defense of Origen. Eusebius writes that it was written by himself and Pamphilus, who did the work jointly. Jerome, about 392 or 393, listed it under Pamphilus's name. Rufinus in 397 put its first book into Latin as Pamphilus's, though he called it the book Pamphilus is said to have written. Jerome, in Letter 84 of 400, doubted that it was Pamphilus's, saying no other work of his could be shown, and suggested it belonged to Didymus or someone else; he added, as a hostile view, that if Pamphilus wrote it, it was before he became a martyr, and that he was counted worthy of martyrdom to wash away his one fault. Rufinus answered that Jerome would no longer spare even the martyrs. About 401 to 402 Jerome said it was Eusebius's, whom he called the chief of the Arians; he had found Eusebius's six volumes in the library at Caesarea, and he charged Rufinus with changing a few statements about the Son and the Holy Spirit. He admitted he had listed it under Pamphilus's name about ten years before.
  • More on who wrote the Defense. Socrates calls it their joint defense and also speaks of books Pamphilus wrote. Photius, in the ninth century, says six books, five written by Pamphilus in prison with Eusebius and the sixth by Eusebius alone after Pamphilus's death. McGiffert holds it certain that Pamphilus was a joint author, and quite probable that Rufinus kept Pamphilus's explanations; he calls Jerome's treatment unfair and dishonest, as Lightfoot also pointed out. W. H. Fremantle, editor of the volume that holds Jerome and Rufinus, says Eusebius was the chief writer, sees no reason to doubt that Pamphilus was associated with him throughout, and says Pamphilus actually wrote the sixth book. Lightfoot reads the Life's statement that Pamphilus wrote nothing of his own except letters as chosen to leave out this work of compiling and partnership. Jerome's own counts differ: in the Apology against Rufinus he says Rufinus translated the first of six books by Eusebius, while in Letter 84 he says the treatise holds nearly the first thousand lines of Eusebius's sixth book; the Letter's translator notes that Jerome is again misled if the text is sound.
  • Whether Eusebius was in prison with him. Photius says Pamphilus wrote it shut up with Eusebius, whose Greek is held only in one sentence quoted by Delarue. Eusebius never says he was imprisoned, and McGiffert thinks it safer to suppose, with most historians, that he visited Pamphilus in prison. McGiffert calls Pamphilus Eusebius's teacher and most intimate friend, and a late Syriac chronicle in Guidi's Latin says Pamphilus taught Eusebius, who was named Pamphilus after him.
  • The year of his death. McGiffert's note on Church History VII.32 says he was put to death in 309, and his note on the Martyrs of Palestine argues for February of 310 and calls the common 309 a mistake. The Library's Jerome volume notes 309 on Jerome's chapter. The translator's notice in the Ante-Nicene volume has him in prison from late 307 to the beginning of 309. Martianay's note gives 309 and February, but the day is unreadable in the scan. The census gives 307 to 310.
  • Where he studied. Photius says Pierius, head of the catechetical school at Alexandria, was his teacher. The translator's notice in the Ante-Nicene volume gives Berytus and study under Pierius as the common account. McGiffert says he studied at Alexandria, more probably under Achillas, and judges Photius's report on Pierius probably false, a mix of Eusebius's account and Jerome's.
  • His birthplace and family. That he came from Berytus, of a noble family, and was himself distinguished in public office there, stands only in Eusebius's longer account of the martyrdom, held in damaged Greek with no other print; the Syriac of the longer form is not held. McGiffert says Eusebius himself was surely its author. The shorter account, in English, does not give it.
  • The manner of his death. The shorter account of the martyrdom, in English, says only that the governor Firmilian punished Pamphilus and his companions like the others; the longer form in Greek says a capital sentence, and Photius says by the sword. Twelve died in all, and Pamphilus was the only presbyter among them.
  • How much he copied. Jerome says the greater part of Origen's works, and Photius says he is said to have copied most of Origen's commentaries on Scripture. In Jerome's Latin, 'manu ejus' says only in his hand; Rufinus's editors Lommatzsch and Delarue read the phrase as Pamphilus's.
  • The end of Jerome's passage on the volumes. His Latin says Pamphilus seems to him to have marked them with the traces of his own blood; the English of the Library's Jerome volume has lines that seem traced in his blood. The Greek of Sophronius printed beside the Latin ends with the traces of his own suffering, read from a scan with letter faults.
  • Copies of the Bible. McGiffert reports a colophon in a Vatican manuscript, in Lightfoot's translation, saying that Pamphilus and Eusebius corrected the copy, and Taylor's report that many copies of the fifth column of Origen's Hexapla were issued from Caesarea under Pamphilus and Eusebius. The manuscripts are not held, so both rest on editors.
  • Other writings. The Ante-Nicene Fathers print an Exposition of the Chapters of the Acts of the Apostles under his name, and its notice says others ascribe it to Euthalius of Sulce; it is not counted here.
  • Photius's view of the Defense. Photius, writing five centuries later, says it is not a refutation of the charges against Origen but rather supports the accusation, and lists fifteen charges that the Defense calls slanders. A hostile late reader, whose Greek is not held except for one sentence in Delarue.
  • The text of the Defense. The Greek is lost. Book I survives only in Rufinus's Latin, held in two rough scans that agree with each other, and no English of it is held. Jerome charged Rufinus with changing passages on the Son and the Holy Spirit; the passage quoted in the story is not on those subjects.
