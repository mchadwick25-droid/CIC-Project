Armenian Christianity in Cilicia

Cilicia, on the Mediterranean coast, c. 1080 to 1300 — an Armenian church in exile that became a kingdom's church, whose greatest poet spent his life writing letters trying to end a seven-hundred-year division.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:04

Read along

After the Seljuks took the Armenian homeland, Armenians moved to Cilicia, on the coast of the Mediterranean Sea. There a small state grew into a kingdom, and Levon I was crowned in 1198. The Armenian church went with them. In 1151 its head, the catholicos, moved his seat to Hromkla, a fortress above the Euphrates. There Nerses Shnorhali, 'the Gracious', wrote the finest Armenian church poetry of his day. He and Manuel I, the Byzantine emperor, wrote each other long and remarkably generous letters. They wrote about how the Armenian and Greek churches might be one again. That never happened. In the next century the church in Cilicia dealt instead with the Latin crusader states next door. Some Armenians were glad of that closeness, and others deeply resented it. For a long time it shaped the church's arguments with itself.

People and Stories

Voices in the Record

  • Nerses Shnorhali — catholicos, poet and hymn-writer, whose letters to Emperor Manuel I are the era's most serious attempt at reunion between the Armenian and Byzantine churches
  • Nerses of Lambron — archbishop of Tarsus, who pushed the same conciliatory line further and was attacked at home for it
  • Levon I — crowned king of Armenian Cilicia in 1198, who bound the kingdom closely to the Latin West
  • Toros Roslin — the illuminator working at Hromkla whose manuscripts are regarded as the summit of Armenian book art

Documented Stories

Nerses Tells the Emperor What Armenians Will Not Do

In 1165 Nerses, then coadjutor to his brother the catholicos at Hromkla, met the Byzantine governor Alexios Axouch at Mopsuestia, and the governor asked him to explain the Armenian faith. Axouch took it to Constantinople; Manuel I read it and wrote back, and when Nerses became catholicos in 1166 the correspondence became a negotiation. He grants that the Armenian formula ‘one nature’ and the Greek ‘two natures’ can mean the same thing if each side will hear the other charitably; he explains why Armenians add ‘who was crucified for us’ to the Trisagion and mix no water with the wine and keep Christmas on 6 January, and says that on such customs each church should be left alone. The Armenians, he writes, will accept a union of faith, but not one imposed: ‘it is not fitting that the Armenians be compelled by force’ — the Greek church must come as an equal, not as a master, and the reunion must be decided by a council of the Armenian bishops, not by the catholicos alone. He died in 1173 before a council could be held; his successor called one, the Byzantine terms hardened, and the Armenian bishops of the east refused. The letters are the most patient attempt at it that either church ever made, and they are the record of a small church declining to be absorbed politely.

Connections

In Its Own Record

The Armenian church in exile became a kingdom's church: the catholicosate moved to the fortress of Hromkla in 1151, Nerses Shnorhali carried on ecumenical correspondence with the Byzantine emperor seeking reunion between the Armenian and Greek churches, and Levon I was crowned king in 1198. The kingdom itself fell in 1375.

Descended from

  • continues Bagratid Armenian Christianity Documented Major — Matthew of Edessa says Khatchig, head of the Armenian church, died in exile in 1065. He grieved for the lost crown of the Bagratid kings and for the high office handed down from Saint Gregory the Illuminator. When Peter held that office, Matthew adds, it had rich lands given by the Armenian kings. Later Matthew shows Basil in the same office, leaving Ani with his bishops in the early 1100s.

Direct descendants

  • continued as Armenian Christianity after the Kingdom (Sis to Etchmiadzin) Documented Major — Hethum, lord of Korikos, kept a table of the years. It says that in 1292 the Mamluk sultan al-Ashraf took Hromkla, where the Armenian patriarchs lived. He seized Stephen, the catholicos or head of the church, and Gregory VII was raised in his place. An unnamed writer carried the Constable Sempad's chronicle on to 1331. He says that in 1322 the king and most of the bishops chose Constantin of Lampron, bishop of Sis, as the next catholicos.

Influence, argument and contact

In Contact With

  • in contact with The Byzantine Church after the Schism Documented — Vartan, an Armenian historian of the 1200s, says Emperor Manuel asked the catholicos Gregory and his brother Nerses to set out their faith. They wrote it down and sought union with the Greeks, but the work was never finished. Michael the Syrian quotes a letter from Nerses. It lists what the Greeks asked of him, among them belief in two natures in Christ and honor for the fourth council.
  • in contact with The Latin Church of Outremer Documented — Kirakos of Gandzak wrote a history of Armenia in the 1200s. He tells how Catholicos Gregory III went on pilgrimage. At Antioch the people led him to the throne of Saint Peter. In Jerusalem, Kirakos says, the Franks and their patriarch made their bond with the Armenians stronger than ever.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

After the Seljuk conquest of the Armenian homeland, Armenians resettled in Cilicia on the Mediterranean coast, where the catholicos moved his seat to the fortress of Hromkla in 1151. There Nerses Shnorhali, 'the Gracious,' wrote the finest Armenian religious poetry of the age and carried on a long, generous correspondence with the Byzantine emperor Manuel I about reuniting the Armenian and Greek churches — a reunion that never happened. The Cilician church spent the following century negotiating instead with the neighboring Latin crusader states, a closeness some Armenians welcomed and others resented deeply.

Legacy

The Armenian Apostolic Church goes on, and this period is a load-bearing part of it. The catholicosate seated here still exists as the Catholicosate of Cilicia. It is now based at Antelias in Lebanon, and it is one of the two catholicosates of the Armenian church. Shnorhali's hymns and prayers are still sung and prayed. His long cycle of prayers of repentance is still one of the most used prayer texts in Armenian. The painted books made in Cilicia in these years, Roslin's above all, are among the great treasures of medieval art.

Experience It Today

  • Gaziantep province, Turkey — Rumkale, the Euphrates fortress the Armenians knew as Hromkla and used as their catholicosate, now reached by boat from Halfeti
