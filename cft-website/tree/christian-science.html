Christian Science

New England and outward, 1875 to 1906 — a church with no clergy and no sermons, built around one book and the healings its readers reported.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:09

Read along

Mary Baker Eddy was a New England woman in poor health for most of her early life who, after a recovery in 1866 that she attributed to reading the Gospels, spent nine years working out what she had understood and published it in 1875 as Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures. Around the book she organized a church in 1879, a college to train practitioners, and a set of periodicals that carried the healing testimonies of ordinary members. Its congregations have no ordained clergy, no sacraments and no sermons in the usual sense. Two lay readers read aloud from the Bible and from Eddy's book, the same lesson in every branch church in the world on any given Sunday. Eddy governed the movement closely through the Manual of The Mother Church, written in 1895, until her death in 1910. And the practitioners who did most of its actual work were, then as later, overwhelmingly women.

Documented Stories

The Fall on the Ice at Lynn

On the evening of Thursday 1 February 1866 Mary Patterson, as she then was, fell on the ice in Lynn, Massachusetts, on her way to a temperance meeting, struck her head and back, and was carried into a nearby house; the Lynn Reporter noted that she had been found insensible and was in a critical condition. Dr Alvin Cushing, a homeopath, attended her, and she was moved to her lodgings at Swampscott. She was forty-four, in chronic ill health since girlhood, and a former patient of the mental healer Phineas Quimby, who had died three weeks before. By her own later account, settled in the form her church has taught since, on the Sunday she read the account in Matthew of the palsied man told to arise, and rose from her bed healed; she dated the discovery of Christian Science from that day and said the doctor had not expected her to recover. Cushing, asked in 1907 by a magazine investigating her, swore an affidavit that he had never thought her injury dangerous, had treated her that summer for other complaints, and had heard nothing of an instantaneous cure. The two accounts are both in the record and cannot both be complete. What is not disputed is what followed: nine years of writing in rented rooms, Science and Health in 1875, a church in 1879, and by 1910 a movement of tens of thousands that dated itself from the third day after the fall.

The Death of Lottie Corner

Abby Corner was a practitioner of Christian Science in West Medford, Massachusetts, one of several hundred trained in Mary Baker Eddy's classes to treat the sick by prayer. In April 1888 she attended her daughter in labor, alone, as her practice required; the birth went wrong, and mother and baby died before a doctor was called. The coroner's inquest led to Mrs Corner's indictment for manslaughter, and the Boston papers made the case a trial of Christian Science. Eddy's response, in the Christian Science Journal and to the press, was that Mrs Corner had not been taught obstetrics in her classes, that Christian Science did not forbid the calling of a surgeon in such cases, and that the movement was not responsible for what she had done; the Journal added that practitioners who attended childbirth without preparation exceeded their competence. Mrs Corner was acquitted in June, the state being unable to prove that a doctor would have saved the mother. Her fellow students were angrier at Eddy than at the court. They had expected Eddy to defend her; the Journal's language read to them as abandonment of a member in trouble to protect the institution. In June 1888 thirty-six members of the Boston church, about a third, resigned together, taking the records, and Eddy spent the rest of the year rebuilding. She then added obstetrics to her college's course and in 1895 wrote the Manual that gave her control of every branch. The church's own account calls 1888 its rebellion.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

After a recovery in 1866 that she attributed to reading the Gospels, Mary Baker Eddy spent nine years working out what she had understood and published it in 1875 as Science and Health. Her congregations have no ordained clergy and no sermons in the usual sense — two lay readers read the same lesson from the Bible and her book in every branch church in the world on a given Sunday. The practitioners who did most of the movement's actual work, then as later, were overwhelmingly women, and their printed healing testimonies are its characteristic literature.

Legacy

It is an ongoing denomination, much smaller now than at its height, with branch churches and reading rooms in many cities and its landmark complex in Boston. Its most widely felt legacy is a newspaper. The Christian Science Monitor, founded by Eddy in 1908 in her late eighties, became a respected international daily that has won multiple Pulitzer Prizes and is read by people with no connection to the church at all. Its position on medical treatment has also been the subject of repeated legal and public debate over the years, particularly where children are concerned.

Sources

Doctrinal Floor Note

Christian Science uses the vocabulary of the Christian creed but, by its own published texts, means something different by it: Science and Health (1875) and the Manual of The Mother Church (1895) read God, Christ, sin, matter and healing in non-material terms rather than in the sense the historic creeds intend. A second and separate question is how far the movement is defined by one person — Mary Baker Eddy governed it personally, by her own written rules, until her death in 1910. Both points are recorded here as factual description of what the movement's own texts say, not as a judgment of it or of its members.

How Sure the Record Is

Rich founder corpus

  • Mary Baker Eddy, Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures (1875; revised to 1910) and the Manual of The Mother Church (1895) (primary) — The movement's own foundational confession, in Eddy's own words.
  • The church's own record and testimony genre: The Christian Science Journal (1883-) and Sentinel with the printed healing testimonies; Mother Church records; the Mary Baker Eddy Library papers (primary) — The testimony genre is this movement's own native form of ordinary-voice literature — self-selected by those who wrote in, worth reading as that.
  • The hostile record: the McClure's serial (1907, Milmine/Cather); Mark Twain, Christian Science (1907); the Next Friends suit record (1907) (primary) — Hostile from three directions at once — journalism, literature, and an adversarial lawsuit. Eddy's winning that suit is part of the record too.
  • Gillian Gill, Mary Baker Eddy (Perseus, 1998), with Stephen Gottschalk, The Emergence of Christian Science in American Religious Life (California, 1973) (secondary) — The current standard biography, written from an explicitly feminist-revisionist position, paired with an insider scholar's own standard account.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 8, ch. 22 'Christian America' and 'Christian Canada' (Noll) (reference)
