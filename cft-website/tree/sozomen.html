The Imperial Church Era · c. 370-380 to after 443 · Gaza, Constantinople

Gaza · Constantinople, today in Turkey

Sozomen

A person whose story no single tradition tells.

Known for
Church historian from near Gaza and an advocate, whose nine-book Church History, dedicated to the emperor Theodosius, sets out to include the founders of monastic life and their successors.
Also called
Salaminius Hermias Sozomen
Dates
Born between 370 and 380, by Chester Hartranft's guess; wrote some time after June 443, by Hartranft's dating; his history stops at 425
Place
Bethelia near Gaza, his family's town; Constantinople
Role
Advocate, church historian and recorder

About This Person

Under the emperor Julian, Christians had to flee from city to city. "My grandfather and many of my ancestors were compelled to flee in this manner," Sozomen writes in his Church History. His grandfather was born to pagans. He and his family, with that of Alaphion, were the first in Bethelia, a town near Gaza, to become Christians. It is said, Sozomen adds, that the monk Hilarion of Gaza won them by driving a demon out of Alaphion.

His grandfather was loved in Gaza and Ascalon for explaining hard places in Scripture. Members of "the other family" founded the first churches and monasteries there. Chester Hartranft, who revised the English translation, thinks it probably means Alaphion's. Sozomen saw some of its good men in his youth, "but they were then very aged."

He became an advocate. A friend was healed at the Michaelium, a shrine near Constantinople. Sozomen calls him "an advocate in the same court of justice as that to which we belong." He says the shrine helped him too.

He sent his history to the emperor, the younger Theodosius. He asked him to "purify it by thy labors," adding or cutting as he saw fit. The dedication says it would run to the emperor's seventeenth consulship. Hartranft dates that to 439, and notes that the history stops at 425.

He searched laws, synod records and letters. Each party, he warns, kept only "such epistles as favored their own heresy."

Sozomen never names Socrates of Constantinople, who wrote a few years before him. Hartranft finds the use of Socrates sure, from stories both tell almost alike. One view, he reports, is that Sozomen plagiarized Socrates. Hartranft calls that charge "wholly unfair" by the custom of the time.

Sozomen also wrote of the first monks. He wanted to leave "such a record of their manner of life that others, led by their example, might attain to a blessed and happy end."

Key Works

  • Church History: nine books dedicated to the emperor Theodosius, meant to run from 323 to 439 but stopping at 425, by Hartranft's dates; it gives a large place to the monks
  • An epitome in two books: his earlier summary of the church's history "from the ascension of Christ to the deposition of Licinius"; lost, as Hartranft notes

Legacy

What traces to them

  • A. C. Zenos reports that Epiphanius Scholasticus put the histories of Socrates of Constantinople, Sozomen and Theodoret of Cyrrhus into Latin under Cassiodorus, as the Tripartite History.
  • Hartranft notes that Theodoret "has taken his stuff from Sozomen, and says nothing about the original."

How they're remembered

  • Hartranft rejects the charge that Sozomen was "a feeble reviser of Socrates" as "wholly unfair and unwarranted by the general usage of his day".
  • Hartranft also grants that Socrates "was the finer mind".

What Is Uncertain

  • His life comes almost only from his own history, through what he says of his grandfather, his youth near Gaza and a friend at the Michaelium. The conversion of his family through the monk Hilarion of Gaza is something Sozomen reports as said: "It is said".
  • Sozomen does not name "the other family" whose members founded the first churches and monasteries near Gaza. Hartranft's note says he "probably means that of Alaphion."
  • His birth year rests on no held line. Hartranft places it between 370 and 380 by reckoning back from his grandfather and the exile under Julian, and says "We can only guess".
  • How far he copied Socrates of Constantinople is disputed. Hartranft lists three views: that he plagiarized Socrates, that both used the same sources, or that Socrates guided him to the sources. Hartranft finds the use sure and rejects the charge of plagiarism.
  • The meaning of his name Salaminius "cannot yet be accurately determined", Hartranft says.
  • No source used here gives the place or year of his death.
