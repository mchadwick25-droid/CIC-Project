The Union of Brest & Ruthenian Greek Catholic Christianity

Ruthenia — today's Ukraine and Belarus — from 1596: a church born inside an argument, Byzantine in worship and Roman in obedience, and never allowed to forget either half.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:00

Read along

In 1596 most of the Orthodox bishops of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth met at Brest and accepted communion with Rome while keeping their Byzantine liturgy, their Church Slavonic, their calendar and their married clergy. Their opponents met in the same town at the same time and rejected it outright, so what the union produced was not one church but two rival hierarchies claiming the same people and arguing in print for a century. The new church had to explain itself constantly — to Rome, to the Orthodox it had left, and to Latin bishops who never quite treated it as an equal. And that argument became the ordinary condition of its life rather than an occasional crisis. Its most famous figure, Archbishop Josaphat Kuntsevych, was killed by a hostile crowd at Vitebsk in 1623.

People and Stories

Voices in the Record

  • Ipatii Potii — bishop, chief architect of the union and its most persistent apologist
  • Josaphat Kuntsevych — Uniate archbishop of Polotsk, killed by a crowd at Vitebsk in 1623
  • Meletij Smotryc'kyj — polemicist who wrote the most eloquent Orthodox lament against the union, and years later joined it
  • Josyf Rutsky — metropolitan who reformed the Basilian monks and gave the church its institutional shape

Documented Stories

Vitebsk, 12 November 1623

Josaphat Kuntsevych became archbishop of Polotsk in 1618, a monk of austere life and a preacher the Orthodox had once crowded to hear, and set about bringing his diocese into the Union of Brest by closing the churches of priests who would not conform. In March 1622 the Lithuanian chancellor Lew Sapieha, a Catholic and a supporter of the union, wrote to him that his methods were losing the cause: the churches of Mogilev and Orsha were sealed, the Orthodox were holding services in fields and barns, the Cossacks were threatening war over it, and the union had been made a scarecrow. Josaphat answered that he was defending what the law gave him. On 12 November 1623 he was at Vitebsk, where his men had arrested an Orthodox priest; the town bells rang, a crowd broke into the archbishop's house, and Josaphat, going out to them, was struck with a halberd and shot, and his body was dragged through the streets and thrown into the Dvina. The king's commission arrived in January 1624: nineteen townsmen were beheaded, the town hall was demolished, and Vitebsk lost its charter of self-government and its bells. Josaphat was beatified in 1643 on the testimony gathered then. The Orthodox archbishop of the same city, Meletij Smotryc'kyj, whom the Uniates blamed for inciting the town, left for Constantinople and Jerusalem, and came back four years later a Uniate.

The Lament, and the Man Who Wrote It

In 1610 the Vilnius Orthodox brotherhood press issued a Polish book called Threnos, the Lament of the One Holy Universal Apostolic Eastern Church, under the name Theophil Ortholog. Its author was Meletij Smotryc'kyj, a young teacher in the brotherhood school. The book is a widow's lament: the Church speaks as a mother stripped of her robes and her children, and names them — the Ostroz'kyj, Sluc'kyj, Zbaraz'kyj, Wiśniowiecki and dozens of other houses who had gone over to Rome or to the Reformed. ‘Where are those priceless jewels of my crown?’ King Sigismund III ordered the book burned and the brotherhood's printer imprisoned; Uniates answered it in print; the Orthodox read it aloud, and his later Uniate biographer says they had it laid in their coffins. In 1620 Smotryc'kyj was secretly consecrated Orthodox archbishop of Polotsk, the see Josaphat Kuntsevych held for the union, and after Josaphat's murder he left for the Christian East. He returned in 1626 doubting whether the Greek patriarchs still taught what he had defended, and in 1627 he privately joined the union. In 1628 he published an Apology asking the Orthodox to follow him. A council at Kyiv that August made him stand while his book was torn up and its pages trodden underfoot; he signed a recantation and, back at his monastery at Derman', withdrew it and denounced the council. He died a Uniate in 1633.

In Its Own Record

A communion born inside an argument, where the pull between East and West was part of daily life. It was the sibling and rival of the Kyiv-Mohyla Orthodox revival. Its line runs on into the Greek Catholic church of the eighteenth century.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 1596, most Orthodox bishops of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth accepted communion with Rome while keeping their Byzantine liturgy, Church Slavonic and married clergy — while their opponents met in the same town and rejected it outright, producing two rival hierarchies that argued in print for a century. Its most famous bishop, Josaphat Kuntsevych, was killed by a hostile crowd at Vitebsk in 1623.

Legacy

It survives as the Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church and the Belarusian Greek Catholic Church. Both belong to the wider family of Eastern Catholic churches around the world. They worship in Eastern ways and are in communion with Rome. The Russian Empire put down the church in the nineteenth century, and the Soviet Union did so again in 1946. The Ukrainian church lived on in secret. It came back in 1989 as the largest Eastern Catholic church anywhere.

How Sure the Record Is

Moderate: synodal acts, brotherhood records, polemical literature (polemic as native genre)

  • The union documents: the 33 articles (1595), the acta of the two simultaneous Brest synods of 1596 (union and anti-union - both records carried), papal and royal instruments, in Documenta Unionis Berestensis (ed. Welykyj) (primary) — Available only in Latin and Church Slavonic.
  • The polemical literature, both sides: Ipatii Potii's union apologetics; Meletij Smotryc'kyj's Threnos (1610, as Orthodox) and his later pro-union works after converting; English access via Frick's scholarship and the Harvard Library of Early Ukrainian Literature (primary) — Polemic was this tradition's native genre, and both sides' literature is treated as formative. Smotryc'kyj's own switch from the Orthodox to the pro-union side is itself evidence of the identity tension at stake.
  • Brotherhood records: the Lviv Stauropegial brotherhood statutes, registers, and school records, with Vilnius and kin (primary) — Lay confraternities formed the backbone of Orthodox resistance — a rare glimpse of lay agency in this era's Eastern sources.
  • The Uniate church's early life: Metropolitan Rutsky's Basilian reform and pastoral correspondence; Josaphat Kuntsevych - pastoral record, murder (1623), canonization-process testimony (primary) — The Kuntsevych material is hagiographic and drawn from canonization-process testimony, so it's structured, guided evidence. The violence during this period ran in both directions.
  • Borys Gudziak, Crisis and Reform: The Kyivan Metropolitanate, the Patriarchate of Constantinople, and the Genesis of the Union of Brest (Harvard, 1998) (secondary) — The current standard work on the subject.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 6, ch. 18 'Latin Christianity and eastern Orthodoxy' (Dmitriev) (reference) — Directly covers this borderland region.
