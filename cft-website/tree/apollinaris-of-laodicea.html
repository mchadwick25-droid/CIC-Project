Apollinaris of Laodicea

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Apollinarian Christianity.

Known for
Scholar and bishop of Laodicea in Syria, who taught that the divine Word took the place of a human mind in Christ, and whose followers formed their own churches.
Also called
Apollinarius, Apolinarius, Apollinaris the younger
Dates
Put out of the church by George of Laodicea in 346, by Lietzmann's date; condemned at Rome in 377, by Lietzmann's date, and at Constantinople in 381; died in the reign of Theodosius; Lietzmann says the year is not known
Place
Laodicea in Syria; he also taught at Antioch
Role
Bishop of Laodicea; teacher of rhetoric and Scripture

About This Person

At Laodicea in Syria, the pagan teacher Epiphanius recited a hymn he had written to the god Dionysus. As the custom was, he told the uninitiated to go out. Among those who stayed were the young Apollinaris, his pupil, and his father, also named Apollinaris. Sozomen, a church historian of the fifth century, says the bishop, Theodotus, put both out of the church. After tears and fasting, Theodotus took them back.

Apollinaris of Laodicea was born in that city to a father from Alexandria, says Socrates of Constantinople. The next bishop, George, put the young man out again. Sozomen says it was for his friendship with Athanasius of Alexandria, then passing through. Socrates blames their friendship with Epiphanius.

When the emperor Julian barred Christians from the Greek classics, Socrates says father and son rewrote Scripture in Greek forms. The father put the books of Moses into epic verse, and the son turned the Gospels into dialogues like Plato's. Sozomen names only one of them. He gives him an epic of Hebrew history, plays and odes. Hans Lietzmann, who gathered his fragments in 1904, finds Sozomen the more credible.

His followers, Socrates says, kept the Nicene teaching on the Trinity. His teaching on Christ reaches us mostly through opponents. Socrates says he taught that Christ took a soul without reason, "God the Word himself being in the place of a mind." Gregory of Nazianzus, who wrote against him, reports the same. "For that which He has not assumed He has not healed," Gregory replied. A fragment kept by Gregory of Nyssa gives his own reason. If perfect God were joined to a perfect man, he wrote, there would be two sons, one by nature and one by adoption.

His followers formed their own churches, Sozomen says. Men sang his songs at their work, and women at the loom. Damasus of Rome condemned him at a Roman council, in 377 by Lietzmann's date. In 381 the council at Constantinople named the Apollinarians in its first canon.

Jerome of Strido heard him lecture at Antioch. "Although he instructed me in the holy scriptures, I never embraced his disputable doctrine as to their meaning," Jerome wrote. Lietzmann found that the church destroyed his books of doctrine wherever it could. Some survived because people who shared his views put them under trusted names, among them Athanasius and Julius of Rome. Jerome says he died under the emperor Theodosius.

Key Works

  • Demonstration of the Divine Incarnation: his main work on Christ, known in fragments that Gregory of Nyssa quotes in his book against it
  • Against Porphyry: thirty books against the pagan philosopher, which Jerome of Strido says were generally counted among his best works; lost
  • The Detailed Confession of Faith: a statement of faith that came down under the name of Gregory the Wonderworker, which Lietzmann gives to Apollinaris

Legacy

What traces to them

  • His followers formed churches of their own in various cities, under their own bishops, says Sozomen, a church historian of the fifth century. At Antioch they followed the priest Vitalius, and were still called Vitalians in Sozomen's day.
  • Theodoret of Cyrus, an opponent, traces to followers of his who returned to the church "the doctrine of the one nature of the Flesh and of the Godhead."
  • Lietzmann shows that works of his, put under the names of Athanasius, Julius and Felix of Rome and Gregory the Wonderworker, were later cited as orthodox witnesses until the fraud was found out.
  • Philip Schaff, quoted by Henry Percival in his English edition of the councils, calls him "the first to apply the results of the Nicene controversy to Christology proper."

How they're remembered

  • Sozomen says that but for the partiality shown to old books, "the writings of Apolinarius would be held in as much estimation as those of the ancients."
  • Jerome of Strido, his pupil at Antioch, says he never accepted "his disputable doctrine", and names him among his masters with Didymus.
  • Gregory of Nazianzus writes of "the boldness of the Apollinarians" as the worst of the troubles of the church.
  • Theodoret says he "assumed a mask of piety" and was soon seen to be an open foe.

At Major Councils

  • First Council of Constantinople, 381: Not present; his followers were condemned in canon 1

What Is Uncertain

  • His teaching on Christ reaches us mostly through opponents: Socrates of Constantinople, Gregory of Nazianzus, Gregory of Nyssa and Theodoret of Cyrus. Lietzmann found that the church destroyed his books of doctrine wherever it could. His own words survive in quotations by opponents, in later collections, and in works put under other names. Which pieces are his is Lietzmann's reconstruction, argued in his study of 1904.
  • Socrates gives the verse of the books of Moses to the father and the dialogues on the Gospels to the son. Sozomen names only one of them, whom Lietzmann takes for the son, and gives him an epic of Hebrew history, plays and odes. Lietzmann finds Sozomen the more credible, and thinks the grammar Socrates names may be the father's.
  • Sozomen says George of Laodicea put him out for his friendship with Athanasius of Alexandria. Socrates says it was for the friendship with the sophist Epiphanius, or, as some told it, because George wavered in his creed. Lietzmann reads the earlier expulsion under Theodotus as aimed at their stand for Nicaea.
  • Socrates says he first denied Christ a soul, then granted a soul without reason. Gregory of Nazianzus says a pamphlet of his made the flesh eternal and the Godhead able to suffer. Theodoret says he spoke both ways about the flesh. Ullman, whom Gregory's translators Browne and Swallow cite, thinks Gregory may have pushed the pamphlet further than Apollinaris meant.
  • Socrates and Sozomen both say his teaching grew from resentment at being put out. Sozomen gives this as "It is said".
  • Jerome calls him bishop of Laodicea. Lietzmann says he was perhaps a rival bishop there from about 360. Ernest Cushing Richardson, who translated Jerome's Lives, gives 362.
  • Lietzmann dates the council at Rome that condemned him to 377. Chester Hartranft, who translated Sozomen, notes two dates for it, 377 after Rade and 374 after Hefele.
  • The year of his death is not known, says Lietzmann. Jerome says he died under Theodosius. Gregory of Nyssa's book against him, written after 385 by Lietzmann's date, treats him as still alive. Richardson's note gives about 390.
