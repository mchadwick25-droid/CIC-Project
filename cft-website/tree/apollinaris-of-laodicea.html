Apollinaris of Laodicea

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Apollinarian Christianity.

Known for
Scholar and bishop of Laodicea in Syria, who taught that the divine Word took the place of a human mind in Christ, and whose followers formed their own churches.
Also called
Apollinarius, Apolinarius, Apollinaris the younger
Dates
Put out of the church by George of Laodicea in 346, by Lietzmann's date; condemned at Rome in 377, by Lietzmann's date, and at Constantinople in 381; died in the reign of Theodosius; Lietzmann says the year is not known
Place
Laodicea in Syria; he also taught at Antioch
Role
Bishop of Laodicea; teacher of rhetoric and Scripture

About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

When the emperor Julian barred Christian children from the schools of the Greek poets, a scholar at Laodicea in Syria answered with poems of his own. The historian Sozomen says he wrote an epic of Hebrew history in twenty-four books, to stand in for Homer, with plays and odes beside it. His name was Apollinaris, and he was already known as a friend of Athanasius.

In the fourth century he stood with Athanasius for the creed of Nicaea, which said the Son is of one being with the Father. The Arians, who followed Arius, held that the Son was created and lower. Then he taught that in Christ the divine Word, the Son of God as John's Gospel names him, took the place of a human mind. The church condemned that teaching at Rome and at Constantinople in 381, and his followers formed churches of their own.

He taught rhetoric, as his father taught grammar. Bishop George put him out of the church. Sozomen says the cause was his friendship with Athanasius, whom George's party shunned. Socrates says it was friendship with a pagan teacher, or, as some told it, a quarrel over George's wavering creed. In 362 Apollinaris sent envoys to a meeting of bishops that Athanasius held at Alexandria, and its letter calls him bishop.

Under Julian, Socrates says, father and son rewrote Scripture: the father the books of Moses in epic verse, the son the Gospels as dialogues like Plato's. Sozomen gives all of it to Apollinaris alone.

Almost all we have of Apollinaris comes through opponents, or under other names. Gregory of Nyssa quoted a book of his to refute it: "If God was joined to a man, complete to complete, they would be two. One would be Son of God by nature, and one adopted." So, he held, the Word replaced the human mind. Epiphanius of Salamis, who had loved him, at first would not believe the reports.

Jerome heard him lecture at Antioch, and says he learned Scripture from him but never took his teaching. His followers formed churches in many cities under their own bishops, and sang his songs. Sozomen says: "Men sang his songs at drinking parties and at work. Women sang them at the loom."

Power came down on them. A council at Rome under Damasus condemned him and his pupil, in the 370s. After the council at Constantinople in 381, Gregory of Nazianzus answered that without a human mind Christ could not heal ours: what he did not take on, he did not heal. Laws of the emperor Theodosius from 383 forbade his followers to meet or to ordain. In 388 their bishops lost the name of bishop and were barred from the cities. Jerome says he died under Theodosius; the year is not known.

His books lived on in disguise. Men who shared his views but stayed inside the main church put honored bishops' names, Athanasius among them, on his writings. So, says a German scholar who gathered his fragments, they saved them. One was a statement of faith to the emperor Jovian, carried as Athanasius's. It says Christ is not two natures but "one nature of God the Word made flesh", that is, God the Word and his flesh are one nature. In 429 Cyril of Alexandria cited it as Athanasius's. Modern editors give it to Apollinaris; one thought it might be Athanasius's. If they are right, the man condemned in 381 gave Cyril's followers, unseen, what one of them calls a watchword of orthodoxy.

In the 540s the emperor Justinian charged the one-nature party of his day with passing such writings off under the names of honored early teachers. He said: "They cunningly push aside the name of Apollinaris, because it plainly proves ungodliness. They put the names of the holy Fathers on his writings and on their own inventions."

Sozomen thought that, but for people's love of old books, his writings would be prized like the ancients'. Socrates said the poems and dialogues from Julian's years were now as if never written.

Quotations are given in our modern English. The printed wording of each is below.

Quotations and references

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “If God was joined to a man, complete to complete, they would be two. One would be Son of God by nature, and one adopted.”
    As printed
    “καὶ εἰ ἀνθρώπῳ (φησὶ) συνήφθη ὁ θεός, τέλειος τελείῳ, δύο ἂν ἦσαν, εἷς μὲν φύσει υἱὸς θεοῦ εἷς δὲ θετός.”
    Apollinaris, Demonstration of the Divine Incarnation, fragment 81, as Gregory of Nyssa quotes it, in Lietzmann's Greek
    The word in brackets, phesi, he says, is Gregory of Nyssa's, not Apollinaris's, and the modern English leaves it out. Teleios is rendered complete, since modern perfect would mislead. Migne's Gregory of Nyssa prints the same lines in a scan with faults, and reads as Lietzmann does. The book's title and the fragment's ascription are Lietzmann's reconstruction.
  • In our modern English
    “Men sang his songs at drinking parties and at work. Women sang them at the loom.”
    As printed
    “Men sang his strains at convivial meetings and at their daily labor, and women sang them while engaged at the loom.”
    Sozomen, Church History 6.25, tr. Chester D. Hartranft, in Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, second series, vol. 2
    Sozomen's Greek is held only in a scan with accent noise between the lines, so the English stands alone.
  • In our modern English
    “one nature of God the Word made flesh”
    As printed
    “μίαν φύσιν τοῦ θεοῦ λόγου σεσαρκωμένην”
    Statement of faith to the emperor Jovian, carried under Athanasius's name, in Lietzmann's Greek
    The ascription to Apollinaris is Lietzmann's and Hefele's, and Pusey thought it might be Athanasius's. Pusey's English puts the same words as One Nature Incarnate of God the Word.
  • In our modern English
    “They cunningly push aside the name of Apollinaris, because it plainly proves ungodliness. They put the names of the holy Fathers on his writings and on their own inventions.”
    As printed
    “τὴν γὰρ ᾿Απολλιναρίου προσηγορίαν ὡς σαφῆ δυσσεβείας ἔλεγχον δολερῶς ἀπωθούμενοι τὰς τῶν ἁγίων πατέρων ἐπωνυμίας τοῖς τούτου συγγράμμασι καὶ τοῖς ἑαυτῶν πλάσμασιν ἐπιτιθέασι”
    Justinian, tractate against the Monophysites, quoted in Lietzmann's Greek
    No second print of Justinian's Greek is held, and the scan of these lines reads clearly. The subject is Justinian's opponents of his own day, the Acephali, a one-nature party, and not Apollinaris's own pupils. The modern English gives hos as because and renders epititheasi as they put.

Key Works

  • Demonstration of the Divine Incarnation: his main work on Christ, known in fragments that Gregory of Nyssa quotes in his book against it
  • Against Porphyry: thirty books against the pagan philosopher, which Jerome of Strido says were generally counted among his best works; lost
  • The Detailed Confession of Faith: a statement of faith that came down under the name of Gregory the Wonderworker, which Lietzmann gives to Apollinaris

Legacy

What traces to them

  • His followers formed churches of their own in various cities, under their own bishops, says Sozomen, a church historian of the fifth century. At Antioch they followed the priest Vitalius, and were still called Vitalians in Sozomen's day.
  • Theodoret of Cyrus, an opponent, traces to followers of his who returned to the church "the doctrine of the one nature of the Flesh and of the Godhead."
  • Lietzmann shows that works of his, put under the names of Athanasius, Julius and Felix of Rome and Gregory the Wonderworker, were later cited as orthodox witnesses until the fraud was found out.
  • Philip Schaff, quoted by Henry Percival in his English edition of the councils, calls him "the first to apply the results of the Nicene controversy to Christology proper."
  • Socrates says his later followers still hold that the persons of the Trinity are of one being, and that this is their only point of difference from the church.
  • Theodoret, hostile, says that as time went on all but a few of his followers were gathered back into the church, but did not quite put away their old unsoundness and infected many sound people with it. He says this was how the doctrine of the one nature came to grow in the church. He also says Bishop Theodotus of Antioch took the sect into fellowship at its members' earnest request, though many of them kept their old unsoundness.
  • A law of 3 December 383 forbade the Apollinarians, with the Eunomians, Arians and Macedonians, to meet, to build churches or to ordain priests, and gave the houses where they met to the treasury. A law of 21 January 384 ordered them driven out of the capital. A law of 10 March 388 ordered them kept from all places and from the walls of the cities, gave their bishops' power to make clergy to no one, and took the name of bishop from their bishops. A law of 397 ordered their teachers out of the emperor's city.
  • A statement of faith to the emperor Jovian, carried in the manuscripts under Athanasius's name, says the one Son is not two natures, one to be worshipped and one not, but one nature of God the Word, made flesh and worshipped with his flesh in one worship. Lietzmann gives it to Apollinaris, and Hefele says it is not genuine and belongs rather to Apollinaris than to Athanasius.
  • Lietzmann says Cyril of Alexandria is the first witness we can reach who cites works of Apollinaris, without knowing it, as works of older orthodox fathers. In 429, in his memorial on the right faith to the empresses, Cyril cites the statement of faith to Jovian as Athanasius's. Leontius of Byzantium, whom Pusey dates to the beginning of the seventh century and translates, says Cyril cited it so against Theodore, and calls it an old error.
  • Hefele says Cyril and Dioscorus referred the phrase one nature of God the Word made flesh to Athanasius, and held it as an acknowledged watchword of orthodoxy.
  • Justinian, in a tractate against the one-nature party that Lietzmann dates, after Loofs, to 542 or 543, charged his opponents with putting the names of the holy Fathers on Apollinaris's writings and on their own inventions. His two false letters were one in the name of Julius, bishop of Rome, and one in the name of Athanasius to the emperor Jovian. His opponents were the Acephali, a one-nature party, and not Apollinaris's own pupils.
  • The Second Council of Constantinople, in 553, in its eleventh anathema, names Apollinaris among those to be anathematized with their impious writings.

How they're remembered

  • Sozomen says that but for the partiality shown to old books, "the writings of Apolinarius would be held in as much estimation as those of the ancients."
  • Jerome of Strido, his pupil at Antioch, says he never accepted "his disputable doctrine", and names him among his masters with Didymus.
  • Gregory of Nazianzus writes of "the boldness of the Apollinarians" as the worst of the troubles of the church.
  • Theodoret says he "assumed a mask of piety" and was soon seen to be an open foe.
  • Socrates says there were two men of the name at Laodicea, a father and a son. The father, a presbyter, came from Alexandria, taught grammar, and settled at Laodicea after teaching at Berytus. The son, a reader in the church, taught rhetoric. Sozomen agrees on their offices.
  • Sozomen says an earlier bishop of Laodicea, Theodotus, put father and son out of the church because they went to hear the sophist Epiphanius recite a hymn he had written to Dionysus. After tears and fasting, Sozomen says, Theodotus restored them to their offices.
  • Sozomen says those who held the Son to be of one being with the Father held the same of the Spirit, and that this was nobly maintained in Syria by Apollinaris, in Egypt by Athanasius.
  • Epiphanius of Salamis, writing about 376 by Lietzmann's date, calls him the aged and venerable man, always dear to him, to the blessed pope Athanasius and to all the orthodox, and says that when he first heard the teaching from some of his pupils he did not believe such a man had brought it in.
  • Basil of Caesarea, about 377, says Apollinarius is no less a cause of sorrow to the churches, that his ready pen and tongue have filled the world with his works, and that he distressed him the more because he seemed at the beginning to be on their side.
  • Philostorgius, as the Suda keeps him, says Apollinarius, Basil and Gregory of Nazianzus so confuted the party of unlikeness that Athanasius must be judged a child beside them, most of all Apollinarius, who was also skilled in Hebrew. Philostorgius was himself of the party they fought.
  • Vincent of Lérins, writing in 434, calls him one of the most distinguished men of his age, says he left the church many works against Porphyry and other enemies, and says his disciples were drawn one way by the church's authority and the other by their master's influence. Vincent says his teachings became the church's trial and not its edification.

At Major Councils

  • First Council of Constantinople, 381: Not present; his followers were condemned in canon 1

What Is Uncertain

  • His teaching on Christ reaches us mostly through opponents: Socrates of Constantinople, Gregory of Nazianzus, Gregory of Nyssa and Theodoret of Cyrus. Lietzmann found that the church destroyed his books of doctrine wherever it could. His own words survive in quotations by opponents, in later collections, and in works put under other names. Which pieces are his is Lietzmann's reconstruction, argued in his study of 1904.
  • Socrates gives the verse of the books of Moses to the father and the dialogues on the Gospels to the son. Sozomen names only one of them, whom Lietzmann takes for the son, and gives him an epic of Hebrew history, plays and odes. Lietzmann finds Sozomen the more credible, and thinks the grammar Socrates names may be the father's.
  • Sozomen says George of Laodicea put him out for his friendship with Athanasius of Alexandria. Socrates says it was for the friendship with the sophist Epiphanius, or, as some told it, because George wavered in his creed. Lietzmann reads the earlier expulsion under Theodotus as aimed at their stand for Nicaea.
  • Socrates says he first denied Christ a soul, then granted a soul without reason. Gregory of Nazianzus says a pamphlet of his made the flesh eternal and the Godhead able to suffer. Theodoret says he spoke both ways about the flesh. Ullman, whom Gregory's translators Browne and Swallow cite, thinks Gregory may have pushed the pamphlet further than Apollinaris meant.
  • Socrates and Sozomen both say his teaching grew from resentment at being put out. Sozomen gives this as "It is said".
  • Jerome calls him bishop of Laodicea. Lietzmann says he was perhaps a rival bishop there from about 360. Ernest Cushing Richardson, who translated Jerome's Lives, gives 362.
  • Lietzmann dates the council at Rome that condemned him to 377. Chester Hartranft, who translated Sozomen, notes two dates for it, 377 after Rade and 374 after Hefele.
  • The year of his death is not known, says Lietzmann. Jerome says he died under Theodosius. Gregory of Nyssa's book against him, written after 385 by Lietzmann's date, treats him as still alive. Richardson's note gives about 390.
  • Where the teaching began. Rufinus, who writes about 402 and is hostile, says Apollinaris, a man well taught in other things, drove himself too hard in his love of contention and made his heresy out of it. Socrates and Sozomen give resentment at being put out. Socrates states it. Sozomen says it is said.
  • The two stages. Rufinus says he first held that the Lord took only a body and no soul, then, pressed with the gospel, that he took a soul but not its reasoning part, with the Word of God filling that part's place. Socrates, who writes later, gives the same two stages after him.
  • Vitalius. Theodoret says Apollinaris had already ordained Vitalius at Antioch. Sozomen says Vitalius, a priest of Antioch, joined Apollinaris and led the followers there, and gives a motive for it with it is said.
  • When he became bishop. Athanasius's letter on the synod of 362 says certain monks of Apolinarius the bishop were sent from him to it. Lietzmann says when and how he got the office can only be guessed.
  • When Jerome heard him. Jerome says he listened to Apollinaris at Antioch. Lietzmann dates the lectures to 374.
  • Julian's works. Lietzmann places the writings in classical forms in 362. Hartranft, Sozomen's translator, sets apart Apollinaris of Hierapolis, a different and earlier man.
  • Who wrote the statement of faith to Jovian, and who put the false names on his works. Lietzmann and Hefele give the statement to Apollinaris. Pusey, another editor, defended Athanasius's authorship. Leontius, whom Pusey places at the beginning of the seventh century, and Justinian, in the 540s, say it is not Athanasius's. Lietzmann says followers who held Apollinaris's views at heart but stayed in the main church put honored names on his works, and so saved them from destruction. Justinian's they are his opponents of the 540s, so no held source ties the false names to the laws against his followers.
  • Epiphanius's words are held only in a damaged scan, with the Greek columns mixed with garbled Latin, and no other held print gives the full Greek. Lietzmann's study confirms only the first words, and the editor's note on Vincent says people would hardly credit it at first. So the card tells it in English and prints no Greek.
  • Lietzmann says the church destroyed his books of doctrine so thoroughly that even his work against Porphyry is lost. No held line says they were burned.
