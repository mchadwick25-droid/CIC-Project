The Age of Monks and Empires · 451-615 · Ethiopia / Eritrea

Aksumite Christianity

Ethiopia and Eritrea, 451 to 615 CE — a Christian kingdom on the Red Sea that sent an army across the water to avenge a massacre, and later gave shelter to the first Muslim refugees.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:26

Read along

Aksum had been Christian since the fourth century. After Chalcedon it was a serious Red Sea power, with its own coinage and its own script, and its Bible was being translated into Ge'ez. Tradition credits the spread of monasticism and much of that translation work to the Nine Saints. They are said to have been monks from the Roman east, arriving around the turn of the sixth century. In 523 the Jewish king of Himyar, across the water in Yemen, massacred the Christians of Najran. The Aksumite king Kaleb crossed the Red Sea in about 525, killed him, and set up a Christian king who paid Aksum tribute. Not long after, the Aksumite soldiers who had stayed behind set up another king, Abramus, and Aksum could not remove him. A Miaphysite historian of the time counted the Aksumite king as a fellow believer. No text from inside these years shows where its church stood on Chalcedon. Evidence from these years is thin: inscriptions, a few very early gospel books, archaeology. Muslim tradition remembers that around 615 Muhammad sent his first followers to its king for protection.

Documented Stories

The Merchant Who Copied the Stone at Adulis

Cosmas, later nicknamed Indicopleustes, ‘the India-sailor’, was a Nestorian-leaning merchant from Alexandria who had traded down the Red Sea and would later become a monk and write a Christian Topography arguing that the earth is flat. In its second book he records a small errand. He was at Adulis, the port of Aksum, at the beginning of Justin’s reign, ‘when Ellatzbaas [Kaleb], king of the Axomites, was about to go to war against the Homerites across the sea’. The governor of Adulis, Asbas, asked him and another merchant to copy a Greek inscription on a marble throne and a basalt stele that stood at the edge of the town — the king wanted a copy — and Cosmas did it, kept a copy for himself, and put it in his book. It was an old inscription of Ptolemy III and a later one of an unnamed Aksumite king recording conquests, and it is the only reason either text survives. Cosmas also describes what he saw: the churches at Adulis, the road inland to Aksum, the Christian communities of the trade route from Ethiopia to Ceylon, ‘with clergy and congregations, though I do not know whether there are Christians beyond them’. He noted the Aksumite king’s obelisk and the gold trade with the interior, and drew the throne. He was an eyewitness to the Christian Red Sea world in the year Aksum went to war for the Christians of Arabia, and he was there because of cargo.

Kaleb Crosses the Sea

In 523 Yusuf As’ar Yath’ar, called Dhu Nuwas, a Himyarite king who had adopted Judaism, took the Christian town of Najran in the Yemen after a siege, and — according to the letter that Simeon, bishop of Beth Arsham, wrote from the Arab camp within months — burned its church with the clergy inside, executed the leading men, and killed the women who refused to deny Christ, among them a noblewoman named Ruhm whose speech to the king the letter records. Kaleb, king of Aksum, whose predecessors had held part of Arabia before, assembled a fleet — Procopius says Justin sent ships from Egypt — and crossed in 525. The Aksumite inscriptions and the Syriac sources agree on what followed: Yusuf was killed, his army destroyed, the Himyarite kingdom garrisoned, a Christian Himyarite named Sumyafa Ashwa installed as client king, and churches built at Najran and Zafar. The Martyrdom of Arethas, the Greek account, describes Kaleb fasting and praying before the crossing and vowing to become a monk afterwards, which Ethiopian tradition says he did, sending his crown to Jerusalem. The Christian sources present the war as vengeance for martyrs and record its slaughter with satisfaction; the Muslim tradition remembers the ‘people of the ditch’ of Najran with sympathy and the Aksumite occupation with none. What is documented is a massacre of Christians answered by a Christian invasion that killed a king and a good part of his people, and a church that made the invader a saint.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Aksum was the parent of the later medieval Ethiopian church. Evidence for its ties to Alexandria in these years is thin, but a Miaphysite historian of the time counted its king a fellow believer. The Najran martyrs of 523 and King Kaleb's campaign in Himyar around 525 are part of its own story.

Descended from

  • continues Aksumite Christianity (conversion era) Documented Major — In the 350s the emperor Constantius wrote to Ezana, ruler of Aksum, about its bishop, Frumentius. In the sixth century the land still had a bishop and a Christian king. A Syriac book from soon after 525 names Euprepios as bishop under King Kaleb. Procopius calls the Ethiopian king of his day a devoted Christian, and Cosmas lists Axum as a land with churches. No bishop is named in these texts between Frumentius and Euprepios.

Direct descendants

  • continued as Post-Aksumite Ethiopian Christianity Widely Accepted Major — The later Ethiopian church kept Aksum's king Kaleb in its calendar of saints. Its Synaxarium remembers him as a holy man 'of the True Faith' who went to war for Najran. Modern histories of Aksum treat the Ethiopian church as the same body carried on.

Influence, argument and contact

In Contact With

  • in contact with The Church of Alexandria and Miaphysite Egypt Inferential-Thin — A Syriac letter on the Najran killings of the 520s asks that the patriarch of Alexandria write to the Ethiopian king for help. John of Ephesus calls the Aksumite king a fellow believer. No surviving text from these years is known to show a letter actually sent between them.
  • in contact with Syriac Orthodox (West Syriac) Christianity Widely Accepted — In 524 Simeon of Beth Arsham wrote a letter on the killing of the Christians of Najran. At an Arab camp he met a Christian envoy sent by the king of Himyar, whom the Ethiopians had set up. One scholar of the 1800s thought the letter a forgery, but most accept it.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Aksum was already a Christian kingdom, set astride the trade routes of the Red Sea. Around 525 its king Kaleb crossed the water to avenge the slaughter of Christians at Najran. For a time after that, southern Arabia paid Aksum tribute. A Miaphysite historian counted its king as one who shared his faith. But direct evidence from this time is thin, and most of it comes from inscriptions, early manuscripts and archaeology.

Legacy

The same institution continued without interruption and is today the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church, one of the largest of the Oriental Orthodox communions, alongside its Eritrean counterpart. Ge'ez, the language its Bible was translated into in this period, is still its liturgical language, and its scriptural canon preserves books — Enoch, Jubilees — that no other church kept. The Garima Gospels, radiocarbon-dated to roughly these centuries and still held at a monastery in Tigray, are among the oldest illustrated Christian manuscripts to survive anywhere.

  • The Garima Gospels (Abba Garima Ge'ez gospel manuscripts) (primary) — Radiocarbon-dated c.5th-7th c. - the earliest Ethiopic gospel books and witnesses to the Ge'ez Bible translation itself; studied and illustrated in McKenzie & Watson, The Garima Gospels (2016).
  • Cosmas Indicopleustes, Christian Topography, esp. bk 2 (trans. J. W. McCrindle, 1897) (primary) — Outside eyewitness who copied the Monumentum Adulitanum at Adulis c.520s and describes Aksumite trade-world Christianity; old English translation online.
  • Stuart Munro-Hay, Aksum: An African Civilisation of Late Antiquity (1991) (secondary) — Standard synthesis of Aksumite history and archaeology incl. Christianization; full text has circulated free online.
  • David W. Phillipson, Foundations of an African Civilisation: Aksum and the Northern Horn (2012) (secondary) — The current archaeological synthesis, updating Munro-Hay; this window's evidence rests mainly on inscriptions, manuscripts and archaeology; James Currey, in print.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity, vol. 2, ch. 4, 'Early Asian and East African Christianities' (David Bundy) (reference) — Reference chapter covering Ethiopia among the non-Roman Christianities.
