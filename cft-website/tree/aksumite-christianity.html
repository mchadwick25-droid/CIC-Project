The Age of Monks and Empires · 451-615 · Ethiopia / Eritrea

Aksumite Christianity

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Aksum had been Christian since the fourth century. After Chalcedon it was a serious Red Sea power, with its own coinage and its own script, and its Bible was being translated into Ge'ez. Tradition credits the spread of monasticism and much of that translation work to the Nine Saints. They are said to have been monks from the Roman east, arriving around the turn of the sixth century. In 523 the Jewish king of Himyar, across the water in Yemen, massacred the Christians of Najran. The Aksumite king Kaleb crossed the Red Sea in about 525, killed him, and set up a Christian king who paid Aksum tribute. Not long after, the Aksumite soldiers who had stayed behind set up another king, Abramus, and Aksum could not remove him. A Miaphysite historian of the time counted the Aksumite king as a fellow believer. No text from inside these years shows where its church stood on Chalcedon. Evidence from these years is thin: inscriptions, a few very early gospel books, archaeology. Muslim tradition remembers that around 615 Muhammad sent his first followers to its king for protection.

In Its Own Record

Continues

  • Aksumite Christianity (conversion era) Documented Major — In the 350s the emperor Constantius wrote to Ezana, ruler of Aksum, about its bishop, Frumentius. In the sixth century the land still had a bishop and a Christian king. A Syriac book from soon after 525 names Euprepios as bishop under King Kaleb. Procopius calls the Ethiopian king of his day a devoted Christian, and Cosmas lists Axum as a land with churches. No bishop is named in these texts between Frumentius and Euprepios.

Continued As

  • Post-Aksumite Ethiopian Christianity Widely Accepted Major — The later Ethiopian church kept Aksum's king Kaleb in its calendar of saints. Its Synaxarium remembers him as a holy man 'of the True Faith' who went to war for Najran. Modern histories of Aksum treat the Ethiopian church as the same body carried on.

In Contact With

  • Cyrilline / Miaphysite Egyptian Christianity Inferential-Thin — A Syriac letter on the Najran killings of the 520s asks that the patriarch of Alexandria write to the Ethiopian king for help. John of Ephesus calls the Aksumite king a fellow believer. No surviving text from these years is known to show a letter actually sent between them.

Sources

  • Ezana inscriptions and Aksumite royal epigraphy (RIE; trans. in Munro-Hay, Aksum, and elsewhere) (primary) — The kingdom's own words - Ezana's shift to Christian formulae, later Kaleb-era texts; scattered scholarly editions (Recueil des inscriptions de l'Ethiopie), no single accessible English corpus.
  • The Garima Gospels (Abba Garima Ge'ez gospel manuscripts) (primary) — Radiocarbon-dated c.5th-7th c. - the earliest Ethiopic gospel books and witnesses to the Ge'ez Bible translation itself; studied and illustrated in McKenzie & Watson, The Garima Gospels (2016).
  • Cosmas Indicopleustes, Christian Topography, esp. bk 2 (trans. J. W. McCrindle, 1897) (primary) — Outside eyewitness who copied the Monumentum Adulitanum at Adulis c.520s and describes Aksumite trade-world Christianity; old English translation online.
  • Stuart Munro-Hay, Aksum: An African Civilisation of Late Antiquity (1991) (secondary) — Standard synthesis of Aksumite history and archaeology incl. Christianization; full text has circulated free online.
  • David W. Phillipson, Foundations of an African Civilisation: Aksum and the Northern Horn (2012) (secondary) — The current archaeological synthesis, updating Munro-Hay; this window's evidence rests mainly on inscriptions, manuscripts and archaeology; James Currey, in print.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity, vol. 2, ch. 4, 'Early Asian and East African Christianities' (David Bundy) (reference) — Reference chapter covering Ethiopia among the non-Roman Christianities.
