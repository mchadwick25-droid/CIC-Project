Isidore of Seville

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Nicene Visigothic Iberia.

Known for
Bishop of Seville after his elder brother Leander of Seville, and author of the Etymologies. His friend Braulio of Zaragoza says it gathers well-nigh everything that ought to be known. He signed first at the Fourth Council of Toledo.
Also called
Isidorus Hispalensis, Isidorus Iunior
Dates
Bishop of Seville for almost forty years, from King Reccared's time to King Sisenand's, Ildefonsus of Toledo says. Theodor Mommsen sets his death in 636, from Braulio, Ildefonsus and an epitaph that gives 4 April. Mommsen puts the start of his time as bishop in Reccared's last years or under King Liuva; Ernest Brehaut gives probably 600. His birth year rests on no sound source used here
Place
Seville, in the province of Baetica; his father Severianus was of the province of Cartagena, Isidore says; Toledo, for councils and the king's court
Role
Bishop of Seville, metropolitan of the province of Baetica; writer

About This Person

It was the seventh year, Braulio of Zaragoza thought, since he had first asked Isidore of Seville for his Etymologies. Now he would turn his prayers into a complaint, he wrote. "Give back, give back what you owe," he pleaded. Their Latin is told here in modern words. One of Isidore's letters to him tells how the book went. Braulio's letters had found him at Toledo, on his way to a council. Weak and tired, Isidore sent the Etymologies on from the road, uncorrected because of his illness.

Isidore was bishop of Seville in the Visigothic kingdom of Spain. Ildefonsus, a later bishop of Toledo, says he followed his brother Leander of Seville in the see. Leander's Rule for their sister Florentina calls Isidore the younger brother, left a child when their parents died.

Ildefonsus says he held the see for almost forty years, from King Reccared's time to King Sisenand's. His speaking, Ildefonsus writes, had such richness that it left his hearers struck with wonder. Theodor Mommsen sets his death in 636.

Under King Sisebut, Isidore is named first among the bishops who met in council at Seville. Their acts tell of a Syrian bishop, as he claimed, from the sect they call the Acephali. He denied two natures in Christ and said the Godhead could suffer. After long debate he gave up his teaching and confessed two natures in one person.

At the Fourth Council of Toledo, in 633, Isidore signed first among sixty-six bishops of Spain and Gaul. The council ruled that from then on no Jew should be forced to believe. Jews already forced into baptism under King Sisebut, it added, must be made to keep the faith they had received. In his History of the Goths, Isidore wrote that Sisebut had zeal, but not according to knowledge. The king compelled by force, he says, those he ought to have drawn to the faith by reason.

Braulio says Isidore wrote the Etymologies at his request and left the work unfinished. Braulio himself divided it into twenty books. In it, he says, Isidore "has gathered well-nigh everything that ought to be known", in Ernest Brehaut's English. Ildefonsus says Isidore seemed to reach his last day still at work on it.

Its first book, on grammar, explains why the origins of words are worth knowing. "For every consideration of a thing is clearer," Isidore wrote, in Brehaut's English, "when its etymology is known."

Key Works

  • Etymologies, or Origins: twenty books on the arts, medicine, law, the church, peoples, the earth and the things of daily life, explained through the origins of their names; Braulio says Isidore left it unfinished and that he divided it into books
  • Sentences: three books, which Braulio says Isidore adorned with passages from Pope Gregory the Great's Moralia
  • History of the Goths, Vandals and Sueves: a history of the Gothic kings down to King Suinthila's reign, with shorter histories of the Vandals and Sueves, opening with a praise of Spain; it says King Sisebut had zeal but not according to knowledge

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Braulio of Zaragoza says he divided the Etymologies into twenty books. Before his own copy came, he wrote to Isidore, many people already had it cut short and damaged.
  • The Fourth Council of Toledo, where Isidore signed first, ruled that from then on no Jew should be forced to believe. Jews already baptized by force under King Sisebut, it said, must be made to keep the faith.
  • Ildefonsus of Toledo carried on Isidore's book On Famous Men and gave Isidore a chapter of his own.

How they're remembered

  • Braulio, in Ernest Brehaut's English: "In him antiquity reasserted itself—or rather, our time laid in him a picture of the wisdom of antiquity."
  • Braulio applies to him words of "the philosopher", which Ernest Brehaut traces to Cicero writing to Varro. In Brehaut's English they run: "your books brought us home, as it were, so that we could at last recognize who and where we were."
  • In a letter signed in his own hand, Braulio calls him a burning lamp that does not fade.
  • Ildefonsus says his speaking had such richness that it left his hearers struck with wonder.

What Is Uncertain

  • His life is known mostly from his friend Braulio of Zaragoza, from Ildefonsus of Toledo, from his own letters and from the acts of councils. Ernest Brehaut says surprisingly little is known of it. Brehaut calls the Lives by Lucas Tudensis and Rodericus Cerratensis, both of the thirteenth century, full of fables; they are not among the sources used here.
  • When he became bishop. Ildefonsus says he followed his brother Leander and held the see for almost forty years. Theodor Mommsen puts his start in Reccared's last years or under King Liuva. Ernest Brehaut gives probably 600. The year of Leander's death is itself disputed.
  • The day of his death. Theodor Mommsen gives 4 April 636, from an epitaph and the martyrologies. He reports the account of his death by the cleric Redemptus, which has him die four days after a public penance. Mommsen thinks the 31 March given with that account is, it seems, an error. Ernest Brehaut gives April 636, from Redemptus. Bernhard Poschmann retells Redemptus's account and quotes its Latin. Isidore, he reports, had himself carried into the church of Saint Vincent and did public penance before the people and two bishops.
  • Whether he presided at the Fourth Council of Toledo. Its acts give him the first signature. Ernest Brehaut says he probably presided there. Francisco Antonio Lorenzana, in his preface to the Breviarium Gothicum, says he did. Brehaut also says he presided at the Second Council of Seville, whose acts name him first among the bishops.
  • The year of the Second Council of Seville. Its acts give the Spanish era 657, which is 619, and King Sisebut's ninth year. Pagi, in a note Mansi prints, says the two do not agree. He follows later Spanish chroniclers, who give Sisebut's seventh year. That sets it in 618, he says, if it met in November or December, which is not clear. Ernest Brehaut gives 619.
  • To whom he sent the Etymologies. Braulio says Isidore wrote it at his request. Its covering letter is addressed to King Sisebut in W. M. Lindsay's edition, and to Braulio in the text Migne reprints from Enrique Flórez. Lindsay reports manuscripts that head the work to Braulio, to Sisebut, or to both. Ernest Brehaut calls the preface addressed to Braulio.
  • The book's early copies. Isidore says he sent it from the road uncorrected, because of illness. Lindsay says the copy behind all the manuscripts was full of faults. It is often unclear, he says, whether a confusion is Isidore's own or a scribe's.
  • His birth. A chronicle printed in Patrologia Latina 80 under the name of Maximus of Zaragoza sets his birth at Seville by the year 556. Nicolás Antonio, in the notice printed with it, treats the chronicles under Maximus's name as a later fable. Common report, he says, gives it to Jerónimo Román de la Higuera.
