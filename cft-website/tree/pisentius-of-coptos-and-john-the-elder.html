Pisentius of Coptos and John the Elder

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Church of Alexandria and Miaphysite Egypt.

Also called Apa Pisentius (bishop and anchorite of the mountain of Tsenti), Pesenthius (in W. E. Crum's English of the Epiphanius letters), Pesynthius (in W. E. Crum's English of the Epiphanius letters), John the Presbyter (the title's name for John)

Pisentius was bishop of Coptos in Upper Egypt, who hid in the mountain of Djeme when the Persians held Egypt. John, his disciple and servant, wrote his Life as a sermon for his feast day, and is a witness in it.

About This Person

When the Persians were masters of Egypt, Pisentius, bishop of Coptos in Upper Egypt, fled to the mountain of Djeme and hid there. "And at that time I John went with him," writes his disciple, John the Presbyter, "and I was with him in the capacity of a servant." When their water ran out, Pisentius said he thought there was water in one of the vessels, which they had forgotten. John found them full to the brim, a wonder, he says, "which I myself saw with my own eyes."

John wrote the Life of Pisentius as a sermon for the bishop's feast day. He is also a witness inside it, so the two share this profile. E. A. Wallis Budge translated it in 1913 from a Coptic manuscript copied in 1006.

Some things John only heard, and he says so. The young Pisentius, "it is said," saw a pillar of fire while he kept his father's sheep. Later, as a monk in his cell, Pisentius recited the whole of Jeremiah and then Ezekiel while his brother waited outside.

When the clergy came to make him bishop, he hid. They found him at last in the region of Djeme. He cried out, in the words of the great John of Constantinople, that he loved the life of peaceful contemplation. As the men who found him told it, John writes, "it was not he who ran in pursuit of the honour, but it was the honour which ran in pursuit of him."

As bishop he gave to the poor in every town and village as far as Syene, John says. He also wrote to his people, warning that God would bring on them "a nation fierce of visage and cruel" unless they repented. The letter names no nation, and Budge takes it to mean the Persians.

He died at sunset on the thirteenth of the month Epep, which Budge reckons as the seventh of July. John gives only "this fifth year", which Budge reads as a year of the indiction, a repeating count that does not fix the year. Pisentius had kept one coin since his years as a monk, to pay for his shroud.

Near the end, John asked how they would go on serving the poor if he left them nothing. Pisentius answered that "whatsoever thou givest — everything — to the poor, the Lord will give unto us twofold."

Timeline

Dates
Late sixth and early seventh centuries. E. A. Wallis Budge says Pisentius flourished in the second half of the sixth century and the first half of the seventh, and guesses his birth about 550. Budge has him consecrated by the patriarch Damian some year between 570 and 603. The Life puts his flight while the Persians were masters of Egypt, which Budge's note dates to 616 by Theophanes, or three or four years later by modern reckoning. Pisentius died on 13 Epep, which Budge reckons as 7 July; John gives only "this fifth year" of the indiction, which does not fix the year. John wrote after the death, in a year no held source gives
Place
Coptos (Keft), on the east bank of the Nile in Upper Egypt, his see; the mountain of Tsenti near Coptos, where he lived as an anchorite; the region of Djeme, on the west bank at Thebes, where he hid twice
Role
Pisentius: monk, anchorite and bishop of Coptos. John: his disciple and servant, a presbyter, author of his Life

Key Works

  • The Life of Bishop Pisentius, by John the Elder: a sermon for the bishop's feast on 13 Epep, in the Sahidic Coptic of a manuscript copied in 1006. Held in E. A. Wallis Budge's English of 1913, with the Coptic
  • Letters of Bishop Pesynthius: letters to and from a bishop of that name among the papers of the monastery of Epiphanius at Thebes, in W. E. Crum's English. Crum warns that not every Pesenthius in them is the bishop

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Budge says Moses, whom Pisentius charged on his deathbed to keep his parchment books, was his successor as bishop. W. E. Crum asks whether a Moses called bishop and confessor was that successor.
  • A Memphitic version of the Life, published by Émile Amélineau, names John together with Moses, bishop of Keft, as its authors. In an appendix Budge translates the three of its episodes that his manuscript lacks, among them the story of the speaking mummy.
  • Abu Salih places a monastery of Saint Pisentius west of Kus, with his tomb.

How they're remembered

  • The Ethiopic Synaxarium keeps his death on 13 Hamle, as bishop of the city of Keft.
  • The Arabic Synaxary of the Coptic church, in René Basset's French, keeps his death on 13 Abib.
  • A discourse on the holy man Apa Onnophrius, in a manuscript of 1042, goes under the name of Apa Pisenthius of Mount Tsinti, in Budge's English.

Open Questions

  • The Life gives no year that can be fixed for any event. Budge guesses Pisentius was born about 550 and consecrated between 570 and 603. His introduction dates the Persian invasion to between 514 and 520, a century too early; his own note gives 616 or a few years later.
  • His year of death. The Life gives the day and the fifth year of the indiction, which Budge marks with a question mark. No held source gives the year.
  • The letter's nation. Pisentius's letter warns of the Barbarians and of a nation fierce of visage, and names none. Budge takes it to mean the Persians.
  • Who wrote the Life. The Sahidic title names John the Presbyter. The Memphitic title names John with Moses, bishop of Keft.
  • His youth. John tells the pillar of fire as something said. Budge says it is not known when or at what age Pisentius became a monk.
  • Budge's introduction names Elijah the Presbyter at the deathbed, where the Sahidic Life has Elisha.
