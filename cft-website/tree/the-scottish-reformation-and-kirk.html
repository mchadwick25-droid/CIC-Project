The Scottish Reformation & Kirk

Scotland, 1560 to 1650 — a church that set out to reform an entire nation parish by parish, and wrote down what it found there.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:00

Read along

In 1560 the Scottish Parliament abolished papal authority and adopted a Reformed confession of faith, and the church that followed set out to remake a whole nation rather than a single city: a minister and a school in every parish, and in each parish a kirk session of elders meeting to deal with quarrels, drunkenness, broken promises and sexual misconduct. Those session registers survive in extraordinary quantity, which makes Scotland the best-documented case anywhere of church discipline as it was actually administered. Usually over small matters, among neighbors who then had to go on living beside each other. The Kirk's covenanting instinct, the idea of a whole people bound to God by public oath, came to a head in the National Covenant of 1638 and in the conflicts that followed it.

People and Stories

Voices in the Record

  • Andrew Melville — who pressed the Kirk toward a fully presbyterian government in the decades after Knox's death
  • Archibald Johnston of Wariston — lawyer, co-drafter of the National Covenant of 1638, and a relentless diarist of his own conscience

Documented Stories

Knox Makes the Queen Weep

John Knox recorded four interviews with Mary Queen of Scots in his History, and they are the most vivid pages he wrote, partly because he gives the queen her lines. In the summer of 1563, with negotiations under way for Mary to marry Don Carlos of Spain, Knox preached at St Giles that if the nobles consented to a Catholic marriage they would betray the realm and the queen would bring God’s plagues on it. Mary sent for him. Knox describes her ‘in a vehement fume’: what had he to do with her marriage, and what was he in this commonwealth? He answered: ‘A subject born within the same, Madam. And albeit I neither be Earl, Lord, nor Baron within it, yet has God made me (how abject that ever I be in your eyes) a profitable member within the same.’ She wept. Knox says he stood silent until she had composed herself, then told her that he had never delighted in the weeping of any of God’s creatures — ‘yea, I can scarcely well abide the tears of my own boys whom my own hand corrects’ — but that he must obey God rather than flatter her. The History is Knox’s own account, written to show himself right; it also shows a minister of the new Kirk telling a queen that a preacher’s standing came from God and not from her, and recording her tears without pretending they moved him.

The Stool of Repentance at St Andrews

The Register of the Kirk Session of St Andrews begins in October 1559, before the Reformation Parliament, and is the oldest continuous record of Reformed discipline in Scotland. Its early pages show the elders — burgesses and lairds elected by the congregation — sitting weekly with the minister to hear cases. The commonest are sexual: a man and woman who had ‘committed fornication together’ were summoned, examined, and ordered to appear on the Sunday on the ‘penitent stool’, a raised seat before the pulpit, in sackcloth for the worst cases, to be admonished by the minister and to confess before the whole congregation before being received back; the register records the names, the confessions, and the reconciliations. Others are quarrels — neighbors who had called each other whore and thief were made to shake hands before the session; slanderers were set on the stool with a paper on their heads naming the sin. Sabbath-breakers, drunkards, absentees from the sermon, and a woman who kept a ‘superstitious’ pilgrimage to a well were dealt with in the same way. The register also records the session refusing to admit to communion those unreconciled with their neighbors, and arranging poor relief from the fines. Historians have read them as an engine of repression and, more recently, as a mechanism of neighborhood reconciliation that people used as much as suffered; the St Andrews book supports both readings, case by case, in the elders’ own hand.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Scotland's kirk sessions kept records even denser than Geneva's, mostly over small quarrels between neighbors who then had to keep living side by side. Its ambition was bigger than any single city's: a whole nation bound together by public covenant, most visibly in the National Covenant of 1638.

Legacy

It became the Church of Scotland, and through emigration and mission the worldwide Presbyterian family. Its ambition of a school in every parish left Scotland unusually literate for its time, and its habit of government by elected elders sitting in courts of the church — session, presbytery, assembly — was carried onto every continent. Metrical psalm-singing in the Scottish manner is still heard in Gaelic congregations today.

How Sure the Record Is

RICH - kirk-session discipline records even denser than Geneva's

  • John Knox, History of the Reformation in Scotland (ed. Dickinson, 1949) and Works (ed. Laing) (primary) — A partisan, first-person history written by one of the movement's own protagonists. The Kirk went on to outgrow Knox himself.
  • Scots Confession (1560), First Book of Discipline (1560), Second Book of Discipline (1578), Book of Common Order (primary) — A national church's own self-design.
  • Kirk-session registers: Register of the St Andrews Kirk Session 1559-1600 (ed. Hay Fleming, SHS, 1889-90), with Perth, Aberdeen, and rural session books (primary) — Shows day-to-day church discipline at a national scale, filtered through the institutional concerns of the session itself. The claim that these records are 'denser than Geneva's' rests on Margo Todd's authority and hasn't been independently checked.
  • The National Covenant (1638) with subscription records; Archibald Johnston of Wariston's Diary; the Scottish Psalter (primary) — Covenanted piety seen from inside one intense, first-person conscience, with psalm-singing as an ordinary devotional voice.
  • Margo Todd, The Culture of Protestantism in Early Modern Scotland (Yale, 2002), with Jane Dawson, John Knox (Yale, 2015) (secondary) — The current standard work on lived Kirk discipline, built on the session registers, along with the current standard biography of Knox.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 6 (reference) — This volume has no chapter dedicated to Scotland; the closest is chapter 8, by Benedict.
