Catholic Lay Renewal Traditions (Catholic Worker; Focolare; Charismatic Renewal; L'Arche)

Global, 1930s to 2010 — the Catholic Worker, Focolare, Charismatic Renewal and L'Arche, four distinct lay movements assessed on their own terms, L'Arche's telling honestly including its founder's own record of abuse.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:31

Read along

Four Catholic movements, all begun by lay people, all still active in 2010. Dorothy Day and Peter Maurin founded the Catholic Worker in 1933: a newspaper, houses of hospitality, and a refusal of war that cost them readers. Chiara Lubich began Focolare in 1943. The Catholic Charismatic Renewal began on a retreat weekend at Duquesne University in February 1967 and reached parishes across the world within a decade; Cardinal Suenens's Malines Documents, from 1974, worked out how it and the wider church would live together. In Brazil the renewal began at Campinas in 1969, brought by the Jesuits Haroldo Rahm and Edward Dougherty. Andrew Chesnut's study of 2003 follows it in Brazil, Mexico and Guatemala. Jean Vanier founded L'Arche in 1964, communities in which people with and without intellectual disabilities share a household. The map treats these as four distinct movements rather than one tradition, because that is what they are — and it does not tell L'Arche's story without the abuse committed by its founder, documented later in the inquiry L'Arche International commissioned and published in 2020 and in the independent study commission's report of 2023.

Documented Stories

Dorothy Day Sits in the Park

Dorothy Day had co-founded the Catholic Worker in 1933, and its paper, at a penny a copy, had argued against every war since, at the cost of most of its readers in 1941. On 15 June 1955 the federal and state governments held Operation Alert, a civil-defense exercise in which New Yorkers were required by law to take shelter when the sirens sounded, to rehearse surviving a hydrogen bomb. Day, Ammon Hennacy and some twenty-five others sat down on the benches of City Hall Park with a leaflet that said the drill was a preparation for war and a deception, since there was no shelter from the bomb, and that they would not pretend otherwise. They were arrested. In 1955 the judge called them murderers of the children who would die because of their example and suspended sentence; in 1956 five days; in 1957 thirty days, which Day served in the Women's House of Detention in Greenwich Village, and wrote about in the paper, describing the strip searches and the prostitutes who shared her cell, whom she called her sisters. They sat down again in 1958, 1959 and 1960, joined each year by more, until in April 1961 some two thousand people refused the drill in the park and the city gave up holding it. Day was sixty-three. The hierarchy said nothing for her and nothing against her, and the campaign is counted as the beginning of the American Catholic peace movement.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Four movements, each begun by lay Catholics and all still active in 2010: Dorothy Day and Peter Maurin's Catholic Worker in 1933, with its newspaper and houses of hospitality; Chiara Lubich's Focolare in 1943; the Charismatic Renewal that began on a retreat weekend at Duquesne University in 1967; and Jean Vanier's L'Arche in 1964, where people with and without intellectual disabilities share a household. L'Arche's story includes the abuse committed by Vanier himself across decades of his leadership. Later inquiries, which the movement commissioned and published in 2020 and 2023, documented it.

Legacy

All four were still going in 2010. Catholic Worker houses of hospitality still operated and the paper still appeared. Focolare had become an international movement with statutes approved by Rome. The Charismatic Renewal reshaped ordinary parish life across the Catholic world within a generation.

Sources

On the Nicene Creed

This label covers several founder-led renewal movements, and each is checked on its own terms rather than as one group: all four had institutionalized beyond their individual founders, in documented form. L'Arche's founder-abuse record is part of any honest telling of its story, and carries its own dates in later documents. These are the movement's own commissioned inquiry of 2020 and an independent report of 2023, both of which it published itself. What's genuinely in question is whether authority can pass beyond a single founder, not the founder's own conduct — the two are kept distinct, not blurred together.
