Hincmar of Reims

About This Person

In 882 the Northmen were burning the country around Laon and making for Reims. Its archbishop, Hincmar, fled by night with the body of Saint Remigius and the treasures of his church. He was so weak he had to be carried in a chair. His canons, monks and nuns scattered, and he barely reached Épernay, beyond the Marne. No wall or human hand defended the city, he wrote, but God's power and the merits of the saints kept the raiders out.

His annals end that year, the year he died, Theodor Mommsen says. Hincmar was archbishop of Reims, in Frankish Gaul. A table of dates in Mansi's council volumes puts his ordination in 845. From 861 he carried on the annals that Prudentius of Troyes had kept. G. H. Pertz, an earlier editor, held that Hincmar wrote them, and warned that they need care where his own affairs are concerned.

He fought for years with his nephew and namesake, Hincmar, bishop of Laon. The annals call the nephew a bishop in name only, a man of singular insolence, who rebelled against the king and scorned his archbishop. At Douzy in 871 a synod deposed him.

Its bishops, with the uncle named first, wrote to Pope Hadrian II. The uncle had raised the nephew as an orphan, they said, taught him his letters and made him a bishop. If anyone tricked Rome into restoring him, they warned, he would do as he liked, knowing they could not send every case to Rome. The pope had blamed the uncle for leaving his nephew without help. In his own letter, the uncle wished he had never seen the man, still less ordained him. Hadrian rejected the sentence, a note in Mansi says, but Pope John VIII later wrote that it was just.

In his own complaint to Pope John, the nephew says he was seized on the road and exiled. He was kept for a time in chains, he says, and after nearly two years he was blinded. At Troyes in 878, the uncle's annals say, the nephew's supporters dressed the blind man in his vestments. Without the pope's order, they led him before the pope and into the church, singing.

Pertz calls the archbishop deeply versed in church law and in the affairs of his own time. Bruno Krusch, who edited his Life of Saint Remigius, judged him vain and deceitful, and his long sentences painful to read.

Key Works

  • Annals of Saint-Bertin, from 861 to 882: the annals he carried on after Prudentius of Troyes. Georg Waitz, who edited them, says some have held them to be official annals and others have doubted it. G. H. Pertz, an earlier editor, says hardly anyone but Hincmar could have told some of what they hold, such as his flight to Épernay
  • Chapters for the priests of his diocese: instructions he gave the priests of the diocese of Reims, printed in J. D. Mansi's council collection
  • Life of Saint Remigius: his Life of Remigius, an early bishop of Reims, edited by Bruno Krusch

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The council of Douzy deposed his nephew Hincmar of Laon in 871, at the request of King Charles the Bald. Pope Hadrian II rejected the deposition, a note in Mansi says, and Pope John VIII later called the judgment just.
  • Mansi's volumes print the writings both Hincmars put before King Charles the Bald in August 868. Ludovicus Cellotius, a Jesuit who wrote to the uncle's credit, says the uncle then fought for his nephew against outside force.
  • The annals for 861 give Prudentius's Spanish birth and his turn in the predestination quarrel. G. H. Pertz, an earlier editor, holds that Hincmar wrote the annals from that year.

How they're remembered

  • G. H. Pertz calls him deeply versed in church law and in the affairs of his own time. He warns that Hincmar needs more care where his own affairs are concerned.
  • Bruno Krusch calls him vain and deceitful. He says Hincmar spiced the tales he made up, and that his long sentences choke the breath.
  • Nicolás Antonio, who defends Prudentius, calls the annal on him a slander. He gives it to an unnamed annalist on Hincmar's side.

What Is Uncertain

  • Much of his story comes from his own annals. G. H. Pertz warns that they need care where his own affairs are concerned. His nephew's own complaint to Pope John survives in Mansi's collection.
  • What happened to his nephew after Douzy. In his own complaint to Pope John, the nephew says he was exiled, chained, and blinded after nearly two years. He does not say who blinded him. Cellotius, the Jesuit who took the uncle's side, says the nephew brought blindness and prison on himself by plotting against the king.
  • His part in the 849 entry on Gottschalk, in Prudentius's part of the annals. G. H. Pertz holds it plain that Hincmar reworked it. A note from Bouquet's edition says the abbé Lebeuf suspected so.
  • His birth. No held source gives it. Theodor Mommsen gives 882, the year the annals end, as the year he died.
