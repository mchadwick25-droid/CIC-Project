Serbian & Bulgarian Medieval Monasticism

The Balkans, c. 1170 to 1396 — Serbian and Bulgarian monasteries that took their rule from Mount Athos, wrote their own kings into the calendar of saints, and carried hesychast prayer into the Slavic world.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:16

Read along

In 1196 the Serbian ruler Stefan Nemanja gave up his throne and became a monk. His youngest son, Rastko, was already a monk on Mount Athos under the name Sava, and Nemanja joined him there. Together they founded Hilandar, a Serbian house on the Holy Mountain. From it the shape of Serbian church life was set. Athonite charters were adapted for Serbian monasteries, and a ruling family produced its own saints and then wrote their lives. In 1219 Sava won a self-governing Serbian church and became its first archbishop. In Bulgaria the restored patriarchate at Tarnovo did much the same. It gathered a school of writers around it, and the cult of hermits like John of Rila. Both churches may have been the road by which hesychast prayer passed from Greek into Slavonic, and from there to Russia. No source quoted here traces that road. At the end of the fourteenth century the Ottoman advance overran Bulgaria, and Serbia followed within two generations.

Documented Stories

Sava Lays His Father on a Mat

Stefan Nemanja had ruled Serbia for thirty years, abdicated in 1196, taken the monastic name Symeon, and gone to join his youngest son on Mount Athos, where Rastko — now the monk Sava — had lived for six years against his father’s original wishes. Sava’s Life of his father, the first Serbian biography, describes the death. Symeon fell ill in February 1199 and asked to be taken from his bed and laid on a rush mat on the floor of his cell, with a stone for a pillow, in the manner of the desert fathers; Sava writes that he protested and obeyed. Through the night the old man had the monks sing the office around him; Sava says he asked for the icon of the Virgin to be held before him, received communion, and toward dawn, as the brothers sang ‘Praise the Lord from the heavens’, said the last words Sava records — ‘Praise God in his saints’ — and died. Sava describes washing the body himself. He later carried the relics home to Studenica, where his father’s cult became the foundation of the Serbian church’s dynastic sainthood, and secured its independence from Constantinople in 1219 as its first archbishop. The Life is a son’s work and shaped by the genre; it is also the account of a Balkan warlord who had blinded rivals and burned heretics choosing to die like a hermit, written by the monk who had refused to be his heir.

Tsar Boril Convenes a Synod Against the Bogomils

The Synodicon of Tsar Boril is one of the few documents the medieval Bulgarian church produced about itself, and it is a list of anathemas. Its preface describes the occasion. Boril, tsar in Tarnovo since 1207, ‘burning with divine zeal’, learned that ‘the thrice-cursed and God-hated Bogomil heresy’ was spreading in his land, and summoned the bishops, the clergy and the boyars to a synod in the capital on 11 February 1211. The heretics were brought in. The tsar, the text says, questioned them himself, refuted them with the Scriptures, and when they persisted handed them over for punishment — some to prison, some to exile, and, the Synodicon implies without stating, some to worse. The council then adopted, in Slavonic translation, the Byzantine Synodicon of Orthodoxy, expanded with Bulgarian material: anathemas on the priest Bogomil and his disciples, on those who say the devil made the visible world, on those who reject the Eucharist, the cross and the icons, on those who call the Mother of God a mere woman — and blessings on the tsar and the patriarchs. It was to be read aloud in the churches on the first Sunday of Lent, and it was, for two centuries, updated with the names of each new ruler. The Bulgarian church’s self-portrait in this document is of a state church curing dissent by decree, and it survives because the same church kept revising it. What the Bogomils said at Tarnovo is not recorded.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 1196 the Serbian ruler Stefan Nemanja gave up his throne to become a monk. He joined his son Rastko, already a monk on Mount Athos as Sava, and together they founded the Serbian house of Hilandar. In 1219 Sava won a self-governing Serbian church and became its first archbishop. In Bulgaria a restored patriarchate at Tarnovo gathered its own school of writers around the cult of hermits like John of Rila. Both churches may have carried hesychast prayer from Greek into the Slavic world and on to Russia, though no source quoted here shows it.

Legacy

Both churches survive, as the Serbian and Bulgarian Orthodox Churches. So do their buildings, which are among the most visited in the region. Nemanja is buried at Studenica, and Rila is still Bulgaria's largest monastery. Hilandar is still one of the twenty ruling monasteries of Mount Athos. The legacy in books outran the politics. The Tarnovo school's revised Slavonic texts, with the hesychast prayer inside them, may have traveled north to shape the church in Russia. No source quoted here shows the way it went. The zhitija, the lives of the royal saints, are still founding texts of Serbian writing.

Experience It Today

  • Kraljevo district, Serbia — Studenica Monastery, founded by Stefan Nemanja in the late twelfth century, with its white marble churches and Byzantine frescoes
  • Rila Mountains, Bulgaria — Rila Monastery, the country's largest, built around the memory of the hermit John of Rila

How Sure the Record Is

Moderate-to-rich: typika, royal saints' lives (zhitija - a native genre), chrysobulls

  • St Sava of Serbia: Life of St Symeon (Stefan Nemanja) + the Karyes and Hilandar/Studenica typika (primary) — Founder-family sanctity and legislation in one hand: the zhitija genre's fountainhead and the typika corpus (adapted from Evergetis).
  • Domentijan and Teodosije, Lives of St Sava (13th c.) (primary) — The native royal-saint genre, built around the dynasty's own monk-king.
  • Kiril Petkov, The Voices of Medieval Bulgaria, Seventh-Fifteenth Century (Brill, 2008) (primary) — The Bulgarian side's primary corpus in English: inscriptions, charters, seals, graffiti, church records, including the Synodicon of Boril (1211), a hostile anti-Bogomil source, and John of Rila materials.
  • Royal chrysobulls and charters: Nemanja's Hilandar founding charter; Ivan Asen II's Tarnovo column inscription; monastic land charters (primary)
  • Dimitri Obolensky, Six Byzantine Portraits (Oxford, 1988) (secondary) — Includes a standard scholarly portrait of Sava; frames both churches inside his Byzantine Commonwealth model (a frame, not a verdict).
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 5, ch. 1 'The Byzantine commonwealth 1000-1500' (Shepard) (reference) — The Balkans are this chapter's core ground.
