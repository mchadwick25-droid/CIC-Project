The High Medieval Era · c. 1170-1396 · Balkans (Hilandar, Studenica, Tarnovo, Rila)

Serbian & Bulgarian Medieval Monasticism

About This Movement

In 1196 the Serbian ruler Stefan Nemanja gave up his throne and became a monk. His youngest son, Rastko, was already a monk on Mount Athos under the name Sava, and Nemanja joined him there. Together they founded Hilandar, a Serbian house on the Holy Mountain. From it the shape of Serbian church life was set. Athonite charters were adapted for Serbian monasteries, and a ruling family produced its own saints and then wrote their lives. In 1219 Sava won a self-governing Serbian church and became its first archbishop. In Bulgaria the restored patriarchate at Tarnovo did much the same. It gathered a school of writers around it, and the cult of hermits like John of Rila. Both churches may have been the road by which hesychast prayer passed from Greek into Slavonic, and from there to Russia. No source quoted here traces that road. At the end of the fourteenth century the Ottoman advance overran Bulgaria, and Serbia followed within two generations.

In Its Own Record

Grew Out Of

  • Athonite & Comnenian Byzantine Monasticism Dominant Modern Reconstruction Major — The Serbian ruler Stefan Nemanja became a monk. With his son Sava he founded Hilandar, a Serbian house on Mount Athos. Paisii, a monk of Hilandar writing in 1762, says Nemanja came to the Holy Mountain and built the monastery. This rests on a modern study of Sava, not on the founding charter or Sava's own writings.

Sources

  • St Sava of Serbia: Life of St Symeon (Stefan Nemanja) + the Karyes and Hilandar/Studenica typika (primary) — Founder-family sanctity and legislation in one hand: the zhitija genre's fountainhead and the typika corpus (adapted from Evergetis).
  • Domentijan and Teodosije, Lives of St Sava (13th c.) (primary) — The native royal-saint genre, built around the dynasty's own monk-king.
  • Kiril Petkov, The Voices of Medieval Bulgaria, Seventh-Fifteenth Century (Brill, 2008) (primary) — The Bulgarian side's primary corpus in English: inscriptions, charters, seals, graffiti, church records, including the Synodicon of Boril (1211), a hostile anti-Bogomil source, and John of Rila materials.
  • Royal chrysobulls and charters: Nemanja's Hilandar founding charter; Ivan Asen II's Tarnovo column inscription; monastic land charters (primary)
  • Dimitri Obolensky, Six Byzantine Portraits (Oxford, 1988) (secondary) — Includes a standard scholarly portrait of Sava; frames both churches inside his Byzantine Commonwealth model (a frame, not a verdict).
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 5, ch. 1 'The Byzantine commonwealth 1000-1500' (Shepard) (reference) — The Balkans are this chapter's core ground.
