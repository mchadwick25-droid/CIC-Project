The Victorian Church of England

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 2:31

Read along

The Church of England entered the nineteenth century as a comfortable establishment and left it a contested one. Three parties argued for its soul. Evangelicals who had inherited the awakening's fervor and its campaigns against slavery and squalor, high churchmen who rediscovered the church's catholic inheritance, and broad churchmen who wanted room for the new biblical criticism and the new geology. The quarrels went to law: the Gorham case of 1850 over what baptism does, the storm over the essayists of 1860 who applied critical methods to Scripture, ritual prosecutions that put clergymen in prison over vestments and candles. Underneath the controversies the church rebuilt itself, in thousands of new buildings and slum parishes at home and in a mission network abroad that by 1867 had produced enough overseas bishops that a first Lambeth Conference had to be called to hold them together.

Samuel Marsden shows the church reaching overseas. A pamphlet of his, printed in 1826 by his biographer's account, calls him 'principal Chaplain to the Colony of New South Wales'. His biographer dates one letter to 7 October 1814, just before Marsden sailed for New Zealand. In it Marsden wrote that he had 'submitted my views to the Church Missionary Society, and solicited their aid.'

On Christmas Day, in 1814 by the biographer's date, Marsden preached in New Zealand. He wrote, 'In this manner, the Gospel has been introduced into New Zealand'. On 5 June 1815 he wrote from Parramatta that the societies in London could not make up their minds to send out a vessel. So he 'at last determined to purchase one for the purpose on my own account'.

In April 1837 he asked a missionary, 'Have they any meeting in the week-days for prayer and religious instruction?' The quoted words are Marsden's own, printed in a biography of 1913. Three of the dates are the biographer's. The book tells the mission from one side only. The Maori side is not in it.

Documented Stories

William Ngidi’s Question

John William Colenso had been bishop of Natal since 1853, a mathematician and translator who had already scandalized the church at home by allowing polygamous converts to keep their wives. In the preface to The Pentateuch and Book of Joshua Critically Examined (1862) he told how it began: William Ngidi, a convert with whom he was translating the Flood narrative, asked him whether he really believed that every animal had come into the ark and that the waters had covered the earth. Colenso wrote that he could not lie to a man who trusted him, and that on examination he did not believe it; the volumes that followed argued, with a mathematician's arithmetic, that the Pentateuch's numbers and Mosaic authorship were impossible as history. Robert Gray, bishop of Cape Town, tried him for heresy in 1863 and, when Colenso refused to appear, deposed him. The Privy Council ruled in 1865 that Gray had no power to try him; he kept his cathedral and his salary, was excommunicated by Gray in 1866 and replaced by a rival bishop, so that Natal had two Anglican churches for a generation. English critics said he had been corrupted by a savage's question. Colenso spent his last years defending the Hlubi chief Langalibalele against a colonial show trial and the Zulu king Cetshwayo after the war of 1879, at the cost of his remaining friends, and the Zulu called him Sobantu, father of the people. Ngidi's own view of the affair is not recorded.

Josephine Butler Against the Acts

The Contagious Diseases Acts of 1864, 1866 and 1869 allowed the police in garrison towns to detain any woman they suspected of prostitution, compel her to examination, and confine her if diseased; the men were not examined. Josephine Butler, the wife of a Church of England clergyman in Liverpool, an evangelical who had brought dying prostitutes home to nurse, agreed in 1869 to lead the Ladies' National Association for Repeal, and published on New Year's Day 1870 a protest signed by 124 women that the Acts punished the women and licensed the men. She then did what no respectable woman was supposed to do: she spoke in public about it before audiences of men. At Colchester in 1870 a hired mob hunted her through the town and she hid in a hotel while they broke the windows; at Pontefract they set fire to the straw under the hayloft where she was speaking. The bishops, with few exceptions, kept silent or supported the Acts; the Nonconformists and the working men supported her. She wrote that the campaign was for her a matter of Christ's treatment of the woman taken in adultery and of the equal law of God for both sexes, and that God and one woman made a majority. The Acts were repealed in 1886. She went on to the age-of-consent campaign of 1885, and died in 1906. The Church of England, which had not helped her, put her in its calendar in the twentieth century.

In Its Own Record

The Oxford Movement was a renewal movement within this church. The era was defined by party divisions between evangelicals, high churchmen and broad churchmen, a wider crisis of faith, and the first Lambeth Conference in 1867, when the church's overseas missions became a worldwide Communion.

Descended from

  • continues The Restoration and Georgian Church of England Documented Major — The 1662 Prayer Book has a sermon or a homily follow the Creed at communion. In 1859 an Oxford editor said this rule stood 'nearly the same as we have it at the present day.' The same book and church carried on into the 1800s.

Direct descendants

  • gave rise to The Oxford Movement & Anglo-Catholic Revival Widely Accepted Major — The Oxford Movement began in 1833 among Church of England clergy who taught at Oxford. A Scottish book list of 1846 calls them "the New Anglican School, commonly called Puseyites". Their critics in the church included the Dean of Bristol.
  • gave rise to East African Christianity & the Uganda Martyrs Dominant Modern Reconstruction Major — In 1877 the Church Missionary Society, an Anglican body from England, sent men to the kingdom of Buganda. Their first converts, mostly young men at the king's court, became the first Anglican church there. Modern histories tell this, Adrian Hastings's among them.
  • continued as The Anglican Communion in the Twentieth Century Widely Accepted Major — The bishops of the Anglican Communion first met at Lambeth in 1867, called together by the Church of England. When they met again in 1920, they spoke of their Communion as the same one. By 1867, they wrote, it was already a federation of self-governing churches.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Evangelical, high church and broad church parties fought so hard over what the Church of England should believe that their quarrels went to court: a case over infant baptism in 1850, and prosecutions that put clergymen in prison over candles and vestments. By 1867 the church's overseas missions had produced so many bishops that the first Lambeth Conference had to be called just to hold them together.

Legacy

The Church of England is still established in England, and the conference it improvised in 1867 became the standing instrument of an Anglican Communion of tens of millions across the world. The parties formed in this century are still recognizable as evangelical, Anglo-Catholic and liberal, and so are their arguments — only now conducted across the whole Communion rather than inside one country's church. The century also left the physical church most English people picture: Victorian gothic parish buildings, surpliced choirs and hymnbooks, all of them inventions of this period rather than inheritances from before it.
