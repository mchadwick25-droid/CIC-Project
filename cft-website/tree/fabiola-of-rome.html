About This Person

In the Lateran basilica at Rome, a widow of a noble family stood among the penitents. Before the bishop, the priests and the people, she showed her "dishevelled hair, pale features, soiled hands and unwashed neck." Jerome of Strido says that all of them wept when they saw her weep.

She was Fabiola of Rome. Her story comes from Jerome's letters, above all the letter of praise he wrote after her death to Oceanus. W. H. Fremantle's English edition of Jerome dates her death to 399. Jerome says she had left her first husband for faults so terrible that he will not name them. She then remarried while that husband still lived. Jerome defends the separation but calls the second marriage a fault, perhaps forced on her by need. After her second husband died, she did public penance.

Then she sold her large property for the poor. "She was the first person to found a hospital," Jerome writes in his praise, "into which she might gather sufferers out of the streets." He says she carried the sick on her own shoulders, washed their wounds and fed them by hand. She gave to monasteries on the islands and along the coast. With Jerome's friend Pammachius, she bought a house at Portus as a home for strangers.

Against her friends' advice, she sailed to Jerusalem and was for a time Jerome's guest. She studied Scripture with him and pressed him with questions. When news came that the Huns were near, she went home to Rome.

Jerome says that crowds filled the streets for her funeral, singing psalms. She had once asked him what the halting-places of Israel in the desert meant, and he had promised her a book. He wrote it after her death as a funeral offering. In its pages, he wrote, "she may rejoice that she has passed through the wilderness of this world and has come at last to the land of promise."

Legacy

What traces to them

  • A hospital for the sick poor, which Jerome says she founded "into which she might gather sufferers out of the streets."
  • A home for strangers at Portus, which she and Pammachius set up together. Jerome writes that "Britain has learned in the summer what Egypt and Parthia knew in the spring."
  • Two of Jerome's works were written for her: a letter on the vestments of the high priest, and a study of the halting-places of Israel in the desert, finished after her death.

How they're remembered

  • Jerome calls her "the praise of the Christians, the marvel of the gentiles, the sorrow of the poor, and the consolation of the monks."
  • Jerome says that all Rome came to her funeral, and the streets, porches and roofs could not hold the crowd.

What Is Uncertain

  • Her story comes from Jerome, her friend and host, above all from his letter of praise to Oceanus after her death. No writing of hers is held.
  • Jerome's words "the first person to found a hospital" are praise. Gregory of Nazianzus, in his funeral oration for Basil of Caesarea, describes a "new city" outside Caesarea, which his translators, Charles Gordon Browne and James Edward Swallow, gloss as a hospital for the sick. Jerome gives no year or place for Fabiola's hospital.
  • Jerome's letter tells of the hospital and of the house at Portus as two works. The heading in Fremantle's edition speaks of "the hospital established by her at Portus".
  • Jerome gives no years. The dates 395 and 399 are those of W. H. Fremantle's edition.
  • Jerome's Letter 55 answers a question about a woman who left a vicious husband and married again while he lived. The edition says she was "supposed by Thierry to have been Fabiola", and its heading to Letter 77 points to Letter 55 for her story.
  • Another Fabiola appears in Jerome's Letter 126. The edition says she "must be carefully distinguished from the lady so often mentioned by Jerome."
