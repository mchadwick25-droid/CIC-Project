The Armenian Church after the Genocide (Medz Yeghern)

Anatolia to Armenia and the worldwide diaspora, 1915 to 2010 — a church carrying the Medz Yeghern forward through dispersal, the Soviet decades and the Cold War split between its two sees.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 0:56

Read along

In 1915 the Armenian population of Anatolia was destroyed, and the church went with the people — clergy killed, dioceses emptied, whole regions left without a parish. What survived did so by moving. Grigoris Balakian, a priest who lived through it, wrote his account in 1922. The Holy See of Cilicia relocated to Antelias in Lebanon in 1930. Etchmiadzin, the mother see, continued inside the Soviet Union under severe constraint; Catholicos Khoren I was killed in 1938. In the Cold War the two sees divided along the politics of the diaspora, a division dated to 1956 whose records both sides kept. After 1991 the church in Armenia was re-established in a country of its own again.

Documented Stories

Christmas Eve at Holy Cross

Leon Tourian had been consecrated archbishop for the Armenian diocese of North America in 1931, sent from Etchmiadzin, the mother see, which since 1920 had lain inside the Soviet Union. The diaspora was divided over what that meant: the Dashnak party, which had governed the short-lived Armenian republic of 1918 to 1920, held that a catholicos under Soviet control could not speak freely, and flew the republic's tricolor as the true flag. On 1 July 1933, at an Armenian Day at the Chicago World's Fair, Tourian found that flag draped on the platform and refused to speak until it was removed; the removal was taken as an insult to the nation's dead, and the months that followed saw disturbances at his services. On Sunday 24 December 1933, at the Holy Cross Armenian Church on West 187th Street in Manhattan, Tourian entered in procession for the Christmas Eve liturgy. In the aisle a group of men closed around him, and he was stabbed with a butcher's knife; he died on the church floor within minutes, in his vestments. Nine men, all Dashnak members, were tried in 1934; two were convicted of first-degree murder and their sentences later commuted, and seven of manslaughter. The parishes divided: those who held to Etchmiadzin remained the Diocese, and those who would not were formed into a separate Prelacy, which from 1957 placed itself under the Catholicosate of Cilicia in Lebanon.

The Empty Throne at Etchmiadzin

Khoren Muradbekyan had been elected catholicos of all Armenians in 1932, at a time when the Soviet state was closing churches across Armenia and the see of Etchmiadzin, the oldest Christian cathedral in continuous use, was governed under constant surveillance. He tried to keep relations with the diaspora open and to keep the monastery functioning, and he resisted attempts to have the church's remaining property surrendered. On the night of 6 April 1938, in the middle of the Great Terror, he died in his quarters at Etchmiadzin. The official cause given was heart failure. The church held from the first that he was strangled by agents of the NKVD, and the accounts of monks who were present, recorded later, describe security men in the residence that night and marks on the body. He was sixty-five. No successor was permitted. The monastery's brotherhood was reduced to a few old men, the seminary was closed, and the printing press was silent; Archbishop Gevorg Chorekchyan administered what remained. Only after the war, when Stalin found a use for the church in rallying the diaspora, was an election allowed: in June 1945 Gevorg was elected Gevorg VI, with the state's blessing, and the see resumed a constrained life. The church came to count Khoren among its martyrs of the Soviet period, and his death is one of the reasons half the diaspora, for the rest of the century, would not trust what came out of Etchmiadzin.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 1915 the Armenian genocide left the church's homeland base destroyed, its clergy killed and its dioceses emptied, and what survived did so by moving — the Holy See of Cilicia relocated to Antelias, Lebanon, in 1930, while Etchmiadzin endured under Soviet rule. By 2010 its worldwide diaspora outnumbered the church in Armenia itself.

Legacy

In 2010 the church was alive in Armenia and in a diaspora larger than the church at home. It was one of the oldest churches anywhere with an unbroken life. Its Mother See at Etchmiadzin dates its founding to the early fourth century. The Cold War split between Etchmiadzin and Antelias had eased by 2010. But it shaped the life of the diaspora for generations. And the record of this century has to be handled the way its own people handled it: as living memory, not distant history.
