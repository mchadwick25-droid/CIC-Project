The Imperial Church Era · 330-400 · Antioch, Rome

Antioch, today in Turkey · Rome, today in Italy

Ammianus Marcellinus

A person whose story no single tradition tells.

Outside witness

Known for
A soldier and historian, outside the church by the general belief that Yonge reports, whose Roman History tells of the deadly fight for the bishopric of Rome, the emperor Julian's dealings with the Christians, and the killing of Bishop George at Alexandria.
Dates
330 to 400 in John Rolfe's dates; Charles Duke Yonge says only that he lived nearly to the end of the fourth century. His history ends with the death of Valens in 378
Place
A native of Antioch, as Yonge reads the history; he wrote at Rome
Role
Roman soldier and historian

About This Person

In Rome in the 360s, two men fought to become bishop. Damasus and Ursinus, writes Ammianus Marcellinus, formed parties that came to blows. "It is certain that on one day one hundred and thirty-seven dead bodies were found in the Basilica of Sicininus, which is a Christian church."

Ammianus calls himself "a Greek by birth, and formerly a soldier." His English translator, Charles Duke Yonge, draws the rest from the history. He served under the general Ursicinus and fought in the emperor Julian's war on Persia. At Rome he wrote a history ending with the emperor Valens's death in 378. Yonge says even his religion is uncertain. The general belief is that he kept the old Roman faith.

Ammianus also mocks the bishops of Rome, "enriched by offerings from matrons, riding in carriages, dressing splendidly." Yet he praises provincial priests for their plain dress and strict fasting. He says Julian gave the Christian sects freedom, expecting it to deepen their quarrels. For Julian had found that "no wild beasts are so hostile to men as Christian sects in general are to one another." Still, he calls Julian's law on teachers of rhetoric and grammar "a cruel action, and one deserving to be buried in everlasting silence."

Ammianus believes he never "knowingly perverted" the truth. Edward Gibbon, whom Yonge quotes, called him "an accurate and faithful guide." Professor Ramsay, whom Yonge also quotes, says the age's belief in omens had some hold on him. Ammianus reports that when Julian tried to rebuild the Temple at Jerusalem, "fearful balls of fire burst forth" and stopped the work. The scholar E. A. Abbott, quoted in Blomfield Jackson's notes to Theodoret of Cyrrhus, judges him impartial. But Abbott accepts only that people "generally believed" a fiery sign had stopped it.

A mob at Alexandria killed its bishop, George. Ammianus says the Christians might have saved him, "if both parties had not been equally exasperated by hatred of George."

Key Works

  • Roman History (Res Gestae): thirty-one books from the accession of Nerva in 96 to the death of Valens in 378; Yonge says the first thirteen are lost

Legacy

How they're remembered

  • Edward Gibbon, quoted by Yonge, called him "an accurate and faithful guide, who has composed the history of his own times." Gibbon goes on to say he wrote without the prejudices that usually affect a contemporary.
  • John Rolfe, who translated Suetonius, says Tacitus "did not find a follower until the time of Ammianus Marcellinus."
  • Charles Joseph Hefele's History of the Councils cites "the heathen historian Ammianus Marcellinus" for the emperor Constantius II's efforts to win Rome's consent to the deposition of Athanasius of Alexandria.

What Is Uncertain

  • Little is known of him beyond what his own history shows, and even his religion is uncertain. Yonge reports that "the general belief is, that he adhered to the religion of the ancient Romans."
  • Professor Ramsay, in Smith's Dictionary of Greek and Roman Biography, quoted by Yonge, praises his honesty and good sense, but says he "did not entirely escape the contagion" of his age's belief in omens and oracles.
  • On Julian's attempt to rebuild the Temple at Jerusalem, E. A. Abbott, quoted by Blomfield Jackson in his notes to Theodoret of Cyrrhus, judges Ammianus an impartial historian. Abbott still accepts only that the work "was generally believed" to have been stopped by a fiery sign. He thinks it "was probably stopped by a violent thunderstorm or thunderstorms."
  • On church matters he reports what was said. He writes that Athanasius was deposed partly because, "it was said", he had foretold the future from auguries and omens.
  • The passage on Damasus and Ursinus gives no year. Yonge's page heading places it in 367.
  • The Library holds Yonge's 1862 English translation, from an uncorrected scan, and no Latin text.
