The Early Medieval Era · 597-793 · England

England, today in United Kingdom

Anglo-Saxon Christianity

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Christianity reached the English twice over and from two directions — from Rome in 597, when Augustine landed in Kent, and from Ireland by way of Iona, whose monks founded Lindisfarne and evangelized Northumbria. And at the Synod of Whitby in 664 the two traditions had to settle publicly which calendar and which customs the English church would keep. What grew out of that settlement was a church with an extraordinary appetite for learning. Bede, writing at Jarrow, finished in 731 the history that is still the main source for the whole period, and monasteries like Whitby, under the abbess Hild, trained bishops. Within a century of being a mission field, England was sending missionaries of its own back across the Channel — Willibrord to Frisia, Boniface into Germany, and with them women such as the abbess Leoba, whose one surviving letter comes down under her own name. The window closes on 8 June 793, when Viking raiders sacked Lindisfarne and a different age began.

In Its Own Record

Grew Out Of

  • The Roman Church in the Age of Gregory the Great Documented — Bede reports that Pope Gregory sent Augustine and several other monks to preach to the English in 596. They landed on the Isle of Thanet, in King Ethelbert's Kent, nearly forty strong.
  • Insular / Irish Monastic Christianity Documented — Bede reports that King Oswald asked the Irish for a bishop, and they sent him Aidan, a monk of Iona, in 635. Bede adds that the English were taught the rules of monastic life by their Irish masters. The Roman mission of 597 was a second, separate source of English Christianity.

Continued As

  • Late Anglo-Saxon Christianity Documented — Alcuin wrote a poem to the monks of Lindisfarne after the Vikings sacked their island. He told the surviving brothers not to let the attack break their spirit. He called Cuthbert the glory of their church and urged them to keep praying. The community he wrote to was the one Aidan had founded.

Influenced By

  • British and Welsh Christianity Documented — In his history of the English church, Bede cites Gildas as the Britons' own historian for their sins after the Romans left. Bede then adds a charge of his own: that the Britons never preached the faith to the English.

Influenced

  • Carolingian Reform Christianity Documented — English churchmen helped lead the Frankish reform from the start. In 742 Carloman, a ruler of the Franks, set the English missionary Boniface as archbishop over the bishops of his realm. Alcuin of York later wrote that he had come to Francia to help King Charles in the Catholic faith.

Argued Against

  • British and Welsh Christianity Documented — Laurentius, who followed Augustine, wrote to the British clergy and urged them into Catholic unity. Later, Bede reports, Aldhelm wrote a book against the way they fixed the date of Easter. Bede himself complained about the Britons. They would not share the faith with the English, he said, yet still held to their errors.
  • Insular / Irish Monastic Christianity Documented — At Whitby in 664, Bede says, the English priest Wilfrid argued against the Easter kept by Bishop Colman and his Irish clergy. He said Rome, Italy, Gaul, Africa, Asia and Egypt kept the Roman date, and he called the other side obstinate. King Oswy then chose to follow Peter, who held the keys of heaven.

In Contact With

  • British and Welsh Christianity Documented — Bede tells of two meetings around 603 between Augustine of Canterbury and the British bishops. The first was at Augustine's Oak. Pope Gregory had put the British bishops under Augustine, but at the second meeting they would not take him as their archbishop. Bede wrote this more than a century later.

Sources

  • Bede, Ecclesiastical History of the English People (ed./trans. Colgrave & Mynors, Oxford Medieval Texts, 1969; Penguin trans. also in print) (primary) — The single richest narrative source of the era in the West; standard edition, cheap translations everywhere.
  • The Letters of St Boniface (trans. Ephraim Emerton, 1940; repr. Columbia, 2000 with introduction by T. F. X. Noble) (primary) — The Boniface circle's correspondence, including the women's letters (Leoba to Boniface).
  • C. H. Talbot, The Anglo-Saxon Missionaries in Germany (1954): Lives of Willibrord, Boniface, Sturm, Leoba, Lebuin + the Hodoeporicon of Willibald (primary) — English translations of the mission-circle hagiography, including Rudolf of Fulda's Life of Leoba - the fullest portrait of an Anglo-Saxon woman religious; on archive.org.
  • Henry Mayr-Harting, The Coming of Christianity to Anglo-Saxon England (3rd ed. 1991) (secondary) — The classic synthesis of the conversion and the converging Roman/Irish streams.
  • John Blair, The Church in Anglo-Saxon Society (Oxford, 2005) (secondary) — The modern ground-level account (minsters, landscape, ordinary practice) - counterweight to great-men narrative.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity, vol. 3, ch. 5: 'Germanic Christianities, 600-1100' (Lesley Abrams) (reference) — From the vol. 3 TOC; covers the Anglo-Saxon world.
