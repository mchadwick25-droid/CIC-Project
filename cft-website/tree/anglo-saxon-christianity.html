Anglo-Saxon Christianity

England, 597 to 793 — converted within living memory, and already sending its own monks and nuns back across the Channel to convert the continent the faith had come from.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:14

Read along

Christianity reached the English twice over and from two directions — from Rome in 597, when Augustine landed in Kent, and from Ireland by way of Iona, whose monks founded Lindisfarne and evangelized Northumbria. And at the Synod of Whitby in 664 the two traditions had to settle publicly which calendar and which customs the English church would keep. What grew out of that settlement was a church with an extraordinary appetite for learning. Bede, writing at Jarrow, finished in 731 the history that is still the main source for the whole period, and monasteries like Whitby, under the abbess Hild, trained bishops. Within a century of being a mission field, England was sending missionaries of its own back across the Channel — Willibrord to Frisia, Boniface into Germany, and with them women such as the abbess Leoba, whose one surviving letter comes down under her own name. The window closes on 8 June 793, when Viking raiders sacked Lindisfarne and a different age began.

Documented Stories

The Sparrow Through the Hall

Bede tells how the Northumbrian court decided. The pagan high priest Coifi spoke first, and his argument was practical: he had served the gods more diligently than anyone and got less from the king than others, so the gods were evidently no use. Then another counselor spoke, and Bede gives him the speech the English have remembered. The present life of man, he said, is like a sparrow that flies swiftly through the king’s hall while he sits at dinner in winter with his thegns, a fire burning, the storm outside; it comes in at one door, is for a moment out of the wind and the rain, and goes out at the other into the dark from which it came. ‘So this life of man appears for a little while; but of what went before or what is to follow we know nothing. If this new teaching has brought any greater certainty, it seems right that we should follow it.’ Coifi then asked to hear Paulinus again, declared the old shrines worthless, borrowed a horse and spear from the king — forbidden to a priest — and rode to the temple at Goodmanham and profaned it himself. Bede was writing a century later from Northumbrian memory, and the speech is his composition; but it is a rare case of a conversion argued on the ground that the old religion had nothing to say about death, rather than that the new one had power.

Leoba Asks to Be Remembered

Leoba was a nun at Wimborne in Wessex, kin to Boniface through her mother, when she wrote him the one letter of hers that survives. She reminds him that he once knew her father Dynne, now dead, and her mother Aebbe, still living and in poor health; she says she is the only child, and asks that he treat her as a brother would, since she trusts no one else. She asks for his prayers and for a letter in return — ‘I beg you not to disdain to send me a few words, so that I may keep them as a pledge of your affection.’ And she closes with four lines of Latin hexameter she has composed herself, ‘according to the rules of poetic art’, learned, she says, from her teacher Eadburga, and asks him to correct them. Boniface answered, and some years later he asked her abbess to send Leoba to Germany, where she became abbess of Tauberbischofsheim and the head of the women’s houses of his mission; the Life written of her a generation later says he ordered that she be buried in his own tomb at Fulda, so that they might rise together. The monks did not quite obey. But the letter is the thing: a woman in an English convent in the 730s, with a classical education, opening a correspondence with the most important churchman of her age on her own initiative, and asking for her poetry to be read.

The Oak at Geismar

In Hesse, Edward Kylie writes in his introduction to Boniface’s English letters, the missionary met a few priests and leading men who looked to reason for the grounds of belief. In a remarkable letter, Bishop Daniel urged him to show such people by argument how absurd it was to worship many gods. But argument would not break the faith of the people at large. The missionary had to humble the gods in some more striking way. So, Kylie writes, Boniface felled at Geismar the oak sacred to Thor. Deeds counted for more than words, and the crash of the sacred tree was ‘the death-song of heathenism in Hesse.’ From the ruins of the oak he built a chapel in honor of Saint Peter, as Gregory the Great had told Augustine to turn the old temples and feasts to Christian use. Through the year 722 he shared the hardships of his flock in Hesse and won their love and trust. On land that local converts gave him, in Hesse and Thuringia, he set up monasteries ‘like fortresses to hold a conquered country.’ He was killed in Frisia in 754. The Frankish annals for that year say that pagans killed him as he preached there. The English church that had received the Gospel from Rome and Iona sent it back across the sea by destroying a tree.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Born of two missions, one from Rome and one from Ireland, which settled their quarrel over Easter at Whitby in 664. Its churchmen later helped lead the Carolingian reform.

Descended from

  • grew out of The Roman Church in the Age of Gregory the Great Documented Major — Bede reports that Pope Gregory the Great sent Augustine and several other monks to preach to the English in 596. They landed on the Isle of Thanet, in King Ethelbert's Kent, nearly forty strong.
  • grew out of Insular / Irish Monastic Christianity Documented Major — Bede reports that King Oswald asked the Irish for a bishop, and they sent him Aidan, a monk of Iona, in 635. Bede adds that the English were taught the rules of monastic life by their Irish masters. The Roman mission of 597 was a second, separate source of English Christianity.

Direct descendants

  • continued as Late Anglo-Saxon Christianity Documented Major — Alcuin wrote a poem to the monks of Lindisfarne after the Vikings sacked their island. He told the surviving brothers not to let the attack break their spirit. He called Cuthbert the glory of their church and urged them to keep praying. The community he wrote to was the one Aidan had founded.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced British and Welsh Christianity Documented — In his history of the English church, Bede cites Gildas as the Britons' own historian for their sins after the Romans left. Bede then adds a charge of his own: that the Britons never preached the faith to the English.

Influenced

  • influenced Carolingian Reform Christianity Documented Major — English churchmen helped lead the Frankish reform from the start. In 742 Carloman, a ruler of the Franks, set the English missionary Boniface as archbishop over the bishops of his realm. Alcuin of York later wrote that he had come to Francia to help King Charles in the Catholic faith.Lasting result: In 742 Carloman, on the advice of Boniface and his fellow bishops, ordered a church synod each year. Its aim was to restore church law and correct Christian practice.

Argued Against

  • argued against British and Welsh Christianity Documented — Laurentius, who followed Augustine, wrote to the British clergy and urged them into Catholic unity. Later, Bede reports, Aldhelm wrote a book against the way they fixed the date of Easter. Bede himself complained about the Britons. They would not share the faith with the English, he said, yet still held to their errors.
  • argued against Insular / Irish Monastic Christianity Documented Major — At Whitby in 664, Bede says, the English priest Wilfrid argued against the Easter kept by Bishop Colman and his Irish clergy. He said Rome, Italy, Gaul, Africa, Asia and Egypt kept the Roman date, and he called the other side obstinate. King Oswy then chose to follow Peter, who held the keys of heaven.Lasting result: In 664 the king and all at the debate decided to follow the Catholic Easter. Bishop Colman went back to Scotland with those who would not.

In Contact With

  • in contact with British and Welsh Christianity Documented — Bede tells of two meetings around 603 between Augustine of Canterbury and the British bishops. The first was at Augustine's Oak. Pope Gregory the Great had put the British bishops under Augustine, but at the second meeting they would not take him as their archbishop. Bede wrote this more than a century later.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Christianity reached the English twice, from Rome through Augustine in 597 and from Ireland through the monks of Iona who founded Lindisfarne, and the two traditions settled their differences publicly at the Synod of Whitby in 664. Within a century a church that had itself just been converted was sending its own missionaries back across the Channel, among them Boniface into Germany and the abbess Leoba, whose one surviving letter is a rare case of a woman's own words reaching us directly from the period.

Legacy

The same church continued. Its story runs straight on into the late Anglo-Saxon church. That church survived the Viking wars and was rebuilt under Alfred and the reforming bishops. Beyond that succession, it left the books. The Lindisfarne Gospels and the Codex Amiatinus came out of these monasteries. Bede's history shaped how the English told their own story for a thousand years. It also gave the Western world the habit of dating years from the Incarnation. And its missionaries in Germany, Boniface above all, helped the Carolingian kings reform the Frankish church.

Experience It Today

How Sure the Record Is

Rich for the era: Bede; Boniface circle's letters including women's correspondence (Leoba)

  • Bede, Ecclesiastical History of the English People (ed./trans. Colgrave & Mynors, Oxford Medieval Texts, 1969; Penguin trans. also in print) (primary) — The single richest narrative source of the era in the West; standard edition, cheap translations everywhere.
  • The Letters of St Boniface (trans. Ephraim Emerton, 1940; repr. Columbia, 2000 with introduction by T. F. X. Noble) (primary) — The Boniface circle's correspondence, including the women's letters (Leoba to Boniface).
  • C. H. Talbot, The Anglo-Saxon Missionaries in Germany (1954): Lives of Willibrord, Boniface, Sturm, Leoba, Lebuin + the Hodoeporicon of Willibald (primary) — English translations of the mission-circle hagiography, including Rudolf of Fulda's Life of Leoba - the fullest portrait of an Anglo-Saxon woman religious; on archive.org.
  • Henry Mayr-Harting, The Coming of Christianity to Anglo-Saxon England (3rd ed. 1991) (secondary) — The classic synthesis of the conversion and the converging Roman/Irish streams.
  • John Blair, The Church in Anglo-Saxon Society (Oxford, 2005) (secondary) — The modern ground-level account (minsters, landscape, ordinary practice) - counterweight to great-men narrative.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity, vol. 3, ch. 5: 'Germanic Christianities, 600-1100' (Lesley Abrams) (reference) — From the vol. 3 TOC; covers the Anglo-Saxon world.
  • Alcuin, poem on the sack of Lindisfarne (De rerum humanarum vicissitudine et clade Lindisfarnensis monasterii), in Migne, Patrologia Latina 100-101 (1863) (primary) — Alcuin's lament to the bishop and monks of Lindisfarne for the day a pagan band came suddenly by ship. A contemporary witness to the raid that closes this period.
