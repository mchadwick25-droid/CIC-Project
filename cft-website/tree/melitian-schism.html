The Melitian Schism

Egypt, c. 306 to the 700s — a rival church born of a quarrel in a time of persecution, whose monks fought Athanasius of Alexandria and left their own letters behind.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 2:48

Read along

In the last great persecution, Melitius was bishop of Lycopolis in Upper Egypt. He began to ordain clergy in other bishops' lands. Four bishops wrote to him in protest. Peter, bishop of Alexandria, wrote that Melitius had made clergy of his own even in the prison. Peter told his people to hold no communion with him.

Why the quarrel began is disputed. Athanasius and Socrates, a church historian, say Peter deposed Melitius. The charge, they say, was that he had offered sacrifice to idols. Both wrote as his opponents. H. Idris Bell, who edited the Melitians' own letters in 1924, read it as a quarrel over the lapsed. The question, he wrote, was how long to wait before taking them back.

Melitius went on making bishops of his own. In 325 the Council of Nicaea let him keep the title of bishop but forbade him to ordain anyone. The peace did not last. Before he died, Melitius named a friend, John, as his successor, and his church stood apart again.

Under Athanasius, bishop from 328, the fight grew bitter. Socrates says his foes used Melitians to bring charges against him. In 335 a synod at Tyre voted to depose him. A man once a Melitian bishop signed the vote. Around then a Melitian monk named Callistus wrote home from Alexandria. Men on Athanasius's side, he says, came with soldiers. They beat Melitian monks and locked some up.

That letter belongs to a small set of papyri from the 330s, published in 1924. Most are letters to Apa Paieous, a priest and confessor. He led a large community of Melitian monks at Hathor, in the eastern desert. Some letters are in Coptic. They speak of prayers for one another, of care for brothers in trouble, and of bread and wheat.

The Melitians lasted long after Athanasius. In the fifth century Theodoret wrote that bodies of their monks still kept their own ways. Papyri of 512 show a Melitian priest buying a monastery near Arsinoe. The History of the Patriarchs says Patriarch Damian, from 569, drove them out of the monasteries of Wadi Habib. Bell, citing a later part of the History of the Patriarchs, puts the last report of them under Michael I, patriarch from 744 to 768.

Documented Stories

A Night of Soldiers at the Camp

Callistus wrote to 'Apa Paiéou and Patabeit, priests', at the Melitian monks' settlement. He tells them that Isaac, a Melitian bishop, came to Alexandria to dine with Heraiscus in the Camp. Men on Athanasius's side heard of it and came with soldiers. Callistus says they were drunk. Friendly soldiers hid the bishop and his brothers in the store-rooms. The others beat four brothers bloody and threw them out of the town. They seized five more at the hostel where the brothers stayed. They asked its keeper, 'For what reason did you admit the monks of the Meletian party into the hostel?' Callistus ends by asking for a few loaves, since wheat had grown dear.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

A quarrel between two bishops in a time of persecution split the church of Egypt for more than two centuries. The Melitians kept their own bishops, priests and monks. They fought Athanasius of Alexandria before councils and emperors. Their letters, in Greek and Coptic, let them speak for themselves.

Legacy

No church today descends from the Melitians. Their papyri give something rare: the losing side of an early church quarrel, in its own words. Most of what is known of such groups comes only from their enemies. The letters also show an organized community of Egyptian monks in the 330s, outside the main church.

Sources

Doctrinal Floor Note

At the start this was a quarrel over church order, not over belief. Later opponents said the Melitians sided with Arius's party. Those charges come only from outside.

How Sure the Record Is

Rare for a schism: some of its own voice survives. A small set of its monks' letters from the 330s, in Greek and Coptic, was published by H. Idris Bell in 1924. They stand beside the hostile accounts of Athanasius of Alexandria, the Council of Nicaea and the church historians. After the fourth century it is known only through others' records.
