About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:06

Read along

The Maronites take their name from a Syriac monk, Maron, and from the monastery built around his memory. By the Middle Ages they were a church of their own, in the West Syriac family of worship. They lived mostly in the mountains and gorges of Lebanon, where they cut their monasteries and hermit cells straight into the cliffs. Their early history is unusually hard to recover. Much of what is told about it was written down later, by people arguing about it. The question was whether the community had once held that Christ had only one will, a view the wider church had rejected. What is clear is that when the crusaders came, the Maronites joined in communion with Rome. William of Tyre describes the union around 1182. In 1215 the Maronite patriarch Jeremiah al-Amshitti went to Rome for the Fourth Lateran Council. After it, a papal bull set out the terms of the union.

Documented Stories

What Rome Asked of the Maronites

William of Tyre, writing in the 1180s, says that ‘about forty thousand’ Maronites of the Lebanon, ‘who had followed the error of a certain heresiarch Maro for almost five hundred years’ — he means the doctrine of one will in Christ — came of their own accord to the Latin patriarch of Antioch, abjured the error, and were received into the Roman church. In 1215 the Maronite patriarch Jeremiah al-Amshitti traveled to Rome for the Fourth Lateran Council, received the pallium from Innocent III, and returned with the bull Quia divinae sapientiae, addressed to him and his church. The bull is a list. The Maronites are to use the Roman form of the words of baptism; to confess belief in two natures and two wills in Christ; to reserve confirmation to bishops; to use unleavened bread and mix water with the wine as the Latins do; to make the sign of the cross with two fingers; to wear Latin-style vestments and ring bells to call the people to church, and — the clause historians quote most — to give up the practice of some priests of marrying, or keeping their wives, after ordination. The patriarch was also to send a report every four years. The community kept its Syriac liturgy and its own patriarch, and adopted much of the rest over the following centuries. A church that entered communion with Rome earlier than any other eastern body did so on terms it had to be told, and the terms survive.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

The Maronites take their name from the Syriac monk Maron, and by the medieval period were a distinct West Syriac church whose monasteries and hermitages were cut straight into the cliffs of Mount Lebanon. Their early history is unusually hard to recover, because much of what is told about it was written later, under argument over whether the community had once held that Christ had only one will. When the crusaders arrived, the Maronites entered communion with Rome — William of Tyre describes the union around 1182, and in 1215 the patriarch Jeremiah al-Amshitti traveled to Rome for the Fourth Lateran Council to settle its terms.

Legacy

The Maronite Church is very much alive. It is one of the largest Eastern Catholic churches and the largest Christian community in Lebanon. It also has a worldwide diaspora. The union begun here has held for eight centuries. It joined communion with the pope to a Syriac liturgy and a patriarch of the church's own. Later Eastern Catholic churches would share that pattern. The Qadisha valley, where monks carved their monasteries and hermit cells into the cliffs, is still a place of pilgrimage. It is protected as world heritage.

Experience It Today

  • Northern Lebanon — the Qadisha (Kadisha) Valley, whose cliffside monasteries and hermitages are the historic heart of Maronite monastic life, with the cedar forest above it

Doctrinal Floor Note

How Sure the Record Is

Thin-to-moderate early; much early history is later reconstruction under polemical pressure

  • Kitab al-Huda (Book of Guidance; Syriac-to-Arabic trans. by Bishop David, 1059; ed. Fahed, 1935) (primary) — The community's own law-liturgy-theology compendium - the lived internal rulebook. Available only in Arabic and French; no English translation exists.
  • Thomas of Kaphartab, Ten Chapters (11th c.) (primary) — Pre-union Maronite apologetics defending the one-will christology - inside evidence bearing on the contested monothelete question, which concerns the historical record rather than the church's present confession. No English translation exists.
  • William of Tyre on the Maronite union with Rome (1180s), in A History of Deeds Done Beyond the Sea (trans. Babcock & Krey, 1943) (primary) — An outside view: the union described through Latin crusader eyes. William frames it with hostility, calling the Maronites' prior teaching heresy.
  • Kamal Salibi, Maronite Historians of Mediaeval Lebanon (Beirut, 1959) (secondary) — Available on archive.org. A classic dissection of how later Maronite historians rebuilt the early record under polemical pressure.
  • Matti Moosa, The Maronites in History (Syracuse, 1986; repr. Gorgias 2005) (secondary) — Critical counter-history; itself contested by Maronite traditionalists - the historiography is a live confessional argument on both sides.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 5, Part III 'East Christianities (eleventh to fourteenth century): Copts, Melkites, Nestorians and Jacobites' (reference) — Maronites are not named in the chapter title, though they fall within its scope of medieval Eastern Christianity.
