The Black Church in the Twentieth Century

The United States, 1906 to 2010 — the Great Migration remaking the Black church urban and northern, COGIC's rise from 1907, the National Baptist split of 1915, the civil-rights church and Black theology: the future the map's nineteenth-century entry named and no row had carried.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:04

Read along

The Great Migration moved Black Americans out of the rural South in enormous numbers, and the church moved with them: congregations founded in the North and in the cities, and a whole religious culture remade in a generation. The Church of God in Christ, which took its Pentecostal turn in 1907 out of Memphis, rose fast and became one of the century's major bodies. The National Baptist Convention split in 1915. From the 1950s the congregations were the civil-rights movement's infrastructure: Martin Luther King Jr and the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, founded in 1957, worked out of them, and much of what survives of that movement's own voice is sermons. At the end of the 1960s James Cone published the founding works of Black theology, in 1969 and 1970, and opened a field in the seminaries that had not existed before them.

Documented Stories

Kansas City, 1961

The National Baptist Convention, U.S.A., Inc., with some five million members, was the largest Black institution in the country, and its president since 1953, Joseph H. Jackson of Chicago, had built a well-run body that did not do civil disobedience; he called the young ministers of the sit-ins irresponsible, and their most prominent member was the convention's vice-president Martin Luther King Jr. In 1960 Gardner Taylor had run against Jackson as the movement's candidate and claimed to have won a vote that Jackson's officers refused to count. At the annual session in Kansas City in September 1961 the two sides came prepared. On the night of 6 September, when Jackson's officers ruled the Taylor delegates' credentials out of order, the Taylor forces moved to take the platform, Jackson's men held it, and in the struggle the Reverend Arthur G. Wright of Detroit, a Jackson supporter, fell from the stage and struck his head; he died the next day. Jackson, re-elected, blamed King personally for the death, stripped him of his office, and removed his name from the convention's programs. In November 1961 the Taylor and King ministers met at Zion Baptist Church in Cincinnati, at the invitation of its pastor L. Venchael Booth, and organized the Progressive National Baptist Convention, which became the movement's denominational home and King's convention for the rest of his life. The two conventions stood apart from then on; the older one did not send an official delegation to the March on Washington.

The Love That Forgives

Sixteenth Street Baptist Church in Birmingham, Alabama had been the marshaling point of the spring campaign of 1963, when the city's children had marched out of its doors into Bull Connor's dogs and fire hoses. Sunday 15 September 1963 was Youth Day; the children were to lead the eleven o'clock service, and the Sunday school lesson that morning was 'The Love That Forgives', on Matthew 5. At 10:22, as five girls were in the basement lounge putting on their white choir robes, a bomb of some fifteen sticks of dynamite under the side steps went off. Addie Mae Collins, Cynthia Wesley and Carole Robertson, all fourteen, and Denise McNair, eleven, were killed; Sarah Collins, Addie Mae's sister, lost an eye. Twenty others were injured, and the face of Christ was blown out of a stained-glass window, the rest of the figure left standing. That afternoon two more Black boys were shot dead in the city. On 18 September Martin Luther King preached at the funeral of three of the girls, telling the congregation that the girls had something to say to every minister who had remained silent behind stained-glass windows, and that they must not lose faith in their white brothers. Robert Chambliss, a Klansman, was convicted of the murder in 1977, Thomas Blanton in 2001 and Bobby Frank Cherry in 2002. The church rebuilt where it was; the people of Wales sent a new window, a Black Christ with arms outstretched, which was set into the wall.

In Its Own Record

This is the twentieth-century continuation of the Black church tradition. The Great Migration remade it as an urban, northern institution; the Church of God in Christ rose from 1907; the National Baptist Convention split in 1915; the civil rights movement grew out of these congregations, with Martin Luther King Jr and the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, founded in 1957; and James Cone's writings opened the field of Black theology. Its worship traditions also share roots with the Pentecostal movement that grew out of the Azusa Street revival.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

The Great Migration carried Black congregations out of the rural South and into northern cities, remaking a whole religious culture within a generation; the Church of God in Christ took its Pentecostal turn out of Memphis in 1907 and grew into one of the century's major denominations, while the National Baptist Convention split in 1915. From the 1950s these congregations were the civil rights movement's infrastructure — Martin Luther King Jr. worked out of the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, founded in 1957 — and at the end of the 1960s James Cone's books opened Black theology as a field of its own.

Legacy

In 2010 it was a living tradition, and one of the great Christian stories of the twentieth century. It gave the civil-rights movement its buildings, its money, its networks and much of its language. Through the century, the sermon was where much of America's public moral argument got made. Black theology became a field with its own books and its own critics. And it carried forward the Black church of the nineteenth century, whose congregations became the civil-rights church of this one.
