Pelagianism

Rome and the wider Mediterranean, c. 390 to 430 — a movement built on the conviction that God never commands what a person cannot do, argued out of the church by Augustine and preserved mostly inside his rebuttals.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:14

Read along

Pelagius was a British-born ascetic teacher. He came to Rome in the late fourth century and found the faith of the capital slack. People there made excuses for their sins, on the grounds that human nature simply could not do better. His answer was that God does not command the impossible. If Scripture tells you to be perfect, then you can be, and the excuse is a lie. Serious, high-minded Christians rallied to that belief and formed a movement. It included the combative Caelestius and, later, the bishop Julian of Eclanum. They pushed the argument into hard questions about infant baptism, inherited guilt, and whether grace is help or takeover. Augustine of Hippo fought them for the last twenty years of his life. The African councils condemned them in 418, and the Council of Ephesus did so again in 431. Almost all that Pelagius himself wrote survives only in fragments, in what his foes quoted, or misfiled for centuries under another man's name.

  • Augustine of Hippo — the opponent whose replies are the main reason we know what the Pelagians said at all

Documented Stories

Three Letters to a Girl Taking the Veil

Demetrias was the granddaughter of Proba, the richest Christian widow in Rome, and had fled with her family to Africa when the Goths took the city in 410. In 413, on the eve of an arranged marriage, she asked to be consecrated a virgin instead, and her grandmother and mother, once they had recovered, wrote to the most famous ascetic teachers of the day asking for letters to guide her. Jerome’s, from Bethlehem, is the letter of an old man who has written this letter before: read, fast, avoid heretics, remember your rank means nothing. Pelagius’ is the fullest statement of his own teaching that exists in his own words. He tells Demetrias that the first thing she must understand is ‘the good of nature’ — that God made her capable of doing what he commands, that the saints of the Old Testament kept the law before grace was given, that ‘whenever I have to speak of the principles of right conduct, I first show the power and quality of human nature’, because people excuse themselves by saying they cannot. Augustine and his friend Alypius wrote not to Demetrias but to her mother Juliana, some months later, having read Pelagius’ letter, to warn her against it: a passage saying the girl’s virtues were ‘her own’ and that she had ‘no reason to thank anyone but herself’ was, they said, precisely the poison, and Juliana should watch what her daughter read.

Influence, argument and contact

Argued Against

  • argued against Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity Inferential-Thin — In 416 or 417, Jerome's monasteries at Bethlehem were attacked and burned, and his deacon was killed. Augustine wrote that followers of Pelagius were said to have done it. Pope Innocent noted that the women who told him of it had not named the man behind it. Yet he saw no doubt about who was guilty. So the charge rests on report, not on a named act.
  • argued against Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity Documented — Julian of Eclanum wrote letters that named Augustine and called the Catholic party Manichaeans. Augustine says the Pope sent them on to him, and he answered in four books. He also says Pelagius at Rome could not bear his prayer, 'Give what Thou commandest.'

Opposed By

  • argued against Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity Documented — Augustine, bishop of Hippo, wrote against Pelagius by name. In On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins, he answers points he says Pelagius made against original sin. In his Retractations he says he replied to a book by Pelagius. That book stood up for human nature against the grace of God.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Pelagius, a British-born ascetic teacher in Rome, argued that God never commands the impossible — if Scripture tells you to be perfect, then you can be, and pleading a weak human nature is an excuse, not an explanation. Around that conviction gathered a movement of serious, high-minded Christians who pushed the argument into questions about infant baptism, inherited guilt, and whether grace helps a person or simply takes them over. Augustine fought them for the last twenty years of his life, and African councils condemned the movement in 418, the Council of Ephesus again in 431.

Legacy

There was never a Pelagian church, and the movement left no institution. But the argument it forced has never closed. To answer it, Augustine worked out the Western doctrines of original sin, prevenient grace, and predestination. He gave them the form that Aquinas, Luther, Calvin, and the Jansenists would all later fight over. So a good deal of the shape of Western theology is the shape of this quarrel. "Pelagian" also lives on as one of Christianity's most lasting insults. It is thrown at anyone thought to make salvation a matter of human effort, usually by people who have never read a line of Pelagius, since so little of him survives to read. A few of his works did come through under Jerome's name. Augustine knew in his own day that one of them, the Letter to Demetrias, was Pelagius's own. Yet it was still printed among Jerome's works in the nineteenth century.

Doctrinal Floor Note

The quarrel here was about grace, free will, and original sin — not about the Trinity or the person of Christ. On the questions the Nicene Creed actually settles, the Pelagians stood where their opponents stood, which is why the condemnations against them had to be framed on other grounds.
