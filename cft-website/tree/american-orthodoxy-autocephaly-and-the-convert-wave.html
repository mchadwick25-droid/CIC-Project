The Global Church Era · 1970-c. 2010 · North America

American Orthodoxy — Autocephaly & the Convert Wave

About This Movement

Orthodoxy came to North America with immigrants and stayed, for most of a century, an affair of ethnic parishes, each answering to a church somewhere else. In 1970 the Orthodox Church in America received autocephaly, and an immigrant church became, at least in part, an American one. Then something less expected: in 1987 the Evangelical Orthodox Church — seventeen parishes and around two thousand people, formed by former evangelical leaders — was received into the Antiochian Archdiocese. Peter Gillquist told that story in Becoming Orthodox in 1989. A convert literature grew with it, and Ancient Faith Radio began broadcasting in 2004. How large the convert stream was rests on parish reports and journalism, since no independent national count exists.

In Its Own Record

Grew Out Of

  • The Alaskan Mission — Herman’s Decades & Veniaminov Inferential-Thin Major — The Russian diocese for the Aleutian Islands and Russian America, set up by 1842, may be the root of the later Orthodox Church in America. A list of 1842 shows the diocese being founded, but nothing of it after that. So the line to the later church is thin.
  • The Russian Orthodox Church — Council, Patriarchate and Reunion Widely Accepted Major — By the early 1900s most Orthodox parishes in North America formed one diocese under the Russian church. That is how the church in America tells its own past. In 1970 the patriarch of Moscow and his synod granted it full self-rule.

Influenced By

  • Emigre Orthodoxy & the Paris School Widely Accepted — Teachers from the St Sergius school in Paris came to St Vladimir's Seminary in New York. Georges Florovsky became its dean. Alexander Schmemann came in 1951, and John Meyendorff in 1959.
