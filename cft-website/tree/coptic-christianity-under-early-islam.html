The Early Medieval Era · 642-1013 · Egypt

Coptic Christianity under Early Islam

Egypt, 642 to 1013 — a church that walked out of Byzantine persecution into Muslim rule, and over four centuries began the long slide from being the country's uncontested majority toward the minority it is now — a shift historians place mostly later, and argue about still.

About This Movement

Egypt's miaphysite Christians had spent the century before the Arab conquest under Byzantine pressure for refusing the Council of Chalcedon. When Arab armies took Alexandria in 641-42 their patriarch Benjamin came out of hiding and was restored to his see. The church's own History has him come out on the conqueror 'Amr's promise of safety. Michael the Syrian, a Syriac patriarch of the twelfth century, found it told that Benjamin had promised the Arabs Alexandria if they would drive out Cyrus, the Chalcedonian patriarch, and give him back the churches. Two European scholars, Hiob Ludolf in 1691 and Eusèbe Renaudot in 1713, read the Copts as having helped the conquerors. What followed was the long transition that made the Coptic church what it still is. No longer persecuted for its confession but taxed and governed as a protected minority, shifting gradually from Coptic into Arabic, and beginning the long, centuries-long drift toward minority status that conversions would eventually bring. Its own record of those years, the History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria, follows the church bishop by bishop with unusual care, though it says far more about patriarchs and taxes than about ordinary Egyptian Christians. The window ends with the persecution under the caliph al-Hakim in the early eleventh century, the harshest single episode the community had yet faced.

Documented Stories

Two Patriarchs Sent to the Rebels

In the Hijra year 216, Makrizi says as Eusèbe Renaudot reports him, the Copts rose in revolt, and the general Afshin led troops against them. He could not subdue them until the caliph al-Ma’mun came to Egypt himself. Eutychius of Alexandria calls the rebels the Bimaidae, of the lower part of Egypt. They refused, he says, to pay their tax and tribute, and al-Ma’mun sent his brother al-Mu’tasim against them before coming himself. Bar Hebraeus, who had the history of the Syriac patriarch Dionysius of Tel Mahre before him, tells what the two church leaders did. Al-Ma’mun ordered Dionysius to come with him to Egypt, to send him as envoy to the Christians of the lower country and turn them from rebellion. Dionysius went with Joseph, patriarch of Egypt, to reconcile them. But Afshin would have no peace. He burned their villages, vineyards and gardens and killed a great many. The rest he sent in chains by ship to Antioch and on to Baghdad, and many died on the way. Dionysius went back and told the caliph that they were being crushed. Makrizi says al-Ma’mun, once he knew the cause, condemned many to death and had the women and children sold, so that a great multitude went into slavery. From then on, he adds, the Copts were so broken that they never again dared to rise against the Muslims.

The Lament Over the Lost Language

Samuel of Qalamun was a seventh-century abbot who had resisted the Chalcedonian governor Cyrus and been tortured for it; a later writer put into his mouth an apocalypse foretelling what would happen to Egypt’s Christians under the Arabs. The text is a prophecy after the fact, and what it ‘foretells’ is what its author saw around him. Christians, the monk is made to say, will abandon the beautiful language of Coptic in which the Holy Spirit spoke through the fathers; they will teach their children Arabic and boast of it; they will take Arab names, swear by the names of Arab rulers, marry among the Muslims, and in the churches the people will no longer understand the readings. The lament is explicit that this is not persecution: no one forces them to do this, the text says — they choose it, for advancement and ease. Most bitterly, it foresees that the Copts will forget the names of the martyrs and the sayings of the fathers because they can no longer read them. The apocalypse survives in Arabic. Its own transmission proves its point: the community that mourned the loss of Coptic preserved the mourning only in the language it had adopted. By the eleventh century the History of the Patriarchs was being compiled in Arabic, and a bishop, Severus ibn al-Muqaffa‘, was explaining that he wrote theology in Arabic because his flock no longer understood anything else. The tradition survived the change; the text records what the change cost.

Direct descendants

  • continued as Coptic Christianity, Fatimid to Mamluk Documented Major — Abu Salih, writing in Egypt around 1200, counts the heads of the Coptic church in one numbered line. The sixty-fourth, Zacharias, lived through the persecution of the caliph al-Hakim. The sixty-sixth, Christodulus, led the church in 1065.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced Desert Monasticism Documented — A Coptic book copied by hand in 1006 ends with a prayer for two monks. They were monks of the Church of Saint Mercurius, near Edfu. The prayer asks that they share the inheritance of the 'coenobite fathers'. It names Pachomius, Theodorus, Palamon, Petronius and Horsiesios.

Influenced

  • influenced Makurian Nubian Christianity Inferential-Thin — Old Nubian was written in the Coptic alphabet, with a few extra letters for Nubian sounds. After the Arab conquest of Egypt, says the scholar F. Ll. Griffith, Coptic monks and Coptic books abounded in Nubia. This rests on Griffith's one account, from 1913.

Argued Against

  • argued against Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition Documented — The History of the Patriarchs of the Coptic Church is said to be by Severus ibn al-Muqaffa, a bishop of the 900s. It says Patriarch Demetrius cut Origen off for his books. It calls the books Origen wrote later blasphemous. Yet the same work honors Dionysius of Alexandria as a patriarch. Dionysius had once run the studies at Alexandria.

In Contact With

  • in contact with Post-Aksumite Ethiopian Christianity Documented — Around 687 the Coptic patriarch Isaac wrote to the king of the Abyssinians, urging peace with Nubia. Abu Salih says the patriarchs wrote to the kings of Abyssinia twice a year. The last to do so was Zacharias, until the caliph al-Hakim forbade it.
  • in contact with Makurian Nubian Christianity Documented Major — A Coptic history of the 700s describes the king of Makuria in Nubia. It says the patriarch in Egypt ordained his chief bishop, who then ordained the bishops and priests of the land. Whenever a chief bishop died, it says, the patriarch of Alexandria chose and ordained the next.Lasting result: In the mid-700s the patriarch in Egypt chose and ordained each chief bishop of Makuria.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

When Arab armies took Alexandria in 641-42, the miaphysite patriarch Benjamin came out of hiding after years of Byzantine persecution and was restored to his see. One later Syriac chronicle says he had promised the Arabs Alexandria. Freed from pressure over its faith but now taxed and governed as a minority, the church shifted gradually from Coptic into Arabic over the centuries that followed, a slow change historians still argue about, until a harsh persecution under the caliph al-Hakim closed the period in the early eleventh century.

Legacy

The same church is still there. Its institutions continued unbroken into the Fatimid and Mamluk centuries and on into the present-day Coptic Orthodox Church of Alexandria, one of the largest Christian bodies in the Middle East. What was set in these centuries is the pattern that has defined it since — a minority church organized around its monasteries and its patriarch, using Coptic in worship long after Arabic became the language of everything else.

Experience It Today
