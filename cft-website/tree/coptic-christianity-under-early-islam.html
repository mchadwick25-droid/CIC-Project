The Early Medieval Era · 642-1013 · Egypt

Coptic Christianity under Early Islam

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Egypt's miaphysite Christians had spent the century before the Arab conquest under Byzantine pressure for refusing the Council of Chalcedon. When Arab armies took Alexandria in 641-42 their patriarch Benjamin came out of hiding and was restored to his see. What followed was the long transition that made the Coptic church what it still is. No longer persecuted for its confession but taxed and governed as a protected minority, shifting gradually from Coptic into Arabic, and beginning the long, centuries-long drift toward minority status that conversions would eventually bring. Its own record of those years, the History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria, follows the church bishop by bishop with unusual care, though it says far more about patriarchs and taxes than about ordinary Egyptian Christians. The window ends with the persecution under the caliph al-Hakim in the early eleventh century, the harshest single episode the community had yet faced.

People

No one is shown. The History of the Patriarchs is linked to Severus ibn al-Muqaffa, but whether he wrote it is disputed.

In Its Own Record

Continues

  • The Church of Alexandria and Miaphysite Egypt Documented Major — Benjamin led the church of Alexandria both before and after the Arab conquest. John of Nikiu says he came back to Alexandria in the thirteenth year after he fled the Romans. The History of the Patriarchs says Amr, the conqueror, told him to govern his churches once more.

Continued As

  • Coptic Christianity, Fatimid to Mamluk Documented Major — Abu Salih, writing in Egypt around 1200, counts the heads of the Coptic church in one numbered line. The sixty-fourth, Zacharias, lived through the persecution of the caliph al-Hakim. The sixty-sixth, Christodulus, led the church in 1065.

Influenced By

  • Desert Monasticism Documented — A Coptic book copied by hand in 1006 ends with a prayer for two monks. They were monks of the Church of Saint Mercurius, near Edfu. The prayer asks that they share the inheritance of the 'coenobite fathers'. It names Pachomius, Theodorus, Palamon, Petronius and Horsiesios.

Influenced

  • Makurian Nubian Christianity Inferential-Thin — Old Nubian was written in the Coptic alphabet, with a few extra letters for Nubian sounds. After the Arab conquest of Egypt, says the scholar F. Ll. Griffith, Coptic monks and Coptic books abounded in Nubia. This rests on Griffith's one account, from 1913.

Argued Against

  • Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition Documented — The History of the Patriarchs of the Coptic Church is said to be by Severus ibn al-Muqaffa, a bishop of the 900s. It says Patriarch Demetrius cut Origen off for his books. It calls the books Origen wrote later blasphemous. Yet the same work honors Dionysius of Alexandria as a patriarch. Dionysius had once run the studies at Alexandria.

In Contact With

  • Post-Aksumite Ethiopian Christianity Documented — Around 687 the Coptic patriarch Isaac wrote to the king of the Abyssinians, urging peace with Nubia. Abu Salih says the patriarchs wrote to the kings of Abyssinia twice a year. The last to do so was Zacharias, until the caliph al-Hakim forbade it.
  • Makurian Nubian Christianity Documented Major — A Coptic history of the 700s describes the king of Makuria in Nubia. It says the patriarch in Egypt ordained his chief bishop, who then ordained the bishops and priests of the land. Whenever a chief bishop died, it says, the patriarch of Alexandria chose and ordained the next.Lasting result: In the mid-700s the patriarch in Egypt chose and ordained each chief bishop of Makuria.

Sources

  • History of the Patriarchs of the Coptic Church of Alexandria (attrib. Severus ibn al-Muqaffa'; ed./trans. B. Evetts, Patrologia Orientalis) (primary) — The church's own institutional memory across the conquest and after. English translation free online at tertullian.org.
  • John of Nikiu, Chronicle (trans. R. H. Charles, 1916) (primary) — Miaphysite bishop's narrative of the 642 conquest itself. Ethiopic-via-Arabic transmission; translation available online.
  • Apocalypse of Pseudo-Samuel of Qalamun (primary) — Coptic-tradition lament over Arabization and language loss — rare interior texture for a window whose ordinary voice is otherwise thin.
  • Maged S. A. Mikhail, From Byzantine to Islamic Egypt (I.B. Tauris, 2014) (secondary) — Current standard monograph on exactly this transition (religion, identity, politics after the conquest).
  • Mark N. Swanson, The Coptic Papacy in Islamic Egypt, 641-1517 (AUC Press, 2010) (secondary) — Institution-focused companion built directly on the History of the Patriarchs.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity, vol. 3, ch. 9: 'Christians under Muslim rule' (Sidney H. Griffith) (reference)
