The Malankara Churches — Orthodox & Jacobite

Kerala and the worldwide diaspora, 1912 to 2010 — the ancient St Thomas majority carried at last: the 1912 Catholicate, a constitution in 1934, mid-century reunion and the renewed split of 1975, and a century of ecclesiology argued before India's civil courts.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:01

Read along

In 1912 Patriarch Abdul Masih II consecrated a Catholicos of the East for Malankara, and the ancient St Thomas community in Kerala divided into two: an autocephalous party, the Malankara Orthodox Syrian Church, and a patriarchal party, the Jacobite Syrian Christian Church, which held to the Syriac Orthodox patriarchate of Antioch. A constitution followed in 1934. A Supreme Court ruling in 1958 brought a reunion that held for a generation; in 1975 the patriarch deposed the Catholicos and the split reopened. A further Supreme Court ruling came in 1995. This is a century of ecclesiology argued in civil courts — over parishes, properties and endowments as much as over jurisdiction — and both parties wrote the record, since both were litigants throughout.

Documented Stories

A Deposed Patriarch at Niranam

The St Thomas Christians of Kerala who had kept the Syriac Orthodox connection since the seventeenth century were governed by a metropolitan under the patriarch of Antioch, and by the twentieth century the question was what that meant in practice. In 1909 Patriarch Ignatius Abdullah II visited Kerala and asked Metropolitan Vattasseril Geevarghese Dionysius to sign a document acknowledging the patriarch's authority over the church's property as well as its faith. Dionysius declined; the property, he held, belonged to the community. In 1911 the patriarch excommunicated him and installed another metropolitan. The community's assembly refused to accept it. Its leaders then turned to Ignatius Abdul Masih II, Abdullah's predecessor, who had been deposed in 1903 by the Ottoman government at the instance of his own synod, but who had never been canonically removed by the church and still regarded himself as patriarch. He agreed to come. In September 1912, at St Mary's Church in Niranam, Abdul Masih consecrated Murimattathil Paulose Mar Ivanios as Baselios Paulose I, Catholicos of the East, reviving for Malankara a title that had lapsed in Mesopotamia, and vested the catholicos and the community's synod with the right to consecrate their own bishops. Those who accepted the act became the Malankara Orthodox Syrian Church; those who held to Abdullah and the patriarchate became the Jacobite Syrian Christian Church. The litigation that followed, from the Vattipanam suit onward, turned on whether a deposed patriarch's consecration was valid. The courts answered more than once.

One Generation of Peace

The division of 1912 had gone at once to the courts, over a trust fund, a seminary and parish churches, and the suits had climbed through the Travancore courts to the Supreme Court of the new republic. On 12 September 1958 the Supreme Court gave judgment in the Samudayam case, holding that the Catholicos party's synod and constitution of 1934 were valid and that the patriarch's excommunication of Dionysius had not been. Something then happened that the litigation had not predicted. The patriarch, Ignatius Yakub III, and the catholicos, Baselios Geevarghese II, opened a correspondence; the patriarch wrote acknowledging the catholicos, the catholicos wrote acknowledging the patriarch's spiritual primacy, and on 16 December 1958 the two parties formally reunited. Bishops who had been rivals concelebrated; parishes that had been locked against one faction opened; the patriarch visited Kerala in 1964 and installed the next catholicos, Augen I, with his own hands. For a generation it held. Then the old question, whether the patriarch's authority over the Indian church was spiritual only or also administrative, came back, and in 1975 Yakub III declared Augen I deposed and consecrated a rival catholicos, Baselios Paulose II. The community divided again along almost exactly its former line, the suits resumed, and the Supreme Court ruled again in 1995, for the 1934 constitution, without ending the matter, because a judgment can settle who owns a church and not who belongs to it.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 1912 Patriarch Abdul Masih II consecrated a Catholicos for Malankara, splitting Kerala's ancient St Thomas Christian community into an autocephalous Malankara Orthodox Syrian Church and a patriarchal Jacobite Syrian Christian Church loyal to the Syriac Orthodox patriarchate of Antioch. A reunion in 1958 held for a generation until the patriarch deposed the Catholicos in 1975, reopening the division. It was fought out in India's civil courts in the decades that followed, over parishes, properties and endowments. The fight ran up to a Supreme Court ruling of 1995.

Legacy

In 2010 both churches were alive and substantial. Both had gone worldwide with the Kerala diaspora, with parishes across many countries. They carried the St Thomas tradition's liturgical life into the new century. Their survival is why that tradition was not only a matter of the distant past. The court record reached the Supreme Court again in 1995.
