The Imperial Church Era · 361-441 · Armenia

Mesrop Mashtots

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Armenian Christianity (conversion era).

Known for
Armenian monk who, by the account of his pupil Koriun, made the Armenian letters with a hermit named Rufinus, whom Koriun's text calls a man of Samos, began putting the Scriptures into Armenian, and set up schools in the Persian and Roman parts of Armenia.
Also called
Mesrob, Maschdotz, Maschthotz
Dates
Born in 361 and died in 441, at eighty, by a note in Victor Langlois's modern edition; Koriun gives no years. Koriun places the making of the letters under the emperor Theodosius the Younger, and Mesrop's death on the eighteenth of the month méhégan, in the year Sahak died
Place
Daron; Koghten; Syrian Mesopotamia; Constantinople and the Roman part of Armenia; Georgia; the New City, in Ararat
Role
Monk, teacher and maker of the Armenian letters

About This Person

A Syrian bishop's letters could not carry the sounds of Armenian, and Mesrop Mashtots prayed with tears, day and night, for letters that could. Then, his pupil Koriun writes: "At once he saw them. It was not in a dream while he slept, nor in a vision while he was awake, but in his heart. There they appeared to the eyes of his mind, traced by the wrist of a right hand writing on stone."

The letters, Koriun means, came whole, as an inner sight. Nearly all we know rests on this pupil, who says he saw these men with his own eyes. By his account, this royal secretary turned monk made the letters of the Armenian language, so that the faith could be preached, written and taught in Armenian. He worked with the king and with Sahak, the catholicos who led the Armenian church. Together they began putting the Scriptures into Armenian and founded schools on both sides of the Persian and Roman border.

As a monk he lived on herbs and slept on bare ground.

In Koghten he preached to people who kept pagan ways, but with no letters for Armenian, preaching was hard. On the Persian side of the divided country, Koriun says, only Syriac learning was allowed.

King Vramshapuh sent Mesrop to Syrian Mesopotamia, the Syriac lands to the south. Mesrop made new letters with a hermit named Rufinus, who shaped them on his directions, after the Greek alphabet.

Translation began at once, with Proverbs. In the days of the emperor Theodosius the Younger, he brought the letters to Sahak and the king. "They rejoiced over them as if they were the divine Tablets," Koriun writes: the tablets of the Law that God gave Moses. "Mesrop gathered children who were bright and well taught, with sweet voices and long breath," and schools opened in every province and village.

Koriun says he then gave the Georgians an alphabet and renewed that of the Albanians, a Christian people of the Caucasus. The Georgian chronicle gives Georgia's script to an ancient king.

Back in Armenia, because of that ban, Koriun says, Sahak and Mesrop went west into Roman Armenia. Sahak wrote to the emperor and the bishop of Constantinople and sent Mesrop to them. In Roman Armenia, children studied the letters at public cost. Koriun tells of a warm welcome; a later historian, Moses of Khoren, says the letters were first refused. In the emperor's letter Moses gives, Sahak is blamed for serving a pagan king and for turning to Syrians, not to Constantinople's scholars.

Mesrop fought the Borborites, a sect Koriun calls rash and insolent. Koriun never says what they taught. Those who would not yield, he writes, were put in prison and chains and tormented; those who still refused were burned, shut up and driven from the land.

Sahak and Mesrop sent pupils to Edessa, a center of Syriac learning, and from there they went to Constantinople. They brought home true copies of the Scriptures, and the first hasty translations were corrected. When the council of Ephesus warned of a man it had shut out, whose books, Koriun says, followed Nestorius, Sahak and Mesrop drove him out. That council's quarrel turned on whether Mary could be called Mother of God; Nestorius urged Mother of Christ instead.

When Sahak died, Mesrop wept, finding no one to share the work of Armenia. After a short illness, he sat up among his disciples, raised his arms, blessed the Trinity and entrusted them to God. Koriun says a cross of light was seen over the house.

Every later Armenian witness we have names him the letters' maker, some beside Sahak. Lazar, a later historian who credits the translating to Sahak, says once the letters and Scriptures came, copyists multiplied, and men and women crowded the churches. Mesrop's pupils became the next writers: Koriun, Eznik, and, by his own account, Moses of Khoren.

The hand Koriun saw writing on stone had left letters, and those pupils carried them on.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Koriun says Sahak and Mesrop sent pupils, Eznik of Goghp and Joseph of Baghin among them, to Edessa, and from there to Constantinople. They brought back sound copies of the Scriptures, and the first hasty translations were corrected against them; Emine's modern French translation supplies Sahak's name in brackets.
  • Koriun says Mesrop gave the Georgians letters, renewed the letters of the Aghouank, and left teachers in both lands.
  • Langlois, the modern editor, writes that the name "caractères mesrobiens" became the usual name for the Armenian alphabet.
  • Lazar of Pharp says that before the letters, church services and study were in Syriac, which the people could not understand. He gives the translating of the Scriptures to Sahak, and says that once it was done schools opened, copyists multiplied, and men and women crowded the churches.
  • Moses of Khoren says Sahak and Mesrop sent him to Alexandria to study.
  • Koriun says Hemayak and Vahan carried Mesrop's body, led by the cross of light, to Oshakan, and that Vahan built a church over the relics three months later.
  • Koriun says the pupils brought home, with the true copies of the Scriptures and the writings of the fathers, the canons of the councils of Nicaea and Ephesus.

How they're remembered

  • Moses of Khoren, whom the modern editor of the Armenian text, as Langlois reports, takes for Mesrop's nephew and pupil, says he died as one "qui surpassait en vertu ses contemporains les plus vertueux".
  • Lazar of Pharp, a later Armenian historian, sends readers to Koriun's Life to learn when, where and through whom the letters were found.
  • Koriun says he was learned in civil and military affairs and liked by great and small, and that he also lived in caves on herbs, coming down only when he was needed in the lands he had taught.
  • The Armenian Synaxary of Ter Israel, of the thirteenth century, keeps a feast of the Armenian Translators, Isaac and Mesrop and others, on 25 November. In another entry it says Isaac, that is Sahak, made the letters with the vardapet Mesrob.
  • Koriun says that Mesrop, who came before the emperor and the patriarch, was given by the patriarch the title of preacher (égégbésigdîgos) and ranked among the most illustrious vartapets.
  • Koriun calls Sahak and Mesrop defenders of the true faith, where he tells of their driving out the man the council of Ephesus had shut out.

What Is Uncertain

  • Almost all that is known of him comes from the Life by his pupil Koriun, the work of a disciple honoring a saint, with demons put to flight and a cross of light over the house where he died. It is held only in J.-Raphaël Emine's French translation, in Langlois's edition. Moses of Khoren and Lazar of Pharp retell parts of it.
  • Koriun gives no year for the letters or for Mesrop's birth or death. The years 361 and 441 and the age of eighty come from a note in Langlois's modern edition.
  • The day and place of his death are told two ways. Koriun gives the eighteenth of méhégan, in the New City, in Ararat, in the same year as Sahak's death and ten months after it, as the French reads. Moses of Khoren and Lazar of Pharp give the thirteenth of méhégan, six months after Sahak, at Vagharschabad, which Lazar says was also called the New City.
  • Koriun says the Syrian bishop Daniel's help came to nothing. Langlois, a modern editor, following the modern scholar Luc Indjidji, holds that earlier attempts at Armenian letters had been made, and that Mesrop "ne fit que reprendre un travail" begun long before. Later writers whom Langlois quotes tell of letters kept by Daniel that Mesrop completed.
  • Koriun's text calls Rufinus a hermit of Samos and a disciple of an Epiphanius whom it does not identify. A note citing the modern scholar Saint-Martin, and Langlois's modern introduction, read Samosata, a town of Syria near Armenia, and Langlois says the copyists all wrote Samos.
  • The French calls the man driven out after the Council of Ephesus "Théodore", and adds "[de Mopsueste]" in brackets. The identification is the modern translator's bracket, not Koriun's.
  • That Mesrop gave the Georgians an alphabet and renewed that of the Aghouank rests on Koriun here; Moses of Khoren later has him make letters for both.
  • Koriun names his father as Vartan. A note in Langlois's modern edition reports a supposition, by the modern scholar Emin, that he was of the Mamikonian family. Langlois says some authors claim Mesrop once served in the army, and thinks the post in the royal archives more likely.
  • No writing of Mesrop's own is held. Koriun says he composed many discourses for ordinary people.
  • Koriun tells a smooth welcome on the Roman side. Moses of Khoren, a later historian whose date modern scholars dispute, has Sahak write to the emperor that those who govern had not accepted the letters, and has the emperor first answer that it pleased him to see his subjects reject the teaching, then order it received once Mesrop said it came by grace from above.
  • Koriun says the Borborites who would not yield were put in prison and chains and tormented, and that those who still refused were burned, shut up and driven from the land, all in the passive; he does not say who did it, and the Armenian is not held. Moses of Khoren has Sahak order that, if neither gentleness nor rigor moved them, punishments be used, including a death justly inflicted on the body.
  • The Georgian chronicle, in Brosset's French, says an early king, Pharnavaz, invented Georgia's national script. Brosset's modern notes take Mesrop's to be the church script, and find it surprising that the Georgian annalist says nothing of it.
  • Koriun gives the translating of the Scriptures to Mesrop and his disciples, and later to Sahak and Mesrop together. Lazar of Pharp gives it to Sahak, and the Armenian Synaxary makes Sahak its chief translator.
  • After Sahak's death a note in Langlois's modern edition says Mesrop administered the patriarchal see until Joseph succeeded in 441, while Moses of Khoren says Mesrop ordered the see given to his disciple Joseph with the title of deputy.
  • Koriun says Mesrop's body was carried to Oshakan. Moses of Khoren tells of a quarrel among three parties over where to bury him, in Daron, in Koghten or at Vagharschabad, which Vahan Amadouni settled by taking the body to Oshakan.
  • Moses of Khoren gives the full texts of five letters: Sahak to the emperor Theodosius, Sahak to Atticus, the bishop of the capital, Sahak to the general Anatole, and the answers of Theodosius and of Atticus to Sahak. Mesrop and Sahak's nephew Vartan carry Sahak's letters and bring back the emperor's.
  • Koriun's French gives the death on the eighteenth of méhégan, and Moses of Khoren and Lazar of Pharp the thirteenth. The modern notes in Langlois's edition, to Moses and to Lazar, both turn the thirteenth into 19 February 441.
  • Koriun does not say what the Borborites taught. Augustine, in his list of heresies, says some call the Gnostics Borborites, as if to say filthy, for the foulness they are said to practice in their mysteries. A Syriac list of heresies credited to Marutha of Maipherqat, in Braun's German, says that for the immorality and great licentiousness of the sect of that name he refuses to report on it. Those are those writers' charges. That Koriun's sect is theirs rests on the name only (Inferential-Thin); Langlois's modern note makes the link, calling the Borborites a Gnostic sect of the second century that denied the Last Judgment.
  • Koriun's French says the books of the man the council of Ephesus had shut out, whom it names Théodore, followed the heresies of Paul of Samosata and of Nestorius.
  • The years for the letters' time are a modern editor's: they come from Langlois's notes, not from Koriun: they put Sahak on the patriarchal see from 390 to 440, and Vramshapuh on the throne from 392 to his death in 414. Koriun himself gives only that Theodosius the Younger was emperor.
  • The brief calls the Albanians a Christian people of the Caucasus. The project's entry for Caucasian Albania places them in the eastern Caucasus, a modern placing. Moses of Khoren gives the house of Sissag the Aghouank plain and its mountain region, from the Araxes to the fortress called Hénaraguerd, and gives Aran, of that house, the great country of the north-east near the Cyrus. He also names the people of the mountain of the Caucasus beside the Aghouank and the Iberians. Neither passage says eastern Caucasus.
