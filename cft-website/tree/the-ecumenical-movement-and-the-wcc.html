The Ecumenical Movement & the World Council of Churches

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:21

Read along

The World Missionary Conference at Edinburgh in 1910 left three currents running, and over the next decades they came together. The International Missionary Council was founded in October 1921. Life and Work, which dealt with the churches' common action in society, took shape in 1925. Faith and Order, which dealt with the teachings that divided them, followed in 1927. The last two merged into the World Council of Churches. It was formed at Amsterdam on 22 August 1948 with 147 member churches, and the missionary council joined it at New Delhi in 1961. It was a council of churches, not a church. It had no power over its members and no creed of its own, and each member kept its own. Orthodox churches were members. The Roman Catholic Church was not. In 1928 Pope Pius XI ruled that Catholics could not take part. After the Second Vatican Council, Rome worked with the council in other ways. In the Middle East, the Middle East Council of Churches held its first assembly at Nicosia from 28 to 30 May 1974.

Documented Stories

Lausanne, 1927

Charles Brent, an Episcopal missionary bishop in the Philippines, had sat in the World Missionary Conference at Edinburgh in 1910, from which doctrine had been excluded to keep the peace, and had come away convinced that the churches would have to talk about exactly what divided them or not be one at all. His church took up the proposal that autumn. The war intervened. On 3 August 1927 the first World Conference on Faith and Order opened in Lausanne with Brent presiding, some four hundred delegates from a hundred and eight churches, Protestant, Anglican, Old Catholic and Orthodox; Rome had declined. For three weeks the sections debated the gospel, the nature of the church, its common confession, its ministry and its sacraments, and produced reports that set agreements and disagreements side by side. On 18 August Archbishop Germanos of Thyateira read a statement for the Orthodox delegation: they had taken part gladly, they could accept the report on the gospel, and on the rest they would not vote, because the Orthodox church could not treat as open questions matters its councils had settled, and because a reunion built on compromise formulae would be no reunion. The conference received the statement without offense and printed it in full. That set the movement's method: to say plainly where the churches stood and to keep meeting. Brent died in 1929. The continuation committee he left became, twenty-one years later, half of the World Council of Churches.

The Special Fund

The Uppsala assembly of 1968, meeting weeks after the murder of Martin Luther King, who was to have preached to it, resolved that the churches must move from statements against racism to action. The Programme to Combat Racism was set up in 1969, and on 3 September 1970 its Special Fund announced its first grants: $200,000 to nineteen organizations, among them the African National Congress, ZANU and ZAPU in Rhodesia and SWAPO in Namibia, all engaged in armed struggle against white-minority or colonial rule. The grants were for humanitarian purposes, health, education and refugees, and were made without conditions; the council said the movements were the authentic voice of the oppressed and that it would not police money given in trust. Governments in Pretoria and Salisbury denounced it; so did member churches in Germany, Britain and America. In June 1978, at the Elim Pentecostal mission at Vumba in eastern Rhodesia, guerrillas of ZANLA, the armed wing of ZANU, killed eight British missionaries and four of their children; a ninth adult died of her wounds six days later. In August the Special Fund announced a grant of $85,000 to the Patriotic Front, the alliance of ZANU and ZAPU. The Salvation Army suspended its membership in the council that month and withdrew in 1981; the Presbyterian Church in Ireland withdrew in 1980; others reduced their contributions. The council did not rescind the grant. The program continued until 1994, when the movements it had funded were in government across the region, and both parties went on writing its history.

In Its Own Record

The World Missionary Conference at Edinburgh in 1910 set three streams running. The International Missionary Council began in 1921, Life and Work in 1925, and Faith and Order in 1927. These came together in the World Council of Churches, formed at Amsterdam in 1948. The missionary council joined it at New Delhi in 1961. Orthodox churches were members. In 1920 the Ecumenical Patriarchate had called for a fellowship of churches. The Roman Catholic Church did not join. In 1928 Pope Pius XI ruled that Catholics could not take part. After the Second Vatican Council, Rome worked with it. In its first years most of its member churches were Protestant and Western. It may have grown in part out of the world of the mainline churches, but the evidence for that is thin. The Lausanne movement of 1974 gave an evangelical answer to the same question. How should divided churches work together? In Africa, churches formed the All Africa Conference of Churches. It was founded at its first assembly in Kampala on 20 April 1963. A meeting at Ibadan in 1958 came first. In 1971 the Kenyan theologian John Gatu first called for a moratorium on missionaries. The conference's third assembly met at Lusaka in May 1974.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

The World Missionary Conference at Edinburgh in 1910 left three currents running. One was the International Missionary Council, founded in 1921. The other two were the Life and Work and Faith and Order movements of the 1920s. They came together in the World Council of Churches, formed at Amsterdam in 1948 with 147 member churches. The missionary council joined at New Delhi in 1961. It was a council of churches, not a church. It had no power over its members and no creed of its own. Orthodox churches belonged to it. The Roman Catholic Church never joined. In 1928 Pius XI ruled that Catholics could not take part. After the Second Vatican Council, Rome worked alongside it.

Legacy

In 2010 it was a living body. For a century it had been the main attempt to get divided churches to act and argue together in one room. Its assemblies and its Faith and Order work produced many shared statements. They dealt with questions that had split churches for centuries, and its member churches used them. This map draws it as the counter-pole to the Lausanne movement of 1974. They are two answers, from the same century, to one question: how should churches that disagree work together? It was also one home of the mainline century, which may have helped to build it.

How Sure the Record Is

Rich institutional record: the International Missionary Council's founding records from October 1921, Faith and Order and Life and Work records, the Amsterdam assembly acts of 22 August 1948 (147 member churches), and the World Council of Churches archives.

  • The founding record of the International Missionary Council (October 1921) (primary) — The council picked up directly from where the Edinburgh 1910 conference left off.
  • The Amsterdam assembly acta (22 August 1948, 147 churches) and the New Delhi assembly record (1961, the International Missionary Council merger) (primary) — These record the council's founding and its later completion with the missionary council's merger.
  • Faith and Order (1927) and Life and Work (1925) records (primary) — The two movements whose merger the council was; dates from standard reference.
  • The World Council of Churches archives in Geneva: assembly reports, Faith and Order studies, and the member-church records (primary) — The institution's own continuing record.
  • All Africa Conference of Churches, 'History' (aacc-ceta.org) (reference) — First General Assembly, Kampala, 20 April 1963, after the 1958 Ibadan conference. The 1963 report, Drumbeats from Kampala, is not confirmed.
  • All Africa Conference of Churches, The Struggle Continues: official report of the Third Assembly, Lusaka, 12-24 May 1974; with Christianity Today (November 1974) and the WCC's obituary of John Gatu (reference) — Gatu first made the call in 1971; where he made it is disputed.
  • Middle East Council of Churches, 'The Birth of the Middle East Council of Churches' (mecc.org, 13 August 2020) (reference) — The inaugural assembly at Nicosia, 28–30 May 1974.
