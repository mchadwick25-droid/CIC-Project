Melania the Elder

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Jerusalem Liturgical-Pilgrimage Christianity.

Known for
A Roman widow who fed the exiled monks of Egypt, founded a monastery in Jerusalem beside Rufinus of Aquileia, and took in the young Evagrius Ponticus.
Also called
Melanium (in the letters of Jerome of Strido)
Dates
Widowed at twenty-two, and back in Rome at sixty, by the account of Palladius of Helenopolis. W. H. Fremantle, in his life of Rufinus of Aquileia, has her reach Italy with Rufinus in the spring of 397. No held source gives the year she died.
Place
Rome; Nitria in Egypt; Jerusalem
Role
Founder and head of a women's monastery in Jerusalem

About This Person

A Roman noblewoman lost her husband, and before he was buried, two of her sons died. Jerome of Strido tells it to Paula of Rome. Not a tear fell, he says. She threw herself at the feet of Christ. "Henceforth, Lord," she said, "I will serve Thee more readily, for Thou hast freed me from a great burden." She gave all she had to her only son and sailed for Jerusalem.

She was Melania, called the Elder because her granddaughter shared her name. Palladius of Helenopolis says she was widowed at twenty-two and sailed first to Alexandria. In Egypt she spent half a year with the monks of Nitria. When their leaders were exiled to Palestine, she followed and fed them, dressed as a slave. The governor threw her in prison. She told him whose daughter and wife she was, "but I am Christ's slave." He apologised and released her.

She then founded a monastery in Jerusalem, Palladius writes, and led fifty women there for twenty-seven years. Rufinus of Aquileia lived there with her. They welcomed pilgrims and healed a split among some four hundred monks. Jerome's Chronicle says her humility earned her the name of Thecla, an early woman saint.

Rufinus, her companion and by then Jerome's opponent, says Jerome cut her from every copy of the Chronicle, for disapproving of some of his deeds. In 415 Jerome wrote of "her whose name bears witness to the blackness of her perfidy." The notes to W. H. Fremantle's edition read this as a pun on her name, meaning black.

At sixty, Palladius says, she sailed back to Rome to guard her family from false teaching, and urged them to give up their wealth. To senators who opposed her she said, "Little children, it was written 400 years ago, It is the last hour." She returned to Jerusalem and died within forty days, leaving "both a monastery in Jerusalem and an endowment for it."

Legacy

What traces to them

  • A monastery in Jerusalem, which Palladius says she led for twenty-seven years, with fifty women, and left with "an endowment for it."
  • The monastic life of Evagrius Ponticus, whom she nursed in Jerusalem and made promise to become a monk, as Palladius tells it.
  • The renunciation of her granddaughter Melania the Younger and her husband Pinianus, whom Palladius says she strengthened in Rome and led out to the monastic life.

How they're remembered

  • Jerome's Chronicle says her virtues, above all her humility, earned her the name of Thecla.
  • Jerome, leaving Rome in 385, named "Paula and Melanium" as the only ladies of Rome whose renunciation caused scandal.
  • Jerome, after the quarrel, wrote of "her whose name bears witness to the blackness of her perfidy"; the notes to W. H. Fremantle's edition read it as a pun on her name, which means black.
  • Palladius of Helenopolis calls her "the thrice-blessed Melania".

What Is Uncertain

  • Her friends and her critic tell her story. Palladius admired her. Rufinus of Aquileia was her companion and wrote against Jerome. Jerome praised her in his early letters and attacked her after the quarrel over Origen.
  • Her father is not agreed. Palladius and Jerome's Chronicle call her the daughter of the consul Marcellinus. Rufinus says she was his granddaughter.
  • Rufinus says Jerome erased her name from every copy of his Chronicle because she disapproved of some of his deeds. John Fotheringham's edition of the Chronicle notes the entry as erased by Jerome from his own copies, citing Rufinus.
  • Her sons are told differently. Jerome says two sons died with her husband and she gave all to "her only son." Palladius calls him her only son, and W. K. Lowther Clarke notes that she had two other sons.
  • Palladius places her monastery "in Jerusalem". The notes to W. H. Fremantle's edition of Jerome place her with Rufinus on the Mount of Olives.
  • Chapter 55 of the Lausiac History is headed for Silvania, a virgin travelling with Palladius. W. K. Lowther Clarke's notes report that Turner and Butler read it as a continuation of the chapter on Melania. Clarke takes Palladius's "we" as Palladius and Melania. In it a woman reads the old commentators seven or eight times over. Clarke notes that the mention of Origen there is missing in the leading witnesses to the text.
