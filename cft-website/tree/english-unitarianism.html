The Enlightenment & Awakening Era · 1774-1813 · England: London, Birmingham and Hackney

England, today in United Kingdom

English Unitarianism

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Theophilus Lindsey was a Church of England clergyman at Catterick, in Yorkshire. He grew uneasy in his post and thought of leaving it. Others in that church were trying to end the rule that clergy must sign its Thirty-Nine Articles. Lindsey left, and on 17 April 1774 he opened a chapel in Essex Street, in London.

In his first sermon there, Lindsey said why they had come together. They were "a separate congregation, distinct from that of our national church". They met so that they "may be at liberty to worship God alone". No one, he said, should make others sign his own reading of Scripture. The prayers that day spoke to the Father: "thou art God alone".

Joseph Priestley came from the other side, the old Dissent. He and his fellow tutors at a Dissenting academy, he wrote, "were likewise all Arians". At Leeds he became what was called a Socinian, holding that Christ was a man and not God. In London he spent his Sundays at Lindsey's chapel and sometimes led its worship. He wrote a pamphlet every year in defense of "the unitarian doctrine".

In Birmingham, Priestley also argued with the new Swedenborgian church. His letters of 1791 rejected its teaching that the one God is Jesus Christ. Robert Hindmarsh, a leader of that church, answered him in 1792. A Socinian, he wrote, had no right to the name of a Christian.

On 14 July 1791 a mob in Birmingham burned the church where Priestley preached. Then they burned his home. It wrecked the houses of many Dissenters too. Priestley moved to Hackney, near London. A church there asked him to be its minister. In April 1794 he sailed for America. Thomas Belsham taught at the college in Hackney. He later served the Essex Street chapel.

The same belief had English voices long before Lindsey. In 1653 John Biddle put a life of Socinus, the Polish Brethren's teacher, into English. In 1777 Joshua Toulmin, a Baptist pastor at Taunton, wrote a new life of Socinus. A Baptist editor wrote in 1910 that some Baptist churches in the west of England turned Unitarian too.

The chapel's prayer book crossed the sea. In 1786 the minister of King's Chapel in Boston wrote to Lindsey about his new liturgy. He said he had tried to bring in Lindsey's whole liturgy. He also credited Lindsey's books for the spread of Unitarian belief in New England.

  • English Old Dissent — Presbyterians and Congregationalists Documented Major — Joseph Priestley trained and served among the English Dissenters. Opposed By
