The Reformation Era · 1491-1650 · Japan, Kongo, China, Americas

Mission-Born Christianities (Kirishitan Japan; Kongo; China; New Spain/Andes)

Japan, Kongo, China and the Americas, 1491 to 1650 — Christian communities born far outside Europe, most of whose surviving records were written by the missionaries rather than by them.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:10

Read along

Between the 1490s and the middle of the seventeenth century, Christian communities took root far outside Europe under very different conditions. The kingdom of Kongo, whose ruler Afonso I adopted the faith and whose letters survive in his own voice. Japan, where a Jesuit mission from 1549 gathered a large Christian population before a savage prohibition drove the survivors underground. China, where Jesuits and Chinese converts argued out how the Christian God could be spoken of in a Confucian idiom. And Spanish America, where conquest and mission arrived together and Andean and Nahua Christians made something of their own out of what they were given. Almost everything written about these communities was written by missionaries, which makes the deepest question about them a hard one — how much of what survives is the mission's story and how much the converts'. And Japan and Kongo are where converts' own voices most clearly break through.

People and Stories

Voices in the Record

  • Afonso I (Mvemba a Nzinga) — king of Kongo from about 1509, whose own letters argue with Portugal about the slave trade and about his church
  • Fabian Fucan — Japanese convert, later apostate, who wrote first for Christianity and then against it
  • Xu Guangqi — Chinese scholar-official and convert, Matteo Ricci's collaborator and co-translator of Euclid into Chinese
  • Guaman Poma de Ayala — Andean nobleman whose vast illustrated letter to the king of Spain protests what colonial Christianity had become

Documented Stories

Afonso of Kongo Writes to the King of Portugal About the Slave Traders

Mvemba a Nzinga had taken the name Afonso at his baptism in 1491, set about making Kongo a Christian kingdom on his own terms: churches at Mbanza Kongo, a school for nobles’ sons, his son Henrique sent to Lisbon and consecrated a bishop in 1518, and a stream of letters to Portugal asking for priests, teachers, doctors. Two from 1526 are about the slave trade. In July he wrote to João III that the Portuguese merchants and factors were ‘taking every day our natives, sons of the land and the sons of our noblemen and vassals and our relatives’, ‘and so great, Sir, is the corruption and licentiousness that our country is being completely depopulated’; he asked the king to send no merchants except for wine, flour and priests. In October, having had no answer, he wrote that he had set up a commission of three nobles to inspect every slave before shipment and free any who had been taken illegally. He did not ask for the trade to end; Kongo sold war captives, and Afonso’s own letters elsewhere accept it. He asked that his own people, and Christians, not be taken. Portugal’s answer was to expand the trade through São Tomé. Afonso ruled until about 1542, and the Kongolese church he built survived him by three centuries; the letters are the first sustained protest by an African ruler against the Atlantic trade, written by a Christian king to a Christian king in the name of a shared faith.

The Twenty-Six at Nagasaki

The regent Toyotomi Hideyoshi had banned the missionaries in 1587 without enforcing it; a Spanish galleon wrecked off Shikoku in 1596, and its pilot’s boast that the friars were the advance guard of conquest, gave him a reason. He ordered the Franciscans of Kyoto and their converts arrested. Twenty-four were taken — six Spanish and Mexican friars, three Japanese Jesuit brothers arrested by mistake, and fifteen Japanese laymen, catechists and members of the Franciscan third order, among them Ludovico Ibaraki, aged twelve, Antonio, thirteen, and Tomás Kozaki, fourteen — and had the lobes of their ears cut off in Kyoto on 3 January 1597. They were then marched nearly six hundred miles to Nagasaki in winter, through towns where the sentence was proclaimed; two Japanese who tried to bring them food were added to the group, making twenty-six. On 5 February they were tied and chained to crosses on the hill of Nishizaka above the harbor, in a row, before a crowd Fróis puts at four thousand, and killed with lances. He records Paul Miki, a Japanese Jesuit and preacher, addressing the crowd from his cross to say that he was Japanese and a Christian, that he forgave Hideyoshi, and that the Christian way was the only way; and the boy Ludovico asking which cross was his and running to it. The bodies were left for months and the Christians of Nagasaki took relics. Their story was the mission’s and the converts’ at once, and the converts were the majority.

Guaman Poma Draws the Priests

Felipe Guaman Poma de Ayala was a Christian of the second generation, a Quechua-speaking noble of Huamanga who had served the Spanish administration as an interpreter, including for the visitador Cristóbal de Albornoz’s extirpation of native religion in the 1570s, and had lost a lawsuit over his family’s lands. Around 1600 he began the Nueva corónica y buen gobierno, addressed to Philip III, and finished it about 1615: nearly twelve hundred pages in Spanish mixed with Quechua, with three hundred and ninety-eight full-page drawings. Half of it is a history of the Andes placing the Incas in a Christian chronology; half is an indictment. The drawings of the clergy are the ones historians reproduce: a priest beating a native with a stick; a priest forcing a woman to weave for him; a priest with his native concubine and their children; a priest refusing the sacraments to the poor; a corregidor and a priest dining together while natives serve. He also drew the good — a native confraternity, a Christian burial, the Andean Christian at prayer — and he insisted throughout that he was a Christian and a loyal subject, that the Andeans had been better Christians before the conquest than the Spaniards were after it, and that the king, if he knew, would act. It is the longest document any indigenous American Christian wrote about the church that had converted him, and its argument is that the church had failed the conversion.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Grew out of Catholic missions. The Portuguese came to Kongo in 1491 and the Franciscans to Mexico in 1524. The Jesuits went to Japan and China. Together they became one root of the spread of Christianity across the world in the centuries that followed.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In Kongo, King Afonso I took up the faith and left letters in his own voice. In Japan, a savage ban drove Christian communities underground. Later studies hold that hidden believers kept the faith alive there for two and a half centuries, with no priest. Almost everything else about these communities was written by missionaries. So whose story survives, the mission's or the converts', is a real and open question.

Legacy

These are among the first steps of Christianity as a faith of the whole world, and several of them were never broken. Later studies hold that the hidden Christians of Japan kept a faith handed down for about two hundred and fifty years with no priests. Their villages are now a UNESCO World Heritage site. Latin American and Philippine Catholicism grew straight out of this time, with their processions, brotherhoods and local saints. Kongo's Christians may also have shaped the beliefs of enslaved Africans in the Americas. But the writings of this time do not show it.

Sources

Doctrinal Floor Note

No question about their creedal faith. The hard question here is a different one: with nearly all the surviving records written by missionaries, how much of what we can read is the mission's story rather than the converts' own.

How Sure the Record Is

Uneven by region but genuinely moderate-to-rich in each named case; heavy missionary-pen gravity; real native voices (Japan, Kongo especially)

  • Kirishitan Japan: Dochirina Kirishitan (1591/1600 catechism as used); Fabian Fucan's Myotei mondo (1605) and Ha Daiusu (1620) in Elison, Deus Destroyed (Harvard, 1973); Tenchi Hajimari no Koto (trans. Whelan, 1996); martyr acts and fumi-e apostasy registers (primary) — Fucan wrote first as a convert apologist, then later as an apostate attacking the faith he'd left. The fumi-e apostasy registers are hostile, coerced evidence — produced under a torture regime designed to force apostasy.
  • Kongo: the letters of King Afonso I (Mvemba a Nzinga, c. 1509-1540s) in Brasio, Monumenta Missionaria Africana (Eng. excerpts in documentary readers); the Kikongo Doutrina christaa (1624) (primary) — A genuine African Christian ruler's own voice: he requested teachers and protested the slave trade. (Dona Beatriz, a later prophetic figure from 1704, belongs to a subsequent period.)
  • China: Ricci, The True Meaning of the Lord of Heaven (trans. Lancashire & Hu, 1985); the converts' own texts - Xu Guangqi, Yang Tingyun; Kouduo richao (Li Jiubiao's Diary of Oral Admonitions, trans. Zuercher, 2007) (primary) — Lists converts' own texts first. The Kouduo richao (Diary of Oral Admonitions) shows ordinary convert experience directly.
  • New Spain/Andes: Nahuatl testaments (The Testaments of Culhuacan); Guaman Poma, Nueva coronica y buen gobierno (1615); the Huarochiri Manuscript (trans. Salomon & Urioste, Texas, 1991); extirpation-trial records (primary) — The Huarochiri Manuscript exists only because a Spanish extirpator of idolatry commissioned it, and the extirpation trial records are hostile evidence. Guaman Poma gives a native Andean Christian voice writing under colonial pressure.
  • Regional standards: Higashibaba, Christianity in Early Modern Japan (2001); Thornton's Kongo-Christianity scholarship; Standaert (ed.), Handbook of Christianity in China vol. 1 (2001); Mills, Idolatry and Its Enemies (1997) (secondary)
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 6, ch. 27 'The naturalisation of Andean Christianities' (Mills) and ch. 29 'Christianity shaped by the Chinese' (Standaert) (reference) — These two chapters directly cover two of the four regions here (the Andes and China). There's no dedicated chapter on Japan or Kongo; the nearest is chapter 30, by Zupanov and Hsia.
