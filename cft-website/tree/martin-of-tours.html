Martin of Tours

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Gallic Monastic-Ascetic Christianity.

Also called Martinus (the Latin of Sulpicius Severus)

A Roman soldier who became a monk and then bishop of Tours, and who pleaded at Trier that Priscillian's case was not for a judge of this world.

About This Person

Audio Brief 3:35

Read along

In the fourth century, before a battle at Worms, the Caesar Julian was paying each of his soldiers a gift of money. A young soldier named Martin asked for his discharge instead. As his friend Sulpicius Severus reports it, he said: "I am Christ's soldier. I am not allowed to fight."

When Julian called it cowardice, Martin offered to stand unarmed before the battle line, and was put back in prison. The next day the enemy surrendered before any battle, and Sulpicius credits Martin with that peace.

From the army Martin went to Hilary, bishop of Poitiers, in Roman Gaul, in what is now France, and later set up a monastery nearby. Years after that, the people of Tours made him bishop, yet he lived on as a monk, in a wooden cell with eighty disciples who held everything in common. Sulpicius wrote his life while he still lived, a book a modern historian calls a model for later lives of saints.

Cities wanted their priests from Martin's monastery, and Sulpicius saw many of his monks made bishops. Almost all we know comes from that one admirer, who met him late in life. Martin's tomb made Tours a place kings and bishops honored, as Gregory, bishop of Tours almost two hundred years later, tells it.

Martin was born to pagans in what is now Hungary. At ten, against their wish, he asked to be taught for baptism. He was fifteen when his father informed on him, and he was chained and sworn into the army.

One winter, at the gate of Amiens, the cold was killing people. Martin had already given away the rest of his clothes, so with his sword he cut his cloak in two and gave half to a man with no clothes. Sulpicius says the next night Martin saw Christ in a dream, wearing the half he had given, and heard him say Martin, still only a catechumen, had clothed him. He was baptized at about twenty, but stayed nearly two more years in the army, because his tribune promised to leave the world with him.

After the army, Arian opponents had him beaten in public and driven from a city, and expelled him from a monastery he had set up at Milan. At his election, some bishops scorned his poor looks, clothes and hair, yet as bishop he kept that plain dress. He tore down pagan temples and built churches where they had stood. Sometimes his preaching moved people to pull down their own, but once a crowd drove him off and he was hurt.

At Trier, bishops were prosecuting Priscillian and his followers for heresy before the emperor Maximus. Martin did not defend their teaching, but he begged that their blood not be shed. In Sulpicius's report he said: "It was a cruel wrong, never heard of before, for a judge of this world to try a case of the church." The trial waited while he was there, but after he left, Priscillian and his companions were put to death. On a later visit, Martin won the lives of men already condemned by sharing communion for one day with the bishops behind the deaths. He refused to sign his name, grieved over it, and never again went to a gathering of bishops.

Old, and knowing his end was near, he went to Candes, a village of his diocese, to make peace among quarreling clergy. He died there, and at his funeral, it was said, almost two thousand monks gathered.

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Monastic houses in Gaul. Sulpicius Severus tells of a monastery near Poitiers and another two miles outside Tours, with eighty disciples. Edgar Gibson, who translated John Cassian, says Martin and Hilary of Poitiers founded monasteries in the Loire country. He dates Ligugé shortly after 360 and Marmoutier after 371.
  • The Life by Sulpicius Severus, which Ernest Brehaut, the translator of Gregory of Tours, says "became a model for saints' lives." Brehaut calls Gregory's own Miracles of Martin "merely a continuation of it."

How they're remembered

  • In Sulpicius's Dialogues, one of Martin's own monks tells how Martin once took communion with the party of Ithacius to save men condemned to die. He regretted it, and "never again did he attend a synod."
  • Gregory of Tours says the people of Poitiers and of Tours quarreled over his body at Candes, and that the people of Tours carried it home by night on the river.
  • Venantius Fortunatus wrote a Life of Martin in four books, which Friedrich Leo prints with his poems.

Open Questions

  • The main record is the work of Sulpicius Severus, his disciple, and it is a saint's life full of miracles. Sulpicius swears he wrote "nothing of which I had not certain knowledge and evidence." Alexander Roberts, his English translator, says it is "extremely difficult to decide as to the real value of his narrative." Roberts quotes Smith's Dictionary, which calls the Life "filled with the most puerile fables", and grants that many stories are of that kind. Whether all the miracles should be rejected, he says, "must be left to the judgment of the reader."
  • Sulpicius is on Martin's side at Trier. In his Chronicle he calls Ithacius "a bold, loquacious, impudent, and extravagant man." He also says that "the accusers were as distasteful to me as the accused."
  • Roberts's English of the Chronicle says the trial was put off "as long as Martin survived". Halm's Latin says it was put off while Martin was at Trier. The brief follows the Latin. The Dialogues say Martin lived sixteen more years after the communion he regretted.
  • Sulpicius gives no years for Martin's life. The years come from Gregory of Tours, who wrote about two centuries later, and from Brehaut's note. Brehaut dates the Life to about 400.
  • Sulpicius places the cloak at the gate of the city of the Ambiani, which Roberts says "is supposed to be the modern Amiens." He does not name the monastery near Poitiers.
  • Sulpicius was not present at Martin's death and tells it from others. He says Martin fell ill on a visit to the church at Condate, which Roberts thinks is probably Cosne. Gregory of Tours places the illness and death at Candes.
