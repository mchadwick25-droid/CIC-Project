Martin of Tours

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Gallic Monastic-Ascetic Christianity.

Known for
A Roman soldier who became a monk and then bishop of Tours, and who pleaded at Trier that Priscillian's case was not for an emperor to judge.
Also called
Martinus (the Latin of Sulpicius Severus)
Dates
Died in 397, in Ernest Brehaut's note to Gregory of Tours; Gregory says he was born in the eleventh year of Constantine and died in the eighty-first year of his life
Place
Born at Sabaria in Pannonia; raised at Ticinum in Italy; bishop at Tours, in Gaul
Role
Monk and bishop of Tours

About This Person

In a hard winter at the gate of Amiens, a young soldier met a naked beggar. He had only his cloak left to give. Sulpicius Severus says he cut it in two with his sword and gave the beggar half. That night, by Sulpicius's account, Christ appeared to him wearing it. "Martin, who is still but a catechumen, clothed me with this robe."

The soldier was Martin of Tours. He was born at Sabaria in Pannonia to pagan parents and grew up at Ticinum in Italy. The main record is the Life by his disciple Sulpicius, who visited and questioned him. It is a saint's life, full of wonders. Alexander Roberts, its English translator, says it is "extremely difficult to decide as to the real value of his narrative." He grants that many stories are fables, and leaves the rest to the reader.

Sulpicius says Martin asked to leave the army before a battle, as the future emperor Julian paid the troops. "I am the soldier of Christ: it is not lawful for me to fight." He joined Hilary of Poitiers, who made him an exorcist. In Illyria he spoke against the Arians and was publicly beaten. Back in Italy he lived as a monk at Milan until Auxentius, the Arian bishop, drove him out. He then founded a monastery near Poitiers.

The people of Tours made him their bishop. Some bishops objected that he looked contemptible, "that his clothing was mean, and his hair disgusting." He still lived as a monk, two miles outside the city, with eighty disciples who held everything in common.

At Trier the bishops Ydacius and Ithacius had brought Priscillian and his followers before the emperor Maximus on charges of heresy. In his Chronicle, Sulpicius says Martin pressed Ithacius to drop the charge, and begged Maximus to spare the accused. To him it was "a foul and unheard-of indignity, that a secular ruler should be judge in an ecclesiastical cause." While he stayed at Trier, the trial was put off. After he left, Priscillian was put to death.

Sulpicius was not with Martin at the end. Gregory of Tours, writing two centuries later, says he died at Candes in his eighty-first year. His monks begged him not to leave them. Sulpicius writes that Martin, it is said, wept and prayed. "O Lord, if I am still necessary to thy people, I do not shrink from toil: thy will be done."

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Monastic houses in Gaul. Sulpicius Severus tells of a monastery near Poitiers and another two miles outside Tours, with eighty disciples. Edgar Gibson, who translated John Cassian, says Martin and Hilary of Poitiers founded monasteries in the Loire country. He dates Ligugé shortly after 360 and Marmoutier after 371.
  • The Life by Sulpicius Severus, which Ernest Brehaut, the translator of Gregory of Tours, says "became a model for saints' lives." Brehaut calls Gregory's own Miracles of Martin "merely a continuation of it."

How they're remembered

  • In Sulpicius's Dialogues, one of Martin's own monks tells how Martin once took communion with the party of Ithacius to save men condemned to die. He regretted it, and "never again did he attend a synod."
  • Gregory of Tours says the people of Poitiers and of Tours quarrelled over his body at Candes, and that the people of Tours carried it home by night on the river.
  • Venantius Fortunatus wrote a Life of Martin in four books, which Friedrich Leo prints with his poems.

What Is Uncertain

  • The main record is the work of Sulpicius Severus, his disciple, and it is a saint's life full of miracles. Sulpicius swears he wrote "nothing of which I had not certain knowledge and evidence." Alexander Roberts, his English translator, says it is "extremely difficult to decide as to the real value of his narrative." Roberts quotes Smith's Dictionary, which calls the Life "filled with the most puerile fables", and grants that many stories are of that kind. Whether all the miracles should be rejected, he says, "must be left to the judgment of the reader."
  • Sulpicius is on Martin's side at Trier. In his Chronicle he calls Ithacius "a bold, loquacious, impudent, and extravagant man." He also says that "the accusers were as distasteful to me as the accused."
  • Roberts's English of the Chronicle says the trial was put off "as long as Martin survived". Halm's Latin says it was put off while Martin was at Trier. The brief follows the Latin. The Dialogues say Martin lived sixteen more years after the communion he regretted.
  • Sulpicius gives no years for Martin's life. The years come from Gregory of Tours, who wrote about two centuries later, and from Brehaut's note. Brehaut dates the Life to about 400.
  • Sulpicius places the cloak at the gate of the city of the Ambiani, which Roberts says "is supposed to be the modern Amiens." He does not name the monastery near Poitiers.
  • Sulpicius was not present at Martin's death and tells it from others. He says Martin fell ill on a visit to the church at Condate, which Roberts thinks is probably Cosne. Gregory of Tours places the illness and death at Candes.
