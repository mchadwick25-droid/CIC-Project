Martin of Tours

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Gallic Monastic-Ascetic Christianity.

Known for

About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

At the gate of Amiens, in a killing winter, a soldier met a naked beggar. He cut his cloak in two and gave the beggar half, while some onlookers laughed and others groaned, because they had done nothing.

The soldier was Martin, in the fourth century, in Roman Gaul, in what is now France, among its monks and monk-bishops. Almost all we know of him comes from one admirer, Sulpicius Severus, who met him late in life and wrote his story while he still lived.

Born to pagans in Pannonia, in what is now Hungary, Martin asked at ten to be prepared for baptism, but his father had him forced into the army. Sulpicius says that after Amiens, Martin saw Christ in a dream, wearing the half-cloak. He was baptized, but stayed in the army nearly two more years.

When the Caesar Julian handed out money to his troops before a battle, Martin asked for his discharge. "I am Christ's soldier. I am not allowed to fight." Julian called it cowardice, so Martin offered to stand unarmed before the battle line. He was jailed, but the next day the enemy surrendered, a peace Sulpicius saw as Martin's victory.

Out of the army, he founded a monastery near Poitiers, where Hilary was bishop. Later a citizen of Tours lured him out with a story of a sick wife, and crowds brought him in under a kind of guard. As bishop he still lived as a monk, in a wooden cell by the Loire where eighty disciples joined him.

At the emperor Maximus's court in Trier, bishops were prosecuting Priscillian for heresy. Martin did not defend his teaching, but begged that no blood be shed. He said that "it was a cruel wrong, never heard of before, for a judge of this world to try a case of the church." After Martin left, Priscillian and his followers were put to death. To save other lives, he shared communion for one day with the bishops behind those deaths, though he refused to sign it.

A later bishop built a great church over his grave, and even the Frankish king Clovis ordered his army to take only grass and water from Martin's lands.

Many of his monks became bishops, and Sulpicius claims the Life he wrote was read in Rome, Carthage and Egypt. At his funeral, it was said, almost two thousand monks gathered.

At a council in Paris, other bishops sat silent before an angry king. Gregory, bishop of Tours almost two hundred years later, reminded them how Maximus had forced Martin to yield. The judgment of the eternal King, he told them, pursued Maximus until he lost his throne and his life.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Monastic houses in Gaul. Sulpicius Severus tells of a monastery near Poitiers and another two miles outside Tours, with eighty disciples. Edgar Gibson, who translated John Cassian, says Martin and Hilary of Poitiers founded monasteries in the Loire country. He dates Ligugé shortly after 360 and Marmoutier after 371.
  • The Life by Sulpicius Severus, which Ernest Brehaut, the translator of Gregory of Tours, says "became a model for saints' lives." Brehaut calls Gregory's own Miracles of Martin "merely a continuation of it."

How they're remembered

  • In Sulpicius's Dialogues, one of Martin's own monks tells how Martin once took communion with the party of Ithacius to save men condemned to die. He regretted it, and "never again did he attend a synod."
  • Gregory of Tours says the people of Poitiers and of Tours quarreled over his body at Candes, and that the people of Tours carried it home by night on the river.
  • Venantius Fortunatus wrote a Life of Martin in four books, which Friedrich Leo prints with his poems.

What Is Uncertain

  • The main record is the work of Sulpicius Severus, his disciple, and it is a saint's life full of miracles. Sulpicius swears he wrote "nothing of which I had not certain knowledge and evidence." Alexander Roberts, his English translator, says it is "extremely difficult to decide as to the real value of his narrative." Roberts quotes Smith's Dictionary, which calls the Life "filled with the most puerile fables", and grants that many stories are of that kind. Whether all the miracles should be rejected, he says, "must be left to the judgment of the reader."
  • Sulpicius is on Martin's side at Trier. In his Chronicle he calls Ithacius "a bold, loquacious, impudent, and extravagant man." He also says that "the accusers were as distasteful to me as the accused."
  • Roberts's English of the Chronicle says the trial was put off "as long as Martin survived". Halm's Latin says it was put off while Martin was at Trier. The brief follows the Latin. The Dialogues say Martin lived sixteen more years after the communion he regretted.
  • Sulpicius gives no years for Martin's life. The years come from Gregory of Tours, who wrote about two centuries later, and from Brehaut's note. Brehaut dates the Life to about 400.
  • Sulpicius places the cloak at the gate of the city of the Ambiani, which Roberts says "is supposed to be the modern Amiens." He does not name the monastery near Poitiers.
  • Sulpicius was not present at Martin's death and tells it from others. He says Martin fell ill on a visit to the church at Condate, which Roberts thinks is probably Cosne. Gregory of Tours places the illness and death at Candes.
