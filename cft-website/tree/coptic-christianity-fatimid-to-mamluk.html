The High Medieval Era · c. 1054-1300 · Egypt

Coptic Christianity, Fatimid to Mamluk

Egypt, c. 1054 to 1300 — a church losing its own language and answering by writing its whole inheritance down again in Arabic, in what turned out to be its greatest literary century.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:02

Read along

By this period Egypt's Christians had lived under Muslim rule for four centuries, and the shape of their life was set by that: a poll tax, stretches of tolerance under the Fatimids alternating with sharp episodes of persecution, and a slow shift out of Coptic and into Arabic as the everyday language. What they did with Arabic is the surprise. In the thirteenth century a single Cairo family, the sons of al-Assal, produced a body of work — canon law, theology, biblical scholarship, philosophy, and Coptic grammars and dictionaries written in Arabic to keep the old language from being lost entirely — that amounted to a renaissance of Coptic learning in a new tongue. Their patriarch for part of that stretch, Cyril III ibn Laqlaq, was a learned and combative man who spent his reign gathering the church's authority into his own hands.

Documented Stories

The Synod That Wrote Rules for Its Own Patriarch

The Coptic patriarchate had been vacant for nineteen years when Dawud ibn Laqlaq, a monk with wealthy backers, secured election in 1235 by a payment to the Ayyubid sultan, and took the name Cyril III. The History of the Patriarchs, compiled by churchmen who lived through it, describes his reign with unusual candor. He sold episcopal consecrations for fixed sums — the History gives figures — consecrated a metropolitan for Jerusalem against the rights of the Syriac Orthodox and provoked a formal complaint from their patriarch, kept the revenues of vacant sees, and was denounced to the sultan by his own bishops. In 1238 the bishops, backed by the Cairo notables and by the scholar al-Safi ibn al-Assal, forced him to a synod at the citadel in the presence of the sultan’s officers. The result was a code: canons regulating the patriarch’s conduct, forbidding simony, requiring him to consecrate bishops without payment, and setting up a council of bishops and notables to oversee the church’s finances; al-Safi drafted them, and they became the core of the Coptic church’s canon law. He then, the History says, ignored much of it, was suspended by a second synod in 1240, and died in 1243 still in office. The same decade saw the Assal brothers produce the theology, canon law and Coptic grammars that historians call the golden age of Copto-Arabic learning; the golden age and the corrupt patriarch shared a city and a council chamber.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Egypt's Christians lived under a poll tax and alternating tolerance and persecution through the Fatimid period, and slowly shifted from Coptic into Arabic as their everyday language. In the thirteenth century, one Cairo family — the four sons of al-Assal — turned that shift into a renaissance, writing canon law, theology, biblical scholarship and Coptic grammars and dictionaries in Arabic to keep the old language from being lost. Their patriarch for part of that stretch, Cyril III ibn Laqlaq, was a learned and combative man who gathered the church's authority into his own hands.

Legacy

The Coptic Orthodox Church is very much alive. It is the largest Christian community in the Middle East, and it runs unbroken through this period. Some of the work done here may have traveled south. The Fetha Nagast, the law book of the Ethiopian state into the twentieth century, may owe a debt to al-Safi's canons. No source quoted here shows that line. The brothers' grammars and dictionaries are much of the reason the Coptic language is recoverable at all today. The Hanging Church in Old Cairo, the patriarch's seat for most of this period, still stands and is still in use.

Experience It Today
