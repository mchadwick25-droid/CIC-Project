The Imperial Church Era · c. 338-373 · Edessa & Nisibis

Edessa · Nisibis, today in Turkey

Ephrem the Syrian

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Syriac Christianity (Edessa/Nisibis).

Known for
Deacon of Edessa who taught the church's faith in hymns and sermons, and who ran the city's famine relief.
Role
Deacon, hymnwriter and teacher

About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

When famine struck Edessa, Ephrem left his cell and asked the rich why they let people die while their wealth went to ruin. Palladius of Helenopolis tells what came next. The rich said they had no one honest enough to run the relief, so Ephrem asked what they thought of him. They said they knew him for a man of God, and he answered: "See, on your behalf I appoint myself hospitaller."

He had come to Edessa years before, from Nisibis, where he learned from its bishop, Jacob, and lived through three Persian sieges. In 363 the emperor Jovian gave Nisibis to Persia, its Christians left, and Ephrem settled in a cell on a hill outside Edessa.

There he faced teachings that rivaled the church's: Manichaeans, Marcionites, and the followers of Bardaisan, a Syrian who had lived some hundred and fifty years earlier. The church historian Sozomen says Bardaisan's son Harmonius set Greek ideas about the soul and the body's death and rebirth to sweet tunes. Ephrem feared the people would absorb them, so he wrote hymns that held the church's doctrine and used the same melodies. In one hymn he says that to deny the body's resurrection slanders God's justice and grace.

It worked. From that time on, Sozomen says, the Syrians sang the hymns of Ephrem. In Theodoret's day they still brightened the festivals of the martyrs, and some churches read his writings aloud after the Scriptures.

Ephrem ran the relief for a year, with some three hundred beds. When the famine ended and the people went home, he returned to his cell, and a month later, Palladius says, he died there.

Key Works

  • The Nisibene Hymns: Songs from the years of the Persian sieges and the bishops of Nisibis.
  • Prose Refutations of Mani, Marcion and Bardaisan: Prose arguments against three rival teachers.
  • Homily Against Heretics: A metrical homily that names Bardaisan's hymns as poison with sweetness.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The church historian Sozomen names his best-known pupils, Abbas, Zenobius, Abraham, Maras and Simeon. He reports it was said that Ephrem wrote three hundred thousand verses, and says his works were put into Greek in his own lifetime.
  • Sozomen adds that Basil, later bishop of Caesarea, greatly admired him. Jerome read in Greek a volume of his On the Holy Spirit and found the incisive power of lofty genius even in translation.
  • Gregory of Nyssa gave a speech in his praise, the account of his life nearest to his own time.

How they're remembered

  • Honored in the Syriac church with the title malpono, teacher. His hymns shaped its ritual, and lessons from his sermons were appointed to be read in its regular worship.
  • Honored beyond Syria. Sozomen calls him the greatest ornament of the Catholic Church.

What Is Uncertain

  • The later Lives of Ephrem add wonders that the early writers do not tell. They say his father was priest of an idol in Nisibis, and that Ephrem's prayer sent a cloud of gnats against the Persian army. Those stories rest on writers long after him.
  • Palladius of Helenopolis says he died in his cell a month after the relief ended, while Sozomen says after a few days. Most writers date his death to 373, and some to 378.
  • Sozomen tells that when men came to carry him off to be a bishop, he ran into the market-place, dragged his clothes and ate in public, until they took him for mad and left. He stayed a deacon all his life.
  • Sozomen and Theodoret blame Harmonius, Bardaisan's son, for the sweet tunes. In his own homily Ephrem blames Bardaisan alone.
