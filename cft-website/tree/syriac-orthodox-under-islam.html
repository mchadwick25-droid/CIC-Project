Syriac Orthodox Christianity under Islam

The Jazira, Syria and Tagrit, c. 650 to 1054 — a church that had been the empire's losing side, and found under the caliphs the room to write its own grammar, chronicles and Bible.

Syriac Orthodox (West Syriac) Christianity→Syriac Orthodox Christianity under Islam→The Syriac Renaissance

About This Tradition

For the West Syriac church the Arab conquest lifted a weight. They had been the losing side under Byzantine emperors who wanted Chalcedon enforced, and under the caliphs they became simply one protected community among others — taxed, but not pressed to change their confession. The centuries that followed were unusually productive. Jacob of Edessa corrected the Syriac Old Testament and, by one later report, wrote the first grammar of his own language. Dionysius of Tel Mahre, patriarch and historian, traveled to Egypt in the caliph al-Ma'mun's own company and wrote a chronicle that later writers mined for centuries; Moses bar Kepha explained the liturgy and the Scriptures for his clergy. The carriers of all this were the monasteries — Mor Gabriel and the other houses of the Tur Abdin plateau, and the Syrian monastery in the Egyptian desert, where monks assembled a library that is now one of the world's great collections of early Syriac manuscripts.

Documented Stories

Jacob of Edessa Burns the Canons at the Patriarch’s Door

Jacob came from ‘Ayndaba, a village in the country of Antioch. He studied Greek at the monastery of Kenneshre and gathered learning in Alexandria. The patriarch Athanasius of Balad made him bishop of Edessa; Bar Hebraeus’s editors put that about 685. After four years he was in trouble with clergy he had barred for their disorders. Before he left his see, Michael the Syrian says, he argued with the patriarch Julian and the bishops over keeping the church’s canons. No one listened, and all told him to bend to the times. So he brought the book of canons and burned it at the door of the patriarch’s monastery. He cried aloud that he was burning, as useless, the canons they trampled underfoot and did not keep. Then he gave up his see and withdrew to the monastery of Mar Jacob at Kaisum. There he wrote two tracts of reproach, one against the church’s shepherds and one against those who break its canons. For eleven years the monks of Eusebona had him teach Greek, whose study had died out there. Brothers who hated the Greeks made trouble for him, so he moved to the great monastery of Tell ‘Ada. There he spent nine years correcting the Old Testament. In about 701 he also revised the Syriac of Severus of Antioch’s sermons against the Greek. When the next bishop of Edessa died, the city called him back. He held the see four months, then went to fetch his books and pupils, and died at Tell ‘Ada. Bar Hebraeus dates his death to 5 June 708; Michael’s manuscript gives a year four years earlier, and Joseph Assemani gives 710. His canons live on in part, quoted under his name in Bar Hebraeus’s book of church law. The story is the record of a scholar who expected the church’s law to mean what it said, and of a church that let him go rather than agree.

Dionysius Tells the Caliph the Rebels Are Being Crushed

Dionysius of Tel Mahre had been a monk of Kenneshre. In 818, by his own account, two monks seized him and held him like a criminal until the bishops came, and he was made patriarch against his tears and protests. He learned to deal with the Abbasid court. In 829 the caliph al-Ma’mun let him come to him alone in his garden, and there ruled that the church could depose a sinful bishop, though it could not shut him out of prayer. Dionysius wrote a history of his own times, which survives only in passages that later writers kept, and Bar Hebraeus tells most of his travels. When al-Ma’mun went to Egypt, Bar Hebraeus says, he ordered the patriarch to go with him, meaning to send him as envoy to the Christians called Byamites, in Lower Egypt, to turn them from rebellion. Dionysius went with Joseph, the patriarch of Egypt, to reconcile them. But Afshin, the general, would have no peace. He burned their villages, vineyards and gardens and killed a great many; the rest he sent in chains by ship to Antioch and on to Baghdad, and many died on the way. Dionysius went back to al-Ma’mun, told him that the Byamites were being crushed, and then took his leave for Damascus. Bar Hebraeus also quotes what Dionysius found in Egypt. The Copts, he wrote, were a chaste and sincere people, who gave him every honor due to their own pope, but the study of Scripture had been taken from them, and no one reached the office of bishop without two or three hundred gold coins. He saw the pyramids and judged them not Joseph’s granaries, as some thought, but the tombs of ancient kings, solid and not hollow. Bar Hebraeus keeps only a few passages in Dionysius’s own words. The rest of the journey is Bar Hebraeus’s telling.

In Its Own Record

The West Syriac church, living as a protected people under the caliphs. Its patriarch, the historian Dionysius of Tel Mahre, went on a journey with the caliph al-Ma'mun. Its monks kept its learning alive.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Having been the losing side under Byzantine emperors who wanted Chalcedon enforced, the West Syriac church found under the caliphs the room to become one protected community among others, and used it productively: Jacob of Edessa corrected the Syriac Old Testament and, by one later report, wrote the first grammar of his own language, while the patriarch Dionysius of Tel Mahre traveled to Egypt in the caliph al-Ma'mun's own company and wrote a chronicle later writers drew on for centuries. Its monasteries, from Mor Gabriel in the Tur Abdin to the Syrian monastery in the Egyptian desert, preserved a manuscript culture that is now one of the world's great collections of early Syriac literature.

Legacy

The same church continued directly into the Syriac Renaissance of the following centuries and exists today as the Syriac Orthodox Church of Antioch, with its own patriarch and a large worldwide diaspora. Its monasteries preserved what would otherwise be lost. The manuscripts gathered at the Syrian monastery in the Egyptian desert are the backbone of the world's collections of early Syriac literature, including some of the oldest surviving Syriac gospel books. Mor Gabriel in the Tur Abdin, founded in 397, is still a working monastery and the seat of a bishop.

Experience It Today

  • Mor Gabriel Monastery, Tur Abdin, south-eastern Turkey — founded in 397 and still inhabited, the oldest surviving Syriac Orthodox monastery

On the Nicene Creed

This church does not accept the Council of Chalcedon's formula of 451 and belongs to the family usually called miaphysite, or Oriental Orthodox. The disagreement is about how to describe the joining of divinity and humanity in Christ, not about the Nicene Creed, which it confesses in full.
