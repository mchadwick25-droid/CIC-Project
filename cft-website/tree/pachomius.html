The Imperial Church Era · c. 292-348 · Tabennisi

Pachomius

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Desert Monasticism.

Also called Pachomius of Tabennisi

The Egyptian monk whom Gennadius of Marseilles calls "founder of the Egyptian monasteries", who bound monks to a common rule in shared houses.

Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition→Desert Monasticism→no descendant recorded

About This Person

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 2:52

Read along

In the Greek Life of Pachomius, by a monk of the next generation, the great hermit Antony speaks to Pachomius's monks after his death. He tells them: "When I first began to live as a monk, there was no community anywhere in which anyone cared or feared for the salvation of others. Once the persecution was over, each of the early monks trained himself in the monk's life on his own. Later, with God's help, your father did this great good."

Before him, in Upper Egypt, each monk lived by his own way, a hermit in a cell, sometimes with a pupil. Pachomius drew such monks into one shared life under one written rule. His brothers lived in houses inside a walled monastery, each house under a head. They wore the same clothes, ate at one table and worked at their trades together. Nine monasteries came to form one fellowship.

Born to pagan parents, he was taken at about twenty for Constantine's war, early in the fourth century. Locked up, he asked who the strangers feeding the recruits were. His own communities' Coptic Life gives the answer: "They are Christian people. They treat us this way out of love for the God of heaven." That night he vowed to serve people all his life. Freed and baptized, he became a hermit's pupil, until at a deserted village called Tabennisi he began to build.

He would have no priests among his monks, and when a bishop wanted him ordained, he hid. At Esneh, monks and bishops questioned him over claims that he read hidden thoughts. As he finished, a man driven by an evil spirit burst in with a sword to kill him, but the Greek Life says the Lord saved him with the help of those present.

In 346 or 348, as plague swept the monasteries, he fell ill and asked for a lighter blanket. When he saw it was finer than the brothers' own, he sent it back: "Take this blanket away from here at once. It is not right that I should be better off in anything than the other brothers." After his death the fellowship split, though Theodore's letters urged the breakaways back.

Palladius, writing about seventy years later, counted some seven thousand monks under the rule. Latin-speaking monks there could not read its Egyptian or Greek, so priests sent Jerome the books, pressing him. About 404 he dictated a Latin version for them, and for his own brothers and Eustochium's nuns. Through that Latin the rule reached the West. In the early ninth century the abbot Benedict of Aniane set it under the chapters of Benedict's Rule, as one of the old rules it agreed with.

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Communal monasteries bound by a shared rule, which Palladius counts at seven thousand monks under the rule in his own day.
  • A rule for women's houses, which Palladius and the Ethiopic version both record living across the river from the men.

How they're remembered

  • George Schodde, translating the Ethiopic rules in 1885, opens by calling Pachomius "the father of monastic institutions."
  • Gennadius of Marseilles, continuing Jerome's On Illustrious Men, calls him "a man endowed with apostolic grace both in teaching and in performing miracles."

Open Questions

  • No writing held here is certainly his own. George Schodde thinks the first part of the Ethiopic rules comes close to him: "if not verbatim from his pen, are yet authentic enough for all practical purposes." His life and rule otherwise reach us through his Coptic and Greek Lives, through Palladius of Helenopolis, who tells them as a holy man's story, and through Sozomen, who follows Palladius.
  • Palladius and Sozomen say an angel gave Pachomius the rule on a tablet, and Gennadius says the rule was "received by angelic dictation." Lowther Clarke, who translated Palladius, reports that the scholar Ladeuze held the Greek Lives of Pachomius more reliable than the rule, thought the angel legendary, and took the lettered groups for Palladius's own invention. Butler, another scholar Clarke cites, did not accept Ladeuze's low view of Palladius's rule and called it "probably the most authentic epitome."
  • Palladius places Pachomius's own house at Tabennisi. Clarke notes that the head house was by then Pabau, not Tabennisi.
  • The rule survives in several collections that differ from one another. Schodde says the first part of the Ethiopic version is the same as the Greek form, and judged it reaches back "almost to the days of Pachomius himself," while its later parts are of different origin.
