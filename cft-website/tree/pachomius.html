The Imperial Church Era · c. 292-348 · Tabennisi

Pachomius

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Desert Monasticism.

Known for
The Egyptian monk whom Gennadius of Marseilles calls "founder of the Egyptian monasteries", who bound monks to a common rule in shared houses.
Also called
Pachomius of Tabennisi
Dates
Born about 292, died 348 (George Schodde; Ernest Cushing Richardson's note to Gennadius) or 346 (Archibald Robertson)
Place
Tabennisi, in the Thebaid of Upper Egypt
Role
Monk and founder of communal monasticism

About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

One night on the upper Nile, strangers brought food to young men locked in a prison, conscripts for Constantine's war. Pachomius, about twenty and the son of pagan parents, asked who they were. His own communities' Coptic Life gives the answer: "They are Christian people. They treat us this way out of love for the God of heaven." That night, both his oldest Lives say, he vowed to serve people all his life.

The persecution was over, early in the fourth century. Once released, he was baptized and nursed villagers through an epidemic. Then a hermit, Palamon, tested him with an older rule: night prayer, hard work and daily fasting. The monks of Egypt already had a way of life.

At a deserted village called Tabennisi, a voice told him to build, for many would come. The Coptic Life says an angel called him three times: "Pachomius, Pachomius, the will of God is that you serve the human race and unite them to Him." Palladius, seventy years later, adds a brass tablet bearing the rule; the Coptic Life has none.

At the start he did all the work for his first three brothers and nursed them himself. Then he gave them rules: the same clothes and food for all, and a steward over each house. He would have no priests among his monks, and when a bishop urged Athanasius to ordain him, Pachomius hid.

When his sister Mary came, he did not see her, but had a house built for her and sent the women who joined her the brothers' rules. The Coptic Life puts their house in the village, Palladius across the river.

At Esneh, as monks and bishops questioned him in church about reading hidden thoughts, a man with a sword burst in to kill him; the Greek Life says the Lord saved him.

He died in 346 or 348, during a plague that killed a hundred or more across the monasteries. On his sickbed he sent back a better blanket: "Take this blanket away from here at once. It is not right that I should be better off in anything than the other brothers."

Petronius, his chosen heir, died within days, and Horsiesius and Theodore led on. The fellowship split, and Theodore's letters urged the breakaways back. Palladius, visiting Egypt, counts about seven thousand monks there under the rule, and Cassian drew on "the monks of Tabenna". More than fifty years after Pachomius died, Latin-speaking monks in those houses could not read the rule.

A priest sent Jerome books that had come from Alexandria, and he dictated a Latin version from the Greek, for them and for his own brothers and Eustochium's nuns.

Through that Latin the rule went west. The abbot Benedict of Aniane set passages from it and other old rules under the chapters of Benedict's Rule, to show that Rule stood on theirs.

Key Works

  • The Rule of Pachomius: the common life of the houses, reported by Palladius and Sozomen and surviving in several later collections, among them an Ethiopic version

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Communal monasteries bound by a shared rule, which Palladius counts at seven thousand monks under the rule in his own day.
  • A rule for women's houses, which Palladius and the Ethiopic version both record living across the river from the men.

How they're remembered

  • George Schodde, translating the Ethiopic rules in 1885, opens by calling Pachomius "the father of monastic institutions."
  • Gennadius of Marseilles, continuing Jerome's On Illustrious Men, calls him "a man endowed with apostolic grace both in teaching and in performing miracles."

What Is Uncertain

  • No writing held here is certainly his own. George Schodde thinks the first part of the Ethiopic rules comes close to him: "if not verbatim from his pen, are yet authentic enough for all practical purposes." His life and rule otherwise reach us through his Coptic and Greek Lives, through Palladius of Helenopolis, who tells them as a holy man's story, and through Sozomen, who follows Palladius.
  • Palladius and Sozomen say an angel gave Pachomius the rule on a tablet, and Gennadius says the rule was "received by angelic dictation." Lowther Clarke, who translated Palladius, reports that the scholar Ladeuze held the Greek Lives of Pachomius more reliable than the rule, thought the angel legendary, and took the lettered groups for Palladius's own invention. Butler, another scholar Clarke cites, did not accept Ladeuze's low view of Palladius's rule and called it "probably the most authentic epitome."
  • Palladius places Pachomius's own house at Tabennisi. Clarke notes that the head house was by then Pabau, not Tabennisi.
  • The rule survives in several collections that differ from one another. Schodde says the first part of the Ethiopic version is the same as the Greek form, and judged it reaches back "almost to the days of Pachomius himself," while its later parts are of different origin.
