Pachomius

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Desert Monasticism.

Known for
The Egyptian monk whom Gennadius of Marseilles calls "founder of the Egyptian monasteries", who bound monks to a common rule in shared houses.
Also called
Pachomius of Tabennisi
Dates

Place

About This Person

Palladius of Helenopolis tells of a stranger who came to the Tabennesiot monastery, in Upper Egypt, dressed as a workman. Its head, Pachomius, said he was too old for the life and would not take him in. After seven days of waiting and fasting, he was let in. Through Lent he stood in a corner weaving palm leaves, and he ate a few cabbage leaves on Sundays. The brothers told Pachomius, "Either drive him out, or know that we are all going." Pachomius prayed and learned that the stranger was Macarius of Alexandria, a famous monk. "I thank you for letting my children feel your fist," he told him, "lest they should be proud of their ascetic achievements."

Pachomius, an Egyptian monk of the fourth century, first lived as a hermit in a cave. Palladius, and the church historian Sozomen after him, say an angel told him to gather young monks. The angel gave him a tablet with a rule written on it. Palladius tells it as the story of a holy man.

By Palladius's account, each monk ate and fasted as his strength allowed, and those who ate more did the heavier work. Monks lived three to a cell and ate together in silence, hoods over their heads. They were sorted into twenty-four groups, each named by a Greek letter. When Pachomius said the prayers were few, the angel answered that the rule was made so "that even the little ones keep the rule and are not afflicted."

Lowther Clarke, who translated Palladius, reports that the scholar Ladeuze trusted the Greek Lives of Pachomius over this rule. Ladeuze thought the angel legendary and the letter groups Palladius's own invention. Butler, another scholar Clarke cites, did not accept Ladeuze's low view, and called Palladius's version "probably the most authentic epitome."

Palladius counts seven thousand monks under the rule. At Panopolis he found three hundred monks working at every craft, and their surplus fed the women's houses and the prisons. The women lived by the same rule across the river.

An Ethiopic version also survives. George Schodde, who translated it in 1885, says its first part follows the Greek, and judged that it reaches back "almost to the days of Pachomius himself." Another part, of different origin, says women gathered around Pachomius's own sister, in a house he made for her.

Schodde's introduction opens by calling Pachomius "the father of monastic institutions."

Key Works

  • The Rule of Pachomius: the common life of the houses, reported by Palladius and Sozomen and surviving in several later collections, among them an Ethiopic version

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Communal monasteries bound by a shared rule, which Palladius counts at seven thousand monks under the rule in his own day.
  • A rule for women's houses, which Palladius and the Ethiopic version both record living across the river from the men.

How they're remembered

  • George Schodde, translating the Ethiopic rules in 1885, opens by calling Pachomius "the father of monastic institutions."
  • Gennadius of Marseilles, continuing Jerome's On Illustrious Men, calls him "a man endowed with apostolic grace both in teaching and in performing miracles."

What Is Uncertain

  • No writing held here is certainly his own. George Schodde thinks the first part of the Ethiopic rules comes close to him: "if not verbatim from his pen, are yet authentic enough for all practical purposes." His life and rule otherwise reach us through Palladius of Helenopolis, who tells them as a holy man's story, and through Sozomen, who follows Palladius.
  • Palladius and Sozomen say an angel gave Pachomius the rule on a tablet, and Gennadius says the rule was "received by angelic dictation." Lowther Clarke, who translated Palladius, reports that the scholar Ladeuze held the Greek Lives of Pachomius more reliable than the rule, thought the angel legendary, and took the lettered groups for Palladius's own invention. Butler, another scholar Clarke cites, did not accept Ladeuze's low view of Palladius's rule and called it "probably the most authentic epitome."
  • Palladius places Pachomius's own house at Tabennisi. Clarke notes that the head house was by then Pabau, not Tabennisi.
  • The rule survives in several collections that differ from one another. Schodde says the first part of the Ethiopic version is the same as the Greek form, and judged it reaches back "almost to the days of Pachomius himself," while its later parts are of different origin.
