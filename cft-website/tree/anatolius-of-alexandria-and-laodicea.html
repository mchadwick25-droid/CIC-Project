Anatolius of Alexandria and Laodicea

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition.

Known for
A learned Alexandrian who led people out of a besieged quarter of Alexandria, became bishop of Laodicea in Syria, and wrote on how to fix the date of Easter.
Also called
Anatolius of Alexandria, Anatolius of Laodicea
Dates
Made bishop of Laodicea on his way to the synod at Antioch on Paul of Samosata; Jerome of Strido says he flourished under the emperors Probus and Carus
Place
Alexandria; Caesarea in Palestine; Laodicea in Syria
Role
Scholar, then bishop of Laodicea

About This Person

A quarter of Alexandria called the Pyrucheium was under siege by a Roman army, and its bread had run out. Eusebius of Caesarea tells the story as others told it to him. Anatolius, a learned Christian, was inside. His friend, a deacon also named Eusebius, was in the part of the city that sided with Rome. The deacon won the Roman commander's promise to spare anyone who came over. Anatolius asked the city's senate to make peace with Rome, and they were angry. So he asked only that old women, children and old men be sent out. They agreed. Church members went first, then others of every age, many "secretly clothed in women's garments." By night they escaped to the Roman camp.

Anatolius of Alexandria was born in that city. In arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, logic and physics, Eusebius says, he "stood first among the ablest men of our time."

After the war Anatolius left Egypt. At Caesarea in Palestine the bishop, Theotecnus, ordained him a bishop, meaning him to succeed. Then a synod at Antioch, held over its bishop, Paul of Samosata, summoned him. On the way he passed through Laodicea in Syria, where his friend the deacon had been bishop and had died. The Christians there kept him.

"Anatolius did not write very many works," Eusebius says. He quotes his rules for Easter, which count the years in cycles of nineteen. Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius, calls him the first Christian to use that cycle for Easter, "so far as we know."

Bede, a priest of Wearmouth and Jarrow in England, tells of a synod at Whitby in 664. Bishop Colman, with his Scottish clergy, argued over Easter with the priest Wilfrid, and both named Anatolius. Colman called him "a holy man, and much commended in church history." Wilfrid agreed he was "a most holy, learned, and commendable man." But Colman's side, he said, did not keep his cycle of nineteen years.

Key Works

  • Paschal Canons: his rules for fixing Easter by a cycle of nineteen years; Eusebius of Caesarea quotes a long extract
  • Institutes of Arithmetic: ten books, of which a few fragments survive

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius, calls him the first Christian to use the nineteen-year cycle to find Easter, "so far as we know."

How they're remembered

  • Eusebius says he "stood first among the ablest men of our time" in arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, logic and physics.
  • Jerome of Strido calls him a man of wonderful learning.
  • At the synod at Whitby in 664, Bede tells, both sides cited him. Colman called him "a holy man, and much commended in church history." Wilfrid called him "a most holy, learned, and commendable man", but said Colman's side did not keep his cycle.

What Is Uncertain

  • Eusebius tells the story of the siege as others told it to him ("They say").
  • McGiffert thinks the siege was most likely the one during the revolt of Aemilian, in 260. Valesius and others place it later, under Claudius.
  • Eusebius reports that the Alexandrians asked him to found a school of Aristotle's philosophy. He gives it as a report, and does not say Anatolius held any church office in Alexandria, McGiffert notes.
  • A longer Latin book on Easter, first printed in 1634, carries his name. Ideler held it to be a work of the seventh century. McGiffert, following Zahn, thinks there are "the best of reasons" for taking it as an early translation of his genuine work.
  • No held source gives the year of his birth or death. Ernest Cushing Richardson, in a note to his translation of Jerome, gives born about 230, bishop 270, died about 283, and names no source. S. D. F. Salmond, who translated his works, calls the dates 230, 270 and 280 conjectural.
  • Eusebius lists arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, logic and physics among his subjects. Jerome has grammar in place of physics.
