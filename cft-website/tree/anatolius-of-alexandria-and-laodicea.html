Anatolius of Alexandria and Laodicea

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition.

Also called Anatolius of Alexandria, Anatolius of Laodicea

Anatolius, a learned Christian of Alexandria, used a vote of the city council to get almost all the besieged out of the palace quarter of the city. He later became bishop of Laodicea in Syria and wrote a rule for finding the date of Easter, and centuries later both sides of a quarrel over Easter claimed him.

About This Person

In a besieged quarter of Alexandria, the bread had run out, and hunger was harder to bear than the enemy outside. Anatolius, a learned Christian, stood before the city council. "I advise sending out of the gates those we have too many of, who are of no use to us at all: old women, infants and old men."

Anatolius used the council's vote to slip out almost all the besieged. He later became bishop of Laodicea in Syria. He also wrote a rule for finding Easter, and three and four centuries on, Irish churchmen and the defenders of Rome's Easter both claimed him.

Born in Alexandria, he lived in the later 200s. Eusebius, the church historian, ranked him first among the men of his time in arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, logic, physics and rhetoric, and reports that the citizens asked him to found a school of Aristotle's philosophy. Of the siege, Eusebius tells one of his deeds, as others told it to him.

The palace quarter held out against a Roman army, while the rest of the city sided with Rome. Anatolius sent word to a deacon of the church in the free part of the city. The deacon, known to the Roman general, won from him safety for any who deserted the besieged side.

Anatolius called the council and urged peace with Rome, but they grew angry. He argued instead that the city should feed only men and youths. Rising first, he voted that all not needed for the fighting, men or women, should leave, since staying meant starving, and the council agreed.

Under cover of that decree he saved almost all the besieged: the church's people first, and with them many more beyond it, men among them secretly dressed in women's clothes. By night they went out to the Roman camp, where the deacon took them in "like a father and a doctor" and nursed them.

After the war, the bishop of Caesarea in Palestine made him a second bishop there, to take over at his death. Then bishops meeting at Antioch called him there, to judge its own bishop, Paul of Samosata, who, Eusebius says, taught that Christ was by nature an ordinary man. On the way, at Laodicea, whose bishop, the same deacon, had died, the believers kept him as bishop.

Eusebius kept part of his Greek book on Easter. Easter's date was tied to the Jewish Passover full moon, the fourteenth day of the first month of spring. That month, his rule ran, must not be counted from a new moon before the spring equinox, in March, when day and night are equal. Those who begin it earlier, he wrote, "make no small mistake, and no careless one." He set his count on a nineteen-year table of the spring new moons, which then begins again. The rule was not his own, he said, since the Jewish people kept it before Christ.

About 600, Columbanus, an Irish abbot, wrote to Pope Gregory the Great to defend his homeland's Easter against an Easter past the moon's twentieth day. He drew on a Latin Easter book under Anatolius's name. Such a moon, he argued, rises after midnight, so darkness rules most of the night, but the feast of the resurrection is a feast of light.

In 664 the quarrel came before a church meeting at Whitby, in Abbess Hild's monastery in northern England, as Bede, writing in 731, tells it. Colman, for the Irish side, said Anatolius, a holy man, had written that Easter could fall any day from the moon's fourteenth to its twentieth. Wilfrid, for Rome's side, claimed him for a different rule: "It is agreed that Anatolius was a most holy man, most learned and most worthy of praise." Anatolius, he said, had set out a cycle of nineteen years, which the whole church of Christ kept.

Bede doubted the Irish claim. The northern Irish and Picts before Whitby, he wrote, believed they followed the writings of the holy and praiseworthy Father Anatolius, and anyone skilled could very easily tell whether that was true.

Key Works

  • Paschal Canons: his writings on finding Easter. Eusebius says that in his works he gave his views on the Passover in particular, and quotes a long extract. In it, the first month, whose fourteenth day is the Passover, must not be counted from a new moon before the spring equinox. The count runs on a cycle of nineteen years, and the rule, he says, was known to the Jewish people before Christ
  • Institutes of Arithmetic: ten books on arithmetic, which Eusebius and Jerome name. The Library does not hold them
  • The Paschal Canon, in Latin: a longer Latin book on Easter under his name, which allows the feast to be kept up to the moon's twentieth day. Columbanus, writing to Pope Gregory the Great, quoted words that stand in it as the words of Anatolius. Whether Anatolius wrote it is disputed

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Columbanus, an Irish abbot, wrote to Pope Gregory the Great against an Easter on the moon's 21st or 22nd day. He cited Anatolius, and quoted words from the Latin Easter book under his name. The words say that if the moon rises only at midnight, darkness rules over the light, and the feast of the Lord's resurrection is light.
  • To a synod of bishops in Gaul, Columbanus wrote that he trusted the tradition of his homeland, and Anatolius, over Victorius, who had written lately and in doubt.
  • Bede wrote that the northern Irish and the Picts kept Easter from the moon's fourteenth to its twentieth day, believing they followed the writings of Anatolius. Anyone skilled, he added, could very easily tell whether that was true.
  • At the synod at Whitby in 664, Colman, for the Irish side, said Anatolius had written that Easter was to be kept from the fourteenth to the twentieth. Wilfrid, for Rome's side, answered that Anatolius had set out a cycle of nineteen years, which the whole church of Christ kept.
  • Bede lists among his own works a letter on the leap year and the equinox, according to Anatolius.
  • Elias of Nisibis cites the words of Anatolius, bishop of Laodicea, to show that the spring equinox fell on the 22nd of Adar in his time. That was two days earlier, Elias says, than in Christ's time.

How they're remembered

  • Eusebius says he "stood first among the ablest men of our time" in arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, logic and physics, and was at the head in rhetoric.
  • Jerome calls him a man of wonderful learning, and says the greatness of his genius can be seen from his book on the Passover and his ten books on arithmetic.
  • In a letter defending Christian writers' use of pagan learning, Jerome names Anatolius, chief priest of the church of Laodicea, among those whose books weave in the teachings of the philosophers.
  • Jerome's Chronicle, under the emperor Probus, says that Anatolius, bishop of Laodicea, learned in the teachings of the philosophers, was much spoken of. Bede's Greater Chronicle repeats it.
  • At Whitby, Colman called him "a holy man, and much commended in church history." Wilfrid called him "a most holy, learned, and commendable man", but said Colman's side did not keep his rules.
  • Barhadbeshabba lists Anatolius, bishop of Laodicea, among the teachers who lived in the land of the Romans. Michael the Syrian says he followed Eusebius at Laodicea and was famous.
  • Bar Hebraeus calls Eusebius, bishop of Laodicea, and Anatolius, both Alexandrians, true leaders in sacred and secular learning.

Open Questions

  • The siege rests on report. Eusebius tells it as others told it to him ("They relate", "They say"), and gives no cause for the siege and no year.
  • The cause of the war. Eusebius says only that the rest of the city was allied with the Roman army. Rufinus calls it a war that broke out among the citizens of Alexandria. Jerome's Chronicle, under the first year of the emperor Claudius, says the palace quarter, besieged for many years, was at last destroyed. No old source says this is the siege Eusebius tells.
  • Rufinus, in his Latin version of Eusebius a century later, tells the siege in other terms. He has Anatolius in the part of the city loyal to Rome, going to the Roman commander himself and receiving the refugees with a parent's and a doctor's care. His speech asks only that all unfit for war be let out of the gates, and Rufinus adds that he freed the city from famine and war alike. Eusebius gives the message and the welcome to the deacon outside the lines.
  • The school. Eusebius gives only the report that the citizens asked him to found a school of Aristotle's philosophy. Rufinus says that he was trained from boyhood, so that all the learned men and philosophers of Alexandria made him teach logic. Neither says he held a church office there.
  • His subjects. Eusebius lists arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, logic, physics and rhetoric. Jerome has grammar in place of physics.
  • The years. No held old source gives a year for his birth, his becoming bishop or his death, or ties a year to the siege he lived through. Jerome says he flourished under Probus and Carus, and his Chronicle names him under Probus. Elias of Nisibis says he lived in the time of the emperor Gallus. Modern editors give years of their own reckoning, which differ.
  • The synod. Eusebius says only that the synod on Paul's case called him to Antioch and that the believers at Laodicea kept him as he passed through. No old source says whether he ever reached the synod. That Paul taught that Christ was by nature an ordinary man is the charge Eusebius gives.
  • His death. No held old source tells how, where or when he died. Eusebius says only that after him Stephen was the last bishop of Laodicea before the persecution.
  • The Easter book. No old source says where or when he wrote it. Eusebius quotes only part of it.
  • The nineteen years. Wilfrid credits Anatolius with setting out a cycle of nineteen years. Abbot Ceolfrid's letter, which Bede copies, says the nineteen-year cycle began in the time of the apostles, at Rome and in Egypt, and does not name him.
  • The Latin book. A longer Latin book on Easter carries his name. It was first printed in 1634. Whether he wrote it, no old source says, and modern scholars disagree: some take it for an early translation of his own work, others for a later forgery.
  • What his days of the moon meant. Colman says Anatolius wrote that Easter was to be kept from the fourteenth to the twentieth. The Latin book under his name allows the celebration to be extended to the moon's twentieth. Wilfrid says Anatolius counted in the Egyptian way, so that his fourteenth was the fifteenth by evening and his twentieth the twenty-first. He adds that Colman's side sometimes kept Easter before the full moon. Bede says a skilled reader can tell whether the Irish truly followed him. No old source says which text either man drew on.
  • A Syriac chronicle, in its Latin version, calls him a teacher at Laodicea after Eusebius, an Alexandrian by birth. It says he taught that the months begin on the eighteenth of Adar, and that the count comes round again after nineteen years. The extract Eusebius quotes puts the new moon at the head of the cycle on the 26th of the Egyptian month Phamenoth, the 22nd of the Macedonian month Dystrus.
  • Socrates, writing against the Easter of Sabbatius, gives the rule that the ancient Hebrews sacrificed after the spring equinox. He cites Eusebius, and Philo, Josephus and Aristobulus, writers the extract from Anatolius names, but does not name Anatolius.
  • The books on arithmetic. The Ante-Nicene Fathers prints fragments of books on arithmetic under his name, taken from a modern collection. That they are his rests on modern editors.
