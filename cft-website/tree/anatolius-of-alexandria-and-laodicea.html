Anatolius of Alexandria and Laodicea

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition.

Also called Anatolius of Alexandria, Anatolius of Laodicea

A learned Alexandrian who led people out of a besieged quarter of Alexandria, became bishop of Laodicea in Syria, and wrote on how to fix the date of Easter.

About This Person

Audio Brief 4:17

Read along

In a besieged quarter of Alexandria, the bread had run out. A Christian, Anatolius of Alexandria and Laodicea, spoke to the city council. "I advise you to send out of the gates those who are surplus, of no use to us at all: old women, small children and old men. Let them walk wherever they wish. Why keep them here for nothing, when they will soon be dead?" Yet the people he called useless were among those he got out: almost all the starving, his own church first.

Later he was kept as bishop of Laodicea, in Syria, and he is the first Christian known to reckon Easter on a table that repeats every nineteen years. Almost four centuries later, in England, both sides of a quarrel over Easter claimed him.

Eusebius, the church historian, ranked him first among the most learned men of his time. Writing a generation later, he tells the siege as something people said.

It came in the 260s or early 270s, though the sources disagree on the year. The palace quarter of the city held out against a Roman army. When bread failed, Anatolius sent word to a deacon outside the lines, who won from the Roman general a promise to spare anyone who came over.

Anatolius first urged the council to make peace with Rome, which angered its members, then made the proposal heard at the start. He voted first that all not needed for the fighting should leave, and all agreed. Under cover of that decree, Eusebius says, the church's members went out first, then others, and with them a multitude more beyond the decree, secretly dressed in women's clothes. By night they reached the Roman camp, where the deacon nursed them back to health.

After the war he left Alexandria. At Caesarea in Palestine, its bishop made him a bishop beside himself, meaning him to take over when he died. Then he was called to Antioch, to a meeting of bishops judging its own bishop, Paul of Samosata, for his teaching about Christ. On the way he passed through Laodicea, a port on the coast of Syria. Its bishop, that same deacon, had died, and around 268 to 270 the believers kept Anatolius in his place.

Easter's date hangs on the Passover moon, the full moon on the fourteenth day of the first spring month. Anatolius wrote that this month must not begin before the spring equinox, the day in March when day and night are equal. To begin it earlier, he said, is no small mistake. He added that the Jews kept this rule even before Christ, and cited their writers for it. He set the rule on a table of nineteen years, after which the moon's phases fall on the same days again. The editors say the cycle was soon taken up at Alexandria, though changed first.

In 664 the Easter quarrel came to Hild's monastery at Whitby. In Bede's account, written two generations later, Colman, the bishop who led the Irish side, claimed Anatolius for keeping Easter from the fourteenth day of the moon to the twentieth. That rule is found in a Latin book under his name. Wilfrid, a priest who spoke for Rome's side, answered: "It is agreed that Anatolius was a most holy and most learned man, most worthy of praise. But what have you to do with him, when you do not even keep his rules? For he followed the rule of truth in his Easter, and set down a cycle of nineteen years. Either you do not know it, or you know it and treat it as nothing, though the whole church of Christ keeps it."

That book may not be his. An American scholar who translated Eusebius takes it as an early translation of his work; a German historian, as a forgery made in the sixth century or earlier. The king ruled for Rome.

Of the man who sent the starving out of Alexandria by night, no early writer tells how he died. Before the Great Persecution, another man led his church.

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius, calls him the first Christian to use the nineteen-year cycle to find Easter, "so far as we know."
  • Bede lists among his own works a letter on the leap year and the equinox, according to Anatolius.
  • Socrates of Constantinople, answering Sabbatius, gives the rule that the Hebrews sacrificed after the spring equinox, on the witness of Philo, Josephus and Aristobulus, the writers Anatolius cites. He does not name Anatolius, and his translator's note takes the passage from Anatolius's extract.
  • In the eleventh century Elias of Nisibis cites the words of Anatolius, bishop of Laodicea, to show that the spring equinox fell on the 22nd of Adar in his time.

How they're remembered

  • Eusebius says he "stood first among the ablest men of our time" in arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, logic and physics.
  • Jerome of Strido calls him a man of wonderful learning.
  • At the synod at Whitby in 664, Bede tells, both sides cited him. Colman called him "a holy man, and much commended in church history." Wilfrid called him "a most holy, learned, and commendable man", but said Colman's side did not keep his cycle.
  • Bar Hebraeus, in the thirteenth century, calls Eusebius, bishop of Laodicea, and Anatolius, both Alexandrians, true leaders in sacred and secular learning.
  • The Bollandists note that the calendars of Maurolycus, Felicius, Galesinius and Ferrarius remember Anatolius, bishop of Laodicea, and that the Roman Martyrology names him in July.

Open Questions

  • Eusebius tells the story of the siege as others told it to him ("They say").
  • McGiffert thinks the siege was most likely the one during the revolt of Aemilian, in 260. Valesius and others place it later, under Claudius.
  • Eusebius reports that the Alexandrians asked him to found a school of Aristotle's philosophy. He gives it as a report, and does not say Anatolius held any church office in Alexandria, McGiffert notes.
  • A longer Latin book on Easter, first printed in 1634, carries his name. Ideler held it to be a work of the seventh century. McGiffert, following Zahn, thinks there are "the best of reasons" for taking it as an early translation of his genuine work.
  • No held source gives the year of his birth or death. Ernest Cushing Richardson, in a note to his translation of Jerome, gives born about 230, bishop 270, died about 283, and names no source. S. D. F. Salmond, who translated his works, calls the dates 230, 270 and 280 conjectural.
  • Eusebius lists arithmetic, geometry, astronomy, logic and physics among his subjects. Jerome has grammar in place of physics.
  • Rufinus, in his Latin version of about 402, tells the siege in other terms. He puts Anatolius in the part of the city that was not besieged, has him go to the Roman commander himself and receive the refugees, and adds that Anatolius freed the city from famine and war. He also gives the speech in other words: Anatolius asks only that the whole crowd unfit for war be let out of the gates. Eusebius gives the message to the deacon outside the lines, who goes to the general and receives the refugees.
  • Duchesne counts him among the leaders of the rebels shut up in the palace quarter and thinks the recent revolt had compromised him. Harnack thinks that may have made him leave Egypt, and says he served as a statesman and sat on the city council. Eusebius says only that he called the council, voted first, and was honored by those in high office.
  • Duchesne calls him head of the Aristotelian school of Alexandria. Eusebius gives only the report that the citizens asked him to found one. Rufinus says that from boyhood he was well trained in philosophy, so that all the learned men and philosophers of Alexandria forced him to teach dialectic.
  • On the year of the siege, McGiffert notes that Eusebius's Chronicle puts it in 272. Salmond gives 262. Harnack ties it to the revolt of Aemilian in the time of Gallienus. Loofs thinks the palace quarter fell probably in 270, and that the journey to Syria, at least the deacon Eusebius's, most likely came before it fell.
  • Harnack puts his becoming bishop in 268 or about then, and calls the dates of Jerome's Chronicle worthless, about ten years too late. Hefele has him bishop of Laodicea since 270. Elias of Nisibis, in the eleventh century, says he lived in the time of the emperor Gallus.
  • Eusebius says only that the synod on Paul called him to Antioch and that the believers at Laodicea kept him as he passed through. McGiffert says he was on his way to the synod, or possibly on his return from it. Harnack says the Laodiceans compelled him. Bardy, who notes that the synod's letter does not name him, holds that several witnesses agree he was present at the council that deposed Paul. Harnack thinks Dionysius of Alexandria, too old to go himself, may have sent him and the deacon Eusebius to Syria; Eusebius does not say so.
  • No held source says where or when he wrote the Easter work. Harnack thinks he wrote it while still at Alexandria. Hefele says he began his cycle with the year 277 and took the 19th of March as the equinox.
  • Hefele says Anatolius discovered the cycle of nineteen years. McGiffert calls it the old Metonic cycle, used by the Greeks in the fifth century before Christ. Abbot Ceolfrid's letter, which Bede copies, says the nineteen-year cycle began in the time of the apostles, at Rome and in Egypt, and does not name Anatolius.
  • McGiffert says the nineteen-year cycle was soon adopted at Alexandria. Hefele says it was first changed, with the equinox moved from the 19th to the 21st of March, and then generally adopted there from the time of Diocletian. Bucherius thought Anatolius's own table was in use only a few years, at Laodicea or nearby, and that the cycle of Eusebius of Caesarea would easily have pushed it out.
  • Harnack holds the Latin book under his name to be a forgery made in the sixth century or earlier, in the quarrel between the British and Roman churches over Easter. He follows Hagen, with Ideler and Krusch, and notes that Zahn raised doubts. The Latin gives only part of the extract Eusebius quotes, and where it matches him it agrees with Rufinus's translation.
  • Colman says Anatolius wrote that Easter was to be kept from the fourteenth to the twentieth moon, and the Latin book under his name allows the celebration to be extended to the twentieth. Wilfrid says Anatolius counted in the Egyptian way, so that his fourteenth was the fifteenth by evening and his twentieth the twenty-first, and that Colman's side did not keep his cycle. Bede, earlier in his History, says the northern Irish and the Picts believed they followed Anatolius, and that a skilled reader can tell whether this is true.
  • A Syriac chronicle, in its Latin version, calls him a teacher at Laodicea after Eusebius, an Alexandrian by birth. It says he taught that the months begin on the eighteenth of Adar, and that the cycle comes round again after nineteen years. That day differs from the 22nd of March in the extract Eusebius quotes.
