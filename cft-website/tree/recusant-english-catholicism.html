The Reformation Era · 1559-1650 · England

England, today in United Kingdom

Recusant English Catholicism

England, 1559 to 1650 — a church driven back into private houses, with priests hidden in the walls and the women of the household keeping it alive.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 0:49

Read along

After Elizabeth I's settlement of 1559, English Catholics who would not attend their parish church were fined as recusants — refusers. And from 1585 it was treason for a priest ordained abroad even to set foot in the country. What survived was a church that had retreated into private houses. Mass said in an upstairs room, priests hidden in cavities built into chimneys and stairwells, and households in which the woman of the house, legally less exposed than her husband, effectively ran the congregation. Well over a hundred priests and lay people were executed under these laws, and the community that remained was small, anchored in gentry households, and stubborn.

Documented Stories

Margaret Clitherow Refuses to Plead

Margaret Clitherow had converted to Catholicism in her twenties, had been imprisoned three times for not attending church, and had for years hidden priests in her house in the Shambles and had Mass said there; her husband, a Protestant, paid her fines and asked no questions. In March 1586 the house was searched on the information of a Flemish boy who had been schooled with her children and was frightened into showing the searchers the priest’s room. She was arraigned at the York assizes for harboring priests, a felony. Asked how she would plead, she answered: ‘Having made no offence, I need no trial.’ The judge explained that if she would not plead she must suffer the peine forte et dure, pressing to death; she said she would not put her children and her neighbors to the sin of swearing against her. The sheriff’s wife brought news that she was thought to be pregnant, and the court did not investigate. On 25 March she was laid in the tollbooth on Ouse Bridge with a sharp stone under her back and a door on top, and weights added until she died, saying ‘Jesu, Jesu, have mercy on me’. Mush says she had sent her hat to her husband and her shoes and stockings to her daughter. She was thirty. Her husband was reported to have said that she was the best wife in England and the best Catholic, and to have asked to be left alone.

John Gerard Crosses the Moat on a Rope

John Gerard had landed in Norfolk in 1588, worked the Catholic gentry houses of the eastern counties for six years, and been arrested in London in 1594. His Autobiography, written in Latin around 1609 at his superiors’ request, describes the Tower. In April 1597 he was hung by his wrists from iron gauntlets on a pillar in the Tower’s cellar, feet off the ground, for hours at a time over several days, to make him name the Jesuit superior Henry Garnet; he fainted repeatedly and did not. He then set about escaping. He obtained oranges from his warder, wrote with the juice — invisible until heated — between the lines of innocent letters to Catholic friends, and, through a fellow prisoner in the Cradle Tower, John Arden, whose window overlooked the moat, arranged for two men to row across on the night of 4 October with a rope. The second night it was fixed to a stake on the wharf; Gerard, his hands still weak, hauled himself along it across the moat, so slowly that he thought he would fall, and was pulled the last yards. He left a letter for the warder, who had helped him unknowingly, exonerating him and arranging for him to be got away too. The Autobiography records the priests he served with who were caught and hanged, and the households — mostly run by women — that hid him; it is the inside account of a church living in attics.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

After 1559, English Catholics who refused to attend their parish church were fined as recusants, and from 1585 it was treason for a priest even to set foot in the country. What survived was a church of private houses, with priests hidden in walls and the women of the household — legally less exposed than their husbands — effectively running the congregation.

Legacy

The community survived. English Catholicism continued unbroken through the penal years and re-emerged legally in the nineteenth century, and forty of its martyrs were canonized together in 1970 as the Forty Martyrs of England and Wales. Its most tangible remains are the houses themselves — priest-holes still shown to visitors in country manors. And a devotional literature, above all Southwell's poetry, that outlived the persecution which produced it.

Experience It Today

  • John Gerard, The Autobiography of a Hunted Priest (trans. Caraman, 1951); William Weston's autobiography (primary) — Inside accounts of the household church, written for edification within the mission — a partisan, inside-voice genre.
  • The women who ran the church: Mush's True Report of the Life and Martyrdom of Mrs Margaret Clitherow; Smith's life of Lady Magdalen Montague; recusancy prosecutions of women who shielded priests (primary) — The life of Margaret Clitherow was written by her own confessor to advance her sainthood cause, so it's a hagiographic work, not a neutral biography.
  • The state's record: recusant rolls, quarter-session presentments, interrogation and trial records (incl. examinations under torture) (primary) — Hostile, administrative records, some obtained under coercion — the community's shape has to be reconstructed from its persecutors' own bookkeeping.
  • The devotional equipment: Rheims New Testament (1582), Douai OT, A Manual of Prayers (1583), secret-press books, Southwell's poetry and epistles (primary) — What the households actually read and prayed.
  • John Bossy, The English Catholic Community 1570-1850 (1975), with Alexandra Walsham, Church Papists (1993/1999) (secondary) — Walsham's work corrects an earlier picture of a hard line between recusants and conformists, showing a wide gray zone in between.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 6, ch. 15 'Persecutions and martyrdom' (Gregory) (reference) — Directly relevant to a community shaped by persecution.
