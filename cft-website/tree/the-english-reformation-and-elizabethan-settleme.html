The Reformation Era · 1530s-1650 · England

England, today in United Kingdom

The English Reformation & Elizabethan Settlement

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

England's Reformation began as a king's quarrel — Henry VIII's break with Rome in the 1530s. And only afterwards became a change in what people believed, swinging hard toward Protestantism under Edward VI, back to Rome under Mary I, and settling under Elizabeth I into a church that kept bishops and cathedrals while preaching a Protestant gospel. What actually altered English religion was less a confession of faith than a book. The Book of Common Prayer, first issued in 1549 and revised in 1552, 1559 and 1662, which fixed the words the whole country was required to say together, week after week. The change can be watched from below in churchwardens' accounts and in the wills of ordinary people, whose formulas about the soul and the dead shift decade by decade. And the settlement held, uneasily, until the civil wars of the 1640s broke it open again.

In Its Own Record

Gave Rise To

  • Puritanism Documented Major — The Admonition to the Parliament of 1572 speaks for ministers forced to sign their assent to the Prayer Book. Its authors, John Field and Thomas Wilcox, were two clergy of the English church. They were sent to Newgate prison that July.

Continued As

  • The Restoration and Georgian Church of England Documented Major — The Act of Uniformity of 1662 begins by recalling the Prayer Book that Parliament made the law in Elizabeth's first year. It put that order back in place after what it called 'the late unhappy Troubles.'

Influenced By

  • Lollardy Documented — In 1521 the bishop of Lincoln tried households around Amersham who had learned from Wyclif's Wicket, an English tract. The Protestant writer John Foxe said this came before Luther's name was heard there. Historians still disagree how far the Lollards fed the English Reformation.
  • Lutheran Wittenberg & Its Congregations Widely Accepted — William Tyndale's book The Obedience of a Christian Man, printed in 1528, defends Martin Luther by name. His editor showed in 1848 that Tyndale's Prologue to Romans is mostly Luther's own preface. At his trial in 1555, Thomas Cranmer was told he had once been a Lutheran, and he granted that he had changed his mind.
  • The Reformed Cities - Zurich & Geneva Documented — In 1548 John Calvin wrote from Geneva to praise the Protector Somerset for his reform of worship in England. In 1562 Bishop John Jewel told Peter Martyr in Zurich that England's teaching did not differ from his 'by a nail's breadth.' In 1566 Archbishop Parker cited Martyr and Bullinger too, to press the clergy to conform.

Argued Against

  • Late-Medieval Lay Parish Piety Documented Major — Edward VI's royal orders of 1547 told churches to take away shrines, rolls of wax, pictures and paintings. Nothing of them was to stay 'in walls, glass-windows, or elsewhere'. The orders' 1910 editors add that a law of the same year closed the guilds and ended the chantries.Lasting result: In 1547 King Edward VI ordered shrines, pictures and paintings taken out of churches. No memory of them was to be left.
  • Family of Love & Spiritualists (Franck, Niclaes) Documented — In 1579 the English preacher John Knewstub printed an attack on the Family of Love. That same year, John Strype later wrote, the bishop of Norwich hunted Familists and put some in prison. A 1606 reply to the Familists notes royal orders issued against their founder.
  • Puritanism Documented — At Hampton Court in January 1604, King James heard the Puritan case against the Prayer Book. Leaving the room, he said he would make them conform 'or I will harry them out of this Land.' In 1572 the authors of the Admonition had been sent to prison.
  • Recusant English Catholicism Documented Major — The Act of Uniformity of 1559 fined anyone who stayed away from the parish church twelve pence each time. In January 1586 a London court found priests guilty of treason. They had been made priests in France by the Pope's authority and had then stayed in England.Lasting result: In January 1586 a London court condemned priests for treason. They had been made priests in France by the Pope's authority. They then stayed in England past a forty-day limit.

Opposed By

  • Puritanism Documented — The Admonition to the Parliament of 1572 told Parliament that England was still far from 'a church rightly reformed.' It called the Prayer Book an imperfect book, picked out of the Mass book.

Broke With

  • The Tridentine Church Documented Major — Henry VIII's orders of 1536 told the clergy to keep the laws that ended the Pope's 'usurped power' in England. Under Mary I, in 1555, London parishes prayed for the Pope again. In 1562 John Jewel wrote that the English had left Rome and gave thanks for it. In 1570 Pope Pius V cut Elizabeth off from the church.

Sources

  • The Book of Common Prayer: The Texts of 1549, 1559, and 1662 (ed. Cummings, Oxford World's Classics, 2011), with the primers and Books of Homilies (primary) — These are the texts ordinary England actually prayed, and the homilies show what parishioners actually heard from the pulpit.
  • John Foxe, Acts and Monuments (1563+; online variorum TAMO edition) (primary) — Worth reading with care: this is martyrology — real documentary content (examinations of ordinary believers, many women) inside an openly polemical frame that shifted across editions; it also digests the Marian regime's own hostile records.
  • Visitation articles and injunctions (Frere & Kennedy, Alcuin Club, 1910) with churchwardens' accounts spanning the changes (Morebath and neighboring parishes) (primary) — This parish paperwork carries the story of Morebath and its neighbors across the religious changes of the period, showing continuity on the ground.
  • Wills tracking belief-change across decades (soul-bequest preamble series), with Jewel's Apology (1562) and Hooker, Of the Laws of Ecclesiastical Polity (1593-) (primary)
  • Duffy, The Stripping of the Altars (Yale, 1992/2005), part II, with Peter Marshall, Heretics and Believers (Yale, 2017) (secondary) — Duffy's revisionist frame is carried here as one interpretive lens, not a final verdict.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 6 (reference)
