The Tridentine Church

Rome and Latin Christendom, 1517 to 1650 — a church that answered the Reformation with a council, a catechism and a single missal, and built a system that lasted until the 1960s.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:02

Read along

The Catholic Church's answer to the Reformation took decades to assemble and then held for four centuries. The Council of Trent met in three widely separated periods between 1545 and 1563, restating Catholic teaching on Scripture and tradition, justification and the sacraments, and — as important in practice — ordering bishops to reside in their dioceses and to found seminaries. What followed was wholesale standardization: a Roman Catechism, a revised Missal and Breviary issued for the whole Latin church, and in 1622 a Roman office for the missions, Propaganda Fide. The same period produced the Roman Inquisition and the Index of Forbidden Books, and a wave of new active orders — Capuchins, Ursulines, Oratorians — doing work the older monastic rules had never been designed for.

Documented Stories

The Primate of Spain in the Inquisition’s Prison

Bartolomé Carranza, a Dominican theologian, had been the emperor's man at Trent, had helped Queen Mary restore Catholicism in England, and in 1557 was made archbishop of Toledo, primate of Spain. In 1558 he published a Commentary on the Christian Catechism in Spanish, and in September that year attended Charles V's deathbed at Yuste. In August 1559 the Spanish Inquisition, under Fernando de Valdés, arrested him at Torrelaguna on charges of Lutheran opinions drawn from his catechism and from witnesses to his conversation, including what he was said to have told the dying emperor about justification. The trial had no end. Carranza was held in Valladolid for seven years while inquisitors, his own Dominican brethren and the Spanish universities fought over his book; the Council of Trent, in 1563, had the Catechism examined and approved it. Pius V demanded the case for Rome, and in 1567 Carranza was moved to Castel Sant'Angelo, where he stayed nine years more, Pius dying before he could judge him. On 14 April 1576 Gregory XIII pronounced: the archbishop was not convicted of heresy but was vehemently suspect of it, must abjure sixteen propositions, was suspended for five years, and his catechism was prohibited. Carranza abjured, said Mass at St John Lateran, fell ill, and died on 2 May 1576, protesting his innocence. He had been archbishop for nineteen years and in his diocese for less than one.

The Last Day at Trent

The Council of Trent's last session opened on 3 December 1563 and ran into the next day, because Pius IV was ill and the legates were afraid of what a vacancy in Rome would do to a council that had already been suspended twice. The decrees on purgatory, the invocation of saints, images, indulgences and the reform of religious orders were read and voted in haste; the decrees of the first two periods, from 1545 to 1552, were read again so that the French and the Spaniards who had not been present could assent to them. Then the Cardinal of Lorraine, who had come to Trent a year earlier as the French crown's critic of Rome, stood and led the acclamations, and the fathers answered him: to the pope, long life; to the emperors Charles and Ferdinand, eternal memory; to the legates, thanks; and finally, ‘Anathema to all heretics,’ answered, ‘Anathema, anathema.’ The secretary, Angelo Massarelli, recorded that many of the bishops wept, and that men who had argued for years embraced. Two hundred and fifty-five signed. Pius IV confirmed the decrees on 26 January 1564. Massarelli's diaries, kept from the first session onward, remain the fullest record of the council. The council never wrote its own history; the two great histories were written by an enemy, Paolo Sarpi, and an answerer, Sforza Pallavicino, and the fight between them lasted as long as the decrees.

Twenty-Eight Names in a Book at Brescia

On 25 November 1535, in Brescia, Angela Merici and twenty-eight women wrote their names in a book in the oratory of St Afra and called themselves the Company of St Ursula. Angela was about sixty, a Franciscan tertiary who had lived as a laywoman, gone on pilgrimage to the Holy Land and to Rome, and had for years taught girls and nursed the sick in the city. What she founded was, by the standards of the age, hardly an order at all: the members took no solemn vows, wore no habit, lived in their own homes, most of them with their families or as servants, met for instruction, and put themselves under a rule that asked for virginity, poverty and obedience, for daily prayer and fasting, and for the care of others. The rule was approved by the bishop's vicar in 1536; Angela dictated her Counsels and her Testament, addressed to the women who would govern the company after her, shortly before her death in January 1540. Paul III approved the Company in 1544. It was Charles Borromeo who, bringing it to Milan, turned it toward the teaching of girls, and the following century that turned the Ursulines into enclosed nuns with schools; by 1650 they were the largest body of teaching women in the Catholic world. What Angela made was something the Latin church did not yet have a name for: a company of consecrated women living in the world.

Connections

In Its Own Record

The Catholic Church's answer to the Reformation: the Council of Trent's three periods between 1545 and 1563, the resulting Roman Catechism and Tridentine Missal, Pope Sixtus V's reorganized curia, and the missionary office Propaganda Fide founded in 1622. Its story also includes the Index of Forbidden Books and the Roman Inquisition, new active orders such as the Capuchins, a wider generation of lay devotion, the Greek College in Rome, and the Catholic remainder of the Augustinian Hermits after the Reformation split the order.

Direct descendants

  • continued as The Catholic Church of the Ancien Régime Documented Major — In 1699 the bishops of the Paris province met to accept a ruling by Pope Innocent XII. Their archbishop said the church of France had received 'the faith of the Council of Trent' without exception. Trent's rules for church order, he added, were not yet received everywhere there.

Broke With

  • broke with The English Reformation & Elizabethan Settlement Documented Major — Henry VIII's orders of 1536 told the clergy to keep the laws that ended the Pope's 'usurped power' in England. In 1570 Pope Pius V cut Elizabeth off from the church.
  • broke with The Scandinavian Reformation Kingdoms Widely Accepted Major — In Sweden the quarrel grew when Rome would not confirm bishops the cathedral chapters chose, the historian Anjou writes. Denmark broke harder. In 1536 its bishops were jailed, and its church law of 1537 charged the 'papist bishops' with tyranny.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

The Council of Trent met across three widely separated periods between 1545 and 1563, restating Catholic teaching on Scripture, justification and the sacraments, and ordering bishops to actually live in their dioceses and found seminaries to train priests. What followed was thorough standardization — a single Roman Catechism, Missal and Breviary for the whole Latin church — alongside new active orders, the Capuchins, Ursulines and Oratorians, doing pastoral work the older monastic rules had never been designed for.

Legacy

This is the Catholic Church most people picture before the twentieth century. The Latin Mass in the form fixed in 1570, the seminary-trained parish priest, the catechism learned by rote, the confessional, the teaching and nursing orders. That framework governed Catholic life until the Second Vatican Council of 1962-65 revised it — and the older Mass is still celebrated today by communities that have kept it. The missionary and educational institutions built in this period carried Catholicism to every continent.
