Disciples of Christ & the Independent Christian Churches

About This Tradition

The other side of the 1906 division took the opposite road. The Disciples of Christ kept their instruments and their national organizations, joined the Federal Council of Churches at its founding in 1908 and the World Council of Churches at its founding in 1948, and in 1968 adopted a plan called the Restructure — 'The Design' — that turned a movement of congregations into a denomination. But that step cost them a second division, which had been forming for decades: the independent Christian Churches had been meeting separately at the North American Christian Convention since 1927, and by 1971 they were listed separately in the yearbooks. So one nineteenth-century plea for Christian unity ended up producing three distinct streams.

Documented Stories

The Design

The Disciples of Christ had grown out of a nineteenth-century plea for the union of all Christians on the New Testament alone, and had always called themselves a brotherhood of free congregations, not a church. By the 1960s that description no longer matched the facts: there were national agencies, a pension fund, a yearbook, and no way to decide anything that bound them all. A commission worked from 1962 to draft a constitution. At the International Convention in Kansas City in September 1968 the delegates adopted the Provisional Design for the Christian Church (Disciples of Christ), which created a General Assembly with representative voting, regional churches, and the recognition of congregations as parts of one church rather than as the whole of it. The name changed on the same day; the convention became an assembly. It was, its framers said, honesty about what already existed. To the conservative wing, which had been meeting separately at the North American Christian Convention since 1927 and had watched the agencies drift toward the ecumenical mainstream, it was the end of the plea. The Design provided that any congregation could remove itself from the yearbook by request. Between 1968 and 1971 more than two thousand did, and others were simply not listed; by 1971 the independent Christian Churches were a separate body of some three thousand congregations without a headquarters, and the Disciples had roughly a million fewer members than a decade earlier. Afterward the two streams cooperated in some towns and, formally, nowhere.

A Congregation in Good Standing

Jim Jones had founded Peoples Temple in Indianapolis in the 1950s as a racially integrated congregation, and in 1960 affiliated it with the Disciples of Christ, whose congregational polity asked nothing of a member church but that it be listed. The Disciples ordained him in February 1964. He moved the church to California in 1965, eventually to San Francisco, where by the mid-1970s it had several thousand members, most of them Black and poor, ran care homes and legal clinics, and was praised in the press as the city's most effective social ministry. It reported to the Disciples annually and its pastor sat on the denomination's regional bodies. Defectors' accounts of beatings, faked healings, coerced property transfers and Jones's claims to divinity appeared in print in 1977; the denomination had no office with authority to investigate a congregation and did not. That summer Jones moved a thousand members to Guyana. On 18 November 1978, after a visiting congressman and four others were shot dead at the airstrip, Jones ordered the community to drink cyanide, and 909 people died, a third of them children. The Disciples' general minister, Kenneth Teegarden, said afterward that Peoples Temple had been a congregation in good standing, and that a body which had just spent a decade defining itself as one church had to ask what that had meant. It removed the Temple from its yearbook. The question of oversight it raised went unanswered in a polity built to refuse it.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

After the 1906 division, the Disciples of Christ kept their instruments and their national organization, becoming founding members of the Federal Council of Churches in 1908 and the World Council of Churches in 1948 before adopting a 1968 plan called 'The Design' that turned a movement of congregations into a denomination. That step came after decades of quiet separation: congregations that became the independent Christian Churches had been meeting apart at the North American Christian Convention since 1927 and were listed separately in the yearbooks by 1971 — one plea for Christian unity having produced three distinct streams.

Legacy

Both streams were alive in 2010. The Disciples' ecumenical commitment was institutional — they were founding members of both the Federal Council in 1908 and the World Council in 1948 — which is what their founding plea for unity became; the independent Christian Churches remained a large, congregationally governed stream. The irony was not lost on either of them: a movement founded to end division had divided twice.
