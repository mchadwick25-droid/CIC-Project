Marcella of Rome

About This Person

When Rome was taken in 410, soldiers broke into the house of an old widow named Marcella. Asked for gold, she is said to have pointed to her coarse dress to show she had none. They did not believe her, and "scourged her and beat her with cudgels." Jerome of Strido tells this in a memorial letter, written in her praise to her friend Principia. He was in Palestine and writes, he says, "what holy men have seen."

Marcella came from a noble Roman family. She was widowed before seven months of marriage. An old nobleman, Cerealis, then offered her his fortune. She told him that "a young man may indeed die early, but an old man cannot live long."

Clergy of Alexandria fled to Rome, Athanasius among them. From them Marcella heard of Antony, the desert monk, and of Pachomius's monasteries in Egypt. No highborn lady at Rome, Jerome says, had yet dared "publicly to call herself a nun." Marcella took up that life, and the virgin Eustochium was trained in her cell.

In the 380s Jerome lived in Rome, and Marcella questioned him about Scripture at every visit. Seventeen of his letters to her are held, several on Hebrew words. After he left, "recourse was had to her to settle" disputes about Scripture. She gave her answers as his or another's, because Paul had written, "I suffer not a woman to teach."

A Latin version of Origen's First Principles later reached Rome. The notes in W. H. Fremantle's English edition name its translator as Rufinus of Aquileia. He and Jerome later wrote against each other. Marcella opposed its teachers in public. "She it was who originated the condemnation of the heretics," Jerome writes. He admits some may think him "giving vent to my own rancour."

A few days after the soldiers came, she died in Principia's arms. Two years later Jerome wrote of her as "Marcella the glory of her native Rome."

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Monastic life for women at Rome, which Jerome says she took up when no highborn lady there had yet dared "publicly to call herself a nun"; Eustochium was trained in her cell.
  • The Roman condemnation of the Origenists, which Jerome credits to her: "She it was who originated the condemnation of the heretics."

How they're remembered

  • Jerome wrote her memorial two years after her death, in a letter to her friend Principia, and calls her "Marcella the glory of her native Rome."
  • Seventeen of Jerome's letters to her are held. Many answer her questions on Scripture, several of them on Hebrew words.

What Is Uncertain

  • Her life is known from Jerome alone, chiefly his memorial letter to Principia, which is a friend's praise. Nothing of hers survives in her own words.
  • Jerome was not at Rome when it was taken. He tells the beating as what he heard from "holy men" who were there, and much of it as what "is said".
  • Jerome was a party to the Origenist quarrel at Rome, and he admits that readers may think him "giving vent to my own rancour." The notes in W. H. Fremantle's English edition name the translator of Origen's book as Rufinus of Aquileia. Jerome's letter does not name him.
  • The Library holds no account of Marcella from the side of those she opposed.
