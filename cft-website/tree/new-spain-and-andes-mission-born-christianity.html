Mission-Born Christianity in New Spain and the Andes

The Franciscan Order — Spirituals Crisis to the Great Division→Mission-Born Christianity in New Spain and the Andes→Colonial Latin American Devotional Catholicism · Philippine Catholicism

About This Movement

In 1536, a Franciscan friar in Mexico wrote down a number. "I believe more than four million souls were baptized from the time the land was won until the time I write this." The land had been won in 1521. The friar was Toribio de Benavente, called Motolinía.

He wrote the mission's story from the inside. Twelve friars, he says, left Spain in January 1523 for the land of Anahuac, now called New Spain. They came for the conversion of its native peoples. We hear the people they baptized only through the friars' pens.

The land the friars reached had already been struck. Smallpox came with a sick man on a Spanish ship, and in most provinces, Motolinía says, more than half the people died. War killed many others, above all around Mexico City. He counts ten plagues in all. After smallpox and war came hunger, and then the weight of Spanish rule: tribute, slavery and forced work in the gold mines. Many houses, he wrote, stood empty.

Motolinía read this as God's work. He wrote that God had killed many of the lords who would not accept the new law, through those same plagues. Their sons, baptized as small children, then inherited their lands. That is his own reading.

He also told a smaller scene. Friars visited two provinces from their house at Cuauhnahuac, and people came from the small town to the larger one to hear them and to have their children baptized. One night, heavy rain turned a stream into a river. The friars preached from one bank to the people on the other, and the people would not go home until their children were baptized. So they made a poor raft of canes. Half in the water and half in their arms, they carried the children across, and the friars baptized them with great effort, he says, because there were so many.

His counts are his own, and they do not agree. In one book he gives three totals for the same fifteen years: more than four million, close to five million, and more than nine million. "In my judgment, and in truth, more than nine million souls of Indians will have been baptized in the time I speak of." The numbers count baptisms. They do not tell what the people believed.

A Jesuit, José de Acosta, wrote about the Indians of Peru and Mexico some fifty years later, around 1590. He said many of their customs resembled the law of Moses, and some came near the Gospel. In Peru, he wrote, people confessed their sins aloud and washed in water to cleanse them. The Mexica even had a kind of baptism of newborn children. He judged these customs mixed with great abomination, and he named the devil's pride as the cause of their idol worship. But some priests told him that after the Christians came to Peru, people there confessed even their thoughts.
