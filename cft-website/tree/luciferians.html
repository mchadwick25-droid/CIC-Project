Rome, today in Italy · Antioch, today in Turkey and beyond

The Luciferians

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Lucifer was bishop of Cagliari in Sardinia. At a council in Milan he refused to condemn Athanasius of Alexandria, and he stood up to the emperor Constantius II to his face. For this he was sent into exile from 355 to 361. He spent most of those years at Eleutheropolis in Palestine. From there he wrote pamphlets to the emperor himself. How could Christians meet, he asked, with the false bishops of the emperor's sect, the Arians? One pamphlet bears the title We Must Die for the Son of God.

In 362, after Constantius died, the exiles came home. A council met at Alexandria under Athanasius. It agreed that bishops who had only given way could stay in their sees. Lucifer did not attend. Two of his deacons signed its letter for him. He went instead to Antioch and made Paulinus bishop of one of the rival parties there. When his friend Eusebius of Vercelli did not approve, Lucifer broke off communion with him. The church historian Socrates says that many followed Lucifer, and a separate sect arose.

Lucifer himself went home to Sardinia and died there in 371. His followers held on. Hilary, a deacon of Rome, went further than Lucifer. He set up his own font and baptized again those who came over from the Arian side. In about 379 Jerome wrote a dialogue against the group. Lucifer was a good shepherd, he said. But in a time of wolves he took a few sheep away and left the rest of the flock to the beasts.

In Rome the group had its own bishop, Ephesius, and a priest, Macarius, who held night vigils in a private house. In 383 or 384 two of its priests, Marcellinus and Faustinus, sent a petition to the emperors. It says that the clergy of Damasus, bishop of Rome, broke up one of Macarius's vigils and dragged him over the stones. The two priests refused the name Luciferians. Christ is our teacher, they wrote, so the only name we bear is Christian.

The emperors answered them. Their letter called the two men priests full of faith. It told the prefect Cynegius to shield Gregory in Spain, Heraclida in the East and priests like them from attack. The group never grew large. Augustine wrote that they had cut themselves off from the unity of the church. Socrates, writing in the next century, says that those who first joined Lucifer's quarrel still stood apart. After that they fade from view.

In Its Own Record

Argued Against

  • Homoian ('Arian') Christianity Documented — In exile from 355 to 361, Lucifer of Cagliari wrote pamphlets to the emperor Constantius II. He called the bishops of the emperor's party false bishops of his sect, the Arians. He asked how Christians could ever meet with them.

Opposed By

  • Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity Documented — In about 379 Jerome wrote a dialogue against the followers of Lucifer. In it a Luciferian named Helladius debates a churchman. Jerome calls Lucifer a good shepherd. But he took a few sheep away, Jerome says, and left the rest of the flock to the wolves.
  • Imperial and Juridical Christianity Inferential-Thin — The Luciferian petition of 383 or 384 says that the clergy of Damasus, bishop of Rome, broke up a night vigil held by the priest Macarius. It says they dragged him over the stones. This rests on the petition alone; no account from Damasus's side is in hand.
  • Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity Documented — Augustine warns his readers not to listen to the Luciferians. They do not baptize again, he says, and in that they do right. But they cut themselves off from the unity of the church, and would rather be called Luciferians than Catholics.

Broke With

  • Imperial and Juridical Christianity Documented Major — After 362 Lucifer's followers broke with the main church. It had taken back bishops who gave way. Socrates of Constantinople says a new sect arose and still stood apart in his day. The group's own petition rejects the name Luciferians. Augustine says they cut themselves off from the unity of the church.

In Contact With
