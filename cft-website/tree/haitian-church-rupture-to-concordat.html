The Haitian Church — Rupture to Concordat

Haiti, 1804 to 1860 — the first Black republic's church, left for fifty-six years without Rome, a bishop or a seminary, and what grew in the gap.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:12

Read along

When Haiti declared independence in 1804 as the first Black republic, its church was cut adrift. Rome would not recognize the new state, the French clergy had fled or been killed, and for more than half a century no properly appointed bishop and almost no seminary-trained priest arrived. The state filled the gap, appointing its own clergy, and the parishes were served by men of irregular standing. Some genuine priests under suspension, some who had simply assumed the title. In that long vacancy Haitian religious life took the shape it has largely kept: Catholic sacraments, feast days and saints woven together with Vodou practice inherited from West Africa, held by most Haitians as one thing rather than two. The rupture formally ended on 28 March 1860, when Haiti and the Holy See signed a concordat restoring a regular hierarchy. And began a much longer argument about what to do with everything that had grown up in the meantime.

Documented Stories

The Coronation of Faustin I

Faustin Soulouque, a former slave, had been made president in 1847 by politicians who thought him manageable, and in August 1849 had himself proclaimed Emperor of Haiti, creating a nobility of dukes, counts and barons from his officers. The coronation waited three years for the regalia. Crowns, sceptre and throne were ordered from Paris, the rite was drawn from Napoleon's coronation of 1804, and on 18 April 1852, in the cathedral of Port-au-Prince, the emperor and the empress Adélina were anointed and crowned. The officiant was a French priest who had come to Haiti without faculties; there had been no bishop since independence, since Rome had never recognized the state, and the clergy were men who had arrived on their own account, some suspended priests, some genuine, some not priests at all, appointed to parishes by the government. The coronation was the schismatic church's one great public act, and the French press, whose contempt for Soulouque was as much racial as political, made it a byword. Faustin ruled until 1859, when General Fabre Geffrard overthrew him, and it was Geffrard's government that signed the concordat with Rome on 28 March 1860 and began importing Breton priests to replace the clergy who had crowned an emperor. What ordinary Haitians had made of the sacraments in fifty-six years without a bishop, the mixture of Catholic and Vodou practice the new clergy found, is documented mostly by those who set out to destroy it.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

For fifty-six years after independence, Haiti's parishes were served by men of irregular standing, some genuine priests under suspension, some who had simply taken the title, since Rome refused to recognize the new republic at all. In that long vacancy, Catholic sacraments and feast days wove together with Vodou practice inherited from West Africa into a religious life most Haitians have held as one thing rather than two.

Legacy

The hierarchy the 1860 concordat established is still the framework of the Haitian Catholic Church, the country's largest institution. The creole religious world formed during the schism is also still there, and the church's relationship to it — stretches of quiet accommodation alternating with campaigns to stamp it out, most violently the anti-superstition drives of the 1940s — has been one of the defining questions of Haitian religion ever since.
