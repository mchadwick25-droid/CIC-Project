Marius Mercator

About This Person

In 429, by the consuls its title names, a Latin writer named Marius Mercator gave the church of Constantinople a short warning, written in Greek. Its title says it concerned Caelestius, a follower of Pelagius. After it was read, the title goes on, Caelestius and Julian of Eclanum were driven from the city with their companions by the emperor's order. They were later condemned at the Council of Ephesus.

Garnier, his first editor, notes that even Gennadius of Marseilles leaves him out of his list of church writers. Augustine of Hippo wrote to him as "my son." In that letter, held here in Latin, Augustine says Mercator's letters reached him at Carthage. He had not known, he says, how far Mercator had grown.

Where he came from is disputed. Garnier guessed southern Italy, since Mercator knew the private lives of Caelestius and Julian so well. Baluze, a later editor, guessed Africa. Schoenemann, a later scholar, says both are guesses. Garnier thought him a layman, since Augustine called him son and not brother. Hefele, the historian of the councils, calls him a layman living in Constantinople.

His chief work was translation. He put sermons of Nestorius, the deposed bishop of Constantinople, into Latin, with letters of Nestorius and of Cyril of Alexandria. He wanted Latin readers to know these sayings and avoid them. Hefele says Nestorius's twelve anathemas against Cyril survive only in Mercator's Latin.

He wrote as a partisan, and Garnier gathers from him rumors about Caelestius and Julian. In a book against Julian, Mercator traced the error back to Theodore of Mopsuestia.

No held line gives his death. Garnier thought he wrote nothing after the Council of Chalcedon. Baluze thought he did, since he names the followers of Eutyches.

In one preface he set out his method. He had tried to turn the Greek word for word, he wrote. He did not want to be proved a forger rather than a true translator.

Key Works

  • Commonitorium on Caelestius: a warning written in Greek in 429 to the church of Constantinople and offered to the emperor Theodosius the Younger; he put it into Latin himself
  • Latin translations of Nestorius: sermons and letters of Nestorius, with letters of Cyril of Alexandria, turned into Latin for Western readers
  • Book of Notes against the Words of Julian: answers to Julian of Eclanum, tracing the error back to Theodore of Mopsuestia

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Hefele, the historian of the councils, says the twelve anathemas Nestorius set against Cyril of Alexandria survive only in his Latin.
  • His volume carries much of the Nestorian and Pelagian record to Latin readers, in his own translations.

How they're remembered

  • Augustine of Hippo writes to him as "my son" and says he had not known how far Mercator had come.
  • Garnier notes that even Gennadius of Marseilles does not name him.

What Is Uncertain

  • Little is known of his life, and no ancient writer tells it. Garnier, his first editor, notes that even Gennadius of Marseilles leaves him out.
  • His origin is disputed. Garnier guessed southern Italy, from his knowledge of Caelestius and Julian of Eclanum. Baluze guessed Africa. Schoenemann says both views are conjectures. B. B. Warfield, in his introduction to Augustine's anti-Pelagian writings, calls him an African layman.
  • That he was a layman is an inference. Garnier draws it from Augustine calling him son and not brother, and from his absence among the bishops at Ephesus. Hefele calls him a layman living in Constantinople. Garnier says copyists wrote "bishop" for "letter" in Possidius's list of Augustine's works.
  • The year of Augustine's letter to him is Garnier's reckoning (418). Augustine's letter is held only in Latin.
  • He wrote as a partisan against Nestorius, Julian and Caelestius. The rumors about Caelestius and Julian that Garnier gathers from him are an opponent's.
  • When he died is unknown. Garnier thought he wrote nothing after the Council of Chalcedon; Baluze thought he did, since he names the followers of Eutyches.
