Symeon the New Theologian

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Byzantine Imperial Church (Macedonian Era).

Known for
Monk and abbot of Saint Mamas in Constantinople, about the year 1000, who taught that God's light can be seen and known in this life. He kept a feast for his own teacher, Symeon the Pious, and was exiled for it. The Philokalia prints his chapters, and later teachers of stillness read him.
Also called
Symeon, abbot of Saint Mamas, Symeon Junior Theologus, in J.-P. Migne's index
Dates
About the year 1000, in the reign of Constantine Porphyrogennetos, say the compilers of the Philokalia. François Combefis's summary of Niketas's Life has him born under Basil and Constantine, and a priest for forty-eight years. No held source gives the year of his birth, exile or death
Place
Galata in Paphlagonia, where he was born, by Niketas's Life; the imperial court at Constantinople; the monasteries of Studios and Saint Mamas in Constantinople; a small town near Chrysopolis, where he was exiled and built a new monastery
Role
Monk, priest and abbot of Saint Mamas; teacher and writer on prayer and the divine light

About This Person

When Symeon was ordained priest, he saw the Holy Spirit come down on him, simple and formless, as light. In a passage printed with his chapters in the Philokalia, he tells it as another monk's story, with a metropolitan reading the prayer. His disciple Niketas Stethatos, in his Life of Symeon, names the patriarch Nicholas Chrysoberges as his ordainer. The vision came to him at the altar, Niketas says, through forty-eight years as a priest.

Symeon, called the New Theologian, was a monk of Constantinople. He lived about the year 1000, say the compilers of the Philokalia, a Greek collection on prayer. Niketas's Life is known through a long summary by François Combefis, a Dominican scholar.

Symeon came from Galata in Paphlagonia, Niketas says, and served young at the imperial court. Advised by a monk of Studios, Symeon called the Pious, he entered that monastery. When he would not soften his strict life, the superior put him out. His teacher sent him to the house of Saint Mamas, where he later became abbot.

His chapters speak often of light. A man may strip his whole body bare, he writes, but if a veil covers his eyes he still cannot see. So one who gives up everything will never see the spiritual light, Christ, unless he frees the eye of the soul from worldly cares.

Symeon kept a yearly feast for his teacher, with hymns and an icon. The patriarch Sergius read the hymns, Niketas says, and praised them. Sixteen years on, Stephen, once metropolitan of Nicomedia and now the patriarch's aide, began to attack the feast. Niketas puts it down to envy. A synod had the word saint blotted out on the icon, and when Symeon would not stop, he was exiled near Chrysopolis. Recalled, he was offered a high see if he would give way. He refused, and was left free to keep the feast. Sergius said, in words Niketas reports, that Symeon had not erred in the church's teaching.

Michel Le Quien, John of Damascus's editor, called him the father of the Palamite error, named for the later theologian Gregory Palamas. Combefis defended him. Gregory of Sinai tells those who seek stillness to read the New Theologian and his disciple.

Some mocked such claims, Symeon wrote. Who has seen God, they asked. Of those who take up the cross, he answered, many saw God before, and more, he thinks, see him now.

Key Works

  • Practical and Theological Chapters: short chapters on the passions, tears, prayer and the light of God, printed in the Philokalia, volume 2, in Greek. The same volume ends with three of his pieces put into simpler Greek, which are not his own wording
  • Discourses and hymns: sermons to his monks and hymns, among them hymns for his teacher Symeon the Pious. J.-P. Migne's index lists thirty-three orations and a book of divine loves in Patrologia Graeca 120, which is not held. François Combefis prints extracts in Greek, Nil Sorsky quotes him in Slavonic, and the Philokalia retells some of his teaching in simpler Greek
  • On the three methods of prayer: printed under his name in the Philokalia, in simpler Greek. Combefis doubts it is his

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The compilers of the Philokalia print his chapters and call him a spring of theology.
  • Gregory of Sinai, in the Philokalia, tells readers to read the works of the New Theologian and of his disciple Stethatos, among the writings on stillness and prayer.
  • Paisius Velichkovsky and Nil Sorsky, in Slavonic, quote him on prayer and the light. Paisius says the whole church named him the New Theologian.
  • His disciple Niketas Stethatos wrote his Life, which François Combefis read in a manuscript once Cardinal Mazarin's and summarized with extracts.

How they're remembered

  • Niketas says grace made Symeon's tongue the pen of a swift scribe and his mind a spring of God's wisdom, though he had no worldly learning.
  • Michel Le Quien calls him the parent of the Palamite error. Combefis writes that he is wrongly counted a source of the Palamites, who twisted his words. Manuel Calecas, a Dominican, wrote against the Palamites. In a note, Combefis reads the deluded monks that Calecas attacks as Symeon and his disciples.
  • Combefis says he advised the Jesuits Henschen and Papebroch, the Bollandists, not to print the Life in their volumes for March, because it was suspected of feeding the Palamite error.

What Is Uncertain

  • The account of his life comes from his disciple Niketas Stethatos, known through François Combefis's summary and extracts. Combefis adds judgments of his own.
  • Who ordained him priest. Niketas's Life, in Combefis's summary, names the patriarch Nicholas Chrysoberges. Symeon's own telling in the Philokalia, given as another monk's story, has a metropolitan say the prayer.
  • His years. The Philokalia gives about the year 1000. No held source gives his birth, the year of his exile, or his death. Combefis reckons from the Life that he lived on into the time of the patriarch Alexius, once a monk of Studios.
  • Why he was condemned. Niketas blames the envy of Stephen, Sergius's aide. Sergius, in the words Niketas reports, says Symeon did not err in the church's teaching but would not stop keeping the feast. Combefis thinks a learned man could well suspect two unlettered men who spoke of God as if by inspiration. W. K. Lowther Clarke, writing in 1925, says he was tried for heresy.
  • Whether his visions leaned toward the Messalians, a sect that claimed to see God. Combefis grants they seem to, and argues that Symeon saw a sign of the Spirit, not God's essence.
  • On the three methods of prayer. The Philokalia prints it as his, and Paisius Velichkovsky quotes it as his. Combefis could not find it in the Mazarin manuscript and doubts he wrote it.
  • His monastery's district. Combefis writes Xerocercus, and Le Quien Xyloxerus.
  • The number of his chapters. The Philokalia's contents give 155. The last numbers in the held print are hard to read and run to 152 or 153.
