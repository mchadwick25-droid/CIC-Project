Julian of Toledo

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Nicene Visigothic Iberia.

Known for
Bishop of Toledo in Visigothic Spain, who signed first at four councils of Toledo. He wrote the Prognosticon, on death and what follows it, and a history of King Wamba's war.
Also called
Julianus
Dates
Bishop after Quiricus. Felix of Toledo says he held the see ten years, one month and seven days. He died in the third year of King Egica, in the Spanish era 728. The notes of Cardinal Lorenzana's Toledo edition read this as 6 March 690, with his consecration on 29 January 680. Nicolás Antonio, in his Bibliotheca Hispana vetus, has Quiricus dying in 681. Julian signed the councils of Toledo of 681, 683, 684 and 688. No held source gives his birth year
Place
Born, baptized and brought up at Toledo, Felix of Toledo says; bishop of Toledo
Role
Bishop of Toledo

About This Person

On the feast of the Lord's Passion, Julian of Toledo slipped away to a quiet room with his friend Idalius, bishop of Barcelona. They lay on separate beds and read the Gospel story of the Passion. At one passage, which Julian later could not recall, both men were shaken. They groaned and sighed, and tears came. Idalius was worn by gout, Julian wrote, but lifted far more by the hope of contemplating God.

They began to ask how the souls of the dead fare before the last resurrection. That same day, at Idalius's urging and if he remembered rightly, Julian called a secretary and set down the questions. He wrote the book, the Prognosticon, when the king rode out to war and the crowds left the city.

Julian, a seventh-century bishop of Toledo in Visigothic Spain, tells this in his letter to Idalius at the head of the work. Felix, a later bishop of Toledo who lived in his time, wrote a short Life of him. Felix says Julian was born and baptized at Toledo and taught by its bishop Eugenius.

Julian became bishop after Quiricus. His name stands first among the signatures at four councils of Toledo, from 681 to 688.

At King Ervig's command he wrote On the Proof of the Sixth Age, against Jews of his day. In the opening letter, Julian says they counted the years from Creation. By that count, they said, the world was still in its fifth age, so Christ had not yet come. He says he believes the king follows the surgeon, who cuts away rot before it reaches healthy limbs. That rot, he writes, he would call the tongues of the Jews.

In 688 the bishops met again at Toledo. Pope Benedict, now dead, had raised points against a statement of their faith two years before. One section of their answer bears Julian's name. If their critics still disagreed with the Fathers, they wrote, there was no point in arguing further.

Felix calls him a man full of the fear of the Lord.

Key Works

  • Prognosticon of the World to Come: three books, on the origin of death, on how the souls of the dead fare before the resurrection, and on the resurrection. His letter to Idalius, bishop of Barcelona, tells how it began
  • On the Proof of the Sixth Age: three books written at King Ervig's command, against Jews of his day. The opening letter says they reckoned the world was still in its fifth age, so Christ had not yet come. It calls their tongues the rot a surgeon cuts away before it reaches healthy limbs
  • History of King Wamba: the story of the rebellion of Paul against King Wamba, and of the king's war against him

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The fifteenth council of Toledo, in 688, defended a statement of the Spanish faith against Pope Benedict's objections. One section of its reply bears Julian's name.
  • Felix of Toledo, a later bishop who lived in his time, added a Life of him to Ildefonsus's book of famous writers. It lists his books.
  • Johannes Cochlaeus printed the Prognosticon at Leipzig in 1536, and Boetius Epo at Douai in 1564. Both meant its witness to defend their faith against the heretics of their day, the preface in Patrologia Latina 96 says.

How they're remembered

  • Felix calls him a man full of the fear of the Lord, generous in alms and quick to help the oppressed.
  • Nicolás Antonio says the Breviary of Toledo keeps his feast on 8 March, and Usuard's martyrology on 6 March. The Toledo edition's notes take 6 March as his death day.

What Is Uncertain

  • His life rests on his own letters and on Felix's short Life, a tribute by a later bishop of Toledo who lived in his time.
  • Whether he came from a Jewish family. Nicolás Antonio reports this as a story among later writers, from Rodericus of Toledo and the chronicle under the name of Isidore Pacensis. That chronicle runs to 754, says Theodor Mommsen, who edits it, and the name is false. Felix does not say it. Some doubt it, Antonio says, but many believe it with reason. The notes of Cardinal Lorenzana's Toledo edition, which Migne follows, warn the reader not to believe it readily, since its source is unknown.
  • When he became bishop. Nicolás Antonio has Quiricus dying in 681. The Toledo edition's notes count back from Felix's ten years, one month and seven days to 29 January 680.
  • The year of the Prognosticon. The preface in Patrologia Latina 96 guesses 688 or 689. Idalius is not known to have come to Toledo except for the council of 688, it says.
  • Whether he was a monk. Felix says he and the deacon Gudila wished to be, but God judged otherwise. Cardinal Lorenzana's notes reject later writers who counted him among the Benedictines.
