The Late Medieval Era · 1341-1351 · Constantinople, Athos, Thessalonica

Athos · Thessalonica, today in Greece

Gregory Palamas

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Hesychasm & the Palamite Synthesis.

Also called Gregory of Thessalonica, Gregorius Palamas, Γρηγόριος ὁ Παλαμᾶς

A monk of Mount Athos, later archbishop of Thessalonica, who answered Barlaam of Calabria when Barlaam mocked the hesychasts, the monks of silent prayer. He taught that God cannot be shared in his essence but is truly given in his uncreated energy. He was held in prison during the civil war that followed, and councils of 1341, 1347 and 1351 upheld his teaching; the historian Nikephoros Gregoras, his opponent, charged him with teaching many gods.

About This Person

Audio Brief 4:35

Read along

John Kantakouzenos, later emperor and an ally of Gregory Palamas, tells how a learned monk named Barlaam, from the south of Italy, went to a simple monk at Thessalonica. The monk was a hesychast, from the Greek word for stillness: one who prayed in silence. He heard that those who pray in quiet feel a joy beyond words, and see a light shining around them with their bodily eyes. Then, Kantakouzenos writes, Barlaam turned on them all, calling them deceivers and "navel-souls."

One of those monks, Gregory Palamas, answered him. Palamas lived on Mount Athos, a peninsula of monasteries in northern Greece. He taught that no one can share in what God is, his essence. Yet God gives himself truly in his energy, the way he acts. That energy is uncreated: not made, but God's own. So a monk joined to God in prayer is joined to God himself.

Raised at the imperial court, Kantakouzenos says, Palamas left his property and the emperor's favor while very young, and lived in the great monastery of Athanasios.

Philotheos says in a speech of praise that the monks of Thessalonica begged him to come down from the Mountain. But Kantakouzenos says he was already in the city, ill. Then he wrote his answer. Barlaam's sneer was that the monks thought the soul sat in the navel. Palamas wrote: "Those who call them navel-souls are, to begin with, plainly slandering the men they accuse. For which of them ever says that the soul is in the navel?"

The great teachers of the early church, the Fathers, had said two things about God. Palamas wrote: "So when you hear the Fathers say God's essence cannot be shared, understand the essence that does not go out and is not shown. When they say again it can be shared, understand the going-out that belongs to God by nature, his showing and his energy. Holding to both in this way, you will agree with the Fathers."

Barlaam then charged the monks with believing in two gods, and brought the case to the patriarch, the bishop of Constantinople. At a council of bishops there in 1341, Palamas made the defense. The council found the monks above Barlaam's charges. Barlaam asked pardon, and was warned he would be cut off from the Church unless he truly repented. The records of this council and the two that followed are the winning side's.

Then came civil war. The empress, ruling for her young son, fought Kantakouzenos, with the patriarch beside her. Palamas was condemned and held for years, at the end in a prison inside the palace. Three witnesses give three reasons. The patriarch wrote that Palamas defied the council and would not show his writings. Kantakouzenos says the patriarch envied him, and punished him for refusing to condemn Kantakouzenos. The historian Nikephoros Gregoras, who stood against Palamas, says the empress's letters gave his teaching as the reason, not politics.

In 1347, the empress had Palamas brought out as the war ended. A council deposed the patriarch and declared that Palamas spoke and thought in line with the church's theologians.

In 1351 Kantakouzenos, now emperor, called another council, and Palamas debated his opponents, Gregoras among them. It ruled for Palamas. Gregoras wrote that Palamas was "in labor with the teachings of many gods, and at the same time of no god." On the night it ended, he says, the bishops rose in anger to lay hands on his side.

The councils made Palamas's defense of silent prayer the teaching of the Orthodox Church. An editor of his sermons writes that a list read aloud in churches on the first Sunday of Lent acclaims his name every year. The next Sunday, he adds, keeps his memory. In the eighteenth century, the Philokalia, a Greek book of the old teachers of prayer, gathered his writings.

Philotheos says that as Palamas lay dying, he kept whispering one phrase: "The heavenly to the heavenly." It showed, Philotheos says, where he was going. He was sixty-three.

Timeline

Dates
No early witness gives his year of birth or of death. Philotheos Kokkinos, in his speech of praise, says his soul lived sixty-three years with its body, twelve of them in his leadership of the church. The 1857 editor of the homilies says the feast was made general by a synod under the patriarch Philotheos in 1368, six years after his death, which points to 1362; twelve years from 1347 would point to 1359, and neither is an early witness's year. The synod that heard Barlaam is headed July 1341 in the patriarchal register; the councils that upheld him met in 1341, 1347 and 1351
Place
Constantinople, where Philotheos says his well-born parents from the east settled and he was born their first child, and where Kantakouzenos says he was raised at court, his family being from Asia by descent; Mount Athos, first in the Great Lavra of Athanasios and later in a cell of quiet outside it, coming in only for the liturgy, and for ten years in a retreat he built near the skete at Beroea, by Kantakouzenos; Thessalonica, of which he was made archbishop, and which Gregoras says he entered by force and with difficulty after many journeys
Role
Monk of Mount Athos; archbishop of Thessalonica; theologian and defender of the hesychasts

Key Works

  • The Triads (Defenses of those who practice sacred quiet): nine treatises in three sets of three, as Philotheos names them, written against Barlaam's false writings; an extract is printed in the Philokalia of 1893. In it he answers the charge that the hesychasts put the soul in the navel, and says the body is not evil, and tells how Elijah put his head on his knees in prayer and ended the long drought
  • The One Hundred and Fifty Chapters: printed in Migne's Patrologia Graeca 150, with Migne's Latin, and in the Philokalia. One chapter names three things in God, his essence, his energy and the divine persons, and says those joined to God are joined by his uncreated energy and not by his essence, which cannot be shared; the end of another says how to read the Fathers when they call the essence both unshareable and shareable
  • The Hagioritic Tome: printed in Migne's Patrologia Graeca 150 under the title Hagioriticus Tomus de Quietistis, the tome of the monks of Mount Athos on the hesychasts

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • The councils of 1341, 1347 and 1351 made his defense of silent prayer the teaching of the Orthodox Church; the tomes are printed in Migne, and the tome of July 1341 in the patriarchal register of Miklosich and Müller. The tradition entry says the hesychast view became the standard of Orthodox theology.
  • The 1857 editor of his homilies writes that the Synodikon read on the first Sunday of Lent, the Sunday of Orthodoxy, gives eternal memory to Gregory of Thessalonica, who brought down Barlaam and Akindynos and defended the doctrine of God's natural energy and the unapproachable light, that the next Sunday was set every year for his memory, and that a synod under Philotheos made the feast general in 1368.
  • The Philokalia, printed at Venice, prints his writings on prayer under the name of Gregory of Thessalonica, and Paisius Velichkovsky's Slavonic Philokalia, printed by Optina in 1849, holds his piece on prayer.

How they're remembered

  • Philotheos Kokkinos, a patriarch and his friend, wrote a speech in his praise, and Neilos wrote a second. Kantakouzenos says that when Palamas was brought out of prison in 1347, he praised him and thanked him because he had borne much suffering for justice and truth on his behalf and had done and said nothing base to the end.
  • Nikephoros Gregoras kept the charge of many gods alive in his history, and letters from Cyprus speak of the godless and many-god Palamites.
  • John Covel, writing against prayer to the saints in 1722, calls him another hot-headed monk and chief of the same gang, and says he held that the light which shone about our Savior was God himself.

Open Questions

  • Why he was imprisoned. The patriarch John Kalekas, his opponent, writes in November 1344 that after the synod Palamas went on writing and speaking, did not come when summoned and ran off to Heraclea, and when brought back would not show his writings or give a confession, so he was held until he proved his mind sound. Kantakouzenos, his ally, says the patriarch envied him and suspected him of wanting the throne, told him to join the censures of Kantakouzenos, and when Palamas openly refused, prosecuted him for impiety and held him and those who thought as he did in prisons; elsewhere he says Palamas opposed the war on the emperor from the start and for it was sentenced to live in prison from beginning to end. Gregoras, his opponent, says letters from the empress and the patriarch, and from the young emperor, to the monks of Athos said Palamas was held not for siding with Kantakouzenos but for teaching a swarm of infinitely many uncreated divinities. All three agree he was held. Why is contested.
  • Whether his teaching made many gods. Barlaam first brought the charge of two gods in 1341, in the synod's own record. Gregoras charges many gods and no god, in his own voice, and says Palamas divided the light into countless uncreated divinities. Philotheos throws the same words back at Barlaam's side, calling it the heresy of those who deny the energies, of many gods and of no god. Gregoras reports Palamas answering that, when they voted him guilty of many gods, he voted them guilty of one god, and that he said he was neither a Jew nor an Ishmaelite, so that he might worship one God; the first report is in a damaged column of the Library's print. Letters from Cyprus use the charge too. The synods of 1347 and 1351 declared him sound; their records are the winning side's. That he taught many gods is contested.
  • What the 1341 synod judged. The tome and Kalekas say the monks were found above Barlaam's charges and Barlaam's words judged blasphemous, and that if Barlaam truly repented he would be pardoned and if not cut off from the church. Kalekas also says the synod forbade anyone to raise such questions further and urged the same on one other man, whose name the Library's scan garbles. The record is the winning side's.
  • Barlaam's own words. As quoted by his opponents in the tome of July 1341, and again by Kalekas, Barlaam wrote against the guide of the teaching for trying to overturn the Christians' mysteries by breathings-in, called him thunderstruck and foul, mocked the rule to pray always, Lord Jesus Christ, Son of God, have mercy on me, and said the light of the Transfiguration was not unapproachable and not the light of God's divinity. The scan of Kalekas's letter is damaged in places and the two documents agree. The Library holds no text of Barlaam's own.
  • Where Barlaam met the simple monk. Kantakouzenos says at Thessalonica. Philotheos says at Constantinople, after Barlaam left Thessalonica for the capital, and says he then went back to Thessalonica and attacked the monks there.
  • How he came to Thessalonica. Philotheos says the monks of Thessalonica, with Isidore leading, wrote begging him to come from his retreat on the Mountain, and that he came unwillingly, judging there is a time for quiet and a time for war; he first tried friends and then speaking face to face, urging Barlaam to keep to outside philosophy and leave prayer alone. Kantakouzenos says that after a long stay in the monasteries of Athos, with his body needing treatment, he came to Thessalonica, where he was when Barlaam's words were passed round. Philotheos quotes him as saying he would rather be insulted with the poor and lowly brothers than enjoy good report and human glory for a time.
  • His age at leaving for Athos. Philotheos says he took his two brothers with him and left Constantinople in late autumn. Kantakouzenos says that he and his two brothers, at a very young age, left property, parents and the emperor's honors to follow Christ. Neither gives an age as a number. Kantakouzenos says they put themselves under a spiritual father, and that after more than eight years under another, when he died, Gregory built a retreat near the skete at Beroea, shut himself in and stayed ten years until illness from the cold of the cave brought him near death.
  • Philotheos on the cell of quiet. He says that after raids drove him off he came back and set his cell of quiet outside the Great Lavra of Athanasios, at the house of Saint Sabbas.
  • The emperor in 1341. Kantakouzenos says the emperor died soon after the synod. The tome's title says the emperor presided while he lived.
  • The council of 1347. Kantakouzenos says the bishops deposed the patriarch John in writing and set out a tome saying Akindynos and his party think wrongly, and that Palamas and those like him speak and think in line with the wise theologians. The tome of 1351 recites it as carrying the signatures of thirty bishops. Kantakouzenos says the empress had Palamas and Andronikos Asan, shut up in prisons inside the palace, brought out and sent to Kantakouzenos to treat for peace.
  • The council of 1351. Kantakouzenos says it met in the palace at Blachernae with the emperor present, for four days, the debate being above all with Palamas of Thessalonica against the followers of Akindynos, whose heads were the metropolitans of Ephesus and Ganos, and Gregoras and Dexios. Gregoras, who was there, says that as they saw and heard it the tears ran like rivers, that it was far into the night and torches had been brought in, and that the bishops with the patriarch leapt up in deep anger and began to lay hands on his side. Gregoras is hostile.
  • Symeon the New Theologian. No held line has Palamas, a council or Philotheos name Symeon. Kallistos Telikoudes, a hesychast teacher of this world, lists among the works of quiet the chapters on prayer, above all those of the New Theologian, and the Philokalia prints both writers. The tradition entry's story on Symeon says Michel Le Quien called Symeon the father of the Palamite error; Le Quien's own line was not located in the Library.
  • Death. Philotheos says that as he lay dying the words the heavenly to the heavenly were heard again and again, as if in a circling round, showing where he was going, and adds a light that filled the room and his face at death. These are Philotheos's account, a partisan's, in one print only. The tradition entry says the second Sunday of Great Lent is kept in his memory across the Orthodox world.
  • Not held: Palamas's full Triads in a critical text; his letters from captivity; a located text of Barlaam's letters. The 1857 editor, who writes five centuries later for the church that keeps his feast, is the only held witness for the Synodikon and the Sunday.
