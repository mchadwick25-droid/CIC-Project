Orthodoxy under the Ottomans — the Second Act

Anatolia, the Balkans and the Greek islands, 1650 to 1821 — a church that was also a department of the Ottoman state, and whose patriarch was hanged from his own front gate when that arrangement finally broke.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 2:42

Read along

For a century and a half after 1650 the Orthodox church of the Ottoman Empire was both a church and an arm of the state: the Ecumenical Patriarch in Constantinople answered to the Sultan for the obedience of all Orthodox subjects, collected their taxes and judged their marriages and inheritances. It was a position of real power and total insecurity — patriarchs were deposed and restored constantly. And it came increasingly to be held under the influence of the Phanariots, the wealthy Greek families of Constantinople's Phanar quarter who supplied the empire's interpreters and the princes of its Danubian provinces. In 1672 the Synod of Jerusalem, under Patriarch Dositheus, issued a confession answering the Calvinist ideas that had been circulating under an Orthodox name — the East's own equivalent of the West's confessional settlements. That power also worked against its neighbors: the Serbian patriarchate of Peć was abolished in 1766 and the archbishopric of Ohrid in 1767, both absorbed into Constantinople. It ended violently — when the Greek revolt broke out in 1821, Patriarch Gregory V was hanged in his vestments from the gate of his own patriarchate.

Not every Orthodox church of these years stood under Constantinople. Fortescue, a Catholic writer, lists two that stood apart. One is Karlovci, made of Orthodox Serbs in Hungary. He dates it to 1765. The other is Montenegro, with one independent diocese on the Black Mountain. He also dates it to 1765. His dates mark independence as he counts it.

A Bulgarian revival began in these years. In 1762 Paisii, a monk of Hilendar, wrote a history of the Bulgarians. Yordan Ivanov printed it in 1914. He says Paisii called to his readers: «Българино! знай своя родъ и езикъ» ("Bulgarian! know your own people and language", a rendering). Ivanov adds that Paisii named three causes of his people's spiritual ruin. They were no freedom, no national hierarchy and no national schools. Ivanov writes in the Bulgarian national tradition. He blames the Greek clergy. No Greek account of this dispute was read.

Documented Stories

The Monk Who Preached under a Wooden Cross

Kosmas of Aetolia was a monk of Philotheou on Mount Athos who left the mountain in 1760, at about forty-six, with the patriarch's blessing to preach. For nineteen years he walked western Greece, Thessaly, Epirus, the Ionian islands and southern Albania, and preached in the open air, standing on a stool beneath a wooden cross his followers planted, in the simplest Greek, for an hour or two, to crowds that walked from other villages to hear him. His hearers wrote down what he said, and the Teachings survive in their versions: that they should keep Sunday and stop trading on it, learn to read, teach their children Greek and not only Albanian or Vlach, forgive, free their slaves, stop cursing, love the poor; that a school mattered more than a church, since a school taught what a church was for. He asked each village to found one and to pay a teacher, and claimed before his death to have founded ten Greek schools and two hundred common ones. The Ottoman authorities tolerated him for years; the pasha of Berat did not, and on 24 August 1779 he was arrested near the village of Kolikontasi in Albania and hanged from a tree, his body thrown in the river and recovered by Christians. His biographer, writing in 1814, blamed the Jews of Ioannina for bribing the pasha, a charge that appears in his own sermons' hostility to Jewish traders and that the sources do not otherwise support.

The Patriarch Who Cursed the Revolt and Was Hanged for It

Gregory V had been patriarch of Constantinople three times, deposed twice, and knew what his office was: the guarantor to the Sultan of the obedience of every Orthodox subject in the empire, answerable with his life. When Alexander Ypsilantis crossed into Moldavia in March 1821 and raised the Greek revolt, and word came of the rising in the Peloponnese, Gregory did what the office required. On 23 March, with the Holy Synod and the patriarch of Jerusalem, he signed and published an anathema against Ypsilantis and all who followed him, as rebels against the divinely ordained power and traitors to their benefactor the Sultan, and ordered it read in every church; he had already, under pressure, denounced the secret society that was behind the revolt. On Easter Sunday, 22 April 1821, after he had celebrated the liturgy, janissaries took him from the patriarchate, and he was hanged in his vestments from the lintel of its central gate, with a sentence pinned to his body naming him a traitor; three bishops were hanged elsewhere in the city that day. The body hung three days, and was then, by the Ottomans' order, given to Jews of the city to drag to the Golden Horn, a detail the Greek accounts record and that fed reprisals against Jews in the Morea. The revolutionaries whom he had cursed made him a martyr, the church canonized him in 1921, and the gate he was hanged from has been kept closed since.

Connections

In Its Own Record

This is the second act of Orthodoxy under Ottoman rule. In 1672 the Synod of Jerusalem, led by Patriarch Dositheus, answered French Calvinists. They had claimed that the Eastern Church shared their faith. Its confession was the East's own statement of faith, to set beside those of the West. This century also saw the most neomartyrs, and the rise of the Phanariot families. The Serbian patriarchate of Peć was done away with in 1766. A year later the same thing happened to the archbishopric of Ohrid. After the Great Migration of the Serbs in 1690, their church at Karlovci lay outside the Ottoman millet system. The story ends at Easter 1821, when Patriarch Gregory V was hanged at his own gate.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

For a century and a half, the Ecumenical Patriarch in Constantinople was both a church leader and an arm of Ottoman government, collecting taxes and judging marriages for Orthodox subjects while patriarchs themselves were deposed and restored constantly. In 1672 the Synod of Jerusalem answered Calvinist ideas that had been spreading under an Orthodox name, and the arrangement ended violently in 1821, when Patriarch Gregory V was hanged in his vestments from the gate of his own patriarchate — a gate that has stayed welded shut ever since.

Legacy

The arrangement kept Orthodox Christianity intact through centuries of Muslim rule. The price was a church tied to a foreign state, with Greek church leaders ruling over Slavs and Arabs. That rule bred resentment. In 1762 the Bulgarian monk Paisii of Hilendar wrote that his people suffered much wrong from Greek bishops. When the empire's Christian provinces became nations in the nineteenth century, each took its own church with it. Today's pattern of national Orthodox churches grew out of that break-up. Gregory V's gate at the Phanar has never been opened since 1821. It is welded shut and painted black, and the patriarchate is still there behind it.

Experience It Today

  • St George's Cathedral at the Phanar, Istanbul — the seat of the Ecumenical Patriarchate, with the gate sealed since Gregory V was hanged from it in 1821
