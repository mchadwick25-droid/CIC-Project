East African Christianity & the Uganda Martyrs

The East African coast and the kingdom of Buganda, 1844 to 1906 — Anglican and Catholic converts burned together at Namugongo, and a church that spread out of their ashes across the region.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:12

Read along

Christianity came back to East Africa along the coast first — Johann Ludwig Krapf landed at Mombasa in 1844 and began the language and mapping work later missions relied on — and then inland to the kingdom of Buganda, where Anglican missionaries arrived in 1877 and French Catholic White Fathers in 1879. Their converts were largely young men of the royal court, and when the kabaka Mwanga II turned on them between 1885 and 1887 he killed both kinds together: Anglican and Catholic pages executed side by side, the largest group burned alive at Namugongo on 3 June 1886 under the leadership of Charles Lwanga, who is said to have arranged his own pyre. Persecution did what it often does. The church grew, and Baganda converts carried it outward as evangelists into the surrounding kingdoms faster than any mission society could have, so that by the agreement of 1900 that settled Buganda's relations with Britain, it was a Christian kingdom.

Documented Stories

The Battle of Mengo

The converts who had survived Mwanga's persecution had by 1889 restored him to the throne, and the Christian chiefs who now ran the kingdom were divided, as their missionaries were, into the Ingleza, the Protestants, and the Fransa, the Catholics. The parties held chieftaincies and guns, and the kabaka leaned toward the Catholics, who were more numerous. In December 1890 Captain Frederick Lugard arrived for the Imperial British East Africa Company with a small force and a Maxim gun, and the factions armed. In January 1892 a Protestant was killed by a Catholic over a stolen gun, the Catholic party refused to surrender the killer, and Lugard, judging that the Catholics meant to drive the Company out, distributed some five hundred rifles to the Protestants. On 24 January the two parties fought in the streets and on the hills of Mengo, the capital; Lugard's Maxim, fired from the Company's fort on Kampala hill, broke the Catholic advance, and Mwanga fled with the Catholic chiefs to an island in the lake, shelled the following week. Hundreds died, most of them Catholic converts; the White Fathers' mission was burned. The peace Lugard imposed divided Buganda's counties between the religions, Protestants holding the majority and the great offices, and the settlement of 1900 wrote the division into the kingdom's constitution. The two churches, whose martyrs had died together, grew for the next century as the parties of a divided state. Both missions' accounts blame the other's, and both record the dead.

Apolo at Mboga

Apolo Kivebulaya had been a soldier in Buganda's religious wars, was baptized in 1895 at about thirty, and within months asked the Church Missionary Society to send him as a teacher to Toro; from there he went further, over the Ruwenzori, to Mboga, a small chiefdom on the Congo side whose people spoke languages he did not. He arrived in 1896 with a Luganda Bible, a hoe and a blanket, built a hut, and began teaching reading to whoever came, with the chief Tabaro's leave. Within the year Tabaro turned. Apolo's diary records that the chief's advisers accused him of bewitching a dead child, and that Tabaro had him flogged and put in the stocks; released, he was ordered out and went, barefoot and beaten, back over the mountains to Toro, where he was tried at Mboga's instance and acquitted. He then asked to go back. In 1897 he returned to Mboga, to the same chief, who received him; the diary says he told Tabaro he had forgiven him and had come back because the people of Mboga had no one else to teach them. Tabaro was baptized some years later. Apolo was ordained priest in 1903 and lived at Mboga until his death in 1933, preaching among the Bambuti of the Ituri forest in his last years, and asking to be buried with his head toward the forest, so the direction of the work would be plain. The Anglican church of the Congo dates itself from his hut.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Johann Ludwig Krapf began mission work on the coast in 1844, and the Universities' Mission to Central Africa followed in 1857. The Church Missionary Society and the Catholic White Fathers reached Buganda in 1877 and 1879. Anglican and Catholic pages together became the Uganda Martyrs, executed between 1885 and 1887, with Charles Lwanga burned at Namugongo in 1886. By the 1900 Agreement that settled Buganda's relations with Britain, it was a Christian kingdom. The revival movement that swept the same churches decades later grew out of this same soil.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

When the kabaka Mwanga II turned on Buganda's Anglican and Catholic converts in 1885-87, he killed both kinds together, the largest group burned alive at Namugongo under Charles Lwanga, who is said to have arranged his own pyre. The persecution did not stop the faith's spread; Baganda converts carried it outward as evangelists to neighboring kingdoms faster than any mission society could have managed on its own.

Legacy

Uganda became one of the most heavily Christian countries in Africa, and the martyrs are honored on both sides of the division that produced them — canonized by the Catholic Church in 1964, commemorated in the Anglican calendar, with shrines of both communions at Namugongo that draw enormous crowds every 3 June. The pattern set here mattered as much as the memory. African Christians carrying the faith to neighboring peoples on their own initiative is how Christianity actually spread across this region, and the revival that swept these same churches from the 1930s grew out of the same soil.

Experience It Today
