The Imperial Church Era · c. 311-383 · Moesia, Constantinople

Moesia · Constantinople, today in Turkey

Ulfila

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Homoian ('Arian') Christianity.

Known for
Bishop of the Goths, who led a crowd of Gothic Christians across the Danube into the Roman Empire, gave the Goths an alphabet and the Scriptures in their own language, and taught the Homoian faith.
Also called
Wulfila, Ulfilas, Ulphilas, Urphilas
Dates
Born about 311; made bishop at thirty; died at Constantinople in 383 (Kauffmann's dates, a modern judgment worked from Auxentius's numbers)
Place
The Goths' land beyond the Danube; later Moesia, on the Roman side
Role
Bishop of the Goths

About This Person

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 4:22

Ulfila had been bishop of the Goths for seven years when persecution drove him out, after many men and women had died as martyrs. He led a great crowd of believers across the Danube, and they were received with honor on Roman soil, under the emperor Constantius. His pupil Auxentius, who wrote in his praise and on whom nearly all of this life rests, told it as a new Exodus.

"Through Moses, God freed his people from the power and violence of Pharaoh and the Egyptians. He made them cross the sea, and made sure they would serve him. So too, through this man, God freed those who confessed his holy only Son from the barbarians' land. He made them cross the Danube, and serve him in the mountains, after the saints' example."

Those who confessed had held to the faith under persecution, and the barbarians' land was the Goths' country. Ulfila gave the Goths letters and the Scriptures in their own tongue. To his death he taught them a creed that called the Son like the Father, according to the Scriptures. His opponents called that faith Arian, after Arius, a priest who taught that the Son had a beginning.

Auxentius says he was made bishop at thirty. Philostorgius, a historian of the next century, says it was at the emperor's court, by Eusebius and other bishops.

Among the Goths, Auxentius says, Ulfila taught a people starved of preaching and made Christians into true Christians.

Philostorgius says Ulfila invented letters for the Goths and translated all of Scripture except the Books of Kings. Those told of war, he explains, and the Goths were too fond of war already. Two church historians, Socrates and Sozomen, credit him with both. Fragments of a Gothic Bible survive without a translator's name.

His pupil records what he taught. The Father alone is unbegotten, never begotten by anyone, without beginning, and the one true God. The Son is God and maker of all creation, yet has the Father as his God, and is like the Father, according to the Scriptures. The Greek for like, homoios, gives these churches the name Homoian. Nicaea had called the Son of one being with the Father, sharing his very being, and Ulfila spurned that as a devilish invention.

Under the emperor Constantius, he joined the bishops at Constantinople in confirming a creed that banned the word "substance" for God, saying Scripture never uses it. But when did he take that faith? It matters because the Goths still held that faith in the historians' day. Socrates puts the Goths' faith down to the emperor Valens, whose help in a Gothic civil war won a chief over to his faith. But Sozomen says Ulfila was why the barbarians on the Danube followed Arius.

Socrates says Ulfila first took the creed under Constantius, holding Nicaea's faith before. Sozomen says he signed from thoughtlessness, and changed sides years later, under Valens, on an embassy seeking land for the Goths. The other side's leaders, he heard, promised help if he joined them. He did, under pressure or perhaps from conviction. Theodoret also puts it under Valens, when a bishop of the emperor's party won him over with eloquence and bribes.

Under Valens, Socrates says, Ulfila also taught the people of the Gothic chief Athanaric. The chief took this as an attack on his ancestors' religion and punished the Christians, so that many died as martyrs.

Later the emperor ordered Ulfila to Constantinople for a debate, to stop his opponents teaching in his churches. As he entered the city, he fell ill at once. He died there.

He left his faith in writing to his people, by testament. Of the Father, he wrote, "alone unbegotten"; of the Son, "subject and obedient in all things" to his God the Father. He wrote that he had always believed so, and that in this faith alone he made his crossing to his Lord.

Quotations are given in our modern English. The printed wording of each is below.

Quotations and references

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “Through Moses, God freed his people from the power and violence of Pharaoh and the Egyptians. He made them cross the sea, and made sure they would serve him. So too, through this man, God freed those who confessed his holy only Son from the barbarians' land. He made them cross the Danube, and serve him in the mountains, after the saints' example.”
    As printed
    “sicuti deus per Moysem de potentia et uiolentia Faraonis et Egyptiorum poptäum suum liberauit et per mare transire fecit et sibi seruire prouidit, ita et per sepe dictum deus confessores sancti fili sui unigeniti de uarbarico liberauit et per Danubium transire fecit et in montibus secundum sanctorum imitationem sibi seruire.”
    Auxentius of Durostorum, Letter on the faith, life and death of Ulfila, ed. Friedrich Kauffmann, Aus der Schule des Wulfila (1899); Latin
    The file is OCR and reads poptäum where the sense is populum, his people; Streitberg's second print of the passage (lines 836-842 of his file) reads populum, but it follows Kauffmann's text almost throughout, so the two prints are not independent. The last clause has no verb of its own and takes fecit from the one before, so the rendering gives serve him in the mountains after He made them cross the Danube. Confessores are those who held to the faith under persecution; de uarbarico is from the barbarians' land, the Goths' country outside the empire.
  • In our modern English
    “substance”
    As printed
    “the terms “substance ” and “hypostasis” should never again be used in reference to God.”
    Sozomen, Church History 4.24, in Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, second series, vol. 2
    Substance is the English term for the Greek ousia, and the same clause bans hypostasis; Socrates words the ban as substance or subsistence (lines 9606-9608 of the same file). The Greek of Sozomen's book 4 is not held.
  • In our modern English
    “alone unbegotten”
    As printed
    “solum ingenitum”
    Ulfila's creed, in Auxentius of Durostorum, Letter on the faith, life and death of Ulfila, ed. Friedrich Kauffmann, Aus der Schule des Wulfila (1899)
    The file is OCR, and Streitberg's second print reads the same (line 886 of his file). Solum may govern ingenitum alone or ingenitum et inuisiuilem together; the brief quotes only alone unbegotten.
  • In our modern English
    “subject and obedient in all things”
    As printed
    “et filium subditum et oboedientem suo in omnibus deo patri”
    Ulfila's creed, in Auxentius of Durostorum, Letter on the faith, life and death of Ulfila, ed. Friedrich Kauffmann, Aus der Schule des Wulfila (1899)
    The file is OCR, and Streitberg's second print reads the same apart from a scan slip, oboedieniem (line 902 of his file). Subditum is subject in rank. The same words stand at lines 15322-15323 of the Holy Spirit, subject to the Son; the brief's are of the Son to the Father. In the Latin, in all things stands between his and God the Father; the brief ends the quotation at all things and gives to his God the Father in its own words.

Key Works

  • The Gothic Bible: the Scriptures in Gothic, which Philostorgius, Socrates and Sozomen say he made; Gothic Gospels survive in part in the Codex Argenteus at Uppsala, written in silver and gold on purple parchment, in the fifth or sixth century by Streitberg's dating, and Paul's letters in palimpsests from Bobbio, now at Milan, all without a translator's name; Streitberg takes them for his, a modern judgment
  • His creed, left in his testament: the faith he left to his people at his death, as Auxentius of Durostorum gives it in Latin

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Socrates of Constantinople and Sozomen both say the Goths of their own day held the Arian faith, and Sozomen traces it to Ulfila's teaching.
  • Sozomen says Selinus, once Ulfila's secretary, succeeded him as bishop and could teach in Gothic and in Greek.
  • Fragments of a Gothic Bible survive without a translator's name, edited by Ernst Bernhardt in 1884 and by Wilhelm Streitberg in 1908.
  • Jordanes, a historian of the Goths writing in 551, says the Lesser Goths, whose priest and primate was Vulfila, still lived in Moesia, a numerous people but poor and unwarlike.
  • Isidore of Seville, writing in Gothic Spain, says Gulfilas, the Goths' bishop, made Gothic letters and turned the books of the new and old testaments into Gothic, and that the Goths then built churches of their own teaching.
  • Theodoret says that up to his own day the Goths held the Father greater than the Son but would not call the Son a creature.
  • Walafrid Strabo, in the ninth century, says some still held the monuments of the divine books the Goths translated into their own tongue; he names no translator.

How they're remembered

  • Photius, in his summary of Philostorgius, says the emperor called Ulfila "the Moses of his day" and that Philostorgius "is loud in his praises of this Urphilas".
  • Sozomen, who calls his teaching Arian, still says he "had exposed himself to innumerable perils in defense of the faith" while the Goths were pagan.
  • His pupil Auxentius of Durostorum calls him a most holy man, a confessor of Christ and a teacher of piety.

At Major Councils

  • Councils of Ariminum and Seleucia, 359: Signed the creed at Constantinople

What Is Uncertain

  • Auxentius of Durostorum, his pupil and a partisan, gives Ulfila's deathbed words that he had always believed so. Socrates of Constantinople, a Nicene historian, says he held the Nicene creed until he signed the creed of the council at Constantinople under Constantius. Sozomen, also Nicene, says he took part in that council without leaving the Nicene faith, and joined the Arians later, under Valens.
  • Philostorgius, a follower of Eunomius of Cyzicus, calls Ulfila the Goths' first bishop. Socrates names an earlier bishop of the Goths, Theophilus, who signed at Nicaea, and calls Ulfila his disciple. Philostorgius survives mainly in the summary of Photius, who was hostile to him.
  • Philostorgius says Ulfila was made bishop by Eusebius and the other bishops with him, while on an embassy to Constantine. Friedrich Kauffmann, Auxentius's editor in 1899, reads Constantius for Constantine, places it at a council of Antioch in 341, and thinks Ulfila was made a country bishop at first, each a modern judgment. Philostorgius gives no see. Louis Duchesne, in his history of the early church (1907), names him Eusebius of Nicomedia, a modern judgment. Kauffmann notes that Eusebius of Nicomedia, by then bishop of Constantinople, was at the synod of 341.
  • Auxentius has Ulfila driven out after seven years as bishop and received by Constantius; Kauffmann dates the crossing to 348 or 349, a modern judgment. Sozomen has Ulfila lead the Goths' embassy when they crossed the Danube fleeing the Huns, under Valens.
  • Auxentius's numbers echo Scripture: bishop at thirty, like David, Joseph and Christ, and forty years in all, the span of the saints he followed. Kauffmann notes that the age often treated numbers this way, but counts them as real years, a modern judgment.
  • The Gothic fragments carry no translator's name. Streitberg sees no doubt that they are the remains of Ulfila's translation, a modern judgment. Philostorgius says he left out the Books of Kings.
  • Photius reports Philostorgius as saying that Ulfila and his Goths held Philostorgius's own views. Auxentius has Ulfila call the Son like the Father according to the Scriptures.
  • Kauffmann reports Georg Waitz's doubt whether the deathbed creed was left in Latin in the form that survives, a modern judgment.
  • Theodoret, a Nicene historian, puts his change under Valens: the bishop Eudoxius won him with eloquence and bribes, and he persuaded the Goths that the quarrel was one of personal rivalry, not doctrine. The bribes are Theodoret's charge.
  • Philostorgius alone says his forebears were captives taken in a Gothic raid under Valerian and Gallienus, from a Cappadocian village called Sadagolthina, near Parnassus.
  • Sozomen says the officers of Athanaric took a statue on a chariot to the tents of those suspected of being Christians and burned those who refused to worship it, together with their tents. He adds that he has heard of a church, which was a tent, burned with women and children inside. Socrates says only that Athanaric punished the Christians, so that many died as martyrs.
  • Auxentius says that for forty years Ulfila preached in Greek, Latin and Gothic and left many treatises and interpretations in all three; he does not mention a translation of the Bible. Philostorgius, Socrates and Sozomen are the witnesses to the letters and the Bible, and Socrates sets them under Valens.
  • In the creed Auxentius keeps, the Holy Spirit is neither God nor Lord but the faithful minister of Christ, not equal to the Son but subject and obedient to him in all things.
  • Auxentius calls the city where Ulfila died Constantinople, or rather Christianople, and says his fellow bishops honored him there in a great crowd of Christians.
  • Socrates names a bishop of the Goths, Selenas, a Goth by his father's side and a Phrygian by his mother's. His translator, Zenos, notes that Sozomen makes him Ulfila's secretary and successor.
  • Philostorgius says the emperor settled the Goths Ulfila brought over in different parts of Moesia, as he thought best, and held Ulfila in such honor that he often spoke of him as the Moses of his day.
  • Sozomen says about fifty bishops met at Constantinople, Ulfila among them, and confirmed the formulary of Ariminum, adding that the terms substance and hypostasis should never again be used of God. He gives the reign of Constantius, not the year; 360 is the date Kauffmann, Duchesne and Streitberg give, a modern judgment that no held old source gives.
  • Philostorgius says the Christian captives carried off from Cappadocia by Gothic raiders, among them Ulfila's forebears, brought a great number of Goths to the faith.
  • Auxentius says the emperor ordered Ulfila to Constantinople once forty years were complete, counting seven years among the Goths and thirty-three on Roman soil.
