The Imperial Church Era · c. 311-383 · Moesia, Constantinople

Moesia · Constantinople, today in Turkey

Ulfila

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Homoian ('Arian') Christianity.

Known for
Bishop of the Goths, who led a crowd of Gothic Christians across the Danube into the Roman Empire, gave the Goths an alphabet and the Scriptures in their own language, and taught the Homoian faith.
Also called
Wulfila, Ulfilas, Ulphilas, Urphilas
Dates
Born about 311; made bishop at thirty; died at Constantinople in 383 (Kauffmann's dates, worked from Auxentius's numbers)
Place
The Goths' land beyond the Danube; later Moesia, on the Roman side
Role
Bishop of the Goths

About This Person

Ulfila had been bishop among the Goths for just seven years when he was forced out. His pupil Auxentius of Durostorum, who wrote in his praise, tells it. A godless judge of the Goths stirred up persecution in their land, and many men and women died as martyrs. Ulfila was driven out with a great crowd of believers. They crossed the Danube, and the emperor Constantius received them with honour on Roman soil.

Ulfila led the Goths' church in the fourth century. Philostorgius, a church historian of the next century and a follower of Eunomius of Cyzicus, says his forebears were Cappadocians taken captive by Gothic raiders. His history survives mainly in a summary by Photius, a later patriarch of Constantinople who was hostile to it. It calls Ulfila the Goths' first bishop, and says the emperor named him "the Moses of his day." Socrates of Constantinople names an earlier one, Theophilus, who signed at Nicaea.

Auxentius says Ulfila was made bishop at thirty, after serving as a reader. Friedrich Kauffmann, who edited Auxentius in 1899, works from these numbers to a birth in 311.

Philostorgius says Ulfila made an alphabet for the Goths and put the Scriptures into their language. He left out the Books of Kings, because the Goths were already fond of war. The surviving Gothic fragments carry no translator's name. Wilhelm Streitberg, who edited them in 1908, sees no doubt that they are the remains of Ulfila's translation.

Auxentius sets out his teacher's faith. The Father alone is unbegotten, the one true God. The Son is God and the maker of all creation, yet the Father is greater. Ulfila called the Nicene teaching of one substance a devil's invention. He turned from the teaching of like substance too. He preached for forty years in Greek, Latin and Gothic.

Socrates, a Nicene historian, says Ulfila held the Nicene creed until he signed the creed of a council at Constantinople in 360. Sozomen, another Nicene historian, says he joined the Arians later, pressed by the needs of an embassy. Or he was "possibly, thinking that it was better to hold such views concerning the Divine nature."

In 383, by Kauffmann's date, the emperor summoned the bishops to Constantinople. Ulfila went, meaning to debate a rival party, but fell ill there and died. He left his people a written creed. In this one true faith, it says, he was passing over to his Lord.

Key Works

  • The Gothic Bible: the Scriptures in Gothic; the Gospels survive in part in the Codex Argenteus at Uppsala, written in silver and gold on purple parchment, and Paul's letters in palimpsests from Bobbio, now at Milan
  • His creed, left in his testament: the faith he left to his people at his death, as Auxentius of Durostorum gives it in Latin

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Socrates of Constantinople and Sozomen both say the Goths of their own day held the Arian faith, and Sozomen traces it to Ulfila's teaching.
  • Sozomen says Selinus, once Ulfila's secretary, succeeded him as bishop and could teach in Gothic and in Greek.
  • The Gothic Bible survives in fragments, edited by Ernst Bernhardt in 1884 and by Wilhelm Streitberg in 1908.

How they're remembered

  • Photius, in his summary of Philostorgius, says the emperor called Ulfila "the Moses of his day" and that Philostorgius "is loud in his praises of this Urphilas".
  • Sozomen, who calls his teaching Arian, still says he "had exposed himself to innumerable perils in defense of the faith" while the Goths were pagan.
  • His pupil Auxentius of Durostorum calls him a most holy man, a confessor of Christ and a teacher of piety.

At Major Councils

  • Councils of Ariminum and Seleucia, 359: Signed the creed at Constantinople

What Is Uncertain

  • Auxentius of Durostorum, his pupil and a partisan, gives Ulfila's deathbed words that he had always believed so. Socrates of Constantinople, a Nicene historian, says he held the Nicene creed until he signed the creed of the council at Constantinople in 360. Sozomen, also Nicene, says he took part in that council without leaving the Nicene faith, and joined the Arians later, under Valens.
  • Philostorgius, a follower of Eunomius of Cyzicus, calls Ulfila the Goths' first bishop. Socrates names an earlier bishop of the Goths, Theophilus, who signed at Nicaea, and calls Ulfila his disciple. Philostorgius survives mainly in the summary of Photius, who was hostile to him.
  • Philostorgius says Ulfila was made bishop by Eusebius and the other bishops with him, while on an embassy to Constantine. Friedrich Kauffmann, Auxentius's editor in 1899, reads Constantius for Constantine, places it at a council of Antioch in 341, and thinks Ulfila was made a country bishop at first. Philostorgius gives no see. Louis Duchesne, in his history of the early church (1907), names him Eusebius of Nicomedia. Kauffmann notes that Eusebius of Nicomedia, by then bishop of Constantinople, was at the synod of 341.
  • Auxentius has Ulfila driven out after seven years as bishop and received by Constantius; Kauffmann dates the crossing to 348 or 349. Sozomen has Ulfila lead the Goths' embassy when they crossed the Danube fleeing the Huns, under Valens.
  • Auxentius's numbers echo Scripture: bishop at thirty, like David, Joseph and Christ, and forty years in all, the span of the saints he followed. Kauffmann notes that the age often treated numbers this way, but counts them as real years.
  • The Gothic fragments carry no translator's name. Streitberg sees no doubt that they are the remains of Ulfila's translation. Philostorgius says he left out the Books of Kings.
  • Photius reports Philostorgius as saying that Ulfila and his Goths held Philostorgius's own views. Auxentius has Ulfila call the Son like the Father according to the Scriptures.
  • Kauffmann reports Georg Waitz's doubt whether the deathbed creed was left in Latin in the form that survives.
