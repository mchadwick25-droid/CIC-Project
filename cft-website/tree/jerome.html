The Imperial Church Era · 374-420 · Bethlehem, Rome

Bethlehem · Rome, today in Italy

Jerome

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity.

Known for
Latin scholar and monk who corrected the Latin Gospels against the Greek at Rome for Damasus, then learned Hebrew and at Bethlehem put the Old Testament into Latin from the Hebrew. His version was fought over in his lifetime, by Rufinus and by Augustine among others, and in time became the Western church's standard Latin Bible. His letters tell of the Roman women who studied with him, above all Paula.
Also called
Hieronymus
Dates
The sources differ on both ends of his life. Prosper's Chronicle, under 420, says he died in the ninety-first year of his age, on 30 September, which puts his birth about 330. Richardson's note to On Illustrious Men gives born 331, died 420. W. H. Fremantle's tables give born 345 and died 20 September 420. A note by Philip Schaff to the City of God gives his death at Bethlehem in 419. By Fremantle's dates he lived in the desert of Chalcis in 374 to 379, was at Rome in 382 to 385, left Rome in August 385, and settled at Bethlehem in 386. Fremantle dates the Old Testament from the Hebrew to about 391 to about 404
Place
Stridon (Strido in the print), which he says stood on the border of Dalmatia and Pannonia and was overthrown by the Goths; the desert of Chalcis; Rome; Bethlehem
Role
Monk, priest and scholar; translator of the Scriptures into Latin

About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Jerome was a young man in the desert when fasting failed to quiet his restless mind. So he went to a brother who, before his conversion, had been a Jew, and asked to be taught Hebrew. Years later he wrote of what it cost a scholar fresh from Rome's great Latin writers. "I began to learn my letters all over again, and worked at sounding out words that were harsh and throaty."

Jerome put the Old Testament into Latin from the Hebrew itself, not from the old Greek translation the churches read, from which their Latin version had been made. His Bible was fought over in his lifetime. In time it became the Western church's standard text.

In the 380s Damasus, the bishop of Rome, urged him to correct the Latin Gospels the churches already had against the Greek. Jerome wrote that there were almost as many forms of the text as copies.

Most of what we know of Jerome, and of the women around him, comes from his own letters. At Rome, he wrote, he often found himself among women vowed to God, and to some he explained the Scriptures. Then Rome turned on him. When the young daughter of Paula, a Roman widow of a great family, died, people in the funeral crowd, he wrote, said fasting had killed her. They asked why the monks were not thrown into the Tiber. He was accused over his friendship with Paula, though he says his accuser later took it back. He sailed in August, at a time he chose, he says.

Paula followed him east and built a monastery for men and houses for women at Bethlehem. When he was stuck over the Bible, he wrote, she pressed him with fresh questions, and she learned Hebrew. Palladius, a monk on his opponents' side, charged instead that Jerome held her back by his jealousy. From about 391 he addressed many prefaces to Paula and her daughter.

Augustine, a bishop in Africa, begged him not to translate the Scriptures afresh unless he marked where he differed from the old Greek, which Augustine held in the highest esteem. Rufinus of Aquileia, once Jerome's close friend, had fallen out with him over Origen, a Greek scholar whose ideas were under attack. In 400 Rufinus wrote against him. The new translation, he charged, was made under the influence of the Jews, and it tampered with the Scriptures as the apostles handed them down. "Who but you would have laid hands on God's gift, and on the inheritance of the apostles?"

In 403 Augustine told Jerome how a bishop at Oea had Jerome's Jonah read in church. The old Latin said the plant God grew to shade the prophet was a gourd. Jerome had written ivy. Augustine reported, and by the Greeks he means the Greek-speakers in the town: "The Greeks above all found fault with the reading, and fanned the charge that it was false. Such an uproar broke out in the congregation that the bishop was forced to demand the Jews' testimony. The town was Oea." The Jews, Augustine says, whether from ignorance or spite, said the Hebrew agreed with the old version, and the bishop corrected the reading so as not to lose his congregation.

Jerome answered sharply that the Hebrew names a plant that is neither gourd nor ivy. Augustine still would not have the Hebrew version read in church. Yet in 418 Jerome wrote that Catholics revered Augustine as the restorer of the ancient faith.

Jerome died at Bethlehem in 420. Two centuries later a bishop in Spain wrote that his translation was rightly preferred to the others. In 1546 the Council of Trent declared the "old and Vulgate edition," long used in the church, the authentic Latin text, and forbade anyone to reject it. The decree does not name Jerome, but that Latin Bible was built largely on his work.

About 411 he wrote of that first hard lesson in Hebrew: "But I thank the Lord that from this seed of learning, sown in bitterness, I now gather sweet fruit."

Quotations are given in our modern English. The printed wording of each is below.

Key Works

  • The Latin Scriptures: the revised Gospels and the Old Testament from the Hebrew: Fremantle describes three stages: at Rome in 382 to 385 only from the Greek, the New Testament and the Psalms; at Bethlehem first from the Septuagint with marks where it differs from the Hebrew; then, from 390 to 404, directly from the Hebrew. The Gospels preface, addressed to Damasus, tells the Roman commission. The Hebrew version was made at Bethlehem at the request of private friends, and many of its prefaces are addressed to Paula and her daughter Eustochium. Only Fremantle's English of the prefaces is held, and no Latin text of the translation itself
  • Letters: Letter 125 tells how he took up Hebrew in the desert; Letter 39 and Letter 45 tell Rome's turn against him; Letter 108 is his memorial of Paula; his letters to Augustine survive in Augustine's collection. His own letter collection is held in English only, in Fremantle's translation of 1893. The letters to Augustine are held in Latin and English
  • On Illustrious Men: a catalog of Christian writers, with an entry on himself that says it was written in the fourteenth year of the emperor Theodosius. Held in Latin and in Richardson's English; Gennadius added authors after his death

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Isidore of Seville, about two centuries later, wrote that Jerome, skilled in three languages, turned the Scriptures from Hebrew into Latin, and that his translation is rightly preferred to the others, holding closer to the words and clearer in sense.
  • The Council of Trent, on 8 April 1546, declared the old and Vulgate edition, approved by long use in the church, to be held as authentic in lectures, disputations, sermons and expositions, and forbade anyone to reject it. The decree does not name Jerome. Waterworth's note says St. Jerome's version, the Vulgate, was selected and proposed as the most ancient and the most used.
  • Fremantle says the Psalter the churches sang was Jerome's revision from the Greek, known as the Gallican Psalter, and that in 1566 it replaced the Roman Psalter in all churches except those of the Vatican, Milan and St. Mark's, Venice.
  • Augustine, in the City of God, calls him a man most learned, skilled in all three languages, who translated the Scriptures from the Hebrew. He grants that the Jews hold the work faithful, and says the churches of Christ judge that no one should be preferred to the authority of the Seventy.
  • His catalog of Christian writers was continued: the Library holds a list of the authors Gennadius added after Jerome's death.

How they're remembered

  • In Sulpicius Severus's Dialogues, the traveler Postumianus says he stayed six months with Jerome at Bethlehem. He says Jerome fought the wicked without rest, that the heretics hated him for attacking them and the clergy for attacking their lives, that all the good admired and loved him, and that he was always reading or writing. Sulpicius also has Postumianus call him learned in Latin and Greek and also Hebrew.
  • Palladius, who took Rufinus's side in the quarrel, writes that a certain Jerome showed a temper so disastrous that it threw into the shade his splendid achievements.
  • Prosper's Chronicle records his death under the year 420.

At Major Councils

  • Councils of Ariminum and Seleucia, 359: Not present; told its end from the acts

What Is Uncertain

  • Jerome's own words. Nearly all of the life comes from Jerome's letters and prefaces, and none of their Latin is held: his letters and the prefaces to his Bible translations are held only in Fremantle's English. The Latin of his letters to Augustine, of On Illustrious Men and of his Apology against Rufinus is held. Rufinus's Apology against Jerome is held only in Fremantle's English. No Vulgate text is held.
  • The dates of his life. Fremantle's dates for the desert, Rome and Bethlehem, and for the work on the Old Testament from 391 to 404, are the editor's reckoning. They are Dominant Modern Reconstruction. Birth and death are given in the date note: the sources differ by fifteen years for his birth and by ten days or a year for his death.
  • The year Damasus died. Fremantle's table gives the death of Damasus in December 384, and a note to the Gospels preface speaks of the pope's death in 385. The preface itself is dated 383 by Fremantle.
  • The Old Testament from the Hebrew. The words I translated the Old Testament from the Hebrew stand in On Illustrious Men 135 in only some manuscripts, as Richardson's note records, so the claim rests on his prefaces and on his letters to Augustine. His preface to Samuel and Kings, which he calls the Helmeted Preface, was addressed to Paula and Eustochium and published about 391 by Fremantle's dating.
  • The Jewish teachers. He writes in Letter 125 that he first went to a brother who had been a Jew, in the desert. In Letter 84 he writes of the trouble and expense of getting Baraninas to teach him by night, because of his fear of the Jews; Fremantle's note says Jerome took lessons from him in the earlier years at Bethlehem and consulted other Jewish scholars. Rufinus calls the teacher Barabbas, and says a translation made by a single man under the inspiration of Barabbas could not outweigh the Seventy. Jerome answers that Rufinus writes Barabbas for Baranina, and that he never called the man his master but only said he had read Origen as he had taken lessons from this Jew. Each is credited to its writer.
  • Why he left Rome. Jerome's account is of envy, a false accuser who took the charge back, and a departure by ship in August, choosing his own time. Rufinus, in words Jerome quotes, hints at more, at his oath and the way he left, and says he will keep back more than he relates. Only Jerome's account is told in full. Jerome writes that his sex was his one crime, and that before he knew Paula's family almost every one judged him worthy of the episcopate; that is his own account, and the only one held.
  • The funeral crowd. The words of the crowd after the death of Paula's daughter Blaesilla in 384 are Jerome's report, in Letter 39. That fasting killed her is the crowd's charge, not a finding.
  • Paula's studies. Jerome says that Paula and her daughter asked to read the Old and New Testaments with him, that when he stuck fast Paula would not rest but forced him by fresh questions, and that she learned Hebrew so well she could chant the psalms in it. Palladius, on Rufinus's side, says in a text printed in square brackets as a critical reconstruction that Jerome hindered her by his jealousy. Elsewhere Palladius reports the prophecy of Posidonius that Paula would die first and be freed from his bad temper. The Syriac version says she died before her time to escape his envy. Each is credited to its writer.
  • The break with Rufinus. Fremantle's editor says the two had been close friends in early youth and fell out in the disputes over Origen, Jerome siding with Epiphanius and Rufinus with John. The editor says they were reconciled before Rufinus left Palestine in 397. Rufinus's Apology was written at Aquileia in 400. It charges that the new translation altered what the Apostles had delivered to the churches, and that it was made under the influence of the Jews.
  • Augustine and the plant in Jonah. Augustine's first letter, dated 394 or 395 by its editor, begs him not to translate the canonical books unless he marks the differences from the Septuagint, whose authority is worthy of highest esteem, and objects to his commentary on Galatians. His letter of 403 tells of a bishop whose congregation rose against Jerome's reading in Jonah; Augustine says the Jewish residents, whether from ignorance or from spite, answered that the Hebrew agreed with the old version. Jerome answers in 404 that the Hebrew names a plant that is neither gourd nor ivy, that he put ivy so as not to differ from all other translators, and that the Jews were either unacquainted with Hebrew or pleased to say what was not true. Augustine writes in 405 that he objected only to the public reading of the Hebrew version for fear of offending the flocks, and that, if the Hebrew word is neither, he would prefer gourd in all Latin versions. Neither is given the verdict. The held files never place Oea; that it was a town in Africa is an inference from Augustine's letter.
  • An old man in his cell. Jerome tells Augustine to send the letter in question signed in his own hand or stop annoying an old man who seeks retirement in his monastic cell. Augustine's earlier letter had reached Italy before it reached him.
  • Reconciled. In 418 Jerome wrote to Augustine that all the world knew him, that Catholics revered him as the restorer of the ancient faith, and that heretics hated them both.
  • The Seventy. Rufinus and Augustine held that the Seventy's version had the authority of the Holy Spirit. Fremantle's summary of Jerome's Genesis preface says he rejected the story of the seventy cells and the notion that they were inspired, and urged his critics to test his version by asking those who knew Hebrew.
  • The Vulgate. The decree of Trent says the old and Vulgate edition and does not name Jerome. That the Vulgate is chiefly his is the editors' view and Widely Accepted. Much of the Latin Bible the West used was not his translation from the Hebrew: the Psalter was his revision from the Greek. Isidore's brief account of him has square-bracketed words in Lindsay's edition that are not in all manuscripts. A second passage of Isidore, in On Church Offices, is too damaged in the Library's scan to read.
  • The attack on Bethlehem. Pope Innocent writes that Eustochium and Paula deplored to him ravages, murders, fires and outrages, and left untold the name and motive of the human agent, and that as Jerome's letters name no one there is no one against whom he can proceed. That the attackers were Pelagians, after the synod of Diospolis, is Fremantle's editor's account.
  • Not told in the brief. Fremantle puts Jerome's illness at Antioch and his anti-Ciceronian dream in 374. His controversies with Jovinian, Helvidius, Vigilantius, the Luciferians, the Pelagians and John of Jerusalem, and his lives of Paul, Hilarion and Malchus, are outside the story. He says he worked for Damasus on the ecclesiastical correspondence and wrote his answers to the questions the councils of the east and west referred to him. He says the old Latin Old Testament reached Latin readers by a descent of three steps, which Fremantle explains as Hebrew to Greek and Greek to Latin.
