Trebizond · Mount Athos, today in Greece

Athanasios the Athonite

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Byzantine Imperial Church (Macedonian Era).

Known for
Founder of the Lavra on Mount Athos, with the help of his friend the emperor Nikephoros Phokas. Philipp Meyer calls it the first great monastery of the Holy Mountain. Athanasios wrote its rule and his own testament for it. Meyer says that rule became the general standard for the other houses of Athos.
Also called
Abramios, his name in the world (Philipp Meyer, from the Life), Athanasius
Dates
Philipp Meyer says his birth year is unknown. Meyer dates the visit to Crete between July 960 and March 961, and reckons the church of the Lavra begun in 963. Meyer places his death between 997 and 1011, since he was still alive in 997 and his successor Eustratios is named in 1011. Paul Peeters says he died about the year 1000
Place
Trebizond, by the Life, and Constantinople, where it says he was raised; Mount Kyminas, where he became a monk under Michael Maleinos; Mount Athos, where he founded the Lavra. His testament calls the place Melana
Role
Monk and abbot; founder of the Lavra on Mount Athos and author of its rule

About This Person

The general Nikephoros Phokas was fighting the Arabs of Crete when he sent for a monk from Mount Athos. The monk tells it in the rule he wrote for his monastery. Nikephoros wrote to him many times, and when he would not come, wrote more often still. On the island he begged the monk to build a lavra, a house of monks, where he too could one day become a monk. The monk held back. He was used to living free and alone, he wrote. He thought it enough if he could save himself.

The monk was Athanasios the Athonite. Philipp Meyer, who edited his writings in 1894, tells his early life from a Life written under his successor. Meyer knew it only in a Modern Greek version of the seventeenth century. By that Life, Athanasios came from Trebizond, was orphaned young, and became a teacher in Constantinople. He became a monk under Michael Maleinos, abbot of Mount Kyminas and uncle of Nikephoros. When Michael wanted him as his successor, the Life says, he fled to Athos.

Meyer dates the visit to Crete to 960 or 961. A manuscript cited in the notes to Leo the Deacon's history says Athanasios never went, and only foretold the victory. Meyer calls that a legend.

By Meyer's reckoning, the church was half built when news came, in 963, that Nikephoros had been crowned emperor. Athanasios went to Constantinople, his rule says, and blamed him to his face. From the start, he told him, you planned one thing and promised us another. The emperor swore he would still come to the Mountain. He asked only that the church not be left half finished. He was murdered in 969, and Athanasios called his death a martyr's end.

Nikephoros's charter, which Athanasios quotes in his rule, made the house self-governing, under its own abbot. Later, the Life says, monks of Athos accused Athanasios of changing the Mountain's ways. A ruling of 972 by the emperor John Tzimiskes records their complaint, and Athanasios signed it as abbot of the greatest lavra. Meyer says his rule became the standard for the other houses of Athos.

The Life says he died with six of his monks when a dome they were finishing fell in. Meyer puts it between 997 and 1011.

In his testament he wrote that he had spent all his days in great sorrow, knowing his weak soul unfit to lead others.

Key Works

  • Rule of the Lavra (Typikon, or Kanonikon): his account of how the Lavra was founded, with passages of Nikephoros's charter quoted in it, and rules for the abbot and monks. In Greek, in Meyer's edition. Meyer dates it perhaps to early 970, after Nikephoros was murdered on 16 December 969
  • Testament (Diatyposis): his last instructions to the Lavra, written to be opened after his death. He names John the Iberian, then John's son Euthymios, as guardians of the house. In Greek, in Meyer's edition, which dates it to the reign of Basil II

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Philipp Meyer says the Lavra's rule became the general standard for the other houses of Athos, and that its constitution set the pattern for them.
  • Meyer says Athanasios is the first who can be shown to have used the rule of Theodore of Stoudios. He finds the opening of Athanasios's charge to the abbot almost word for word in Theodore's testament.
  • Meyer writes that the first great monastery on the Holy Mountain was a Greek one, the Lavra of Athanasios.
  • George the Hagiorite, writing in Georgian about 1045, tells how John the Iberian served two years or more in the Lavra's kitchen. On Athanasios's advice, John's company then built a church and cells about a mile from the Lavra. Meyer says John founded his great monastery, Iviron, while Athanasios was alive.

How they're remembered

  • Meyer says Athanasios organized flight from the world after the model of Theodore of Stoudios.
  • George the Hagiorite calls him "magnus Athanasius", the great Athanasios, in Paul Peeters's Latin. He says John the Iberian, dying, told his monks to keep the memory of their spiritual father Athanasios every year.
  • Old copies of the works of Gregory Palamas, described in Migne's Greek Fathers, are marked as books of the holy Lavra of Saint Athanasios on Athos.
  • Joseph Georgirenes, archbishop of Samos, wrote in 1678 that the monks of Athos told a long story of how the Virgin met Athanasios when he lacked money to finish the great church, and promised to provide.

What Is Uncertain

  • His early life rests on a Life by a monk of the Lavra who lived under Eustratios, Athanasios's successor. Philipp Meyer knew it only in the Modern Greek version of the monk Agapios, printed at Venice in 1657. The Life's own Greek is not among the sources used here.
  • Whether he went to Crete. His own rule and the Life say he did. A manuscript cited in the notes to Leo the Deacon's history says he only foretold Nikephoros's victory, and was given money from the spoils to build. Meyer calls that a legend.
  • What he did when Nikephoros was crowned. His rule says he went straight to Constantinople and reproached him. The Life, as Meyer reports it, says he first fled the monastery in grief and reached the city by a miracle.
  • The year of his death. Meyer gives 997 to 1011. Paul Peeters, editing the Georgian Life of John and Euthymius, says about the year 1000.
  • How he died rests on the Life alone. Meyer reports that a dome fell on him and six monks as they set its keystone, and that they were pulled from the rubble dead.
  • His teacher. His own rule calls Michael, uncle of Nikephoros, his abbot. Mount Kyminas and the family name Maleinos come from Meyer, who draws on the Life of Athanasios and on Michael's own Life.
  • A third text, rules for the services and meals of the Lavra, is printed after the testament. Meyer says it is uncertain whether Athanasios wrote it in that form, since it first appears in the later of the two old copies.
  • His origin. Marie-Félicité Brosset, citing the Georgian Life of Euthymius, calls the founder of the house on Athos a Georgian. Frédéric Macler calls the Lavra that of John the Iberian's countryman. The Life, as Meyer reports it, says Athanasios came from Trebizond, and George the Hagiorite's Life of John and Euthymius does not call him Georgian.
  • The Athanasios who brought the complaint of 972 is a different man: Athanasios, chief of the monks of the Mountain. Both sign the ruling of John Tzimiskes.
