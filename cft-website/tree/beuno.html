About This Person

Beuno was traveling near a ford on the river Severn when he heard a voice across the water. An Englishman was urging his dogs after a hare, shouting "Cergia". Beuno turned back at once. "My sons, put on your clothes and your shoes, and let us leave this place," he told his disciples. The man's nation "has a strange language, and is abominable", he said, and "they have invaded this place, and it will be theirs".

The story is told in the Welsh Life of Saint Beuno. W. J. Rees printed it in 1853 from two manuscripts, with an English translation. It is a late telling. Rees thought the Lives of the Welsh saints were probably compiled about the twelfth century. The few other accounts of him are late as well.

The Life makes him a Welsh clergyman and a founder of churches. A note in W. J. Rees's edition, quoting a Professor Rees, places his life in the sixth century and into the seventh. The Life says he was born to parents in old age and taught by a saint named Tangusius at Caerwent. Welsh kings and princes gave him land, estate after estate, and he built churches on it.

Cadwallon, king after his father Cadvan, gave him land in Arvon, and Beuno began to build. A woman came to him with a crying baby. The land, she said, belonged to the child's father. Beuno took her to the king. The child "who is in that woman's arms owns that land, and is heir thereof", he said, in W. J. Rees's English. The king refused, and Beuno left him under a curse. A cousin of the king gave Beuno a township called Kelynauc.

The Life also says he raised the girl Winefred from the dead, after a king cut off her head. Where her blood fell, it says, a spring rose that is called Winefred's Well. W. J. Rees places it in Flintshire.

The same note says Beuno founded the religious house at Clynog Fawr in 616, and it counts eleven churches dedicated to him.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • A note in W. J. Rees's edition names eleven churches dedicated to him in Montgomeryshire, Merionethshire, Caernarvonshire, Anglesey and Herefordshire.
  • Hugh Williams, in his notes on Gildas, cites the Ancient Laws of Wales for the phrase clas Beuno, the monastery of Beuno.

How they're remembered

  • The Latin Life of Saint Winefred, printed by W. J. Rees, makes Beuno her teacher, who raised her after she was killed. Each year, it says, she sent him a cloak she had made.
  • John Mirk's English sermon book, the Festial, tells the same story of a holy hermit called Bewnow.
  • The Life says he gave food to the hungry, drink to the thirsty and lodging to strangers, and visited the sick.

What Is Uncertain

  • Every held account of him is late. The Welsh Life is from manuscripts W. J. Rees printed in 1853, and he thought such Lives were probably compiled about the twelfth century. No held witness is near his own time.
  • The year 616 and his centuries come from a note in W. J. Rees's edition quoting a Professor Rees's Welsh Saints. The Life itself gives no year.
  • His wonders. The Life says he raised Winefred and others from the dead, and that a king who killed her melted into a pool.
