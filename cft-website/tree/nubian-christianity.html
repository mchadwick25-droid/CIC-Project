The Age of Monks and Empires · 543-652 · Nile Sudan (Makuria, Nobadia)

Nile Sudan, today in Egypt and Sudan

Nubian Christianity

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Christianity reached the Nubian kingdoms south of Egypt in the 540s, through two competing missions. A Miaphysite priest named Julian went with the empress Theodora's backing, and a Chalcedonian effort went with Justinian's. The story of that race survives only because a Syrian bishop, John of Ephesus, wrote it down from the winning side. What Nubia itself left is not stories but walls. Built a few generations later, the cathedral at Faras lay buried in sand until it was rescued before the Aswan dam flooded the region. It gave up a list of its bishops and more than a hundred painted figures: Nubian bishops, kings and queens, and saints. Some have dark faces and some pale, in a style nobody had seen before. When Arab armies pushed south after taking Egypt, Nubian archers held them at Dongola. In 652 the two sides signed the Baqt, a treaty of exchange rather than of conquest, and it left Christian Nubia standing on its own terms.

In Its Own Record

Grew Out Of

  • Cyrilline / Miaphysite Egyptian Christianity Documented Major — John of Ephesus says the priest Julian, backed by the empress Theodora, converted the king of the Nobadae. Julian was one of the clergy of Theodosius, the exiled patriarch of Alexandria. He told them to 'walk in the ways of pope Theodosius'. They were placed under Theodosius as their patriarch.

Continued As

  • Makurian Nubian Christianity Widely Accepted Major — John of Biclarum says the Makurians took up the Christian faith in the third year of Justin II, about 569. Al-Baladhuri shows the Nubians still bound by their treaty in the 770s and 780s, under the caliph al-Mahdi. Modern histories treat Christian Nubia as one church that ran on through these centuries.

In Contact With

  • The Byzantine Imperial Church (Justinianic) Documented — John of Ephesus says Justinian sent envoys with gold and baptismal robes to the Nobadae, asking them to join his church. The king accepted the gifts but refused the emperor's faith, and sent a written answer. John wrote for the rival party, and his is the only account of this exchange.

Sources

  • John of Ephesus, Ecclesiastical History, Part III, bk 4 (trans. R. Payne Smith, 1860; online tertullian.org) (primary) — The near-contemporary narrative of Nobadia's conversion (Julian 543, Longinus 569-80s), told from the Miaphysite mission's side — an outside (Syrian) voice, not an inside one. Compare John of Biclarum for the rival Chalcedonian claim on Makuria.
  • Faras cathedral excavations and murals (K. Michalowski, Faras publications, 1960s-70s) (primary) — The material inside-voice: bishops' portraits, painted liturgy, bishop lists from the rescued cathedral; publications scattered (Polish/French/German series; murals split Warsaw/Khartoum museums).
  • Qasr Ibrim excavation texts (Egypt Exploration Society reports and text editions) (primary) — Documentary and liturgical finds from the fortress-cathedral site; mostly later than this era but anchoring the tradition's texture; specialist publications only.
  • Derek A. Welsby, The Medieval Kingdoms of Nubia (2002) (secondary) — The standard accessible synthesis of Christian Nobadia/Makuria/Alwa from conversion onward - verified (British Museum Press).
  • Cambridge History of Christianity, vol. 2, ch. 4, 'Early Asian and East African Christianities' (David Bundy) (reference) — Reference chapter whose scope includes Nubia; verified in vol. 2 TOC. No dedicated Cambridge chapter for Christian Nubia in this era exists.
