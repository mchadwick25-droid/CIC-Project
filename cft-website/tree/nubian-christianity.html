Nubian Christianity

The Nile in what is now Sudan, 543 to 652 CE — three Christian kingdoms whose clearest surviving face comes from a cathedral's painted walls, from the centuries just after this one, which stopped an Arab army and then negotiated their own terms.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:14

Read along

Christianity reached the Nubian kingdoms south of Egypt in the 540s, through two competing missions. A Miaphysite priest named Julian went with the empress Theodora's backing, and a Chalcedonian effort went with Justinian's. The story of that race survives only because a Syrian bishop, John of Ephesus, wrote it down from the winning side. What Nubia itself left is not stories but walls. Built a few generations later, the cathedral at Faras lay buried in sand until it was rescued before the Aswan dam flooded the region. It gave up a list of its bishops and more than a hundred painted figures: Nubian bishops, kings and queens, and saints. Some have dark faces and some pale, in a style nobody had seen before. When Arab armies pushed south after taking Egypt, Nubian archers held them at Dongola. In 652 the two sides signed the Baqt, a treaty of exchange rather than of conquest, and it left Christian Nubia standing on its own terms.

Documented Stories

Julian Preaches from a Cave Full of Water

John of Ephesus is the only narrative source for how the Nubian kingdoms became Christian, and he tells it as a story of court intrigue. A priest named Julian, an old man in the entourage of the exiled Alexandrian patriarch at Constantinople, conceived a desire to convert the Nobadae, the people south of the First Cataract, and got the empress Theodora’s backing. Justinian, hearing of it, sent a rival mission of his own, Chalcedonian, with gold and baptismal robes. The Nobadian king received Julian, heard both cases, and, having been told that the emperor’s own wife had sent the first party, chose the Miaphysite faith and sent the imperial envoy home. What follows is the detail John cannot resist. The heat in Nubia was such, he writes, that Julian ‘used to say that from the third to the tenth hour he sat in caves full of water, naked except for a linen garment, and taught the people from there’, and that the only way to bear it was to keep still. He stayed two years, baptized the king and his nobles, left a bishop of Philae in charge, and went back to Constantinople. Nubia was Christian for nine hundred years. Its own account of the beginning was never written, or has not survived; the cave full of water comes from a Syrian who heard Julian tell it.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Two rival missions sent from Constantinople brought the faith here in the 540s. Nobadia took the faith of the Miaphysite patriarch of Alexandria, then in exile. It turned down the faith that Justinian's envoys brought. A chronicle written on the other side of the split says a people it calls the Maccurritae became Christian around 569. Whether they are the Makurians of Nubia is not settled; Theodor Mommsen, its editor, thought them a people of Mauretania.

Direct descendants

  • continued as Makurian Nubian Christianity Widely Accepted Major — John of Biclaro says the Maccurritae took up the Christian faith in the third year of Justin II, about 569; whether he means the Makurians is not settled. Al-Baladhuri shows the Nubians still bound by their treaty in the 770s and 780s, under the caliph al-Mahdi. Modern histories treat Christian Nubia as one church that ran on through these centuries.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Christianity reached the Nubian kingdoms south of Egypt through rival missions in the 540s, one Miaphysite and one Chalcedonian, and the story survives only because a Syrian bishop wrote it down from the winning side. Nubia itself left walls rather than words: the painted bishops, kings and saints of the cathedral at Faras, and the archers who held an invading Arab army at Dongola until both sides signed the Baqt treaty in 652.

Legacy

Christian Nubia outlasted this window by many centuries and only faded in the later Middle Ages; no Nubian church continues today. What survives is largely visual, and it is remarkable: the Faras murals, rescued from the rising waters of Lake Nasser in the 1960s and now divided between museums in Warsaw and Khartoum, are one of the great surviving bodies of medieval African painting. Documents from the fortress-cathedral at Qasr Ibrim show a church writing in Greek, Coptic and Old Nubian at once. And the Baqt held, in one form or another, for centuries — an unusually durable settlement between a Christian state and its Muslim neighbor.

Experience It Today

  • The Faras Gallery at the National Museum in Warsaw — the only permanent European exhibition of medieval Nubian wall paintings, rescued from the Faras cathedral before it was flooded

How Sure the Record Is

  • John of Ephesus, Ecclesiastical History, Part III, bk 4 (trans. R. Payne Smith, 1860; online tertullian.org) (primary) — The near-contemporary narrative of Nobadia's conversion (Julian, then Longinus in the 560s to 580s), told from the Miaphysite mission's side — an outside (Syrian) voice, not an inside one. Compare John of Biclaro's Chalcedonian note on the Maccurritae (see the first story's caveat).
  • Faras cathedral excavations and murals (K. Michalowski, Faras publications, 1960s-70s) (primary) — The material inside-voice: bishops' portraits, painted liturgy, bishop lists from the rescued cathedral; publications scattered (Polish/French/German series; murals split Warsaw/Khartoum museums).
  • Qasr Ibrim excavation texts (Egypt Exploration Society reports and text editions) (primary) — Documentary and liturgical finds from the fortress-cathedral site; mostly later than this era but anchoring the tradition's texture; specialist publications only.
  • Derek A. Welsby, The Medieval Kingdoms of Nubia (2002) (secondary) — The standard accessible synthesis of Christian Nobadia/Makuria/Alwa from conversion onward - verified (British Museum Press).
  • Cambridge History of Christianity, vol. 2, ch. 4, 'Early Asian and East African Christianities' (David Bundy) (reference) — Reference chapter whose scope includes Nubia; verified in vol. 2 TOC. No dedicated Cambridge chapter for Christian Nubia in this era exists.
