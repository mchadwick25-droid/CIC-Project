John of Biclaro

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Nicene Visigothic Iberia.

Known for
Gothic monk and chronicler who studied at Constantinople, founded the monastery of Biclaro and became bishop of Gerona. His Chronicle carries on from Victor of Tunnuna's and runs to King Reccared's fourth year, the year of the Third Council of Toledo.
Also called
Iohannes abbas Biclarensis, Joannes Gerundensis
Dates
No source used here gives his birth year or a firm year of his death. Isidore of Seville says he came home from Constantinople after seventeen years, while King Leovigild was pressing the Arian teaching. Theodor Mommsen thinks the return likely about 576, and places the ten years of exile in 577 to 586. Place

Role
Monk, founder and abbot of Biclaro; bishop of Gerona; chronicler

About This Person

In the royal city of Constantinople, John of Biclaro writes, a plague of groin sores broke out. He saw many thousands of people die of it, he says. That same year, envoys of a people he calls the Maccurritae came to the city. They brought the emperor Justin the Younger elephant tusks and a giraffe as gifts.

John was a Goth, born at Scallabis in Lusitania, Isidore of Seville writes. His life is known mainly from Isidore's notice of him. As a young man John went to Constantinople and was schooled there in Greek and Latin learning. He came home to Spain after seventeen years, Isidore says. Some copies of Isidore read the seventh year. Theodor Mommsen, who edited John's Chronicle, holds to seventeen, since its first ten years report the news of Constantinople.

King Leovigild was then pressing the Arian teaching. John refused it, and the king exiled him to Barcelona, where he suffered ten years of plots from the Arians. He later founded a monastery called Biclaro and wrote a rule for its monks. Isidore says he became bishop of Gerona. Mommsen takes him to be the John of Gerona who signs the acts of a council at Barcelona and a decree of King Gundemar.

His Chronicle carries on from that of the African bishop Victor of Tunnuna. It runs from Justin's first year as emperor to King Reccared's fourth. John tells the revolt of Leovigild's son Hermenegild as a seizure of power, urged on by Leovigild's queen, Gosuintha. At the Third Council of Toledo, he says, Reccared's presence renewed what Constantine had done at Nicaea. Mommsen notes that John writes of Leovigild, the king who exiled him, with great admiration.

In his preface John says he wrote down the deeds of his own times for those who would come after. He set down partly what he saw with his own eyes, and partly what he learned from the reports of faithful men.

Key Works

  • Chronicle: year-by-year notes from the first year of the emperor Justin the Younger to King Reccared's fourth, on the empire, the Lombards, the Franks and the Visigoths. Its last year tells of the Third Council of Toledo and a plot against Reccared

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Isidore of Seville drew on the Chronicle for his own chronicle and History of the Goths. Theodor Mommsen says Isidore plundered John as he did Victor of Tunnuna.
  • His account of the Third Council of Toledo says its business lay chiefly with Leander of Seville and Eutropius, abbot of the monastery of Servitanum. It likens Reccared to Constantine at Nicaea and Marcian at Chalcedon.
  • Ildefonsus of Toledo says Nonnitus followed a John in the see of Gerona.

How they're remembered

  • Isidore of Seville calls the Chronicle a very useful history, in a historical and well-ordered style.
  • Isidore says his rule for Biclaro was useful for the monastery and needful for all who fear God.

What Is Uncertain

  • His life is known mainly from Isidore of Seville's notice of him in On Famous Men, which Theodor Mommsen calls fuller than Isidore's usual. Mommsen says Isidore wrote it while John was still alive. The Chronicle does not tell of his own exile. Mommsen notes that it writes of Leovigild, the king who exiled him, with great admiration.
  • How long he stayed at Constantinople. Isidore's text in Mommsen's edition gives seventeen years. Some copies read the seventh year. Mommsen holds to seventeen, since from 567 to about 576 the Chronicle reports embassies, triumphs, plots and plagues at Constantinople.
  • When he became bishop of Gerona. Isidore gives no year. Andrea Gallandi, whose notice Migne reprints, reports Nicolás Antonio's view that Reccared recalled him from exile and raised him to Gerona in 592. At the Third Council of Toledo the bishop of Gerona who signs is Alicius. Mommsen takes the John of Gerona who signs at Barcelona and under Gundemar to be the chronicler.
  • When he died. Gallandi reports Juan de Mariana's view that he died after 621, under King Suinthila, leaving Gerona to Nonnitus. He reports Nicolás Antonio's view that he lived to 620. Ildefonsus of Toledo says Nonnitus followed a John at Gerona, and gives no year. Nonnitus signs as bishop of Gerona at the Fourth Council of Toledo.
  • Where Biclaro stood. Theodor Mommsen says it is unknown. He rejects the view of Spanish writers that it was at Vallclara near Tarragona, which he says rests only on a likeness of names. Gallandi says most writers after Mariana place it at the foot of the Pyrenees.
  • His rule for Biclaro. Isidore names it. A note printed with Benedict of Aniane's Concordia of Rules says it could no longer be found. The note quotes Augustin Calmet, who places Biclaro at Valclara in Catalonia.
  • The years. Mommsen wrote the years in his margin to his own scheme. He warns that it is not certain John counted them so, still less that each event fell in the year shown. If his reckoning holds, he says, the Chronicle was written in 590.
  • The people he calls the Maccurritae, whose envoys brought gifts to Justin and who, he says, received the faith of Christ in these years. Mommsen suggests they may be the Makkourai of Mauretania that Ptolemy names. Isidore's chronicle calls them the Mascuritae.
