Christadelphians

Britain and North America, 1848 to 1906 — a doctor who set out to start the Bible again from scratch, and small lay congregations who took a new name so they could refuse to fight.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:08

Read along

John Thomas was an English doctor who emigrated to America in 1832, nearly drowned in a shipwreck on the way, and resolved that if he survived he would work out the Bible's teaching for himself rather than take it on anyone's authority. He concluded that most of what the churches taught had been added long after the apostles, and set out the alternative in Elpis Israel in 1848. His followers organized as "ecclesias" — small congregations with no clergy, where members taught and presided in turn. And in 1864 adopted the name Christadelphians, brothers of Christ, largely so they could claim conscientious-objector status in the American Civil War. Robert Roberts, a Scottish printer's apprentice who became the movement's second great figure, gave them a magazine that has run continuously since 1864 and a written confession, the Birmingham Statement of Faith, that they still use.

Documented Stories

A Name for the Draft Board

John Thomas's followers had, by his own insistence, no name. They were believers of the things concerning the kingdom of God and the name of Jesus Christ, gathered in small ecclesias in Britain and America without clergy or creeds, and Thomas, who had left the Campbellites over such questions, would not have them become a denomination. The Civil War changed that. The federal draft law of 1864 allowed exemption from combat only to members of religious denominations whose articles of faith forbade the bearing of arms. The brethren of Ogle County, Illinois, who held from Thomas's teaching that a believer might not take part in the wars of the nations, applied for exemption and were asked what they were called. Thomas supplied an answer: in a certificate he drew up for them in 1864 he described them as Brethren of Christ, in Greek Christadelphians, a body whose faith forbade military service, and signed it as their teacher. The name was accepted, and the Ogle County men were exempted. Brethren in Virginia made a similar application in 1865 under the same name. Thomas wrote that a name was a necessity forced on them by the powers of the world and not a badge, and his followers in Britain took it up at once. Robert Roberts's magazine became The Christadelphian in 1869. A movement that had refused to be a denomination had acquired the one thing that made it one, in order not to kill.

The Inspiration Division

Robert Ashcroft, a former Baptist minister, became in 1884 joint editor with Robert Roberts of a new monthly, The Exegetist. In an early number he published an article arguing that the inspiration of the Bible was to be understood as the inspiration of its doctrine and its writers, not as a guarantee of every word and detail. For a community whose whole existence rested on the Bible as the one infallible authority, this was not a nuance. Roberts answered; Ashcroft replied; the argument ran through the ecclesias for a year. In 1885 the Birmingham ecclesia, Roberts's own, meeting at the Temperance Hall, resolved that fellowship required belief in the full and entire inspiration of the Scriptures in the original writings, and asked each member to declare it. Those who held Ashcroft's view, or that no such test should be imposed, would not, and withdrew; they met from then on at Suffolk Street, and the Birmingham Statement of Faith adopted in 1886 by Roberts's party wrote the inspiration clause in. Ecclesias across Britain and North America divided along the same line, some by vote and some by families. The Suffolk Street fellowship continued for seventy years and reunited with the Temperance Hall side in 1957; another division of 1898, over the resurrection of the unbaptized, produced the Amended and Unamended bodies that still exist in North America. A movement that had left every church for the Bible had found that the Bible required a church to say what it was.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

John Thomas, an English doctor who survived a shipwreck on his way to America in 1832, resolved to work out the Bible's teaching for himself rather than take it on any church's authority, and set it out in Elpis Israel in 1848. His followers organized into small congregations called ecclesias, with no clergy and members teaching and presiding in turn, and in 1864 took the name Christadelphians largely so they could claim conscientious-objector status in the American Civil War. Robert Roberts gave them a magazine that has run continuously since that year and a statement of faith they still use.

Legacy

It continues as a small worldwide body — tens of thousands of people in autonomous ecclesias across Britain, North America, Australia, Africa and elsewhere — with no clergy, no central headquarters, the same statement of faith, and a magazine published without a break since 1864. Its tradition of conscientious objection outlasted the war it was named for. Christadelphians were among the recognized objectors in both world wars, and their case files are part of the legal record of conscience in Britain and the United States.

Sources

Doctrinal Floor Note

Christadelphians explicitly reject the doctrine of the Trinity, and say so in their own confession — the Birmingham Statement of Faith — and in John Thomas's Elpis Israel. That is a plain difference from the historic creeds, recorded here as a fact about what the community teaches rather than as a judgment of it. Unlike several movements of its era, this one organized itself around a way of reading Scripture rather than around its founder's person: Thomas is not treated as an authority in his own right.

How Sure the Record Is

Modest own record

  • John Thomas, Elpis Israel (1848) and Eureka (1861-68); Robert Roberts, Christendom Astray (1862; as Twelve Lectures 1884) (primary) — These are the movement's own foundational texts, in the founders' own words rather than outside reports about them.
  • The Birmingham (Amended) Statement of Faith (1886); The Christadelphian magazine (1864-) (primary) — This is the movement's explicit confessional statement, along with its ongoing self-published record.
  • Ecclesial life: ecclesia minute books, fraternal-gathering records; the conscientious-objection filings (the name 'Christadelphian' coined for 1860s draft-exemption) (primary) — This is a modest but genuine self-record. The conscientious-objection filings are a state-facing record of how the movement defined itself to outside authorities.
  • Charles Lippy, The Christadelphians in North America (Mellen, 1989), with Andrew Wilson, The History of the Christadelphians 1864-1885 (1997) (secondary) — These are the standard works in a sparse scholarly field.
