The Age of Monks and Empires · wrote c. 511 · Noricum, Naples

Eugippius

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Early Benedictine / Italian Monasticism.

Known for
Priest and head of the monastery at the tomb of Severinus of Noricum, in the Lucullan castle at Naples, who wrote the Life of Severinus from the accounts of the elder monks and gathered a book of passages from Augustine of Hippo for a holy woman named Proba.
Also called
Eugipius, Eugyppius
Dates
Flourished after the consulship of Importunus, while Anastasius was emperor, in Isidore of Seville's words. G. W. Robinson puts that consulship in 509 and dates Eugippius's letter sending the Life to the Roman deacon Paschasius to 511. No source used here gives the year of his birth or death
Place
The Danube lands of Noricum, where G. W. Robinson says his earlier years were spent and where Eugippius tells of the monks opening Severinus's grave; the Lucullan castle at Naples, as head of the monastery there
Role
Priest and head of a monastery; writer of the Life of Severinus

About This Person

When Count Pierius forced the Romans out of Noricum's Danube towns, the priest Lucillus sang the evening psalms with the monks. Then he had the grave of Severinus opened. "A fragrance of such sweetness surrounded us who stood by, that we fell on the earth for joy and wonder," Eugippius wrote. The monks carried the body to Italy, and it came to rest in the Lucullan castle at Naples.

Eugippius later led the monastery built there at the tomb. He calls the monk Marcianus the man "who preceded me in the headship of the monastery". Isidore of Seville calls him abbot, and those who wrote to him called him a priest.

His best-known book is the Life of Severinus, a holy man of the Danube borderlands. In a letter to the Roman deacon Paschasius he tells how it began. After reading a layman's life of another monk, he began to tell the clergy that the great miracles of Severinus "ought not to be hidden". He gathered a memoir "filled full with testimonies from the daily narrations of the elder brethren", and sent it to Paschasius to polish. G. W. Robinson, his English translator, dates the letter to 511.

How well he knew Severinus is not clear. He writes "we" at the deathbed and at the grave, but says he drew on what the elder monks told him. Paschasius replies that teachers are more easily portrayed by their pupils. Andrea Gallandi, an editor of the 1700s, calls him Severinus's disciple.

Cassiodorus, a statesman of Gothic Italy, says he saw him, and calls him not deeply trained in worldly learning but full of the Scriptures. For Proba, a holy woman, Eugippius collected passages from Augustine of Hippo into three hundred and thirty-eight chapters. Isidore says he wrote a rule for his monks, left to them at his death.

Paschasius chose to add nothing to the memoir. "The deeds of the good cannot perish with time," he wrote.

Key Works

  • Life of Severinus, with his letter to the deacon Paschasius: the deeds, miracles and death of Severinus in Noricum, the monks' journey to Italy with his body and its burial in the Lucullan castle; Eugippius calls it a memoir and sent it to Paschasius to work up, and Paschasius chose to add nothing
  • Excerpts from the works of Augustine: deep questions and sayings from Augustine of Hippo set in 338 chapters for Proba, a consecrated virgin, in the account of Cassiodorus, a statesman of Gothic Italy; by Eugippius's own preface, as Andrea Gallandi quotes it, he made them at the urging of the abbot Marinus. No copy is among the sources used here
  • Rule for the monks of the monastery of Saint Severinus: named by Isidore of Seville, who says Eugippius left it to them at his death as if by will. No copy is among the sources used here

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Cassiodorus, in his Institutions, tells his readers they ought to read the works of the priest Eugippius.
  • The Anonymus Valesianus, a Latin chronicle of Ostrogothic Italy, copies the Life on Severinus and King Odoacer, as Theodor Mommsen shows in his edition. W. D. Foulke says Paul the Deacon used the Life once, in his History of the Langobards.
  • Dionysius Exiguus put a book of Gregory of Nyssa on the making of man into Latin at Eugippius's request, and Ferrandus of Carthage wrote to him on the Trinity and the two natures of Christ, against a question an Arian count had raised.

How they're remembered

  • Paschasius answered the memoir: "Thou hast sent me a memoir to which the eloquence of the trained writer can add nothing, and in a short compendium hast produced a work which the whole church can read."
  • Isidore of Seville gives him a chapter in his book of famous men, as abbot of the Lucullan town at Naples.
  • G. W. Robinson opens his English translation with the words of Teuffel's History of Roman Literature, "the incomparable biography of Saint Severinus".

What Is Uncertain

  • How well he knew Severinus. Eugippius writes "we" at the deathbed and at the opening of the grave, and says that "Even in the lifetime of Saint Severinus" he heard no more of the saint's homeland than he tells. He also says he built the memoir from "the daily narrations of the elder brethren", and names those he heard stories from, among them Marcianus and the subdeacon Marcus. Paschasius, answering the memoir, writes that the virtues of teachers "are more easily depicted by their pupils". Andrea Gallandi, an editor of the 1700s, calls him a disciple of Severinus and of Pope Gelasius, and W. D. Foulke calls him Severinus's pupil. No source used here gives his age when Severinus died.
  • His years. No source used here gives the year of his birth or death. Eugippius's letter gives one time mark: he read the layman's letter "About two years ago, in the consulship of Importunus". G. W. Robinson and Theodor Mommsen give 511 for the Life, and W. D. Foulke about 511.
  • One Eugippius or two. Faustino Arevalo's notes to Isidore report that Fabricius and others take the Eugippius whom Cassiodorus saw to be a different, younger man. Andrea Gallandi rejects a like view, held by Miraeus, Bollandus and Cave, of two men named Eugippius, one Italian and one African. He blames it on Sigebert of Gembloux, who placed an abbot Eugippius in the time of Pope Pelagius II and the emperor Tiberius.
  • His origin. Gallandi says he was Italian, and Foulke calls him an Italian monk. Robinson says his earlier years were spent in the Danube lands. No source used here gives his birthplace or family.
  • The Lucullan castle. G. W. Robinson places it at what is now Pizzofalcone in Naples; Thomas Hodgkin's index puts it at the Castel dell'Ovo, and Heinrich Kihn between Naples and Pozzuoli.
