Ferrandus of Carthage

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Latin African Christianity under Byzantium.

Known for
Deacon of Carthage whom two Roman deacons consulted on condemning the dead in the Three Chapters dispute, and who answered that what councils had decided should not be reopened.
Also called
Fulgentius Ferrandus
Dates
Sixth century. Victor of Tunnuna says he was held in high regard in the year Theodor Mommsen, his editor, gives as 546; Gallandi takes him to be dead by 547, when Facundus of Hermiane wrote. Gallandi reports Pagi and Natalis Alexander giving 548 without witnesses
Place
Carthage; by the Life of Fulgentius, if it is his, Sardinia
Role
Deacon of the church of Carthage; theologian and compiler of church law

About This Person

Two deacons of Rome, Pelagius and Anatolius, wrote to Ferrandus, a deacon of Carthage, with a question. They asked him to answer after talking with his bishop. Facundus of Hermiane, an African bishop, tells this in a book addressed to the emperor Justinian.

Facundus says that enemies of the Council of Chalcedon wanted three things condemned. These were Theodore of Mopsuestia and his teaching, and some writings of Theodoret of Cyrrhus. The third was a letter of Ibas of Edessa that the council had judged sound. His book defends all three, the Three Chapters of its title, and he writes as a partisan.

A short book on church writers, printed with Isidore of Seville's works, says the Romans asked whether a man may be condemned after death. Ferrandus's answer survives. What holy bishops decide in council, he writes, must be followed and not reopened. Then he asks what good it does to fight with the dead. If God has pardoned a man, our severity does him no harm. If God has prepared his punishment, our kindness does him no good.

He also wrote to Fulgentius of Ruspe, whom he calls his absent teacher. One letter asks about an Ethiopian baptized when illness had taken his mind, who died before receiving communion.

Whether he wrote the Life of Fulgentius is disputed. Its author says that he himself became a monk in the small monastery Fulgentius built in exile in Sardinia. Chifflet, his editor of 1649, gave the Life to him. A note in Migne's Fulgentius says others doubt it, since the author seems never to have left Fulgentius's side. Gallandi, another editor, notes that the Sardinian years come only from the Life.

Victor of Tunnuna's chronicle says Ferrandus was held in high regard in the year Theodor Mommsen, its editor, gives as 546. Facundus speaks of him as a man of praiseworthy memory, so Gallandi takes him to be dead by 547.

Key Works

  • Letter to Pelagius and Anatolius: to two deacons of Rome, for the letter of Ibas of Edessa and so for the Three Chapters; it holds that what holy bishops decide in council must be followed and not reopened, and that the dead should be left to God's judgment
  • Breviatio canonum: a short digest of church laws, each rule with the council it comes from; Gallandi says the year it was made is unknown
  • Letter to Count Reginus: on how a religious commander should act in war, set out as seven rules of innocence

Legacy

What traces to them

  • His letter to Pelagius and Anatolius was quoted in a short book on church writers printed with Isidore of Seville's works. Gallandi calls its author unknown; Baronius, as Gallandi reports, and Faustino Arevalo, Isidore's editor, give it to Isidore. Gallandi says the quotation there is cut and corrupt, and gives it from the letter.
  • Other letters go to Eugippius, a priest, to Severus, a scholar at Constantinople, and to Anatolius on the two natures of Christ.

How they're remembered

  • Facundus of Hermiane, writing to the emperor Justinian, calls him a man of praiseworthy memory in Christ.
  • Victor of Tunnuna's chronicle gives him one line: Ferrandus, deacon of the church of Carthage, was held in high regard.

What Is Uncertain

  • Whether he wrote the Life of Fulgentius of Ruspe is disputed. Chifflet, his editor of 1649, gave it to him, and Gallandi reports Pithou, Miraeus, Christianus Lupus and Natalis Alexander on the same side. A note in Migne's edition of Fulgentius says others doubt it, since the Life's author seems never to have left Fulgentius's side. Gallandi reports Noris, Pagi, Mangeant and Fontanini against it, argues that Noris later changed his mind, and prints the Life after Ferrandus's own works.
  • The story of his years as a monk with Fulgentius in Sardinia, and of his return to Africa when King Hilderic recalled the bishops, comes from Noris as Gallandi quotes him. Gallandi says Noris could have had the Sardinian years only from the Life of Fulgentius.
  • Chifflet thought he was a kinsman of Fulgentius, from his first name, Fulgentius, and from the way he speaks of the bishop's noble family. It is a guess.
  • Chifflet, as Gallandi quotes him, did not know whether he died in exile or at home, worn down by grief in the Three Chapters storm.
  • The year 546 is Theodor Mommsen's for Victor of Tunnuna's entry; Victor dates it by the years after the consulship of Basilius.
