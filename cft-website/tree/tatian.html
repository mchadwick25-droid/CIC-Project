Tatian

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Syriac Christianity (Edessa/Nisibis).

Tatian, born in the land of the Assyrians, became a Christian in the middle of the second century and wrote an Address to the Greeks that scorned their learning. He wove the four Gospels into one book, the Diatessaron, which Syriac-speaking churches read in worship.

About This Person

Tatian says he had been initiated into the mysteries, the secret rites of the gods, and that at Rome he found Jupiter honored with human blood. He turned inward and asked how he might find the truth.

The answer he found made him a Christian in the middle of the second century. In his Address to the Greeks, a book written as a speech, he scorned their learning as younger and worse than the old writings of Moses. Irenaeus, his opponent, says he heard Justin teach and left the church after Justin died. He wove the four Gospels into one book, read in worship by the Syriac-speaking churches of the East. In the 400s one bishop put away the copies in his own churches.

Born, he says, in the land of the Assyrians, he was trained in Greek learning and followed rhetoric, the art of public speaking. The Greeks called every other people barbarians, and Tatian claimed the word for his own faith. "While I was thinking hard about what mattered, I came upon some barbarian writings. They were older than the teachings of the Greeks, and more divine than their error. I was won over by their plain words, and by the artlessness of those who spoke them. I was won over by their clear account of how all things were made, and by their foreknowledge of what was to come. I was won over by their excellent commands, and by the rule of one over all things." He calls Moses the founder of all barbarian wisdom, so he meant the writings of Moses and the prophets.

Every art the Greeks boast of, his Address says, came from barbarians: astronomy from Babylon, geometry from Egypt, letters from Phoenicia. Moses is older than Homer, he argues, citing Greek, Chaldean, Phoenician and Egyptian writers rather than Christian ones.

Tatian charges that Crescens, a philosopher at Rome who claimed to despise death, so feared it that he worked to bring death on Justin, and on Tatian too. Justin, he says, had shown the philosophers to be gluttons and cheats. At Rome Tatian taught Rhodon, a pupil from Asia.

Irenaeus, writing not long after, tells what Tatian taught after Justin's death. "As long as he was with him, he let nothing of this kind show. But after his martyrdom he left the church. Lifted up by the conceit of being a teacher, and puffed up as if he were better than the rest, he set up a school with a character all its own."

Irenaeus says Tatian invented aeons, invisible heavenly powers, like the followers of Valentinus, a teacher at Rome. Like Marcion, another Roman teacher, he called marriage nothing else than corruption and fornication. On his own he taught that Adam, the first man, was not saved. A later Roman teacher gives Tatian's reason: disobedience began with Adam. Irenaeus puts him among the Encratites, the self-controlled, who refused marriage, and some of them meat, a group he says sprang from earlier teachers. Eusebius, a bishop, makes Tatian the source of the Encratites, and places this about 172. Some two centuries later Epiphanius, a bishop in Cyprus, says Tatian crossed to the East and set up his school in Mesopotamia.

Eusebius, about 150 years after the break, did not know how Tatian combined the Gospels into one. He says Tatian called it the Diatessaron, Greek for "through four": one gospel story made from the four. Tatian's most used book, he says, was the Address. Later writers of the Church of the East say Ephrem, a deacon of Nisibis, commented on the Diatessaron.

Theodoret, bishop of Cyrrhus under Antioch, found more than two hundred copies held in honor in his churches. Tatian, he says, had cut out the lists of Jesus's ancestors and other passages showing the Lord born, as a man, of King David's family. Even believers true to the apostles' teaching, he writes, used it simply as a short book, not seeing the harm. He gathered them all, put them away, and brought in the four Gospels.

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “While I was thinking hard about what mattered, I came upon some barbarian writings. They were older than the teachings of the Greeks, and more divine than their error. I was won over by their plain words, and by the artlessness of those who spoke them. I was won over by their clear account of how all things were made, and by their foreknowledge of what was to come. I was won over by their excellent commands, and by the rule of one over all things.”
    As printed
    “while I was giving my most earnest attention to the matter, I happened to meet with certain barbaric writings, too old to be compared with the opinions of the Greeks, and too divine to be compared with their errors; and I was led to put faith in these by the unpretending cast of the language, the inartificial character of the writers, the foreknowledge displayed of future events, the excellent quality of the precepts, and the declaration of the government of the universe as centred in one Being.”
    Tatian, Address to the Greeks 29, tr. J. E. Ryland
    The modern English follows Otto's Greek of 1851, with each clause confirmed by Otto's Latin on the same page. That Greek is held only in two damaged scans, Otto's edition and Prudentius Maran's quotation of the passage in Otto's ninth volume, and some words are misread in both, so Ryland's English is given as the printed wording. Ryland leaves out the clause on the clear account of how all things were made.
  • In our modern English
    “As long as he was with him, he let nothing of this kind show. But after his martyrdom he left the church. Lifted up by the conceit of being a teacher, and puffed up as if he were better than the rest, he set up a school with a character all its own.”
    As printed
    “as long as he continued with him he expressed no such views; but after his martyrdom he separated from the Church, and, excited and puffed up by the thought of being a teacher, as if he were superior to others, he composed his own peculiar type of doctrine.”
    Irenaeus, Against Heresies I.28.1, ANF 1
    The modern English follows the Greek of Irenaeus as Eusebius keeps it, which Karl Holl and Paul Wendland quote in their notes, two prints that agree. Both leave out the clause puffed up as if he were better than the rest. Prudentius Maran's prolegomena, in Otto's ninth volume, and Otto's Tatian of 1851 both quote the second half of the passage with that clause, and the rendering agrees with them there. Otto's Tatian prints that half cleanly. The other three are held only in damaged scans: a word in the first sentence is misread in every print that has it, and Maran's quotation misreads a word in that clause. So the English of ANF 1 is given as the printed wording. The Greek word the English renders doctrine means a school.

Timeline

Dates
No ancient source gives a year for his birth or his death. Irenaeus says he left the church after Justin's death. Eusebius's Chronicle names him a heretic, from whom the Encratites come, under the twelfth year of the emperor Marcus Aurelius, which is about 172. Jerome says he flourished in the reign of Marcus Antoninus Verus and Lucius Aurelius Commodus.
Place
Born, he says, in the land of the Assyrians; at Rome, which he calls the great city, where he taught; later in Mesopotamia, by Epiphanius's report. Of the old sources held here, only Epiphanius names Mesopotamia.

  • Address to the Greeks: a book written as a speech, telling of his conversion, his scorn for Greek religion and philosophy, and his claim that Moses is older than Homer
  • Diatessaron: one gospel story woven from the four, whose Greek name means through four; read in worship in the Syriac-speaking churches, and now known from later translations
  • Problems: a lost book, known only from his pupil Rhodon through Eusebius, which promised to explain the dark and hidden passages of the Scriptures

Connections

Tradition

Syriac Christianity (Edessa/Nisibis)

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • The Diatessaron. Edessa's later founding story, the Doctrine of Addai, pictures crowds coming day by day to the prayers and to the reading of the Old and New Testament, of the Diatessaron.
  • Later writers of the Church of the East say Ephrem, a deacon of Nisibis, commented on the Diatessaron. Isho'dad of Merv says so, and the Chronicle of Seert lists the commentary among Ephrem's works.
  • Theodoret, bishop of Cyrrhus, found more than two hundred copies held in honor in his churches. He gathered them all, put them away, and brought in the Gospels of the four evangelists.
  • It survives in an Arabic translation made from Syriac by a priest, Abu'l Faraj 'Abdulla ibn at-Tayyib. That copy opens with "In the beginning was the Word" and closes by naming it the Gospel which Tatianus compiled and named Diatessaron.
  • Victor, bishop of Capua, wrote a preface to a Latin book that wove the four Gospels into one. He says Tatian, Justin's disciple, made one Gospel from four, and he judges the book before him to be Tatian's.
  • The Address to the Greeks survives. Eusebius calls it the most used of his books, and Jerome the most important. Clement of Alexandria says it treated the age of Moses with accuracy.

How they're remembered

  • Irenaeus of Lyon, his opponent, remembers him as the first to deny that Adam was saved, and calls the man who first started this idea false.
  • Hippolytus of Rome, a generation later, repeats Irenaeus's charges and calls him a disciple of Justin the Martyr.
  • Eusebius of Caesarea and Jerome of Strido name him the founder of the Encratites. Jerome heads his chapter Tatian the heresiarch, the founder of a heresy.
  • Theodoret of Cyrrhus says that after his teacher's death he longed to become the head of a sect.
  • A writer quoted by Eusebius names Tatian with Justin, Miltiades and Clement. These brethren, he says, wrote for the truth against the heathen and the heresies, and in all their works Christ is spoken of as God.

Open Questions

  • The witnesses. His own Address tells his turn to the faith, his view of the Greeks and his charge against Crescens. Irenaeus, his opponent, writing not long after, tells the break. Eusebius wrote about 150 years later, Epiphanius about two centuries later, and Theodoret later still.
  • Where he came from. He says he was born in the land of the Assyrians. Epiphanius and Theodoret call him a Syrian. The preface of the Arabic Diatessaron and the Chronicle of Seert call him a Greek.
  • When and where he wrote the Address. No old source says.
  • Rome. No old source says whether he was at Rome once or twice, or whether he broke with the church at Rome or in the East.
  • Crescens. Tatian says Crescens worked to bring death on Justin, and on Tatian too. Eusebius quotes the passage without the words about Tatian, and adds that such was the cause of Justin's martyrdom. Tatian's own words do not say that Crescens caused it. Crescens's side is not held.
  • Rhodon. No old source says whether Tatian taught him before or after the break.
  • The break. Irenaeus gives the reason as pride in being a teacher. That is his opponent's judgment. Tatian's own side of the break is not held.
  • The Encratites. Irenaeus says the group sprang from Saturninus and Marcion, and that Tatian first brought in among them the teaching that Adam was not saved. Eusebius's Chronicle, his Church History, Jerome and Epiphanius make Tatian the source or founder of the group. Irenaeus says they refused marriage, and some of them animal food. Theodoret says they also refused wine and offered water in its place, and Epiphanius says Tatian used only water in his rites.
  • The Diatessaron. No old source says when or where he made it, in what language, or whether he made it before or after the break. Eusebius says Tatian made it "I know not how"; the story takes the words in their plain sense, that Eusebius did not know how it was made.
  • What it left out. Theodoret says Tatian cut out the lists of Jesus's ancestors and the other passages that show the Lord born of David's family as a man. 'Abdisho, a late Syriac writer who praised the work, says Tatian did not add a single saying of his own. Michael the Syrian, a much later chronicler, says Tatian wrote treatises to show that Christ was of David's family.
  • Rabbula, bishop of Edessa, ordered that every church have and read a copy of the Distinct Gospel, the four Gospels kept apart. His rule is held only in English, as a modern editor quotes it, and it does not name the Diatessaron.
