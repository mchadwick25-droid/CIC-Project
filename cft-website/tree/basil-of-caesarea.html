Basil of Caesarea

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Cappadocian Christianity.

Also called Basil the Great, Basilius

Bishop of Caesarea in Cappadocia. He gave up teaching rhetoric for the ascetic life, wrote the Rules as answers to his brothers' questions on how monks should live, built a lodging for the poor and the sick a little way from the city, and in On the Holy Spirit defended the Spirit's place beside the Father and the Son. In 787 the Second Council of Nicaea told double monasteries already in existence to keep his rule.

About This Person

Years later, defending himself in a letter against men who, he wrote, were spreading lies about him, Basil of Caesarea looked back. He had wasted nearly all his youth, he wrote, in the vain labor of learning a wisdom God had made foolish, the learning of this world. "Then one day, like a man roused from deep sleep, I turned my eyes to the wonderful light of the truth of the gospel."

That waking set the course of his life in Caesarea, an inland city in Asia Minor, in the fourth century. Basil gave up teaching rhetoric, the art of public speaking, for a life of prayer and shared work. He wrote rules that taught monks to live together and serve one another, and he built a great lodging for the poor and the sick a little way outside the city. As its bishop, he defended the Holy Spirit's place beside the Father and the Son, and he died before a council, a meeting of bishops, declared it in its creed.

The old writers differ on what turned him. In his own telling, he read in the gospel of selling goods and sharing with the poor. His brother, Gregory of Nyssa, says their sister Macrina turned him, while the historian Rufinus says his friend Gregory of Nazianzus drew him away from teaching.

In Pontus, near the Black Sea, Basil founded a community and led it. His rules were answers to his brother monks' questions, and one asks whether to live alone or with others. Love does not seek its own good, Basil answers, and Christ washed his disciples' feet, but a man alone has no one to serve. "Whom, then, will you wash? Whom will you care for? Behind whom will you be last, if you live by yourself?"

The bishop of Caesarea made him a priest, but they quarreled, and Basil went back to Pontus. When the emperor Valens, who favored the party that ranked Christ, the Son, below God the Father, moved against the churches that held them equal, Basil came back to serve the bishop.

When the old bishop died, Basil was made bishop, though the bishops of the province fought it out of envy, Gregory of Nazianzus says in his speech in Basil's memory. The emperor's highest official ordered him to accept the emperor's faith, or else lose his goods and face exile, torture and death. In Gregory's telling, Basil answered that a man who owns only rags and a few books cannot be robbed, and that death would only send him sooner to God. When the province was divided, a rival bishop claimed part of his churches and once blocked his road with armed men. Basil answered by making more bishops for the province, Gregory says.

His accusers were filling the governor's ears, and Basil wrote to ask what harm the state suffered from him, or from building a lodging for strangers, travelers and the sick, with doctors and transport. The rich gave to it at his urging. Lepers driven from their towns were taken in, and Basil greeted them as brothers and tended them himself.

Basil closed prayers by giving glory to the Father in two forms: with the Son, together with the Holy Spirit, and through the Son, in the Holy Spirit. The first set the Spirit beside the Father and the Son. Some attacked these as new, contradictory words, holding that the Spirit could not be ranked with the Father and the Son. He answered in a book, On the Holy Spirit, that the church already baptized new believers in the name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit together. A letter of his puts the same thought in two lines: "As we were baptized, so we confess our faith. As we confess our faith, so too we give praise." Gregory says that Basil, out of caution, held back from writing in plain words that the Spirit is God, though he often preached it.

The whole city poured around Basil as he died. In 381, after his death, a council at Constantinople declared in its creed that the Spirit is worshipped and glorified with the Father and the Son. Rufinus put his works, his rules for monks among them, into Latin. In the sixth century Benedict of Nursia closed his own Rule by pointing his monks to Basil's as an example of good living.

Quotations and references

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “Then one day, like a man roused from deep sleep, I turned my eyes to the wonderful light of the truth of the gospel.”
    As printed
    “Then once upon a time, like a man roused from deep sleep, I turned my eyes to the marvellous light of the truth of the Gospel”
    Basil, Letter 223.2, tr. Blomfield Jackson, in Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, second series, vol. 8
    The Greek of the letter is not held, so the English stands alone. The modern English keeps Jackson's order and gives once upon a time as one day, and marvellous as wonderful.
  • In our modern English
    “Whom, then, will you wash? Whom will you care for? Behind whom will you be last, if you live by yourself?”
    As printed
    “Τίνα οὖν ἀπονίψεις ; τίνα θεραπεύσεις ; τίνος ἔσχατος ἔσῃ, αὐτὸς καθ᾽ ἑαυτὸν διάγων ;”
    Basil, Longer Rule 7, Greek of the Patrologia Graeca, vol. 31
    The Greek has whom, with no word for feet. Clarke's English adds whose feet from the sentence before, where Christ washes the feet of the disciples. The modern English follows the Greek, and the narrator gives the feet as the plain sense.
  • In our modern English
    “As we were baptized, so we confess our faith. As we confess our faith, so too we give praise.”
    As printed
    “As we were baptized, so we profess our belief. As we profess our belief, so also we offer praise.”
    Basil, Letter 159.2, tr. Blomfield Jackson, in Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, second series, vol. 8
    The Greek of the letter is not held, so the English stands alone. The modern English gives profess our belief as confess our faith, and so also as so too.

Timeline

Dates
Gregory of Nyssa says only that he died eight years after his election. Jerome says he died in the reign of Gratian. All the held sources place it before 381
Place
Caesarea in Cappadocia, an inland city once called Mazaca, now in Turkey. Athens, where he studied with Gregory of Nazianzus. The monastery on the river Iris in Pontus, where he founded a community
Role
Bishop of Caesarea; teacher of monks; author of the Rules, the letters and On the Holy Spirit

Key Works

  • The Rules (the Longer and Shorter Rules, in the Ascetic Works): his rules for monks, written as answers to his brothers' questions on the common life. Longer Rule 7 asks whether a man who has left the world should live by himself or with like-minded brothers, and answers that a life lived in common is more useful, since the solitary life serves only the needs of one person. Gregory of Nazianzus calls them legislation for the monastic life
  • On the Holy Spirit: written for Amphilochius, who asked for clear instruction after Basil was attacked for closing prayer in two forms, with the Son together with the Holy Spirit and through the Son in the Holy Spirit, as new and contradictory words. Basil reports that his opponents held that the Spirit could not be ranked with the Father and the Son. He holds it apostolic to keep the unwritten traditions, and he cites Dionysius of Alexandria for the form with the Son together with the Spirit
  • Letters: Letter 223 defends him against Eustathius of Sebaste and tells how he turned from worldly learning. Letter 94 answers the governor Elias and asks whom it harms to build a lodging for strangers and the sick, with doctors and transport. Letter 159, to Eupaterius and his daughter, argues that as we are baptized, so we confess our faith, and so we give praise

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Gennadius says Rufinus put works of Basil into Latin. Rufinus's own preface to his Latin Rule says that the abbot Urseius asked him to turn the work into Latin, and that it gives the rules for monks of Basil, bishop in Cappadocia, which he set down as answers to the monks who questioned him.
  • Benedict of Nursia, near the end of his Rule, lists the Rule of our holy Father Basil among the books that are examples of the good living and obedience of monks. He does not say which Latin text he read, and he does not say his monks kept it.
  • The Second Council of Nicaea, in 787, decreed that no new double monastery be built, and that the double monasteries already in existence keep the rule of Basil and be ordered by his precepts, with monks and nuns not dwelling together.
  • Sozomen, about sixty-five years after Basil's death, calls the Basileias the most celebrated hospice for the poor, and says Basil, bishop of Caesarea, set it up and that it took its name from him and still keeps it.
  • The First Council of Constantinople, in 381, after his death, confessed the Spirit worshipped and glorified with the Father and the Son. No held source says the council took up his teaching.
  • Theodoret, in his Church History, calls the see of Caesarea the see of the great Basil, a light of the world, and in the Eranistes gives a testimony of the holy Basil among his authorities.
  • Photius read twenty-five books by Theodore of Antioch against Eunomius in defense of St. Basil, and an attack on Eunomius by Sophronius, also in defense of St. Basil. Jerome says Basil himself wrote books against Eunomius.
  • Gregory of Nyssa, in a letter to his brother Peter that opens his books against Eunomius, says that when Basil died the controversy with Eunomius passed to him, and that he wrote in part to defend Basil's good name against Eunomius's abuse.
  • Palladius says Evagrius of Pontus was ordained reader by the holy Basil, bishop of Caesarea.
  • The tree's own entry for his tradition says his rules for communal ascetic life remain foundational for Eastern Orthodox monasticism.

How they're remembered

  • Gregory of Nazianzus says he and Basil were at Athens together, like two branches of one river, and that on Basil's return the city of Caesarea took possession of him as a second founder and patron.
  • In his letter against Eustathius, Basil says he went to many monks in Alexandria and the rest of Egypt, in Palestine, in Coele-Syria and in Mesopotamia.
  • Rufinus says Basil went through the cities and countryside of Pontus and taught people to come together, build monasteries, give time to psalms, hymns and prayer, care for the poor and give them decent lodging.
  • Gregory of Nazianzus says Basil joined hermits and communities instead of walling them apart, so that the contemplative spirit would not be cut off from society nor the active life lack the contemplative.
  • Gregory says a disagreement arose between Basil and the bishop of Caesarea before him, that he passes over its cause, and that the monks wanted to break away from the church with Basil. Basil, he says, would not rend the body of the church, and he returned from Pontus to the fight for the truth when the heretics grew strong.
  • Gregory says that in the great famine Basil moved the owners of grain to open their stores, and gathered the hungry, men, women, children and the old, and set before them soup and such meat as there was. He says Basil served them with his own servants and tended them himself.
  • Gregory says the bishop before Basil died in Basil's arms, and that Basil was raised to the bishop's throne not without the envious struggles of the bishops of his native land. Socrates says instead that Meletius of Antioch raised Basil from deacon to the bishopric of Caesarea.
  • Gregory tells Basil's answer to the emperor's official: that a man who has nothing is beyond the reach of confiscation, that every land is God's, and that death will send him the sooner to God. In Gregory's telling the official said no one had ever spoken so boldly to Modestus, and Basil said perhaps he had never met a bishop. Sozomen, about sixty-five years after, tells that the official threatened death and that Basil refused.
  • Gregory says the rival bishop who claimed part of Basil's churches seized the bridles of his mules and barred the road with a band of robbers. He says the new bishops were Basil's answer, and that he himself was treated as an appendage to the scheme, a cause of pain which even time has not obliterated. Socrates says Gregory was first made bishop of Sasima, one of the cities of Cappadocia.
  • Gregory says the lepers, driven from their cities, homes and families, were taken into the new city, and that Basil saluted them as brothers and kissed them.
  • Gregory says that Basil, dying, ordained the best of his attendants so that his disciples would not be defrauded of the priesthood. At his funeral, Gregory says, the market places, porticos and houses were full, Jews, Greeks and foreigners mourned with his own people, and in the crush many died.
  • Sozomen says Basil was a great admirer of Ephrem the Syrian and was astonished at his learning.

Open Questions

  • Who turned him from rhetoric. Basil's own letter, written in self-defense against Eustathius of Sebaste, tells of his waking to the gospel and of the monks he sought out. His brother Gregory of Nyssa, in the Life of his sister, credits Macrina. Rufinus, about twenty-three years after Basil's death, credits Gregory of Nazianzus, who took him from the teacher's chair to a monastery. Basil teaching rhetoric rests on Rufinus.
  • The date of his death. No held old source gives the year. Jerome says only that he died in the reign of Gratian.
  • The Rules. Their date is not held. Sozomen reports that some said Eustathius of Sebaste wrote the ascetic treatises commonly attributed to Basil.
  • Gregory of Nazianzus's account is a speech in Basil's memory, so it is praise. The famine, the dialogue with the emperor's official, the lepers and the funeral are told in his voice, and Sozomen gives a shorter version of only the official's threat.
  • Who made him bishop. Gregory, who was there, says bishops brought from a distance, his own father among them, anointed him. Socrates, much later, says Meletius of Antioch raised him.
  • The held-back words. Gregory says Basil postponed for the time the exact term, the Spirit is God, because enemies were watching for it in order to banish him, and that he often preached it publicly and confessed it in private.
  • Where the house stood. Gregory says a little way from the city.
  • The Emperor Valens's gift of lands for the poor Basil cared for rests on Theodoret alone, about seventy years later.
  • When On the Holy Spirit was written. No held old source dates it or Letter 159. The letter answers Eupaterius and his daughter, and the book answers the attack, so the letter is not his reply to the attackers.
  • The English of the three quotations. Neither the Greek of Basil's letters nor the Greek of On the Holy Spirit is held, so the first and third quotations are rendered from Jackson's English. The second is rendered from the Greek of the Longer Rules, whose Greek column reads clearly, and Clarke's English confirms the reading.
  • The second quotation. Clarke's English asks whose feet the solitary will wash. The Greek asks whom, and has the feet only in the sentence before. The narrator gives the feet as the plain sense.
