The Imperial Church Era · c. 329-379 · Cappadocia / Anatolia

Cappadocia, today in Turkey

Basil of Caesarea

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Cappadocian Christianity.

Known for
Bishop of Caesarea in Cappadocia from 370. He gave up teaching rhetoric for the ascetic life, wrote the Rules as answers to his brothers' questions on how monks should live, built a lodging for the poor and the sick a little way from the city, and in On the Holy Spirit defended the Spirit's place beside the Father and the Son. In 787 the Second Council of Nicaea told double monasteries already in existence to keep his rule.
Also called
Basil the Great, Basilius
Dates
Born in 329 or 330, by the chronology of Blomfield Jackson, who translated his letters. Made bishop in 370, by the same chronology. Jackson and W. K. L. Clarke give his death as 1 January 379. Gwynn gives the end of 378. Gregory of Nyssa says only that he died eight years after his election. All the held sources place it before 381
Place
Caesarea in Cappadocia, an inland city once called Mazaca, now in Turkey. Athens, where he studied with Gregory of Nazianzus. The monastery on the river Iris in Pontus, where he founded a community
Role
Bishop of Caesarea; teacher of monks; author of the Rules, the letters and On the Holy Spirit

About This Person

Years later, defending himself in a letter, Basil of Caesarea looked back. He had wasted nearly all his youth, he wrote, in the vain labor of learning a wisdom God had made foolish. "Then one day, like a man roused from deep sleep, I turned my eyes to the wonderful light of the truth of the gospel."

That waking set the course of his life in Caesarea, an inland city in Asia Minor. Basil gave up teaching rhetoric for a life of prayer and shared work. He wrote rules that taught monks to live together and serve one another. He built a great lodging for the poor and the sick a little way outside the city. As bishop from 370, he defended the Holy Spirit's place beside the Father and the Son. He died in 378 or 379, before a council confessed it.

He taught rhetoric, the art of public speaking, the historian Rufinus says. Who turned him away? In his own telling, he read in the gospel of selling goods and sharing with the poor, and went looking for monks. His brother, Gregory of Nyssa, gives the credit to their sister Macrina. Rufinus says his friend Gregory of Nazianzus took him from the teacher's chair.

In Pontus, near the Black Sea, Basil founded a community and led it, Gregory says. His rules were answers to his brothers' questions, and one asks whether to live alone or with others. Love does not seek its own good, Basil answers, and Christ washed his disciples' feet. "Whom, then, will you wash? Whom will you care for? Behind whom will you be last, if you live by yourself?" A man alone has no one to serve.

The bishop of Caesarea made him a priest, though they quarreled. When the emperor Valens, who favored the party that ranked the Son below the Father, moved against the churches that held them equal, Basil came back to serve the bishop.

When the old bishop died, Gregory says, Basil was chosen against the bishops of the province. The emperor's highest official ordered him to accept the emperor's faith, on pain of losing his goods, exile, torture and death. Gregory's speech in Basil's memory is praise. In his telling, Basil answered that a man who owns only rags and a few books cannot be robbed, and that death would only send him sooner to God. A rival bishop once blocked his road with armed men. Basil answered by making his brother bishop of Nyssa and his friend bishop of Sasima, a hurt, Gregory said, that time had not healed.

Accused before the governor, Basil wrote: whom does it harm to build a lodging for strangers, travelers and the sick, with doctors and transport? The rich filled it at Basil's urging, Gregory says. Lepers driven from their towns were taken in, and Basil greeted them as brothers and tended them himself, Gregory says.

Basil closed prayers with praise to the Father with the Son, together with the Holy Spirit, and at other times through the Son, in the Holy Spirit. Some attacked these as new, contradictory words. The Spirit, they held, could not be ranked with the Father and the Son. He answered from what the church already did, and in a letter put it this way: "As we were baptized, so we confess our faith. As we confess our faith, so too we give praise." Gregory admits that in writing Basil held back the bare words that the Spirit is God, from caution, though he often preached it.

In 381, after his death, a council at Constantinople confessed the Spirit worshipped and glorified with the Father and the Son. Rufinus put his Rule into Latin in 397 or 398. In the sixth century Benedict of Nursia closed his own Rule by pointing his monks to Basil's as an example of good living. In 787 a council told the houses still standing where men and women lived as monks, side by side but apart, to keep his rule.

Gregory says the whole city poured around Basil as he died.

Quotations and references

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “Then one day, like a man roused from deep sleep, I turned my eyes to the wonderful light of the truth of the gospel.”
    As printed
    “Then once upon a time, like a man roused from deep sleep, I turned my eyes to the marvellous light of the truth of the Gospel”
    Basil, Letter 223.2, tr. Blomfield Jackson, in Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, second series, vol. 8
    The Greek of the letter is not held, so the English stands alone. The modern English keeps Jackson's order and gives once upon a time as one day, and marvellous as wonderful.
  • In our modern English
    “Whom, then, will you wash? Whom will you care for? Behind whom will you be last, if you live by yourself?”
    As printed
    “Τίνα οὖν ἀπονίψεις ; τίνα θεραπεύσεις ; τίνος ἔσχατος ἔσῃ, αὐτὸς καθ᾽ ἑαυτὸν διάγων ;”
    Basil, Longer Rule 7, Greek of the Patrologia Graeca, vol. 31
    The Greek has whom, with no word for feet. Clarke's English adds whose feet from the sentence before, where Christ washes the feet of the disciples. The modern English follows the Greek, and the narrator gives the feet as the plain sense.
  • In our modern English
    “As we were baptized, so we confess our faith. As we confess our faith, so too we give praise.”
    As printed
    “As we were baptized, so we profess our belief. As we profess our belief, so also we offer praise.”
    Basil, Letter 159.2, tr. Blomfield Jackson, in Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, second series, vol. 8
    The Greek of the letter is not held, so the English stands alone. The modern English gives profess our belief as confess our faith, and so also as so too.

Key Works

  • The Rules (the Longer and Shorter Rules, in the Ascetic Works): his rules for monks, written as answers to his brothers' questions on the common life. Longer Rule 7 asks whether a man who has left the world should live by himself or with like-minded brothers, and answers that a life lived in common is more useful, since the solitary life serves only the needs of one person. Gregory of Nazianzus calls them legislation for the monastic life
  • On the Holy Spirit: written for Amphilochius, who asked for clear instruction after Basil was attacked for closing prayer in two forms, with the Son together with the Holy Spirit and through the Son in the Holy Spirit, as new and contradictory words. Basil reports that his opponents held that the Spirit could not be ranked with the Father and the Son. He holds it apostolic to keep the unwritten traditions, and he cites Dionysius of Alexandria for the form with the Son together with the Spirit
  • Letters: Letter 223, placed in 375, defends him against Eustathius of Sebaste and tells how he turned from worldly learning. Letter 94, placed in 372, answers the governor Elias and asks whom it harms to build a lodging for strangers and the sick, with doctors and transport. Letter 159, placed about 373, to Eupaterius and his daughter, argues that as we are baptized, so we confess our faith, and so we give praise

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Gennadius says Rufinus put works of Basil into Latin. Fremantle lists first among Rufinus's works the Rule of St. Basil, put into Latin at Pinetum for the abbot Urseius in 397 or 398.
  • Benedict of Nursia, near the end of his Rule, lists the Rule of our holy Father Basil among the books that are examples of the good living and obedience of monks. He does not say which Latin text he read, and he does not say his monks kept it.
  • The Second Council of Nicaea, in 787, decreed that no new double monastery be built, and that the double monasteries already in existence keep the rule of Basil and be ordered by his precepts, with monks and nuns not dwelling together.
  • Sozomen, about sixty-five years after Basil's death, calls the Basileias the most celebrated hospice for the poor, and says Basil, bishop of Caesarea, set it up and that it took its name from him and still keeps it.
  • The First Council of Constantinople, in 381, after his death, confessed the Spirit worshipped and glorified with the Father and the Son. No held source says the council took up his teaching.
  • Theodoret, in his Church History, calls the see of Caesarea the see of the great Basil, a light of the world, and in the Eranistes gives a testimony of the holy Basil among his authorities.
  • Photius read twenty-five books by Theodore of Antioch against Eunomius in defense of St. Basil, and an attack on Eunomius by Sophronius, also in defense of St. Basil. Jerome says Basil himself wrote books against Eunomius, and Jackson's chronology places that work at 364.
  • Palladius says Evagrius of Pontus was ordained reader by the holy Basil, bishop of Caesarea.
  • The tree's own entry for his tradition says his rules for communal ascetic life remain foundational for Eastern Orthodox monasticism.

How they're remembered

  • Gregory of Nazianzus says he and Basil were at Athens together, like two branches of one river, and that on Basil's return the city of Caesarea took possession of him as a second founder and patron.
  • In his letter against Eustathius, Basil says he went to many monks in Alexandria and the rest of Egypt, in Palestine, in Coele-Syria and in Mesopotamia. Jackson's chronology dates the visit to 358, before Basil withdrew to the Iris.
  • Rufinus says Basil went through the cities and countryside of Pontus and taught people to come together, build monasteries, give time to psalms, hymns and prayer, care for the poor and give them decent lodging.
  • Gregory of Nazianzus says Basil joined hermits and communities instead of walling them apart, so that the contemplative spirit would not be cut off from society nor the active life lack the contemplative.
  • Gregory says a disagreement arose between Basil and the bishop of Caesarea before him, that he passes over its cause, and that the monks wanted to break away from the church with Basil. Basil, he says, would not rend the body of the church, and he returned from Pontus to the fight for the truth when the heretics grew strong.
  • Gregory says that in the great famine Basil moved the owners of grain to open their stores, and gathered the hungry, men, women, children and the old, and set before them soup and such meat as there was. He says Basil served them with his own servants and tended them himself.
  • Gregory says the bishop before Basil died in Basil's arms, and that Basil was raised to the bishop's throne not without the envious struggles of the bishops of his native land. Socrates says instead that Meletius of Antioch raised Basil from deacon to the bishopric of Caesarea.
  • Gregory tells Basil's answer to the emperor's official: that a man who has nothing is beyond the reach of confiscation, that every land is God's, and that death will send him the sooner to God. In Gregory's telling the official said no one had ever spoken so boldly to Modestus, and Basil said perhaps he had never met a bishop. Sozomen, about sixty-five years after, tells that the official threatened death and that Basil refused.
  • Gregory says the rival bishop who claimed part of Basil's churches seized the bridles of his mules and barred the road with a band of robbers. He says the new bishops were Basil's answer, and that he himself was treated as an appendage to the scheme, a cause of pain which even time has not obliterated.
  • Gregory says the lepers, driven from their cities, homes and families, were taken into the new city, and that Basil saluted them as brothers and kissed them.
  • Gregory says that Basil, dying, ordained the best of his attendants so that his disciples would not be defrauded of the priesthood. At his funeral, Gregory says, the market places, porticos and houses were full, Jews, Greeks and foreigners mourned with his own people, and in the crush many died.

What Is Uncertain

  • Who turned him from rhetoric. Basil's own letter, written in self-defense against Eustathius of Sebaste, tells of his waking to the gospel and of the monks he sought out. His brother Gregory of Nyssa, in the Life of his sister, credits Macrina. Rufinus, about twenty-three years after Basil's death, credits Gregory of Nazianzus, who took him from the teacher's chair to a monastery. Basil teaching rhetoric rests on Rufinus, and Jackson's note cites him.
  • The date of his death. Jackson and Clarke give 1 January 379, and the Greek calendar keeps that day in his honor, by Jackson's note. Gwynn gives the end of 378. Hefele dates a synod at Antioch to September 378 and calls it the ninth month after Basil's death, which would put the death about a year before January 379. Jerome says only that he died in the reign of Gratian.
  • The Rules. Their date is not held: Morison's chronology puts their composition at 358 to 361 and a revision at 364, and a note in Clarke's edition asks whether Basil could have been a bishop when he wrote one of them. Sozomen reports that some said Eustathius of Sebaste wrote the ascetic treatises commonly attributed to Basil.
  • Gregory of Nazianzus's account is a speech in Basil's memory, so it is praise. The famine, the dialogue with the emperor's official, the lepers and the funeral are told in his voice, and Sozomen gives a shorter version of only the official's threat. The year of the famine, 368, is Jackson's.
  • Who made him bishop. Gregory, who was there, says bishops brought from a distance, his own father among them, anointed him. Socrates, much later, says Meletius of Antioch raised him.
  • The held-back words. Gregory says Basil postponed for the time the exact term, the Spirit is God, because enemies were watching for it in order to banish him, and that he often preached it publicly and confessed it in private. Jackson's note says some have blamed Basil for this.
  • Where the house stood. Gregory says a little way from the city. The word suburbs is Jackson's note.
  • The Emperor Valens's gift of lands for the poor Basil cared for rests on Theodoret alone, about seventy years later. Jackson's chronology repeats it.
  • Hefele, a modern historian, says a synod at Iconium about 376 laid down the doctrine of the Spirit as Basil had in his book, and a synod in Cappadocia confirmed the book. No held source of the time says so.
  • When On the Holy Spirit was written. Jackson places it at 374, and the tree's entry for the pneumatomachian current says about 375. Letter 159 is placed about 373, before the attack Basil answers in the treatise, so it is not his reply to the attackers.
  • The English of the three quotations. Neither the Greek of Basil's letters nor the Greek of On the Holy Spirit is held, so the first and third quotations are rendered from Jackson's English. The second is rendered from the Greek of the Longer Rules, whose Greek column reads clearly, and Clarke's English confirms the reading.
  • The second quotation. Clarke's English asks whose feet the solitary will wash. The Greek asks whom, and has the feet only in the sentence before. The narrator gives the feet as the plain sense.
