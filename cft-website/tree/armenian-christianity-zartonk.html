Armenian Christianity — the Zartonk Century

Constantinople, Anatolia and the Caucasus, 1815 to 1906 — a national awakening carried by a church, which wrote its people a constitution and then watched the massacres begin.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:43

Read along

Zartonk — awakening — is what Armenians call their nineteenth century. A revival of language, schools, printing and civic life carried largely inside the church, because under Ottoman rule the church was the nation's legal body. Its central document is the Armenian National Constitution of 1863, which seated lay delegates in a national assembly and limited what the Patriarch of Constantinople could do alone, an unusual experiment in giving a laity formal power over its own hierarchy. The century also divided the community: converts of American missionaries were expelled from the Apostolic Church and organized as the Armenian Evangelical Church in 1846, recognized by the Ottoman state as a separate Protestant community in 1850, while an Armenian Catholic patriarchate continued alongside. And it ended in blood — the Hamidian massacres of 1894-96 killed tens of thousands and emptied whole districts.

Russia took Erivan and Etchmiadzin in 1828. Armenian volunteers helped.

In 1836 a Russian rule, the Polozhenie, tied the catholicos's rule to the state. Ormanian says it let the state meddle.

It also left the last word on bishops to the tsar. The catholicos named two men, and the tsar chose.

A Catholic work says a papal bull of 1867 caused a schism in Armenia. The bull was called Reversurus.

Documented Stories

The Anathema of 1846

American missionaries had come to Constantinople in 1831 to reform, as they said, the Armenian Apostolic Church from within, and by the 1840s they had a following among Armenian teachers, artisans and clergy who met for Bible reading and preached against the veneration of saints and the fasts. The church, which under the Ottoman millet system was the Armenian nation's legal government as well as its faith, read this as sedition. In January 1846 Patriarch Matteos Chukhajian issued a bull of excommunication against the evangelicals, read in every church: they were cut off from the sacraments, from burial and from the nation, and the faithful were forbidden to trade with them, rent to them or speak to them. The bull was enforced. Evangelical Armenians lost their shops, were turned out of their lodgings, and in some cases were beaten in the street; the missionaries recorded forty families reduced to destitution in the first months. On 1 July 1846 thirty-seven men and three women met in a rented house in Pera and constituted themselves the Evangelical Church of Armenians, with a confession and a covenant, the first Protestant church among an Eastern Christian people. The Porte, under British pressure, recognized the Protestants as a separate millet in 1847 and by imperial charter in 1850, which gave them legal existence and cut them from the nation. The Apostolic Church has held ever since that the missionaries divided a people under siege; the Evangelicals that they were driven out.

The Paper Ladle

Mkrtich Khrimian had been a village teacher, an editor who printed the first Armenian paper in the provinces, a bishop who lived among the peasants and was called Hayrik, little father, and patriarch of Constantinople from 1869 until the notables forced him out in 1873 for taking the peasants' side. In 1878, after the Russo-Turkish war, the Armenian National Assembly sent him to the Congress of Berlin to ask the powers for reforms in the eastern provinces. The Congress inserted Article 61 into its treaty, promising reforms, and provided no means of enforcing it; the Bulgarians, Serbs and Montenegrins went home with states. Khrimian returned and preached the sermon his people have quoted since. In Berlin, he said, there stood a great cauldron of harissa, the stew of Armenian feasts, and each nation came to take its share; the Bulgarians, the Serbs and the Montenegrins came with iron ladles and took; and the Armenians came with a paper ladle, the petition, and when they dipped it in the harissa it dissolved, and they went home with nothing. Go home, he told them, and take up iron ladles. The sermon was understood, by the government and by the young men who would found the revolutionary parties, as a call to arms from a bishop, and Khrimian was exiled to Jerusalem in 1890; elected catholicos in 1892, he lived through the massacres of 1894–96 and died in 1907. The paper ladle is the century's own verdict on itself, spoken from a pulpit.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Because the Ottoman state treated the Armenian church as the legal body of the whole nation, a century of awakening in language, schools and printing ran largely through it. Its 1863 National Constitution gave lay delegates a formal check on the Patriarch's own authority, an unusual arrangement that outlived the massacres of 1894-96 that closed the century in blood.

Legacy

The Armenian Apostolic Church remains the national church of Armenia and of a diaspora this century's emigrations began. The Armenian Evangelical Church founded in 1846 and the Armenian Catholic Church are both still here, small and continuous. The habit of lay representation written into the 1863 constitution survived into the Apostolic Church's later governance. And the awakening's institutions — its schools, presses and newspapers — carried a national literature and a national self-understanding that the catastrophe of the following decade would scatter across the world but not extinguish.
