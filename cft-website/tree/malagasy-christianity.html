The Missionary Era · 1818-1895 · Madagascar

Malagasy Christianity — the Self-Carried Church

Madagascar, 1818 to 1895 — a church whose missionaries were expelled and whose members were speared and thrown off cliffs, and which was larger at the end of the persecution than at the start.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:14

Read along

The London Missionary Society reached Madagascar in 1818, and its most consequential work turned out to be alphabetical. Within a decade Malagasy had a written form, a printing press and, by 1835, a complete Bible. Then the door slammed. Queen Ranavalona I banned Christianity in 1835 and expelled the missionaries. Rasalama, the first Malagasy martyr, was speared in 1837, and for the next quarter-century believers were hunted, enslaved, and thrown from the cliff at Ampamarinana above the capital. The church did not merely survive the persecution. It grew through it, without foreign clergy, on the Bible it had been left and hymns its own people wrote, and when the missionaries were allowed back they found far more Christians than they had left behind. Ranavalona II was baptized in 1869 and burned the royal talismans, and the church became the establishment it had once been martyred by, until the French conquest of 1895 ended the kingdom that housed it.

Documented Stories

The Eighteen of March 1849

Queen Ranavalona I had outlawed Christianity in 1835 and expelled the missionaries, but the Malagasy Christians of the highlands had kept the Bible the London Missionary Society had left them, met at night in houses and caves, and grown; the first martyr, Rasalama, had been speared in 1837. In March 1849 the largest denunciation came. Some 1,900 people were accused of praying, possessing books and meeting; the sentences ranged from fines to perpetual slavery, and eighteen were condemned to death. On 28 March 1849 fourteen of them, commoners, were thrown from the rock of Ampamarinana, the precipice below the royal hill; four of noble rank, whose blood the law would not shed, were taken to the hill of Faravohitra and burned alive at stakes. The accounts that reached the missionaries in Mauritius from Malagasy witnesses, and that the LMS published, say that the four sang hymns as the fires were lit and prayed for the queen, and that a rainbow stood over the hill; the Christians of the city took that as a sign and told it for a generation. More than a hundred others were flogged and put in irons. The church went on. When the missionaries returned in 1862, after the queen's death, they found not the few hundred they had left but several thousand Christians, with their own preachers and hymns and a memory of their dead; memorial churches stand on both sites.

The Kingdom Made to Pray

Ranavalona II was baptized with her prime minister on 21 February 1869, and in September the royal talismans that had guaranteed the monarchy were burned publicly on her order. The kingdom's religion changed at the top in a season, and the government then did what Merina governments did: it commanded. The forced labor owed to the sovereign was turned to building churches, and villages were ordered to erect them, hundreds within a few years, whether or not anyone in the village had been converted; officials who did not attend lost their standing, and governors reported the number of praying people to the capital as they reported tribute. Mivavaka, to pray, became the word for being on the side of the state, and refusal a kind of treason. The London Missionary Society's men, who had prayed for this outcome for thirty years, found themselves objecting to it: that the congregations were full of people who had been marched there, that chiefs were made deacons for their rank, and that the faith the martyrs had kept in caves was being administered by the officers who had hunted them. The Norwegian and Catholic missions, arriving in these years, found the same and were treated by the government as rivals to the state church. The Malagasy pastors who had built the church under persecution were now its officials, and some of them said so. The French conquest of 1895 ended the monarchy, disestablished the church, and left the Malagasy Christians, once more, to themselves.

The church then grew for twenty-five years with no missionaries at all. Ranavalona II converted in 1869, and the French conquest of 1895-96 ended the kingdom that housed the church.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Missionaries gave Madagascar's language its first written form and, by 1835, a complete Bible, the same year Queen Ranavalona I banned Christianity outright and drove them out. For the next quarter-century believers had no foreign clergy at all, only the Bible and hymns they had been left, and when missionaries were finally allowed back they found far more Christians than they had left behind.

Legacy

Most people in Madagascar are still Christians. The churches grown from this time remain among the island's largest bodies. Four memorial churches stand in the capital, Antananarivo, on the spots where believers were killed. The story also went far beyond the island. Sermons and mission magazines told it again and again in the 1800s. It became their standard example of a church that held together with no clergy and no protection at all.
