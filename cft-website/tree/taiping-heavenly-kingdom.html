The Missionary Era · 1850-1864 · China

The Taiping Heavenly Kingdom

Southern China, 1850 to 1864 — a failed examination candidate who concluded he was the younger brother of Jesus Christ, and the kingdom founded on that conviction, which cost perhaps twenty million lives.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:20

Read along

Hong Xiuquan was a village schoolteacher in Guangdong who failed the imperial examinations four times. After one failure he collapsed into a delirium and saw visions he could not interpret until, years later, he reread a Christian tract handed to him in the street. From it he concluded that the old man in his vision had been God the Father, the elder brother beside him Jesus Christ, and that he himself was God's second son, sent to sweep China clean of demons. The God Worshippers he and his cousin Feng Yunshan gathered in Guangxi became an army. They proclaimed the Heavenly Kingdom of Great Peace in 1851, took Nanjing in 1853 and held it for eleven years — banning opium, alcohol and footbinding, destroying idols and ancestral tablets, holding land in common, and printing their own scriptures alongside the Bible. The war fought to destroy them was among the deadliest in human history, with estimates of the dead running to twenty million, and when Nanjing fell in 1864 Hong was already dead and the kingdom went with him.

Documented Stories

The Tianjing Incident

By 1856 the Heavenly Kingdom had held Nanjing three years, and its government was in the hands of Yang Xiuqing, the East King, a former charcoal-burner who since 1848 had fallen into trances in which God the Father spoke through him. Hong Xiuquan accepted the voice as authentic, his own authority resting on visions, and Yang used it: in 1853 the Father had, through Yang, ordered Hong beaten for mistreating his concubines, and Hong had knelt to receive it. Yang ran the armies and administration, and in August 1856 the Father spoke again, requiring that Yang be hailed as Ten Thousand Years, the title reserved to Hong. Hong agreed and sent secretly for Wei Changhui, the North King. Wei entered Nanjing by night on 1 September with three thousand men and killed Yang and his household at dawn; Hong then decreed that Wei be flogged for exceeding his orders, and Yang's followers were invited to watch, locked into the courtyard and killed, and the killing went on through the city for weeks, some twenty thousand by most estimates. Shi Dakai, the Wing King, protested; Wei killed his family. In November Hong had Wei executed, and in 1857 Shi left with the best of the army, never to return. The kingdom lasted eight more years, printing its scriptures, and fell in 1864, Hong already dead. The Taiping's own documents of 1856 record the East King's death as a treason punished; the numbers are in the Qing reports and foreigners' letters.

Roberts Leaves Nanjing

Issachar Jacox Roberts, a Southern Baptist missionary in Canton, had taught Hong Xiuquan for two months in 1847 and declined to baptize him, judging him insufficiently instructed. When Hong took Nanjing in 1853, Roberts claimed a share in the movement and tried for years to reach the capital; in October 1860, with Hong's cousin Hong Rengan, who had lived with missionaries in Hong Kong, now prime minister, he was admitted, given a robe, a house and a title, and set to advise on religion. He was the only Western missionary the Taiping ever received in that way. What he saw is in his letters. The kingdom's worship, he wrote, was addressed to a God whose second son ruled in Nanjing; the Bible was printed with Hong's corrections; the churches he had been promised were not built; his advice was not taken; and Hong Rengan was losing his influence to the generals. On 13 January 1862, according to Roberts, Hong Rengan in a rage killed one of his servants before his eyes; on 20 January Roberts fled to a British gunboat. From Shanghai he published a letter, copied in America and England, declaring that Hong Xiuquan was a crazy man, unfit to rule, and opposed to commerce and Christianity alike. The missionaries, who had hoped for a Christian China from the rebels, read it as the end of the hope, and the British government, deciding for the Qing that spring, cited their change of mind. Roberts died in Illinois in 1871.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Hong Xiuquan and his cousin Feng Yunshan gathered the God Worshippers in Guangxi. In 1851 they declared the Heavenly Kingdom. They took Nanjing in 1853 and held it until 1864. The war to crush them cost some twenty million lives. It was the largest movement of the century built on Christian ideas, and its worst disaster. It left no church behind. But it left many Chinese wary of Christianity, and later missionaries met that fear everywhere.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Hong Xiuquan, a village schoolteacher who had failed China's imperial examinations four times, came to believe after rereading a Christian tract that he was God's second son and the younger brother of Jesus Christ. The kingdom he and his followers built on that conviction banned opium and footbinding, held land in common, and lasted eleven years before the war fought to destroy it killed an estimated twenty million people.

Legacy

The Heavenly Kingdom left no church. Its institutions were destroyed with it in 1864 and no continuing body traces its descent from Hong Xiuquan. What it left instead was consequence. The Qing dynasty was permanently weakened by the war and by the regional armies raised to win it; the lower Yangtze countryside took generations to recover. And Chinese officials and villagers alike had been handed a reason to associate Christianity with rebellion and mass death, a suspicion that later missionaries met everywhere they went. Later Chinese revolutionaries, Sun Yat-sen among them, looked back on the Taiping as forerunners, and the movement remains a fixture of Chinese historical memory and museum display.

Sources

On the Nicene Creed

Hong Xiuquan claimed to be God's second son and the younger brother of Jesus Christ, and the kingdom's own printed scriptures rest on that claim. Both the claim and the authority built on it sit outside anything the historic creeds confess — recorded here as a description of what the movement's own texts say, not as a verdict on the people caught up in it.

How Sure the Record Is

Strong for the movement's own voice: it printed its own scriptures and edicts. Hostile Qing and missionary accounts survive alongside them, and modern scholarship, notably Jonathan Spence's, draws on both.
