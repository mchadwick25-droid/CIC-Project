Leander of Seville

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Nicene Visigothic Iberia.

Known for
Bishop of Seville and elder brother of Isidore of Seville, friend of Pope Gregory the Great, who addressed his Moralia on Job to him. Isidore says the Gothic people turned from Arian teaching to the Catholic faith through his faith and work.
Also called
Leander Hispalensis
Dates
Bishop of Seville under King Leovigild and King Reccared. John of Biclaro names him a famed bishop in Leovigild's seventeenth year, which Theodor Mommsen's margin gives as 585 with a query. Nicola Turchi gives his years as bishop as about 584 to 600 or 601. His death year is disputed; Isidore says only that he died in Reccared's time. No source used here gives his birth year
Place
Seville, in the province of Baetica; his father Severianus was of the province of Cartagena, Isidore says; Constantinople, on an embassy; exile abroad, at a place no source used here names
Role
Monk; bishop of Seville, metropolitan of the province of Baetica; writer

About This Person

Pope Gregory the Great remembered where he came to know Leander of Seville. "When I knew you long since at Constantinople, my most blessed brother," he wrote. Leander had come on an embassy "on counts touching the faith of the Wisigoths". Monks who had followed Gregory there begged him to explain the book of Job, and Leander added his voice. Gregory's letter to him opens the finished Moralia on Job.

Leander was bishop of Seville in the Visigothic kingdom of Spain. His younger brother, Isidore of Seville, followed him as bishop and wrote of him. Through Leander's faith and work, Isidore says, the Gothic people turned from the madness of Arius's teaching to the Catholic faith. Isidore writes as his brother and from the Catholic side.

In exile abroad, Isidore says, Leander wrote two books against the heretics. Isidore names no place or year. Gregory the Great says King Leovigild had greatly persecuted Leander.

Travelers from Spain told Gregory that Leander turned the king's son Hermenegild to the Catholic faith. Gregory of Tours, a Frankish bishop, credits Hermenegild's wife Ingund.

Near death, Gregory the Great writes, Leovigild entrusted his other son Reccared to Leander. The historian Altamira, as O. M. Dalton reports, found no proof of this in records of the time.

Reccared became Catholic, and in 589 his bishops met at Toledo for its Third Council. John of Biclaro, a chronicler of the time, says its business lay chiefly with Leander and Eutropius, abbot of Servitanum. A homily under Leander's name is told here in modern words. It calls this the most solemn of feasts, because the conversion of so many peoples is new. We once groaned at their harshness, it says, and now rejoice at their faith.

He also wrote a rule for his sister Florentina. He searched for riches to leave her as his heir, he says, and found nothing under the sun worthy of her. Christ is her bridegroom, brother and friend, and her share of the inheritance. He asks her not to forget their younger brother Isidore, left a child when their parents died.

When Leander died is disputed. Isidore says only that it was under Reccared. An epitaph's date comes to 603 in Theodor Mommsen's reckoning, and he doubts the epitaph. Nicola Turchi gives 600 or 601, and Edmund Gardner 599.

Gregory once wrote to him of carrying "the image of thy countenance" stamped deep within his heart.

Key Works

  • Rule for his sister Florentina: which Isidore names a book on the training of virgins and contempt of the world, set out under chapter headings; it urges her to keep her pact with Christ and not to look back to the world
  • Homily in praise of the church, after the Third Council of Toledo: a homily under his name on the conversion of the Goths, printed after the council's acts; it rejoices that the church has suddenly given birth to new peoples, and ends with a prayer for the kingdom and its people

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Pope Gregory the Great says that Leander, with Gregory's monks, pressed him at Constantinople to explain the book of Job; the finished Moralia opens with Gregory's letter to him.
  • John of Biclaro says the business of the Third Council of Toledo lay chiefly with Leander and Eutropius, abbot of the monastery of Servitanum. Leander signed its acts as metropolitan bishop of Seville in the province of Baetica.
  • The Fourth Council of Toledo, held in 633 by Theodor Mommsen's year, quotes Gregory's answer to Leander's question on baptism by one immersion or three.

How they're remembered

  • His brother Isidore calls him sweet in speech, outstanding in mind, and famed for his life and learning.
  • The Roman Martyrology keeps his feast on 27 February and says his preaching and work turned the Visigoths to the Catholic faith, with Reccared their king helping.
  • Gregory the Great: "I carry the image of thy countenance stamped within the bowels of my heart."
  • William Cave, whose notice Migne reprints, says he earned the name apostle of the Goths.

At Major Councils

  • Third Council of Toledo, 589: Signed third; carried the council's business with Eutropius, by John of Biclaro's account; his sermon is printed with the acts

What Is Uncertain

  • His life rests mainly on his younger brother Isidore of Seville, who writes as family and from the Catholic side, and on his friend Gregory the Great, who heard of Spanish events from travelers. O. M. Dalton calls Gregory's account of Hermenegild's martyrdom fictitious, and suggests Gregory may have had it from Leander.
  • The year of his death. Isidore says only that he died in Reccared's time. An epitaph gives 27 February, era 641, which Theodor Mommsen reckons as 603; Mommsen doubts the epitaph. The Observatio critica printed with the Rule in Migne gives 603. Nicola Turchi gives 600 or 601, and a note signed Perez in Migne's Isidore gives 600. Edmund Gardner gives 599, and the notes to the 1844 Oxford edition of the Moralia give 595. Ludwig M. Hartmann, who edited Gregory's letters after Paul Ewald's death, dates a letter of Gregory to Leander to August 599.
  • His exile. Isidore says he wrote two books against the heretics while in exile abroad, and gives no place or year. Gregory the Great says Leovigild had greatly persecuted him. William Cave says Leovigild exiled him after he came back from Constantinople, and entrusted Reccared to him the year after. Nicola Turchi says Leovigild exiled him for turning Hermenegild to the Catholic faith, and gives no year. O. M. Dalton says instead that Leander withdrew to Constantinople and did not return to Spain until Leovigild died.
  • The purpose of his embassy. Gregory says only that it touched the faith of the Visigoths. William Cave says he went to the emperor Tiberius for help for Hermenegild; the notes to the 1844 Oxford edition of the Moralia report the same view from Baronius, and an ancient Roman breviary that has him go to a council on the articles of the Trinity.
  • Who turned Hermenegild to the Catholic faith. Gregory the Great names Leander. Gregory of Tours credits Hermenegild's wife Ingund. O. M. Dalton thinks it was probably both, and notes that neither Isidore nor John of Biclaro records the conversion.
  • Whether Leovigild entrusted Reccared to him. Gregory the Great says so. O. M. Dalton reports Altamira's view that no record of the time proves it.
  • Whether he presided at the Third Council of Toledo. John of Biclaro gives the council's business to him and Eutropius, abbot of the monastery of Servitanum. The notes to the 1844 Oxford edition of the Moralia say he perhaps presided, citing Baronius.
  • Whether he was Reccared's uncle. Late sources make his sister the mother of Hermenegild and Reccared: Lucas of Tuy's additions to Isidore's History of the Goths, a breviary notice the Bollandists print, and Edmund Gardner's note. Isidore, John of Biclaro and the two Gregories do not say it.
  • The lines on Leander in some copies of Isidore's History of the Goths are later additions, from certain manuscripts and from Lucas of Tuy, as Mommsen's edition shows; Isidore's own text there does not name him.
  • The homily. Its authorship rests on its heading. The Madrid editors of 1808 note that two of their manuscripts lack it.
