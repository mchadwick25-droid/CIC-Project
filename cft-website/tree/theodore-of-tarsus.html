The Early Medieval Era · c. 602-690 · Tarsus, Canterbury

Theodore of Tarsus

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Anglo-Saxon Christianity.

Also called Theodorus

Archbishop of Canterbury from 668 to 690, sent to the English by Pope Vitalian. Bede calls him the first archbishop whom all the English church obeyed. With Hadrian, an African abbot from near Naples, he taught pupils at Canterbury, and he presided at the synods of Hertford and Hatfield.

About This Person

Pope Vitalian needed an archbishop for the English. Hadrian, an abbot near Naples, would not take the post, and in the end he put forward a monk he knew. Bede of Jarrow describes this monk, then in Rome, as "born at Tarsus in Cilicia" and learned in Greek and Latin. He was "venerable for age, being sixty-six years old". The pope agreed, but sent Hadrian with him. Hadrian was to see that he did not, "according to the custom of the Greeks, introduce anything contrary to the true faith".

He was Theodore of Tarsus, archbishop of Canterbury in England from 668 until he died in 690. Bede, writing about forty years later, is the main witness. Stephen, biographer of Bishop Wilfrid of Ripon, adds more from Wilfrid's side.

Soon after reaching Canterbury he visited all the English peoples, teaching the canons' way of keeping Easter. "This was the first archbishop whom all the English church obeyed," Bede writes.

In 673 he called the bishops to Hertford. The acts speak in his own voice: "myself, Theodore, the unworthy bishop of the see of Canterbury". He showed them ten chapters he had marked in a book of canons. Among them were Easter, bishops keeping to their own dioceses, and a synod each year.

A book of penances for sinners went under his name. Its compiler calls himself a disciple of the Northumbrians. Most of it, he reports, came from questions the priest Eoda put to Theodore. F. W. H. Wasserschleben, who edited it in 1851, holds that Theodore wrote no such book himself.

In 678, Bede says, Egfrid, king of the Northumbrians, drove Wilfrid from York, and Theodore ordained new bishops for his diocese. Stephen says the king and queen won Theodore over with gifts. In Stephen's telling, the old archbishop later confessed that he had wronged Wilfrid.

Theodore died in 690, aged eighty-eight. In Bede's words, the English churches gained more in his time "than ever they had done before."

Timeline

Dates
Bede says he was sixty-six when Pope Vitalian ordained him, on 26 March 668. He reached his church at Canterbury on 27 May of the next year. He died in 690, aged eighty-eight, in Bede's account, and his epitaph gives the day as 19 September. By those counts he was born about 602
Place
Born at Tarsus in Cilicia. A monk in Rome when he was chosen. Archbishop at Canterbury, where he was buried in the church of Saint Peter
Role
Archbishop of Canterbury; teacher, with Abbot Hadrian; president of the synods of Hertford and Hatfield

Key Works

  • Acts of the synod of Hertford (673): the acts as Bede gives them, in Theodore's own voice. He shows the bishops ten chapters he had marked in a book of canons. Among them are Easter, bishops keeping to their own dioceses, monks, wandering clergy, a yearly synod and marriage
  • Acts of the synod of Hatfield (680): the faith set down by the bishops under his presidency, as Bede gives it, against what Bede calls the heresy of Eutyches. They received five councils and the synod held at Rome under Pope Martin
  • Penitential of Theodore (Poenitentiale Theodori): rulings on penance gathered under his name. Its compiler calls himself a disciple of the Northumbrians and says most of it came from questions the priest Eoda put to Theodore. F. W. H. Wasserschleben edited it in 1851

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Bede says Theodore and Hadrian taught Scripture with church poetry, astronomy and arithmetic. Some of their pupils, still living when he wrote, knew Greek and Latin as well as their own tongue.
  • Bede names among those pupils Abbot Albinus and Tobias, bishop of Rochester. In his preface Bede calls Albinus, educated at Canterbury by Theodore and Hadrian, his principal authority.
  • Bede says that from this time all the English churches began to learn church music, known till then only in Kent.
  • Bede says he ordained Bosa, Eata and Edhed bishops at York after Wilfrid of Ripon was driven out in 678. He also says Theodore presided at Twyford, where Cuthbert of Lindisfarne was chosen bishop.
  • F. W. H. Wasserschleben says Theodore has always been praised as the author of the first English book of penance. He adds that such a book was long a main source for penitentials in France and Germany.

How they're remembered

  • Bede writes that the English churches received more advantage in his time than ever before. His epitaph at Canterbury, in Giles's verse, begins "Here rests fam'd Theodore, a Grecian name".
  • The Roman Breviary, in the Marquess of Bute's English, keeps his feast on 26 September. Its lessons say he published a book of penitentials. They also say Bede held that brighter times never shone on England than under Theodore.
  • Bede's own words in J. A. Giles's English are "Nor were there ever happier times since the English came into Britain".
  • Eadmer, in his Life of Archbishop Bregwin, writes of a learning handed down from Theodore and his disciples that watered the whole land.
  • Henry of Huntingdon writes that the archbishops of Canterbury had been Romans up to Theodore's death, and were English from then on.

Open Questions

  • Most of what is known of him comes from Bede, writing about forty years after his death. Stephen's Life of Wilfrid of Ripon, written from Wilfrid's side, adds more. Bede tells nothing of his life before he was a monk in Rome.
  • His part in Wilfrid's fall. Bede says Egfrid, king of the Northumbrians, drove Wilfrid out in 678, and that Theodore ordained new bishops for his diocese. Stephen says the king and queen won Theodore over with gifts.
  • Stephen tells that Theodore, old and ill, called Wilfrid to London and confessed he had wronged him. The later Lives by Fridegod and Eadmer, which draw on Stephen, tell it too. In Stephen's account he also wished Wilfrid to succeed him at Canterbury.
  • James Raine, who edited Stephen in 1879, writes that Theodore and King Egfrid divided the diocese without Wilfrid's assent.
  • The penitential. Its compiler gives most of it as Theodore's answers to the priest Eoda. Wasserschleben holds that Theodore wrote no penitential himself. He says a third man gathered sayings of Theodore, perhaps while Theodore still lived. Wasserschleben adds that Bede never mentions one. Charles Plummer notes the same silence, but still speaks of the penitential as Theodore's.
  • A note in the Liber pontificalis, the book of the popes' lives, says Theodore set down how many years each sin should be repented. Wasserschleben took it for part of the book's eighth-century text, repeated by Paul the Deacon. Louis Duchesne prints it as an added note borrowed from Paul the Deacon.
  • Chad. Bede says Theodore told Chad he had not been duly consecrated, kept him a bishop and completed his ordination. Stephen says Theodore had Chad removed from a see not his own, and that Chad was later ordained in full for Lichfield.
  • The four months. J. A. Giles's English has Hadrian wait four months for his hair to grow into the Roman tonsure. In Plummer's Latin the sentence names no one, and the next one, on the ordination, is Theodore's.
  • His years in office. Bede gives twenty-one years, three months and twenty-six days from his arrival at Canterbury, and also twenty-two years as bishop.
