Nineteenth-Century Baptists

Britain and North America, 1815 to 1906 — congregations that answered to no bishop, arguing their way into becoming one of Protestantism's largest families.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:02

Read along

Baptists began the century as a scattering of self-governing congregations and ended it as one of the largest Protestant families in the English-speaking world, without ever agreeing to be governed from anywhere. In London, Charles Haddon Spurgeon preached weekly to thousands at the Metropolitan Tabernacle and published sermons that sold in the millions. Late in life he broke with the Baptist Union over what he called the Down-Grade, convinced his fellow ministers were quietly giving up the doctrines they had been ordained to preach. In America the family split in 1845, when Baptists in the South, refused a slaveholding missionary appointment, formed the Southern Baptist Convention. Running alongside was Landmarkism, which held that only true Baptist congregations were churches at all and traced an unbroken Baptist succession back to the apostles.

Documented Stories

The Night at the Surrey Gardens Music Hall

Charles Haddon Spurgeon had come to the New Park Street chapel in Southwark in 1854, at nineteen, and within two years his preaching, plain, vivid, Calvinist and funny, had outgrown the chapel, and his congregation took the Surrey Gardens Music Hall, the largest auditorium in London, for Sunday evenings. On the first evening, 19 October 1856, some ten thousand people were inside and as many more outside when the service began. During the prayer voices cried ‘Fire!’ and that the galleries were falling; there was no fire, and the cries were thought malicious. The galleries rushed the stairs, a balustrade gave way, and in the crush seven people were killed and twenty-eight seriously injured. Spurgeon, on the platform, could see the confusion but not the dead, tried to go on, and then broke down and was carried from the hall. The press, which had mocked him as a boy preacher, blamed him for the size of his crowds. For days he could not pray or read the Bible; he wrote afterwards that he had thought his reason would go, and that it was a verse, ‘Him hath God exalted’, coming to him in a garden, that brought him back. He preached again on 2 November, and at the Music Hall from the 23rd, to larger crowds than before, until the Metropolitan Tabernacle opened in 1861. He referred to that night, obliquely, for the rest of his life, and his depressions, which were recurrent, he traced to it.

The Test Case, and the Answer from Boston

American Baptists had cooperated since 1814 in a Triennial Convention for foreign missions and a Home Mission Society, on an understanding that slavery would not be discussed. By the 1840s northern abolitionists were pressing the boards to refuse slaveholders, and the boards said they were neutral. Southern Baptists decided to test it. In November 1844 Georgia Baptists nominated James E. Reeve, a slaveholder, as a home missionary, stating openly that this would settle the question; the board declined to act on a nomination made as a test. The Alabama Baptist Convention demanded of the foreign board whether a slaveholder was eligible for appointment on the same terms as anyone else. The board replied in December that ‘if any one should offer himself as a missionary, having slaves, and should insist on retaining them as his property, we could not appoint him. One thing is certain; we can never be a party to any arrangement which would imply approbation of slavery.’ That was the answer. On 8 May 1845 some 293 delegates from nine southern states gathered at the First Baptist Church of Augusta, Georgia, and constituted the Southern Baptist Convention, declaring that they had been denied their rights and that the gospel was not the property of the North. It became the largest Protestant body in the United States. A hundred and fifty years later, in 1995, it adopted a resolution acknowledging that its founding was rooted in the defense of slavery, repenting of it, and apologizing to African Americans.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Baptists never agreed to be governed from anywhere. Yet in London one preacher, Charles Haddon Spurgeon, drew thousands each week and sold his printed sermons by the million. Late in life he broke with the Baptist Union over what he called the Down-Grade. He was sure his fellow ministers were quietly giving up what they had been ordained to preach. Forty years before, in 1845, American Baptists had split. Southern churches had been refused when they put forward a slaveholder as a missionary.

Legacy

The bodies formed in this century are still the shape of Baptist life. The Southern Baptist Convention became the largest Protestant denomination in the United States. The Baptist Union of Great Britain and its sister bodies carry on. Spurgeon's sermons have never gone out of print. Two older Baptist lines also ran on through these years. The Black Baptist churches of America go back to the 1770s. William Carey's Baptist mission society was founded in 1792. In his own call for it, Carey said no one had matched the Moravians as missionaries.
