Niketas Stethatos

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Byzantine Imperial Church (Macedonian Era).

Known for
Monk and priest of the monastery of Studios in Constantinople, disciple of Symeon the New Theologian and writer of his Life. His three hundred chapters on the spiritual life are in the Philokalia. His book against the Latin church, which he condemned before the emperor and the pope's envoys in 1054, is held in Latin.
Also called
Nicetas Pectoratus, the Latin form, Niketas, presbyter of Studios
Dates
Eleventh century. The compilers of the Philokalia say he lived in 1030. The envoys' record dates his recantation to the feast of John the Baptist's birth in the twelfth year of Constantine Monomachos, which Cornelius Will reckons as 24 June 1054. Some copies read the eleventh year. Will puts this down to an error in dating, since many placed the embassy in 1053. No held source gives his birth or death

Role
Monk and presbyter of Studios; spiritual writer; disciple and biographer of Symeon the New Theologian

About This Person

On the feast of John the Baptist's birth in 1054, the monk Niketas stood before the emperor Constantine Monomachos at the Studios monastery in Constantinople. At the urging of the pope's envoys, he condemned a book against the Latin church that bore his name, and the emperor had it burned. The next day, the envoys' own record says, Niketas went to them of his own accord. He took their answers, again condemned every word and act against the see of Rome, and became their close friend.

Niketas, called Stethatos, was a monk and priest of Studios. The compilers of the Philokalia, a Greek collection on prayer, say he lived in 1030 and was the truest disciple of Symeon the New Theologian. He took on his teacher's virtues so fully, they write, that he shone beside him like a second sun.

His own three hundred chapters stand in the Philokalia. Not every tear is compunction, he writes. Tears come from regret for old sins, like boiling water that cleans the heart. Compunction comes down from the Spirit like dew, once the soul has gone deep into humility. Humility brings about the blessed life, he writes elsewhere. Holy compunction is its nurse and mother, the vision of the divine light its friend and sister, and its end the Holy Trinity.

The burned book, which survives in Latin, says the Latins use unleavened bread, which is lifeless, fast on Saturdays, and forbid their priests to marry. Cornelius Will, its editor, suspects the patriarch Michael Cerularius was behind it. The reply printed under the name of Humbert of Silva Candida, one of the envoys, ends by telling Niketas to learn at last to be silent.

Michel Le Quien, John of Damascus's editor, reasons that Niketas wrote against the Latins on the Holy Spirit after the envoys left. A Latin writer, who calls him abbot of Studios, complained that the Greeks still read his name in the liturgy.

Unceasing prayer, Niketas writes, is not seen in lifted hands or the sound of the tongue. It is known in the inner work of the mind and the remembrance of God.

Key Works

  • Three centuries of chapters: three hundred chapters, practical, natural and gnostic, on tears, compunction, prayer and the divine light. In the Philokalia, volume 2, in Greek
  • Book against the Latins: on unleavened bread, the Saturday fast and the marriage of priests, which he later condemned. Held in a Latin translation in Cornelius Will's edition of 1861
  • Life of Symeon the New Theologian: known through François Combefis's long summary with Greek extracts, in Patrologia Graeca 152. The Life itself is not held

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Gregory of Sinai, in the Philokalia, puts the works of the New Theologian and of his disciple Stethatos on a short list of writings on stillness and prayer.
  • Paisius Velichkovsky translated his three hundred chapters into Slavonic. A monk in a skete on Athos told him the skete held only twenty-two in Greek. Paisius says the older Slavonic had only parts of the three hundreds.
  • Paisius Ligarides, in the 1600s, cites his book for blaming the Latins for saying masses in Lent against the canons.

How they're remembered

  • The compilers of the Philokalia call him the truest disciple of Symeon, and say his teacher's virtues shone in him like a second sun.
  • Nil Sorsky quotes him on vainglory: if you find no evil deeds in yourself, think of the perfection of the commandments, and you will find yourself a small basin beside the sea.
  • Michel Le Quien counts him among the authors of the schism, with the patriarch Michael Cerularius.

What Is Uncertain

  • The account of 1054 is the envoys' own record, which Cornelius Will judges to be by Humbert of Silva Candida. No held text of Niketas speaks of it.
  • Whether the book was his own idea. Will suspects that the patriarch Michael Cerularius had him write it, and that it was not from his own conviction. Combefis says he took Cerularius's side.
  • Whether his change of heart lasted. Michel Le Quien reasons that he wrote against the Latins on the procession of the Holy Spirit after the envoys left. A Latin writer complains that the Greeks did not strike him from the lists of names read in the liturgy. Edmond Martène, who printed that writer, dates him to about 1050 to 1100, and says Niketas strove with all his strength to defend the schism.
  • Presbyter or abbot. The Philokalia and his book call him presbyter and monk. The same Latin writer calls him abbot of Studios.
  • Who wrote the reply. Will gives it to Humbert. Wibert's Life of Leo IX, as Will reports it, says Frederick the chancellor answered him.
  • The reply calls him a cursed child of a hundred years, after Isaiah 65:20. The words are an insult and say nothing of his age.
