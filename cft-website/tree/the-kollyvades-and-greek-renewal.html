The Kollyvades & Greek Renewal

Mount Athos and the Greek islands, 1750s to 1860s — monks mocked over a quarrel about boiled wheat, who published the book that reshaped Orthodox prayer.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:13

Read along

It began as an argument about when to hold memorial services for the dead — Saturday, as tradition had it, or Sunday, as convenience on Mount Athos had made usual. And the monks who insisted on the older practice were nicknamed for the boiled wheat, kollyva, served at those services. The quarrel was small; what came out of it was not. Its leaders argued for frequent communion against a general practice of receiving perhaps once or twice a year, and in 1782 published in Venice the Philokalia, an anthology of Greek ascetic writers on prayer that became one of the most influential books ever printed in Orthodoxy. Under Ottoman rule they also did something with few parallels anywhere in Christian history. They prepared, pastorally and deliberately, people who expected to be executed for refusing to convert, and then wrote up their deaths in a New Martyrologion in 1799. For their trouble the Kollyvades were driven off Athos and scattered through the islands.

People and Stories

Voices in the Record

  • Nicodemus the Hagiorite — the movement's great editor and compiler, co-editor of the Philokalia and author of a handbook of spiritual counsel
  • Macarius of Corinth — bishop and co-editor of the Philokalia, and the movement's advocate of frequent communion
  • Athanasios Parios — its most combative theologian and defender in the controversy
  • Kosmas Aitolos — itinerant preacher who founded hundreds of village schools across Greek lands and was executed in 1779; he wrote almost nothing, and survives in what his hearers wrote down

Documented Stories

Constantine of Hydra Prepares to Die

Under Ottoman law a Christian who accepted Islam and then returned to Christianity was an apostate, and the penalty was death; the church had many who had converted under pressure and lived afterwards in shame, and clergy, the Kollyvades among them, who had thought about what to do for them. Constantine, born on Hydra about 1770, had gone as a youth to Rhodes in the household of the Ottoman governor, had there become a Muslim, and had fled in remorse, at last to Mount Athos, where he asked the monks of Iviron to help him make amends by confessing Christ in Rhodes, where he had denied him. The New Martyrologion, which Nicodemus the Hagiorite compiled the year before, describes the preparation such candidates received, and Constantine received it: months of fasting, confession, prayer and instruction under an elder, tests of his resolve, the counsel that he must neither seek death rashly nor flee it once he had declared himself, and finally the blessing to go. He returned to Rhodes in 1800, presented himself to the governor, declared that he was a Christian and had been one, endured the beatings and the prison, and was hanged on 14 November 1800. His body was recovered by the Christians; his relics went to Hydra, where a church holds them; and the Kollyvades fathers composed his service. The church had helped a man to his execution and calls it his crown, and its own texts describe the process without embarrassment.

Unseen Warfare, and Where It Came From

In 1796 there appeared at Venice, under the name of Nicodemus the Hagiorite, a book called Unseen Warfare, a manual of the Christian's struggle against his own passions, in short chapters on distrust of oneself, trust in God, prayer, the sacraments and the hour of death. Nicodemus wrote in his preface that the work was by a certain wise man, whom he did not name, and that he had corrected and supplemented it. The book was Lorenzo Scupoli's Combattimento spirituale, published in Venice in 1589 by a Theatine priest, one of the most read Catholic devotional books of the Counter-Reformation, which Francis de Sales carried in his pocket for eighteen years. Nicodemus rewrote it: he removed the references to Catholic practice, added chapters on the Jesus Prayer and the guarding of the heart from the hesychast fathers he was editing for the Philokalia, and supplied Orthodox citations throughout. The book was read everywhere in the Greek world. In 1886 Theophan the Recluse, the Russian bishop translating the Philokalia, translated Unseen Warfare too, revising it and again omitting Scupoli's name, and it became a staple of Russian monastic and lay reading; it was translated into English from Theophan's version in 1952, with an introduction that explained its history. The most anti-Latin of the Kollyvades had, in the same years in which he was denouncing Latin errors, given his church a Latin book, and the church kept it because it was true.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced Desert Monasticism Documented — In 1782 Macarius of Corinth and Nikodemos of Mount Athos printed the Philokalia at Venice, a book of old Greek writings on prayer. It opens with counsels credited to Antony the Great. It also holds works by Evagrius of Pontus and by John Cassian.

Influenced

  • influenced The Russian Church — the Synodal Century Widely Accepted — In 1782 the Kollyvades printed the Philokalia, a Greek book on prayer, and Paisius Velichkovsky put it into Slavonic. A book of 1847 from Optina says the Synod's press in Moscow first printed it. Then, through the work of Philaret of Moscow, it was printed there again.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

The dispute began over which day to hold memorial services for the dead, and the monks who insisted on the older practice were mocked with a nickname for the boiled wheat served at them. What they produced was serious: a 1782 anthology of Greek ascetic writing on prayer, the Philokalia, and an argument for frequent communion against a general practice of receiving only once or twice a year. Under Ottoman rule they also prepared, deliberately and pastorally, people who expected to be executed for refusing to convert, and wrote up their deaths in a New Martyrologion in 1799.

Legacy

The Philokalia went straight out of Greek. Paisius Velichkovsky translated it into Slavonic in 1793, Theophan the Recluse into Russian later in the century, and English translations in the twentieth century put it into the hands of readers with no Orthodox background at all. Frequent communion, the practice the Kollyvades were mocked for, is now ordinary across most of the Orthodox world. The movement is generally read as the seed of the modern Greek Orthodox revival, and its leading figures have since been canonized by the church that once expelled them.

Experience It Today

  • Mount Athos, Greece — the monastic republic where the dispute began; visits require a permit and, by the peninsula's own ancient rule, are open to men only

How Sure the Record Is

Moderate-to-rich; new-martyr preparation as documented pastoral practice

  • The Kollyvades fathers' own texts: the Philokalia (1782); Concerning Frequent Communion; Nicodemus's Handbook of Spiritual Counsel; Athanasios Parios; the controversy record (memorial-services dispute, Athonite expulsions) (primary) — This includes voices from both sides of the controversy over memorial services.
  • New-martyr preparation: Nicodemus's New Martyrologion (1799) with the documented pastoral practice of preparing candidates for martyrdom under Ottoman rule (primary) — This material is hagiographic in genre, and the executions are recorded only from the Christian side.
  • The renewal's spoken and diffused layer: Kosmas Aitolos's teachings and prophecies as recorded by hearers, his school-founding mission and martyrdom (1779); island circles (Paros, Hydra, Chios) in disciples' lives and correspondence (primary) — Kosmas himself wrote almost nothing; what survives comes through his hearers' memory. Treat the disciples' lives of him as hagiographic in genre.
  • Constantine Cavarnos, St. Nicodemos the Hagiorite (1974), with Kitromilides's essays on the Orthodox world of the era (secondary) — Cavarnos writes from an insider, devotional stance; Kitromilides writes as an academic historian.
