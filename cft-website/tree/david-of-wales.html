David of Wales

A person. The tradition that tells their story: British and Welsh Christianity.

Known for
A British bishop remembered as the founder of the monastery in the valley of Rosina and the preacher at the synod of Brefi, known almost wholly from Lives written centuries after him.
Also called
Dewi, Dewi Sant, David of Menevia, Devius Aquaticus (in the Life of Paul Aurelian)

About This Person

A great synod of the British church had gathered at Brefi, the Lives of David say, to answer a revival of the Pelagian heresy. No preacher could make the crowd hear. Paulinus, an old bishop who had once taught David, told them to send for him. He would not stand on the hill. He began to preach from the flat ground, and, says the Welsh Life, "the ground arose as a high mount under his feet".

David, called Dewi in Welsh, was a British bishop whose death the annals place around the year 600. Almost all that is told of him comes from Lives written centuries later. The Latin Life is signed by Ricemarch. Hugh Williams calls him a writer of the eleventh century and quotes a Welsh chronicle that names him under 1096.

The Lives make David the son of Sant and of Non, a nun. They tell how he founded twelve monasteries and settled in the valley of Rosina, which the Britons call Hodnant. His monks plowed without oxen, the Latin Life says, for "every one is an ox to himself". The Life says he lived one hundred and forty-seven years.

Williams thinks the David of the wonders is "the creation, probably, of Ricemarcus", meant to magnify the see of Saint David's. W. J. Rees, who printed both Lives, cites the view that such Lives were rooted in general truth but written to stir devotion, perhaps by exaggerating.

A list of Irish saints, which Williams dates to the 700s, says their second order received the mass from Bishop David and the writer Gildas. The Welsh annals give his death in the year their editor numbers 601. The Irish annals that Williams quotes give 588.

The Welsh Life gives his words after his last sermon. "Lords, brethren, and sisters, be joyful, and keep your faith and belief," he said, "and perform the small things which you have heard and seen with me."

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The Latin Life gives his monks a hard rule of work, silence and plain food: they plowed without oxen, "every one is an ox to himself".
  • A list of Irish saints, which Williams dates to the 700s, says the second order of Irish saints received the mass from Bishop David and from Gildas and Docus.
  • Some penitential canons survive in a manuscript under the heading "De libro Davidis", printed by Wasserschleben.

How they're remembered

  • The Welsh Life gives his last words: "perform the small things which you have heard and seen with me". The Latin Life has "persevere in those things, which ye have learned of me".
  • Williams: the David of the wonders is "the creation, probably, of Ricemarcus", written to magnify the see of Saint David's.

What Is Uncertain

  • Almost all that is told of him comes from Lives written centuries after him, two of them printed by W. J. Rees and one, from a Utrecht manuscript, by the Bollandists, who judge none of his Acts written by men of his time. The Latin Life is signed by Ricemarch; Hugh Williams calls him a writer of the eleventh century and quotes a Welsh chronicle naming him, son of Bishop Sulien, under 1096. Rees says the Welsh Life is an abridgement of the Latin, and cites the view that such Lives were rooted in general truth but written to stir devotion.
  • His death year. The Welsh annals give the year their editor numbers 601; the Irish annals Williams quotes give 588. W. J. Rees reports Ussher's 554, at the age of 82, and Rice Rees's "short of 566". Wasserschleben gives 544. The Bollandists argue for 544 or 550, years when the first of March fell on a Tuesday.
  • His age. The Latin Life says he lived 147 years. Ussher, as Rees reports him, says 82.
  • His birth. A bracketed entry from later manuscripts of the Welsh annals has him born thirty years after Patrick left Menevia, in a year the editor numbers 458. The Bollandists, in the Acta Sanctorum for the first of March, put Patrick's stay in the valley of Rosina in 414. They count his birth thirty years after it.
  • His teacher. His Lives name Paulinus, whom Rice Rees, as W. J. Rees quotes him, places at the White House on the Tave. The Life of Illtud makes him one of Illtud's four pupils; Williams notes that the Life of David does not know Illtud as his teacher.
  • The synod of Brefi. The Bollandists report that Spelman, Ussher and others put it in 519. The miracle of the rising ground is told by both Lives.
