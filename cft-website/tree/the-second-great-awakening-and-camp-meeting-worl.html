The Second Great Awakening & Camp-Meeting Tradition

The American frontier, 1795 to 1840 — week-long camp meetings in the woods that reset what a whole continent expected conversion to feel like.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 0:56

Read along

At the turn of the nineteenth century, settlers on the Kentucky and Tennessee frontier began traveling for days by wagon to sacramental gatherings that ran for a week at a time — and something broke loose. At Cane Ridge in August 1801 the crowd was estimated at anywhere from ten to twenty thousand people, camped in the woods around a log meeting house, with several preachers going at once and people weeping, shouting and falling to the ground. Methodist circuit riders and Baptist farmer-preachers carried the pattern across the continent, and by the 1830s Charles Finney was writing up the revival as a method that could be worked deliberately rather than waited for. Whether or not it counts as a tradition of its own, it changed what an entire continent expected conversion to feel like.

People and Stories

Voices in the Record

  • Peter Cartwright — frontier circuit rider whose blunt, funny autobiography is the genre's best-known example
  • Charles G. Finney — the lawyer-turned-evangelist who theorized the revival into a repeatable method in his Lectures on Revivals (1835)

Documented Stories

The Exercises at Cane Ridge

Barton W. Stone, the Presbyterian minister at Cane Ridge in Bourbon County, Kentucky, had gone the year before to see James McGready's sacramental meetings in Logan County and had come home to arrange one. It began on Friday 6 August 1801 and ran until the food and the people gave out the following Thursday. Estimates of the crowd ranged from ten to twenty-five thousand, in a state whose largest town had fewer than two thousand; Presbyterian, Methodist and Baptist preachers spoke at once from stumps and wagons, day and night, with the communion itself on the Sunday. In the autobiography he wrote in old age Stone set down, without defending them, the bodily exercises he had witnessed. The falling exercise, in which people dropped as if shot and lay for hours; the jerks, in which the head snapped back and forth so violently that a woman's hair cracked like a whip; the dancing exercise, which he said began with the jerks and ended in solemn dancing; the barking, which he explained as a grunt forced out by the jerks that enemies had renamed; the laughing exercise; the running; the singing exercise, in which a person sang from the breast with the mouth closed. He recorded that sinners and scoffers were struck as often as believers, and that many who fell rose changed. He also recorded that the meeting produced divisions, that the Presbytery pursued him, and that within four years he had left it.

New Lebanon, 1827: The Old Revivalists Try to Stop the New

By 1826 Charles Grandison Finney, a former law clerk, had led revivals through the towns of western New York that alarmed the revival's own establishment. His new measures, as they were called, included praying for sinners by name in public, letting women pray aloud in mixed meetings, calling the anxious forward to a bench at the front, holding meetings night after night for weeks, and addressing sinners in the language of a courtroom. Asahel Nettleton, the most respected itinerant of the older school, and Lyman Beecher of Boston, who had made the revival respectable in Connecticut, feared it would bring the whole enterprise into disgrace. A meeting was arranged at New Lebanon, New York, from 18 July 1827, between the eastern ministers and Finney's supporters from the west. It sat nine days. Resolutions were passed on both sides; Finney's party would not condemn women praying in mixed assemblies or name any practice of his as wrong, and the easterners would not endorse them; Nettleton read letters and was ill; and Beecher, according to Finney's later memory, told him that if he came to Connecticut Beecher would meet him at the state line and call out the artillery. Beecher denied it. Within five years Beecher had invited Finney to Boston. The revival of the settled churches and the revival of the frontier had met and neither had given way, and the method the frontier had made was on its way to being written down as a science.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Methodist preachers helped to lead these revivals. So did Presbyterians and Baptists. Barton Stone's side of the Stone-Campbell movement grew out of them. Later the Holiness movement took up their camp meeting.

Descended from

  • grew out of The Methodist Revival Widely Accepted Major — In 1810 Jesse Lee, a Methodist preacher, wrote about the early camp meetings. In some cases, he said, Methodist, Presbyterian and Baptist ministers joined to run the same one. In 1805 Bishop Francis Asbury hoped for a camp meeting in every circuit of one district. So the revival grew in part out of Methodist work.

Direct descendants

  • gave rise to The Stone-Campbell Restoration Movement Documented Major — Richard McNemar, a preacher in the Kentucky revival, wrote its history in 1807. He names Barton W. Stone as one of five ministers who left the Synod of Kentucky. Many of the revival's converts joined them. This was the start of the Stone side of the Stone-Campbell movement.
  • gave rise to Restorationist-Apocalyptic Ferment & Adventism Inferential-Thin Major — William Miller's followers of the 1830s and 1840s came from many American churches, in the decades of the great frontier revivals. The evidence is thin. The tie rests on that timing and on modern books on Miller and on Adventist belief.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced

  • influenced The Holiness Movement Dominant Modern Reconstruction — The Holiness movement grew from Methodist teaching in the 1830s, not straight out of the frontier revivals. But historians show that it took over their camp meeting. From 1867 Holiness camp meetings were run on a national scale.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

At Cane Ridge in August 1801, crowds put as high as twenty thousand camped in the woods for a week of preaching, weeping and shouting. Methodist circuit riders and Baptist preachers carried that pattern across the frontier. By the 1830s Charles Finney was teaching revival as a method. It could be worked for on purpose, he said, not just waited for. Barton Stone's side of the Stone-Campbell Restoration movement grew straight out of this ferment. The later Holiness movement took up its camp meeting.

Legacy

It made revival a lasting part of American religion, not a rare event. It handed on a whole toolkit that is still in use: the protracted meeting, the altar call, the anxious bench, the campground and its choruses. Barton Stone's wing of the Stone-Campbell Restoration movement came straight out of its ferment. The Holiness movement grew from Methodist teaching, but it later held camp meetings of its own. And out of Holiness came Pentecostalism. Many of its old campgrounds outlived the frontier, and some are still in use.

Experience It Today

  • Cane Ridge Meeting House, near Paris, Kentucky — the 1791 log meeting house where the great revival of 1801 was held, preserved inside a protective stone shell and open seasonally

How Sure the Record Is

Rich

  • Itinerants' own accounts: Barton Stone's autobiography (1847, incl. Cane Ridge eyewitness); Peter Cartwright, Autobiography (1856); James B. Finley, Autobiography (1853); Francis Asbury's Journal (primary) — These are firsthand accounts from circuit-riding preachers. The autobiographies were written well after the events they describe, so treat them as retrospective accounts rather than contemporary records.
  • Charles G. Finney, Lectures on Revivals of Religion (1835) and Memoirs (1876; critical ed. Rosell & Dupuis, 1989) (primary) — Finney theorized revival from inside the movement, but his writing postdates the camp-meeting practices it discusses and should be read as later reflection, not a contemporary account of Cane Ridge-era revivalism.
  • The camp meeting's own practice record: songsters and choruses; Methodist quarterly-meeting, conference, and class records; Cane Ridge (1801) participant testimonies (primary) — This is the revival's own lived practice and sung theology. Sources are scattered across archives and editions, and not all are easy to access.
  • Women's and Black participants: conversion narratives in the Methodist Magazine; Zilpha Elaw's camp-meeting preaching (Memoirs, 1846); John F. Watson, Methodist Error (1819) (primary) — Watson's Methodist Error is a hostile insider's complaint, but it happens to be the earliest description of Black chorus and ring-shout practice, which gives it real evidential value despite the author's hostility.
  • Nathan Hatch, The Democratization of American Christianity (Yale, 1989), with Paul Conkin, Cane Ridge: America's Pentecost (Wisconsin, 1990) (secondary)
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 8, ch. 22 'Christian America' and 'Christian Canada' (Noll) (reference)
