The Early Church Era · mid-2nd c. to after 404 · Palestine, Syria

Ebionite / Nazoraean Current

The Ebionites and Nazoraeans were Jews who believed that Jesus was the Christ and went on keeping the Law of Moses: circumcision, the Sabbath and the food laws. Later church writers placed them in Syria and beyond the Jordan.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 4:37

Read along

In the middle of the second century, at Ephesus, as Eusebius later recorded, a Jew named Trypho questioned the Christian teacher Justin. Suppose a man believes that Jesus is the Christ, the Messiah God had promised, and obeys him, but still wants to keep the Law of Moses: will he be saved? In my view he will, Justin answered, if he does not press the Law on non-Jewish believers. Trypho asked whether some said no. "There are some, Trypho, I answered, who do not dare to share even company or a home with such people. I do not agree with them."

Such people were Jews who believed that Jesus was the Christ and went on keeping that Law: circumcision, the Sabbath, the food laws. Their own books are lost, so we meet them only through other church writers, nearly all of whom argued against them, and those writers do not agree on what they believed about Jesus. Church writers wrote of them from Justin's day to about 400, and the later ones place them in Syria and beyond the Jordan.

By about 180 they were called Ebionites, and Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon in Gaul, counted them among the heresies, the teachings he held false. They were circumcised, he writes, and lived so much as Jews that they revered Jerusalem "as if it were the house of God". They rejected Paul as "an apostate from the Law", one who had abandoned it. They held, he writes, that Jesus was the son of Joseph.

One of them, Symmachus, put the Hebrew Scriptures into Greek, and writers who called them heretics used his version. In his last years Origen, the teacher from Alexandria, said that their name came from the Hebrew word for "poor". In another book he scorned them as poor in understanding, named from the poverty of their minds. He knew two kinds of them: some confessed with the church that Jesus was born of a virgin, and some denied it and said he was born like other men.

The Nazoraeans named themselves after Nazareth, says Epiphanius, a bishop on Cyprus, who about 375 wrote of them and the Ebionites as two heresies, side by side. They were Jews in everything: they read the Law and the prophets in Hebrew, and believed in one God and in his child Jesus Christ. What they held about Christ himself, he says, he cannot tell, whether a mere man, or born of Mary by the Holy Spirit. In the synagogues, he says, the Jews prayed three times a day that God would curse the Nazoraeans, because they were Jews who preached that Jesus is the Christ.

In Beroea, a city of Syria, the Nazoraeans used a gospel written in Hebrew letters, and they let Jerome copy it, as he wrote in 392. Elsewhere he calls it the Gospel according to the Hebrews, and says he put it into Greek and Latin. In it, after the resurrection, the Lord appears to James, his brother. James had sworn not to eat bread from the hour he drank the Lord's cup until he saw him risen. Jerome quotes what follows, where the dead are those who sleep: "He took bread and blessed it and broke it, and gave it to James the Just. He said to him, 'My brother, eat your bread, for the Son of Man has risen from among those who sleep.'"

In 404 the Nazoraeans were still a sect among the Jews in all the synagogues of the East, Jerome wrote to Augustine, and the Pharisees, the Jewish teachers, still condemned them. What they believed about Jesus he set down as the church's own faith, and in the same breath gave his verdict. "They believe in Christ, the Son of God, born of the Virgin Mary. They say he is the one who suffered under Pontius Pilate and rose again. We too believe in him. But while they want to be both Jews and Christians, they are neither Jews nor Christians."

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Some Jewish communities of believers in Jesus kept observing Torah, circumcision, and the Sabbath, regarding Paul as someone who had abandoned the Law. Nothing written by these communities survives whole - everything known of them comes through other writers who disagreed with each other and wrote from the outside, so even what they actually believed remains genuinely uncertain.

Legacy

No community descends from them. Jerome wrote in 404 that the Nazoraeans were still a sect among the Jews in all the synagogues of the East, and Augustine, about 400, that their number was very small but the sect still continued; the gospel material attributed to them survives only as quotations in the writers who opposed them. What they left behind is a question rather than an institution. Modern Messianic Jewish movements are sometimes compared to them, but no line of descent connects them.

Their books passed through the hands of the writers who opposed them. Origen set Symmachus's Greek version beside the other Greek versions of the Hebrew Scriptures in his book of columns, and Jerome used his renderings. Jerome copied the gospel the Nazoraeans used, and says he put it into Greek and Latin. Later writers made Ebion a byword for those who held Christ only a man: Damasus's creed condemns Photinus "renewing the heresy of Ebion", and Hilary calls Photinus "the Ebion of our day."

Open Questions

  • What they believed about Jesus. Irenaeus says they held that he was begotten by Joseph, and would not accept that the Holy Spirit came upon Mary. Origen knew two kinds, one confessing that Jesus was born of a virgin and one denying it. Eusebius gives two kinds as well: the first held him a plain man, the son of a man and Mary; the second did not deny that he was born of a virgin and the Holy Spirit, but would not grant that he existed before, being God, Word and Wisdom. Hippolytus says they held that Jesus was called the Christ because he alone kept the Law fully, and that they too, by keeping it, could become Christs. Epiphanius says the Ebionites held Jesus born of a man's seed, and of the Nazoraeans he cannot say. Jerome, in 404, says the Nazoraeans believed in Christ as the church did. Augustine, writing later and naming Epiphanius, says the Nazoraeans confess Christ the Son of God, and the Ebionites call him only a man.
  • One people or two. Origen and Eusebius give one name, Ebionites, to two kinds. Epiphanius writes of the Nazoraeans and the Ebionites as two heresies, side by side, the ninth and tenth in his list. Jerome, in one letter, speaks of the heresy of Cerinthus and Ebion, and of a sect among the Jews called the sect of the Minaeans and commonly called Nazoraeans. Augustine says Faustus spoke of them as Symmachians or Nazoraeans.
  • Their names. Origen says in Against Celsus that they received their very name "from the poverty of the law, according to the literal acceptation of the word", since Ebion means poor; in On First Principles he calls them poor in understanding. Eusebius says the ancients called them Ebionites because they held poor and mean opinions concerning Christ. Tertullian, Hippolytus and Epiphanius name a founder, Ebion. Epiphanius says the Nazoraeans named themselves after the place Nazareth.
  • Their gospel. Irenaeus says the Ebionites used only Matthew's Gospel. Eusebius counts the Gospel according to the Hebrews among the disputed books, one "with which those of the Hebrews that have accepted Christ are especially delighted", and says the Ebionites used only it. Epiphanius says the Nazoraeans had Matthew's gospel complete in Hebrew, still in Hebrew letters, though he does not know whether they took out the genealogies; he quotes the Ebionites' gospel as one they altered, giving John the Baptist wild honey that tasted of manna, like a cake in oil, so that, he says, they put cakes in place of locusts, and having Jesus say he came to abolish the sacrifices. In 392 Jerome calls the book the Nazoraeans of Beroea used Matthew's Hebrew gospel, and says a copy was in the library at Caesarea. Later he says the Gospel according to the Hebrews was written in the Chaldee and Syrian language in Hebrew letters, and that it is the Gospel according to the Apostles, or, as most maintain, according to Matthew.
  • The baptism scene. Jerome also quotes this gospel's account of the Lord's mother and brothers urging him to go and be baptized by John. He answers by asking what sin he has committed that he should go, unless what he has just said is ignorance.
  • Justin's terms. Justin says such people keep the Law "through weak-mindedness". If they chose to live with the Christians and did not press circumcision, the Sabbath or the like on them, he would join them and "associate with them in all things as kinsmen and brethren." He did not approve of those of Trypho's people who compel non-Jewish believers to keep the Law, or will not live with them.
  • Their charge against Paul. Epiphanius reports that the Ebionites said Paul was a Greek, the son of Greek parents, who went up to Jerusalem, wanted to marry the priest's daughter, and so became a proselyte and was circumcised. When he did not win her, they said, he grew angry and wrote against circumcision, the Sabbath and the Law. Eusebius and Origen also say they rejected Paul's letters.
  • Neither side. Epiphanius says the Nazoraeans differed from the Jews in one thing, believing in Christ, and from the Christians in one thing, being still bound by the Law, circumcision, the Sabbath and the rest.
  • The Sabbath and the Lord's day. Eusebius says they kept the Sabbath and the rest of the Jewish way just as Jews did, and also, like other Christians, kept the Lord's day in memory of the Savior's resurrection.
  • Food. Origen says the Ebionites, who differ little from the "material Jews", accused other Christians of breaking the Law's rules on clean and unclean meat.
  • The prophets. Irenaeus says the Ebionites expound the prophets in a way of their own. Epiphanius says the Nazoraeans read the Law, the prophets and the other writings in Hebrew, and that the Ebionites accept Abraham, Isaac, Jacob, Moses, Aaron and Joshua, but no prophet after them, and scorn David and Solomon.
  • Where they lived. Justin, Irenaeus and Origen give no place for them. Epiphanius places the Nazoraeans at Beroea in Coele Syria, in the Decapolis near Pella, and at Kokaba in Bashan. He places the Ebionites in Nabataea, at Paneas, in Moab, at Kokaba in Bashan beyond Adraa, and on Cyprus. Jerome places the Nazoraeans at Beroea, a city of Syria.
  • A beginning at Pella. Eusebius says that before the war the church in Jerusalem was commanded by a revelation to leave the city and live at Pella, a town of Peraea, beyond the Jordan. Epiphanius says the Nazoraeans began there, after all the disciples had moved from Jerusalem to Pella, since Christ had told them to leave the city before its siege, and that the Ebionites began after Jerusalem was taken, among the believers then living at Pella. He is the only witness to either beginning.
  • Symmachus and Juliana. Origen set Symmachus's Greek version beside those of Aquila, Theodotion and the Seventy. Eusebius, keeping Origen's own note, says Symmachus wrote commentaries, still read in his day, in which he seems to support the heresy by attacking Matthew's Gospel, and that Origen had these and other writings of Symmachus on the Scriptures from a woman, Juliana, who had the books from Symmachus himself. Jerome calls Symmachus "an adherent of the same sect".
  • Epiphanius on the Ebionites' life. He says they have elders and synagogue rulers, and call their church a synagogue, and that their teachers press the young to marry early. Besides baptism, he says, they wash every day, and once a year they keep a rite with unleavened bread and with water only. They once prized virginity for the sake of James, the Lord's brother. They use the Circuits of Peter, written through Clement, which he says they altered, and Ascents of James that speak against the Temple, the sacrifices and the fire of the altar. They hold that God appointed two, Christ over the world to come and the devil over this one.
  • Jerome on John's Gospel. Jerome writes that John wrote his Gospel against Cerinthus and other heretics, and especially against "the then growing dogma of the Ebionites, who assert that Christ did not exist before Mary."

On the Nicene Creed

Some outside writers report that they denied Jesus's virgin birth and divinity; others report the opposite of the group they call Nazoraeans. Because nothing written by these communities survives whole, what they actually confessed cannot be established either way — an open question about the evidence, not a settled answer about their doctrine.
