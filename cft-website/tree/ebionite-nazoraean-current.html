The Early Church Era · 1st-4th c. · Palestine, Syria

Ebionite / Nazoraean Current

Known to church writers from the second century to about 400, and placed by the later ones in Syria and beyond the Jordan — Jewish followers of Jesus who never stopped keeping the Law, and whose own books have not survived to tell us what they actually believed.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 4:37

Read along

In the middle of the second century, at Ephesus, as Eusebius later recorded, a Jew named Trypho questioned the Christian teacher Justin. Suppose a man believes that Jesus is the Christ, the Messiah God had promised, and obeys him, but still wants to keep the Law of Moses: will he be saved? In my view he will, Justin answered, if he does not press the Law on non-Jewish believers. Trypho asked whether some said no. "There are some, Trypho, I answered, who do not dare to share even company or a home with such people. I do not agree with them."

Such people were Jews who believed that Jesus was the Christ and went on keeping that Law: circumcision, the Sabbath, the food laws. Their own books are lost, so we meet them only through other church writers, nearly all of whom argued against them, and those writers do not agree on what they believed about Jesus. Church writers wrote of them from Justin's day to about 400, and the later ones place them in Syria and beyond the Jordan.

By about 180 they were called Ebionites, and Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon in Gaul, counted them among the heresies, the teachings he held false. They were circumcised, he writes, and lived so much as Jews that they revered Jerusalem "as if it were the house of God". They rejected Paul as "an apostate from the Law", one who had abandoned it. They held, he writes, that Jesus was the son of Joseph.

One of them, Symmachus, put the Hebrew Scriptures into Greek, and writers who called them heretics used his version. In his last years Origen, the teacher from Alexandria, said that their name came from the Hebrew word for "poor". In another book he scorned them as poor in understanding, named from the poverty of their minds. He knew two kinds of them: some confessed with the church that Jesus was born of a virgin, and some denied it and said he was born like other men.

The Nazoraeans named themselves after Nazareth, says Epiphanius, a bishop on Cyprus, who about 375 wrote of them and the Ebionites as two heresies, side by side. They were Jews in everything: they read the Law and the prophets in Hebrew, and believed in one God and in his child Jesus Christ. What they held about Christ himself, he says, he cannot tell, whether a mere man, or born of Mary by the Holy Spirit. In the synagogues, he says, the Jews prayed three times a day that God would curse the Nazoraeans, because they were Jews who preached that Jesus is the Christ.

In Beroea, a city of Syria, the Nazoraeans used a gospel written in Hebrew letters, and they let Jerome copy it, as he wrote in 392. Elsewhere he calls it the Gospel according to the Hebrews, and says he put it into Greek and Latin. In it, after the resurrection, the Lord appears to James, his brother. James had sworn not to eat bread from the hour he drank the Lord's cup until he saw him risen. Jerome quotes what follows, where the dead are those who sleep: "He took bread and blessed it and broke it, and gave it to James the Just. He said to him, 'My brother, eat your bread, for the Son of Man has risen from among those who sleep.'"

In 404 the Nazoraeans were still a sect among the Jews in all the synagogues of the East, Jerome wrote to Augustine, and the Pharisees, the Jewish teachers, still condemned them. What they believed about Jesus he set down as the church's own faith, and in the same breath gave his verdict. "They believe in Christ, the Son of God, born of the Virgin Mary. They say he is the one who suffered under Pontius Pilate and rose again. We too believe in him. But while they want to be both Jews and Christians, they are neither Jews nor Christians."

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Some Jewish communities of believers in Jesus kept observing Torah, circumcision, and the Sabbath, regarding Paul as someone who had abandoned the Law. Nothing written by these communities survives whole - everything known of them comes through other writers who disagreed with each other and wrote from the outside, so even what they actually believed remains genuinely uncertain.

Legacy

No community descends from them. Jerome wrote in 404 that the Nazoraeans were still a sect among the Jews in all the synagogues of the East, and Augustine, about 400, that their number was very small but the sect still continued; the gospel material attributed to them survives only as quotations in the writers who opposed them. What they left behind is a question rather than an institution. Modern Messianic Jewish movements are sometimes compared to them, but no line of descent connects them.

Their books passed through the hands of the writers who opposed them. Origen set Symmachus's Greek version beside the other Greek versions of the Hebrew Scriptures in his book of columns, and Jerome used his renderings. Jerome copied the gospel the Nazoraeans used, and says he put it into Greek and Latin. Later writers made Ebion a byword for those who held Christ only a man: Damasus's creed condemns Photinus "renewing the heresy of Ebion", and Hilary calls Photinus "the Ebion of our day."

Sources

Doctrinal Floor Note

Some outside writers report that they denied Jesus's virgin birth and divinity; others report the opposite of the group they call Nazoraeans. Because nothing written by these communities survives whole, what they actually confessed cannot be established either way — an open question about the evidence, not a settled answer about their doctrine.
