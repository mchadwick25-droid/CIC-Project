Polish Catholicism — Wyszyński and John Paul II

Poland, 1918 to 2010 — the church of the Jasna Góra Vows, the bishops' letter to Germany and John Paul II's Warsaw homily of 1979.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:26

Read along

Cardinal Stefan Wyszyński wrote the Jasna Góra Vows of the Polish Nation while interned at Komańcza. He finished them on 16 May 1956. On 26 August 1956 Bishop Michał Klepacz read them at Jasna Góra. On 18 November 1965, in Rome, the Polish bishops sent a message to their German brother bishops. Archbishop Bolesław Kominek drafted it, and 34 bishops signed it, Wyszyński and Karol Wojtyła among them. On 2 June 1979 Wojtyła, now Pope John Paul II, preached in Victory Square in Warsaw. He prayed for the Spirit to come down and renew the face of the earth, and of this land. A lay renewal grew inside the church too. The Light-Life movement began with a first oasis retreat in 1954, led by Father Franciszek Blachnicki. From 1969 it was called the Movement of the Living Church, and in 1976 it took the name Light-Life. In neighboring Lithuania, Father Sigitas Tamkevičius produced the Chronicle of the Catholic Church in Lithuania at Simnas. Its first issue is dated 19 March 1972, and its 81 issues ran to 19 March 1989.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 1956 Cardinal Stefan Wyszyński, interned at Komańcza, wrote the Jasna Góra Vows of the Polish Nation. In November 1965 the Polish bishops wrote to their German brother bishops. Wyszyński and Karol Wojtyła were among the 34 who signed. In June 1979 Wojtyła, by then Pope John Paul II, preached in Warsaw's Victory Square.

Legacy

Jerzy Kłoczowski told its story as part of the whole history of Polish Christianity. Brian Porter-Szűcs read it through faith, modernity and the nation in Faith and Fatherland (2011).

Sources

On the Nicene Creed

No question. It is the Catholic Church in Poland, which confesses the Creed.

How Sure the Record Is

Strong. The bishops' dated acts are on record, among them the Jasna Góra Vows of 1956 and the letter to the German bishops of 1965. Kłoczowski's history (in Polish 1987–91, in English 2000) and Porter-Szűcs's Faith and Fatherland (2011) carry its story.
