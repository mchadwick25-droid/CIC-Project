The Imperial Church Era · c. 350-430 · Antioch

Antioch, today in Turkey

Antiochene Exegetical Christianity (Chrysostom-centered)

Antioch, c. 350 to 430 — a school of preachers who refused to allegorize the hard sayings away, and told a city of shopkeepers and slaves exactly what the plain text asked of them.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:31

Read along

Antioch was one of the largest cities of the Roman East, and the Christianity that grew there developed a distinctive habit of reading. Where Alexandria hunted for the hidden spiritual sense beneath the text, Antioch's teachers insisted on what the words actually said in their own historical setting — and then pressed hard on what that demanded of the people in front of them. John Chrysostom, trained in rhetoric by the pagan orator Libanius and in Scripture by Diodore of Tarsus, preached in Antioch through the 380s and 390s to congregations of tradesmen, householders, and slaves: on money, on anger, on how you treat your servants, on the theater and the racetrack, on almsgiving as a matter of justice rather than generosity. In 387, after the city's crowds smashed the imperial statues and Antioch waited to learn whether it would be destroyed in reprisal, he preached the sermons On the Statues to a terrified population. And the reason hundreds of his sermons survive at all is largely that people wanted to hear them again. At the end of 397 he was taken to Constantinople and made its archbishop, where he preached the same way to the imperial court, which is roughly why he died in exile in 407.

Documented Stories

‘Do Not Make Me a Widow a Second Time’

John — not yet called Chrysostom — was about twenty when his friend Basil proposed that they leave Antioch together for the monastic life in the hills. His father had died when he was an infant; his mother Anthusa had been widowed at twenty and had refused to remarry, raising him and his sister on the estate and paying for his education under the pagan orator Libanius, who is said to have remarked, ‘What women these Christians have.’ John tells what happened next in the opening of his book On the Priesthood, written years later. When Anthusa learned of the plan she took him by the hand, led him into her own room, sat him down on the bed where she had borne him, and wept. She reminded him of what it had cost her to stay a widow — the household to run, the lawsuits, the servants’ contempt, the family pressure — and that she had done it for his sake. ‘I ask one favour: do not make me a widow a second time. Wait until I am dead. Then go wherever you like, even across the sea.’ He stayed. He recorded her plea without softening it or defending himself against it, and it remains one of the few places in patristic literature where a mother’s claim on a son is allowed to win an argument against the call of the desert.

The Eight Sermons Against the Judaizers

In the autumn of 386, a year into his ministry as a presbyter of Antioch, John learned that members of his congregation were going to the synagogue for the Jewish New Year and the Day of Atonement, fasting with the Jews, taking oaths there because they thought the oaths more binding, and seeking cures from Jewish healers. Antioch had a large and ancient Jewish community and the two peoples lived and worked side by side; to many Christians the festivals were simply the city’s holy days. John preached against it in a series delivered over that autumn and the next: eight homilies that survive under the title Against the Jews, though his target was his own people. Their rhetoric is the problem. He called the synagogue a brothel, a den of thieves, the dwelling of demons; he said the Jews were fit for slaughter, that God hated them, that they had killed Christ and been abandoned; he used every weapon the rhetorical schools taught to make the festivals repulsive. The sermons were preached to keep Christians away from Jewish worship, and they may have done that in Antioch. Copied, translated and quoted for fifteen centuries, they supplied Christian anti-Judaism with a vocabulary from a saint, and were used in pogrom-era Russia and in Nazi Germany. The tradition that gave the church its most searching sermons on wealth, anger and the poor also produced these, in the same voice, in the same two years.

In Its Own Record

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced The Church of Antioch in the Third Century Inferential-Thin — Jerome says Lucian, a priest of Antioch, studied Scripture so closely that copies still carried his name. Jerome does not tie his work to Diodore, Theodore of Mopsuestia or Chrysostom. The tie to these later teachers at Antioch is an inference, not a report.

Influenced

  • influenced The Church of the East under Persia Documented — Barhadbeshabba wrote about the year 600. He says Theodore of Mopsuestia's commentaries were put into Syriac at Edessa, where future Persian bishops studied them. In 585 a synod of the Church of the East met. It defended 'the books and the teaching of saint Theodore' against those who attacked him.
  • influenced The Reformed Cities - Zurich & Geneva Documented — Calvin's Institutes quote Chrysostom with delight: 'The foundation of our philosophy is humility.' Yet Calvin also faults him and the other Greek Fathers. He says they gave the human will too much power.
  • influenced Lutheran Wittenberg & Its Congregations Documented — The Augsburg Confession of 1530 calls on Chrysostom as a witness to how the early church shared Communion. Melanchthon wrote a defense of the Confession, known as the Apology. In it he quotes a saying of Chrysostom. It runs: contrition in the heart, confession in the mouth, humility in the work.
  • influenced The Church of the East under Persia Widely Accepted — Nestorius wrote his defense, the Bazaar of Heracleides, in Greek, and his books were ordered burned. It survives only in a Syriac version, made around 535 by one scholar's dating. The one surviving copy was kept in the library of the patriarch of the Church of the East at Kotchanes.

Argued Against

  • argued against Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition Documented — Facundus of Hermiane took up the defense of Theodore of Mopsuestia in the sixth century. He names a book that Theodore wrote against Origen, called On Allegory and History. The book itself is lost. Socrates of Constantinople and Sozomen say that Diodore, who taught Theodore, kept to the literal sense of Scripture. He steered clear of allegory.
  • argued against Anomoean (Eunomian) Christianity Documented — Theodoret, who wrote in the fifth century, says Bishop Leontius of Antioch made Aetius a deacon. Two ascetics of the city, Flavian and Diodore, protested in public that this brought shame on the church. Leontius then suspended Aetius.
  • argued against The Church of Alexandria and Miaphysite Egypt Documented — In exile, around 451, Nestorius wrote his own defense. He called it the Bazaar of Heracleides. In it he speaks straight to Cyril of Alexandria. He says Cyril wrote him a hostile letter that showed he was his enemy.

Opposed By

  • argued against Imperial and Juridical Christianity Documented Major — Two synods condemned John Chrysostom. Then the emperor barred him from his own church. Two months later, as Socrates of Constantinople writes, 'a decree of the emperor sent him into exile'.Lasting result: After synods condemned John Chrysostom, his followers left the church. They met on their own and were known as the Johannites.
  • argued against The Byzantine Imperial Church (Justinianic) Documented Major — In 553 a council at Constantinople condemned Theodore of Mopsuestia by name. He had been dead for more than a hundred years. Its twelfth canon curses anyone who defends him or says his reading of Scripture is orthodox.Lasting result: The Second Council of Constantinople cursed Theodore of Mopsuestia. It also cursed all who stood up for him or said the way he read Scripture was orthodox.
  • argued against The Church of Alexandria and Miaphysite Egypt Documented Major — Cyril of Alexandria heard of the sermons of Nestorius, the new bishop in the capital. He wrote to warn him twice. Then he wrote five books against him. In 431 the council at Ephesus said Nestorius was no longer a bishop.Lasting result: In 431 the council at Ephesus put Nestorius out as bishop. Maximian took his place.

In Contact With

  • in contact with Imperial and Juridical Christianity Documented Major — John Chrysostom was made bishop of Constantinople. Socrates says it was the emperor's court that chose him. The chamberlain Eutropius told Theophilus of Alexandria to consecrate John or face trial. Back in 381 the emperor had made a law. It named the bishops whose communion marked a Catholic church, and Diodore of Tarsus was one of them.Lasting result: A law of July 381 named Diodore of Tarsus and others. It said that every church must go to bishops in communion with them.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Where Alexandria's teachers hunted for a hidden spiritual sense beneath Scripture, Antioch's insisted on what the words actually said, and pressed hard on what that demanded of their listeners. John Chrysostom preached that way through the 380s and 390s to Antioch's tradesmen, householders, and slaves — on money, anger, and how they treated their own servants — and in 387, after crowds smashed the imperial statues, preached his sermons On the Statues to a city waiting to learn whether it would be destroyed in reprisal. Taken to Constantinople in 397 to preach the same way to the imperial court, he died in exile a decade later.

Legacy

Chrysostom's sermons never stopped being read. Reformation writers quoted him too. Calvin prized his saying that humility is 'the foundation of our philosophy'. But he thought Chrysostom gave the human will too much power. The Augsburg Confession of 1530 cited him on how the early church shared Communion. And the Communion liturgy that carries his name is still the normal Sunday rite of the Eastern Orthodox churches. The Antiochene way of reading held that a text means what it meant to those who first heard it. Later Western Bible scholars made a similar historical-grammatical method standard. How much they owed to Antioch directly is not clear. The school's own fate was harsher. Theodore of Mopsuestia was condemned at Constantinople in 553, more than a century after his death. Much of his work survives only in Syriac translation, kept safe by the Church of the East.

Experience It Today
