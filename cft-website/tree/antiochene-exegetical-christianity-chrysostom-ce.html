The Imperial Church Era · c. 350-430 · Antioch

Antioch, today in Turkey

Antiochene Exegetical Christianity (Chrysostom-centered)

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Antioch was one of the largest cities of the Roman East, and the Christianity that grew there developed a distinctive habit of reading. Where Alexandria hunted for the hidden spiritual sense beneath the text, Antioch's teachers insisted on what the words actually said in their own historical setting — and then pressed hard on what that demanded of the people in front of them. John Chrysostom, trained in rhetoric by the pagan orator Libanius and in Scripture by Diodore of Tarsus, preached in Antioch through the 380s and 390s to congregations of tradesmen, householders, and slaves: on money, on anger, on how you treat your servants, on the theater and the racetrack, on almsgiving as a matter of justice rather than generosity. In 387, after the city's crowds smashed the imperial statues and Antioch waited to learn whether it would be destroyed in reprisal, he preached the sermons On the Statues to a terrified population. And the reason hundreds of his sermons survive at all is largely that people wanted to hear them again. At the end of 397 he was taken to Constantinople and made its archbishop, where he preached the same way to the imperial court, which is roughly why he died in exile in 407.

In Its Own Record

Influenced By

  • The Church of Antioch in the Third Century Inferential-Thin — Jerome says Lucian, a presbyter of Antioch, studied Scripture so closely that copies still carried his name. No source we hold links his work to Diodore, Theodore or Chrysostom. The tie to the later Antiochene teachers is an inference, not a report.

Influenced

  • The Church of the East under Persia Documented — Barhadbeshabba, writing about 600, says Theodore's commentaries were translated into Syriac at Edessa, where future Persian bishops studied them. In 585 a synod of the Church of the East defended 'the books and the teaching of saint Theodore' against his critics.
  • The Reformed Cities - Zurich & Geneva Documented — Calvin's Institutes quote Chrysostom with delight: 'The foundation of our philosophy is humility.' Calvin also faults him and the other Greek Fathers for giving the human will too much power.
  • Lutheran Wittenberg & Its Congregations Documented — The Augsburg Confession of 1530 cites Chrysostom as a witness to the early church's shared Communion. Melanchthon's Apology of the Confession quotes his saying: contrition in the heart, confession in the mouth, humility in the work.

Argued Against

  • Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition Documented — Facundus of Hermiane, a sixth-century defender of Theodore, names a book On Allegory and History that Theodore wrote against Origen. The book itself is lost. Socrates and Sozomen say Theodore's teacher Diodore kept to the literal sense of Scripture and avoided allegory.
  • Anomoean (Eunomian) Christianity Documented — Theodoret, writing in the fifth century, says Bishop Leontius of Antioch made Aetius a deacon. Two ascetics of the city, Flavian and Diodore, protested in public that this disgraced the church. Leontius then suspended Aetius.

Opposed By

  • Imperial and Juridical Christianity Documented — After two synods condemned John Chrysostom, the emperor barred him from his church. Two months later, Socrates writes, 'a decree of the emperor sent him into exile'.
  • The Byzantine Imperial Church (Justinianic) Documented — The Council of Constantinople in 553 condemned Theodore of Mopsuestia by name, more than a century after his death. Its twelfth canon anathematizes anyone who defends him or calls his exegesis orthodox.

In Contact With

  • Imperial and Juridical Christianity Documented — Socrates says the imperial court chose John Chrysostom as bishop of Constantinople. The chamberlain Eutropius made Theophilus of Alexandria consecrate him or face trial. An imperial law of 381 had already named Diodore of Tarsus among the bishops whose communion marked a Catholic church.
