Melitius of Lycopolis

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Melitian Schism.

Known for
An Egyptian bishop whose quarrel with Peter of Alexandria over the lapsed led to a long schism, the Church of the Martyrs.
Also called
Meletius (the usual spelling in the Greek and Latin writers)
Dates
Bishop of Lycopolis; excommunicated by Peter about 306, by H. Idris Bell's dating; died after the Council of 325
Place
Lycopolis, in the Thebaid of Upper Egypt
Role
Bishop and schism leader

About This Person

Epiphanius of Salamis tells of a prison in Egypt during the Great Persecution, early in the fourth century. Peter, bishop of Alexandria, was held there with Melitius of Lycopolis and other clergy. They split over Christians who had given way. Peter wanted to be gentle with them. Melitius wanted to be strict. Peter hung a cloak across the prison like a curtain. A deacon called out that Peter's friends should come to his side, and the others to Melitius. Most of the clergy and monks, Epiphanius says, went to Melitius.

Epiphanius, a foe of every heresy, wrote some seventy years later. Archibald Robertson, who edited Athanasius, calls this the Melitians' own account. H. Idris Bell, who published papyri of their church in 1924, thinks Epiphanius had good sources.

Melitius was bishop of Lycopolis, in Upper Egypt. Two letters of the time speak against him. Four bishops in prison scolded him for making priests in their churches. One of them was Phileas of Thmuis. Peter wrote that Melitius had "ordained in the prison several unto himself." He told his flock to shun him.

Athanasius of Alexandria later held Peter's see. He wrote that Peter put Melitius out for many crimes, among them "offering sacrifice to idols." Bell doubts this. He calls Athanasius an unbending foe of the Melitians. Such charges came easily after a persecution, he says. He and Robertson agree the split began over church order. It did not begin over doctrine.

In 325 the Council of Nicaea let Melitius keep the title of bishop. But he could not ordain. Its letter, kept by Socrates of Constantinople, left him "a nominal dignity." Sozomen, the church historian, says that near the end he named his friend John to follow him. This broke the council's terms.

His followers called themselves the Church of the Martyrs, Epiphanius says. Later they sided with the Arians against Athanasius. Bell traces them in Egypt into the eighth century.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The Melitian churches, which called themselves the Church of the Martyrs. H. Idris Bell traces Melitian monks in Egypt into the eighth century.

How they're remembered

  • Epiphanius of Salamis tells of the prison during the Great Persecution, where Peter of Alexandria hung a cloak like a curtain and most of the clergy went to Melitius's side.

At Major Councils

  • Council of Nicaea, 325: His split in Egypt was settled; he kept the bare title of bishop

What Is Uncertain

  • Epiphanius of Salamis, a foe of every heresy, tells the prison story some seventy years later. Archibald Robertson calls it the Melitians' own account, and H. Idris Bell thinks Epiphanius had excellent informants, probably Egyptian bishops exiled to Palestine.
  • Athanasius of Alexandria wrote that Peter deposed Melitius for many crimes, among them offering sacrifice to idols. Socrates of Constantinople also says he denied the faith and sacrificed. Bell doubts the charge, since it came readily to quarrelling churchmen after a persecution. Bell and Robertson both hold that the schism did not begin over doctrine; Sozomen says the Melitians later took up Arian teaching.
  • The two main accounts differ. The contemporary letters of the four bishops and of Peter describe the quarrel over ordinations. Epiphanius tells the prison scene and the order of events differently. Bell holds that both can be reconciled as stages of one schism.
  • His birth and the year of his death are not given in a held source. He died after the Council of 325, since near his end he named John as his successor, against the council's decree.
