Lucifer of Cagliari

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Luciferians.

Known for
The bishop of Cagliari who defied the emperor over Athanasius, and whose refusal to forgive former Arians began the Luciferian schism.
Also called
Luciferus; Lucifer Calaritanus (the Latin forms; Calaris is Cagliari)
Dates
Bishop by 353; died about 370 or 371
Place
Cagliari, in Sardinia
Role
Bishop, polemicist and leader of a schism

About This Person

In exile in Palestine, Lucifer of Cagliari wrote a book against the emperor Constantius and sent it to him to read. Jerome of Strido, in his Lives of Illustrious Men, says he wrote it "with wonderful constancy and willingness to meet martyrdom." Lucifer was bishop of Cagliari in Sardinia. Liberius of Rome had sent him to the emperor. With him went the Roman priest Pancratius and the deacon Hilary.

The emperor wanted the Western bishops to condemn Athanasius of Alexandria. Lucifer refused at a council in Milan. W. H. Fremantle, who translated Jerome, says he opposed the emperor with much violence. He was exiled from 355 to 361. He spent most of it at Eleutheropolis in Palestine, and the rest in Egypt. His pamphlets from exile survive in Latin, with titles such as Of Apostate Kings.

When Constantius died in 361, the emperor Julian let the exiles go home. Theodoret of Cyrrhus says the church at Antioch was split in two, though both sides held the same creed. Lucifer skipped the council at Alexandria and went straight to Antioch. There he made Paulinus, a presbyter who led one party, its bishop. Eusebius of Vercelli, who had shared his exile, disapproved. Socrates of Constantinople says Lucifer took this as an insult and broke with him.

Lucifer went home to Sardinia. Fremantle says he refused church office to anyone who had given way to the Arians. Socrates writes that "a distinct sect arose under the name of 'Luciferians.'" It had died out by early in the next century, he adds. Jerome's Chronicle, Fremantle notes, gives his death in 371.

Jerome had been ordained by Paulinus, the bishop Lucifer made. About 379 he wrote a Dialogue against the Luciferians. In it he calls Lucifer "that saintly man," yet blames him for the split. "He himself was a good shepherd," Jerome writes, "but he was leaving a vast spoil to the beasts of prey."

Key Works

  • Pamphlets against Constantius (On Apostate Kings; On not holding communion with heretics; and others): the hard Nicene attacks on the emperor's church policy, written in exile; the Latin texts survive

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The Luciferian party, which refused church office to anyone who had given way to the Arians. Fremantle says it died out by early in the fifth century, and Theodoret of Cyrrhus says it went out and was forgotten.

How they're remembered

  • Jerome, who was ordained by Paulinus, the bishop Lucifer made at Antioch, wrote a Dialogue against the Luciferians about 379. He calls Lucifer "that saintly man" and a good shepherd, yet blames him for the split.
  • Theodoret of Cyrrhus says Lucifer's consecration of Paulinus prolonged the split at Antioch for eighty-five years.

At Major Councils

  • Councils of Ariminum and Seleucia, 359: Not present; refused communion with those who received the signers

What Is Uncertain

  • How far Lucifer himself was a schismatic is unclear. J. Ll. Davies, in the Dictionary of Christian Biography quoted by Blomfield Jackson, writes that "To what extent he was an actual schismatic remains obscure." Socrates of Constantinople says he kept to the church's teaching on going home, while his followers stayed apart. Ambrose of Milan and Augustine of Hippo, Fremantle notes, speak of him as fallen into schism. Jerome blames the split on him but calls him saintly.
  • Fremantle dates the council at Milan to 354. Jerome's Chronicle, which Fremantle and Jackson report, gives his death in 371; Richardson, in a note to Jerome's Lives, gives 370.
  • Reports that he acted from love of glory, or from a grudge against Eusebius of Vercelli, Jerome says he does not believe.
