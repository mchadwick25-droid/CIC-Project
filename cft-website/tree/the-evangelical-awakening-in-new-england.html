New England, today in United States

The Evangelical Awakening in New England

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

It began in the winter of 1734-35 in Northampton, Massachusetts. There the town's minister, Jonathan Edwards, watched an ordinary church in the Connecticut Valley come apart and remake itself in a wave of conversions. Then he did something unusual: he wrote down what had happened, case by case. His cases included a young woman the town had thought beyond hope and a four-year-old girl. The English preacher George Whitefield toured the colonies from 1739, and scattered local revivals became a general one. Preachers crossed parish lines, and crowds met in the open air. The awakening split New England's churches. The New Lights defended it. The Old Lights, led by Charles Chauncy of Boston, attacked it as 'enthusiasm', false claims to the Spirit, and disorder. Hundreds of Separate congregations broke away. Many of them may later have become Baptist, but how many is unclear. Black and Native Christians of its world also began to write. Among them were the poet Phillis Wheatley and the preacher Samson Occom.

In Its Own Record

Influenced By

  • Puritanism Documented — John Winthrop's journal says that Thomas Shepard came to Massachusetts in October 1635, with other ministers. In 1746 Jonathan Edwards quoted from Shepard's book on the ten virgins. He did so in his study of true and false faith.
  • The Methodist Revival Documented — In 1743 Charles Chauncy of Boston, a critic of the revival, wrote that preaching from town to town began, in those parts, with George Whitefield. Whitefield's own journal tells how he preached at Northampton. Jonathan Edwards wept the whole time.
  • English Old Dissent — Presbyterians and Congregationalists Documented — Early in 1742, at the height of the revival in Northampton, Sarah Edwards heard a friend read a hymn by Isaac Watts. She wrote that it moved her so strongly she leaped from her chair. Words from another of his hymns had moved her too.

Influenced

  • The Baptists Documented — In 1792 the Baptist William Carey pleaded for a mission to the heathen. He praised David Brainerd's work among the Indians from about 1743. He named Brainerd, with Paul and Elliot of New England, as a model for missionaries.
  • English Old Dissent — Presbyterians and Congregationalists Documented — Two London ministers, Isaac Watts and John Guyse, read Jonathan Edwards's first short report of the Northampton revival. They told its story to large crowds. Then they printed his full account in London in 1737, with their own preface.

Sources

  • Edwards's corpus: A Faithful Narrative (1737, with the Hutchinson and Bartlet case histories), The Distinguishing Marks (1741), Religious Affections (1746), Life of Brainerd (1749) - Works of Jonathan Edwards (Yale; WJE Online) (primary) — Life of Brainerd belongs to the missionary-biography genre, which shaped how Edwards told the story.
  • The conversion-narrative genre: Nathan Cole's Spiritual Travels; Sarah Osborn's diaries and memoir (ed. Brekus, Sarah Osborn's Collected Writings, Yale, 2017); Sarah Edwards's 1742 narrative; The Christian History (1743-45, ed. Prince) (primary) — The revival's serialized self-documentation.
  • Black and indigenous voices: Phillis Wheatley (Whitefield elegy 1770; Poems 1773), Jupiter Hammon, Lemuel Haynes (sermons; Liberty Further Extended ms. c. 1776), Samson Occom (Short Narrative of My Life, 1768; hymns); Richard Allen's ministry begins a generation later (primary) — These voices mostly date from the 1760s-70s, a generation after the main revival years, but are carried here as the awakening's direct fruit. Occom's own account describes discrimination within the mission system in the first person.
  • The contested revival: Chauncy, Seasonable Thoughts (1743); Davenport's Confession and Retractions (1744); Separate congregations' records and Isaac Backus's early diary (primary) — Chauncy writes from inside the opposition — a hostile source, though his dossier is itself full of revival data. Backus's diary marks the point where the Separate movement became Baptist — the same transition covered under the Baptist history.
  • Thomas Kidd, The Great Awakening (Yale, 2007), with George Marsden, Jonathan Edwards: A Life (Yale, 2003) (secondary)
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 7, 'Evangelical awakenings in the North Atlantic world' (Ward), with 'British and French North America to 1765' (Noll) (reference) — Noll's chapter frames the real question of whether this is one tradition or two.
