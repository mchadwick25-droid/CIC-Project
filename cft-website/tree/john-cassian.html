The Imperial Church Era · c. 360-432 · Marseilles

John Cassian

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Gallic Monastic-Ascetic Christianity.

Known for
Monk of Marseilles who carried the teaching of the Egyptian desert monks into Latin in his Institutes and Conferences, and whose thirteenth Conference Prosper of Aquitaine wrote against.
Also called
Cassian, Cassianus, Johannes Cassianus
Dates
Born about 360 and in Egypt between about 380 and 400, both by Edgar Gibson's reckoning; died at Marseilles in the reign of Theodosius and Valentinian, by Gennadius of Marseilles, which Gibson places not long after 432
Place
Bethlehem, the desert of Scete in Egypt, Constantinople and Marseilles
Role
Monk, presbyter and founder of two monasteries at Marseilles

About This Person

In the Egyptian desert of Scete, an old monk named Sarapion threw himself on the ground and wept. A letter from Theophilus, bishop of Alexandria, had taught that God has no human shape. A learned deacon had just won Sarapion over to it. But at prayer, the picture of God he had always held was gone. He cried out that "they have taken away my God from me." John Cassian was among the monks there and told the story in his Conferences.

Gennadius of Marseilles, who wrote late in the fifth century, calls Cassian "Scythian by race." Edgar Gibson, who translated his works, doubts it. Gibson thinks he was probably a Westerner, perhaps from Provence, and grants that no one can be sure. From Cassian's writings, Gibson traces how he and his friend Germanus joined a monastery at Bethlehem as young men. Then they spent years among the monks of Egypt, between about 380 and 400.

At Constantinople, he wrote later, "I was admitted into the sacred ministry by the Bishop John, of blessed memory." Gibson takes this John for John Chrysostom. Gennadius says Cassian later became a presbyter at Marseilles and founded two monasteries, one for men and one for women. For Castor, a bishop in Gaul, he wrote the Institutes, twelve books on the monks' way of life. He would skip the wonders, he said, and write "about the way to improve our character." The Conferences followed, talks of the desert fathers.

The thirteenth Conference started a quarrel. Its speaker is Chaeremon, an old hermit near Panephysis in the Nile delta. He says that grace and free will "seem opposed to each other, but really are in harmony." When God sees us "inclined to will what is good," he meets and strengthens us. Prosper of Aquitaine, a layman who followed Augustine of Hippo, wrote against it. Gibson calls him "an ardent Augustinian." Gennadius, who sides with Cassian, believed an unsigned attack on works "which the church of God finds salutary" was Prosper's. Gibson calls Cassian's teaching Semi-Pelagian and judges it wrong. Still, he adds, "the teaching of Augustine was one-sided and required to be balanced."

At the request of Leo, then archdeacon of Rome, Cassian wrote seven books against Nestorius, bishop of Constantinople. With them, Gennadius says, he "made an end, both of writing and living, at Marseilles." Gibson notes that Benedict of Nursia had the Conferences read daily to his monks.

Key Works

  • The Institutes: twelve books for Castor, a bishop in Gaul, on the dress, prayers and rules of the monks of Egypt and Palestine, and the remedies for eight principal faults
  • The Conferences: twenty-four talks with the fathers of the Egyptian desert, in three parts; the thirteenth, on the protection of God, is the one Prosper of Aquitaine attacked
  • On the Incarnation of the Lord, against Nestorius: seven books written at the request of Leo, then archdeacon of Rome

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The Rule of Benedict of Nursia has the "Conferences" read aloud to the monks after supper, and later names the Conferences of the Fathers and their Institutes among a monk's tools. Edgar Gibson takes these for Cassian's books.
  • Gennadius of Marseilles says Eucherius of Lyons, "revising and condensing certain works of Saint Cassianus, he compressed them into one volume."
  • Gibson calls him "the first organizer and systematizer, of Western monachism", though not its founder.

How they're remembered

  • Gennadius of Marseilles says "He wrote from experience, and in forcible language".
  • Gibson says he was "Never formally canonized" and is known in the West as "the blessed Cassian", while in the East he is regarded as a saint.

What Is Uncertain

  • His early life is known mainly from his own writings. Gennadius of Marseilles calls him "Scythian by race." Edgar Gibson, who translated his works, doubts it. The word may point to the desert of Scete, he suggests, and he thinks a Western birth, perhaps in Provence, most likely, while granting "it is impossible to speak with certainty". Gibson adds that Photius of Constantinople calls him a Roman, which "need mean no more than born within the Roman Empire".
  • Gibson says the Conferences were written many years after the visits, and that no one can tell "how far they really represent the discourses actually spoken by the Egyptian" fathers, or how far they are Cassian's own.
  • Prosper of Aquitaine, a layman and, by Gibson's account, "an ardent Augustinian", wrote against the thirteenth Conference. Prosper's book is not among the sources used here. Gennadius, who takes Cassian's side, believed an anonymous book "against certain works of Cassianus, which the church of God finds salutary, but which he brands as injurious" was Prosper's. Gibson says Prosper took twelve propositions from that Conference and allowed only the first, and "never once names him directly".
  • Gibson, and William Bright whom he quotes, call this teaching Semi-Pelagian. Cassian does not use the word. Gibson judges that "a verdict of guilty must be returned on the charge of Semi-Pelagianism", and adds that "the teaching of Augustine was one-sided and required to be balanced."
  • Gibson notes that the monks of Egypt called him John in his own writings, and that Prosper joins the two names. Gennadius calls him only Cassianus.
  • Gennadius gives no year for his death. Ernest Cushing Richardson's note gives 450. Gibson says his last known act is Prosper's attack, after 432, and that he probably died soon after.
  • Gibson says "Of his family we know nothing", except a sister he mentions once.
