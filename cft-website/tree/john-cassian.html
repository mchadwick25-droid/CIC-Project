The Imperial Church Era · c. 360-432 · Marseilles

John Cassian

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Gallic Monastic-Ascetic Christianity.

Also called Cassian, Cassianus, Johannes Cassianus

Monk of Marseilles who carried the teaching of the Egyptian desert monks into Latin in his Institutes and Conferences, and whose thirteenth Conference Prosper of Aquitaine wrote against.

About This Person

Two monks of southern Gaul wanted more of the Egyptian desert. One wanted his monks taught its rules; the other longed to sail there, leaving a land frozen, as John Cassian put it, with the cold of Gaul. Cassian, who had lived among those monks, wrote seven Conferences for the two, so that one need not make so risky a journey.

The books made the journey instead. At Marseilles, Cassian carried Egypt's teaching into Latin for the monks of the West. His Conferences were talks with the old monks of the desert. A century later, Benedict's Rule named the Conferences first for the monks' evening reading. But his thirteenth Conference, on grace, God's free help, and free will, our own power to choose, drew an attack from Prosper of Aquitaine.

Most of his early life comes from his own books. As a young man he joined a monastery at Bethlehem with his friend Germanus. The two sailed to Egypt, promising a quick return, but stayed seven years, went back to keep their word, then returned to the desert of Scete.

At Constantinople, Bishop John Chrysostom made him a deacon, in the word of Gennadius, a later priest of Marseilles. When John was sent into exile, Germanus and Cassian carried his clergy's letters to the bishop of Rome. They wrote that bishops had plotted and soldiers helped drive their bishop out.

At Marseilles, Cassian became a priest and founded two monasteries, one for men and one for women. For a bishop whose province had none, he wrote the Institutes, on the dress, prayers and psalms of the Egyptian houses. Eight of its books give the eight chief faults, from greed for food to pride, and their remedies.

In the 420s came twenty-four Conferences. In the thirteenth, as Cassian writes him, an old hermit says God gives the first good thought. Yet when God sees a good will stirring, he strengthens it, whether he planted it or it grew from our effort, as with Zacchaeus and the thief on the cross. "So these two, the grace of God and free will, seem opposed to each other. But really they are in harmony."

In 429 Prosper told Augustine that many at Marseilles thought Augustine's teaching on God's choosing ran against the Fathers. If God chose before time who would be saved, they said, effort was in vain. The letter does not name Cassian.

Leo, later bishop of Rome, asked him to answer Nestorius, the new bishop of Constantinople, who would not call Mary the Mother of God. In seven books, the year before a council at Ephesus removed Nestorius, Cassian charged him with saying Christ was born a mere man. He tied that error to Pelagius's followers, who, he wrote, said people could live without sin if they liked.

Prosper then wrote a book against "the Conferencer", as its title calls him, defending Augustine. By the translator's account, it takes twelve sentences from the thirteenth Conference and accepts only the first, that God begins every good thought. It rejects the rest, among them that a good will may sometimes begin in us, and never names Cassian directly. He died at Marseilles, but no year is known.

Benedict's Rule names no author for its Conferences, but the title is Cassian's. Those monks did not come from his houses; they read his books.

Gennadius, on Cassian's side, took the unnamed attack to be Prosper's, and said some of their views on grace and free will were at odds. Of Cassian's works and Prosper's attack he wrote, "The church of God finds them wholesome, but he brands them as harmful."

Cassiodorus, a century and more later, told his monks to read Cassian with care and hear him gladly on the eight faults. But on free will, he warned, Prosper had rightly blamed him.

Cassian had named his own hope when the first Conferences went out. "May we set the men themselves before you, embodied, as it were, in their own rules. And, what matters more, may they be speaking in Latin."

Timeline

Dates
Born about 360 and in Egypt between about 380 and 400, both by Edgar Gibson's reckoning; died at Marseilles in the reign of Theodosius and Valentinian, by Gennadius of Marseilles, which Gibson places not long after 432
Place
Bethlehem, the desert of Scete in Egypt, Constantinople and Marseilles
Role
Monk, presbyter and founder of two monasteries at Marseilles

Key Works

  • The Institutes: twelve books for Castor, a bishop in Gaul, on the dress, prayers and rules of the monks of Egypt and Palestine, and the remedies for eight principal faults; in its preface Cassian says he will leave out the wonders and tell only the rules of the monasteries and the remedies for the eight faults, and that he will temper Egypt's rules by the customs of Palestine and Mesopotamia where harsh climate or different ways made them hard
  • The Conferences: twenty-four talks with the fathers of the Egyptian desert, in three parts; the thirteenth, on the protection of God, is the one Prosper of Aquitaine attacked; Gibson sets out the dedications, the first ten to the bishop Leontius and the brother Helladius, the next seven to Honoratus and Eucherius, and the last seven to Jovinian, Minervius, Leontius and Theodore
  • On the Incarnation of the Lord, against Nestorius: seven books written at the request of Leo, then archdeacon of Rome

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • The Rule of Benedict of Nursia has the "Conferences" read aloud to the monks after supper, and later names the Conferences of the Fathers and their Institutes among a monk's tools. Edgar Gibson takes these for Cassian's books.
  • Gennadius of Marseilles says Eucherius of Lyons, "revising and condensing certain works of Saint Cassianus, he compressed them into one volume."
  • Gibson calls him "the first organizer and systematizer, of Western monachism", though not its founder.
  • Gregory of Tours, writing in the late sixth century, tells of a monastery in Gaul, founded by the abbot Aredius of Limoges, where the Rule of Cassian was kept beside the Rules of Basil and of other abbots.
  • Gibson, citing Cassiodorus, says Victor, bishop of Martyrites, made a recension of Cassian's works for use in Africa, taking out some things and adding others in the interests of orthodoxy. It is no longer extant.
  • The Gallic Chronicle of 452 notes at the year 429 that Cassian sets out, in books given to many, the lives, teachings and rules of the fathers he came to know in Egypt. The held print is damaged in places, but the sense is clear.
  • The Philokalia, in its 1893 Greek print, gives his work to Bishop Castor on the eight thoughts of evil under the name of Cassian the Roman.
  • The preface to the Philokalia's pieces by Cassian says they are two, on the eight thoughts and on discernment, and says the most learned Photius mentions them. It says the Church remembers him on the twenty-ninth of February with honors and praise.

How they're remembered

  • Gennadius of Marseilles says "He wrote from experience, and in forcible language".
  • Gibson says he was "Never formally canonized" and is known in the West as "the blessed Cassian", while in the East he is regarded as a saint.

Open Questions

  • His early life is known mainly from his own writings. Gennadius of Marseilles calls him "Scythian by race." Edgar Gibson, who translated his works, doubts it. The word may point to the desert of Scete, he suggests, and he thinks a Western birth, perhaps in Provence, most likely, while granting "it is impossible to speak with certainty". Gibson adds that Photius of Constantinople calls him a Roman, which "need mean no more than born within the Roman Empire".
  • Gibson says the Conferences were written many years after the visits, and that no one can tell "how far they really represent the discourses actually spoken by the Egyptian" fathers, or how far they are Cassian's own.
  • Prosper of Aquitaine, a layman and, by Gibson's account, "an ardent Augustinian", wrote against the thirteenth Conference. Prosper's book is not among the sources used here. Gennadius, who takes Cassian's side, believed an anonymous book "against certain works of Cassianus, which the church of God finds salutary, but which he brands as injurious" was Prosper's. Gibson says Prosper took twelve propositions from that Conference and allowed only the first, and "never once names him directly".
  • Gibson, and William Bright whom he quotes, call this teaching Semi-Pelagian. Cassian does not use the word. Gibson judges that "a verdict of guilty must be returned on the charge of Semi-Pelagianism", and adds that "the teaching of Augustine was one-sided and required to be balanced."
  • Gibson notes that the monks of Egypt called him John in his own writings, and that Prosper joins the two names. But Theodor Mommsen prints the line Gibson means, which calls him John surnamed Cassian, among fifteenth-century additions to Prosper's Chronicle, not Prosper's own words. Gennadius calls him only Cassianus.
  • Gennadius gives no year for his death. Ernest Cushing Richardson's note gives 450. Gibson says his last known act is Prosper's attack, after 432, and that he probably died soon after.
  • Gibson says "Of his family we know nothing", except a sister he mentions once.
  • A tract on the Irish cursus, which Frederick Warren says was written by an eighth-century monk of the Irish tradition on the Continent, names Cassian and says a blessed Honorius was his companion at the monastery of Lérins. No earlier held source puts Cassian at Lérins, and Warren grants that the text is corrupt and has errors of detail.
  • Socrates, a historian of the same century, says the people charged Nestorius with teaching that the Lord is a mere man. Having read his writings, Socrates says he cannot grant that Nestorius denied the divinity of Christ; he seemed scared at the word for Mother of God. Cassian's charge in the seven books is that Nestorius declared our Lord born a mere man.
