About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 0:58

Read along

Ethiopian Christianity nearly ended in this period and then reasserted itself twice over. From 1529 the armies of Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi — remembered as Gragn, the left-handed — swept through the highlands, burning churches and monasteries and destroying manuscripts on a scale that still limits what can be known about earlier Ethiopian history. The invasion was broken only at the battle of Wayna Daga in 1543, with Portuguese help. That help brought Jesuits, and after decades of argument Emperor Susenyos converted to Catholicism and tried to impose it, provoking a revolt so fierce that he abdicated in 1632. His son Fasilides expelled the Jesuits, restored the ancient church, and built a new capital at Gondar.

Documented Stories

‘We Restore to You the Faith of Your Forefathers’

Emperor Susenyos had accepted the Roman faith in 1622 and, under the Jesuit patriarch Afonso Mendes from 1626, had tried to impose it on the country: the Sabbath abolished, the calendar changed, the clergy re-ordained, the people re-baptized, and the church's fasts and feasts realigned. The revolts came one after another, in Lasta, in Gojjam, among the monks, and were put down with slaughter. In June 1632 Susenyos defeated the rebels of Lasta in a battle in which, the chronicle says, eight thousand peasants died. His son Fasiladas, riding over the field with him afterwards, is recorded as saying that the dead were not pagans or Muslims but his own people, Christians, and that this was not victory but the slaughter of the kingdom. On 24 June 1632 Susenyos had a proclamation read: ‘Hear ye, hear ye. We first gave you this faith believing that it was good. But innumerable people have been slain — Julius, Gabriel, Takla Giyorgis, Sarsa Krestos, and now these peasants. For which reason we restore to you the faith of your forefathers. Let the former clergy return to their churches, let them put in their altars, let them say their own liturgy. And do ye rejoice.’ The chronicle says the people rejoiced through the night. Susenyos died three months later, and Fasiladas, who succeeded him, expelled Mendes and the Jesuits in 1633 and closed the country to Europeans.

The Emperor’s Confession of Faith

The Jesuits' first envoy to Ethiopia, Gonçalo Rodrigues, arrived at the court of Emperor Galawdewos in 1555 with a treatise attacking the errors of the Ethiopians, and the emperor answered him in writing. The Confession of Galawdewos, short enough to be read in a quarter of an hour, states the Ethiopian faith in the Trinity and the Incarnation in language the Latins could not fault, and then turns to the customs the Portuguese had been calling Jewish. The Ethiopians keep the Sabbath, the emperor writes, not as the Jews do, who will not draw water or light a fire on it, but as the apostles commanded, in memory of the creation, as they keep the Lord's day in memory of the resurrection. They circumcise as the Ethiopians always have, as a custom of the land like the marking of the face among some nations, not as the Jews for salvation. They abstain from pork not as Jews but as men who dislike it, as Christians elsewhere refuse horse-meat. He closed by naming the Latin missionaries' own churches as his witnesses that the Ethiopian faith was the faith of the apostles and the councils. Galawdewos, who had spent his whole reign fighting the invasions from Adal and had received the Portuguese as allies against them, was killed in battle against Nur ibn Mujahid on 23 March 1559, and his head was carried to Harar. His Confession remained the Ethiopian Church's standard answer to Rome for the next century.

Ahmad Gragn's jihad lays waste to the church from 1529 to 1543, until the battle of Wayna Daga saves it. Then the Jesuits come. Emperor Susenyos turns Catholic in 1622, his people revolt, and the old faith comes back in 1632. His son Fasilides drives out the Jesuits in 1634. It is the era turned upside down: here the Counter-Reformation is the outside force pressing on an old church.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

From 1529 the armies of Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi, remembered as Gragn, burned Ethiopian churches, monasteries and manuscripts on a scale that still limits what can be known of earlier Ethiopian history, until the invasion broke at the Battle of Wayna Daga in 1543. The Portuguese help that won that battle brought Jesuits who later converted Emperor Susenyos to Catholicism — provoking a revolt so fierce that his son Fasilides expelled the Jesuits, restored the ancient church, and built a new capital at Gondar.

Legacy

The Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church survives as one of the largest of the Oriental Orthodox churches, with tens of millions of members, still keeping its own calendar, its Ge'ez liturgy, its long fasts and the tabot at the center of every church. Gondar's castles and painted churches, built in the century after the expulsion, are a UNESCO World Heritage site. And the memory of this period — an invasion survived, a foreign form of the faith refused — remains part of how the church understands itself.
