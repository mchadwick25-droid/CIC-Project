The High Medieval Era · c. 1143-1321 · Languedoc

Languedoc, today in France

Cathars / Albigensians

Languedoc, c. 1143 to 1321 — a dissenting church with its own ministers and its own initiation, destroyed by crusade and inquisition, and known almost entirely through the records of the men who destroyed it.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:14

Read along

From the 1140s, church writers in the Rhineland and then in southern France describe communities with their own ministers and their own rite of entry. They taught that the visible world of matter was not the work of the good God. This dualism placed them outside Christianity as the councils had defined it. In Languedoc they had noble protection and something close to a parallel church. In 1209 the papacy launched a crusade against them. Two decades of war followed, then the inquisition, then the siege of Montségur in 1244. There about two hundred of their ministers walked into a fire rather than recant. Their ministers were called the perfect. The last known perfect of Languedoc, Guillaume Bélibaste, was burned in 1321. Almost everything known about them comes from the people who destroyed them. Historians now argue hard about how much of 'Catharism' was one movement at all. Some of it may have been a label that inquisitors built out of scattered local dissent.

People and Stories

Voices in the Record

  • Guilhabert de Castres — the most prominent Cathar bishop of Languedoc in the movement's strongest years
  • Bertrand Marty — the bishop who led some two hundred of his people into the fire at Montségur in 1244
  • Rainerius Sacconi — a former member of seventeen years who became a Dominican inquisitor and wrote the fullest surviving description of their beliefs: uniquely informed and thoroughly hostile at once
  • Guillaume Bélibaste — the last known Languedoc perfect, burned in 1321

Documented Stories

Béziers: ‘Kill Them All’

The crusade Innocent III had called against the heretics of Languedoc reached Béziers in July 1209 and demanded that the townspeople hand over some two hundred named heretics. On 22 July a sortie by the townsmen let the camp followers of the army — the ribauds — into the gates, and the knights followed; the city fell within hours. The legate Arnaud Amaury, abbot of Cîteaux, wrote to the pope within weeks: ‘Our men spared no one, irrespective of rank, sex or age, and put to the sword almost twenty thousand people.’ The Catholics of Béziers died with the rest, including those who had taken refuge in the church of the Madeleine; the legate’s letter calls it ‘divine vengeance’. The famous order — Caedite eos; novit enim Dominus qui sunt eius, ‘Kill them all; the Lord knows his own’ — is reported only by Caesarius of Heisterbach, a Cistercian writing in Germany some years later, who says the crusaders asked the legate how to tell Catholics from heretics and received that answer. The Song of the Cathar Wars, a vernacular poem by a Catholic sympathetic to the crusade, says the killers were the ribauds and that the knights arrived too late to take the plunder for themselves. What was destroyed in Béziers was a city of Catholics and dissenters who had refused to sort themselves for an army, and the church’s own legate recorded the result as a triumph.

Pierre Maury Explains Himself to the Bishop

Jacques Fournier, bishop of Pamiers and later Pope Benedict XII, ran an inquisition in the county of Foix from 1318 to 1325 and kept the fullest register any inquisitor ever compiled; it is the reason the village of Montaillou is the best-known peasant community of the Middle Ages. Among the depositions is that of Pierre Maury, a shepherd of Montaillou who had spent his working life moving flocks between the Pyrenees and Catalonia and had, since about 1300, fed, guided and hidden the surviving ‘good men’ of the region — Pierre Authié, Guillaume Bélibaste and their few companions — as they moved from safe house to safe house. Arrested in 1323 after Bélibaste had been betrayed and burned, Maury talked for days. He described the good men’s fasts and their blessing of bread, the consolamentum given to the dying, the arguments in shepherds’ huts about whether God had made the world, and his own conviction that these men, poor and hunted, were closer to the apostles than the priests who lived off tithes. He also described Bélibaste’s failings — his mistress, his lies, his fear — without softening them, and said he had stayed loyal anyway. The register records the interrogator’s questions and the shepherd’s answers in the third person, in Latin, translated from Occitan by clerks; it is a hostile document, and it is also the only place a man like Pierre Maury was ever allowed to explain himself for as long as he liked.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced Balkan Bogomilism, 1054-1300 Contested — Rainerius Sacconi was an Italian inquisitor. About 1250 he wrote that all the Cathar churches came from two in the east. He named them as Bulgaria and Dugunthia. The Cathars of Concorezzo, in Italy, had a secret book. A note in one copy says their bishop Nazarius brought it from Bulgaria. Scholars still argue over whether such hostile reports show a real link.

Opposed By

  • argued against The Dominican Order Documented — Bernard Gui signed himself in 1309 as a friar of the Order of Preachers and inquisitor of heresy in France. His handbook for inquisitors sets out the errors of 'the Manichaeans of modern times'. That was his name for the Cathars.
  • argued against The Latin Papal Church (Gregorian Reform to Innocent III) Documented Major — In 1179 a great church council at Rome condemned the heretics of Gascony and the lands around Albi and Toulouse, 'whom some call Cathars'. A later papal decree puts the Cathars first among the heretics it condemns. In 1209 the papacy went further and sent crusading armies against them.Lasting result: In 1179 the Third Lateran Council cut off the Cathars and all who defended or sheltered them. It barred anyone from housing them.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

From the 1140s, communities in Languedoc with their own ministers and their own initiation rite taught that the visible material world was not the good God's making, a dualism that placed them outside the councils' Christianity. In 1209 the papacy launched a crusade against them, and at the siege of Montségur in 1244 roughly two hundred of their ministers walked into a fire rather than recant; the last known Languedoc perfect, Guillaume Bélibaste, was burned in 1321. Almost everything known about them comes from the people who destroyed them, and historians still argue over how much of 'Catharism' was ever a coherent movement at all.

Legacy

Nothing of the movement itself survived; it was extinguished, and no continuing community descends from it. What it left behind belongs mostly to the people who suppressed it. The Albigensian Crusade attached Languedoc to the French crown, and the papal inquisition was built in the 1230s largely to deal with this problem and then became a permanent institution that long outlived its original target. In the modern Languedoc the Cathars have been rediscovered as regional heritage, and ruined hilltop castles are marketed as 'Cathar country', though the ruins standing today are mostly later rebuildings and the popular picture of the movement owes as much to romantic reconstruction as to the sources.

Experience It Today

  • Ariège, southern France — the ruined Château de Montségur, above the field where roughly two hundred Cathars were burned in March 1244 (the standing walls are a later rebuilding)

Sources

Doctrinal Floor Note

By every account we have, they taught that the visible material world was not the good God's making, and that Christ only appeared to have a body — both outside the common Christian creed. Their own voice has not survived to test it against.

How Sure the Record Is

Inquisition records plus the movement's own surviving ritual

  • The Book of the Two Principles (Liber de duobus principiis, c. 1240s-60s), trans. in Wakefield & Evans, Heresies of the High Middle Ages (1969), p. 511 (primary) — Radical-dualist theology from the circle of John of Lugio (Italy). Non-Catholic authorship is broadly accepted, but whether it speaks for 'the Cathars' (esp. Languedoc) is exactly what Pegg and Moore deny.
  • The Cathar Rituals (Occitan Lyon ritual; Latin Florence ritual), trans. in Wakefield & Evans (1969), p. 465 (primary) — The consolamentum liturgy. Scholars still debate whether this really reflects a coherent Catharism, or a small literate circle later projected back onto Languedoc.
  • Biller, Bruschi & Sneddon (ed./trans.), Inquisitors and Heretics in Thirteenth-Century Languedoc (Brill, 2011) - Toulouse depositions 1273-82 (primary) — A hostile source, but the only place ordinary believers' voices survive — filtered through the interrogators' own categories; the words 'Cathar' and 'perfect' are largely the inquisitors', not the deponents'.
  • Rainerius Sacconi, Summa de Catharis (1250), in Wakefield & Evans (primary) — A hostile source from a former insider: a 'heretic' of seventeen years who turned Dominican inquisitor — uniquely informed, and uniquely motivated to condemn what he'd left.
  • Antonio Sennis (ed.), Cathars in Question (York Medieval Press, 2016) (secondary) — The coherence debate itself: Pegg ('The Paradigm of Catharism; or, the Historians' Illusion'), Moore, Biller, Hamilton, Taylor, Stoyanov face to face - the honest state of the question.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 4, ch. 12 'Christians and heretics' (Biller) (reference) — Biller is a leading voice on one side of the live debate, not a neutral summary of it; read alongside Sennis.
