The High Medieval Era · c. 1143-1321 · Languedoc

Languedoc, today in France

Cathars / Albigensians

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

From the 1140s, church writers in the Rhineland and then in southern France describe communities with their own ministers and their own rite of entry. They taught that the visible world of matter was not the work of the good God. This dualism placed them outside Christianity as the councils had defined it. In Languedoc they had noble protection and something close to a parallel church. In 1209 the papacy launched a crusade against them. Two decades of war followed, then the inquisition, then the siege of Montségur in 1244. There about two hundred of their ministers walked into a fire rather than recant. Their ministers were called the perfect. The last known perfect of Languedoc, Guillaume Bélibaste, was burned in 1321. Almost everything known about them comes from the people who destroyed them. Historians now argue hard about how much of 'Catharism' was one movement at all. Some of it may have been a label that inquisitors built out of scattered local dissent.

In Its Own Record

Influenced By

  • Bogomils Contested — Around 1250 the inquisitor Rainerius Sacconi, once a Cathar himself, listed the Cathar churches. He said all of them came from two churches in the Balkans, Bulgaria and Dugunthia. Historians still argue over how much passed west. Some doubt that the Cathars were one movement at all.
  • Balkan Bogomilism, 1054-1300 Contested — About 1250 Rainier Sacconi, an Italian inquisitor, wrote that all the Cathar churches came from two in the east. He named them as Bulgaria and Dugunthia. The Cathars of Concorezzo, in Italy, had a secret book. A note in one copy says their bishop Nazarius brought it from Bulgaria. Scholars still argue over whether such hostile reports show a real link.

Opposed By

  • The Dominican Order Documented — Bernard Gui signed himself in 1309 as a friar of the Order of Preachers and inquisitor of heresy in France. His handbook for inquisitors sets out the errors of 'the Manichaeans of modern times'. That was his name for the Cathars.
  • The Latin Papal Church (Gregorian Reform to Innocent III) Documented Major — In 1179 a great church council at Rome condemned the heretics of Gascony and the lands around Albi and Toulouse, 'whom some call Cathars'. A later papal decree puts the Cathars first among the heretics it condemns. In 1209 the papacy went further and sent crusading armies against them.Lasting result: In 1179 the Third Lateran Council cut off the Cathars and all who defended or sheltered them. It barred anyone from housing them.

Sources

  • The Book of the Two Principles (Liber de duobus principiis, c. 1240s-60s), trans. in Wakefield & Evans, Heresies of the High Middle Ages (1969), p. 511 (primary) — Radical-dualist theology from the circle of John of Lugio (Italy). Non-Catholic authorship is broadly accepted, but whether it speaks for 'the Cathars' (esp. Languedoc) is exactly what Pegg and Moore deny.
  • The Cathar Rituals (Occitan Lyon ritual; Latin Florence ritual), trans. in Wakefield & Evans (1969), p. 465 (primary) — The consolamentum liturgy. Scholars still debate whether this really reflects a coherent Catharism, or a small literate circle later projected back onto Languedoc.
  • Biller, Bruschi & Sneddon (ed./trans.), Inquisitors and Heretics in Thirteenth-Century Languedoc (Brill, 2011) - Toulouse depositions 1273-82 (primary) — A hostile source, but the only place ordinary believers' voices survive — filtered through the interrogators' own categories; the words 'Cathar' and 'perfect' are largely the inquisitors', not the deponents'.
  • Rainerius Sacconi, Summa de Catharis (1250), in Wakefield & Evans (primary) — A hostile source from a former insider: a 'heretic' of seventeen years who turned Dominican inquisitor — uniquely informed, and uniquely motivated to condemn what he'd left.
  • Antonio Sennis (ed.), Cathars in Question (York Medieval Press, 2016) (secondary) — The coherence debate itself: Pegg ('The Paradigm of Catharism; or, the Historians' Illusion'), Moore, Biller, Hamilton, Taylor, Stoyanov face to face - the honest state of the question.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 4, ch. 12 'Christians and heretics' (Biller) (reference) — Biller is a leading voice on one side of the live debate, not a neutral summary of it; read alongside Sennis.
