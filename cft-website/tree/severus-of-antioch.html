Severus of Antioch

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Syriac Orthodox (West Syriac) Christianity.

Known for
Bishop of Antioch from 512 to his flight in 518, by E. W. Brooks's dates, who condemned the Council of Chalcedon and wrote against Julian of Halicarnassus; his letters survive in Syriac.
Also called
Severus, patriarch of Antioch, Mar Sévère
Dates
Born at Sozopolis in Pisidia. Made bishop of Antioch on 6 November 512 and fled on 29 September 518, by Brooks's dates; Evagrius of Epiphania gives the flight as September of the year 567 of the era of Antioch, which Brooks reads as 519. Died in Egypt on 8 February 538, by Brooks's date. No source used here gives his birth year
Place
Sozopolis in Pisidia; Alexandria and Berytus as a student; Majuma near Gaza as a monk; Constantinople; Antioch as bishop; Egypt in exile
Role
Bishop of Antioch; monk; theologian and letter writer

About This Person

In the first year of his reign, the emperor Justin ordered the arrest of Severus, bishop of Antioch. Evagrius of Epiphania, a later historian on the side of the Council of Chalcedon, says some reported that Severus was to lose his tongue. He fled, and E. W. Brooks, the English translator of his letters, dates the flight to 29 September 518. Evagrius seems to give 519, Brooks notes, but Severus's sermons, kept in date order, decide it.

Severus was born at Sozopolis in Pisidia. His fellow student Zachariah Rhetor wrote a Life of him. It says his widowed mother sent him and his two older brothers to Alexandria to study grammar and rhetoric. Later he studied law at Berytus, now Beirut. Brooks says Zachariah wrote to answer enemies who claimed Severus had followed pagan rites in his youth. In Brooks's view, "the answer is a very lame one."

Zachariah says he was baptized at the shrine of the martyr Leontius at Tripolis. He became a monk between Gaza and Majuma, where Peter the Iberian had lived. Nephalius, who had once shared his view and then taken Chalcedon's side, drove him and other monks out. Severus pleaded their cause at Constantinople and won the notice of the emperor Anastasius. Evagrius tells this from a Life of Severus, which Brooks takes for Zachariah's.

He replaced Flavian as bishop of Antioch in 512, Brooks says. Evagrius says his letters as bishop condemned Chalcedon.

After his flight he lived mostly in hiding in Egypt. There, Brooks says, he wrote against Julian of Halicarnassus, who held that Christ's body could not decay. Evagrius says the empress Theodora, who favored the single nature, persuaded Justinian to send for Severus. Brooks says he and Anthimus of Constantinople were expelled from the city in 536 and condemned at a synod that May. Evagrius says Justinian then cursed him by law. Brooks gives his death in Egypt as 8 February 538.

To the chamberlain Misael he wrote that, when he was raised to the see, he wished "not to know even the very name" of interest. Then he saw the church's debts. "I forgot the spiritual laws," he admits.

Brooks reckons his letters once numbered at least 3,759; a Syriac version made in 669 keeps 123. In the letter to Misael, Severus says God alone is "able to heal our church that is groaning under many wounds, and to change need into abundance."

Key Works

  • Select Letters, Book 6: 123 letters on church business from before, during and after his time as bishop, in the Syriac version Athanasius of Nisibis made in 669; Brooks reckons the whole collection held at least 3,759
  • Works against Julian of Halicarnassus: written in his Egyptian exile, by Brooks's account, against the teaching that Christ's body could not decay; not in the translations used here
  • Philalethes: written at Constantinople, by Brooks's account, against those who found Chalcedon's teaching in Cyril of Alexandria; not in the translations used here

Legacy

What traces to them

  • His letters as bishop condemned the Council of Chalcedon, Evagrius says, and those received at Alexandria came down to Evagrius's own day.
  • A Syriac version of the sixth book of his Select Letters was made in 669 by the priest Athanasius of Nisibis, at the request of the bishops Matthew of Berrhoea and Daniel of Edessa, Brooks says.

How they're remembered

  • Zachariah Rhetor, his fellow student, wrote a Life of him to answer the charge that he had followed pagan rites; a second Life is by John, head of the monastery of Beth Aphthonia.
  • Evagrius of Epiphania, writing for Chalcedon's side, tells his rise and flight and quotes a Palestinian letter against him.

What Is Uncertain

  • His early life rests on the Life by Zachariah Rhetor, written by a friend to defend him. Brooks calls its answer to the charge of pagan practice "a very lame one", and leaves open whether there was any truth in the charge.
  • Evagrius says only that "some say" the emperor Justin's order was to cut out his tongue, and he gives a second report that Vitalian, then high in Justin's favor, demanded it.
  • The year of his flight: Brooks gives 29 September 518 and says Evagrius seems to place it in 519; Brooks holds that the dated series of his sermons, ending with Lent 518, settles the earlier year.
  • Whether his monastery near Gaza was Peter the Iberian's own: Brooks places him in Peter's monastery, and notes that the Syriac chronicle known as Zachariah's calls it the monastery of Theodore, Peter's successor.
  • What happened in his years as bishop rests mostly on his letters and on what his enemies said at the synod of 536, Brooks says.
  • Why he came to Constantinople in the winter of 534 to 535: Brooks says the court's leaning to his side had grown, "probably" under the empress Theodora's influence; Evagrius says she persuaded the emperor Justinian to send for him.
