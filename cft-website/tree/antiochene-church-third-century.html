The Church of Antioch in the Third Century

A council removed the bishop of Antioch for what he taught, and could not make it stick until an emperor decided who owned the building.

Paul of Samosata became bishop of Antioch around 260. The bishops who judged him wrote that he had changed the psalms, the sacred songs, sung in his own church. "He stopped the psalms to our Lord Jesus Christ, as if they were too new, the work of newer men. But on the great day of Easter he trains women to sing psalms to himself, in the middle of the church. Anyone who heard them would shudder."

In the later third century the Christians of Antioch, in Roman Syria, were one church under a bishop, with presbyters, its priests, and deacons, its helpers, bound by letters to churches across the empire. When their own bishop was charged with refusing to confess that the Son of God came down from heaven, the bishops of the East met again and again over him, until at last they put him out.

Almost all we know of him comes from the letter his judges sent out, which Eusebius of Caesarea copied into his history of the church. They wrote that Paul said Jesus Christ was from below, and Eusebius says he held that Christ was by nature an ordinary man.

Bishops hurried to Antioch from every side, Eusebius says, as against a man ravaging Christ's flock, and they met there often while Paul's side hid what he taught. Firmilian, bishop of Caesarea in Cappadocia, came twice and condemned his new teaching, but when Paul promised to change, Firmilian believed him and put the matter off. On his third journey he died at Tarsus, while the bishops waited for him.

The bishops also wrote that Paul built himself a high throne and a curtained private room, like a magistrate. He scolded those who would not cheer and wave handkerchiefs as in the theater, though they listened with reverence, as in the house of God. A bishop, they held, must be an example to the people.

At last Malchion, a presbyter who ran the city's school of Greek learning, questioned Paul while shorthand writers took the debate down. A final meeting of many bishops cut Paul off from the whole church, because, they wrote, he set himself against God and refused to obey. No church was to send him letters of communion, the letters bishops exchanged to show they shared one church.

Paul refused to give up the church's house, so the case went to the emperor Aurelian. He gave the house to whichever side the bishops of Italy and Rome wrote to. Paul's judges had asked them to write to a new bishop, not Paul, so he was driven out of the church by the worldly power.

In the persecution under the emperor Maximinus, Lucian, a presbyter of the same church, defended his faith before the emperor at Nicomedia and was imprisoned and killed. Two generations later, John Chrysostom told in a sermon how he answered under torture. "To each question he answered only, I am a Christian. When the torturer asked, What is your homeland? I am a Christian, he said. What is your trade? I am a Christian. Who are your parents? To all of it he said, I am a Christian."

Later, in Alexandria, a presbyter named Arius said that the Son of God had a beginning, and God had none. His bishop, Arius wrote, preached that the Son always was, and drove him out of the city because he would not agree. That bishop set Lucian in a line from Paul to Arius: Lucian, he wrote, kept out of communion with the bishops for many years, and Arius and his party were shoots of the same root.

A church historian of the next century, on Arius's side, says that some of Lucian's pupils became bishops. One was Eusebius, bishop of Nicomedia, not Eusebius of Caesarea. Arius had written to him about being driven out, and ended that letter with the name Lucian's pupils gave themselves. "I pray you fare well in the Lord, remembering our troubles, fellow-Lucianist."

People and Stories

Voices in the Record

  • Paul of Samosata - bishop of Antioch, deposed on the charge of refusing to confess that the Son of God came down from heaven, surviving only in his enemies' minutes
  • Malchion - head of the city's rhetorical school, who cross-examined Paul of Samosata before stenographers and, by Jerome's account, drafted the letter that deposed him
  • Lucian of Antioch - biblical text-critic and martyr in 312, whose pupils, Arius among them, called themselves Lucianists
  • Serapion - bishop about 191 to 211, who let the Gospel of Peter be read at Rhossus, read it properly, and withdrew the permission
  • Aurelian - who took the city back from Palmyra in 272 and settled the dispute by ordering the church's house given to those to whom the bishops of Italy and Rome wrote

Documented Stories

Lucian of Antioch, Nine Years in Prison

Lucian was a presbyter of Antioch who spent his life on the text of the Greek Bible, producing a revision of the Septuagint and Gospels used across the Greek East for centuries. He was also, on the later testimony of his pupils, a teacher whose circle — Arius and Eusebius of Nicomedia among them — called themselves ‘fellow-Lucianists’ long after he was dead. What the record preserves about the man himself is the manner of his death. Eusebius, writing within a few years, says Lucian was brought before the emperor Maximinus at Nicomedia, delivered a defense of the faith, and was put in prison and killed; a later notice puts it on 7 January 312 and adds that he had been held for nine years. Chrysostom, preaching on his feast at Antioch two generations later, gave the tradition his congregation knew. Under questioning, Lucian gave every question the same answer: he was a Christian. Later still comes the story that, chained on his back in the cell and forbidden the sacrament, he celebrated the Eucharist on his own chest for the Christians imprisoned with him. The scholar is documented; the confessor is attested; the prisoner-priest is devotion. All three belong to how Antioch remembered him, and the awkward fact that the most notorious heresy of the next century traced itself to his classroom did not stop the church from keeping his feast.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Third-century Antioch was the seedbed of the fourth-century quarrel over the Son. Lucian's pupils led the Arian party. Three of them later taught Aetius, the first Anomoean teacher, whose opponents said he called the Son unlike the Father in substance.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced

  • influenced Antiochene Exegetical Christianity (Chrysostom-centered) Inferential-Thin — Jerome says Lucian, a priest of Antioch, studied Scripture so closely that copies still carried his name. Jerome does not tie his work to Diodore, Theodore of Mopsuestia or Chrysostom. The tie to these later teachers at Antioch is an inference, not a report.
  • influenced Anomoean (Eunomian) Christianity Documented — Philostorgius wrote history on the side of Eunomius. He says Aetius learned from three pupils of Lucian of Antioch. They were Athanasius of Anazarbus, Antonius of Tarsus, and Leontius, then a priest at Antioch. Only Photius's short version of his book is left.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

A council of bishops at Antioch deposed Paul of Samosata. The bishops wrote that he said Jesus Christ was from below, and Eusebius says he held that Christ was by nature an ordinary man. The bishops also wrote against his conduct, including a raised throne in church and hymns sung to himself. He would not leave the church's house until the emperor Aurelian was petitioned. Aurelian settled the matter by ordering the house given to the side the bishops of Italy and Rome wrote to. In the 350s, bishops wary of Nicaea's word 'homoousios', which says the Son is of one being with the Father, pointed back to this council. They said it had turned the word down, and Athanasius of Alexandria had to explain why.

Legacy

Three things went out of this council into the fourth century. The word homoousios - one substance - seems to have been rejected here as something Paul of Samosata had misused. A generation after Nicaea adopted it in 325, Eastern bishops who were not Arians still cited this council when they balked at it. And Lucian's school gave the Arian party its leadership and its self-description: Arius wrote to Eusebius of Nicomedia as one Lucianist to another. Paul's own followers kept a church of their own: the council of Nicaea ruled that the "Paulianists" who came over to the Catholic Church must be rebaptized, and that their clergy found blameless be rebaptized and ordained again.

Sources

Doctrinal Floor Note

The city's church itself is not in question. But Paul of Samosata's own teaching is a live, unresolved question about where the doctrinal line falls.

How Sure the Record Is

Only one document survives directly from this period: the synodal letter deposing Paul of Samosata, about three thousand words, the collective act of a council rather than an individual's book. Serapion's fragments fall just before this window. Everything else about these hundred years arrives through Eusebius of Caesarea, quoting archives at second hand, and through what the fourth century said about Lucian once his pupils had become notorious.
