About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In 335, at a council in Tyre, Athanasius's accusers held up a severed hand. They said he had murdered a bishop named Arsenius and used the hand for magic. The church historian Socrates, who tells it with plain sympathy, says Arsenius was found alive and led in with his hands under his cloak. Athanasius showed both. Another church historian, Sozomen, adds a rumor that Athanasius had hidden the man himself. Either way, the council deposed him in his absence.

He had been a young deacon at Nicaea in 325, where Socrates says he spoke strongly against the party of Arius, and three years later, at about thirty, he became bishop of Alexandria. Athanasius quotes Arius's poem, which said God was not always a Father and that the Son was made out of nothing. Nicaea answered that the Son is "begotten not made, One in essence with the Father."

If the Son were only a creature, Athanasius argued, then becoming man would have left humanity just as it was, not joined to God. In his own words: "For He was made man that we might be made God."

But the price was high. Constantine the Great sent him to Trier, Socrates says, on a charge of threatening the grain ships. Socrates gives another account too: some affirm the emperor did it for the unity of the church, since Athanasius refused any communion with Arius and his followers. Four more exiles followed. In one, the emperor's agents searched towns, monasteries and tombs for him, and no one gave him up. From hiding he wrote more than half of his surviving works. He came home for good in 366, named Peter as his successor, and died in May of 373.

Years later, Gregory of Nazianzus began a sermon: "In praising Athanasius, I shall be praising virtue." Sixty years after his death, as Cyril of Alexandria made peace with the bishops of the East, he wrote that he followed, especially, "our blessed and all-celebrated Father Athanasius."

Key Works

  • On the Incarnation: why the Word became man
  • Discourses Against the Arians: quotes Arius's Thalia and answers it
  • Life of Antony: the hermit's life, by one who had attended him

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The church historian Socrates of Constantinople declines to tell Antony's story because Athanasius of Alexandria had already given a whole book to it. Evagrius, later bishop at Antioch, put that book into Latin with a short apology for how freely he rendered it.
  • Gregory of Nazianzus gave him a whole sermon at Constantinople, and in it says that all who have lived according to God still live to God.
  • In 433 Cyril of Alexandria wrote to John of Antioch that some had garbled Athanasius of Alexandria's letter to Epictetus of Corinth. He sent transcripts from old copies he held, which he called genuine.

How they're remembered

  • Honored sixty years after his death: Cyril of Alexandria singles him out among the Fathers whose teaching he refuses to step outside of.
  • Disputed in his own time: the bishops at Tyre deposed him, and the reasons for his exile to Trier were reported two ways, as Socrates of Constantinople's telling shows.

At Major Councils

  • Council of Nicaea, 325: Present as Alexander's deacon; later wrote an account of the debate
  • Council of Sardica, 343: The accused; cleared by the Western bishops, condemned by the Eastern; kept the Western letters
  • Councils of Ariminum and Seleucia, 359: Not present; wrote On the Councils against the proceedings

What Is Uncertain

  • How Arsenius reached Tyre is not settled. Socrates of Constantinople credits God's providence: the governor's servants heard at an inn that he was hiding in a citizen's house, and the governor had him found. The church historian Sozomen gives two reasons, a divine prompting or, "as it is said", a plan of Athanasius of Alexandria. He says Arsenius slipped into Tyre by night the day before the trial.
  • The historian Rufinus of Aquileia tells that Bishop Alexander of Alexandria once watched boys on the shore playing at church, and that the boy who played bishop was young Athanasius of Alexandria. Socrates of Constantinople, who names Rufinus as his source, thinks it not improbable, and Sozomen tells it too.
  • Socrates of Constantinople says Athanasius of Alexandria hid for four months in a family tomb. The Historia Acephala, a record of his life, says he went to a country house of his near the New River.
