About This Person

One night in 356, soldiers surrounded a church in Alexandria, in Egypt, as its people prayed. Their bishop, the leader of the city's church, later wrote: "I thought it wrong to leave the people in such an uproar, rather than face the danger for them first." He sent the people home, and then monks, men who had left ordinary life for prayer, dragged him out past the soldiers.

This was Athanasius, bishop for forty-five years, who had to leave his city five times. At Nicaea, in today's Turkey, bishops agreed a creed, a statement of faith, in 325: God's Son is "of one being" with the Father, fully God. He held to it against the party of Arius, a priest, or church elder, who said the Son had a beginning. The Son became human, he taught, so that people might be made divine, joined to God and freed from death. Only a Son who was truly God could do that. He also wrote the life of a hermit, a monk living alone, and listed the books to be read as God's word.

At Nicaea he was a deacon, an officer under the bishop, says Socrates, a later historian. He became bishop in 328. When Arius was allowed home, he refused him communion, the shared bread and wine of one church, and the emperor Constantine threatened to remove him.

Most of our record comes from Athanasius or writers who used him, but a monk of the Melitians, a breakaway church in Egypt, gave the other side. In 335 he wrote that Athanasius's followers came with soldiers and beat their monks bloody. That year a council of bishops at Tyre, a meeting that judged church matters, removed him from office. Constantine sent him far away to Trier, now in Germany.

He came home in 337, and when a rival bishop came in 339 he fled to Rome, returning in 346. After that night in 356, under Constantine's son, now emperor, he hid for six years. A chronicle of his church records that searchers tortured a woman vowed to God to find him. From hiding he wrote his defenses. Yet in 359 he wrote that those who accepted all of Nicaea's statement but its one word should be met "as brothers with brothers". Those who rejected the council he still called enemies of Christ. In those years, by his editor's dating, he wrote the Life of Antony, for monks abroad who had asked how Antony lived. The emperor Julian drove him out in 362, and in 365 he hid four months near the city before he was led home.

Each year the bishop of Alexandria wrote to the churches of Egypt and Libya to set the date of Easter. In his Easter letter of 367, Athanasius set out the books passed down as Scripture, those the church reads as God's word. He named twenty-two of the Old Testament and twenty-seven of the New. He set other books apart for new believers. Still others, he said, were made up by heretics, teachers he held false, and passed off as old. Of the true books he wrote: "Let no one add to them, or take anything away from them."

A churchman of Antioch put the Life into Latin, Jerome says, and later it reached readers in Syriac, the language of the churches of Syria and Mesopotamia. In 692 a council made his rulings church law, and Greek copies of that law carry his list. In 1921 a Church of England proposal for sharing communion with the Orthodox churches of the East took the books as Athanasius had defined them.

He died in his own city in 373. Gregory of Nazianzus, preaching a few years later, said that many tears were shed for him.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The church historian Socrates of Constantinople declines to tell Antony's story because Athanasius of Alexandria had already given a whole book to it. Evagrius, later bishop at Antioch, put that book into Latin with a short apology for how freely he rendered it.
  • Gregory of Nazianzus gave him a whole sermon at Constantinople, and in it says that all who have lived according to God still live to God.
  • In 433 Cyril of Alexandria wrote to John of Antioch that some had garbled Athanasius of Alexandria's letter to Epictetus of Corinth. He sent transcripts from old copies he held, which he called genuine.

How they're remembered

  • Honored sixty years after his death: Cyril of Alexandria singles him out among the Fathers whose teaching he refuses to step outside of.
  • Disputed in his own time: the bishops at Tyre deposed him, and the reasons for his exile to Trier were reported two ways, as Socrates of Constantinople's telling shows.

At Major Councils

  • Council of Nicaea, 325: Present as Alexander's deacon; later wrote an account of the debate
  • Council of Sardica, 343: The accused; cleared by the Western bishops, condemned by the Eastern; kept the Western letters
  • Councils of Ariminum and Seleucia, 359: Not present; wrote On the Councils against the proceedings

What Is Uncertain

  • How Arsenius reached Tyre is not settled. Socrates of Constantinople credits God's providence: the governor's servants heard at an inn that he was hiding in a citizen's house, and the governor had him found. The church historian Sozomen gives two reasons, a divine prompting or, "as it is said", a plan of Athanasius of Alexandria. He says Arsenius slipped into Tyre by night the day before the trial.
  • The historian Rufinus of Aquileia tells that Bishop Alexander of Alexandria once watched boys on the shore playing at church, and that the boy who played bishop was young Athanasius of Alexandria. Socrates of Constantinople, who names Rufinus as his source, thinks it not improbable, and Sozomen tells it too.
  • Socrates of Constantinople says Athanasius of Alexandria hid for four months in a family tomb. The Historia Acephala, a record of his life, says he went to a country house of his near the New River.
