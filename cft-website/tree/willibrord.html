The Early Medieval Era · bishop 695; died 738 or 739 · Utrecht, Echternach

Willibrord

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Anglo-Saxon Christianity.

Known for
A Northumbrian monk who went to preach to the Frisians and became their archbishop. Pope Sergius consecrated him and named him Clement, and his see was at Utrecht, in Bede's account. Alcuin of York says he built the monastery of Echternach and was buried there.
Also called
Clement, Wilbrord, Willebrord
Dates
His own note, printed by Louis Duchesne, says he crossed the sea to Francia in 690 and was ordained bishop in 695. He was writing it in 728. Bede gives 696 for the consecration. Alcuin gives 7 November as the day he died, where Mabillon's text reads 6 November, and gives no year. Edward Kylie gives 739 and the Roman Breviary 738
Place
Born in Northumbria, in northern England. Raised at Ripon, then twelve years in Ireland. Archbishop of the Frisians, with his see at a fort Bede says the French call Utrecht. Buried at Echternach
Role
Missionary bishop; archbishop of the Frisians; founder of Echternach

About This Person

In the margin of a calendar he used, a bishop named Clement Willibrord wrote a short note about himself in Latin. He came over the sea to Francia in 690, he says. In 695 Pope Sergius ordained him bishop in Rome, and he calls himself unworthy of it. Louis Duchesne, editor of the early lives of the popes, prints the note.

Willibrord came from Northumbria, in northern England, and became archbishop of the Frisians, across the sea. Bede of Jarrow wrote of him in about 731, while he was still alive. Alcuin of York wrote his Life, full of miracle stories, more than fifty years after his death, by the reckoning of Froben, its editor.

Alcuin says that as soon as the boy was weaned, his father Wilgis gave him to the brothers of Ripon. He later spent twelve years in Ireland. Bede says he went to preach in Frisia with others, twelve in all.

Bede says Pippin, duke of the Franks, sent him to Pope Sergius, who consecrated him archbishop of the Frisians in 696 and named him Clement. Duchesne trusts the year in Willibrord's own note over Bede's. Pippin gave him a see at a fort that Bede says the French call Utrecht.

Alcuin tells of an island the pagans held sacred to their god Fosite. No one dared touch its cattle, and water was drawn from its spring only in silence. Willibrord baptized three men there and had the cattle killed for food. Radbod, king of the Frisians, cast lots to punish him, and one companion was put to death. Willibrord told the king his god was a devil. Radbod sent him back to Pippin.

Alcuin says he built the monastery of Echternach and was buried there after he died on 7 November. He gives no year. In a sermon for his feast, Alcuin names the patron saints of many cities, from Rome to Paris. To the monks Willibrord gathered, he says, God gave no small help in him.

Key Works

  • Note in his calendar (728): a short Latin note in the margin of a calendar he used, now in Paris, which Louis Duchesne prints. It gives the years he came to Francia and was ordained bishop, and calls himself "quamvis indignus", though unworthy

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Bede says Pippin gave him a see at Utrecht, where he built a church, and that he founded churches and monasteries and made other bishops.
  • Alcuin says that on gifts of land he had churches built and set a priest over each. The new people then had a place to gather and learn.
  • Alcuin says he built the monastery of Echternach, where he was buried, and that his successors still held it when Alcuin wrote.
  • Stephen's Life of Wilfrid of Ripon says Willibrord, raised at Ripon, was building on the faith Wilfrid had first planted in Frisia.
  • Edward Kylie writes that Boniface of Mainz worked three years in Frisia under Willibrord. Later, Kylie says, Boniface sought Pope Sergius's letter, which had made Utrecht a lasting see.
  • Alcuin says he baptized Pippin, father of the Charles who ruled the Franks when Alcuin wrote. Willibrord foretold that the child would be greater than all the Frankish leaders before him.

How they're remembered

  • Alcuin of York wrote his Life in two books, one in prose and one in verse, with a sermon for his feast. Froben, abbot of Saint Emmeram, who edited Alcuin, says Beornrad, archbishop of Sens, asked for it.
  • The Roman Breviary, in the Marquess of Bute's English, keeps his feast on 29 November. Its Martyrology names him on 7 November, as the bishop who preached in Friesland and Denmark.
  • The calendar of the York Prymer, which Canon Wordsworth edited, keeps his feast on 7 November.
  • Adam of Bremen says the island the Life calls Fositesland is the one later called Heligoland.

What Is Uncertain

  • Alcuin wrote his Life more than fifty years after his death, by Froben's reckoning, and much of it is miracle stories. Bede wrote of him while he was alive, in about 731.
  • The year of his consecration. Bede gives 696, on Saint Cecilia's day. His own note gives 695. Louis Duchesne says Bede's figures are wrong, and takes the 21 November his calendar marks, in 695.
  • Where he was consecrated. Bede says in the church of Saint Cecilia. Alcuin says in Saint Peter's.
  • Who sent him. Bede says Egbert, a priest living in Ireland, sent him with others, twelve in all. Alcuin has him decide to go himself, in his thirty-third year.
  • Who gave him Utrecht. Bede says Pippin. Alcuin says he was given the see under Pippin's son Charles, after Radbod, king of the Frisians, was beaten. L. Cecil Jane, in his notes to Bede, takes Bede's Wiltaburg for Wiltenburg, not Utrecht.
  • The year he died. No early source used here gives it. Bede says he was still living, thirty-six years a bishop, when he wrote. Edward Kylie gives 739, and the Roman Breviary 738, aged eighty-one.
  • His years at Ripon. Alcuin says his father gave him to the brothers of Ripon as soon as he was weaned. The Roman Breviary says he was taken there before he was seven and given to Wilfrid.
