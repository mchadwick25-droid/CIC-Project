Late Anglo-Saxon Christianity

England, c. 793 to 1066 — a church rebuilt twice, once by a king who could barely find a literate priest and once by monks, and unusual in Europe for preaching and writing in the language people actually spoke.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:23

Read along

In the Viking wars, Alfred of Wessex wrote, the churches were ravaged and burned, and learning all but died out. Whole dioceses lapsed. When Alfred set out to rebuild, he wrote, he could not think of one man south of the Thames who could translate a Latin letter. His answer was a program of translation. Gregory the Great's Pastoral Care and other books the clergy needed were put into English, so that they could read them at all. And the Chronicle begun in his reign is the first sustained history written in English. A century later came a second rebuilding, led by the bishops Dunstan, Æthelwold and Oswald. They refounded the monasteries on the Rule of Benedict and drew up one agreed customary for the whole country. The English language stayed central throughout. Ælfric wrote sermons in English, well over a hundred by his editors' count, so that ordinary people could hear careful teaching in their own tongue. Archbishop Wulfstan preached in 1014 as the Danish invasions closed in. He told the English that their own sins had brought the calamity on them. The Norman Conquest of 1066 ended the church's independent growth.

Documented Stories

Ælfheah Will Not Be Ransomed

The Danish army under Thorkell the Tall took Canterbury in September 1011, helped, the Chronicle says, by a traitor, and carried off the archbishop, Ælfheah, along with the abbess of St Mildred’s and a good part of the clergy and townspeople. The Anglo-Saxon Chronicle records what Ælfheah did: the Danes demanded a separate ransom of three thousand pounds for him, and he refused to allow it to be paid — his church and his people had been bled enough — and forbade anyone to offer anything on his behalf. On the Saturday after Easter, at a feast where the wine had come in from the south, the army, ‘greatly excited against the bishop, because he would not promise them any money’, took him to their assembly and pelted him with bones and the heads of cattle until one of them, a man named Thrum whom Ælfheah had confirmed the day before, struck him on the head with the back of an axe, and, the Chronicle says, ‘his holy blood fell on the earth, and his holy soul he sent forth to God’s kingdom’. The body was carried to St Paul’s in London, and to Canterbury in 1023 under Cnut, the Danish king who had by then become the church’s patron. Ælfheah was the first archbishop of Canterbury to be killed in office, and he died refusing to let the poor pay for him.

Dunstan Drags the King Back to His Coronation Feast

The first Life of Dunstan, written about 1000 by a cleric who signs himself only ‘B’ and had known Dunstan, tells the story that every later chronicler repeated. At the feast after Eadwig’s coronation at Kingston the young king got up and left the hall. The nobles, insulted, sent Dunstan and Bishop Cynesige to bring him back; they found him in a private chamber with a noblewoman named Æthelgifu and her daughter Ælfgifu, his crown thrown on the floor. B describes Dunstan seizing the king, jamming the crown back on his head, and hauling him bodily back to the feast, with words to the women that B makes very rude. Eadwig married Ælfgifu; within the year Æthelgifu had Dunstan outlawed, his property confiscated, and Dunstan fled to Flanders; and within two years Archbishop Oda had annulled the marriage on grounds of kinship and — B says — had Ælfgifu branded and driven out of the kingdom. Every source for this is from Dunstan’s side, and the treatment of the two women in them — B calls the mother a shameless harlot — has been read by modern historians as the monastic reform party’s revenge on a rival faction at court. What is documented is that the man who led the tenth-century reform of English monasticism laid hands on his king at a banquet, was exiled for it, and came back to rule the church for thirty years when the king’s enemies won.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In the Viking wars, by Alfred of Wessex's own account, England's churches were ravaged and burned. When he set out to rebuild, he wrote, he could not think of one man south of the Thames who could translate a Latin letter. So he set out to put the books the clergy most needed into English. A century later the bishops Dunstan, Æthelwold and Oswald refounded the monasteries on the Rule of Benedict. Ælfric wrote English sermons, well over a hundred by his editors' count, so that ordinary people could hear sound teaching in their own tongue. Archbishop Wulfstan preached in 1014, as Danish invasions closed in. He told the English that their own sins had brought the disaster on them.

Legacy

The institutions did not vanish at the Conquest. The dioceses, the cathedral communities and the parish network built in these centuries carried straight on into the Norman and later medieval English church, and many English cathedrals still stand where these bishops put them. What is distinctive here is the language. For two centuries England produced Christian literature in the vernacular on a scale unmatched anywhere else in western Europe, from Alfred's translations to Ælfric's sermons to the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle. Buildings survive too — late Anglo-Saxon towers and churches, like the decorated tower at Earls Barton, are among the oldest working parish churches in England.

Experience It Today

  • All Saints' Church, Earls Barton, Northamptonshire — a late Anglo-Saxon tower of about 970, still a working parish church
