The Scandinavian Reformation Kingdoms

Denmark-Norway and Sweden-Finland, the 1520s to 1650 — Reformations settled by kings and diets, then taught to whole rural nations out of hymnbooks and catechisms.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:03

Read along

In Scandinavia the Reformation arrived from the top down and stayed. Sweden's break began at the Diet of Västerås in 1527, when Gustav Vasa took the church's property and its independence, and was only completed at the Uppsala Synod of 1593, which committed the kingdom to the unaltered Augsburg Confession. In Denmark-Norway it came faster and harder. Christian III won a civil war, arrested the bishops outright in 1536, and the church ordinance drawn up with Luther's colleague Johannes Bugenhagen reorganized the churches of Denmark, Norway and Iceland at a stroke. Icelandic resistance ended in 1550 with the execution of the island's last Catholic bishop. What followed everywhere was the long, slow business of translating Bibles and hymnbooks and teaching a rural population its catechism.

Documented Stories

Skálholt, 7 November 1550

Jón Arason, bishop of Hólar in the north of Iceland, was a poet, the man who brought the first printing press to the island, the father of a family by his housekeeper Helga, and the last Catholic bishop in the Nordic lands. When the southern see of Skálholt went Lutheran in 1541 and the Danish crown sent a new church ordinance, Jón refused it; he was outlawed in 1548 but went on ruling his diocese, and in 1549 and 1550 rode south with armed men, seized the Lutheran bishop Marteinn Einarsson, occupied Skálholt and took over its cathedral. In October 1550 he and two of his sons, Ari and Björn, were captured at Sauðafell by a band under the farmer Daði Guðmundsson. There was no court in session to try them. The royal bailiff Christian Skriver and Marteinn's supporters decided at Skálholt that the country could not hold them safely through the winter, and on 7 November 1550 the three were beheaded in the churchyard there. The sons went first and the bishop last; tradition says the axeman needed seven strokes, and gives Jón a last question, whether there was any world other than the one he was leaving. In the spring the fishermen and farmers of the north came south, killed Christian Skriver and his men, and carried the three bodies home to Hólar. Lutheranism was established across Iceland the following year, and Jón's hymns were sung in it.

The Bishop’s Speech to the Parish

Peder Palladius, a shoemaker's son from Ribe who had studied under Luther, was made bishop of Zealand in 1537 by Bugenhagen, and in the following years visited every one of the 390 parishes in his diocese, sitting in each church with the congregation to explain what the new order meant. Out of these visits he wrote, in Danish, a Visitation Book, which is less a handbook than the speech he gave. He tells the parish how its church should look now that the side-altars are gone and the pulpit matters; how the children are to be taught the catechism by the parish clerk, and how the parents will answer for it; that the poor box is to be kept filled and the poor of the parish fed from it before any beggar from outside; that the midwife is to be a godly woman who can baptize in emergency and must be paid; that the peasants are to come to church on time, stay awake, and not leave before the blessing; and that the women are to sit on one side and the men on the other. There is a passage on the hymns the congregation is to learn, and one on the churchyard, which is not for cattle. The book was not printed in his lifetime; a copy was found and published in 1867. It is the closest thing the Danish Reformation has to a description of itself from the parish end.

The King Condemns His Reformers

In January 1540 King Gustav Vasa had the two men who had made the Swedish Reformation, the preacher Olaus Petri and the former chancellor Laurentius Andreae, tried at Örebro before a court of nobles, and condemned to death. The charge was that they had known of a conspiracy against the king's life, confessed to Olaus in 1536, and had not reported it; behind it lay the king's anger at Olaus's sermons against royal greed and at his chronicle of Sweden, which did not flatter the dynasty or its founder. Olaus had already been dismissed as the king's secretary. He had written the first Swedish New Testament of 1526, the Swedish hymnbook and the Swedish Mass of 1531, and had preached in the Stockholm cathedral for years. The sentences were commuted on payment of enormous fines, which the burghers of Stockholm raised for Olaus. He was given back the pulpit of the Great Church in 1543 and died in 1552; Laurentius died at Strängnäs the same year. The king had by then brought in a German superintendent, Georg Norman, to run the church as an office of the crown. The episode is the Swedish Reformation's own record that the reform and the crown were not the same thing, though the crown won; the church Sweden confirmed at Uppsala in 1593 was Olaus's in its books and Gustav's in its government.

In Its Own Record

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Sweden's break began at the Diet of Västerås in 1527, when Gustav Vasa seized the church's property, and wasn't completed until the Uppsala Synod of 1593 committed the kingdom to the unaltered Augsburg Confession. Denmark-Norway moved faster and harder: Christian III arrested the bishops outright in 1536, and Icelandic resistance ended only in 1550, with the execution of the island's last Catholic bishop.

Legacy

These became the Lutheran churches of Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Finland and Iceland — still the largest churches in their countries, and in several cases still formally established or closely tied to the state. Their most durable everyday inheritance is linguistic: the vernacular Bibles and hymnbooks of this period helped standardize written Swedish, Danish and Finnish. The parish register, kept from this era for baptisms, marriages and burials, also made Scandinavia one of the most thoroughly documented populations on earth.
