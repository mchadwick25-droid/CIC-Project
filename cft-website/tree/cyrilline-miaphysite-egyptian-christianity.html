The Age of Monks and Empires · 328-642 · Alexandria and Egypt

The Church of Alexandria and Miaphysite Egypt

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In 328 Athanasius became bishop of Alexandria. The Coptic church still counts him as its twentieth patriarch. He wrote and fought against the teaching of Arius. His foes had him deposed at Tyre in 335, and the Melitians, a rival church in Egypt, helped bring the charges.

Cyril became bishop in 412. He clashed with Nestorius, bishop of Constantinople, over a name for Mary. The name was Theotokos, the one who gave birth to God. In 431 a council met at Ephesus. Cyril's side began before the bishops from Syria came. They deposed Nestorius, and he was sent into exile.

In 451 the Council of Chalcedon said Christ must be confessed "in two natures". It also deposed Dioscorus, Cyril's successor, who would not come when called three times. In Egypt, clergy, monks and people broke with the bishop the council put in his place. From then on Alexandria had two lines of bishops. One was backed by the emperors. This page follows the other, the line most Egyptians kept.

The monks of the desert stood behind this line. In 457 its people asked the monks to help choose a bishop, and they chose Timothy, once a monk in the desert. Only one bishop could be found to consecrate him, so the people brought in a second, Peter the Iberian. After 518 Severus of Antioch, driven from Syria, lived in exile among them. Over these years Coptic, the language of Egypt, took a larger place beside Greek.

Who were the true heirs of Athanasius and Cyril? That is still disputed. Chalcedon itself took Cyril's letters as a guide to the faith. The Coptic church holds that Dioscorus kept that faith in the see of Saint Mark, and that the council broke it. Both sides claim the same teachers.

Arab armies took Egypt between 640 and 642. Benjamin, the patriarch who had hidden from the emperor's men, came back to Alexandria. The conqueror told him to govern his churches again, and the church went on under Muslim rule.

In Its Own Record

Gave Rise To

  • Nubian Christianity Documented Major — John of Ephesus says the priest Julian, backed by the empress Theodora, converted the king of the Nobadae. Julian was one of the clergy of Theodosius, the exiled patriarch of Alexandria. He told them to 'walk in the ways of pope Theodosius'. They were placed under Theodosius as their patriarch.
  • Aksumite Christianity (conversion era) Documented Major — Frumentius came to Alexandria. He asked Athanasius of Alexandria to send a bishop to his land. Socrates of Constantinople says Athanasius told him to take the post himself. Later the emperor sent a letter to the rulers of Aksum. In it he named Frumentius as the bishop Athanasius had made for them.

Continued As

  • Coptic Christianity under Early Islam Documented Major — Benjamin led the church of Alexandria both before and after the Arab conquest. John of Nikiu says he came back to Alexandria in the thirteenth year after he fled the Romans. The History of the Patriarchs says Amr, the conqueror, told him to govern his churches once more.

Influenced By

  • Desert Monasticism Documented — In 457 the Alexandrians who refused Chalcedon took counsel with 'the whole order of the monks'. They chose Timothy as bishop. He was an ascetic who had once been brought from the desert to Cyril of Alexandria. Centuries later the church of Egypt wrote down its History of the Patriarchs. It says the letters of Peter, its head, were kept at the Monastery of Macarius. It says Benjamin fled through the monasteries of Wadi Habib to hide in Upper Egypt.
  • Syriac Orthodox (West Syriac) Christianity Documented Major — Severus of Antioch wrote to Egyptian clergy after his exile began. He told them he cared even more for Alexandria's church than for Antioch's. He says groups long cut off from the Egyptian church joined it through his work. Dioscorus II, patriarch of Alexandria, took his advice, he writes.Lasting result: Dioscorus of Alexandria took Severus of Antioch's advice. He won back men who had long kept out of communion, and they joined the Egyptian church.

Argued Against

  • The Melitian Schism Documented Major — Athanasius of Alexandria wrote against the Melitians by name. He said they were 'even yet called Meletians instead of Christians'. More than two hundred years later, Damian became head of the church. The History of the Patriarchs says he drove the Melitians out of the monks' houses of Wadi Habib.Lasting result: Damian, head of the church, sent the Melitians away from the four houses of monks in Wadi Habib.
  • Antiochene Exegetical Christianity (Chrysostom-centered) Documented Major — Cyril of Alexandria heard of the sermons of Nestorius, the new bishop in the capital. He wrote to warn him twice. Then he wrote five books against him. In 431 the council at Ephesus said Nestorius was no longer a bishop.Lasting result: In 431 the council at Ephesus put Nestorius out as bishop. Maximian took his place.

Opposed By

  • The Melitian Schism Documented Major — Socrates of Constantinople says the foes of Athanasius of Alexandria used Melitians to bring charge after charge against him. In 335 the synod at Tyre voted to depose him. Arsenius had been a Melitian bishop, and Athanasius was said to have killed him. He turned up alive and signed the sentence.Lasting result: In 335 a synod at Tyre voted to put Athanasius of Alexandria out of his see, on charges the Melitians helped to bring.
  • Antiochene Exegetical Christianity (Chrysostom-centered) Documented — In exile, around 451, Nestorius wrote his own defense. He called it the Bazaar of Heracleides. In it he speaks straight to Cyril of Alexandria. He says Cyril wrote him a hostile letter that showed he was his enemy.

Broke With

  • The Byzantine Imperial Church (Justinianic) Documented Major — The historian Evagrius Scholasticus quotes a petition from Egypt's bishops to Emperor Leo. It says Timothy Aelurus broke from the Catholic church right after the Council of Chalcedon. The other side kept its own record of the split. John of Ephesus records that Bishop Theodosius would not accept the council. So the emperor drove him from his throne.
  • Imperial and Juridical Christianity Documented Major — On 13 October 451 the Council of Chalcedon told Dioscorus of Alexandria that he had been deposed. A chronicle from the Egyptian side, written in the sixth century, tells what came next. Clergy, monks and people went off to their monasteries. They broke communion with the bishop put in his place.
  • The Melitian Schism Documented Major — Athanasius of Alexandria wrote that the followers of Melitius were 'even yet called Meletians instead of Christians'. A Melitian monk wrote from the other side, around 335. Men of Athanasius's party, he said, beat his brothers and locked them up. Socrates of Constantinople says the Melitians of Egypt still stood apart from the church in his own day.

In Contact With

  • Aksumite Christianity Inferential-Thin — A Syriac letter on the Najran killings of the 520s asks that the patriarch of Alexandria write to the Ethiopian king for help. John of Ephesus calls the Aksumite king a fellow believer. No surviving text from these years is known to show a letter actually sent between them.
  • Syriac Orthodox (West Syriac) Christianity Documented — After 518, says a Syriac chronicle of the 560s, many bishops went to Alexandria in the steps of Severus of Antioch. It also copies the letters of communion that he and Theodosius of that city sent each other in the 530s. Each greets the other as his brother and fellow minister.
  • Peter the Iberian Documented — The Life of Peter the Iberian says only one bishop could be found to make Timothy bishop at Alexandria. So the people carried Peter to the church where the crowd was waiting. Evagrius Scholasticus, a later writer on the other side, cites this Life for the event.

Sources

  • Severus of Antioch, Select Letters (ed./trans. E. W. Brooks, 2 vols, 1902-04) (primary) — Inside voice of the anti-Chalcedonian leadership in exile in Egypt (518-538); Greek originals lost, survives in Syriac translation; Brooks's English translation is old but standard and online.
  • History of the Patriarchs of the Coptic Church of Alexandria (attrib. Severus ibn al-Muqaffa') (primary) — The Miaphysite church's own continuous narrative of its Alexandrian patriarchs; Arabic compilation of earlier Coptic sources; ed./English trans. B. Evetts in Patrologia Orientalis 1, 5, 10 (1904-15), online at tertullian.org.
  • John of Nikiu, Chronicle (trans. R. H. Charles, 1916) (primary) — Egyptian Miaphysite bishop's world chronicle ending with the Arab conquest (642) - the era's terminus seen from inside; survives only via Ethiopic from Arabic; Charles's English translation online. Plus Coptic/Greek documentary papyri (e.g. Dioscorus of Aphrodito archive) for ordinary texture.
  • W. H. C. Frend, The Rise of the Monophysite Movement (1972) (secondary) — Still the standard single narrative of the anti-Chalcedonian movement 451 onward; dated in places (terminology 'Monophysite' now avoided) but widely available.
  • Stephen J. Davis, The Early Coptic Papacy (2004) (secondary) — Modern history of the Egyptian church's leadership and identity in exactly this window; accessible English monograph (AUC Press).
  • Cambridge History of Christianity, vol. 2 (Constantine to c.600), ch. 3, 'Greek Christianities' (F. W. Norris); cf. ch. 4 (D. Bundy) (reference) — Reference-tradition anchor for eastern-Roman (incl. Egyptian) developments to c.600.
  • Athanasius, Select Works and Letters (NPNF 2nd series, vol. 4, ed. A. Robertson) (primary) — Athanasius's own voice for 328-373: the ‘Defence against the Arians’, the ‘Defence before Constantius’, the Festal Letters and the Life of Antony. Hostile to the Melitians and to his other opponents.
  • Cyril of Alexandria, Five Tomes against Nestorius; Three Epistles (trans. P. E. Pusey, 1872, 1881) (primary) — Cyril's own voice against Nestorius before and after Ephesus, 431. The Library shelf of the retired Cyrilline tradition entry, now on this one.
  • Socrates Scholasticus and Sozomen, Ecclesiastical Histories (NPNF 2nd series, vol. 2) (primary) — Fifth-century church histories from Constantinople. Outside accounts of Athanasius, the Melitians, Tyre in 335, Hypatia and Ephesus in 431.
