The Age of Monks and Empires · c. 596–609 · Dvin, chief city of Persian Armenia

Council of Dvin

Major council

Known for
The synod at Dvin, dated 609 by Malachia Ormanian and 596 by Marie-Félicité Brosset, at which the Armenian church cursed the Georgian catholicos Kyrion for accepting Chalcedon, so that the two churches of the Caucasus parted.
Dates
The year is disputed and no ancient witness held gives it. Malachia Ormanian has Abraham elected catholicos on 30 April 607 and presiding at the council against the Georgians in 609. Marie-Félicité Brosset, in a note of 1876 to Samuel of Ani, puts the council in 596. James Issaverdens gives no year for the synod, but sets Abraham's accession in a year of the 590s whose last digit is lost in the print, and has Kyrion appeal to the emperor Maurice; Stephen of Taron also sets Abraham's quarrel under Maurice. James Barmby dates the accession of Maurice's successor, Phocas, to November 602
Rivers
Caucasus
Called by
Abraham, catholicos of Armenia, by the account of James Issaverdens; Ormanian says Abraham presided

About This Council

Abraham, the catholicos or head of the Armenian church, did all he could to win the Iberians to his teaching. When he failed, he drew the spiritual sword and cursed them, with the assent of all the Armenians. So Stephen of Taron, an Armenian historian writing around the year 1000, tells it. Stephen names no council and gives no year.

Malachia Ormanian, a former Armenian patriarch of Constantinople, calls these Iberians the Georgians. Their own church head, Kyrion, had been made catholicos by Movses, who led the Armenian church before Abraham. Shortly before Movses died, Kyrion came to doubt what Stephen calls the orthodox faith. He accepted the Council of Chalcedon. Ormanian says he did it to win the emperor's favor.

No record of the council survives among the books this account draws on, and no letter between the two church heads. Of the writers drawn on here, only modern ones tell of a synod at Dvin. The Georgian chronicle, in Marie-Félicité Brosset's French, does not name Kyrion.

Kyrion's own words reach us only through James Issaverdens, a priest of the Armenian Catholic monastery of San Lazzaro in Venice, writing in 1875. He praises Kyrion's learning and piety. He says Abraham sent Kyrion three letters. He gives Kyrion's reply: "I am astonished that you are at variance with all other churches of Christ." Issaverdens names no source for it.

Issaverdens says Abraham called a synod of bishops at Dvin, the chief city of Persian Armenia. They cursed Kyrion "and all those who accepted it," meaning Chalcedon. No writer names the bishops or counts them.

The year is disputed. Ormanian has Abraham elected on 30 April 607 and the council at Dvin in 609. Brosset, in a note of 1876, puts it in 596. Stephen and Issaverdens both set Abraham's quarrel in the reign of the emperor Maurice.

Sebeos, an Armenian bishop of the 600s, tells of a debate before the Persian king. In it the catholicos of Georgia and Albania took Chalcedon's side. Issaverdens adds that Kyrion complained to Maurice, who called bishops of both sides to Constantinople. No other writer tells of that meeting.

Ormanian says the Georgian church, with Kyrion at its head, "became part of the Greek Church."

Why They Met

  • Kyrion, catholicos of the Iberians, had been consecrated by the Armenian catholicos Movses (Stephen of Taron). James Issaverdens says the Georgians, unable to agree on a successor, asked Movses to choose for them, and he chose Kyrion, a Georgian already a bishop in Armenia.
  • Shortly before Movses died, Kyrion came to doubt the orthodox faith, as Stephen puts it, and accepted the Council of Chalcedon. Movses often rebuked him, without success (Stephen of Taron).
  • Malachia Ormanian says Kyrion meant to secede from the Armenian see and join that of Constantinople "in order to win the imperial favour". Issaverdens says Kyrion had Chalcedon's acts and Leo's Tome brought from Constantinople and translated.
  • On one side stood the Armenian church under Vertanes, who ran it after Movses's death, and then Abraham; on the other, Kyrion and the Georgian church. Kyrion's words reach us only in the report of James Issaverdens, who praises his learning and piety: "I am astonished that you are at variance with all other churches of Christ, which declare that the creed of the Council of Chalcedon is precisely the same with that of the three other councils."

Who Came

  • No held source counts the bishops or names any of them besides Abraham.
  • Stephen of Taron says Abraham acted with the assent of all the Armenians.

What They Decided

  • The bishops at Dvin anathematized Kyrion "and all those who accepted it", the Council of Chalcedon, and so cut the Armenians off from all who approved it (Issaverdens).
  • Abraham drew the spiritual sword and anathematized the Iberians, with the assent of all the Armenians (Stephen of Taron, who names no council and gives no date).
  • The synod ratified the Georgian secession from the Armenian church (Ormanian).

What Came of It

  • Ormanian says the Georgian church, with Kurion at her head, "definitely accepted the Chalcedonian faith, and became part of the Greek Church".
  • Issaverdens says Kyrion complained to the emperor Maurice, who called Greek and Armenian bishops to Constantinople, where Chalcedon was declared orthodox; Abraham's envoys Vertanes and Gregory refused it. No other held source tells of this meeting.
  • Stephen of Taron says the emperor Maurice set up a separate catholicos, Yohan, over the Greek part of Armenia, while Abraham resided at Dvin.
  • Sebeos, an Armenian bishop of the 600s, tells of a debate before the Persian king Khosrow in which the catholicos of Georgia and Albania, with many bishops of the Greek region, took Chalcedon's side.
  • Ormanian, from the Armenian side, says the event had "unpleasant consequences for the Georgian Church" under Russian rule in the nineteenth century, when that church was absorbed by the Russian one.
  • Brosset, drawing on the Armenian historian Chamich, wrote in 1849 that from about 580 the Georgians drew near the Greeks and parted from the Armenians in religion for ever.

Traditions It Touched

Gathered

  • Armenian Christianity before the Bagratids: Abraham, catholicos of Armenia, called the synod at Dvin, by Issaverdens's account, and presided, by Ormanian's; Stephen of Taron says he acted with the assent of all the Armenians.

Condemned

  • Georgian Christianity: The synod cursed Kyrion and all who accepted Chalcedon (Issaverdens); Ormanian says it ratified the Georgian church's secession, and the census row ends here.

Affected

  • The Byzantine Imperial Church (Justinianic): Ormanian says the Georgian church, with Kyrion at its head, became part of the Greek Church; Issaverdens alone says Kyrion appealed to the emperor Maurice.
  • The Church of Caucasian Albania: Sebeos places the catholicos of Georgia and Albania on Chalcedon's side in a debate before the Persian king; Ormanian says a later Albanian head, Nerses Bakour, was 'tempted by the example of Kurion'.

Whose Account We Have

  • The acts of the council and the letters of Abraham and Kyrion (acts): Not held in any form, in the original or in translation. Brosset knew Abraham's letter to Kyrion from a manuscript of Ukhtanes, which is not held.
  • Sebeos, History of Heraclius (Macler's French) (later narrative): An Armenian bishop of the 600s, the earliest held writer; he does not name Kyrion or the council, but places the catholicos of Georgia and Albania on Chalcedon's side.
  • Stephen of Taron (Asoghik), Universal History (Gelzer and Burckhardt's German) (later narrative): An Armenian historian writing around 1000; on the Armenian side. He tells Kyrion's consecration, his turn to Chalcedon and Abraham's anathema, with no council named and no date.
  • Samuel of Ani, Chronological Tables, as quoted in Brosset's note (later narrative): A twelfth-century Armenian chronicler, on the Armenian side: the Iberians, having adopted the heresy of Chalcedon, separated. Brosset calls the chronology of that entry wholly wrong.
  • Malachia Ormanian, The Church of Armenia (1912) (modern reconstruction): A former Armenian patriarch of Constantinople; the Armenian church's own account. Gives 607 for Abraham's election and 609 for the council.
  • James Issaverdens, Armenia and the Armenians, vol. 2 (Venice, 1875) (modern reconstruction): A priest of the Mekhitarist monastery of San Lazzaro in Venice, whose founder, Ormanian says, had to yield to the demands of the Roman Curia. Favorable to Kyrion, whom he calls a most learned and pious man. The only held source for the letters, Kyrion's reply and the appeal to Maurice; he names no sources.
  • Marie-Félicité Brosset, notes to the Georgian chronicle (1849) and to Samuel of Ani (1876) (modern reconstruction): The French historian of Georgia. Puts the council at Dvin in 596 and the Georgian turn to the Greeks about 580; the Georgian chronicle he translates does not name Kyrion.

What Is Uncertain

  • No record of the council and no letter between Abraham and Kyrion is held. Among the writers held, the synod at Dvin is told only by modern ones: Issaverdens in 1875, Brosset in 1876 and Ormanian in 1912. Brosset cites the medieval Armenian historians Kirakos and Ukhtanes for it; neither is held. Stephen of Taron, writing around 1000, tells Abraham's anathema but names no council and gives no year. Kyrion's own words reach us only through Issaverdens, who is favorable to him; the Georgian chronicle in Brosset's French does not name him.
  • The year: 609 for Ormanian, with Abraham elected on 30 April 607; 596 for Brosset in 1876, who calls the chronology of one edition of Samuel of Ani wholly wrong; no year for the synod in Issaverdens or Stephen of Taron, though both set Abraham's quarrel under the emperor Maurice, whose successor Phocas, by James Barmby's date, took the throne in November 602. Brosset in 1849 dates a letter of Abraham to 595, and Frédéric Macler's notes to Sebeos give Abraham's years in a garbled line.
  • Kyrion's reply and the letters come only from Issaverdens, who names no source.
  • The appeal to the emperor Maurice and the meeting at Constantinople come only from Issaverdens.
  • Whether Gregory the Great's letter of 601 to Quiricus, a bishop in Hiberia, is to Kyrion: its editors do not name him, but place the letter in the eastern, Pontic Iberia rather than Ireland or Spain.
