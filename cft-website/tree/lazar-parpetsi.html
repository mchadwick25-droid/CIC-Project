Lazar Parpetsi

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Armenian Christianity after Avarayr.

Known for
An Armenian historian who wrote, at the order of Vahan Mamikonian, a History of Armenia from the time of Sahak the Great and Mesrop Mashtots through the war of Vardan Mamikonian to the day Vahan took the government of Armenia.
Also called
Lazar of Pharp, Lazarus of Parpi, Lazar the Rhetor
Dates
Victor Langlois places him at the end of the fifth century or the start of the sixth. His History ends with Vahan made governor of Armenia, which a note in the French edition dates to 486 and Charles Neumann, in the preface to his English Elishe, to 485. No source used here gives his birth or death
Place
Armenia: the monastery of Pharp in Ararat; Etchmiadzin; Tigranocerta
Role
Historian; by Langlois's account, in charge of the mother church at Etchmiadzin

About This Person

After the battle of Avarayr, the Persian king held Armenian princes captive. Lazar Parpetsi, also called Lazar of Pharp, says he saw some of them with his own eyes. He writes that they set up schools to learn church teaching. They said the psalms in their minds as they took up their weapons.

Lazar was an Armenian historian. Victor Langlois, who edited his History in French, places him at the end of the fifth century or the start of the sixth. Langlois says the name Parpetsi came from a long stay at the monastery of Pharp, a village in Ararat, and not from his birthplace.

Most of his life comes from Langlois's introduction, which draws partly on a letter of Lazar's that the Library does not hold. Langlois calls him a childhood companion of Vahan Mamikonian. He says Lazar studied in Greece with leave from the patriarch Sahak the Great and from Mesrop Mashtots. When Vahan governed Armenia for the Persian king, he put Lazar in charge of the mother church at Etchmiadzin.

Langlois says jealous clergy accused his teaching of going against the true faith, and Vahan listened to them. His enemies drove him out and would not let him take the Greek books he had gathered. From Tigranocerta he wrote Vahan a long letter answering each charge. Langlois says it seems to have worked, for Vahan called him back.

Lazar says Vahan ordered him to write the History, which ends on the day Vahan took the government of Armenia. Lazar doubted some stories in Faustus of Byzantium, suspecting that another writer had added them or a poor copyist had altered the text. He also tells that his patron Vahan, worn down by slander, weakened in his faith at the Persian court.

He called his History the third part of Armenia's annals, after the histories of Agathangelos and Faustus. In his own phrase, it was the work of his weakness.

Key Works

  • History of Armenia: from the partition of Armenia and the deaths of Sahak the Great and Mesrop Mashtots, through the war of 451 and the captivity of the princes, to Vahan Mamikonian's revolt and his appointment; in Samuel Ghesarian's French
  • Letter to Vahan Mamikonian: his defense against the charges that drove him from Etchmiadzin; known here only from Victor Langlois's account, since the Library does not hold it

Legacy

What traces to them

  • With Elishe's History, it is one of the two full Armenian accounts of the war of 451 that the Library holds, and it carries the story on through Vahan Mamikonian's revolt.
  • Victor Langlois's notes to Moses of Khoren and Elishe in the same volume cite Lazar again and again.

How they're remembered

  • Victor Langlois: he closes the series of writers of the golden age of Armenian letters, and his Letter to Vahan is rightly held a masterpiece of style.
  • Malachia Ormanian, a former Armenian patriarch of Constantinople, names "Lazarus of Parpi" among the churchmen whose histories show the learning of the Armenian clergy.

What Is Uncertain

  • Most of his life comes from Victor Langlois's introduction, which leans on Lazar's Letter to Vahan Mamikonian. The Library does not hold the letter. Langlois says only that the letter seems to have won Vahan back, and he thinks it probable that Lazar then wrote the History in Vahan's palace.
  • Langlois's dates for him are broad: the end of the fifth century or the start of the sixth. The notes in the French edition date Vahan's rule as governor from 486 to 511. Charles Neumann has the History run from 428 to 485.
  • That he was Vahan's childhood companion, studied in Greece with leave from Sahak the Great and Mesrop Mashtots, and lived two years with a hermit in Siunik all rest on Langlois's introduction.
  • Langlois says Vahan put him in charge of the mother church at Etchmiadzin. Stephen of Taron, an Armenian historian of about the year 1000, says instead that Vahan made him head of the monks of Surenay-Anapat.
  • Lazar wrote at Vahan's order and tells his patron's story, though he also tells that Vahan weakened in his faith at the Persian court.
  • Langlois says a tomb of Lazar is said to stand at Pharp still, and he gives it as hearsay.
  • Langlois says Samuel Ghesarian translated from the only printed text, the Venice edition, and that this text is faulty.
