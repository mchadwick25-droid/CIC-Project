The Early Church Era · 2nd-6th c. · Phrygia, spreading

Phrygia, today in Turkey and beyond

Montanism (the New Prophecy)

In Phrygia, in what is now western Turkey, Christians followed the prophets Montanus, Priscilla and Maximilla. They held that the Holy Spirit spoke through them, and called their words the New Prophecy.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 4:52

Read along

Ardabau was a village in the uplands of what is now western Turkey. There, in the second century, a recent Christian convert named Montanus fell into what an enemy calls a frenzy and began to say strange things. Some who heard him called him possessed and told him to be silent. Others, who thought themselves filled with the Holy Spirit, urged him on, and he could no longer be kept quiet.

Montanus and his followers were Christians of Phrygia, and two women, Priscilla and Maximilla, prophesied as he did. Enemies called them Montanists or Phrygians, but they spoke of the New Prophecy, their name for the prophets and their words. They held that the Paraclete, the Holy Spirit Jesus had promised as Helper, spoke through their prophets now, in ecstasy, carrying them out of their own senses, and called the church to a stricter life.

That enemy, a church elder, argued with them while the movement was new, and most of the record, like his, comes from their opponents.

The elder says Montanus longed to lead. Apollonius, another opponent writing forty years after Montanus began, says Montanus gave the name Jerusalem to two small Phrygian towns, Pepuza and Tymion, to gather people there from everywhere.

The faithful of the Roman province of Asia met often, in many places, to examine the new sayings, whose manner, the elder says, broke the church's settled custom. Finding them novel and profane, they drove the prophets' followers out, barred from communion: shut out of the church's fellowship and its shared meal. The prophets' side called their opponents killers of the prophets, for refusing prophets who, they said, were the ones the Lord had promised to send. When bishops came to refute the spirit in Maximilla, her followers stopped them. She said: "I am driven away from the sheep like a wolf. I am not a wolf. I am word and spirit and power." If the spirit had such power, the elder answered, let it make those bishops acknowledge it.

Maximilla also said, in words a later opponent kept, that after her there would be no prophet any more, but the end of the age. The end she foretold did not come, and around 200, at Carthage in North Africa, the writer Tertullian took up their cause in Latin.

The quarrel, he says, was this: "The new prophecies are rejected, not because Montanus and Priscilla and Maximilla preach another God. Nor is it because they divide Jesus Christ, or overturn any rule of faith or hope. It is because they plainly teach fasting more often than marrying." The rule of faith was the short creed he set out: one God the Creator, and his Son Jesus Christ, born of the Virgin, crucified, risen and coming to judge. The main church blamed them for fasts of their own, Stations kept on into the evening, and dry food, with no meat, juice or wine. He defended their rule of one marriage only: no second marriage, even after a husband or wife had died. When Christians were hunted for their faith, he urged them to face death rather than flee. He held that such rules were the Paraclete's teaching.

Epiphanius, that later opponent, writing about 375, says that in his day Pepuza lay deserted, yet the believers still went there for their rites, saying that the Jerusalem from above, the heavenly city, comes down there. He gives the vision of a prophetess, Quintilla or Priscilla, he could not say which: "Christ came to me in the form of a woman, clothed in a shining robe. He put wisdom into me. He showed me that this place is holy, and that Jerusalem comes down here from heaven."

Under the emperor Justinian, in the sixth century, the empire ordered groups such as the Montanists to give up their beliefs for the established faith, says Procopius, a writer hostile to Justinian. He claims the emperor wanted blood and riches. In Phrygia, the Montanists shut themselves inside their churches, set them alight and died in the flames.

People and Stories

Voices in the Record

  • Montanus — the prophet whose name opponents attached to the movement, though its own name for itself was the New Prophecy
  • Priscilla and Maximilla — the two prophetesses who spoke alongside him, and whose quoted oracles are among the very few first-person Montanist words that survive at all
  • Tertullian of Carthage — the movement's most articulate Latin defender, and the only sympathetic writer whose work survives in quantity
  • The ordinary members are the gap in the record: almost everything else comes through hostile writers such as Apollonius and the anonymous author quoted by Eusebius of Caesarea

Documented Stories

The Sister Who Saw Visions During the Service

Tertullian, defending the New Prophecy against the charge of disorder, described how it actually worked in his own congregation. ‘We have now amongst us a sister whose lot it has been to be favoured with sundry gifts of revelation, which she experiences in the Spirit by ecstatic vision amidst the sacred rites of the Lord’s day in the church.’ She converses with angels, sometimes with the Lord; she sees and hears ‘mysterious communications’; she reads hearts and prescribes remedies for those who ask. What Tertullian wants his readers to notice is the discipline around it. She does not interrupt. ‘After the people are dismissed at the conclusion of the sacred services, she is in the regular habit of reporting to us whatever things she may have seen in vision, for all her communications are examined with the most scrupulous care, in order that their truth may be probed.’ On the day in question she had seen a soul in bodily form — ‘tender and lucid and of the colour of air’ — which is why Tertullian cites her in a treatise on the soul. It is one of the very few descriptions of a Montanist gathering from a participant rather than an enemy, and it does not look like the frenzy the opponents described: a woman with a recognized gift, a congregation that valued it, and elders who tested every word before receiving it.

Zoticus Tries to Silence Maximilla’s Spirit

The anonymous writer against the Montanists whom Eusebius quotes was working within living memory of the movement’s beginnings, and he tells two stories about Maximilla, the prophetess who outlived Montanus and Priscilla. In the first, the bishops Zoticus of Comana and Julian of Apamea came to ‘test and refute the spirit that spoke in her’. They were prevented — ‘hindered by those who were with her’, Themiso and the others — and never got to conduct their examination. The writer gives the Montanists’ obstruction as proof of bad faith; from the other side it reads as a community protecting its prophet from men who had come to treat her as possessed. The second story is her oracle. Maximilla had said, in words Epiphanius later quotes: ‘After me there will be no prophet any more, but the end.’ The writer, recalling her predictions of wars and anarchy, counts the years — more than thirteen since her death — during which there had been neither local nor general war, and Christians had enjoyed a stretch of peace, and asks how the prophecy could be true. He also records, more darkly, the report that Montanus and Maximilla both hanged themselves, and admits in the same breath that he does not know whether it is true. The New Prophecy survived both the exorcists and the failed date by two centuries; what it could not survive was becoming, in the eyes of the wider church, a movement whose promised end had not arrived.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced

  • influenced Tertullian Documented — Tertullian of Carthage took up the New Prophecy's claims. He quotes a saying of the Paraclete given 'by the mouth of the prophetess Prisca'. In Against Praxeas he writes that his side withdrew from the 'carnally-minded' over the Paraclete.

Opposed By

  • argued against Post-Apostolic Household-Church Christianity Documented Major — Eusebius of Caesarea quotes a writer of about 192 whose name is lost. He says the faithful of Asia met many times to test what the prophets of Phrygia taught. They found it to be heresy, and cut those who held it off from communion. Apolinarius, bishop of Hierapolis, also wrote against the Phrygians.Lasting result: Meetings of the faithful across Asia judged the New Prophecy to be heresy. Its followers were thrown out of the church and barred from communion.
  • argued against Imperial and Juridical Christianity Documented Major — Constantine sent an edict to the heretics, kept in Eusebius of Caesarea's Life of Constantine. It names 'ye who are called Cataphrygians', a common name for the Montanists. It forbids their meetings. It takes away the houses where they met.Lasting result: Constantine's law said the Montanists could not meet, not even in a private house. It urged them to join the catholic Church instead.
  • argued against The Byzantine Imperial Church (Justinianic) Documented Major — Procopius was a hostile witness to Justinian. He says the emperor told groups such as the Montanists to give up their beliefs. He says the Montanists of Phrygia shut themselves in their churches. Then they set them on fire. A law of Justinian also names them.Lasting result: Justinian ordered the Montanists to give up their faith, Procopius says. He threatened those who held out with losing the right to leave property to their families.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In a village on the edge of Phrygia, in the second century, a man named Montanus began prophesying that the Holy Spirit was speaking through him. Two women, Priscilla and Maximilla, prophesied alongside him, and their followers came to expect the New Jerusalem to descend on the small town of Pepuza.

Legacy

No church descends from the New Prophecy. In the sixth century, under Justinian, the empire moved to crush it. Procopius says the Montanists of Phrygia shut themselves in their churches and set them alight. Its own writings were destroyed. What remains is a handful of quoted oracles. There are also some graves in Phrygia with inscriptions, but whether those are Montanist is itself argued over. That is the honest difficulty: nearly all the evidence comes through the three named prophets and their enemies. Whether any of the community's own words remain is not yet settled. What outlasted it are the questions it forced. Did prophecy go on after the apostles? Who may authorize it? How does a church tell genuine inspiration from enthusiasm? Critics of later revivals have reached for the comparison. In 1743 Charles Chauncy likened the cries and falling down of the Great Awakening to the Montanists of old. No line of descent links any later movement to it.

Open Questions

  • When it began. The elder Eusebius copied says it began when Gratus was proconsul of Asia, and gives no year. Epiphanius says about the nineteenth year of Antoninus Pius. Apollonius wrote in the fortieth year after Montanus began. Eusebius sets a letter from the churches of Gaul about the followers of Montanus in the time of the martyrs of Lyons, but gives none of its judgment.
  • What they believed about God. Tertullian says the quarrel was not over God or Christ. Hippolytus, writing at Rome, allows that they confess God the Father and Creator as the church does, and receive what the Gospel says of Christ, but says some of them followed Noetus in making the Father the Son. Jerome, of the Montanists of his own day, says they force the Trinity into one person. Epiphanius says they think as the catholic church does of Father, Son and Holy Spirit, then quotes oracles of Montanus in which God speaks in the first person, among them the oracle of the lyre, and reads them as claims to be God.
  • Who the Paraclete spoke through. Eusebius says they boasted that Montanus was the Paraclete, and Basil that they gave the title to Montanus and Priscilla. Tertullian says the Paraclete spoke through the prophetess Prisca. The later Against All Heresies printed with Tertullian's works says the Paraclete spoke in Montanus.
  • Apollonius's charges. He says Montanus taught that marriages be dissolved, made laws for fasting, appointed collectors of money, took gifts as offerings and paid his preachers; that the prophetesses left their husbands; and that a prophetess took gold, silver and costly clothes. He says Themiso bought his way out of prison, and wrote a letter to the churches as if he were an apostle, and that Alexander, honored by them as a martyr, had been condemned as a robber. These are an opponent's charges.
  • Other bishops who tried. A letter of Serapion of Antioch carries the signature of a bishop of Debeltum in Thrace, who swears that Sotas of Anchialus wanted to cast the demon out of Priscilla and was not allowed.
  • Martyrs. The elder says they claimed many martyrs, and that at Apamea on the Maeander martyrs from the church would not die in fellowship with theirs.
  • Rome. Only Tertullian tells it: a bishop of Rome, whom he does not name, acknowledged the prophetic gifts of Montanus, Prisca and Maximilla and sent his letter of peace to the churches of Asia and Phrygia, a letter recognizing them as in fellowship with his own. A man newly come from Asia then pressed false charges and the authority of the bishop's predecessors, and the bishop recalled it.
  • Oracles Tertullian keeps. Through Prisca, he says, the Spirit taught that purity brings visions and voices. An oracle of the Spirit urged believers to die as martyrs, not on beds or in fevers. He also says that, as their prophecy foretold, a city hung in the sky over Judaea every morning for forty days, a sign of the heavenly Jerusalem: a wonder in his own voice.
  • Maximilla's oracle of the end. Epiphanius gives it, writing about two centuries later. Holl's print of his Greek reads no prophet after her; Oehler's print reads no prophetess.
  • Which prophetess saw Christ at Pepuza. Epiphanius says it was Quintilla or Priscilla, and he cannot say which.
  • Women among them. Epiphanius says that in a branch of his own day, the Quintillians, also called Pepuzians, women were bishops and presbyters, and seven virgins dressed in white came into their church with lamps to prophesy. He says their women were called prophetesses, though he is not sure whether this was among them or among the Phrygians. He says they were called Artotyrites for setting bread and cheese in their mysteries.
  • A charge of child murder. Cyril of Jerusalem says Montanus killed small children for their mysteries. Jerome prefers not to believe it, and Cyril himself says the same charge was laid on all Christians in time of persecution.
  • After the councils of Asia. Constantine's law took away their meeting houses and forbade them to meet, even in private. Bishops gathered at Iconium, Firmilian says, refused their baptism, and Basil later did the same. Jerome says they set the patriarchs of Pepuza first, then stewards, and bishops third, kept three Lents a year, and held a second marriage to be adultery. Sozomen says that in Phrygia and the lands near it they had been many since Montanus and still were in his day, and that they kept Easter by the sun.
  • Their own books. Hippolytus says they had countless books. Jerome says Tertullian wrote seven books On Ecstasy, the last against Apollonius. None of these is held.
  • The end in Phrygia. Procopius, hostile to Justinian, says Justinian also threatened those who held out with being unable to leave their property to their wives and children, and Justinian's law names the Montanists. Michael the Syrian, writing some six hundred years later, says John of Asia burned their house at Pepuza on the emperor's order, with bones found there in a marble chest marked as those of Montanus and his women.

On the Nicene Creed

What the New Prophecy confessed about God, Christ and the Spirit is not, Tertullian says, what its opponents objected to — by his account, and Hippolytus's for most of them, it held the same doctrine as the churches that condemned it. The dispute was about authority and discipline: whether new prophecy could bind the church, and how strict its common life should be.
