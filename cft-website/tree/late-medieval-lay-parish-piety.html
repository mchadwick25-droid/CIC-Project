Late-Medieval Lay Parish Piety

England, c. 1400 to 1517 — the religion of people who left no writings, reconstructed from what they paid for, prayed from, and left behind in their wills.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:11

Read along

For most people in late-medieval England, Christianity was the parish. It meant the mass seen from the nave, the yearly round of feasts and processions, and the guild you joined. It meant the light you paid to keep burning before an image, and the church ale that funded the roof. It was not passive. Churchwardens were ordinary laymen, elected and accountable to their neighbors. They collected and spent the money. Their accounts show parishes rebuilding naves, raising towers, buying vestments and hiring carvers on their own initiative. Books of hours, soon printed by the thousand, put the same daily prayers into the hands of any household that could afford them. Sermon collections like John Mirk's Festial show roughly what people in the pews were hearing. Almost none of these people wrote about what they believed. What survives instead is the record of what they did with their money and their time. It is a different kind of evidence, and in some ways a more honest one.

Documented Stories

Alice Chester Pays for the Rood Loft

All Saints’ in Bristol kept, from the 1460s, a Church Book in which the churchwardens recorded what the parish’s benefactors had given, ‘so that they should not be forgotten but be had in remembrance and prayed for’. The longest entry is for Alice Chester, widow of Henry Chester, a draper and ironmonger, who after his death in 1470 carried on his trade in iron, wine and cloth and, the book says, prospered. She had a new rood loft built across the chancel arch, with images of the Crucifixion, Mary and John, and twenty-two other figures, ‘carved and gilded’, at a cost the wardens record; she gave a new tabernacle for the image of Jesus, a gilt altarpiece for the high altar with the Passion carved on it, vestments, a silver-gilt cross, and a new front for the Lady altar; she paid for the church’s crane on the quay — the parish owned property — to be rebuilt, ‘for the common profit of the town’; and she left money for a priest to sing for her soul and her husband’s. The book records that she did these things ‘in her lifetime, with her own hands’, so that the parish would see them done. The parish’s account of what she paid for — a rood loft to look up at during Mass, an altarpiece of the Passion, a crane — is the kind of evidence this Christian tradition leaves, and it is more candid than a creed.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

For most people in late-medieval England, Christianity was the parish. It was the mass seen from the nave and the guild joined. It was the light paid for before an image, and the church ale that funded the roof. Ordinary laymen elected as churchwardens managed the money. On their own initiative they rebuilt naves, raised towers and hired carvers. Cheap printed books of hours put the same daily prayers into any household that could afford them. Almost none of these people wrote about what they believed. What survives instead is the record of what they did with their money and their time.

Legacy

Much of it is still standing. In these decades the great parish churches of East Anglia, the Cotswolds and the West Country were built or rebuilt. Ordinary people paid for them. Their towers, painted screens and carved roofs are the largest things left anywhere of lay religion in the late Middle Ages. Most of the rest was taken apart on purpose. The English Reformation abolished the guilds and chantries. It whitewashed the paintings and broke up the images. So much of what is known of the old practice comes from accounts. They record first what was bought, and then how it was destroyed. Something like it was built again elsewhere. A generation later, the parish-centered faith of Carlo Borromeo's Milan ran on the same instincts under very different rules.

How Sure the Record Is

Rich but oblique: churchwardens' accounts, wills, primers - practice more than interior voice

  • Churchwardens' accounts: Morebath (ed. Binney, 1904), All Saints' Bristol (ed. Burgess), Bassingbourn and kin (primary)
  • Books of hours and primers: The Prymer or lay folks' prayer book (ed. Littlehales, EETS, 1895-97) and the mass-market horae (primary) — The ordinary household's actual prayer equipment, surviving in thousands of worn copies.
  • Wills and gild records: bequests for obits, lights, and fabric; the 1389 gild returns; The Lay Folks Mass Book (EETS) (primary) — Practice documented at the moment of death and in fraternity - the two points where ordinary people wrote themselves down.
  • John Mirk, Festial (ed. Powell, EETS, 2009-11) (primary) — The era's most-copied English sermon collection, written for parish priests preaching to lay congregations - the closest surviving proxy for what the pew actually heard.
  • Eamon Duffy, The Stripping of the Altars: Traditional Religion in England, c.1400-c.1580 (Yale, 1992; 2nd ed. 2005), with The Voices of Morebath (2001) (secondary) — Duffy's revisionism is a position in a live historiographical argument, not a settled conclusion.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 4, ch. 15 'Sacramental life' (Rubin); cf. ch. 21 'Saints and pilgrimages' (Vauchez) (reference) — The parish's sacramental round is this chapter's ground. Its coverage ends in 1517, before this practice's continuation into Tridentine parish renewal, which a later volume would cover.
