The Age of Monks and Empires · 5th c. · Armenia

Elishe

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Armenian Christianity after Avarayr.

Known for
Armenian historian whose History of Vartan is the main account of the rising against the Persian king's religion that ended at Avarayr in 451.
Also called
Eghishe, Elisaeus, Élisée Vartabed
Dates
Fifth century. Charles Neumann places him among the bishops at Artashat in 450; Victor Langlois says some writers place his death in 480. Langlois says his birthplace and date are unknown
Place
Armenia; by Neumann's and Langlois's accounts, the mountain of Mog and the shore of Lake Van
Role
Historian; vardapet, and by Neumann's account bishop of the Amadunians

About This Person

A priest named David, of the Mamikonian house, asked Elishe for a history of the Armenian war. The dedication says its seven chapters tell the brave deeds of the battle and the misery that followed. In the first chapter he writes, "Truly, not of our own free will, do we describe, in lamenting tones, all the miseries of which we ourselves have been eye-witnesses."

Elishe wrote of the rising Vardan Mamikonian led, which ended at Avarayr in 451. Victor Langlois, his French editor, says his birthplace and date are unknown. Charles Neumann, his English translator, calls him a disciple of Sahak the Great and Mesrop Mashtots, and Vardan's secretary. Langlois reports that view, and another, that he served in Vardan's army as a young man.

Neumann also makes him bishop of the Amadunians. Elishe's own list of the bishops who met at Artashat names an Elishe with that title. Neumann takes that bishop for the author; Langlois says only that some writers think so.

The History tells how the Persian court pressed the religion of the Magi on Armenia. Elishe copies an edict of Mihrnerseh, the Persian minister, and the bishops' answer. It reads, "From this belief no one can move us, neither angels nor men—neither fire nor sword, nor water."

His last years are told two ways. Neumann cites Thomas Ardsruni, a ninth-century historian, for a retreat by Lake Van and a death at a great age. Langlois, following his biographers, has him live as a hermit in caves, first on the mountain of Mog and then by the lake. He writes that hardship shortened his life. Langlois adds that some writers place his death in 480.

Langlois says Elishe resisted Greek influence more than any of his fellow students. Armenians count the History among the finest works of their classical literature, he adds.

In the dedication to David, Elishe wrote, "Concord is the mother of good; and Dissension, the nurse of evil."

Key Works

  • History of Vartan and of the Battle of the Armenians: seven chapters on the Persian pressure to accept the religion of the Magi, the Armenian league, the battle of Avarayr and the misery that followed, written at the request of the priest David of the Mamikonian house
  • Discourse to the Solitaries: by Langlois's account, written to fellow students who had become monks, urging them to follow the first hermits and to pray in their country's sorrows; not in Neumann's or Langlois's translation

Legacy

What traces to them

  • His History holds the copied edict of the Persian minister Mihrnerseh and the bishops' answer from Artashat.
  • Langlois: the History is valued for its account of mid-fifth-century Armenia and for details of Zoroastrian religion.

How they're remembered

  • Thomas Ardsruni, a ninth-century historian, as a note in Langlois's edition quotes him: the History of the holy Vartanians "rédigée par le bienheureux prêtre Elisée".
  • Langlois counts him among the fifth-century scholars who opposed Greek influence on the Armenian language, and says his example was not followed.

What Is Uncertain

  • His life rests on Charles Neumann's preface and on the biographers Victor Langlois follows, not on the History, which says only that he saw what he describes. Neumann calls him a disciple of Sahak the Great and Mesrop Mashtots and Vardan Mamikonian's secretary; Langlois reports that view and another, that he served in Vardan's army as a young man.
  • That he is the Elishe, bishop of the Amadunians, in his own list of the bishops at Artashat is Neumann's view. Langlois says only that some writers hold it.
  • His last years are told two ways. Neumann, citing Thomas Ardsruni, has him retire by Lake Van and die at a great age. Langlois has him live as a hermit in two caves and says hardship shortened his life.
  • The death year 480 is one Langlois gives from "quelques écrivains", unnamed.
  • Langlois reports Thomas Ardsruni's story that a Nestorian named Barsouma borrowed the History and struck out every mention of the Ardzruni house, so that it was left unfinished. Langlois gives no other witness for it.
  • Neumann holds that the editors joined to the History a separate work on the sufferings of the priests. Langlois thinks the supplement is Elishe's own, against critics who give it to a continuator.
