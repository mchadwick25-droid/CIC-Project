Cyrillo-Methodian Slavic Christianity

Moravia to Ohrid, c. 863 to 916 — two brothers who made an alphabet so that a people could hear the gospel in the language they actually spoke.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:16

Read along

In 863 two brothers from Thessaloniki — Constantine, better known by his monastic name Cyril, and Methodius — were sent from Constantinople to Moravia at its ruler's request, and did something unusual for their day: rather than teach in Greek or Latin, they devised an alphabet for the Slavic language and translated the Scriptures and the liturgy into it. The idea was fought hard, by clergy who held that God could properly be worshipped only in Hebrew, Greek and Latin, and the brothers traveled to Rome to argue the case, where the pope approved the Slavonic books. After Methodius died in 885 their disciples were driven out of Moravia and carried the work south, where Clement and Naum built schools at Ohrid and Preslav that turned it into a permanent literature. And where the alphabet now called Cyrillic took the shape it still has. A monk writing under the name Hrabar defended the new letters in a short, sharp treatise: a rare case of a tradition arguing publicly, in its own words, that its language was fit for worship.

Documented Stories

The Argument in Venice About Three Languages

The Life of Constantine, written by a disciple within a few years of his death in 869, gives the mission’s own account of the objection it faced. Constantine and Methodius had been in Moravia for more than three years teaching the Slavs to read the Gospel and pray the liturgy in their own tongue, and were traveling to Rome with disciples to be ordained when they stopped at Venice. There, the Life says, ‘bishops, priests and monks gathered against him like ravens against a falcon’ and put the case: only three languages were fit for the worship of God, the three in which Pilate had written the inscription over the cross. Constantine answered, the Life records, with a question — does not God send rain on all alike, and does the sun not shine on all? And he quoted Paul at length — ‘I would rather speak five words with my understanding than ten thousand in a tongue’ — and Psalm 116, ‘Praise the Lord, all nations’. In Rome, carrying the relics of Clement that he had found in the Crimea, he was received by Pope Hadrian II, who laid the Slavonic books on the altar of St Mary Major and had the liturgy sung in Slavonic in four Roman churches. Constantine fell ill there, took the monastic name Cyril, and died in February 869, asking his brother not to abandon the work for the sake of their mountain monastery at home.

Methodius in the Bavarian Prison

Hadrian II had made Methodius archbishop of Pannonia and Moravia in 869, with the pope’s letter permitting the Slavonic liturgy provided the Epistle and Gospel were read first in Latin. The bishops of Bavaria — Salzburg, Passau, Freising — regarded Moravia and Pannonia as their mission territory, and when the Moravian prince Rastislav was overthrown by his nephew with Frankish help in 870, they took their chance. Methodius was seized, brought before a synod at Regensburg in the presence of King Louis the German, and tried. The Life of Methodius gives fragments of the exchange: told he was teaching in their territory, he answered that if he had thought it theirs he would have kept away, but it was St Peter’s; when the bishops became heated, he told them they were sweating like men arguing round a stove. Bishop Hermanrich of Passau struck at him with a whip. He was imprisoned in a monastery in Swabia — probably Ellwangen — in the open in winter, and held for two and a half years. Pope John VIII’s letters survive, and they are furious: to Hermanrich, that his savagery ‘would bring tears to a stone’, and suspension from office; to Louis, that Pannonia belonged to Rome; to the papal legate, orders to free Methodius and bring him back. He was restored, and worked in Moravia for twelve more years, translating, the Life says, most of the Old Testament with two shorthand priests in eight months.

Influenced

  • influenced Kievan Rus' Christianity Inferential-Thin — Kievan Rus' took its Christian books and its letters from the Cyril and Methodius mission. Mouravieff, a Russian church historian writing in 1838, says the brothers' Slavonic Bible later helped win the Russians to the faith. Herberstein retold the Rus' annals in 1549. He says the Rus' began to write down their past once the Slavonic letters came. This rests on these later accounts and one more from 1814. No modern study of it has been consulted.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 863 the brothers Cyril and Methodius were sent from Constantinople to Moravia and, rather than teach in Greek or Latin, devised an alphabet for the Slavic language and translated Scripture and the liturgy into it, over objections from clergy who held that God could only properly be worshipped in Hebrew, Greek and Latin. After Methodius died and their disciples were driven out of Moravia, Clement of Ohrid and Naum carried the work south to Ohrid and Preslav, and a monk named Hrabar wrote a treatise defending the new Slavonic letters as fit for worship.

Legacy

Almost everything that came after it in the Slavic Orthodox world begins here. Its Slavonic books and letters passed to Kievan Rus', and on to the Russian, Ukrainian, Serbian and Bulgarian churches. Church Slavonic is still the language of worship in much of the Orthodox world. The Cyrillic alphabet is named for one brother, though their followers gave it its shape, and hundreds of millions of people use it today. Cyril and Methodius are honored as saints in both East and West. In 1980 the Catholic Church named them co-patrons of Europe.
