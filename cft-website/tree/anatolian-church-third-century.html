The Early Church Era · 3rd to early 4th c. · Neocaesarea in Pontus, Olympus in Lycia

Neocaesarea · Olympus, today in Turkey

The Anatolian Church in the Third Century

In Pontus, Gregory, who had studied with Origen, was a bishop and wrote rules for a church after a raid by the Goths. In Lycia, Methodius, bishop of Olympus, wrote the Banquet, where ten virgins praise chastity.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:34

Read along

Gregory of Neocaesarea went south to study law and came back a bishop. Instead of law, he had fallen in with Origen at Caesarea and stayed five years. A farewell address bears his name. It is one of the warmest pictures of a teacher that anyone wrote in antiquity, though some scholars doubt that he wrote it. What he did in Pontus after that is mostly legend. One story says he found seventeen Christians in the city and left seventeen pagans. But that story comes from a century after his time. One document survives, though, that is not legend at all. When Gothic raiders came through in the 250s, Gregory wrote a canonical letter, a letter of church rules. It worked out what to do about Christians who had looted alongside the raiders, or informed, or kept what the raiders left. It shows a bishop dealing with a wrecked province, case by case. A generation later and further south, Methodius was bishop of Olympus in Lycia. He wrote the Banquet of the Ten Virgins, a Christian rewriting of Plato's Symposium. In it, ten women take turns praising chastity. He also became one of the first substantial critics of Origen, whose most devoted pupil had been Gregory. Methodius died in the persecution around 311.

Documented Stories

After the Raid: Gregory of Neocaesarea’s Case Book

When the Goths and Borani came through Pontus in the 250s, they left a province in which some Christians had been captured, some had collaborated, and some had helped themselves. Gregory of Neocaesarea’s canonical letter to a neighboring bishop works through the cases one by one, and its interest lies in the temper of its judgments. Those who had eaten food the barbarians gave them had not sinned, since the raiders did not sacrifice to idols. Women taken captive and raped were not to be treated as defiled unless their earlier life gave reason to think they had consented — and Gregory is uneasy even about that qualification. But then the harder cases. Some Christians had ‘forgotten that they were men of Pontus and Christians’ and joined the barbarians in killing and in pointing out houses and roads; these were to be excluded until a council could decide. Some had found property the raiders dropped and kept it, or ‘discovered’ goods in other men’s houses and claimed them as spoil. Some, ‘what is most grievous of all’, had seized their own neighbors who escaped from the enemy and held them for ransom. For these Gregory prescribes the graded penance the letter is famous for — weeping outside the church door, hearing, kneeling, standing. It is not heroic material. It is a record of a church discovering, after its first catastrophe, how many of its members would behave like everyone else, and building a discipline of repentance rather than expulsion.

Connections

In Its Own Record

This is the ground the Cappadocian tradition grows from, and it knows it. Basil of Caesarea's grandmother Macrina taught him the words of Gregory Thaumaturgus. Basil points to Gregory's practice as a precedent. Origen's reach runs through it both ways. Gregory was his devoted pupil. Methodius of Olympus was one of his first serious critics.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Gregory of Neocaesarea was a devoted pupil of Origen. In the 250s, some Christians looted with Gothic raiders. Gregory wrote a canonical letter, a letter of rules for the church, about them. It worked out what to do with them, case by case. Methodius of Olympus came a generation later. He turned Plato's Symposium into a Christian dialogue in which ten women take turns to praise chastity. He was also one of the first serious critics of Origen. Then he died in the persecution around 311.

Legacy

The Cappadocian tradition says plainly that it comes from this ground. When Basil of Caesarea wants to show that something is old, he cites Gregory of Neocaesarea's practice as precedent. Gregory's Canonical Epistle entered the standard books of church law in the East, and it is still there. And Methodius of Olympus's Banquet is an early landmark of Christian writing on virginity. It is a whole dialogue in its praise. Tertullian and Cyprian had already written about virgins. Jerome knew the Banquet by name. But how much he, Ambrose or Gregory of Nyssa drew from it is hard to show.

Open Questions

  • Who made Gregory a bishop, and when. Eusebius says both brothers were made bishops in Pontus after five years with Origen. Jerome says Origen sent them home to their mother. Neither gives a year.
  • The raid's year. The Canonical Letter names the Boradi and Goths, and gives no year. The old sources held here do not date it.
  • The eleventh canon. It differs from the ten before it: it answers no case from the raid, and sets out the four stages of a penitent. The old sources held here do not say who wrote it.
  • The seventeen. Basil says seventeen Christians were handed over to Gregory, and Gregory of Nyssa that no more than seventeen had received the faith. Both wrote in the 4th century.
  • Gregory's wonders. Jerome reports what was said of his signs and wonders. Basil tells of rivers turned and a lake dried. Both wrote in the 4th century.
  • Methodius's death. Jerome says he was martyred at Chalcis in Greece, at the end of the recent persecution or, as others say, under Decius and Valerian.
  • Methodius and Origen. Jerome names two books of his against Origen. Socrates, who defends Origen, says Methodius later praised him in a dialogue named Xenon.

How Sure the Record Is

Modest and mixed. Gregory of Neocaesarea's Panegyric to Origen and his Canonical Epistle survive and both are first-hand; Methodius of Olympus's Banquet survives whole. But a substantial part of what circulates under both names is marked dubious or spurious by scholars, and the Life of Gregory that carries most of the stories about him was written a century later by Gregory of Nyssa.
