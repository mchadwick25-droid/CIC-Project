Ossius of Cordoba

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Imperial and Juridical Christianity.

Known for
Bishop of Cordoba and a confessor in the persecution under Maximian, a leading bishop at Nicaea and Sardica, who told the emperor Constantius to keep out of church affairs and in great age was forced to yield at Sirmium.
Also called
Hosius of Cordova, Osius
Dates
A confessor under Maximian, by his own letter; bishop for "more than sixty years" and "now a hundred years old" when held at Sirmium, by Athanasius of Alexandria, which Archibald Robertson's notes to Athanasius place in 357. No ancient source used here gives the year of his death. Henry Percival reports Cave's view that his episcopate ended in 361, and Hefele's that he was dead before the Synod of Gangra
Place
Cordoba in Spain; Alexandria; Nicaea; Sardica; Sirmium
Role
Bishop and confessor

About This Person

Ossius, bishop of Cordoba in Spain, wrote to the emperor Constantius, a son of Constantine. "I was a Confessor at the first, when a persecution arose in the time of your grandfather Maximian," he began. The emperor had pressed him to sign against Athanasius of Alexandria. Ossius told him to "remember that you are a mortal man." "Intrude not yourself into Ecclesiastical matters," he wrote. God had given the emperor his kingdom, and the bishops the affairs of the church.

The letter survives only in Athanasius's History of the Arians. It defends Athanasius, who praises Ossius all through, so his account takes a side. He says Ossius had been a bishop for "more than sixty years."

Socrates of Constantinople says Constantine sent Ossius with a letter to Alexander of Alexandria and Arius. The Latin lists of those who signed at the Council of Nicaea put "Osius" first. Theodoret of Cyrrhus calls him the "most highly distinguished" bishop there. Whether he led that council is disputed. Athanasius asks when a council was ever held "in which he did not take the lead." Henry Percival, who edited the council records, finds no proof that he was in the chair.

At the council of Sardica, about 343 by Percival's date, Theodoret says he held "the first place." Its canons open with his words against bishops who move from a small city to a larger one. The eastern bishops met apart and deposed him, says Sozomen, a church historian of the next century.

Athanasius says Constantius held him for a whole year at Sirmium. Ossius was "now a hundred years old." At last, broken by suffering, he agreed to share communion with the bishops his letter had warned against. Even so, he would not sign against Athanasius. In Socrates's telling, he was beaten and tortured until he signed their creed.

As death drew near, Athanasius says, "he bore witness to the force which had been used towards him, and anathematized the Arian heresy."

Key Works

  • Letter to the emperor Constantius: refuses to sign against Athanasius and tells the emperor not to meddle in church affairs; kept by Athanasius in his History of the Arians
  • Canons of Sardica: proposals made in his name at the council, beginning with a ban on bishops moving from a small city to a larger one

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The Latin subscription lists of Nicaea, in the edition of Gelzer, Hilgenfeld and Cuntz, open with Osius, bishop of Cordoba.
  • His proposals stand among the Canons of Sardica, many of them introduced "Bishop Hosius said" in Henry Percival's English.

How they're remembered

  • Athanasius of Alexandria: "the Abraham-like old man, Hosius, truly so called".
  • Theodoret of Cyrrhus: he "was the most highly distinguished of all those who assembled at the council of Nicæa; he also obtained the first place among those convened at Sardica".
  • Sozomen: the eastern bishops at Sardica deposed "Hosius, the confessor", partly as "the friend of Paulinus and Eustathius" of Antioch.

At Major Councils

  • Council of Nicaea, 325: First name on the Latin lists; whether he presided is disputed
  • Council of Sardica, 343: Led the Western bishops and proposed the canons; deposed by the Eastern bishops

What Is Uncertain

  • His letter and the story of his lapse come mainly through Athanasius, whom the letter defends and who praises him, so the account takes a side.
  • Whether he presided at Nicaea is disputed. Athanasius asks what council he did not lead. A note in Archibald Robertson's edition of Athanasius says he presided. Henry Percival says there is no evidence for it. Robertson says his name stands first on the extant list of signatures, "although the lists are bad witnesses".
  • His lapse is told two ways. Athanasius says that after a year's confinement at Sirmium he shared communion with Valens and Ursacius but would not sign against Athanasius. In his Defence of his Flight he speaks of "repeated stripes above measure", after which "he yielded to them for a time". Socrates says he was beaten until he signed a creed there; Sozomen says so too, "as it is reported". A. C. Zenos notes that Socrates confused the councils of Sirmium; Zenos dates the one of the lapse to 357.
  • Athanasius says that before his death he condemned the Arian heresy and named the force used on him. No other ancient source used here tells his death. Percival reports that Hefele thought him dead before the Synod of Gangra, while Cave ends his episcopate in 361 and Pagi thinks he may have been at Gangra in 358.
  • Athanasius also speaks of conspiracies against his kinsfolk. No source used here names them.
  • A note on the letter in Robertson's edition asks whether Athanasius translated it from Latin. The letter survives only in Athanasius's Greek, here in English.
