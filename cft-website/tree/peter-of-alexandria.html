Peter of Alexandria

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition.

Also called Peter the Martyr, Peter (Bishop and Martyr)

Peter was bishop of Alexandria through the persecution that began in 303. He set out how the church should take back Christians who had given way and offered sacrifice to the gods; one of his bishops, Melitius, broke from him, and in 311 he was beheaded.

About This Person

At the fourth Easter of the persecution, Peter, the bishop of Alexandria, wrote about Christians who had been jailed and tortured until they gave way and offered sacrifice to the gods. Some were already in a third year of mourning their fall. "They did not come to this by deliberate choice. They were betrayed by the weakness of the flesh, since they show the marks of Jesus in their bodies." The marks were the scars of their torture.

Peter led the churches of all Egypt and Libya through the persecution that began in 303. He set out how the church should take back the fallen, weighing what each had suffered and chosen. In those years one of his own bishops, Melitius, broke from him, and in 311 Peter was beheaded.

He had become bishop about 300, less than three years before the emperors ordered the churches pulled down and the Scriptures burned, and bishops of Egypt were thrown into prison.

Those who had given way asked to share communion again: a place in the prayers and in the body and blood of Christ. In that Easter writing, a sermon on repentance, Peter gave each of them a time of fasting and prayer, and its rules are known as his canons. The tortured would fast forty more days, the days Christ fasted, and those jailed without torture would wait a year more. For those who fell from fear alone, he pointed to the parable of the fig tree, spared one more year to bear fruit. Those who paid to be left alone, or left everything and fled, were not to be blamed. Clergy who gave themselves up to the persecutors of their own accord, fell, and then took up the struggle again could share communion, but could no longer serve in their office.

Four bishops of Egypt, writing from prison, rebuked Melitius, a bishop of Upper Egypt, in the south, for making priests and deacons in churches that were not his. By the law of their fathers, they wrote, no bishop could ordain outside his own church, so that those ordained were known and confusion avoided. He should have waited for the judgment of Peter, whom they called their great bishop and father.

An account kept with that letter says Melitius did not answer. After the four bishops were martyred, he went to Alexandria. The priests Peter had appointed to visit the churches were then in hiding, and Melitius set them aside and put men of his own in their place. Peter wrote to his people that Melitius was invading his church and trying to pull its priests from his authority. "Take heed of this. Do not share communion with him until I meet him with some wise and sensible men, and see what he has in mind."

Later writers tell the break otherwise. Athanasius, a later bishop of Alexandria and an enemy of the Melitians, says a council of bishops under Peter removed Melitius for many crimes, among them sacrificing to the gods. Epiphanius of Salamis, writing some sixty years later, says the two were in prison together and split over the fallen. Peter, he says, pleaded to receive them as they repented, while Melitius would wait until the persecution ended and would not restore fallen clergy to their rank.

His end came after an order from the emperors had been posted letting Christians worship again, which the eastern emperor Maximinus would not accept. Eusebius of Caesarea, who wrote at the time, gave it in these words: "He was seized for no cause at all, without warning, and suddenly and without reason, as if Maximinus had ordered it, he was beheaded." It was 311, the ninth year of the persecution, and many other bishops of Egypt died with him.

The split outlived him, and in 325 the bishops gathered at the Council of Nicaea let Melitius keep his city but took away his power to ordain. Athanasius remembered Peter as bishop and martyr. His rules were copied into the collections of church law, and in 692 a council set its seal on the canons of the fathers, naming among them those of Peter, archbishop of Alexandria and martyr.

    “Take heed of this. Do not share communion with him until I meet him with some wise and sensible men, and see what he has in mind.”
    As printed
    “take ye heed to this, and hold no communion with him, until I meet him in company with some wise and discreet men, and see what the designs are which he has thought upon”
    Peter of Alexandria, Letter to the Church at Alexandria, tr. James B. H. Hawkins

    “He was seized for no cause at all, without warning, and suddenly and without reason, as if Maximinus had ordered it, he was beheaded.”
    As printed

    Eusebius of Caesarea, Church History IX.6.2, tr. A. C. McGiffert

Key Works

  • The Canonical Epistle: his canons of 306 on receiving Christians who lapsed in the persecution, and on those who fled. He held that those who paid to be left in peace, or who withdrew to save their lives, are not to be blamed, and that those who rushed into the contest unbidden, though they ignored Christ's words about praying not to enter temptation, still share communion. Of those forced against their will to the pagan sacrifice, whose hands were burned, he wrote that they may be counted among the confessors, as the martyrs in prison had written to him about those in Libya
  • Letter to the Church at Alexandria: telling his people to hold no communion with Melitius
  • Fragments: On the Godhead, On the Advent of Our Saviour, On the Passover, and others

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • His canons on the lapsed passed into the collections of church law, with the commentaries of Theodore Balsamon and John Zonaras.
  • The Melitian church that broke away under him lasted beyond his death. The Council of Nicaea let Melitius keep only "a nominal dignity."
  • In 692 the Council in Trullo set its seal on the canons of Peter of Alexandria, with those of other fathers. A Greek book of church law, Nikodemos's Pedalion, prints them with notes and says that by that seal they take on, in a way, the force of a council of the whole church.

How they're remembered

  • Eusebius of Caesarea calls him "a divine example of a bishop" and "one of the most excellent teachers of the religion of Christ."
  • Athanasius of Alexandria calls him "the blessed Peter, Bishop and Martyr."
  • Socrates of Constantinople, retelling Rufinus, speaks of "the day in which the memory of the martyr and bishop Peter was celebrated" at Alexandria.

Open Questions

  • Athanasius, an enemy of the Melitians, says Peter's council removed Melitius for many crimes, among them sacrifice to idols. Socrates of Constantinople says the same; Theodoret says only that Peter convicted him of certain crimes and deposed him. Epiphanius of Salamis tells it another way. He says both men were in prison together and split over how to receive the fallen, Peter for mercy and Melitius for strictness, and that most of the prisoners took Melitius's side.
  • The letter of the four bishops and Peter's letter are known only in a Latin translation first printed by Scipio Maffei. Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius, sees nothing in the bishops' letter to disprove it.
  • Sozomen says Peter ordained Arius a deacon and later cast him out over the Melitians.
  • The Genuine Acts of Peter, a later Passion, tell of his last night, a vision about Arius and his death at the tomb of Mark.
  • No early source gives his birth, family or schooling. Later Coptic and Ethiopic lives give a family, and do not agree with each other.
  • McGiffert counts fourteen canons; the Ante-Nicene Fathers prints fifteen.
  • Peter dates his canons by his own words, "the fourth passover of the persecution". Nikodemos, in the Greek book of church law, gives 304.
  • The account kept with the four bishops' letter says that the priests Peter had appointed were in hiding. It does not say where Peter was.
  • Eusebius gives the ninth year of the persecution for Peter's death, and no day. The Genuine Acts of Peter give the day as 25 November.
  • Eusebius gives two statements about why Peter died. In Book 7 he says Peter held himself to a stricter discipline for the rest of his life, cared openly for the churches, and was beheaded on that account. In Book 9 he says Peter was seized for no cause and without warning, as if by Maximinus's order.
