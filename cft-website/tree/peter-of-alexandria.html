About This Person

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 4:17

Read along

At the fourth Easter of the persecution, the fourth year since it began, Peter, bishop of Alexandria, wrote about Christians tortured until they gave way. "They did not come to this by their own choice. The weakness of the flesh betrayed them. For they show the marks of Jesus in their bodies." He meant the scars of their torture.

Peter led the churches of all Egypt through the Great Persecution. In 306 he set rules for taking back those who had given way, weighing what each had suffered and chosen, and in 311 he was beheaded. During the persecution, too, one of his own bishops, Melitius, broke away, starting a rival church that outlived him. That church came before the first great council of the whole church, at Nicaea.

Peter became bishop around 300, and in 303 the emperors ordered the churches pulled down. Soon every Christian was told to sacrifice to the gods, burning incense as the officials demanded, and bishops of Egypt were jailed.

Those who had given way now asked for communion again, to share the Lord's Supper with the church. Peter set each case a time of fasting and prayer first. The tortured, some already mourning into a third year, needed forty more days, the days Christ fasted, while prison without torture meant a year more. Fear alone meant longer, like the gospel's barren fig tree, spared one more season to bear fruit. Those who tricked the officials, by walking past the altar, handing in a written statement or sending a pagan in their place, needed six months. Slaves forced by their masters needed a year, and the masters three. Fallen clergy kept communion, but were not to return to their office.

In those same years Melitius, a bishop in Upper Egypt, began making priests in other bishops' churches. In a letter of the time, four imprisoned bishops rebuked him, since no bishop may ordain outside his own church. An account kept with it says that after they died for the faith, he came to Alexandria, put out the men Peter had left there and ordained his own. Peter, probably in hiding, wrote to his people to hold no communion with Melitius until he could meet him with wise men. Athanasius, writing decades later as a bitter foe of the followers of Melitius, says Peter's council of bishops removed him for many crimes, among them sacrifice to the gods.

Epiphanius, writing seventy years on, says the two were in prison together and split over the fallen. In his telling, Melitius would take them back only after the persecution, and not as clergy, while Peter begged to receive them now. "Peter's case was for mercy and kindness. The case of Melitius and those with him was for truth and zeal." Yet a historian of the councils answered that Peter's own rules kept fallen clergy out of office, the very strictness Epiphanius gives Melitius. Historians still weigh the two.

The persecution eased, then returned, and in its ninth year, 311, it reached Peter. Eusebius, who lived through it, tells how. "He was seized for no cause, when no one expected it. All at once and with no reason given, as though on Maximinus's order, he was beheaded." Maximinus was the emperor, and many other bishops of Egypt, Eusebius adds, died with Peter.

His rules outlived the persecution. In 692 a council at Constantinople sealed them with the rules of other fathers, and the Greek books of church law kept them.

The split outlived him too. In 325 that council, at Nicaea, took up this same quarrel. Its letter left Melitius only the title of bishop, with no power to ordain, yet the rival church lived on.

Athanasius, who fought that rival church all his life, still called Peter blessed, a bishop and a martyr. And Alexandria kept the day of his memory.

Key Works

  • The Canonical Epistle: his canons of 306 on receiving Christians who lapsed in the persecution, and on those who fled. He held that those who paid to be left in peace, or who withdrew to save their lives, are not to be blamed, and that those who rushed into the contest unbidden, though they ignored Christ's words about praying not to enter temptation, still share communion. Of those forced against their will to the pagan sacrifice, whose hands were burned, he wrote that they may be counted among the confessors, as the martyrs in prison had written to him about those in Libya
  • Letter to the Church at Alexandria: telling his people to hold no communion with Melitius
  • Fragments: On the Godhead, On the Advent of Our Saviour, On the Passover, and others

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • His canons on the lapsed passed into the collections of church law, with the commentaries of Theodore Balsamon and John Zonaras.
  • The Melitian church that broke away under him lasted beyond his death. The Council of Nicaea let Melitius keep only "a nominal dignity."
  • In 692 the Council in Trullo set its seal on the canons of Peter of Alexandria, with those of other fathers. A Greek book of church law, Nikodemos's Pedalion, prints them with notes and says that by that seal they take on, in a way, the force of a council of the whole church.

How they're remembered

  • Eusebius of Caesarea calls him "a divine example of a bishop" and "one of the most excellent teachers of the religion of Christ."
  • Athanasius of Alexandria calls him "the blessed Peter, Bishop and Martyr."
  • Socrates of Constantinople, retelling Rufinus, speaks of "the day in which the memory of the martyr and bishop Peter was celebrated" at Alexandria.
  • Arthur Cleveland Coxe, the American editor of the Ante-Nicene Fathers, quotes Dupin's judgment that no canons of antiquity on the lapsed are "more judicious or more equitable."

Open Questions

  • Athanasius, an enemy of the Melitians, says Peter's council removed Melitius for many crimes, among them sacrifice to idols. Socrates of Constantinople says the same; Theodoret says only that Peter convicted him of certain crimes and deposed him. Epiphanius of Salamis tells it another way. He says both men were in prison together and split over how to receive the fallen, Peter for mercy and Melitius for strictness, and that most of the prisoners took Melitius's side.
  • Harold Idris Bell, who edited papyri of the Melitians in 1924, thinks the sacrifice charge "need not be taken very seriously", and that Epiphanius had good sources, probably bishops of Egypt banished to Palestine. He reads the two accounts as stages of one quarrel. Charles Joseph Hefele, historian of the councils, says the first letters show neither man was in prison when the split began, and that Epiphanius was mistaken on the main points.
  • The letter of the four bishops and Peter's letter are known only in a Latin translation first printed by Scipio Maffei. Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius, sees nothing in the bishops' letter to disprove it.
  • Sozomen says Peter ordained Arius a deacon and later cast him out over the Melitians. Sozomen's translator, Chester Hartranft, notes that no one else links Arius with the Melitians early. Archibald Robertson, editor of Athanasius, says such tales "deserve little credit".
  • The Genuine Acts of Peter, a later Passion, tell of his last night, a vision about Arius and his death at the tomb of Mark. McGiffert calls them "spurious and historically quite worthless."
  • No early source gives his birth, family or schooling. Later Coptic and Ethiopic lives give a family, and do not agree with each other.
  • The date of the split is given as 301 (Robertson), about 304 or 305 (Hefele) and probably 305 (Bell).
  • McGiffert counts fourteen canons; the Ante-Nicene Fathers prints fifteen.
  • The year of the canons is read two ways. Hawkins and McGiffert give 306, counting from Peter's own words, "the fourth passover of the persecution". Nikodemos, in the Greek book of church law, gives 304.
  • On the prison, Bell thinks Peter and Melitius were set free soon after the quarrel began, probably when the persecution paused.
  • That Peter himself was in hiding when he wrote to his people is Bell's and Hefele's reading. The account kept with the four bishops' letter says only that the priests he had appointed were in hiding.
  • Eusebius gives the ninth year of the persecution for Peter's death. McGiffert reckons that year from February 311 to February 312 and puts the death not long before the spring of 312. Hawkins and Bell give 311, and the day, 25 November, comes from the Egyptian church's later reckoning.
