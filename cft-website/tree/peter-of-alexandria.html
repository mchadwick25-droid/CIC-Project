About This Person

Four Egyptian bishops were in prison at Alexandria during the Great Persecution. Word reached them that a fellow bishop, Meletius of Lycopolis, was making clergy "in parishes having no manner of connection with thee." They wrote to him. He had ignored, they said, "the honour of our great bishop and father, Peter." An old account kept with the letter says Meletius never answered. When the four had died as martyrs, he went to Alexandria. Peter was in hiding. Meletius set aside the priests Peter had left to visit the needy, and named men of his own. Peter wrote to his people to "hold no communion with him, until I meet him in company with some wise and discreet men."

Peter of Alexandria followed Theonas as bishop of the city, about the year 300. He led the church less than three years before the persecution, says Eusebius of Caesarea. Eusebius calls him "a divine example of a bishop," for his life and his study of the Scriptures.

In 306, at "the fourth passover of the persecution," Peter wrote rules for Christians who had given way. Those who gave in only after long torture were to fast forty more days, and then be taken back. He would not let anyone blame those who had fled "for the safety of their life."

Athanasius of Alexandria, a later bishop, was an enemy of the Meletians. He says Peter's council removed Meletius for many crimes, "and among the rest of offering sacrifice to idols." Epiphanius of Salamis tells it another way. He says both men were in prison together. They split over the fallen. Peter asked for mercy, and Meletius for strictness. Harold Idris Bell, who edited papyri of the Meletians in 1924, thinks the sacrifice charge "need not be taken very seriously." Charles Joseph Hefele, historian of the councils, says the first letters show neither man was then in prison.

In the ninth year of the persecution, Eusebius says, Peter was seized "for no cause and quite unexpectedly" and beheaded. Many bishops of Egypt died with him. Bell dates it to 25 November 311. A later Passion tells of his last night, but Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius, calls it "spurious and historically quite worthless."

The split outlived him. In 325 the Council of Nicaea let Meletius keep only "a nominal dignity." Athanasius dated the Meletians "from the times of the blessed Peter, Bishop and Martyr."

Key Works

  • The Canonical Epistle: his canons of 306 on receiving Christians who lapsed in the persecution, and on those who fled
  • Letter to the Church at Alexandria: telling his people to hold no communion with Meletius
  • Fragments: On the Godhead, On the Advent of Our Saviour, On the Passover, and others

Legacy

What traces to them

  • His canons on the lapsed passed into the collections of church law, with the commentaries of Theodore Balsamon and John Zonaras.
  • The Meletian church that broke away under him lasted beyond his death. The Council of Nicaea let Meletius keep only "a nominal dignity."

How they're remembered

  • Eusebius of Caesarea calls him "a divine example of a bishop" and "one of the most excellent teachers of the religion of Christ."
  • Athanasius of Alexandria calls him "the blessed Peter, Bishop and Martyr."
  • Socrates of Constantinople, retelling Rufinus, speaks of "the day in which the memory of the martyr and bishop Peter was celebrated" at Alexandria.
  • Arthur Cleveland Coxe, the American editor of the Ante-Nicene Fathers, quotes Dupin's judgment that no canons of antiquity on the lapsed are "more judicious or more equitable."

What Is Uncertain

  • Athanasius, an enemy of the Meletians, says Peter's council removed Meletius for many crimes, among them sacrifice to idols. Socrates of Constantinople and Theodoret say the same. Epiphanius of Salamis tells it another way. He says both men were in prison together and split over how to receive the fallen, Peter for mercy and Meletius for strictness, and that most of the prisoners took Meletius's side.
  • Harold Idris Bell, who edited papyri of the Meletians in 1924, thinks the sacrifice charge "need not be taken very seriously", and that Epiphanius had good sources, probably bishops of Egypt banished to Palestine. He reads the two accounts as stages of one quarrel. Charles Joseph Hefele, historian of the councils, says the first letters show neither man was in prison when the split began, and that Epiphanius was mistaken on the main points.
  • The letter of the four bishops and Peter's letter are known only in a Latin translation first printed by Scipio Maffei. Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius, sees nothing in the bishops' letter to disprove it.
  • Sozomen says Peter ordained Arius a deacon and later cast him out over the Meletians. Sozomen's translator, Chester Hartranft, notes that no one else links Arius with the Meletians early. Archibald Robertson, editor of Athanasius, says such tales "deserve little credit".
  • The Genuine Acts of Peter, a later Passion, tell of his last night, a vision about Arius and his death at the tomb of Mark. McGiffert calls them "spurious and historically quite worthless."
  • No early source gives his birth, family or schooling. Later Coptic and Ethiopic lives give a family, and do not agree with each other.
  • The date of the split is given as 301 (Robertson), about 304 or 305 (Hefele) and probably 305 (Bell).
  • McGiffert counts fourteen canons; the Ante-Nicene Fathers prints fifteen.
