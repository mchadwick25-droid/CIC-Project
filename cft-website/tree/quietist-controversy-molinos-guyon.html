The Enlightenment & Awakening Era · 1675-1699 · Spain, France

Quietist Controversy (Molinos, Guyon)

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In 1675 a Spanish priest living in Rome, Miguel de Molinos, published The Spiritual Guide, teaching a prayer of total interior passivity: stop striving, stop even asking, and let God act. It was hugely popular until it was not; Molinos was arrested in 1685 and sixty-eight propositions drawn from his work were condemned in 1687. The quarrel then moved to France, around Jeanne Guyon, a widow whose Short and Easy Method of Prayer taught something similar, and who found a defender in Fénelon, archbishop of Cambrai, and an implacable opponent in Bossuet, bishop of Meaux. Guyon spent years in confinement, including in the Bastille; Fénelon's defense of her was condemned by Rome in 1699, and he read out his own submission from his own pulpit.

In Its Own Record

Influenced

  • German Pietism - Spener, Francke, Halle Documented — In 1687 Rome censured Molinos. Soon after, the young August Hermann Francke put his Spiritual Guide into Latin at Leipzig. Critics later said Francke had taken in Molinos's ideas. He replied that reading and translating Molinos had not been the start of his conversion.
  • The Quakers (Religious Society of Friends) Widely Accepted — A Quaker memoir of 1821, the life of Mary Penington, carried a book list. It offered Guyon's Life and her Short Method of Prayer. Ellwood's editor wrote in 1900 that Quaker booksellers sold Molinos. Woolman's editor wrote in 1922 that he read the French quietist writers in English.
  • The Methodist Revival Documented — John Wesley read Guyon's Short Method of Prayer in 1742 and dismissed her as a 'poor Quietist'. By 1770 he wrote that she was a woman of 'excellent piety'. In 1776 he wrote a preface for a shortened Life of her.

Opposed By

  • The Catholic Church of the Ancien Régime Documented Major — In 1687 Rome condemned errors drawn from the writings of Miguel de Molinos. In France, Bossuet tried in person to show Fénelon the errors in Guyon's books. In 1699 Rome condemned twenty-three propositions from Fénelon's Maxims of the Saints, and Fénelon condemned them himself in a pastoral letter.Lasting result: In 1687 Rome condemned errors drawn from Molinos, and in 1699 it condemned 23 claims from a book by Fénelon.

In Contact With

  • Port-Royal Jansenism Documented — In her own Life, Jeanne Guyon tells of visiting Pierre Nicole, who had often spoken against her. She says they read her Short Method through together and he found no error in it. Nicole was one of the Port-Royal circle, a close colleague of Arnauld and Pascal.

Sources

  • Molinos, The Spiritual Guide (1675); the Inquisition process (1685-87) and Coelestis Pastor's 68 condemned propositions (1687) (primary) — The condemned-proposition lists reflect the prosecutor's framing, and confessions were extracted under trial pressure.
  • Madame Guyon: A Short and Easy Method of Prayer (1685), her autobiography, the prison narratives (Mourad & Guenin-Lelle, The Prison Narratives of Jeanne Guyon, OUP, 2012) (primary) — A woman's voice at the center of the controversy, documented under real institutional pressure - the autobiography was written under her spiritual directors' orders and revised while she was imprisoned.
  • The Fenelon-Bossuet contest: Maximes des saints (1697), Bossuet's Relation sur le quietisme, the 1699 condemnation (Cum alias) (primary) — The controversy's public record, in both principals' own words.
  • Bernard McGinn, The Crisis of Mysticism: Quietism in Seventeenth-Century Spain, Italy, and France (2021), with Patricia Ward, Experimental Theology in America (2009) (secondary) — Ward covers the Protestant afterlife of Guyon's texts.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 7, 'Continental Catholic Europe' (Aston) (reference) — The nearest chapter covering this intra-Catholic spirituality dispute.
