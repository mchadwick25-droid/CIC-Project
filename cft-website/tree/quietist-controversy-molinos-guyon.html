Quietist Controversy (Molinos, Guyon)

Spain and France, 1675 to 1699 — an argument about whether the highest prayer is to stop trying, which cost one priest his freedom, one widow years in the Bastille, and an archbishop his standing at court.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 0:58

Read along

In 1675 a Spanish priest living in Rome, Miguel de Molinos, published The Spiritual Guide, teaching a prayer of total interior passivity: stop striving, stop even asking, and let God act. It was hugely popular until it was not; Molinos was arrested in 1685 and sixty-eight propositions drawn from his work were condemned in 1687. The quarrel then moved to France, around Jeanne Guyon, a widow whose Short and Easy Method of Prayer taught something similar, and who found a defender in Fénelon, archbishop of Cambrai, and an implacable opponent in Bossuet, bishop of Meaux. Guyon spent years in confinement, including in the Bastille; Fénelon's defense of her was condemned by Rome in 1699, and he read out his own submission from his own pulpit.

People and Stories

Voices in the Record

  • Miguel de Molinos — Spanish priest whose Spiritual Guide (1675) set off the controversy; condemned in 1687 and imprisoned for the rest of his life
  • Jeanne Guyon — widow and spiritual writer whose Short and Easy Method of Prayer made the dispute a French one; imprisoned for years, and author of her own prison narratives
  • François Fénelon — archbishop of Cambrai, who defended the substance of Guyon's teaching in Maximes des saints (1697) and submitted publicly when Rome condemned it
  • Jacques-Bénigne Bossuet — bishop of Meaux and the prosecution's voice, who broke Fénelon's position at Louis XIV's court

Documented Stories

Molinos Abjures at the Minerva

Miguel de Molinos, an Aragonese priest, had lived in Rome since 1663 and had become, after the Spiritual Guide of 1675, the confessor and correspondent of cardinals, nuns, Queen Christina of Sweden and much of the city's devout society, teaching a prayer of quiet in which the soul ceased its own acts, asked nothing, resisted nothing, and left everything to God. On 18 July 1685 the Inquisition arrested him without warning and seized his papers, some twelve thousand letters. The process took two years and drew in the confessions of penitents, some of them under pressure, who testified that Molinos had taught that in the state of quiet the body's acts, including sexual acts, were no sin because the will did not consent, and that he had practiced what he taught. Whether he had said this, or whether the tribunal had assembled it from letters and from women's frightened depositions, historians still dispute; he admitted, according to the record, to acts he described as not sinful. On 3 September 1687, in the church of Santa Maria sopra Minerva, before a crowd that filled the piazza and, witnesses said, hooted him, he abjured sixty-eight propositions read aloud, was condemned to perpetual imprisonment and to recite the Creed daily, and was led away. He died in the Inquisition's prison in December 1696. The Guide, in Protestant translation, went on being read in England and Germany, and Wesley printed extracts.

Madame Guyon before the Lieutenant of Police

Jeanne Guyon was a widow of forty-seven, the author of A Short and Easy Method of Prayer and of commentaries on Scripture, and the spiritual friend of the abbé Fénelon and of the duchesses of Louis XIV's court, when the conferences at Issy in 1695, presided over by Bossuet, condemned her books. In December 1695 she was arrested anyway, on a royal order, and taken to the fortress of Vincennes, where the lieutenant-general of police, Gabriel Nicolas de La Reynie interrogated her through the winter about her writings, her friendships, her travels and her confessor, Father La Combe, who was already in prison and whose mind was failing. She answered, she says, briefly and calmly, and would not name anyone. She was moved to a convent at Vaugirard in 1696, and in June 1698, when Fénelon's defense of her had turned the affair into a war between the two greatest bishops of France, to the Bastille, where she was held in solitary confinement until March 1703, ill, without her books, and, for a time, without a confessor. Released to her son's house at Blois, she lived fourteen more years receiving visitors from across Protestant Europe. Her account of the interrogations and of the Bastille, written afterwards and hidden from her editors, was first printed in 1992. Bossuet had said that she was no heretic but a dangerous woman; the record of what was done to her makes the second charge his own.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced

  • influenced German Pietism - Spener, Francke, Halle Documented — In 1687 Rome censured Molinos. Soon after, the young August Hermann Francke put his Spiritual Guide into Latin at Leipzig. Critics later said Francke had taken in Molinos's ideas. He replied that reading and translating Molinos had not been the start of his conversion.
  • influenced The Quakers (Religious Society of Friends) Widely Accepted — A Quaker memoir of 1821, the life of Mary Penington, carried a book list. It offered Guyon's Life and her Short Method of Prayer. Ellwood's editor wrote in 1900 that Quaker booksellers sold Molinos. Woolman's editor wrote in 1922 that he read the French quietist writers in English.
  • influenced The Methodist Revival Documented — John Wesley read Guyon's Short Method of Prayer in 1742 and dismissed her as a 'poor Quietist'. By 1770 he wrote that she was a woman of 'excellent piety'. In 1776 he wrote a preface for a shortened Life of her.

Opposed By

  • argued against The Catholic Church of the Ancien Régime Documented Major — In 1687 Rome condemned errors drawn from the writings of Miguel de Molinos. In France, Bossuet tried in person to show Fénelon the errors in Guyon's books. In 1699 Rome condemned twenty-three propositions from Fénelon's Maxims of the Saints, and Fénelon condemned them himself in a pastoral letter.Lasting result: In 1687 Rome condemned errors drawn from Molinos, and in 1699 it condemned 23 claims from a book by Fénelon.

In Contact With

  • in contact with Port-Royal Jansenism Documented — In her own Life, Jeanne Guyon tells of visiting Pierre Nicole, who had often spoken against her. She says they read her Short Method through together and he found no error in it. Nicole was one of the Port-Royal circle, a close colleague of Arnauld and Pascal.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Miguel de Molinos taught a prayer of total interior passivity — stop even asking, and let God act — until he was arrested in 1685 and dozens of his propositions condemned. The argument moved to France around the widow Jeanne Guyon, whose similar teaching found a defender in the archbishop Fénelon and an implacable opponent in Bossuet; Guyon spent years imprisoned, including in the Bastille, and Fénelon read out his own submission from his own pulpit when Rome condemned him in 1699.

Legacy

The condemnation ended quietism as a movement inside Catholicism, but not its books. They had a long second life among Protestants. In 1687 the young August Hermann Francke put Molinos's Spiritual Guide into Latin; he later led German Pietism. Quaker booksellers sold Molinos, and a Quaker memoir of 1821 still listed Guyon's Life and Short Method. John Wesley at first called Guyon a 'poor Quietist'. By 1770 he praised her 'excellent piety', and in 1776 he wrote a preface for a short Life of her. So a condemned French Catholic widow became a devotional author for English-speaking evangelicals, and still is. Fénelon's Telemachus and his letters of spiritual guidance stayed in print and are still read. The word 'quietism' lived on too, as a charge against any prayer thought too passive.
