The Imperial Church Era · c. 310-403 · Salamis, Eleutheropolis

Epiphanius of Salamis

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Early Palestinian Ascetic Monasticism.

Known for
Bishop of Salamis in Cyprus, author of the Panarion against the heresies, and a leading accuser of Origen in the quarrels of his old age.
Also called
Epiphanius of Cyprus, Epiphanius (bishop of Constantia in Cyprus)
Dates
The fourth century. No held ancient source gives the year of his birth or death. He dates the Panarion himself to the eleventh year of Valentinian and Valens and the seventh of Gratian, about 375. He died at sea in old age, sailing home from Constantinople
Place
Born at Besauduc near Eleutheropolis in Palestine; bishop at Salamis, also called Constantia, in Cyprus
Role
Bishop and heresy-writer

About This Person

Epiphanius called his book the Panarion, which means a medicine chest. In its preface he says what it is for. "We are going to show you the names of the heresies. We will uncover their unholy deeds, which are like venom and poison. Alongside them we will fit antidotes: cures for those who have been bitten, and protection for those who may yet fall into it."

A heresy, to Epiphanius, was a wrong belief held by people led astray ever since Adam, the first man. Epiphanius was a monk from Palestine who became bishop of Salamis, the chief city and port of Cyprus. He named eighty heresies as poisons and set a cure beside each. Later writers, from Augustine to a council at Nicaea four centuries after Epiphanius, took his book as their list. In old age he carried the same fight to Jerusalem and Constantinople, against the followers of Origen, a Christian teacher who had died long before.

He wrote the Panarion about 375. Each chapter names a heresy, tells its teaching and answers it, twenty from before Christ's coming and sixty after. He knew some heresies from books, some by report, some with his own eyes and ears. He copied one opponent's own letter whole, so that no one could say he answered from report alone.

He preached against Origen in the church at Jerusalem. In a letter to the bishop of Jerusalem Epiphanius says why. Origen, he writes, taught that souls were angels who fell into bodies, denied that this flesh would rise from the dead, and read the garden of Eden as hidden meanings. Epiphanius held to the plain sense. Genesis, the first book of the Bible, names the Euphrates among the rivers of Eden, and he writes: "I have also drunk from the great river Euphrates. It was not spiritual water but real water, the kind you can touch with your hand and take into your mouth."

In the same letter he defends what he did to the scholar Jerome's brother, who had several times run away from being made a priest. During a service near Epiphanius's own monastery, he says, he had deacons, the clergy who serve under priests, seize the man and hold his mouth shut. Then he made him a deacon, then a priest, as the man cried that he was unworthy. The monks of Jerome's monastery had complained for a year that no one celebrated the sacraments, the church's holy rites, for them, he explains, and he acted from the fear of God, to provide what the church needed. In a village church he found a curtain painted with an image of Christ or a saint. Holding that an image of a man in church went against Scripture, he tore it apart and asked that such curtains be banned.

Then Theophilus, bishop of Alexandria, wrote to win him to his own campaign against Origen. His letter said he had put a few monks who followed Origen out of the church in Egypt. Epiphanius had the bishops of Cyprus forbid reading Origen's books. The historian Socrates says Theophilus wanted revenge on those monks, the Long Brothers, and used Epiphanius, a man so devout that he was simple in mind and manners. Another historian, Sozomen, says instead that Epiphanius had long loathed Origen's books, and that Theophilus saw no danger in following his example, because the people praised Epiphanius.

At Theophilus's urging, he sailed to Constantinople. There he made a man a deacon in the territory of its bishop, John Chrysostom, against church rules. He would not pray with John unless John condemned Origen's books and drove the Long Brothers from the city. One of the Long Brothers asked him whether he had read any of their books. He had not, and had judged them by report. He sailed for Cyprus and died at sea before he reached home.

Augustine wrote his own book on the heresies in the order of Epiphanius's list. Four centuries on, when Christians quarreled over pictures of Christ and the saints in churches, both sides claimed him, and opponents of images quoted writings under his name. In 787 the council at Nicaea ruled that those writings were not his, arguing that his eighty chapters never listed the making of images. A book written to answer the council's argument replied with his own letter about the torn curtain.

Epiphanius set one thing more after the eighty heresies in his chest of cures: the church, which he calls Christ's holy bride and says was there from the beginning.

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “We are going to show you the names of the heresies. We will uncover their unholy deeds, which are like venom and poison. Alongside them we will fit antidotes: cures for those who have been bitten, and protection for those who may yet fall into it.”
    As printed
    “ἐπειδὴ γὰρ μέλλομεν ὑμῖν τά τε ὀνόματα τῶν αἱρέσεων δηλοῦν τάς τε παρ᾽ αὐτοῖς ἀϑεμίτους πράξεις ἀνακαλύπτειν, ὥσπερ ἰοὺς καὶ δηλητήρια ὄντα, σὺν αὐτοῖς δὲ ἅμα καὶ ἀντιδότους ἐφαρμόσαι, ἀλεξητήρια τῶν δεδηγμένων καὶ προκαταληπτικὰ τῶν μελλόντων εἰς τοῦτο ἐμπίπτειν”
    Epiphanius, Panarion, Proem I, Greek, ed. Karl Holl
    Holl's edition is the only print of this preface the Library holds, and its scan has slips inside the span: τε is read as TE in line 12388, καὶ as zal in line 12390, and κ as χ in προκαταληπτικὰ in line 12391. They are read here as the only words that fit. The English is the brief's own rendering of the Greek; no printed English is held.
  • In our modern English
    “I have also drunk from the great river Euphrates. It was not spiritual water but real water, the kind you can touch with your hand and take into your mouth.”
    As printed
    “I have drunk also from the great river Euphrates, not spiritual but actual water, such as you can touch with your hand and imbibe with your mouth.”
    Epiphanius, letter to John, bishop of Jerusalem (Jerome's Letter 51), tr. W. H. Fremantle, in Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, series 2, vol. 6
    The Greek letter and Jerome's Latin of it are not held, so the English stands alone. The modern wording follows Fremantle's English.

  • Ancoratus: "The Anchored", which the monk Ammonius says in Sozomen's account he had read often; held in Greek

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The Panarion's account of the heresies. It keeps writings of some of those it attacks, among them the propositions of Aetius of Antioch, quoted in heresy 76.
  • The condemnation of Origen's books in Cyprus, which Socrates of Constantinople and Sozomen say he decreed at a synod there.
  • Augustine, in On Heresies, says that in listing the heretics he followed Epiphanius's order but not his measure. He says he took fifty-seven heresies from Epiphanius's own work, and that among the Greeks Epiphanius was counted among the great.
  • Photius, a patriarch of Constantinople in the ninth century, judged that Epiphanius wrote more fully and effectively against heretics than any of his predecessors, and that his style is poor.
  • Jerome says Epiphanius, who had much intercourse with Hilarion, set out Hilarion's praises in a short letter that was widely circulated. The letter is not held.
  • In heresy 77 of the Panarion he lists the followers of Apollinaris of Laodicea, whom he calls always dear to him, for teaching that Christ took flesh and a soul but no human mind.

How they're remembered

  • Sozomen calls him "the most revered man under the whole heaven", and says it was reported that demons were driven out and diseases healed at his tomb.
  • Jerome says his books were read by the learned for their matter, "and also by the plain people, on account of their language."
  • Socrates of Constantinople says that "on account of his extraordinary piety" he was "a man of simple mind and manners", and that Theophilus of Alexandria used him.
  • Sozomen says he was instructed from his youth by the most celebrated ascetics and for that reason passed most of his time in Egypt.
  • Sozomen says he was extremely liberal to the needy, among them those who had suffered shipwreck, and that after spending his whole inheritance on them he applied the treasures of the church to the same purpose.
  • Jerome says monks came to Cyprus from all parts of the world for love of him.
  • The Dialogue on the life of Chrysostom says he ruled the church in Cyprus for thirty-six years.
  • Theophilus of Alexandria, in a letter in Jerome's Latin, says he "has shone throughout all the world a bright star among bishops".
  • Sozomen says he made his home near the village of Besauduc, his birthplace, in the district of Eleutheropolis, and that the people of Cyprus chose him as bishop of the chief city of their island. Jerome calls his house there the old monastery.

What Is Uncertain

  • Epiphanius wrote against the heresies as their enemy, and his reports of them are a foe's.
  • Socrates and Sozomen report his last words to John Chrysostom, and John's answer, as hearsay. Socrates says he cannot be sure his informants spoke the truth.
  • Theophilus of Alexandria once accused him of thinking that God has a human form, Socrates and Sozomen say. Jerome, who was there, says Epiphanius condemned that view in public at Jerusalem.
  • Theophilus's own judgment of him changed. The Dialogue on the life of Chrysostom, which Herbert Moore gives to Palladius of Helenopolis, says Theophilus first called him "a heretic, or a paltry schismatic", and later "a most holy saint".
  • His dates are Richardson's reckoning. No held ancient source gives the year of his birth.
  • The Library holds the Panarion in Greek from heresy 1 on, and the Ancoratus in Greek.
  • The books of the Panarion are counted three ways. His own preface says three. Photius says three volumes holding seven books. Augustine says Epiphanius made six books of the whole.
  • In his letter to John of Jerusalem he gives reasons for two acts. For ordaining Jerome's brother, he says the monks of that monastery had no one to celebrate the sacraments for them, he feared that too great strictness would turn them from the Church and its ancient faith, and the monastery was not under the bishop of Jerusalem. For tearing the curtain, he says an image of a man should not hang in Christ's church against the teaching of the Scriptures. The letter is held only in Fremantle's English, from Jerome's Latin of his Greek.
  • Jerome says he put Epiphanius's letter to John into Latin at a friend's request, and that when the translation reached Jerusalem he was accused of falsifying it; he answers the charge in his Letter 57 to Pammachius. The letter itself names the presbyter Rufinus among those to be kept from the heresy of Origen. In his Apology against Rufinus, Jerome quotes Rufinus as saying that one of Origen's detractors once declared before a great crowd that he had read six thousand of his books, and Jerome takes this as aimed at Epiphanius.
  • Socrates says that a short time after Epiphanius sailed, John Chrysostom was driven from his see. No held source makes Epiphanius the cause.
  • Sozomen says it was reported that on the shore, about to board, Epiphanius told the bishops who had come with him that he left them the city, the palace and the pretense, and that he was going in a great hurry. The Greek word for the pretense is the one for acting or hypocrisy; Hartranft's English has the stage, and Herbert Moore's note has the hypocrisy.
  • Whether the writings against images that the council of 754 quoted under his name are his is disputed. The council of 787 ruled that the passage was none of his, because his eighty chapters list no heresy of making images. The Caroline Books, a reply to the council, answered with his own letter to John, which they accept as his.
  • Jerome says Paula was detained ten days in Cyprus by Epiphanius. The Dialogue on the life of Chrysostom says Olympias made gifts of lands and money to him among others.
  • Late sources keep his day: the Ethiopic Synaxarium on 17 Genbot, and the Acta Sanctorum on 12 May. Both are late legend.
  • Jerome, who was there and on his side, says that while Epiphanius preached against Origen in the church at Jerusalem, the bishop of Jerusalem's party mocked him as a silly old man, and that crowds pressed to meet him and kiss his feet.
