William Tyndale

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The English Reformation & Elizabethan Settlement.

Known for
English priest and scholar who put the New Testament into English from the Greek and printed it abroad, at Worms in 1526, so that merchants could smuggle it into England, where the bishop of London ordered every copy handed in and the king ordered the books burned. He also put the first five books of the Bible into English from the Hebrew, and he offered the king his life if the Bible's bare text went out among the people. He was betrayed at Antwerp in 1535 and, by John Foxe's account, strangled at the stake and burned at Vilvoorde in 1536.
Also called
Hychins, Hotchens
Dates
No source held here gives a sure year of birth; his 1848 editor, Henry Walter, says only that the probability is 1484. The editor puts his leaving England for Hamburg in 1524. Cochlaeus, an enemy of Luther writing in 1549, places the stopped press at Cologne in 1525; the Testament was printed at Worms in 1526 and the first five books of the Bible at Marburg in 1530. Foxe gives his death at Vilvoorde in 1536, and his calendar the day, 6 October
Place
Born near the Welsh border, by Foxe; Oxford and Cambridge; a manor in Gloucestershire as tutor; London, 1523 to 1524; Cologne; Worms; Marburg; Antwerp; the castle of Vilvoorde, near Brussels
Role
Priest and scholar; translator of the Bible into English; writer in exile

About This Person

In 1525, at Cologne on the Rhine, an English New Testament, the second part of the Bible, was on the press. A German churchman, an enemy of Luther, told years later how he heard the printers boasting of it over their wine and had the city council stop the press. Two Englishmen, he wrote, fled up the river with the printed sheets.

One was William Tyndale, a priest ordained to lead worship, and a scholar. He was putting the New Testament, its gospels and letters, into English straight from the Greek they were first written in, not the church's Latin. At home that needed leave from a bishop, a senior church officer, so he printed abroad and had the books smuggled in, where the king ordered them burned. He would offer that king his life for the Bible's words alone, and in 1536 he was put to death.

Only this, he wrote, moved him to translate: people outside the church's ordained ranks could hold no truth unless the Bible lay plain before their eyes in their own tongue. At Worms in 1526 he printed the Testament in thousands.

Tyndale then wrote about kings and their power, and one book taught that the king answers to God alone. The king praised it, in the story told by John Foxe, who later collected the stories of Protestants put to death. In 1530 Tyndale printed the first five books of the Bible, put into English from the Hebrew they were written in. Another book, on the bishops' dealings with kings, angered the king most, Tyndale told the king's envoy. And he called the churchmen who fought his translation hypocrites.

In 1531 that envoy met him outside Antwerp and asked him to come home. Tyndale would not come home on any promise. He asked only for the bare text of Scripture, the Bible's words without his notes. The envoy reported his answer: "Suppose the king, of his grace, grants his people only the bare text of the Scripture … in the translation of anyone he pleases. Then I will at once make a faithful promise never to write again. … I offer my body to suffer whatever pain or torture, yes, whatever death the king wills, if only this is granted."

No answer from the king is known, and within a year the king's envoys were ordered to seize him. In 1535, as Foxe tells it, an Englishman who had won his trust pointed him out to waiting officers in Antwerp. After more than a year in the castle of Vilvoorde, near Brussels, he was condemned under the law of the emperor, whose officers ruled there. He was tied to a post and strangled, and his body was burned. Foxe says he cried out, "Lord, open the king of England's eyes."

Foxe kept the readers' stories too, of people whipped, jailed and questioned for the book. John Maundrel, a Wiltshire farmer who could not read, had others read the Testament to him until he knew most of it by heart. Some readers were later burned as heretics, for teaching the church condemned as false. Maundrel himself was burned at Salisbury in 1556, when the old church was restored under Queen Mary. Smuggled in faster than bishops could burn it, the book had reached people no one had allowed to have it.

Within a year of Tyndale's death, the archbishop of Canterbury, England's senior bishop, praised a new English Bible, and the king allowed it to be bought and read. It bore another man's name, but editors find that most of it was Tyndale's.

In 1533 he had written to a friend in prison. He called God to witness that he had never changed one syllable of God's word against his conscience, and never would, for all the pleasure, honor or riches on earth.

Quotations are given in our modern English. The printed wording of each is below.

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “Suppose the king, of his grace, grants his people only the bare text of the Scripture … in the translation of anyone he pleases. Then I will at once make a faithful promise never to write again. … I offer my body to suffer whatever pain or torture, yes, whatever death the king wills, if only this is granted.”
    As printed
    “if it would stand with the king's most gracious pleasure to grant only a bare text of the scripture to be put forth among his people, like as is put forth among the subjects of the emperor in these parts, and of other Christian princes, be it of the translation of what person soever shall please his majesty, I shall immediately make faithful promise never to write more, nor abide two days in these parts after the same; but immediately repair into his realm, and there most humbly submit myself at the feet of his royal majesty, offering my body to suffer what pain or torture, yea, what death his grace will, so that this be obtained.”
    Stephen Vaughan to Henry VIII, May 1531, reporting Tyndale's answer, in Walter's Biographical Notice
    The brief leaves out two stretches of the printed wording: that the bare text goes out among the people as it does among the emperor's subjects and other Christian princes, and his promise to leave within two days and kneel before the king.

Key Works

  • The New Testament in English: put into English from the Greek, printed at Worms in 1526 and revised against the Greek again in 1534; the printed 1535 title says it was diligently corrected and compared with the Greek. The Library holds his prefaces and prologues to it, not the Testament itself
  • The Five Books of Moses: the first five books of the Bible put into English from the Hebrew; the first book, Genesis, was printed at Marburg in Hesse in 1530, with a preface in which he gives his reason for translating
  • The Obedience of a Christian Man: a book of 1528 on obedience: it teaches that God makes the king judge over all, with no judge over him but God, and that to resist the king is to resist God. In John Foxe's story, told through a later historian, the king praised it

Legacy

What traces to them

  • In October 1526 the bishop of London ordered every copy of the English New Testament handed in within thirty days, under pain of excommunication. King Henry VIII, in his reply to Luther that year, wrote that the translations were to be burned, with punishment for those who kept or read them.
  • Foxe tells of readers: Richard Bayfield, a monk given Tyndale's Testament, who was whipped, gagged and put in the stocks; James Bainham, who said in 1531 that the people should have the English Testament as it is; and John Maundrel, a Wiltshire farmer who could not read, who had others read it to him and knew most of it by heart, and who was burned at Salisbury in 1556 under Queen Mary.
  • In 1537 Thomas Cranmer, archbishop of Canterbury, sent the king's minister an English Bible, saying he liked it better than any translation made before, and asked a license for it to be sold and read; he then thanked the minister for obtaining the king's permission to have it bought and read in the realm. It bore the name Thomas Matthew; Henry Walter finds that it began with a reprint of Tyndale's first five books, closed with a reprint of his New Testament, and held his translation of the historical books to the end of Second Chronicles.
  • Walter says that before the end of 1536 the king's own printer issued Tyndale's New Testament in England, with his name on the title page.

How they're remembered

  • Foxe says he may be worthily called an apostle of England, and in the story of Maundrel calls him the faithful apostle of England.
  • Thomas Poyntz, the merchant who lodged him, wrote to his brother in August 1535 that the king had no truer-hearted subject and that the death of this man would be a great hindrance to the gospel.
  • In 1533 Tyndale wrote to John Frith in the Tower that he had never altered one syllable of God's word against his conscience, and would not for all the pleasure, honor or riches on earth.

What Is Uncertain

  • The Cologne press. The account of the stopped press and the flight up the Rhine comes from Cochlaeus, an enemy of Luther, writing in 1549 about 1525, held here only in Walter's English. His three thousand copies and the six thousand printed at Worms, which a diary of August 1526 gives as hearsay, are both reports, not counts.
  • Luther. The charges of 1528 against Tyndale's London host, Foxe and Cochlaeus say he went to Luther; Walter says Tyndale denied any confederacy, and nothing held shows they met. His enemies called the Testament Luther's. Contested.
  • His reason and his offer. The reason he gives for translating is in his own 1530 preface. His offer to give up writing and suffer any death comes through the king's envoy, Stephen Vaughan, who wrote to please the king and whom Walter suspects of adding the praise of the king that follows it. John Frith made the same offer in print in 1533.
  • The king and his books. That the king praised the Obedience is Foxe's story, through a later historian. The Practice of Prelates is not held; what Tyndale told the envoy is that it only warned the king against his clergy and his cardinal. That it opposed the king's plan to end his marriage is the 1848 editor's view only.
  • The betrayal. Foxe tells it from the merchant Poyntz: Henry Phillips befriended him, borrowed forty shillings and pointed him out to officers in a narrow entry. Who sent Phillips is not known; Poyntz thought it was arranged from England without the king's knowledge, and Crespin says some thought the bishops did. Contested.
  • The death. Foxe says he was condemned under the emperor's decree, tied to the stake, strangled and then burned; the French martyrologist Crespin says only that he was taken to Vilvoorde to be burned. The last words, Lord, open the king of England's eyes, rest on Foxe alone, decades later. The ploughboy saying rests on him alone as well.
  • The Bible of 1537. That most of it was Tyndale's work, printed under another name, is the editors' finding, not the archbishop's claim. Widely Accepted.
  • Foxe's own life of Tyndale is not held; it is held only as Walter quotes it. Walter, a warm admirer, writes in providential language that is his own, not the record's.
