Kievan Rus' Christianity

Kyiv and Novgorod, 988 to 1054 — two generations in which a prince's baptism became a cathedral, a monastery and a literature, and the Slavic north became Christian.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:19

Read along

In 988 Prince Vladimir of Kyiv was baptized and had his people baptized with him — an act of state that the Rus' chronicle later dressed in one of the great stories of medieval Christianity. Envoys sent out to sample the religions who reported that in Hagia Sophia at Constantinople they had not known whether they stood in heaven or on earth. Within two generations there was a Christian culture to match the decision. Vladimir's son Yaroslav built his own St Sophia in Kyiv and filled it with mosaics; a monastery in the caves above the Dnieper became the school of Rus' monasticism. And around 1049 a native churchman, Ilarion — whom Yaroslav would make the first native-born metropolitan two years later — preached a Sermon on Law and Grace that is the first substantial theology written by a Rus' author. The window is short, barely two generations, but almost the whole later history of Russian, Ukrainian and Belarusian Christianity runs back through it. Most of what tells the story was written down afterwards, by monks looking back.

Documented Stories

Boris Sends His Retinue Away

Vladimir died in July 1015 and his son Sviatopolk seized Kyiv. Boris, another son, was returning with his father’s army from a campaign against the Pechenegs when the news reached him on the river Alta, and the retinue, the chronicle says, offered to take Kyiv for him: ‘Behold, your father’s army is with you.’ Boris refused. ‘Be it not for me to raise my hand against my elder brother; since my father is dead, let him take my father’s place.’ Warned that Sviatopolk’s men were coming, he stayed in his tent, had vespers sung, read the Psalter — the chronicle quotes the verses — and lay down. The killers came at dawn, thrust spears through the tent, and killed him and his servant George, a Hungarian who threw himself over the prince’s body; they cut off George’s head to take the gold torque Boris had given him. Sviatopolk then sent to the younger brother Gleb at Murom, telling him their father was ill; Gleb set out, learned the truth from a message, and was murdered on his boat by his own cook, who cut his throat ‘like a lamb’. The two brothers were the first saints the Rus’ church canonized, and it canonized them not as martyrs for the faith but as strastoterptsy, ‘passion-bearers’ — men who accepted death without resisting rather than shed a brother’s blood. Their brother Yaroslav, who defeated Sviatopolk and promoted the cult, was also the man who benefited most from their not having fought.

Vladimir’s Carts

Under the year 996 the Primary Chronicle records what Vladimir did after his baptism, and it reads like a list of things a chronicler thought a Christian prince should be seen doing. He heard the Gospel words ‘blessed are the merciful’ and ‘sell that thou hast’, and, the chronicle says, ordered that any beggar or poor person might come to the prince’s court and receive food, drink and money from the treasury. Then he thought of those who could not walk, ‘the weak and the sick’, and had wagons loaded with bread, meat, fish, vegetables, mead in barrels and kvass, driven through the city with the cry, ‘Where is there a sick man or a beggar who cannot walk?’ And then the chronicle records a scruple. Robbery increased, and the bishops asked why Vladimir did not punish it. He answered: ‘I am afraid of sin.’ The bishops told him he had been appointed by God to punish the wicked and show mercy to the good, and that he should execute robbers after examination; he did so. Later they told him wars needed money and he should return to the old system of fines, and he did that too. The passage documents a prince and his bishops working out, in the first decade of a Christian state, what the new religion meant for a ruler — and the bishops arguing him back toward severity. The chronicle presents both his mercy and their correction as right.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 988 Prince Vladimir of Kyiv was baptized and had his people baptized with him, an act the Rus' chronicle later wrapped in the story of envoys who, standing in Hagia Sophia at Constantinople, could not tell whether they were in heaven or on earth. Within two generations his son Yaroslav had built his own St Sophia in Kyiv, a monastery had risen above the Dnieper, and the native churchman Ilarion preached the first substantial theology written by a Rus' author.

Legacy

The same church continued without a break into the Kievan Rus' of the following two centuries and, through it, into the Russian, Ukrainian and Belarusian Orthodox churches — the largest body of Orthodox Christians in the world traces its founding to these two generations. It also left things you can still stand in front of. St Sophia in Kyiv keeps its eleventh-century mosaics and frescoes, the Caves Monastery is still a monastery, and Boris and Gleb, venerated as 'passion-bearers' who would not raise a hand against their own brother, became a model of holiness distinctive to the Slavic churches.

Experience It Today

  • Saint Sophia Cathedral and the Kyiv-Pechersk Lavra, Kyiv — the eleventh-century cathedral with its original mosaics and the caves monastery, together a UNESCO World Heritage Site

  • The Paterik of the Kievan Caves Monastery (trans. Muriel Heppell, Harvard, 1989) (primary) — First complete English translation. A 13th-century compilation reaching back to the mid-11th-century foundation, so it needs careful, critical reading.
  • Simon Franklin & Jonathan Shepard, The Emergence of Rus, 750-1200 (Longman, 1996) (secondary) — The standard English history of the period, source-critical about the chronicle.
