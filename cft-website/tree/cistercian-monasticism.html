The High Medieval Era · 1098-1300 · Burgundy, then Europe

Burgundy, today in France and beyond

Cistercian Monasticism

Burgundy and then all of Europe, 1098 to 1300 — monks who walked out of a rich abbey to keep Benedict's Rule plainly, and ended up building one of the best-organized institutions in medieval Christendom.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:14

Read along

In 1098 a group of monks walked out of the great Burgundian abbey of Molesme. They thought Benedictine life there had grown too comfortable. They settled in a marshy clearing called Cîteaux to keep the Rule of St Benedict as plainly as they could. That meant bare churches, simple food, no elaborate liturgy, and their own hands in the fields. Within a generation the reform had a constitution, the Carta Caritatis. It bound every daughter house to its mother house through yearly visits and a yearly general chapter. After Bernard of Clairvaux arrived in 1113, the order spread across Europe at extraordinary speed. Much of the actual labor was done by lay brothers, the conversi. They took vows but lived a separate, largely manual life beside the choir monks, on farms called granges. The order never ended. Its writers, Bernard on the Song of Songs and Aelred of Rievaulx on friendship, stayed among the most-read spiritual authors of the Latin West for centuries.

Documented Stories

Bernard Rides to the Rhineland to Stop Radulf

Radulf was a monk of Bernard’s own order who left his monastery without leave in 1146 and began preaching the crusade along the Rhine, and, according to both Christian and Jewish witnesses, telling the crowds that the enemies of Christ were nearer than Jerusalem. Attacks on Jews followed at Cologne, Mainz, Worms and Speyer. Bernard wrote at once to the archbishop of Mainz and to the people of the Rhineland. The letter that survives is unambiguous: the Jews are not to be persecuted, killed or driven out; they are ‘living words’ of Scripture, witnesses to the passion, and God has scattered rather than destroyed them; whoever touches them, he says, is as one who touches the apple of the Lord’s eye. He then traveled to Mainz himself, confronted Radulf, and ordered him back to his cloister; Radulf went. The Jewish chronicler Ephraim of Bonn, writing within a generation, recorded it from the other side: Bernard, ‘a decent priest’, had said that whoever harmed a Jew laid hands on Jesus himself, and the killing largely stopped. Ephraim also recorded that the same Bernard preached the crusade that had made the killing likely, and Bernard’s letter itself contains the standard theology of Jewish subjection that made Radulf’s hearers ready to listen. The tradition that produced the most listened-to preacher of holy war in Europe also produced, in the same man and the same year, the intervention that saved the Jews of the Rhineland from a repeat of 1096.

The Lay Brothers of Schönau Refuse to Work

The Cistercian conversi took vows of obedience and celibacy, wore beards, lived in a separate range of the monastery, and did most of the farm labor that made the order rich; they could not become choir monks, could not learn to read, and had no voice in the chapter. The statutes of the annual general chapter, the order’s legislative record, mention them mostly when something went wrong. The earliest recorded revolt is at Schönau in the Rhineland in 1168, where the lay brothers rose against the abbot; the chapter ordered the ringleaders expelled and the rest put on bread and water. Over the following century the statutes record risings at more than twenty houses: at Eberbach in 1261 the conversi drove the abbot out; at Margam in Wales in 1206 they pursued the abbot on horseback and barricaded themselves in the dormitory; at Coggeshall they refused the bread ration; at several houses the grievance was beer — the chapter had ordered the lay brothers’ ale weakened. The penalties are recorded too: expulsion, imprisonment in the monastery’s cell, transfer to a distant house, loss of the daily wine. What the conversi thought is not recorded, because no one asked them; what they wanted can be read from the complaints. Both are Cistercian documents, and the tradition that preserved Bernard’s sermons on the Song of Songs also preserved the minutes of the men who kept the granges and rioted over the beer.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced Early Benedictine / Italian Monasticism Inferential-Thin — The monks of Cîteaux set out to keep the Rule of St Benedict more plainly. The tie rests on a 1907 editor of the Rule, not on the early Cistercians' own words. Oswald Hunter Blair notes only that early Cistercian statutes, like the Rule, set times when monks might talk.

Influenced

  • influenced The English Mystics and Devout Documented — Richard Rolle, the Yorkshire hermit who died in 1349, quotes 'S. Bernard' on fasting and keeping watch. Walter Hilton's Scale of Perfection quotes Bernard too. In 1914 Evelyn Underhill named Bernard as one of the three masters who shaped Rolle most.
  • influenced Military-Religious Orders (Templars, Hospitallers) Dominant Modern Reconstruction — Bernard of Clairvaux, the Cistercians' best-known abbot, wrote In Praise of the New Knighthood in defense of the Templars. He argued that a knight who killed in this cause was killing evil, not a man. This rests on the modern history of the order, not on Bernard's own text.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 1098 a group of monks walked out of the abbey of Molesme. They went to keep the Rule of St Benedict plainly at Cîteaux, with bare churches, manual labor and no elaborate liturgy. The reform grew fast after Bernard of Clairvaux joined in 1113. Much of the actual work was done by lay brothers, the conversi, who farmed the order's granges beside the choir monks. Bernard's sermons and Aelred of Rievaulx's writing on friendship stayed among the most-read spiritual works in the West for centuries.

Legacy

The order is still here. Cistercian and Trappist houses keep the same rule today. Its buildings became a style of their own: the bare, unpainted, finely shaped church that architects still simply call Cistercian. Fontenay in Burgundy survives almost whole. Its writers were read for centuries. Bernard's sermons on the Song of Songs and Aelred's Spiritual Friendship fed a warm, loving strand of Western prayer. Two hundred years on, the English writers Richard Rolle and Walter Hilton still quoted Bernard by name. And the granges left a mark on European farming, on water and on the clearing of land that outlasted the monks who worked them.

Experience It Today

  • Marmagne, Burgundy, France — the Abbey of Fontenay, founded from Clairvaux in 1118 and one of the most complete surviving Cistercian abbeys in Europe

  • Aelred of Rievaulx, Spiritual Friendship (trans. Braceland, Cistercian Fathers 5); with Walter Daniel, Life of Aelred (primary) — The Life gives an abbot seen up close by his own monk.
  • Chrysogonus Waddell (ed.), Cistercian Lay Brothers: Twelfth-Century Usages, with Related Texts (Studia et Documenta 10, 2000) (primary) — The conversi's regulated world — the order's ordinary, manual-labor half, made concrete.
  • Janet Burton & Julie Kerr, The Cistercians in the Middle Ages (Boydell, 2011) (secondary) — Current standard survey. Read with Constance Berman, The Cistercian Evolution (2000), which argues the founding documents are later constructions.
