Naples · Paris, today in France and beyond

Thomas Aquinas

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Dominican Order.

Known for
Dominican friar and master of theology at Paris whose kin held him to stop him joining the friars, and who wrote the Summa of Theology, a summary of the whole faith that sets out the case against each point and then answers it, on his rule that the opposite of a truth can never be proved. By a royal official's sworn account he wrote nothing more after Mass at Naples around December 1273, saying that everything he had written seemed to him straw. He died at Fossanova on 7 March 1274 on the road to the Council of Lyon, and John XXII declared him a saint in 1323.
Also called
Thomas of Aquino, The Angelic Doctor, The Dumb Ox
Dates
Born around 1225: the Bollandists reckon the start of 1225 from William of Tocco's words that he was completing his forty-ninth year when he died, and a note by Henry Walter to Tyndale says 1224. The Bollandists put his entry into the Dominicans in 1243, at nineteen, and Conway's note puts his seizure in 1243 and his release in 1244; both are editors' reckonings. Gerard says he became a master of theology at Paris and gives no year; the Bollandists say 1253 or the next year and the July Bollandists report Echard for not before 1257. Bartholomew of Capua puts the end of his writing around the feast of Saint Nicholas, in December; the year 1273 follows from the order of his events, not from his words. Tocco puts the death in the morning of 7 March 1274. The Bollandists give his canonization by John XXII as 1323, with a bull dated the fifteenth day before the Kalends of August; Denifle gives 16 July 1323; Walter's note to Tyndale says 1318, against every other held source. The bull itself is not held. Pius V's bull on his feast is dated 1567
Place
Aquino and Roccasecca, which the Bollandists call the seat of his family of counts and which Tocco names as his mother's castle on the border of the kingdom of Sicily and Campania; Monte Cassino, where Tocco says his parents sent him at five; Naples; Cologne; Paris; the castle where he was held, which Tocco puts at Monte San Giovanni and Roccasecca and Bartholomew calls his father's castle; San Severino, his sister's castle, by Tocco, for the stopping of his writing; Maenza and the monastery of Fossanova near Terracina, where he died
Role
Dominican friar and master of theology at Paris; author of the Summa of Theology

About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

"It seems that God does not exist. For if one of two opposites were without limit, the other would be wholly destroyed. But by the name God we mean a good without limit. So if God existed, no evil would be found. But evil is found in the world. So God does not exist."

So begins one question in the Summa of Theology of Thomas Aquinas, his summary of the whole faith, where each question sets out the case against, then answers it. He answered this argument in Augustine's words. A God supremely good, Augustine wrote, would let evil into his works only if he were so powerful and good that he could bring good even out of it.

Thomas was one of the Dominicans, the Order of Preachers, poor traveling friars trained in theology to preach, and he taught theology at Paris in the thirteenth century. In the Summa he taught that faith and reason cannot truly conflict, because nothing true can be proved false. The church declared him a saint in 1323.

He was born around 1225 to the counts of Aquino, between Rome and Naples. He studied at Naples, and there he joined the Dominicans.

On the road toward Paris, his kin seized him and shut him in a castle. The order's own book of its friars, written in his lifetime, has a story the editors take to be his. His kinsmen held him about a year, it says, and tried every means to make him leave, but could not make him put off the habit, the friars' robe. William of Tocco, a friar who pressed for him to be declared a saint, wrote decades later that his brothers did it at his mother's order. They tore his habit, he says, and sent a woman to tempt him. A royal official who had known him at Naples told the inquiry of 1319 it was his father, for more than a year. In the end, they let him go.

The order sent him to study under Albert the Great at Cologne, and he became a master of theology at Paris, a full teacher in the university. He wrote the Summa, he said, to teach beginners in good order. His rule was this: "Faith rests on a truth that cannot fail. And the opposite of a truth can never be proved. So it is clear that the proofs brought against the faith are not true proofs. They are arguments that can be answered." He also wrote there that heretics who would not turn back could be left to the rulers to be put to death. Corrupting the faith, he said, was worse than forging money.

By the royal official's sworn account, around the feast of Saint Nicholas in December 1273, after Mass at Naples, Thomas wrote and dictated nothing more. His companion Reginald begged him to go on. "Reginald, I cannot. For everything I have written seems to me straw." To Reginald alone he added that it was straw beside what had been shown to him. What he saw, no source says. Reginald told this on his deathbed to another friar, who told the official. Tocco tells it differently, at Thomas's sister's castle, and has him say that what he had written seemed small.

The pope called him to a council of the whole church, at Lyon. Thomas fell ill on the way, and died at the monastery of Fossanova on 7 March 1274.

Within forty years, his own order bound its teachers to teach by his teaching and works. Tocco reports that Pope John XXII said Thomas had lit up the church more than all the other teachers. A pope later ordered his feast kept like those of the four great Doctors, the church's chief teachers. And the English reformer William Tyndale complained that preachers quarreled, "one followeth Duns, another St Thomas." Duns Scotus was a Franciscan teacher.

The Summa still breaks off, in its third part, in its questions on penance, the sacrament of confession and forgiveness, where he stopped.

Quotations are given in our modern English. The printed wording of each is below.

Quotations and references

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “It seems that God does not exist. For if one of two opposites were without limit, the other would be wholly destroyed. But by the name God we mean a good without limit. So if God existed, no evil would be found. But evil is found in the world. So God does not exist.”
    As printed
    “Videtur quod Deus non sit. Quia si unum contrariorum fuerit infinitum, totaliter destruetur aliud. Sed hoc intelligitur in hoc nomine Deus, scilicet quod sit quoddam bonum infinitum. Si ergo Deus esset, nullum malum inveniretur. Invenitur autem malum in mundo. Ergo Deus non est.”
    Summa theologiae I, question 2, article 3, first objection, Latin of the Leonine edition
    In the first lines the scan sets stray marks into contrariorum, aliud and nomine; they are no part of the text. Good without limit renders bonum infinitum. No English of the Summa is held.
  • In our modern English
    “Faith rests on a truth that cannot fail. And the opposite of a truth can never be proved. So it is clear that the proofs brought against the faith are not true proofs. They are arguments that can be answered.”
    As printed
    “Cum enim fides infallibili veritati innitatur, impossibile autem sit de vero demonstrari contrarium, manifestum est probationes quae contra fidem inducuntur, non esse demonstrationes, sed solubilia argumenta.”
    Summa theologiae I, question 1, article 8, Latin of the Leonine edition
    A note mark stands after vero in the scan. True proofs renders demonstrationes, proof that settles a question, and proofs renders probationes, what is offered as proof. No English of the Summa is held.
  • In our modern English
    “Reginald, I cannot. For everything I have written seems to me straw.”
    As printed
    “Raynalde, non possum”
    Bartholomew of Capua, testimony of 1319, in the Acta Sanctorum, March I
    Only the first clause is printed. The rest of the sentence reads in the 1865 print omnia, quae scripsi, videntur mihi paleae, with faults of the scan in each of its last words (omnià, qua seripsi, palez); the 1735 print of the same page is damaged in other ways (paleae is read palcx) and no held print confirms the word. No English is held. The same words come again in the testimony, with other faults, at lines 187886-187888.
  • In our modern English
    “one followeth Duns, another St Thomas.”
    As printed
    “one followeth Duns4, another St Thomas5”
    William Tyndale, The Obedience of a Christian Man, ed. Henry Walter
    The figures after Duns and Thomas are the editor's note marks, printed as the page has them; the words are Tyndale's own English.

Key Works

  • Summa of Theology (Summa theologiae): his summary of the whole faith, in questions that each set out the case against and then answer it. In its prologue he says a teacher of the truth must also instruct beginners, and that beginners were hindered by useless questions and by matter not taught in the order of the subject. The third part breaks off at question 90 on the parts of penance, and the Supplement printed after it is not his: a fourteenth-century note the editors print says he was prevented by death and names William Sudre, later a cardinal, as completing it from his books, and the editors dispute who compiled it. Only the Latin is held
  • Summa against the Gentiles (Summa contra Gentiles): known here only from Tocco, who says it shows to other nations, not subject to the faith, how great is the truth of the Christian faith, which natural reasons make likely even where it passes reason, and whose arguments the defenders of the faith can answer. Tocco's judgment; the book itself is not held
  • The book against the Greeks: Tocco says he carried a small book written against the Greeks to the Council of Lyon at Urban IV's order. It is named by Tocco only and is not held

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The Dominican general chapter at Saragossa in 1309 told all its lectors and sub-lectors to read and decide by the teaching and works of Thomas and to train their students in it, and to punish any who notably did the contrary and would not retract when warned. The chapter at Metz in 1313 held his teaching sounder and more commonly held, forbade any friar teaching, deciding or answering to hold assertively the contrary of what was commonly believed to be his opinion, and removed from the lectureship, as a penalty, one found to have done so after being noted before, with sharper punishment if scandal followed. These were the order's own rules for its own teachers.
  • Tocco, who promoted the cause, and sworn witnesses at Naples took part in the inquiry that ended on 18 September 1319. Tocco reports that John XXII said Thomas had lit up the church more than all the other Doctors, that a man gains more from his books in one year than from others' teaching in a lifetime, and that the cardinals would count it great glory to enroll him among the saints if some miracles could be found.
  • Pius V in 1567 ordered his feast kept like those of the four Doctors of the Church; the bull calls his teaching a most certain rule of Christian doctrine, says he lit up the church by refuting heresies without number, and says John XXII, when he enrolled him among the saints, ordered his feast kept.
  • Tyndale complained that one preacher followed Duns and another St Thomas; Foxe says that in the dispute over the conception of Mary some held with Scotus and some with Thomas Aquinas, the Minorites holding with Scotus.
  • Pius X, in Pascendi of 1907, reprinted in 1910, ordered that scholastic philosophy be the foundation of sacred studies, meaning above all the philosophy of Thomas Aquinas, and warned that to depart from Aquinas even a little, especially in metaphysics, was not without great harm; he cites Leo XIII's Aeterni Patris, which is not held.
  • The editors of Gennadius Scholarius list Greek versions by Gennadius of Thomas's work On Being and Essence and of his commentary on Aristotle's On the Soul.

How they're remembered

  • Tocco says the friars at Cologne called him the Dumb Ox for his silence and, in a report he marks as hearsay, that Albert the Great answered that this ox would one day bellow so loud that the whole world would hear; he also says so many students crowded his classes at Paris that the room barely held them, and that his way of teaching was short, clear and easy.
  • Tocco says he refused the archbishopric of Naples offered by a bull of Clement IV, and excused himself from King Louis's table because of the Summa he was then dictating.
  • Tocco says a girl was sent to tempt him in his room and that he drove her out with a burning brand from the fire, after which angels girded him, and that his sister, whom their parents had sent to lead him astray, was turned by him to the love of God; these are wonders told in Tocco's voice. Tocco also says a voice from the crucifix at Naples said Thomas, you have written well of me, and asked what reward he would have; the Summa's third part was then being written.
  • Tocco says Innocent IV, told of the seizure by the friars, ordered the emperor to punish the brothers, and the emperor had them seized.
  • Bartholomew says that on the road to Lyon, when Thomas struck his head on a fallen tree, Reginald told him that he and Bonaventure would be made cardinals and raise their orders, and that Thomas answered that he could never be as useful to the order in any other state and would never change his own; Bartholomew had it from Abbot Roffredo, who was there.
  • Tocco and Bartholomew both give his words at communion: he took the sacrament as the price of the redemption of his soul, for whose love he had studied, kept vigil, labored, preached and taught, he had never said anything against it, and if he had said anything wrongly he left it all to the correction of the Roman Church.
  • The sixteenth-century Life of Bonaventure by Galesini says that on the day Thomas won his chair at Paris Bonaventure won his and gave him first place from humility, and that both were called by Gregory X to Lyon; the July Bollandists doubt it, since the authors they know say nothing of the two studying together.
  • In the Summa he tests Boethius's definition of a person, a person being an individual substance of a rational nature.
  • Tocco tells of his book against William of Saint-Amour and others in the quarrel at Paris over the friars, and its dating in Tocco is uncertain. The Bollandists tell of the later moves of his body.

What Is Uncertain

  • Witnesses. Gerard de Frachet's book of the friars was written in Thomas's lifetime, and the Bollandists say Gerard died in 1271; it does not name Thomas, and the Bollandists identify him. William of Tocco heard him teach and preach, promoted his canonization and wrote about forty-five years after his death; the Bollandists print his Life and the sworn inquiry of 1319. Bartholomew of Capua, logothete of the kingdom of Sicily, saw Thomas as a young man at Naples and testified under oath in 1319, mostly from hearsay.
  • His birth. The Bollandists reckon the start of 1225 from Tocco's age at his death; Walter's note to Tyndale says 1224. A notary's act of 1650 says Roccasecca held by old tradition that he was born there.
  • Monte Cassino. Tocco says his parents sent him at five to be taught holy ways and, in a hermit's prophecy, that they hoped he would rise to its head. Bartholomew, from common talk, says his father made him a monk as a boy meaning to place him over the abbey of Monte Cassino. No held source gives this as the reason for the seizure.
  • Who seized him and for how long. Gerard says his kinsmen seized him on the road, held him in a remote castle for about a year and could not make him put off the habit, then despaired and let him go. Tocco says his mother sent word to her sons who were with the emperor, that they seized him, held him for nearly two years, and that she let him down from a window by a rope. Bartholomew says it was by his father's favor and power, that he was held in the father's castle for more than a year, refused the monastic or lay clothes his father offered, and that the father freed him at his wife's many prayers. Conway's English renders Gerard's about a year as for more than a year, which the Latin does not say. Walter's note to Tyndale says it was against the will of his widowed mother. The scenes of the girl, the angels and the window are Tocco's alone in the held sources, although the inquiry of 1319 repeats the girl from a later witness.
  • How he entered the order. Tocco says the abbot of Monte Cassino advised sending him to Naples to study and that Brother John of San Giuliano drew him to the Dominicans; Bartholomew says he entered while still not grown and that the friars, fearing his father's power, had him taken out of the kingdom and sent to their general house of study. The years 1243 and 1244 are the Bollandists' and Conway's.
  • Paris. Gerard says he was made a master of theology; the year is open (the Bollandists give 1253 or the next, Echard not before 1257). Galesini's same-day claim for him and Bonaventure is doubted by the July Bollandists.
  • What the Summa was for. The prologue says its aim is to teach beginners in good order. That faith and reason cannot truly conflict is his teaching inside it, in his own words: since faith rests on a truth that cannot fail, and the opposite of a truth cannot be proved, the proofs brought against the faith are not true proofs but arguments that can be answered. He also writes that grace does not destroy nature but perfects it, so natural reason must serve faith, and that sacred teaching uses the philosophers where reason could know the truth. In the same article he holds that the existence of God can be proved in five ways and is not an article of faith but a preamble to the articles. What he holds reason cannot do is prove the articles of faith themselves.
  • The case against God. The first objection of the article on whether God exists, which the brief gives, is answered in the Summa, as Thomas cites Augustine's Handbook, by saying that a supremely good God would let no evil be in his works unless he were so powerful and good that he could bring good even from evil; that answer is to the first argument only.
  • Heretics. In the Summa, II-II, question 11, article 3, Thomas writes that heretics deserve not only to be separated from the church by excommunication but to be put out of the world by death, since it is much graver to corrupt the faith, by which the soul lives, than to forge money; he says the church warns a first and second time and then, if the man is still obstinate, leaves him to the secular judgment to be put to death.
  • The end of the writing. Bartholomew says that after he said Mass in the chapel of Saint Nicholas at Naples he was moved by a wonderful change and wrote and dictated nothing more, leaving off in the third part of the Summa in the treatise on penance. His companion Reginald asked why he had left so great a work, and Thomas answered that he could not; pressed again he said that everything he had written seemed to him straw. Reginald told the countess, Thomas's sister, that he had been in this state from around the feast of Saint Nicholas. Under a promise of secrecy Thomas added that all he had written seemed to him straw beside what he had seen and what had been revealed to him. The chain is that Reginald, dying years later, confessed it to John del Giudice, who told Bartholomew at Anagni shortly before Boniface was seized; so it is third-hand, sworn in 1319. Tocco says instead that it happened at San Severino, his sister's castle, where Thomas was in ecstasy, and has him say to Reginald that the end of his writing had come because what he had written and taught seemed small. What he saw is not known. In the printed Latin the words Reginald, I cannot, are clear; the rest of the sentence is damaged in both the 1865 print and the 1735 print.
  • The Summa's end. The Leonine edition ends the third part at question 90 on the parts of penance, with the Supplement after it; the fourteenth-century note the editors print says he did not finish the last part because he was prevented by death, which does not fit Bartholomew's date for the stopping.
  • The road to Lyon. Bartholomew says he was going to the Council of Lyon at Gregory X's call when he struck his head on a fallen tree, and that he fell ill at the castle of Maenza and asked to be carried to the monastery of Fossanova, at whose door he said, This is my rest for ever and ever. Tocco says he carried a small book against the Greeks. The remark Bartholomew attaches to Reginald about cardinals does not fit the tree's own entry on Bonaventure, which has Gregory X name him a cardinal in 1273, before this road.
  • His death. Tocco says he died at the morning hour on 7 March 1274 in the fourth year of Gregory X, completing his forty-ninth year. The Bollandists tell of the later moves of his body.
  • Canonization and honors. Tocco, who promoted the cause, reports the words of John XXII; the bull of 1323 is not held. The title Angelic Doctor is given by his admirers, by Walter's note; the rubric of Pius V's bull of 1567 as printed uses it, and no held text gives a date it began. Pius V ordered the feast kept like those of the four Doctors, not that he be named one of them.
  • Not held in the Library: the canonization bull of 1323, Bernard Gui's and Ptolemy of Lucca's accounts, Thomas of Cantimpre, the Summa against the Gentiles, Leo XIII's Aeterni Patris, the Paris condemnations of 1270 and 1277, and the year he began the Summa.
