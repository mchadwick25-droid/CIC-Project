The Imperial Church Era · c. 364-425 · Constantinople

Constantinople, today in Turkey

Philostorgius

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Anomoean (Eunomian) Christianity.

Known for
Church historian of the Eunomian party, whose twelve-book history survives only in the hostile summary of Photius, a later patriarch of Constantinople.
Dates
Born about 364, by Edward Walford's reckoning, or about 368, by J. Quasten's; came to Constantinople at twenty; his history runs down to 425, by Walford's date
Place
Borissus in Cappadocia; Constantinople
Role
Church historian

About This Person

At twenty, Philostorgius came to Constantinople and saw Eunomius of Cyzicus, the leader of his party. In his history he praised the man's looks and speech. He admitted that Eunomius lisped, then made even the lisp a sign of grace. Photius, a later patriarch of Constantinople, summed up the history. He scorned it. Philostorgius, he says, "is not ashamed to exalt this lisp into a mark of great grace and elegance."

His book is lost. It survives through Photius's summary, and Photius was hostile throughout. He calls Philostorgius an "impious contriver of lies."

Through Photius, Philostorgius tells of his family. His mother's father, Anysius, was a priest at Borissus in Cappadocia. His mother, Eulampia, came from families who held "the consubstantial faith" of Nicaea. His father, Carterius, followed Eunomius and won her over. She then won "her brothers, then her father," and in time the rest of her family.

Photius names Aetius of Antioch and Eunomius as leaders of those who held that the Son is unlike the Father in substance. Philostorgius praised them both. He rated Aetius higher in argument, and Eunomius higher for "a style well suited to learners." He even faulted Arius, whom he praised, for teaching that God cannot be known, "not even by His own only-begotten Son." He wrote a book in praise of Eunomius, and another against Porphyry of Tyre "on behalf of the Christians."

His history ran in twelve books, from the quarrel of Arius with Alexander of Alexandria to 425, by Edward Walford's date. The first letters of the books spelled his name. Walford, who translated the summary, notes his love of far lands and wonders.

A later Greek dictionary keeps a passage in his own words. In it he praises three foes of his party, Basil of Caesarea, Gregory of Nazianzus and Apollinaris of Laodicea. They argued so well for the Nicene faith, he says, that "Athanasius must be judged a mere child in comparison with them."

Key Works

  • Church History: twelve books from the quarrel of Arius with Alexander of Alexandria to the proclamation of Valentinian the Younger; the first letters of the books spelled his name; lost, and known from Photius's summary and a few fragments
  • Encomium of Eunomius: a work in praise of Eunomius of Cyzicus, which Photius says he "does not blush to avow"; not held
  • Against Porphyry: a book he wrote "on behalf of the Christians", as Photius reports; not held

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Photius, a patriarch of Constantinople whom Edward Walford dates to 853, kept a summary of the history in extracts. It is the main way the work is known.
  • J. Quasten, quoted in the 2002 note to the electronic edition, says the surviving remains show that he used excellent sources no longer extant, especially documents of Arian origin.

How they're remembered

  • Photius opens his summary by calling the history "an encomium on the heretical party, and an attack and assault upon the orthodox, rather than a history."
  • Photius calls him an "impious contriver of lies".
  • Edward Walford, who put the summary into English in 1855, says he was "rather inclined to credulity" about portents and wonders, and filled his work with details of remote countries.

What Is Uncertain

  • His history is lost. It survives mainly in the summary of Photius, who was hostile to it throughout. What Philostorgius says reaches us in Photius's words and with Photius's judgments.
  • Walford puts his birth about 364. Quasten, quoted in the 2002 note, puts it about 368 and calls him a layman. No source used here gives his own date of birth.
  • Through Photius, Philostorgius says that at twenty he came to Constantinople and saw Eunomius. Photius reports his praise of Eunomius's looks, his speech and even his lisp.
  • Photius reports his story that Eunomius answered a critic in five books, and that the critic died of remorse after reading the first. Walford's English leaves open whether Basil of Caesarea or Apollinaris of Laodicea is meant. Photius calls it falsehood.
  • Photius says the ninth book is full of miracles worked by Aetius of Antioch, Eunomius and others, "inventions of the author".
  • The fragment that praises Basil, Gregory of Nazianzus and Apollinaris of Laodicea comes from the later Greek dictionary Walford calls Suidas, which gives it in Philostorgius's own words. It calls the teaching of his own side "that heresy".
  • Walford says it is uncertain whether he trained for the law or the church.
