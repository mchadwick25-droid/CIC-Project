The Church of Utrecht

The Netherlands, from 1723 — a Catholic chapter that got tired of waiting for Rome, elected its own archbishop, and has been electing them ever since.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 0:55

Read along

The Catholic Church in the Dutch Republic was a church without legal standing, tolerated in hidden chapels, and its clergy had absorbed a good deal of the Jansenist temper of the age. When Rome refused for years to confirm any archbishop for Utrecht, the cathedral chapter fell back on what it held to be its own ancient right and, in 1723, elected Cornelius Steenoven itself. A French missionary bishop, Dominique Marie Varlet — himself already in trouble with Rome — consecrated him in 1724, which gave the small church a valid succession and a permanent breach with the papacy in the same act. It stayed Catholic in everything else, went on electing its own bishops, and survived on a few thousand members for a century and a half.

Documented Stories

A Bishop on His Way to Persia

Dominique Marie Varlet, a Paris priest who had served ten years in the Mississippi missions, was consecrated in 1719 as coadjutor bishop of Babylon, with his seat at Hamadan in Persia, and set out overland. Passing through Amsterdam, he was asked by the Catholic clergy of the Dutch mission to confirm the children of the hidden churches; he confirmed some six hundred in a few days and went on to Persia. There a letter reached him from Rome suspending him from his functions, for having confirmed without the nuncio's leave and for having failed, at his consecration, to sign the formulary against Jansenism, which he had been allowed to omit. Meanwhile the cathedral chapter of Utrecht, which held that its right to elect the archbishop had never lapsed, elected Cornelius Steenoven on 27 April 1723 and asked Rome to confirm him; Rome did not reply. The chapter took the silence as refusal and asked Varlet, a validly consecrated bishop under suspension, to act. He hesitated for a year, took canonists' opinions, and on 15 October 1724, in a private house in Amsterdam, consecrated Steenoven archbishop of Utrecht, alone, without assisting bishops. Steenoven died five months later, and Varlet consecrated his successor, and two more archbishops after that, before he died in 1742, still appealing. The church he had given a succession went on electing, consecrating and appealing to Rome for a general council, and a hundred and fifty years later became the mother church of the Old Catholics.

Connections

In Its Own Record

This church grew out of the Jansenist quarrel: the Utrecht cathedral chapter elected Cornelius Steenoven without Rome's approval in 1723, and Dominique Varlet consecrated him in 1724. The small communion that resulted survived and became the root of the later Old Catholic Union of Utrecht.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

When Rome refused for years to confirm an archbishop for the tolerated but legally unrecognized Catholic church of the Dutch Republic, its cathedral chapter fell back on what it held to be its own ancient right and elected Cornelius Steenoven itself in 1723. A French missionary bishop, Dominique Varlet — already in trouble with Rome himself — consecrated him the following year, giving the small church a valid succession and a permanent breach with the papacy in the same act, though it kept the Catholic faith in everything else.

Legacy

It is still there, and it grew a family. When the First Vatican Council defined papal infallibility in 1870, Catholics in Germany, Switzerland and Austria who rejected the definition needed bishops, and Utrecht — the one Western church with an unquestioned Catholic succession outside Rome — supplied them. The resulting Old Catholic churches bound themselves together in the Declaration of Utrecht in 1889, and that Union of Utrecht continues today, in full communion with the Anglican churches since 1931. St Gertrude's Cathedral in Utrecht is its mother church.

Doctrinal Floor Note

No question of creed arises. This church held, and holds, the Catholic faith it always had; the break with Rome was over jurisdiction — who may appoint a bishop — and over the grace controversy of the previous century, neither of which touches the creed.

How Sure the Record Is

The 1723 chapter election and the 1724 Varlet consecration are recorded in standard historical references; the Utrecht chapter's own archives and the later records of the Union of Utrecht provide fuller documentation of the church's early history.
