Chinese Catholicism — the First Chinese Bishops to the Divided Church

China's Catholics, from their first Chinese bishops in 1926 to a church divided over who could name its bishops.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:18

Read along

In 1926 Pope Pius XI consecrated Chinese bishops in Saint Peter's Basilica. His successor Pius XII later recalled them, in Pius XI's words, as "the flowers and . . . latest buds of the Chinese episcopate". On 18 February 1946 Thomas Tian Gengxin became the first Chinese cardinal. On 11 April 1946 Pius XII set up a full hierarchy in China, of 137 dioceses and prefectures in 20 regions. Chinese clergy led 28 of them. From 17 July to 2 August 1957 the authorities held a national assembly of Catholic representatives in Beijing. It approved the statutes of the Chinese Catholic Patriotic Association. In 1958 Pius XII wrote that missionaries had been driven out, and that bishops, priests and laypeople had been imprisoned. He ruled that bishops elected and consecrated against Rome's orders had no power to teach or to govern. In May 1980 a national assembly under state control set up a bishops' conference. Rome judged such bodies incompatible with Catholic teaching.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 1926 Pope Pius XI consecrated Chinese bishops in Saint Peter's Basilica. Twenty years later Pius XII set up a full hierarchy of dioceses in China. In 1957 the state called an assembly that approved a Catholic Patriotic Association. The church then divided over who could name its bishops.

Legacy

In 2007 Benedict XVI wrote to China's Catholics. Bodies set up by the state to stand above the bishops, he wrote, did "not correspond to Catholic doctrine". Richard Madsen told their story as one of tragedy and hope.

Doctrinal Floor Note

No question. The Catholic Church holds the Nicene Creed. In 1958 Pius XII ruled that bishops consecrated against Rome's orders had no power to teach or to govern. Their sacramental acts, he wrote, were valid if their consecration was valid, but gravely illicit.

How Sure the Record Is

Good. Richard Madsen's China's Catholics (1998) comes from a university press. Two papal letters, of 1958 and 2007, add to it. So does a study of the 1946 hierarchy from the Holy Spirit Study Centre in Hong Kong.
