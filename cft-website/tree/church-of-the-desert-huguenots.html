The Church of the Desert

Languedoc and the Cévennes, 1685 to 1787 — a church that was legally abolished and rebuilt itself in quarries and forests, holding synods for the seventy-two years from its first one to the day it was made legal again.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:18

Read along

When Louis XIV revoked the Edict of Nantes in 1685, French Protestantism was simply abolished. Its churches were pulled down, its pastors expelled, its people forbidden to emigrate and required to attend Mass. Those who stayed called what they did next 'the Desert', after Israel's wilderness — they met at night in ravines, quarries and woods, several hundred at a time, with lookouts posted. In 1715 a twenty-year-old lay preacher named Antoine Court gathered a handful of others and set about rebuilding an actual church. He held a synod, restored ordained ministry and discipline, discouraged the prophesying that had led to the Camisard rising, and founded a seminary at Lausanne to train pastors who went back over the border knowing they could hang for it. The penalties were exactly that — pastors executed, men sent to the galleys for life, women shut up in towers, Marie Durand among them for thirty-eight years. Until the Edict of Versailles in 1787 finally gave Protestants a civil existence again.

Documented Stories

The Last Pastor Hanged, and the Three Brothers

François Rochette was twenty-five and had been a pastor of the Desert in Quercy for two years, when on the night of 13 September 1761 he was stopped by a watch near Caussade, north of Montauban, without papers, and recognized. Three brothers of the Protestant gentry of the district, Jean, Pierre and Antoine Grenier, glassmakers by trade and sword-bearing gentlemen by rank, gathered a party to rescue him from the town jail; the attempt failed and they were taken with weapons. All four were sent to Toulouse, where the Parlement, the most Catholic of the sovereign courts of France, tried them under the edicts of 1724, which made a pastor's ministry a capital crime and armed assembly another. Rochette was condemned to be hanged and the brothers, as nobles, to be beheaded. On 19 February 1762, in the Place du Salin, Rochette went to the gallows in his shirt with a placard naming him minister of the pretended reformed religion, refused the crucifix, and sang, witnesses said, from Psalm 118; the three brothers were beheaded after him, the youngest first. Three weeks later the same court broke the Toulouse merchant Jean Calas on the wheel for a murder he had not committed, and Voltaire took up the merchant and made the city's Protestant blood a European scandal. The Desert's synods sent his mother a pension, and Paul Rabaut, who had been hidden in the hills for twenty years, wrote that the church would go on holding its assemblies and did.

The Synod in the Quarry

Antoine Court was nineteen, a self-taught preacher who had been holding assemblies in the Vivarais and Languedoc since he was seventeen, when he called a meeting of the preachers he knew for 21 August 1715, in a disused quarry at Les Montèzes near Monoblet, in the hills between Anduze and Sauve. Nine came. What they knew was that the Protestants who had stayed in France had had no organized church for thirty years, that the prophets who had carried them through the Camisard war had also led them into a rebellion that burned the Cévennes, and that the surviving assemblies were disorderly, unlettered and full of inspired women. The synod, as they called it, passed a set of articles: that worship was to follow the old Reformed order, with Scripture, psalms and a sermon; that elders were to be chosen in every place to keep discipline and to gather the faithful; that preachers were to be examined and set apart; and that the inspirations were to be tested against Scripture and, in effect, ended, and that women were not to speak in the assemblies at all. He was ordained in 1718 by a pastor who had received orders in Zurich, held provincial and national synods through the 1720s, and founded the Lausanne seminary in 1729 to train men who would go back knowing they might hang. Some of the prophets' families never forgave him, and the Desert church he built was the one that survived to 1787.

Letters from the Tour de Constance

Marie Durand was arrested in 1730, at about nineteen, because her brother Pierre was a pastor of the Desert; her father was already in prison and her brother was hanged at Montpellier two years later. She was put in the Tour de Constance at Aigues-Mortes, a medieval round tower on the edge of the salt marshes of the Camargue, where the crown held Protestant women who would not abjure, some twenty or thirty at a time, in a circular room. She was there for thirty-eight years. Her letters, of which some fifty survive, begin in the 1750s and are addressed to Paul Rabaut, to the Walloon churches of Amsterdam and to Protestant benefactors in Geneva and Holland: they ask, with dignity and persistence, for money and cloth for the prisoners, describe the winters, the damp and the sickness, and arrange for the education of her orphaned niece Anne, who had been brought up in the tower with her. They do not describe her own faith at length and never discuss abjuring, which would have freed her any day. The word RESISTER cut into the stone rim of the well in the prisoners' room is traditionally hers and is not provably anyone's. She was released in April 1768, at fifty-seven, by the prince of Beauvau, the new governor of Languedoc, who visited the tower and ordered it emptied; she went home to a ruined house at Le Bouschet-de-Pranles and lived eight more years on a pension from Amsterdam.

In Its Own Record

This is the story of what survived after Louis XIV's 1685 Revocation of the Edict of Nantes outlawed French Protestantism. Antoine Court's first Desert synod in 1715 began rebuilding a proscribed church in hiding, at the cost of galley slavery and, for Marie Durand, thirty-eight years imprisoned in the Tour de Constance. The Edict of Toleration in 1787 finally ended that illegality. The Camisard prophetic uprising belongs to this same story's opening decades.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

When Louis XIV outlawed French Protestantism in 1685, its people met at night in ravines and quarries, calling what they did 'the Desert' after Israel's wilderness. In 1715 a twenty-year-old lay preacher, Antoine Court, began rebuilding an actual church there — holding a synod, restoring ordained ministry, and founding a seminary at Lausanne to train pastors who returned knowing they could hang for it — while Marie Durand spent thirty-eight years imprisoned in a tower rather than recant, until the Edict of Versailles finally granted Protestants a civil existence again in 1787.

Legacy

It survived, and it is the direct ancestor of the Reformed Church in France. The church that came into the open after 1787 was Antoine Court's church, organized in synods and served by trained pastors. The Revolution finished what the edict began, and Rabaut Saint-Étienne sat in the National Assembly that declared freedom of religion in 1789 — he was guillotined four years later. In the Cévennes the memory is deliberately kept: the Musée du Désert stands in a Camisard leader's birthplace, and an assembly gathers there every September.

Experience It Today

  • Musée du Désert, Mialet, Cévennes — the museum of the French Protestant underground, in a Cévenol farmhouse, with an assembly held there each September
  • The Tour de Constance, Aigues-Mortes — the tower where Protestant women, Marie Durand among them, were held for decades; open as a national monument
