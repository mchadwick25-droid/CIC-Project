The Church of the East — Three Patriarchates

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

The Church of the East began these years with two rival patriarchs. One lived in the mountains of Hakkari. Malech says a patriarch of that line, Denkha Shimon, later moved its seat to the village of Qudshanis. The other lived on the plain, at Alqosh and Mosul.

Each patriarch of the plain held an order from the Sultan, called a firman. It confirmed his power over his people in the empire. The mountain patriarchs had no such order. Badger writes that the Kurdish emirs tolerated and protected them.

Rome then added a third line. In 1672 Joseph, the church's archbishop at Diyarbakır, broke with the patriarch Elias. Labourt says he followed the advice of Capuchin missionaries. In 1681 Pope Innocent XI gave him the pallium and the title of patriarch. Wigram dates the new line to 1680.

Five men named Joseph held this see in turn. Badger says neither the old bishops nor the Ottoman government recognized the line at first. It did not reach beyond Diyarbakır.

In 1778 the line of the plain began to turn to Rome. Patriarch Elia died of plague at Alqosh on 29 April. His nephew Hormizd, a metropolitan, wrote the next part himself, in Badger's English: “So on the 30th of April I embraced all that the holy Church believes.” Another nephew, Yeshua-yau, was to be patriarch. Within a year he turned back, and the two became enemies. In 1782 Pope Pius VI let Hormizd act as patriarch, but not use the patriarch's seal.

The sources disagree on when the plain line truly joined Rome. Wigram puts it in 1778. Labourt puts it in 1830, after Rome had cut Hormizd off in 1818. Wigram says the Joseph line was then allowed to lapse, though its last holder lived until 1828.

Sources
