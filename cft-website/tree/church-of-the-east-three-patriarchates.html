The Church of the East — Three Patriarchates

Diyarbakır, Mosul and the Hakkari mountains, 1650 to 1815. An ancient church with three patriarchs at once. In 1778 the patriarch's nephew turned to Rome.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 2:14

Read along

The Church of the East began these years with two rival patriarchs. One lived in the mountains of Hakkari. Malech says a patriarch of that line, Denkha Shimon, later moved its seat to the village of Qudshanis. The other lived on the plain, at Alqosh and Mosul.

Each patriarch of the plain held an order from the Sultan, called a firman. It confirmed his power over his people in the empire. The mountain patriarchs had no such order. Badger writes that the Kurdish emirs tolerated and protected them.

Rome then added a third line. In 1672 Joseph, the church's archbishop at Diyarbakır, broke with the patriarch Elias. Labourt says he followed the advice of Capuchin missionaries. In 1681 Pope Innocent XI gave him the pallium and the title of patriarch. Wigram dates the new line to 1680.

Five men named Joseph held this see in turn. Badger says neither the old bishops nor the Ottoman government recognized the line at first. It did not reach beyond Diyarbakır.

In 1778 the line of the plain began to turn to Rome. Patriarch Elia died of plague at Alqosh on 29 April. His nephew Hormizd, a metropolitan, wrote the next part himself, in Badger's English: “So on the 30th of April I embraced all that the holy Church believes.” Another nephew, Yeshua-yau, was to be patriarch. Within a year he turned back, and the two became enemies. In 1782 Pope Pius VI let Hormizd act as patriarch, but not use the patriarch's seal.

The sources disagree on when the plain line truly joined Rome. Wigram puts it in 1778. Labourt puts it in 1830, after Rome had cut Hormizd off in 1818. Wigram says the Joseph line was then allowed to lapse, though its last holder lived until 1828.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

The Church of the East had two patriarchs in 1650, one in the mountains and one on the plain. In 1681 the pope named a third, at Diyarbakır. In 1778 the patriarch of the plain died of plague, and the next day his nephew Hormizd turned to Rome. He wrote down the date: 30 April.

Legacy

By 1815 the church stood divided. On the plain, Hormizd led a party in union with Rome, but Rome had not yet made him patriarch. The line of the mountains stayed outside Rome's obedience. Its later story is told in the entry on the Church of the East and the Chaldean Consolidation.

How Sure the Record Is

The evidence is thin. Most texts come from outside the mountain church. Badger was an English churchman who disliked Rome's work. Wigram led a church mission to the Assyrians. Labourt wrote for a Catholic reference work. Malech was an Assyrian Protestant from Urmia. He gives the mountain side in a few lines. Labourt says few documents survive for these years.

  • George Percy Badger, The Nestorians and their Rituals, vol. 1 (London, 1852) (primary) — An English churchman who met the patriarchs. He prints the Syriac autobiography of Hormizd in English. Held in the Library. He does not like Rome's work.
  • W. A. Wigram, The Assyrians and their Neighbours (London, 1929) (secondary) — An English churchman's history of the Assyrians. Held in the Library.
  • George David Malech, History of the Syrian Nation and the Old Evangelical-Apostolic Church of the East (Minneapolis, 1910) (secondary) — By an Assyrian Protestant from Urmia. Held in the Library. He gives the mountain line in a few lines.
  • Jérôme Labourt, "Chaldean Christians", Catholic Encyclopedia, vol. 3 (1908) (secondary) — A Catholic reference article. Read in full text online. It says few documents survive for these years.
