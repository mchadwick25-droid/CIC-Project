Primasius of Hadrumetum

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Latin African Christianity under Byzantium.

Known for
Bishop of Hadrumetum in the African province of Byzacena, who wrote a commentary on the Apocalypse of John drawing on Augustine of Hippo and the Donatist Tyconius, and who signed Pope Vigilius's ruling on the Three Chapters at Constantinople in 553.
Also called
Primasius Adrumetanus, Primasius d'Hadrumète
Dates
Vigilius's ruling, which he signed, is dated the day before the Ides of May in Justinian's twenty-seventh year, which Otto Günther gives as 14 May 553. Victor of Tunnuna tells of his summons to Constantinople under the year Theodor Mommsen gives as 551. Victor tells of the fall and death under 552, an entry that runs past that year, since Primasius signed in May 553. No source used here gives the year of his birth or death
Place
Hadrumetum, also called Justinianopolis, in Byzacena, as his own signature names it; Constantinople, on the business of the African church
Role
Bishop of Hadrumetum; commentator on Scripture

About This Person

In Constantinople, an African bishop met a fellow countryman, Junillus. The first thing he asked, as was his way, was whether any Greek there burned to study the holy books. Junillus tells this in Latin, in the letter that opens his handbook on the Bible. He told the bishop of a Persian named Paul, taught in the school at Nisibis. The bishop pressed him to write down Paul's rules for reading Scripture, until Junillus gave in.

The bishop was Primasius of Hadrumetum, in the African province of Byzacena. He had come, the German scholar Heinrich Kihn reckons, over the Three Chapters, writings the emperor Justinian wanted the church to condemn. Victor of Tunnuna says the emperor summoned him there with other African bishops. Victor's editor, Theodor Mommsen, gives the year as 551.

In May 553, as the Second Council of Constantinople met, he signed the ruling of Pope Vigilius on the Three Chapters. His signature survives. It names his city as Hadrumetum, also called Justinianopolis.

The rest is told only by Victor of Tunnuna, who defended the Three Chapters and tells it as betrayal. Victor says Primasius was sent to a monastery. When Boethius, the head bishop of Byzacena, died, Primasius agreed to condemn the Three Chapters so that he might take his place. Back home he persecuted those who held what he had once held. His own province's bishops condemned him, and he died an unhappy death. Kihn, writing in 1880, warned that Victor wrote as a party man.

His five books on the Revelation of John survive, as Cassiodorus, a writer of his own time, describes them. In the preface, Primasius says he drew on Augustine of Hippo and also on Tyconius, a Donatist, and cut away his errors. A precious gem lying in dung, he wrote, should be picked up and restored to its worth. In the same way, wherever truth shines, it should be won back for the Catholic Church.

Key Works

  • Commentary on the Apocalypse of John, in five books: dedicated to Castorius; his preface says he added what he found in Augustine and took from the Donatist Tyconius what agrees with sound sense, cutting and correcting the rest; Cassiodorus, a writer of his own time, describes it as a careful exposition in five books
  • What Makes a Heretic (Quid faciat haereticum): Cassiodorus names one book under this title, and Isidore of Seville names three books on heresies, sent to Bishop Fortunatus, to finish what Augustine's book on heresies had left undone at his death. The notice from Cave that Migne prints calls it long lost; it reports an old manuscript that gives the surviving Praedestinatus to Primasius, and doubts it.
  • Commentaries on the letters of Paul: printed in Migne's volume under his name; Otto Bardenhewer counts the commentary under the name of Primasius of Hadrumetum among the later reworkings of Pelagius's commentary

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Junillus wrote his handbook on reading the Bible, On the Parts of Divine Law, at his urging, from the rules of Paul the Persian of Nisibis, and dedicated it to him.
  • F. C. Burkitt names him first among the writers who used Tyconius as their main authority on the Apocalypse. Paul Monceaux says Tyconius's commentary can be largely restored today through him and the Spanish priest Beatus, the two who copied it most systematically.

How they're remembered

  • Cassiodorus calls him the eloquent bishop of Justinianopolis and sets his commentary among the expositions of the Apocalypse in his Institutions.
  • Joseph Mesnage calls him one of the most learned bishops of his time. Paul Monceaux calls him an influential bishop, a party leader and an author famous in his day.

At Major Councils

  • Second Council of Constantinople, 553: African bishop; signed Vigilius's Constitutum

What Is Uncertain

  • His fall. What happened after he signed is told only by Victor of Tunnuna, who defended the Three Chapters and tells it as a betrayal. Victor says he was sent to a monastery, agreed to condemn the Three Chapters so that he might succeed Boethius as head bishop of Byzacena, persecuted his former side at home, was condemned by his own province's bishops and died an unhappy death. No second witness tells it. Heinrich Kihn wrote in 1880 that how much truth the account holds is hard to say, and that it is not free of party coloring.
  • The monastery. The manuscripts of Victor read aquimetensis, and Migne prints Aquimetensis. Theodor Mommsen corrects it to Acoemetensi, the monastery of the Sleepless monks. Heinrich Kihn quotes the line with Adrumetinus, naming Primasius by his city, and names no monastery. He places the sending after Primasius's refusal to attend the council in May 553, though Victor tells it under 552.
  • His refusal to attend. Heinrich Kihn reports from the council's acts, in Mansi's collection, that on 8 May 553 the council named Primasius first among the Western bishops still keeping away, and that he answered its envoys, "Papa non praesente non venio": with the pope not present, I will not come. The acts themselves are not among the sources used here. A notice Migne prints from Cave also says he would not attend though often called.
  • His earlier stand. Kihn reports, again from Mansi, that Primasius signed Vigilius's sentence against Theodore Askidas on 14 August 551, and that he first appears at a synod of Byzacena in 541. Neither record is among the sources used here.
  • Whether he became head bishop. Victor gives his aim, to succeed Boethius, and does not say that he did. A note to Liberatus of Carthage that Migne prints calls him primate of Byzacena.
  • His see. Joannes Gagneius, a Paris theologian, in the dedication of his edition to King Francis I that Migne prints, calls him bishop of Utica. His own signature names Hadrumetum, and Kihn says Baronius had already set Utica aside.
  • Another Primasius. Primosus, deacon and later bishop of Carthage, is spelled Primasius in Migne's text of Victor; Heinrich Kihn warns that he is often confused with the bishop of Hadrumetum.
  • His age. Andrea Gallandi wonders whether Junillus calls him Father for his venerable gray hair.
