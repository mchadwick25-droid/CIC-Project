The Early Medieval Era · c. 700-1009 · Palestine, Syria

Melkite Arabic Christianity

Palestine and Syria, c. 700 to 1009 — the first Christians to argue their faith in Arabic, writing from desert monasteries that lay beyond any emperor's reach.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:18

Read along

When the Arab conquests cut Palestine and Syria off from Constantinople, the Chalcedonian Christians of those provinces — Melkites, 'the emperor's people', as their neighbors labeled them — found themselves a minority under a new government and, within a few generations, doing something no church had done before: praying, arguing and writing theology in Arabic. Their intellectual heart was the monastic world of the Judean desert, above all the monastery of Mar Saba, where John of Damascus, from a family that had served in the Muslim administration, wrote the great defense of religious images that Byzantine emperors could not silence because he lived beyond their reach. A generation later Theodore Abu Qurrah was writing theology in Arabic for a mixed audience of Christians and Muslims, and anonymous works such as the treatise now called On the Triune Nature of God were explaining the Trinity in the vocabulary of the world around them. The window closes hard in 1009, when the caliph al-Hakim ordered the Church of the Holy Sepulchre razed.

Documented Stories

Twenty Monks in the Cistern

Stephen Mansur, a monk of Mar Saba and a relative of John of Damascus, was in the monastery when the raiders came in March 797, and his account is one of the few in this era written by someone who saw what he describes. The Judean desert had become lawless as Abbasid authority weakened; bands of Arab tribesmen had already attacked the laura once that month and been bought off. On 20 March they returned in force, convinced the monks were hiding gold. Stephen names the dead. They beat the monks to make them show the treasure; the monks, who had none, offered the church plate. They shot the deacon Sergius as he ran for help; they cut open old Patricius; and finally they drove some twenty monks into the cave that served as the monastery’s cistern, lit a fire of brushwood at the mouth, and let the smoke do the rest. Eighteen died there; two more died of their injuries. The raiders left with little. Stephen’s narrative, written in Greek and soon translated into Georgian and Arabic, spends as much time on the monks’ conduct as on the raiders’: they did not flee to the fortified tower, he says, because the fathers would not leave the sick and old, and they did not resist. The Palestinian church added them to its calendar. Under Islamic rule the Melkite monasteries were not persecuted by the state, but they were undefended, and this is what that meant.

Why the Christians of Edessa Stopped Bowing to the Icon

Theodore Abu Qurrah, bishop of Harran and a former monk of Mar Saba, was the first Christian theologian to write his major works in Arabic, and his Treatise on the Veneration of the Holy Icons opens with a pastoral emergency rather than a doctrine. A friend from Edessa had written to him, he says, that ‘many Christians are abandoning prostration to the icon of Christ our God’ — the famous image of Edessa — ‘because non-Christians, and especially those who claim to have in hand a scripture sent down from God, rebuke them for it, and reproach them with idolatry’. The Christians had no answer ready, were ashamed, and stopped. Abu Qurrah writes to give them the answer in the language their neighbors used. He argues from Scripture that God commanded images for the tabernacle, from the Incarnation that the invisible had made himself visible, and from ordinary social practice that bowing to a king’s portrait honors the king; he meets Muslim and Jewish objections one by one, quoting them fairly; and he closes by telling the Edessenes not to be embarrassed. It is the same argument John of Damascus had made in Greek two generations earlier against the Byzantine emperors — but Abu Qurrah’s opponents were not emperors, they were the people his readers did business with every day, and the icon in question was being abandoned not by decree but by embarrassment. The book records a church learning to explain itself in Arabic because it had no choice.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Cut off from Constantinople by the Arab conquests, the Chalcedonian Christians of Palestine and Syria became the first church to pray and argue theology in Arabic. From the monastery of Mar Saba, John of Damascus wrote his defense of religious images beyond the reach of the Byzantine emperors trying to suppress them, and a generation later Theodore Abu Qurrah was writing Arabic theology for an audience of Christians and Muslims alike.

Legacy

Arabic-speaking Chalcedonian Christianity did not stop here. The same communion lives on in the Greek Orthodox churches of Antioch and Jerusalem. After a split in the 1700s, it also lives on in the Melkite Greek Catholic Church. John of Damascus's case for images became the settled teaching on icons in the Orthodox world. A council in 754 cursed him by name. The council of 787 that brought back the icons undid its work. His Exposition of the Orthodox Faith was put into Latin, and Western scholars read it for centuries. Mar Saba, where he wrote, is still a working monastery.

Experience It Today
