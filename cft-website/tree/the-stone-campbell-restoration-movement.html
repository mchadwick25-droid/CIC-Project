The Stone-Campbell Restoration Movement

The American frontier, 1801 to 1906 — preachers who wanted no creed but the Bible and no name but Christian, and who argued the case in public debates that ran for days.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:14

Read along

On opposite sides of the Appalachians, two sets of Presbyterians came independently to the same conclusion. That the creeds and denominational names Christians used to define themselves were the very things keeping them apart. Barton W. Stone and his colleagues dissolved their own presbytery in 1804 with a document called the Last Will and Testament of the Springfield Presbytery. Five years later Thomas Campbell published the Declaration and Address, and with his son Alexander built a movement around the plea for no creed but the Bible and no name but Christian. In 1832 the two groups shook hands and merged. Debating was their native genre — Alexander Campbell argued in public for days at a stretch against skeptics, Presbyterians and a Roman Catholic bishop, and published the transcripts himself — and by 1906, when the federal religious census listed the Churches of Christ separately for the first time, a movement founded to end division had recorded a division of its own.

Documented Stories

New Year's Day at Hill Street

By 1831 the Christians who had followed Barton Stone out of the Presbyterian church in 1804 and the Disciples who had followed Thomas and Alexander Campbell out of the Baptists were meeting in the same Kentucky towns, and asking why two bodies that both refused every name but Christian's and every creed but the Bible should be two. They differed on things that mattered: the Stone people were uneasy about baptism for the remission of sins, and the Campbell people about the Stone people's reserve on the Trinity. A meeting was called at the Hill Street church in Lexington for the last days of December 1831. On the Saturday John Smith, a Disciples preacher known as Raccoon John, rose to speak for the Campbell side. His speech was remembered and written down by those present: that God had but one people on the earth and had given them one book; that the differences between them were opinions, and no one should be required to believe an opinion in order to be received; that they should be no longer Campbellites or Stoneites, New Lights or Old Lights, but come to the Bible and to the Bible alone, and be one. Stone replied briefly that he had nothing to add and gave Smith his hand, and the congregation followed. On Sunday 1 January they took communion together, and Smith and John Rogers were sent out to ride the state together and bring the congregations in.

The Sand Creek Address and Declaration

By the 1880s the movement that had united at Lexington was divided between congregations, mostly northern and prosperous, that had adopted instrumental music and supported the American Christian Missionary Society, and congregations, mostly southern and rural, that regarded both as the kind of human addition the fathers had renounced. The argument had been fought in the papers for a generation, Lipscomb's Gospel Advocate against the innovations and Errett's Christian Standard for them. Daniel Sommer, an Indiana editor, decided that argument had failed. At the annual meeting of the Sand Creek church in Shelby County, Illinois, on 18 August 1889, before a crowd reckoned at five or six thousand, he preached for two hours, and then an elder read an Address and Declaration that Sommer had drafted: that the congregations there represented had borne long with the innovations, that the innovators had not repented, and that 'all such as are guilty of teaching, or allowing and practicing the many innovations and corruptions to which we have referred' could not, after a reasonable time, be regarded as brethren. It was printed and circulated. Lipscomb thought it too much and said so, but the line had been drawn in writing. When the United States census of religious bodies of 1906 asked Lipscomb whether the Churches of Christ were a separate body, he answered that they were, and the count listed them apart from the Disciples of Christ for the first time. Neither side has accepted that it was the one that left.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 1804 Barton Stone dissolved his own Presbyterian presbytery rather than keep a denominational name, and five years later Thomas Campbell published a plea for "no creed but the Bible and no name but Christian." The two movements merged in 1832, and Alexander Campbell argued their case in public debates that ran for days and that he then published himself. By 1906, a movement founded to end division had recorded one of its own, when the federal census counted the Churches of Christ separately for the first time.

Legacy

Three living streams descend from it — the Churches of Christ, the Christian Church (Disciples of Christ), and the Christian Churches and Churches of Christ — together numbering several million people worldwide, along with the colleges, journals and mission agencies they built. Its habits traveled further than its name: weekly communion in an otherwise low-church setting, baptism by immersion of believers, congregational independence, and a deep suspicion of anything that looks like a required creed.

Sources

Doctrinal Floor Note

The movement refused to make subscription to any creed a test of fellowship — "no creed but the Bible" — while its mainstream nonetheless taught the content the historic creeds state. Barton Stone himself held reservations about how the doctrine of the Trinity had been worded, and those are part of the record; they were a strand inside the movement rather than its confession. This is a description of what the movement actually did, not a charge against it.

How Sure the Record Is

Rich: journals, congregational records, debate transcripts (debating was the native genre)

  • Founding documents: Last Will and Testament of the Springfield Presbytery (1804); Thomas Campbell, Declaration and Address (1809) (primary) — These founding documents state the movement's 'no creed but the Bible' position in its own words.
  • The journals (native genre per census): Christian Baptist (1823-30), Millennial Harbinger (1830-70), Stone's Christian Messenger (1826-45) (primary) — These journals were the movement's own periodicals. Stone's reservations about how the doctrine of the Trinity was worded appear in the Messenger's exchanges.
  • Debate transcripts: Campbell-Walker (1820), Campbell-McCalla (1823), Campbell-Owen (1829), Campbell-Purcell (1837), Campbell-Rice (1843) (primary) — Public debate was one of this movement's characteristic genres, and these transcripts were published verbatim by the movement itself.
  • Congregational minute books; Stone's 1847 autobiography; pioneer-preacher memoir literature ('Raccoon' John Smith biography) (primary) — Treat the memoir material as retrospective and hagiographic - written well after the fact and admiring in tone. Black congregations from this era are documented mainly through white church minutes, a real filter on the record.
