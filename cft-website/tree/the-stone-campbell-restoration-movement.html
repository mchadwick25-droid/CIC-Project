American frontier, today in United States

The Stone-Campbell Restoration Movement

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

On opposite sides of the Appalachians, two sets of Presbyterians came independently to the same conclusion. That the creeds and denominational names Christians used to define themselves were the very things keeping them apart. Barton W. Stone and his colleagues dissolved their own presbytery in 1804 with a document called the Last Will and Testament of the Springfield Presbytery. Five years later Thomas Campbell published the Declaration and Address, and with his son Alexander built a movement around the plea for no creed but the Bible and no name but Christian. In 1832 the two groups shook hands and merged. Debating was their native genre — Alexander Campbell argued in public for days at a stretch against skeptics, Presbyterians and a Roman Catholic bishop, and published the transcripts himself — and by 1906, when the federal religious census listed the Churches of Christ separately for the first time, a movement founded to end division had recorded a division of its own.

In Its Own Record

Grew Out Of

  • The Second Great Awakening & Camp-Meeting Tradition Documented Major — Richard McNemar, a preacher in the Kentucky revival, wrote its history in 1807. He names Barton W. Stone as one of five ministers who left the Synod of Kentucky. Many of the revival's converts joined them. This was the start of the Stone side of the Stone-Campbell movement.

Gave Rise To

  • Churches of Christ Widely Accepted Major — The Stone-Campbell movement split in two in the early 1900s. One side, the Churches of Christ, sang in worship without instruments. A government survey of churches for 1906 listed them on their own for the first time.
  • Disciples of Christ & the Independent Christian Churches Widely Accepted Major — The Stone-Campbell movement split in two in the early 1900s. The Disciples of Christ were the side that kept instruments in worship and kept their national mission societies. The 1906 United States census of churches listed the other side, the Churches of Christ, on its own.

Argued Against

  • The Catholic Church of the Long Nineteenth Century Dominant Modern Reconstruction — In 1837 Alexander Campbell held a public debate with a Catholic bishop, John Purcell. It went on for days, and the movement printed the whole of it. This rests on one modern work, a reference book on the movement.

Sources

  • Founding documents: Last Will and Testament of the Springfield Presbytery (1804); Thomas Campbell, Declaration and Address (1809) (primary) — These founding documents state the movement's 'no creed but the Bible' position in its own words.
  • The journals (native genre per census): Christian Baptist (1823-30), Millennial Harbinger (1830-70), Stone's Christian Messenger (1826-45) (primary) — These journals were the movement's own periodicals. Stone's reservations about how the doctrine of the Trinity was worded appear in the Messenger's exchanges.
  • Debate transcripts: Campbell-Walker (1820), Campbell-McCalla (1823), Campbell-Owen (1829), Campbell-Purcell (1837), Campbell-Rice (1843) (primary) — Public debate was one of this movement's characteristic genres, and these transcripts were published verbatim by the movement itself.
  • Congregational minute books; Stone's 1847 autobiography; pioneer-preacher memoir literature ('Raccoon' John Smith biography) (primary) — Treat the memoir material as retrospective and hagiographic - written well after the fact and admiring in tone. Black congregations from this era are documented mainly through white church minutes, a real filter on the record.
  • Foster, Blowers et al. (eds.), The Encyclopedia of the Stone-Campbell Movement (Eerdmans, 2004), with Richard Hughes, Reviving the Ancient Faith (Eerdmans, 1996) (secondary)
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 8, ch. 22 (Noll), with 'The growth of voluntary religion' (Bebbington) (reference) — The Bebbington chapter's exact number in this volume could not be confirmed.
