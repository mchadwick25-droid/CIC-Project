About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

"From Syria all the way to Rome I fight with beasts, by land and by sea, by night and by day." Ignatius, a prisoner, wrote that early in the second century. The beasts on his road, he explains, were his guards, a squad of ten soldiers who grew worse the better they were treated. The real beasts waited at Rome.

He was bishop of the church at Antioch in Syria, sentenced to be killed by wild animals in the arena. On the road he wrote seven letters, most of them to churches that came out to meet him, and almost all we know of him comes from them. Polycarp, the bishop of Smyrna, sent on the ones he held, and later writers quoted them.

Of his life before his arrest nothing is known.

At Smyrna, on the west coast of today's Turkey, he met Polycarp face to face, and bishops came to him from three other cities. He wrote back to their churches that each should act as one around its bishop, its deacons and the elders who led it with him. They should come together at one altar, breaking one bread.

He also warned them against teachers who said that Christ only seemed to have a body and to suffer. Christ, he wrote, was truly born, truly crucified under Pontius Pilate, and truly raised. "Some who are without God, that is, unbelievers, say that he only seemed to suffer. They themselves only seem to exist. But if they are right, why am I in chains? Why do I long to face the wild beasts? Do I die for nothing, then?"

From Smyrna he also wrote ahead to the church at Rome. He begged them not to work to spare him but to let him be food for the beasts, which he would coax, and force if they held back. "I am God's wheat, and let me be ground by the teeth of wild beasts, so that I may be found the pure bread of Christ." As grain is ground into bread, his torn body would become an offering to God.

At Troas, a port on the same coast, he called the teachers he opposed beasts in human shape, who cared nothing for the widow, the orphan or the hungry. To another church he wrote that he had cried aloud among them: do nothing without the bishop. Some thought he had heard of their quarrels, but the words, he said, came from God's Spirit, not from any man.

No early witness saw his death. Polycarp, writing soon after to the church at Philippi, which Paul had once written to, held up "the blessed Ignatius" as a model of patience. He also asked for any news of him. He sent on the letters he held, as Philippi had asked. Within a lifetime Irenaeus quoted the wheat line, without naming him, and two centuries later Eusebius wrote that "report says" he was given to the beasts at Rome. Later bishops used his words on Christ's real flesh in their own quarrels.

Because the letters show each church led by one bishop, some scholars thought them too developed for so early a date, and they argued for centuries over which were his. Fifteen letters had come down under his name. By 1890 most scholars accepted seven as his, though some still did not.

The last we hear in his own words, he has to sail suddenly from Troas. The prisoner who fought beasts all the way from Syria sails on toward the real ones at Rome.

Key Works

  • Letter to the Romans: his plea that the church at Rome not save him from the beasts
  • Letter to the Smyrnaeans: against those who said Christ only seemed to suffer; where the phrase "Catholic Church" first appears, Cleveland Coxe says
  • Letter to Polycarp: to the bishop of Smyrna, asking him to care for the church at Antioch

Legacy

What traces to them

  • He told the churches to do nothing without their bishop, and wrote that "wherever Jesus Christ is, there is the Catholic Church." Cleveland Coxe, who edited the American edition of the letters, notes that this is the first use of that phrase in the early church's writings.

How they're remembered

  • Polycarp of Smyrna, in his letter to the Philippians, holds up "the blessed Ignatius" as an example of patience.
  • Irenaeus of Lyon quotes the wheat line as the words of a man of the church condemned to the beasts. Eusebius of Caesarea gives Irenaeus's words as "As one of our people said."
  • Eusebius of Caesarea says his fame "is still celebrated by a great many."

What Is Uncertain

  • Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius of Caesarea, says almost all we know of him comes from his own letters. Eusebius adds that he was the second bishop of Antioch after Peter, and that report said he died by the beasts at Rome.
  • Which letters are his was argued for a long time. Fifteen carry his name; eight are set aside as forgeries. Daillé, in 1666, rejected all of them. Bishop Pearson defended the seven shorter Greek letters in 1672. William Cureton, in 1845, argued that only the three letters in a Syriac version were genuine; Bunsen agreed with him and Hefele did not. McGiffert wrote in 1890 that Zahn and Lightfoot had finally settled it for the shorter Greek letters, though he notes that some still denied them.
  • Eusebius's Chronicle dates his death to the tenth year of Trajan, about 107. Zahn and Lightfoot would say only that it was under Trajan. Harnack placed it under Hadrian.
  • Accounts of his martyrdom were written from the fourth century on. McGiffert says they contradict one another and cannot be relied on.
