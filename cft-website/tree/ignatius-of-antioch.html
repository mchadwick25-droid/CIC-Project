Ignatius of Antioch

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Post-Apostolic Household-Church Christianity.

Also called Theophorus

Ignatius was bishop of the church at Antioch in Syria, taken under guard to Rome to face the wild beasts. On the road he wrote seven letters, urging the churches to keep together around their bishops and to hold that Christ truly suffered, and asking the church at Rome to let him die.

About This Person

Ignatius, bishop of the church at Antioch in Syria, was a prisoner bound for death in the arena at Rome when he wrote: "From Syria all the way to Rome I fight with beasts, by land and by sea, by night and by day. I am bound to ten leopards, I mean a band of soldiers. Even when they are treated well, they grow all the worse."

On that road he wrote seven letters, six to churches and one to a fellow bishop, and they tell nearly all we know of him. Three messages run through them: keep together around your bishop, hold that Christ truly suffered, and, to Rome, do not save me. Polycarp of Smyrna gathered the letters he held and sent them on, and later bishops quoted them for centuries.

He was taken in chains as a Christian, for the name of Christ. It was the reign of the emperor Trajan, as the church historian Eusebius wrote two centuries later. Across Asia, the Roman province that is now western Turkey, churches came out city by city to greet him, even those off his route. At Smyrna he met its bishop, Polycarp. The churches of Ephesus, Magnesia and Tralles sent their bishops to him there, and he wrote to those three churches and to Rome.

His first message was unity: each church should act as one with its bishop, its elders and its deacons. At Ephesus, he wrote, the elders, the council who led a church with its bishop, were fitted to him like strings to a harp, and he urged the whole church to sing with one voice. Nothing should be done without the bishop: one thanksgiving meal of one bread and one cup, at one altar, as there is one bishop. He warned that God does not live where there is division and anger.

His second message was aimed at teachers who said Christ only seemed to have a body and to suffer: "Some who are without God, that is, unbelievers, say that he only seemed to suffer. They themselves only seem to exist. If that is so, why am I in chains? Why do I long to fight the wild beasts? Do I die for nothing, then?" They shunned the thanksgiving meal, he wrote, because they would not say it was Christ's flesh. They thought the same of his own sufferings and those of others like him, he wrote, and he charged that they cared nothing for the widow, the orphan or the hungry.

His third message went to Rome. He was afraid, he wrote, that the love of the church there would try to spare him. If they spared him, it would be hard for him to reach God; if they said nothing on his behalf, he would become God's. "I am God's wheat. I am ground by the teeth of wild beasts, so that I may be found pure bread." The bread was an offering: by the teeth of the beasts, he wrote, he would be found a sacrifice to God.

At Troas, a port on the coast, he wrote to the churches of Philadelphia and Smyrna, and to Polycarp. News had come that the church at Antioch, which he had left in chains, was at peace and whole again, and he asked them to send a messenger to Syria to rejoice with it.

Later Polycarp wrote to the church in the city of Philippi, which had seen Ignatius pass, and counted him among those whose endurance they had witnessed. At their request he sent on the letters of Ignatius he held, because they spoke of faith and patience, and asked for surer news of him and those with him. Later still, Irenaeus, writing at Lyon in Gaul, quoted the wheat line, without naming him, as the words of a fellow believer condemned to the beasts.

Eusebius also wrote that, by report, Ignatius was sent from Syria to Rome and became food for wild beasts, for his testimony to Christ.

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Polycarp of Smyrna sent the letters of Ignatius he held to the church at Philippi, at its request, because they spoke of faith and patience.
  • Eusebius of Caesarea writes that on his road through Asia he warned the churches against the false teachings then spreading, urged them to hold fast to the tradition of the apostles, and set that tradition down in writing.
  • Eusebius of Caesarea named all seven letters, said which were written from Smyrna and which from Troas, and copied passages from them into his Church History.
  • Irenaeus of Lyon quoted the wheat line, without his name, to show that suffering makes those who are saved ready for God, as wheat is ground fine to make bread.
  • Athanasius of Alexandria, writing on the quarrel over whether the Son shares the Father's being, quoted his words on Christ as both of the flesh and of God, and held that Ignatius was right.
  • Theodoret of Cyrrhus, in his dialogue Eranistes, on how God became flesh, set out his words on Christ truly born of a virgin and crucified in the flesh as witness to the teaching of the apostles.

How they're remembered

  • Polycarp of Smyrna, in his letter to the Philippians, holds up "the blessed Ignatius" as an example of patience.
  • Irenaeus of Lyon quotes the wheat line as the words of a man of the church condemned to the beasts. Eusebius of Caesarea gives Irenaeus's words as "As one of our people said."
  • Eusebius of Caesarea says his fame "is still celebrated by a great many."
  • John Chrysostom, preaching at Antioch on his feast, told the church there that it had sent out a bishop and received back a martyr. Jerome says his remains lay at Antioch, in the cemetery outside the Daphnitic gate.
  • The Great Horologion of the Greek Church keeps his feast on 20 December and prints the wheat line there. The Roman Breviary keeps it on 1 February.

Open Questions

  • The witnesses. Nearly all that is known of him comes from his own seven letters, written on the road. Polycarp, who met him, and Irenaeus, who does not name him, add a little. Eusebius, about two centuries later, adds that he was the second bishop of Antioch, and that report said he died by the beasts at Rome.
  • The year. His letters give none. Eusebius's Chronicle sets his death among the events of Trajan's persecution of the Christians. Jerome gives the eleventh year of Trajan, and the later Martyrdom of Ignatius the ninth, though its editors call that numeral uncertain. Modern editors do not agree on the year.
  • His place among the bishops. Eusebius calls him the second bishop of Antioch, after Evodius. Jerome calls him the third after the apostle Peter, counting Peter as the first. Theodoret, writing much later, says Peter himself made him bishop.
  • John's disciple. Eusebius's Chronicle, in Jerome's Latin, names him among those who heard the apostle John, and the Great Horologion calls him John's disciple. His own letters do not say so.
  • The name Theophorus. Each letter opens with Ignatius, who is also called Theophorus, a Greek name meaning God-bearer. The Great Horologion explains it as one who carried God living in his soul. The later Martyrdom of Ignatius has him tell Trajan that it means one who has Christ within his breast.
  • Which letters are his. Fifteen letters carry his name. Polycarp, Irenaeus and Eusebius treat the seven as his, and Eusebius names those seven. The seven survive in a shorter and a longer Greek form, and three of them also in a shorter Syriac version. Which form is his, and whether the seven are his at all, was argued among modern editors for centuries.
  • The peace at Antioch. He writes that the church at Antioch was at peace and restored to its proper greatness, but not what trouble it had passed through. A chapter heading in the English, on the close of the persecution, is the editors' own.
  • The sailing from Troas. He writes that he must suddenly sail from Troas to Neapolis, as the will enjoins. The words of the emperor that follow in the English are the translators' own, in brackets.
  • His end. No writer of his own century tells how he died. Polycarp counts him among those now with the Lord, yet later in the same letter asks the Philippians for surer news of him and those with him; that later part survives only in Latin, and some modern scholars have questioned whether it is Polycarp's. Eusebius gives only what report said. Chrysostom and Jerome, writing later still, set his words on the beasts at the moment of his death.
  • The trial. The later Martyrdom of Ignatius has the emperor Trajan try him in person at Antioch. His letters say only that he was led bound for the name of Christ, and Irenaeus that he was condemned because of his testimony to God.
