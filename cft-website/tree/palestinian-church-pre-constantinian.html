The Church of Roman Palestine before Constantine

A coastal town, a library, and the men who built it — where Origen ended up, where Eusebius of Caesarea learned his trade, and where the persecution of 303 was recorded by someone who watched it.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:27

Read along

When Origen left Alexandria for good around 231 he went to Caesarea on the Palestinian coast, and taught there for the last twenty years of his life. What grew around him outlasted him by a long way. His books stayed, and a presbyter named Pamphilus spent his life and his money enlarging them into the best Christian library in the world - the place where the Hexapla sat, where texts were corrected and copied, and where a young man called Eusebius learned to work, and afterwards called himself Eusebius of Pamphilus out of loyalty to his teacher. Up the road at Jerusalem, Bishop Alexander had built a library of his own and was collecting letters and records; further inland at Emmaus, Julius Africanus - soldier, scholar, and correspondent of Origen - wrote the first Christian universal chronicle and a careful little treatise on why the two gospel genealogies of Jesus do not agree. Then the persecution came. Pamphilus was killed in 310 and Alexander had already died in prison; Eusebius wrote down what he had watched happen to his own province, name by name, and afterwards used the library to write the history of the church.

Documented Stories

Apphianus Seizes the Governor’s Hand

Apphianus was from a wealthy family in Lycia, had studied law at Beirut, and had come to Caesarea to live in the household of Pamphilus — ‘with us’, Eusebius says, since he shared that house — studying Scripture and living an ascetic life. He was not yet twenty. When the governor Urbanus ordered the whole city to sacrifice, with soldiers calling the names from lists, Apphianus told no one in the house what he meant to do, slipped past the guards, and walked up to Urbanus as he was pouring a libation. He took hold of the governor’s right hand, stopped the sacrifice, and told him he should not turn from the one God to idols. He was beaten, imprisoned overnight in the stocks, and the next day tortured: his sides torn, his feet wrapped in linen soaked in oil and set alight — Eusebius says he watched, and describes the flesh dripping — and finally, still alive, thrown into the sea. Eusebius then records what the city saw: a storm, and the body cast up on the shore in front of the gates. It is the account of a man who loved the boy and was appalled, and who also plainly thought that what Apphianus did was right. The wider church, since Polycarp’s day, had said Christians should not seek out martyrdom. Eusebius knew that and told the story anyway, because he had been in the room when Apphianus did not come back.

Africanus Tells Origen the Susanna Story Is Not Genuine

Julius Africanus lived at Emmaus, had served in the army, had been to the court at Edessa, and would later design a library for the emperor in Rome. He had heard Origen, in a public disputation, cite the story of Susanna and the elders from the Greek book of Daniel as Scripture, and he wrote to him — politely, and with real affection — to say it could not be. His argument was philological. When Daniel traps the two elders, the Greek text makes a pun on each of the trees they name and the punishment that awaits them; the puns work in Greek and cannot work in Hebrew. The story therefore could not have been written in Hebrew, was not in the Jewish Bible, and was ‘a modern forgery’. He added other doubts: the style, the implausibility of Jewish courts in exile passing capital sentences. Origen answered with a long letter that concedes the puns are Greek and argues everything else — that the churches had received the Greek Daniel, that the Jews had suppressed passages discreditable to their elders, and that ‘we are not to reject what is read in the churches’ because a Hebrew copy lacks it. Both letters survive. Modern scholarship sides with Africanus on the fact and with neither man entirely on the method. What the exchange documents is a church in which a layman could challenge its greatest scholar on the text of the Bible, and get a reasoned answer instead of a rebuke.

Pamphilus and the Boy Who Asked for the Bodies

Pamphilus, presbyter of Caesarea, had spent his fortune and his life on the library Origen left behind — buying, copying, correcting, lending out to anyone who wanted a Bible — and had been in prison for two years when the governor Firmilian had him brought out with eleven others. Eusebius, who had shared his house and his work and afterwards called himself ‘Eusebius of Pamphilus’, gives the names. Five were Egyptians caught escorting confessors to the mines; asked their cities, they gave the names of Old Testament towns, having taken new names in baptism. Pamphilus and the rest were sentenced to be beheaded. Then, Eusebius says, a young man in the household named Porphyry — eighteen, a slave or servant whom Pamphilus had taught to write and who copied books for the library — stepped forward and asked that the bodies be given up for burial. Firmilian had him tortured, ‘not as a man but as a stone’, Eusebius writes, and when he still would not sacrifice had him burned over a slow fire. Another member of the household, Seleucus, who came to congratulate him, was executed too. Twelve died that day. Eusebius wrote a Life of Pamphilus in three books, now lost, and went on to build the first history of the church out of the shelves his teacher had filled. The library survived into the seventh century.

Connections

In Its Own Record

This is the province where Origen taught for the last twenty years of his life. Eusebius of Caesarea was formed here too. It bridges two worlds. One is the Alexandrian school, whose greatest teacher moved here. The other is the Jerusalem church of liturgy and pilgrimage, which begins where this one ends.

Direct descendants

  • continued as Jerusalem Liturgical-Pilgrimage Christianity Inferential-Thin Major — In the library at Aelia, as Jerusalem was then called, Eusebius of Caesarea saw letters that Bishop Alexander had gathered. He names the next bishops of the city down to Hermon. He says their chair was kept there until his own time. Later, Constantine wrote to Macarius, its bishop, about building the Church of the Savior. So the see of Jerusalem carried on. But the earlier church was the church of the whole province, not of Jerusalem alone. To call the later pilgrim church the same community is only an inference.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition Documented — Eusebius of Caesarea says that Origen left Alexandria for Caesarea about 231. He left his school in the hands of Heraclas. Eusebius adds that Pamphilus, a martyr of his own day, built up a store of Origen's books.

Influenced

  • influenced Imperial and Juridical Christianity Documented — Eusebius of Caesarea says he drew on a library at Aelia, or Jerusalem, for his Church History. Bishop Alexander had founded it. The letters kept there had lasted to his own time.
  • influenced Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity Documented — Jerome says Pamphilus copied most of Origen's works by hand. The copies were still kept at Caesarea, in its library. Jerome owned twenty-five books in Pamphilus's own hand. In his book against Rufinus, he says he found the Apology for Origen by Eusebius of Caesarea in that same library.
  • influenced The Church of Aquileia Documented — Chromatius, bishop of Aquileia, asked Rufinus to put the Church History of Eusebius of Caesarea into Latin. Rufinus did so around 401. He cut the tenth book down and added two books of his own.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

When Origen left Alexandria for Caesarea around 231, he spent his last twenty years teaching there, and a presbyter named Pamphilus built his books into the best Christian library in the ancient world. A young Eusebius of Caesarea trained in that library and later called himself 'Eusebius of Pamphilus' out of loyalty to his teacher. When persecution came, Pamphilus was killed in 310, and Eusebius wrote down, name by name, what he watched happen to his own province.

Legacy

Almost all that anyone knows about the first three Christian centuries comes through this library. Eusebius of Caesarea wrote his Church History from the shelves Pamphilus and Alexander of Jerusalem had filled. It quotes texts that survive nowhere else. So the shape of early Christian memory is partly the shape of what one library in Palestine happened to hold. The Hexapla sat here. It was Origen's Old Testament, laid out in six columns. Jerome later searched these shelves. He prized copies of Origen made in Pamphilus's own hand. And Africanus wrote a chronicle. Eusebius built his own timeline on it. That timeline in turn set the frame for each history of the world written in the Middle Ages.

How Sure the Record Is

Good for the end, thin for the beginning. Eusebius of Caesarea's Martyrs of Palestine is a first-hand record of the persecution he lived through, and fragments of Julius Africanus' letters and Chronography survive. Before that the evidence is fragments transmitted by Eusebius himself, which makes him both the principal witness and the principal filter.
