The Age of Monks and Empires · 451-636 · Syria, Mesopotamia

Syriac Orthodox (West Syriac) Christianity

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

After Chalcedon, the Christians of Syria and Mesopotamia who refused the council's formula lived inside the Roman Empire, as the Egyptian Miaphysites did. But they lost the throne of Antioch, and for long stretches under Justinian they had no legal standing at all. Their leader, Severus of Antioch, was driven from his see in 518. Like many of his bishops, he withdrew to Egypt and went on writing from exile. Their churches kept meeting, and their clergy kept being ordained, mostly because of Jacob Baradaeus. He was made a bishop at the request of the Ghassanid Arab king Harith, with the backing of the empress Theodora. For decades he crossed Syria and Mesopotamia, ordaining priests and bishops for a church the empire would not recognize. He would finish his work in a district in a day and a night, then move on thirty or forty miles. The bishops hunting him never caught him. John of Ephesus was himself a Miaphysite bishop and one of Justinian's own missionaries. He wrote down fifty-eight portraits of these people as it all took place. In 636 the Byzantines lost at Yarmouk, and with that the empire lost its power to enforce against them. What stood on its own was a separate church whose bishops and clergy had never stopped working.

In Its Own Record

Grew Out Of

  • Syriac Christianity (Edessa/Nisibis) Inferential-Thin Major — The Chronicle of Edessa says Paul, the city's bishop, would not accept the synod of Chalcedon. He was removed in 522. That December the new bishop drove out the monks who also refused it. Later Jacob Baradaeus was made bishop for Edessa. But these few notes are thin evidence for a line of descent.

Continued As

  • Syriac Orthodox Christianity under Islam Documented Major — Michael the Syrian, a patriarch of this church in the 1100s, records an unbroken line of patriarchs across the Arab conquest. John of the Sedre took over from Athanasius in about 631. After the Roman defeat at the Yarmouk, the same John ordained a bishop for Edessa. He was also questioned by the Arab emir 'Amr.

Influenced

  • Cyrilline / Miaphysite Egyptian Christianity Documented Major — Severus of Antioch wrote to Egyptian clergy after his exile began. He told them he cared even more for Alexandria's church than for Antioch's. He says groups long cut off from the Egyptian church joined it through his work. Dioscorus II, patriarch of Alexandria, took his advice, he writes.Lasting result: Dioscorus of Alexandria took Severus's advice. He won back men who had long kept out of communion, and they joined the Egyptian church.
  • The Syriac Renaissance Documented — Michael the Syrian, patriarch from 1166, built his world chronicle partly from older Syriac histories. At one point he marks where the chronicles of John of Asia and Zacharias the Rhetor take over as his sources. Elsewhere he quotes John speaking in his own voice about a dispute at the emperor's court.

Opposed By

  • The Byzantine Imperial Church (Justinianic) Documented Major — Justinian confirmed the anathema that the sees of the patriarchs and the monks had passed on Severus of Antioch. His law said Severus's books must be burned, and it kept him out of the capital. Evagrius says it also threatened those who held to his views with the highest penalties.Lasting result: Justinian's law said every copy of Severus's writings must be burned. It also kept him out of the capital and the land around it.
  • The Church of the East under Persia Documented — In 612 the Persian king had the bishops of the Church of the East debate the Miaphysites at his court. A statement drawn up for that meeting says Christ has two natures. It charges Severus and his disciples with leaving the foundation of the faith by teaching one.

Broke With

  • The Byzantine Imperial Church (Justinianic) Documented Major — Evagrius, a historian who backed the Council of Chalcedon, says Severus fled his see at Antioch in September 518. Paul took his place, and he was told to proclaim the council in the open. A Miaphysite chronicle of the same century says Severus left for fear of the emperor. In December 522 the bishop of Edessa threw out the monks who would not accept the council.

In Contact With

  • Cyrilline / Miaphysite Egyptian Christianity Documented — After 518, says a Syriac chronicle of the 560s, many bishops went to Alexandria in the steps of Severus of Antioch. It also copies the letters of communion that he and Theodosius of that city sent each other in the 530s. Each greets the other as his brother and fellow minister.

Sources

  • John of Ephesus, Lives of the Eastern Saints (ed./trans. E. W. Brooks, Patrologia Orientalis 17-19, 1923-26) (primary) — 58 portraits of Miaphysite ascetics by an insider - extraordinary inside-voice material; Syriac with facing English translation, online (archive.org / tertullian.org).
  • John of Ephesus, Ecclesiastical History, Part III (trans. R. Payne Smith, 1860) (primary) — Contemporary narrative of persecution and church-building under Justin II/Tiberius from the persecuted side; Syriac; the Victorian English translation is online; parts I-II survive only in excerpts.
  • Pseudo-Zachariah Rhetor, Chronicle (trans. Greatrex, Phenix, Horn, TTH 55, 2011) (primary) — Sixth-century Syriac Miaphysite compilation covering 451 onward, including Chalcedon's aftermath; excellent modern annotated English translation (Translated Texts for Historians).
  • Volker L. Menze, Justinian and the Making of the Syrian Orthodox Church (2008) (secondary) — The standard modern account of how a separated West Syriac church actually formed (518-553), ordinations, memory, liturgy; Oxford Early Christian Studies, in print.
  • W. H. C. Frend, The Rise of the Monophysite Movement (1972) (secondary) — Broad narrative frame connecting the Syrian and Egyptian anti-Chalcedonian movements; dated but standard and available.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity, vol. 2, ch. 3, 'Greek Christianities' (F. W. Norris) (reference) — Reference anchor for the eastern-Roman provinces incl. Syria to c.600.
