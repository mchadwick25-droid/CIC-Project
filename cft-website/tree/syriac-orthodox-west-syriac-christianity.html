The Age of Monks and Empires · 451-636 · Syria, Mesopotamia

Syriac Orthodox (West Syriac) Christianity

Syria and Mesopotamia, 451 to 636 CE — a church built without legal permission to exist, its bishops ordained on the move by a wandering missionary in patched rags.

Syriac Christianity (Edessa/Nisibis)→Syriac Orthodox (West Syriac) Christianity→Syriac Orthodox Christianity under Islam

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:35

Read along

After Chalcedon, the Christians of Syria and Mesopotamia who refused the council's formula lived inside the Roman Empire, as the Egyptian Miaphysites did. But they lost the throne of Antioch, and for long stretches under Justinian they had no legal standing at all. Their leader, Severus of Antioch, was driven from his see in 518. Like many of his bishops, he withdrew to Egypt and went on writing from exile. Their churches kept meeting, and their clergy kept being ordained, mostly because of Jacob Baradaeus. He was made a bishop at the request of the Ghassanid Arab king Harith, with the backing of the empress Theodora. For decades he crossed Syria and Mesopotamia, ordaining priests and bishops for a church the empire would not recognize. He would finish his work in a district in a day and a night, then move on thirty or forty miles. The bishops hunting him never caught him. John of Ephesus was himself a Miaphysite bishop and one of Justinian's own missionaries. He wrote down fifty-eight portraits of these people as it all took place. In 636 the Byzantines lost at Yarmouk, and with that the empire lost its power to enforce against them. What stood on its own was a separate church whose bishops and clergy had never stopped working.

Documented Stories

The Bishop in the Horse-Blanket

John of Ephesus knew Jacob, and his portrait in the Lives of the Eastern Saints is the only near-contemporary account. Jacob was a monk from the monastery of Pesilta near Tella who had gone to Constantinople to plead for the persecuted anti-Chalcedonians and spent fifteen years living quietly in the capital under the empress Theodora’s protection. Around 542 the Ghassanid king al-Harith, whose Arab federates were Miaphysite, asked the empress for bishops for his people, and Jacob was consecrated — by the exiled patriarch of Alexandria, Theodosius, in secret — as bishop of Edessa with a roving commission over the whole East. He dressed in a ragged horse-cloth, the burd‘tha that gave him the nickname Baradaeus, so as to pass as a beggar; he covered twenty or thirty miles a day; he slept where he could; and he ordained, by John’s count, twenty-seven bishops and ‘a hundred thousand’ priests and deacons — a figure to be read as ‘beyond counting’. Imperial agents hunted him for decades and never caught him. He was, John admits, a poor administrator, and the church he created fell into quarrels between his own bishops in his last years; he died in 578 on his way to Egypt to settle one of them. His name became the label ‘Jacobite’ that outsiders attached to the West Syriac church for a thousand years, though it never called itself that. What he had done was keep a hierarchy alive by refusing to stay in one place.

John of Ephesus Converts the Pagans of Asia for Justinian

The same John of Ephesus who chronicled the persecution of his own church by the Chalcedonian emperors accepted a commission from one of them. In Justinian’s reign he was appointed teacher of the pagans in the four provinces of Asia, Caria, Phrygia and Lydia. His English translator, R. Payne Smith, dates the mission to 542. John reports the results himself. In the Lives he counts eighty thousand people converted from paganism in those provinces. He also counts ninety-eight churches and twelve monasteries founded, and seven more churches made from Jewish synagogues. His Ecclesiastical History gives ninety-nine new churches and twelve monasteries over the thirty-five years his colleague Deuterius worked with him. His deacons, he writes, uprooted altars, destroyed shrines and cut down trees. At Derira, in the mountains near Tralles, he leveled a famous temple to its foundations and built his chief monastery on the site. Justinian paid for it and for the other monasteries and churches. Elsewhere he records with approval the later trials in Constantinople of people accused of secret pagan worship, some of whom were put to death. Yet his own church suffered persecution in Justinian’s time, and he would later be imprisoned under Justin II for refusing Chalcedon. His own pages record both: the persecution his church suffered, and the campaign he led for an emperor against the old religion.

Connections

In Its Own Record

It grew up on the Roman side of the Syriac tradition, around Ephrem's Edessa, though few texts trace that line of descent. Its bishops lived on because the Ghassanid Arab king Harith asked for new ones. Jacob Baradaeus was one of them.

Descended from

  • grew out of Syriac Christianity (Edessa/Nisibis) Inferential-Thin Major — The Chronicle of Edessa says Paul, the city's bishop, would not accept the synod of Chalcedon. He was removed in 522. That December the new bishop drove out the monks who also refused it. Later Jacob Baradaeus was made bishop for Edessa. But these few notes are thin evidence for a line of descent.

Direct descendants

  • continued as Syriac Orthodox Christianity under Islam Documented Major — Michael the Syrian, a patriarch of this church in the 1100s, records an unbroken line of patriarchs across the Arab conquest. John of the Sedre took over from Athanasius in about 631. After the Roman defeat at the Yarmouk, the same John ordained a bishop for Edessa. He was also questioned by the Arab emir 'Amr.

Broke With

  • broke with The Byzantine Imperial Church (Justinianic) Documented Major — Evagrius Scholasticus, a historian who backed the Council of Chalcedon, says Severus fled his see at Antioch in September 518. Paul took his place, and he was told to proclaim the council in the open. A Miaphysite chronicle of the same century says Severus left for fear of the emperor. In December 522 the bishop of Edessa threw out the monks who would not accept the council.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced

  • influenced The Church of Alexandria and Miaphysite Egypt Documented Major — Severus of Antioch wrote to Egyptian clergy after his exile began. He told them he cared even more for Alexandria's church than for Antioch's. He says groups long cut off from the Egyptian church joined it through his work. Dioscorus II, patriarch of Alexandria, took his advice, he writes.Lasting result: Dioscorus of Alexandria took Severus of Antioch's advice. He won back men who had long kept out of communion, and they joined the Egyptian church.
  • influenced The Syriac Renaissance Documented — Michael the Syrian, patriarch from 1166, built his world chronicle partly from older Syriac histories. At one point he marks where the chronicles of John of Asia and Zacharias the Rhetor take over as his sources. Elsewhere he quotes John speaking in his own voice about a dispute at the emperor's court.

Argued Against

  • argued against The Church of the East under Persia Documented — John of Ephesus says Simeon of Beth Arsham fought the school of Nestorius all his life. Once he met the catholicos Babai in a debate before a Persian governor. Babai answered by calling Simeon's side the king's enemies.

Opposed By

  • argued against The Byzantine Imperial Church (Justinianic) Documented Major — Justinian confirmed the anathema that the sees of the patriarchs and the monks had passed on Severus of Antioch. His law said Severus's books must be burned, and it kept him out of the capital. Evagrius Scholasticus says it also threatened those who held to his views with the highest penalties.Lasting result: Justinian's law said every copy of Severus of Antioch's writings must be burned. It also kept him out of the capital and the land around it.
  • argued against The Church of the East under Persia Documented — In 612 the Persian king had the bishops of the Church of the East debate the Miaphysites at his court. A statement drawn up for that meeting says Christ has two natures. It charges Severus of Antioch and his disciples with leaving the foundation of the faith by teaching one.

In Contact With

  • in contact with The Church of Alexandria and Miaphysite Egypt Documented — After 518, says a Syriac chronicle of the 560s, many bishops went to Alexandria in the steps of Severus of Antioch. It also copies the letters of communion that he and Theodosius of that city sent each other in the 530s. Each greets the other as his brother and fellow minister.
  • in contact with Aksumite Christianity Widely Accepted — In 524 Simeon of Beth Arsham wrote a letter on the killing of the Christians of Najran. At an Arab camp he met a Christian envoy sent by the king of Himyar, whom the Ethiopians had set up. One scholar of the 1800s thought the letter a forgery, but most accept it.
  • in contact with Peter the Iberian Documented — John Rufus was a priest of Antioch's party against Chalcedon. When that party lost Antioch, he went to Palestine and joined Peter the Iberian, then followed him as bishop. Severus of Antioch later held up Peter as a bishop who kept the rules.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Under Justinian, this church had no legal right to exist. Jacob Baradaeus was made its bishop when the Ghassanid Arab king Harith asked, and the empress Theodora backed it. For decades he crossed Syria and Mesopotamia, ordaining priests and bishops one step ahead of the bishops hunting him. When Byzantine power fell at Yarmouk in 636, its clergy stood on their own. They had never stopped their work, though for long years they had no legal standing at all.

Legacy

The same institution is still here. It continued under Muslim rule and is today the Syriac Orthodox Church of Antioch, with congregations across the Middle East, in India and in a wide diaspora. It kept Syriac alive as a living liturgical language, and its translators preserved Greek theological works whose originals were lost. Including most of Severus of Antioch's own writing, which survives only in Syriac. Several of its monasteries in the Tur Abdin, in what is now south-eastern Turkey, were founded before or during this period and are still occupied.

Experience It Today

  • Mor Gabriel Monastery, Tur Abdin, Turkey — founded in 397 and still a working Syriac Orthodox monastery and the seat of its metropolitan

How Sure the Record Is

Moderate-to-rich; John of Ephesus's Lives is extraordinary inside-voice material

  • John of Ephesus, Lives of the Eastern Saints (ed./trans. E. W. Brooks, Patrologia Orientalis 17-19, 1923-26) (primary) — 58 portraits of Miaphysite ascetics by an insider - extraordinary inside-voice material; Syriac with facing English translation, online (archive.org / tertullian.org).
  • John of Ephesus, Ecclesiastical History, Part III (trans. R. Payne Smith, 1860) (primary) — Contemporary narrative of persecution and church-building under Justin II/Tiberius from the persecuted side; Syriac; the Victorian English translation is online; parts I-II survive only in excerpts.
  • Pseudo-Zachariah Rhetor, Chronicle (trans. Greatrex, Phenix, Horn, TTH 55, 2011) (primary) — Sixth-century Syriac Miaphysite compilation covering 451 onward, including Chalcedon's aftermath; excellent modern annotated English translation (Translated Texts for Historians).
  • Volker L. Menze, Justinian and the Making of the Syrian Orthodox Church (2008) (secondary) — The standard modern account of how a separated West Syriac church actually formed (518-553), ordinations, memory, liturgy; Oxford Early Christian Studies, in print.
  • W. H. C. Frend, The Rise of the Monophysite Movement (1972) (secondary) — Broad narrative frame connecting the Syrian and Egyptian anti-Chalcedonian movements; dated but standard and available.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity, vol. 2, ch. 3, 'Greek Christianities' (F. W. Norris) (reference) — Reference anchor for the eastern-Roman provinces incl. Syria to c.600.
