About This Person

In the Roman province of Bithynia, people accused of being Christians were brought before the governor, Pliny. He asked each one if they were Christians. If they said yes, he asked a second and a third time, and threatened them. Those who held firm he sent to be punished. Whatever their belief, he wrote, "pertinacious and inflexible obstinacy ought to be punished." Then he wrote to the emperor Trajan for advice.

Pliny the Younger was not a Christian. Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius of Caesarea in 1890, calls him "a man of great literary attainments and an intimate friend of the Emperor Trajan." He was the nephew of Pliny the Elder. Pliny became governor in 110 or 111, McGiffert says, and probably wrote within a year.

Some, he told Trajan, denied the charge, offered incense and wine to the emperor's image, and cursed Christ. Others said they had stopped being Christians years before, "one even twenty years before." They told him they met "on a fixed day before daylight" to "sing responsively a song unto Christ as God." They bound themselves by an oath not to steal, rob or commit adultery. Pliny had two slave women "who were called deaconesses" questioned under torture. He found "nothing except a superstition depraved and immoderate." It had reached the villages and the countryside as well as the cities, he wrote.

Trajan answered that Christians "are not to be searched for; if they are accused and convicted, they are to be punished." Anyone who denied it and prayed to the gods could be pardoned.

Tertullian of Carthage, who wrote to defend the Christians, attacked the ruling. "It forbids them to be sought after as innocent," he wrote, "and it commands them to be punished as guilty." Eusebius, retelling it from Tertullian, says that after Trajan's reply the persecution "was to a certain degree checked."

Key Works

  • Letters, Book 10, letter 96, with Trajan's reply: his trials of Christians, their worship before dawn, and Trajan's ruling that they were not to be sought out

Legacy

How they're remembered

  • Tertullian of Carthage, defending the Christians, attacked Trajan's reply to him: "It forbids them to be sought after as innocent, and it commands them to be punished as guilty."
  • Eusebius of Caesarea, working from Tertullian, calls him "one of the most noted of governors" and says the persecution "was to a certain degree checked" after Trajan's reply.
  • John Rolfe, who translated Suetonius, says Suetonius's life is known largely from Pliny's letters, and that Pliny got him a military post.

What Is Uncertain

  • Pliny writes as the official who held the trials. What he reports of the Christians' worship, he says, came from those who had left the faith.
  • The Library holds no edition of Pliny. The full English of the letter and of Trajan's reply is in Arthur McGiffert's note to Eusebius. Tertullian and Eusebius give only a short summary, without the deaconesses, the torture or the temples.
  • McGiffert dates his governorship to 110 or 111. Adolf Harnack dates the letter about 111 to 113.
  • No held primary source gives the years of his birth or death.
