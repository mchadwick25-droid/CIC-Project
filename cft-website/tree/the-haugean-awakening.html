The Missionary Era · 1796-1842 · Norway

The Haugean Awakening

Norway, 1796 to 1842 — a farmer's son who walked the country preaching without a license, wrote thirty-three books, built factories to pay for it, and spent most of a decade in prison.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 0:56

Read along

In 1796 a Norwegian farmer's son named Hans Nielsen Hauge had a religious experience while he was working in his father's field. He set out on foot to tell people about it. That was against the law, because Norway's Conventicle Act kept religious meetings for the parish clergy. He wrote thirty-three books and tracts and walked thousands of miles. He founded mills, paper works and trading businesses to support the network of "friends" that grew around him. The state answered by arresting him in 1804. It kept him in prison and under prosecution for most of the next ten years. The movement long outlasted the man. After his death in 1824 the friends' societies went on meeting and trading. In 1842 the Conventicle Act itself was repealed.

Documented Stories

Let Out to Make Salt

Hans Nielsen Hauge had been arrested in October 1804, at thirty-three, for preaching without ordination in breach of the Conventicle Act, and for running a network of trading houses and mills that made him, his prosecutors suspected, more powerful than any bishop; he had been held in Christiania since, while a commission collected evidence from six hundred witnesses. In 1807 Denmark-Norway was drawn into Napoleon's war, the British fleet cut the sea lanes, and by the winter of 1808–09 Norway, which imported all its salt, could no longer preserve fish or meat; there was famine in the inland valleys. Hauge's friends, who had asked for years that he be tried or freed, now told the government what the friends' network already knew: that Hauge, who had built paper mills, a printing works, a shipyard and a bone-meal factory, understood the boiling of sea water into salt. In February 1809 he was released, still under indictment, on condition that he go to the coast and build saltworks. He went, with the friends' money and labor, and within the year works were producing at Lillesand and other places along the southern coast, on methods he wrote up for others to copy. That autumn he was taken back into custody. The salt was the state's, the technique was the friends', and the prisoner had shown the country what the movement it was prosecuting was made of. He was later allowed to live under surveillance in Christiania while the case dragged on.

Ten Years to a Verdict

The case against Hauge was the largest the Danish-Norwegian state had ever brought against a layman. Arrested on 24 October 1804, he was questioned for months, first on the Conventicle Act of 1741, which forbade religious meetings not led by the parish clergy, then on the charge that his trading network was fraudulent, then on his books, which the clergy read for heresy and found chiefly plain. A royal commission was appointed in 1806 and sent questions to every bailiff and parish in Norway; more than six hundred witnesses were examined, most of them peasants who said that Hauge had told them to work hard, obey the authorities and stop drinking. Hauge was held for the first years in close confinement and his health was permanently damaged; he was allowed out in 1809 to make salt. The commission's verdict came in December 1813: two years' hard labor in the fortress, for breaking the Conventicle Act and for having led people astray. He appealed. In December 1814, in the year Norway received its own constitution, the high court reduced the sentence to a fine and costs. The friends paid. Hauge settled on a farm outside Christiania, married, and in his Testament to his Friends of 1821 told them to keep to the Lutheran church, hold their meetings, and do their work. He died in 1824. The Conventicle Act was repealed in 1842 by a Storting many of whose members were farmers his friends had taught to read.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 1796 a Norwegian farmer's son named Hans Nielsen Hauge had a religious experience in his father's field and set out on foot to preach, defying a law that reserved religious meetings to the parish clergy. He wrote thirty-three books and founded mills and trading businesses to support the network of "friends" that grew around him, while the state answered by imprisoning him for most of the following decade. Those friends' societies kept meeting and trading long after his death in 1824, until the law he had defied was finally repealed in 1842.

Legacy

Haugean piety became one of the permanent layers of Norwegian Lutheranism: a lay preaching culture, a network of mission societies and prayer houses, and a suspicion of clerical monopoly that outlived the law it was reacting against. Norwegian emigrants carried it into the American Midwest, where Haugean congregations became one of the founding strands of Norwegian-American Lutheranism. In Norway today Hauge is honored about equally as a revivalist and as an entrepreneur, which would have puzzled almost nobody who knew him.

Sources

On the Nicene Creed

A fair question about any movement named for its founder is whether it was really a tradition or just one man's following. The Haugeans clearly outgrew Hauge: the friends' societies kept their own records, ran their own businesses, and went on meeting for generations after his death in 1824, and the lay culture he set going long outlived him.

How Sure the Record Is

Moderate-to-rich: Hauge's own 33 books and letters; friends' societies' records

  • Hauge's own corpus: his 33 books and tracts (from Betragtning over Verdens Daarlighed, 1796), his letters to the friends' network, and his Testament to his Friends (1821) (primary) — Hauge was a lay peasant preacher who ran his own itinerant press. Most editions are in Norwegian, with only limited material translated into English.
  • The prosecution record: the 1804-14 case under the Conventicle Act - interrogation records, commission files, Hauge's prison writings (primary) — This is hostile legal material: the founder documented at length by the state church that prosecuted and jailed him.
  • The friends' societies' records: haugianere society minutes, correspondence and business networks, lay preachers' memoirs; Hauge's authorization of women itinerant speakers in his letters (primary) — These records show the movement's own societies continuing to meet, correspond, and run businesses well beyond Hauge's own lifetime - evidence the movement outgrew its founder. Hauge's letters also document his authorization of women as itinerant speakers.
