The Missionary Era · 1815-1906 · Philippines

Philippine Catholicism — Gomburza to the Aglipayan Schism

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Spanish friars had run the Philippine church for three centuries, and the century's great church fight was over whether Filipinos could run their own parishes. The secularization campaign — transferring parishes from Spanish religious orders to diocesan clergy, most of them native-born — turned into a national grievance, and in 1872, after a mutiny at the Cavite arsenal, three priests who had championed it were garrotted: Mariano Gomez, Jose Burgos and Jacinto Zamora, remembered ever since by the single name Gomburza. Their execution did more for the independence movement than their campaign ever had; Jose Rizal dedicated his second novel to their memory. When Spanish rule ended, part of the church went with it. In 1902 the labor organizer Isabelo de los Reyes proclaimed an Iglesia Filipina Independiente and named the former revolutionary chaplain Gregorio Aglipay its head, and for a few years a substantial minority of Filipino Catholics followed it.

A lay movement inside this church met refusal and then force. In 1840, by Montero's account, many people began to meet at Lucban, in Tayabas province, as a Confraternity of St Joseph. Its founder was Apolinario de la Cruz, a native lay servant of a Manila hospital. He told his questioners he was a Roman Catholic and meant to honor the saint. He asked the church authorities for leave to make the group lawful. They refused, and he appealed to the colony's high court.

In October 1841 the governor of Tayabas marched on the group's camp with about 300 men. He was killed. On 1 November troops overran its next camp. On 4 November Apolinario was shot. Montero counts 240 dead in the battle and about 260 more in the pursuit. Govantes, whom he cites, says more than a thousand. Montero also quotes a letter of 16 November from the colony's finance chief. It praised the officer of Batangas for keeping "the fire of rebellion" out of his province.

The movement speaks here only through its enemies. Montero wrote in 1895. He draws on a parish priest's report, an official's letter and a writer named Mas. Montero says Apolinario began it only to live off the gullible. Apolinario said he meant to honor St Joseph. Asked what they wanted, pardoned members answered, as Mas tells it: to pray. Asked what they would have done in victory, they said they would have tied the Spaniards and friars to trees for the women to shoot with arrows.

Montero says the group let in no Spaniards and no Chinese mestizos. He takes that as proof of a political aim. That is his reading. Apolinario's letters were read aloud at the meetings, and were seized in 1841. The Library holds none of them. A modern study, by Reynaldo Ileto, starts its story of these movements in 1840. It is called Pasyon and Revolution (1979).

In Its Own Record

Gave Rise To

  • The Philippine Independent Church in the Twentieth Century Widely Accepted Major — In 1947 the Philippine Independent Church said that it had left the Roman Catholic Church. Its head bishop dated the break to 3 August 1902. A letter he approved says Gregorio Aglipay left with other priests and their flocks.

Continues

  • Philippine Catholicism — Era-8 Segment Widely Accepted Major — A Franciscan wrote that in 1771 soldiers took the Augustinian friars' parishes and gave them to thirty-one Filipino priests. In 1899 the Filipino writer T. H. Pardo de Tavera traced the long fight over Filipino priests. He tied it to the three of them that Spain put to death: Burgos, Zamora and Gomez.

Continued As

  • Philippine Catholicism in the Twentieth Century Widely Accepted Major — In 1906 Jorge Barlin was made the first Filipino bishop. He had turned down the chance to lead Gregorio Aglipay's new church. In 2007 the Philippine bishops wrote that his rise proved native priests could hold high office.
