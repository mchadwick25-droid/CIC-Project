Taborite Radicalism

Southern Bohemia, 1420 to 1452 — Hussite radicals who built a town on a hill expecting the world to end, and became the most feared army in Europe instead.

The Hussite & Bohemian Brethren Movement→Taborite Radicalism→no descendant recorded

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:23

Read along

In 1420 the most radical wing of the Hussite movement founded a fortified town in southern Bohemia. They named it Tábor, after the mountain of the Transfiguration. They expected Christ to return at any moment, and believed only a few chosen places would be spared. The year passed, the world went on, and the community stayed. Tabor stripped worship down to what it could find in scripture: worship in Czech, no vestments, no purgatory, no cult of the saints. It chose its own bishop, Nicholas of Pelhřimov, and in its early years held property in common. It also fielded the Hussite wars' most effective army. Its wagon forts, under Jan Žižka and then Prokop the Bald, broke crusade after crusade. In the end it was fellow Hussites who destroyed it. Utraquist lords and Prague's Old Town, joined by Catholic lords such as Ulrich of Rosenberg, crushed the Taborite field armies at Lipany in 1434. In 1452 George of Poděbrady, himself a Utraquist, took the town. He made it use the moderate Utraquist service and had the Taborite priest Wenceslas Koranda locked up.

People and Stories

Voices in the Record

  • Nicholas of Pelhřimov, called Biskupec — the elected Taborite bishop, who defended and chronicled the community's teaching and died a prisoner
  • Jan Žižka — the Hussite commander who made Tabor's wagon-forts unbeatable, and who also turned on radicals he judged had gone too far
  • Prokop the Bald — the priest who led the Taborite field armies after Žižka's death

Documented Stories

‘Flee to the Mountains’: The Five Cities and the Date That Passed

In the autumn of 1419, with Prague’s moderates negotiating with the new king and the countryside gathering in open-air congregations on hills renamed Tabor and Horeb, a group of priests — Lawrence of Březová, the Utraquist chronicler who disliked them, names Jan Čapek, Martin Húska and others — began to preach that the end was at hand. Their letter survives in his chronicle. The wrath of God was about to fall on every town and village; only five cities would be spared — Plzeň, Žatec, Louny, Slaný and Klatovy — and the faithful should sell what they had, go to them, and wait; the date was fixed between 10 and 14 February 1420, Shrovetide. Lawrence records the response with contempt and precision: peasants and townspeople across Bohemia sold their fields and houses ‘for a trifle’, brought the money to the priests, who put it in common chests, and marched to the five cities singing. Shrovetide came. Nothing happened. Lawrence says some went home ashamed; others, refusing to be wrong, moved on to a new teaching — that Christ had come secretly and that the faithful must now themselves be the avenging angels who cleansed the land — and in March 1420 a body of them seized the hill above the Lužnice river, called it Tabor, and founded the town whose armies would hold off five crusades. The date that failed produced the town that succeeded, and the chronicler who despised both preserved the letter.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 1420 the most radical wing of the Hussite movement built a fortified town in southern Bohemia. They named it Tabor after the mountain of the Transfiguration, and expected Christ to return at any moment. When the world did not end, the community stayed. It stripped worship down to what it could find in scripture: no vestments, no purgatory, no cult of saints. For a time it held property in common under its own chosen bishop. Its wagon-fort armies broke crusade after crusade. Then, at Lipany in 1434, an alliance led by moderate Hussites destroyed the Taborite forces.

Legacy

As a church, nothing continued: Tabor's worship and its community of goods ended with its surrender. Under Jan Žižka its armies made the armed wagon fort the great weapon of the Hussite wars. Petr Chelčický, the lay thinker behind the Unity of the Brethren, held that all bloodshed is sin. The horrors of the Hussite wars may have hardened that belief. The Unity, founded a few years after Tabor's fall, chose to defend itself by prayer, not by arms as the Hussites had done. The town itself is still there.

Sources

Doctrinal Floor Note

No creedal question — Tabor stripped worship back to what it could find in scripture, but the argument was about practice and property, not the Creed.

How Sure the Record Is

Chronicled largely by opponents and moderate Hussites

  • Taborite manifestos and chiliast articles (1419-20), with the priests' adventist letters, in Fudge, The Crusade against Heretics in Bohemia (Ashgate, 2002) (primary) — The radicals' own program, in English - mountain assemblies, community of goods, the avenging Christ. Genuine inside texts survive, despite Tabor being chronicled mostly by its opponents.
  • Nicholas of Pelhrimov (Biskupec), Taborite confession and chronicle (Chronicon Taboritarum) (primary) — The movement's own bishop defending Taborite doctrine and recording its history - the strongest inside text. Available only in Latin editions (Hoefler).
  • Lawrence of Brezova, Hussite Chronicle; John Pribram, The Lives of the Taborite Priests; Aeneas Sylvius Piccolomini's account of Tabor (1451) (primary) — Prague moderates writing against Tabor (Pribram's tract is an intra-Hussite heresiology), plus a later outside papal observer. Partial English translation available via Fudge.
  • Howard Kaminsky, A History of the Hussite Revolution (California, 1967) (secondary) — Still the standard for Tabor's theology-into-politics arc, built directly on these sources.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 4, ch. 28 'Wyclif's Bohemian reception' (Ghosh) and ch. 15 'Sacramental life' (Rubin) (reference) — No chapter is dedicated specifically to the Hussites or Taborites; these are the nearest anchors, shared with the wider Hussite movement this radical wing belongs to.
