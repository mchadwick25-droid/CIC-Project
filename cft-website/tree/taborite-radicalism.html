The Late Medieval Era · 1420-1452 · Bohemia

Bohemia, today in Czech Republic

Taborite Radicalism

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In 1420 the most radical wing of the Hussite movement founded a fortified town in southern Bohemia. They named it Tábor, after the mountain of the Transfiguration. They expected Christ to return at any moment, and believed only a few chosen places would be spared. The year passed, the world went on, and the community stayed. Tabor stripped worship down to what it could find in scripture: worship in Czech, no vestments, no purgatory, no cult of the saints. It chose its own bishop, Nicholas of Pelhřimov, and in its early years held property in common. It also fielded the Hussite wars' most effective army. Its wagon forts, under Jan Žižka and then Prokop the Bald, broke crusade after crusade. In the end it was fellow Hussites who destroyed it. Utraquist lords and Prague's Old Town, joined by Catholic lords such as Ulrich of Rosenberg, crushed the Taborite field armies at Lipany in 1434. In 1452 George of Poděbrady, himself a Utraquist, took the town. He made it use the moderate Utraquist service and had the Taborite priest Wenceslas Koranda locked up.

In Its Own Record

Grew Out Of

  • The Hussite & Bohemian Brethren Movement Documented Major — The Prague writer Laurence of Březová tells how, in March 1420, brethren who stood for the chalice left Plzeň with their priests. They went to the mountain of Hradiště, also called Tabor, and the brethren already there took them in.

Argued Against

  • Inquisition-Record 'Sects' (Adamites and kin) Documented — The Hussite writer Laurence of Březová tells of a band of men and women cast out of Tabor for the Picard heresy. They settled on an island near Veselí. He says Jan Žižka, the blind captain of the Taborites, sent their articles to Prague after they were killed. Lützow's 1914 history says Žižka wiped them out almost at once.

Opposed By

  • The Hussite & Bohemian Brethren Movement Documented Major — Tabor's own chronicle says the Prague masters attacked its priests for dropping vestments and the Roman rite. It tells of a dispute between them in 1421. In 1434 Prague's Old Town and its allies marched against the Taborites, and in 1452 the Utraquist regent George of Poděbrady took Tabor.Lasting result: In 1452 George of Poděbrady, regent of Bohemia, took Tabor. He forced the moderate Utraquist service on the town.

Sources

  • Taborite manifestos and chiliast articles (1419-20), with the priests' adventist letters, in Fudge, The Crusade against Heretics in Bohemia (Ashgate, 2002) (primary) — The radicals' own program, in English - mountain assemblies, community of goods, the avenging Christ. Genuine inside texts survive, despite Tabor being chronicled mostly by its opponents.
  • Nicholas of Pelhrimov (Biskupec), Taborite confession and chronicle (Chronicon Taboritarum) (primary) — The movement's own bishop defending Taborite doctrine and recording its history - the strongest inside text. Available only in Latin editions (Hoefler).
  • Lawrence of Brezova, Hussite Chronicle; John Pribram, The Lives of the Taborite Priests; Aeneas Sylvius Piccolomini's account of Tabor (1451) (primary) — Prague moderates writing against Tabor (Pribram's tract is an intra-Hussite heresiology), plus a later outside papal observer. Partial English translation available via Fudge.
  • Howard Kaminsky, A History of the Hussite Revolution (California, 1967) (secondary) — Still the standard for Tabor's theology-into-politics arc, built directly on these sources.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 4, ch. 28 'Wyclif's Bohemian reception' (Ghosh) and ch. 15 'Sacramental life' (Rubin) (reference) — No chapter is dedicated specifically to the Hussites or Taborites; these are the nearest anchors, shared with the wider Hussite movement this radical wing belongs to.
