The Reformation Era · 1453-1650 · Anatolia, Balkans, Greek islands

Anatolia, today in Turkey · Balkans and beyond

Orthodoxy under the Ottomans - Parish, Millet & Neomartyrs

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

When Constantinople fell in 1453 the Orthodox Church did not disappear; it was reorganized. The sultan recognized the Ecumenical Patriarch as head of the empire's Orthodox Christians — a civil as well as a religious office, taxing, judging and appointing. And for the next four centuries most Orthodox Christians lived as a tolerated but subordinate community under Muslim rule. Parish and monastic life continued, Mount Athos kept its monasteries, and a distinctive kind of saint appeared. The neomartyr, executed for refusing to convert or for returning to the faith after having converted, sometimes prepared for that death by a confessor beforehand. The period also produced the strangest episode in Orthodox history, the patriarch Cyril Lucaris, whose Calvinist-sounding Confession of 1629 was repudiated by a succession of church councils after his death in 1638.

In Its Own Record

Continues

  • The Byzantine Church, Palaiologan Era Documented Major — After the conquest of 1453 the sultan told the Christians to make a patriarch in their own way, since the old one had died. The bishops chose George Scholarios, still a layman, and named him Gennadios. A Greek chronicle under the name of George Sphrantzes tells the story.

Continued As

  • Orthodoxy under the Ottomans — the Second Act Documented Major — In 1672 a synod met at Jerusalem under Patriarch Dositheus. It set out the acts of two earlier synods against the chapters of Cyril Lucaris, held in 1638 and 1642. It said they 'most plainly shew the Orthodoxy of the Eastern Church,' and it praised their sentence.

Influenced By

  • The Reformed Cities - Zurich & Geneva Documented — Cyril Lucaris wrote to the pastors and teachers of Geneva as Patriarch of Constantinople. In that letter he called Calvin a most holy and wise teacher, now with the saints. A Latin copy of his Confession, printed in 1645, said it was so close to Calvin that it could have been copied from him.
  • The Kyiv-Mohyla Orthodox Revival Documented — Mohyla's circle in Kyiv sent its statement of the faith to the church of Constantinople to be judged. The patriarch's envoys and three Kyiv scholars worked on it together in Moldavia. In March 1643 the four Eastern patriarchs told every Orthodox Christian to read it and take it to heart.

Argued Against

  • The Reformed Cities - Zurich & Geneva Documented Major — Two synods of the Greek church condemned the Confession printed in Cyril Lucaris's name. The first condemned him for saying the whole Eastern Church thought as Calvin did. The second met under Patriarch Parthenius. It condemned 'Calvin's heresy' and every part of the book.Lasting result: Two synods of the Greek church condemned the book in Lucaris's name as Calvin's teaching and put it under a curse.

Opposed By

  • The Tridentine Church Documented — A papal brief for the envoy Canachio Rossi set out the case against Patriarch Cyril Lucaris. It said he denied prayer to the saints, honor to images and relics, Christ's real presence in the Eucharist, and free will. Thomas Smith printed it in 1707. He named Greeks raised on Rome's money at the Greek College among Lucaris's worst enemies.

Broke With

  • The Union of Brest & Ruthenian Greek Catholic Christianity Documented Major — At Brest on 8 October 1596 the metropolitan of Kyiv and his bishops placed themselves under Pope Clement VIII. A rival synod met in the same town. Three days later Nikephoros, who spoke for the patriarch in Constantinople, said they were cast out as traitors to the faith.

In Contact With

  • The Russian Church, Stoglav to the Eve of Nikon Documented Major — Patriarch Jeremias II of Constantinople came to Moscow in 1588. Giles Fletcher, the English envoy, wrote that the head of the Moscow church was made a patriarch. He thought the Greek had handed over his own see. A later Russian church history says Jeremias himself consecrated Job.Lasting result: In Moscow, Jeremias II made Job the first patriarch of the Russian church, and Moscow gained a patriarch of its own.

Sources

  • The neomartyrologies: Nikodemos the Hagiorite, Neon Martyrologion (1799); English access via Vaporis, Witnesses for Christ: Orthodox Christian Neomartyrs of the Ottoman Period 1437-1860 (SVS, 2000); Ottoman execution records as the hostile-administrative layer (primary) — Worth reading with genre in mind: the collection is itself a late-18th-century spiritual project. It documents a real pastoral texture — spiritual fathers preparing, and sometimes dissuading, would-be martyrs.
  • The Jeremias II-Tuebingen correspondence: Augsburg and Constantinople (trans. Mastrantonis, Holy Cross, 1982) (primary) — The patriarch's three Answers (1576-81) - captive Orthodoxy defining itself against the Reformation in its own measured voice.
  • The Lucaris controversy, both sides: Cyril Lucaris's Confession (1629) and the condemnations - Synod of Constantinople (1638), Synod of Jerusalem with Dositheus's Confession (1672; trans. Robertson, 1899) (primary) — An internal boundary contest under double outside pressure (Protestant embassies, Roman missions), with documents surviving from both sides of the dispute.
  • Parish/millet texture: patriarchal berats (Ottoman appointment warrants), kadi-court records used by Christians, parish and monastic registers, Athonite archives (primary) — Church life seen through the sultan's own bureaucracy — an outside, administrative pen, not the church's own voice.
  • Steven Runciman, The Great Church in Captivity (1968), with Papademetriou, Render unto the Sultan (2015) (secondary) — The classic account, dated in parts, paired with a current corrective.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 5, ch. 7 'The Great Church in captivity 1453-1586' (Zachariadou), with ch. 6 'Mount Athos and the Ottomans' (Zachariadou) (reference) — This period runs past where this particular volume ends; a related chapter (Kitromilides, "Orthodoxy and the West") may also be relevant but hasn't been directly confirmed.
