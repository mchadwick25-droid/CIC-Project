Charismatic Renewal & the Jesus Movement

North America, the UK and then the world, 1960 to the 1980s — Dennis Bennett's Van Nuys announcement, the mainline charismatic wave, Calvary Chapel and the Jesus movement, closing at the Vineyard's founding as the wave subsided and the churches it left carried on.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:10

Read along

On 3 April 1960 Dennis Bennett told his congregation in Van Nuys, California, that he had spoken in tongues. The announcement is conventionally taken as the start of the charismatic movement inside the historic Protestant churches, and it went where the earlier Pentecostal revival had not: into mainline Protestant parishes, whose members mostly stayed where they were. Bennett published his own account, Nine O'Clock in the Morning, in 1970. Alongside it and overlapping it came the Jesus movement, roughly 1967 to 1972, which reached young people outside the churches altogether and brought a great many of them in — Calvary Chapel, begun in 1965, became its best-known institutional home, and the music that came out of it changed what congregational worship sounded like. The Vineyard association formed in 1982, at the edge of this window, as the wave handed on to what came next.

Documented Stories

Nine O'Clock in the Morning

Dennis Bennett was rector of St Mark's Episcopal Church in Van Nuys, California, a congregation of some 2,500 in a new suburb, well regarded and by his own account spiritually dry. In November 1959 a couple from his parish told him they had received the baptism in the Holy Spirit and spoken in tongues, and after weeks of questioning them he prayed with them and did the same. Over the winter perhaps seventy of his parishioners followed. Word spread, and on Passion Sunday, 3 April 1960, Bennett decided to tell the whole congregation rather than let it be told for him. At the first two services he described what had happened plainly, without asking anyone to seek it. At the third, an assistant priest pulled off his vestments and walked out, saying he could no longer work with him; a vestryman stood and demanded his resignation; and Bennett, to prevent the parish tearing, resigned that morning. The bishop of Los Angeles, Francis Eric Bloy, issued a pastoral letter forbidding the practice of tongues under church auspices in the diocese. Newsweek and Time both carried the story that summer, and it was the coverage that made it a beginning: Episcopalians, Lutherans and Presbyterians across the country who had had the same experience wrote to Bennett, and found each other. The bishop of Olympia offered him St Luke's in Seattle, a failing mission of a few dozen; he took it in July 1960, and within a decade it had two thousand members.

The Preacher Two Movements Forgot

Lonnie Frisbee was nineteen in 1968, an art student from Costa Mesa who had taken LSD in the canyons and, by his own account, met Jesus there, when Chuck Smith, pastor of a small church called Calvary Chapel, took him in. Smith had been praying for a way to reach the hippies on the beaches; Frisbee was one. Within months the church was full of them. Frisbee preached with an intensity witnesses compared to the revivalists of the previous century, prayed for the sick and saw people fall, and led the mass baptisms at Pirate's Cove, Corona del Mar, where hundreds waded into the Pacific on a single afternoon; the photographs ran in Life and, in June 1971, on the cover of Time. Calvary Chapel grew into a movement of hundreds of congregations. Frisbee left in 1971 over Smith's discomfort with the gifts of the Spirit, and in 1980 was invited to speak at a small church in Yorba Linda led by John Wimber. On Mother's Day, 11 May 1980, he ended his sermon by asking the Holy Spirit to come, and what followed Wimber took as the beginning of the signs-and-wonders ministry that became the Vineyard. Frisbee also struggled, throughout, with homosexuality, and both movements knew it; both parted with him, and both later published histories that did not mention him. He died of AIDS on 12 March 1993, aged forty-three. At his funeral Chuck Smith spoke; the comparison made was to Samson.

Connections

In Its Own Record

This renewal is the North Atlantic middle. Behind it lay the early Pentecostal movement, which had mostly settled by the 1920s. Ahead lay the Global South Pentecostal explosion, from the 1950s on. Its usual starting point is Dennis Bennett's announcement in Van Nuys on 3 April 1960. Then came the charismatic wave in the mainline churches, Calvary Chapel in 1965 and the Jesus movement of 1967 to 1972. The Vineyard association of 1982 began the third wave. Calvary Chapel and the Vineyard built big new churches, of a kind some writers called megachurches. How far the seeker megachurches and the New Calvinist networks drew on this renewal is less clear. The Word of Faith movement grew in the same years. Kenneth Hagin's Rhema Bible Training Center dates from 1974. The renewal itself faded as a wave, but the bodies it made lived on and are named here.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

On 3 April 1960 Dennis Bennett told his Van Nuys, California congregation that he had spoken in tongues, an announcement conventionally marking the start of a charismatic movement that spread through mainline Protestant parishes without most of their members ever leaving. Alongside it, the Jesus movement of 1967 to 1972 reached young people outside any church, finding its best-known home at Calvary Chapel, founded in 1965, before the wave handed on to the newly formed Vineyard association in 1982.

Legacy

The wave faded, but the bodies it created did not. In 2010 Calvary Chapel and the Vineyard were still active, with churches worldwide. Its longest effect was probably musical. Chuck Smith of Calvary Chapel founded Maranatha! Music in 1971. Larry Eskridge's history of the Jesus People gives Jesus music a chapter. The worship style of the Jesus movement spread through a very large part of English-speaking Protestantism. It spread whether or not the singers knew where it came from. What else the renewal planted is harder to judge. The megachurches and the church-planting networks of the 2000s may have grown in ground it had turned over. That debt has not been shown directly. Roman Catholics shared in the same renewal. A Presbyterian report of 1969 already counted them in it.
