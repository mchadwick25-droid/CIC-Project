The Imperial Church Era · 393-c. 458 · Cyrrhus

Theodoret of Cyrrhus

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Antiochene Exegetical Christianity (Chrysostom-centered).

Known for
Bishop of Cyrrhus in Syria and a leading voice of the Antiochene side, who answered the anathemas of Cyril of Alexandria, was deposed at Ephesus in 449 and admitted at Chalcedon in 451, and wrote a Church History that takes up where Eusebius of Caesarea stopped.
Also called
Theodoret of Cyrus, Theodoretus
Dates
Born in 393 by Tillemont's reckoning, which Blomfield Jackson follows (Garnier gave 386 and Möller 390); bishop from 423 by Jackson's reckoning; deposed at Ephesus in 449; died in the reign of the emperor Leo, by Gennadius of Marseilles, which Jackson thinks was probably not later than 458
Place
Antioch, by Jackson's account; Cyrrhus in Syria; Chalcedon
Role
Bishop, theologian and church historian

About This Person

At the Council of Chalcedon, the bishops stood before the altar rails and shouted, "Theodoret must now anathematize Nestorius." They wanted him to curse Nestorius, the deposed bishop of Constantinople, by name. Theodoret asked for his papers to be read. They wanted nothing read. "Unless I set forth at length my faith I cannot speak," he said. They called him a heretic. At last he said, "Anathema to Nestorius." The council's acts record the scene, and Blomfield Jackson, his translator, quotes them.

Theodoret was bishop of Cyrrhus, in Syria. His letter to the consul Nomus says his parents promised him to God before he was conceived. He lived in a monastery and was "unwillingly consecrated" bishop, in 423 by Jackson's reckoning.

He wrote that letter to answer his accusers. "From the revenues of my see I erected public porticoes; I built two large bridges," he says. He brought eight villages of Marcion's followers into the church, and he was stoned many times. To Leo of Rome he wrote, "I have done pastoral duty in eight hundred churches."

Nestorius was his close friend, Jackson says. After Rome condemned Nestorius in 430, John of Antioch asked Theodoret to answer the anathemas of Cyril of Alexandria. Theodoret held that God the Word "was not made flesh by mutation, but took flesh." Mary is the bearer of God, he wrote, "not because she gave birth in natural manner to God, but to man united to the God that had fashioned Him." Jackson judges that he and Cyril "were substantially at one."

In 449 a second council at Ephesus deposed him in his absence. Its Syriac acts have Dioscorus of Alexandria condemn him for writing against Cyril, and ask the emperors to burn his books. Theodoret wrote to Leo that the bishop of Alexandria had stabbed him "with a pen," when "I was five and thirty days’ march away." He added, "I await the sentence of your apostolic see."

Leo restored him, and Chalcedon let him in two years later. Jackson reports that Bertram, a modern scholar, thinks Theodoret then changed his views, and Möller that he kept them. Leo wrote to him of "the victory you and we together had won." In 553 a council at Constantinople condemned what "Theodoret impiously wrote" against Cyril.

His Church History, which takes up where Eusebius of Caesarea stopped, opens with painters. "Historians substitute books for panels, bright description for pigments," he wrote.

Key Works

  • Church History: five books from the rise of Arius to the death of Theodore of Mopsuestia, a span the author counts as 105 years, beginning where Eusebius of Caesarea's history ends
  • Counter-statements to the anathemas of Cyril: his answers, one by one, to the twelve anathemas Cyril of Alexandria issued against Nestorius
  • Eranistes, or Polymorphus: dialogues against a speaker whom Blomfield Jackson reads as a picker-up of scraps of heresy from different quarters

Legacy

What traces to them

  • At Chalcedon the council's officials ordered that he enter "because the holy Archbishop Leo had restored the bishopric to him", as the acts quoted by Blomfield Jackson record.
  • In 553 a council at Constantinople condemned "those things which Theodoret impiously wrote against the right faith, and against the Twelve Chapters of the holy Cyril". Jackson notes that he was reinstated at Chalcedon in spite of what he had written, and what he had written was condemned in spite of his reinstatement.
  • A. C. Zenos, in the edition of Socrates of Constantinople, reports that Epiphanius Scholasticus put the histories of Socrates, Sozomen and Theodoret into Latin under Cassiodorus, as the Tripartite History.

How they're remembered

  • Leo of Rome, writing to him after Chalcedon, speaks of "the victory you and we together had won by assistance from on high over the blasphemy of Nestorius, as well as over the madness of Eutyches."
  • Dioscorus of Alexandria, in the Syriac acts of the council of 449 that deposed him, calls him one "who has brought trouble on all the Churches of the East".
  • Gennadius of Marseilles lists his works, among them books "Against Eutyches the presbyter and Dioscorus bishop of Alexandria".

At Major Councils

  • Council of Ephesus, 431: Sat with John of Antioch's assembly; named among those Cyril's council cut off
  • Council of Chalcedon, 451: Deposed in 449; admitted at Chalcedon after he anathematized Nestorius

What Is Uncertain

  • Much of his life comes from his own letters, written to answer his accusers and to win the help of Leo of Rome. The sentence of 449 is in the acts of the council that deposed him, which his opponents led. Blomfield Jackson, his translator, is the source for his friendship with Nestorius of Constantinople and for John of Antioch's request that he answer Cyril.
  • Jackson takes his birth and childhood at Antioch from Theodoret's Religious History, which the Library does not hold. Jackson gives 393, after Tillemont; he reports that Garnier gave 386 and Möller 390.
  • Whether his views changed at Chalcedon is disputed. Jackson reports that Bertram, writing in 1883, thinks Theodoret changed them, and Möller that he kept them, though put differently under pressure.
  • Jackson says the letter from John of Antioch's synod urging Nestorius to accept the title Theotokos "has been generally attributed to Theodoret". He does not settle it.
  • His death is not dated in any held line. Gennadius of Marseilles says he died in the reign of the emperor Leo. Ernest Cushing Richardson's note gives 457. Jackson finds "some probability that Theodoret survived till 458; none that he lived longer."
  • Gennadius speaks of ten books of his Church History. The held translation has five.
  • The English of his letters spells his see Cyrus; Richardson's note spells it Cyrrhaus.
