The Imperial Church Era · 393-c. 458 · Cyrrhus

Theodoret of Cyrrhus

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Antiochene Exegetical Christianity (Chrysostom-centered).

Known for
Bishop of Cyrrhus in Syria and a leading voice of the Antiochene side, who answered the anathemas of Cyril of Alexandria, was deposed at Ephesus in 449 and admitted at Chalcedon in 451, and wrote a Church History that takes up where Eusebius of Caesarea stopped.
Also called
Theodoret of Cyrus, Theodoretus
Dates
Born in 393 by the modern reckoning of Tillemont, which Blomfield Jackson, his modern translator, follows (the modern scholars Garnier gave 386 and Möller 390); bishop from 423 by Jackson's reckoning; deposed at Ephesus in 449; died in the reign of the emperor Leo, by Gennadius of Marseilles, which Jackson thinks was probably not later than 458
Place
Antioch, by the account of Blomfield Jackson, his modern translator; Cyrrhus in Syria; Chalcedon
Role
Bishop, theologian and church historian

About This Person

Theodoret of Cyrrhus wrote to the deposed Nestorius. "I will never agree to what was done against you, unjustly and wickedly, not even if someone cuts off both my hands, as God's grace works with me and strengthens my weak soul."

In the fifth century, Theodoret was a leading voice of Antioch's teaching that Christ is one, God and man, with neither mixed away into the other. He answered Cyril of Alexandria's twelve curses and was deposed in 449, and at Chalcedon, two years later, he was let back only after he cursed Nestorius by name. He was a historian too. His Church History carried the church's story on from Eusebius, and his Religious History kept the lives of Syrian monks.

Much of his life comes from letters he wrote to answer his accusers, who said he split Christ into two sons, and to win Leo of Rome's help. He says he lived in a monastery and was made bishop of Cyrrhus, a small town, "unwillingly". He led villages of Marcion's followers, a rival church that set aside the Old Testament, into his own and, he says, was stoned for it many times.

Cyril had sent out twelve curses against Nestorius's teaching, solemn acts that cut a person off from the church. The first fell on anyone who would not call Mary Mother of God. Theodoret charged that they mixed God and man. Asked by John of Antioch to answer them, he wrote: "The form of God was not changed into the form of a servant. It stayed what it was, and took the form of the servant." The form of a servant is Paul's phrase for a human life: God stayed God, and took one on. So Mary is rightly Mother of God, he wrote, not because she bore God by nature, but because of the union. At Ephesus in 431, Cyril's side condemned Nestorius before John's bishops arrived; Theodoret stood with John's side.

In time the two sides made peace, and Theodoret found that Cyril's letter agreed with his faith. But he would not accept that Nestorius had been rightly removed.

His Religious History told of many monks he says he saw and heard. Of Simeon, who stood on a pillar, he writes: "First he ordered it cut six cubits high, then twelve, after that twenty-two, and now thirty-six. For he longs to fly up to heaven, and to be set free from this life on earth." A cubit is the length of a forearm.

An order from the emperor kept him in Cyrrhus, with no charge and no accuser, he wrote, only for calling bishops together. The council of 449 at Ephesus, led by Dioscorus of Alexandria, deposed him in his absence. Its acts, his opponents' record, say he had written against Cyril, and ask the emperors to burn his books.

At Chalcedon, the council's acts record, the bishops at the altar rails demanded that he curse Nestorius. He said he condemned him, but asked to explain his faith first; they shouted for a plain curse and cried that he was a heretic. Then he said it: "Anathema to Nestorius", a curse on the man whose removal he had refused to accept, and on all who split the one Son into two. He was given back his church.

Long after, Evagrius, a Greek historian, called his History and three others "a most excellent record", and carried it on. A Syriac chronicle from the churches that refused Chalcedon's teaching of two natures, God and man, begins where he and two others stopped, so both sides carried his story on. Yet that same chronicle says he won Leo over with a bribe. In 553 a council at Constantinople condemned what he had written against Cyril.

To Leo he had written that in all his years as bishop he had gained not a house, not a piece of ground, not a coin, "not a tomb". He died in the reign of the emperor Leo, a later writer says; the year is not known.

Key Works

  • Church History: five books from the rise of Arius to the death of Theodore of Mopsuestia, a span the author counts as 105 years, beginning where Eusebius of Caesarea's history ends
  • Counter-statements to the anathemas of Cyril: his answers, one by one, to the twelve anathemas Cyril of Alexandria issued against Nestorius
  • Eranistes, or Polymorphus: dialogues against a speaker whom Blomfield Jackson, its modern translator, reads as a picker-up of scraps of heresy from different quarters

Legacy

What traces to them

  • At Chalcedon the council's officials ordered that he enter "because the holy Archbishop Leo had restored the bishopric to him", as the acts quoted by Blomfield Jackson record.
  • In 553 a council at Constantinople condemned "those things which Theodoret impiously wrote against the right faith, and against the Twelve Chapters of the holy Cyril". Blomfield Jackson, his modern translator, notes that he was reinstated at Chalcedon in spite of what he had written, and what he had written was condemned in spite of his reinstatement.
  • A. C. Zenos, a modern editor, in the edition of Socrates of Constantinople, reports that Epiphanius Scholasticus put the histories of Socrates, Sozomen and Theodoret into Latin under Cassiodorus, as the Tripartite History.
  • At the first session of Chalcedon, as the acts quoted by Blomfield Jackson record, the bishops of Egypt, Illyricum and Palestine called out as he entered, "Cast out the teacher of Nestorius." Later the Egyptians and the bishops with them cried, "Do not call him a bishop! He is not a bishop! Cast out the fighter against God! Cast out the Jew!" The bishops of the East and of Pontus, Asia and Thrace shouted, "We had to sign a blank paper; we were scourged, and so we signed." The officials answered, "The vulgar cries are not worthy of bishops".
  • Blomfield Jackson tells that the emperor Theodosius, hunting near his capital in July 450, was thrown from the saddle into a stream, hurt his spine and died a few days afterwards. He says a council was then conceded "to reconsider and rectify the crimes and blunders of the Latrocinium", his name for the council of 449; his note names Marcian as the emperor who summoned the bishops to Chalcedon. He adds that the decree keeping Theodoret in Cyrrhus appears to have been rescinded, so that he was free to present himself.
  • Evagrius, writing from the acts, says that at the council of 449 the deposition of Eutyches was revoked by Dioscorus and his associates, sentence was passed on Flavian and on Eusebius, president of the church of Doryleum, and they deposed Theodoret of Cyrus.
  • A. C. Zenos adds that the Tripartite History, the Latin of the three histories made under Cassiodorus, consists of twelve books.

How they're remembered

  • Leo of Rome, writing to him after Chalcedon, speaks of "the victory you and we together had won by assistance from on high over the blasphemy of Nestorius, as well as over the madness of Eutyches."
  • Dioscorus of Alexandria, in the Syriac acts of the council of 449 that deposed him, calls him one "who has brought trouble on all the Churches of the East".
  • Gennadius of Marseilles lists his works, among them books "Against Eutyches the presbyter and Dioscorus bishop of Alexandria".
  • Juvenal of Jerusalem, in the Syriac acts of 449, gives his decision that Theodoret be removed from the priesthood and from communion, calls all men to witness that "not even a single communication between him and them ought to take place, nor ought they to offer salt, or (address even) a mere word, to him."
  • The Syriac chronicle under the name of Zachariah, from the side that refused Chalcedon, says "the story goes" that Dioscorus, seeing Theodoret sitting upon the throne in the Council and speaking from it, rose, came down from the throne and sat upon the pavement, saying "I will not sit with the wicked, nor with vain persons will I enter in." The chronicle does not name the council in that sentence.

At Major Councils

  • Council of Ephesus, 431: Sat with John of Antioch's assembly; named among those Cyril's council cut off
  • Council of Chalcedon, 451: Deposed in 449; admitted at Chalcedon after he anathematized Nestorius
  • Second Council of Constantinople, 553: Long dead; his writings against Cyril condemned

What Is Uncertain

  • Much of his life comes from his own letters, written to answer his accusers and to win the help of Leo of Rome. The sentence of 449 is in the acts of the council that deposed him, which his opponents led. Blomfield Jackson, his modern translator, is the source for his friendship with Nestorius of Constantinople.
  • Jackson takes his birth and childhood at Antioch from Theodoret's Religious History, which the Library holds in Greek. Jackson gives 393, after Tillemont; he reports that Garnier gave 386 and Möller 390. All four are modern scholars.
  • Whether his views changed at Chalcedon is disputed. Jackson reports that Bertram, writing in 1883, thinks Theodoret changed them, and Möller that he kept them, though put differently under pressure.
  • Jackson says the letter from John of Antioch's synod urging Nestorius to accept the title Theotokos "has been generally attributed to Theodoret". He does not settle it.
  • His death is not dated in any held line. Gennadius of Marseilles says he died in the reign of the emperor Leo. Ernest Cushing Richardson, a modern translator of Gennadius, gives 457 in a note. Jackson finds "some probability that Theodoret survived till 458; none that he lived longer."
  • Gennadius speaks of ten books of his Church History. The held translation has five.
  • The English of his letters spells his see Cyrus; Richardson's note spells it Cyrrhaus.
  • His letter to Nestorius survives only in Latin: Jackson says the Greek copies have perished and three Latin versions exist, and his English follows one of them. No held line dates it. Jackson thinks it fell not long after Theodoret received Cyril's Twelve Chapters, but in the letter he speaks of having accepted the Egyptian writings, which fits the peace that Jackson dates to 433. The date is Inferential-Thin; the refusal to agree to what was done to Nestorius is Documented.
  • That no charge was brought and no accuser appeared is Theodoret's own complaint, in his letter to the consul Nomus. The Syriac acts of 449 show the presbyter Pelagius laying a written charge against him. No held line dates the emperor's order that kept him in Cyrrhus; Jackson puts the edict about a year after the deposition of Irenaeus on 17 February 448.
  • The Syriac acts are of the session of 22 August 449. Jackson gives 8 August for the opening of the council and 11 August for Theodoret's deposition.
  • Jackson calls the Chalcedon session at which Theodoret was made to curse Nestorius the eighth; Evagrius calls it the ninth meeting.
  • Jackson says some doubt hangs over whether Theodoret went back to Cyrrhus after Chalcedon or lived at his monastery of Nicerte.
  • That he won Leo over with a bribe is the charge of the Syriac chronicle, written from the side that refused Chalcedon, which says he did it with the gift which blinds the eyes of the soul. No other held witness says so.
  • Jackson dates the Church History to 438-439 or a little later. No held line dates the Religious History, which the Library holds only in Migne's Greek of 1864.
  • His parents' promise comes from his own letter to the consul Nomus, written to answer his accusers: "Even before my conception my parents promised to devote me to God".
  • In his letter to Leo, bishop of Rome, he says "I have been condemned without a trial", and he asks Leo whether he ought to put up with the deposition: "If you bid me abide by the sentence of condemnation, I abide". These are his own claims, made to win Leo's help.
  • In his Letter 119 he asked to be allowed to go to the West and plead his cause, or at least to be commanded to live in his monastery, which Jackson's note names as Nicerte. That the second request was granted is Jackson's statement, not Theodoret's.
  • In his Prologue he gives his reason for writing the Church History: he thinks it not right to look on without an effort "while oblivion robs noble deeds and useful stories of their due fame".
  • In Letter 150 to John of Antioch he says that Cyril's teaching renews "that vain and impious teaching of Apollinarius which was long ago stamped out". This is his charge against Cyril, written while he answered the anathemas.
  • Blomfield Jackson says that at Ephesus in 431 Theodoret arrived with his metropolitan, Alexander of Hierapolis, and was one of the deputies of the Easterners who hurried to Constantinople but "were allowed to proceed no further than Chalcedon".
  • In the first dialogue of the Eranistes a testimony of Methodius, bishop and martyr, from his discourse on the martyrs, is quoted; a note by Blomfield Jackson, its modern translator, says this is the only fragment of Methodius that Theodoret preserved.
  • Blomfield Jackson lists a Compendium of Heretical Fables among Theodoret's controversial works beside the Eranistes, and says Photius counts five books of it.
  • Hope W. Hogg, a modern editor, in the Ante-Nicene Fathers edition of Tatian's Diatessaron, cites Theodoret's book on Heresies, which he dates to 453, for the statement that Tatian cut out the genealogies and whatever other passages show that the Lord was born of the seed of David according to the flesh. Hogg adds that Theodoret found more than two hundred copies of the Diatessaron "held in respect in the churches". The book itself is not printed in the held volume, so the wording is the editor's.
  • In the Religious History he says the crowds who came to Simeon were past counting and all tried to touch him and take a blessing from his leather clothes, and that Simeon devised standing on a pillar. This is his own account, read from the Greek of one damaged print.
  • In the Religious History he says that Ishmaelites came to Simeon in bands of two hundred, three hundred and sometimes a thousand, broke their idols before him and took part in the divine mysteries, and that he was an eyewitness of these things and heard them. This is his own account, read from the Greek of one damaged print.
  • In his letter to the consul Nomus he says that from the revenues of his see he built two large bridges and, finding that the river did not water the town, a conduit that supplied it with water. These are his own claims, made to answer his accusers.
  • In the Prologue to his Church History he says that Eusebius wrote a history of the Church from the apostles to the reign of Constantine, and that he will begin his history from the period at which Eusebius's ends.
  • Blomfield Jackson summarizes that Theodoret refused to accept the deposition of Nestorius, and that several years after the peace of 433 he found means of attaching a meaning to the current anathemas on Nestorianism, not, as he said, on Nestorius, which allowed him to submit. This is the translator's summary of events, not Theodoret's own account.
  • In the acts of Chalcedon as Jackson quotes them, Theodoret answered that he anathematized Nestorius and Eutyches and everyone who says there are two Sons, and asked to set out his faith; the bishops shouted for him to speak plainly and anathematize Nestorius and those who think with him. Evagrius reports that Eutyches said he allowed two natures before the union but confessed only one after it.
  • Blomfield Jackson, his translator, describes Cyrrhus as a town of the district Cyrestica, "a wretched little place" and scantily inhabited. This is the translator's description, a modern judgment. Theodoret's own words are "solitary as it is, and with only a small population, and that a poor one".
  • Blomfield Jackson says Nestorius "was bound by ties of close friendship both to Theodoret and to John, patriarch of Antioch", and calls him Theodoret's "ancient friend" when he tells of the demand for the curse at Chalcedon. This is the translator's word, a modern judgment. Theodoret's own letter shows only that he would not agree to what was done against Nestorius.
  • The year of the peace between Cyril and John is not given in any held old line. 433 is the modern reckoning of Blomfield Jackson, his translator. The heading of Walford's modern English edition puts the close of Evagrius's history at 594 AD, also a modern reckoning. Evagrius himself says only that he closes it in the twelfth year of the reign of Maurice Tiberius.
