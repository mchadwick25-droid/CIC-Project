Alexandria, today in Egypt

Alexander II of Alexandria

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Coptic Christianity under Early Islam.

Known for
Coptic patriarch of Alexandria under a run of hard governors. Twice he had to beg his own ransom, and he died fleeing a branding.
Dates
B. Evetts heads his life 705 to 730. The History says he was consecrated on the feast of Saint Mark, the last day of Barmudah, in the year 420 of Diocletian. It gives him twenty-four and a half years but no date of death. The Arabic Synaxarium keeps him on the 7th of Amshir
Place
Alexandria. A monk of the monastery of Az-Zajaj, which a note in Evetts's 1895 English of Abu Salih places nine miles west of Alexandria. He died at Tarnut
Role
Patriarch of Alexandria, the forty-third in the History's count

About This Person

Alexander II, the Coptic patriarch of Alexandria, went to Misr to greet the new governor, Abd Allah, as patriarchs did. "What is this man?" Abd Allah asked. He held the patriarch until he paid three thousand dinars. Then George, a deacon from Dimru, went to the governor. "Dost thou desire the life of the patriarch or money?" he asked. "I wish for the money," Abd Allah answered. George took Alexander round the North for two months, begging, until they had the sum.

The History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria, a later Arabic history of the Coptic church, tells it this way. Alexander was a monk of the monastery of Az-Zajaj. He was consecrated on the feast of Saint Mark, after the see had stood empty three years. B. Evetts, the History's translator, gives his years as 705 to 730.

The next governor, Kurrah, sent by the caliph al-Walid, arrested him as well. "Our Law bids us not to lay up treasure," Alexander told him. Alexander asked leave to beg in Upper Egypt, and Kurrah let him go. "Since the time of the Father Benjamin we have not seen a patriarch in Upper Egypt," the people said.

Kurrah took the church's gold and silver vessels. At Easter, the people and clergy of Alexandria demanded their dues. He told them they now used chalices of glass and patens of wood. They reviled him, the History says, and he bore it.

The History says that under the governor Usamah, monks were branded on the left hand with a ring-shaped iron. Eusèbe Renaudot, a Catholic scholar writing in Latin in 1713, says instead that they had to wear an iron ring on the right hand. The governor Ubaid Allah had the Christians counted and marked, and tried to brand Alexander too. Alexander refused. He asked for three days, and he prayed to die rather than be branded. On the third day he fell sick.

"Prepare the boat at sunset that we may depart, for to-morrow the Lord Jesus Christ will visit me," he told the brothers. They fled down the river and reached Tarnut by morning, and there he died. Shamul, bishop of Wasim, was the only bishop with him.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • He accepted a synodical letter from Elias, whom the bishops of Antioch seated while the caliph al-Walid forbade a patriarch, the History says.
  • Later he wrote to Athanasius of Antioch blessing the caliph Hisham, who had let Athanasius be made patriarch.
  • He took in Onopes, a Chalcedonian physician who had been set up as patriarch, when the people drove him out.

How they're remembered

  • When he begged in Upper Egypt, the History says, people said they had not seen a patriarch there since Benjamin I.
  • The Arabic Synaxarium, the Coptic church's book of saints' days, keeps him on the 7th of Amshir in René Basset's French. The Ethiopic, in E. A. Wallis Budge's English, keeps him on the 7th of Yakatit.
  • Both synaxaria add a Melkite patriarch named Anastasius, who came over to him and asked only to be his disciple.
  • The History's life of Michael I recalls that the church of Alexandria lost its marble and fine woodwork in Alexander's time.

What Is Uncertain

  • His life rests on the History of the Patriarchs, a later Arabic history of the Coptic church. It says the emperor Anastasius "is still reigning". A note in it explains that the writer means at the time he wrote. B. Evetts says a translator added that note.
  • Who wrote it. John the deacon, who wrote the History's life of Michael I, says the archdeacon George's history ran down to the time of the caliph Sulaiman. John says he himself told what followed, down to the death of Theodore, patriarch of Alexandria. Eusèbe Renaudot, a Catholic scholar writing in Latin in 1713, reads John the same way. He puts the years under the governor Usamah in John's part.
  • The monks under al-Asbagh. B. Evetts's English says they were mutilated and taxed. Renaudot says they were counted.
  • The brand. The History says Usamah branded each monk on the left hand with a ring-shaped iron, adding the name of his church and monastery. Renaudot says they had to wear an iron ring on the right hand, with the name of their church or monastery.
  • Ubaid Allah's marks. The History gives a leaden badge on the neck and a lion on the hand. Renaudot gives one leaden seal with a lion.
  • His dates. Renaudot calls the chronology of these patriarchs most confused, and says the vacancy before him lasted nearly five years. He reports Makrizi's date for his death, 724.
  • Onopes. Renaudot doubts the story, since Eutychius names no Melkite patriarch of Alexandria under al-Walid. The synaxaria tell of a Melkite named Anastasius instead.
  • His monastery. The History says Az-Zajaj. Basset's synaxarium says Babaroun, the monastery of the Fathers.
