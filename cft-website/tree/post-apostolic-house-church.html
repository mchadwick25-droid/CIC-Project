About This Movement

Early in the second century, Pliny governed a Roman province. He wrote to the emperor that Christians had spread through its cities, villages and countryside. He questioned people who said they had stopped being Christians. An informer had named them. Pliny was unsure whether to punish the name Christian itself, or crimes that went with it. Part of their guilt, or their error, they told him, was this: "On a fixed day they used to meet before dawn. They sang a hymn to Christ as to a god, taking turns among themselves." To find the truth, Pliny, a hostile witness, had two slave women tortured. They were called ministrae, servers.

In the hundred years after the apostles, Christians in Antioch, in the cities of Asia Minor and in Rome met together, and their letters greet one another household by household. They taught newcomers a way of life before baptism, gave thanks over bread and a cup on the Lord's day, and were led by elders, bishops and deacons. Letters and messengers carried between their churches held them together.

A short manual, whose writer, date and place no one knows, opens on the Two Ways. "There are two ways, one of life and one of death, and there is a great difference between the two ways. The way of life is this. First, you shall love God, who made you. Second, your neighbor as yourself." Newcomers learned this way of life before being baptized in the name of the Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Only the baptized could share the thanksgiving, the eucharist in Greek.

Near the end of the first century, the church at Corinth, in Greece, removed some of its elders, the senior men who led the church together. At Corinth's request, the church of Rome wrote. Its letter, the only side that survives, blames envy, and one or two people who stirred Corinth against men who had served blamelessly. God sent Christ, it argues, and Christ sent the apostles. They appointed bishops and deacons, knowing there would be strife over the office, and ordered that approved men succeed them. Men appointed with the whole church's consent, who served blamelessly, could not justly be removed.

Ignatius, bishop of Antioch, was brought from Syria as a prisoner, he wrote, for the name of Christ. On the road he wrote to the churches of Asia, a Roman province in today's western Turkey. Each church, he urged, should act as one around its bishop, with its elders and deacons. He greeted households, and asked Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna, to have churches send messengers or letters to Antioch.

Justin, a Christian, wrote to the emperor Antoninus Pius what Christians did each week: "On the day named after the Sun, all who live in the towns or in the country gather in one place. The memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets are read, for as long as time allows." These memoirs are called Gospels. Bread, wine and water were brought to the one who presides. He gave thanks as he was able, and the people said Amen. Deacons gave to each and carried a share to those absent. They met on the Sun's day because on it God made the world and Christ rose from the dead.

Later, Dionysius, bishop of Corinth, wrote, in words the church historian Eusebius keeps, that his church still drew counsel from Rome's letter in the quarrel, written through Clement.

Near the end of the century, the churches of Asia kept Easter on the Passover date, the moon's fourteenth day; others kept it only on Sunday. Rome's bishop tried to cut them off from fellowship. Irenaeus of Lyon reminded him of the elders who had led Rome's own church before Soter, an earlier bishop. These same men, Irenaeus wrote elsewhere, stood in Rome's line of bishops, which carried the apostles' teaching to his day. Those elders did not keep Asia's day, yet lived at peace with visitors who did, and sent them a share of the thanksgiving.

Voices in the Record

  • Ignatius, bishop of Antioch, wrote seven letters to congregations while he was marched toward Rome under armed guard, c. 107-117. Polycarp's own letter later speaks of him as already dead, though it admits not knowing the details of what happened.
  • Clement of Rome wrote on behalf of the Roman church to the Corinthians near the end of the first century, addressing a dispute over deposed leaders. The letter is the earliest Christian writing outside the New Testament to survive.
  • Polycarp of Smyrna was addressed as bishop by Ignatius of Antioch, though his own letter never claims the title, opening only as 'Polycarp, and the presbyters with him.' He was put to death at Smyrna; his own congregation recorded his death.
  • Justin Martyr was a philosopher who kept teaching Christianity as a school in Rome. He left the earliest detailed description of what their Sunday actually looked like.
  • Irenaeus had seen Polycarp at Smyrna as a boy, and later led the church at Lyon in Gaul. While Eleutherius was bishop of Rome he wrote five books Against Heresies, naming Rome's bishops in order down to his own day. His works sit on this tradition's Library shelf.
  • Most of these communities' hosts and householders are never named in the record. The gatherings met in their homes, but no record says whose.
  • Two enslaved women who served among these communities, called ministrae, are named in their Roman interrogator's letter only by what they were called — never by their own names. A governor had them tortured early in the second century to learn what Christians did; it is the earliest outside evidence that women here held a formal title at all.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In the decades after the apostles died, these house churches held together through letters and messengers carried between them: Ignatius of Antioch wrote seven letters while under armed guard, and Rome wrote at length to settle a quarrel in Corinth.

Legacy

Almost every Christian tradition claims a root in these early gatherings, and the basic rhythm of Christian worship shows up earliest here. Believers met on the first day of the week and read aloud from the prophets and from the apostles' memoirs. And they shared the bread and the cup, either in a full common meal or as the bread and wine alone.

With the apostles no longer there to consult, these communities had to work out how to live together: how to treat one another, how to pray, and how to forgive and restore those who fell into sin or caused division. The roles of bishop, presbyter, and deacon were already named in the apostolic writings, but trying to live out those instructions under local pressures gave the offices more concrete form.

The apostolic texts themselves gained authority in much the same way. Reading the apostles' letters aloud week after week became an enduring habit, and it was through this regular use in worship—not by an early decree—that these writings came to be heard alongside the ancient Jewish Scriptures. Carried by travelers from city to city, these letters bound scattered households together, and writings like those of Clement of Rome, Ignatius of Antioch, and Polycarp were preserved and read for generations to come.

In the early 300s, Eusebius says that the letter Rome sent to Corinth had been "publicly used in a great many churches both in former times and in our own." In 367, Athanasius set "the Teaching of the Apostles, and the Shepherd" among the books "appointed by the Fathers to be read by those who newly join us."

Sources

Doctrinal Floor Note

These communities lived two centuries before the Nicene Creed was written. What they confessed is part of the raw material the creed was later drawn from, not a departure from it.

How Sure the Record Is

Moderate to rich for communal life — letters, church order, worship — but its most vivid material leans on one contested corpus: Ignatius of Antioch's seven letters, whose authorship and dating (c. 107–117 CE traditionally; rival readings place them decades later, even as later composition) remain a genuine, unresolved scholarly dispute. Individual, first-person voice stays thin throughout: no woman and no non-literate believer — the majority of this movement — speaks to us in words of their own.

  • Ignatius of Antioch, Epistle to the Ephesians (primary) — One of seven letters Ignatius wrote to congregations along his route to execution in Rome, c. 107–117.
  • Clement of Rome, First Epistle to the Corinthians (primary) — Written from the Roman church to Corinth around 96 to settle a dispute over deposed leaders — the earliest Christian letter outside the New Testament.
  • The Didache (Teaching of the Twelve Apostles) (primary) — A short manual of church practice and baptismal instruction, lost for centuries and recovered from a single surviving manuscript only in the 1870s.
  • Martyrdom of Polycarp (primary) — A letter from the church at Smyrna to Philomelium recounting Polycarp's death around 155, written partly as an eyewitness account by his own congregation.
  • Polycarp, Epistle to the Philippians (primary) — Polycarp's own letter, forwarding Ignatius's letters onward at another church's request and urging the Philippians toward the same practical, everyday faithfulness Ignatius wrote about.
  • Justin Martyr, First Apology (primary) — An open defense addressed to the Roman emperor and Senate; contains the earliest detailed outside description of what a Christian Sunday gathering actually looked like.
  • The Martyrdom of Justin and Companions (tradition) — An anonymous trial transcript of Justin's interrogation before the Roman prefect Rusticus, in which he describes living and teaching above a public bath in Rome; ends in his execution, c. 165.
  • Shepherd of Hermas (primary) — A vision-and-parable text widely read — and by some congregations nearly treated as Scripture — in the House-Churches of second-century Rome.
  • Pliny the Younger, Letters, Book 10.96 (primary) — A Roman governor's official report to the emperor Trajan on investigating Christians in Bithynia, including the torture of two enslaved women called ministrae — deaconesses.
