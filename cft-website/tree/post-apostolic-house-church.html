Antioch · Asia Minor, today in Turkey and beyond

Post-Apostolic Household-Church Christianity

Meet Chloe on Church in Conversation

About This Movement

In the generations following the apostles, the earliest Christian movement had no documented church buildings and no single leadership structure. They had the Old Testament. The gospels and letters of the apostles moved between communities as copies were made and passed along, though no fixed collection had been settled yet. It was a network of faithful households, scattered across Antioch, Asia Minor, and Rome, meeting wherever they could. New believers were taught the Two Ways on the road to baptism; only then were they brought into the fellowship to share the weekly breaking of bread and prayer. Across long distances, these household communities were held together by carried letters.

Some communities were guided by a single bishop alongside elders and deacons, others by a circle of elders alone—both forms were real, and neither was settled. The questions they wrestled with had no easy answers: How do we make room for genuine doubt? How should conflict within the community be resolved? What did women's leadership look like? Following Jesus routinely meant loss of social standing, suspicion from neighbors, and periodic waves of official violence.

In his surviving letters, Ignatius of Antioch describes writing to sister churches while being marched toward Rome under armed guard — soldiers he called 'ten leopards,' who, in his own words, 'show themselves all the worse' the more kindly he treats them. Justin Martyr, who told his imperial interrogator that he taught above a public bathhouse in Rome, wrote down the earliest record of what their Sunday actually looked like—the reading of the memoirs, the common loaf and cup, and the collection gathered for orphans, widows, and travelers. Justin was executed for refusing to abandon his Lord; tradition has it that Ignatius met the same end.

The earliest outside account of these communities comes from Roman authority, not from inside the movement. Around 112 CE, the governor Pliny the Younger wrote to the emperor Trajan describing an investigation: believers who had recanted told him, under threat of ordinary punishment, that these communities met before dawn to sing a hymn to Christ 'as God,' bound themselves by oath against theft and adultery, then met again later for a meal they called 'common yet harmless.' Still unsatisfied, Pliny had two enslaved women who held office among them — titled ministrae, deacons — questioned under torture. He learned, he wrote, only 'a superstition depraved and immoderate.' Their names do not survive; his letter is the earliest outside evidence that women held a formal title here at all.

In Its Own Record

Continued As

  • The Roman Church in the Third Century Widely Accepted Major — Irenaeus lists the bishops of Rome down to Eleutherius in the 180s. Hippolytus shows Zephyrinus at the head of the same church about a generation on. But Peter Lampe argues that Rome had no single bishop in charge until about 189 to 199.

Argued Against

  • Montanism (the New Prophecy) Documented Major — Eusebius of Caesarea quotes a writer of about 192 whose name is lost. He says the faithful of Asia met many times to test what the prophets of Phrygia taught. They found it to be heresy, and cut those who held it off from communion. Apolinarius, bishop of Hierapolis, also wrote against the Phrygians.Lasting result: Meetings of the faithful across Asia judged the New Prophecy to be heresy. Its followers were thrown out of the church and barred from communion.
  • Marcion / Marcionism Documented Major — Tertullian says Marcion gave money to the church at Rome. Later the church threw out both him and his gift. Irenaeus knew Polycarp of Smyrna. He says Polycarp turned many in Rome away from Marcion. He also tells how Polycarp called Marcion 'the first-born of Satan' to his face.Lasting result: The church later threw Marcion out, Tertullian says, and turned away the money he had given it along with him.
  • Valentinian & other Gnostic Christianities Documented Major — Irenaeus says Valentinus came to Rome when Hyginus was bishop. He stayed until the time of Anicetus. Polycarp of Smyrna came to Rome, he says, and turned many from Valentinus and Marcion to the church. Tertullian adds that the church at Rome threw Valentinus out more than once.Lasting result: Tertullian says the church at Rome threw out Valentinus more than once for spreading his ideas among its members.

Sources

  • Ignatius of Antioch, Epistle to the Ephesians (primary) — One of seven letters Ignatius wrote to congregations along his route to execution in Rome, c. 107–117.
  • Clement of Rome, First Epistle to the Corinthians (primary) — Written from the Roman church to Corinth around 96 to settle a dispute over deposed leaders — the earliest Christian letter outside the New Testament.
  • The Didache (Teaching of the Twelve Apostles) (primary) — A short manual of church practice and baptismal instruction, lost for centuries and recovered from a single surviving manuscript only in the 1870s.
  • Martyrdom of Polycarp (primary) — A letter from the church at Smyrna to Philomelium recounting Polycarp's death around 155, written partly as an eyewitness account by his own congregation.
  • Polycarp, Epistle to the Philippians (primary) — Polycarp's own letter, forwarding Ignatius's letters onward at another church's request and urging the Philippians toward the same practical, everyday faithfulness Ignatius wrote about.
  • Justin Martyr, First Apology (primary) — An open defense addressed to the Roman emperor and Senate; contains the earliest detailed outside description of what a Christian Sunday gathering actually looked like.
  • The Martyrdom of Justin and Companions (tradition) — An anonymous trial transcript of Justin's interrogation before the Roman prefect Rusticus, in which he describes living and teaching above a public bath in Rome; ends in his execution, c. 165.
  • Shepherd of Hermas (primary) — A vision-and-parable text widely read — and by some congregations nearly treated as Scripture — in the House-Churches of second-century Rome.
  • Pliny the Younger, Letters, Book 10.96 (primary) — A Roman governor's official report to the emperor Trajan on investigating Christians in Bithynia, including the torture of two enslaved women called ministrae — deaconesses.
