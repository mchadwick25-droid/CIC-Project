About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:17

Read along

The Russian revolution scattered a whole church's worth of thinkers, monks and parishioners across Europe, and a remarkable number of them landed in Paris. The St Sergius Institute was founded there in 1925 and became the center of it. Sergei Bulgakov's theology drew a controversy of its own between 1935 and 1937, argued in print on both sides; Georges Florovsky's Ways of Russian Theology (1937) called the emigration back to the Greek fathers; Vladimir Lossky's Mystical Theology of the Eastern Church, written in French and published in 1944, put Orthodox theology in front of Western readers who had never had it. YMCA Press printed what they wrote. Mother Maria Skobtsova ran a hostel in Paris, sheltered Jews under the occupation, and died at Ravensbrück on 31 March 1945. The next generation — Alexander Schmemann, John Meyendorff — carried the work to America, and Schmemann's journals from 1973 to 1983 record what that cost and what it was for.

Documented Stories

The Condemnations of 1935

Sergei Bulgakov, a Marxist economist turned priest in 1918 and expelled from Russia in 1922, had been dean of the St Sergius Institute in Paris since 1925 and had built a theology in which Sophia, the divine Wisdom, was the ground of God's relation to creation, a scheme his critics found closer to Soloviev than to the fathers. On 7 September 1935 Metropolitan Sergius of Moscow issued an ukaz condemning the teaching as alien to the Orthodox faith, without, Bulgakov noted, any indication that its author had read the books. In October the Karlovtsy synod of the Russian Church Abroad, which rejected Moscow's authority, declared the teaching heretical too. Bulgakov's own bishop, Metropolitan Evlogy of the Russian exarchate in Western Europe, appointed a commission of the institute's professors, which reported in 1936 that the teaching contained errors of expression and dangerous novelties but no heresy, and that its author was a faithful priest; a minority dissented. Vladimir Lossky, then a young layman, published in 1936 a pamphlet defending Moscow's condemnation on patristic grounds; Florovsky, Bulgakov's colleague, kept a silence that everyone read; Bulgakov published his answer and went on teaching. The affair split the emigration's theologians for a generation, put the Paris school's two great tendencies, the sophiological and the neo-patristic, on record against each other, and left Bulgakov, who died in 1944 with his last book unfinished, neither condemned by his own church nor cleared by the others. The question was still open in Orthodox theology in 2010.

Mother Maria at the Vélodrome d’Hiver

Elizaveta Skobtsova, a poet of the Petersburg avant-garde, twice married, was professed as a nun in Paris in 1932 on condition that she live not in a convent but in the world; as Mother Maria she rented a house in the rue de Lourmel and filled it with the emigration's destitute, begging food at Les Halles at dawn, and writing that the way to God ran through the neighbor's need and nowhere else. Her chaplain from 1939 was Father Dimitri Klepinin. Under the occupation the house became a place where Jews could obtain baptismal certificates, which Klepinin issued to anyone who asked, and be hidden or passed on. On 16 and 17 July 1942 the French police rounded up some thirteen thousand Jews of Paris into the Vélodrome d'Hiver, a cycling stadium, and held them for days without water before deportation. Mother Maria, in her habit, got inside, how the accounts differ, spent three days among the prisoners, and, according to testimony gathered after the war, carried out at least four children hidden in the rubbish bins. The Gestapo arrested her son Yuri in February 1943, then Klepinin, then her; Klepinin and Yuri died at Dora in 1944. She was sent to Ravensbrück, where survivors remembered her embroidering an icon and telling them that it was not a question of how much one could bear. On 31 March 1945 she was gassed, either selected or, in one account, in place of another prisoner. All three were canonized in 2004.

Connections

In Its Own Record

The Russian Revolution scattered a generation of Orthodox monks, thinkers and parishioners across Europe, many settling in Paris. Its roots run through more than one strand of Russian religious life — the Optina monastic tradition and the religious-philosophical renaissance associated with Sergei Bulgakov and Nikolai Berdyaev both feed into it. Much of English-speaking Orthodoxy in the twentieth century descended from this Paris generation. The church the émigrés left behind in Russia has its own entry, the Russian Orthodox Church.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

The Russian revolution scattered a generation of Orthodox monks, thinkers and parishioners across Europe, and many landed in Paris, where the St Sergius Institute opened in 1925. Mother Maria Skobtsova ran a hostel there, sheltered Jews under the German occupation, and died at Ravensbrück on 31 March 1945. Vladimir Lossky's Mystical Theology of the Eastern Church, written in French and published in 1944, put Orthodox theology in front of Western readers for the first time.

Legacy

The Paris generation's own episode closed around 1970, with the handoff its institutions had been building toward: the Orthodox Church in America received autocephaly in 1970, and St Vladimir's Seminary in New York became the school this tradition had built in a second country. Much of English-speaking Orthodoxy is descended from here. Skobtsova was canonized in 2004. Schmemann's For the Life of the World was put into students' hands in every tradition. Lossky became the book people were given when they asked what Orthodox theology actually says.
