Martin of Braga

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Nicene Visigothic Iberia.

Known for
Bishop of the monastery of Dumio and then of Braga in the kingdom of the Sueves in Galicia. Isidore of Seville ties him to the Sueves' turn from the Arian teaching to the Catholic faith. Martin led the Second Council of Braga and wrote a sermon against the pagan customs of country people.
Also called
Martinus Dumiensis, Martinus Bracarensis, Martinus Galliciensis episcopus
Dates
No early source used here gives his birth year or the year of his death. Gregory of Tours tells of his death among the events of the fifth year of King Childebert II of the Franks, which C. P. Caspari and the Bollandists reckon as 580; Bruno Krusch gives 580, and O. M. Dalton about 580, on 20 March. Gregory says he was bishop about thirty years; the Bollandists hold it was about twenty. Caspari places his birth in about the second decade of the sixth century and his coming to Galicia about 550. The Second Council of Braga, which he led, is dated in King Miro's second year, Spanish era 610, which the Bollandists give as 572
Place
Pannonia by birth, Gregory of Tours and his own epitaph say; the East, where Gregory says he visited the holy places; Galicia, in the kingdom of the Sueves; the monastery of Dumio, which C. P. Caspari places near Braga; Braga
Role
Monk; founder and bishop of the monastery of Dumio; bishop of Braga; writer of a sermon, moral treatises, a collection of canons and verse

About This Person

Chararic, a king in Galicia, had a gravely ill son, Gregory of Tours writes in a book of the miracles of Martin of Tours. The king sent to Martin's tomb for relics. A man named Martin, from a far country, came into a Galician port with them. The son was healed, and the king confessed the unity of Father, Son and Holy Spirit.

The newcomer was Martin of Braga, a bishop among the Sueves, a people settled in Spain. C. P. Caspari, who edited his sermon, reckons he came there about 550. Isidore of Seville calls him bishop of the monastery of Dumio, which Caspari places near Braga. Gregory says he had gone East to visit the holy places, and was held second to none in learning in his time.

Once the Sueves turned from the Arian teaching to the Catholic faith, Isidore says, Martin set down a rule of faith for them. Isidore credits the change to King Theudemir, backed by Martin, while Gregory names Chararic.

At the Second Council of Braga, Martin, by then bishop of Braga, spoke first. The faith was in no doubt in this province, he told the bishops. So they should correct their own faults, his among them, by the Scriptures and the old canons.

Polemius, bishop of Astorga, asked him for something on the origin of idols, for country people who still honored demons more than God. Martin sent him a sermon to preach in country speech, On the Correction of Peasants. Some who renounced the devil at baptism, it says, now light candles at stones, trees, springs and crossroads, and that is the devil's worship.

Gregory places his death among events that Caspari and the Bollandist editors date to 580. His people mourned him greatly, Gregory says.

In his epitaph Martin speaks to Martin of Tours. Born in Pannonia, it says, he crossed vast seas to Galicia by God's will. Following his patron, he rests in the peace of Christ, the same in name but not in merit.

Key Works

  • On the Correction of Peasants (De correctione rusticorum): a sermon written for Polemius, bishop of Astorga, to preach to country people. It tells of the origin of idols and of the pagan customs they still kept after baptism. C. P. Caspari dates it between 572 and 574
  • Chapters of Martin (Capitula Martini): canons of the councils of the East, rewritten in plainer Latin and set out in separate parts for clergy and for lay people. Martin sent them to Nitigis, bishop of Lugo, and his council, saying the translators and copyists had left them obscure
  • Rule for an Honest Life (Formula vitae honestae): a short book on the four virtues of prudence, magnanimity, continence and justice, addressed to King Miro. The Bollandists take it to be the book on the four virtues that Isidore of Seville says he read

Legacy

What traces to them

  • At the Second Council of Braga he had the canons of the first council read again. He then laid ten chapters on church discipline before the bishops, who signed them. C. P. Caspari thinks Martin wrote the chapters, and very probably the acts.
  • His testament was read out at the Tenth Council of Toledo. Its acts call him the builder of the monastery of Dumio. They say he left its carrying out in the care of the kings who would follow.
  • Paschasius, a deacon at Dumio, put sayings of the Egyptian fathers into Latin for him from a Greek book Martin gave him, C. P. Caspari reports from Paschasius's prologue.
  • C. P. Caspari traces the sermon on the peasants in later preaching. He finds it in Eligius and Pirminius, in an Old English homily of Abbot Aelfric, and in an Old Norse sermon.

How they're remembered

  • Gregory of Tours says he was held second to none in learning in his time. Gregory adds that he wrote the verses over the south door of the church of Saint Martin.
  • Venantius Fortunatus, in a poem to him, bids Galicia, kept for a new Martin, to applaud. Martin was a man of the apostles' lot, the poem says, and became the salvation of the Galician Sueves. In a letter Fortunatus hails him as first centurion in the army of Christ after the captain Paul.
  • Isidore of Seville calls him bright in faith and learning. Isidore says he himself read Martin's book on the four virtues and a volume of his letters.
  • The Bollandists report that the churches of Braga and Évora kept his feast on 20 March, as their breviaries show.

What Is Uncertain

  • His life rests on writers of his own time or soon after. Gregory of Tours wrote of his coming while Martin was still bishop in Galicia. Venantius Fortunatus wrote to him. Isidore of Seville wrote later. His own epitaph, his writings and the acts of the councils of Braga and Toledo add to them. Gregory's account of his coming is a miracle story, in a book of the miracles of Martin of Tours. C. P. Caspari's life of him joins these sources, and where it goes beyond them the reasoning is his.
  • Which king turned to the Catholic faith. Gregory of Tours names Chararic. Isidore of Seville names Theudemir, and the acts of the First Council of Braga name the reigning king Ariamir. Bruno Krusch says no one but Gregory names a King Chararic of Galicia. Many, Krusch says, have guessed that Chararic and Theudemir were one man. Krusch judges Theudemir more likely Chararic's son. C. P. Caspari also holds them two kings, while saying he is not wholly sure. The Bollandists take Theudemir and Ariamir to be one king, whom Gregory calls Chararic in a corrupt form.
  • How long he was bishop. Gregory says about thirty years. The Bollandists hold that a copyist put thirty for about twenty. A note signed R. in Wilhelm Arndt's edition of the History also says about twenty.
  • When he became bishop. Gregory says he was made bishop when the relics came; C. P. Caspari calls this an error, and has him first abbot of Dumio. A Martin is named third of eight bishops, with no see, at the First Council of Braga. It was held in King Ariamir's third year, Spanish era 599. Caspari, and a note by Perez in Migne's edition of Isidore, take him to be Martin as bishop of Dumio. The Bollandists say the year he moved to Braga is not clear.
  • Where his verses stood. Gregory says he wrote the verses over the south door of the church of Saint Martin. O. M. Dalton takes this to be at Tours. C. P. Caspari reports that Hieronymus de Prato placed them at Tours. Florez and Mabillon placed them in the Galician church of Martin, which Mabillon puts at Dumio and Florez at Orense.
  • Dumio and Mondonedo. A note signed R. in Wilhelm Arndt's edition of Gregory glosses Dumio as Mondonedo. The Bollandists tell how monks of Dumio, fleeing the Moors, built a second monastery near Mondonedo and also called it Dumio. They say Martin's body stayed in the old church of Dumio until it was moved to Braga in 1606.
  • Where his epitaph stood. C. P. Caspari says it was to stand in the church of Saint Martin built by Chararic. The Bollandists say the same church was built by Theudemir, whom they take to be Gregory's Chararic.
  • The end of the Suevic kingdom. John of Biclaro says King Leovigild of the Goths took the Sueves, their treasure and their land, and made them a province of the Goths. He tells it under a year Theodor Mommsen's margin gives, with a question mark, as 585.
  • His name. He is a different man from Martin of Tours. Gregory says he reached Galicia together with that saint's relics, and he wrote verses and his epitaph to him. Andrea Gallandi, whose notice Migne reprints, quotes Mabillon on his names. Writers called him Pannonian from his homeland, Galician from his province, and of Dumio and of Braga from his sees. Gallandi reports Tamayo's note that Baronius made three Martins out of one.
