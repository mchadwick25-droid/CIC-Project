Hippolytus of Rome

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Roman Church in the Third Century.

Also called Hippolytus

Hippolytus taught in the church at Rome in the early 200s, wrote in Greek, and held that the Son was another beside the Father, yet one God with him. He would not give way to two bishops of Rome on this or on forgiveness, and in 235 he was sent to Sardinia with Rome's bishop.

About This Person

A teacher from Smyrna named Noetus said that Christ was the Father himself, so that the Father was born, suffered and died. Twice the elders, the church's priests, called him before the church, and they put him out.

Hippolytus, who tells that story, taught in the church at Rome in the early 200s and wrote in Greek. Against Noetus's teaching he held that the Son was another beside the Father, yet one God with him. He would not give way to two bishops of Rome, Zephyrinus and Callistus, on this, or on how far the church could forgive. He set the quarrel down in a book against every teaching he held false. In 235 he was sent to Sardinia with Rome's bishop, and Rome kept his day among its martyrs.

In Hippolytus's telling, the only account of the quarrel that survives, Zephyrinus let Noetus's teaching spread at Rome, with Callistus as his adviser. Hippolytus and his side stood against them for the truth.

He called the Son the Word, a name from John's gospel for the Son as he was with the Father before he became man: "When I say 'another,' I do not mean two Gods. I mean it as light from light, or water from a spring, or a ray from the sun. For there is one power, which comes from the whole. The whole is the Father, and from him comes the power, the Word." He imagined someone asking whether he spoke of two Gods, and answered that he spoke of one God, in two persons, with the grace of the Holy Spirit as a third.

While Zephyrinus still lived, Hippolytus says, Callistus called him and his side worshippers of two gods, because they would not go along with Callistus.

When Zephyrinus died, Callistus took his place. Hippolytus charges that Callistus forgave every sin of the body to anyone who came over to him, and held that a bishop should not be removed even for the gravest sin. Some whom Hippolytus's own side had judged and put out of the church went over to Callistus.

His book, the Refutation of All Heresies, traced the teachings he held false back to Greek philosophers, mystery cults and astrologers. Its last part turned to the teachings of his own day, Callistus's among them. In its opening he wrote that the apostles had received the Holy Spirit and handed it on to those who believed rightly: "We are their successors. We share in the same grace, in the high priesthood and in the teaching, and we are counted guardians of the church. We do not let our eyes droop, and we do not keep the right word silent." The high priesthood meant the church's chief priestly office.

He drew up a table, on a cycle of sixteen years, for finding the day of Easter, a day over which Christians differed, some keeping the moon's fourteenth day. Jerome says it gave Eusebius the occasion for his own nineteen-year cycle.

In 235, Rome's list of bishops records, Pontianus, the bishop, and Hippolytus, whom it calls an elder, were deported to Sardinia. Rome's calendar of martyrs kept them both on 13 August. In the East, Eusebius wrote that Hippolytus had led another church, without saying which, and a later bishop there quoted him for the faith and called him bishop and martyr.

More than a century later, Damasus, bishop of Rome, set a verse on his tomb on the Tiburtine Road, telling what he had heard. Hippolytus, an elder, it was said, had always stayed in the split of Novatus while a tyrant's orders pressed. This was the split that Novatian's party made from the church at Rome in 251, sixteen years after the exile, refusing to take back those who had given way in persecution. As Hippolytus went to the kingdom of the faithful, the people asked where to go, and he told them all to follow the catholic faith, the faith of the whole church. So, the verse says, he earned his place as a martyr.

  • In our modern English
    “When I say 'another,' I do not mean two Gods. I mean it as light from light, or water from a spring, or a ray from the sun. For there is one power, which comes from the whole. The whole is the Father, and from him comes the power, the Word.”
    As printed
    “But when I say another, I do not mean that there are two Gods, but that it is only as light of light, or as water from a fountain, or as a ray from the sun. For there is but one power, which is from the All; and the Father is the All, from whom cometh this Power, the Word.”
    Hippolytus, Against Noetus 11, tr. S. D. F. Salmond; Greek ed. Paul de Lagarde
    The modern English is rendered from Lagarde's Greek, lines 3705 to 3708 of its file, and follows Salmond's English where the scan cannot be read. The scan is damaged inside the sentence. It prints ix φωτὸς where Salmond has of light, Ἡ ὡς and ἡ ὡς where he has or as, and ἀκτῆα where he has a ray, and line 3706 is a row of stray marks. No other held print gives this Greek, so the Greek is not printed here. The words I mean it as supply the verb the Greek leaves understood in the second clause.
  • In our modern English
    “We are their successors. We share in the same grace, in the high priesthood and in the teaching, and we are counted guardians of the church. We do not let our eyes droop, and we do not keep the right word silent.”
    As printed
    “But we, as being their successors, and as participators in this grace, high-priesthood, and office of teaching, as well as being reputed guardians of the Church, must not be found deficient in vigilance, or disposed to suppress correct doctrine.”
    Hippolytus, Refutation of All Heresies, preface, tr. J. H. MacMahon; Greek ed. Paul Wendland
    The Greek is printed as the scan reads it; for χαὶ and zal it reads καὶ. The modern English follows Wendland's Greek, with the verbs in the present, we do not let our eyes droop and we do not keep silent, where MacMahon's English follows the future reading. Their means the apostles and those to whom they handed on the Holy Spirit, as the sentence before it says.

Key Works

  • Refutation of All Heresies: a long book that traces every teaching he held false back to the Greek philosophers, the mystery cults and the astrologers. Its last part turns to the teachings of his own day at Rome, Callistus's among them, and it ends with his own account of the truth. It survives in one manuscript, found on Mount Athos in 1842 and printed in 1851 under the name of Origen; that it is his rests on its modern editors
  • Against Noetus: a work against Noetus of Smyrna, who taught that Christ was the Father himself, so that the Father was born, suffered and died. It answers that the Son is another beside the Father, yet one power with him: one God, in two persons, with the grace of the Holy Spirit as a third. Its manuscript calls it a homily of Hippolytus
  • On the Passover: a book named by Eusebius and Jerome, with a table of dates and a cycle of sixteen years for finding the day of Easter. Eusebius says the table ran to the first year of the emperor Alexander, and Jerome that it gave Eusebius the occasion for his own nineteen-year cycle

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Eusebius found his writings kept in the church library at Aelia, that is Jerusalem. He wrote that many other books of his were kept by many people.
  • Eusebius and Jerome each list his books. Jerome's list includes commentaries on books of Scripture, Against Marcion, On the Passover and Against All Heresies.
  • Jerome says his sixteen-year Easter cycle gave Eusebius the occasion for a nineteen-year cycle of his own.
  • Epiphanius names him with Clement and Irenaeus among those who refuted the followers of Valentinus.
  • Theodoret quotes him as a witness of the faith, as "the Holy Hippolytus, Bishop and Martyr". In a letter he ranks him with Ignatius, Polycarp, Irenaeus and Justin, most of whom, he says, were both bishops and martyrs.
  • Writing to Damasus, bishop of Rome, Jerome gives the words of Hippolytus the martyr on Isaac's blessing. Elsewhere he calls him eloquent, and says that Ambrose's book on the six days of creation followed the views of Hippolytus and Basil.

How they're remembered

  • Rome's calendar of martyrs, in the book of 354, keeps him on 13 August, on the Tiburtine Road, and Pontianus on the same day, in the cemetery of Callistus.
  • Damasus, bishop of Rome, set a verse on his tomb, telling what he had heard. It says that Hippolytus, an elder, had stayed in the split of Novatus. As he went to the kingdom of the faithful, it says, he told the people to follow the catholic faith, the faith of the whole church, and so he earned his place as a martyr.
  • The poet Prudentius, in a hymn, tells of finding the tomb of Hippolytus, an elder who had once had a part in the split of Novatus. In the poem the judge has him torn apart by wild horses, the death of Hippolytus son of Theseus in the old Greek story.
  • The later Book of the Popes says that Pope Hadrian restored the cemetery of the blessed martyr Hippolytus, beside Saint Lawrence, which had decayed since early times.

Open Questions

  • One witness. The quarrel with Zephyrinus and Callistus is known only from Hippolytus's own Refutation. It is an enemy's account, so what it says Callistus taught and ruled rests on him alone.
  • Callistus's side, as Hippolytus reports it. Callistus held that the Father and the Son are one Spirit, and that the Father suffered along with the Son. For his rule on sin he cited Paul's question about judging another man's servant, the parable of the wheat and the weeds, and Noah's ark, which held animals clean and unclean.
  • Who wrote the Refutation. Its one manuscript bore the name of Origen, and no old source names it among Hippolytus's books. Eusebius and Jerome name a book of his Against All Heresies. That the Refutation is his rests on its modern editors.
  • Elder or bishop. Rome's list of bishops calls him an elder, one of the priests who led the church with its bishop. Eusebius says he led another church, without saying which. Jerome calls him bishop of a church whose city he could not learn, and Theodoret calls him bishop and martyr.
  • His own claim. In the Refutation he counts himself and his side as the apostles' successors, sharing in their grace, their high priesthood and their teaching. The high priesthood was the church's chief priestly office. His English translator notes that the words may not mean a bishop's office only.
  • His side and Callistus's. Hippolytus says some whom his side had judged and put out of the church went over to Callistus. He calls Callistus's following a school, a teacher's following set up against the church, and its people Callistians. They, he says, "attempt to call themselves a Catholic Church", the church of the whole.
  • The split of Novatus. Damasus says he tells what he has heard. No old source we hold says whom he meant by Novatus. Rome's list of bishops names Novatus as the man who split Novatian off from the church at Rome, in the time of the bishop Cornelius. By the list's own dates that split came in 251, sixteen years after the exile.
  • His death. No old source tells how or where he died. Rome's calendar keeps him among its martyrs. The wild horses are Prudentius's legend.
  • Origen. Jerome says one of Hippolytus's sermons, On the praise of our Lord and Savior, shows him speaking in church with Origen present. Jerome does not say when.
  • The third in God. In Against Noetus he speaks of one God, in two persons, with the grace of the Holy Spirit as a third. The Greek makes the Spirit's grace a third in God's ordering of things, which the English prints as economy.
  • The statue. A seated marble statue was found on the Tiburtine Road in 1551. Its chair carries tables for the day of Easter, in cycles of sixteen years, and a list of books. Its tie to Hippolytus is a modern identification.
  • Photius, writing centuries later, calls him a pupil of Irenaeus.
