The Age of Monks and Empires · c. 507-586 · Amida, Constantinople

Amida · Constantinople, today in Turkey

John of Ephesus

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Syriac Orthodox (West Syriac) Christianity.

Known for
A bishop and historian of the party that refused the council of Chalcedon, who converted pagans in western Asia Minor for Justinian and wrote the Lives of the Eastern Saints and a Church History of his own times.
Also called
John of Asia (Jean d'Asie), John of Amida (in Michael the Syrian), John the Syrian, the converter of the pagans (in his Lives)
Dates
Born about 507, by E. W. Brooks's reckoning; R. Payne Smith gives about 516. His history ends in 585, and Brooks thinks he probably died in 586
Place
Born in the country around Amida, by Brooks; a monk at Amida; worked in Asia, Caria, Phrygia and Lydia; bishop of Ephesus in title; lived mostly at Constantinople
Role
Monk, missionary and bishop; historian

About This Person

Every son born to the parents of John of Ephesus had died by the age of two. When John fell ill too, they carried him, weeping, to Maro, a holy man who lived on a pillar. "Be quiet, be quiet," Maro told them. "John shall not die before Maro." John tells this himself, as his parents used to tell it to him, in his Lives of the Eastern Saints.

John was a bishop and historian of the party that refused the council of Chalcedon. E. W. Brooks, who edited his Lives, says he was born about 507 in the country around Amida, and grew up in monasteries there. R. Payne Smith, who put his Church History into English, gives about 516.

In Justinian's time, John writes, he was made teacher of the heathen in the provinces of Asia, Caria, Phrygia and Lydia. In the hills above Tralles alone he converted many thousands and built twenty-four churches. Brooks says the emperor set one condition: that the converts be brought to the faith of Chalcedon.

His Lives record that Jacob Baradaeus made him bishop of Ephesus. Brooks finds nothing to show he ever lived there. He led his party in Constantinople, and he was jailed more than once. In one prison he told the emperor's envoys, "Even though you eat me roasted, if I be but quit of the sight of you, I am ready on these terms to be delivered to a painful death."

In his Lives he says he writes only of people he saw with his own eyes. Payne Smith joined the History's broken pieces with lines of his own, he says. Brooks says John is not a literary writer and that his dates are often loose.

His history ends in 585, Brooks says, and he probably died the next year. Payne Smith values the History because its writer was "a busy actor in the scenes which he describes."

Key Works

  • Lives of the Eastern Saints: short Lives of holy men and women of his party, written, he says, only of people he saw himself
  • Ecclesiastical History, part 3: the church of his own day, ending in 585 by Brooks's account, written amid persecution and surviving in pieces

Legacy

What traces to them

  • In the hills above Tralles he converted many thousands, built twenty-four churches and four monasteries, and leveled the temple at Derira, by his own history.
  • His Lives and History tell of Jacob Baradaeus, the empress Theodora's refugees and the mission to Nubia, and later chroniclers copied him. Michael the Syrian quotes him by name.

How they're remembered

  • R. Payne Smith: the History's chief value is that it is contemporary, and its author "a busy actor in the scenes which he describes".
  • Brooks: "John is not a literary writer", and "he shows little interest in theology".

What Is Uncertain

  • He wrote from inside his own party, which Payne Smith notes refused Chalcedon. In the quarrel of 575, Brooks places him on the side opposed to Jacob Baradaeus, whom Brooks calls his hero. Michael the Syrian says he did not break with Jacob's party but rebuked both sides.
  • His years. Brooks's dates are his own, built on Dyakonov's work, and John gives few years himself. Brooks puts the birth about 507 and Payne Smith about 516.
  • The terms of his mission. John says only that he was made teacher of the heathen in Justinian's time. Brooks says the emperor made it a condition that the converts be brought to the faith of Chalcedon.
  • How many he converted. His own history says many thousands in the hills above Tralles. Michael the Syrian, quoting him, gives seventy thousand; that figure is J.-B. Chabot's correction of a manuscript reading of seven thousand. In the same chapter Michael also says he later baptized twenty-three thousand in Asia.
  • When he was made bishop. Brooks thinks probably 558. Michael says forty years before the quarrel of the 570s.
  • His death. Brooks says he probably died in 586 after a year in prison at Chalcedon. His source is Michael the Syrian, who tells of a bishop named only as John. He had been kept a year in prison at Chalcedon, and he died on the day he had foretold.
  • Payne Smith joined the broken pieces of the History with lines of his own, and marks in the margin where John's words begin.
