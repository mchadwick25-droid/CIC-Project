Bede

Also called The Venerable Bede, Beda, Baeda

Monk and priest of Wearmouth and Jarrow who wrote the Ecclesiastical History of the English nation. He also wrote commentaries on many books of the Bible, drawn from the Fathers, and books on nature and the reckoning of time.

About This Person

On his last evening, Bede was dictating to a boy named Wilbert. The boy said one sentence was still unwritten. "Well, then, write it," Bede said. When the boy said it was written, Bede agreed, "it is finished." He asked the boy to hold his head while he sat facing the place where he used to pray. He died on the floor of his cell singing "Glory be to the Father."

The scene comes from a letter by his pupil Cuthbert to a fellow pupil, Cuthwin. Charles Plummer, who prints it, holds that Bede died on the eve of Ascension Day in 735, though the Saxon Chronicle gives 734.

Bede was a monk and priest of Wearmouth and Jarrow, in northern England. At the close of his History, finished in 731, he tells his own life. He was born on the monastery's land. At seven he was given to Abbot Benedict Biscop, and afterwards to Ceolfrid, to be educated. He became a deacon in his nineteenth year and a priest in his thirtieth. "I always took delight in learning, teaching, and writing," he wrote.

An anonymous History of the Abbots tells of a plague at Jarrow. It killed every monk who could read or sing the services, except Abbot Ceolfrid and one small boy he had raised. The two kept up the psalms with their antiphons until others were trained. Plummer dates the plague to 686, and says the boy can hardly be anyone but Bede.

His own list names commentaries on many books of the Bible, drawn from the Fathers. It also lists saints' lives, hymns, and books on nature and the reckoning of time. Bede wrote that rowdy peasants at their drink charged him with heresy over his book On the Times, which Plummer dates to 703. He answered in a letter to Plegwin, a Hexham monk, on the six ages of the world.

Bede names his sources in the History. Nothelm, a London priest, searched the papal archives and found letters of Gregory the Great. For Northumbria, Bede leaned on "the faithful testimony of innumerable witnesses." L. Cecil Jane, who edited the 1903 English edition, calls the History practically the only authority for England from the Saxon invasion to 732.

He closed the History with a prayer to "good Jesus." He asked that he, who had been given "the words of thy wisdom," might "come to thee, the fountain of all wisdom."

Timeline

Dates
Born, Charles Plummer reckons, in 672 or 673; L. Cecil Jane gives about 673. Bede wrote the close of his History in his fifty-ninth year, he says, and its last annals reach 731. Plummer holds that he died on the eve of Ascension Day in 735; the Saxon Chronicle and several other chronicles give 734
Place
Wearmouth and Jarrow, the monastery of Saints Peter and Paul in Northumbria, on whose land he was born, he says. Plummer finds him also at Lindisfarne, and at York with Egbert of York in 733
Role
Monk, priest, teacher and historian; writer on Scripture and on the reckoning of time

Key Works

  • Ecclesiastical History of the English Nation: five books on the church of Britain, and above all of the English, finished in 731 and sent to King Ceolwulf of Northumbria. In its preface Bede names the people who sent him his facts, among them Abbot Albinus of Canterbury and the London priest Nothelm
  • Life of Cuthbert: the monk and bishop of Lindisfarne, first in verse and then in prose, Bede's own list says
  • On the Times: his book on reckoning time, which Plummer dates to 703. Plummer reports that a charge of heresy was later brought against Bede over it. In the History, Bede says he proved in his book "De Temporibus" that Aidan of Lindisfarne kept Easter wrongly. Plummer notes that Bede also calls his larger book on time De Temporibus

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Alcuin of York, in his poem on the bishops and saints of York, says Bede's parents placed him at seven in the monastery of Jarrow. There, Alcuin says, he became a master and wrote on Scripture, on meter, on time and on history.
  • In his seven books against Felix of Urgel, Alcuin cites blessed Bede the priest on Acts and on the Song of Songs. Migne dates Alcuin's letter sending the books to Charlemagne to 800.
  • The Welsh annals note his death in three words, "Beda presbyter dormit": Bede the priest falls asleep. John Williams ab Ithel, who edited them, says that one manuscript of the annals, under 735, credits Bede with compiling its chronicle year by year.
  • L. Cecil Jane says King Alfred paraphrased the History into Anglo-Saxon, and that it was first printed in England at Cambridge in 1643.
  • An English Lollard tract still leans on Bede. With him and with Gregory the Great, it says the signs of miracles were needed only at the church's beginning. We water plants until they have taken root, it says, and then stop.
  • A Wycliffite English tract, The Great Sentence of Curse Expounded, warns priests who say Mass in pride and covetousness. It cites Saint Bede, who says we sell Christ when we forsake truth and take up falsehood and uphold it.

How they're remembered

  • The Roman Breviary keeps Bede the Venerable on 27 May as Confessor and Doctor of the Church. Its lessons also read his homilies and passages of his History. An English Roman Missal of 1843 gives, under 29 October, a prayer to God who enlightened his church by the learning of blessed Bede.
  • Charles Plummer says the name Venerable dates from the ninth century, and was a common title for priests. He retells two legends of how Bede earned it, one with angels answering his sermon and one with an angel finishing his epitaph.
  • Plummer notes that Dante places Bede beside Isidore of Seville in Paradise.
  • In the Breviary's lessons for Theodore of Tarsus, the Venerable Bede is quoted saying that brighter times never shone on England than under Theodore.

Open Questions

  • The year of his death. Charles Plummer holds to 735, which the earliest northern witnesses give. He says 734 is the date of all the manuscripts of the Saxon Chronicle and of several other chronicles. He sets aside the case for 742 made by Mayor and Lumby, from whose edition he prints the letter of Cuthbert, Bede's pupil. He also sets aside the earlier years some foreign chronicles give. That letter places the death on the Wednesday before Ascension Day, after a day of dictation. Its manuscripts differ on the day of the month.
  • What he was translating. L. Cecil Jane says he had just finished translating the Gospel of John. Plummer prints Cuthbert's letter from a manuscript at St. Gallen. In it, Bede translated the Gospel into English from the beginning to the words "but what are they among so many?" The ordinary text names the Gospel of John without that limit. Plummer finds the limit hard to square with the one chapter and one sentence left at the end.
  • His age and birth. Jane says he died at sixty-two. Plummer reckons his birth from Bede's own words as 672 or 673. He notes that the Lindisfarne annals and Simeon of Durham put it in 677, and Florence of Worcester in 678. Stevenson, in his Church Historians, puts it in 674, on grounds Plummer calls very precarious.
  • The boy in the plague. The anonymous History of the Abbots does not name the small boy who sang the services with Ceolfrid. Plummer identifies him as Bede, noting that Bede, who takes much from that History, leaves the story out.
  • Travel. Plummer rejects the claim that Bede went to Rome, which rests on a doubtful reading in a letter of Pope Sergius. He finds Bede at Lindisfarne and at York. Jane says a tradition that made him a professor at Cambridge has no foundation.
  • The charge of heresy is known here only from Plummer, who quotes Bede's Letter to Plegwin. The letter itself is not among the sources used here.
  • Bede means to be fair but does not hide his side. He praises the peace, humility and care for the poor of Aidan of Lindisfarne, while rejecting his Easter, writing "like an impartial historian." He defends Aidan against those who said he kept Easter on the fourteenth moon like the Jews. Of the Britons he writes that most of them, through innate hatred, oppose the English and the appointed Easter.
  • His name. The Cuthbert who wrote of his death was his pupil, later abbot of Wearmouth and Jarrow in Plummer's account. He is a different man from Cuthbert of Lindisfarne, the monk and bishop whose Life Bede wrote.
