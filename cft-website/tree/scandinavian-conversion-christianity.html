The Early Medieval Era · c. 826-1104 · Denmark, Norway, Sweden, Iceland

Scandinavian Conversion Christianity

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Christianity came north in fits and starts over nearly three centuries. A Frankish monk named Anskar preached in Denmark and at Birka in Sweden in the 820s and 830s, with modest and short-lived results. Then came the kings. Harald Bluetooth set up a great carved stone at Jelling claiming that he had 'made the Danes Christian'. In Norway there were the two Olafs. As the sagas tell it, their methods included force, and one of them was killed in battle and venerated as a saint almost at once. Iceland took a different road. In the year 1000 the question was put to the Althing, the island's assembly. The pagan law-speaker Þorgeir thought it over for a night. Then he ruled that all Icelanders should be baptized, though some old practices were allowed in private for a time. A whole community changed its religion by public decision rather than by conquest. The window closes in 1104, when the northern church received its own archbishop at Lund. It stopped being mission ground run from Germany. The sagas that tell these stories most vividly were written down two centuries later.

In Its Own Record

Grew Out Of

  • Carolingian Reform Christianity Documented Major — In 823, the Frankish royal annals report, Archbishop Ebo of Reims went to preach among the Danes. He went with the emperor's backing and the pope's authority, and he baptized many. In 826 the Danish king Harald was baptized at Mainz with his wife and a great crowd of Danes. From 829 Anskar, a monk of Corbie, preached in Sweden.
  • Late Anglo-Saxon Christianity Inferential-Thin Major — The Swedish historian L. A. Anjou wrote in 1850 that teachers came to Sweden first from France and later chiefly from England. He also repeats the story that St David set out from England for Sweden in search of martyrdom. This rests on one later account, so the English share in the mission is thin evidence.

Continued As

  • The Scandinavian Reformation Kingdoms Inferential-Thin Major — The historian Anjou traces the see of Skara in Sweden to Sigfrid, the missionary who baptized King Olof Skötkonung. When its bishop left in 1529, the provost Sven was chosen, and a council at Uppsala backed him in 1530. Denmark broke harder: in 1536 its bishops were jailed, and the church law of 1537 set a superintendent over each diocese. This rests on one history from the 1850s, so the tie is thin.

Sources

  • Rimbert, Vita Anskarii / Anskar: The Apostle of the North (trans. C. H. Robinson, 1921) (primary) — Near-contemporary missionary life (Anskar d. 865, by his successor) — the 826+ Danish/Swedish missions from inside; translation free online (archive.org, Fordham sourcebook).
  • Adam of Bremen, History of the Archbishops of Hamburg-Bremen (trans. F. J. Tschan, Columbia, 1959; repr. 2002 with intro by T. Reuter) (primary) — A contemporaneous narrative (written c. 1070s, at the era's end), including the famous Book IV description of pagan Uppsala — a Hamburg-Bremen institutional viewpoint, so read with that bias in mind.
  • Rune stones (Jelling stones; wider corpus) (primary) — Contemporary material evidence — e.g. Harald Bluetooth's Jelling claim to have 'made the Danes Christian' (c. 965). Corpus accessible via Rundata / Samnordisk runtextdatabas.
  • Islendingabok and Kristni saga (trans. Sian Gronlie, Viking Society for Northern Research Text Series 18, 2006) (primary) — Ari's Book of the Icelanders (c. 1122-33) with the Althing conversion-by-vote account of 1000. One-century retrospect - much closer than the 13th-century sagas, but still needs careful, critical reading; free PDF from the Viking Society (vsnrweb-publications.org.uk).
  • Anders Winroth, The Conversion of Scandinavia (Yale, 2012) (secondary) — Standard modern account; deliberately source-critical about mission narratives and saga retrospectives.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity, vol. 3, ch. 5: 'Germanic Christianities, 600-1100' (Lesley Abrams); cf. ch. 11: 'The northern frontier' (Ian N. Wood) (reference) — Both chapters are from the vol. 3 TOC; Abrams is the scholar of Scandinavian conversion - a direct fit.
