About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:19

Read along

The kingdom of Aksum, straddling what is now Ethiopia and Eritrea, was a Red Sea trading power with its own script and its own coinage when Christianity arrived by an almost accidental route. A young man from Tyre named Frumentius, shipwrecked and taken into the royal household, rose to be tutor and adviser, then traveled to Alexandria to ask Athanasius for a bishop. And was consecrated for the job himself, around 330. King Ezana's conversion followed, and it is legible in the most concrete way imaginable. His coins stop carrying the disc and crescent of the old religion and start carrying the cross, one of the earliest such declarations by any state anywhere. The faith took root in Ge'ez, the kingdom's own language, in which Scripture and liturgy were eventually rendered, and Frumentius's consecration bound the Ethiopian church to the Egyptian one for the next sixteen centuries. What survives from these years is mostly inscriptions, coinage, and later chronicles rather than the everyday voices of ordinary believers.

Documented Stories

The Emperor Writes to Aksum to Recall Its Bishop

Around 356, with Athanasius driven out of Alexandria for the third time and the Arian George installed in his place, the emperor Constantius II turned his attention to the church Athanasius had consecrated a bishop for twenty-five years earlier at the far end of the Red Sea. It is addressed to ‘Aezanes and Sazanes’, the rulers of Aksum, and it is a masterpiece of imperial condescension: the emperor, ‘considering that you are deserving of the same provident care as the Romans’, commands that the same doctrine be professed in their churches as in his, and that they ‘send speedily into Egypt the Bishop Frumentius to the most venerable Bishop George’, who will examine the circumstances of his appointment. Frumentius, the letter explains, was consecrated by Athanasius, ‘a man who is guilty of ten thousand crimes’, and if he does not obey he will be deposed and the Aksumites will have been deceived. Nothing in the Aksumite record suggests Frumentius went. Ezana’s later inscriptions invoke ‘the Lord of Heaven’ and then the Trinity; the church he and Frumentius founded stayed in communion with Alexandria, not with the emperor’s appointee, and Ethiopian tradition remembers Frumentius as Abba Salama, ‘father of peace’, who died in his see. The letter is the only contemporary document that names the first bishop of Ethiopia, and it survives because his consecrator kept it to prove what they were both up against.

In Its Own Record

Child of Alexandria (Frumentius consecrated by Athanasius of Alexandria)

Descended from

  • grew out of The Church of Alexandria and Miaphysite Egypt Documented Major — Frumentius came to Alexandria. He asked Athanasius of Alexandria to send a bishop to his land. Socrates of Constantinople says Athanasius told him to take the post himself. Later the emperor sent a letter to the rulers of Aksum. In it he named Frumentius as the bishop Athanasius had made for them.

Direct descendants

  • continued as Aksumite Christianity Documented Major — In the 350s the emperor Constantius wrote to Ezana, ruler of Aksum, about its bishop, Frumentius. In the sixth century the land still had a bishop and a Christian king. A Syriac book from soon after 525 names Euprepios as bishop under King Kaleb. Procopius calls the Ethiopian king of his day a devoted Christian, and Cosmas lists Axum as a land with churches. No bishop is named in these texts between Frumentius and Euprepios.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Christianity reached the Red Sea kingdom of Aksum by an almost accidental route: a shipwrecked young man from Tyre named Frumentius rose to tutor the royal household, then traveled to Alexandria and returned as the kingdom's first bishop around 330. King Ezana's own conversion is visible on his coinage, which stops carrying the disc and crescent of the old religion and starts carrying the cross — one of the earliest such declarations by any state. The faith took root in Ge'ez, the kingdom's own language, in which Scripture and liturgy were eventually rendered.

Legacy

This became, and remains, one of the great ancient churches of the world: the Ethiopian Orthodox Tewahedo Church and the Eritrean Orthodox Tewahedo Church together count tens of millions of members. It carried down a whole distinctive Christian culture — Ge'ez as a scriptural and liturgical language, a broader Old Testament canon than any other church's, drums and sistra and liturgical dance in worship, and, centuries later, the rock-hewn churches of Lalibela. The link Frumentius's consecration made with Alexandria held for roughly sixteen centuries, until the Ethiopian church received a patriarch of its own in the twentieth.
