The Imperial Church Era · c. 320–430 CE · Nile Valley & Desert, Egypt

Desert Monasticism

Meet Papnoute on Church in Conversation

About This Movement

By the early fourth century the last empire-wide persecution had ended, and Constantine's toleration closed off a path that had once been open to any Christian: dying for the faith. Antony, a young Egyptian landowner, gave away his family's land after hearing one Gospel reading in church — "if thou wouldest be perfect, go and sell that thou hast" — and withdrew by stages: the village's edge, an abandoned fort, then the desert's inner reaches, for the rest of his life. What his biographer Athanasius of Alexandria describes there is not escape but combat: the war once waged against a persecutor from outside, now fought against demons and the thoughts they whispered, from within. Teaching passed mostly in a single exchange — a younger monk asked an elder for "a word," and the elder answered in one sentence, no more. Generations later, those sentences were gathered into what other Christians would call the Sayings of the Desert Fathers and Mothers, arranged by the name of the elder who spoke them, not necessarily as anyone first heard them said.

Not everyone lived Antony's way. Around 320, a man named Pachomius founded the desert's first rule-governed community at Tabennesi in the Thebaid; by his death in 346 it had grown into a federation of nine houses for men and two for women, bound by a written rule and appointed offices rather than one elder's own example. Between the two lay a third pattern: loose settlements at Nitria, Kellia, and Scetis, where ascetics lived mostly alone but gathered weekly for worship and correction. These settlements sat closer to ordinary life than their own rhetoric of total withdrawal suggests: excavation at Kellia has uncovered at least one commercial center operating alongside the ascetic cells, real evidence of trade with the surrounding villages. Which of these actually held authority — a life earned through discipline, or an office conferred by a rule — was never settled. Even Pachomius, the rule's own founder, was remembered afterward less as an officeholder than as a father whose own example, not his rule alone, had formed his community.

What kept any of this from tipping into excess was discernment. It was the skill most prized of all: judging how much fasting and how much solitude one person could truly bear. The tradition's most common story shape is an eager newcomer who asks for an extreme practice and is steered toward something milder. Evagrius of Pontus was the most formally educated man the desert produced. He tried to build the struggle into a scheme of his own. He named eight kinds of tempting thought, and he drew a ladder that rises from practice through passionlessness to contemplation. He died at Kellia in January 399, just before a dispute his own learned circle could not survive. A bishop's letter on how literally to picture God split simple monks from learned ones. Soon after, Evagrius's circle was driven out of Egypt. His writings survived anyway, and part of them was handed down for centuries under another name.

In Its Own Record

Grew Out Of

  • Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition Contested Major — Scholars disagree on whether the desert monks grew out of Christian teaching at Alexandria. Samuel Rubenson reads Antony's letters as the work of a man shaped by Origen's ideas. But Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, paints Antony as an Egyptian. His parents were Christians, and he grew up at home with them. He went to the city when the martyrs were led there.

Influenced

  • Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity Documented — Jerome says Marcella, a Roman ascetic, heard of Antony from Athanasius of Alexandria. He and other men in exile from Alexandria told her in Rome. They told her too of the houses for monks that Pachomius set up. Jerome tells how Paula went into the monks' cells at Nitria in Egypt. In Letter 22 he says Antony made the hermit life famous.
  • Iconophile Byzantine Monasticism Inferential-Thin — Basil of Caesarea wrote that he found many monks in Alexandria and the rest of Egypt. In 787 the Second Council of Nicaea told double houses of monks and nuns to keep Basil's rule. In its acts, a bishop honors images of 'the fathers and cultivators of the desert'. But none of these texts shows the monks who fought for icons drawing on Egypt itself.
  • Early Benedictine / Italian Monasticism Widely Accepted — Benedict's Rule tells monks to read 'the Lives of the Fathers' when they meet in the evening. Its last chapter names the Fathers' Lives and Basil of Caesarea's Rule as models for monks. Its editor, Cardinal Gasquet, takes these to be the Lives of the Desert Fathers.
  • Insular / Irish Monastic Christianity Inferential-Thin — An Irish monk on the Continent wrote in the eighth century, mixing fact with legend. He traced the Irish daily prayers back to Antony, Paul, Macarius and other early hermits. Warren edited the Bangor Antiphonary in 1895. He judged this one late account sound at its core but wrong in detail. He held that any Eastern features reached Ireland through Gaul.
  • Coptic Christianity under Early Islam Documented — A Coptic book copied by hand in 1006 ends with a prayer for two monks. They were monks of the Church of Saint Mercurius, near Edfu. The prayer asks that they share the inheritance of the 'coenobite fathers'. It names Pachomius, Theodorus, Palamon, Petronius and Horsiesios.
  • Gallic Monastic-Ascetic Christianity Documented — A bishop in Gaul asked Cassian to write his Institutes. In them Cassian set out the customs of the monks he had seen across Egypt and Palestine. Gennadius says he later founded two monasteries at Marseilles.
  • Early Palestinian Ascetic Monasticism Documented — Jerome's Life of Hilarion says Hilarion set out for the Egyptian desert to see Antony. He stayed with him about two months. It adds that no one knew of a monk in Palestine or Syria before Hilarion.
  • The Roman Church in the Age of Gregory the Great Inferential-Thin — Cassian heard Abbot Serapion of Scetis list eight principal faults, ending with pride. Gregory the Great later made pride 'the queen of sins' and set seven principal sins beneath her. Gregory names no source, so the tie rests on the likeness of the two lists.
  • The Kollyvades & Greek Renewal Documented — In 1782 Macarius of Corinth and Nikodemos of Mount Athos printed the Philokalia at Venice, a book of old Greek writings on prayer. It opens with counsels credited to Antony the Great. It also holds works by Evagrius of Pontus and by John Cassian.
  • The Church of Alexandria and Miaphysite Egypt Documented — In 457 the Alexandrians who refused Chalcedon took counsel with 'the whole order of the monks'. They chose Timothy as bishop. He was an ascetic who had once been brought from the desert to Cyril of Alexandria. Centuries later the church of Egypt wrote down its History of the Patriarchs. It says the letters of Peter, its head, were kept at the Monastery of Macarius. It says Benjamin fled through the monasteries of Wadi Habib to hide in Upper Egypt.
  • Chalcedonian Monasticism - Judean Desert & Gaza Documented — Dorotheus of Gaza, teaching his monks in the sixth century, quotes the Egyptian desert elders by name. He cites Antony saying he no longer feared God, and he praises a saying of Abba Poemen. He also quotes the Book of the Elders, the collected sayings of the desert.
  • The Coptic Monastic & Sunday-School Revival Widely Accepted — In 1969 Patriarch Kyrillos VI sent Matthew the Poor and twelve monks to the Monastery of St Macarius at Scetis. The monastery's own history says they had lived in the spirit of the desert fathers. It dates its founding to 360.

Argued Against

  • The Melitian Schism Documented — The Life of Antony says Antony 'never held communion with the Meletian schismatics'. On his deathbed he told his monks to have nothing to do with them.

Sources

  • Athanasius, Life of Antony (primary) — The biography that made the desert movement famous across the empire and became the model for later Christian hagiography.
  • Apophthegmata Patrum (Sayings of the Desert Fathers) (primary) — Short exchanges — a younger monk asking an elder for "a word," the elder answering in a sentence — passed down orally before being gathered into the collections still read as spiritual reading today.
  • The Pachomian Corpus (Rules and Lives) (primary) — The cenobitic federation's own governing texts; the Rules survive complete only through a chain of translation (Coptic to Greek to Latin, and separately to Ethiopic), and the Lives through several only-partly-overlapping recensions.
  • Evagrius Ponticus, Praktikos (primary) — The desert's most systematic surviving account of the inner struggle, in Luke Dysinger's English translation; his other works transmit mostly in Syriac and Armenian, or in Greek under another name.
  • Palladius, Lausiac History (primary) — A retrospective monastic history written by a participant-adjacent insider-outsider around 419–420; this Christian tradition's principal narrative source alongside the Historia Monachorum.
  • John Cassian, Conferences (primary) — Cassian's own record of years spent among the Egyptian monks, carried west in Latin and read by Benedict; the New Advent text collects all twenty-four conferences.
  • Samuel Rubenson, The Letters of St. Antony: Monasticism and the Making of a Saint (reference) — Fortress Press, 1995 — argues from the seven letters attributed to Antony that he was philosophically literate, against his biographer's portrait of an unlettered rustic; contested, not settled, by this Christian tradition's own record.
  • William Harmless, Desert Christians: An Introduction to the Literature of Early Monasticism (secondary) — Oxford University Press, 2004 — a standard modern guide to the sayings, Antony, Pachomius, Evagrius and Cassian, and to how the desert's small written corpus should be read.
