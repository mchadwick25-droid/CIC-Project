The Late Medieval Era · 1402-1415 · Prague, Bohemia, and Constance

Jan Hus

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Hussite & Bohemian Brethren Movement.

Known for
Priest and master of Prague's university who preached in Czech at the Bethlehem Chapel from 1402 and would not condemn John Wyclif. He went to the Council of Constance under King Sigismund's safe-conduct, was arrested, refused to abjure what he said he had never taught, and was burned there on 6 July 1415. The lords and knights of Bohemia and Moravia then wrote to the council that he was a good, just and catholic man, and the lay cup he defended became the emblem of the Hussite and Bohemian Brethren movement.
Also called
John Hus, John Huss, Joannes Hus, John of Husinec
Dates
Born in 1369 by Palacký and Tomek, in 1373 by the oldest traditions, and between 1373 and 1375 by Count Lützow, who says the exact year cannot be affirmed and the day is quite uncertain; bachelor of divinity in 1394 and master of arts in 1396, priest probably in 1400, rector of the university in 1402 and again after the German masters left in 1409, all by Lützow; preacher at the Bethlehem Chapel from 1402; left Prague on 11 October 1414 and reached Constance on 3 November, by Lützow; arrested on 28 November 1414, by Peter of Mladoňovice; died on Saturday 6 July 1415, which Peter's text gives as the sixth day of July and Richental as the Saturday after St. Ulrich's day, though Palacký notes that Peter's manuscript reads the seventh day of July
Place
Husinec, near the small town of Prachatice, not far from the frontiers of Bavaria, by Lützow; Prague, where he was a master of the university and preacher at the Bethlehem Chapel; Constance, where he was burned in a meadow outside the gate and his ashes were thrown into the Rhine
Role
Priest, master of the university and preacher at the Bethlehem Chapel in Prague; martyr

About This Person

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 4:57

In October 1414, Sigismund, king of Hungary and the elected head of the empire, wrote a safe-conduct, a royal letter of protection, for a priest from Prague. It ordered everyone to let Jan Hus "pass, stay, remain and return freely." He had already set out for Constance, where a council of the bishops and teachers of the Western church was to meet.

Hus, a master, or teacher, at Prague's university, preached to the city in Czech. He read the books of John Wyclif, an English scholar dead since 1384, whose books the church had condemned, and he would not condemn him. At Constance he refused to swear that he had taught what he said he never taught, and on 6 July 1415 he was burned. His death turned a university quarrel into a nation's movement.

In 1402 he became preacher at the Bethlehem Chapel, built so that preachers in Czech need no longer wander through houses and hidden corners.

In 1403 the university forbade anyone to teach Wyclif's articles, and Hus protested. In 1410 the archbishop of Prague burned Wyclif's books and cut Hus off from the church's sacraments. Hus wrote that he hoped Wyclif was among the saved. In 1412 he spoke against papal pardons said to cancel the punishment for sin. His ban was made heavier. No one was to give him food or drink or speak to him, and the city was put under a ban that stopped Mass, sermons and church burials. He appealed from the pope to Christ, and in October he left Prague. In exile he wrote his book on the Church: Christ alone is its head. Much of it, his translator writes, comes from Wyclif's own book.

At Constance, Hus was arrested within weeks. A papal official at the council wrote that he had gone on teaching Wyclif's doctrine at his lodging after he was warned to stop. Most of what followed comes from Peter of Mladoňovice, a Czech in Hus's party and his friend. Ulrich Richental, a citizen of Constance who watched from the other side, wrote that the learned told the king no heretic could hold a safe-conduct, and he let it be.

At his hearing on 7 June, Hus was charged with wishing his soul were with Wyclif's. He answered: "I do not know where John Wyclif's soul is. I hope that he is saved, and I fear that he may be damned. Yet in hope I would wish my soul to be where John Wyclif's soul is." The council laughed. The king told him, Peter writes, that he would burn a stubborn heretic with his own hands. From prison Hus defended the cup, the wine at communion, which only priests had long drunk and the council had just forbidden to lay people.

On 6 July the council condemned him. Urged to abjure, to give up publicly the teachings charged against him, he said he feared to lie before God. He was stripped of his priesthood and crowned with a paper hat painted with devils. The council handed him to the king's officers, and at the king's command the city's executioners burned him. Asked at the stake once more to recant, he answered: "God is my witness that I never taught or preached the things falsely charged to me, laid on me by false witnesses. The chief aim of my preaching, and of all my other acts and writings, was only to turn people from sin. I wrote, taught and preached the truth of the gospel from the words and teachings of the holy teachers. In that truth I will gladly die today." He sang a prayer, Peter writes; Richental writes that he cried out badly. The executioners broke up his bones and roasted his heart on a stick, Peter writes, and his ashes were thrown into the Rhine.

Within two months, the lords and knights of Bohemia and Moravia set 452 seals on a letter to the council: "Master John Hus was truly a good, just and catholic man. For many years he lived among us in our kingdom in a way worthy of praise, proven in his life, his conduct and his good name." By catholic they meant true to the church's faith. They vowed to defend the law of Christ and its preachers, whatever it cost them. From his death grew the Hussite and Bohemian Brethren movement, and the cup became its emblem.

The king's letter had promised that Hus could "return freely." In 1520, Martin Luther wrote that Hus was burned against a sworn safe-conduct, and that he had no wish to make a saint or a martyr of him.

Key Works

  • De Ecclesia (the book on the Church): written in exile between 1412 and 1414; it holds that the holy catholic church is the whole body of the predestinate, past, present and future, and that Christ alone is its head. David Schaff, its English translator, judges that Hus took paragraph after paragraph from Wyclif's own book on the Church, as Loserth showed. The council condemned the book at his sentence, and an edition was printed at Mainz in 1520
  • Letters: in Latin and Czech, with English by Herbert Workman and R. Martin Pope: among them his letter to the English master John Stokes about Wyclif's soul, his Czech letter to the Bethlehem congregation as he set out for Constance, and his letter from prison, after 18 June 1415, against the council's decree forbidding the cup to lay people
  • Czech works: Count Lützow names the Mirror of Sin and the Smaller Mirror, a series of expositions on the faith, the commandments and the Lord's Prayer from 1412, and a short work called the Daughter; he says scholars have added so many works, rightly or wrongly, that he mentions only a few

Legacy

What traces to them

  • On 2 September 1415 the barons, nobles and knights of Bohemia and Moravia, with 452 seals added, wrote to the council reproaching it for his condemnation and death; they said he was a good, just and catholic man, and vowed to defend the law of Christ and its preachers to the shedding of blood.
  • David Schaff writes that what Wyclif's Bible was to the small company of dissenters in England, Hus's book on the Church was to the large body of Bohemians, that Hussites sent a copy to Luther, and that Wyclif was not quoted by the Reformers, who knew him through Hus.
  • In 1520 Luther wrote in his Address to the Christian Nobility that Hus and Jerome of Prague were burned at Constance in violation of the papal, Christian and imperial oath and safe-conduct. He said he had no wish to make a saint or a martyr of Hus, as some Bohemians did, but owned that he was treated unjustly and that his books and doctrines were wrongfully condemned, and that he could find no error in him.
  • His burning turned a university movement into a national one. Its emblem was the chalice, its program was set out in the Four Articles of Prague in 1420, and in 1436 the Compacts of Basel gave it legal recognition.

How they're remembered

  • 6 July, the day Hus was burned, is a public holiday in the Czech Republic.
  • Count Lützow reports a saying of Luther's, written when he had just begun to study Hus's works, that all had been Hussites without knowing it. The Library holds it only in Lützow's English, with no Latin or German.
  • Aeneas Silvius Piccolomini, later Pope Pius II, a hostile humanist writing about 1458, says that Hus and Jerome of Prague bore death with a steady mind, went to the fire as if invited to a feast, and sang hymns, and that no philosopher is said to have borne death so bravely. The Library's scan of this page is damaged and no second print is held, so the reading is uncertain.

What Is Uncertain

  • Birth. Palacký and Tomek give 6 July 1369, the oldest traditions 6 July 1373, and Lützow, after the latest research, a year between 1373 and 1375; he says the 6 July date rests on a fanciful likeness to the day of his death. Lützow says his parents were peasants of small means.
  • When he first spoke the wish that his soul be where Wyclif's is. At Constance, in Peter of Mladoňovice's record, Hus said he had said it twelve years before, when Wyclif's theological books were not yet in Bohemia and he knew only of his good life. Workman and Pope say, in a note, that he said it in a sermon before the synod in the presence of Archbishop Zbyněk, probably in June 1408. Hus's own reply to Stokes, as Workman and Pope give it, says he would not grant that Wyclif is a heretic but hopes he is among the saved; his written Latin reply confesses he said he wished to be in hope where Wyclif's soul is.
  • Wyclif's teaching on the bread. David Schaff says Hus was charged with holding that the bread and wine remain after the words of consecration, and that he declined to accept the university's decision of 1403. At Constance, in Peter's record, Hus answered that he had defended no error of Wyclif. Whether he held that teaching is contested.
  • The safe-conduct: when it was given. The letter is dated at Speyer on 18 October 1414, a week after Hus left Prague on 11 October, by Workman and Pope and Lützow. Workman and Pope say he set out without waiting for it, after Sigismund's promise dated at Rothenburg on 8 October, and Hus wrote that he had set out without safe-conduct. Peter says Sigismund promised the safe-conducts and afterwards sent them to meet him on the road. Sigismund told Hus at the hearing that he had given it before Hus left Prague. Some at the council said Hus's friends got it fifteen days after the arrest, and the Bohemian lords answered that it was shown and read to many on the day of the arrest. The exact sequence is contested.
  • The safe-conduct: what it covered. The Bohemian lords at Constance complained that Hus was held against the king's safe-conduct, and Hus wrote that in Bohemia he had been told to beware of it. Ulrich Richental, a Constance citizen hostile to Hus, writes that the king wished to help, thinking it a great shame if his safe-conduct were broken, but the learned told him that by no law could a heretic have a safe-conduct, and that when he heard their earnestness he let it be. Sigismund said at the hearing that he gave it so Hus could have a public hearing, and that some said he could not give it to a heretic. Workman and Pope judge that only the pope's safe-conduct could have protected him from the Inquisition, and that Sigismund's did not reach the church's courts. Luther judged that Hus was burned against the oath and safe-conduct. Cerretanus, a papal official, records that a knight who had brought Hus to Constance protested that the pope had given Hus a safe-conduct, which the pope denied. What it covered is contested.
  • The arrest. Peter of Mladoňovice, an eyewitness and Hus's partisan, dates it 28 November 1414, when the cardinals sent two bishops, the burgomaster and a knight for him; he says Hus told the bishops he would sooner choose death than deny a truth known to him from Scripture or otherwise. Peter also reports the rumor that Hus had been carried out of the city in a hay wagon and says it was false. Richental says Hus hid in a knight's hay wagon to get away, was found, and was then handed to the pope; he is hostile and inconsistent on the date. Cerretanus, a papal official, gives the council's reason: in his lodging at Constance Hus taught Wyclif's condemned doctrine to those who came to him, and when warned would not stop. Peter's date stands with Lützow and the editors. Workman and Pope, from Peter, say he was moved on 6 December to a dark cell hard by the latrines in the Dominican house.
  • The hearing of 7 June 1415. Peter, who was present, records that the king said he would defend no heretic and that if one stayed stubborn in his heresy he alone would light the fire and burn him; he also records that the council laughed at Hus and shook their heads.
  • The sentence of 6 July. The council's own text, in Mansi's volume, whose scan is damaged and has no second print here, says as far as it can be read that Hus had seduced the Christian people, most of all in Bohemia, was not a true preacher of Christ's gospel, was stubborn and past correction, and was to be deposed, degraded and left to the secular court. Peter says the sentence condemned his books, above all the book on the Church. Cardinal Fillastre's Gesta says he was convicted of many heresies and errors both by witnesses and by his book called On the Church, written in his own hand and acknowledged by him, and that in some points he made difficulty about abjuring because he said he had not said the things charged, but the contrary was proved. Peter and Fillastre each give why he would not abjure: Peter, that he feared to lie before God and to offend the truth and the many he had preached to; Fillastre, that he believed he would offend God and the saints and did not wish to scandalize the people to whom he had preached.
  • His death. Peter says he sang a prayer aloud until the wind blew the flame into his face, and then died praying silently with moving lips and head. Richental says that when the fire was lit he cried out very badly and was soon burned. Piccolomini, decades later and hostile, says they sang hymns and made no sound of a wretched mind. Count Lützow prints a contemporary Czech account of the death that Patera found; Patera doubts that its author was an eyewitness, though the writer says he was. The Library's scan of Piccolomini is damaged.
  • His confession. Richental says a priest offered to hear his confession if he would renounce his heresy, and Hus answered that there was no need, for he was no mortal sinner; he also says Hus wished to preach in German to the crowd and Duke Ludwig would not allow it. Peter says a priest on horseback said Hus should not be heard or given a confessor, as a heretic, and that Hus had confessed in prison to a monk and been absolved, as he wrote in his notes.
  • The ashes. Peter says the executioners broke the bones, burned the heart apart on a stick, threw his coat into the fire so that the Bohemians should not keep it for relics, and threw the ashes into the Rhine. Richental agrees that the ashes were carried into the Rhine.
  • Why he left Prague in 1412. Lützow says King Wenceslas begged him to leave for a short time and that Hus was also moved by the people's suffering under the interdict; he dates the departure probably to October 1412. Lützow says Hus disputed the papal indulgences publicly in June 1412, and that a decree of John XXIII granted a plenary indulgence to all who fought in or paid for the war against Ladislas, king of Naples; a second bull, Lützow says, ordered the Bethlehem Chapel, which it called a nest of heretics, destroyed and leveled to the ground. Hus defended his appeal to Christ at Constance, saying no appeal is safer than to Jesus Christ, who is not bent by a bribe or deceived by false witness.
  • The Bethlehem Chapel. Lützow dates its founding document, by John of Mühlheim, to 24 May 1391 and Hus's appointment as preacher to 1402. The bull of Gregory XII of 15 May 1408 confirms the foundation by the Prague citizen Kříž and the knight John of Mühlheim and says that, because no place in Prague was set aside for preaching, preachers in Czech were mostly forced to wander through houses and hidden corners.
  • Sigismund's titles. The safe-conduct is dated in his thirty-third year as king of Hungary and his fifth as king of the Romans, and it takes Hus under the protection of himself and the holy empire. Lützow says he was crowned emperor only in 1433, so he was king, not emperor, in 1414 and 1415.
  • The turn from a university quarrel into a national movement is an interpretation, widely accepted. The held primary witness for it is the lords' letter of 2 September 1415, which its heading puts at 452 seals; Hus's own letter on the cup is the bridge to the movement's emblem.
