John Wesley

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Methodist Revival.

Priest of the Church of England who, after feeling his heart warmed at a society meeting in Aldersgate Street in 1738, preached in the open air from 1739 and gathered those who answered into societies and weekly classes of about twelve. Those groups held the Methodists together and became the frame of the Methodist churches.

About This Person

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 4:09

Read along

One May evening in 1738, John Wesley went very unwillingly to a society meeting, a weekly prayer group inside the church, in Aldersgate Street, London. As someone read Luther's preface to Paul's letter to the Romans, Wesley felt his heart warmed.

He wrote, "I felt that I did trust in Christ, in Christ alone, for salvation. And I was given an assurance that he had taken away my sins, even mine, and saved me from the law of sin and death." He meant a felt certainty of God's forgiveness, and freedom from sin's hold and from death, its end. That night, his brother wrote, he declared, "I believe."

He was a priest of the state Church of England, a minister formally set apart, or ordained. From 1739 he preached outdoors to thousands. He gathered his converts into societies, split into weekly classes of about twelve under a leader who asked how each lived. Mocked as Methodists, a nickname at first, they became the Methodist churches.

In 1739 George Whitefield called him to Bristol, a port city in the west of England, where Whitefield had begun preaching outdoors. Wesley could barely accept it, for he had thought saving souls, bringing people to faith and forgiveness, almost a sin outside a church. Yet on 2 April, in a brickyard by the city, he preached to about three thousand people.

By his own account, the bishop of Bristol, the senior churchman over that region, told him he had no business preaching in his area and advised him to go. Wesley stayed.

The class came by accident. In 1742 the Bristol society agreed to pay off a building debt at a penny a week from each member who could. To collect it, they divided into classes of about twelve, each with one collector. When the collectors told him some members were not living as they should, he saw in it the very thing they had long wanted. From then on each leader saw every member weekly, and advised, rebuked or comforted them. Some changed, and some were put out.

The work drew violence. At Wednesbury, in 1743, a mob surrounded the house where he was writing, crying, "Bring out the minister." That night a second mob dragged him through Walsall, many crying out to knock his brains out. That winter, one of his preachers reported, mobs in that district beat Methodists and plundered their houses.

His preachers were ordinary men who had not been ordained. At a yearly meeting with his preachers, first held in 1744, they allowed such men only in cases of necessity.

After American independence, in 1784, Wesley ordained two preachers for America himself, though only bishops normally did so.

That year the Americans formed their own Episcopal church, meaning one led by bishops. In 1791 his preachers counted more than 130,000 members in Europe and America.

An American preacher later wrote that the American Methodists kept the written Rules Wesley had drawn up for the societies, with very little alteration. Their rule book still set out classes under a leader. Richard Allen, who later founded the African Methodist Episcopal Church, chose Methodism for its good discipline. Yet he wrote that some preachers became tyrants to Black members, turning them out without a trial.

The day before he died, too weak to make himself understood, he gathered his strength and said twice, "The best of all is, God is with us." A woman who kept his house wrote it down. He died the next morning, 2 March 1791, nearly eighty-eight, with friends kneeling around the bed.

Timeline

Dates
Wesley's own Journal runs from his voyage to Georgia in 1735 to 1790. He left Georgia in December 1737 and met the society in Aldersgate Street on 24 May 1738. He preached in the open air at Bristol on 2 April 1739, began the classes at Bristol in February 1742, and was dragged through Walsall by a mob in October 1743. The first Conference met in June 1744, and in September 1784 he ordained men for America. Jesse Lee says he died in London on 2 March 1791, not quite eighty-eight years old. No held source gives a birth date
Place
Oxford (Lincoln College); Georgia; London (Aldersgate Street, Moorfields, the Foundery); Bristol; Wednesbury and Walsall
Role
Priest of the Church of England and Fellow of Lincoln College, Oxford; open-air preacher; organizer of the Methodist societies and classes

Key Works

  • Journal: his own account of his life and travels from the voyage to Georgia in 1735 to 1790, published by him in extracts; Curnock's edition sets his private Diary entries and notes beside it
  • A Plain Account of the People called Methodists: his telling of how the first society arose in London, the one condition for joining, how the class began at Bristol as a way to pay a debt, and what a class leader was to do
  • The Character of a Methodist: a tract of 1742 in which he says the name Methodist was fixed on them as a reproach, first given to three or four young men at Oxford, and that he was one of the first to bear it

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Jesse Lee, an American Methodist preacher, wrote in 1810 that the Rules Wesley had drawn up in England for the societies were kept in America with very little alteration. The Discipline of 1798 prints them, with classes of twelve under a leader.
  • In 1784 the American preachers resolved to form an Episcopal church, and answered the question of God's design in raising up the Methodist preachers with the aim of reforming the continent and spreading scriptural holiness over these lands.
  • The Conference of July 1791 counted 72,476 members of the societies in Europe and 136,622 in Europe and America.
  • At that Conference, after his death, the preachers wrote that they would show their veneration for him by trying to follow and imitate him in doctrine, discipline and life.
  • John Nelson, who heard him preach in Moorfields in 1739, was one of the first lay preachers, men who were not ordained.

How they're remembered

  • Elizabeth Ritchie, who kept his house in his last months, wrote that on the day before he died he cried out with all his remaining strength that the best of all is God with us, and said it again. She says the last word he was heard to say was Farewell.
  • Richard Allen, founder of the African Methodist Episcopal Church, wrote that he chose the Methodist connection for its plain doctrine and good discipline, and that some preachers became tyrants, especially to the colored people, turning them out of society with no trial.

Open Questions

  • Most of what is known of these years rests on Wesley's own Journal, which he edited and published in extracts in his lifetime. Independent checks exist at three points: Charles Wesley's journal for the night of 24 May 1738, John Nelson's account of hearing him preach in the open air, and the Conference Minutes and American records for what the societies became.
  • The Aldersgate meeting. Curnock says two rival sites claim to be the place and judges the balance of evidence to favor the Nettleton Court site. He also reports a suggestion that William Holland was the one reading Luther's preface. Both are an editor's views, and the Journal's own text names neither.
  • The bishop of Bristol. The talk survives through Henry Moore's Life, which says the original in Wesley's hand was before him. Curnock dates it to the second interview with Bishop Butler, on 18 August 1739.
  • The size of the first crowd. Wesley says about three thousand. A Moravian letter of 9 April 1739, quoted by Curnock, says three or four thousand.
  • Who proposed the penny a week. Wesley does not name him. Curnock's note names Captain Foy, citing Wesley's Thoughts upon Methodism, which the Library does not hold.
  • The size of a class. The Journal says about twelve in each class. In Wesley's later telling the first collector takes eleven of the poorest with him. The American Discipline of 1798 fixes it at twelve persons.
  • John Nelson wrote his account later in life. His own lines say he heard Wesley from a stand in Moorfields, where he says Whitefield also preached; that the sermon was in the open air is read from this.
  • The ordinations of 1784. The published Journal says he appointed Whatcoat and Vasey to serve in America. His Diary for the same days says he ordained them, and Dr. Coke; the words as a Superintendent are the editor's, set in brackets beside it. The reasons are Curnock's account: Wesley held that presbyters and bishops were no different in rank.
  • The count of 1791. The Library's text prints the America total as 61,146, a scan fault: the United States figure of 57,621 and the West Indies figure of 6,525 make 64,146, and 64,146 with 72,476 gives the printed 136,622. A note says only half the West Indies return was set down.
  • Richard Allen wrote in old age, and Jesse Lee wrote about forty years after the Rules came to America. Allen's charge against some preachers is his own view.
