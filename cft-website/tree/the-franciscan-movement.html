The High Medieval Era · 1209-1300 · Italy, then everywhere

The Franciscan Movement

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Francis of Assisi was a cloth merchant's son who gave away his inheritance, took the gospel's instructions to the apostles as a literal rule of life, and began preaching and caring for lepers in the towns of central Italy. By 1209 others had joined him and Pope Innocent III gave the group verbal approval. What made the movement new was that it did not withdraw behind walls — the friars owned nothing, begged, worked, and preached in the streets of the fast-growing commercial towns. Clare of Assisi built the movement's women's branch and wrote her own Form of Life, the first monastic rule known to have been composed by a woman, and a third order let married lay people live a version of the same discipline without leaving ordinary life. Within a generation of Francis's death in 1226 the order was quarreling bitterly over how literally his poverty had been meant. A fight that eventually split it into Spirituals and Conventuals, and that also produced repeated rewritings of his own life story, by his own brothers, within living memory of him.

In Its Own Record

Gave Rise To

  • Fraticelli (Spiritual Franciscan rigorists) Documented — In 1294 Pope Celestine V freed Brother Liberato and a companion from obedience to their Franciscan superiors. To keep the peace, he told them to call themselves poor hermits, not Friars Minor. In 1325 Pope John XXII wrote against some brothers of the order 'who call themselves Spirituals'.

Continued As

  • The Franciscan Order — Spirituals Crisis to the Great Division Documented — Francis's Rule of 1223 binds the brothers to obey Francis and his successors. The same Order of Friars Minor carried on past 1300. In 1325 Pope John XXII wrote that his ruling was not against the order itself, only against some of its brothers.

Influenced By

  • Canons Regular / Victorine School Widely Accepted — Bonaventure, the Franciscan friar and teacher, wrote a Journey of the Mind into God. It turns on a pattern of three that Richard of St Victor had used: outside us, within us and above us. In 1924 Justin McCann, who edited The Cloud of Unknowing, called Bonaventure 'a disciple of Richard' in his teaching on prayer.
  • Joachimite Current Documented — Joachim's view of history spread among the Franciscan friars in the mid-1200s. The friar Angelo Clareno writes that a companion of John of Parma, head of the order from 1248, praised Joachim far beyond measure in a sermon. In 1254 another friar, Gerard of Borgo San Donnino, built a book on Joachim's three main works.

In Contact With

  • The Dominican Order Widely Accepted — A Franciscan book, the Mirror of Perfection, tells how Francis and Dominic met in Rome before the cardinal bishop of Ostia. Its date is disputed. In it Dominic wishes that their two orders could become one. In 1888 the historian Henry Charles Lea called them rival orders.
  • The Latin Papal Church (Gregorian Reform to Innocent III) Documented — Francis's first Rule opens by saying he asked Pope Innocent III to grant and confirm this way of life. The pope, it says, granted and confirmed it for Francis and his brothers. Angelo Clareno copied the passage into his commentary on the Rule in the 1320s.

Sources

  • Francis's own writings (Testament, both Rules, Canticle of the Creatures, letters), in Francis of Assisi: Early Documents, vol. 1 (ed. Armstrong, Hellmann & Short, 1999; free online) (primary) — The founder's undisputed first-person layer, kept distinct from all hagiography.
  • The layered hagiography: Francis of Assisi: Early Documents, vols. 1-3 (1999-2001) - Celano's Lives, Legend of the Three Companions, Assisi Compilation, Bonaventure (primary) — Printed in datable layers, with a scholarly apparatus that lets readers trace how the sources built up over time.
  • Clare of Assisi: Early Documents (ed. Regis Armstrong, rev. 2006) (primary) — Clare's letters to Agnes of Prague and her Form of Life (the first monastic rule written by a woman) - the movement's female branch in its own voice.
  • Scripta Leonis, Rufini et Angeli (writings of Brother Leo and companions; ed./trans. Rosalind Brooke, Oxford Medieval Texts, 1970) (primary) — The companions' counter-memory against the official portrait; central evidence in the poverty dispute.
  • Augustine Thompson, Francis of Assisi: A New Biography (Cornell, 2012) (secondary) — Deliberately source-critical, layer by layer.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 4, ch. 3 'Religious poverty and the search for perfection' (Kienzle); cf. ch. 4 (McGuire) (reference)
