The Franciscan Movement

Assisi and then most of the known world, from 1209 — a merchant's son who took the gospel's poverty literally, and an order that began arguing about what he meant almost before he was buried.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:18

Read along

Francis of Assisi was a cloth merchant's son who gave away his inheritance, took the gospel's instructions to the apostles as a literal rule of life, and began preaching and caring for lepers in the towns of central Italy. By 1209 others had joined him and Pope Innocent III gave the group verbal approval. What made the movement new was that it did not withdraw behind walls — the friars owned nothing, begged, worked, and preached in the streets of the fast-growing commercial towns. Clare of Assisi built the movement's women's branch and wrote her own Form of Life, the first monastic rule known to have been composed by a woman, and a third order let married lay people live a version of the same discipline without leaving ordinary life. Within a generation of Francis's death in 1226 the order was quarreling bitterly over how literally his poverty had been meant. A fight that eventually split it into Spirituals and Conventuals, and that also produced repeated rewritings of his own life story, by his own brothers, within living memory of him.

Documented Stories

‘The Lord Led Me Among Lepers’

The Testament Francis dictated in the last weeks of his life is the one account of his conversion in his own words, and it does not mention the crucifix at San Damiano, the stripping before the bishop, or any of the scenes his biographers would make famous. It begins: ‘The Lord gave me, Brother Francis, thus to begin doing penance in this way: for when I was in sin, it seemed too bitter for me to see lepers. And the Lord himself led me among them and I showed mercy to them. And when I left them, what had seemed bitter to me was turned into sweetness of soul and body. And afterwards I delayed a little and left the world.’ That is the whole of it. The biographies fill in what he does not — a kiss, a leper met on the road, coins pressed into a rotting hand — but the Testament records only that he was taken among them and that his revulsion changed. Everything after follows from it: the Rule’s instruction that the brothers should ‘rejoice when they live among people of little worth and who are looked down upon, among the poor and the powerless, the sick and the lepers’; the early friars’ service in the leper houses; and the Testament’s own insistence, near the end, that the brothers must not gloss his words but keep them ‘simply and without gloss’. They glossed them within a decade.

Francis Walks Into the Sultan’s Camp

Jacques de Vitry, bishop of Acre, was with the Fifth Crusade at Damietta in the Nile delta, and his letter of early 1220 is the earliest account of the episode, written by a witness who did not yet know Francis would be a saint. He describes a man ‘simple and unlettered, but beloved by God and men’, the founder of an order of ‘Lesser Brothers’ that was spreading fast, who came to the army and, ‘inflamed with zeal for the faith’, went across to the Saracen camp ‘and was not afraid’. He preached to the sultan for some days; the sultan, Jacques says, listened, treated him with honor, and had him escorted back — ‘Pray for me,’ al-Kamil is reported to have said, ‘that God may reveal to me the law and faith that pleases him most.’ Jacques also records, without approval, that Francis did not stop the crusaders from fighting, and that some of the friars in the camp were converting Christian soldiers to their own order faster than the army liked. The later Franciscan lives added an ordeal by fire that Francis proposed and the sultan’s clergy declined; the Testament and Rule add Francis’ own instruction that brothers among the Saracens should first ‘not engage in arguments or disputes but be subject to every human creature for God’s sake’. The crusade went on and failed. The visit changed nothing about the war, and a great deal about how one order would think of Muslims.

The Order Orders the Old Lives Destroyed

By the 1260s the Franciscans had four lives of their founder in circulation — Thomas of Celano’s first and second, the Legend of the Three Companions, and a body of recollections attributed to Brother Leo and the other companions — and they did not agree. The earlier texts remembered a Francis who forbade the brothers to own books or buildings, who wept at the sight of the friars’ new house in Bologna, and who had said the Rule was to be kept without gloss; the order, by then, had studia in every university and papal privileges interpreting the Rule. Bonaventure, minister general, was commissioned in 1260 to write a new life that would settle matters, and he did. At the general chapter of Paris in 1266 the order decreed, in a statute that survives, that ‘all the legends of blessed Francis formerly made should be deleted’, and that wherever they could be found, in or outside the order, the brothers should try to remove them, since Bonaventure’s was compiled from the accounts of those who had known Francis and should suffice. The decree was remarkably effective. Celano’s first life survived in a handful of manuscripts; his second in one; the companions’ material was scattered into later compilations, and its recovery was the work of nineteenth- and twentieth-century scholars. Francis had asked that his words be kept without gloss. His order kept the words and destroyed the witnesses, and the record of its doing so is its own.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Sibling and rival of the Dominicans, and parent of the Spirituals. Its lay third order, the Order of Penance, dates from 1221. Later the Observants kept its rule to the letter.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced Canons Regular / Victorine School Widely Accepted — Bonaventure, the Franciscan friar and teacher, wrote a Journey of the Mind into God. It turns on a pattern of three that Richard of St Victor had used: outside us, within us and above us. In 1924 Justin McCann, who edited The Cloud of Unknowing, called Bonaventure 'a disciple of Richard' in his teaching on prayer.
  • influenced Joachimite Current Documented — Joachim's view of history spread among the Franciscan friars in the mid-1200s. The friar Angelo Clareno writes of a companion of John of Parma, head of the order from 1248. In a sermon, this friar praised Joachim far beyond measure. In 1254 another friar, Gerard of Borgo San Donnino, built a book on Joachim's three main works.

Influenced

  • influenced Observant Reform & Popular Preaching Documented — In 1440 Pope Eugenius IV wrote of a hermitage in Spain where Franciscan friars lived. He said they lived under the observance of the Rule of blessed Francis. In 1438 he had confirmed Bernardino of Siena as head of the order's Observant houses in Italy. That letter says the houses were set up 'under the holy name of observance'.

In Contact With

  • in contact with The Dominican Order Widely Accepted — A Franciscan book, the Mirror of Perfection, tells how Francis and Dominic met in Rome before the cardinal bishop of Ostia. Its date is disputed. In it Dominic wishes that their two orders could become one. In 1888 the historian Henry Charles Lea called them rival orders.
  • in contact with The Latin Papal Church (Gregorian Reform to Innocent III) Documented Major — Francis's first Rule opens by saying he asked Pope Innocent III to grant and confirm this way of life. The pope, it says, granted and confirmed it for Francis and his brothers. Angelo Clareno copied the passage into his commentary on the Rule in the 1320s.Lasting result: Pope Innocent III granted and confirmed Francis's way of life for him and for all his brothers, present and future.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Francis of Assisi gave away a merchant's inheritance to preach and care for lepers in the streets of Italy's fast-growing towns, and by 1209 the friars who joined him owned nothing, begged, and worked rather than withdrawing behind monastery walls. Clare of Assisi built the movement's women's branch and wrote the first monastic rule known to have been composed by a woman. Within a generation of Francis's death in 1226 the order was fighting bitterly over how literally his poverty had been meant, a quarrel serious enough that his own brothers rewrote his life story more than once.

Legacy

The Franciscans are still one of the largest religious families in the world, in several branches, with a lay third order alongside them. Beyond the order, the movement changed the ordinary furniture of Western devotion: the Christmas crib, which Francis is credited with staging at Greccio in 1223. The Stations of the Cross, spread through Europe largely by Franciscans; an affectionate attention to Christ's humanity and to the poor; and a strand of Christian language about creation — the Canticle of the Creatures, with its brother sun and sister moon. That is quoted constantly to this day. The basilica raised over Francis's tomb at Assisi carries some of the most famous fresco cycles in European art.

Experience It Today

  • Assisi, Italy — the Basilica of Saint Francis, built over his tomb from 1228 and frescoed by Cimabue, Giotto and Simone Martini

How Sure the Record Is

Rich, with a famous layered-hagiography problem — sources built up over time in overlapping layers of legend and memory.

  • Francis's own writings (Testament, both Rules, Canticle of the Creatures, letters), in Francis of Assisi: Early Documents, vol. 1 (ed. Armstrong, Hellmann & Short, 1999; free online) (primary) — The founder's undisputed first-person layer, kept distinct from all hagiography.
  • The layered hagiography: Francis of Assisi: Early Documents, vols. 1-3 (1999-2001) - Celano's Lives, Legend of the Three Companions, Assisi Compilation, Bonaventure (primary) — Printed in datable layers, with a scholarly apparatus that lets readers trace how the sources built up over time.
  • Clare of Assisi: Early Documents (ed. Regis Armstrong, rev. 2006) (primary) — Clare's letters to Agnes of Prague and her Form of Life (the first monastic rule written by a woman) - the movement's female branch in its own voice.
  • Scripta Leonis, Rufini et Angeli (writings of Brother Leo and companions; ed./trans. Rosalind Brooke, Oxford Medieval Texts, 1970) (primary) — The companions' counter-memory against the official portrait; central evidence in the poverty dispute.
  • Augustine Thompson, Francis of Assisi: A New Biography (Cornell, 2012) (secondary) — Deliberately source-critical, layer by layer.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 4, ch. 3 'Religious poverty and the search for perfection' (Kienzle); cf. ch. 4 (McGuire) (reference)
