Smyrna, today in Turkey · Rome, today in Italy and beyond

Modalist Monarchianism

The attempt to keep God one by making Father and Son the same person - and the reply that this means the Father died on the cross.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:06

Read along

Around the turn of the third century a way of protecting the oneness of God became briefly popular and then notorious. Noetus of Smyrna, and after him Praxeas and Sabellius at Rome, taught that Father, Son and Spirit are not three who are distinct but one who appears in three ways - so that the Son is the Father under another aspect. The objection was immediate and blunt: then it was the Father who was born, and the Father who died. Tertullian wrote Against Praxeas to say so, in a book that also gave Latin theology much of the vocabulary it would use for the opposite view. Hippolytus wrote against Noetus, and accused Callistus, the bishop of Rome, of sheltering the teaching - an accusation tangled up with his own rivalry for that see. Sabellius' name stuck to the position longest, and modalism is still sometimes called Sabellianism, though very little is known about him.

Documented Stories

‘What Evil Am I Doing in Glorifying Christ?’

Hippolytus opens his treatise against Noetus with the only narrative the movement has. Noetus was ‘a native of Smyrna’ who taught that Christ was the Father himself, and that the Father was born, suffered and died. When the presbyters heard of it they summoned him and questioned him before the church. He denied at first that he taught any such thing. Later, having gathered followers — Hippolytus says he ‘formed a sect’ — and grown bolder, he was summoned again and this time stood his ground. Asked to account for himself, he answered with the line Hippolytus preserves because he could not resist it: ‘What evil am I doing in glorifying Christ?’ The presbyters replied that they too glorified Christ, but as the Son of the Father, one God in the sense the apostles handed down; they expelled him from the church, and with him, Hippolytus says, about ten others. The rest of the treatise is argument. What the opening shows is how doctrinal disputes were actually handled in a provincial church around 200 — not by a council or a bishop’s decree but by a body of presbyters summoning a member, hearing him, giving him a second chance, and then putting him out. It also shows the motive Noetus’ side claimed: not innovation but reverence, a refusal to say anything of Christ that fell short of God. Noetus’ own words survive only in the mouths of the men who expelled him.

In Its Own Record

The mirror-image error to Arianism and earlier than it: both are attempts to protect the one God, one by subordinating the Son and one by dissolving him into the Father. Hippolytus accused Callistus, bishop of Rome, of sheltering it.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Around the turn of the third century, teachers including Noetus of Smyrna and Praxeas at Rome argued that Father, Son, and Spirit were not truly distinct, but one God appearing in three different ways. Their opponents pressed the blunt consequence: if the Son simply is the Father, then it was the Father who was born and died on the cross. Tertullian's reply, Against Praxeas, forged much of the Latin vocabulary later theology would use to say the opposite.

Legacy

Tertullian's Against Praxeas is where a great deal of Latin trinitarian vocabulary was first put to work, so the tradition's language for the doctrine was partly forged in the argument against this position. Modalism has also proved unusually persistent: it is the account of the Trinity that ordinary believers most often reinvent without being taught it, and 'Sabellianism' is still the name theologians reach for when they see it.

Doctrinal Floor Note

Modalist Monarchianism defended the oneness of God by holding that Father, Son and Spirit are not distinct from one another but are one and the same, presenting himself under different aspects or in different phases. Its opponents drew the conclusion the position resists: if the Son just is the Father, then it was the Father who suffered on the cross - hence Patripassianism, the name Tertullian uses. It sits outside the Nicene definition from the opposite side to Arianism, collapsing the distinctions where Arianism made the Son a creature.

How Sure the Record Is

Entirely through opponents, and unusually well through them: Tertullian's Against Praxeas and Hippolytus' Against Noetus and Refutation are all hostile, and all quote the position at length in order to take it apart.
