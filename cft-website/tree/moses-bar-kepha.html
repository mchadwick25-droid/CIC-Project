Moses bar Kepha

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Syriac Orthodox Christianity under Islam.

Also called Severus (his name as bishop, in Bar Hebraeus), Moses Bar-Cepha, Moses Barcepha

Syriac Orthodox bishop of Beth Raman and commentator on the Scriptures. Bar Hebraeus says he interpreted the whole Bible and the books of logic. His book on paradise survives in Latin, and Bar Hebraeus lists his commentaries among the books read in the church.

About This Person

Someone may ask, a Syriac bishop wrote, in what part of the world paradise lies. Is it toward the east or the west, the south or the north? Basil, the brother of Gregory of Nyssa, said we face east to pray, to train our eyes on paradise. Others, the bishop added, think church builders prove it, since they lay out churches to face the sun rising in the month of Nisan. In that month, they say, the sun rises over paradise. Ephrem said paradise circles the whole earth beyond the Ocean, as a ring circles the moon.

The bishop was Moses bar Kepha, of the Syriac Orthodox Church. The historian Bar Hebraeus calls him Severus, bishop of Beth Raman, also called Moses bar Kepha, a commentator on the holy Scriptures.

Bar Hebraeus's editors, J. B. Abbeloos and T. J. Lamy, add more from an anonymous Syriac life that Joseph Assemani reports. Moses lived about ninety years, it says. For forty years he held three sees in the region of Nineveh: Beth Raman, Beth Ceno and Mosul. For ten years he was also visitor of the church of Tagrit, which then had no bishop of its own.

Bar Hebraeus puts his death on the twelfth of Shebat, in February, and his burial at the monastery of Mar Sergius, called Moallak. One part of his chronicle gives the year 913, and another gives 903. His editors take 903, and call 913 a slip in the numeral.

His book on paradise, addressed to Ignatius, whom he calls his brother, survives in the Latin of Andreas Masius, printed in 1569. He meant it to be exact and clear, he wrote, with each chapter set apart, so that readers would understand it all and forget nothing.

In his book of church law, Bar Hebraeus lists Moses's commentaries among the books of the teachers read in the church.

J. Rendel Harris and Alphonse Mingana quote a line from Moses's tract on baptism, as a Mr. Aytoun gives it. It tells the one baptized that "thou goest down to baptism one person, and comest up another instead, the new instead of the old".

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • In his book of church law, Bar Hebraeus lists the commentaries of Moses bar Kepha with those of Ephrem and Bar Salibi among the books of the teachers read in the church.
  • Dionysius Bar Salibi names him among the true and orthodox teachers, as E. A. Wallis Budge quotes him. J. Rendel Harris finds that Bar Salibi took a passage on baptism from him.
  • Eusèbe Renaudot, in the notice Migne prints before On Paradise, says a liturgy in his name is found in nearly all the books of the Syrian Jacobites.
  • Bar Hebraeus says Nasicha, a disciple of Moses, taught logic to 'Abdisho', catholicos of the Church of the East, as Joseph Assemani quotes him.

How they're remembered

  • Bar Hebraeus calls him most devout, and famous in church learning and in secular wisdom.
  • J. B. Abbeloos and T. J. Lamy say he is rightly counted among the most famous writers of his church.
  • Andreas Masius, who printed On Paradise in 1569, wrote that he knew nothing of his history and found nothing of him in church histories.

Open Questions

  • The year of his death. One part of Bar Hebraeus's chronicle, as printed, gives 903, and another 913. Abbeloos and Lamy follow 903 and call 913 a slip in the numeral. Andreas Masius gave 914 or 913, as Joseph Assemani reports him, and Assemani later wrote 903. F. C. Burkitt, introducing the second volume of C. W. Mitchell's edition of Ephrem, gives 903.
  • The day. Bar Hebraeus gives the twelfth of Shebat. Masius, as Assemani reports him, gives the thirteenth, a Saturday.
  • His life outside Bar Hebraeus. The three sees, the forty years, the ninety years and the ten years at Tagrit come from an anonymous Syriac life that Abbeloos and Lamy know through Assemani's second volume. That volume is not among the sources used here.
  • Ignatius, to whom On Paradise is addressed. Moses calls him his brother, and no source used here says whether by blood or as a monk.
  • His pupils. George David Malech makes 'Abdisho', catholicos of the Church of the East, Moses's own pupil. Bar Hebraeus, as Assemani quotes him, makes him a pupil of Moses's disciple Nasicha.
  • The anaphora under his name. Renaudot says it is given to him, and Abbeloos and Lamy say only one of the two survives. A book on the priesthood is given to him by one copy, and to others by two.
  • The held Latin of On Paradise breaks off in its third part.
  • Andreas Masius, in the preface Migne prints, thought him not much later than John Chrysostom. Eusèbe Renaudot calls this a great mistake, and Migne's heading puts him about the middle of the tenth century. Bar Hebraeus names him in the time of the patriarch Dionysius, ordained in 898, and of Athanasius, the maphrian, the church's head in the east.
  • Margarinus Binius, as Assemani and Renaudot call him, took him for Moses of Mardin, Masius's teacher. Both correct this.
