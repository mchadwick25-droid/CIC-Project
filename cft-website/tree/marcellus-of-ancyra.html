Marcellus of Ancyra

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Cappadocian Christianity.

Known for
Bishop of Ancyra in Galatia who, by his own letter, refuted at Nicaea the men who later accused him, was deposed for his teaching on the Son, was cleared by Julius of Rome and the Western bishops at Sardica, and was condemned by the Eastern bishops.
Dates
At the Council of Nicaea, by his own letter. Epiphanius of Salamis says he had died about two years before Epiphanius wrote. The year of his death, 374 by Blomfield Jackson's note or 372 or 374 by E. C. Richardson's note, after Ffoulkes, is an editors' date
Place
Ancyra in Galatia; Nicaea; Constantinople; Rome; Sardica

About This Person

In Rome, Marcellus, bishop of Ancyra in Galatia, wrote to Julius, bishop of the city. He had waited there a whole year and three months, he says. At the Council of Nicaea, he reminds Julius, he had refuted the men now writing against him. He confesses the Word who always exists with the Father, and Christ's kingdom, which will have no end. The letter survives only in Greek, in a book against heresies by Epiphanius of Salamis.

His opponents' charges explain why he was in Rome. Socrates of Constantinople says Marcellus wrote against Asterius, a writer on the side of Arius. In doing so he fell into the opposite error and taught, as Paul of Samosata had, that Christ was a mere man. Sozomen says he taught that the Son began when he was born of Mary, and that his kingdom would end. Bishops meeting at Constantinople deposed him and put Basil in his place at Ancyra.

Julius received him, and the Western bishops at Sardica cleared him. They said he had written that Christ's kingdom had neither beginning nor end. The Eastern bishops, in a letter quoted in A. C. Zenos's notes to Socrates, called him "the most execrable of all heretics."

Whether Athanasius of Alexandria stood by him is disputed. Epiphanius once asked him. Athanasius only smiled, Epiphanius says, hinting that Marcellus was not far from error, yet treating him as cleared. The dossier of Hilary of Poitiers says Athanasius refused him communion. E. W. Watson, who introduced the English Hilary, says it is "notorious that he never did so."

In 381 the Council of Constantinople condemned the Marcellians by name. Hefele, quoted in Henry Percival's edition, says Marcellus himself came back to the truth. A Benedictine note to Basil's letters thinks he erred at the end of his life.

Epiphanius left the judgment open. The hidden things of Marcellus's mind, he wrote, are known to God.

Key Works

  • Letter to Julius of Rome: his defence and creed, written in Rome; held only in Greek, in Epiphanius's Panarion 72
  • Book against Asterius: the book for which he was deposed; not held, and known through his opponents and the council that cleared him

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The Council of Constantinople of 381 names the Marcellians in its first canon.
  • Socrates of Constantinople says Photinus of Sirmium was his disciple.

How they're remembered

  • The Western bishops at Sardica: "he had written that His kingdom was both without beginning and without end".
  • The Eastern bishops: "the most execrable of all heretics".
  • Epiphanius of Salamis: the hidden things of his mind are known to God.

At Major Councils

  • Council of Sardica, 343: Cleared by the Western bishops; called a heretic in the Eastern letter
  • First Council of Constantinople, 381: Not present; the Marcellians were condemned in canon 1

What Is Uncertain

  • His own letter survives only in Greek in the Panarion of Epiphanius of Salamis, who files the Marcellians among the heresies; the held scan is rough, so the brief quotes none of it.
  • What he taught is known mostly through opponents. Socrates of Constantinople and Sozomen give the charges, and Sozomen adds that some held he had only raised questions. The Western bishops at Sardica and Julius of Rome cleared him; the Eastern bishops and Basil of Caesarea condemned him.
  • Whether Athanasius of Alexandria broke with him is disputed. Epiphanius says Athanasius only smiled when asked. The dossier of Hilary of Poitiers says Athanasius refused him communion; E. W. Watson calls it "notorious that he never did so"; Archibald Robertson speaks of a personal estrangement. The Benedictine note in the edition of Basil's letters holds that Athanasius removed him from communion in his last years.
  • Whether he came back to the truth at the end is disputed. Hefele, quoted in Henry Percival's edition, says he did. A Benedictine note in the edition of Basil's letters says he seems to have erred at the end of his life.
  • Eusebius of Caesarea's books against him are not held.
