The Sayfo & the Syriac Diaspora

Tur Abdin and Urmia, then the worldwide diaspora, 1915 to 2010 — the Syriac church's longest lane on the map, carried forward after a silence since around 1300 that its communities experienced as erasure.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:15

Read along

In 1915 the Syriac Christians of eastern Anatolia and the surrounding regions were massacred and driven out — the Sayfo, the sword. Tur Abdin, the Syriac Orthodox heartland, and Urmia, where the Church of the East had its own communities, were emptied by killing and flight. Survivors testified almost at once: Joseph Naayem published Shall This Nation Die? in 1921, and an Assyro-Chaldean delegation put its case at the Paris peace conference in 1919. What followed was a century of relocation. The Syriac Orthodox patriarchate moved to Homs in 1933 and to Damascus in 1959; Church of the East exile communities gathered in Chicago and elsewhere; By 2010 European diaspora parish registers recorded families whose villages no longer had them. The Assyrian side of the story has its own twentieth century, including the assassination of Mar Shimun XXIII in 1975, a split in 1968, and a Common Christological Declaration with Rome in 1994.

Documented Stories

Shall This Nation Die?

Joseph Naayem was a Chaldean Catholic priest in Urfa, the ancient Edessa, when the deportations and killings of 1915 reached the Syriac Christians of the region. His father was among those killed. Naayem himself was arrested, released, and escaped the city in disguise, reaching Aleppo and eventually the Allied lines, where he served as an interpreter. He then did what almost no one in the immediate aftermath was in a position to do: he wrote down what he had seen, and he sought out other survivors and wrote down what they had seen, from Seert, from Urmia, from the villages of Tur Abdin. In 1919 he was in Paris, part of the Assyro-Chaldean delegation that put the communities' case to the peace conference, asking for a homeland and for recognition that a people had been destroyed. The conference gave them neither. In 1921 his book appeared in New York under the title Shall This Nation Die?, with an introduction by James Bryce, the former ambassador who had compiled the British parliamentary report on the Armenian massacres. It is among the earliest published survivor testimony of the Sayfo, and for decades it was almost the only one in English. The nation did not die; it dispersed. The patriarchate of the Syriac Orthodox moved to Homs in 1933 and Damascus in 1959, and by 2010 the families of the villages Naayem's witnesses named lived more in Södertälje and Chicago than in Anatolia.

The Patriarch Shot at His Own Door

Eshai Shimun became patriarch of the Church of the East in 1920, at the age of twelve, the last of a hereditary line that had passed from uncle to nephew since the fifteenth century. He led a people in exile: driven from the Hakkari mountains in 1915, then from Iraq after the Simele massacre of 1933, when the Iraqi government deported him and he settled eventually in the United States. From Chicago and then San Francisco he governed a scattered church, and in 1964 he ordered the adoption of the Gregorian calendar, a decision that split the church, with the bishops in Iraq consecrating a rival patriarch in 1968. In 1973 he did something no patriarch of his church had done: he announced that he would marry, and resigned the patriarchate. He married Emama Yokhanan that August. A synod in 1975, unable to agree on a successor and unwilling to break the line, asked him to resume office, and he agreed on condition that the hereditary succession end with him. On 6 November 1975, at the door of his home in San José, California, he was shot dead by David Malek Ismail, an Assyrian from his own community who had objected to his marriage and to his return. Ismail was convicted of murder. Mar Dinkha IV, elected in London the following year, was the first patriarch outside the hereditary line, and the succession Mar Shimun had asked to end ended with him.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 1915, Syriac Christians across eastern Anatolia were massacred and driven from their homes in what survivors called the Sayfo, the sword; Joseph Naayem, a survivor and priest, published his account, Shall This Nation Die?, within six years. What followed was a century of relocation: the Syriac Orthodox patriarchate moved to Homs in 1933 and then Damascus in 1959, while exiled communities of the Church of the East regathered as far away as Chicago. The Assyrian wing of this history kept writing its own hard chapters, including the assassination of Mar Shimun XXIII in 1975 and a Common Christological Declaration with Rome in 1994.

Legacy

In 2010 the lane was alive, and its communities lived mostly somewhere other than where they started. By then the Sayfo had its standard scholarly study, David Gaunt's of 2006, built on archives and the survivors' oral histories. The edited collection Let Them Not Return followed later, in 2017. On this map the entry does one more thing: it carries the oldest lane on the whole atlas honestly to 2010, after a silence from around 1300 that Syriac Christians experienced, understandably, as erasure. The silence is named rather than smoothed over, and this is where the lane resumes.
