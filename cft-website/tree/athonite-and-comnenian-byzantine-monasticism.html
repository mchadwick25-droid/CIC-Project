The High Medieval Era · c. 963-1300 · Athos, Constantinople

Athos, today in Greece · Constantinople, today in Turkey

Athonite & Comnenian Byzantine Monasticism

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In 963 the monk Athanasios founded the Great Lavra on the mountain peninsula of Athos in northern Greece. Later accounts say a Byzantine emperor backed him. The loose scatter of hermits already living there began to take the shape it has kept ever since. Athos became a self-governing league of monasteries. Each ran on a written charter, called a typikon. In it the founder set down exactly what the house was for, down to the diet, the liturgy and the charity. In Constantinople at the same time, Symeon the New Theologian preached to his monks. He taught that God can be known directly and consciously, in experienced light. He pressed the claim so hard that he was pushed out of his monastery. Between them, the houses of Athos and the monasteries of the capital kept the practice of inner prayer alive. The next century would gather it up and name it hesychasm.

In Its Own Record

Gave Rise To

  • Serbian & Bulgarian Medieval Monasticism Dominant Modern Reconstruction Major — The Serbian ruler Stefan Nemanja became a monk. With his son Sava he founded Hilandar, a Serbian house on Mount Athos. Paisii, a monk of Hilandar writing in 1762, says Nemanja came to the Holy Mountain and built the monastery. This rests on a modern study of Sava, not on the founding charter or Sava's own writings.

Continues

  • Iconophile Byzantine Monasticism Inferential-Thin Major — Stoudios, the house Theodore led, still had its monks two centuries later. The Philokalia's compilers name Niketas Stethatos, a priest-monk there around 1030, as a close disciple of Symeon the New Theologian. No source shows Athos's founders taking up the Stoudite way, so this line is thin.

Influenced By

  • The Byzantine Imperial Church (Macedonian Era) Dominant Modern Reconstruction — Robert Curzon was an English visitor to Athos in the 1830s. He cites an account from 1701: the Great Lavra 'was founded by Nicephorus Phocas', the emperor. That report is late and secondhand. But a modern study by Rosemary Morris, from 1995, tells how Phocas paid for the house that the monk Athanasios built.

Influenced

  • Hesychasm & the Palamite Synthesis Documented — Kallistos Telikoudes, a teacher of hesychast prayer, told monks to read chapters on prayer. He named 'especially' those of the New Theologian. That is Symeon, an abbot in Constantinople around the year 1000. The hesychasts' own case also went out from Athos, as the Tome of the Holy Mountain.
  • The Hesychast Revival - Paisius & the Philokalia Documented — The Philokalia, a book of writings on prayer first printed in 1782, carries work by Symeon the New Theologian. Its 1893 Athens edition lists a talk on faith by 'our holy father Symeon the New Theologian'.
  • Kievan Rus' Christianity, 1054-1240 Inferential-Thin — A. N. Mouravieff's history of the Russian church, published in 1838, says Anthony took his vows on Mount Athos. He then came home and began the Caves Monastery at Kyiv. The same history says the house's rule came from the Stoudios monastery in Constantinople. It names no earlier witness, so the tie to Athos rests on this one account.
  • The Georgian Golden Age Inferential-Thin — Marie-Félicité Brosset edited the Georgian chronicles. In 1849 he wrote that under King Bagrat III, the Georgian monastery on Athos was home to learned translators. They put scripture and the best Greek books of theology into Georgian. This rests on his one account.

In Contact With

  • Georgian Revival — Tao-Klarjeti and Athos Dominant Modern Reconstruction — Georgian monks founded the monastery of Iviron on Mount Athos in the late 900s. Brosset, a historian writing in 1849, puts the founding at about 976, though others date it to between 980 and 983. He says its scholars put the holy books and the best Greek theology into Georgian.

Sources

  • Byzantine Monastic Foundation Documents (ed. Thomas & Constantinides Hero, Dumbarton Oaks, 5 vols, 2000; free online) (primary) — The founders' typika are complete in English: Athanasios's Great Lavra documents, the Evergetis typikon, Pantokrator, and more - legislation, liturgy, diet, and charity in lived detail.
  • Symeon the New Theologian, The Discourses (trans. deCatanzaro, Classics of Western Spirituality, 1980) (primary) — Catecheses actually preached to his monks — formation as it was delivered, and the era's boldest claim of direct experience of God. One nuance worth naming: Symeon's own writing comes from Constantinople, representing the 'Comnenian' half of this tradition rather than Mount Athos itself.
  • Symeon the New Theologian, Hymns / Divine Eros (trans. Daniel Griggs, SVS Press, 2010) (primary) — The same claim in first-person poetry.
  • Holy Men of Mount Athos (ed./trans. Greenfield & Talbot, Dumbarton Oaks Medieval Library, 2016) (primary) — Lives of the mountain's founders incl. Athanasios of Athos - Athonite hagiography in facing-page translation.
  • Rosemary Morris, Monks and Laymen in Byzantium, 843-1118 (CUP, 1995) (secondary) — Standard study of the monastic world's social ecosystem: patrons, property, holy men.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 5, ch. 4 'The rise of hesychasm' (Krausmuller); cf. ch. 3 'The culture of lay piety in medieval Byzantium' (Gerstel & Talbot) (reference)
