Shakers

England and then America, 1774 to 1815 — a celibate communal society founded by a Manchester factory-hand, which built the plainest and most beautiful villages in America and sang thousands of songs.

The Quakers (Religious Society of Friends)→Shakers→no descendant recorded

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:11

Read along

The Shakers began among a small group of dissenters in Manchester who worshipped with shaking, dancing and ecstatic speech, and were transformed by one of them. Ann Lee, a factory-hand who had buried four infant children and came to believe that celibacy was the way out of the world's fallenness. In 1774 she led eight followers to New York, and the communities that grew after her death in 1784 became one of the most successful communal experiments in American history. Property held in common, men and women living apart but governing jointly, work treated as a form of worship. Their published confession identified Christ's second appearing with Ann Lee herself, which set them apart from the churches around them, and their dancing worship gave them the name their neighbors used. What they built was startlingly beautiful: villages of plain, exact buildings, furniture pared down to its function, and thousands of songs.

Documented Stories

Mother Ann in the Albany Jail

Ann Lee and her eight companions had settled in 1776 at Niskeyuna, in the wet woods north-west of Albany, and for three years almost no one came. In the spring of 1780 a revival in the Baptist settlements at New Lebanon, across the Massachusetts line, sent its disappointed converts looking for something more, and they found the Shakers: a community that danced, confessed its sins aloud, held that celibacy was the way out of the fallen world, and taught that no follower of Christ could take up arms. The last point, in a war, and from English people, was enough. In July 1780 the Albany County Commissioners for Detecting and Defeating Conspiracies, whose minutes survive, had several of the Shakers arrested for refusing to take the oath of allegiance and for dissuading others from bearing arms; Ann Lee, two of the elders and others were confined in the Albany jail. In the autumn she was moved to Poughkeepsie, out of the way, and held there until December, when Governor George Clinton, who had found no evidence of treason, ordered her released. The Commissioners' record calls them ‘a set of people who are enemies to the country’; the Shakers called the imprisonment their first persecution in America and dated their growth from it, since the case had made them known through the whole Hudson valley. Ann Lee could not read or write, and nothing of her own survives; the state's minute book is the earliest document that mentions her by name.

Dragged Out Feet First at Petersham

From May 1781 to September 1783 Ann Lee and the elders traveled through Massachusetts and Connecticut on foot and by wagon, staying in the houses of converts, and the journey is remembered in the society's Testimonies of 1816, compiled from the memories of those who had been there, as a sequence of meetings and mobs. At Petersham in December 1781 a company of men broke into the house where she was staying at night, dragged her out of an upstairs room by her feet threw her into a sleigh and drove her three miles to a tavern, where, the witnesses said, they tore her clothes to see whether she was a woman or a man in disguise, and let her go towards morning when her companions caught up. At Harvard in the summer of 1782 a mob assembled to drive the Shakers from the town, marched the men seven miles to the county line with whips, and beat several of them, James Shepherd severely, before letting them go; a stone was later set up at the spot. At Shirley, Enfield and elsewhere the pattern repeated, with the charge sometimes British sympathy, sometimes the breaking up of families by celibacy, sometimes simply the dancing. She returned to Niskeyuna in September 1783 worn out and died a year later, on 8 September 1784, at forty-eight. The society's account presents these as sufferings borne without resistance, and it was the men whipped at Harvard whose children became the settled communities.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

The Shakers grew from a small circle of Manchester dissenters who worshipped with shaking, dancing and ecstatic speech, transformed by one of them, Ann Lee, who had buried four children and came to believe celibacy was the way out of the world's fallenness. Their communities held property in common, let men and women govern jointly, and treated work itself as worship; their own published confession identified Christ's second coming with Ann Lee, and the villages they built were so plain and exact that Shaker furniture and architecture remain reference points in American design today.

Legacy

The society still exists, barely — a single small community at Sabbathday Lake, Maine, has carried it into the twenty-first century. Its material legacy is far larger than its membership ever was. Shaker furniture and architecture are permanent reference points in American design, several of the villages are museums visited by hundreds of thousands of people a year, and the Shaker song 'Simple Gifts' entered the wider culture when Aaron Copland used it in Appalachian Spring. They were also inventive in practical things, from the flat broom to packaged garden seeds sold by mail, and ran them as businesses that paid for the villages.

Experience It Today

  • Hancock Shaker Village, Pittsfield, Massachusetts — twenty original Shaker buildings including the Round Stone Barn, with the farm and gardens still worked

Sources

Doctrinal Floor Note

The Shakers' own published confession — The Testimony of Christ's Second Appearing (1808), together with their church covenants — identifies Christ's second coming with the founder, Ann Lee. That is a plain departure from the historic creeds, on their own account. The earliest accounts of the founding generation come either from hostile former members or from testimony collected thirty years later, so some caution is warranted there.

How Sure the Record Is

Luminous community records

  • Testimonies of the Life, Character, Revelations and Doctrines of Our Ever Blessed Mother Ann Lee (Hancock, 1816) (primary) — The society's compiled oral testimony about the founding generation, gathered more than 30 years after Ann Lee's death; women's voices are structurally central to it.
  • The Testimony of Christ's Second Appearing (1808; Youngs), the church covenants (from 1795), the Millennial Laws (primary) — The society's own confession, stating the second-coming claim in their own words.
  • The lived community record: village journals and daybooks, membership rolls, hymnody (Millennial Praises, 1813; the gift-song tradition) (primary) — The richest record starts from the 1790s gathering onward; the founding generation itself is documented only retrospectively or by hostile sources.
  • Valentine Rathbun, An Account of the Matter, Form, and Manner of a New and Strange Religion (1781); Amos Taylor's narrative (1782); the 1780 Albany imprisonment records (primary) — Ex-member polemic that forms the earliest documentation of Ann Lee's generation - an asymmetry worth noting, since hostile and later accounts dominate the early record.
  • Stephen Stein, The Shaker Experience in America (Yale, 1992) (secondary) — Covers both readings of the Shakers: the identification of Mother Ann with Christ's second appearing in their own confession, and the institutional growth under Meacham and Wright after her death.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 7: nearest coverage in Ward's 'Evangelical awakenings in the North Atlantic world' chapter, at the radical-prophetic periphery (reference)
