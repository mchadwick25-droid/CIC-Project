Georgian Revival — Tao-Klarjeti and Athos

Tao-Klarjeti and Mount Athos, c. 780 to 1000 — Georgian monks who rebuilt their church from a mountain province and then went abroad and turned a corner of Athos into a translation house.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:18

Read along

The Arab conquests reduced the old Georgian kingdoms to a handful of mountain principalities. Georgian Christian life was then rebuilt in the highland province of Tao-Klarjeti. There a monk named Grigol founded the monastery of Khandzta around 782. Over a very long life he seeded a whole district with monasteries. What makes him unusual for this period is that we can hear about him from inside. A Georgian monk called Giorgi Merchule wrote his life in 951. It contains the famous sentence that defines Georgia itself as the land where the liturgy is celebrated in the Georgian tongue. The movement also went abroad. Georgian monks lived in Palestine, and probably on Sinai and near Antioch too. Between 980 and 983 they founded Iviron on Mount Athos, a house that turned Greek books into Georgian. It may have worked the other way as well. A Christian retelling of a story from India, the life of the Buddha, may have passed from Georgian into Greek there. As Barlaam and Josaphat, it went on into most of the languages of Europe.

Documented Stories

Grigol and the Prince’s Mistress

Giorgi Merchule wrote the Life of Grigol of Khandzta in 951, a century after the abbot’s death, from the traditions of the monasteries Grigol had founded across Tao-Klarjeti, and much of it is the record of a working partnership: the Bagratid prince Ashot the Kuropalates gave the land and the money, and Grigol built the houses. Ashot had taken a mistress, a woman Merchule describes as of good family, and kept her in his fortress at Artanuji; his wife and the court knew. Grigol came to the prince and demanded that he send her away; Ashot, the Life says, wept and admitted the fault and could not bring himself to do it. So Grigol went to the woman himself, spoke with her, and persuaded — or compelled; the Life allows both readings — her to leave the fortress for the convent of Mere, where the abbess Pebronia received her. Ashot, discovering it, rode to the convent and demanded her back, and Pebronia refused him at the gate, telling him that the prince who protected the churches of Georgia should not be seen dragging a woman out of one. He went home. Merchule presents the episode as Grigol’s courage before princes; it also shows a monastic reform that depended on a ruler’s patronage insisting, at some risk, that the patron’s household was not exempt from the rules, and a woman whose own view of being moved from a fortress to a convent is nowhere recorded.

The Monk Who Went Back to War to Build a Monastery

John Tornike had been a commander in the service of the Georgian ruler David of Tao before he took vows and joined his kinsman John the Iberian on Mount Athos, in the community around Athanasius’ Great Lavra. In 978 the general Bardas Skleros rose against the young emperors Basil II and Constantine VIII and took most of Anatolia. The court, desperate, sent to Athos for Tornike: David of Tao would lend twelve thousand Georgian horsemen if his old general commanded them. The Life of John and Euthymius, written by a monk of Iviron about sixty years later, describes the monks’ dilemma. Athanasius and John the Iberian told Tornike he must go, for the sake of the empire and of the church; Tornike, the Life says, wept and said he had left all that. He went. In March 979 the Georgian cavalry broke Skleros’ army at Pankaleia, and Tornike returned to Athos with an enormous share of the spoils — the Life says twelve hundredweight of gold — and imperial charters. He and John the Iberian used it to found Iviron, the Monastery of the Iberians, between 980 and 983: a Georgian house on the Holy Mountain where Euthymius, John’s son, would spend his life translating Greek theology into Georgian and Georgian texts into Greek. It is also the record of a monastery paid for by a war, by a man who had renounced war, at the request of the abbots who had received his renunciation.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Grigol Khandzteli rebuilt Georgian monasticism from Khandzta starting around 782, and Georgian monks founded Iviron on Mount Athos between 980 and 983. This revival leads directly into the Georgian Golden Age that follows it.

Descended from

  • continues Georgian Christianity Widely Accepted Major — The Georgian church broke with Armenia around 600. After that, a catholicos of Georgia still appears in the Armenian historian Sebeos. The historian Marie-Félicité Brosset names Georgian catholicoi in the mid-600s and around 790. He doubted that the see ever lapsed, though the sources for these years are thin.

Direct descendants

  • continued as The Georgian Golden Age Inferential-Thin Major — The historian Marie-Félicité Brosset, writing in 1849, tells how David, the ruler of Tao, adopted the young Bagrat. By 1008 Bagrat ruled a single Georgian kingdom. Brosset also shows the head of the Georgian church passing from Suimon to Melkisedek in Bagrat's reign. This rests on Brosset's one account.

Influence, argument and contact

In Contact With

  • in contact with Athonite & Comnenian Byzantine Monasticism Dominant Modern Reconstruction — Georgian monks founded the monastery of Iviron on Mount Athos in the late 900s. Brosset, a historian writing in 1849, puts the founding at about 976, though others date it to between 980 and 983. He says its scholars put the holy books and the best Greek theology into Georgian.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

The Arab conquests left the Georgian kingdoms as a few small mountain states. Then, around 782, a monk named Grigol founded the monastery of Khandzta and seeded the whole district with monasteries. We can see this work from inside, because his biographer, Giorgi Merchule, wrote about it in 951. Georgia, he wrote, was above all the land where the liturgy was sung in the Georgian tongue. Georgian monks later took the movement to Mount Athos. Between 980 and 983 they founded Iviron there, a house that turned Greek books into Georgian. Its monks may also have carried a story from India out of Georgian into Greek. As Barlaam and Josaphat, it passed into most of the languages of Europe.

Legacy

The same tradition runs straight on into the Georgian Golden Age of the following centuries and into the Georgian Orthodox Church of today. Its most visible legacy is Iviron itself, still one of the twenty ruling monasteries of Mount Athos, and the body of translated literature its monks produced, which gave the Georgian church a full library in its own language. In Tao-Klarjeti many of the churches these monks built still stand, ruined or converted, in what is now north-eastern Turkey.

Experience It Today
