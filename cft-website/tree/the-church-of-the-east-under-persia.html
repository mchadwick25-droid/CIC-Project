The Age of Monks and Empires · 424-651 · Persian Mesopotamia

The Church of the East under Persia

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In 424 the bishops of the Persian Empire ruled that their catholicos at Seleucia-Ctesiphon answered to no bishop beyond their own frontier. This church lived under a Zoroastrian shah. It had taken the Nicene creed as its own in 410, but it would answer to no Roman bishop. Its answer to the question of Christ was not Chalcedon's, nor the Miaphysites'. It stood in the line of Theodore of Mopsuestia and held firmly that his divinity and his humanity were distinct. Its opponents fixed the name "Nestorian" on it. Its center of learning was the School of Nisibis. Written statutes there set out the students' day, their lodging and their discipline. Almost nothing else this early has left formation documents like them. Its communion reached far beyond Mesopotamia. In the 550s a traveler named Cosmas reported a bishop appointed from Persia at Kalyan, in western India. He also reported a church on the pepper coast further south. When the Sasanian empire fell to Arab armies by 651, the church carried straight on under new rulers and kept spreading east.

In Its Own Record

Continues

  • Persian Church of the East (early) Documented Major — In 424 the Persian bishops met under their catholicos, Dadisho. The acts of the synod call Isaac and Yahbalaha 'our masters and fathers'. These two catholicoi had led the synods of 410 and 420. The same synod ruled that Eastern bishops could not go over their head to the Western patriarchs.

Continued As

  • The Church of the East on the Silk Road Documented Major — The acts of the Church of the East keep its synods in one series, before and after the Arab conquest of Persia. Gregory held one as patriarch in 605, and George another as catholicos in 676. No source from the time ties the China mission of 635 to the catholicos directly.

Influenced By

  • Syriac Christianity (Edessa/Nisibis) Documented Major — Barhadbeshabba wrote about the year 600. He says Ephrem set up a school at Edessa after Nisibis passed to Persia in 363. Later, he says, Narsai set up the school at Nisibis, and many came to it from Edessa to learn. Sources differ on when Narsai left Edessa.Lasting result: Narsai founded a new school at Nisibis in Persian lands, and most of the students from the school of Edessa came to him.
  • Antiochene Exegetical Christianity (Chrysostom-centered) Documented — Barhadbeshabba wrote about the year 600. He says Theodore's commentaries were put into Syriac at Edessa, where future Persian bishops studied them. In 585 a synod of the Church of the East met. It defended 'the books and the teaching of saint Theodore' against those who attacked him.

Influenced

  • St. Thomas Christians of India Inferential-Thin — Cosmas, an Alexandrian traveler writing around 550, reported a church on India's pepper coast and a bishop at Kalyan appointed from Persia. He does not say the pepper-coast church was under Persia. No source from his time ties it to the Kerala church of the 800s, so the evidence is slight.

Argued Against

  • Syriac Orthodox (West Syriac) Christianity Documented — In 612 the Persian king had the bishops of the Church of the East debate the Miaphysites at his court. A statement drawn up for that meeting says Christ has two natures. It charges Severus and his disciples with leaving the foundation of the faith by teaching one.

Sources

  • Synodicon Orientale (ed./trans. J.-B. Chabot, 1902) (primary) — The Church of the East's own collected synods 410-775 - its constitutional self-record; Syriac with French translation only (no complete English); the indispensable institutional source.
  • Adam H. Becker (trans.), Sources for the History of the School of Nisibis (TTH 50, 2008) (primary) — First English translations of the Cause of the Foundation of the Schools, Barhadbshabba's school narratives, and related texts - important formation-culture documents; in print (Liverpool UP).
  • The Statutes of the School of Nisibis (ed./trans. A. Vööbus, 1961) (primary) — The school's actual rule-texts (496, 602) - formation documents found almost nowhere else; Syriac with English translation; older Estonian-series edition, findable in research libraries.
  • Adam H. Becker, Fear of God and the Beginning of Wisdom (2006) (secondary) — Standard modern study of the School of Nisibis and East Syrian scholastic culture; Penn Press, in print.
  • Richard E. Payne, A State of Mixture (2015) (secondary) — How East Syrian Christians actually lived within Sasanian political culture - corrects older persecution-only narratives; UC Press, in print.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity, vol. 2, ch. 4, 'Early Asian and East African Christianities' (David Bundy) (reference) — The reference-tradition chapter covering Christianity beyond the Roman frontier incl. the Persian church.
