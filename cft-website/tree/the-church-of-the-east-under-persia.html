The Church of the East under Persia

Persian Mesopotamia, 424 to 651 CE — a self-governing church outside every Christian empire, with a theological school run by written statutes and a communion reaching as far as India.

Persian Church of the East (early)→The Church of the East under Persia→The Church of the East on the Silk Road

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:23

Read along

In 424 the bishops of the Persian Empire ruled that their catholicos at Seleucia-Ctesiphon answered to no bishop beyond their own frontier. This church lived under a Zoroastrian shah. It had taken the Nicene creed as its own in 410, but it would answer to no Roman bishop. Its answer to the question of Christ was not Chalcedon's, nor the Miaphysites'. It stood in the line of Theodore of Mopsuestia and held firmly that his divinity and his humanity were distinct. Its opponents fixed the name "Nestorian" on it. Its center of learning was the School of Nisibis. Written statutes there set out the students' day, their lodging and their discipline. Almost nothing else this early has left formation documents like them. Its communion reached far beyond Mesopotamia. In the 550s a traveler named Cosmas reported a bishop appointed from Persia at Kalyan, in western India. He also reported a church on the pepper coast further south. When the Sasanian empire fell to Arab armies by 651, the church carried straight on under new rulers and kept spreading east.

  • Barsauma of Nisibis — the metropolitan who secured the school's place and pressed the church's independence from Roman jurisdiction
  • Mar Aba I — a convert from Zoroastrianism who became catholicos in 540, reorganized the church's law, and was imprisoned and exiled for it
  • Babai the Great — abbot and theologian who gave the church its settled language about Christ in the early seventh century
  • Henana of Adiabene — head of the school at Nisibis whose teaching split it and drove students away, a controversy the school's own documents record

Documented Stories

The Ferry Across the Tigris

Aba was a Persian of Zoroastrian family, secretary to the governor of Beth Garmai, and, his Life says, a persecutor of Christians in a small way, when he came to the bank of the Tigris and found a ferry about to cross. In it sat a poorly dressed man whom Aba took for a Jew or a Christian; he ordered him out, and the man got out without a word. The boat pushed off and was driven back by a squall. Aba ordered the man aboard again, then off again; the boat would not cross while he was excluded and would not cross while Aba was angry. Finally they sat together, and Aba asked who he was. He was Joseph, called Moses, a student of the school of Nisibis, and he answered Aba’s questions about Christianity, about which Aba knew only insults, with patience. He went to Nisibis, was baptized, studied there and at Edessa, traveled to Constantinople and Alexandria, and returned to teach. In 540 the bishops elected him catholicos of a church in disarray. He reorganized its law, held a synod in 544, forbade the Zoroastrian-style marriages some Christians had adopted, and was then tried by the Magian priests as an apostate from their religion — the one crime the Persian state would not forgive. He spent seven years in exile in Azerbaijan governing the church by letter, walked back to the capital of his own accord, was imprisoned, and died in 552 still catholicos.

Three Hundred Students Walk Out of Nisibis

The School of Nisibis had written rules for its students’ day, their examinations and their discipline. For a century it had trained the clergy of the Church of the East in one way of reading Scripture. It was the way of Theodore of Mopsuestia, ‘the Interpreter’, and his commentaries were the school’s course of study. Around 572 Henana of Adiabene became its head. All accounts call him brilliant, and he began to teach differently. He leaned on Chrysostom rather than Theodore, and he allowed allegory. His opponents said his views on the will and on Christ came from the Greeks. The school split. Barhadbshabba, writing the school’s own history while Henana still taught, praises him. The church’s synods of 585 and 596 condemned teaching that Jérôme Labourt takes as aimed at Henana and his followers, though the acts never name him. The Chronicle of Seert tells it as a fight inside Nisibis. Gregory, metropolitan of the city, condemned Henana’s books, and Henana sought help from the king’s physicians. In the Chronicle the catholicos Sabrisho takes Henana’s side. Labourt finds the same odd turn. Sabrisho’s own synod of 596 spoke against the Henanians, yet he helped them against Gregory. The Chronicle says the students were moved by Sabrisho’s choice of Henana’s word over Gregory’s. About three hundred of them walked out. Only twenty people were left in the school, and scarcely as many children. The exiles founded or strengthened schools elsewhere: at Balad, at Seleucia, and above all at the monastery of Izla. There Babai the Great wrote the church’s standard teaching on Christ, partly against Henana. The synod of 605 formally bound the church to Theodore. Addai Scher’s notes disagree on Henana’s death. One says he died under the catholicos Sabrisho, who died in 604; another says he was still alive in 610. Nisibis never fully won back its standing. This is the Church of the East’s own account of how it settled its teaching: not by council alone, but by students voting with their feet.

In Its Own Record

This church is a direct heir of the Syriac Christian tradition. By the 550s a bishop appointed from Persia served at Kalyan, in western India. A traveler also reported a church on the pepper coast further south.

Descended from

  • continues Persian Church of the East (early) Documented Major — In 424 the Persian bishops met under their catholicos, Dadisho. The acts of the synod call Isaac and Yahbalaha 'our masters and fathers'. These two catholicoi had led the synods of 410 and 420. The same synod ruled that Eastern bishops could not go over their head to the Western patriarchs.

Direct descendants

  • continued as The Church of the East on the Silk Road Documented Major — The acts of the Church of the East keep its synods in one series, before and after the Arab conquest of Persia. Gregory held one as patriarch in 605, and George another as catholicos in 676. No source from the time ties the China mission of 635 to the catholicos directly.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 424 the bishops of Persia declared that their catholicos answered to no one beyond their own frontier. They built a church that lived under a Zoroastrian shah, not a Christian emperor. Its school at Nisibis ruled its students by written rules. By the 550s a bishop appointed from Persia served at Kalyan, in western India.

Legacy

The same church carried on without a break into the following centuries. It became the most widely spread Christian body in the world, and its missions reached Central Asia and China along the Silk Road. In later centuries the St Thomas Christians of Kerala belonged to its communion. Several churches that worship there today come from them. Its heirs also live on as the Assyrian Church of the East and the Chaldean Catholic Church. Along the way its scholars put Greek medicine, philosophy and logic into Syriac. That work was later carried on into Arabic. It was one of the roads by which Greek learning reached the wider medieval world.

Experience It Today

  • Nusaybin, Turkey — the fourth-century church of Mar Yakup still stands in ancient Nisibis, the frontier city whose school trained this church's clergy

Sources

Doctrinal Floor Note

"Nestorian" was a name its opponents gave it. Its own synods say something different: the assembly of 410 formally adopted the Nicene Creed, and the bishops' statement of 612 affirms Christ as fully divine and fully human.

How Sure the Record Is

Moderate-to-rich; the School of Nisibis statutes are formation documents almost nowhere else has

  • Synodicon Orientale (ed./trans. J.-B. Chabot, 1902) (primary) — The Church of the East's own collected synods 410-775 - its constitutional self-record; Syriac with French translation only (no complete English); the indispensable institutional source.
  • Adam H. Becker (trans.), Sources for the History of the School of Nisibis (TTH 50, 2008) (primary) — First English translations of the Cause of the Foundation of the Schools, Barhadbshabba's school narratives, and related texts - important formation-culture documents; in print (Liverpool UP).
  • The Statutes of the School of Nisibis (ed./trans. A. Vööbus, 1961) (primary) — The school's actual rule-texts (496, 590 and 602) - formation documents found almost nowhere else; Syriac with English translation; older Estonian-series edition, findable in research libraries.
  • Adam H. Becker, Fear of God and the Beginning of Wisdom (2006) (secondary) — Standard modern study of the School of Nisibis and East Syrian scholastic culture; Penn Press, in print.
  • Richard E. Payne, A State of Mixture (2015) (secondary) — How East Syrian Christians actually lived within Sasanian political culture - corrects older persecution-only narratives; UC Press, in print.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity, vol. 2, ch. 4, 'Early Asian and East African Christianities' (David Bundy) (reference) — The reference-tradition chapter covering Christianity beyond the Roman frontier incl. the Persian church.
