The Optina Elders & Russian Ascetic Renewal

Russia, 1821 to 1906 — a forest monastery where monks kept a door open to anyone who knocked, and answered by letter the thousands who could not come.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:04

Read along

At a small monastery in the forest near Kozelsk, south of Moscow, a hermitage was founded in 1821. It became the center of something Russian Orthodoxy had never had at this scale. Elders, called startsy, kept the door open to anyone who walked up to it. Peasants, students, landowners and novelists queued outside the cell of Father Ambrose. The elders could not see everyone, so they answered by letter. They left thousands of pages of spiritual advice. They wrote not to monks but to ordinary people trying to live ordinary lives. They were also publishers. From the 1840s, working with the Slavophile thinker Ivan Kireevsky, Optina ran a press. It printed the Greek monastic fathers for Russian readers. Dostoevsky came in 1878 after the death of his small son. He turned what he found there into the elder Zosima.

Documented Stories

The Bishop Forbids the Elder to Receive the People

Leonid Nagolkin, in the schema Lev, had come to Optina in 1829 at sixty, from the northern forests and Paisius Velichkovsky's line, and had brought the practice they had learned: the daily disclosure of thoughts to an elder, and the elder's door open to any layperson. They came, from the villages and then from farther, with sickness, debts, marriages and possessions, and Leonid, a huge man with a peasant's speech and blunt jokes, sat with them for hours and sent them away with a word, a blessing and often oil for the sick. The clergy of the district complained to the diocese that a monk was hearing confessions he had no faculty to hear and drawing crowds from their parishes. Bishop Nikolai of Kaluga acted in 1835: Leonid was ordered to stop receiving laypeople, was moved to the main monastery where he could be watched, and forbidden to wear the schema. He obeyed in the moving and not in the rest. The Life written by a disciple records his answer to the abbot, who begged him to close his door: he had never called anyone, he said, and could not turn away those who came in need; the bishop could send him to Siberia, and he would go, but while he was at Optina he would receive them. The people kept coming and the bishop, after a visit, let it be. Leonid died in 1841, having trained Macarius, who trained Ambrose. The diocese's suspicion of the elders never quite ended.

Shamordino

Ambrose Grenkov had been an elder at Optina since Macarius's death in 1860, receiving daily the peasants, merchants, landowners and students who stood for hours in the yard; he was ill for most of those thirty years and received lying down. In 1884 he founded at Shamordino, eight miles from Optina, a women's community with a purpose the other convents did not have: to take women without dowries, peasant widows, orphans, the blind and the old, who could not be received elsewhere because they could bring nothing. Within a few years it held several hundred, with an orphanage, an almshouse and a school, supported by the gifts that came to Ambrose. Tolstoy's sister Maria became a nun there. In the summer of 1890 Ambrose went to Shamordino to settle the community's affairs after its abbess's death, fell ill, and stayed through the winter and the following summer, directing the sisters and receiving visitors as before. The bishop of Kaluga, Vitaly, regarded an elder's absence from his monastery for more than a year as disorder and sent repeated instructions that he return; Ambrose, too ill to be moved and unwilling to leave the community unsettled, sent excuses. In October 1891 the bishop set out to fetch him. Ambrose died at Shamordino on 10 October, two days before the bishop arrived, and his body was carried back to Optina in a procession that took two days, with the villages turning out along the road. Shamordino grew to a thousand sisters before 1917.

In Its Own Record

Grew out of the Philokalia tradition of ascetic prayer and became the world Dostoevsky drew on for The Brothers Karamazov. Its spirituality carried forward into twentieth-century émigré Orthodox theology, as a renewal movement within the wider Russian Orthodox Church of the same era.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

At a forest hermitage founded in 1821 near Kozelsk, elders known as startsy kept their door open to anyone who came. Those they could not see in person, they answered by letter. They wrote thousands of pages to ordinary people rather than to monks. From the 1840s the monastery also ran a press. It printed the Greek monastic fathers for Russian readers. Dostoevsky came in 1878 after the death of his small son. He turned what he found there into the elder Zosima.

Legacy

The Soviet state closed Optina in 1918 and killed or scattered its monks. The monastery was given back to the church in 1987 and is active again. It receives thousands of pilgrims a year, and its elders are now saints. The books it printed did more traveling than the monks. The Philokalic spirituality it made available to Russian readers carried into the émigré theology of twentieth-century Paris, and from there into English. The Way of a Pilgrim, with its Jesus Prayer, became one of the very few Orthodox devotional books widely read in the West. J. D. Salinger built a novel around it in 1961.

Experience It Today

  • Optina Pustyn, near Kozelsk, Kaluga region — the monastery of the elders, returned to the church in 1987 and receiving pilgrims again

How Sure the Record Is

RICH and profoundly inside-voice: thousands of letters of spiritual direction to laypeople; Way of a Pilgrim; John of Kronstadt's diary

  • The elders' letters: Macarius of Optina (Eng. sel. Russian Letters of Spiritual Direction, trans. de Beausobre, 1944); Ambrose of Optina's letters; Leonid; the skete's records (primary) — Thousands of letters of monastic direction addressed to ordinary lay people. Most of this correspondence remains untranslated from Russian.
  • The Philokalia-in-Russian line: Paisius's Slavonic Dobrotolubiye (1793); Optina's own publishing enterprise (from 1846, with Kireevsky); Theophan the Recluse's Russian Dobrotolubiye (1877-89) and letters; Brianchaninov, The Arena (primary) — This shows the renewal as a translation and publishing effort, with the elders themselves working as editors and translators.
  • The lay side: The Way of a Pilgrim (Kazan, 1880s); John of Kronstadt, My Life in Christ; pilgrims' memoirs of Optina (Leontiev) (primary) — The Way of a Pilgrim's author is unknown, and its textual history is debated among scholars.
  • The Dostoevsky-adjacent layer: The Brothers Karamazov's Zosima (1880, after the 1878 visit to Ambrose); Kireevsky/Slavophile essays; the Tolstoy visits (primary) — This is the renewal's literary and cultural afterlife - Dostoevsky's fiction and the Slavophile essays it inspired - rather than firsthand documentation of the movement itself.
