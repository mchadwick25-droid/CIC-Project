Tyconius

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Donatism.

Known for
Donatist writer of Africa whose Book of Rules gave seven keys for reading Scripture, and who argued against his own party without leaving it.
Also called

About This Person

"Of all the things that occur to me, I consider none so necessary as to write a little book of rules." So Tyconius opened his Book of Rules. His seven rules would be keys and windows into "the secret places of the law." With them, a reader crossing "the vast forest of prophecy" would not lose the way.

Tyconius was an African of the late fourth century and a Donatist. The Donatist church had split from the main body in Africa over those who lapsed in the Great Persecution. F. C. Burkitt edited the Book of Rules. Burkitt says the little known of Tyconius's life comes chiefly from Augustine of Hippo, a Catholic bishop who wrote against the Donatists.

Augustine says Tyconius wrote "in defence of the Church" against his own party. He reported a Donatist council that took back those who had handed over the Scriptures, without baptizing them again. Parmenian, the Donatist bishop of Carthage, answered him and warned him not to write such things. Tyconius still kept apart from the Catholics. Augustine calls this "a most inconsistent disposition."

Augustine set out the seven rules in his guide to the Bible, On Christian Doctrine. The second rule held that the Lord's body has two parts, good and bad, joined for now. Tyconius found it in the Song of Songs, where the bride says she is dark and beautiful. Augustine renamed the rule "the mixed Church." He faulted the third rule for saying that faith does not come to us from God. The book "must be read with caution," he warned, chiefly for what its author taught as a Donatist.

Gennadius of Marseilles says Tyconius also explained the whole Apocalypse, "regarding nothing in it in a carnal sense." Burkitt says that commentary is believed to be lost, though later writers copied much of it. Augustine commended the Book of Rules to students, "for it is of very great assistance in understanding Scripture."

Key Works

  • The Book of Rules (Liber Regularum): seven rules for reading Scripture, from the Lord and his body to the devil and his body
  • Commentary on the Apocalypse: the whole book read in a spiritual sense; believed lost, though later writers took much of it into their own
  • On Internal War: on the quarrels within the church, named by Gennadius; Burkitt says it has perished

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Augustine of Hippo, a Catholic bishop who wrote against the Donatists, set out all seven rules in On Christian Doctrine, Book 3, and commended the book to students.
  • F. C. Burkitt names Primasius, Bede and above all Beatus, a Spanish priest of Charlemagne's time, as writers who used his Apocalypse commentary as their main authority.

How they're remembered

  • Augustine calls him one "who, although a Donatist himself, has written most triumphantly against the Donatists."
  • Gennadius says he was, "it is said, sufficiently learned in sacred literature, not wholly unacquainted with secular literature and zealous in ecclesiastical affairs."
  • Burkitt says that throughout his review Augustine "treats Tyconius as an authority to be explained rather than as a theorist to be criticised."

What Is Uncertain

  • Burkitt says the little known of his life comes chiefly from Augustine of Hippo, a Catholic bishop who wrote against the Donatists. Augustine criticised his book and also drew on it. He calls him a Donatist who wrote against the Donatists, and "of a most inconsistent disposition" for staying with them.
  • What Tyconius reported about a Donatist council of two hundred and seventy bishops reaches us through Augustine's Letter 93. Augustine says Parmenian checked him by his reply but did not refute the facts, and that Tyconius said many were still living who could prove them.
  • Augustine faults his teaching that faith is our own and does not come from God. He adds that Tyconius wrote before the Pelagian quarrel, "having no enemy to guard against" on this point.
  • Burkitt's date before 383 rests on his reading of one passage, and he hedges it: "it would seem that Tyconius wrote before 383 AD."
  • The sources used here do not give his birth, death, home town or whether he was ordained.
