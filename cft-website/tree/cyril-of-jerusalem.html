Cyril of Jerusalem

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Jerusalem Liturgical-Pilgrimage Christianity.

Known for
Priest and then bishop of Jerusalem, whose lectures to the people preparing for baptism survive, and who was driven from his see three times.
Also called
Cyril, Archbishop of Jerusalem, Cyrillus
Dates
Born about 315 or a few years before, by Gifford's reckoning; bishop from about 350; died 386, by Gifford's reckoning from Jerome's eight years under Theodosius
Place
Jerusalem
Role
Bishop of Jerusalem

About This Person

Cyril had lately followed Maximus as bishop of Jerusalem when a cross of light appeared in the sky. Sozomen, a church historian of the next century, says it stretched from Calvary to the Mount of Olives. Men, women and children ran to the church to sing hymns to Christ. Cyril wrote to the emperor Constantius about the sign. Edwin Gifford, his English translator, dates the letter to 7 May 351.

Cyril of Jerusalem had been a priest of the city. In Lent of 348, by Gifford's date, he taught the people who were to be baptized at Easter. He gave them the creed to learn by heart, since not all could read the Scriptures. "We comprise the whole doctrine of the Faith in a few lines," he said.

Socrates of Constantinople and Sozomen say the party of Acacius, bishop of Caesarea, pushed Maximus out and put Cyril in his place. Socrates counts that party among the Arians. Jerome of Strido is hostile. In his Chronicle, as Gifford quotes it, he says Cyril gave up the priesthood Maximus gave him, to be made bishop by the Arians. Gifford, who defends Cyril, calls the charge "strongly marked by personal prejudice." In 382 the bishops at Constantinople wrote that Cyril "was some time ago canonically ordained by the bishops of the province."

Cyril and Acacius soon quarrelled over the rank of their sees. In a famine the poor came to Cyril for food, Sozomen says. He had no money, so he sold the veil and sacred ornaments of the church. It is said that a man later saw his own gift worn by an actress. Acacius used this as a pretext to depose him. Socrates says Cyril had refused to answer the summons for two years, "owing to fear." Cyril then appealed to the emperor, against church custom, he adds.

Gifford counts three exiles, about 357, in 360, and about 367. In 381, Socrates says, Cyril accepted the Nicene word for the Son being of one essence with the Father, "having retracted his former opinion." Gifford, following Touttée, who edited Cyril for the Benedictines, rejects this. The lectures teach the doctrine, he says, though they never use the word.

Jerome says he held his see eight years under Theodosius. Gifford dates his death to 386. In a lecture on the cross he had told the candidates, "here is Golgotha to confute me, near which we are now assembled."

Key Works

  • Procatechesis and Catechetical Lectures 1 to 18: given in Lent, in 348 by Gifford's date, to those to be baptized at Easter, going through the creed of Jerusalem clause by clause
  • Mystagogic Lectures 1 to 5: five lectures to the newly baptized on baptism, the anointing with chrism, and the Eucharist
  • Letter to the emperor Constantius: on the cross of light seen over Jerusalem at the start of his time as bishop

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Gifford's chapters on the lectures draw from them the order of baptism, the anointing and the Eucharist as they were kept at Jerusalem.
  • Theodoret of Cyrus, in his Dialogues, cites the fourth lecture as a testimony of Cyril, bishop of Jerusalem.

How they're remembered

  • Jerome of Strido calls him "bishop of Jerusalem often expelled by the church, and at last received."
  • The bishops at Constantinople in 382 wrote that he "has in several places fought a good fight against the Arians."
  • Gifford quotes the Roman Martyrology, which keeps his day on 18 March.

At Major Councils

  • Councils of Ariminum and Seleucia, 359: His case came before Seleucia; deposed again at Constantinople
  • First Council of Constantinople, 381: Present; Socrates says he now accepted the Nicene word

What Is Uncertain

  • Socrates of Constantinople and Sozomen, a church historian of the fifth century, say the party of Acacius of Caesarea pushed out Maximus and put Cyril in his place. Jerome of Strido, hostile to Cyril, says in his Chronicle, as Gifford quotes it, that Cyril gave up the priesthood Maximus had given him to be made bishop by the Arians, and that he later pushed aside Heraclius, whom Maximus had named. The bishops at Constantinople in 382 wrote that Cyril was canonically ordained by the bishops of the province. Gifford, who defends Cyril, calls the accounts of Socrates and Jerome "irreconcileable, and both improbable", and follows Reischl's suggestion that Cyril was named in Maximus's lifetime and ordained in due form. Gifford notes that Jerome and Cyril took opposite sides in the split at Antioch.
  • Sozomen, who says he looked into why Acacius deposed him, gives the sale of the church's veil and ornaments in a famine, and calls it a pretext. Socrates says he could not learn the charges, and that Cyril stayed away for two years "owing to fear." Theodoret of Cyrus says Acacius "seized some small occasion." Theodoret also gives Acacius's later tale of a gold-threaded robe sold to a dancer, who fell and died.
  • Socrates says that in 381 Cyril accepted the word homoousion, "having retracted his former opinion." Sozomen says he had once followed Macedonius. Gifford, following Touttée, the Benedictine editor, rejects both, since the lectures teach the Son's unity of essence with the Father without the word.
  • Some manuscripts name John, Cyril's successor, as the author of the Mystagogic Lectures. Gifford, with Touttée, holds that all the lectures are Cyril's, since the eighteenth lecture promises the later ones and they match it.
  • Socrates says that when Julian let the Jews begin to rebuild the Temple, Cyril foretold that one stone would not be left upon another, and an earthquake followed that night. Gifford thinks Cyril may well have expected the attempt to fail, but finds "no satisfactory evidence that he ventured to foretell any miraculous interposition."
  • The dates of his exiles are reckoned by Gifford, about 357, 360 and about 367. The names of the men set in his place differ between the witnesses.
  • Gifford dates the letter on the cross to 7 May 351, from the letter itself, which is not held in the Library. Gifford reports a doubt over the word "Consubstantial" in its last prayer, and holds the letter as a whole genuine.
  • No source used here gives his birth, parents or schooling. Gifford names one relative, his sister's son Gelasius, whom Cyril made bishop of Caesarea. Gifford reckons his birth from what Cyril seems to have seen of the holy places as a boy.
