Cyril of Jerusalem

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Jerusalem Liturgical-Pilgrimage Christianity.

Also called Cyril (Archbishop of Jerusalem), Cyrillus

Priest and then bishop of Jerusalem, whose lectures to the people preparing for baptism survive, and who was driven from his see three times.

About This Person

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 4:33

Read along

"Do you see this place, Golgotha?" Cyril of Jerusalem asks his hearers, and they answer with a shout. He answers it: "You answer with a shout of praise, as if you agree."

Golgotha is the hill where Jesus was crucified, close by the great church that Constantine built at the tomb. Around 348, Cyril taught the people preparing for baptism through the forty days of fasting before Easter. He went through the creed, their short statement of faith, line by line. After Easter he explained the rites they had just been through. His lectures survive because some hearers took them down as he spoke, an old note in one copy says. As bishop he was driven from his city three times in the quarrels over Arius's teaching that the Son was less than the Father. Jerusalem's way of making new Christians became a pattern that a pilgrim from the West wrote home about.

He welcomed even a man who had come only to court a woman: Jesus, he said, was angling for him.

He gave the creed in a few lines, since not everyone could read the Scriptures, to be learned by heart and not kept on paper. He told the candidates to tell no one unbaptized what they heard. And he pointed to the places around them, Golgotha and the tomb, as his witnesses.

In Easter week he explained the rites of baptism, anointing and communion, in the small chapel over the tomb itself. "I knew well that seeing persuades far more than hearing. So I waited for this time." They had faced west, the side of darkness, stretched out a hand and renounced Satan, then turned east toward the light. They had stripped and stood naked, unashamed, and he taught them to make the left hand a throne for the right at communion. Some old copies name his successor John as author of these five lectures. His modern editors hold them Cyril's, since the last Lent lecture promises them.

About 350 he became bishop, and how is disputed. Socrates and Sozomen, church historians writing about ninety years later, say the party of Acacius, bishop of Caesarea, pushed out the old bishop and put Cyril in. Jerome, hostile to him, says worse: that Cyril gave up his ordination as a priest to be given the see by the followers of Arius. The bishops who met in 382 wrote that he was rightly ordained by the bishops of the province, and had fought a good fight against the Arians in several places.

Acacius was the senior bishop of the province, and Cyril claimed rank for Jerusalem as an apostle's see. In a famine, Sozomen says, Cyril sold the church's veil and sacred ornaments to feed the poor. Acacius, Sozomen says, used the sale as a pretext to depose him. Socrates does not know the charges. He says Cyril was deposed for staying away two whole years, though summoned again and again, owing to fear. Once deposed, about 357, he gave notice that he would appeal to a higher court, and the emperor allowed it.

Two more removals followed. In 360 a synod at Constantinople deposed him again. The emperor Julian recalled the exiles, but under a later emperor he was driven out a third time, probably about 367.

In 381 he sat among the leaders of the great council at Constantinople. Socrates says he had by then accepted the word for the Son being of one being with the Father, giving up his former view. His modern editors reply that the lectures taught this from the start, without the word. He died about 386.

The lectures were kept. A note in the copies lets them be lent to the baptized and to those preparing, never to outsiders. Jerome listed them within a few years of his death. The pattern held too. Jerome, writing against Cyril's successor John, says forty days of public teaching was their custom. Egeria described it for the women at home. Of Easter week in the tomb chapel she wrote: "The bishop talks through each point and sets it out. Meanwhile the voices of those applauding are so loud that they can be heard outside the church." She never names the bishop, and her editors date her visit to the end of the fourth century, so it may have been Cyril or John.

After the shout, Cyril says to his hearers: "See that you do not take it back when persecution comes."

Quotations are given in our modern English. The printed wording of each is below.

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Gifford's chapters on the lectures draw from them the order of baptism, the anointing and the Eucharist as they were kept at Jerusalem.
  • Theodoret of Cyrus, in his Dialogues, cites the fourth lecture as a testimony of Cyril, bishop of Jerusalem.
  • Egeria, a pilgrim from the West, wrote home to the women of her community about the teaching of the candidates at Jerusalem: the bishop's chair in the great church, with the candidates sitting around him and their fathers and mothers standing by; the Scriptures taught from Genesis through the forty days; the creed taught after five weeks; and in Easter week the bishop explaining the rites in the cave of the Anastasis, with the doors shut. She never names the bishop.
  • Jerome, writing against John of Jerusalem in 397 or 398, says it was their custom to give public lectures for forty days to those to be baptized, and speaks of what John was accustomed to give the candidates in church through Lent.
  • Jerome lists Cyril's catechetical lectures in Lives of Illustrious Men, chapter 112, which Gifford says was written about six years after Cyril's death.
  • A note at the end of the Procatechesis lets copies be lent to those preparing for baptism and to the baptized, and says they are not to be given to catechumens or to anyone who is not a Christian. Gifford says it is doubtful whether Cyril or a later editor wrote it.

How they're remembered

  • Jerome of Strido calls him "bishop of Jerusalem often expelled by the church, and at last received."
  • The bishops at Constantinople in 382 wrote that he "has in several places fought a good fight against the Arians."
  • Gifford quotes the Roman Martyrology, which keeps his day on 18 March.

Open Questions

  • Socrates of Constantinople and Sozomen, a church historian of the fifth century, say the party of Acacius of Caesarea pushed out Maximus and put Cyril in his place. Jerome of Strido, hostile to Cyril, says in his Chronicle, as Gifford quotes it, that Cyril gave up the priesthood Maximus had given him to be made bishop by the Arians, and that he later pushed aside Heraclius, whom Maximus had named. The bishops at Constantinople in 382 wrote that Cyril was canonically ordained by the bishops of the province. Gifford, who defends Cyril, calls the accounts of Socrates and Jerome "irreconcileable, and both improbable", and follows Reischl's suggestion that Cyril was named in Maximus's lifetime and ordained in due form. Gifford notes that Jerome and Cyril took opposite sides in the split at Antioch.
  • Sozomen, who says he looked into why Acacius deposed him, gives the sale of the church's veil and ornaments in a famine, and calls it a pretext. Socrates says he could not learn the charges, and that Cyril stayed away for two years "owing to fear." Theodoret of Cyrus says Acacius "seized some small occasion." Theodoret also gives Acacius's later tale of a gold-threaded robe sold to a dancer, who fell and died.
  • Socrates says that in 381 Cyril accepted the word homoousion, "having retracted his former opinion." Sozomen says he had once followed Macedonius. Gifford, following Touttée, the Benedictine editor, rejects both, since the lectures teach the Son's unity of essence with the Father without the word.
  • Some manuscripts name John, Cyril's successor, as the author of the Mystagogic Lectures. Gifford, with Touttée, holds that all the lectures are Cyril's, since the eighteenth lecture promises the later ones and they match it.
  • Socrates says that when Julian let the Jews begin to rebuild the Temple, Cyril foretold that one stone would not be left upon another, and an earthquake followed that night. Gifford thinks Cyril may well have expected the attempt to fail, but finds "no satisfactory evidence that he ventured to foretell any miraculous interposition."
  • The dates of his exiles are reckoned by Gifford, about 357, 360 and about 367. The names of the men set in his place differ between the witnesses.
  • Gifford dates the letter on the cross to 7 May 351, from the letter itself, which is not held in the Library. Gifford reports a doubt over the word "Consubstantial" in its last prayer, and holds the letter as a whole genuine.
  • No source used here gives his birth, parents or schooling. Gifford names one relative, his sister's son Gelasius, whom Cyril made bishop of Caesarea. Gifford reckons his birth from what Cyril seems to have seen of the holy places as a boy.
  • Taken down as spoken. The only statement that hearers took the lectures down is a note added at the end of the eighteenth lecture in the older of the Munich manuscripts. It says these lectures alone were taken down when spoken, by some of the earnest students, in a year it counts as 352 and equates with 360 of our era. Gifford says the lectures cannot be so late. The note's author is not known. The title of the first lecture in the manuscripts says only that it was delivered extempore.
  • Egeria never names the bishop whose teaching she describes. Her name and her date are reconstructed by modern scholars. Her editors, following Férotin, place her visit toward the end of the fourth century, in the reign of Theodosius, so the bishop may have been Cyril or his successor John; Karl Meister argued in 1909 for about 534. The editors say her descriptions tally in most respects with Cyril's lectures, but they date the lectures to 386.
  • Socrates says Cyril was the first and indeed the only clergyman to appeal against his deposition in the way commonly done in the secular courts.
  • Theodoret says that after Acacius deposed him Cyril went by way of Antioch to Tarsus, where the bishop Silvanus, whose people greatly enjoyed the stranger's teaching, refused to stop him from taking part in the church's services, though Acacius had written to tell him of the deposition. Theodoret also says that at Seleucia Acacius refused to sit in council with Cyril as a man stripped of his bishopric, and that when Cyril would not withdraw Acacius left.
  • Sozomen says that when Theodosius was raised to the throne all the churches of the East except Jerusalem's were in the hands of the Arians.
  • Rufinus of Aquileia, in his Latin Church History, says that Cyril took up the bishop's office after Maximus when the ordination was already confused, and that he wavered sometimes in faith and more often in communion.
  • The men set in Cyril's place. Socrates gives Arrenius, then Heraclius, then Hilary; Sozomen gives Erennius, Heraclius and Hilarius; Jerome's Chronicle sets Eutychius, Hireneus and Hilarius between four tenures of Cyril. Epiphanius, Cyril's contemporary, says that a party was split into rival groups because Cyril and Eutychius bore anger against each other, and he places Cyril with Basil the Galatian, Anianus of Antioch and George of Laodicea.
  • The third removal rests on Gifford. He dates it probably to 367 and bases it on an order of Valens, which he quotes from Sozomen, that bishops banished under Constantius and back in office under Julian be ejected; Gifford applies this to Cyril, of whose third banishment he says no particulars are known.
  • Later readers and editions are known here only through Gifford. He lists Gelasius of Rome and Leontius of Byzantium as quoting the fourth lecture, and Theophanes as defending Cyril's orthodoxy on the ground that he avoided the word of one being, which then offended most people, and taught the same doctrine in other words. He reports a Slavonic version put into Polish before 1560, John Grodecq's Latin version of 1564 with a preface dated at Trent in 1563, and an Armenian version printed at Vienna in 1832. In the seventeenth century two French Protestant theologians attacked the Easter-week lectures on doctrinal grounds, with more zeal than success in Gifford's judgment, and Touttée defended them. None of those texts is held here.
