St Thomas Christians, 1300-1517

The Malabar coast, 1300 to 1517 — an Indian church worshipping in Syriac and governed from Mesopotamia, in the last two centuries before Europe arrived.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:20

Read along

On the pepper coast of south-west India there had been Christians for longer than anyone could document. They traced their founding to the apostle Thomas and worshipped in Syriac. They belonged to the Church of the East and got their bishops from its patriarch, far away in Mesopotamia. In this window they come into focus. A Syriac book copied at Cranganore in 1301 is the oldest dated Syriac manuscript from India. Its closing note names the patriarch Yahballaha III, which shows the link intact. The community was no small minority on the margins. It was an established trading group of high standing, with privileges from Hindu rulers and a headman of its own. A papal envoy at Kollam in the mid-1300s found that its people owned the pepper gardens and ran the public weighing-house. By the late 1400s the church's home in Mesopotamia lay in ruins, and the link took real effort to keep. In 1490 a delegation went to the patriarch to ask for bishops. The bishops who came back wrote home in 1504. They described the community, and the arrival of Portuguese ships.

Documented Stories

Four Bishops Write Home from Malabar

Around 1490 the St Thomas Christians of Malabar, whose last bishops from Mesopotamia had died, sent three men to find the patriarch of the Church of the East, now living in a monastery near Gazarta on the Tigris after Timur’s wars. In 1504 the bishops in India — Mar Yahballaha, Mar Denha, Mar Jacob and Mar Thomas — wrote to the new patriarch Eliya V, and their letter survives in Syriac. They describe the journey: to Hormuz, by ship to Cannanore, on to Cranganore, the reception by the Christians ‘with great joy’, the churches they consecrated and the ordinations they performed. They estimate the community at about thirty thousand families in some twenty towns, ‘rich and honoured’, with their own headman, living among Hindus and Muslims under Hindu rulers; they describe the pepper trade and the church at Quilon. Then, near the end, they report news: ‘powerful ships’ had arrived from the west, from a nation called Portugal, whose people were Christians and had a bishop with them, who had received the Syriac bishops kindly and celebrated the liturgy with them at Cannanore, and who were building a fortress and fighting the Muslims of Calicut. The letter is the last document of the St Thomas Christians as a church of the East before the Portuguese began, within a generation, to bring them under Rome; the men who wrote it did not know that, and it is also the only description of the Malabar church by its own bishops before 1500.

In Its Own Record

A note in a book copied in 1301 shows the community still tied to its patriarch in Mesopotamia. A delegation in 1490 and a letter in 1504 show the tie held later too. Timur's armies never reached this province.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Christians on India's Malabar coast traced their founding to the apostle Thomas and worshipped in Syriac. They belonged to the Church of the East. They got their bishops from a patriarch far away in Mesopotamia. A book copied at Cranganore in 1301 is the oldest dated Syriac manuscript from India. It names the patriarch Yahballaha III, which shows the link still held. The community was a trading group of high standing, with privileges from local Hindu rulers. Its people owned the pepper gardens around Kollam. In 1490 a delegation went to the patriarch to ask for bishops. The bishops who came back wrote home in 1504. They described the community and the first Portuguese ships.

Legacy

The community is still here. After 1517 came pressure from the Portuguese. Then came the Synod of Diamper in 1599 and the Coonan Cross revolt of 1653. Between them, these split the St Thomas Christians into the several churches they are now. They include the Syro-Malabar and Syro-Malankara Catholics, the Malankara Orthodox and Jacobite Syrian churches, the Mar Thoma Church and others. Together they have several million members, in Kerala and far beyond. Syriac is still used when they worship. The texts of this era already take it as known that the apostle Thomas founded the church. That is still how the community sees itself.

How Sure the Record Is

Thin, though richer than in the previous period: a manuscript (Vatican Syriac 22) copied at Cranganore in 1301 — the oldest dated Syriac manuscript from India, naming Patriarch Yahballaha III — plus fourteenth-century travelers' notices, the record of a delegation to the patriarch around 1490 and the bishops' letter of 1504, and accounts from Vasco da Gama's 1498 voyage.
