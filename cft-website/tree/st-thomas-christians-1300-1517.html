The Late Medieval Era · 1300-1517 · Malabar coast (Cranganore, Quilon)

Malabar coast, today in India

St Thomas Christians, 1300-1517

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

On the pepper coast of south-west India there had been Christians for longer than anyone could document, who traced their founding to the apostle Thomas, worshipped in Syriac, and belonged to the Church of the East, receiving their bishops from its patriarch far away in Mesopotamia. In this window they come into focus. A Syriac manuscript copied at Cranganore in 1301 — the oldest dated Syriac manuscript from India — names the patriarch Yahballaha III in its colophon, showing the link intact. Socially the community was no marginal minority but an established, high-status trading group with privileges from Hindu rulers and a headman of its own. By the late fifteenth century, with the church's Mesopotamian heartland shattered, the connection had to be actively maintained. A delegation traveled to the patriarch around 1490 asking for bishops, and the bishops who came back wrote home in 1504 describing the community — and the arrival of Portuguese ships.

  • St. Thomas Christians of India Widely Accepted Major — In the 850s a church law of the patriarch Theodosius told the far-off metropolitans, India's among them, to write to him every six years. A Malabar manuscript of 1301 names Jacob, metropolitan of 'the holy see of the Apostle St. Thomas'. At Kollam in the fourteenth century, the papal envoy Marignolli found the pepper gardens owned by 'the Christians of St. Thomas'. Few texts survive from the centuries between, so the line rests on place, name and office.
