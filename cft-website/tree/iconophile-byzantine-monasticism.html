The Early Medieval Era · 726-843 · Constantinople, Bithynia

Constantinople · Bithynia, today in Turkey

Iconophile Byzantine Monasticism

Constantinople and Bithynia, 726 to 843 — monks who argued that a God who had taken a human face could be painted, and were beaten, exiled and killed for saying so.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:18

Read along

Twice over a century and more, the Byzantine court tried to purge Christian worship of images. Both times the strongest organized resistance came from the monasteries. The monks argued that God had truly become a human being who could be seen, so he could be painted. To refuse the image was, in the end, to refuse the Incarnation. And they paid for the argument. Stephen the Younger, his Life says, was killed in 765. Theodore, abbot of the great Stoudios monastery in Constantinople, tells in his own letters how he was beaten, imprisoned and exiled more than once. Through it all he kept writing letters, talks to his monks, and treatises on the holy icons. Images were restored once at the Council of Nicaea in 787, and for good in 843. The Orthodox churches still keep that day as the Triumph of Orthodoxy. One thing is worth knowing about the story as we have it. The winning side wrote the record. The iconoclasts' own case survives almost entirely in quotations made by the people refuting it.

Documented Stories

Stephen the Younger Dragged Through the Mese

Stephen had lived for thirty years on Mount Auxentius across the Bosporus and had become the man iconophile monks looked to when the emperor Constantine V began to enforce the decisions of the council of 754 against images. The Life written of him some forty years later by a deacon of Hagia Sophia is the fullest account of the persecution from the losing side, and it is built around a confrontation. Brought before the emperor, Stephen was told that the honor paid to an icon was idolatry. He asked for a coin. Whose image was on it? The emperor’s. And if he threw it down and stamped on it, would he be punished for insulting metal or for insulting the emperor? The argument — that honor passes to the person depicted — was the iconophile case in a sentence, and Constantine, the Life says, had him imprisoned in the Praetorium with three hundred and forty-two other monks, many of them mutilated. On 28 November 765, the account continues, a mob from the palace faction dragged him from the prison, tied a rope to his foot, and hauled him along the Mese, the city’s ceremonial avenue, beating him until he died; his body was thrown into the pit for executed criminals. The Life is a martyrdom and shaped as one; modern historians who have re-read the iconoclast era warn that the iconophile sources inflated the scale of persecution to make their victory a deliverance.

Theodore Writes from the Prison at Bonita

Theodore had rebuilt the monastery of Stoudios in Constantinople into a house of some seven hundred monks with a scriptorium, a school and a rule, and had already been exiled twice for defying emperors over a royal remarriage when Leo V revived iconoclasm in 815. On Palm Sunday Theodore had his monks process round the monastery’s vineyard carrying icons in full view of the palace. He was arrested and sent to a fortress in Anatolia, and the community was dispersed. What followed is documented in his own hand: more than five hundred of his letters survive, and a large part of them were written in these years to monks in other prisons, to nuns, to laypeople, to the patriarch in exile and to the pope. He set up a network of couriers; he told his scattered monks how to keep the rule alone, whom to accept communion from and whom not, how to answer interrogators; he wrote to grieving parents and to a monk who had given way under torture, telling him to get up again. When the authorities intercepted a letter in 819 they gave him a hundred lashes and left him, his biographer says, half dead in a cell where his disciple Nicholas cut away the rotted flesh with a knife. He survived, was released when Leo was murdered in 820, and died in exile in 826 still refusing the compromise the next emperor offered. The letters are the reason the movement’s inner life is known at all.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Twice over a century, Byzantine emperors tried to purge Christian worship of images. Both times monks led the resistance. They argued that a God who had become visible in a human being could rightly be painted. Stephen the Younger's Life says he was killed for the argument in 765. Theodore of Stoudios, by his own letters' account, was beaten, imprisoned and exiled more than once. Icons were restored for good in 843, a day still kept as the Triumph of Orthodoxy.

Legacy

The monks' way of life carried on after 843, and Stoudios still had its monks two centuries later. Athonite monks are usually counted their heirs, and through them the wider Orthodox monastic world, though little survives to show the handover. The monks' theology of the image became settled Orthodox teaching. It is why icons look the way they do in Orthodox churches today, and why people treat them as they do. The Sunday of Orthodoxy, kept each year on the first Sunday of Lent, still marks how the argument ended. Theodore's rule for Stoudios also shaped later Byzantine and Slavic rules for monks, well beyond his own house.

How Sure the Record Is

Rich: Theodore the Studite's huge pastoral corpus; iconoclast side survives only in hostile quotation (named problem)

  • John of Damascus, Three Treatises on the Divine Images (trans. Andrew Louth, SVS Popular Patristics) (primary) — The foundational iconophile theology, written from Mar Saba outside imperial reach; Louth's translation is the standard English version.
  • Theodore the Studite, On the Holy Icons (trans. Catharine Roth, SVS Press, 1981); Letters (ed. Fatouros, CFHB 31, 1992) (primary) — Roth's translation of the icon treatises. The ~550 letters exist in Fatouros's Greek edition with German summaries; there is no complete English translation, only selections.
  • The Acts of the Second Council of Nicaea (787) (trans. Richard Price, TTH 68, Liverpool, 2018) (primary) — The iconophile victory documents, first full English translation with commentary. Also the main source for the iconoclast side's own arguments, which survive only as excerpts quoted in order to refute them - the 754 Horos survives only this way.
  • Life of Stephen the Younger (ed./French trans. M.-F. Auzépy, 1997) (primary) — The premier iconophile martyr-life (monastic milieu, Bithynia); French only, no complete English translation.
  • Leslie Brubaker & John Haldon, Byzantium in the Iconoclast Era, c. 680-850 (Cambridge, 2011) (secondary) — The standard modern history. It is deliberately revisionist, arguing that persecution was far more limited than iconophile sources claim.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity, vol. 3, ch. 2: 'The emergence of Byzantine Orthodoxy, 600-1095' (Andrew Louth) (reference) — From the vol. 3 TOC; the iconoclast controversy sits at its center.
