The Early Medieval Era · 726-843 · Constantinople, Bithynia

Constantinople · Bithynia, today in Turkey

Iconophile Byzantine Monasticism

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Twice over a century and more, the Byzantine court tried to purge Christian worship of images. Both times the strongest organized resistance came from the monasteries. The monks argued that God had truly become a human being who could be seen, so he could be painted. To refuse the image was, in the end, to refuse the Incarnation. And they paid for the argument. Stephen the Younger, his Life says, was killed in 765. Theodore, abbot of the great Stoudios monastery in Constantinople, tells in his own letters how he was beaten, imprisoned and exiled more than once. Through it all he kept writing letters, talks to his monks, and treatises on the holy icons. Images were restored once at the Council of Nicaea in 787, and for good in 843. The Orthodox churches still keep that day as the Triumph of Orthodoxy. One thing is worth knowing about the story as we have it. The winning side wrote the record. The iconoclasts' own case survives almost entirely in quotations made by the people refuting it.

In Its Own Record

Continued As

  • Athonite & Comnenian Byzantine Monasticism Inferential-Thin Major — Stoudios, the house Theodore led, still had its monks two centuries later. The Philokalia's compilers name Niketas Stethatos, a priest-monk there around 1030, as a close disciple of Symeon the New Theologian. No source shows Athos's founders taking up the Stoudite way, so this line is thin.

Influenced By

  • Desert Monasticism Inferential-Thin — Basil of Caesarea wrote that he found many monks in Alexandria and the rest of Egypt. In 787 the Second Council of Nicaea told double houses of monks and nuns to keep Basil's rule. In its acts, a bishop honors images of 'the fathers and cultivators of the desert'. But none of these texts shows the monks who fought for icons drawing on Egypt itself.

Influenced

  • The Byzantine Imperial Church (Macedonian Era) Documented — Photius, patriarch from 858, read and praised the work of two iconophile writers. One was the patriarch Nicephorus, the other Sergius the Confessor. His notes on Sergius speak of the lawless and vile acts of Constantine V. His church kept a feast each year for the victory of the icons.
  • Kievan Rus' Christianity, 1054-1240 Dominant Modern Reconstruction — Mouravieff's history of the Russian church draws on the chronicler Nestor. It says Theodosius of the Kyiv Caves wrote out the rule of the Stoudios house for his monks. A monk from Constantinople had brought it. Other Rus' houses then took it as their model.

Argued Against

  • The Byzantine Imperial Church (Heraclian & Iconoclast Eras) Widely Accepted Major — John of Damascus answered those who "find fault with us" for honoring images, and the iconoclast council of 754 cursed his teaching. Monks also sat at the council of 787 that reversed it. Harnack wrote that the resistance came from the ranks of the monks.Lasting result: At the Second Council of Nicaea, bishops who had been against images read out confessions that venerated them. The synod then took them back.

Opposed By

  • The Byzantine Imperial Church (Heraclian & Iconoclast Eras) Documented Major — In 754 a council called by the emperor Constantine V banned holy images. At its close the bishops cursed John of Damascus as a teacher of impiety, calling him by his family name, Mansur. Later historians write that the emperor then pressed the monks to obey. Many fled, they say, and some were killed.Lasting result: In 754 a council against icons said John of Damascus was deposed and cursed. It said the same of George and of Germanus of Constantinople.

Sources

  • John of Damascus, Three Treatises on the Divine Images (trans. Andrew Louth, SVS Popular Patristics) (primary) — The foundational iconophile theology, written from Mar Saba outside imperial reach; Louth's translation is the standard English version.
  • Theodore the Studite, On the Holy Icons (trans. Catharine Roth, SVS Press, 1981); Letters (ed. Fatouros, CFHB 31, 1992) (primary) — Roth's translation of the icon treatises. The ~550 letters exist in Fatouros's Greek edition with German summaries; there is no complete English translation, only selections.
  • The Acts of the Second Council of Nicaea (787) (trans. Richard Price, TTH 68, Liverpool, 2018) (primary) — The iconophile victory documents, first full English translation with commentary. Also the main source for the iconoclast side's own arguments, which survive only as excerpts quoted in order to refute them - the 754 Horos survives only this way.
  • Life of Stephen the Younger (ed./French trans. M.-F. Auzépy, 1997) (primary) — The premier iconophile martyr-life (monastic milieu, Bithynia); French only, no complete English translation.
  • Leslie Brubaker & John Haldon, Byzantium in the Iconoclast Era, c. 680-850 (Cambridge, 2011) (secondary) — The standard modern history. It is deliberately revisionist, arguing that persecution was far more limited than iconophile sources claim.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity, vol. 3, ch. 2: 'The emergence of Byzantine Orthodoxy, 600-1095' (Andrew Louth) (reference) — From the vol. 3 TOC; the iconoclast controversy sits at its center.
