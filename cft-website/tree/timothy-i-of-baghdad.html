Timothy I of Baghdad

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Church of the East on the Silk Road.

Also called Mar Timothy, Timotheus

Patriarch of the Church of the East from 780, under five caliphs. He sent monks as bishops to Gilan and Dailom, and set rules for choosing a patriarch.

About This Person

Read along

Early in 781, Christians in China's capital set up a great stone. On it, in Syriac, the language of their church, they named their patriarch, the head of the whole church, far away in Mesopotamia. But he was dead, and a new patriarch had been installed months before; the news, it seems, had not yet reached them.

The new patriarch was Timothy. For more than forty years he led the Church of the East, the church of the Christians of Mesopotamia and Persia. He led it from Baghdad, under five caliphs, the Muslim rulers of the empire. Historians of his church say he argued for his faith at the caliphs' court. And he sent monks out as bishops, the church's leaders for a region, and let them make more bishops where they were. His letters show a church that reached toward the Turks and China.

Timothy was already a bishop when the patriarch before him died. Mari, a historian of his church writing more than three centuries later, says Timothy showed the electors sacks he said held money for them, and won. When the electors asked for their money, he answered, "The priesthood is by no means sold for money," and showed them the sacks held only stones. Other later histories tell the trick differently.

The chief bishop of Elam, a rival for the post, refused to worship or work with him. Then the chief bishop of Elam and thirteen bishops declared Timothy cursed and cut off from the church, and he cut them off in turn. The split lasted about two years, by Timothy's own count, and in it Christians fought, killed and jailed one another.

A list of his writings, made five centuries later, names a debate with the caliph al-Mahdi. Another historian of his church, five centuries later, says the next caliph, al-Hadi, sent for him on most days to put hard questions to him about religion.

Thomas of Marga, a monk who wrote about twenty years after Timothy's death and had read his letters, tells of the bishops Timothy sent. Two brothers from one monastery, Kardagh and Yahballaha, went as chief bishops to Gilan and Dailom, south of the Caspian Sea. When many peoples there came to the faith, Yahballaha asked leave to make bishops. But to ordain a bishop, to make one, took three bishops, and the brothers were two, far from Baghdad, where a new bishop normally went to have his making confirmed.

Timothy wrote back. The book of the four Gospels, the life of Jesus, read in worship, would stand in for the missing bishop, laid on the throne, the bishop's chair in the church. "Make bishops of whomever you and the pious Kardagh choose. In place of the third bishop, let the Book of the Gospels be laid on the throne at the right hand. So, by the hand of God, ordain the first bishop." They made seven bishops. From Timothy's letters, Thomas learned of a chief bishop of Beth Sinaye, which scholars take to be China.

He died in the early 820s, under the fifth caliph; one chronicle gives 9 January 823. Mari says he was ninety-five. He was buried at Baghdad, in the monastery he had restored and made his home.

Timeline

Dates
Enthroned on Sunday 7 May 780, by Elias of Nisibis and J.-B. Chabot. Chabot places it at Seleucia, after eight months of dispute. Elias says he died on a Friday, 9 January 823. Bar Hebraeus puts his death in the Arab year 204, which began in June 819. Chabot's index gives 825. Mari ibn Sulayman says he died in his ninety-fifth year, in the forty-third year of his office
Place
Hazza in Adiabene, by birth; Bashosh, where he studied; Beth Beghash, his first see; Seleucia, where he was enthroned. Joseph Assemani says he lived at the monastery of Kelilisho at Baghdad. Amr ibn Matta says he was buried there, and Mari says he restored it
Role
Catholicos and patriarch; writer of canons, letters and treatises

Key Works

  • Synod of 790 and its letter to Ephrem of Elam: canons on how a patriarch must be chosen, sent to Ephrem's province, in Syriac with J.-B. Chabot's French. Chabot says it may be only part of the decisions of his first synod. 'Abdisho of Nisibis names a second synod, in 804 or 805
  • Letters: 'Abdisho counts about two hundred, in two volumes. Thomas of Marga read his letter to Yahbalaha of Dailom and cites it. Alphonse Mingana quotes the letter to the monks of Mar Maron from a manuscript of his own
  • Disputation with the caliph al-Mahdi: named in 'Abdisho's list of his works. Amr ibn Matta says the caliph al-Hadi often called Timothy to argue about religion. Joseph Assemani takes Amr to mean the same debate. Its text is not among the sources used here

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Thomas of Marga lists monks of his own monastery who became bishops. One was Elijah, bishop of Mukan, whom Thomas says Timothy ordained.
  • Bar Hebraeus reports it was said that the bishops of the Persians had worn white, eaten meat and married. It was also said that they had not obeyed the catholicos of Seleucia. They called themselves disciples of the apostle Thomas.
  • Timothy won them over, Bar Hebraeus says, and made a Simeon their metropolitan. Alphonse Mingana holds that the Syriac names the province of Fars, where Assemani and Lamy read the country of Persia.
  • 'Abdisho of Nisibis cites the canons of his synod in his own collection, J.-B. Chabot notes.
  • Chabot writes that the Church of the East prospered under him, above all through its missions in the Far East.

How they're remembered

  • Thomas of Marga tells of an old monk of his own monastery, Beth 'Abhe, who met the boy Timothy on his way to school. The monk foretold that he would be patriarch for forty-two years.
  • Thomas says Timothy loved Beth 'Abhe and gave it many gifts, because there it was shown to him that he would be patriarch.
  • Mari says there was no king to whom he did not write, or whom he did not bring to the faith.
  • Mari says Joshua bar Nun, who studied with him under Abraham and later succeeded him, hated him and wrote against him. When Joshua fell ill, Mari says, he ordered those books burned.
  • Mari says Timothy was no public speaker, but wrote treatises in every field and a book on the stars.

Open Questions

  • How he won the election. Mari ibn Sulayman and Amr tell of sacks filled with stones. Bar Hebraeus tells of sealed bags of copper money, promised to the school.
  • Thomas of Marga, writing about 840, calls it gaining the office "by fraud," though "truth acted with subtlety". He says Timothy talked the favored candidate, Isho'yahbh, into standing aside for a promise of Adiabene. J.-B. Chabot writes that, as is said, he won the metropolitans over by his promises.
  • The year of his death. Elias of Nisibis gives Friday 9 January 823, and so does Chabot's note, though Chabot's index gives 825. Bar Hebraeus gives the Arab year 204, which began in June 819, and Amr the Arab year 205.
  • E. A. Wallis Budge, in his notes to Thomas of Marga, gives the Arab years 204 or 205, which he reckons as 819 to 821. Mari ibn Sulayman says he died in the year al-Ma'mun entered Baghdad, which Mari puts in the Arab year 204.
  • The year of his first synod. Chabot gives 790 and calls the 786 of a Paris copy an error. The editors of Bar Hebraeus give 786 and 804.
  • The caliph of the disputation. 'Abdisho's list names al-Mahdi. Joseph Assemani takes Amr's report of debates with al-Hadi to be the same disputation, with al-Hadi in place of al-Mahdi.
  • The letter to the monks of Mar Maron is known here only from Alphonse Mingana. He says it was not yet printed, and that he read it in a manuscript of his own.
  • Beth Sinaye. Budge glosses it as China. Thomas of Marga learned of its metropolitan David from Timothy's letters, but does not say who ordained him.
  • Why he is named for Baghdad. Chabot and Bar Hebraeus place his enthronement at Seleucia, and Amr ibn Matta at Madaina. Joseph Assemani says the patriarchs then lived at Baghdad, Timothy at the monastery of Kelilisho.
  • An earlier Timothy, bishop of Beth Bagash, signs the synods of the catholicoi Isho'yahb I and Gregory in Oskar Braun's German. He is a different man.
