The Missionary Era · 1815-1880 · Switzerland, France, Netherlands, Germany

The Reveil & Inner-Mission Awakening

Switzerland, France, the Netherlands and Germany, 1815 to 1880 — an awakening that turned personal conversion into hospitals, orphanages and the first Protestant order of nursing sisters.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:08

Read along

After the wars with Napoleon, a wave of fresh, personal faith ran through Protestant churches in Switzerland, France, the Netherlands and Germany. In Geneva it rose against a comfortable rationalist establishment. In the Netherlands it came in the same years as a split from the state church in 1834. Everywhere it held that faith must be felt, not just handed down. What set it apart was what it built. In Hamburg in 1833, Johann Hinrich Wichern opened the Rauhes Haus for street children. It grew into the Inner Mission, a whole web of Christian social work. In 1836 Theodor and Friederike Fliedner opened a house for deaconesses at Kaiserswerth. It trained women as nurses and teachers. In 1851 Florence Nightingale was one of its guests. Within a generation it had built a lasting network of hospitals, schools, orphan homes and training houses.

Documented Stories

‘Love Belongs to Me as Faith Does’

Johann Hinrich Wichern had founded the Rauhes Haus in 1833, a house outside Hamburg where he took in street children and lived with them in families under a brother, and had trained the brothers into lay workers who went out to prisons and the industrial towns. He read the revolutions of 1848 as judgment on a church that had let the poor go. In September the first Kirchentag, an assembly of some five hundred clergy and laymen from the German Protestant churches, met in Wittenberg to discuss a federation. Wichern was not on the list of speakers. On 22 September he asked for the floor and spoke for more than an hour without notes; the shorthand record became the founding text of the Inner Mission: that the masses had fallen away from the church because the church had fallen away from them; that the answer to socialism was not police but the church's own love in action, in schools, hospitals, prisons, the homes of the poor; that this was not charity added to the gospel but the gospel; and ‘Love belongs to me as faith does.’ The assembly appointed a committee, and the Central Committee for the Inner Mission was constituted the following year, with Wichern's memorandum as its program. It coordinated deaconess houses, city missions and orphanages across the German states, and it did so, from the beginning, on the side of the existing order, which was Wichern's conviction and his movement's limit.

Monod Dismissed at Lyon

Adolphe Monod had been touched by the Réveil as a student in Geneva and called in 1827, at twenty-five, to the Reformed church of Lyon, whose consistory was of the rationalist temper the awakening had risen against. He preached the doctrines of grace to congregations that had not heard them in a generation, and drew crowds; the consistory objected. In 1831 he published a sermon, Who Should Communicate?, arguing that the Lord's Supper could not in conscience be administered to those who did not believe in the Lord it proclaimed, and announced that he would not give it to them; since that described several members of the consistory, the consistory asked the government, which under the Concordat appointed and paid Reformed pastors, to remove him. The ministry of worship obliged, and a royal ordinance of March 1832 dismissed him from his post. Monod stayed in Lyon. With the families who followed him he founded an independent Evangelical Church, which met in a hired hall without the state's money, one of the first free churches of the French Réveil. In 1847 he was called to the Oratoire in Paris, where he became the most admired preacher in French Protestantism; the addresses he gave from his deathbed in 1856 to friends gathered on Sunday afternoons were taken down and printed as Les Adieux. The Lyon consistory's minutes and his own account of the affair agree on what happened and differ about which side had left the Reformed faith.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

After the Napoleonic wars, awakened piety across Switzerland, France, the Netherlands, and Germany turned quickly into institutions: Johann Hinrich Wichern's Rauhes Haus for street children in Hamburg, opened in 1833, grew into a whole program of organized Christian social work. At Kaiserswerth in 1836, Theodor and Friederike Fliedner founded a deaconess motherhouse that trained women as nurses, and one of its visitors in 1851 was Florence Nightingale. Wichern also left something almost everyone has met without knowing it — he made the first Advent wreath, for the children in his care, in 1839.

Legacy

The Inner Mission became the German Diakonie, a Protestant social-service network. It is still one of the largest welfare groups in Europe. The deaconess model spread across the Protestant world and helped shape nursing as a profession. In the Netherlands, the secession of 1834 grew into a major strand of Reformed Christianity, and emigrants carried it to North America. How far the awakening shaped that secession is less clear. And Wichern left one thing almost everyone has met without knowing it. He made the first Advent wreath at the Rauhes Haus in 1839, so the children in his care could count the days to Christmas.
