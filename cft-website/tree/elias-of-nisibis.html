The Early Medieval Era · 975-after 1049 · Nisibis, Beth Nuhadra

Nisibis, today in Turkey · Beth Nuhadra

Elias of Nisibis

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Church of the East under the Abbasids.

Known for
The metropolitan of Nisibis in the Church of the East who wrote the Chronography, a year-by-year record that E. W. Brooks dates to 1019. For most years it names its sources, and most of those are lost.
Also called
Elias bar Shinaya, Elias Bar-Sinaeus (Joseph Assemani), Elias Barsinaeus (J. Parisot), Elias Bar Shinaji (George David Malech)
Dates
Born 975, by a note later hands added to his Chronography. E. W. Brooks dates the Chronography to 1019, and M.-A. Kugener to 1008. Joseph Assemani puts his death after 1049, J.-B. Chabot soon after it; Frédéric Macler gives about 1056. The note's years are reckoned from its Greek and Arab years
Place
Nisibis; earlier Beth Nuhadra. He was ordained priest at the monastery of Father Simeon, facing Senn in Brooks's Latin
Role
Metropolitan of Nisibis; chronicler and writer

About This Person

Under the Arab year 397, which began in 1006, Elias of Nisibis recorded a split among Christians. The Christians of the West and of the East did not agree on how to reckon the fast before Easter and the feasts. The West set the start of the fast on 17 Shebat. The East set it on the 24th.

Elias was metropolitan of Nisibis in the Church of the East. He set this down in his Chronography, which E. W. Brooks, who put its first part into Latin, dates to 1019. It runs year by year, and for almost every year it names its sources, Greek, Syriac and Arabic works, most of them now lost. For its last years it names none. There, Brooks writes, Elias tells what he remembered or had heard.

A note added after Elias's own entries tells his life, and Brooks marks it as the work of later hands. It says he was born in 975. John, bishop of Senn, ordained him priest in 994 at the monastery of Father Simeon. John later became catholicos, the head of the Church of the East. He made Elias bishop of Beth Nuhadra in 1002, and metropolitan of Nisibis in 1008.

In 1020 Isho'yahb son of Ezekiel was made catholicos. Bar Hebraeus, a later Syriac historian, says Isho'yahb paid the governor five thousand denarii. The governor ordered that all who would not consent be drowned, and many bishops refused to proclaim him. Joseph Assemani, writing in 1725, says Elias stood firm against him. In a letter to the people of Baghdad, Assemani says, Elias called Isho'yahb a buyer of his office.

Elias gathered rules on inheritance from a work of the catholicos Elias I. Ebedjesu of Soba, which is Nisibis, copied them into his book of canons. In his own preface to those rules, Elias prays for the soul of Elias I, who died in 1049. Assemani puts the metropolitan's death after that. J.-B. Chabot says he died soon after. Frédéric Macler gives about 1056.

The second part of the Chronography, on reckoning years and feasts, opens with a prayer for its reader, his beloved brother. May the Lord guide you, Elias wrote, to please him in your thoughts, words and deeds.

Key Works

  • Chronography: in two parts. The first holds lists of patriarchs and kings, a short history of the catholicoi, and year-by-year tables, most years naming their sources. The second sets out how peoples reckon their years and feasts
  • Grammar: a Syriac grammar in ten headings, on letters, points and sounds. Assemani says a Syriac-Arabic word list by Elias follows it
  • Four books on judging church cases: named in the catalog of Ebedjesu of Soba, as Assemani prints it, and by J.-B. Chabot. Ebedjesu copied Elias's preface, and the rules on inheritance he gathered from the catholicos Elias I, into his collection of canons, held in Angelo Mai's Latin

Legacy

What traces to them

  • E. W. Brooks lists the works the Chronography cites, Greek, Syriac and Arabic, and says most of them are lost.
  • Passages of Jacob of Edessa's chronicle, which E. W. Brooks calls partly lost, survive in his quotations. The Chronica Minora prints them as Jacob's testimonies quoted by Elias.
  • J. Parisot notes that a British Museum manuscript gives the Chronography in Syriac and Arabic.

How they're remembered

  • Joseph Assemani writes that he died having won great fame for learning among his people.
  • Ebedjesu of Soba calls him a most exact and sharp-minded man, who told all lovers of truth to follow the catholicos Elias I's treatise on inheritance alone.
  • George David Malech lists him, as Elias Bar Shinaji, among the Syrian forebears whose writings trace their nation from Shem.
  • J. B. Abbeloos and T. J. Lamy, editing Bar Hebraeus's church history, often cite him on dates.

What Is Uncertain

  • His life is told in a note that later hands added to his Chronography, and in Assemani, who draws one detail from Elias's own words.
  • When he wrote the Chronography. E. W. Brooks dates it to 1019. M.-A. Kugener dates it to 1008, and says the British Museum copy was made in Elias's own time.
  • When he died. In a preface on inheritance, Elias prays for the soul of the catholicos Elias I, who died in 1049. Assemani puts his death after that, and Chabot soon after. Macler gives about 1056.
  • The Isho'yahb affair. Assemani says Elias opposed Isho'yahb son of Ezekiel and called him a simoniac. The letter itself is not among the sources used here. Angelo Mai describes a Vatican manuscript that holds it.
  • Peace with Elias I. Mari ibn Sulayman says Elias I kept writing to the metropolitan of Nisibis until they met at Hazza and made peace. Mari does not name the metropolitan.
  • Why he wrote the second part. J.-B. Chabot thinks it not unlikely that al-Biruni's book on reckoning moved him to write. Chabot adds that Elias seems to have taken nothing from it.
  • Where he was a monk. Assemani, citing Elias's own words, says he was a monk at Saint Michael's near Mosul. The note says only that he was ordained priest at the monastery of Father Simeon.
