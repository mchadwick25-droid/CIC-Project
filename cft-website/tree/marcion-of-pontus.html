Marcion of Pontus

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Marcion / Marcionism.

Known for
A teacher at Rome who held that the God of the law was not the Father of Jesus, and whose own churches spread widely.
Also called

Role

About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Once, Marcion met Polycarp, the bishop of Smyrna, and asked him, "Do you know me?" Polycarp answered, "I do know you: the firstborn of Satan." Irenaeus, who had seen Polycarp in his early youth, tells the story, and it shows how hard the church set itself against this man.

In the middle of the second century, in the church at Rome, a shipmaster from Pontus, on the Black Sea, gave money in the warmth of new faith. Everything we know of Marcion comes from the men who wrote against him.

Tertullian says Marcion first believed as the Roman church did, until he began to brood over where evil comes from. Jesus taught that a good tree cannot bear bad fruit, yet in Isaiah God says he creates evil. So, in Tertullian's account, Marcion judged the Creator a bad tree and found another, good God in Christ, although his real reasons remain uncertain.

Justin, writing to the emperor while Marcion lived, said: "Even now he is still teaching the people he has won over. He teaches them to believe in some other god, greater than the Creator."

His lost book, the Antitheses, set the gospel against the law of Moses. In Tertullian's words, "Separating the law from the gospel is Marcion's own work, and his chief one." His opponents say he shortened Luke's Gospel and Paul's letters. Marcion claimed the opposite, Tertullian reports: the defenders of Judaism had added to the Gospel, and he was restoring it.

Tertullian says Rome expelled Marcion more than once, together with another teacher, Valentinus. The money Marcion had given in his first faith was cast out with him.

The church answered him in book after book, arguing that one God made the world and sent Jesus. Irenaeus defended four Gospels against him, Tertullian wrote five books, and Polycarp, Irenaeus says, turned many back at Rome. Yet Justin saw people of every nation follow Marcion, who founded churches of his own, the churches of the Marcionites. Tertullian wrote that just as wasps make combs, so these Marcionites make churches. Those churches also claimed martyrs of their own. Two centuries later, Epiphanius still found Marcionites in Rome and Italy, in Egypt, Syria and Cyprus, as far away as Persia, and elsewhere.

Key Works

  • Antitheses: set the law against the gospel. It is lost and known through Tertullian of Carthage's answer

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Justin Martyr says many "of every nation" followed him. Tertullian of Carthage says the Marcionites built churches of their own.

How they're remembered

  • Irenaeus of Lyon tells that Polycarp of Smyrna called him "the first-born of Satan."
  • Tertullian wrote five books Against Marcion, arguing from Marcion's own Gospel and Antitheses.

What Is Uncertain

  • Almost everything known of him comes from his opponents: Justin Martyr, Irenaeus of Lyon, Tertullian of Carthage and Epiphanius of Salamis. None of his own books survives.
  • The teaching rests on more than one of them. Justin, writing while Marcion lived, and Tertullian, who read Marcion's own books, agree that he set another god above the Creator.
  • Several life details rest on Tertullian alone: the shipmaster, the money cast out with him, and the late offer to repent.
  • Tertullian places Marcion at Rome under Bishop Eleutherus. Irenaeus has him flourish under Anicetus, an earlier bishop.
  • That he was a bishop's son put out for violating a young woman is told by the book Against All Heresies printed with Tertullian's works, and at more length by Epiphanius. Karl Holl, who edited Epiphanius's Greek, thought Tertullian's silence spoke against the story.
  • Justin Martyr and Irenaeus of Lyon say he came from Pontus. Only Epiphanius, two centuries later, names the city of Sinope; Holl notes that the one other early writer to name it copied him.
