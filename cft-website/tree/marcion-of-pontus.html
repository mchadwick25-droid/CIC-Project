Marcion of Pontus

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Marcion / Marcionism.

About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

"There is a certain Marcion, from Pontus. Even now he is still teaching the people he has won over. He teaches them to believe in some other god, greater than the Creator." Justin, a Christian opponent, wrote this to the emperor while Marcion lived, in the middle of the second century.

At Rome, Marcion taught that the God of the law of Moses and the prophets, who made the world, was not the Father of Jesus. He kept a Scripture of his own, one Gospel and Paul's letters, and he founded churches of his own, which spread far. Against him, writer after writer argued that the God who made the world is the Father of Jesus. Everything we know of him comes from the men who opposed him.

"Separating the law from the gospel is Marcion's own work, and his chief one," Tertullian writes of his book, the Antitheses, now lost. His opponents say he cut his Scripture down from Luke's Gospel and Paul's letters. He claimed, Tertullian reports, that "the defenders of Judaism" had added to the Gospel and he was restoring it.

Tertullian calls him a shipmaster from Pontus, on the Black Sea. At first, he says, Marcion believed as the church at Rome did, and gave it money, two hundred sesterces, in the first warmth of his faith. Then Rome expelled him more than once, Tertullian says, for a restless curiosity that spread to others. He says this of Valentinus too, who taught at Rome with him. Marcion's gift, Tertullian says, was cast out with him.

Still his churches spread. Justin says that, with the demons' help, he had led many "in every race of people". Tertullian says with scorn that the Marcionites make churches as wasps make combs. Two centuries later, Epiphanius still found them from Rome and Italy through Egypt, Syria and Cyprus, and even in Persia.

The answers kept coming, from Justin in Marcion's lifetime to Tertullian some sixty years later. In a book that Irenaeus quotes, Justin said he would not have believed the Lord Himself if He had announced any God but our maker. Tertullian's own Against Marcion ran to five books. Irenaeus argued that the Gospel is fourfold, told in four books, and he named Marcion among those who make it fewer.

Irenaeus tells, too, of a single meeting between Marcion and Polycarp, the bishop of Smyrna. Marcion asked, "Do you know me?" Polycarp answered, "I do know you: the firstborn of Satan."

Key Works

  • Antitheses: set the law against the gospel. It is lost and known through Tertullian of Carthage's answer

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Justin Martyr says many "of every nation" followed him. Tertullian of Carthage says the Marcionites built churches of their own.

How they're remembered

  • Irenaeus of Lyon tells that Polycarp of Smyrna called him "the first-born of Satan."
  • Tertullian wrote five books Against Marcion, arguing from Marcion's own Gospel and Antitheses.

What Is Uncertain

  • Almost everything known of him comes from his opponents: Justin Martyr, Irenaeus of Lyon, Tertullian of Carthage and Epiphanius of Salamis. None of his own books survives.
  • The teaching rests on more than one of them. Justin, writing while Marcion lived, and Tertullian, who read Marcion's own books, agree that he set another god above the Creator.
  • Several life details rest on Tertullian alone: the shipmaster, the money cast out with him, and the late offer to repent.
  • Tertullian places Marcion at Rome under Bishop Eleutherus. Irenaeus has him flourish under Anicetus, an earlier bishop.
  • That he was a bishop's son put out for violating a young woman is told by the book Against All Heresies printed with Tertullian's works, and at more length by Epiphanius. Karl Holl, who edited Epiphanius's Greek, thought Tertullian's silence spoke against the story.
  • Justin Martyr and Irenaeus of Lyon say he came from Pontus. Only Epiphanius, two centuries later, names the city of Sinope; Holl notes that the one other early writer to name it copied him.
