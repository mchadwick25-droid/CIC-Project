The Early Church Era · 3rd-7th c. · Persia to the Mediterranean

Manichaeism

From Persia to the Mediterranean and on to China, from 216 CE into the centuries beyond — a prophet who called himself the last of God's messengers, and whose lay followers' prayer of confession was kept in a cave library near Dunhuang.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 5:56

Read along

On 28 August 392, at Hippo in Roman Africa, a Manichaean priest named Fortunatus debated Augustine, then a priest of the city's church. Augustine had once been one of them, and Fortunatus called on him as witness to how they lived: "You were at our prayer." Augustine answered that he had been only a Hearer, never one of the Elect. At their prayer he had seen nothing shameful, only that they prayed facing the sun, which went against the faith he later learned.

They were the followers of Mani, a prophet of Babylonia. From the 200s they held that Light and Darkness were two powers without beginning, now mixed in the world, and that in the end the two would be parted again. The Elect lived in strict purity and poverty, while the Hearers lived in the world and fed and served them. Hunted by Persian kings and Roman emperors, they spread from Mesopotamia west to Roman Africa and east to the Turks and China.

Mani was born in 216 in Babylonia, in Mesopotamia. He wrote a book for King Shapur of Persia, and Biruni, a Muslim scholar of about 1000, quotes how it begins. Mani says God's messengers brought wisdom and deeds to people from age to age: the Buddha to India, Zoroaster to Persia, Jesus to the West. Now, in this last age, the revelation had come down through Mani himself, God's messenger to Babylonia. He said he was the Paraclete, the Helper Jesus had promised, and he wrote his own scriptures.

His followers held that they must harm no animal, fire, water or plant. The Elect kept from marriage, owned only a day's food and a year's clothing, and went about preaching. The Hearers gave a tenth of what they had, fasted and married one wife. The Hearers' own confession, a prayer for forgiveness, says the Light was in the food they ate, and that their sins sent it "to the Bad Place." Augustine mocks this. Eating the Hearers' food, he says, the Elect freed bits of God inside it. Their stomachs made it into "angels and gods", who would free the Hearers.

Biruni says King Bahram had Mani hunted down, and gives the king's reason: "This man has come forward calling people to destroy the world." Bahram had him killed, but the accounts differ on how. Some say he was flayed and his skin hung at the gate of the city of Gundeshapur. A book by one of his pupils, which Biruni reports, says he failed to cure a relative of the king and was chained in prison until he died.

His followers kept the day he died as their great feast, the Bema. Augustine says he asked why they honored it far above Easter. They told him the day to keep was the death of one who had truly suffered, since Christ, they held, was never born in real flesh and only seemed to suffer. After Mani's death, a Baghdad scholar of the 900s says, the king killed his followers wherever he found them, and they fled east into the lands of the Turks.

In 372 the Roman emperors ordered that wherever their meetings were found, the teachers be heavily fined, the members shut out of society and the houses seized, for teaching what the law called a profane doctrine. Faustus of Milevis, a bishop of theirs, wrote a book answering their opponents' questions on the gospel, and Augustine, a Hearer for nine years, kept his words, quoting each part before answering. Faustus wrote: "If you press me whether I accept the gospel, I may answer: I have left all that was mine. Father, mother, wife, children, gold, silver, eating, drinking, comforts, pleasures. Take this as answer enough to your questions. And believe that you will be blessed, if you do not stumble because of me."

Christians brought Faustus before the governor's court, and, found to be a Manichaean, he was exiled to an island. Augustine calls this the lightest of penalties, since those same Christians pleaded for him.

In 732 a Chinese emperor's order called Mani's teaching a perverse belief that falsely took the name of Buddhism and deceived the people, and forbade it, though the foreigners from the West, whose own religion it was, might keep it themselves. A later emperor's letter says China let the faith grow because the Uyghurs, a Turkish people, revered it. In 843, with the Uyghurs defeated, an edict closed the faith's temples. A Chinese chronicle written four centuries later says some seventy of their women religious at the capital all died.

Near the oasis of Dunhuang, a cave library kept a scroll written in Mani's script, in a Turkish tongue. It is the Hearers' confession, and one of its articles begins: "Every day, how many evil thoughts we think! How many wretched words we speak that should not be spoken! How many deeds we do that should not be done!" Each article ends the same way: my God, cleansing ourselves of sin, we pray, forgive my sin.

People and Stories

  • Augustine of Hippo — a Hearer for about nine years, then the movement's Latin opponent, and an unintended preserver of its arguments

Documented Stories

Nine Years Waiting for Faustus

Augustine had been a Manichaean Hearer for about nine years — attending the assemblies, supporting the Elect, following the prayers and fasts of an ordinary lay adherent — and for much of that time the local teachers had told him that his questions about astronomy, and about how the Manichaean books squared with what the philosophers had measured, would be answered when Faustus came. Faustus of Milevis was the movement’s most admired bishop in Africa. When he finally arrived in Carthage, Augustine found him charming, fluent and pleasant to listen to, ‘a great snare of the devil’ as he wrote long afterwards; and when he got him alone with the questions, Faustus declined to pretend. He said he did not know these things. Augustine records that this candor made him like the man more, not less — ‘he knew that he did not know, and was not ashamed to confess it’ — and that Faustus, discovering Augustine’s literary training, asked to read the Latin authors with him instead. The two spent time together over books. But the questions were not answered, and Augustine dates the loosening of his attachment to the sect from those conversations. Years later, as a bishop himself, he wrote thirty-three books against Faustus’ own work. The picture the Confessions gives, though, is of a lay religious community whose leader would rather admit ignorance than bluff, and a young man who respected him for it even as he left.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Mani was born in Mesopotamia in 216 and raised by a sect of baptizers. He broke from them after what he described as revelations from an angel he called the Companion. He went on to teach that the world is a battleground between eternal Light and Darkness, where sparks of light lie trapped in matter. He wrote his own scriptures, and his faith spread from Persia all the way to China. Augustine of Hippo followed it as a Hearer for nine years before his conversion.

Legacy

In the West, emperors and the church hunted it down. It had largely vanished by the sixth century. Yet the word 'Manichaean' survived for centuries afterwards. It became an insult aimed at anyone thought too fond of dualism, the belief that two opposed powers divide the world. In the East it lasted far longer. Many Uyghur Turks of Central Asia followed it, and it held on in southern China for many centuries. Mani wrote his own books. Over the last century, manuscripts have also been recovered from Turfan in western China and from Medinet Madi in Egypt. There is also the tiny Cologne Mani Codex. Because of all this, it can now be read largely in its own words. It is no longer known only through its enemies.

The old record also shows how long they lasted in the East. In the 900s, the Fihrist says, some five hundred of them gathered at Samarkand. When the ruler of Khorasan wished to kill them, the ruler of China, as the Fihrist calls him, swore that he would kill all the Muslims in his own lands if one of them were killed, and they were left alone and taxed. The Fihrist's author had known about three hundred of them in Baghdad, but in his own day barely five were left there. About the year 1000 Biruni wrote that most of the eastern Turks, and many of the people of China and Tibet, still held Mani's law. In the West their opponents went on answering and hunting them: Ephrem the Syrian wrote against Mani in Syriac, and at Rome in 443 Bishop Leo had many of them found where they hid in the city, exposed before the whole church, and their books burned.

Experience It Today

  • Cao'an, Jinjiang, Fujian, China — long regarded as the only intact surviving Manichaean temple, with its carved image of Mani, inscribed as part of the Quanzhou UNESCO World Heritage site in 2021
