Leo of Rome

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Imperial and Juridical Christianity.

Also called Leo the Great, Leo Magnus

Bishop of Rome whose letter to Flavian of Constantinople, the Tome, set out the two natures of Christ and was received at the Council of Chalcedon in 451.

About This Person

Audio Brief 4:34

Read along

In October 451, at Chalcedon, across the water from Constantinople, Greek-speaking bishops heard a letter read aloud. Its writer, Leo, bishop of Rome, had not come. The letter said: "To be hungry, to be thirsty, to grow tired and to sleep is plainly human." When it ended, the minutes say, the bishops cried out that Peter had spoken through Leo.

That letter is called the Tome. Leo of Rome wrote it in 449 to say that Christ is one person, fully God and fully human, and the council's own statement of faith followed it. Yet Leo also held that Peter still spoke and ruled through the bishop of Rome, and he refused the council's rule that ranked Constantinople next to Rome. This was the age of Church and Empire.

Leo was a deacon, away in Gaul, when he was chosen bishop of Rome in 440. In his first years he had the Manichaeans, followers of a sect that taught good and evil were two rival powers, hunted out and exposed before the church. Prosper of Aquitaine, who wrote in those years and praises Leo throughout, writes with approval that their books were burned.

In the East, the monk Eutyches taught that after God became man there was one nature only. A nature is what Christ is; a person is who he is. A meeting of bishops under Flavian, bishop of Constantinople, condemned him. In June 449, in the Tome, Leo told Flavian that each nature stays wholly itself in one person. He wrote in Latin and, his English editor thinks, knew no Greek.

The emperor then called a council at Ephesus, with Dioscorus of Alexandria presiding. Leo sent envoys and his letter. By that editor's account, with soldiers and monks present, the meeting turned riotous, and the letter was never read. Eutyches was restored and Flavian deposed, and Flavian, that editor says, died soon after from rough treatment. Prosper says many bishops were forced by violence and fear to agree. Leo begged the emperor, with groans and tears, for a new council in Italy.

After the emperor died, his successors called one in the East. In July 451 Leo accepted it and sent his envoys. To the empress he wrote: "Nor need a letter set down everything that could be committed at Ephesus, in what was not a trial but a robbery." His envoys presided. Some bishops thought the Tome stressed the two natures too far, and it was adopted only at the fourth session, days later.

The council also ranked Constantinople, the new imperial city, next after Rome, because the fathers had honored old Rome as the imperial city. In May 452 Leo wrote to the emperor: "Yet worldly things stand on one footing, and the things of God on another. No building will stand firm except on the rock the Lord laid as its foundation." That rock is the one on which, in the Gospel, Christ said he would build his church. To the empress he wrote that, by the authority of Peter, he declared the vote void. He broke off contact with the bishop of Constantinople until that bishop wrote to make peace. The rule stood.

That year, Attila, king of the Huns, was in northern Italy. Leo went to him with two high Roman officials. Prosper says Leo trusted in God's help, and that the king, pleased by the bishop's presence, stopped the war and went home. A historian of the Goths, writing a century later, says the Huns already feared the fate of the Gothic king who sacked Rome. When Leo came, Attila put aside his fury. A chronicler in Spain never mentions Leo and names famine and disease sent from heaven, and an army sent by the eastern emperor.

The faith of the Tome held in the Greek and Latin churches, and the churches of Egypt and Syria refused it. The editors of the councils write that Rome gave way on Constantinople's rank only centuries later.

Leo died late in 461, after twenty-one years as bishop. He was buried in the church of St. Peter.

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “To be hungry, to be thirsty, to grow tired and to sleep is plainly human.”
    As printed
    “To be hungry and thirsty, to be weary, and to sleep, is clearly human”
    Leo, Letter 28, the Tome, to Flavian, tr. Charles Lett Feltoe
    The Latin stands in Hefele's print, and a damaged scan of Mansi gives the same words. Plainly renders evidenter, which Feltoe gives as clearly, and grow tired renders lassescere, which Feltoe gives as be weary.
  • In our modern English
    “Nor need a letter set down everything that could be committed at Ephesus, in what was not a trial but a robbery.”
    As printed
    “nor is the need to put down in the pages of a letter all that was allowed to be perpetrated in that meeting, not of judges but of robbers, at Ephesus”
    Leo, Letter 95 to the empress Pulcheria, 20 July 451, tr. Charles Lett Feltoe
    The Latin is held only in a damaged scan of Mansi, where the long s is read as f, and no other print of it is held, so the English stands alone. The brief gives its own rendering of Leo's Latin; Feltoe's English reads not of judges but of robbers, as in the key works above.
  • In our modern English
    “Yet worldly things stand on one footing, and the things of God on another. No building will stand firm except on the rock the Lord laid as its foundation.”
    As printed
    “Yet things secular stand on a different basis from things divine: and there can be no sure building save on that rock which the Lord has laid for a foundation.”
    Leo, Letter 104 to the emperor Marcian, 22 May 452, tr. Charles Lett Feltoe
    The Latin is held only in faulty scans, in Mansi and in the Acta Sanctorum, neither of which prints the word for worldly cleanly, so the English stands alone. The brief gives its own rendering of Leo's Latin: worldly things for things secular, and one footing and another for a different basis.

Key Works

  • Letter 28, the Tome, to Flavian of Constantinople: his answer of 449 to the case of Eutyches, read at Chalcedon in 451, on the two natures in the one person of Christ
  • Letters: letters to bishops, emperors and the empress Pulcheria, among them Letter 95, which calls the Ephesus meeting of 449 one "not of judges but of robbers"
  • Sermons: sermons to the people of Rome on the feasts of the year and on the anniversaries of his ordination

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • At Chalcedon in 451, after the reading of the Tome, the bishops cried, "Peter has spoken thus through Leo." Feltoe says the fourth session adopted it.
  • Prosper of Aquitaine closes his entry on Chalcedon by saying the faith confirmed there was the one "per sanctum papam Leonem praedicabatur", preached by the holy pope Leo.
  • Feltoe says Leo saw his office as carrying an authority that "extended over the whole of Christendom as the successor of S. Peter", and calls the Eutychian quarrel the one "on which Leo’s chief claim to our thanks and praise rests".
  • In his sermon on the anniversary of his consecration, Leo told his people that Peter was recognized and honored in his humble person, in whom abides the care of all the shepherds, and that Peter's dignity was not abated even in so unworthy an heir.
  • Feltoe says Leo cut off all communication with Anatolius, the bishop of Constantinople, over the canon, and that Anatolius at last yielded, though the disputed canon was never annulled. The editors of the councils say Leo never gave over his opposition, although the breach was made up between him and Anatolius by an apparently insincere letter on the part of Anatolius.
  • The editors of the councils say that Leo himself confesses, in a letter to the empress Pulcheria about a year later, that the Illyrian bishops had since the council subscribed the canon. They say that the opposition of Rome gave way after seven centuries and a half, when at Florence the second rank was given to the Greek patriarch of Constantinople, and that the Fourth Council of the Lateran in 1215 had acknowledged Constantinople's rank as immediately after Rome while Constantinople was in the hands of the Latins.
  • Feltoe, writing in 1895, gives him as saint and confessor from the earliest times and doctor of the church since 1754, commemorated in the East on 18 February and in the West on 11 April.

How they're remembered

  • Charles Gore, quoted by Feltoe, on the Tome: "a Latin bishop, ignorant of Greek, defining the faith for Greek-speaking bishops".
  • Feltoe calls him "the first great Latin-speaking pope and bishop of Rome", and says the Church owes to his part in the Eutychian quarrel "the final and complete definition of the cardinal doctrine of the Incarnation".
  • Prosper of Aquitaine, in his chronicle, calls him "beatissimus papa Leo", the most blessed pope Leo, when he goes to meet Attila.
  • Nestorius, whose own teaching had been condemned at Ephesus, wrote in exile that God appointed Leo, who used to preach the truth undaunted, as a preacher guiltless of suspicion.
  • A Syriac chronicle from the churches that refused the Council of Chalcedon says Theodoret of Cyrrhus went up to Leo and, with the gift which blinds the eyes of the soul, got the better of him, whereupon Leo composed the letter called the Tome. The chronicle is hostile, and its order of events does not fit the date of the letter, which Feltoe puts in June 449.
  • Feltoe quotes Cassian, whom Leo had urged to write on the Incarnation, as calling him "the ornament of the Roman church and of the Divine ministry".
  • Hydatius, a chronicler in Spain, records Leo as bishop of Rome and notes his writings, and Flavian's letters to Leo with Leo's answers.

Open Questions

  • The meetings with Attila in 452 and with Geiseric in 455 are told by Prosper of Aquitaine, who writes of Leo with reverence and credits God's help. Feltoe says Leo himself makes "none but the slightest indirect allusion" to either. He sees "no reason, however, to doubt the substantial truth" of what Prosper and others relate. He adds that Leo's arguments were "aided, it is thought, by rumours of threatened invasion at home". The place of the meeting with Attila, Lake Benacus, is Feltoe's and is not in Prosper's entry.
  • His birthplace is uncertain. Feltoe reports a tradition that his father was a Tuscan named Quintian and that Volaterrae was his birthplace. Prosper and Leo both call Rome his home, and Feltoe says no city "can claim the honour with certainty".
  • Feltoe reports that "it is thought" the acolyte Leo whom Augustine of Hippo mentions in the Pelagian quarrel is the future pope.
  • Prosper counts him the forty-third bishop of Rome. Feltoe calls him the forty-seventh.
  • What happened at Ephesus in 449 is told here as Feltoe tells it. Leo, who was not there, calls it a meeting of robbers. The acts of that council, held in Syriac, are not among the sources used here.
  • The words of Eutyches are given as Leo quotes them in the Tome, from the record of the synod at Constantinople.
  • Prosper dates his entries by the consuls of each year. The years 440, 452 and 455 are given by Theodor Mommsen's edition and by Feltoe.
  • The election. Prosper says Rome waited more than forty days for the deacon Leo, whom peacemaking between the generals Aetius and Albinus kept in Gaul, and that he was summoned by a public embassy and then ordained. Feltoe says the clergy and people elected him in his absence, a deputation was sent to hasten his return, and he arrived after forty days. The Book of the Popes, a later Roman compilation, calls him by nationality a Tuscan, son of Quintianus, and says he held the see twenty-one years, one month and thirteen days.
  • That he knew no Greek is Feltoe's inference, from Leo's self-confessed ignorance of Greek, that his education was limited to Christian and Latin culture.
  • The Manichaeans. Prosper sets the discovery of the Manichaeans in Rome under 443. He says they were brought out of hiding, exposed before the whole church and made to condemn and betray their teachings, and that their books were burned. Feltoe sets it in 444, with a trial before a combined bench of civil and church judges, and banishment for life for those who would not recant, by the emperor Valentinian's decree. The heading of Letter 8 says the emperor's ordinance turned the Manichaeans out of the army and the city and took away their rights as citizens.
  • The quarrel with Hilary of Arles. Feltoe says Hilary deposed a bishop, Celidonius, in a provincial synod, that Celidonius appealed to Rome, and that Leo gave orders that Celidonius was to be restored and Hilary deprived of all his metropolitical rights in the province of Vienne.
  • Who wrote Leo's letters. A later writer, Gennadius, says Leo's letters against Eutyches are thought to have been dictated by Prosper. In Mommsen's text of Gennadius on Prosper, the words counselor of pope Leo stand in square brackets.
  • The Tome. Leo writes that it is not part of the same nature to be moved to tears of pity for a dead friend and, when the stone that closed the four-days' grave was removed, to raise that same friend to life with a voice of command.
  • Ephesus in 449. Prosper says many bishops were forced by violence and fear to consent, and that the deputies of Leo protested; one recension names the deacon Hilary. Leo's own letter says his deacon Hilary escaped with great difficulty, lest he should be compelled by force to subscribe. Feltoe says Hilary protested with a single word and escaped. Feltoe says the end of Flavian is variously recorded, and that the most accurate version has him beaten, thrown into prison, driven into exile and dead within a few days. Feltoe says Leo afterwards called the meeting brigandage and that it is generally known as the Robber council; the letter that calls it a robbery is dated 20 July 451, two years after the meeting.
  • After Ephesus. Leo and a Roman synod asked the emperor, with groans and tears, to order a general synod in Italy. Feltoe says that in the spring of 450 Dioscorus and ten bishops joined in excommunicating the bishop of Rome, that the emperor Theodosius was killed by a fall from his horse before the legates arrived, and that his sister Pulcheria succeeded him and shared the eastern empire with Marcian. A new council was granted, but in the East and not in Italy.
  • The canon. Feltoe says Leo's appeal to Nicaea rested on a canon that belongs to the council of Sardica, a confusion between the Nicene canons and those of Sardica that both Quesnel and the Ballerini note, and that he calls unpardonable. The editors of the councils say that Quesnel holds Leo rejected the canon because it encroached on the prerogatives of his own see, and the Ballerini that he acted only out of zeal for the Nicene decree. In his letter to the emperor Marcian, Leo wrote that Anatolius should not disdain a city which is royal, though he cannot make it an Apostolic See. In his letter to the empress Pulcheria of 22 May 452 he wrote that he did not recognize the bishops' assents and by the authority of the blessed Apostle Peter absolutely dis-annulled them.
  • The meeting with Attila, in full. Prosper says the emperor, the senate and the Roman people judged that peace should be sought through envoys, and that Leo undertook the task with Avienus, a former consul, and Trygetius, a former prefect, trusting in God's help. Jordanes, writing in 551 and drawing on the historian Priscus, who wrote in Attila's time, says Attila's followers took him away from Rome because they remembered that Alaric did not live long after the sack of Rome. He says an embassy came from Rome and that Pope Leo himself came to meet him at the ford of the river Mincius in the Ambuleian district of the Veneti, where Attila put aside his usual fury and departed with the promise of peace. Jordanes adds that Attila declared with threats that he would bring worse things upon Italy unless Honoria, the sister of the emperor Valentinian, were sent to him. Hydatius, a chronicler in Spain writing in the same years, says the Huns in Italy were struck by heaven with famine and some disease, and cut down by forces the emperor Marcian sent under Aetius; they made peace and went home, and Hydatius does not mention Leo. Cassiodorus says Leo was sent by the emperor Valentinian. The Book of the Popes says Leo undertook an embassy and delivered all Italy from the peril of the enemy, and Victor of Tunnuna says Attila, after receiving Leo's embassy, stopped devastating the empire and withdrew beyond the Danube. Prosper names no place, Jordanes names the ford of the Mincius, and Feltoe names Lake Benacus.
  • The Vandals in 455. Prosper says that when Geiseric took Rome Leo met him outside the gates, and that his plea, God acting, so softened the king that, though everything was in his power, he held back from fire, slaughter and torture; but Rome was stripped of its wealth for fourteen days and many thousands of captives were taken to Carthage. Feltoe says there is some doubt about the occasion of Leo's Sermon 84, which seems to have been connected with the yearly commemoration of that pillage. The sermon asks who restored the city to safety, "the games of the circus-goers or the care of the saints".
