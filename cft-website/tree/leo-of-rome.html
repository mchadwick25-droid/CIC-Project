Leo of Rome

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Imperial and Juridical Christianity.

Known for
Bishop of Rome whose letter to Flavian of Constantinople, the Tome, set out the two natures of Christ and was received at the Council of Chalcedon in 451.
Also called
Leo the Great, Leo Magnus
Dates
Made bishop of Rome in 440, on 29 September by Charles Lett Feltoe's account; died late in 461, by Feltoe, after twenty-one years as bishop. Feltoe thinks it probable that he was born between 390 and 400
Place
Rome; Gaul, on an embassy; northern Italy, on the embassy to Attila
Role
Deacon, then bishop of Rome

About This Person

In 452 Attila and his Huns came into Italy. Prosper of Aquitaine says in his chronicle that Rome sent its bishop, Leo, to ask for peace. Prosper writes of Leo with reverence. He says the king was so glad of the bishop's presence that he ordered an end to the war. Attila went back beyond the Danube. Charles Lett Feltoe, who translated Leo's letters and sermons, sees no reason to doubt the story. He notes that rumours of trouble at home may also have moved Attila, and that Leo hardly mentions it.

Feltoe says nothing is known of Leo's youth. In 440, by Feltoe's date, Xystus, bishop of Rome, died. Prosper says Rome's church waited more than forty days for its deacon Leo. He was in Gaul, making peace between two generals. On his return he was made bishop of Rome. On an anniversary of that day, he preached that the apostle Peter "has not abandoned the helm of the Church."

In 448 a synod at Constantinople under its bishop, Flavian, condemned Eutyches, the head of a monastery. As Leo quotes him, Eutyches had said, "I confess that our Lord was of two natures before the union, but after the union I confess one nature." The next year Leo answered Flavian in a letter known as the Tome. Christ, he wrote, was born "whole in what was his, whole in what was ours." Each nature, he said, keeps what is its own. Charles Gore, whom Feltoe quotes, saw in it "a Latin bishop, ignorant of Greek, defining the faith for Greek-speaking bishops."

At a council in Ephesus in 449, Feltoe says, under Dioscorus of Alexandria, the Tome was never read. Eutyches was restored and Flavian deposed. Leo, writing to the empress Pulcheria, called it a meeting "not of judges but of robbers."

Two years later, at Chalcedon, the Tome was read. The bishops cried, "Peter has spoken thus through Leo." Feltoe adds that Leo would not accept the council's twenty-eighth canon, which gave Constantinople second place after Rome.

In 455 the Vandal king Geiseric took Rome. Prosper says Leo met him outside the gates, and God used his plea to soften the king. The city was spared fire and slaughter, but it was stripped of its wealth for fourteen days. Leo died late in 461, Feltoe says, after twenty-one years as bishop.

In a Christmas sermon he told his people, "Christian, acknowledge thy dignity."

Key Works

  • Letter 28, the Tome, to Flavian of Constantinople: his answer of 449 to the case of Eutyches, read at Chalcedon in 451, on the two natures in the one person of Christ
  • Letters: letters to bishops, emperors and the empress Pulcheria, among them Letter 95, which calls the Ephesus meeting of 449 one "not of judges but of robbers"
  • Sermons: sermons to the people of Rome on the feasts of the year and on the anniversaries of his ordination

Legacy

What traces to them

  • At Chalcedon in 451, after the reading of the Tome, the bishops cried, "Peter has spoken thus through Leo." Feltoe says the fourth session adopted it.
  • Prosper of Aquitaine closes his entry on Chalcedon by saying the faith confirmed there was the one "per sanctum papam Leonem praedicabatur", preached by the holy pope Leo.
  • Feltoe says Leo saw his office as carrying an authority that "extended over the whole of Christendom as the successor of S. Peter", and calls the Eutychian quarrel the one "on which Leo’s chief claim to our thanks and praise rests".

How they're remembered

  • Charles Gore, quoted by Feltoe, on the Tome: "a Latin bishop, ignorant of Greek, defining the faith for Greek-speaking bishops".
  • Feltoe calls him "the first great Latin-speaking pope and bishop of Rome", and says the Church owes to his part in the Eutychian quarrel "the final and complete definition of the cardinal doctrine of the Incarnation".
  • Prosper of Aquitaine, in his chronicle, calls him "beatissimus papa Leo", the most blessed pope Leo, when he goes to meet Attila.

At Major Councils

  • Council of Chalcedon, 451: Not present; sent the legates and the Tome; rejected canon 28

What Is Uncertain

  • The meetings with Attila in 452 and with Geiseric in 455 are told by Prosper of Aquitaine, who writes of Leo with reverence and credits God's help. Feltoe says Leo himself makes "none but the slightest indirect allusion" to either. He sees "no reason, however, to doubt the substantial truth" of what Prosper and others relate. He adds that Leo's arguments were "aided, it is thought, by rumours of threatened invasion at home". The place of the meeting with Attila, Lake Benacus, is Feltoe's and is not in Prosper's entry.
  • His birthplace is uncertain. Feltoe reports a tradition that his father was a Tuscan named Quintian and that Volaterrae was his birthplace. Prosper and Leo both call Rome his home, and Feltoe says no city "can claim the honour with certainty".
  • Feltoe reports that "it is thought" the acolyte Leo whom Augustine of Hippo mentions in the Pelagian quarrel is the future pope.
  • Prosper counts him the forty-third bishop of Rome. Feltoe calls him the forty-seventh.
  • What happened at Ephesus in 449 is told here as Feltoe tells it. Leo, who was not there, calls it a meeting of robbers. The acts of that council, held in Syriac, are not among the sources used here.
  • The words of Eutyches are given as Leo quotes them in the Tome, from the record of the synod at Constantinople.
  • Prosper dates his entries by the consuls of each year. The years 440, 452 and 455 are given by Theodor Mommsen's edition and by Feltoe.
