The Imperial Church Era · c. 300-367 · Poitiers

Poitiers, today in France

Hilary of Poitiers

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Gallic Nicene Episcopate.

Known for
The leading Latin defender of the Nicene faith in the West, bishop of Poitiers, exiled under Constantius, and author of On the Trinity.
Also called
Hilarius (the Latin form; Hilary Bishop of Pictavium in Rufinus)
Dates
Born about 300; bishop by 356; died about 367
Place
Poitiers, in Aquitania, in Gaul
Role
Bishop and theologian

About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Hilary, bishop of Poitiers in Gaul, wrote these words from exile, in a book for the bishops of Gaul and Britain. "I was baptized some time ago, and I had been a bishop for a while. I never heard the faith of Nicaea until I was going into exile. But the Gospels and the Apostles showed me what 'one in substance' and 'like in substance' mean."

Nicaea was the council of 325, whose creed said the Son is "one in substance" with the Father, sharing his very being. In 356 the emperor Constantius sent him to Phrygia, in Asia Minor. There he learned the Eastern bishops' quarrel over the Son. He wrote against the Arian party, who held the Son was less than the Father, and explained the Eastern creeds to the West. Then he came home and led the bishops of Gaul back to the faith of Nicaea. Before the exile, Martin, later bishop of Tours, had sought him out.

Most of the outward story rests on his own letters and Sulpicius Severus, who wrote about forty years later and was openly on Hilary's side. Sulpicius says Martin, fresh from the army, stayed with him a while. Hilary tried to make him a deacon. Martin refused, crying he was unworthy. So Hilary made him an exorcist, who prayed over people thought troubled by evil spirits. The office was so lowly Martin could not refuse it without seeming proud.

Hilary and the bishops of Gaul had refused communion, the shared sacrament that shows churches belong together, to Saturninus, bishop of Arles. He had, Sulpicius says, harassed Gaul for the Arian party. At a council in Béziers he denounced that side's leaders. Saturninus then deceived the emperor, Hilary says, and a faction had him exiled on false reports.

In Phrygia he wrote twelve books against the Arians, now called On the Trinity. The bishops of Gaul asked him what the Eastern bishops had confessed, and he answered with On the Councils. In it he also wrote, as to brothers, to the Eastern bishops who said the Son is "like in substance," his being like the Father's. To accept "like," he told them, they need not reject "one in substance."

In 359 the governors sent him to the Eastern council at Seleucia, where he set out a faith matching Nicaea, vouched for the West, and was received. At Constantinople he asked three times, in writing, to debate before the emperor. The Arian party blocked it, and he was sent back to Gaul as a troublemaker in the East, his exile not lifted.

That same year, at Ariminum in Italy, Sulpicius says, Western bishops, pressed by the emperor's threats, had signed a creed that dropped the word "substance." In Gaul many wanted nothing to do with the signers. Hilary chose to bring them back to repentance, and in frequent councils almost all the bishops confessed their error.

The bishops of Gaul wrote to the East that the letters Hilary brought showed them the devil's fraud and the heretics' plots against the church. Those plots had split East from West, each side misled about the other. They cut off Auxentius, bishop of Milan, and other Arian leaders from communion. Saturninus resisted, and was cast out of the church, Sulpicius says. Another exiled bishop broke even with those who took the signers back. Sulpicius will not say who was right. Martin followed Hilary home and set up a monastery near Hilary's town. Sulpicius wrote: "All agree on this: by the good work of Hilary alone, our provinces of Gaul were freed from the guilt of heresy." Augustine, writing his own On the Trinity, studied Hilary's account of the three persons, and called him a real authority in reading the Scriptures and stating the faith.

In 364, at Milan, Hilary accused Auxentius, who then wrote a confession that sounded orthodox. The new emperor took communion with him. Hilary called it a pretense and was ordered out of Milan. Only Hilary tells it.

Hilary died at Poitiers, in the sixth year after his return, about 367. Nothing is recorded of how.

Key Works

  • On the Trinity: twelve books against the Arians, begun in exile; Jerome counts twelve, and Watson treats it as his chief work
  • On the Councils: a letter to the bishops of Gaul; Watson says it argued that the newer Eastern creeds could be read in an orthodox sense
  • To Constantius; On Constantius; Against Auxentius: his writings on the emperor's church policy and against the Arian bishop of Milan, which Jerome lists

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The Nicene cause in Gaul, which Sulpicius Severus credits him with clearing of heresy after Ariminum.
  • Sulpicius Severus says Martin, later bishop of Tours, left the army to seek out his company; Hilary tried to make him a deacon and, when Martin refused, made him an exorcist. Martin followed him home from exile and set up a monastery not far from Poitiers.
  • Sulpicius says Martin left to visit his parents, with Hilary's prayers and tears that he would return in due time.
  • Augustine, writing his own On the Trinity, studied a line from Hilary's On the Trinity on what is proper to each of the three persons, and called him a man of no mean authority in handling the Scriptures and in asserting the faith.
  • John Cassian, in 430, quotes his first book on the faith, calling him a confessor; Theodoret of Cyrrhus quotes him in the Greek East under the title of bishop and confessor. A confessor is one who suffered for the faith and lived.
  • Socrates of Constantinople says he defended the doctrine of the homoousion with great power in books written in Latin.
  • Rufinus calls him gentle and calm by nature, learned and most able at persuading, and says his books on the faith corrected even those far away whom he could not speak to in person.
  • Jerome's Chronicle records in one line that through Hilary Gaul condemned the tricks of the treachery of Ariminum.
  • Jerome asks a friend to send him Hilary's work on the councils, which he had copied for Rufinus at Trier with his own hand.

How they're remembered

  • Augustine of Hippo named him with Cyprian of Carthage, Lactantius and others as men who came out of Egypt loaded with gold and silver, meaning the pagan learning they brought into the church.
  • Orosius, Watson notes, ranked him within a generation of his death with Cyprian and Ambrose as one of the three chief glories of Western Christendom.
  • Jerome calls him "a confessor and bishop of my own day".
  • Gregory of Tours, writing about two centuries later, says he "is said to have raised the dead to life", and tells that before Clovis fought the Arian Goths a fiery beacon came from Hilary's church over the king's head.

At Major Councils

  • Council of Sardica, 343: Not present; his dossier keeps the Eastern bishops' letter
  • Councils of Ariminum and Seleucia, 359: Sat at Seleucia from exile, then condemned Ariminum in councils in Gaul

What Is Uncertain

  • Watson, who wrote the life in the English edition, warns that Hilary's own account of his search for God in On the Trinity is too neatly shaped to read as literal history, though it shows a real search.
  • Little of his life is dated firmly. Watson puts his birth about 300, from Hilary's own words rather than any record, and his death in 367, following Sulpicius Severus's "sixth year after his return".
  • Hilary is silent about himself and is never named in all the works of Athanasius, Watson notes, so the narrative is short and drawn mostly from Hilary's own writings.
  • His legendary daughter Abra, named in a letter once printed with his works, is now generally abandoned, Watson says.
  • Sulpicius Severus, on whom most of the outward story of the exile and return rests beside Hilary's own letters, wrote about forty years later and openly on Hilary's side, and is hostile to Saturninus.
  • Why he was exiled rests on his own word. Hilary says Saturninus deceived the emperor and that he was exiled by a faction on false reports, still a bishop in communion with all Gaul.
  • How he came home is told three ways. Sulpicius's Chronicle says he was sent back as a troublemaker with his exile not lifted; the Life of Martin says permission to return was granted through the king's penitence; Gregory of Tours says Constantius ordered his release.
  • Sulpicius says the exiled bishops offered to suspend Athanasius from communion if the bishops would first inquire into the faith, and that the other side preferred to remove the most celebrated men from the dispute. This rests on Sulpicius alone.
  • Sulpicius says that at Seleucia Hilary was first asked about the faith of the Gauls, whom the Arians had made suspect of Sabellius's teaching, and that he set out a faith agreeing with Nicaea and was admitted to communion.
  • Sulpicius will not say whether Hilary or Lucifer of Cagliari, who broke even with those who received the signers of Ariminum back, was right.
  • His failure at Milan in 364 is told only by Hilary, in Against Auxentius; the year is Watson's.
  • Against Constantius. Jerome says it was written after the emperor's death; Watson thinks it more probably the work of the long journey home in 360, while he still lived.
  • Gregory of Tours writes two centuries after Hilary, and the raising of the dead and the fiery beacon over Clovis are his late legend, kept in his voice.
  • The Latin of the Gallic bishops' letter to the East is held only in damaged prints. Feder's text reads that the letters were entrusted to Hilary; the text in Migne reads that they were sent to him.
