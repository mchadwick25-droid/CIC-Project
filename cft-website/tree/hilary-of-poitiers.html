The Imperial Church Era · c. 300-367 · Poitiers

Poitiers, today in France

Hilary of Poitiers

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Gallic Nicene Episcopate.

Known for
The leading Latin defender of the Nicene faith in the West, bishop of Poitiers, exiled under Constantius, and author of On the Trinity.
Also called
Hilarius (the Latin form; Hilary Bishop of Pictavium in Rufinus)
Dates
Born about 300; bishop by 356; died about 367
Place
Poitiers, in Aquitania, in Gaul
Role
Bishop and theologian

About This Person

In Milan, soon after the emperor Valentinian arrived there in 364, Hilary of Poitiers and Eusebius of Vercelli accused the city's bishop. He was Auxentius of Milan, an Arian who had held the see under the emperor Constantius. Before an imperial commission, Hilary says, he questioned Auxentius until he signed an orthodox confession. Valentinian still kept Auxentius, and Hilary was ordered out of Milan. E. W. Watson, who wrote the life in the English edition, accepts Hilary's account. He notes that Auxentius's own report of the hearing does not contradict it.

Hilary was bishop of Poitiers in Aquitania, in Gaul. In the opening of On the Trinity, he tells how he searched for God. "I chanced upon the books which, according to the tradition of the Hebrew faith, were written by Moses and the prophets." There he found God's own name, "I Am that I Am." Watson warns that the account is too neatly shaped to read as literal history.

In 356 a council at Béziers, led by Saturninus of Arles, condemned Hilary. The charges, Watson says, were about his conduct. His faith was not in question. He was banished to Phrygia, in Asia Minor. There, Jerome of Strido records, he wrote twelve books against the Arians, the work now called On the Trinity. He also wrote On the Councils to the bishops of Gaul. Watson says it argued that the newer Eastern creeds could bear an orthodox meaning.

In 359, in the fourth year of his exile, Hilary was sent with the other bishops to the council at Seleucia. He followed its envoys to the emperor at Constantinople. Sulpicius Severus says he asked in three petitions to debate the faith with his opponents. The Arians blocked him. He was sent back to Gaul as "a troubler of the East," with his sentence of exile still in force.

At home he held councils that condemned the creed signed at Ariminum, in Italy. Sulpicius is firmly on Hilary's side. He says the regions of Gaul "were set free from the guilt of heresy through the kind efforts of Hilarius alone." Hilary died, Sulpicius says, in the sixth year after his return. Watson places this in 367.

Augustine of Hippo named Hilary with Cyprian of Carthage, Lactantius and others. They had all come out of Egypt, he wrote, loaded with gold and silver. He meant the pagan learning they brought into the church.

Key Works

  • On the Trinity: twelve books against the Arians, begun in exile; Jerome counts twelve, and Watson treats it as his chief work
  • On the Councils: a letter to the bishops of Gaul; Watson says it argued that the newer Eastern creeds could be read in an orthodox sense
  • To Constantius; On Constantius; Against Auxentius: his writings on the emperor's church policy and against the Arian bishop of Milan, which Jerome lists

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The Nicene cause in Gaul, which Sulpicius Severus credits him with clearing of heresy after Ariminum.

How they're remembered

  • Augustine of Hippo named him with Cyprian of Carthage, Lactantius and others as men who came out of Egypt loaded with gold and silver, meaning the pagan learning they brought into the church.
  • Orosius, Watson notes, ranked him within a generation of his death with Cyprian and Ambrose as one of the three chief glories of Western Christendom.

At Major Councils

  • Council of Sardica, 343: Not present; his dossier keeps the Eastern bishops' letter
  • Councils of Ariminum and Seleucia, 359: Sat at Seleucia from exile, then condemned Ariminum in councils in Gaul

What Is Uncertain

  • Watson, who wrote the life in the English edition, warns that Hilary's own account of his search for God in On the Trinity is too neatly shaped to read as literal history, though it shows a real search.
  • Little of his life is dated firmly. Watson puts his birth about 300, from Hilary's own words rather than any record, and his death in 367, following Sulpicius Severus's "sixth year after his return".
  • Hilary is silent about himself and is never named in all the works of Athanasius, Watson notes, so the narrative is short and drawn mostly from Hilary's own writings.
  • His legendary daughter Abra, named in a letter once printed with his works, is now generally abandoned, Watson says.
