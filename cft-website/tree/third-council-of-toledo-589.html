The Age of Monks and Empires · 589 · Toledo, royal city of the Visigothic kingdom

Third Council of Toledo

Major council

Known for
The council of 589 at which the Visigothic king Reccared, his queen, and the Gothic bishops and nobles condemned the Homoian faith they had held and signed the Catholic faith of their subjects.
Dates
589. The acts date the council to the eighth day before the Ides of May, era 627, in Reccared's fourth year. The year 589 is given by Henry Percival and James Barmby; no held ancient witness gives an anno Domini year
Rivers
Latin West, Non-Nicene
Called by
King Reccared. The acts say he had ordered all the bishops of his realm to meet, and John of Biclaro that the synod gathered "praecepto principis", at the king's command

About This Council

A three-day fast had been proclaimed for the bishops of Spain and Gaul at Toledo. On 8 May, the council's acts say, they took their seats and prayed. Then King Reccared of the Visigoths appeared in their midst and prayed with them. Spain had long labored in the error of the Arians, he told them.

The acts date the council to his fourth year, which Henry Percival, the English editor of the councils, gives as 589. They survive in Latin, with the signatures, and are told here in modern words. Every witness here writes from the Catholic side.

The Gothic kings and their bishops held the Homoian faith, which their opponents called Arian. An envoy of Reccared's father, King Leovigild, once put it to Gregory of Tours, the Frankish bishop. It "was a wrong opinion of the ancient bishops which asserted the Son equal to the Father," he said.

John of Biclaro, a Catholic chronicler exiled by Leovigild, tells of a synod of Leovigild's bishops. It ruled that Catholics joining them need not be baptized again.

The king had called them, the acts say. The Latin heading counts sixty-two bishops, as does one text of John's chronicle. Another reads seventy-two. Masona, bishop of Mérida, signed first after the king. John says the council's business lay with Leander of Seville and Eutropius, abbot of Servitanum.

A notary read out the king's book of faith. It says the Holy Spirit proceeds from the Father and the Son, and Queen Baddo signed it. Then the Gothic bishops, clergy and nobles condemned the faith they had held, in twenty-three articles. They condemned the booklet of Leovigild's twelfth year, which had drawn Romans to their side. Eight former Arian bishops signed. A canon ordered the people to recite the creed aloud before the Lord's Prayer.

John compared Reccared to the emperor Constantine at the Council of Nicaea. There, he wrote, the heresy was condemned with its roots left in. At Toledo it was cut out by the roots. After the council, a duke named Argimund tried to seize the throne. He was shorn, lost his right hand and was paraded through Toledo on a donkey.

Pope Gregory the Great wrote to Leander that he could not "at all fully express in words" his joy. Isidore of Seville, Leander's brother, later wrote that through Leander the Goths turned from the Arian madness to the Catholic faith.

Why They Met

  • The Visigothic kings and their bishops held the Homoian faith, which the acts and every held witness call Arian. King Leovigild's envoy Agilan told Gregory of Tours that it "was a wrong opinion of the ancient bishops which asserted the Son equal to the Father" (Gregory of Tours, History V.43, a hostile report).
  • Under Leovigild a synod of his bishops at Toledo ruled that those coming over from the Roman religion to what it called "nostram catholicam fidem", our catholic faith, need not be baptized again, only receive the laying on of hands and communion (John of Biclaro, under Leovigild's twelfth year). The acts later condemn a booklet of that year, with its doxology "gloria Patri per Filium in Spiritu Sancto", glory to the Father through the Son in the Holy Spirit, as having drawn Romans to the Arian side.
  • Reccared became Catholic in the tenth month of his first year, John of Biclaro says. Gregory of Tours tells instead of a debate between the bishops of the two sides, after which the king received chrism. Reccared told the council it was not many days after his father's death (the acts).
  • On one side stood the king, the Catholic bishops of Spain and Gaul, and the Gothic bishops, clergy and nobles who turned with him; on the other, those who kept the Homoian faith. John of Biclaro tells of plots by the Arian bishops Sunna and Uldila, and by Leovigild's widow, Goswintha.

Who Came

  • Sixty-two bishops, by the heading of the held Latin text and by the text of John of Biclaro in Migne; Theodor Mommsen's text of John reads seventy-two.
  • The signed list has sixty-two bishops in person and five who signed through deputies; one bishop also signs for an absent colleague. Two places in the printed text are damaged.
  • The bishops came from all Spain and Gaul, the acts say: the Gaul of the Visigothic kingdom, with Narbonne, Béziers and Agde among its sees.
  • Masona of Mérida signs first after the king, then Euphemius of Toledo and Leander of Seville. Eight former Arian bishops sign the profession and the canons, among them Ugnas of Barcelona and Murila of Valencia.
  • Queen Baddo signs the king's book of faith; Gothic nobles sign the profession, and all the elders of the Goths with them (the acts).

What They Decided

  • The king's book of faith was received from him and read aloud by a notary. It confesses the Father, Son and Holy Spirit as one substance and the Holy Spirit as proceeding "a Patre et Filio", from the Father and the Son; it condemns Arius with all his teaching. Reccared and Queen Baddo signed it (the acts). O. M. Dalton, translating Gregory of Tours, says the king's speech at the council was composed by Leander.
  • The Gothic bishops, clergy and nobles professed the Catholic faith and pronounced twenty-three articles of condemnation, the last of them their closing subscription. The first falls on anyone who still holds the faith and communion that came from Arius; the fifteenth on rebaptism; the sixteenth on the booklet of Leovigild's twelfth year; the nineteenth to the twenty-second receive Nicaea, Constantinople, Ephesus and Chalcedon (the acts).
  • Twenty-three canons. The second orders that the creed of the hundred and fifty bishops of Constantinople be recited by the people with a clear voice before the Lord's Prayer, in the manner of the Eastern churches; the ninth gives the churches that had been Arian to the Catholic bishop of the place; the fourteenth forbids Jews Christian wives or slaves; the sixteenth is against idolatry, which had grown up through almost all Spain and Gaul (the acts).
  • The king confirmed the council by edict: what was defined in the holy council held at Toledo in his fourth year may be despised by none (the acts).

What Came of It

  • John of Biclaro compared Reccared to Constantine at Nicaea and Marcian at Chalcedon. At Nicaea, he wrote, the Arian heresy was condemned with its roots left in; at Toledo, after long killings of Catholics, it was cut out by the roots (John of Biclaro).
  • Plots surrounded the change. Before the council, John says, the Arian bishop Sunna and Seggo sought the throne; the bishop Uldila plotted with Goswintha, who was always hostile to Catholics. After it, the duke Argimund tried to seize the throne; he was beaten, shorn, lost his right hand and was paraded through Toledo on a donkey (John of Biclaro).
  • Leander's sermon in praise of the church for the conversion of the people is printed with the acts, though the acts' own note says two manuscripts lack it.
  • Gregory the Great wrote to Leander that he could not "at all fully express in words" his joy at the conversion, and later to Reccared that the whole Gothic people had been brought from the error of the Arian heresy (Gregory, letters in Barmby's English, Book I no. 43 and Book IX no. 122).
  • Isidore of Seville, Leander's younger brother, wrote that through Leander's faith and work the Gothic people turned from the Arian madness to the Catholic faith (Isidore, On Famous Men).
  • Henry Percival notes that 589 is the earliest date at which the words 'and the Son' are found brought into the creed; at Toledo they stand in the king's book of faith and in the third anathema.

Traditions It Touched

Gathered

  • Nicene Visigothic Iberia: The king and the Catholic bishops of Spain and Gaul met at Toledo; Masona of Mérida signed first after the king, and John of Biclaro says the business lay with Leander of Seville and the abbot Eutropius.

Condemned

  • Gothic, Vandal & Lombard Homoian Christianity: The Gothic bishops, clergy and nobles condemned the faith and communion that came from Arius and that they had held until then, in twenty-three anathemas; eight of their bishops signed.
  • Homoian ('Arian') Christianity: The acts anathematize anyone who still desires to hold the faith and communion that came from Arius; John of Biclaro says the Arian heresy, condemned at Nicaea with its roots left in, was cut out by the roots at Toledo.

Affected

  • The Roman Church in the Age of Gregory the Great: Gregory the Great wrote to Leander of his joy at the king's conversion, and to Reccared that the whole Gothic people had been brought from the Arian error.

Whose Account We Have

  • The acts of the Third Council of Toledo, in the Hispana collection (Madrid, 1808) (acts): The king's address, his book of faith, the Gothic profession and anathemas, the canons, the king's edict and the signatures, in Latin. The record of the Catholic side that won; no English translation is held.
  • Reccared's book of faith, within the acts (participant): The king's own statement, offered by him and read by a notary. Dalton says Leander composed the king's speech on that occasion.
  • Leander of Seville, sermon in praise of the church, printed with the acts (participant): Attributed to Leander; the acts' note says two manuscripts lack it.
  • Letter in Reccared's name to Gregory the Great (Barmby's English) (participant): Tells the conversion in the king's voice; Barmby says its genuineness is considered doubtful.
  • John of Biclaro, Chronicle (later narrative): A Catholic Goth exiled by Leovigild, writing year by year under the emperor's and the king's years; he dates nothing by anno Domini.
  • Gregory of Tours, History of the Franks (Dalton's English) (later narrative): A Frankish bishop of the time, hostile to the Arians; tells Reccared's conversion by debate and reports the Homoian envoy Agilan.
  • Gregory the Great, letters to Leander and to Reccared (Barmby's English) (later narrative): Rome's welcome of the conversion; a friend of Leander.
  • Isidore of Seville, History of the Goths and On Famous Men (later narrative): Leander's younger brother, writing in the next generation; Catholic.
  • Henry R. Percival and James Barmby (Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers) (modern reconstruction): Give the year 589 and Reccared's conversion in 587; Percival translates canon II and weighs the creed's 'and the Son'.

What Is Uncertain

  • Every witness is from the Catholic side. No Homoian text from Visigothic Spain is held; the Homoian view reaches us through the acts' anathemas, John of Biclaro and Gregory of Tours's report of the envoy Agilan.
  • The number of bishops: sixty-two by the heading of the held text and Migne's John of Biclaro; seventy-two in Mommsen's text of John.
  • Who presided: the acts name no president. John of Biclaro says the business lay with Leander and the abbot Eutropius; Masona signs first.
  • Whose words the king's speech was: the acts give it in the king's voice; Dalton, citing Labbe, says Leander composed it.
  • When and how Reccared became Catholic: in his first year's tenth month (John), after a debate of bishops (Gregory of Tours), not many days after his father's death (the acts); 587 is Barmby's and Gardner's year.
  • The year 589 is Percival's and Barmby's reckoning of era 627; no held ancient witness gives it.
