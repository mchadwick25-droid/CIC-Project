The Early Church Era · c. 150–400 CE · Alexandria, Egypt

Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition

Meet Theon on Church in Conversation

About This Movement

Alexandria was the ancient world's great city of libraries and lecture halls, and Christianity there took the same shape: a school. Teachers received inquirers, catechumens preparing for baptism, and educated pagans curious about this new philosophy, and walked them through Scripture as a text with a surface and a depth: the letter first, then the moral sense, then the spiritual meaning that opens onto the Logos himself.

Clement of Alexandria wrote for cultivated Greeks who wanted to know whether a Christian could keep his learning. Origen, who succeeded him, wrote commentaries across most of Scripture and produced the Hexapla, a six-column parallel edition of the Old Testament assembled to see what the Hebrew actually said — before falling out with his bishop and moving his teaching to Caesarea around 231. Historians still argue about how formal the whole thing was — whether Alexandria had one continuous institution with a succession of appointed heads, as Eusebius of Caesarea later described it, or a looser sequence of private Christian schools whose teachers happened to be remembered together. That tidy succession comes almost entirely from Eusebius: Clement's own writings never once describe a formal school that he headed.

The allegorical way of reading Scripture is this tradition's best-known signature, but it was never uncontested even at home. Nepos, a bishop out in the Egyptian countryside at Arsinoe, wrote a book refuting the allegorists and won real support for a plainer, literal reading — until Dionysius, by then bishop of Alexandria, spent three days in patient public conversation with Nepos's own community and brought them back around.

In Its Own Record

Gave Rise To

  • Desert Monasticism Contested Major — Scholars disagree on whether the desert monks grew out of Christian teaching at Alexandria. Samuel Rubenson reads Antony's letters as the work of a man shaped by Origen's ideas. But Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria, paints Antony as an Egyptian. His parents were Christians, and he grew up at home with them. He went to the city when the martyrs were led there.

Influenced By

  • The Second-Century Greek Apologists Documented — Clement of Alexandria praises Tatian's Address to the Greeks. He says it gives an accurate account of the age of Moses. Tatian was one of the Greek apologists of the second century.
  • Apocryphal and Pseudepigraphal Literature in Christian Transmission Documented — In his Commentary on Matthew, Origen cites a tradition from the Gospel of Peter and the Book of James. That book is now called the Protevangelium. It says the brothers of Jesus were sons of Joseph by a wife he had before Mary. Origen shares its aim, which is to honor Mary's virginity.

Influenced

  • Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity Documented — In 392 Jerome wrote a preface to his new version of Job. In it he defends his work by pointing to Origen. Origen had marked every Old Testament book with signs called obeli and asterisks. They showed where the old Greek text fell short.
  • The Anatolian Church in the Third Century Documented — Eusebius of Caesarea says the brothers Gregory of Neocaesarea and Athenodorus studied under Origen for five years. Then both became bishops in Pontus. Gregory is said to have made a speech to Origen as he left him. Some scholars doubt that it is his.
  • The Church of Roman Palestine before Constantine Documented — Eusebius of Caesarea says that Origen left Alexandria for Caesarea about 231. He left his school in the hands of Heraclas. Eusebius adds that Pamphilus, a martyr of his own day, built up a store of Origen's books.
  • The Church of Aquileia Widely Accepted — Rufinus put Origen into Latin. The editors of his works say he did much of this at Aquileia. In his preface to First Principles, written in 397, he says he is carrying on a task Jerome began. A preface of 410 recalls his promise to put Origen's work on the Law of Moses into Latin for his people.
  • Cappadocian Christianity Documented — Socrates of Constantinople, a historian of the fifth century, says Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nazianzus studied Origen's works. They drew their reading of Scripture from them. Then they used Origen against the Arians. Around 382 Gregory sent a friend 'the volume of the Philocalia of Origen'. The editors of both men's letters credit that book to the two of them.
  • Ambrosian Milan (standalone) Documented — Jerome wrote a letter in 400. In it he says Ambrose had just taken over Origen's work on the six days of creation. But he adds that Ambrose's version took more from Hippolytus and Basil of Caesarea than from Origen.

Argued Against

  • Ebionite / Nazoraean Current Documented — Origen wrote On First Principles at Alexandria. In it he rejects how the Ebionites read Jesus' words about the lost sheep of Israel. He mocks their name as meaning 'poor in understanding'.
  • Homoian ('Arian') Christianity Documented — Athanasius of Alexandria wrote a Defence Against the Arians. In it he quotes the letter of the council held at Sardica. That letter names the leaders of the party against him, Ursacius and Valens among them.

Opposed By

  • Coptic Christianity under Early Islam Documented — The History of the Patriarchs of the Coptic Church is said to be by Severus ibn al-Muqaffa, a bishop of the 900s. It says Patriarch Demetrius cut Origen off for his books. It calls the books Origen wrote later blasphemous. Yet the same work honors Dionysius of Alexandria as a patriarch. Dionysius had once run the studies at Alexandria.
  • The Anatolian Church in the Third Century Documented — Methodius, bishop of Olympus in Lycia, wrote On the Resurrection against Origen and On Free Will against him too. Jerome lists both works. Photius summarizes the first as arguing that the body is not the soul's prison.
  • Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity Documented — In Letter 84 Jerome calls Origen's teachings poisonous. He says they do violence to the Holy Scriptures. He adds that his own commentaries show he has always opposed them.
  • Antiochene Exegetical Christianity (Chrysostom-centered) Documented — Facundus of Hermiane took up the defense of Theodore of Mopsuestia in the sixth century. He names a book that Theodore wrote against Origen, called On Allegory and History. The book itself is lost. Socrates of Constantinople and Sozomen say that Diodore, who taught Theodore, kept to the literal sense of Scripture. He steered clear of allegory.

Sources

  • Clement of Alexandria, Stromata (Miscellanies) (primary) — Clement's case, addressed to educated Greek readers, that philosophy had served as a tutor leading toward Christ.
  • Origen, De Principiis (On First Principles) (primary) — The first attempt by a Christian writer at a systematic account of the faith; some of its speculative proposals were condemned by a church council three centuries after Origen's death.
  • Eusebius, Church History, Book VI (primary) — The source of the traditional picture of Alexandria as one continuous school with a succession of appointed heads — the very account modern scholars now question.
  • Gustave Bardy, "Aux origines de l'École d'Alexandrie" (reference) — The 1932 study that first argued, against Eusebius's tidy succession-list, that Alexandria's teaching looked more like a sequence of private Christian tutors than one formal institution — the opening move in a scholarly debate that remains unsettled today.
