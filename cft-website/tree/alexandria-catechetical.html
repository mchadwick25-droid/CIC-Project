About This Movement

Around 200, the threat of persecution under the emperor Severus had driven every teacher of the Christian faith out of Alexandria, in Egypt. With no one else left to teach, pagans came to Origen, then in his eighteenth year, to hear the word of God. Nearly all we know of these classrooms comes from Eusebius of Caesarea, writing about a century later from letters and from people who had known Origen, and he tells of the pupils who were put to death. One was a catechumen, or learner not yet baptized, and Eusebius borrows Origen's phrase: "Among the women, too, Herais was still a catechumen. She received baptism, as he himself says somewhere, the baptism by fire, and left this life." Origen counted her death as her baptism.

From about 180 to the middle of the next century, the Christians of Alexandria learned their faith from teachers, as the city's pagans learned grammar and philosophy, in what Eusebius calls a school. Teachers took pagans and catechumens through Scripture, read for a deeper sense beneath the plain story.

Origen, still a boy, had been one of the pupils of Clement of Alexandria, who taught there in those years. Clement wrote that the Word, his name for Jesus, first urges people on, then trains them, and last of all teaches them. A sick body needs a doctor before it can learn anything, he explained. In the same way, a soul must first be healed of its passions, then trained, and only then taught, once it can take in the Word. So a learner was led step by step toward salvation. He wrote, too: "Faith is taught without letters," that is, to those who cannot read. "Its handbook, unpolished and divine at once, is called love, a spiritual book."

In Origen's classroom the crowds came one after another from morning to evening and scarcely left him time to breathe, or to study Scripture himself, so he divided them. Heraclas, one of his first pupils, took the beginners, and Origen kept the advanced. Scripture, Origen wrote before he left the city, has a body, a soul and a spirit, as a person does. The plain story feeds the simple, its soul feeds those who have gone further, and its spirit those made perfect.

When Origen left for Caesarea, about 231, Heraclas took over the instruction and then became the city's bishop. Dionysius, another of Origen's pupils, followed him in both.

Nepos, a bishop in Egypt, had written a book called Refutation of Allegorists, against those who read Scripture for hidden meanings. As his opponents describe it, he taught that Christ would reign on this earth for a thousand years of bodily luxury. In the district of Arsinoe, in the Egyptian countryside, his teaching had held for a long time, and whole churches had broken away and fallen away. Dionysius loved Nepos for his faith and his work on Scripture, but he would answer the book. Some teachers who relied on it, he wrote, led simpler believers to hope for small and mortal things in God's kingdom, and left them no lofty thought of the resurrection and of being made like Christ.

In his own account, the only one left to us, Dionysius called together the presbyters, or elders, and the teachers of the villages, and asked for a public examination. They brought him the book like a weapon and a fortress no one could take, and he sat with them for three days, from morning to evening. "If the argument won," he wrote, "we were not ashamed to change our minds and agree."

At the end, Coracion, the leader of this teaching, said before all the believers who were there that he was convinced, and would no longer hold or teach it.

Voices in the Record

  • Pantaenus was the earliest teacher named in the tradition, remembered as a philosopher who had converted from Stoicism. Clement of Alexandria later paid tribute to a beloved teacher as 'the true, the Sicilian bee,' though Clement himself never names him, and the identification with Pantaenus comes only from Eusebius of Caesarea.
  • Clement of Alexandria wrote for educated Greeks, arguing that philosophy had been a tutor leading them toward Christ.
  • Origen dominates the surviving record here: a commentator, a textual scholar, and the first Christian to attempt a systematic account of the faith.
  • Dionysius of Alexandria studied under Origen and later became bishop, steering the Egyptian church through persecution and plague. Of Christians who nursed the dying while an epidemic emptied the city, he wrote: they 'held fast to each other and visited the sick fearlessly... and died with them most joyfully, taking the affliction of others, and drawing the sickness from their neighbors to themselves.'
  • Didymus the Blind lost his sight at four, yet taught in Alexandria for decades — the Latin church history of Rufinus says he became the teacher of the church's school, and the fifth-century historian Sozomen calls him its president. Jerome studied under him. Didymus's own books vanished for over fifteen centuries, until a cache of them turned up by chance in the Egyptian sand near Tura in 1941; none has yet been translated into English, so his voice here comes only through his students.
  • Most of the ordinary people who filled these classrooms and congregations left no words of their own. Potamiaena, a young woman the church remembered by name for her courage under execution, is typical of what survives: her story, never her own words. Everything that survives here was written by the literate few — teachers, bishops, and their own students.

Direct descendants

  • gave rise to Desert Monasticism Contested Major — Scholars disagree on whether the desert monks grew out of Christian teaching at Alexandria. Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced The Second-Century Greek Apologists Documented — Clement of Alexandria praises Tatian's Address to the Greeks. He says it gives an accurate account of the age of Moses. Tatian was one of the Greek apologists of the second century.
  • influenced Apocryphal and Pseudepigraphal Literature in Christian Transmission Documented — In his Commentary on Matthew, Origen cites a tradition from the Gospel of Peter and the Book of James. That book is now called the Protevangelium. It says the brothers of Jesus were sons of Joseph by a wife he had before Mary. Origen shares its aim, which is to honor Mary's virginity.

Influenced

  • influenced Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity Documented — In 392 Jerome wrote a preface to his new version of Job. In it he defends his work by pointing to Origen. Origen had marked every Old Testament book with signs called obeli and asterisks. They showed where the old Greek text fell short.
  • influenced The Anatolian Church in the Third Century Documented — Eusebius of Caesarea says the brothers Gregory of Neocaesarea and Athenodorus studied under Origen for five years. Then both became bishops in Pontus. Gregory is said to have made a speech to Origen as he left him. Some scholars doubt that it is his.
  • influenced The Church of Roman Palestine before Constantine Documented — Eusebius of Caesarea says that Origen left Alexandria for Caesarea about 231. He left his school in the hands of Heraclas. Eusebius adds that Pamphilus, a martyr of his own day, built up a store of Origen's books.
  • influenced The Church of Aquileia Widely Accepted — Rufinus put Origen into Latin. The editors of his works say he did much of this at Aquileia. In his preface to First Principles, written in 397, he says he is carrying on a task Jerome began. A preface of 410 recalls his promise to put Origen's work on the Law of Moses into Latin for his people.
  • influenced Cappadocian Christianity Documented — Socrates of Constantinople, a historian of the fifth century, says Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nazianzus studied Origen's works. They drew their reading of Scripture from them. Then they used Origen against the Arians. Around 382 Gregory sent a friend 'the volume of the Philocalia of Origen'. The editors of both men's letters credit that book to the two of them.
  • influenced Ambrosian Milan (standalone) Documented — Jerome wrote a letter in 400. In it he says Ambrose had just taken over Origen's work on the six days of creation. But he adds that Ambrose's version took more from Hippolytus and Basil of Caesarea than from Origen.

Argued Against

  • argued against Ebionite / Nazoraean Current Documented — Origen wrote On First Principles at Alexandria. In it he rejects how the Ebionites read Jesus' words about the lost sheep of Israel. He mocks their name as meaning 'poor in understanding'.
  • argued against Homoian ('Arian') Christianity Documented — Athanasius of Alexandria wrote a Defence Against the Arians. In it he quotes the letter of the council held at Sardica. That letter names the leaders of the party against him, Ursacius and Valens among them.

Opposed By

  • argued against Coptic Christianity under Early Islam Documented — The History of the Patriarchs of the Coptic Church is said to be by Severus ibn al-Muqaffa, a bishop of the 900s. It says Patriarch Demetrius cut Origen off for his books. It calls the books Origen wrote later blasphemous. Yet the same work honors Dionysius of Alexandria as a patriarch. Dionysius had once run the studies at Alexandria.
  • argued against The Anatolian Church in the Third Century Documented — Methodius, bishop of Olympus in Lycia, wrote On the Resurrection against Origen and On Free Will against him too. Jerome lists both works. Photius summarizes the first as arguing that the body is not the soul's prison.
  • argued against Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity Documented — In Letter 84 Jerome calls Origen's teachings poisonous. He says they do violence to the Holy Scriptures. He adds that his own commentaries show he has always opposed them.
  • argued against Antiochene Exegetical Christianity (Chrysostom-centered) Documented — Facundus of Hermiane took up the defense of Theodore of Mopsuestia in the sixth century. He names a book that Theodore wrote against Origen, called On Allegory and History. The book itself is lost. Socrates of Constantinople and Sozomen say that Diodore, who taught Theodore, kept to the literal sense of Scripture. He steered clear of allegory.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Teachers here read Scripture in layers, the plain story and a deeper meaning beneath it, confident that Greek philosophy could serve Christ rather than threaten him - a method that shaped how much of the church has read the Bible ever since.

Legacy

The Alexandrian way of reading Scripture — that the text has depths beneath its surface, and that reading it is a lifelong formation rather than an act of information-gathering — became one of Christianity's most influential approaches, shaping how the East and West alike read Scripture for centuries.

Origen worked closely on texts. He set the standard for taking seriously the languages they were first written in. Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nazianzus studied his books. A fifth-century church historian, Socrates, says they drew from them "the right interpretation of the sacred Scriptures." Gregory sent a friend "the volume of the Philocalia of Origen," a book of passages from his work that opens with chapters on Scripture and how to read it. Ambrose and Jerome read him closely too, even when they were careful about saying so.

The school ran on. In Eusebius's own day, Achillas, a presbyter, or elder, of Alexandria, "was placed over the school of the sacred faith."

Whether the tradition also fed Egypt's monks is still argued. In his Life of Antony, Athanasius of Alexandria shows the monk as a boy raised at home by Christian parents, a boy who would not be taught to read.

The church of Alexandria later remembered the school in two minds. Its History of the Patriarchs was put together much later, and it condemns the books of Origen. Yet the same book honors Dionysius of Alexandria as patriarch. It also tells a legend of how he came to the faith: a pagan philosopher, he bought some of Paul's letters from an old widow, read them, and was baptized.

Sources

Doctrinal Floor Note

This tradition's core confession does not diverge from the doctrine the Nicene Creed later stated — though one of its own greatest figures held things more loosely. Origen speculated boldly toward the soul's pre-existence and the final restoration of all things. The rupture of his own lifetime was not over these ideas: his bishop condemned his ordination, not his doctrine. The dispute over the doctrines themselves broke into open controversy only around 399–400, nearly a century and a half after his death, and a church council formally condemned them three centuries after that.

How Sure the Record Is

Rich (Clement of Alexandria, Origen); school-institution question is live scholarship. One real gap: Clement's fullest surviving treatment of marriage and the body exists only in his own Greek — no public-domain English translation has ever been made of it.

  • Clement of Alexandria, Stromata (Miscellanies) (primary) — Clement's case, addressed to educated Greek readers, that philosophy had served as a tutor leading toward Christ.
  • Origen, De Principiis (On First Principles) (primary) — The first attempt by a Christian writer at a systematic account of the faith; some of its speculative proposals were condemned by a church council three centuries after Origen's death.
  • Eusebius, Church History, Book VI (primary) — The source of the traditional picture of Alexandria as one continuous school with a succession of appointed heads — the very account modern scholars now question.
  • Gustave Bardy, "Aux origines de l'École d'Alexandrie" (reference) — The 1932 study that first argued, against Eusebius's tidy succession-list, that Alexandria's teaching looked more like a sequence of private Christian tutors than one formal institution — the opening move in a scholarly debate that remains unsettled today.
