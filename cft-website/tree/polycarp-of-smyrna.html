Polycarp of Smyrna

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Post-Apostolic Household-Church Christianity.

Polycarp was bishop of the church in Smyrna and wrote a letter to the church at Philippi. As an old man he was put to death at Smyrna, after telling the governor he had served Christ for eighty-six years.

About This Person

"For eighty-six years I have served him, and he has done me no wrong. How can I blaspheme my King, who saved me?"

Polycarp, an old man, gave that answer in a stadium in Smyrna, in the second century. The Roman governor had offered to free him if he would curse Christ. Smyrna was a city in the Roman province of Asia, and he was the bishop of its church. Irenaeus, who heard him as a boy, wrote that Polycarp had known people who had seen Jesus.

Earlier, Polycarp had visited Rome, where Anicetus was bishop. The two could not agree about keeping Easter. Polycarp kept it as he had with John and the other apostles he had known. Anicetus said he must follow the customs of the elders before him. "Even so, they shared communion," Irenaeus wrote, and the two parted in peace.

In a time of persecution, the crowd had shouted, "Away with the atheists!", meaning the Christians, who would not worship their gods. They demanded that Polycarp be found.

Because many wished it, he withdrew to a farm, and then to another. There searchers seized two young slaves of his household, and one gave him away under torture. When the police came, he could have escaped. He said, "Let the will of God be done." He had food and drink set before them and asked for an hour to pray. He prayed for two hours, and many were sorry they had come for such a godly old man.

The governor threatened him with wild beasts, and then with fire. Polycarp answered, "You threaten fire that burns for an hour and soon goes out." The crowd cried out against him as the destroyer of their gods. They asked for a lion, but the beast shows were over, so they shouted for him to be burned.

The church in Smyrna says its own people saw the flames curve around him like a ship's sail. The fire could not consume his body, they say, so an executioner killed him with a dagger. Later they laid his bones in a fitting place. They wrote that the Lord would let them gather there to "keep the birthday of his martyrdom," the day he died for Christ. They would do this "to remember those who have already competed," the martyrs before him, "and to train and prepare those still to come."

Decades after that visit, Victor, another bishop of Rome, tried to cut off the churches of Asia over the Easter date. Irenaeus wrote to remind him that Polycarp and Anicetus had kept communion though they differed.

Polycarp's letter to the church at Philippi survives. More than two hundred years later, Jerome wrote that it was still read when the churches of Asia met. It calls its readers back from false teaching to "the word handed down to us from the beginning."

Dates
No ancient source gives a year for his birth or his death. His church's account dates his death to a great Sabbath, the second day of the month Xanthicus, and gives no year. Irenaeus says he was at Rome when Anicetus was bishop there, and Eusebius and Jerome put his death in the reign of Marcus Aurelius. The second century comes from the modern dating of those reigns.

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • His letter to the Philippians survives. Irenaeus commended it to anyone who wished to learn the character of his faith, Eusebius quotes from it, and Jerome says it is "read to the present day in the meetings in Asia".
  • With it he sent the Philippians the letters Ignatius of Antioch had written to him and his church at Smyrna, and all the others he had by him.
  • His church sent its account of his death to the church at Philomelium and asked that it be sent on to Christians farther away. Eusebius copied most of it into his Church History, and a later note at its end names those who copied it in turn: Gaius from the copy of Irenaeus, Socrates at Corinth, and Pionius.
  • When Victor, bishop of Rome, tried to cut off the churches of Asia over the Easter date, Irenaeus reminded him that Polycarp and Anicetus had kept communion though they differed. Eusebius calls Irenaeus a peacemaker in this matter.

How they're remembered

  • Irenaeus, who saw him in Lower Asia as a boy, could still describe the place where he sat and taught, and called him "a more stedfast witness of truth" than Valentinus, Marcion and the rest of the heretics.
  • His own church called him an apostolic and prophetic teacher, and wrote that even the pagans spoke of him everywhere and that all wished to imitate his martyrdom, as one in keeping with the gospel of Christ.
  • Polycrates, bishop of Ephesus, writing to Victor of Rome, named him among the great lights of Asia who kept the fourteenth day of the passover.

Open Questions

  • The witnesses. Nearly all that is known comes from his one surviving letter; his church's account of his death, which speaks of the yearly gathering as still to come and so seems to have been written within a year of it; and Irenaeus, who heard him as a boy and wrote many years later. Eusebius and Jerome wrote long after and did not know him.
  • The eighty-six years. Polycarp says he has served Christ eighty-six years. The text does not say whether they count from his birth or from his baptism, so they do not give his age. Irenaeus says only that he lived a very long time and died a very old man. A note in the Ante-Nicene Fathers turns the figure into his age; that is a modern inference.
  • The date. His church's account gives a day and a month but no year. The English prints the seventh day before the Kalends of May, where Gebhardt's Greek reads March, and the English editor's notes call the translation very doubtful and the chronology obscure. Jerome dates his own book to the fourteenth year of Theodosius; that this is more than two hundred years after Polycarp, like the second century itself, comes from modern dating, not from the sources.
  • The trial. In the Greek the proconsul promises to free him if he will swear by the Fortune of Caesar, and then, as a second command, tells him to revile Christ; the story joins the two, because Polycarp's answer meets the second. The governor of the story is the proconsul, and Asia is the name the sources give, with no word of a Roman province. The crowd's word atheists meant people who reject the gods; it first rose after Germanicus died bravely in the stadium, with the cry that Polycarp be sought out.
  • The arrest. The Greek says the searchers came at once and, not finding him, seized two young slaves, but not where; the English, by its word whither, puts it at the second farm. The Greek word can mean slave boys or young servants. That they were of his household rests on the account's word for those who betrayed him.
  • The wonders. The flame shaped like a sail, and the body the fire could not consume, are the Smyrna church's own report, and it names no eyewitness. The English also has a dove coming from the wound; Gebhardt's Greek brackets it as doubtful and Eusebius leaves it out, so the story does not tell it. The vision of the burning pillow, the voice from heaven and the sweet smell are left out too. The account makes hostile claims about the Jews of Smyrna that no other source tells from their side. It was addressed to Philomelium, though Eusebius says Pontus, and its messenger is Marcus in the English and Marcion in Gebhardt's Greek.
  • The yearly gathering. The church does not say it resolved to meet. It says the Lord will let them gather where his bones lay, as far as they can, in gladness and joy. Each year is implied by the word birthday, the day of his death seen as his birth into life. The sources used here do not say whether the gathering was kept.
  • The apostles. That he knew John and others who had seen the Lord rests on Irenaeus, and the sources do not say which John. Who made him bishop is told differently: Irenaeus says apostles in Asia, Tertullian that the church of Smyrna's records name John, Jerome that John ordained him, and Eusebius those who had seen and heard the Lord.
  • The visit to Rome. No source gives how long before his death it came; Eusebius tells it first, and the modern dates in McGiffert's notes would put it only a little before. Irenaeus's words on what Polycarp kept do not name the feast, and he does not give it as the reason for the trip; Eusebius does both. Irenaeus also says the two differed a little on other things. His words that Anicetus conceded the eucharist to Polycarp may mean that Polycarp presided or only that he took part; the story leaves them out. From the visit to Victor's accession, Eusebius's own figures give about twenty-one to thirty-two years, and the modern dates in McGiffert's notes about thirty-five.
  • The letter. It survives, but not whole in Greek, and the Library holds no Greek of it in readable form, so its modern English follows the English translation alone. Irenaeus says he wrote other letters, to neighboring churches and to some of the brethren; only this one is held. The letter as printed leaves it unclear whether Ignatius was still alive when it was written.
  • The modern English of the answer follows the Greek, my king, the one who saved me, as Eusebius's translator has it, "my king who saved me", where the Ante-Nicene English prints "my King and my Saviour". Of the yearly gathering it likewise follows the Greek word birthday, where that English prints anniversary, and the athletes' word for those who have competed, where it prints finished their course.
  • Marcion. Irenaeus tells of the meeting but not where it happened. Jerome, writing long after, puts it at Rome, at the time of the visit to Anicetus.
