The Byzantine Imperial Church (Justinianic)

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

For nearly two centuries after Chalcedon, the church of the eastern Roman Empire was inseparable from the empire itself. Emperors summoned its councils, legislated for its clergy and monasteries, appointed and deposed its patriarchs, and treated doctrinal unity as a condition of political order. Justinian pushed this furthest — rebuilding Hagia Sophia in under six years, codifying Roman law with the church written into it, and in 553 calling a council to condemn the writings of three long-dead theologians, two of whom Chalcedon itself had cleared, in the hope of winning back the Miaphysites. It did not work: the condemnation alienated much of the Latin West without reconciling Egypt or Syria, and each attempted formula produced a fresh schism instead. The same church, in the same years, produced Romanos the Melodist, whose sung sermons in verse are among the finest poetry in Greek, and sent missionaries as far as the Nile kingdoms.

In Its Own Record

Continued As

  • The Byzantine Imperial Church (Heraclian & Iconoclast Eras) Documented Major — The patriarchate of Constantinople ran on through the reign of Heraclius. Sergius was its patriarch when Heraclius was crowned in 610. The council of 681 names Sergius, and after him Pyrrhus, Paul and Peter, 'who died bishops of this God-preserved city'.

Influenced

  • Georgian Christianity Widely Accepted — Around 600, Kurion, head of the church in Georgia, turned to Constantinople and accepted the Council of Chalcedon. Writing later in the 600s, the Armenian historian Sebeos puts the head of the Georgian church on Chalcedon's side. Bishops from the Greek lands stand with him.

Argued Against

  • Montanism (the New Prophecy) Documented Major — Procopius, a hostile witness to Justinian, says the emperor ordered heresies such as the Montanists' to be given up. He reports that the Montanists of Phrygia shut themselves in their churches and burned them. A law of Justinian also names the Montanists.Lasting result: Justinian ordered the Montanists to give up their faith, Procopius says. He threatened those who held out with losing the right to leave property to their families.
  • Antiochene Exegetical Christianity (Chrysostom-centered) Documented Major — In 553 a council at Constantinople condemned Theodore of Mopsuestia by name. He had been dead for more than a hundred years. Its twelfth canon curses anyone who defends him or says his reading of Scripture is orthodox.Lasting result: The Second Council of Constantinople cursed Theodore of Mopsuestia. It also cursed all who stood up for him or said the way he read Scripture was orthodox.
  • Donatism Documented Major — In 535 the emperor Justinian made a law for Africa, which his armies had won back. It named the Donatists along with Arians, Jews and pagans, and barred them from holding services. In the 590s Pope Gregory the Great wrote to the emperor Maurice. He praised the orders Maurice had given against the Donatists.Lasting result: Justinian banned Donatists, Arians, Jews and pagans from having places to meet. Nor could they hold rites as the church does.
  • Syriac Orthodox (West Syriac) Christianity Documented Major — Justinian confirmed the anathema that the sees of the patriarchs and the monks had passed on Severus of Antioch. His law said Severus's books must be burned, and it kept him out of the capital. Evagrius says it also threatened those who held to his views with the highest penalties.Lasting result: Justinian's law said every copy of Severus's writings must be burned. It also kept him out of the capital and the land around it.
  • Gothic, Vandal & Lombard Homoian Christianity Documented Major — Belisarius took Carthage in 533. The Arian priests fled, and the orthodox took back the shrine of Cyprian. Then, Procopius says, Justinian let no Christian outside the orthodox faith be baptized or share in any other sacrament. So Arian soldiers could not baptize their children.Lasting result: Justinian let no Christian outside the orthodox faith have baptism or any other rite of the church. Arian soldiers in the Roman army took part in a mutiny over it.

Opposed By

  • The Roman Church in the Age of Gregory the Great Documented — In June 595 Gregory wrote to Emperor Maurice that John, patriarch of Constantinople, was trying to be called universal bishop. He says the title is a new name, taken against the Gospel and the canons.
  • Latin African Christianity under Byzantium Documented Major — Facundus of Hermiane wrote twelve books in defense of the Three Chapters. He addressed them to Justinian himself. Victor of Tunnuna says that in 550 the bishops of Africa wrote to the emperor in their defense. He adds that Reparatus of Carthage was deposed and sent into exile because he would not condemn them.Lasting result: Reparatus of Carthage would not condemn the Three Chapters. For this he lost his office and was exiled, and his own envoy was made bishop in his place.
  • Armenian Christianity after Avarayr Dominant Modern Reconstruction Major — In 554 a council met at Dvin under the catholicos Nerses II. It confirmed that the Armenian church said no to the Council of Chalcedon. Against the claims of that council, it proclaimed the faith of Ephesus. This rests on modern histories of that church, and most of all on the one by Malachia Ormanian.Lasting result: In 554 Catholicos Nerses II held a synod at Dvin. It confirmed that the Armenian church would not accept Chalcedon, and set out the faith of Ephesus against it.

Broke With

  • Cyrilline / Miaphysite Egyptian Christianity Documented Major — In October 451 the Council of Chalcedon deposed Dioscorus, bishop of Alexandria. He had been called three times to answer his accusers, and each time he would not come. The historian Evagrius quotes a petition to Emperor Leo. It says Timothy Aelurus broke from the Catholic church right after the council. A Miaphysite chronicle of the 500s tells the other side. In it, clergy, monks and people broke off from the new bishop's communion.
  • Syriac Orthodox (West Syriac) Christianity Documented Major — Evagrius, a historian who backed the Council of Chalcedon, says Severus fled his see at Antioch in September 518. Paul took his place, and he was told to proclaim the council in the open. A Miaphysite chronicle of the same century says Severus left for fear of the emperor. In December 522 the bishop of Edessa threw out the monks who would not accept the council.

In Contact With

  • Nubian Christianity Documented — John of Ephesus says Justinian sent envoys with gold and baptismal robes to the Nobadae, asking them to join his church. The king accepted the gifts but refused the emperor's faith, and sent a written answer. John wrote for the rival party, and his is the only account of this exchange.
  • Chalcedonian Monasticism - Judean Desert & Gaza Documented — Evagrius says the patriarch of Jerusalem sent leading monks from Judea to Constantinople. This was before the council of 553. Conon was from the monastery of Saba, and Rufus was from that of Theodosius. At court it was they who raised the questions about what Origen taught.
  • The Roman Church in the Age of Gregory the Great Documented — Before he was pope, Gregory lived at Constantinople as the pope's envoy. In his Morals on Job, he recalls a debate on the resurrection with Eutychius, the city's bishop. It went on until Emperor Tiberius heard them both. Paul the Deacon tells the same story in the late 700s.
