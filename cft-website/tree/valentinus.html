Valentinus

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Valentinian & other Gnostic Christianities.

Valentinus taught at Rome in the middle of the second century and founded a school there, a circle of students around a teacher. His opponents say it taught that heavenly powers came forth in pairs, and his own few words survive only in their books.

About This Person

In a letter, Valentinus, who taught at Rome in the middle of the second century, compared the heart to an inn for travelers. It has holes and ruts and is often full of dung, and the men who stay there live filthily and take no care of a place that belongs to others.

At Rome he founded a school, a circle of students around a teacher. By his opponents' accounts, it taught that heavenly powers came forth in pairs from a first pair beyond any name, and that the world came from the fall of one of them. Of the writers near his time, Tertullian alone says he broke with the church. His students carried the school on in two branches, while the church's writers answered it with the faith in one God who made the world. For eighteen centuries his school was known almost only through their books, until writings from these circles were found in Egypt in 1945.

In the same letter he went on: "So it is with the heart, while no one gives it any thought. It is unclean, a home for many demons. But when the Father, who alone is good, visits it, it is made holy, and it shines with light." Those words survive because Clement of Alexandria quoted them to argue against him, and almost everything known of Valentinus comes from men who wrote against him.

Irenaeus says Valentinus came to Rome under one bishop and stayed until the time of a third. Tertullian, writing at Carthage early in the next century, tells why he left. "Valentinus had hoped to be made a bishop, because he had the talent and the gift of speech for it. But another man won the place, by the claim his suffering for the faith gave him. Angry at this, he broke away from the church of the true rule." By the true rule Tertullian meant that faith in one God, the maker of the world. He puts the break down to ambition, and to the wish for revenge that men who want first place feel. In another book he gives a second reason, a restless curiosity that spread to other believers, for which Valentinus was put out of the church at Rome more than once.

In Irenaeus's account, a pair beyond any name, Unspeakable and Silence, gave rise to Father and Truth, then to Word and Life, and Man and Church, and to more powers after them. One of the last broke away and fell, and from its fall came the world below and a lesser maker who made it. Tertullian says Valentinus held these powers inside God, as his thoughts, feelings and movements.

In a sermon Clement also keeps, Valentinus told his hearers they had come into death only to use it up and end it. "You are immortal from the beginning, and children of eternal life. You wanted death shared out to you, to spend it and squander it, so that death might die in you and through you." Clement answers that this makes some people saved by nature, by what they were born as, a different race come down from above. Then the commandments would be useless, he says, and salvation would come without the Savior.

Tertullian says Valentinus's follower Ptolemy made the powers into beings outside God. Hippolytus says the school split into an Italian branch, Ptolemy's, and an Eastern one.

Irenaeus wrote five books against them, aimed above all at the school of Ptolemy, which he calls a bud of Valentinus's. Tertullian wrote a book against the Valentinians, and Hippolytus a long work answering their teaching.

So far had his students moved from him, Tertullian says, that Valentinus himself was now nowhere. Yet there were still Valentinians, named after him, and only one man, Axionicus at Antioch, kept his teaching whole.

Dates
No ancient source gives a year for his birth or his death. Irenaeus says he came to Rome when Hyginus was bishop there, flourished under Pius and stayed until Anicetus. Clement and Tertullian put the teachers of his kind in the reign of the emperor Antoninus, which falls in the middle of the second century. Tertullian also places him in the church at Rome under Eleutherus, a later bishop.

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Tertullian of Carthage says his follower Ptolemy made the divine powers into separate beings outside God, where Valentinus had held them inside God. Heracleon, Secundus and Marcus the magician each struck off paths of their own.
  • Hippolytus of Rome says the school split into an Italian branch, of Heracleon and Ptolemy, and an Eastern one, of Axionicus. The Italians said the body of Jesus was a body of soul, not of spirit, and the Spirit came down on it at his baptism. The Easterners said it was spiritual, because the Holy Spirit came upon Mary.
  • Tertullian says his students had moved so far from him that Valentinus himself was now nowhere. Yet there were still Valentinians, named after him, and only Axionicus at Antioch kept his teaching whole.
  • Justin Martyr, writing in his own time, already names Valentinians among groups called after the man who began each teaching.
  • Origen of Alexandria answers the reading of John's Gospel given by Heracleon, "the friend, as it is said, of Valentinus".
  • Eusebius of Caesarea says Bardaisan of Edessa first followed Valentinus, then rejected his teaching. He also says Ambrose, who held the teaching of Valentinus, was won over by Origen.
  • Irenaeus says those who come from Valentinus gave one of their recent writings the title "the Gospel of Truth".
  • For eighteen centuries his school was known almost only through the books of the men who answered it. In 1945, writings from these circles were found in Egypt.
  • No community survives, and none claims descent.

How they're remembered

  • Irenaeus of Lyon says he adapted the principles of the Gnostic teaching, that of people who claimed a special knowledge, to his own school. Irenaeus wrote five books against what he calls knowledge falsely so called, aimed above all at Ptolemy's school, "a bud from that of Valentinus".
  • Irenaeus sets against the school the faith the church received from the apostles: one God, the Father Almighty, the maker of heaven and earth.
  • Clement of Alexandria quotes his words to argue against them. Clement says Valentinus held that some people are saved by nature, by what they were born as. Then the commandments would be useless, Clement says, and salvation would come apart from the Savior. If a different race came down to end death, "it is not Christ who has abolished death".
  • Tertullian calls him "the disciple of Platonism." He wrote a book Against the Valentinians, and puts Justin Martyr among those who wrote against such teachers in their own lifetime.
  • Hippolytus of Rome charges that he took his teaching from Pythagoras and Plato, not from the Gospels, and so should be reckoned "a Pythagorean and Platonist, not a Christian".
  • Hippolytus of Rome says Valentinus claimed to have seen a newborn child who told him he was the Word, a name for God's Son.
  • Irenaeus says Polycarp of Smyrna, at Rome when Anicetus was bishop, turned many away from Valentinus and Marcion to the church.

Open Questions

  • Almost everything known of him comes from his opponents: Irenaeus of Lyon, Tertullian of Carthage, Clement of Alexandria and Hippolytus of Rome. His own words survive in their quotations, chiefly Clement's.
  • What is his and what is his school's. Irenaeus gives a short account of Valentinus himself; his long account of the powers is of Ptolemy's school. There Irenaeus reports that the school held its own members would be saved because they were spiritual by nature. Clement gives the teaching of a class saved by nature to Valentinus himself. Tertullian sets the teacher against his pupil. Hippolytus speaks of Valentinus, Heracleon, Ptolemy and the whole school together, so the teacher and his students cannot always be told apart.
  • Why the powers came in pairs. Hippolytus gives the school's answer: the Father was alone and was all love, and "love is not love except there may be some object of affection", so he brought forth the first pair. Hippolytus does not name the man who says it.
  • The fall. Irenaeus says one of the last powers broke away and fell, and from its fall came everything outside. From that line came a lesser maker who ruled what lay below. Irenaeus gives no reason for the fall here.
  • Tertullian places him at Rome under Bishop Eleutherus. Irenaeus places him from Hyginus to Anicetus, earlier.
  • The break. Of the writers near his time, only Tertullian says he broke with the church, and he gives two reasons. In one book it is anger at losing a bishopric, with ambition and a wish for revenge. In another it is a restless curiosity that spread to other believers, for which he was put out of the church at Rome more than once. Tertullian names no city for the bishopric, no year and no rival. Irenaeus tells no break.
  • Epiphanius of Salamis, writing long after and from hearsay, says he preached at Rome, then went to Cyprus and there left the faith. No held source gives his death. Irenaeus says only that he stayed at Rome until Anicetus.
  • No early source we hold says where he was born. Epiphanius says most people do not know. He passes on, as doubtful, a report that Valentinus was born on the coast of Egypt and schooled in Greek learning at Alexandria.
  • That he was taught by Theudas, a pupil of Paul, is his followers' claim, as Clement reports it.
  • The sermon. The English print gives its author as Valentinian, but its index files the passage under Valentinus, and Clement goes straight on to answer his teaching. Clement's Greek is not held. The quotation stops where the print's next clause, on dissolving the world, mixes up "we" and "yourselves".
  • The ending. The English print says Valentinus was "as yet nowhere". Tertullian's Latin, nusquam iam, means now nowhere, and the story follows the Latin.
  • The Gospel of Truth. Irenaeus says the followers of Valentinus gave a recent writing that title. No held old source says who wrote it.
  • The psalm. In its first word and in its verb for the soul, the held Greek is an editor's correction of a damaged manuscript. The English follows another reading.
  • The Eastern branch. Hippolytus names a second man in it beside Axionicus. Wendland's Greek keeps the manuscript's name for him, Ardesianes. The English prints a corrected name, and the held old sources do not say who he was.
  • The vision of the newborn child is a wonder, told only by Hippolytus, an opponent.
