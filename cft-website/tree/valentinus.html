About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Valentinus, a teacher at Rome, once wrote that the heart is like an inn on the road. Its guests leave it rutted and full of dung, since they care nothing for a place that is not theirs. So it is, he wrote, with a heart no one looks after: "It is unclean, the home of many demons. But when the Father, who alone is good, visits it, it is made holy and shines with light." The words survive because Clement of Alexandria quoted them to argue against him.

Irenaeus places him at Rome in the middle of the second century, from the time of one bishop there to another, some twenty years. He founded a school, a circle of students around a teacher. By his opponents' account, it taught that a hidden Father lived beyond the world, that heavenly powers came from him in pairs, and that a lesser maker made this world. Almost everything we know of him comes from the men who wrote against him.

Tertullian, writing at Carthage about fifty years later, alone tells the turn in his life: "Valentinus had hoped to become a bishop, for he was able in mind and in speech. But another man won the place, by the claim his suffering for the faith had given him. Angry at this, Valentinus broke away from the church of the true rule." By that Tertullian meant the church that kept the true teaching. He puts the break down to ambition and a wish for revenge. He adds that the church at Rome expelled Valentinus more than once.

As Irenaeus tells the teaching, a pair beyond any name, Unspeakable and Silence, gave rise to Father and Truth, then Word and Life, and more powers after them. When one of the last fell away, its fall brought forth the world and its maker. Hippolytus, another opponent, reports the school's teaching differently: the Father was alone, and he was all love, and "love is not love unless there is something to love." Tertullian says Valentinus kept these powers inside God, as his thoughts and feelings, until his pupil Ptolemy made them separate beings. The accounts do not agree, and we cannot always tell the teacher from his students.

In a sermon, Valentinus told his hearers they were immortal from the beginning, children of eternal life. They wished for death to be given to them, he said, so that "death may die in you." Clement answered that this made some people saved by their nature, not by Christ's coming.

No source says when or how he died. His students carried the teaching on, and Hippolytus says the school split into an Italian branch and an Eastern one. Irenaeus, Tertullian and Hippolytus wrote long books against it, and in answering they set out what the church held, that one God made the world. For eighteen centuries the school was known almost only through those same books, until its own writings were found in Egypt in 1945.

By Tertullian's day, he says, Valentinus himself was now nowhere, though his followers still bore his name. Only one of them, Axionicus at Antioch, kept his teaching whole.

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “It is unclean, the home of many demons. But when the Father, who alone is good, visits it, it is made holy and shines with light.”
    As printed
    “being unclean, and the abode of many demons. But when the only good Father visits it, it is sanctified, and gleams with light.”
    Valentinus, a letter, in Clement of Alexandria, Stromata II.20, ANF English
    Clement's Greek is not held, so the modern English is rendered from the ANF English.
  • In our modern English
    “Valentinus had hoped to become a bishop, for he was able in mind and in speech. But another man won the place, by the claim his suffering for the faith had given him. Angry at this, Valentinus broke away from the church of the true rule.”
    As printed
    “Valentinus had expected to become a bishop, because he was an able man both in genius and eloquence. Being indignant, however, that another obtained the dignity by reason of a claim which confessorship had given him, he broke with the church of the true faith.”
    Tertullian, Against the Valentinians 4, ANF English
    The modern English is rendered from Kroymann's Latin. "Suffering for the faith" renders martyrii, where the ANF has "confessorship"; "the true rule" renders authenticae regulae, where the ANF has "the true faith".
  • In our modern English
    “love is not love unless there is something to love.”
    As printed
    “love is not love except there may be some object of affection.”
    Hippolytus of Rome, Refutation of All Heresies VI.29, ANF English
    Hippolytus gives this as the teaching of the school, and the "he" who says it is not named. The Greek is not held, so the modern English is rendered from the ANF English.
  • In our modern English
    “death may die in you”
    As printed
    “death may die in you and by you”
    Valentinus, a sermon, in Clement of Alexandria, Stromata IV.13, ANF English
    The ANF prints "Valentinian"; its index files the passage under Valentinus, and Clement goes on to answer his teaching. The modern words leave out "and by you".

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Tertullian of Carthage names Ptolemaeus, Heraclean, Secundus and Marcus the magician as teachers of his school, and Axionicus at Antioch as the one who kept his rules in full.

How they're remembered

  • Irenaeus of Lyon says he adapted the principles of the Gnostic teaching to his own school.
  • Tertullian calls him "the disciple of Platonism."
  • Hippolytus of Rome says Valentinus claimed to have seen a newborn child who told him he was the Word.

What Is Uncertain

  • Almost everything known of him comes from his opponents: Irenaeus of Lyon, Tertullian of Carthage, Clement of Alexandria and Hippolytus of Rome. His own words survive in their quotations, chiefly Clement's.
  • The teaching of paired divine powers is described by two opponents, Irenaeus and Hippolytus, though Hippolytus copies long passages of Irenaeus elsewhere in his book, as the American edition's note on his Book 6 shows. The division of people by nature is reported by Irenaeus and Clement, of his followers. The story of the lost bishopric is Tertullian's alone.
  • Tertullian places him at Rome under Bishop Eleutherus. Irenaeus places him from Hyginus to Anicetus, earlier.
  • That he was taught by Theudas, a pupil of Paul, is his followers' claim, as Clement reports it.
  • No early source we hold says where he was born.
