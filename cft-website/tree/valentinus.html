About This Person

Tertullian of Carthage says that Valentinus once expected to become a bishop, because he was "an able man both in genius and eloquence." Another man was chosen, because he had suffered for the faith as a confessor. Valentinus, Tertullian says, "broke with the church of the true faith." Tertullian was his opponent, writing decades later, and he alone tells this story. Almost everything we know of Valentinus comes from writers who opposed him.

The outline of his life rests mainly on Irenaeus of Lyon. He says Valentinus "came to Rome in the time of Hyginus, flourished under Pius, and remained until Anicetus." These were three bishops of Rome in turn, in the middle of the second century. Tertullian dates him later, under Bishop Eleutherus. He calls Valentinus "the disciple of Platonism," and says he and Marcion of Pontus, another teacher at Rome, were put out of the church at Rome more than once.

Two opponents, Irenaeus and Hippolytus of Rome, describe the same teaching, though Hippolytus copies long passages of Irenaeus elsewhere in his book. Both set out a heaven of divine powers that came forth in pairs. In both, one of the first pairs includes Truth. Irenaeus names the first pairs as the Unspeakable and Silence, then the Father and Truth, then the Word and Life, and Man and Church. In his account one of the later powers fell, and from that fall came the rest of the universe. Irenaeus and Clement of Alexandria both report that his followers divided people by nature. Clement says they left faith to "the simple," and kept knowledge for themselves.

Clement also quotes from Valentinus's own letters. In one, the heart is like a roadside inn, a caravanserai, full of holes and dung and home to many demons. "But when the only good Father visits it," Valentinus wrote, "it is sanctified, and gleams with light."

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Tertullian of Carthage names Ptolemaeus, Heraclean, Secundus and Marcus the magician as teachers of his school, and Axionicus at Antioch as the one who kept his rules in full.

How they're remembered

  • Irenaeus of Lyon says he adapted the principles of the Gnostic teaching to his own school.
  • Tertullian calls him "the disciple of Platonism."
  • Hippolytus of Rome says Valentinus claimed to have seen a newborn child who told him he was the Word.

What Is Uncertain

  • Almost everything known of him comes from his opponents: Irenaeus of Lyon, Tertullian of Carthage, Clement of Alexandria and Hippolytus of Rome. His own words survive in their quotations, chiefly Clement's.
  • The teaching of paired divine powers is described by two opponents, Irenaeus and Hippolytus, though Hippolytus copies long passages of Irenaeus elsewhere in his book, as the American edition's note on his Book 6 shows. The division of people by nature is reported by Irenaeus and Clement, of his followers. The story of the lost bishopric is Tertullian's alone.
  • Tertullian places him at Rome under Bishop Eleutherus. Irenaeus places him from Hyginus to Anicetus, earlier.
  • That he was taught by Theudas, a pupil of Paul, is his followers' claim, as Clement reports it.
  • No source we hold says where he was born.
