The Kyiv-Mohyla Orthodox Revival

Kyiv, 1615 to 1700 — Orthodoxy answering Counter-Reformation pressure by building an academy, a catechism and a confession of its own.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 0:57

Read along

Orthodox Kyiv answered the Union of Brest not with a lament but with a school. A lay brotherhood founded one in 1615, and Petro Mohyla, metropolitan from 1633, turned it into a college on the Jesuit model — Latin, rhetoric, philosophy, theology. On the reasoning that a church cannot defend itself against arguments it cannot read. He also gave the Kyivan church its own instruments. An Orthodox Confession of faith, approved in revised form by a council at Iaşi in 1642, a short catechism, and a great service book, the Trebnyk of 1646, which regularized how priests were actually to celebrate the sacraments. It is the clearest case anywhere of a tradition taking up its rivals' tools in order to remain itself.

Documented Stories

Feed for the Sturgeon of the Dnieper

When Petro Mohyla, then archimandrite of the Kyiv Caves monastery, opened a school at the monastery in 1631 with Latin as its language and the Jesuit colleges as its model, the reaction in Kyiv was not gratitude. The Cossacks and the burghers who had defended Orthodoxy against the union for a generation could not see the difference between a Latin school and a Latin church, and word went round that the teachers were secret Uniates. Sylvester Kossov, the school's prefect, wrote three years later that there had been a time when he and his colleagues, having made their confessions, expected at any moment to be seized and used to feed the sturgeon of the Dnieper, or to be burned. It took the intervention of the brotherhood and the Cossack hetman to make the school safe, and in 1632 it was merged with the brotherhood's own school on the Podil as a compromise, under the brotherhood's oversight and Mohyla's protection. Kossov's Exegesis of 1635 was written to answer the charge that Latin schooling was foreign to Orthodoxy: without Latin, he wrote, Orthodox children would be fools before the courts, the Diet and their Catholic neighbors, and would go to the Jesuits for what their own church could not give them. He became metropolitan of Kyiv himself in 1647, and the college outlived the Commonwealth.

Digging Up the Church of the Tithes

Kyiv in Mohyla's time was a city of ruins, with the churches of the Kyivan princes lying broken since the Mongol sack of 1240 and several of the best preserved in Uniate hands. Mohyla made their recovery a program. He regained St Sophia from the Uniates in 1633 and began repairing it; he rebuilt the Church of the Saviour at Berestove; and in 1635 he had the rubble of the Church of the Tithes, Vladimir's first stone church, cleared, and announced that among the foundations two sarcophagi had been found, one of them holding the remains of St Vladimir himself. He took the skull and other bones, placed the skull in the Dormition cathedral of the Caves monastery, sent a jawbone to the tsar in Moscow, and built a small church over the site. The monastery's press published the finds in the Teraturgema of 1638, a catalog of the monastery's miracles by the monk Afanasij Kal'nofojs'kyj, which set out, with maps, what Kyiv had been. Historians doubt the identification, and the bones were dispersed after 1917. What is not in doubt is what Mohyla was doing: giving the Orthodox of the Commonwealth back the city of their baptism, in stone, as an answer to the argument, made by Uniate and Catholic polemicists alike, that theirs was a church without a past.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Orthodox Kyiv answered the Union of Brest not with a lament but with a school: a lay brotherhood founded a college in 1615, and Metropolitan Petro Mohyla built it on the Jesuit model — Latin, rhetoric, philosophy, theology — on the reasoning that a church cannot defend itself against arguments it cannot read. He also gave the church its own confession of faith and a service book, the Trebnyk of 1646, that fixed how priests actually celebrated the sacraments.

Legacy

The Kyiv Mohyla Academy became the leading school of the Orthodox Slavic world. Moscow drew on its learning. The tsar asked Kyiv for monks from the academy. Later Stephen Yavorsky, sent to Moscow from Kyiv, led the Russian church under Peter the Great. Mohyla's Confession was read far beyond Ukraine. The four Eastern patriarchs approved it in 1643, and it stayed a standard Orthodox guide into modern times. The academy was shut under Soviet rule. It opened again in 1991 and is now one of the best universities in Ukraine.

How Sure the Record Is

  • Mohyla's confessional corpus: the Orthodox Confession (Kyiv synod 1640; Syrigos's revision approved Iasi 1642; Eng. trans. from Overbeck 1898 onward) and the Short Catechism (1645) (primary) — The printed Confession is Syrigos's Greek revision, not exactly the original Kyiv text; the 1645 Short Catechism stays closer to Mohyla's own hand. Together they show Orthodoxy answering Reformation and Tridentine pressure with its own confessional formation.
  • The Trebnyk of 1646 (Mohyla's Euchologion) with its pastoral rubrics (primary) — The revival's most parish-facing text — formation carried out through worship itself.
  • Academy and brotherhood records: the Kyiv Epiphany brotherhood statutes and school records, the Mohyla Collegium's curriculum, disputation, and panegyric materials (primary) — Latin-style schooling put in service of Orthodoxy — the underlying identity tension is visible in the syllabus itself.
  • The apologetic-patristic layer: Kopystens'kyj's Palinodija (1620s) and the Kyiv Caves Lavra press's patristic and hagiographic printing program (primary) — The revival's scholarly self-defense and publishing arm.
