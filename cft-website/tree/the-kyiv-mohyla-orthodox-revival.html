The Reformation Era · 1615-1700 · Kyiv

Kyiv, today in Ukraine

The Kyiv-Mohyla Orthodox Revival

About This Movement

Orthodox Kyiv answered the Union of Brest not with a lament but with a school. A lay brotherhood founded one in 1615, and Petro Mohyla, metropolitan from 1633, turned it into a college on the Jesuit model — Latin, rhetoric, philosophy, theology. On the reasoning that a church cannot defend itself against arguments it cannot read. He also gave the Kyivan church its own instruments. An Orthodox Confession of faith, approved in revised form by a council at Iaşi in 1642, a short catechism, and a great service book, the Trebnyk of 1646, which regularized how priests were actually to celebrate the sacraments. It is the clearest case anywhere of a tradition taking up its rivals' tools in order to remain itself.

In Its Own Record

Influenced By

  • The Society of Jesus Dominant Modern Reconstruction — A memoir printed in 1814 says the Kyiv academy was set up after the manner of the Polish schools, and Latin became its main language. In 1883 the historian Stefan Golubev wrote about the college. He said the showy learning and clever speech of Western colleges, the Jesuit ones above all, took deep root there at once.

Influenced

  • Orthodoxy under the Ottomans - Parish, Millet & Neomartyrs Documented — Mohyla's circle in Kyiv sent its statement of the faith to the church of Constantinople to be judged. The patriarch's envoys and three Kyiv scholars worked on it together in Moldavia. In March 1643 the four Eastern patriarchs told every Orthodox Christian to read it and take it to heart.
  • The Russian Church — Nikon to the Holy Synod Widely Accepted — Thomas Consett, an English chaplain in Russia, wrote in 1729 that the Russians still used Mohyla's Slavonic service book. A Russian church history says the tsar asked the head of the church in Kyiv for monks from his school. Stephen Yavorsky, sent to Moscow from Kyiv, later led the Russian church under Peter the Great.

Argued Against

  • The Union of Brest & Ruthenian Greek Catholic Christianity Documented — In his own notes Petro Mohyla called the union a wicked falling away from the faith. In August 1628 a synod at the Caves monastery in Kyiv sent envoys to Meletius Smotrytsky. They asked about his Apology, a book that leaned toward Rome. The envoy told him the synod held the whole book to be against the faith and wicked.

Sources

  • Mohyla's confessional corpus: the Orthodox Confession (Kyiv synod 1640; Syrigos's revision approved Iasi 1642; Eng. trans. from Overbeck 1898 onward) and the Short Catechism (1645) (primary) — The printed Confession is Syrigos's Greek revision, not exactly the original Kyiv text; the 1645 Short Catechism stays closer to Mohyla's own hand. Together they show Orthodoxy answering Reformation and Tridentine pressure with its own confessional formation.
  • The Trebnyk of 1646 (Mohyla's Euchologion) with its pastoral rubrics (primary) — The revival's most parish-facing text — formation carried out through worship itself.
  • Academy and brotherhood records: the Kyiv Epiphany brotherhood statutes and school records, the Mohyla Collegium's curriculum, disputation, and panegyric materials (primary) — Latin-style schooling put in service of Orthodoxy — the underlying identity tension is visible in the syllabus itself.
  • The apologetic-patristic layer: Kopystens'kyj's Palinodija (1620s) and the Kyiv Caves Lavra press's patristic and hagiographic printing program (primary) — The revival's scholarly self-defense and publishing arm.
  • Ihor Sevcenko, 'The Many Worlds of Peter Mohyla' (HUS, 1984), with current Kyivan-academy scholarship (Sharipova/Charipova) (secondary) — A classic orientation essay, plus current archive-based studies.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 6, ch. 18 'Latin Christianity and eastern Orthodoxy' (Dmitriev) (reference) — No dedicated chapter on this Ruthenian revival was found in volume 5.
