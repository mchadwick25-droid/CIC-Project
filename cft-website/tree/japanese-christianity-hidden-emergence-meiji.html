About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:46

Read along

On 17 March 1865, weeks after a French church opened in Nagasaki's foreign settlement, a group of villagers from nearby Urakami came in, and one woman quietly told the priest that the hearts of all present were the same as his, and asked where the statue of Mary was. They were descendants of Christians driven underground in the 1600s, who had kept baptism, prayers and a liturgical calendar for over two hundred years without a single priest. In the months that followed tens of thousands more made themselves known. The government's ban was still in force, and the Urakami villagers were deported and held until 1873. When it was lifted, a Christianity of three kinds grew quickly in Meiji Japan. Catholic parishes rebuilt around the emerging hidden communities, Protestant bands of samurai-class students at Yokohama, Kumamoto and Sapporo, and a Russian Orthodox mission that Nikolai of Japan had begun in 1861 and staffed largely with Japanese clergy. Not all the hidden Christians rejoined: some, the Kakure, kept the tradition exactly as they had received it, and remain separate to this day.

The priests who met them belonged to the Paris Foreign Missions Society. In his history of the church's return, Marnas wrote in 1896 that this Society found the descendants of the old Christians in 1865. He said it was born long ago from the inspiration of Alexandre de Rhodes.

Documented Stories

The Fourth Fall of Urakami

The Urakami villagers who had told Father Petitjean at Oura in March 1865 that their hearts were the same as his had stopped calling the Buddhist priest to their funerals, and in 1867 the Nagasaki magistrate arrested their leaders. The new imperial government, which had reposted the old edict boards against the evil sect, decided in 1868 to remove the problem entirely. In three waves between 1868 and 1870 some 3,400 people, the whole Catholic population of Urakami, were shipped and marched to twenty domains, split so that no community remained, and handed to local lords to be persuaded to apostatize. The persuasion varied by domain. In many, people were confined, starved and lectured; in some, beaten or put naked in the snow; in Tsuwano, where 153 were sent, officials built a cage three feet square in which those who would not recant were kept through the winter, and a young man named Yasutarō died there after twenty days. The Catholic accounts count some 660 dead, a fifth of the deportees; about a third of the survivors recanted, most returning to the faith afterwards. The Western ministers protested, and in 1873, when the Iwakura mission was told in every Western capital that treaties would not be revised while Christians were persecuted, the government took down the boards and let the survivors go home. They called the exile tabi, the journey. They came back to fields given away, and in 1895 began the cathedral that stood until August 1945.

Nikolai Tells His Church to Pray for Japan

Nikolai Kasatkin had come to Hakodate in 1861 as consular chaplain, had learned Japanese for eight years before his first convert, and by 1904 was archbishop of a Japanese Orthodox Church of some thirty thousand members with a clergy almost entirely Japanese, since he held that Japan must be converted by Japanese. On 10 February 1904 Japan declared war on Russia. The Russian legation left; Nikolai, sixty-seven, stayed, and the press asked whether the Orthodox were Russian agents. On 11 February he issued a pastoral letter to the whole church, and recorded in his diary what it cost him. The faithful were to do their duty as subjects of the emperor: to pray for the Japanese armies' victory, to serve in them when called, and to remember that the church was not Russian but Christ's. He himself, he wrote, could not pray for Russia's defeat, and so withdrew from the cathedral's services for the war's duration and prayed alone. The government posted police to protect him and let him be. When seventy thousand Russian prisoners of war arrived in Japan from 1904, he organized the Japanese priests to minister to them in the camps, and the diary records the Japanese Orthodox raising money for Russian prisoners while their sons fought Russians. The church lost few members; the emperor decorated him after the peace; and he died in 1912, mourned in both countries. The cathedral on the Kanda hill is still called Nikolai-dō.

In Its Own Record

On 17 March 1865, Urakami villagers revealed themselves at Ōura, and roughly 30,000 hidden believers came forward in the months that followed. The Urakami community faced a final persecution until 1873. Some, the Kakure, chose to stay separate from the visible church. Protestant bands grew alongside, including Uchimura Kanzo's Mukyōkai movement founded in 1901, and Nikolai's Russian Orthodox mission dated from 1861.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

On 17 March 1865 a group of villagers walked into a newly opened church in Nagasaki and quietly told the priest their hearts were the same as his, descendants of Christians driven underground more than two centuries earlier who had kept baptism, prayer and a liturgical calendar with no priest at all. Not everyone who surfaced rejoined the visible church; some, the Kakure, chose to keep the faith exactly as they had received it, and remain separate to this day.

Legacy

Christianity in Japan stayed small — roughly one per cent of the population then and now — but its institutions outlasted the century. Doshisha and other mission-founded universities, the Orthodox cathedral in Tokyo that Nikolai built, and a Catholic community around Nagasaki that is still the largest in the country. The hidden Christians' story became the basis of a UNESCO World Heritage listing of Nagasaki-region sites in 2018, and it has had a long literary afterlife, most famously in Endo Shusaku's novel Silence.
