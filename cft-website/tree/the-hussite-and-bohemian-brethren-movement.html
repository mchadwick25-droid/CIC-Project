The Hussite & Bohemian Brethren Movement

Bohemia, 1415 to 1517 — a whole country that took communion in both kinds, was crusaded against five times for it, and kept its own church anyway.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:22

Read along

Jan Hus preached reform in Czech at Prague's Bethlehem Chapel for more than ten years. Then in 1415 the Council of Constance burned him as a heretic. The shock turned a university movement into a national one. Its emblem was the chalice. It stood for the demand that ordinary lay people, and not only priests, should receive the wine at communion. Its program was set out in the Four Articles of Prague in 1420. Crusades were called against Bohemia, and the Hussite armies under Jan Žižka and then Prokop the Bald beat them one after another. In 1436 the Compacts of Basel granted what no other medieval dissent ever won: legal recognition. Two bodies grew out of that settlement. The Utraquist church held the parishes, and for a time, under George of Poděbrady, the crown itself. Then from 1457 came the Unity of the Brethren. It was a smaller, stricter, pacifist body, formed at Kunvald under the influence of the layman Petr Chelčický. By 1501 it had printed what is often called the first hymnbook in a European vernacular.

Documented Stories

The Sixth of July at Constance

Peter of Mladoňovice was a Bohemian notary who had come to Constance in Hus’s party and attended every session; his Relatio is the eyewitness account, and it is precise. On 6 July the council met in the cathedral with the emperor Sigismund presiding. Hus was made to stand on a platform while the charges were read; when he tried to answer he was silenced, and he knelt and prayed aloud instead. He was condemned as a heretic and his books ordered burned; seven bishops vested him as a priest and then unvested him piece by piece, scraping his tonsure, and set a paper crown on his head painted with three devils and the word Heresiarch, saying they committed his soul to the devil; Hus answered that he committed it to Christ. Peter followed the procession out of the city gate to a meadow between the gardens, where Hus was tied to a stake with a sooty chain, straw and wood piled to his chin. The marshal asked him once more to recant; he said he had taught nothing but the truth of the Gospel and would die gladly in it. He sang ‘Christ, son of the living God, have mercy on me’ until the wind blew the flames into his face. Peter describes the executioners breaking up the bones and burning the heart separately, and the ashes carted to the Rhine so that the Bohemians should have no relic.

The Brethren at Kunvald Elect Their Own Priests by Lot

Gregory, called the Patriarch, was a nephew of the Utraquist archbishop-elect Rokycana and a former monk who had heard Rokycana preach that the church of the chalice had become as worldly as Rome, and had read Petr Chelčický’s Net of Faith, which held that a Christian could not swear, fight, hold office or own more than he needed. In 1457 or 1458 he led a small group to the village of Kunvald in north-east Bohemia, on land belonging to the king’s administrator George of Poděbrady, to live by Chelčický’s teaching: manual work, no oaths, no litigation, discipline by the community. In 1467, at a secret assembly in the village of Lhotka near Rychnov, the Brethren decided that they could no longer depend on Utraquist priests they did not trust and would have their own. Their own account of the day survives. Nine men were chosen as candidates; twelve slips of paper were prepared, nine blank and three marked ‘jest’ — ‘it is’ — and a boy drew them; the three men who received a marked slip, Matthias of Kunvald among them, were declared chosen by God, and were then ordained by a Waldensian elder who had been brought from Austria, and by the assembly’s own laying-on of hands. It was excommunicated by Rokycana, persecuted by the king who had given it land, and survived to print a hymnbook in 1501 and, much later, to become the Moravian church.

Connections

In Its Own Record

A working non-Roman national church a century before Luther. It took shape in Prague between 1402 and 1414, before Hus was burned. Hus drew heavily on the books of John Wyclif, the English thinker whose teaching also began Lollardy. Its stricter branch, the Unity of the Brethren, lived on into the 1600s. It was later renewed as the Moravian Church at Herrnhut in Saxony.

On 6 July 1415 the Council of Constance condemned Wyclif's errors. It sentenced Hus the same day. Wyclif's teaching began Lollardy. But this shows his influence on Hus, not that English Lollard groups shaped the Bohemians.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Jan Hus preached reform in Czech for over ten years. Then in 1415 he was burned as a heretic. The shock turned a movement of the university into one of the whole nation. It was built around one demand: that ordinary lay people, not only priests, should receive the wine at communion. Hussite armies beat back crusade after crusade. In 1436 Bohemia won what no other medieval dissent ever had: legal recognition for its own church. A stricter branch, the Unity of the Brethren, was pacifist. By 1501 it had printed what is often called the first hymnbook in a European vernacular.

Legacy

It did not end in 1517. The Utraquist church and the Unity of the Brethren went on as Bohemia's non-Roman churches for another full century. They produced the Kralice Bible. Their last bishop, Jan Amos Comenius, became one of Europe's great reformers of schooling. Then the Habsburg crackdown of the 1620s drove them into exile. Remnants of the Unity lived on in secret and came back to light in 1722 at Herrnhut in Saxony, as the Moravian Church. That church still exists worldwide. In Bohemia the memory never went away. 6 July, the day Hus was burned, is a public holiday in the Czech Republic. And in 1520 Luther wrote that he had not yet found any errors in Hus's writings.

How Sure the Record Is

Rich: confessions, hymnody, discipline records

  • The Letters of John Hus (trans. Matthew Spinka, Manchester, 1972) (primary) — All 105 surviving letters and documents, from Latin and Czech - his own voice, including the Constance prison letters.
  • Peter of Mladonovice, John Hus at the Council of Constance (trans. Spinka, Columbia, 1965) (primary) — The eyewitness Relatio by Hus's companion - a sympathetic account that also embeds the council's own hostile record of the charges and condemnation.
  • The Four Articles of Prague (1420), Hussite and Taborite manifestos, war songs, and chronicle excerpts, in Thomas Fudge, The Crusade against Heretics in Bohemia, 1418-1437 (Crusade Texts in Translation 9, Ashgate, 2002) (primary) — 208 documents from Czech, German, and Latin, most translated into English for the first time - the movement's own program and its enemies' bulls printed side by side.
  • The Unity of Brethren (from 1457): Gregory the Patriarch's letters, the Brethren's confessions, discipline ordinances, and hymnody culminating in the first printed vernacular hymnal (1501); Peter Chelcicky's Net of Faith as its intellectual spring (primary) — Confessions, hymnody, and discipline records from the movement's second, pacifist generation. English translations exist only piecemeal, mostly through Moravian-tradition scholarship.
  • Howard Kaminsky, A History of the Hussite Revolution (California, 1967), with Thomas Fudge, Jan Hus: Religious Reform and Social Revolution in Bohemia (2010) (secondary) — The standard history, alongside a current entry.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 4, ch. 28 'Wyclif's Bohemian reception' (Ghosh) and ch. 15 'Sacramental life' (Rubin) (reference) — No chapter is dedicated specifically to the Hussites; these are the nearest anchors - one on Wyclif's Bohemian reception, the other on utraquism as a sacramental claim.
