The Late Medieval Era · 1402-1517 · Bohemia

Bohemia, today in Czech Republic

The Hussite & Bohemian Brethren Movement

About This Movement

Jan Hus preached reform in Czech at Prague's Bethlehem Chapel for more than ten years. Then in 1415 the Council of Constance burned him as a heretic. The shock turned a university movement into a national one. Its emblem was the chalice. It stood for the demand that ordinary lay people, and not only priests, should receive the wine at communion. Its program was set out in the Four Articles of Prague in 1420. Crusades were called against Bohemia, and the Hussite armies under Jan Žižka and then Prokop the Bald beat them one after another. In 1436 the Compacts of Basel granted what no other medieval dissent ever won: legal recognition. Two bodies grew out of that settlement. The Utraquist church held the parishes, and for a time, under George of Poděbrady, the crown itself. Then from 1457 came the Unity of the Brethren. It was a smaller, stricter, pacifist body, formed at Kunvald under the influence of the layman Petr Chelčický. By 1501 it had printed what is often called the first hymnbook in a European vernacular.

In Its Own Record

Gave Rise To

  • Taborite Radicalism Documented Major — The Prague writer Laurence of Březová tells how, in March 1420, brethren who stood for the chalice left Plzeň with their priests. They went to the mountain of Hradiště, also called Tabor, and the brethren already there took them in.

Continued As

  • The Czech Churches' Last Century (Utraquists and Unitas) Documented Major — The Unity of the Brethren met in synod once or twice a year from its founding and wrote down its decrees. In 1617 a brother sorted them by the Unity's periods, from "the beginning of the Unity" onward. The Moravian historian David Cranz traced the same church from 1457 to its scattering in 1627.

Influenced By

  • Lollardy Widely Accepted — The English editor of Hus's On the Church reports that Hus took paragraph after paragraph from Wyclif's treatise on the same subject. On 6 July the Council of Constance condemned Wyclif's errors and sentenced Hus the same day. Wyclif's teaching began Lollardy, but this shows his influence on Hus, not that English Lollard communities shaped the Bohemians.

Influenced

  • Lutheran Wittenberg & Its Congregations Documented — In his 1520 letter to the German nobility, Luther wrote that he had not yet found any errors in Hus's writings. He said Hus's books and teaching were unjustly condemned, and he urged union with the Bohemians.

Argued Against

  • The Latin Papal Church (Avignon to Fifth Lateran) Documented — In De ecclesia (1413) Jan Hus argued that neither the pope nor the cardinals are the head of the universal church. He wrote that Christ alone is its head and the predestinate are its body.
  • Taborite Radicalism Documented Major — Tabor's own chronicle says the Prague masters attacked its priests for dropping vestments and the Roman rite. It tells of a dispute between them in 1421. In 1434 Prague's Old Town and its allies marched against the Taborites, and in 1452 the Utraquist regent George of Poděbrady took Tabor.Lasting result: In 1452 George of Poděbrady, regent of Bohemia, took Tabor. He forced the moderate Utraquist service on the town.

Opposed By

  • The Latin Papal Church (Avignon to Fifth Lateran) Documented — The diary of Cardinal Fillastre records that on 6 July 1415 the Council of Constance condemned Jan Hus as a heretic. It stripped him of his orders and handed him to the secular court. The same session condemned Wyclif's errors.

In Contact With

  • The Latin Papal Church (Avignon to Fifth Lateran) Documented Major — In January 1434 the legates of the Council of Basel took Prague masters back into communion. The masters had agreed to stand by the Compacts. The Compacts were sealed at Jihlava in July 1436, and the Hussite war was over.Lasting result: In 1434 the Council of Basel's legates let Prague masters who had accepted the Compacts back into the church. They told all Christians to share worship with them.

Sources

  • The Letters of John Hus (trans. Matthew Spinka, Manchester, 1972) (primary) — All 105 surviving letters and documents, from Latin and Czech - his own voice, including the Constance prison letters.
  • Peter of Mladonovice, John Hus at the Council of Constance (trans. Spinka, Columbia, 1965) (primary) — The eyewitness Relatio by Hus's companion - a sympathetic account that also embeds the council's own hostile record of the charges and condemnation.
  • The Four Articles of Prague (1420), Hussite and Taborite manifestos, war songs, and chronicle excerpts, in Thomas Fudge, The Crusade against Heretics in Bohemia, 1418-1437 (Crusade Texts in Translation 9, Ashgate, 2002) (primary) — 208 documents from Czech, German, and Latin, most translated into English for the first time - the movement's own program and its enemies' bulls printed side by side.
  • The Unity of Brethren (from 1457): Gregory the Patriarch's letters, the Brethren's confessions, discipline ordinances, and hymnody culminating in the first printed vernacular hymnal (1501); Peter Chelcicky's Net of Faith as its intellectual spring (primary) — Confessions, hymnody, and discipline records from the movement's second, pacifist generation. English translations exist only piecemeal, mostly through Moravian-tradition scholarship.
  • Howard Kaminsky, A History of the Hussite Revolution (California, 1967), with Thomas Fudge, Jan Hus: Religious Reform and Social Revolution in Bohemia (2010) (secondary) — The standard history, alongside a current entry.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 4, ch. 28 'Wyclif's Bohemian reception' (Ghosh) and ch. 15 'Sacramental life' (Rubin) (reference) — No chapter is dedicated specifically to the Hussites; these are the nearest anchors - one on Wyclif's Bohemian reception, the other on utraquism as a sacramental claim.
