The Imperial Church Era · c. 390 · Rome

Rome, today in Italy

Jovinian

A person whose story no single tradition tells.

About This Person

Jovinian is known only through Jerome and Ambrose. No tradition on this map tells his story, and none of his own writing survives whole. What remains is what his opponents chose to quote.

Jerome's editor calls him a Roman monk, or rather a solitary, who put off the monk's dress and lived like other men. He wrote a book in Latin at Rome. Jerome quotes four points from it. Baptized virgins, widows and wives are of equal merit. Those reborn in baptism with full faith cannot be overthrown by the devil. Fasting is no better than eating with thanks. And all who keep their baptismal vow share one reward in heaven.

Pammachius, a friend of Jerome, brought the book to Siricius, bishop of Rome. By the editors' account, synods at Rome and at Milan condemned it about 390. Ambrose and his synod wrote back to Siricius to join the sentence. In a letter to the church at Vercelli, Ambrose warned against two monks who said there is 'no merit in abstinence' and none in virginity. His editor takes them for followers of Jovinian.

In 393 Jerome answered the book with two books of his own, Against Jovinianus. He calls Jovinian 'the Epicurus of Christianity'. Even Jerome's editor gives a warning here. He says Jerome's charges about the man's life are guesses drawn from his views. They are not known facts.

In Its Own Record

Opposed By

  • Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity Documented — In 393 Jerome answered Jovinian's book in two books. He says the brothers at Rome sent him the work and asked him to crush it. He quotes Jovinian's points, among them that baptized virgins, widows and wives are of equal merit.
  • Ambrosian Milan (standalone) Widely Accepted — About 390, Ambrose and a synod at Milan wrote back to Siricius of Rome. They joined his sentence on Jovinian. That letter is known here only from the editor's summary. In a letter to Vercelli, Ambrose attacks two monks who taught 'no merit in abstinence'. His editor takes them for Jovinian's men.
  • Imperial and Juridical Christianity Inferential-Thin — Pammachius brought Jovinian's book to Siricius, bishop of Rome, and a synod at Rome condemned it about 390. Siricius then sent word of the sentence to Milan. His own letter is not held here; both facts come from the editors of Jerome and Ambrose.
