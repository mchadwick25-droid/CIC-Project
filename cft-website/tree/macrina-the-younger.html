Macrina the Younger

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Cappadocian Christianity.

Known for

About This Person

In 379, about nine months after their brother Basil of Caesarea died, Gregory of Nyssa went to see his sister Macrina in Pontus. They had not met for nearly eight years. He found her lying on the floor on a sack spread over a board, worn out by fever. She raised herself on her elbow, but could not get up to meet him.

Macrina the Younger was the eldest child of a Christian family of Pontus, named for her grandmother, who had confessed Christ in the persecutions. Gregory's Life of her is a loving record, written in her honor, and it tells dreams and healings as signs from God.

When she was about twelve, Gregory says, her father chose a young man for her to marry, but he died. She held that her father's choice already bound her, and refused all the suitors who came after. Basil came home from his studies "puffed up beyond measure with the pride of oratory." Gregory says she drew him to a life of prayer and of work with his hands. She also persuaded their mother to give up her servants and live as an equal with her maids. W. K. Lowther Clarke, who translated the Life in 1916, reads their home as a double monastery. The women were under Macrina and the men under her brother Peter of Sebaste. In a famine, she took in children left by the roadside and raised them.

Gregory later wrote down their talks at her deathbed as a dialogue, On the Soul and the Resurrection. It is his written form of her teaching, and in it he calls her "the Teacher." When he doubted that the soul lives on after death, Gregory writes, "Away, she cried, with that pagan nonsense!"

Near the end, Gregory says, she stopped speaking to those around her and spoke to God in prayer. "Thou, O Lord, hast freed us from the fear of death," she began.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Gregory of Nyssa says she drew their brother Basil from the pride of oratory to a life of prayer and work. Gregory of Nazianzus, Basil's friend, says Basil had already resolved on that life. Blomfield Jackson, who translated Basil's letters, gives both, and reads Macrina as giving Basil the resolve to carry it out.
  • W. K. Lowther Clarke reads the community she led with her brother Peter of Sebaste as a double monastery, and says the Life throws light on such houses in early times.
  • Gregory's dialogue On the Soul and the Resurrection gives its teaching in her voice.

How they're remembered

  • Gregory calls her "the Teacher" in the dialogue, and "the great Macrina" in the Life.
  • William Moore and Henry Austin Wilson, who translated the dialogue, write that "the Church should possess her Phædo, or Death-bed Dialogue", and that Gregory supplied it.
  • Lampadia, the deaconess who led the sisters, told Gregory at her death, "you have in front of you all her treasure."

What Is Uncertain

  • Her life is known from her brother Gregory. His Life of her is a loving record, written in her honor, and it tells dreams and healings as signs from God. Clarke holds that the plain style of its later pages shows a true account written soon after the events.
  • On the Soul and the Resurrection is Gregory's written form of their talk. It does not say how much of the argument is hers and how much his. Moore and Wilson think it was probably written in 380.
  • The double monastery is Clarke's reading. He says it is not clear whether Basil's own monastery across the Iris still stood when Gregory came.
  • The healing of a tumor by the sign of the cross was told to Gregory by Vestiana, a widow who lived with the sisters, after Macrina's death. The healing of a soldier's young daughter's eye was told to him by her father. Gregory says he leaves out more wonders he heard, among them corn that did not run out in a famine. The secret name Thecla comes from a dream of her mother's, as Gregory tells it.
  • The Life speaks of four sons and five daughters, and an estate divided in nine parts. Clarke says the parents had ten children, of whom those nine survived infancy. Moore and Wilson say the father left ten children, five sons and five daughters.
