Korean Catholic Origins

Korea, 1784 to 1886 — the one church in the world that converted itself out of books, and then paid for it with some ten thousand lives.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:33

Read along

Korea is the only country in the world where the Catholic Church began without a missionary. In the late eighteenth century a circle of Confucian scholars near Seoul studied Chinese-language Catholic books, convinced themselves, and sent one of their number, Yi Sung-hun, to Beijing to be baptized in 1784. He came home and baptized the others, and a church of several thousand people existed before a priest had ever set foot in the country. It was written in Korean from the start — Chong Yak-chong's catechism was in the vernacular script rather than in the scholarly Chinese of the elite. Which is part of why it reached women, servants and the poor as fast as it reached the literati. The Choson state read it as treason against the Confucian order, and the church spent a century under persecution, with something on the order of ten thousand Korean Catholics killed in successive purges between 1801 and 1866, until a treaty with France in 1886 finally brought the killing to an end.

The Paris Foreign Missions Society had an establishment in Korea.

In 1866, says the Catholic Encyclopedia of 1908, two bishops and seven priests of the Society were beheaded in March. Only three priests of the Society were left, it says: Ridel, Féron and Calais.

Documented Stories

Yun Ji-chung Burns the Tablets

The church the Korean scholars had founded in 1784 without a priest had written to the bishop of Beijing for guidance, and in 1790 the answer came back on the question that mattered most: that the rites to the ancestors, the offerings and prostrations before the tablets that held the dead, were forbidden to Christians by Rome's decision of 1742. For a Confucian society the rites were the substance of filial piety, on which the family and the state rested, and to abandon them was to declare oneself no longer human. Many of the scholar-founders left the church at that news. Yun Ji-chung, baptized Paul, a young man of the gentry of Jinsan in Jeolla province, did not. When his mother died in May 1791 he mourned her with every observance except the rites, and he and his cousin Kwon Sang-yeon burned the ancestral tablets. Relatives denounced them. Brought before the magistrate, Yun said that he could not perform rites that his religion forbade, that the tablets were wood and not his mother, and that he honored her by praying for her soul; he refused to recant under interrogation and beating. On 8 December 1791 he and Kwon were beheaded outside the south gate of Jeonju, the first Koreans executed for the faith. The church's first persecution took its name from the year. The ancestral rites remained the charge against Korean Catholics for a century; Rome permitted them, as civil observances, in 1939.

What the Silk Letter Asked For

The persecution of 1801 was the first aimed at destroying the Korean church. Under a regency hostile to the church's scholars, arrests began in the spring; the Chinese priest Zhou Wenmo, the only priest the church had ever had, was executed in the summer, and some three hundred Christians died in all. Hwang Sa-yong, a young scholar baptized Alexander, escaped to the mountains and hid in a kiln at Baeron with Christian potters. There he wrote, in tiny characters on a piece of white silk that could be sewn into a garment, a letter of 13,311 characters to the bishop of Beijing. It gave the history of the persecution, the names and deaths of the martyrs, in detail found nowhere else; and then it asked for help. It proposed that the pope ask the Chinese emperor to compel Korea to admit missionaries; that Korea be made a Chinese protectorate; and, failing those, that the Western nations send a fleet of some hundreds of ships and fifty or sixty thousand soldiers to demand freedom of religion by force. The courier was caught in September. The state council read it as proof that Christians were traitors who would bring foreign armies on the kingdom, and it shaped the state's view of the church for sixty years. Hwang was executed by dismemberment in December 1801 at twenty-six. The silk lay in the state archives until 1894, and went to Rome in 1925. The church keeps it as a martyr's testimony and a mistake.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Korea's Catholic Church was begun by lay scholars before any priest came. That makes it a rare case in the church's long history. Roughly ten thousand of its people died as martyrs in the persecutions that followed. Protestant missionaries came to Korea a hundred years after it began, from 1884.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Korea is the only country where the Catholic Church began without a missionary: in the late eighteenth century a circle of Confucian scholars near Seoul studied Chinese-language Catholic books, convinced themselves, and sent Yi Sung-hun to Beijing to be baptized in 1784. He came home and baptized the rest, and the church he founded was written in Korean from the start, reaching women, servants, and the poor as fast as the educated elite. The Choson state read it as treason against the Confucian order, and roughly ten thousand Korean Catholics were killed in successive purges between 1801 and 1866.

Legacy

It became one of the largest and most active Catholic communities in Asia — several million people in South Korea today, in a church that still traces its beginning to laypeople rather than clergy and takes considerable pride in the fact. In 1984 Pope John Paul II canonized 103 of its martyrs in Seoul itself rather than in Rome, and further beatifications followed in 2014. The execution grounds are now national shrines, and the martyrs' letters and trial testimony remain among the most vivid first-person religious documents anywhere in nineteenth-century Asia.

Experience It Today

  • Jeoldusan Martyrs' Shrine, Seoul — the riverside cliff where Catholics were executed in 1866, now a church and museum holding the martyrs' relics and records

How Sure the Record Is

Moderate-to-rich: martyrs' letters and trial records; the pre-missionary church documented by the missionaries who finally reached it

  • The self-evangelized church's own documents: Chong Yak-chong, Chugyo yoji (c. 1798-1801, vernacular han'gul catechism); the community's letters to Beijing and Rome (1784-, incl. the 1811 letter to the Pope); Yi Byeok's Essence of Sacred Doctrine (primary) — This is the lay church teaching itself, in its own hand. The attribution of the Essence of Sacred Doctrine to Yi Byeok is debated among scholars. English-language access is chiefly through Baker and Rausch's translations.
  • The Hwang Sa-yong silk letter (1801) (primary) — A believer's appeal written on silk during the Sinyu persecution, intercepted by authorities. It survives only in the persecutors' own archive, and it combines genuine faith testimony with a politically sensitive appeal for foreign intervention - both parts of the record, without softening either.
  • The persecution records: Sinyu (1801), Kihae (1839), Pyongo (1846), Pyongin (1866) interrogation and execution records in the Choson annals and police archives (primary) — These are the persecutors' own records: the martyrs' testimony as it survives through interrogation and torture, not their own words freely given.
  • Women's voices: Columba Kang Wan-suk (catechist, executed 1801) in trial records and martyrology; the women catechists' cell-structure centrality; Daveluy's martyr-account compilations (primary) — This material passes through two filters: hostile court records and later hagiographic compilation. Daveluy's martyr-account compilations were made by missionaries who reached Korea only after these events, adding another layer of outside mediation.
  • Don Baker with Franklin Rausch, Catholics and Anti-Catholicism in Choson Korea (Hawai'i, 2017), with James Grayson, Korea: A Religious History (rev. ed. 2002) (secondary) — The current standard study, including English translations of the Chugyo yoji and the silk letter.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 8, ch. 30 'Christianity in East Asia: China, Korea and Japan' (Bays & Grayson) (reference) — Grayson is a specialist in Korean church history, making this a direct fit.
  • The Catholic Encyclopedia, vol. 3 (New York, 1908), article 'China' (reference) — A Catholic reference work, read in a public-domain copy; it names the martyrs of 1866 and the three priests of the Paris Society who were left.
