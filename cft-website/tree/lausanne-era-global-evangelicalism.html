Lausanne-Era Global Evangelicalism

Global, 1974 to 2010 — the movement launched at the Lausanne Congress, where Global South voices bent world evangelicalism toward integral mission, organized from the start as a network rather than a denomination.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:07

Read along

Evangelicals from around the world met at Lausanne from 16 to 25 July 1974, and what came out of it was not a denomination but a network with a document. The Lausanne Covenant was drafted by a committee chaired by John Stott and adopted at the congress, and the congress's own record, Let the Earth Hear His Voice, was published the following year with the addresses in it — including those of the Latin Americans René Padilla and Samuel Escobar, who argued that evangelism and social responsibility could not be separated. That argument won enough of the room to change the movement's direction, which is why 'integral mission' became a Lausanne word. The movement restated itself twice more, in the Manila Manifesto of 1989 and the Cape Town Commitment of 2010. Together they form a chain of dated statements of what it took itself to be, unusual in its consistency.

Documented Stories

The Gospel Has No Nationality

The International Congress on World Evangelization met at Lausanne from 16 to 25 July 1974, called by Billy Graham's organization to plan the completion of the missionary task. René Padilla, an Ecuadorian working in Argentina, was given a plenary paper on evangelism and the world, and used it to say that much of what passed for the gospel in the world's mission fields was 'culture Christianity', the gospel fused with the American way of life, and that a message which left injustice untouched was not the whole message. Samuel Escobar of Peru argued in his paper that a church which preached personal salvation while ignoring the structures that crushed people would not be believed. The papers were printed as given; some in the hall were angry. An ad hoc group met through the week and produced a statement on 'radical discipleship' which about five hundred participants signed, insisting that there was no biblical dichotomy between the word spoken and the word made visible. John Stott, chairing the drafting committee, carried the substance into the Lausanne Covenant's fifth paragraph: 'we express penitence both for our neglect and for having sometimes regarded evangelism and social concern as mutually exclusive.' When the continuation committee met in Mexico City in January 1975 and some wished to limit its work to evangelism alone, Stott told them he would resign unless the whole Covenant, social responsibility included, was the mandate. It was. The phrase that came out of the argument, integral mission, is Padilla's.

Two Hundred Empty Seats

The Third Lausanne Congress met in Cape Town from 16 to 25 October 2010, four thousand participants chosen by national committees from 198 countries, the most representative gathering of evangelicals ever held, by the organizers' count and by most others. It had invited some two hundred Chinese Christians, most of them from the unregistered house churches, who had for the first time been willing to send delegates openly under their own names. In the days before the congress, Chinese authorities confiscated passports, stopped delegates at airports in Beijing, Shanghai and elsewhere, and in some cases detained them briefly and sent them home. Not one of the two hundred arrived. The congress left their seats empty. On the opening night the chairman read a letter from the delegation that could not come, and the hall prayed for them; the Chinese participants, for their part, sent word that they were meeting in China at the same hours to pray for the congress. Through the week the congress's internet feeds and its online participation platform, on which several hundred remote sites in other countries depended, were repeatedly attacked and disabled; the organizers said the attacks were traced to China, which the Chinese government did not answer. The Cape Town Commitment, the document the congress produced, was drafted with those seats in view; it includes a section on suffering and persecution that reads differently for knowing why the chairs were empty.

Connections

In Its Own Record

At the 1974 Congress, Global South voices — chiefly the Latin Americans René Padilla and Samuel Escobar — argued for holding evangelism and social responsibility together, bending the whole movement toward what became known as integral mission. It worked as a network rather than a denomination, restating itself through a series of dated documents: the Lausanne Covenant (1974), the Manila Manifesto (1989) and the Cape Town Commitment (2010). It stands as a counter-pole to the conciliar ecumenical movement centered on Geneva — two different twentieth-century answers to how separated churches should act together. At the 1974 Congress, Ralph and Roberta Winter prepared a paper on the peoples that existing missions did not reach. In 1997 the Southern Baptist International Mission Board took up an approach called the Church Planting Movement. David Garrison set it out in a book in 2004. Evangelical relief agencies worked on the same ground, and some were older than the congress. Tearfund grew out of the Evangelical Alliance in 1968. Brian Stanley's history names this evangelical age after Billy Graham and John Stott. Graham's evangelistic association was incorporated in 1950. In 1971 the Kenyan theologian John Gatu called for a moratorium on missionaries. At Lausanne in 1974 most leaders appeared to reject a broad moratorium. Still, the Lausanne Covenant allowed that fewer foreign missionaries might sometimes help a national church grow in self-reliance. The Christian Right, a political movement that American ministers began with the Moral Majority in 1979, has a separate entry.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

At the Lausanne Congress in July 1974, the Latin American theologians René Padilla and Samuel Escobar argued that evangelism and social responsibility could not be separated, and they won enough of the room to bend the whole movement in that direction -- which is why "integral mission" became a Lausanne word. What came out of the congress was not a denomination but a network built around a document, the Lausanne Covenant, and the movement restated itself twice more, in the Manila Manifesto of 1989 and the Cape Town Commitment of 2010.

Legacy

In 2010 it was a living network rather than a church, and that was the point. It convened, published and connected, and the churches doing the work belonged to other bodies entirely. Its 1974 covenant became the network's shared statement. By 2010 'integral mission' (evangelism and social action held together) had become mainstream evangelical language. That was largely because of what the Global South delegates said in 1974. It stands as the counter-pole to the conciliar ecumenical movement centered on Geneva: two different answers, from the same century, to the question of how separated churches should act together.
