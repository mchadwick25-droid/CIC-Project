Benedict of Nursia

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Early Benedictine / Italian Monasticism.

Known for
Founder of the monasteries of Subiaco and Monte Cassino and author of the Rule for monks, whose life is known through Gregory the Great's Dialogues; the Frankish church took up his Rule to restore the monastic life, and in 802 Charlemagne ordered monasteries put right by it.
Also called
Benedictus, Bennet
Dates
Born, by Edmund Gardner, about 480, and by Francis Gasquet in 480; died, by the tradition of his order, in 543, as both give it. Gregory the Great gives no years. Gardner dates the founding of Monte Cassino to 528 or 529
Place
Nursia; Rome; Subiaco; Monte Cassino
Role
Monk and abbot; author of the Rule

About This Person

Around the year 500, Italy was ruled by Gothic kings. Gregory the Great tells of a young man from the province of Nursia who was sent to Rome to study. There he saw many of the students fall into loose living, so he left his books and his father's house. He was Benedict of Nursia, and the early Benedictines of Italy begin with him. Nearly all we know of his life comes from Gregory's Dialogues, written about fifty years after Benedict died, from what four of his disciples told him. Gregory wrote to show what God did through a saint, so the book is full of wonders.

Benedict built twelve monasteries near Subiaco and later moved to Cassino, where country people still worshipped Apollo. There, Gregory says, he broke the idol and by his preaching brought the people to the faith. When the Gothic king Totila came to him, Benedict told him, "You do many evil things." From then on, Gregory says, the king was less cruel.

The Rule is Benedict's own words. In it he founds "a school for the Lord's service, and in setting it up we hope to lay down nothing harsh and nothing heavy." His monks promise "stability, a changed way of life, and obedience," and divide their days between prayer, work with their hands and holy reading. The abbot must so temper all things "that the strong have something to long for and the weak do not run away." For more, the last chapter points to the Lives of the Fathers, to the Rule of Basil of Caesarea, and to John Cassian's Conferences of the Fathers.

Gregory, by then pope, praised the Rule as "outstanding for its sound judgment and clear in its language." In 742 the first council of Carloman, duke of the Franks, told monks and nuns to live by Benedict's Rule. At the next, the abbots and monks took it up to restore the norm of the regular life. In 802 Charlemagne had the monks at Aachen read it together, and ordered that whatever in the monasteries went against it be put right by it.

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “You do many evil things.”
    As printed
    “Much wickedness do you daily commit.”
    Dialogues, Book II: the Life and Miracles of St. Benedict, lines 4472–4473
  • In our modern English
    “a school for the Lord's service, and in setting it up we hope to lay down nothing harsh and nothing heavy.”
    As printed
    “a school for the service of God, in which we hope nothing harsh nor burdensome will be ordained.”
    The Rule of Saint Benedict (tr. Francis Aidan Gasquet), lines 768–770
    A reads "in which" as the school and says "God"; B reads it as the founding ("institutione") and says "the Lord" ("dominici"); both readings kept.
  • In our modern English
    “stability, a changed way of life, and obedience,”
    As printed
    “stability, amendment of manners and obedience,”
    The Rule of Saint Benedict (tr. Francis Aidan Gasquet), lines 3595–3596
    "conversio morum" is a turning of one's way of life (A: "a change", B: "reformed"); both readings kept.
  • In our modern English
    “that the strong have something to long for and the weak do not run away.”
    As printed
    “that the strong may have their scope and the weak be not scared.”
    The Rule of Saint Benedict (tr. Francis Aidan Gasquet), lines 3960–3961
    "refugiant" = the weak flee of themselves (A: "frightened away"); B's "driven away" implies an outside push; both readings kept.
  • In our modern English
    “outstanding for its sound judgment and clear in its language,”
    As printed
    “excellent for discretion and also eloquent for the style,”
    Dialogues, Book II: the Life and Miracles of St. Benedict, lines 5567–5568
    "discretio" is judgment and a sense of measure (A: "sound judgment", B: "wise moderation"); "luculentus" is clear rather than eloquent; both readings kept. The FINAL drops Gardner's "also", which the Latin lacks.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • His Rule, which Gregory the Great calls "excellent for discretion and also eloquent for the style", survives in Latin, and Francis Gasquet and Oswald Hunter Blair translate it into English.
  • Gregory says he built twelve monasteries near Subiaco, with twelve monks in each, before he founded Monte Cassino.
  • Carloman, duke of the Franks, had his council of 742 tell monks and nuns to live by the Rule of Saint Benedict, and at the next council the abbots and monks received it "ad restaurandam normam regularis vitae".
  • In 802, the annals of Lorsch say, Charlemagne had the abbots and monks at Aachen read the Rule together and ordered whatever in the monasteries went against it to be put right by it.

How they're remembered

  • Gregory the Great gave the whole second book of his Dialogues to his miracles and wrote that "the holy man could not otherwise teach, than himself lived."
  • Gregory names the four disciples he had his stories from: Constantinus, the next abbot after him; Valentinianus, long in charge of the Lateran monastery; Simplicius, the third head of his order; and Honoratus, abbot of the house where Benedict first began.

What Is Uncertain

  • Nearly all that is known of his life comes from one witness, Gregory the Great's Dialogues, a book of miracles written to honor a saint. Gregory says he could not learn all that Benedict did and had his stories from four disciples. At least one story he had at second hand: Honoratus heard it from other monks.
  • Gregory gives no years. The birth about 480 and the death in 543 are Edmund Gardner's and Francis Gasquet's; both call 543 the tradition. Gardner says the date he left Rome is disputed and puts it a few years before 500.
  • Gasquet thinks Benedict probably conceived the Rule at Subiaco and finished it at Monte Cassino. Gregory says only that he wrote it.
  • What the Rule did. Francis Gasquet, a Benedictine editor, judges that the Rule put a written code in place of an abbot's personal will, and calls it practically the only monastic Rule of the West from the ninth to the twelfth century.
  • The second council. The edition of the capitularies records that Hefele and Hahn held the council of Estinnes to be Carloman's and Pippin's together; its editor argues it was Carloman's.
  • The modern English of Benedict's words to Totila follows Gregory's Latin, "Multa mala facis, multa mala fecisti" (Acta Sanctorum, March, vol. 3), which has no word for "daily"; the 1608 English adds it. Gregory's praise of the Rule likewise leaves out the 1608 "also", which the Latin lacks.
