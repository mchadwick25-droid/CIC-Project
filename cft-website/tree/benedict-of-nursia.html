The Age of Monks and Empires · c. 480-543 · Subiaco, Monte Cassino

Benedict of Nursia

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Early Benedictine / Italian Monasticism.

Also called Benedictus, Bennet

Founder of the monasteries of Subiaco and Monte Cassino and author of the Rule for monks, whose life is known through Gregory the Great's Dialogues; the Frankish church took up his Rule to restore the monastic life, and in 802 Charlemagne ordered monasteries put right by it.

About This Person

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 2:53

Read along

Pope Gregory the Great filled his life of Benedict with wonders, and then pointed his readers somewhere else, to the rule Benedict wrote for monks. "For the holy man could in no way teach otherwise than he lived."

Benedict lived around the year 500, and founded houses of monks in Italy, twelve at Subiaco, then one at Monte Cassino. For monks living together under an abbot they choose, he wrote a rule. "So we must found a school for the Lord's service. In founding it, we hope to lay down nothing harsh and nothing heavy." Yet the promise binds for life: a monk vows to stay in one community, to turn his whole way of life to God, and to obey. About two centuries later, Frankish rulers ordered the monasteries of their lands to live by it.

Gregory records that in his own lifetime the Lombards sacked Monte Cassino. Paul the Deacon later wrote that the monks fled to Rome with the manuscript of the Rule. In 742, Carloman's council bound the monks and nuns of his lands to the rule of Saint Benedict. And in 802, at Aachen, Charlemagne gathered the abbots and monks who were there, and they read it together. Then he ordered that whatever in the monasteries went against it be put right by it.

Because the Rule calls idleness an enemy of the soul, it sets times for work with the hands and other hours for holy reading. Even the last monk to join may be chosen as abbot. Of the abbot, the Rule says: "Let him so temper everything that the strong have something to long for, and the weak do not run away." Every guest is received as Christ. Benedict called it a little rule for beginners.

Benedict's life was written down by Gregory, about fifty years after his death, from the memories of four of his monks. Gregory says that in Rome, Benedict saw many students fall into loose living. So he left his books and his father's house to serve God alone, and spent three years in a cave near Subiaco.

Later, monks of a nearby house begged him to lead them. He held them to its regular life, and some of them put poison in his wine. Gregory says the glass shattered when Benedict made the sign of the cross over it.

Gregory says that at Monte Cassino, where country people still worshipped Apollo, Benedict smashed the idol, overturned the altar, burned the sacred woods and built chapels. He tells too how Totila, king of the Goths, threw himself to the ground before Benedict, who raised him and told him to stop his evil. From then on, Gregory says, the king was less cruel.

At the end, Gregory says, Benedict died standing in the chapel, held up by his disciples, his hands raised to heaven.

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • His Rule, which Gregory the Great calls "excellent for discretion and also eloquent for the style", survives in Latin, and Francis Gasquet and Oswald Hunter Blair translate it into English.
  • Gregory says he built twelve monasteries near Subiaco, with twelve monks in each, before he founded Monte Cassino.
  • Carloman, duke of the Franks, had his council of 742 tell monks and nuns to live by the Rule of Saint Benedict, and at the next council the abbots and monks received it "ad restaurandam normam regularis vitae".
  • In 802, the annals of Lorsch say, Charlemagne had the abbots and monks at Aachen read the Rule together and ordered whatever in the monasteries went against it to be put right by it.

How they're remembered

  • Gregory the Great gave the whole second book of his Dialogues to his miracles and wrote that "the holy man could not otherwise teach, than himself lived."
  • Gregory names the four disciples he had his stories from: Constantinus, the next abbot after him; Valentinianus, long in charge of the Lateran monastery; Simplicius, the third head of his order; and Honoratus, abbot of the house where Benedict first began.

Open Questions

  • Nearly all that is known of his life comes from one witness, Gregory the Great's Dialogues, a book of miracles written to honor a saint. Gregory says he could not learn all that Benedict did and had his stories from four disciples. At least one story he had at second hand: Honoratus heard it from other monks.
  • Gregory gives no years. The birth about 480 and the death in 543 are Edmund Gardner's and Francis Gasquet's; both call 543 the tradition. Gardner says the date he left Rome is disputed and puts it a few years before 500.
  • Gasquet thinks Benedict probably conceived the Rule at Subiaco and finished it at Monte Cassino. Gregory says only that he wrote it.
  • What the Rule did. Francis Gasquet, a Benedictine editor, judges that the Rule put a written code in place of an abbot's personal will, and calls it practically the only monastic Rule of the West from the ninth to the twelfth century.
  • The second council. The edition of the capitularies records that Hefele and Hahn held the council of Estinnes to be Carloman's and Pippin's together; its editor argues it was Carloman's.
  • The modern English of Benedict's words to Totila follows Gregory's Latin, "Multa mala facis, multa mala fecisti" (Acta Sanctorum, March, vol. 3), which has no word for "daily"; the 1608 English adds it. Gregory's praise of the Rule likewise leaves out the 1608 "also", which the Latin lacks.
