About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:19

Read along

By the middle of the seventeenth century the Philippines were the one substantially Christian society in East or Southeast Asia, and its basic unit was the friar parish: a Spanish religious — Augustinian, Dominican, Franciscan, Recollect or Jesuit — often the only Spaniard in a town, holding both spiritual and civil authority over a population whose language he had learned and which he alone among the officials could preach in. Filipino Christianity took its own shape inside that arrangement, above all in the pasyon, a long verse narrative of Christ's passion in the vernacular, chanted by ordinary people through Holy Week in their own houses. The earliest surviving text dates from 1704, and the practice continues. The century's strains were institutional: a long fight over whether bishops could inspect the friars' parishes, the British occupation of Manila in 1762-64, the expulsion of the Jesuits in 1768, and above all the question of whether Filipino secular priests could hold parishes at all. Which became the great grievance of the century that followed.

Documented Stories

The Bishop, the Rebel and the Assassin

When the British took Manila in October 1762 the Spanish government of the islands collapsed into the hands of the oidor Simón de Anda, who declared himself governor at Bacolor, and the provinces were left to themselves. In Ilocos, on the north-west coast of Luzon, Diego Silang, a mestizo of Vigan who had worked as a courier between Manila and the province, led a rising in December against the tribute, the forced labor and the alcalde mayor, and, having driven out the Spanish officials, offered to hold the province for the king under his own government. The bishop of Nueva Segovia, Bernardo Ustáriz, a Dominican, refused him, and when Silang accepted a commission from the British and began to expel the Augustinian friars from their parishes, the bishop excommunicated him and called on the province to abandon him. In May 1763 two men of Vigan, Miguel Vicos, a Spanish mestizo who had been Silang's friend, and Pedro Becbec undertook to kill him; the Spanish accounts say that they were promised pardon and reward by the Spanish authorities and were encouraged and absolved in advance by the bishop and the clergy. On 28 May Vicos shot Silang in the back in his own house and claimed the reward. Silang's widow, Gabriela, took command of the remaining rebels with his uncle Nicolás Cariño, fought through the summer, and was defeated, captured and hanged at Vigan on 20 September 1763 with some ninety of her followers, in the plaza before the cathedral.

‘No More Oarsmen on the Pasig’

Basilio Sancho de Santa Justa y Rufina, a Piarist, arrived as archbishop of Manila in 1767 with the Bourbon government's instructions to enforce two things the friar orders had resisted for two centuries: that their parishes be subject to the bishop's visitation like any others, and that parishes be given, as the Council of Trent intended, to secular priests, which in the Philippines meant Filipinos and mestizos, since almost no Spanish seculars came. The expulsion of the Jesuits in 1768 emptied their parishes at once, and the Augustinians, refusing visitation, surrendered many of theirs in Pampanga. Sancho filled them. He reopened the diocesan seminary, ordained Filipino candidates rapidly and in numbers, and by the early 1770s had placed native priests in scores of parishes around Manila and in the provinces. The friars, who had held for two centuries that Filipinos were unfit for the priesthood by nature, said that the archbishop had ordained so many that there were no more oarsmen to be found on the Pasig river, and reported to Madrid every failure of the new priests. The provincial council Sancho convened in 1771 confirmed his policy; the crown, alarmed by the friars' reports and by the revolts of the 1760s, reversed itself in a royal order of 1776, which halted the transfer of parishes and returned many to the orders. The question of whether a Filipino could be trusted with a parish became, over the next century, the grievance on which the nation formed.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

By the mid-seventeenth century the Philippines were the one substantially Christian society in East or Southeast Asia, built parish by parish around Spanish friars who were often the only European in town and the only one who could preach in the local language. Filipino Christianity found its own voice inside that arrangement in the pasyon, a long verse account of Christ's passion chanted by ordinary people through Holy Week in their own houses — the earliest surviving text dates to 1704 — even as the century's real strain built around whether Filipino priests, and not only Spanish friars, could ever hold parishes of their own.

Legacy

This is a living church and a very large one. The Philippines has one of the largest Catholic populations in the world. Its devotions are still much as they were in these centuries. The pasyon is chanted through Holy Week, each town keeps a fiesta for its patron saint, and the processions go on. The period left one question open: could Filipino priests run Filipino parishes? Writing in 1899, a Filipino scholar linked that quarrel to the killing of three priests in 1872. They are remembered as Gomburza. It is also often tied to the national revolution and to the independent Philippine church that broke from Rome in 1902. The friar churches still stand. Four of them, San Agustín in Manila among them, are UNESCO World Heritage Sites.
