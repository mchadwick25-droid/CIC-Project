The Early Church Era · c. 240s-430 · Carthage, Hippo Regius

Carthage, today in Tunisia · Hippo Regius, today in Algeria

Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity

About This Movement

This is the ordinary congregational life of Latin-speaking North Africa — not the councils and not the controversies, but the week in and week out work of a bishop with a flock. Cyprian became bishop of Carthage around 248, went into hiding when the emperor Decius ordered universal sacrifice, and returned to the hardest pastoral problem of the age. What to do with the thousands of his own people who had complied and now wanted back in. He organized relief through the epidemic that still bears his name, telling his congregation to nurse pagan neighbors as well as their own, and was beheaded outside Carthage in 258. A century and a half later Augustine, bishop of Hippo Regius from 395 until his death in 430, preached to the same congregation several times a week for thirty-five years, with stenographers taking much of it down. Hundreds of those sermons survive, arguing, joking, scolding and consoling actual people whose situations he knew. Between the two: catechumens under instruction, baptisms at Easter, penitents doing public penance, the care of widows and the poor, and the bishop sitting as judge in his own people's disputes.

In Its Own Record

Influenced By

  • Tertullian's Voice Documented — Jerome reports that Cyprian read Tertullian every day and asked for him as 'the master'. Jerome had this from an old man who had been Cyprian's secretary. Augustine later called Tertullian's many works eloquent and still read.
  • Ambrosian Milan (standalone) Documented — Augustine says in his Confessions that he was led to Ambrose at Milan so that Ambrose might lead him to God. Before his baptism in 387 he wrote to Ambrose about his past errors and asked what he should read.
  • The Gallic Nicene Episcopate Documented — Augustine quotes Hilary in his own book On the Trinity. He gives Hilary's short formula for the three persons and names Hilary's book as its source. He calls Hilary a man of real authority on Scripture and the faith.
  • Donatism Documented — Tyconius was a Donatist writer of about 380. In On Christian Doctrine, Augustine calls him a Donatist and sets out the seven rules of his Book of Rules. He says they give real help in opening the hidden sense of Scripture.

Influenced

  • The Reformed Cities - Zurich & Geneva Documented — In his Institutes, Calvin appeals to Augustine's authority on election and the grace of God. He writes that he could build a whole volume from Augustine's words and need no others.
  • Donatism Documented — Cyprian of Carthage was a leading African authority in the 250s. Augustine tells the Donatists that they keep quoting Cyprian's letters, his opinion and his council. He asks why they use Cyprian's authority to defend their schism.

Argued Against

  • Pelagianism Documented — Augustine, bishop of Hippo, wrote against Pelagius by name. In On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins, he answers points he says Pelagius made against original sin. In his Retractations he says he replied to a book of Pelagius that defended human nature against the grace of God.
  • Donatism Documented — Augustine, bishop of Hippo, wrote a long book to the Donatists after the 411 conference at Carthage between their bishops and his. He lists it in his Retractations. Bishop Optatus of Milevis had earlier written against the Donatist bishop Parmenian.
  • Manichaeism Documented — Augustine, bishop of Hippo, spent nine years among the Manichaeans in his youth, as his Confessions record. Around 400 he wrote a long reply to Faustus, a Manichaean teacher he had once met.
  • Novatianism Documented — Cyprian of Carthage refused communion to envoys who brought word that Novatian had been made bishop. He later wrote that Novatian 'is not in the Church'. Augustine asked whether repentance had been fully taught 'before the Novatians opposed'.
  • Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity Documented — Augustine urged Jerome to translate from the Greek Septuagint, not from the Hebrew. He feared the Latin and Greek churches would end up reading different Bibles. Even after Jerome died, he still held that no translator should be put above the Seventy.
  • Gallic Monastic-Ascetic Christianity Documented — Prosper said the men who raised doubts lived at Marseilles. Augustine answered them in On the Predestination of the Saints. He wrote it in 428 or 429 to Prosper and Hilary, for the brothers they were worried about. Prosper adds that Augustine's earlier book On Rebuke and Grace had met like doubts in Africa.

Opposed By

  • Donatism Widely Accepted — Optatus quotes Parmenian, the Donatist bishop of Carthage, saying that a stained minister cannot wash away sins in baptism. Optatus also says the Donatists re-baptized the faithful. The Donatists applied this rule to Catholic clergy.
  • Gallic Monastic-Ascetic Christianity Documented — Around 428 Prosper of Aquitaine wrote to Augustine from Gaul. He reported that many servants of Christ at Marseilles thought Augustine's writing on election ran against the Fathers. They thought it ran against the mind of the church as well.
  • Pelagianism Documented — Julian of Eclanum wrote letters that named Augustine and called the Catholic party Manichaeans. Augustine says the Pope sent them on to him, and he answered in four books. He also says Pelagius at Rome could not bear his prayer, 'Give what Thou commandest.'

Broke With

  • Donatism Documented — Optatus of Milevis was a Catholic bishop. He says the split began when a rival party made Majorinus bishop at Carthage. Yet Caecilian was bishop there by then. Optatus also copies a plea that the Donatist bishops sent to Constantine. In it they say that disputes had come up between them and the rest of the bishops of Africa.

In Contact With

  • Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity Documented — Jerome and Augustine wrote to each other across the sea for years. Augustine told Jerome of a bishop whose congregation rose up over the new wording of a line in Jonah. Jerome later wrote that he was resolved to love and respect Augustine and to defend his opinions as his own.
