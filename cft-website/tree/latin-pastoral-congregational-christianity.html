The Early Church Era · c. 248 to 430 · Carthage, Hippo Regius

Carthage, today in Tunisia · Hippo Regius, today in Algeria

Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity

In Carthage and Hippo, in Roman Africa, Latin-speaking Christians gathered around their city's bishop. He taught them, cared for their poor, judged their quarrels, and decided with them when to take back those who had fallen away.

About This Tradition

In the 390s, at Hippo, in today's Algeria, on a feast day, the people wanted their old feast in the church, with eating and drinking, which they called the Gladness. Their priest Augustine read them Scripture against drunken feasts and pleaded with them. He wrote: "I did not move their tears with my own. But as these things were said, I confess, their weeping came first, and I could not hold back mine. When we had wept together, my sermon ended in the fullest hope that they would mend their ways." On the feast day itself, from the rival church, whose people he called heretics, came the sound of their feasting.

From the 250s to 430, the Latin-speaking Christians of Roman Africa lived as congregations around one bishop, at Carthage under Cyprian and later at Hippo under Augustine. Their bishop taught them, fed their poor, bought back their captives, judged their quarrels and decided with them whom to take back after sin. Persecution, plague and a rival church in the same towns pressed on them, and the record keeps asking what a bishop and his people owed each other in danger.

In 250 the emperor Decius began a persecution, and his order called for sacrifice to the gods. At Carthage, in today's Tunisia, the crowd kept shouting violently for Cyprian, and he withdrew, as he wrote, to keep his people in peace. From hiding he led his church by letter, and told his clergy: "I could not write back alone. From my first days as bishop I resolved to do nothing on my own private judgment, without your counsel and the people's consent."

Many had given way and sacrificed, and now these fallen Christians asked to come back. Cyprian held that no one should be barred from penance, a time of public repentance, or from the hope of peace. Peace was the church's word for taking someone back, reconciling them, to share the bread and cup. At Rome a stricter party held that sharing communion with the fallen stained the rest. They set up Novatian as a second bishop of Rome, against the one already chosen. When his envoys came to Carthage, Cyprian and the bishops with him kept them from communion at once, since Novatian had been made bishop against the Catholic Church.

A few years later plague struck. Pontius, Cyprian's deacon and admirer, says the bishop gathered the people and told them that caring only for their own was nothing great. The tasks were shared out, and the poor, who had no money to give, gave their work.

Then the emperors ordered all who did not keep the Roman religion to take part in the Roman rites. Cyprian was arrested, and all his people gathered in the street outside where he was held. He refused, and the sentence called him an enemy of the Roman gods and their rites, and the leader of men gathered in a wicked conspiracy. He was led out and beheaded on 14 September 258.

Soon after the great persecution of the early 300s, the people of Carthage chose a new bishop. A party said the bishop who had made him bishop had handed over the Scriptures to be burned, so they made a rival bishop and set up altar against altar. The rival side, the Donatists, held that one who was himself stained could not wash away sins, and they baptized again those who came over. A Catholic writer some sixty years later said the Donatists had split away, yet shared his church's true sacraments, such as baptism. So he did not call them heretics: to him, heretics taught falsely about God.

Later, as bishop of Hippo, Augustine judged the disputes that Christians brought him, because Paul had told believers not to go to law before unbelievers. Against the rival church he had first wanted argument only. Then, he wrote, his fellow bishops pointed to his own town. Fear of the emperors' laws of exile and fines had brought it over to the Catholics, and he came to back those laws.

In his last years the Vandals, enemies who came by sea, overran Africa. Of countless churches, only three still stood: Carthage, Hippo and Cirta. A fellow bishop wrote to ask whether bishops and clergy should leave their churches. Augustine answered: "But if the clergy are there, everyone is helped, as far as the Lord gives them strength. Some are baptized, others are reconciled. No one is cheated of communion in the Lord's body. All are comforted, built up and urged to ask God, who has the power to turn away all that is feared."

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “I did not move their tears with my own. But as these things were said, I confess, their weeping came first, and I could not hold back mine. When we had wept together, my sermon ended in the fullest hope that they would mend their ways.”
    As printed
    “non ego illorum lacrimas meis lacrimis moui, sed cum talia dicerentur, fateor, eorum fletu praeuentus meum abstinere non potui, et cum iam pariter fleuissemus, plenissima spe correctionis illorum finis sermonis mei factus est.”
    Augustine, Letter 29.7, to Alypius (ed. A. Goldbacher, CSEL 34, 1895)
    The scan prints a stray mark, a misread line number, before plenissima; it is left out here.

    “solus rescribere nihil potui, quando a primordio episcopatus mei statuerim nihil sine consilio uestro et sine consensu plebis mea priuatim sententia gerere.”
    Cyprian, Letter 14.4, to his priests and deacons, 250 (ed. W. Hartel, CSEL 3.2, 1871)
  • In our modern English
    “But if the clergy are there, everyone is helped, as far as the Lord gives them strength. Some are baptized, others are reconciled. No one is cheated of communion in the Lord's body. All are comforted, built up and urged to ask God, who has the power to turn away all that is feared.”
    As printed
    “Si autem ministri adsint, pro viribus quas eis Dominus subministrat, omnibus subvenitur; alii baptizantur, alii reconciliantur, nulli Dominici corporis communione fraudantur, omnes consolantur, aedificantur, exhortantur, ut Deum rogent, qui potens est omnia quae timentur avertere”
    Augustine, Letter 228.8, to Honoratus, as Possidius copies it in his Life of Augustine (ed. and tr. H. T. Weiskotten, 1919)

  1. date unknown Vandals and Alans, with some Goths and other peoples, cross by ship from Spain and overrun Africa. Of countless churches, only those of Carthage, Hippo and Cirta still stand, and the hymns and praises of God perish from the churches. (Possidius, Life of Augustine 28, cic/texts/possidius_vita-augustini_weiskotten1919.txt lines 3982-3985, 4011-4013, 4068-4071)
  2. date unknown A fellow bishop, Honoratus, writes to ask whether bishops and clergy should leave their churches as the enemy approaches. Augustine answers that one who is sought in particular may flee, but when the danger is common to all, those who need the clergy must not be left by them. (Possidius, Life of Augustine 30, cic/texts/possidius_vita-augustini_weiskotten1919.txt lines 4201-4209, 4281-4285, 4318-4326)

Documented Stories

Cyprian’s Flight and the Letter from Rome

Decius’ order that everyone sacrifice reached Carthage early in 250, and the crowd in the amphitheater was already shouting for Cyprian by name. He left the city. The Roman clergy, whose bishop Fabian had just been executed, wrote to the Carthaginian clergy a letter that Cyprian later had to answer. It opened by noting that they had heard ‘the blessed father Cyprian has for a certain reason withdrawn’, said he had ‘acted quite rightly, because he is a person of eminence’, and went on to quote the Gospel: the good shepherd lays down his life for the sheep, but ‘the hireling, whose own the sheep are not, seeth the wolf coming, and leaveth the sheep, and fleeth.’ Cyprian’s reply, and the stream of letters he sent from hiding, make his case: his presence would provoke worse violence against the congregation; he had continued to govern the church by correspondence; he had ordered the clergy to keep visiting the imprisoned confessors, and to do it in shifts so as not to draw attention. ‘Nothing on my own private opinion,’ he wrote of the readmission of the lapsed, promising to decide with the clergy and people when he could return. He did return, faced down a schism over exactly this question, and eight years later stood trial without flinching. But the charge of cowardice followed him, and his own biographer Pontius spends paragraphs answering it. The record keeps both the flight and the reasons.

The Night Watch Outside the Officer’s House

Under Valerian’s second edict, bishops were to be executed. Cyprian was arrested at his garden estate and taken to the proconsul’s residence, then, because the hearing was put off to the next day, held overnight at the house of an officer of the guard. Pontius, his deacon, was there. He says a rumor ran through the city ‘that now Thascius was brought forward’, and that everyone came — ‘to us glorious from the devotion of faith, and to be mourned over even by the Gentiles.’ The custody was gentle; the friends were allowed in. And ‘the whole people, in anxiety that nothing should be done throughout the night without their knowledge, kept watch before the officer’s door.’ Pontius’ comment is that God granted that ‘God’s people should watch on the passion of the priest’. The next morning the proconsul Galerius Maximus asked Cyprian to confirm his name and to perform the rites; the official record of the exchange, the Acta, gives his refusal in a handful of words and the sentence: to be executed by the sword. Cyprian said ‘Thanks be to God’, and the crowd, by Pontius’ account, cried out to be killed with him. He was beheaded in a field outside the city, having ordered that the executioner be paid twenty-five gold pieces. The vigil is the detail to keep: a congregation that had watched him flee eight years earlier now stood all night outside a door.

The Psalms on the Wall of Augustine’s Room

Possidius, bishop of Calama, had known Augustine for nearly forty years and was in Hippo Regius when the Vandal army closed round it in the summer of 430. His Life of Augustine, written within a few years, describes the end. Refugee bishops and clergy had crowded into the city; Augustine watched churches burn across the province and took comfort in a wise man’s saying: ‘He is not to be thought great who thinks it strange that wood and stones should fall and mortals die.’ In the third month of the siege he fell ill with fever. He asked that ‘the shortest penitential Psalms of David’ be copied out and fixed to the wall opposite his bed, and he lay reading them and weeping. He asked that no one come in except the physicians and those bringing food, so that he could pray. He had always said, Possidius notes, that even a good Christian and bishop should not leave this life without repentance. He died on 28 August. He made no will, Possidius adds, ‘because as a poor man of God he had nothing from which to make it’ — the library he asked to be kept for the church. The city fell the following year.

In Its Own Record

The churches led by Cyprian at Carthage and Augustine at Hippo. From the 310s a rival Donatist church stood in the same towns.

Direct descendants

No direct descendant is recorded. Its other links are influence, argument and contact.

Broke With

  • broke with Donatism Documented Major — Optatus of Milevis was a Catholic bishop. He says the split began when a rival party made Majorinus bishop at Carthage. Yet Caecilian was bishop there by then. Optatus also copies a plea that the Donatist bishops sent to Constantine. In it they say that disputes had come up between them and the rest of the bishops of Africa.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced Tertullian Documented — Jerome says Cyprian read Tertullian every day and asked for him as 'the master'. Jerome had this from an old man who had been Cyprian's secretary. Augustine later said that many of Tertullian's works were still read, and that they were very well written.
  • influenced Ambrosian Milan (standalone) Documented — Augustine says in his Confessions that he was led to Ambrose at Milan so that Ambrose might lead him to God. Before his baptism in 387 he wrote to Ambrose about his past errors and asked what he should read.
  • influenced The Gallic Nicene Episcopate Documented — Augustine quotes Hilary in his own book On the Trinity. He gives Hilary's short formula for the three persons and names Hilary's book as its source. He calls Hilary a man of real authority on Scripture and the faith.
  • influenced Donatism Documented — Tyconius was a Donatist writer of about 380. In On Christian Doctrine, Augustine calls him a Donatist and sets out the seven rules of his Book of Rules. He says they give real help in opening the hidden sense of Scripture.

Influenced

  • influenced The Reformed Cities - Zurich & Geneva Documented — In his Institutes, Calvin calls on Augustine as his guide on grace and on God's choice of the saved. He says he could fill a whole book with Augustine's words alone and need no others.
  • influenced Donatism Documented — Cyprian of Carthage was a leading voice of the church in Africa in the 250s. Augustine tells the Donatists that they keep quoting Cyprian's letters, his opinion and his council. He asks why they use Cyprian's authority to defend their split.
  • influenced Latin African Christianity under Byzantium Documented — Around 550 Facundus of Hermiane wrote in defense of the Three Chapters. In it he cited 'blessed Cyprian, bishop of Carthage and martyr'. He called Augustine the 'most wise and most famous teacher of the churches of Christ'. Procopius says the people of Carthage in 533 revered Cyprian above all other holy men.
  • influenced Port-Royal Jansenism Documented — Port-Royal took its view of grace from Augustine of Hippo. Rome condemned five propositions in 1653. They were said to come from Cornelius Jansen's book, which he named Augustinus. In 1656 Pascal mocked the idea that Augustine's grace became false the moment Antoine Arnauld defended it.

Argued Against

  • argued against Pelagianism Documented — Augustine, bishop of Hippo, wrote against Pelagius by name. In On the Merits and Forgiveness of Sins, he answers points he says Pelagius made against original sin. In his Retractations he says he replied to a book by Pelagius. That book stood up for human nature against the grace of God.
  • argued against Donatism Documented — Augustine, bishop of Hippo, wrote a long book to the Donatists. He wrote it after their bishops and his met at Carthage in 411. He lists it in his Retractations. Earlier, Bishop Optatus of Milevis had written against the Donatist bishop Parmenian.
  • argued against Manichaeism Documented — Augustine, bishop of Hippo, spent nine years among the Manichaeans in his youth, as his Confessions record. Around 400 he wrote a long reply to Faustus, a Manichaean teacher he had once met.
  • argued against Novatianism (the Katharoi) Documented — Envoys came to Cyprian of Carthage with word that Novatian had been made bishop. Cyprian would not take them into communion. He later wrote that Novatian 'is not in the Church'. Augustine asked whether repentance had been fully taught 'before the Novatians opposed'.
  • argued against Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity Documented — Augustine urged Jerome to translate from the Greek Septuagint, not from the Hebrew. He feared the Latin and Greek churches would end up reading different Bibles. Even after Jerome died, he still held that no translator should be put above the Seventy.
  • argued against Gallic Monastic-Ascetic Christianity Documented — Prosper said the men who raised doubts lived at Marseilles. Augustine answered them in On the Predestination of the Saints. He wrote it in 428 or 429 to Prosper and Hilary, for the brothers they were worried about. Prosper adds that Augustine's earlier book On Rebuke and Grace had met like doubts in Africa.
  • argued against The Luciferians Documented — Augustine warns his readers not to listen to the Luciferians. They do not baptize again, he says, and in that they do right. But they cut themselves off from the unity of the church, and would rather be called Luciferians than Catholics.
  • argued against The Roman Church in the Third Century Documented — In 255 Cyprian and a council of African bishops wrote to Stephen of Rome. Those coming over from heretics, they said, must be baptized. Stephen replied that nothing new should be done, only hands laid on in penance. Cyprian sent his reply to Pompey and called it Stephen's error.

Opposed By

  • argued against Donatism Widely Accepted — Optatus quotes Parmenian, who led the Donatists at Carthage as their bishop. He said that 'one who was stained' could not wash away sins in baptism. Optatus says the Donatists baptized the faithful a second time. They used this rule on the clergy of the Catholic side.
  • argued against Gallic Monastic-Ascetic Christianity Documented — Around 428 Prosper of Aquitaine wrote to Augustine from Gaul. He reported that many servants of Christ at Marseilles thought Augustine's writing on election ran against the Fathers. They thought it ran against the mind of the church as well.
  • argued against Pelagianism Documented — Julian of Eclanum wrote letters that named Augustine and called the Catholic party Manichaeans. Augustine says the Pope sent them on to him, and he answered in four books. He also says Pelagius at Rome could not bear his prayer, 'Give what Thou commandest.'

In Contact With

  • in contact with Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity Documented — Jerome and Augustine wrote to each other across the sea for years. Augustine told Jerome of a bishop whose congregation rose up over the new wording of a line in Jonah. Jerome later wrote that he was resolved to love and respect Augustine and to defend his opinions as his own.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Cyprian, bishop of Carthage in the mid-200s, told his congregation, when plague struck, that caring only for their own was nothing great, before he was beheaded outside the city in 258. A century and a half later, at Hippo, Augustine preached in church and in homes, and listeners, the rival church's people too, brought shorthand writers to take down what he said.

Legacy

The North African church itself did not survive. The Vandals took the province in the 430s. The Arab conquest came in the seventh century. After it, Latin Christianity there slowly died out. In Geneva, Calvin wrote that he could fill a whole book with Augustine's words on God's choice of the saved. Both churches claimed Cyprian: when the Donatists brought his authority forward for baptizing again, Augustine answered that they should keep Cyprian's example of unity instead. On Cyprian's feast Augustine preached that where a savage crowd had once gathered to shed his blood, a crowd now gathered to honor him, at an altar called Cyprian's table because he was offered there. Possidius copied Augustine's letter on clergy staying with their people into his Life, as very useful, even necessary, for priests. After Augustine's death, Hippo, abandoned by its people, was burned by the enemy. A century later, Procopius says, the people of Carthage revered Cyprian above all saints and kept a festival called the Cypriana at his church by the sea, which the Vandals had taken from the Christians. More than a century after Augustine, Facundus, a bishop of Byzantine Africa, still called Cyprian bishop of Carthage and martyr, and Augustine the most wise and most famous teacher of the churches of Christ.

Experience It Today

  • Hippo Regius and the Basilica of Saint Augustine, Annaba, Algeria — the excavated ruins of Augustine's city lie below a nineteenth-century basilica overlooking the site, both open to visitors

Open Questions

  • Cyprian's withdrawal in 250. His own letters give his reason: the crowd kept shouting for him, and he left for his people's peace, so as not to stir the riot further, and led them by letter. The Roman clergy's letter on the hired man who leaves the sheep, and his deacon Pontius's long defense, show that the withdrawal was questioned.
  • The charge behind the split at Carthage. The rival side said that the bishop who had made Caecilian bishop had handed over the Scriptures to be burned. Optatus, a Catholic bishop writing some sixty years later, says the charge was false, and quotes a hearing that cleared that bishop, Felix. The old sources give the split no year beyond soon after the great persecution.
  • The rival church speaks only through its enemies. Its own rule, that one who was stained could not wash away sins, survives in Optatus's quotation of its bishop Parmenian. Possidius says that in Augustine's early years the rebaptizing Donatist party held a large multitude of the Africans, while the Catholic church had long lain prostrate.
  • Who the heretics were at the Gladness. Augustine's letter says only that the sound of feasting came from the church of the heretics. That they were the Donatists rests on Possidius, who says Donatists lived in Hippo and the towns nearby. Optatus used the word differently: he called the Donatists schismatics, who had gone outside the church yet shared its true sacraments, and kept heretics for those who taught falsely about God.
  • The plague. Its account rests on Pontius, Cyprian's deacon and admirer, who says that people fled the sick and left their own, and that the dead lay through the whole city.
  • Several dates rest on modern reckoning, not on the old sources: Cyprian's election as bishop, the year of the plague, the years Augustine was made priest and bishop, and the years of his letters on the Gladness and on staying with the people.
