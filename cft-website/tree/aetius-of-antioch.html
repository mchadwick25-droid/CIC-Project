Aetius of Antioch

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Anomoean (Eunomian) Christianity.

Known for
A bold Arian logician who taught that the Son is unlike the Father, founding the Anomoean party; his own allies gave him up in 360.
Also called
Atheus, "the godless", a name Socrates and Sozomen report and Athanasius uses
Dates
Active from about 350; died at Constantinople after 366
Place
From Coele-Syria; taught at Antioch and Alexandria
Role
Deacon, teacher and logician

About This Person

About the year 350, at Antioch, Bishop Leontius made a man named Aetius a deacon and let him preach. Flavian and Diodorus, who led the orthodox party in the city, raised such an uproar that Leontius had to stop him. Archibald Robertson, who edited Athanasius in English, tells the story. Aetius spoke so boldly about God, Sozomen, the church historian, says, that many people called him Atheus, the godless one.

Philostorgius, a later historian who admired him, says Aetius came from Coele-Syria and was poor. His history survives only in a hostile summary by Photius, a later patriarch of Constantinople. In it Aetius works as a goldsmith to keep himself and his mother, then turns to logic. Socrates of Constantinople, who did not admire him, says he leaned on Aristotle's book of Categories. He built arguments, Socrates says, that deceived himself.

Aetius taught that the Son is not like the Father. If God alone is unbegotten in his being, he reasoned, then the Son, who is begotten, cannot share that being. He set this out in a short book of claims, the Syntagmation. It survives because Epiphanius of Salamis copied it out to refute it. Those who followed Aetius were called Anomoeans, from the Greek word for "unlike."

Many Arians found this too much. They liked to say the Son was like the Father, and to stop using the word "substance" at all. A council met at Constantinople in 360. There his friend Eudoxius, bishop of Antioch, gave him up. In Robertson's words, Aetius was "a sacrifice to the Emperor's scruples." The council took away his office of deacon.

When the emperor Julian came to power, he called Aetius back from exile and sent him warm letters. Philostorgius says Julian gave him land on the island of Lesbos. Aetius later lived at Constantinople, where he died. His pupil Eunomius of Cyzicus carried the teaching on, and in time the sect took his name.

Key Works

  • The Syntagmation: a short book of logical propositions arguing the Son is unlike the Father, kept inside Epiphanius's Panarion 76, which copies it out to refute it

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The Anomoeans, later called Eunomians after his pupil Eunomius of Cyzicus, who carried his teaching on.

How they're remembered

  • Sozomen says he reasoned so boldly about God that many people called him Atheus, the godless one.
  • At the Council of Constantinople in 360 his friend Eudoxius, then bishop of Antioch, gave him up, and the council took away his office of deacon.

At Major Councils

  • Councils of Ariminum and Seleucia, 359: His teaching was a cause of the council; deposed at Constantinople

What Is Uncertain

  • His life is told mostly through partisans. Philostorgius, who praised him, gives the story of the poor goldsmith who rose by logic; his history survives only in a hostile summary by Photius. Socrates, Sozomen and Epiphanius, who opposed him, tell the rest.
  • Socrates of Constantinople, a hostile witness, says he leaned on Aristotle's Categories and framed arguments that deceived himself, and that his fellow Arians pronounced him a heretic. Philostorgius praised the force of his arguments.
  • The years of his birth and death are not given in a held source. Philostorgius places his death at Constantinople, after the revolt of Procopius and after Julian had given him land on Lesbos.
