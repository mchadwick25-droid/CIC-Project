The Enlightenment & Awakening Era · 1666-1815 · Russian north, Siberia

Russian north · Siberia, today in Russia

The Old Believers

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In the 1650s Patriarch Nikon set out to correct the Russian service books and rituals against contemporary Greek practice — two fingers in the sign of the cross became three, the spelling of Jesus's name changed, processions reversed direction. And a great part of the Russian church refused. To them the old rite was not a detail but the faith itself, handed down and now being altered by a church they concluded had fallen; the council of 1666-67 anathematized them, and the state hunted them for generations. Their great voice was Archpriest Avvakum, who wrote his own life story from an underground prison at Pustozersk before he was burned in 1682 — the first sustained autobiography in Russian, plain-spoken, furious and unexpectedly funny. Cut off from bishops, the movement divided between those who found priests and those who concluded the priesthood had ended altogether, and survived in the far north, the forests and Siberia, in some cases by mass self-immolation rather than submission.

In Its Own Record

Grew Out Of

  • The Russian Church, Stoglav to the Eve of Nikon Documented Major — Archpriest Avvakum, the great voice of the Old Believers, was ordained in the Russian church before Nikon's reforms. He writes that he became a deacon at twenty and a priest two years later. His translators date this to about 1644. His spiritual father was John Neronov, an archpriest in Moscow.

Continued As

  • The Old Believers After 1815 Documented Major — In 1814 an English account says the Old Believers were divided into priestly and priestless groups. The priestly group took priests from the national church. By 1827 Nicholas I cut off the runaway priests the Popovtsy relied on. In 1846 the same priestly Old Believers found a bishop at Belaya Krinitsa.

Influenced By

  • Muscovite Monastic Christianity Inferential-Thin — F. C. Conybeare's 1921 history of the Russian dissenters looks for the roots of the Old Believer schism in older Moscow. He names Joseph of Volokolamsk and his disciples as the men who built its church nationalism. The tie rests on this one reading.
  • The Russian Church, Stoglav to the Eve of Nikon Documented — The Moscow council of 1551, the Stoglav, ordered bishops and priests to bless and sign themselves with two fingers. When the new books required three, the monks of Solovetsky refused. In their statement of 1666 they cited chapter 31 of the Stoglav on the sign of the cross.

Argued Against

  • The Russian Church — Nikon to the Holy Synod Documented — In prison at Pustozersk, Avvakum and the men held with him wrote out a statement for an officer. It named the visiting Patriarch Paisius and his fellows as cursed, a gathering of heretics. It called Nikon the maker of the heresy. Avvakum also wrote that the ban on him came from heretics.

Opposed By

  • The Russian Church — Nikon to the Holy Synod Documented Major — The council's record says Avvakum and the priest Lazar would not repent and called the council false to the faith. So the council, with the patriarchs of Alexandria, Antioch and Moscow, laid them under anathema once more. A history of 1838 by an official of the Synod says the council threw out their case on the cross and the Creed.Lasting result: A Moscow church council, with two Eastern patriarchs on it, laid a solemn curse on Avvakum and the priest Lazar.

Broke With

  • The Russian Church — Nikon to the Holy Synod Documented Major — Both sides recorded the break. The council's record says Avvakum was stripped of his priesthood and laid under anathema in the cathedral on 13 May. Avvakum wrote that the bishops cursed him and the deacon Fyodor, and that he cursed them back.

In Contact With

  • The Russian Church — Nikon to the Holy Synod Inferential-Thin Major — In 1799 Old Believers in Moscow asked the official church for priests and holy oil. Metropolitan Plato looked over their sixteen terms, and in 1800 Tsar Paul I approved them. The terms let them keep the old books under priests of the church. The arrangement came to be called Edinoverie. This rests on one older account, by F. C. Conybeare in 1921. No modern study of it has been consulted.Lasting result: In 1800 Tsar Paul I approved terms that let Old Believers who joined the official church keep using the old books.

Sources

  • Archpriest Avvakum: The Life Written by Himself (ed./trans. Brostrom, Michigan Slavic, 1979; new trans. Gluck & Brostrom, Columbia Russian Library, 2021), with his Pustozersk letters and petitions (primary) — A first-rank inside voice, written in an underground earthen prison, 1669-75.
  • Boyarynia Feodosia Morozova: Avvakum's letters to her and the Tale of Boiarynia Morozova (trans. in Ziolkowski's edition, 2000) (primary) — A noblewoman starved to death for the old rite (1675); women were numerically central in the priestless communities.
  • The communities' own layer: the Solovki petitions (1667) and siege record (1668-76); the Vyg community - Pomorskie otvety (1723, bespopovtsy self-defense), Semyon Denisov's Vinograd Rossiiskii and Solovki-fathers history; popovtsy settlement records (Vetka, Kerzhenets); community rules (primary) — Denisov's work follows the martyrology genre, so read it with that framing in mind. The division between priestly and priestless communities appears directly in the sources. Largely available only in untranslated Russian editions.
  • The establishment record: the 1666-67 Moscow council's acts and anathemas, state persecution decrees (1685 articles), interrogation records (primary) — The schism documented by its persecutors; testimony given under threat of death, so treat it as coerced evidence.
  • Robert Crummey, The Old Believers and the World of Antichrist (Wisconsin, 1970) and Old Believers in a Changing World (2011), with Georg Michels, At War with the Church (Stanford, 1999) (secondary) — Michels complicates the movement's early coherence. The later merchant records referenced elsewhere are 19th-century, later than this movement's founding period.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 5, ch. 13 'Eastern Orthodoxy in Russia and Ukraine in the age of the Counter-Reformation', with 'The Russian Orthodox Church in imperial Russia 1721-1917' (Dixon) (reference) — The Dixon chapter's number is inconsistent across listings - cited as ch. 14 in some, ch. 15 in others.
