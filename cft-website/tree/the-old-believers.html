The Old Believers

The Russian north and Siberia, 1666 to 1815 — Christians who would not change how they crossed themselves, and who took to the forests, the prisons and sometimes the fire rather than accept a corrected service book.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:18

Read along

In the 1650s Patriarch Nikon set out to correct the Russian service books and rituals against contemporary Greek practice — two fingers in the sign of the cross became three, the spelling of Jesus's name changed, processions reversed direction. And a great part of the Russian church refused. To them the old rite was not a detail but the faith itself, handed down and now being altered by a church they concluded had fallen; the council of 1666-67 anathematized them, and the state hunted them for generations. Their great voice was Archpriest Avvakum, who wrote his own life story from an underground prison at Pustozersk before he was burned in 1682 — the first sustained autobiography in Russian, plain-spoken, furious and unexpectedly funny. Cut off from bishops, the movement divided between those who found priests and those who concluded the priesthood had ended altogether, and survived in the far north, the forests and Siberia, in some cases by mass self-immolation rather than submission.

People and Stories

Voices in the Record

  • Archpriest Avvakum — the movement's leading writer and martyr, who composed his autobiography in an earth-prison and was burned at the stake in 1682
  • Boyarynia Feodosia Morozova — a noblewoman who gave up her rank for the old rite and was starved to death in an underground cell in 1675
  • Patriarch Nikon — the reformer whose corrections caused the split, and who was himself deposed by the same council that condemned his opponents
  • Semyon Denisov — of the Vyg community on the White Sea, who wrote the movement's own histories and helped compose the Pomorian Answers (1723)

Documented Stories

‘Until Our Very Death, Markovna’

Avvakum Petrov had been exiled to Siberia in 1653 for opposing Nikon's corrections, and spent ten years there, most of them in the Dauria beyond Lake Baikal under the voevoda Afanasy Pashkov, who flogged him, kept him in an unheated tower through a winter, and worked him with the expedition's men until, as he wrote, ‘the children and I cried, and the wife did also’. Two of his sons died. His Life, written a decade later in an earthen prison at Pustozersk on the Arctic coast, tells the journey home. Somewhere on the ice of the Siberian rivers his wife Anastasia Markovna, who had followed him into exile with the children and had lately given birth, slipped and fell, and another exhausted man fell on top of her. Avvakum came up and helped her rise. ‘How long, archpriest,’ she asked him, ‘will these sufferings last?’ ‘Markovna, until our very death.’ ‘Good enough, Petrovich,’ she said; ‘then let us be getting on.’ He records it in three lines and moves on to the next hardship. They reached Moscow in 1664; he refused the tsar's offers and was exiled again, then confined at Pustozersk from 1667 with three companions, where he wrote the Life at his confessor's urging in the plain, angry, comic Russian of speech rather than of books. He was burned in a log house at Pustozersk on 14 April 1682 with the others. Markovna was still alive; the movement remembered her as well as him.

The Monastery That Held Out for Eight Years

The Solovetsky monastery on its islands in the White Sea, was one of the holiest places in Russia and one of the richest, a fortress with its own cannon and hundreds of monks, servants and pilgrims. When the corrected service books arrived in 1657 the brothers locked them in the treasury and went on using the old ones, and when the council of 1667 confirmed the corrections they wrote to the tsar. Their fifth petition, of 1667, is the fullest early statement of the Old Believer case: that the fathers of the Russian church had been saints under the old books, that the Greeks who supplied the new ones had lived under the Turks and lost their faith, and that they would rather die than change; it circulated in copies across the north for a century. Tsar Alexei sent troops in 1668. The siege was slack for years, since the monastery's walls were strong and the besiegers few, and inside the community was divided between monks who wanted only to keep the old rite and lay fugitives who wanted to fight the tsar. In January 1676 a monk named Feoktist showed the voevoda Ivan Meshcherinov a way in through a blocked window. The garrison was massacred, some hanged, some frozen on the ice, and the monastery reconsecrated by the new books; of several hundred inside, a few dozen were left alive. Tsar Alexei died in Moscow on 29 January, a week later, and the Old Believers took it as judgment.

The Burnings, and the Archpriest’s Blessing

The first mass self-burnings by Old Believers are recorded from 1672 in the forests of the Nizhny Novgorod region, where communities that had fled the new rite, hearing that soldiers and priests were coming to take them to be rebaptized or tortured, gathered into a church or a barn with their children, barred the doors and set fire to the building. The state's own decrees of 1685, which ordered the unrepentant burned at the stake, made the choice for many seem one between fires. Avvakum, in his prison at Pustozersk, was asked what he thought and wrote, in letters that circulated, that those who burned themselves rather than fall into the hands of Antichrist did well: ‘Blessed is this deed; they took their bodies and went to God.’ At the Paleostrovsky monastery on Lake Onega in March 1687, some two thousand seven hundred people who had seized the monastery under a former Solovki monk, Ignatii, burned themselves when the troops arrived; a second group of about fifteen hundred did the same there in 1688. By the 1690s the movement's own leaders were divided: the founders of the Vyg community, who had lived through the Paleostrov fires, argued that self-burning was permitted only when capture was certain, and later that it was not permitted at all, and the practice declined, though it recurred as late as the 1780s. The Old Believers have never denied it; they have argued about it, in their own books, ever since.

Connections

In Its Own Record

The Old Believers grew out of Muscovite Christianity, standing as the enduring counterpart to the official church's Nikonian reforms. Edinoverie, established in 1800, sits on the boundary shared between the two churches. The next entry tells how the priestly Old Believers got bishops of their own in 1846, at Belaya Krinitsa.

Descended from

  • grew out of The Russian Church, Stoglav to the Eve of Nikon Documented Major — Archpriest Avvakum, the great voice of the Old Believers, was ordained in the Russian church before Nikon's reforms. He writes that he became a deacon at twenty and a priest two years later. His translators date this to about 1644. His spiritual father was John Neronov, an archpriest in Moscow.

Direct descendants

  • continued as The Old Believers After 1815 Documented Major — In 1814 an English account says the Old Believers were divided into priestly and priestless groups. The priestly group took priests from the national church. By 1827 Nicholas I cut off the runaway priests the Popovtsy relied on. In 1846 the same priestly Old Believers found a bishop at Belaya Krinitsa.

Broke With

  • broke with The Russian Church — Nikon to the Holy Synod Documented Major — Both sides recorded the break. Avvakum wrote that the bishops cursed him and the deacon Fyodor, and that he cursed them back.

Influenced By

  • influenced The Russian Church, Stoglav to the Eve of Nikon Documented — The Moscow council of 1551, the Stoglav, ordered bishops and priests to bless and sign themselves with two fingers. When the new books required three, the monks of Solovetsky refused. In their statement of 1666 they cited chapter 31 of the Stoglav on the sign of the cross.

Argued Against

  • argued against The Russian Church — Nikon to the Holy Synod Documented — In prison at Pustozersk, Avvakum and the men held with him wrote out a statement for an officer. It named the visiting Patriarch Paisius and his fellows as cursed, a gathering of heretics. It called Nikon the maker of the heresy. Avvakum also wrote that the ban on him came from heretics.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

When Patriarch Nikon corrected the Russian service books in the 1650s — changing how the faithful crossed themselves and how Jesus's name was spelled — a large part of the church refused to accept it as a mere detail and was anathematized for it in 1666. Their great voice, Archpriest Avvakum, wrote the first sustained autobiography in Russian from an underground prison before he was burned at the stake in 1682, and the movement survived in the forests and Siberia, in some communities by mass self-immolation rather than submission.

Legacy

Old Belief is still practiced. Old Believer churches exist today in Russia, Romania, the Baltic states, Alaska, Oregon and South America. Some have their own bishops and some have no clergy at all. All keep the chant, icons and books of the time before Nikon. Their stubbornness saved a whole layer of medieval Russian church culture that the official church had modernized away. Old styles of icon painting, znamenny chant and the copying of books by hand lived on in these groups. Collectors and scholars later found them there. Avvakum's own Life, written in his prison, came out in English in 1924.

Experience It Today

  • The Rogozhskoye settlement, Moscow — since 1771 the spiritual center of the largest Old Believer church, with its cathedrals and old-rite services

Sources

Doctrinal Floor Note

No question of doctrine arises here at all. Old Believers held exactly the same creed as the church they left — the split was about ritual: how to make the sign of the cross, how to spell the name of Jesus, which way a procession should walk around a church.
