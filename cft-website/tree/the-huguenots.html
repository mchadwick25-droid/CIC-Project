The Huguenots

France, 1559 to 1685 — a church that organized itself in secret, survived a century of civil war, and then scattered across the world rather than convert.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 0:56

Read along

France's Protestants organized themselves before any ruler told them to. In 1559 delegates met secretly in Paris to adopt a confession of faith and a book of discipline, creating a national church inside a Catholic kingdom. What followed was nearly a century of civil war and precarious tolerance — the St Bartholomew's Day massacres of August 1572, the Edict of Nantes in 1598 that gave them legal existence and fortified towns, then the slow erosion of that edict until Louis XIV revoked it in 1685. Rather than convert, several hundred thousand left, carrying their trades, their psalters and their memory of what it was to flee into England, the Dutch Republic, Prussia, Switzerland, South Africa and the American colonies.

Documented Stories

Charlotte Arbaleste Walks Out of Paris

Charlotte Arbaleste was twenty-two, widowed the year before, and living near the Louvre with her baby daughter when the bells of Saint-Germain-l’Auxerrois rang before dawn on St Bartholomew’s Day and the killing of Admiral Coligny and the Protestant nobles in the city began. She wrote the account years later in the memoir she addressed to her son, so that he would know what his mother and his stepfather, Philippe Duplessis-Mornay, had come through. She describes hearing from her window that the admiral was dead; her host, a Catholic, telling her she must leave; hiding with her child in the attic of a neighbor who sold cloth, then in another house whose master let her stay only if she would go to Mass, which she refused; the streets full of bodies and of men marking the doors; a soldier who came looking for her and was told she had gone. On the fourth day she paid a boatman to take her up the Seine, was stopped at the city gate and questioned — she said she was a Catholic going to her lands, and produced a rosary — and reached Sens, where a Catholic relative took her in. She married Mornay in 1576, and they spent forty years as the Huguenot cause’s chief diplomat and his partner. The memoir is one of the few accounts of the massacre by a woman who lived through it, and its detail is what the histories lack.

Jean Migault’s Dragoons

Jean Migault was the Protestant schoolmaster and notary of Mougon, a village in Poitou, when the intendant Marillac began the first dragonnades in the summer of 1681, four years before the Edict of Nantes was formally revoked. His journal, written for his children after the family reached the Netherlands, is the fullest account of the method from a household that suffered it. Soldiers were quartered on Protestant families with orders to live at their expense and make themselves intolerable until the family abjured; conversion ended the billeting at once. Migault describes the dragoons arriving at his neighbors’ houses, the noise through the night, the furniture broken and sold, the food and wine consumed, the women threatened; and he lists, name by name, the families of Mougon who signed the abjuration within days — most of them — and the few who did not. His own wife, pregnant, was so terrified by the soldiers in the house that she miscarried and died some months later; he records it in a page. He moved the children from village to village for six years, teaching where he could, refusing to sign, and in 1688 got them, in two parties, to La Rochelle and onto ships for Holland, bribing officers and lying about their destination. It is not a martyrology: it records the neighbors who gave in without contempt, and it records his own fear. Several hundred thousand Huguenots left France; the Migaults were eleven of them, and their father counted the cost.

In Its Own Record

Shaped by Calvin's Geneva, which wrote its creed and sent it pastors. Hunted at home, its people later fled across Europe and beyond, in one of history's clearest cases of a church driven into exile.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

French Protestants organized themselves before any king told them to, adopting a confession of faith and a book of discipline in secret in 1559. After a century of civil war and the revocation of the Edict of Nantes in 1685, several hundred thousand chose exile over conversion — and gave the English language the word 'refugee.'

Legacy

The tradition still exists in France, in the united Protestant church that grew out of it, and the dispersion left permanent traces abroad. Huguenot surnames, silk-weaving and watchmaking trades, and congregations founded in London, Berlin, Amsterdam, Cape Town and Charleston. The English word 'refugee' comes from the French réfugié, a word that came into use for them.

Experience It Today

  • The Musée du Désert in the Cévennes, France — the memorial museum of Huguenot persecution and clandestine worship, in the farmhouse country where it happened

How Sure the Record Is

Rich: synod acts, memoirs, martyrologies, refugee narratives

  • Gallican Confession (1559) and Discipline ecclesiastique, with the national synod acts (Quick, Synodicon in Gallia Reformata, 1692, English) (primary) — A persecuted national church's complete self-governing record.
  • Jean Crespin, Histoire des martyrs (1554 and expansions) (primary) — An early example of the Reformed martyrology genre taking shape — documentary letters presented within a shaping, partisan frame. Available in full only in French.
  • Memoirs and refugee narratives: Charlotte Arbaleste de Mornay's memoirs (Eng. trans. Crump, 1926); Jean Migault's Journal (dragonnades and flight) (primary) — A woman's massacre-survival account and a schoolmaster's family refugee narrative from the 1680s.
  • The Marot-Beze Psalter in Huguenot practice - sung at the stake, in battle, in secret assemblies (primary) — Psalm-singing was the tradition's most ordinary act — and, sung at the stake or in secret, its most defiant.
  • Barbara Diefendorf, Beneath the Cross (OUP, 1991), with Philip Benedict, Christ's Churches Purely Reformed (2002), chapters (secondary)
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 6, ch. 8 'The second wave of Protestant expansion' (Benedict), with ch. 15 (Gregory) (reference) — The chapter's author, Philip Benedict, is the tradition's leading historian.
