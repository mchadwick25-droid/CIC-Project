From Cilicia to Ararat, 1300 to 1441 — a church that lost its kingdom and moved its see back to where it had started.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:24

Read along

The Armenian kingdom of Cilicia lay on the Mediterranean. Through the fourteenth century it was pressed by Mamluk Egypt and by Latin Europe at the same time. Its kings needed help from the west, and again and again they pushed for a union with Rome. Inside the church, those who saw its independence at stake resisted. The kingdom fell in 1375, when the Mamluks took Sis, and its last king died in exile in Paris in 1393. The catholicos, the head of the church, stayed at Sis for two more generations. He was more and more cut off from most Armenians, who lived far to the east under other rulers. Then in 1441 a national council met at Vagharshapat and elected a catholicos at Etchmiadzin. This was the church's original see, left behind nearly a thousand years earlier, and the center of Armenian church life moved home. Sis did not give up its own succession, so the church has had two catholicoi ever since. Much of what we hear from Armenians of this period comes from colophons. These are the notes scribes wrote at the ends of the books they copied, saying who they were and what was happening around them.

Documented Stories

Tovma Metsopetsi Brings the Catholicosate Home

Tovma Metsopetsi was abbot of the monastery of Metsop near Lake Van and had written a history of Timur’s campaigns from what he had seen of them. In the 1430s he became the leading voice of the eastern Armenian clergy who held that the catholicosate at Sis, in Cilicia under Mamluk rule, had become corrupt, latinizing and cut off from the great majority of Armenians living under Turkmen and Persian rulers, and that it should return to Etchmiadzin, the see of Gregory the Illuminator, empty for nearly a thousand years. He negotiated with the Qara Qoyunlu ruler Jahanshah for permission, and in May 1441 some three hundred bishops, abbots and vardapets met at Vagharshapat. Tovma wrote the account. He describes the debate over whether the church could elect a catholicos while the incumbent at Sis lived, the decision that it could since Sis had forfeited its standing, and the candidates. Tovma’s own party favored the learned Zakaria of Havuts Tar; the assembly, he says, was swayed by a hermit’s reputation and elected Kirakos of Virap, a monk from the monastery of Khor Virap, whom Tovma describes with visible disappointment as pious and unlettered. Kirakos was consecrated; the catholicosate has been at Etchmiadzin since; Sis did not resign, and the church has had two catholicoi ever since. His account is the record of a national church deciding to move its center by vote, of the man who made it happen not getting the result he wanted, and saying so.

A council of the Armenian church met in 1342 under the catholicos Mekhitar and King Constantine to answer it. An eighteenth-century editor adds that an earlier king, Leo, had asked the pope for help against the Saracens. He was told to renounce the errors first.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Through the fourteenth century the Armenian kingdom of Cilicia was squeezed between Mamluk Egypt and Latin Europe. Europe pressed for union with Rome, and many in the church resisted it again and again. The kingdom fell to the Mamluks in 1375, and its last king died in exile in Paris in 1393. The catholicos, the head of the church, stayed at Sis for two more generations. Then in 1441 a national council elected a catholicos at Etchmiadzin. This was the church's original see, left behind nearly a thousand years earlier. Sis kept its own line of succession too, so the church has had two catholicoi ever since.

Legacy

Since 1441 Etchmiadzin has been the Mother See of the Armenian Apostolic Church, and it still is today. The see at Sis, whose holder did not move, kept its own line. It goes on as the Catholicosate of Cilicia, based since the 1930s at Antelias in Lebanon. The two sees are still in communion with one another. The books of these centuries, with their colophons, form the core of the Matenadaran collection in Yerevan. Scribes kept up the habit of writing those notes. So historians have a running record of ordinary disaster and survival as full as any in the medieval world.

Experience It Today

  • Etchmiadzin Cathedral, Vagharshapat, Armenia — the Mother See the church returned to in 1441, a UNESCO World Heritage Site
