Nicene Visigothic Iberia

Visigothic Spain, c. 560 to 711 CE — the one barbarian kingdom whose own council voted to change its creed, and then governed itself through its bishops until the conquest.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:19

Read along

For over a century the Visigoths ruled Spain as Homoian Christians, and lived uneasily beside the Nicene people under their rule. Then King Reccared announced his own conversion. At the third council of Toledo in 589, he brought the Gothic bishops with him. Nothing quite like it took place anywhere else: a kingdom's own church council formally dropped its creed and took up the creed of its subjects. What came next was one of the more striking feats of learning of the early Middle Ages. Isidore of Seville set out to gather what was left of classical learning into a single encyclopedia. A circle grew up around him. His brother Leander, Braulio of Zaragoza and Ildefonsus of Toledo wrote letters, liturgy, histories and rules. The councils of Toledo worked as a cross between a synod and a parliament, and made laws for church and kingdom at once. Their record also holds a harsh body of law against Spain's Jews, which grew worse with time. The Gothic kingdom fell at a stroke to the Muslim conquest of 711, but its church lived on under the new rulers.

Documented Stories

Hermenegild: Rebel to One Gregory, Martyr to the Other

Hermenegild was the elder son of Leovigild, the strongest of the Arian Visigothic kings, and had been given Seville to govern with his bride Ingund, a Catholic Frankish princess whom the Arian queen-mother had tried to force into rebaptism by throwing her into a fishpond. Leovigild besieged Seville, took it in 584, and imprisoned his son at Tarragona, where in 585 he was killed — on the king’s order, by an axe, after refusing communion from an Arian bishop at Easter, according to the one account; simply killed, according to the other. Gregory of Tours, writing within a few years in Gaul, is cold: Hermenegild had rebelled against his father, and the Franks who might have helped him did not; Gregory records the death without a martyr’s crown. Gregory the Great, writing in Rome a decade later from what Leander had told him in Constantinople, makes him a martyr in the Dialogues — the refusal of the Arian Eucharist, the axe, lights seen over the body, and the conversion of his brother Reccared as the fruit of his blood. The Spanish church itself said almost nothing: Isidore, Leander’s own brother, calls the revolt a ‘tyranny’, and no Spanish council or calendar commemorated Hermenegild for five hundred years. The two Gregories are both reading the same events; one saw a civil war, the other saw a witness, and the church that had the most reason to remember him chose to forget.

Toledo IV, Canon 57

Around 615 King Sisebut ordered the Jews of his kingdom to be baptized or leave. The sources say that tens of thousands were baptized under threat of flogging and the loss of their goods. In 633 the bishops of Spain met at Toledo, in the fourth of the great councils, to make law for church and kingdom together. Isidore of Seville’s name stands first among their signatures. Their canon on the Jews begins as a statement of principle that the later church would quote for centuries. From now on, the synod decreed, no one was to be forced to believe, for God has mercy on whom he will and hardens whom he will. Such people were to be saved willingly, not against their will, so that the form of justice would be whole. Then the canon turns. Some had already been baptized under Sisebut. They had shared in the divine sacraments, the canon says: they had received the grace of baptism, been anointed with the chrism and taken the body and blood of the Lord. So they must be made to keep the faith they had taken under force or necessity. Otherwise the name of the Lord would be blasphemed, and the faith they had taken would be held cheap and contemptible. The canons that follow forbade the forcibly baptized to keep the Sabbath or to circumcise their sons. They could not hold office or own Christian slaves. Their children were to be taken from them and raised by Christians. The principle in the first sentence and the practice in the second have both been part of the tradition ever since. The same document holds them both.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

The Visigoths ruled Spain as Homoian Christians for over a century until King Reccared announced his own conversion, and at the third council of Toledo in 589 brought his bishops with him into the Nicene church of his subjects. What followed included Isidore of Seville's attempt to gather all of surviving classical learning into a single encyclopedia, and councils at Toledo that worked as something between a synod and a parliament, legislating for church and kingdom together.

Legacy

The same institution continued under Muslim rule as the Mozarabic Christianity of al-Andalus. Its liturgy went with it: the Mozarabic rite, formed in this period, is still celebrated in a chapel of Toledo Cathedral. Isidore of Seville's Etymologiae became one of the most copied books of the Middle Ages and served for centuries as the standard reference work of the Latin West. He is sometimes called the last scholar of the ancient world. The councils of Toledo left a large body of canon law that shaped later Western practice, and it was in Visigothic Spain that the Filioque clause first entered official use in the Creed. Several of its churches — small, sharply cut, horseshoe-arched — still stand in northern Spain.
