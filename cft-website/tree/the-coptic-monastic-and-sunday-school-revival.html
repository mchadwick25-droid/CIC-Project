The Coptic Monastic & Sunday-School Revival

Egypt, 1940s to 2010 — a monastic and Sunday-school revival continuing the map's longest unbroken lane back to the Desert itself.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 0:57

Read along

Two renewals ran together in the Coptic Orthodox Church and remade it. The first was catechetical. Habib Girgis, born in 1876, taught and wrote from the 1890s and built a church-wide Sunday-school movement, organized around 1918, that trained a generation of lay teachers and then of bishops. The second was monastic: from the middle of the century the desert monasteries, which had been emptying, were repopulated, and the movement gathered pace under Kyrillos VI, patriarch from 1959 to 1971. Matthew the Poor, of the monastery of St Macarius, wrote a body of spiritual work that came to be read well outside Egypt. Shenouda III, who came out of the Sunday-school generation, became patriarch in 1971 and published steadily from then to 2010.

Documented Stories

The Deacon Who Taught the Bishops

Habib Girgis was born in Cairo in 1876 to a church that had, by its own account, almost no educated clergy and no religious instruction for its children; the Coptic Clerical College, founded in 1893, admitted him in its first class. He never left it. Appointed a teacher in 1898 and dean in 1918, he taught at the college for fifty-three years and wrote the textbooks the church did not have: catechisms in Arabic, a manual of doctrine, hymns, and, from 1907, a magazine, al-Karma, in which he argued that a church that did not teach would lose its people to the missionaries and to indifference. From about 1900 he had begun gathering children on Fridays and Sundays in the churches of Cairo to teach them the faith in classes, with lay teachers he trained himself; the movement was formally organized in 1918, and by his death it had classes in most parishes of Egypt and a generation of young men who had grown up in it. That generation was the revival. Among his pupils and his pupils' pupils were Nazir Gayed, who became Shenouda III, Yusuf Iskandar, who became Matthew the Poor, and the monks who repopulated Wadi Natrun from the 1950s; by 1971 the church could elect one of them patriarch. Habib Girgis died in 1951, a deacon and a layman.

The Patriarch Sent to the Desert

Shenouda III had been patriarch since 1971, the first from the Sunday School generation, and had made the church a public body as his predecessors had not: preaching to thousands each Wednesday in the cathedral, and protesting to the government, with fasts and canceled Easter celebrations, against the attacks on Copts that had multiplied with the Islamist revival. In June 1981 sectarian fighting in Cairo killed dozens. On 3 September 1981 Anwar Sadat ordered the arrest of some fifteen hundred people, eight bishops among them, and, by presidential decision, revoked the state's recognition of Shenouda as patriarch, appointed a committee of five bishops to administer the church, and confined him to the monastery of St Bishoy in Wadi Natrun, the desert his own generation had repopulated. Shenouda went. He said no public word against the decree; the Holy Synod did not accept the committee; and the faithful kept his name in the liturgy. On 6 October Sadat was shot by Islamist officers, and his successor Hosni Mubarak released most of the detainees and left the patriarch where he was. Shenouda spent forty months in the desert writing and refusing to make the statements that would have bought his return; on 2 January 1985 Mubarak restored him, and he returned to a cathedral that could not hold the crowd. He was still patriarch a quarter-century later; the church came to count the exile as the moment it learned that its patriarch could be removed by a president and could outlast him.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Habib Girgis built a church-wide Sunday-school movement from the 1890s that trained generations of teachers and, later, bishops, while from mid-century the Egyptian desert monasteries, long emptying, filled again under Patriarch Kyrillos VI.

Legacy

By 2010 it was a living church whose lane on this map ran back, unbroken, to the desert fathers themselves. The Sunday schools had become the church's teaching system and the route by which much of its clergy was formed. The monasteries had filled again. Matthew the Poor's books found readers in traditions with no connection to Egypt at all. This church had always kept its martyrs in its liturgy: not remembered in the abstract, but named.
