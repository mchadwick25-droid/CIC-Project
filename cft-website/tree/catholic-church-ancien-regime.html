The Catholic Church of the Ancien Régime

Rome and Catholic Europe, 1650 to 1815 — the church at its most established and its most helpless, which lost its greatest order to its own kings and then nearly lost everything to a revolution.

About This Movement

Read along

This was the Catholic Church at the height of its establishment and on the edge of its worst crisis: enormously wealthy, woven into the monarchies of Europe, and increasingly unable to control them. Rome's condemnation of Jansenism in the bull Unigenitus (1713) turned into a fifty-year political crisis in France, with parlements and bishops fighting over it. Then the Catholic monarchies turned on the church's most effective order, expelling the Jesuits from Portugal, France, Spain and Naples and forcing the pope to suppress the Society altogether in 1773. Then came the Revolution: church property nationalized, clergy required to swear to a Civil Constitution that split them into jurors and refractories, thousands killed or driven abroad, and Pope Pius VI carried off to die a French prisoner in 1799. His successor signed a concordat with Napoleon in 1801, was imprisoned by him in turn, outlived him, and restored the Jesuits in 1814.

Rome ran its missions through Propaganda, the congregation for spreading the faith. Its collected decrees show three acts in these years.

In 1656 the Holy Office ruled on a set of questions. Missionaries in China had sent them to Propaganda in 1645. Chinese Christians could keep certain rites, it said. The cult, it said, seemed merely civil and political.

In 1659 Propaganda sent orders to its vicars apostolic. They were not to urge any people to change its rites, customs and ways. What is more absurd, it asked, than to carry France, Spain or Italy into China (in a plain English rendering)?

In 1726 Propaganda ruled that native priests must swear an oath too. This covered China, Tonkin and Cochinchina. The oath was the one set by the constitution Ex illa die.

Documented Stories

Sixteen Carmelites Sing to the Scaffold

The Carmel of Compiègne, north of Paris, had been suppressed with every other religious house in 1792, and its twenty-one nuns had gone on living in the town in four separate lodgings. In 1792 or 1793, as the persecution of the refractory clergy grew, the prioress, Thérèse of St Augustine proposed that the community offer itself to God as a sacrifice for the peace of France and the church, and the nuns made the act together, renewing it daily. They were arrested in June 1794 on the evidence of letters and devotional objects found in their rooms, taken to Paris, and tried by the Revolutionary Tribunal on 17 July as fanatics and enemies of the people; when a nun asked what fanaticism meant, the prosecutor answered that it was their religion. Sixteen were condemned that morning, aged from twenty-nine to seventy-eight, and taken that evening in carts to the Place du Trône-Renversé. Sister Marie of the Incarnation, who had been away when the others were arrested and survived to write the account, gathered from witnesses that they sang on the way, and that at the foot of the scaffold they sang the Veni Creator and renewed their vows to the prioress, who then sent them up one by one, the youngest, Constance, first, each kneeling for her blessing, and went last. The crowd, which was accustomed to jeer, was silent. Robespierre fell on 27 July, ten days later, and the nuns' community remembered its offering as answered.

The Last General in Castel Sant’Angelo

Lorenzo Ricci, a Florentine, had been general of the Society of Jesus since 1758, through the expulsions of the Jesuits from Portugal in 1759, France in 1764 and Spain and Naples in 1767. On 21 July 1773 Clement XIV signed the brief Dominus ac Redemptor suppressing the Society throughout the world, giving as his reasons the peace of the church and the complaints of the princes, and stating no crime. Ricci was taken first to the English College and then, in September, to Castel Sant'Angelo, where he was held in a cell without books other than a breviary, without visitors for long periods, and without ever being told the charge or brought before a judge; the cardinals' commission that examined him found nothing to try him for and did not release him. Pius VI, elected in 1775, was petitioned for Ricci's release and delayed. On 19 November 1775 Ricci, dying, dictated and signed a protestation before witnesses: that the Society of Jesus had given no cause for its suppression, which he declared with the certainty of a superior who knew it; that he himself had given no cause for his imprisonment; and that he forgave those responsible. He died on 24 November, aged seventy-two, and was buried at the Gesù with the honors the pope had refused him living. The Society was restored in 1814; the protestation was printed in every language its members spoke.

Liguori Signs What He Cannot Read

Alphonsus Liguori had founded the Congregation of the Most Holy Redeemer in 1732 to preach missions to the peasants and shepherds of the Kingdom of Naples, had written the moral theology that governed Catholic confessionals for two centuries, had served as a bishop, and in 1779 was living at Pagani, retired, crippled, deaf and nearly blind. The congregation had never been legally recognized by the Neapolitan crown, which regarded religious vows with suspicion, and its superiors, negotiating for recognition, accepted a revised rule, the Regolamento, which dropped the vows and altered the government of the order to satisfy the king's ministers. They brought it to Alphonsus as the rule he had written, approved by Rome in 1749, and he signed it. When the houses in the Papal States learned what had been done they appealed to Pius VI, who in September 1780 recognized the Papal States houses alone as the true congregation and declared that the Neapolitan houses, having accepted another rule, were no longer members; the founder, who had been deceived, was excluded with them. Witnesses at his canonization process recalled that he wept, said that the pope's will was God's will, and that he had deserved it, and asked the community to obey. He lived seven more years outside the congregation he had founded, in the house at Pagani, and died on 1 August 1787, aged ninety. The two branches were reunited in 1793, after his death, under the original rule.

Connections

In Its Own Record

This was the era's largest institutional story inside the Catholic Church: the Gallican Articles, the bull Unigenitus, and the Jansenist crisis it set off in France; the expulsion and suppression of the Jesuits between 1759 and 1773; the catastrophe of the French Revolution, which split the clergy between those who swore the required oath and those who refused, and left Pope Pius VI to die a prisoner in 1799; and the Concordat and the standoff between Pius VII and Napoleon that followed, ending with the Jesuits restored in 1814. The same period held the Quietist controversy, a Jansenist current that ran through the eighteenth century, Alphonsus Liguori's founding of the Redemptorists, and the broader current of Catholic Enlightenment thought.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Rome's condemnation of Jansenism in the bull Unigenitus (1713) set off a fifty-year political crisis in France, and within decades the Catholic monarchies themselves turned on the church's most effective order, forcing the pope to suppress the Jesuits altogether in 1773. Then came the Revolution — church property seized, clergy split by a forced oath, and Pope Pius VI dying a French prisoner in 1799 — until his successor Pius VII signed a concordat with Napoleon, was imprisoned by him in turn, and lived to restore the Jesuits in 1814.

Legacy

It survived, which was not obvious in 1799. The Revolution stripped away the wealth and political ties that had defined the church for centuries. What came out after 1815 was poorer, more clerical and far more turned toward Rome. The same line of popes carried it into the nineteenth century, when Catholics looked to Rome as never before. How much this crisis shaped that later church is a judgment, not a recorded fact. The loss of the Jesuits and their return set terms the church still works under. So did the loss of church lands, and the concordat as a way to make peace with a secular state. Its parish churches, seminaries and religious orders across Europe are still, physically, largely those of this period.
