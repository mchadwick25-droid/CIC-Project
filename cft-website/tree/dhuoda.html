Dhuoda

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Carolingian Reform Christianity.

Known for
Frankish noblewoman who wrote at Uzès, from 841, a handbook of Christian conduct for her elder son William. It holds her own verses and her epitaph.
Also called
Dodana, Duodana, Dodane
Dates
Married in 824, in Léopold Delisle's date. Édouard Bondurand reckons from the book's closing note that she began it on 30 November 841. He dates its completion to 2 February 843, where earlier scholars, as he reports, put it in 842. No source used here gives the year of her birth or death.
Place

Role
Noblewoman, wife of Bernard, duke of Septimania; author of a handbook of faith and conduct for her son

"I see many people in this world who rejoice in their children with them. But I, Dhuoda, see myself kept far away from you, my son William." So begins the handbook of faith that a Frankish noblewoman, living at Uzès in the south of France, dictated for her elder son. She began it in November 841, when William had just turned fifteen and had been sent to serve King Charles. That March she had borne a second son, and before he was baptized, her husband Bernard had the baby brought to him, with the bishop of Uzès.

She meant the book to stand in her place. Reading it and praying, she wrote, William could see her, "mind and body, as in a picture in a mirror." She was a noblewoman, not one of the clergy. Other teachers would teach him more, "but they will not teach on equal terms, with a heart burning in the breast, as I do, your mother." And she told William to show the book to his little brother when the boy could read.

She worked on it for more than a year, and in seventy-three chapters she set out a Christian life for a young man at court. He must love God first and then his father, and pray in short, pure words. He must serve Charles faithfully and honor priests, help the poor, widows, orphans and pilgrims, and judge without taking gifts. And he must pray for the Church, for kings and for the dead.

When the emperor Louis, Charlemagne's son, died in 840, his sons, Charles among them, went to war over the empire. Bernard, duke of Septimania, was in Aquitaine, and Dhuoda stayed on at Uzès, as she says, at her lord's command.

In 844 the Franks judged Bernard guilty of treason, and on Charles's order he was put to death. In 848 William seized Ampurias and Barcelona by trickery, and in 850 he was captured by greater trickery and killed at Barcelona.

Her book outlived the family. It was copied and kept, and printed in part in 1677 and whole in 1887. Its editor called it the oldest writing by a woman born in France to reach us. In 1923 two translators of the Catholic catechism named her manual among early works of Christian instruction, to answer the claim that the catechism began with Luther.

Almost all we know of her comes from her own book. The chronicles of her day name her husband and her son, but not her. In the book she writes her own epitaph and asks William to have it written on her grave, so that passers-by will pray for her. No record tells when or where Dhuoda died.

Key Works

  • Liber manualis (Handbook for William): a handbook of Christian conduct in seventy-three chapters, by Léopold Delisle's count, written for her son William. It teaches the love and search for God, honor for his father and loyalty to King Charles the Bald. It also teaches respect for priests, the virtues and the Beatitudes, and prayer for the living and the dead. Édouard Bondurand finds in it quotations from the poet Prudentius and from Donatus. He also finds a passage of Pliny the Elder retold, and countless passages of the Bible
  • Verses in the Handbook: her own lines, which she says she dictated herself, with all the rest. Léopold Delisle found that the first letters of the opening verses spell, in Latin, a greeting from Dhuoda to her beloved son William. They end with a word telling him to read. Édouard Bondurand found, from the Nîmes copy, that the first letters of chapter 69 spell, in Latin, verses to William her son. She tells William to read the book and later give it to his younger brother
  • Her epitaph (chapter 73): verses she asked William to have written on her tomb, whose first letters spell her name. They ask passers of every age and sex to pray for her

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Two manuscripts survive, Bondurand says. One is thirty-two leaves of a Carolingian copy, a small part of the text, found in the papers of E. Germer-Durand, which the library of Nîmes bought in 1885. The other is a seventeenth-century copy at Paris, made from a lost manuscript of the abbey of La Grasse.
  • Léopold Delisle reports that Mabillon printed the preface, the table and thirteen chapters in 1677. Baluze printed the preface and the closing note in 1688.
  • Bondurand's edition of 1887 printed the whole book. Where the Nîmes fragments survive, he prints their text, with the Paris readings in his notes.

How they're remembered

  • The authors of the Histoire littéraire de la France, as Bondurand quotes them, called the book a monument of literature and piety. They regretted that only part of it had been printed.
  • Bondurand calls it the oldest writing to survive by a woman born on the soil of France.
  • John A. McHugh and Charles J. Callan wrote the introduction to their 1923 English translation of the Roman Catechism. There they list the manual of Dhuoda, explaining Christian faith and morality, among works of popular instruction from the ninth century on.

What Is Uncertain

  • No source used here gives the year of Dhuoda's death. Bondurand thinks she died soon after the book was finished, before her husband and sons met their ends.
  • Her family. Léopold Delisle, as Bondurand quotes him, says her origin is unknown. Bondurand notes that she names none of her own kin, though she calls William's descent illustrious on both sides. Her name alone, he adds, would point to the nobility of Septimania. He gives two other women of the name, a Dodana dead by 813 and a Deda of 814. He says that is all that is known of her origin.
  • Outside her book, no source of her own time used here names her. Nithard and the Annals of Saint-Bertin name Bernard and William, but not Dhuoda.
  • Her birth. In her first chapter she calls herself small and of the lowest birth. Bondurand says it should not be taken literally, since she is hard on herself throughout the book. The words may mean the clay all people are made from.
  • Whether she was Charlemagne's daughter. The Paris copy has Dhuoda call the late emperor Louis, Charlemagne's son, her brother. Some writers therefore made her Charlemagne's daughter. Baluze, Dom Vaissete and Dom Rivet rejected this. Léopold Delisle notes that Émile Mabille had not wholly given it up. The Nîmes fragment reads the late emperor Louis instead, and Delisle says the idea must be given up.
  • When the book was finished. Its closing note gives 2 February with no year. Earlier scholars, Bondurand reports, took this as 842, sixty-four days after she began. Bondurand argues for 843, since the verses of chapter 69 were finished on 30 November 842, when William had turned sixteen.
  • Bernard's death. The Annals of Saint-Bertin say only that he was judged guilty of treason by the Franks and put to death on Charles's order. Bondurand gives the story from the old chronicle of Odo Héribert that Charles stabbed him at Saint-Sernin in Toulouse. He says popular imagination surrounded the death with such details.
