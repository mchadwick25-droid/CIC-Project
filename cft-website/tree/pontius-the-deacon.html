About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

When Cyprian, the bishop of Carthage, was put to death, his people wanted to hear everything about him. So writes the man who set out to tell them, in a book that bears no name. Jerome, about 130 years later, names him as Pontius, the bishop's deacon.

His church was one of the Latin-speaking churches of North Africa. Pontius chose to share his exile, and was with him on his last night. Soon after, he wrote The Life and Passion of Cyprian: the story of his life, and of his suffering and death as a martyr. He wrote it, he says, so that Cyprian's example would reach those who came after. A German church historian, Adolf Harnack, called it the oldest Christian biography that survives. A French historian called it a speech of praise. We know Pontius only from this book, and much of Cyprian's last year comes to us through it; he writes as an admirer.

In 257 the governor sent Cyprian away. Pontius went with him to Curubis, a small coastal town. Cyprian chose him, he writes, as "a willing exile", though he wished he had been chosen to share his death as well.

On their first day there, Cyprian told them a dream, which Pontius gives in Cyprian's words. A young man of more than human size led him before the governor. The governor wrote a sentence without asking him anything, and the young man, standing behind the governor, read it. "He spread his hand out flat, like a sword blade, and mimed the stroke of the usual execution. What he wanted understood, he made as clear as plain speech. I understood the sentence still to come: my martyrdom." Cyprian begged a day's delay to put his affairs in order, and it was granted.

Pontius read the day as a year, and says Cyprian died a year later to the day. Harnack found that reading unconvincing, and said it was not Cyprian's meaning.

When friends later urged Cyprian to withdraw, Pontius says he refused. Cyprian's own last letter says he did leave his gardens for a while, so that he would not be taken to another city to die. His companions, Pontius writes, were with him on the night after his arrest, and the next day, 14 September 258, he was beheaded.

Earlier Christians, Pontius says, had recorded in great detail how ordinary believers died as martyrs. It would be hard, he writes, if so great a bishop were passed over, when he had so much to teach beyond his death. "I have decided to write down a few things, in brief. Not that the life of so great a man is hidden from anyone, even the pagans. It is so that this great, matchless example may be handed on to those after us too, into undying memory."

He began at Cyprian's baptism, the day he was born of God, and passed over everything before it. He tells no wonders but the dream, and says nothing of Cyprian's councils, or of his quarrel with Rome over whether those baptized outside the church must be baptized again. Scholars differ on why.

By 359 the book was listed with Cyprian's own writings and copied with them. A Belgian scholar saw in it a blend of biography and praise.

No early source says how or when Pontius died. His book ends torn between joy and grief. "Shall I grieve that I was not his companion? But his victory must be celebrated. Shall I celebrate his victory? But I grieve that I am not his companion. Yet I must confess to you plainly what you know too: this is how I felt. I rejoice much, even too much, in his glory. But I grieve more that I remained behind."

Quotations are given in our modern English. The printed wording of each is below.

    Passion in the English translation means the martyr's suffering and death, so the modern wording says martyrdom, and spata is a sword, so the modern wording says sword blade.

    As printed
    “I have thought it well to prepare this brief and compendious narrative. Not that the life of so great a man can be unknown to any even of the heathen nations, but that to our posterity also this incomparable and lofty pattern may be prolonged into immortal remembrance.”
    Pontius, Life and Passion of Cyprian 1, tr. Ernest Wallis
    Hartel's Latin reads quo, where Harnack prints quod and queries quo; the sense is the same. The English translation has pattern for documentum, and the modern wording has example. Hartel's Latin goes on with a further clause, which Harnack strikes because the manuscripts omit it or place it differently; the quotation stops before it.

    “Shall I grieve that I was not his companion? But his victory must be celebrated. Shall I celebrate his victory? But I grieve that I am not his companion.     As printed
    “Shall I grieve that I was not his associate? But yet I must triumph in his victory. Shall I triumph at his victory? Still I grieve that I am not his companion. Yet still to you I must in simplicity confess, what you also are aware of, that it was my intention to be his companion. Much and excessively I exult at his glory; but still more do I grieve that I remained behind.”
    Pontius, Life and Passion of Cyprian 19, tr. Ernest Wallis
    The English translation reads the Latin in hac me fuisse sententia as it was my intention to be his companion. Harnack's print sets a colon after sententia, where Hartel's Latin has a full stop, so that the words point forward to the joy and the grief, and the modern wording follows that. The English translation has I must triumph in his victory, where the modern wording says his victory must be celebrated.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The Cheltenham list of 359 sets the Life at the end of Cyprian's works, and Harnack counts 23 manuscripts that hold it.

How they're remembered

  • Jerome of Strido calls the Life "a notable volume."
  • Adolf Harnack, in 1913, called it the oldest Christian biography that survives.

What Is Uncertain

  • The Life does not name its author. Jerome is the witness that Pontius wrote it, and Wilhelm Hartel's Latin edition calls it commonly attributed to him.
  • Pontius writes as an admirer, and passes over Cyprian's life before his conversion.
  • Arthur Cleveland Coxe, in a note to the American edition, says Pontius is said to have died a martyr. Ernest Cushing Richardson, in a note to his translation of Jerome, gives his death as about 260. Neither gives a source.
  • Pontius says Cyprian sold his gardens for the poor. Otto Ritschl thought they were confiscated later and that Pontius mistook this for a sale. Archbishop Benson rejects that view and follows Pontius.
  • Nothing in the Life gives its author's name, or his office as deacon. Both come from Jerome. Harnack says Jerome followed a tradition whose source is not known, and a handbook of 1922 says most scholars accept his word. Dessau tried to identify the author with a magistrate surnamed Pontius, honored in an inscription at Curubis, and Corssen showed that this identification has little support.
  • Scholars disagree about what kind of book the Life is. Harnack calls it the oldest Christian biography that survives. Monceaux calls it not a true biography but a speech of praise. Corssen sees a speech of praise that also defends Cyprian, though Delehaye thinks the defense is overstated. Reitzenstein, as Delehaye and a handbook of 1922 report him, sets it with the accounts of famous men's deaths and thinks Pontius a poor rhetorician. Delehaye says Pontius wanted a book for Cyprian as weighty as the Passion of Perpetua, and in telling Cyprian's merits fell into biography in the tone of a speech of praise.
  • The Life says Pontius was with Cyprian at Curubis and on the night after his arrest. Reitzenstein held that his part as an eyewitness was invented, as a handbook of 1922 reports. Delehaye answers that a forger would not have kept so quiet about it, and would have told more than the dream.
  • Pontius reads the one day's delay in Cyprian's dream as one year, and says Cyprian died a year later on the same day. He also says the delay was for church business, above all the care of the poor. Harnack finds this reading unconvincing and says it was not Cyprian's meaning. He thinks Cyprian meant his private affairs, which Pontius found awkward.
  • Pontius says Cyprian did not consent when friends urged him to withdraw before his arrest in 258. Cyprian's own last letter says that on his friends' advice he left his gardens for a while, so that he would not be taken to Utica, and that a bishop should confess in his own city.
  • Pontius says nothing of the councils Cyprian led, his dealings with Rome or the quarrel over baptism by heretics. Delehaye finds the silence strange. Harnack says these matters did not belong in a book about Cyprian's deeds and merits as Pontius understood them. Monceaux says Pontius wished to praise the Christian and the cleric rather than the church leader.
  • In his preface Pontius says he will tell what he was present for and what he learned of earlier things. The English translation renders the second part as any of his more illustrious labors, while Hartel's Latin and Harnack's print read antiquioribus. Harnack takes it that Pontius saw Cyprian's deeds himself, but not from the start.
  • Pontius says the court record tells what Cyprian answered the governor at his first hearing. Monceaux found that Pontius did not know the record as we have it, and Harnack agrees that he knew only a report of the first hearing and the words of the sentence. Delehaye thinks it more likely that he knew it, and von Soden suggests the Life was meant to replace it.
  • Archbishop Benson calls Pontius a devoted, grandiloquent deacon whose lines are affected. Delehaye calls him a barren mind who sees nothing beyond the written document.
  • Jerome ends his list in the fourteenth year of the Emperor Theodosius. The English translation's note gives that year as 492, but the same volume has Theodosius named emperor in 379, so the year is about 392.
