Pontius the Deacon

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity.

Also called Pontius

Pontius, a deacon of Cyprian of Carthage, went with his bishop into exile at Curubis in 257 and was with him on his last night in 258. After Cyprian's death he wrote The Life and Passion of Cyprian, so that later readers would have his example.

About This Person

In 257 the governor at Carthage sent the city's bishop, Cyprian, into exile at Curubis, a small town on the coast, because he would not take part in the ceremonies of Rome's gods. One of the men who went with him wrote later that Cyprian had chosen him from among the men of his household as "a willing exile." In the same breath he wished he could have shared Cyprian's martyrdom too.

His book never gives his name, but Jerome, more than a century later, calls him Pontius, one of Cyprian's deacons. Beyond that, almost all we know of him is his own book, the work of a man who loved and praised his bishop. Pontius went into exile by choice, heard Cyprian's dream there, and was with him on his last night. After Cyprian's death, Pontius wrote The Life and Passion of Cyprian, the story of his life and of his suffering and death as a martyr, so that later readers would have his example.

Cyprian told them of a dream he had on their first day at Curubis. Before he fell asleep, he said, a young man of more than human size seemed to lead him to the governor's court, where the governor wrote his judgment on his tablet. The young man standing behind the governor read it and showed it by a sign. In Cyprian's words, as Pontius gives them: "For he held his hand open and flat, like a sword blade, and copied the stroke of the usual execution. What he wanted understood, he showed as clearly as plain speech. I understood the sentence of martyrdom that was to come."

Cyprian begged a delay of one day, and the young man signaled that it was granted. Pontius read the one day as a year more of life.

Back from Curubis, Cyprian stayed at his gardens. Pontius says he turned down friends' offers of places to go. But Cyprian's own last letter says that when he heard officers had been sent to take him to another city, his dearest friends persuaded him to leave for a while. He agreed, he wrote, because a bishop should confess his faith in the city where he leads the church. Arrested, he spent his last night in an officer's house, and Pontius and others who shared Cyprian's table were with him. Next day the governor sentenced him to the sword as an enemy of the Roman gods. He died on 14 September 258, on the very day of the year, Pontius says, on which his sentence had been shown him.

The church's forebears, Pontius writes, had honored ordinary believers who died as martyrs and written down their sufferings. It would be hard if the death of so great a bishop were passed over, and his deeds in life stayed hidden. "I have decided to write down a few things, briefly. It is not that the life of so great a man is hidden from anyone, even among the pagans. It is so that this great and matchless example may be passed on to those after us too, into undying memory."

He began where Cyprian's faith began, because the deeds of a man of God, he wrote, should be counted only from the time he was born of God. Of Cyprian's earlier studies, he says, "these things I pass over." For what Cyprian told the governor at the hearing that sent him into exile, he sends readers to the court record.

The book was copied with Cyprian's own writings. In his catalog of Christian writers, Jerome called it "a notable volume."

His book ends in grief. "Shall I grieve that I was not his companion? But his victory must be celebrated. Shall I celebrate the victory? But I grieve that I am not his companion. Yet I must confess it to you plainly, and you know it too: this was how I felt. I rejoice much, far too much, in his glory. Yet I grieve more that I remained behind."

Key Works

  • The Life and Passion of Cyprian: a short book, written after Cyprian's death for people who had heard him, so that later readers would have his example. It tells his life from the time his faith began, his care for everyone in a plague, his exile and his dream at Curubis, and his suffering and death as a martyr. It never gives its author's name

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • The Life was copied with Cyprian's own writings.
  • Bede copied Jerome's sentence on Pontius and his book into his chronicle, and the Roman calendar of saints repeats its words.

How they're remembered

  • Jerome, in his catalog of Christian writers, names Pontius, Cyprian's deacon, as the author, and calls the Life "a notable volume".
  • Later calendars of saints keep Pontius's day at Carthage, on 8 March.

Open Questions

  • The witnesses. Almost all that is known of Pontius comes from his own book, which praises his bishop throughout. Jerome's one sentence adds his name and his office.
  • The author's name. The Life never names its author or gives his office. Jerome is the one old witness that its author was Pontius, Cyprian's deacon. The heading By Pontius the Deacon, in the English translation, is its editors' title.
  • Jerome's date. Jerome's catalog runs to the fourteenth year of the emperor Theodosius. The English translation's note prints that year as 492. Another note in the same volume has Theodosius named emperor in January 379, so the year is about 392, some 130 years after Cyprian's death.
  • Where Pontius was. The Life puts him with Cyprian in exile at Curubis and on the night after his arrest. It does not say that he saw the execution.
  • The day of the dream. The first day at Curubis is the day Cyprian had the dream. The Life does not say when he told it.
  • A year to the day. Pontius reads the one day's delay in the dream as one year. He says Cyprian was put to death on the very day of the year on which his sentence had been shown him. No other old source gives the day Cyprian reached Curubis, so this rests on Pontius alone.
  • The delay. In the dream Cyprian asks for a delay to put his affairs in order. Pontius says these affairs were church business, above all the care of the poor. The court record says only that Cyprian, back at his gardens, expected every day that they would come for him, as had been shown to him.
  • The young man in the dream. Cyprian calls him a young man of more than human size. The Life does not call him an angel.
  • The last withdrawal. Pontius says Cyprian would not agree when friends of high rank urged him again and again to withdraw and offered him places to go. Cyprian's own last letter says that when he heard officers had been sent to take him to Utica, his dearest friends persuaded him to leave his gardens for a while. He agreed, he writes, because a bishop should confess the Lord in the city where he leads the church. He waited in a hidden place for the governor's return to Carthage.
  • His sources. In his preface Pontius says he will tell what he was there for, and what he learned of earlier things. The prints differ at the second phrase. Hartel's and Harnack's Latin read of earlier things, and the Acta Sanctorum print reads of his earlier works, which the English translation follows as his more illustrious labors.
  • What the book leaves out. The Life says nothing of the councils of bishops Cyprian led, or of his quarrel with the bishop of Rome over whether a washing given outside the church counted as baptism. No old source says why.
  • The court record. For what Cyprian answered the governor at the first hearing, Pontius sends readers to the court record. A Christian record of the two hearings and the death survives, and it is listed among the sources.
  • His own end. No early source says how or when Pontius died. The Roman calendar of saints, as the Acta Sanctorum quotes it, copies Jerome's words on him. It then adds that in his own sufferings he earned the crown of life. No earlier witness held says so.

Modern Works

  • Das Leben Cyprians von Pontius: Die erste christliche Biographie — Adolf Harnack, 1913 Hartel's Latin text of the Life with Harnack's changes, a German commentary on each chapter, and a study of its manuscripts, its date and its author.
  • Les passions des martyrs et les genres littéraires — Hippolyte Delehaye, 1921 A study of the old accounts of the martyrs by kind, with a chapter on the Life of Cyprian and how it compares with them.
  • Histoire littéraire de l'Afrique chrétienne, vol. 2: Saint Cyprien et son temps — Paul Monceaux, 1902 A literary history of Christian Africa; this volume covers Cyprian and his time, with a section on the Life and what it tells and leaves out.
  • Cyprian: His Life, His Times, His Work — Edward White Benson, 1897 A life of Cyprian in English, with notes on the places of his exile and death, among them the town of Curubis on the coast.
  • Geschichte der römischen Litteratur, Teil 3 — Martin Schanz, Carl Hosius and Gustav Krüger, 1922 A handbook of Latin literature from Hadrian to Constantine, with a section on the Life and a report of the scholarship on it to 1922.
  • Die cyprianische Briefsammlung: Geschichte ihrer Entstehung und Ueberlieferung — Hans von Soden, 1904 A study of the manuscripts and old lists of Cyprian's letters and works, which records the Life copied with them.
  • S. Thasci Caecili Cypriani Opera Spuria (CSEL 3, part 3) — Wilhelm Hartel (ed.), 1871 The Latin edition of the works once ascribed to Cyprian, among them the Life and the court record of his hearings and death.
  • Hippolytus, Cyprian, Caius, Novatian (Ante-Nicene Fathers, volume 5) — Ernest Wallis (tr.), 1886 English translation of Cyprian's works and of the Life, with the American editor's notes.
  • Theodoret, Jerome, Gennadius, Rufinus (Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, second series, volume 3) — Ernest Cushing Richardson (tr.), 1892 English translation of Jerome's Lives of Illustrious Men, with notes.
  • Ausgewählte Märtyrerakten — Rudolf Knopf (ed.), third edition by Gustav Krüger, 1929 The Latin court record of Cyprian's two hearings and death, with other early accounts of martyrs.
  • Acta Sanctorum, Martii tomus primus — The Bollandists, 1865 The saints of the first eight days of March, with a notice on Pontius for 8 March that prints parts of the Life and quotes the Roman calendar of saints.
