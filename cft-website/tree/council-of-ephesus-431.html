The Imperial Church Era · 431 · Ephesus

Council of Ephesus

Major council

Known for
The council of 431 at Ephesus that deposed Nestorius over the name Theotokos for Mary, split into two rival assemblies under Cyril of Alexandria and John of Antioch, and was healed only by their peace two years later.
Dates
431. Called for Pentecost; Cyril's council first met on 22 June by Charles Joseph Hefele's dating, which turns the Egyptian date Cyril gives (28 Payni) into the Roman calendar. Socrates of Constantinople puts the deposition on 28 June. The council was dissolved later that year
Rivers
Syriac East, Greek East, Africa
Called by
The emperor Theodosius II, in his name and Valentinian III's, for Pentecost 431 (the council's letter to Celestine; Socrates of Constantinople). Count Candidian sat in the emperors' place as protector of the council (the emperor's edict, as Hefele summarizes it). By the same edict, he was to take no part in the debates on the faith, and was to keep order by removing from the city the monks and laymen who came out of curiosity

About This Council

Years after a council of bishops removed him, Nestorius, once bishop of Constantinople, wrote from exile: "Who was judge? Cyril. Who was the accuser? Cyril. Who was bishop of Rome? Cyril. Cyril was everything." Cyril of Alexandria had led it, in the place of Rome's bishop.

The emperor Theodosius II called that council to Ephesus. Could Mary rightly be called Mother of God, the one who gave birth to God? Nestorius would not simply call her that, but Cyril said she was, since her child was God's own Son, made man. At stake was how God and a man are one in Christ.

In June 431, Cyril opened the council before John of Antioch and Syria's bishops arrived, over the protest of the emperor's officer. It deposed Nestorius, removing him from office. John's smaller assembly deposed Cyril in turn; peace came only two years later. The verdict held in the empire, but not in Persia.

The bishops had waited sixteen days past Pentecost, the appointed day. Count Candidian, the emperor's officer, read the emperor's letter and begged four days more for John of Antioch. His protest says: "I warned you of this, then, not once but many times, and humbly begged you, and I gained nothing." They drove him out, he says, with insult and by force.

Nestorius, summoned three times, would not come. By the council's own record, the winning side's, he had soldiers guard his house. The bishops heard the creed of Nicaea, the statement of faith from a century before, and judged that Cyril's letter agreed with it and Nestorius's did not. They removed him and cut him off from the church's fellowship. Nestorius's side told the emperor that agitators surrounded their houses, and that Memnon, bishop of Ephesus, shut the churches to them and threatened death.

When John of Antioch arrived, his assembly deposed Cyril and Memnon for holding their council apart, against the emperor's orders. It charged that Cyril's twelve curses, added to his last letter to Nestorius, held old heresies. Cyril's council cut John's men off in turn, leaving their final sentence to Rome.

The council also ruled that no one may put forward any creed but Nicaea's, and upheld the condemnation of Pelagius and his followers.

In August, a new commissioner had the emperor's letter read "by persuasion and force", as he reported. He proclaimed Nestorius, Cyril and Memnon deposed and arrested all three, and that autumn the emperor dissolved the council.

In 433, John of Antioch and his bishops sent Cyril a confession of Christ's natures, what he is as God and as man: "A union of two natures has taken place. So we confess one Christ, one Son, one Lord. In keeping with this idea of a union without mixing, we confess the holy Virgin as Mother of God." Cyril accepted it, and John accepted Nestorius's deposition, the condition Cyril had set, says a historian of the councils. Some of John's bishops refused that price: Theodoret of Cyrrhus accepted Cyril's faith, but not the deposition.

In 451 the council of Chalcedon took as its guide Cyril's letter of peace to John of Antioch's bishops, and his letter to Nestorius.

Wigram, an English historian of the Church of the East, in Persia, wrote in 1910 that it had not accepted this council, nor formally rejected it. It had never been asked to do either.

An English writer on that church's rites was surprised, in 1852, that the rites often censured Nestorius's being cut off, yet never mentioned the council that expelled him.

Quotations are given in our modern English. The printed wording of each is below.

Why They Met

  • At Constantinople, a presbyter close to its bishop, Nestorius, preached that no one should call Mary Theotokos, the one who gave birth to God. Socrates of Constantinople says Nestorius backed him and rejected the name (Socrates, Church History VII.32).
  • Nestorius, in his own later defense, says he found one party calling Mary Mother of God and another Mother of man, and urged them to use a word all could accept, Mother of Christ (Nestorius, Book of Heracleides).
  • Cyril, bishop of Alexandria, wrote to him that the holy Fathers had called the Virgin Mother of God, not because the Word began from her, but because of her was born the body to which the Word was united (Cyril's second letter to Nestorius, read at the council).
  • The question was whether Mary could rightly be called Theotokos, and so how God and a man are one in Christ. Cyril's side held that Nestorius split Christ in two; Nestorius denied it, and John of Antioch's bishops charged Cyril's anathemas with old heresies (acts; John's sentence as Hefele gives it).
  • Celestine, bishop of Rome, had already condemned Nestorius and asked Cyril to act on it (the council's decree against Nestorius cites his letter). The emperor then called a council, and everything was held over until it met (Bossuet's account, printed in the English edition of the canons; Hefele).

Who Came

  • Cyril's council: "over two hundred bishops," its letter to Celestine says. Hefele counts 160 at the first session and 198 signatures on the deposition, rising to over 200. Cyril, in his letter home, says about two hundred.
  • Cyril came with fifty bishops, by Hefele's account and the chronology of G. R. Driver and Leonard Hodgson, the English editors of Nestorius. Juvenal of Jerusalem brought the bishops of Palestine, and Flavian of Philippi those of Macedonia (Driver and Hodgson).
  • Celestine's legates, the bishops Arcadius and Projectus and the presbyter Philip, arrived after the first session (acts, Session II).
  • Capreolus, bishop of Carthage, did not come and sent a letter excusing his absence, which was read and placed in the acts (acts, Session I).
  • John of Antioch's assembly: forty-three bishops, by Hefele from its signed sentence; Driver and Hodgson give the same. Cyril's council calls them "a little more than thirty" and names them, Theodoret of Cyrrhus among them.
  • Nestorius came with sixteen bishops by Hefele's count, ten by Driver and Hodgson's. He did not attend Cyril's sessions.
  • Count Candidian came to the church on the first day, read the emperor's instructions and asked the bishops to wait four more days for John of Antioch and for the bishops with Nestorius. Hefele says the demand was disregarded and the session opened under Cyril (Candidian's protest; Hefele).
  • At the first session Hefele counts 126 bishops who gave short speeches on Cyril's letter, and 34 who declared Nestorius's letter not in agreement with the Nicene faith.
  • Celestine's legates confirmed the sentence at the third session: Philip said the minutes showed it decided by the canons, and Arcadius declared Nestorius deprived of all episcopal dignity (acts, Session III).
  • After the sixteen days, two of John's metropolitans, Alexander of Apamea and Alexander of Hierapolis, arrived first. They said John had told them to announce that if anything delayed him, the council should not be put off but should do what was right (the council's letter to Celestine).
  • Nestorius, by Hefele's account, sent word that he would appear as soon as all the bishops were assembled.
  • When Celestine's letter was read at the second session, the bishops cried out, "To Cœlestine, a new Paul! To Cyril a new Paul!" and "One Cœlestine! One Cyril!" At the third session the legate Philip said that Peter, head of the Apostles, lives and judges in his successors, and that Celestine holds his place (acts, Sessions II and III).
  • At John's assembly, Candidian told how Cyril's side had met five days before, in spite of all warnings, and declared that Nestorius had been condemned unheard (Hefele, in the editor's excursus to the English acts).
  • Socrates of Constantinople says Cyril made some delay and did not arrive until near Pentecost, and that Juvenal of Jerusalem arrived five days after Pentecost (Socrates, Church History VII.34).

What They Decided

  • Cyril's second letter to Nestorius agreed with the creed of Nicaea; Nestorius's reply did not (acts, Session I).
  • Nestorius, having refused three summonses, was excluded "from the episcopal dignity, and from all priestly communion" (the decree against Nestorius).
  • No one may bring forward or compose "a different" faith from the one set out at Nicaea; the creed brought by the presbyter Charisius and the teachings of Nestorius were condemned (Canon VII).
  • John of Antioch and the bishops with him were cut off from communion and their priestly power, the final sentence left to Celestine (the council's letter to Celestine; its circular letter).
  • The council upheld Rome's earlier deposition of Pelagius, Caelestius and their followers (the council's letter to Celestine).
  • John's assembly, for its part, declared Cyril and Memnon of Ephesus deposed and excommunicated all who sided with them until they rejected Cyril's anathemas (John's sentence, as Hefele gives it).
  • Canon VIII: after the bishop of Antioch had held ordinations in Cyprus, the churches of Cyprus kept the right to ordain their own bishops, and the same rule was to hold in every province (Canon VIII).
  • The Messalians were condemned, and their book, the Asceticon, was anathematized (the council's letter to the bishops of Pamphylia).
  • The decree says the bishops came, "with many tears, to this sorrowful sentence" (the decree against Nestorius).

What Came of It

  • The emperor's new commissioner, Count John, announced the deposition of Nestorius, Cyril and Memnon and put all three under arrest (chronology of Driver and Hodgson, from the acts).
  • Theodosius dissolved the council. Cyril returned to Alexandria and Memnon stayed bishop of Ephesus (the emperor's decree, as Hefele gives it). Nestorius went back to his monastery at Antioch, and Maximian was made bishop of Constantinople (Driver and Hodgson; Socrates of Constantinople).
  • In 433, by Hefele's dating, Cyril accepted a confession sent by John's side: "there became a union of two natures. Wherefore we confess one Christ, one Son, one Lord", with Mary confessed as Mother of God (Cyril's letter to John of Antioch).
  • In 435 imperial edicts ordered Nestorius's writings burned, and he was banished, ending in Egypt (Driver and Hodgson). Socrates says he was sent to the Oasis.
  • In 451 the Council of Chalcedon received Cyril's letters and kept the order of Ephesus, "of which Celestine of Rome and Cyril of Alexandria, of holy memory, were the leaders" (Definition of Chalcedon).
  • The Church of the East in Persia kept Nestorius's memory. Barhadbeshabba's history titles a chapter for the pious Mar Nestorius and calls Cyril impious (in Nau's French). Hefele says that after the emperor Zeno closed their last refuge, the school at Edessa, in 489, the followers of Nestorius had their home in the kingdom of Persia.
  • The emperor's edict, as Hefele gives it, says that as long as he lives he will not condemn the Orientals, since they were not confuted in his presence. By the chronology of Driver and Hodgson, he had received eight delegates from each side at Chalcedon on 11 September, and no agreement was reached.
  • By Hefele, the formula John's side sent in 433 is, apart from its introduction and some closing words, the one the Antiochenes at Ephesus had earlier presented to the emperor through Count John.
  • In John's letter, as Hefele gives it, his side agreed to recognize Nestorius as deposed and to accept the consecration of Maximian of Constantinople. Hefele calls Nestorius's condemnation the condition of church communion with them. Driver and Hodgson say neither side mentioned the twelve anathemas.
  • Cyril opened his letter of peace to John of Antioch by calling on the heavens to rejoice and the earth to be glad, because the middle wall of partition had been taken away (Cyril's letter to John, in the acts of Chalcedon).
  • At the synod of Zeugma, by Driver and Hodgson's chronology, Theodoret of Cyrrhus, Andrew and John of Germanicia acknowledged Cyril's orthodoxy but refused to accept Nestorius's deposition. Alexander and some Cilician bishops broke with both Alexandria and Antioch.
  • Barhadbeshabba's history also gives a chapter as a reply to the accusations of the impious Cyril, by which the good deeds of saint Nestorius will be known (in Nau's French).

Traditions It Touched

Gathered

  • The Church of Alexandria and Miaphysite Egypt: Cyril of Alexandria led the council that deposed Nestorius, with the bishops of Egypt.
  • Antiochene Exegetical Christianity (Chrysostom-centered): The bishops of the East under John of Antioch held their own assembly at Ephesus and deposed Cyril; in 433 John and Cyril made peace.

Condemned

  • Nestorius: Cyril's council deposed Nestorius from the see of Constantinople and from all priestly communion.

Affected

  • Persian Church of the East (early): W. A. Wigram, its English historian, says the church took no corporate action on Ephesus at the time. George Percy Badger says the Eastern bishops were cut off by its decree.
  • The Church of the East under Persia: Its historian Barhadbeshabba, near the end of the sixth century by François Nau's dating, calls Nestorius the pious Mar Nestorius, and Cyril impious.

Whose Account We Have

  • The acts of Cyril's council (extracts in the English edition of the canons; Latin and Greek in Mansi, tomus 4) (acts): The minutes, decree, canons and synodal letters of the council Cyril presided over. On Cyril's side. Its letter to Celestine calls John an enemy of the faith.
  • The acts of John of Antioch's assembly (Mansi, tomus 5, the 'Conciliabulum Ephesinum') (acts): The Eastern bishops' own documents, in Latin. On John's side. Hefele narrates them, with open hostility to John.
  • Cyril of Alexandria, letters (his letter home to Alexandria in Mansi, tomus 4; his letters to Nestorius and to John) (participant): The bishop who led the council. Partisan for it.
  • Nestorius, The Book of Heracleides (Driver and Hodgson's English; Nau's French) (participant): The deposed bishop's own account, written in exile and kept only in a Syriac translation. Hostile to Cyril and the council.
  • Socrates of Constantinople, Church History VII.32 and 34 (later narrative): Written at Constantinople; his history stops with the year 439, A. C. Zenos notes. Critical of Cyril, and judges Nestorius ignorant rather than a heretic. His order of events differs from the acts.
  • Barhadbeshabba, Ecclesiastical History, part 2 (Nau's French) (later narrative): The Church of the East's own history of Nestorius and Ephesus, near the end of the sixth century by François Nau's dating. On Nestorius's side.
  • W. A. Wigram, An Introduction to the History of the Assyrian Church (1910) (modern reconstruction): An Anglican missionary historian of the Church of the East. Argues that it had not accepted Ephesus, nor formally rejected it.
  • Charles Joseph Hefele, A History of the Councils, vol. 3 (modern reconstruction): Supplies the Roman-calendar dates, the counts of those present and the sequence. A Roman Catholic historian, openly on Cyril's side.
  • G. R. Driver and Leonard Hodgson, introduction to The Bazaar of Heracleides (modern reconstruction): A dated chronology of the controversy from the acts, keyed to Nestorius's book.
  • Candidian's protest of 22 June 431 (Latin: Mansi, tomus 5, and Lupus's text in Driver and Hodgson's edition of Nestorius) (participant): The emperor's officer in his own words. He asks the bishops to wait four days for John and for Nestorius's bishops, says he warned and begged them in vain, and says he was driven out.
  • Report of the Eastern bishops to the emperor, Letter CLII among the letters of Theodoret (English, Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, series 2, vol. 3) (participant): John's assembly's own account. It says Cyril and Memnon mustered a mob and shut the churches, and that it deposed them and cut off their followers until they rejected Cyril's chapters, which it calls full of the Eunomian and Arian heresies. On John's side.
  • Evagrius Scholasticus, Church History I (English of 1846) (later narrative): Written about 593. Tells the council in the terms of Cyril's side, and says John came late, but not on purpose.

What Is Uncertain

  • The minutes we have are those of Cyril's council, which he led. John of Antioch's assembly survives in its own documents in Latin, and Nestorius's side in his own book, written in exile.
  • The day of the first session: Cyril gives an Egyptian date, 28 Payni, which Hefele reckons as 22 June. Socrates of Constantinople says the deposition was done on 28 June.
  • The size of John's assembly: forty-three by Hefele and by Driver and Hodgson, from its signatures; Cyril's council says a little more than thirty, and in its letter to Celestine, about thirty.
  • The order of events: Socrates says Nestorius's party met and deposed Cyril before John arrived, and that John then condemned Cyril. The acts and Hefele have John's own assembly depose Cyril after he arrived.
  • Socrates says Nestorius at last cried out that Mary might be called Theotokos, and that no notice was taken. Hefele follows the acts in seeing no such retreat.
  • How the Church of the East stood to Ephesus is disputed. George Percy Badger, in 1852, says the Easterns were cut off by its decree. W. A. Wigram, in 1910, says they had not accepted it, nor formally rejected it, and had never been asked to; he dates the real separation some fifty years later.
  • Whether Nestorius taught what Cyril's council condemned is still argued. Socrates, who read his works, judged he did not deny Christ's divinity. His own book denies the charge.
  • Why John came late: Cyril's council says he held back with a hidden plan, and that he refused to attend, whether from friendship to Nestorius or for another reason. John's letter, as Hefele gives it, says the length of the road and the death of several horses had delayed him. Evagrius, a century and a half later, says it was not on purpose. The council says it waited sixteen days past Pentecost. Hefele adds that sixty-eight Asiatic bishops, Theodoret of Cyrrhus among them, had asked Cyril and Juvenal to wait for the bishops from Antioch.
  • What Candidian did: his own protest is the only text of his we hold. The editor of the English acts, giving Hefele, says he cut off the council's food and had his soldiers insult its members, while Nestorius had a body-guard. The editor's note to John's assembly's report, in the volume of Theodoret's letters, says he was at first disposed to side with the Orientals. Nestorius's book says Cyril acted with full authority after shutting out the man the emperor had charged.
  • The torches: Cyril's letter home says the people waited from dawn, and that when the sentence was heard they led the bishops to their lodgings with torches, with the city lit and women walking ahead with censers. Hefele repeats it. It is Cyril's own account, and partisan.
  • The Eastern bishops' signatures: Barhadbeshabba, near the end of the sixth century, says Cyril and Memnon met as in a theater of madness in a corner of a church and got everyone to sign, some by gold and others by blows. The acts record the bishops giving their opinions one by one. The charge is the Church of the East's memory, and is hostile.
  • George Percy Badger, in 1852, also writes that the rites of the Church of the East contain no formal condemnation of Ephesus. He says the excommunication of Nestorius is often referred to and censured, but the council that expelled him is never mentioned, and he finds it surprising. This sits beside his other claim, above. He also prints the account of the presbyter Sleewa ibn Yohanan of Mosul, dated 1332, in which John's bishops refuse the verdict because an adversary should not be judge, and he warns that it holds numberless mistakes.
  • Chalcedon's definition says it received Cyril's letters to Nestorius and the Easterns. That the second is his letter of peace to John of Antioch rests on the second session of Chalcedon, where the letter to John, on the peace, is read after the letter to Nestorius.
