The Ruthenian Uniate Church — Zamość to the Partitions

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 2:24

Read along

A century after the Union of Brest, the Greek Catholic church of the Ruthenian lands — today's Ukraine and Belarus — was at its numerical height, holding the loyalty of most of the peasantry of the eastern Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth. The Synod of Zamość in 1720 consolidated it and also latinized it, importing Roman devotional practices into a Byzantine rite and settling its identity as something distinctly its own rather than a temporary arrangement. Then the political ground vanished: the partitions of Poland between 1772 and 1795 handed most of the church's people to Catherine II's Russia, which regarded the union as an amputation to be reversed, and campaigns of forced reversion from 1794 stripped it of the great majority of its parishes within two years. What survived did so in Austrian Galicia, where the Habsburgs raised the Greek Catholic clergy's status and paid for their education. And where the church became, over the following century, the carrier of Ukrainian national identity.

The Ruthenian church of the Carpathian lands had a union with Rome of its own, apart from the union of Brest. Nilles's heading dates the union at Uzhhorod to 1646. It dates the solemn confirmation to 1652.

In a petition of 15 January 1652, Greek-rite priests tell the pope that they were won back to Rome in the Latin church of the Uzhhorod castle, on 23 April. Their printed text names the year as 1649. Nilles notes that some read it as 1646, because the bishop of Eger who received them died in 1647.

Their earlier bishop at Mukachevo, they say, had left the union.

They joined on three terms. They would keep the Greek rite. They would have a bishop of their own choosing, confirmed by Rome. They would keep their church privileges. In their Latin, as the scan prints it: ritus graecae ecclesiae nobis servare Iice«t.

They asked Rome to confirm Peter Parthenius, the bishop they had chosen.

Documented Stories

Uman, June 1768

In the spring of 1768 the Orthodox peasants of the Polish Right Bank Ukraine, where the Uniate church held most parishes and the Polish nobility most land, rose in the revolt remembered as the Koliivshchyna. Its leader, Maksym Zalizniak, a Zaporozhian Cossack, had been a novice at the Orthodox Motronynsky monastery, whose superior, Melkhisedek Znachko-Yavorsky, had for years been campaigning to bring Uniate parishes back to Orthodoxy under Russian protection. Uman, a fortified town of the Potocki family, was crowded with refugees, Polish nobles, Jews and Uniate clergy, and defended by the family's Cossack militia under Ivan Gonta. When Zalizniak's bands arrived on 18 June, Gonta went over to them with his men, and on 20 and 21 June the town was sacked. The Poles were killed in the streets and the manor, the Jews in the synagogue and the market, the Uniate priests and the Basilian schoolboys in their church and school; the Jewish chronicles and the Polish accounts give figures from two thousand to twenty thousand dead, and the truth is unknown. Russian troops, whose empress had not ordered any of it, suppressed the revolt within weeks; Gonta was handed to the Poles and tortured to death, Zalizniak sent to Siberia, and Znachko-Yavorsky, disowned by the Russian Synod, ended his life as an archimandrite in Russia. The Uniate church of the Right Bank buried its clergy and went on for a generation, until 1794 finished what 1768 had begun, by decree rather than by knives.

The Bohohlasnyk at Pochaiv

The Pochaiv Lavra in Volhynia, a monastery of the Uniate Basilian order since 1713 and the greatest shrine of the Ruthenian church, ran one of the most productive presses in the Commonwealth, for a church whose services were in Slavonic and whose people spoke Ukrainian, Belarusian and, among the gentry, Polish. In 1790 and 1791 it issued the Bohohlasnyk, ‘the voice of God’, a collection of some two hundred and fifty spiritual songs arranged by the church year and by their subjects, the feasts of Christ and the Mother of God, the saints, and death and judgment, with melodies indicated for many, in Church Slavonic, in the vernacular Ruthenian of the villages, in Polish and in Latin. Its editors, Basilian monks whose names are not given, drew on what was already being sung: on manuscript songbooks of the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, on the songs of the wandering blind singers and the church cantors, on Polish Catholic hymns and Latin sequences, on the songs sung at Pochaiv itself by the pilgrims to its icon. The Bohohlasnyk was reprinted at Pochaiv and Lviv through the following century, passed across the confessional line into Orthodox use after 1839, and became the common possession of Ukrainians of both churches, which is more than either church managed on its own. It is the clearest surviving evidence of what the Uniate faithful of the eighteenth century actually sang, as distinct from what the Synod of Zamość had told them to believe.

The Ukase of 1794

The second and third partitions of Poland, in 1793 and 1795, brought most of the Uniate church's people and parishes under Russian rule, and Catherine II, who regarded the union as a Polish device for stealing Orthodox souls, moved at once. Her ukase of 22 April 1794 declared that the Uniates of the annexed provinces, having been forced into union by their former lords, were now free to return to the Orthodox church of their fathers, ordered the Orthodox archbishop of Minsk, Viktor Sadkovsky, to receive them, and directed the civil authorities to see that no one hindered them. Orthodox priests, escorted by officials and where necessary by soldiers, went from village to village reading the ukase, taking the signatures or marks of parishioners on prepared petitions for reunion, and reconsecrating the churches; Uniate priests who resisted were removed and their places filled, and those who agreed were received in their orders. The Russian returns claim a million and a half converts, some two thousand three hundred parishes, by the end of 1796, most of them in the Kyiv, Podolia and Volhynia regions where the Right Bank Uniate church had been strongest. The Uniate metropolitan, Teodosii Rostotsky, was summoned to St Petersburg in 1795 and kept there under surveillance until his death; all the Uniate dioceses but Polotsk were abolished the same year. In Austrian Galicia, across the new border, it kept its bishops, its seminaries and its people, and became something else.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

A century after the Union of Brest, the Greek Catholic church of the Ruthenian lands held the loyalty of most of the region's peasantry, and the Synod of Zamość in 1720 consolidated it while importing Roman devotional practices into its Byzantine rite. When the partitions of Poland handed most of its people to Catherine II's Russia, which treated the union as an amputation to be reversed, forced-reversion campaigns from 1794 stripped away the great majority of its parishes within two years — leaving Austrian Galicia, where the Habsburgs funded the clergy's education, as the church's real survival zone.

Legacy

The Galician remnant became one of the most consequential churches in Eastern Europe. The Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church carried Ukrainian language, schooling and national feeling through the nineteenth century, was suppressed outright by Stalin in 1946, survived underground for four decades as the largest banned church in the world, and came back into the open in 1989. It is a substantial living church today. Its cathedral of St George in Lviv, built in the decades after Zamość, remains its historic center. The Belarusian and central Ukrainian branches, dissolved in this period and again in 1839, did not recover.
