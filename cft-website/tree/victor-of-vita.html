Victor of Vita

A person whose story no single tradition tells.

Known for
A Catholic churchman of Vandal Africa whose History of the Persecution is the main account of how the Vandal kings Geiseric and Huneric treated the Catholic church.
Also called
Victor, bishop of Vita, Victor Vitensis
Dates
Wrote, he says, in the sixtieth year since the Vandals crossed into Africa. Huneric's edict, which he copies, is dated at Carthage six days before the Kalends of March; Michael Petschenig puts it in 484. Joseph Mesnage dates the History to 488. No source used here gives his birth or death
Place
Africa under the Vandals; Carthage; Vita
Role
Bishop and historian

About This Person

Felix, bishop of Abbir, had been a bishop for forty-four years when King Huneric sent him into exile. He was paralyzed and could neither feel nor speak. Victor of Vita writes that he and others asked that the dying man be left at Carthage. The king, Victor reports, answered that wild oxen should be yoked to drag him with ropes. So they carried Felix the whole way, Victor says, tied across a mule like a log.

Victor wrote the History of the Persecution in Africa under the Vandal kings Geiseric and Huneric. Most manuscripts of his book call him a bishop of Vita; one reads Utica. He calls Pampinianus the bishop "nostrae civitatis" (of our city), and Karl Halm, who edited the Latin, takes that to mean Vita.

He wrote in the sixtieth year since the Vandals crossed from Spain. He calls them a cruel and savage people, and their faith the Arian heresy. His book is the polemic of a victim and an enemy of their church.

Some of it he lived through. Four thousand nine hundred and sixty-six bishops, priests, deacons and other church members were sent into the desert, he writes. At Sicca and Lares they shouted that they were Christians and Catholics. Victor and others were let in to encourage them and celebrate the liturgy.

At Tipasa, Victor says, the king had the tongues and right hands of Catholics cut off, and they still spoke. Anyone who doubts it, he adds, can find one of them, the subdeacon Reparatus, at Constantinople. Procopius of Caesarea, the Greek historian of the Vandal War, also writes of many whose tongues Huneric cut out. They "even up to my time were going about in Byzantium having their speech uninjured."

Near the end he calls on the angels of God to look at Africa. Once it was held up by so many churches, he says, and now it sits a widow, cast down.

Key Works

  • History of the Persecution in Africa: three books on the Vandal kings Geiseric and Huneric and the Catholic church, with Huneric's edicts copied in

Legacy

What traces to them

  • He keeps the texts of Huneric's edicts against the Catholic church.
  • He keeps the Book of Faith the Catholic bishops gave in to the king; Michael Petschenig thinks it likely that Victor put it into his history himself.

How they're remembered

  • Procopius of Caesarea, on the tongues Huneric (his Honoric) cut out: "even up to my time were going about in Byzantium having their speech uninjured."

What Is Uncertain

  • Victor is a victim and an enemy of the Vandal church, and his book is polemic. He calls the Vandals a cruel and savage people and their faith the Arian heresy.
  • That he was bishop of Vita rests on most manuscripts of his book; one reads Utica. The list of Catholic bishops called to Carthage in 484, which Karl Halm prints with the History, names a Victor of Vita who did not come; Joseph Mesnage takes him for the historian. Victor calls Pampinianus the bishop "nostrae civitatis" (of our city); Halm takes that to mean Vita.
  • That Catholics still spoke after their tongues were cut out rests on Victor, who tells it of Tipasa and names the subdeacon Reparatus at Constantinople, and on Procopius of Caesarea, who names no town but says such people were still in Byzantium in his own day.
  • Michael Petschenig prints the prologue that opens the History in brackets, and discusses it beside the spurious additions to the text as very obscure.
  • Beyond his History, Joseph Mesnage says he did not come to Carthage in 484, fled into exile and wrote his History there in 488. No source used here gives his birth or death. He is not Victor of Tunnuna, the later African chronicler.
