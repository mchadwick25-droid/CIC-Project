About This Person

"I confess that it is beyond my power to write a perfect and complete history. Since I am the first to enter on this subject, I am trying to travel, as it were, a lonely and untrodden path." So wrote Eusebius, bishop of Caesarea in Roman Palestine, opening his Church History.

He set out to tell the whole church's story from its own letters, lists and books, copying their words into his pages. He wrote that he had "plucked like flowers from a meadow the appropriate passages from ancient writers."

Born around 260, Eusebius studied under Pamphilus, a friend whose name he took.

In 303 the emperors began a persecution, ordering the churches torn down. Lactantius, who was at Nicomedia, says Galerius, urged on by his mother, pressed the emperor Diocletian to destroy the Christians. Diocletian long refused, then gave way. Eusebius, whose own pages tell most of his life, saw it: "With our own eyes we saw the houses of prayer thrown down from top to ground, to their very foundations. We saw the inspired and sacred Scriptures handed over to the fire in the middle of the market squares. We saw the shepherds of the churches hiding shamefully here and there, and some of them caught in disgrace and mocked by their enemies."

By houses of prayer he meant churches, and by shepherds the clergy. Pamphilus was imprisoned and died a martyr for the faith.

Later Eusebius became bishop of Caesarea. He traced each great church's bishops back to the apostles, drawing letters from Jerusalem's church library. He set a rule: those who gave way in the persecution he would leave out, keeping only what would profit his own time and those after.

He had seen the young Constantine, and later knew him as emperor.

At the Council of Nicaea in 325, by his own account, his church's statement of faith was read. The bishops added words, among them "of one being" with the Father. He signed, and wrote home that all agreed "not without inquiry", for the sake of peace. Socrates, a historian a century later, says he held back briefly first. Athanasius of Alexandria, an opponent who was there, says he refused the day before.

Ten years later the emperor called bishops to Tyre to judge Athanasius. Socrates says Athanasius's enemies charged that he had murdered a bishop named Arsenius and used the dead man's hand in magic. They also charged that his priest Macarius had broken a sacred cup. Eusebius sat among the judges, says Epiphanius, writing some forty years later on Athanasius's side. In his account Potamo, an Egyptian bishop who had lost an eye in the persecution, cried out: "You sit there, Eusebius, and Athanasius, an innocent man, is judged by you?" And then: "Tell me, were you not with me in prison in the persecution? I lost an eye for the truth. But you seem to have no part of your body maimed. You did not suffer as a martyr; you stand here alive, with nothing cut away. How did you get out of prison, unless you promised our persecutors to do something unlawful, or did it?"

Eusebius rose in anger and broke up the court, Epiphanius says. Socrates says Arsenius turned up alive, which ended that charge. Yet the council deposed Athanasius after men it sent to Egypt reported that his accuser spoke the truth about the cup. About three years later the bishops of Egypt wrote that he had been "accused" of offering sacrifice to the Roman gods. Rufinus, writing later in Latin, tells a like scene at Tyre that does not name Eusebius. These were charges; no source shows he sacrificed.

After Constantine died in 337, Eusebius wrote a Life of the emperor, telling, by his own rule, only what showed his faith. Its account of Tyre names neither Athanasius nor Potamo. Socrates judged it more praise than accurate history.

The letter of Polycrates of Ephesus on Easter survives only because Eusebius copied it. He is the first known to quote Josephus on Jesus, and he retold, from a Latin writer, Pliny the Younger's letter about the Christians. Socrates began his history where Eusebius stopped, and Rufinus put the Church History into Latin.

Athanasius said Eusebius had written that Christ was not true God, while Socrates defended him, saying Eusebius had been at Nicaea and agreed to what it decided.

Eusebius died about 339. His pupil and successor as bishop wrote his life. That book is lost.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • He has long been called the Father of Church History. Arthur McGiffert, who translated him, says the title is deserved.
  • Socrates of Constantinople built his own account of the Council of Nicaea on Eusebius's words.
  • Arthur McGiffert says the works of many earlier church writers are lost and are known only through the extracts Eusebius made.
  • The letter of Polycrates of Ephesus on Easter is known only in the part Eusebius quotes. McGiffert says Eusebius is the first writer known to quote the passage about Jesus in Josephus. Eusebius also retold, from the Latin Apology of Tertullian, Pliny the Younger's letter to the emperor Trajan about the Christians and Trajan's reply.
  • Socrates of Constantinople began his own history where Eusebius stopped, with the end of the persecution. Rufinus of Aquileia put the Church History into Latin and carried it on.
  • Eusebius says he was on the spot in Egypt and watched large crowds in one day, some beheaded and others tortured by fire. McGiffert says he was an eyewitness of many martyrdoms in Palestine, at Tyre and in Egypt.

How they're remembered

  • Sabinus, bishop of the Macedonians at Heraclea in Thrace, scorned the Council of Nicaea but still called Eusebius a trustworthy witness, Socrates of Constantinople says.

At Major Councils

  • Council of Nicaea, 325: Read his church's creed; signed after a delay; wrote to his church and the Life of Constantine

What Is Uncertain

  • Potamo, a bishop from Heraclea in Egypt, charged at Tyre in 335 that Eusebius had escaped prison by some unlawful promise. Epiphanius of Salamis tells it, writing decades later on the side of Athanasius of Alexandria. Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius, notes that Potamo only guessed, and the bishops of Egypt, in a letter Athanasius kept, say only that Eusebius was "accused by our confessors of sacrificing to idols."
  • How far he truly accepted the Nicene wording is argued. His own letter says he accepted it only after asking what it meant. Eustathius of Antioch said he twisted it.
  • Constantine's vision of the cross rests on what the emperor told Eusebius under oath. Ernest Richardson, who translated the Life of Constantine, notes that the account was doubted from the first.
  • Eusebius wrote that he was the first to enter on the subject of a church history. Arthur McGiffert agrees, and names Hegesippus and Julius Africanus as the only two who in any sense went before him.
  • The month the persecution began is told three ways. Eusebius puts the edict ordering the churches torn down in March, in the nineteenth year of Diocletian, and his Martyrs of Palestine gives April. Lactantius, who was at Nicomedia, gives February 23. McGiffert trusts Lactantius and thinks Eusebius may have given the month the edicts reached other places. All agree on the year 303.
  • How he came to sign at Nicaea is told three ways. His own letter says he and the others agreed after questions, for the sake of peace. Socrates of Constantinople, about a century later, says he held back for a short time before he signed, and sent his people the creed so that no one would doubt his motives. Athanasius of Alexandria, who was at the council and was his opponent, says he had refused the day before.
  • Socrates says that when Eustathius of Antioch accused him of twisting the creed, Eusebius denied it and said in turn that Eustathius defended the opinion of Sabellius. After Eustathius was deposed, Socrates says, one party at Antioch wanted Eusebius moved there from Caesarea, and he refused.
  • Whether he presided at Tyre is not settled. Epiphanius says the emperor ordered him to judge there, with some others. McGiffert says it cannot be decided whether Eusebius of Caesarea, Eusebius of Nicomedia or another bishop presided. Karl Joseph von Hefele says Eusebius of Caesarea presided.
  • Rufinus of Aquileia, writing later in Latin, tells a like scene at Tyre that does not name Eusebius. In it Paphnutius, a confessor who had lost an eye, tells Maximus, bishop of Jerusalem, who had lost an eye and been hamstrung, that he will not let him sit in the council of the wicked. Hefele finds Rufinus's version plainly more probable than Epiphanius's, and thinks Epiphanius's may be a false version of the same event.
  • In his Life of Constantine he says he will pass over the emperor's wars and laws and speak only of what bears on his religious character. Socrates says the book is more intent on the emperor's praise than on an accurate account. Its telling of the council at Tyre names neither Athanasius nor Potamo and says only that envy troubled the churches of Egypt.
  • Whether he held that the Son was less than the Father is judged both ways. Athanasius says that in a letter to Euphration, Eusebius said plainly that Christ was not true God. Socrates answers that Eusebius was at Nicaea and agreed to what it decided. Philostorgius, known through Photius's summary, praises him but accuses him of erroneous opinions in religion. Hefele lists writers who held him orthodox and writers who called him an Arian, and judges that he did not wish to be an Arian and sought a middle way.
