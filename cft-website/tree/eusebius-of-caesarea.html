Eusebius of Caesarea

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Church of Roman Palestine before Constantine.

Also called Eusebius Pamphili

About This Person

Eusebius of Caesarea opened his History of the Church with an admission. "I admit that a perfect and complete history is beyond my power. I am the first to take up this subject, so I am trying to travel, as it were, a lonely and untrodden path." Those before him, he wrote, had left only brief fragments, voices calling from far off like torches from a watchtower.

Eusebius, bishop of Caesarea on the coast of Roman Palestine, set out to tell the church's past from Christ to his own day. He built his History from the letters, lists and books of the Christians before him. Later historians of the church began their books where his History ended. He lived through the emperors' persecution of the church and wrote the emperor Constantine's Life. At Nicaea he signed the creed of a council of bishops, its statement of faith, after asking what its words meant. At Tyre a bishop who had suffered in the persecution charged that Eusebius had got out of prison by giving way.

His friend in Caesarea was Pamphilus, a priest there. Eusebius's plan, in his own list, was to trace the lines of bishops in the great churches and the men who spoke or wrote for the faith. He would also tell of those who led others into error, and those who stood firm under torture down to his own day. He told it by gathering fitting passages from the old writers and copying letters whole.

In 303 the emperors ordered the churches leveled and the Scriptures burned, and soon after, church leaders were jailed and forced by every means to sacrifice, to make an offering to the Roman gods. Eusebius saw it, and wrote of the leaders of the churches as shepherds: "We saw with our own eyes the houses of prayer thrown down from top to ground, foundations and all. We saw the inspired and holy Scriptures handed to the fire in the middle of the market squares. We saw the shepherds of the churches hiding shamefully here and there, and some of them caught in disgrace and mocked by their enemies." His dearest friend, Pamphilus, was jailed and killed with his companions. In his History he set himself a rule: he would not record those who had been shaken by the persecution, only what would help his readers and those after them.

By the council at Nicaea in 325 he was bishop, and the church was divided over whether God's Son had always been, or had a beginning, as Arius taught. Eusebius wrote to his church that there the emperor urged one added word, the Greek homoousios, which says the Son is of the same substance as the Father. Eusebius asked what it meant, and after questions and answers, he wrote, he signed for the sake of peace and so as not to fall from the right belief. A century later Socrates, a historian of the church, says he held back a short time before signing. Athanasius, then a deacon of Alexandria who was there, says he had refused the day before.

In 335 Constantine called bishops to Tyre to judge charges that opponents in Egypt had brought against Athanasius, by then bishop of Alexandria. They accused him over a cup used at the Lord's Supper, the church's meal of bread and wine, that had been broken. Epiphanius, telling it about forty years later and on Athanasius's side, calls them slanders and says the emperor ordered Eusebius to judge, with others.

Potamo, a bishop from Egypt, was a confessor, a Christian who had suffered for the faith in the persecution and lived. When he saw Eusebius seated as judge and Athanasius standing, he wept, then cried out at Eusebius. "Tell me, were you not with me in prison in the persecution? I lost an eye for the truth. But you seem to have nothing maimed in your body. You did not suffer as a martyr. You stand alive, with nothing cut away. How did you get out of prison, unless you promised those who forced the persecution on us to do the forbidden thing, or did it?" Eusebius rose in anger and broke up the court. If Athanasius's side came here and accused the judge like this, he said, then the charges against them must be true, for if they acted like tyrants here, they did much more at home.

The bishops of Egypt, on Athanasius's side, later wrote that their confessors had accused Eusebius of sacrificing. Socrates says the council removed Athanasius as bishop when a one-sided inquiry in Egypt sided with his accuser.

After the death of Constantine, whom he had come to know, Eusebius wrote the emperor's Life, a book about his faith which, it says itself, leaves aside his wars and laws. In its pages envy, like a dark cloud, stirs up quarrels in Egypt's churches, and the emperor calls the bishops to Tyre against it, while Athanasius and Potamo go unnamed. He died after the emperor, in his sons' reign.

Socrates began his own history of the church with what Eusebius had left out, and he defended Eusebius against the charge of favoring Arius, since he had signed at Nicaea. Jerome, writing in Latin, called his History fine, and called him the most open champion of Arius's ungodly teaching.

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “I admit that a perfect and complete history is beyond my power. I am the first to take up this subject, so I am trying to travel, as it were, a lonely and untrodden path.”
    As printed
    “I confess that it is beyond my power to produce a perfect and complete history, and since I am the first to enter upon the subject, I am attempting to traverse as it were a lonely and untrodden path.”
    Church History I.1.4, tr. Arthur McGiffert
    The Greek of Books 1 to 5 is not held, so the wording rests on McGiffert's English. The modern wording gives confess as admit, its sense here, not a confession of wrong.
  • In our modern English
    “We saw with our own eyes the houses of prayer thrown down from top to ground, foundations and all. We saw the inspired and holy Scriptures handed to the fire in the middle of the market squares. We saw the shepherds of the churches hiding shamefully here and there, and some of them caught in disgrace and mocked by their enemies.”
    As printed
    “we saw with our own eyes the houses of prayer thrown down to the very foundations, and the Divine and Sacred Scriptures committed to the flames in the midst of the market-places, and the shepherds of the churches basely hidden here and there, and some of them captured ignominiously, and mocked by their enemies.”
    Church History VIII.2.1, tr. Arthur McGiffert
    The Greek is held only in a damaged scan of Schwartz's edition (lines 13614 to 13620 of the Greek and Latin Church History file), so the card prints McGiffert's English. The modern wording follows the Greek, checked by hand: from top to ground, foundations and all, is the Greek from a height to the ground, with the foundations themselves. The Greek has one verb, we saw, for all three sights; the modern wording says it once for each. Rufinus's Latin in the same file renders the passage freely.
  • In our modern English
    “Tell me, were you not with me in prison in the persecution? I lost an eye for the truth. But you seem to have nothing maimed in your body. You did not suffer as a martyr. You stand alive, with nothing cut away. How did you get out of prison, unless you promised those who forced the persecution on us to do the forbidden thing, or did it?”
    As printed
    “Pray tell me, wast thou not with me in prison during the persecution? And I lost an eye in behalf of the truth, but thou appearest to have received no bodily injury, neither hast thou suffered martyrdom, but thou hast remained alive with no mutilation. How wast thou released from prison unless thou didst promise those that put upon us the pressure of persecution to do that which is unlawful, or didst actually do it?”
    Epiphanius, Panarion 68.8, as Arthur McGiffert gives it in the Prolegomena
    These are the words Epiphanius gives Potamo, written about forty years later on Athanasius's side. The Greek is held only in a damaged scan (lines 44592 to 44604 of the Epiphanius file); the modern wording follows it, checked by hand, and agrees with Petavius's Latin in the same file (lines 44670 to 44675). Those who forced the persecution on us renders those who brought the compulsion of the persecution on us, and the forbidden thing renders the Greek that which is not permitted. The brief leaves out the question Who can bear such things? that stands before the first sentence.

Open Questions

  • At Tyre in 335 Potamo, a bishop from Heraclea in Egypt and a confessor, which is a Christian who suffered for the faith in the persecution and lived, asked Eusebius how he had got out of prison unless he had promised or done what was forbidden. Only Epiphanius of Salamis tells it. He wrote about forty years later, on the side of Athanasius of Alexandria, and calls the charges against Athanasius slanders. No work of Eusebius used here says he was jailed, and no old source used here says he gave way, or that he was cleared.
  • The bishops of Egypt, in a letter Athanasius kept in his Defence Against the Arians, ask whether Eusebius was not accused by their confessors over a sacrifice, an offering to the Roman gods of the kind the persecuting orders forced on church leaders. The Greek says only over a sacrifice; the words to idols are the English translator's. The letter is on Athanasius's side.
  • How he came to sign at Nicaea is told three ways. His own letter to his church says that he and the others agreed after questions about what the added word meant, for the sake of peace and so as not to fall from the right belief. The word is homoousios, which says the Son is of the same substance as the Father. Socrates of Constantinople, about a century later, says he held back for a short time before he signed, and sent his people the creed, the church's short statement of belief, so that no one would doubt his motives. Athanasius of Alexandria, who was at the council and was his opponent, says he had refused the day before.
  • How far he truly accepted the wording of the Nicene creed, with its added word, is argued. His own letter says he accepted it only after asking what it meant. Eustathius, bishop of Antioch, accused him of twisting it, Socrates says, and Eusebius denied it and answered with a charge of his own against Eustathius. After Eustathius was deposed, Socrates says, one party at Antioch wanted Eusebius moved there from Caesarea, and he refused.
  • Constantine's vision of a cross of light in the sky rests on what the emperor told Eusebius long afterwards, confirming it by an oath, as Eusebius says in the Life of Constantine.
  • Eusebius wrote that he was the first to enter on the subject of a church history and knew of no church writer who had given himself to it. Sozomen, about a century later, names Clement, Hegesippus and Africanus, as well as Eusebius, among the writers who had set down the church's past before him.
  • The month the persecution began is told three ways. Eusebius puts the edict ordering the churches torn down in March, in the nineteenth year of Diocletian, and his Martyrs of Palestine gives April. McGiffert's note reports that Lactantius, who was at Nicomedia, gives February 23. The year, 303, is not disputed.

  • Whether he presided at Tyre is not said by any old source used here. Epiphanius says the emperor ordered him to judge there, with some others, who he says leaned somewhat toward the Arians, the followers of Arius, who taught that God's Son had a beginning. Sozomen says Athanasius had earlier refused to attend a council at Caesarea, fearing the artifices of Eusebius, bishop of that city, and of Eusebius, bishop of Nicomedia. Modern historians differ on who presided; Hefele's history of the councils, listed under Modern works, treats the question.
  • Rufinus of Aquileia, writing later in Latin, tells a like scene at Tyre that does not name Eusebius. In it Paphnutius, a confessor who had lost an eye, tells Maximus, bishop of Jerusalem, who had also been a confessor, with an eye put out and a leg hamstrung, that he will not let him sit in the council of the wicked.
  • Socrates says the charge that Athanasius's side had cut off a man's hand failed at Tyre when the man, Arsenius, was brought in alive with both hands. He says the council nevertheless voted to depose Athanasius as bishop, on the report of a one-sided inquiry at Mareotis, by Alexandria, and that its sentence said nothing of the failed charge. Eusebius's own part in the vote is not given.
  • In his Life of Constantine he says he will pass over the emperor's wars and laws and speak only of what bears on his religious character. Socrates says the book is more intent on the emperor's praise than on an accurate account. Its telling of the council at Tyre names neither Athanasius nor Potamo and says only that envy troubled the churches of Egypt.
  • Whether he held that the Son was less than the Father is judged both ways. Athanasius says that in a letter to Euphration, Eusebius said plainly that Christ was not true God. Socrates answers that Eusebius was at Nicaea and agreed to what it decided. Philostorgius, whom Photius's summary preserves, praises him but accuses him of erroneous opinions in matters of religion. Jerome calls him the most open champion of the Arian impiety in one letter and the standard-bearer of the Arian side in another work. In 787 the Second Council of Nicaea, answering those who used his letter to Constantia against holy images, read his letter to Euphration.
