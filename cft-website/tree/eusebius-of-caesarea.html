About This Person

In the persecution of his own day, Eusebius of Caesarea watched Christians die in the Thebais, in Egypt. "We, also being on the spot ourselves," he wrote, "have observed large crowds in one day." So many were killed, he says, that "the murderous sword was blunted," and the executioners took turns.

He was bishop of Caesarea in Palestine for many years. He was also called Eusebius Pamphili, after his friend Pamphilus, a presbyter there who died a martyr. Arthur McGiffert, who translated him into English, gives this reason for the name.

His great work was the Church History. It tells the story of the church from Christ to his own time. "Since I am the first to enter upon the subject," he wrote, "I am attempting to traverse as it were a lonely and untrodden path." He built it from letters, martyr stories and lists of bishops, which he copied into his pages. He also wrote a Life of the emperor Constantine, whom he knew. There he says Constantine told him of a cross of light seen in the sky, with the words "Conquer by this." The emperor swore to it, Eusebius says.

At the Council of Nicaea in 325, Eusebius read out his own statement of faith, and the emperor said it was sound. In a letter home, Eusebius says Constantine then asked for one word to be added. The Son was to be called "One-in-essence" with the Father. Eusebius accepted the council's creed, but he told his people that "we did not let it pass without inquiry."

After the council, Socrates of Constantinople says, Eustathius of Antioch accused Eusebius of twisting the Nicene creed. Eusebius answered that Eustathius held the views of Sabellius. That name, McGiffert explains, came to stand for treating the Father and the Son as one. Epiphanius of Salamis tells of a council at Tyre in 335. He wrote decades later, on the side of Athanasius of Alexandria. At Tyre, Potamo, a bishop from Heraclea in Egypt who had lost an eye for the faith, confronted Eusebius. Potamo said they had been in prison together, and he asked how Eusebius had come out unharmed unless he had promised to do something unlawful. McGiffert points out that Potamo only guessed, and that Athanasius says only that Eusebius was "accused of sacrificing."

Socrates later built his own account of Nicaea on Eusebius's words, "treating what he has said as an authority."

Key Works

  • Church History: the church from Christ to his own day, built from the documents he could find
  • Martyrs of Palestine: the persecution he lived through
  • Life of Constantine: the emperor he knew, with the story of the cross of light

Legacy

What traces to them

  • He has long been called the Father of Church History. Arthur McGiffert, who translated him, says the title is deserved.
  • Socrates of Constantinople built his own account of the Council of Nicaea on Eusebius's words.

How they're remembered

  • Sabinus, bishop of the Macedonians at Heraclea in Thrace, scorned the Council of Nicaea but still called Eusebius a trustworthy witness, Socrates of Constantinople says.

At Major Councils

  • Council of Nicaea, 325: Read his church's creed; signed after a delay; wrote to his church and the Life of Constantine

What Is Uncertain

  • Potamo, a bishop from Heraclea in Egypt, charged at Tyre in 335 that Eusebius had escaped prison by some unlawful promise. Epiphanius of Salamis tells it, writing decades later on the side of Athanasius of Alexandria. Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius, notes that Potamo only guessed, and Athanasius says only that Eusebius was "accused of sacrificing."
  • How far he truly accepted the Nicene wording is argued. His own letter says he accepted it only after asking what it meant. Eustathius of Antioch said he twisted it.
  • Constantine's vision of the cross rests on what the emperor told Eusebius under oath. Ernest Richardson, who translated the Life of Constantine, notes that the account was doubted from the first.
