The Seventh-day Adventist Church in the 20th Century

North America and then the world, 1906 to 2010 — the Adventist church after Ellen White's death in 1915, its confession explicitly trinitarian in the 1931 yearbook statement and again in the Fundamental Beliefs of 1980, its self-documentation as compulsive as ever.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:36

Read along

Ellen White died on 16 July 1915, and the movement she had helped found carried on without the person whose writings had shaped it — which is itself one of the plainest facts about it. Its confession settled in public over the following decades. In 1931 the church's yearbook carried a statement of twenty-two beliefs, explicitly trinitarian; in 1980 the General Conference session at Dallas adopted the Fundamental Beliefs, twenty-seven of them, restating the same. Between those two documents the church became a genuinely global body, and it kept documenting itself with the thoroughness it had shown since the beginning: General Conference minutes, yearbooks, statistical reports, periodicals. Its own internal arguments, including the long controversy that followed the book Questions on Doctrine, are on that same public record rather than hidden in it. In 1911 Ferdinand and Ana Stahl joined Manuel Camacho at Utawilaya, in Platería district, Puno province, on the Peruvian side of Lake Titicaca. Camacho, an Indigenous visionary, was one of the first Adventist converts on the altiplano. He had opened a free school in his home about 1902. The Stahls lived in an adobe building that served as a school on weekdays and a place of worship on Sabbaths.

Documented Stories

Questions on Doctrine

In 1955 Walter Martin, a young evangelical researcher on the sects, and Donald Grey Barnhouse, the Philadelphia preacher who edited Eternity magazine, opened conversations with leaders at the Seventh-day Adventist General Conference in Washington to find out what the church actually taught. The Adventist side, LeRoy Froom, W. E. Read and Roy Allan Anderson chief among them, answered questions in writing over eighteen months. In September 1956 Barnhouse published in Eternity an article concluding that Adventists were fellow Christians, and that positions he had thought Adventist, that Christ had a sinful human nature and that the atonement was unfinished at the cross, were held only by 'a lunatic fringe'. The answers were published in 1957 as Seventh-day Adventists Answer Questions on Doctrine, without an author's name, as the church's own. M. L. Andreasen, eighty-two, the church's most respected theologian of the previous generation, read it and concluded that the men who had written it had given away, for the sake of evangelical acceptance, the doctrine that Christ took fallen human nature and that the final generation would perfect its character before he came. He wrote a series of Letters to the Churches in 1959 saying so, and circulated them himself when the church's press would not. In 1961 the General Conference suspended his ministerial credentials. He died in February 1962; the credentials were restored posthumously that year. The argument he began did not close; the book was reissued in 2003 with notes from both sides.

Glacier View

The teaching that in 1844 Christ began an investigative judgment in the heavenly sanctuary was the Seventh-day Adventist Church's own, the doctrine that had made sense of the Great Disappointment, and it rested on Daniel 8:14. Desmond Ford, an Australian who had taught theology at Avondale College for sixteen years and was then at Pacific Union College in California, had doubted the exegesis for years. On 27 October 1979, invited to address a campus forum, he said so publicly: that the text did not support a judgment beginning in 1844, whatever the doctrine's spiritual meaning. The General Conference gave him six months' leave to write his case, and he produced a manuscript of nearly a thousand pages. From 10 to 15 August 1980 some 115 administrators and scholars met at Glacier View Ranch in the Colorado mountains as the Sanctuary Review Committee. The discussion, by most accounts, was serious; the consensus statements reaffirmed the traditional doctrine while conceding points of exegesis, and a smaller committee then asked Ford to affirm the doctrine as the church stated it. He would not. In September 1980 his ministerial credentials were withdrawn. In the years that followed something over a hundred ministers in Australia, New Zealand and the United States resigned or were dismissed over the same question, and congregations divided. Ford remained an Adventist member, preaching to those who would have him.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Ellen White, whose writings had shaped the movement from its founding, died on 16 July 1915, and the church settled its confession in public over the decades that followed: a 1931 yearbook statement of twenty-two beliefs declared it explicitly trinitarian, a position restated in the twenty-seven Fundamental Beliefs adopted at Dallas in 1980. Through the century it grew into a worldwide body, documenting itself, including its own internal controversies, with unusual thoroughness.

Legacy

In 2010 it was a living worldwide church. Its membership figures are its own count; no independent counter is named here. Its self-documentation is unusually thorough, which makes it one of the better-documented denominations of its size. On this map it does one specific job: the nineteenth-century Adventist entry left a question about this church's confession open by name, to be settled when the twentieth century could be looked at, and the 1931 and 1980 documents are where the church itself settled it, in its own words and on the record.
