Russian Evangelicals — Stundists to the Baptist Union

Ukraine and Russia, 1860 to 1906 — peasants who read the Bible in their own language and aristocrats who read it in a palace, and both of them breaking the law.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:29

Read along

In the 1860s Ukrainian peasants in the south worked beside German colonists. They began meeting for Bible studies that their neighbors called Stunden, hours. They read the Bible for themselves, in the Russian translation then coming out. Reading became conversion, and conversion became congregations. Then the movement met a second one, coming from the other end of society. From 1874 in St Petersburg, nobles won by the preaching of the English evangelist Lord Radstock gathered around Colonel Vasily Pashkov. He opened his house to meetings where servants and nobles sat side by side. In 1884 Pashkov invited the leaders of every strand to his house to unite. On the fourth day the police broke up the meeting. That same year the Baptist churches of the south formed a Russian Baptist Union. Pashkov's followers stayed a separate stream. The state answered with everything it had. Pashkov was sent into exile and meetings were broken up. Children were taken from Stundist parents, and whole families were deported. Leaving the Orthodox Church was a crime as well as a sin. The toleration decree of 1905 gave them their first legal breathing space in forty years.

Documented Stories

The Congress the Police Broke Up

Vasily Pashkov, a Guards colonel and one of the richest landowners in Russia, had been converted in 1874 under the preaching of the English evangelist Lord Radstock, and had opened his house to meetings where princesses and their coachmen sat together to hear the gospel preached in Russian; the Pashkovites, as the capital called them, had spread through his estates and beyond. In the south, Ukrainian peasants who had learned to read the Bible in the Stunden of their German neighbors, Russian Baptists, Mennonite Brethren and Molokans had grown into scattered congregations that the state watched. In April 1884 Pashkov and Count Modest Korff invited representatives of all of them to St Petersburg, some seventy delegates, peasants and nobles, to find a common confession. They met in Pashkov's house from 1 April. On the fourth day the police arrested the provincial delegates, held them in the Peter and Paul fortress overnight, and sent them home under escort. In June the emperor, on the advice of Pobedonostsev, the Holy Synod's chief procurator, required Pashkov and Korff to sign an undertaking to cease all religious activity; they refused, and were ordered to leave Russia. Neither returned. Pashkov died in Paris in 1902. The delegates who went home united, the same year and without the nobles, at a meeting in the Caucasus, as the Union of Russian Baptists, and the Pashkovites and the Baptists remained two streams until the Soviet state forced them together in 1944.

‘An Especially Harmful Sect’

Leaving the Orthodox Church was a crime in imperial Russia, and southern peasants who had become Stundists and Baptists were fined, flogged and imprisoned under the general laws; from 1884, under Pobedonostsev, the campaign became policy. On 4 July 1894 the Committee of Ministers, at the Synod's request, declared Stundism an especially harmful sect, a category stripping its members of the protections other dissenters had: their meetings were forbidden outright, their preachers could be exiled without trial, their marriages went unregistered and their children were therefore illegitimate. Families were deported to settlements in the Transcaucasian mountains, where many died; preachers such as Ivan Ryaboshapka, who had baptized the first Ukrainian Stundists in 1870, were exiled and driven abroad; congregations were fined for every gathering found; and in some cases, recorded in the Stundists' own petitions, children were removed from Stundist households by the village authorities and given to Orthodox relatives or institutions, on the ground that dissenting parents could not be allowed to raise them. Orthodox missionaries were sent to argue the peasants back and reported their failures. The Stundists kept meeting in barns and steppe ravines, sang hymns translated from German and composed in Russian, and appealed, without result, to the emperor. The toleration edict of 17 April 1905, forced by revolution, allowed them to register, and the Baptist union counted more than a hundred thousand members when it could count. Pobedonostsev, who had written that the sects were a disease of the national body, retired that October.

Connections

In Its Own Record

It grew from two roots. In the 1860s Ukrainian peasants began to meet to study the Bible, and their neighbors called them Stundists. From 1874 a second circle met in St Petersburg around Vasily Pashkov. Their leaders met in 1884 to unite, but the police broke up the meeting. That year the Baptist churches of the south formed a Russian Baptist Union, while Pashkov's followers stayed apart. Konstantin Pobedonostsev, the lay official who ran the Orthodox Church for the tsar, led the state's campaign against them. They had left that church, and to leave it was a crime. Their churches are the ancestors of the Soviet-era Initsiativniki Baptists.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Ukrainian peasants working alongside German colonists in the 1860s began holding Bible study meetings their neighbors called Stunden, hours, and read Russia's own new translation of Scripture for themselves. A decade later, a guards colonel named Vasily Pashkov opened his St Petersburg house to preaching that servants and nobles attended side by side, a movement the state answered with exile, broken-up meetings and children taken from their parents, since leaving the Orthodox Church was a crime as well as a sin.

Legacy

These congregations are the ancestors of Russian and Ukrainian evangelical Protestantism as it now exists. The Baptist and Evangelical Christian unions descended from them survived the Soviet century, went through their own painful split in the 1960s between those who registered with the state and those who refused, and are today, with the Pentecostals, the largest Protestant presence across the former Soviet Union. Emigrants carried the tradition abroad as well, into Slavic Baptist communities in North and South America and Germany that are still identifiably from this root.
