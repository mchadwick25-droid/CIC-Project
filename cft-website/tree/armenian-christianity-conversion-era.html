Armenian Christianity (conversion era)

Armenia, c. 314 to 451 — the first kingdom to make Christianity its own, which then had to invent an alphabet before it could read the Bible in its own tongue.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:17

Read along

Armenia's own tradition holds that it was the first kingdom to adopt Christianity as its official religion, when Gregory the Illuminator — imprisoned for years in a pit, by the telling — converted King Trdat and the court around the turn of the fourth century. What followed over the next century and a half is the more remarkable part. An entire national church built from nothing, catechized at first in Greek and Syriac because Armenian had no script of its own, until the monk Mesrop Mashtots devised an alphabet around 405 and the church set about putting the Bible and the liturgy into the people's own language. That translation, made under the catholicos Sahak Partev, is still called by Armenians the Queen of Translations, and it did more than any royal decree to make Christianity Armenian rather than imported. The story of these years ends at the battle of Avarayr in 451, where Armenian nobles fought a Persian attempt to impose Zoroastrianism. A defeat on the field that became the founding story of Armenian religious identity.

Documented Stories

Mashtots Comes Home with the Letters

Koriwn was one of Mashtots’ own students, and his Life of Mashtots, written within a few years of the master’s death in 440, is the earliest original work in the Armenian language — which is fitting, since its subject is how that language came to be written. Mashtots had been a royal secretary and soldier before becoming a monk and then a preacher in the province of Goghtn, where, Koriwn says, he was tormented by the fact that the people could not read the Scriptures: services were held in Greek or Syriac with translators standing by, and ‘the great grace’ was not reaching them. He took the problem to the catholicos Sahak and to King Vramshapuh, who had heard of an Armenian script held by a Syrian bishop named Daniel. It was fetched and tried and found useless — Koriwn says it could not render the sounds of Armenian. So Mashtots went to Edessa and Samosata to work with Greek and Syriac scribes, and there, Koriwn writes, ‘with his holy right hand he became the father of new and wonderful offspring, letters of the Armenian language’. The first sentence he wrote in the new letters, tradition says, was from Proverbs: ‘To know wisdom and instruction; to perceive the words of understanding.’ He came back to Vagharshapat with the alphabet, and Koriwn describes the king and the catholicos coming out of the city to meet him, and schools opened across the country. The translation of the Bible followed.

Hripsime, Gayane and the Conversion That Followed

The History attributed to Agathangelos, composed in the fifth century as the official account of Armenia’s conversion, opens the story of the church not with Gregory the Illuminator but with women. Hripsime, Gayane and a company of consecrated virgins had fled Rome to escape the emperor Diocletian’s attentions and taken refuge near Vagharshapat. King Trdat, told of Hripsime’s beauty, tried to take her; she fought him off, in the narrative’s telling with the strength of a man, and escaped; and in his rage he had her, Gayane and the others tortured and killed. Trdat was then struck with madness — the text says he took the form of a wild boar — and was healed only when his sister, warned in a vision, had Gregory brought up from the pit where he had been imprisoned for thirteen years. Gregory gathered the martyrs’ bodies and built chapels over them; the king was baptized; and then, the same text says, king and bishop went together with the army through the country pulling down the temples of Anahit, Vahagn and Aramazd, killing priests who resisted, and giving the temple lands to the church. But it is the story the Armenian church chose to tell about itself, and it does not hide that the first Christian kingdom was made by a king who had murdered nuns and by an army that destroyed the old religion by force.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced Syriac Christianity (Edessa/Nisibis) Documented — Koriun was one of the translators who worked with Mesrop Mashtots. He says King Vramshapuh sent Mashtots to Syrian Mesopotamia to look for an alphabet. Later Sahak and Mashtots sent two of their pupils to Edessa. There the two were to put the Scriptures into Armenian from the Syriac.
  • influenced Cappadocian Christianity Widely Accepted — Moses of Khoren says the young Nerses was then a student at Caesarea in Cappadocia. Nerses later became head of the Armenian church. Ormanian, a modern historian of that church, says only Caesarea and Edessa had schools that Armenians could reach then. When Moses wrote is disputed. Scholars date him anywhere from the fifth to the eighth century.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

By its own tradition, Armenia became the first kingdom to make Christianity its official religion, after Gregory the Illuminator — said to have spent years imprisoned in a pit — converted King Trdat and his court. Because Armenian had no script of its own, the church first taught in Greek and Syriac, until the monk Mesrop Mashtots devised an alphabet around 405 so Scripture and liturgy could be rendered into Armenian, a translation still called the Queen of Translations. The story closes at Avarayr in 451, where Armenian nobles died resisting a Persian attempt to impose Zoroastrianism.

Legacy

This one did not end. The Armenian Apostolic Church is a living communion of several million people with a worldwide diaspora, still using the alphabet Mashtots made and the liturgical language it created. That alphabet outgrew the church that commissioned it and became the vehicle of all Armenian literature, secular as well as sacred. Armenian manuscript illumination and the carved cross-stones called khachkars grew from the same root. Avarayr is still kept as a feast, and the pattern it set — religious identity as national survival — has shaped Armenian self-understanding ever since.
