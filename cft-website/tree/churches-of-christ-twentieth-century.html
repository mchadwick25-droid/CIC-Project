About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:06

Read along

In 1906 the United States religious census listed the Churches of Christ separately from the Disciples of Christ for the first time — a bureaucratic act that recorded a division which had been widening for a generation. David Lipscomb's own correspondence with the Census Bureau is part of how that listing came about. What was listed was the a cappella wing: congregations that sing without instruments and recognize no authority above the local congregation. Their journals did the work an office would have done elsewhere — the Gospel Advocate, the Firm Foundation and others carried the arguments, the news and the appeals for money. They founded colleges, sent missionaries widely, and divided again in the middle of the century over whether congregations could support institutions in common. They confessed the content of the historic creeds while refusing to make subscription to any creed a test. They said so plainly.

Documented Stories

The Tent Meetings of Marshall Keeble

Marshall Keeble was born in Tennessee in 1878, the son of former slaves, and kept a grocery in Nashville before he gave himself to preaching around 1914. He had a seventh-grade education, a prodigious memory for scripture, and a way of answering hecklers with a proverb that disarmed crowds; the sermons taken down by stenographers read as plain argument from the text. He preached in tents and borrowed halls across the South, where a Black man addressing a mixed crowd could be dangerous, and the crowds came, white and Black, seated apart. By the movement's count he baptized some forty thousand people and established more than two hundred congregations. His work was funded by white members, above all A. M. Burton, the founder of Life and Casualty Insurance in Nashville, who paid for tents, travel and the Nashville Christian Institute, a school for Black students Keeble led from 1942 to 1958. The white congregations that paid for his meetings did not, as a rule, receive the converts he made; the churches he planted were Black, and Keeble did not publicly challenge that arrangement, which some of his later admirers have named as the cost of his access. He died in 1968, the year after the Nashville Christian Institute closed and its assets went to a white university, a decision his students contested in court. He became the most honored evangelist in the movement's history, and the arrangement he worked within is part of what the honor has to carry.

The Quarantine

The Churches of Christ had no structure above the congregation, which was their principle, and the question of the mid-century was whether congregations could nonetheless act together. In 1952 the Highland Church in Abilene, Texas began the Herald of Truth, a national radio and later television program paid for by hundreds of other congregations and administered by Highland's elders. Orphan homes and colleges were likewise being supported from church treasuries. A minority, led by preachers such as Roy Cogdill and Fanning Yater Tant and the journal Gospel Guardian, held that the New Testament showed no pattern for one eldership spending other churches' money or for churches funding human institutions, and that to do so was the first step back toward the missionary societies of the nineteenth century. The Cogdill–Woods debate of 1957 in Birmingham ran six nights. In December 1954 B. C. Goodpasture, editor of the Gospel Advocate, the movement's oldest and most influential paper, wrote that the time had come to 'quarantine' those who were sowing discord, and the word became policy: objecting preachers were no longer invited, colleges did not hire them, and congregations divided, sometimes quietly and sometimes with lawsuits over the building. By the 1960s an estimated two thousand congregations, perhaps a tenth of the whole, stood apart as non-institutional Churches of Christ, alike in every other respect, and the two bodies had almost no fellowship in the decades that followed. Both sides kept the argument in print, and each said the other had left.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 1906 the United States religious census listed the Churches of Christ separately from the Disciples of Christ for the first time, recording a division that had been widening for a generation over instrumental music and church authority; the resulting a cappella congregations recognized no authority above themselves and set up no denominational headquarters. They confessed the content of the historic creeds while refusing to make subscription to any creed a test of fellowship, and their journals, the Gospel Advocate and Firm Foundation among them, did the organizing work a central office would have done elsewhere.

Legacy

In 2010 it was a living body, in North America and well beyond it through a century of mission work. Its a cappella singing was the thing a visitor noticed first, and it remained the practice. Its refusal of denominational machinery held remarkably well: in 2010 there was still no headquarters to write to. Its mid-century division over institutional cooperation left a lasting non-institutional stream inside it.

Doctrinal Floor Note

Through the twentieth century, Churches of Christ confessed the same content as the historic Christian creeds, but refused to make subscription to any written creed a test of fellowship — a real and stated distinction, not a blurred one.
