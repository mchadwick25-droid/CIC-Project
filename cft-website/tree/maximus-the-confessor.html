Maximus the Confessor

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Byzantine Imperial Church (Heraclian & Iconoclast Eras).

Known for
Monk of Constantinople who taught that Christ has two wills and two workings, against the court's teaching of one will. He was tried, had his tongue and right hand cut off, and died in exile among the Lazi.
Also called
Maximus Confessor, Maximus the Theologian
Dates
Debated Pyrrhus in Africa in 645, by J. A. Fabricius's date. Tried at Constantinople in 655, by Louis Duchesne's dates. Died on 13 August, a Saturday, in the fifth indiction, as Anastasius the envoy writes; Migne's Patrologia sets the year at 662
Place
Born in Constantinople, his Greek Life says, and a monk at Chrysopolis, across the water from the city. Later in Africa, Rome and Bizya in Thrace; he died at Schemaris, a fort among the Lazi near the Alans
Role
Monk and abbot, writer on Christ's two wills; before that, by his Greek Life, first among the emperor Heraclius's secretaries

About This Person

At sunset, guards took the old monk Maximus the Confessor and a companion off a ship at Constantinople, barefoot and without cloaks. Days later, before the senate, the emperor's treasurer angrily asked whether he was a Christian. By the grace of Christ, he answered, he was. The treasurer said that was not true. You say so, Maximus replied, but God knows I am a Christian and remain one.

The scene comes from his own side's record of that hearing. Louis Duchesne, editor of the Book of the Popes, dates it to 655. Maximus alone, the treasurer said, had handed Egypt, Alexandria and Africa to the Saracens.

His Greek Life, written in his honor, says he was born in Constantinople. The emperor Heraclius made him first among his secretaries. He left the court for a monastery at Chrysopolis as the teaching that Christ had one will spread.

In Africa he debated Pyrrhus, a former patriarch of Constantinople. Later the emperor Constans forbade talk of one will or two in an edict, the Typos.

His questioners asked why he shunned communion with Constantinople's church. He named the Typos and an earlier statement by the patriarch Sergius. By them, he said, its leaders had cast out the four councils and deposed themselves. One must speak of two wills and two workings in Christ, he said, since the Fathers teach that no nature exists without its own working.

The chronicler Theophanes the Confessor says Maximus went from Africa to Rome and stirred Pope Martin I to act. Martin's synod of 649 condemned Sergius and Pyrrhus.

At Bizya in Thrace, a bishop promised the Typos would be withdrawn if Maximus took communion. Maximus asked that the Roman synod's verdict be accepted first. The bishop said it lacked force without the emperor's order. If emperors' orders made synods valid, Maximus answered, the synods against the Nicene faith would count too.

A synod then condemned him with two companions, both named Anastasius. The sentence ordered their tongues cut out and their right hands cut off. They were paraded through the city and exiled among the Lazi.

There, Anastasius the envoy wrote, guards carried Maximus on a litter of woven branches to a fort near the Alans. He foretold the day of his death, the envoy adds, and died on it, 13 August.

He had told his questioners he held no teaching of his own, only the common teaching of the Catholic church.

Key Works

  • Disputation with Pyrrhus: his debate in Africa with Pyrrhus, a former patriarch of Constantinople, held through the efforts of the patrician Gregory. Fabricius dates it to 645
  • Chapters on Charity: four hundred short chapters, set in hundreds to match the four Gospels, sent to Father Elpidius. In his prologue Maximus says he gathered them from the books of the holy Fathers
  • Questions to Thalassius: his answers to Thalassius, a priest and abbot, on hard questions in Holy Scripture. He signs as Maximus, a humble monk

Legacy

What traces to them

  • A council at Constantinople met from November 680 into 681. It declared two natural wills and two natural workings in Christ, the teaching Maximus had argued.
  • That council said the two wills are not contrary to each other. The human will follows the divine will and is subject to it, in Henry R. Percival's English.
  • At the same council, Macarius of Antioch held to one will. His profession of faith cursed Maximus and his disciples among the heretics. The council anathematized Macarius and deposed him.

How they're remembered

  • Anastasius the envoy wrote that three bright lamps lit his tomb every night. He says the people of the fort and the land around it saw this and spoke of it.
  • Theophanes the Confessor, in his chronicle, says the emperor Constans cut out his tongue and right hand. The reason he gives is that Maximus and his disciples had written much against the emperor's teaching.
  • Fabricius, in a notice Migne prints, says the Greeks call him Theologian and Confessor. The church counts him among the saints with his disciple Anastasius, Fabricius adds.

What Is Uncertain

  • Who wrote his Greek Life. Fabricius names Anastasius the envoy, his disciple. He also reports that Leo Allatius called its author uncertain. The Life itself cites Anastasius the envoy as its source in one place. François Combefis, who edited it, notes that Maximus never calls the envoy his disciple.
  • The year of the first hearing. The record says Rome's envoys had arrived the day before. Louis Duchesne dates their arrival to May 655 and draws that date from the record of Maximus's questioning.
  • Whether he was at the Lateran synod of 649. Fabricius says he took part, and Theophanes says he moved Pope Martin I of Rome to hold it.
  • Severinus Binius, whose notes Mansi reprints, finds no mention of Maximus in the synod's acts. Abbots could attend only to advise, Binius says. A "Maximus monachus" signs a petition of Greek monks there, but the acts do not say which Maximus.
  • His last exile. The sentence and Anastasius the envoy send him to the land of the Lazi. Theophanes has Constans exile Martin and Maximus together to the region of Cherson.
  • How long he lived in his last prison. The Greek Life, in Combefis's Latin, keeps him three years in the fort, the word set in brackets. Anastasius the envoy dates their arrival among the Lazi to 8 June and his death to 13 August, both in the fifth indiction.
  • His age. Fabricius says he died aged eighty-two; the year in that line cannot be read in the held copy. No held source gives his birth year.
  • The record of his hearings is written from his side and calls him the servant of God. The court's charges come down through it and through the sentence of his last trial.
  • He named three acts when asked why he would not take communion with Constantinople. They were the nine chapters made at Alexandria, the Ekthesis of the patriarch Sergius and the Typos. The brief tells of the last two.
