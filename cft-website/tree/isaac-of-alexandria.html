Alexandria, today in Egypt

Isaac of Alexandria

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Coptic Christianity under Early Islam.

Known for
Coptic patriarch of Alexandria after John III. He wrote to two Christian kings of the south to make peace, and nearly died for it. A long Coptic Life of him by Mina survives.
Dates
B. Evetts heads his life in the History of the Patriarchs 686 to 689, and E. Porcher's edition of Mina's Life gives the same years. The History gives him two years and nine months, or three years in another copy. It puts his death on the 2nd of the Coptic month Hatur, Mina on the 9th
Place
Alexandria. Born at Shubra, the History says, or at a village called Picho, Mina says. A monk of Saint Macarius in the desert of Scetis
Role
Patriarch of Alexandria, the forty-first in the History's count

About This Person

An archbishop wanted a secretary, and he sent for a young monk of the desert of Scetis. Asked to write a letter, the monk wrote it badly on purpose, because he loved solitude. The archbishop saw through it. The second letter came out wonderfully. The monk was let go back to the desert, on condition that he spend a month each year writing the rules for the feasts.

So says the Life of Isaac by Mina, a monk of Saint Macarius who later became a bishop. Isaac of Alexandria followed John III of Samannud as patriarch. B. Evetts, who translated the History of the Patriarchs, heads his life there 686 to 689. That History, a later Arabic history of the Coptic church, gives him a short life. E. Porcher, who edited Mina's Life, says it was written to edify. It is not history as we would write it today, he says.

Mina says Isaac was a clerk before he was a monk. His parents placed him with a kinsman, Meneson, to train as a secretary. Meneson served the governor of Egypt. The History says John III called Isaac and made him his partner in governing the church.

When John died, some bishops tried to seat George, a deacon of Sakha, without asking the Amir Abd al-Aziz. Mark, the archdeacon of the city, would not take part unless they waited for Sunday, as the canons require. The Amir's officers found that John had not named George, the History says, and the Amir ordered Isaac appointed.

Isaac wrote to the king of the Abyssinians and the king of the Nubians, bidding them make peace. Slandered, he was summoned by the Amir to be killed. The History says the secretaries had swapped his letters for others, so the Amir found nothing in them. Mina names the kings of Makuria and of a land he calls Maurotania. In Mina, the apostles Peter and Mark walk beside Isaac, and the Amir sees them and is afraid.

Mina says Isaac told the Amir that whoever afflicts the Church afflicts God.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • He repaired the great church of Saint Mark in Alexandria and renewed the bishop's residence, the History says.
  • He built a church at Hulwan, where he went to visit the Amir Abd al-Aziz, the History says. A book on the churches of Egypt, under the name of Abu Salih, dates a monastery there to his time.

How they're remembered

  • Mina's Life was read as a sermon on his feast, the 9th of Hatur. E. Porcher reports this from a note on its manuscript.
  • The Ethiopic Synaxarium, a book of saints' days, keeps him on the 9th of Khedar in E. A. Wallis Budge's English. It gives him three and a half years.
  • Mina says a lamp broke over him at his acclamation and drenched him. The crowd, he says, hailed him as the thirteenth apostle.

What Is Uncertain

  • His life rests on two accounts. The History of the Patriarchs, a later Arabic history of the Coptic church, gives a short one. Mina's Coptic Life is long.
  • E. Porcher, who edited Mina's Life, says its aim was to edify. It is not a work of history as history is written today, he says.
  • Who Mina was. The Life's title calls him bishop of Pchati. Porcher says he was a monk of Saint Macarius who followed John of Nikiu as bishop. Porcher gives no date for the Life.
  • The archbishop in the letter story. Mina does not name him. Budge's Ethiopic tells the same story and names him Abba John the archbishop.
  • His birthplace. The History has Shubra, and Mina a village called Picho. Porcher says the Arabic Synaxarium has Bourlos. Budge's Ethiopic has Borles.
  • The kings he wrote to. The History names the kings of the Abyssinians and the Nubians. Mina names the kings of Makuria and of a land he calls Maurotania. Porcher thinks the scribe may have had Mauritania in mind.
  • How the danger passed. The History says the secretaries swapped his letters. Mina says the apostles Peter and Mark walked beside him, and the Amir was afraid.
  • His years. F. Nau, in a note to Porcher's edition, cites three and a half years from the Arabic Synaxarium. He takes a Sunday consecration from Mina and a Tuesday death from Peter ibn Rahib, and reckons 684 to 687. He doubts those weekdays himself.
  • The rival, George of Sakha. The History says the Amir found that John III had not named him. Mina says George was married and had wicked sons. A note in Evetts's 1895 English of Abu Salih calls the rival John.
  • The crosses. The History says Abd al-Aziz ordered all the crosses in Egypt destroyed in Isaac's time. Mina puts an order against crosses earlier, before Isaac, and has a dream stop it.
