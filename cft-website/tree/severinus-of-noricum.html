Severinus of Noricum

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Early Benedictine / Italian Monasticism.

Known for
Monk who lived among the Danube towns of Riverside Noricum in the later 400s. Eugippius tells how he urged them to fasting, prayer and alms through barbarian raids, stood up to the Rugian king and queen, and foretold the Romans' removal to Italy, where his monks carried his body.
Also called
Saint Severinus
Dates
Died on 8 January, Eugippius says, but he gives no year. George W. Robinson puts the death about 482, and a note in W. D. Foulke's translation of Paul the Deacon gives 482, after Waitz. Eugippius says he came to Noricum about the time of Attila's death, which Robinson dates to 453
Place
Riverside Noricum, on the Danube: Asturis, Comagenis, and Favianis, near which he lived; buried at last in the Lucullan castle at Naples
Role
Monk; head of a community of monks in Noricum

About This Person

On the fifth of January, Severinus of Noricum felt a pain in his side. Three days later he called his monks to him at midnight. He forbade them to weep, made the sign of the cross over his body, and told them to sing a psalm. Grief kept them silent, so he began it himself, "Praise ye the Lord in his sanctuary". On the eighth of January, Eugippius writes, "while we could hardly make the responses, he fell asleep in the Lord."

Eugippius, a monk of his community, wrote this Life from the elder brothers' daily stories. He gives no year for the death, which his translator George W. Robinson puts about 482, some thirty years before the Life. It is a saint's Life, full of wonders.

Severinus was a monk among the towns of Riverside Noricum, on the Danube, often raided by barbarians. Eugippius says he came there from the East about the time Attila died. When the Italian priest Primenius asked his country, Severinus said a servant of God gains nothing by naming it, since silence guards him from vainglory. Eugippius heard in his speech a man of purest Latin stock.

He urged the towns to fasting, prayer and alms, and refused to be made a bishop. The young Odoacer, later king of Italy, came to his cell in wretched hides. "Go forth to Italy!" Severinus told him. Giso, queen of the Rugians, tried to rebaptize some Catholics, but her husband, King Feva, would not allow it, out of reverence for Severinus.

He told his monks to take his bones when the Romans left, as Joseph asked of Israel. When Odoacer's brother ordered the Romans to Italy, the monks did so. With Pope Gelasius's leave, Bishop Victor laid him to rest at Naples, in the Lucullan castle.

Eugippius sent the book to the deacon Paschasius, who added nothing. The virtues of teachers, Paschasius wrote back, "are more easily depicted by their pupils."

Legacy

What traces to them

  • His monks took his body to Italy when Odoacer's brother ordered the Romans out, and Bishop Victor laid it in the Lucullan castle at Naples, in a tomb built by the lady Barbaria, with the leave of Pope Gelasius.
  • Eugippius, a monk of his community, wrote his Life and sent it with a letter to the deacon Paschasius, about two years after the consulship of Importunus, which George W. Robinson dates to 509.
  • Paul the Deacon, writing the History of the Langobards, names his monastery in Noricum and his warnings to the Rugian king Feletheus and his wife, whom Paul calls Gisa, and says Naples keeps his remains.
  • Pope Gregory the Great, writing to Anthemius, subdeacon of Campania, names Amandus, priest of the oratory of Saint Severinus in the Lucullan castle, chosen as bishop by the people of Sorrento.

How they're remembered

  • The deacon Paschasius would add nothing to Eugippius's account, since the virtues of teachers are more easily drawn by their pupils.
  • Albert Hauck, whom Robinson quotes on his title page, called Severinus a personality without parallel.
  • Robinson notes that the Life of Willibald, a Saxon pilgrim at Naples in 729, says Saint Severinus rests there, near the castle.

What Is Uncertain

  • His life comes from a saint's Life, full of wonders, by Eugippius, a monk of his community, written from the elder brothers' daily stories some thirty years after the death by George W. Robinson's dates. Paul the Deacon's later account draws on it, W. D. Foulke says.
  • His country. Eugippius says no one knew it and Severinus would not say. From his speech Eugippius judged him of purest Latin stock, and understood that he had first gone to a desert place in the East. Robinson warns against Theo Sommerlad's detailed account of his early life. A note in Foulke's translation of Paul the Deacon says he was born in Southern Italy or in Africa, citing Waitz.
  • The year of his death. Eugippius gives the day only. The year 482 is Robinson's and Waitz's. Eugippius's own clue is relative. He says the sixth year since the burial had passed when the grave was opened, as the Romans left for Italy after Odoacer's war on the Rugians. Robinson dates the war to 487 and the removal, with a query, to 488.
  • The Rugians' faith. Eugippius does not name Queen Giso's church when he says she tried to rebaptize Catholics, though he has Severinus tell her father-in-law, King Flaccitheus, that they are not united in the one Catholic faith. Robinson concludes that the Rugian rulers were Arians, and quotes Johann Adlzreitter, who speaks of the Arians' font.
