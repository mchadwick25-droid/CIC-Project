Indonesian Christianity — the Independent Century

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Over the twentieth century Indonesia came to hold one of the largest Christian populations in the Muslim-majority world. By 2010 its churches had been running themselves for most of a century. The Batak church, the HKBP, became independent in 1930 — the first self-governing church in the Dutch East Indies. The archipelago's Christianity is regional rather than national: Sumatra, Java, Timor and the eastern islands each have their own story. Two of the century's turning points fall close together. Between 1965 and 1971 a revival ran through Timor, whose best-known account, Mel Tari's Like a Mighty Wind (1971), is disputed for embellishment, and whose most careful evaluation, George W. Peters's Indonesia Revival: Focus on Timor (1973), was written on the ground. And after the violence of 1965 and 1966, in a state that required every citizen to declare a religion, church membership grew very fast — a growth that cannot honestly be told apart from its context.

In Its Own Record

Continues

  • Indonesian Christianity Dominant Modern Reconstruction Major — The Batak church of North Sumatra gave itself a written order in 1881. It is the same church, the HKBP, that became fully free of the mission in 1930. The churches of Java and the eastern islands also lived on into the next century. Jan Aritonang and Karel Steenbrink tell both parts of the story in their history of Indonesia's churches.
