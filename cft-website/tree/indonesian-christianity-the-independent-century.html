Indonesian Christianity — the Independent Century

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:15

Read along

Over the twentieth century Indonesia came to hold one of the largest Christian populations in the Muslim-majority world. By 2010 its churches had been running themselves for most of a century. The Batak church, the HKBP, became independent in 1930 — the first self-governing church in the Dutch East Indies. The archipelago's Christianity is regional rather than national: Sumatra, Java, Timor and the eastern islands each have their own story. Two of the century's turning points fall close together. Between 1965 and 1971 a revival ran through Timor, whose best-known account, Mel Tari's Like a Mighty Wind (1971), is disputed for embellishment, and whose most careful evaluation, George W. Peters's Indonesia Revival: Focus on Timor (1973), was written on the ground. And after the violence of 1965 and 1966, in a state that required every citizen to declare a religion, church membership grew very fast — a growth that cannot honestly be told apart from its context.

Documented Stories

The Night at Soe

Soe is a market town in the hills of West Timor, and its congregation belonged to the GMIT, the Protestant Evangelical Church of Timor. On the evening of 26 September 1965, four days before the attempted coup in Jakarta that would change the country, a service there passed into something the congregation had not seen: confession of sins aloud, weeping, people speaking of visions, and a fire some said they saw over the building. Over the following weeks lay teams formed, numbered as they went out, and walked to villages across the island, preaching, praying for the sick, and calling on people to burn the fetishes and charms of the older religion; the GMIT's records list the teams and where they went. The church grew fast. In 1971 Mel Tari, a young Timorese who had been on one of the teams, published in the United States Like a Mighty Wind, an account that included water turned to wine for communion, the dead raised, and teams crossing flooded rivers on foot. In 1972 George Peters, a missiologist from Dallas, spent weeks on Timor interviewing team members, pastors and villagers. His book of 1973 concluded that a genuine and lasting awakening had occurred, that the testimony of changed lives and burned fetishes was overwhelming, and that many of the spectacular claims did not survive questioning of the people said to have witnessed them. The church they describe went on.

Two Million in Six Years

On 30 September 1965 a group of army officers killed six generals in Jakarta; the army under Suharto blamed the Communist Party and, over the following months, led and licensed a slaughter of people accused of belonging to it. Estimates of the dead run from five hundred thousand to a million, most of them in Java and Bali, killed by soldiers and by militias, some of which were organized through Muslim youth organizations and some, in a few places, through Christian ones. The new order then required every citizen to hold one of the recognized religions; not to have one was to be suspected of atheism, which was to be suspected of communism. In the years that followed, the Protestant and Catholic churches of Java received people in numbers they had never seen: with baptisms in the tens of thousands a year, so that by the early 1970s something like two million people had entered the churches across Indonesia. The churches knew why some had come. Pastors' accounts from those years speak of villages associated with the party whose survivors turned to a church that had not taken part in the killing and did not ask questions, and of catechism classes that were also, plainly, refuge. They also speak of people who stayed. Missionary writers called it revival; Indonesian church historians insisted on the whole context, and the churches themselves largely adopted that honesty.

Connections

In Its Own Record

The HKBP, the Batak church, became fully independent in 1930. Between 1965 and 1966, in the wake of mass political violence and a new state requirement that every citizen declare a religion, church membership grew rapidly across the archipelago. A revival swept through Timor from 1965 to 1971 in the same period. In the decades that followed, Indonesian church life ran under the state ideology of Pancasila, with periodic episodes of persecution. Indonesia's Catholics are part of this story too. Karel Steenbrink called the years 1903 to 1942 "the spectacular growth of a self-confident minority". Jan Aritonang and Steenbrink's history of 2008 tells the Catholic and Protestant churches together.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

The Batak church, the HKBP, became the first self-governing church in the Dutch East Indies in 1930. Between 1965 and 1971 a revival swept through Timor — recounted in Mel Tari's widely read but disputed Like a Mighty Wind (1971) and evaluated more carefully on the ground by George Peters in 1973 — in the same years that a state requirement to declare a religion, following the violence of 1965-66, drove rapid church growth across the archipelago.

Legacy

In 2010 these were living churches, and large ones. Christians were a minority in the country, and their place was at times contested. The HKBP's independence in 1930 is an early marker of a long shift, from mission to self-governing church. The Timor revival left a widely read account in Mel Tari's book. Tari and Peters together are a rare case. A movement's own account and its critical evaluation survive side by side.
