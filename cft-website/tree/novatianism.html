Novatianism (the Katharoi)

Rome and across the empire, from 251 CE into the centuries after — a rival church founded the day a Roman theologian decided that Christians who had denied Christ under threat could never be readmitted.

The Roman Church in the Third Century→Novatianism (the Katharoi)→no descendant recorded

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:28

Read along

When the emperor Decius ordered every subject of the empire to sacrifice in 250, a great many Christians complied, and once the pressure lifted they wanted to come back. Novatian — the most learned theologian in the Roman church and the first there to write substantial theology in Latin — held that the church had no authority to receive them. God might forgive apostasy, but the church could not presume to. When Cornelius, who took the lenient view, was elected bishop of Rome in 251, Novatian's supporters had him consecrated as a rival bishop, one of the earliest such rival consecrations the city had seen. His congregations called themselves the Katharoi, the pure, and they spread across the empire and lasted for centuries. At the end of the fourth century they still held several churches in Constantinople and more across Phrygia and Bithynia. What stands out is how little separated them in doctrine. Their bishop attended the Council of Nicaea in 325 and accepted its creed, and the council's eighth canon set out terms for receiving Novatianist clergy — terms they largely declined. Later tradition remembers Novatian himself as a martyr, though the record on that is not firm.

Documented Stories

The Confessors Come Back

Novatian’s rival episcopate in Rome had one great asset: the confessors. Maximus, Urbanus, Sidonius, Nicostratus and their companions had survived prison and torture under Decius, and their support for the man who said the lapsed could never be readmitted carried moral weight nothing else could match. Cyprian wrote to them from Carthage begging them to return to Cornelius, the bishop the church had elected. Within the year, most of them did. Cornelius’ letter to Cyprian describes the scene: the confessors came before the Roman clergy and a large assembly of laity, said that they had been ‘circumvented by crafty and deceitful words’, that they had not understood what they were joining, and asked to be received; the people wept; Maximus was restored to his place as presbyter and the others to communion. Their own short letter to Cyprian survives too, admitting that ‘we were ignorant’ of what Novatian intended and thanking him for his persistence. Nicostratus, a deacon, did not come back and went to Africa with Novatian’s party. The episode shows the Novatianist claim from inside and at its most fragile: the people whose sufferings had certified the movement’s purity concluding, publicly, that purity was not a reason to divide the church. Novatian’s congregations went on without them for two hundred years.

The Peasants of Mantinium Take Up Sickles

Socrates, the fifth-century historian, had unusual access to Novatianist sources and unusual sympathy for them, and he tells this story as an atrocity committed against them. Macedonius, the Homoian bishop of Constantinople under Constantius, was compelling Novatianists to communicate with him, and, finding that the district of Mantinium in Paphlagonia was full of them, persuaded the emperor to send four companies of troops to bring them to heel by force. The Novatianists there ‘were peasants and rustics’. Socrates says they armed themselves ‘with long reaping hooks, hatchets, and whatever weapons came to hand’, and met the soldiers. Most of the troops were killed; so were very many of the farmers. Socrates adds that he had it from an old peasant who had fought that day as a young man. He tells the story alongside other outrages of Macedonius’ — the seizing of Novatianist churches in the capital, the forced communion of women and children with wedges driven between their teeth — and places it in a narrative whose moral is that coercion in religion breeds this. It is a rare glimpse of a dissident church’s rank and file: not theologians disputing the readmission of the lapsed, but a countryside that had been Novatianist for a century and would rather die than take communion from the emperor’s bishop. Socrates does not conceal that they killed soldiers to do it.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

When many Christians complied with the emperor Decius' order to sacrifice in 250 and then wanted back into the church, the Roman theologian Novatian argued that the church had no authority to forgive such apostasy, however much God might. His followers, who called themselves the Katharoi, 'the pure,' consecrated him as a rival bishop and spread across the empire for centuries; a Novatianist bishop even attended the Council of Nicaea in 325 and accepted its creed.

Legacy

No church descends from them; the last Novatianist communities faded in the East by around the seventh century. Two things outlasted the schism. The first is the doctrine sharpened against it — that the church is a hospital rather than a society of the already-perfect, and that no sin lies beyond the reach of the church's ministry of reconciliation — worked out by Cyprian in the moment and by Augustine at length afterwards, along with the graded system of public penance that grew out of exactly this argument. The second is Novatian's own On the Trinity, which was copied, read and valued in the mainstream church as a careful Latin statement of orthodox doctrine long after his party was gone, handed down in manuscripts among the works of Tertullian.

Doctrinal Floor Note

Novatian's own On the Trinity is a careful and orthodox statement of Christian doctrine, and the Novatianist bishop present at Nicaea accepted the council's creed. The break was over discipline — whether the church could readmit those who had denied the faith under persecution — not over belief.
