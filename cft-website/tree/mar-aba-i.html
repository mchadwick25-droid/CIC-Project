The Age of Monks and Empires · patriarch 540-552 · Seleucia-Ctesiphon

Mar Aba I

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Church of the East under Persia.

Known for
Catholicos of the Church of the East in the Persian empire, a convert from the old Persian religion, whose synod, dated by J.-B. Chabot to 544, records the end of a split between rival patriarchs.
Also called
Mar Abba, Abas
Dates
Elected in January 540 and died in 552, in J.-B. Chabot's dates; Chabot dates the election from the synod's own dating and the death from the acts of the synod of his successor Joseph. The Latin editors of Bar Hebraeus, Jean-Baptiste Abbeloos and Thomas Lamy, give 538 to 552. Mari ibn Sulayman says he led the church for sixteen years. No source used here gives his birth year
Place
Seleucia-Ctesiphon, the seat of his church; Nisibis and Edessa in his early years; Adorbigan, in exile
Role
Catholicos and patriarch; teacher

About This Person

Mar Aba and his bishops came into the district of Kashkar. Two men there had each claimed to be its bishop in the years of quarrel. At the town of Zizwarda, the record of his synod says, the people of Kashkar agreed and chose one bishop, Samuel. The two rivals lost their titles and gladly served under Samuel as priests.

Mar Aba was catholicos, head of the Church of the East in the Persian empire. The French scholar J.-B. Chabot dates his election to January 540, from the synod's own dates.

Mari ibn Sulayman, who wrote his church's history in the 1100s, says Aba had been a magus, a follower of the old Persian religion. Mari tells his conversion as a wonder. Aba refused a Christian scholar named Joseph a place in his boat on the Tigris. Each time he refused, winds held the boat back, until he took Joseph aboard.

A Life of Mar Aba survives, which Chabot says needs careful critical study. It has him baptized in the village of Akda, a student at Nisibis, a learner of Greek at Edessa, and later a teacher.

Before him, his church had two rival patriarchs, Narses and Elisha. Mari says each ordained his own bishops, so that two altars stood in every church. The synod's record opens by saying that split had wholly ended. A letter in Mar Aba's name calls marriage a bond of love in the chaste and lawful union of a man and a woman. It forbids marriage to a father's wife and other close kin, as the magi did.

The magi turned against him, Mari says, because he had been one of them and changed their customs. He spent seven years in prison in Adorbigan, Mari writes, and kept caring for his flock. He told the king, Mari says, to kill him if the king wanted him dead.

How he died is told two ways. The Life has him regain the king's favor and die of illness, as head of his church. Bar Hebraeus, of the rival Syriac Orthodox church, says the king chained him when he returned to Seleucia, and he died in prison. Chabot gives the year as 552.

The Life has him buried in the monastery of Seleucia. Mari says his disciple Qyura carried his body to Hira and built a monastery over the grave. Chabot notes that his church honors him as a saint.

Key Works

  • The Synod of Mar Aba (dated by Chabot to 544): six documents issued under his name with his bishops, of which Chabot prints the first, third, fourth and fifth in full; they include the record of the end of the split, the bishops' journey through the dioceses, and the bishops' signatures
  • Letter on the rule of conduct of the faithful: one of the synod's documents, on Christian marriage, forbidding marriage to a second wife while the first lives and to close kin

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The synod's record says the split between the rival patriarchs Narses and Elisha had wholly ended and the single rule of the see of Seleucia-Ctesiphon was restored.
  • Chabot says he founded a school at Seleucia. Mari ibn Sulayman says the same, and that a school he built stood until the patriarch Ezekiel, his disciple, restored it. J. Labourt doubts that the school at Seleucia existed so early.

How they're remembered

  • Chabot: the Church of the East honors him as a saint.
  • Mari ibn Sulayman calls him Mar Abba the Great and tells his conversion, his wonders, his trial before the king and his death.
  • J. W. McCrindle, the English translator of Cosmas Indicopleustes, takes Cosmas's teacher Patricius, who became Bishop Catholic of all Persia, for Mar Aba.

What Is Uncertain

  • His life outside the synod's record rests on later accounts. His Life, by a disciple in J. Labourt's judgment, is held in Oskar Braun's German translation from Paul Bedjan's Syriac; Chabot says it needs careful critical study and lacks dates. Mari ibn Sulayman wrote in the 1100s and tells the conversion as a wonder.
  • How he died. The Life has him regain the king's favor and die of illness, near the church of Bet Narkos, as head of his church. Bar Hebraeus, writing for the rival Syriac Orthodox church, says he came back to Seleucia from exile, was chained and died in prison. Mari ibn Sulayman says he died of an illness after some days, grieving over what his clergy suffered.
  • Where he was baptized and buried. The Life puts his baptism in the village of Akda and his burial in the monastery of Seleucia. Mari ibn Sulayman puts both at Hira, and Chabot's outline follows him on the baptism.
  • How long he was held. Mari ibn Sulayman gives seven years in prison in Adorbigan; the Life speaks of nine years before he came back to his see.
  • The day of his death. The Life gives the Friday of the second week of Lent; Mari gives a Friday, the third day of Lent. J. Labourt gives 29 February 552.
  • His teacher of Greek. Chabot's outline says he learned Greek at Edessa from his disciple Thomas. Mari makes him a hearer of Thomas of Edessa and later counts Thomas among his disciples. Bar Hebraeus calls Thomas a Jacobite and says the two went on to Alexandria.
  • Whether Cosmas's teacher Patricius is Mar Aba. McCrindle says so, from the list of the patriarchs of his church. The Latin of Montfaucon, the Benedictine who first edited the Greek, which McCrindle rejects, reads the passage as making Thomas of Edessa the head of the church of Persia.
  • The year of his election: Chabot gives January 540; Abbeloos and Lamy give 538.
