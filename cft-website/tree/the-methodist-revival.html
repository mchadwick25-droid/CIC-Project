The Methodist Revival

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

John Wesley was an Oxford-trained Church of England priest whose methodical little religious society had earned him a mocking nickname and whose mission to Georgia had ended in failure. Until, at a meeting in Aldersgate Street, London, in May 1738, he felt his heart 'strangely warmed' and began preaching assurance and holiness to anyone who would stand still for it. Shut out of many pulpits, he and George Whitefield preached in fields and at pit-heads to crowds of thousands. But Wesley did something rarer than preaching, which was to organize, dividing converts into 'classes' of a dozen who met weekly to give an account of their lives, licensing lay preachers to ride circuits, and holding an annual conference. His brother Charles wrote the movement's theology into thousands of hymns, so that people who could not have read a treatise sang one instead. Wesley meant all of this to renew the Church of England from inside and insisted to the end that he had not left it. After his death in 1791 the connexion became a separate church anyway.

In Its Own Record

Grew Out Of

  • The Restoration and Georgian Church of England Documented Major — Methodism began as societies of people inside the Church of England, gathered by John Wesley. In 1766 the preachers' conference said, 'we are not Dissenters from the Church now'. In 1785 Wesley recalled how these 'little companies' had spread through the three kingdoms.

Gave Rise To

  • The Black Church in America Documented Major — Richard Allen joined a Methodist society in Delaware. Later his Black church in Philadelphia fell out with the white elders sent to lead it. In Baltimore, too, white preachers and trustees drove many Black members away. In April 1816 Black delegates from both towns and others met. They agreed to form one body of their own. They named it the African Methodist Episcopal Church.
  • The Second Great Awakening & Camp-Meeting Tradition Widely Accepted Major — In 1810 Jesse Lee, a Methodist preacher, wrote about the early camp meetings. In some cases, he said, Methodist, Presbyterian and Baptist ministers joined to run the same one. In 1805 Bishop Francis Asbury hoped for a camp meeting in every circuit of one district. So the revival grew in part out of Methodist work.
  • The Caribbean Churches of Slavery and Emancipation Inferential-Thin Major — The Wesleyans began work in Jamaica in 1789, under Dr. Coke, and set up a permanent station at Kingston. They worked the same island as the Baptists, another strand of this story. In 1832 a Wesleyan, Mr. Box, was arrested after the rising, as Bleby tells. This rests on two accounts: Phillippo, a Baptist missionary, in 1843, and Bleby in 1868. No modern study was opened.

Continued As

  • Nineteenth-Century Methodism Widely Accepted Major — Wesley died in 1791. The Methodist Conference he had built kept meeting and ruling its preachers. In America the Methodists had already formed their own church at Baltimore in 1784. They called it the Methodist Episcopal Church, and Jesse Lee carried its story on to 1809.

Influenced By

  • The Remonstrants and Dort Documented — In July 1741 John Wesley picked up the works of Simon Episcopius by mistake and read their account of the Synod of Dort. 'What a scene is here disclosed!' he wrote. Later he drew up plans for a magazine he called The Arminian Magazine.
  • German Pietism - Spener, Francke, Halle Documented — In July 1738 John Wesley went to Halle and toured its orphan house. He wrote that August Hermann Francke's 'name is indeed as precious ointment'. George Whitefield read Francke's account of the Halle orphan house and saw it as the pattern for his own orphan house in Georgia.
  • The Moravian Church at Herrnhut Documented — In March 1738 the Moravian Peter Böhler convinced John Wesley, by Wesley's own account, 'of the want of that faith whereby alone we are saved'. That summer Wesley traveled to Herrnhut, 'to see the place where the Christians live'.
  • Puritanism Widely Accepted — In 1747 John Wesley read The History of the Puritans. He wrote with anger of the persecution that drove 'those venerable men' out of the Church. Historians of Methodism, Henry Rack among them, see a Puritan strand in the Wesleys' family and faith.
  • Quietist Controversy (Molinos, Guyon) Documented — John Wesley read Guyon's Short Method of Prayer in 1742 and dismissed her as a 'poor Quietist'. By 1770 he wrote that she was a woman of 'excellent piety'. In 1776 he wrote a preface for a shortened Life of her.
  • Lutheran Wittenberg & Its Congregations Documented — In May 1738 John Wesley went, against his will, to a meeting in Aldersgate Street, London. Someone was reading Luther's preface to Romans, and there Wesley felt his heart 'strangely warmed'. Yet in 1741 he read Luther's book on Galatians and called it shallow and confused.

Influenced

  • The Holiness Movement Widely Accepted — In his Large Minutes, John Wesley told his preachers to urge all believers to 'go on to perfection'. He meant being saved from all sin, as love for God and neighbor fills the heart. Holiness writers of the 1800s took up this teaching and called it Wesley's own.
  • The Evangelical Awakening in New England Documented — In 1743 Charles Chauncy of Boston, a critic of the revival, wrote that preaching from town to town began, in those parts, with George Whitefield. Whitefield's own journal tells how he preached at Northampton. Jonathan Edwards wept the whole time.
  • The Missionary Movement's Native Churches (Serampore India; Sierra Leone & the Niger; Pacific Islands; China Coast & CIM) Documented — In his 1792 appeal for a mission society, William Carey pointed to John Wesley's mission in the West Indies. He wrote that he had 'seen pleasing accounts of their success' among the Caribs and the Black people of the islands. Carey went on to lead the Baptist mission at Serampore in India.

Broke With

  • The Restoration and Georgian Church of England Widely Accepted Major — In 1785 John Wesley wrote that he had varied from the Church of England's discipline, though not from its doctrines, and only 'by constraint'. He had preached in the fields because the churches were shut to him. If anyone called this separating from the Church, he wrote, 'he may'. In 1795 the Methodist Conference let a chapel hold the Lord's Supper if its trustees, its leaders and the Conference agreed. It was a long step toward a separate church.

In Contact With

  • English Old Dissent — Presbyterians and Congregationalists Documented — In September 1745 John Wesley called on Philip Doddridge at his academy in Northampton, England. It was the hour when Doddridge taught the young men in his care, and he asked Wesley to take his place.
  • Camisard Prophetism Inferential-Thin — In January 1739 John Wesley went to hear a young woman 'commonly called' a French prophet, to test her spirit. His editor names her as probably Mary Plewit and calls the French prophets refugee Camisards. The tie to the Camisard rising, thirty years on, rests on that name alone.
  • The Welsh Revival Tradition Documented — Howell Harris, a leader of the Welsh revival, first met George Whitefield at Cardiff in March 1739. In 1746 an English visitor sat in on a meeting of the Welsh leaders. He wrote in his diary what they told him. God had raised up Harris and Daniel Rowland, they said, at the same time as Whitefield and the Wesleys.

Sources

  • Wesley's Journal and the Works of John Wesley (Bicentennial Edition, Abingdon, 1975-), sermons, A Plain Account of Christian Perfection (primary) — Wesley's own writings, kept distinct from the connexion's communal record.
  • The class meeting and connexional machinery: General Rules (1743), band and class records and class papers, society and circuit records, annual Conference Minutes (from 1744) (primary) — The movement's lived organizational core, in records scattered across archives, chiefly the Methodist Archives in Manchester.
  • Charles Wesley's hymns: A Collection of Hymns for the Use of the People Called Methodists (1780), with Charles's journal (primary) — Hymnody as actually used: the movement's theology in its sung, memorized, everyday form.
  • Women's and ordinary voices: Wesley-Crosby correspondence (1761), Mary Bosanquet Fletcher's 1771 letter, Hester Ann Rogers's Account, the Arminian Magazine (1778-) lay narratives; Richard Allen's conversion c. 1777 at the era's edge (primary) — The women's-preaching debate in its own protagonists' words; a vehicle for ordinary members' narratives. Allen's narrative wasn't published until 1833, and the Primitive Methodist accounts (from 1807) come from a later period than this movement's core years.
  • Henry Rack, Reasonable Enthusiasm (1989; 3rd ed. 2002), with David Hempton, Methodism: Empire of the Spirit (Yale, 2005) (secondary)
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 7, 'Evangelical awakenings in the North Atlantic world' (W. R. Ward) (reference) — A direct fit for this movement.
