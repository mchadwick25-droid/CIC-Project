The Early Medieval Era · born 675-680; killed 754 · Mainz, Dokkum

Boniface of Mainz

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Anglo-Saxon Christianity.

Known for
English monk who preached in Frisia, Hesse and Thuringia and called English monks and nuns to help him. At the pope's command and the Frankish rulers' request, he wrote, he gathered a synod in the hope of restoring the law of Christ. Pagans killed him in Frisia in 754.
Also called
Boniface, Wynfrith, Uinfridus, Bonifatius, Bonifacius
Dates
Born between 675 and 680, by Edward Kylie's reckoning; no older source used here gives the year. Killed in 754, the year the Royal Frankish Annals and the Continuation of Bede give. Kylie gives the day as 5 June, and the Bollandists give the Nones of June, the same day. He dates the first journey to Frisia to 716 and the coming to Rome to 718. He dates the consecration as bishop to 722 and the appointment as archbishop to 732
Place
Crediton by tradition, Kylie says, with the monastery at Exeter as his first school; Nursling, near Winchester; Frisia, Hesse and Thuringia; Mainz, whose archbishop the Royal Frankish Annals call him; Fulda, the monastery he founded, to which his body was carried from Utrecht; Dokkum in Frisia, where he was killed. Kylie spells it Docking, Altfrid's Life of Liudger Doceinga, and the Bollandists Dockum
Role
Monk and teacher of the Bible at Nursling; missionary; bishop and archbishop; legate in Germany of the universal Church, as he called himself; reformer of the Frankish church

About This Person

Boniface, archbishop of Mainz, sailed down the Rhine toward Frisia for the last time. Edward Kylie, who translated his letters in 1911, says he had a winding-sheet packed in the chest with his books. On the fifth of June 754 at Dokkum, he writes, a pagan band surrounded his tents, and he forbade his companions to resist. The Royal Frankish Annals record that pagans killed him in Frisia as he preached.

He was English, born Wynfrith between 675 and 680, by Kylie's reckoning. For his early life, Kylie's modern account is the main witness. At Nursling, near Winchester, he lectured on the Bible. A first mission to Frisia in 716 met resistance. In Rome in 718, Kylie says, his English name took a Latin form.

In Hesse, Kylie writes, he felled the oak at Geismar sacred to Thor and built a chapel to Saint Peter from its ruins. No missionary before him had called women to his aid, Kylie says. He wrote to Lioba, Tecla and Cynehilda, "I beseech, nay all but command you," and asked them to pray for him.

About 737, by Kylie's date, he asked all the English to pray that God would turn the hearts of the pagan Saxons. Have pity on them, he wrote, for they themselves say, "We are of one blood and one bone."

In 742 Carloman, prince of the Franks, called a synod. His decree sets Archbishop Boniface, the envoy of Saint Peter, over the bishops of the cities, and orders a synod every year. In a letter printed with the decree, Boniface told Pope Zacharias that Carloman had promised to put right something of the church's order. It had been trodden down, he wrote, for no less than sixty or seventy years.

After a synod Kylie dates to 747, he sent its decrees to Cuthbert, archbishop of Canterbury. He likened himself to a watchdog that sees robbers breaking in, but with no one to help him "only complains and grieves." When Pippin was made king of the Franks, the Royal Frankish Annals say, Boniface anointed him.

Cuthbert later wrote to Boniface's helper Lul that an English synod had fixed the day of his martyrdom as a yearly feast.

Kylie quotes Boniface's greeting to a new pope, Stephen. "If I have accomplished anything for the good of the Church during the thirty-six years of my Roman mission I still wish to complete and perfect it."

Key Works

  • Letters to his English friends: in Edward Kylie's English selection of 1911, made from Ernst Dümmler's text in the Monumenta Germaniae Historica. They ask for prayers, books and help. Among them are letters to Lioba, Tecla and Cynehilda, to Abbess Eadburga of Thanet, and to Egbert of York asking for the works of Bede
  • Letter to all the English on the Saxons: dated about 737 by Kylie. He asks them to pray that God would turn the hearts of the pagan Saxons. The Saxons themselves say, he writes, "We are of one blood and one bone." Torhthelm, bishop of Leicester, answered with a gift and a promise of prayers
  • Letter to Cuthbert, archbishop of Canterbury, after the synod of 747: sends the synod's rules. They include a profession of unity with the Roman church and a synod every year. Every priest is to give an account each Easter, and each bishop is to go round his diocese against pagan practices. It adds advice on checking women's pilgrimages to Rome and on monasteries ruled by laymen

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Cuthbert, archbishop of Canterbury, wrote to Boniface's helper Lul that an English synod had fixed the day of his martyrdom as a yearly feast. They would seek him as patron alongside Gregory the Great and Augustine of Canterbury.
  • Kylie writes that Lul was to follow him at Mainz and complete the church at Fulda. Lioba was to stay at her work in Germany and be buried beside him.
  • Altfrid's Life of Liudger says Liudger was taught by Gregory of Utrecht, the disciple and successor of Boniface the martyr.
  • Alcuin of York wrote verses for the church that Liudger built where Boniface and his companions were killed. Altfrid gives them in his Life of Liudger.
  • The revised Royal Frankish Annals tell of the year 774. Saxons tried to burn the church at Fritzlar that Boniface had dedicated, and fled in sudden fear.

How they're remembered

  • Milret, bishop of Worcester, wrote to Lul that Boniface was now "the glory and support of all those sprung from this land."
  • The Continuation of Bede, in Charles Plummer's edition, records under 754 that Boniface, also called Wynfrith, bishop of the Franks, was martyred with fifty-three others.
  • Edward Kylie ranks him with those who uphold tradition and law against those who question authority. He notes that Clemens the Scot and Virgil, a Celt in Bavaria, both fell under his ban.
  • The Roman Breviary in the Marquess of Bute's English gives a prayer from the diocese of Plymouth. It names the zeal of Boniface as the means by which God caused many peoples to know his Name.

What Is Uncertain

  • Edward Kylie's modern account is the main witness for his early life. Among the sources used here, Kylie alone gives his birth years, his first school and his Latin name in Rome. He alone tells of the oak at Geismar and the scene of his death. The Life of Boniface ascribed to Willibald is not among these sources. The Bollandists quote one phrase of it in a note.
  • His death. The Royal Frankish Annals say pagans killed him in Frisia as he preached, in 754. The Continuation of Bede gives the same year. The Bollandists report that some put it in 755, and follow the annals for 754.
  • Archbishop. Kylie dates his appointment as archbishop to 732. He also writes that Boniface was chosen archbishop at the first Frankish synod. The decree of 742 by Carloman, prince of the Franks, says he set Archbishop Boniface over the bishops of the cities.
  • His see. Kylie writes that a plan to make him archbishop of Cologne was dropped, and that he was left to run the diocese of Mainz. The Royal Frankish Annals call him archbishop of Mainz.
  • His birthplace. Kylie gives Crediton only as tradition, and says there can be little doubt that the monastery at Exeter was his first school.
  • His name. He is a different man from the popes named Boniface.
