The Russian north, c. 1340 to 1517 — monks who walked into the forest to be alone, and ended up settling a country.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:26

Read along

Sergius of Radonezh withdrew into the woods north of Moscow in the 1340s to live alone. A monastery grew up around him. Within a generation his disciples had founded dozens more, pushing ever deeper into the northern forests. Later Russians called this the northern Thebaid. The texts followed: Epiphanius the Wise's life of Sergius, and the skete rules of Nil Sorsky. So did the art. Andrei Rublev painted his Trinity in Sergius's memory, for Sergius's own monastery. The same years saw the sharpest quarrel in Russian church history. Nil Sorsky and the non-possessors held that monasteries should own no land and no peasants. Monks should live by their own work. Joseph of Volotsk and the possessors held that rich monasteries could feed the poor, train clergy and carry the church's work. The monasteries kept their lands. Soon after 1496 Grand Prince Ivan planned to take them, but the metropolitan talked him out of it. In the middle of all this, in 1448, Russian bishops chose their own metropolitan without waiting on Constantinople. It was the beginning of an independent Russian church.

People and Stories

Voices in the Record

  • Sergius of Radonezh — the hermit whose Trinity monastery became the center of Russian monastic life and, later, of Russian national memory
  • Epiphanius the Wise — Sergius's disciple and biographer, who wrote the movement's founding life
  • Stephen of Perm — missionary to the Komi, for whom he devised an alphabet and translated the services
  • Nil Sorsky — leader of the non-possessors, who wanted monks poor, few and silent
  • Joseph of Volotsk — leader of the possessors, who wanted monasteries endowed, disciplined and socially useful
  • Andrei Rublev — monk and icon painter, whose Trinity was made for Sergius's own monastery

Documented Stories

Stephen of Perm and the Shaman’s Ordeal

Stephen was a monk of Rostov who went in 1379 to the Komi, the Permian people of the Vychegda basin, devised for them an alphabet — the Abur, drawn partly from their own property marks — and translated the liturgy into their language before he preached in it. The Komi shaman Pam, whom Epiphanius calls their sorcerer and chief, argued with Stephen before the people: that the Russians were their oppressors and Stephen’s God the Russians’ God; that one Christian could not know more than the many gods the Komi had always had; that the old ways had kept them fed. Then the ordeal. Pam proposed that the two of them enter a burning hut together and then go under the ice of the river at one hole and come up at another, and that whoever survived had the true God. Stephen agreed and took his hand to go in; Pam pulled back, and again, and again, before the crowd. The people, Epiphanius says, wanted to kill Pam; Stephen would not allow it and had him banished. The Life records Stephen cutting down the sacred birch, burning the idols and their offerings of furs — the furs he would not let his people take — and being appointed bishop of Perm in 1383. Epiphanius tells the ordeal as a triumph; it is also the record of a mission that arrived with the tax collectors and knew it.

Nil Sorsky Stands Up at the Council of 1503

Nil had lived on Athos, come home to found a skete on the Sora river in the northern forests where monks lived in twos and threes by their own labor, and written a rule for it that is mostly about the watching of thoughts. In 1503 Ivan III called a council to legislate on clerical fees and widowed priests. According to the account written a generation later by the non-possessor party, when the business was done Nil ‘began to say that the monasteries should not have villages, but that monks should live in the wilderness and feed themselves by the work of their hands’, and the hermits of the Trans-Volga who had come with him supported him. Ivan III, who had his own reasons for wanting monastic land, took the proposal seriously and asked the council to consider it. Joseph, abbot of Volokolamsk, had already left; the metropolitan sent for him, and he came back with the argument the possessors would make for a generation: that endowed monasteries fed the poor, trained bishops, kept the liturgy in splendor, and that if monks were poor, ‘honourable and noble’ men would not become monks and there would be no one fit to be bishops. The council ruled for Joseph. Nil went back to his skete and died in 1508, asking that his body be thrown in the forest for the beasts. The Russian church took Joseph’s road, and canonized both men.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Child of the Kievan and hesychast lines. Its way of prayer came back in the nineteenth-century elders of Optina. The Old Believers' schism may also have roots in its church. A 1921 history of the Russian dissenters traces it there.

Descended from

  • grew out of Kievan Rus' Christianity, 1054-1240 Documented Major — A ruling of the patriarch's council in Constantinople, from the 1300s, says Kyiv had long held the cathedral of the chief bishop of Rus'. When Kyiv fell into ruin, the bishops moved their seat to Vladimir. Herberstein, an envoy to Moscow in the 1500s, wrote that the seat had since moved on to Moscow.

Direct descendants

  • continued as The Russian Church, Stoglav to the Eve of Nikon Documented Major — Herberstein, the emperor's envoy, came to Moscow in 1517. Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Sergius of Radonezh withdrew into the forests north of Moscow in the 1340s to live alone. Within a generation his disciples had founded dozens more houses, deep in what Russians called the northern Thebaid. The era's sharpest quarrel was over what monks should own. Nil Sorsky and the non-possessors wanted monks poor and silent. Joseph of Volotsk and the possessors wanted monasteries rich enough to serve the people. In the end the monasteries kept their lands. Andrei Rublev painted his Trinity for Sergius's own monastery. It is now the most famous icon ever made.

Legacy

Almost everything that follows in Russian Christianity runs back through here. Sergius's monastery is still at work. It is a lavra, a great monastery, and a spiritual heart of the Russian church. This generation took hesychast prayer from the Greeks. That prayer came back in the elders of the nineteenth century. The quarrel between possessors and non-possessors over the wealth of monks has been fought again in Russian writing on faith ever since. Rublev's Trinity became the most famous icon ever painted.

Experience It Today

  • Trinity Sergius Lavra, Sergiev Posad — Sergius's own foundation, still a working monastery and a UNESCO World Heritage Site

How Sure the Record Is

Moderate-to-rich: lives, skete rules, the possessors/non-possessors polemics; peasant voice thin

  • Epiphanius the Wise, Life of Sergius of Radonezh (c. 1417-18) (primary) — The movement's founding hagiography, by Sergius's own disciple. It survives only as reworked through Pachomius the Serb's redactions. English translation is available only in abridged form, in Zenkovsky's Medieval Russia's Epics, Chronicles, and Tales.
  • Nil Sorsky, The Authentic Writings (trans. David Goldfrank, Cistercian Studies 221, 2008; alt. Maloney, CWS) (primary) — The non-possessor leader's own writings: the Predanie, the Ustav, and four authentic letters - the hesychast tradition transplanted north.
  • Joseph of Volokolamsk, The Monastic Rule of Iosif Volotsky (trans. Goldfrank, Cistercian, 1983) (primary) — The possessor side's own legislation, so both sides of the possessors/non-possessors dispute are represented in their own words, not through summary.
  • The monastic colonization record: northern founders' lives (Kirill of Beloozero; Zosima and Savvatii of Solovki), foundation charters and land acts; Stoglav (1551) retrospectives (primary) — Expansion into the Russian north as the movement's lived, economic form. The Stoglav-era (1551) material reflects back on this period from about a century later. The voice of ordinary peasants appears only indirectly, filtered through monastic land documents.
  • David B. Miller, Saint Sergius of Radonezh, His Trinity Monastery, and the Formation of the Russian Identity (2010) (secondary) — Current standard study of cult and colonization together.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 5, 'Russian piety and Orthodox culture 1380-1589' (Stella Rock) (reference) — This chapter's time span covers this period directly.
