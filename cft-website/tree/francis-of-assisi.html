The High Medieval Era · c. 1205-1226 · Assisi, Italy

Francis of Assisi

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Franciscan Movement.

Also called Brother Francis, Little Brother Francis

Trader's son of Assisi who gave up his family's wealth, served the lepers and took to the roads to preach, and founded the Friars Minor, brothers who were to own nothing, work with their hands and beg when no wage came. His Rule, his Testament and the Canticle of Brother Sun survive in his own words; after his death the order's leaders asked the pope whether the Testament bound them, and for a century his brothers fought over the Rule's poverty.

About This Person

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 4:13

Read along

"The Lord gave me, Brother Francis, to begin to do penance in this way. When I was in sin, it seemed too bitter to me to see lepers. And the Lord himself led me among them, and I showed mercy to them. And as I left them, what had seemed bitter to me was turned into sweetness of soul and body."

So Francis of Assisi remembered it in the testament he dictated in 1226: his parting words to the brothers. To do penance, for him, was to turn his life to God. Soon after, he says, he left the world: his old life of family, trade and money.

A trader's son from Assisi, in central Italy, he gave up his family's wealth in his early twenties, served the lepers, and took to the roads to preach. The brothers who joined him were the Friars Minor, the lesser brothers. They were to own nothing, work with their hands, and beg when no wage came. When a cardinal urged him to become a monk or a hermit instead, he refused.

He was born around 1182. Thomas of Celano, a friar, wrote his Life within three years of his death, for the pope who made him a saint. He says his father dragged him home and used blows and chains to bend him. Then his father brought him before the bishop to give up all he had. Celano says Francis took off every piece of clothing, handed it back, and stood naked, and the bishop wrapped him in his own cloak. Bonaventure, who wrote the order's official Life a generation later, calls it giving up his claim to his father's inheritance.

After that, Celano writes, he lived among the lepers and washed their sores. At the Portiuncula, a little church of St Mary near Assisi, he heard the Gospel where Christ sends out his disciples with no money, no bag and no shoes. He put off his shoes and took to the roads.

With eleven brothers he went to Rome, and Pope Innocent III, the Rule's own opening says, confirmed their short written rule of life in 1209 or 1210.

A bishop at the papal court wrote in 1216 that the brothers went into towns by day and lonely places by night. In 1223 the next pope confirmed a shorter Rule. It said the brothers should go for alms, gifts of food or help begged from door to door. And it said what owning nothing meant: "Let the brothers make nothing their own, not a house, not a place, not anything."

Ill at San Damiano, he made a song of praise in his own Italian, the Canticle of Brother Sun.

Then he dictated the Testament. No brother was to ask Rome for letters of privilege, which grant special rights. Some brothers might not say the daily prayers set by the Rule, or might not be Catholics. They were to be held day and night like prisoners and brought to the cardinal who protected the order. And the Rule and his words were to be read simply, without notes saying how they ought to be understood.

Four years after his death, the order's leaders asked the pope whether the Testament bound them. Pope Gregory IX, that same cardinal, wrote that Francis had meant well, but could not bind them without their leaders' consent. They were not bound. For a century his brothers fought over the Rule's poverty. In 1323 the order's lawyer stood before the pope at Avignon and quoted its words: nothing of their own, neither house nor place. The sisters of San Damiano kept his last wish, that they live always in poverty, in their own Rule.

A later book, said to gather his companions' memories, says a physician told Francis he would die. "Welcome, my Sister Death," he said, and had two companions sing his song, to which, it says, he added a verse.

"Be praised, my Lord, for our Sister Bodily Death, from whom no one living can escape."

He died in October 1226 at the Portiuncula, the little church, Celano says.

Timeline

Dates
Born around 1182, by the date table in A. G. Ferrers Howell's edition of Thomas of Celano, which puts his turn from the world in 1204 to 1206; Celano gives no birth year and says only that until almost his twenty-fifth year he wasted his time. He died in October 1226 at the Portiuncula, on Sunday the fourth of October by Celano's own date; the Library's scan of Howell's table is garbled at that date, so the table is not relied on there. Innocent III confirmed the first Rule on April 23, 1209 by Paschal Robinson, who follows the Bollandists and calls it far more probable than 1210; Howell's table gives 1210, and Robinson reports Sabatier and Wadding for 1210. Honorius III confirmed the Rule of 1223 on November 29, 1223 by Robinson. Howell's table puts the canonization by Gregory IX in 1228. Robinson, from Gregory IX's bull of 1230, says the Testament was published only a few days before his death
Place
Assisi, which Celano says stands on the borders of the valley of Spoleto; San Damiano, a church near Assisi that he repaired and where the manuscript heading of the Canticle puts the song; the Portiuncula, the church of Saint Mary where Celano says he died, in the city of Assisi; Rome; Avignon, for the appeal of 1323. Robinson says everything seems to point to the Testament having been written at the hermitage of the Celle near Cortona in the summer of 1226, though some think it was dictated in the hut nearest the Portiuncula
Role
Founder of the Friars Minor; author of their Rule and his Testament; maker of the Canticle of Brother Sun

Key Works

  • The Rule of the Friars Minor (1223): the Rule that begins by telling the brothers to keep the Gospel by living in obedience, without property and in chastity; chapter 4 forbids them to take coins or money, chapter 5 lets them take what the body needs as the reward of their work, and chapter 6 says they are to own nothing, no house and no place, and to go confidently for alms. Robinson says the first Rule, which Innocent III confirmed, has not come down to us, that the longer Rule often dated 1221 is the same Rule after twelve years of change, and that the Rule of 1223 is the same Rule again, shorter and more orderly. Sabatier held the two Rules to be opposites; Robinson and the Quaracchi editors answer him. The earlier Rule, in chapter 9, says the brothers should rejoice when they live among mean and despised persons, the poor and the weak, the sick and the lepers, and those who beg by the wayside
  • The Testament (1226): his memory of how it began, with the lepers, the brothers, the one patched tunic and the work with his hands, and his commands: no brother is to ask any letter from the Roman curia, brothers who will not say the Office by the Rule or are not Catholics are to be held day and night like prisoners and brought to the cardinal who protects the order, and the Rule and these words are to be read together at every chapter and understood simply and purely, without glosses. He says it is not another Rule but a reminder, a warning and an exhortation to keep the Rule better. It also carries his greeting, The Lord give thee peace
  • Canticle of Brother Sun (the Praises of the Creatures): a song of praise to God for the sun, the creatures and Sister Bodily Death, made in his own Italian when he was ill at San Damiano. Robinson says it is the only one of his songs in Italian known to survive, dates it to the close of 1225, and accepts it as authentic. The Mirror of Perfection says two strophes were added later, one for a feud between the bishop and the magistrates of Assisi and one when he knew death was near

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • In 1230, four years after his death, Gregory IX answered the order's envoys and its minister general, who asked whether the Testament bound the brothers. He wrote that Francis had meant well but could not bind the brothers without the consent of the ministers, whom it touched, or bind his successor; the brothers were not bound to keep that command. Gregory had known Francis and, as Cardinal Ugolino, was the Lord of Ostia the Testament names as protector.
  • On 14 January 1323 Bonagratia of Bergamo, procurator general of the order, appealed in person to John XXII at Avignon and quoted the Rule's opening and its words that the brothers should have nothing of their own, no house and no place; he also quoted Gregory IX's answer of 1230 on use without ownership.
  • Thomas of Celano wrote the First Life between 1228 and February 1229 by commission of Gregory IX, and the Second Life between 1244 and 1247 at the request of the minister general Crescentius of Jesi; Bonaventure, minister general, wrote the Legenda maior in 1260 to 1263.
  • Clare's Rule, which Innocent IV confirmed in the bull Solet annuere of August 1253, carries the form of life Francis wrote for her sisters, with his promise of care, and his last wish that they live always in poverty.
  • The Canticle survives in the manuscripts in his own Italian.
  • Jacques de Vitry, in his history of the West, says there was hardly a Christian province without brothers of the order.

How they're remembered

  • Jacques de Vitry, bishop of Acre, wrote from Genoa in October 1216 that at the papal court, among much that he disliked, he found one comfort: rich and worldly people of both sexes, leaving everything for Christ, were called Friars Minor and were held in great reverence by the pope and cardinals; by day the brothers went into cities and villages, by night they went back to lonely places, and the women lived together near the cities and by the work of their hands. He does not name Francis or Clare there.
  • In the same history Jacques de Vitry writes that we saw the founder of this order, a simple man, who went to the camp of the sultan of Egypt at Damietta and preached to him for some days; the sultan, he says, had him escorted back and asked him to pray that God would reveal to him the law and faith that pleased him most.
  • Celano writes that near the end Francis wished to return to the service of lepers and to the first beginnings of humility, and Bonaventure tells the same.
  • Celano writes that at La Verna, two years before he died, after a vision of a man like a seraph with six wings fixed to a cross, marks of nails began to appear in his hands and feet.
  • Ubertino of Casale, a leader of the strict friars writing in 1310, reports the pope's judgment that the brothers were not bound to keep the Testament's command, because he could not bind his successor, an equal having no command over an equal. Ubertino says he does not dissent from the pope, but that Francis meant by it to explain his intention in the Rule.

Open Questions

  • Layers of witness. His own writings are the one layer no one disputes. Thomas of Celano's First Life was written within three years of his death by commission of Gregory IX; his Second Life, of 1244 to 1247, adds scenes the first lacks; Bonaventure wrote his Life in 1260 to 1263, a generation after the death. The Mirror of Perfection, which Evans translates, is attributed on its title page to Brother Leo; its early date and that attribution are Sabatier's, and the Library's file calls both contested.
  • His birth. No year is given by Celano; around 1182 is the editor's date in Howell's table.
  • The lepers. In the Testament the Lord led him among the lepers and afterwards he waited a little and left the world, so the lepers come first. Celano tells him going to serve the lepers after the scene before the bishop, with only a kiss given to one leper while he was still in the habit of the world; Bonaventure tells the meeting on horseback and the kiss and says the leper then vanished, and Robinson notes that some manuscripts of the Testament read that he made a sojourn with them where the printed Latin has he showed mercy to them.
  • The scene before the bishop. Celano says his father brought him before the bishop to give up all his property; Francis took off all his clothes, said nothing, and the bishop covered him with his own mantle. Bonaventure says the father wished him to resign his claim unto his father's inheritance, and puts a speech in his mouth; the Second Life of Celano gives him a like speech, 'our Father who art in heaven', when he gives back the money. No source here gives a motive for the stripping. The father's blows and chains, the mud and stones thrown by the townsmen, and the mother who loosed his chains are Celano's account.
  • The speaking crucifix. The Second Life of Celano, of 1244 to 1247, says the painted image of Christ crucified at San Damiano moved its lips and told him to repair the house. The Testament does not mention it, and Celano's First Life tells the repair of San Damiano with no voice.
  • The Rule confirmed by Innocent III. The Rule's own opening says Innocent conceded and confirmed the life to Francis and his brothers; the Quaracchi heading adds that he confirmed it without a bull, and Robinson says viva voce. Its first text is lost. The year is 1209 by Robinson and the Bollandists, and 1210 by Howell's table, Sabatier and Wadding. Celano says a cardinal, the bishop of Sabina, urged Francis to become a monk or a hermit and that Francis refused.
  • What the Rule of 1223 says about begging. Chapter 6 says only that the brothers go confidently for alms. That they beg when the price of labor is not given is the Testament's condition.
  • The Canticle. The sun line follows the Assisi manuscript as Sabatier prints it, which has the sun as the day; other manuscripts in Sabatier's apparatus have a verb, bringing the day, and one has another word again, and Robinson's English reads the sun as the one that lights up the day. The word for in the death verse can also be read through. The Mirror of Perfection says he made the song after being told of the kingdom promised him in his sickness, and that for sixty days or more he could not bear the light; the Second Life of Celano also puts the song after that promise. Robinson's date of late 1225 is the editor's, and Celano's First Life mentions treatment for the infirmity of his eyes late in his life. The death verse and the words Welcome, my Sister Death rest on the Mirror of Perfection and, for the greeting, on the Second Life; Celano's First Life instead tells him carried to the Portiuncula and breaking into the psalm I cried to the Lord with my voice.
  • The Testament. Francis wished it read with the Rule, without glosses; Gregory IX ruled that its command did not bind. Ubertino of Casale says Francis had given up his office of leadership two years or more before, as Brother Leo testifies, so that he could not command, and that he meant the Testament to explain his intention in the Rule. The Assisi manuscript reads without gloss where the Quaracchi text has purely. Where it was dictated is uncertain, as the place note says.
  • The lawyer of 1323. Bonagratia quoted the Rule's words as fratres nihil habeant seu sibi approprient nec domum nec locum nec aliquam rem, which differs a little from the Rule's own text, and he raised a question he said was old, from the beginning of the order's institution and approval. He spoke for the order.
  • His leadership. Howell's table says Francis resigned his office of Minister General in September 1220 and that the order then passed under the control of the Holy See.
  • The canonization. Richard of San Germano, a contemporary, says in one passage that Gregory IX went from Perugia to Assisi in July and canonized Francis for two miracles done openly on a blind man and a lame man, and in another that in June, at Perugia, by his brothers' counsel, he decided to enroll Francis among the saints.
  • Jacques de Vitry. His letter of 1216 does not name Francis or Clare. The Damietta account is from his history, printed in 1597 in a long-s type the Library's scan reads imperfectly; his letter of 1220 from Damietta is not held.
  • Not held in the Library: the Legend of the Three Companions, the Assisi Compilation, the full text of Solet annuere of 1223, Brother Elias's letter on his death, and Bonaventure's Latin. Quo elongati is held in full in Sabatier's appendix.
  • The Mirror of Perfection: The attribution to Brother Leo and the early date are Sabatier's, and the Library's file calls both contested.
