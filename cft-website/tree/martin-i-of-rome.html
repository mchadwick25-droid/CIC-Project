Martin I of Rome

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Byzantine Imperial Church (Heraclian & Iconoclast Eras).

Also called Pope Martin I, Martinus papa

Bishop of Rome who held the Lateran synod of 649 against the teaching of one will in Christ and the emperor's Typos. He was seized, tried at Constantinople and exiled to Cherson, where he died.

About This Person

In June 653, Pope Martin I lay sick before the altar of the Lateran church in Rome. Soldiers came in with lances, swords and bows ready. The candles fell under their weapons, he later wrote, like leaves in a winter wind. He gave himself up. He judged it better to die ten times, he said, than to let anyone's blood be shed.

He told the story in a letter to his friend Theodore. Martin was born at Todi in Tuscany and became bishop of Rome in 649.

The emperor Constans had issued an edict, the Typos, forbidding anyone to say that Christ had one will or two. Martin's own speech to his synod blames Paul, patriarch of Constantinople, for urging the edict. Paul had also overturned the Roman see's altar in that city, so its envoys could not say Mass. In October 649 Martin gathered his synod at the Lateran. Its bishops condemned Cyrus of Alexandria and Sergius, Pyrrhus and Paul, patriarchs of Constantinople, for teaching one will.

After Olympius, governor of Italy, failed to seize him, the Book of the Popes says, his successor, Theodore Calliopas, took him. Martin wrote that he spent a year on the island of Naxos.

An account sent to the West by one of his clergy says he reached Constantinople on 17 September. After ninety-three days in prison he was carried to his trial, gravely ill. Witnesses swore that he had plotted with Olympius. When Martin began to speak of the Typos, the prefect Troilus cut him off. Do not bring the faith in here, Troilus said; the charge was rebellion.

Before a crowd he was stripped of his priestly cloak and led through the city in irons. Spared death, he sailed for Cherson the next spring, on Holy Thursday. There, he wrote, people spoke of bread but never saw it. His own church of Saint Peter had forgotten him, he complained. He prayed that God would keep its people firm in the faith, and above all the shepherd who now led them.

The clergyman says Martin died on 16 September and was buried outside Cherson. Bede says a Roman abbot sent by Pope Agatho brought "the decision of the synod of the blessed Pope Martin and 105 bishops" to Wearmouth.

The Lord would care for his poor body, Martin wrote, in endless trouble or in a little relief. The Lord was near, so he need not be anxious.

Key Works

  • Two letters to Theodore: written after his seizure. In the first he denies ever sending letters or money to the Saracens. He gave only a little alms to some servants of God, none of it sent to the Saracens. He will defend the faith living and dying, he says. The second tells of the soldiers in the Lateran church and the voyage
  • Two letters from Cherson: to a friend at Constantinople. They tell of hunger in the land and of friends and a church that had forgotten him. In the second he prays for the shepherd who now led Rome. Louis Duchesne dates them to about June and early September
  • Acts of the Lateran synod of 649: the synod that condemned Cyrus of Alexandria and Sergius, Pyrrhus and Paul of Constantinople. The Book of the Popes and Bede count 105 bishops. The Book of the Popes copies its account of the Typos from Martin's own speech there, Louis Duchesne says

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Bede says the bishops of Britain met at Heathfield under Theodore, archbishop of Canterbury, in the eighth indiction. They received the synod held at Rome in the time of the blessed Pope Martin.
  • John, archchanter of Saint Peter's and abbot of Saint Martin's, had lately come from Rome on Pope Agatho's orders. Bede says he brought the decision of Martin's synod to be copied at Wearmouth.
  • Pope Agatho and a Roman synod of 125 bishops wrote to the emperors, for the legates they sent to the council of Constantinople in 680. The letter calls the faith of two wills the faith set out in a council over which Pope Martin presided, as Henry R. Percival sums it up.

How they're remembered

  • The Roman Breviary keeps him on 12 November as "Martin, Pope of Rome, Martyr". Its martyrology says he died "worn out by his sufferings for the Catholic faith's sake."
  • The Breviary says his body was later brought to Rome and buried in the Church of Saints Sylvester and Martin. An 1843 English Roman Missal gives a Mass for him on the same day.
  • The clergyman who wrote of his trial calls him a confessor and martyr of recent days. The Book of the Popes says that he works many wonders to this day.
  • Severinus Binius, in notes Mansi reprints, quotes Pope Gregory II on his tomb at Cherson. The city, the whole north and its people come to it, Gregory says, and are healed.

Open Questions

  • Most of his story comes from his own side: his letters, and the account sent west by one of his clergy. The charges against him are known through that account.
  • The Book of the Popes adds that the sword-bearer sent to kill him at Mass was struck blind. The Roman Breviary's lessons repeat the story.
  • The day and year of his death. The clergyman's account gives 16 September, in the fourteenth indiction. The Roman Breviary's lesson gives 12 November 655.
  • The Breviary's own note, after Alban Butler, says he died on 16 September and calls 12 November the day his relics came to Rome. Binius, in Mansi, gives 12 November 654.
  • The year he reached Constantinople. The Breviary's added dates give September 655, and send him to the Crimea in May "645". Louis Duchesne puts the arrival in 654 and the sailing for Cherson in March 655.
  • Whether he backed Olympius, the emperor's governor in Italy, against the emperor. The witnesses at his trial swore so, and William Dudley Foulke calls Olympius's rising one Martin supported.
  • Louis Duchesne thinks pope and governor stayed on good terms longer than the Book of the Popes implies. Martin answered at his trial that he could not have stood against a man who commanded the army of Italy. Nor had he made him governor, he said.
  • His election. Foulke says it lacked the emperor's confirmation. Theodore Calliopas, the governor who arrested him, handed the clergy a decree. As Martin reports it, the decree said he had seized the see irregularly and without law.
  • The size of his synod. The Book of the Popes, Bede and the Breviary give 105 bishops. Theophanes the Confessor gives 150 in Migne's Latin; Severinus Binius, quoting him, reads 110.
  • Who followed him. Louis Duchesne names Eugene, and Foulke says Eugenius was chosen while Martin still lived. Theophanes says Agatho was made pope once Martin was exiled.
