Cogitosus

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Medieval Irish Christianity.

Writer of a Latin Life of Brigid of Kildare. He describes her great church at Kildare and calls her house the head of almost all the Irish churches. Newport White takes him for the father whom Muirchu names in his Life of Patrick.

About This Person

In the great church of Kildare, in Ireland, two tombs stood on either side of the altar. Brigid lay in one and Bishop Conleath in the other, under crowns of gold and silver hung above them. Painted boards adorned the church, and one wall bore pictures. The bishop came to the altar by one door, and the abbess with her virgins and faithful widows by another. So, the writer says, a great people in its ranks prayed with one mind to God.

The writer was Cogitosus. His Latin Life of Brigid of Kildare is a saint's Life, mostly miracles. Migne's collection of the Latin Fathers prints him under the year 591. Jacques Basnage, whose note Migne prints, thought him much later. Cogitosus did not see Brigid alive, Basnage argues, and the painted images he describes belong to later centuries.

The brothers compelled him to write, Cogitosus says, and the task was beyond his small skill. He tells a few things out of many, handed down by elders and the most learned. He has seen miracles with his own eyes, he adds, in the monastery where her body lies.

He makes Brigid's house the head of almost all the Irish churches, its jurisdiction reaching across Ireland from sea to sea. Brigid, he says, called a hermit from the wilderness to govern the church with her as bishop. When Cogitosus wrote, an archbishop and an abbess still ruled it in unbroken succession.

In his Life of Patrick, Muirchu names his own father Cogitosus, on Bishop Graves's correction of the Latin. Newport White, Muirchu's translator, takes that father for Brigid's biographer. Since White puts Muirchu in the 690s, that would place Cogitosus before about 700. Whitley Stokes, in an index, dates Cogitosus between 800 and 835.

He signs the Life asking the brothers to pray for "Cogitoso nepote culpabili", Cogitosus the guilty nepos. Nepos can mean grandson or nephew, and Basnage could not say in what sense he used it.

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • He says that Brigid called a hermit to rule the church at Kildare with her as bishop. When he wrote, an archbishop and an abbess still ruled it in unbroken succession.
  • He describes her church, with the tombs of Brigid and Bishop Conleath on either side of the altar. It had painted boards, and one door for the bishop and another for the abbess.
  • In his Life of Patrick, Muirchu says no boat but his father Cogitosus's had sailed the sea of sacred story before him.

How they're remembered

  • Basnage, whose note Migne prints, writes that the Life teems with miracles and that its style shows the barbarism of its age.
  • Basnage marvels that a virgin summoned a hermit to rule the church with her as bishop. He is astonished too that, as Cogitosus tells it, she sowed the word among all by custom, and says these things go against the laws.

Open Questions

  • His Life is a saint's Life, mostly miracles, and he gives no year for himself or for Brigid.
  • His date. Migne prints the Life under the year 591. Basnage writes that he places Cogitosus where he does following others, not on his own view, for he believes him much later.
  • Basnage argues that Cogitosus did not see Brigid alive, since he saw only the miracles in her church. He adds that painted images in an Irish church belong to later centuries.
  • Muirchu's father. The name in Muirchu's preface rests on Bishop Graves's correction of "cognito si". Newport White calls it generally accepted and takes the father for Brigid's biographer, which would place him before about 700.
  • Whitley Stokes, in an index to his Tripartite Life, dates Cogitosus 800 to 835. Both his index entries, "Cogitosus" and "Coguitosus", point to the page of Muirchu's preface. That page is missing from the copy used here, so his reasoning cannot be read.
  • Nepos. He signs himself "nepote culpabili". Basnage notes that he does not call himself Brigid's nepos. He says the sense is as uncertain to him as to Henricus Canisius, whose collection Basnage re-edited.
