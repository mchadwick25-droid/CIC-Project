The Hesychast Revival - Paisius & the Philokalia

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:06

Read along

In 1746 a young Ukrainian monk named Paisius Velichkovsky arrived on Mount Athos and began working through its monastery libraries for the old Greek texts on inner prayer that almost nobody was reading any more. He gathered a community, moved with it to Moldavia, and at Neamț built a translation workshop where monks turned those fathers into Slavonic; meanwhile on Athos, Macarius of Corinth and Nicodemus the Hagiorite assembled the same material into an anthology printed at Venice in 1782 under the title Philokalia, 'the love of the beautiful'. Paisius's Slavonic version, the Dobrotolubiye, was printed in Moscow in 1793, and the effect was enormous and largely unplanned: a body of monastic teaching on the Jesus Prayer and the watching of one's own heart, gathered for specialists, went into general circulation and reached ordinary lay readers.

Documented Stories

The Manuscripts at the Skete of St Basil

Paisius Velichkovsky came to Mount Athos in 1746 at the age of twenty-four, in search of an elder who could teach him the prayer of the heart. He found none. In the account he wrote late in life to a monk of the Sophroniev hermitage, he describes searching the Slavonic books for the fathers who taught the Jesus Prayer, Hesychios, Philotheos, Gregory of Sinai, Symeon the New Theologian, and finding the copies so corrupt that sentences made no sense; he set out to correct them from the Greek and found that the Greek monks did not know the books existed, and told him that the originals had been lost with the fall of the empire. He searched for years. Then, at the skete of St Basil, a poor settlement of monks from Caesarea in Cappadocia, he was shown a manuscript copy of Peter Damascene, and the monk who kept it told him the skete had copied the fathers from the library of Vatopedi, where the old books lay unread; there, in time, he found the rest. He began copying and translating them himself, with the disciples who had gathered around him, first in a cell, then in a skete, then, when there were sixty of them, in Moldavia from 1763. The workshop at Neamț, where he died in 1794, had by then produced the Slavonic Dobrotoliubie, printed at Moscow in 1793, and the pattern of elder and disciple that Russia's nineteenth-century monasteries inherited from it.

The Kollyvades Driven from the Mountain

In 1754 the monks of the skete of St Anne on Athos, building a new church, moved the memorial services for their benefactors from Saturday, the day the church's tradition assigned to the dead, to Sunday, because the workmen were paid on Saturdays and the boiled wheat, kollyva, blessed at the services took the morning. A party in the skete objected that Sunday was the day of resurrection and no day for mourning, and were mocked as Kollyvades, the kollyva people. The quarrel widened as the traditionalists took up a second cause, that of receiving communion often, weekly or oftener, against the Athonite custom of a few times a year, and a third, the return to the fathers' texts as against the customs of the last two centuries. Fights broke out in the sketes; the Kollyvades were beaten and expelled from their cells. In 1772 a patriarchal synod tried to settle the memorial question by allowing both days, and in 1776 a synod under Patriarch Sophronios II condemned the Kollyvades outright, deposed and excommunicated Athanasios Parios, and sent the party into exile on the islands, where they founded monasteries and schools on Hydra, Skiathos, Paros and Chios and where much of the Greek revival of the next century began. Macarius and Nicodemus compiled the Philokalia in these years of disgrace and printed it at Venice in 1782. In 1819 the patriarchate reversed itself and blessed frequent communion. The book the losers made became the tradition's most widely read text.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 1746 a young monk named Paisius Velichkovsky searched the libraries of the monasteries on Mount Athos. He was looking for old Greek teaching on inward prayer that almost nobody read anymore. At Neamț in Moldavia he set his monks to putting it into Slavonic. The Greek texts were printed as the Philokalia at Venice in 1782. Paisius's own Slavonic version came out at Moscow in 1793. The book carried a monk's teaching on the Jesus Prayer out to ordinary readers. Paisius's own disciples, in Russia, trained the first of the Optina elders.

Legacy

This is one of the clearest cases anywhere of a book changing a church. The Philokalia is still in print and still read — the standard English translation has been appearing volume by volume since 1979. In Russia it fed the elders of Optina, who put out Paisius's life and writings at Moscow in 1847. The Jesus Prayer is now practiced far outside the monasteries the texts were written in. Neamț Monastery still stands and still functions, with Paisius's relics in it; he was canonized in 1988.

Experience It Today
