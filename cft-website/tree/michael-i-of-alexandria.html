About This Person

On the bank of the Nile, the caliph Marwan faced the patriarch Michael. "Art thou the chief of the enemies of our religion?" he asked. "I am not the chief of wicked men, but of good men," Michael answered. Marwan had the hair of his beard pulled out and thrown into the river. "I saw it with my own eyes floating on the water," writes John the deacon, who stood near him.

Michael I of Alexandria, also called Khail, was a priest at the church of Saint Macarius. B. Evetts's English of the History of the Patriarchs, a later Arabic history of the Coptic church, sets his years at 744 to 768. Michael's life in it is told by an eyewitness who shared his prisons. He calls himself "John, the sinner," and calls Moses, bishop of Wasim, his spiritual father.

Under the governor Abd al-Malik, son of Musa, Michael was jailed for the taxes on his churches. Letters from Egypt reached Cyriacus, the Christian king of Nubia, while Michael was in prison. The governor then seized Michael again, John says. The king marched north with a hundred thousand horsemen. Michael was freed and wrote the king letters of peace, and the army turned back.

When Marwan came to Egypt, his general seized Michael in Alexandria and demanded money. "There is nothing in my church; therefore I give myself instead of the money," Michael said. Led out to be beheaded, he drew his cap over his face and stretched out his neck. The general spared him. Later he lay in chains at Giza until Marwan was killed.

When the caliph made a bishop patriarch of Antioch, Michael would not accept him. Sword, fire, lions, exile or captivity did not trouble him, he said. He had signed with his own hand a ban "that no bishop shall become patriarch."

He died on the 16th of the Coptic month Barmahat, after twenty-three and a half years, by a record in the library of Saint Macarius.

The Arabic Synaxarium is the Coptic church's book of saints' days. In its notice of a bishop who shared his sufferings, it calls Michael a martyr who shed no blood.

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • He won back the church of Saint Mennas at Mareotis from the Melkites before a judge. The founders' names on its pillars proved his case, the History says.
  • He ordered by letter that none of the faithful should take communion unless fasting, the History says.
  • He restored the church of Alexandria, stripped in Alexander II's time, and built churches east and west of the town.
  • He would not accept Isaac of Harran, whom the caliph made patriarch of Antioch, because no bishop should become patriarch.

How they're remembered

  • The Arabic Synaxarium, the Coptic church's book of saints' days, keeps him on the 16th of Barmahat in René Basset's French. It makes the king who marched for him a king of Abyssinia.
  • The Ethiopic Synaxarium, in E. A. Wallis Budge's English, keeps him on the 16th of Magabit.
  • The Arabic Synaxarium's notice of Masis, bishop of Ousim, who shared his sufferings, calls Michael a martyr without the shedding of blood. One manuscript reads the bishop's name as Mosas.
  • Eusèbe Renaudot, a Catholic scholar writing in Latin in 1713, mocks the life's many miracles. He calls the king's march nearer fable than history.

Open Questions

  • His life rests on the History of the Patriarchs, a later Arabic history of the Coptic church. Its writer, John the deacon, was an eyewitness. He calls Moses, bishop of Wasim, his spiritual father.
  • Who marched for him. John calls Cyriacus king of the Nubians and of al-Mukurrah. The synaxaria make him a king of Abyssinia or Ethiopia. Renaudot thinks he was probably Ethiopian.
  • The army. The History gives a hundred thousand horsemen, a hundred thousand horses and a hundred thousand camels. Budge's Ethiopic gives ten thousand horses.
  • His years. B. Evetts's heading gives 744 to 768, and the title of Evetts's volume gives 766. Basset's synaxarium brackets 743 to 767. Renaudot argues for an ordination about 741 or 742.
  • Who John was. He wore a monk's habit and once spoke of the villages of his diocese. No held line says he was made a bishop.
  • His successor. Michael's life does not name him. Renaudot names Mennas, a monk of Saint Macarius and one of his disciples.
  • The governor's name. The History calls him Abd al-Malik, son of Musa, son of Nasir. A book on the churches of Egypt, under the name of Abu Salih, names Saladin. B. Evetts, its translator, calls that a copyist's slip.
