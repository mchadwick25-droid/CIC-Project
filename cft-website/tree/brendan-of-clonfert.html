The Age of Monks and Empires · 6th c. · Ireland, Clonfert

Brendan of Clonfert

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Insular / Irish Monastic Christianity.

Known for
Irish founder of monasteries in the time of Columba of Iona. Adomnan of Iona names him as Brendan Mocu Alti, and later writers take him to be the founder of Clonfert. Lives written centuries later make him the voyager who sought the Land of Promise.
Also called
Brendan Mocu Alti, Brendan Mocualti, Brenainn son of Finnlug, Brenainn maccu Altai, Brendanus, Brandan
Dates
No source used here from near his own time gives a year. Adomnan places him in the lifetime of Columba of Iona. Wentworth Huyshe, Adomnan's translator, gives born 482, founded Clonfert 559 and died 577, without naming his source. Roderic O'Flaherty, as John O'Donovan quotes him, also gives 577
Place
Ireland; the island of Hinba, where Adomnan says he visited Columba of Iona; Clonfert, which John O'Donovan places in county Galway. The Lismore Life says he was of the Ciarraige Luachra, of the Altraige Caille. Wentworth Huyshe says Kerry was the chief district of his kin
Role
Abbot and founder of monasteries; founder of Clonfert, as Wentworth Huyshe and later writers identify him

About This Person

Four founders of monasteries once came over from Ireland to visit Columba of Iona on the island of Hinba, Adomnan of Iona writes. While Columba celebrated Mass at their request, one of them, Brendan Mocu Alti, saw a blazing globe of fire over his head. It rose like a pillar until the rite was done, as Brendan later told two of the others.

Adomnan was abbot of Iona from 679 to 704, his translator Wentworth Huyshe says, and Huyshe puts Columba's death in 597. Huyshe takes this Brendan to be Brendan of Clonfert, founder of that Irish monastery. Adomnan names him once more. A devout man, Aidan son of Fergno, is said to have served him for twelve years. Huyshe gives Brendan's years as born 482, founded Clonfert 559, and died 577, without saying where they come from.

Adomnan also tells of a second Brendan, the founder of Birr, whom Huyshe warns must be kept apart from the first.

Most of what is told of Brendan of Clonfert comes from Lives written centuries later. The Irish Life in the Book of Lismore calls him Brenainn son of Finnlug. Whitley Stokes, its editor, says the book was compiled in the later 1400s. In that Life an angel promises Brendan the Land of Promise. He sails for years, keeps Easter on a whale's back and is shown the gate of hell. Then the nun Ita, his foster mother, tells him he will never find that holy land in boats of dead hides. So he builds a great ship, and at last he reaches it.

A Latin Life printed by W. J. Rees calls him the father of nearly three thousand monks, and breaks off as he plans his voyage. Rees thinks the Lives in his book were probably compiled about the twelfth century.

The Lismore Life likens him to Noah. As Noah raised the ark over the flood, it says, Brendan will raise his monks and households over the fire of Judgment Day.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The Annals of the Four Masters, in John O'Donovan's English, name bishops of "Clonfert-Brendan" in the volume held here, which begins in 1172.
  • Roderic O'Flaherty, as John O'Donovan quotes him, says the cathedral of Enaghdun was dedicated to Saint Brendan, who died there.
  • The Book of Lismore keeps a story set at Clonfert, which Whitley Stokes calls the story of the harper and the bird-like angel. In it Brenainn maccu Altai has not smiled or heard worldly music for seven years.

How they're remembered

  • The Catalogue of the Saints of Ireland, which Hugh Williams dates about 730, names two Brendans among the second order of Irish saints. It does not say which two.
  • The Stowe Missal, which Hugh Williams dates in its present form about 750, names a Brendinus twice among the priests it remembers.
  • The Lismore Life likens him to a long line of figures from the Bible and the church. They run from Abraham to Gregory of Rome, and last to Noah. As Noah raised the ark over the flood, it says, Brendan will raise his monks and households over the fire of Judgment Day.
  • John Mirk, an English preacher, tells in a sermon for the feast of Matthias how Saint Brandan, at sea, found Judas sitting on a stone. Judas was let out of hell's heat at set times, the story says.
  • A Latin Life of David of Wales that W. J. Rees prints has Barre ride David's horse over the sea and meet Brendan living a wondrous life on a sea creature.

What Is Uncertain

  • Adomnan of Iona is the earliest writer used here who names him, and he does so twice, in passing. Most of what is told of Brendan comes from Lives written centuries later. Whitley Stokes says the Book of Lismore was compiled in the later 1400s. He calls its language mixed, with Old Irish forms beside late Middle and even Modern ones. W. J. Rees thinks the Lives in his book were probably compiled about the twelfth century.
  • Which Brendan. Wentworth Huyshe takes Adomnan's Brendan Mocu Alti to be the founder of Clonfert, and Mocu Alti to be his clan name. A late Lismore story joins the two, setting Brenainn maccu Altai at Clonfert. The Lismore Life calls him son of Finnlug, of the Altraige, and Rees's Latin Life son of Finloc, grandson of Altus. Adomnan also tells of Brendan of Birr, who greeted Columba at a synod and whose death Columba saw in a vision. Huyshe says the two must be kept apart, and gives about 573 for the death of Brendan of Birr. John O'Donovan, from the Calendar of the O'Clerys, gives 572.
  • Two Brendans in the early lists. The Catalogue of the Saints of Ireland and the Stowe Missal each name two Brendans and do not say which is which.
  • His years. Huyshe gives 482 for his birth, 559 for the founding of Clonfert and 577 for his death, and names no source. Roderic O'Flaherty, as O'Donovan quotes him, gives 577 and says he died at Enaghdun. No annal with his death year is among the sources used here. The Annales Cambriae record only the death of a Brendan "Byror", whom John Williams ab Ithel, who edited them, takes for Brendan of Birr.
  • The voyage. The Land of Promise is told by the late Lives. The Lismore Life has him sail five years, then seven, in three ships of hides. At last he reaches the land in a great ship built in Connaught at Ita's word. Rees's Latin Life breaks off as the voyage is planned; Rees notes that the manuscript ends abruptly. Huyshe quotes a Latin Life of Brendan on how the voyagers built their boat of wicker and ox hides.
  • His feast. Huyshe gives his day as 16 May, and the calendar of the York prymer keeps Brandan, bishop, on that day. O'Flaherty, as O'Donovan quotes him, says he died on the 16th of May. The Lismore Life, in Whitley Stokes's English, places his festival on the seventh of the calends of June, which is 26 May.
  • The link with Wales. W. J. Rees printed the Latin Life in the appendix of his Lives of the Cambro-British Saints. He says he put it there with the Lives of Aidus and Tathan. These saints, he says, were natives of Ireland who had passed much of their time in Wales. A Latin Life of Brendan that Hugh Williams quotes, after Warren, has Brendan at Gildas's monastery, singing Mass from a book in Greek letters.
