The Church of the East under the Mongols

Central Asia, Persia and China, c. 1220 to 1317 — the church Europe called Nestorian, briefly favored at the Mongol court, whose head had walked there from China and whose ambassador reached Rome and Paris.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:07

Read along

The Church of the East had spread far into Central Asia centuries earlier, and when the Mongols swept west a good many of the peoples they absorbed — the Kerait and the Öngüd among them — were already Christian in this tradition, as were several of the great khans' wives and mothers. For a few decades the church that Latin Europe called Nestorian had extraordinary access: tolerated and sometimes favored at the Mongol court, and in 1281 a monk who had traveled the whole width of Asia from China was elected its catholicos as Yahballaha III. His teacher, Rabban Bar Sauma, had set out with him on pilgrimage toward Jerusalem and ended up instead as the Ilkhan's ambassador to the West, reaching Rome and the courts of France and England and writing the journey down — one of the very few medieval descriptions of Europe by a traveler from Asia. Yahballaha died in 1317, and the favor did not survive him.

Documented Stories

The Cardinals Ask Rabban Sauma About the Holy Spirit

Rabban Sauma was an Öngüd Turk born near Beijing who had set out with his pupil Markos on pilgrimage to Jerusalem, been stopped by war, and seen Markos elected catholicos of the Church of the East as Yahballaha III. In 1287 the Ilkhan Arghun sent him west to propose a joint campaign against the Mamluks. The History written by a fellow churchman from his own notes describes the journey: Constantinople, Naples, then Rome, where the pope had just died and twelve cardinals were meeting to elect another. Then they asked what he believed about the Spirit. Sauma set out the eastern creed — the Spirit proceeds from the Father — and the cardinals pressed him with the Latin doctrine. He answered that he had come as an ambassador, ‘not to dispute about the faith but to receive a blessing and to speak of the affairs of the king’; if they wanted a theological debate, they might come to Baghdad. He went on to Paris, where the king of France showed him the university, and to Gascony, where Edward I of England received communion from his hands at Bordeaux — a Nestorian monk giving the sacrament to a Latin king. The History is one of the few medieval descriptions of Europe by an Asian, and it records the Church of the East meeting Rome as an equal for a week.

The Citadel of Erbil

The favor the Church of the East had enjoyed under the Mongols ended with the conversion of the Ilkhan Ghazan to Islam in 1295. Yahballaha III, the catholicos, was old and had already been beaten and hung upside down by a mob at Maragha; in 1310 the Christians of Erbil in Iraq, where the church had a strong community, held the citadel against a Kurdish and Arab force after clashes in the town. The History of Yahballaha, whose author was with the catholicos, tells what happened over the following months. Yahballaha went to the Mongol governor and to the Ilkhan Öljeitü’s court to plead; the Christians in the citadel would not surrender to the besiegers on the governor’s word, having seen what happened to those who did; the governor’s troops joined the siege. On 1 July 1310 the citadel was stormed and, the History says, the defenders and their families were killed, the women and children carried off, and the churches of the lower town destroyed. Yahballaha, who had been held in the town during the assault, ‘wept and could not be comforted’; he went back to Maragha, gave up all further dealings with the court, and died in 1317. The History ends with his burial and a lament for a church that had been, within living memory, the confidant of khans. The Church of the East survived in the Hakkari mountains and in India; its Mongol century ended at Erbil.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

When the Mongols swept west, many of the peoples they absorbed — the Kerait and the Öngüd among them — were already Christian in the Church of the East tradition, as were several of the great khans' wives and mothers, giving the church real access at the Mongol court. In 1281 a monk who had traveled the whole width of Asia from China was elected catholicos as Yahballaha III, while his teacher Rabban Bar Sauma ended up as the Ilkhan's ambassador to the West, reaching Rome and the courts of France and England and writing the journey down. Yahballaha died in 1317, and the church's favor at court did not survive him.

Legacy

The Church of the East still exists, but this was its last age of long reach. Ghazan, the Mongol ruler of Persia, had turned to Islam in 1295, while Yahballaha was still head of the church. Then the fourteenth century brought disaster, above all Timur's wars. A church that once ran from Cyprus to China shrank to a mountain remnant in Hakkari. Its traces now are mostly in the ground: Syriac gravestones across Central Asia, and Christian tomb carvings in China's port cities. The account of Bar Sauma's embassy is still the most remarkable thing this age produced.
