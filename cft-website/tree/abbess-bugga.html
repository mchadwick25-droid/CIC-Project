Abbess Bugga

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Anglo-Saxon Christianity.

Known for
An English abbess and friend of Boniface of Mainz. Her own letter of thanks to him survives. She governed a house of women, went to Rome to pray, and met Boniface there.
Also called
Heaburg, called Bugga (her mother's letter), Bugga

Role
Abbess; letter-writer to Boniface

About This Person

Around 720, an Englishwoman wrote to Boniface of Mainz, the English missionary, to give thanks for his success. His letter had told her that Rathbod, king of the Frisians, "that enemy of the Catholic Church", had been laid low. With the messenger she sent "fifty shillings and an altar pall".

Her greeting names her "Bugga, a lowly handmaiden". Another letter, from her mother Eangyth, an abbess, names her as "her only daughter Heaburg, called Bugga". Both are in Edward Kylie's English translation of 1911. Mother and daughter wrote of the souls "of different sex and age committed to us", and of poverty.

Bugga became an abbess. About 725, by Kylie's uncertain date, Boniface wrote "To the lady Bugga, abbess". She had asked whether to go abroad. He would not urge it strongly, he wrote, but if she found no quiet at home, going seemed better. Their sister Wiethburga, at the shrine of Saint Peter, advised waiting until the dangers "from the Saracens" in Italy were over, and Boniface agreed.

In her old age, Boniface heard of "the storms of tribulation" that had come on her. She had laid down "the greater anxiety of caring for the monasteries", he wrote, and heavier sorrows had followed.

Ethelbert the Second, king of Kent, wrote to Boniface of "the venerable Abbess Bugga", his kinswoman, and Kylie takes her for the same woman. She had prayed at the holy places of Rome, the king said, and had met Boniface there. She came home to "the monastery of consecrated women, which formerly she governed well under the law of the Church".

Bregwin, archbishop of Canterbury, later wrote to Lul, who followed Boniface at Mainz. Bregwin and his church, he wrote, kept the burial day of an abbess named Bugga, 27 December. While she lived, she had asked that Mainz be told and keep it too, since Boniface had been her father in Christ. The Bollandists, editors of the Acta Sanctorum, report a belief that Bugga was another name of Abbess Eadburga, to whom Boniface wrote about a vision. Kylie treats them as two women.

In her letter of thanks Bugga had told Boniface that "no temporal vicissitudes can move my mind from its steady guardianship of thy love."

Key Works

  • Letter to Boniface: she gives thanks for his safe journey and his success, asks his prayers, and promises The Sufferings of the Martyrs. Kylie dates it to between 720 and 722 and gives it in English only

Legacy

What traces to them

  • With her mother, the abbess Eangyth, she wrote to Boniface of the souls in their care, their poverty and the hostility of the king.
  • Ethelbert the Second, king of Kent, writes that she governed a monastery of consecrated women "well under the law of the Church".
  • She sent Boniface fifty shillings, an altar pall, gifts and garments, and asked him for passages of Scripture.

How they're remembered

  • Boniface wrote to her as "dearest friend and sister" and called her "mother and sweet lady".
  • Kylie writes that of Boniface's older friends, Bugga alone outlived the rest.
  • Bregwin, archbishop of Canterbury, wrote to Lul at Mainz that his own church kept the burial day of an abbess Bugga, whose father in Christ was Boniface.

What Is Uncertain

  • Her life is known from a few letters, her own and others', in Kylie's English. No source used here names her monastery or gives her years.
  • Whether she was also Eadburga. The Bollandists, editors of the Acta Sanctorum, report a belief that the Eadburga to whom Boniface wrote of a vision was surnamed Bugga. They place her in Kent and do not name Thanet. Kylie keeps the two women apart.
  • Whether Bregwin's Bugga is she. Bregwin names an abbess Bugga whose father in Christ was Boniface. The Bollandists give the same day, 27 December, for the death of their Eadburga called Bugga.
  • Whether Ethelbert's Abbess Bugga is the same woman. Kylie's index and introduction join them; Ethelbert names no mother and no earlier letters.
  • A Bugga who built a church. The index to Migne's Alcuin asks whether a Bugga who built a church, a West Saxon king's daughter, is the Bugga who wrote to Boniface. The same volume says the poem about her is not Alcuin's, since it was written under Ine, king of the West Saxons.
  • Kylie's dates. He dates Boniface's letter of advice to her to "About 725 (?)", with a question mark.
  • Her journey. Ethelbert says she met Boniface in Rome. No source used here gives the year.
