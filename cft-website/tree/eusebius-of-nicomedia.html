Nicomedia · Constantinople, today in Turkey

Eusebius of Nicomedia

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Homoian ('Arian') Christianity.

Known for
Bishop of Berytus, Nicomedia and at last Constantinople, who took up Arius's cause against Alexander of Alexandria and led the party his opponents named after him.
Dates
Exiled within three months of the Council of Nicaea of 325, by Philostorgius; recalled after three years, by Philostorgius, in 328 by A. C. Zenos's note to Socrates of Constantinople; died soon after the synod of Antioch held in the fifth year after Constantine's death, by Socrates, which Zenos dates to 341
Place
Berytus; Nicomedia in Bithynia; Nicaea; Constantinople; Antioch
Role
Bishop and leader of a church party

About This Person

A bishop was leaving the Council of Nicaea for exile. Secundus of Ptolemais had refused to sign its creed. He turned to Eusebius of Nicomedia, who had signed. "You subscribed, Eusebius, in order to escape being sent into banishment," he said. Within a year, he warned, Eusebius would be exiled too. Within three months, he was.

Philostorgius tells this story. He was on Eusebius's side, but his book lives only in a hostile summary by Photius of Constantinople. Most other accounts come from men who fought him, like Alexander of Alexandria and his successor Athanasius.

Eusebius had been bishop of Berytus before he moved to Nicomedia. Sozomen, a church historian of the next century, says he was learned and stood high at the palace. Arius wrote to him as "my fellow-Lucianist." Philostorgius names him as a pupil of Lucian of Antioch.

When Alexander put Arius out, Eusebius took up his cause. One of his own letters is kept in the history of Theodoret of Cyrrhus. In it he asks Paulinus of Tyre to win Alexander over. The Son, he says, was not made from the Father's own being. "There is, indeed, nothing which is of His substance," he writes. All things came to be by God's will.

After Nicaea, Constantine wrote to the people of Nicomedia against him. He called him "the co-operator in the cruelty of the tyrants." He said Eusebius had taken in men from Alexandria whom he had ordered seized. He sent him away with Theognis of Nicaea. Socrates of Constantinople says instead that Eusebius would not accept the creed, and was sent away for that. Later the two asked to come home. In their plea, which Socrates gives, they wrote, "we subscribed the declaration of faith; we did not subscribe the anathematizing." They were brought back.

In time, Socrates says, Constantine's son Constantius made him bishop of Constantinople. Philostorgius says Ulfila was made bishop of the Goths "by Eusebius and the other prelates." Louis Duchesne, a modern historian, says it was this Eusebius. Theodoret tells of Constantine's baptism and names no one. Blomfield Jackson, who translated him, says in a note that Eusebius gave it. Eusebius died soon after a synod at Antioch, Socrates says. It met in the fifth year after Constantine's death.

To Athanasius, "Eusebius and his fellows have always been the supporters and associates of the impious heresy."

Key Works

  • Letter to Paulinus of Tyre: urges Paulinus to write for Arius's side and holds that the Son was not made from the Father's substance; kept by Theodoret of Cyrrhus
  • Plea of Eusebius and Theognis to the bishops: asks to be restored, saying they signed the creed of Nicaea but not the condemnation of Arius; kept by Socrates

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Philostorgius says Ulfila was made bishop of the Goths "by Eusebius and the other prelates that were with him". Louis Duchesne names this Eusebius as Eusebius of Nicomedia.
  • Socrates names Theognis of Nicaea, Maris of Chalcedon, Theodore of Heraclea, Ursacius of Singidunum and Valens of Mursa as those who "had formerly co-operated with Eusebius" and took up his authority after his death.
  • Blomfield Jackson's note to Theodoret says Constantine was baptised "At the hand of Eusebius of Nicomedia".

How they're remembered

  • Athanasius of Alexandria, in his Encyclical Letter: "Eusebius and his fellows have always been the supporters and associates of the impious heresy of the Arian madmen".
  • Constantine, writing to the people of Nicomedia: "the co-operator in the cruelty of the tyrants".
  • Archibald Robertson, editor of Athanasius, calls the Eusebians the "personal entourage, of one man, a master of intrigue".

At Major Councils

  • Council of Nicaea, 325: Of Arius's party; exiled after the council and later restored

What Is Uncertain

  • Most accounts of him come from opponents: Alexander of Alexandria, Athanasius, Constantine's letter and the Nicene historians. Philostorgius was on his side, but survives only in the hostile summary of Photius of Constantinople.
  • Whether he signed the creed of Nicaea is told two ways. Philostorgius says he signed and has Secundus of Ptolemais taunt him for it. Socrates says he was one of five who would not accept it. His own plea, in Socrates, says he signed the creed but not the condemnation of Arius. Walford, translating Philostorgius, takes Socrates to be mistaken.
  • Why he was exiled is told two ways. Constantine's letter blames his sheltering of men from Alexandria. Socrates ties it to his support for Arius, and Zenos says Socrates "does not give the correct reason".
  • When he took the see of Constantinople is told two ways. Socrates says Constantius moved him there after deposing Paul, bishop of Constantinople. Theodoret and Philostorgius place it at the death of Alexander of Constantinople, which Walford's note calls a mistake.
  • Theodoret does not say who baptised Constantine. The name is Blomfield Jackson's note.
  • Philostorgius gives Ulfila's consecrator only as "Eusebius". The identification is Duchesne's.
  • Philostorgius's story that Constantine's will was entrusted to him comes only through Photius's summary.
  • The year of his death, 341, is Zenos's reckoning from Socrates's dating of the synod of Antioch.
