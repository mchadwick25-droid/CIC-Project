Eusebius of Nicomedia

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Homoian ('Arian') Christianity.

Known for
Bishop of Berytus, Nicomedia and at last Constantinople, who took up Arius's cause against Alexander of Alexandria and led the party his opponents named after him.
Dates
Exiled within three months of the Council of Nicaea of 325, by Philostorgius; recalled after three years, by Philostorgius, in 328 by A. C. Zenos's note to Socrates of Constantinople; died soon after the synod of Antioch held in the fifth year after Constantine's death, by Socrates, which Zenos dates to 341
Place
Berytus; Nicomedia in Bithynia; Nicaea; Constantinople; Antioch
Role

About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Arius, a priest driven out of Alexandria, wrote to Eusebius, bishop of Nicomedia, and greeted him as a fellow pupil of Lucian, a teacher at Antioch who died a martyr. He told him why he was being hounded: "We are persecuted because we said: the Son has a beginning, but God has no beginning." Eusebius answered by taking up his cause.

That choice set the course of his life. Nicomedia was the emperor's city, in north-west Asia Minor, and that gave its bishop weight. After a short exile he led the bishops who drove Arius's opponents out of their churches, and he died bishop of Constantinople. Nearly everything we know of him was written by his enemies.

First he wrote to a fellow bishop at Tyre, urging him to write for their side. His letter says the Son was not made from the Father's own being, and that everything came to be by God's will. He also wrote to Alexander of Alexandria, and to bishops everywhere, asking them to take Arius back. Alexander told the bishops to ignore his letters.

Then came Nicaea, in 325, and what Eusebius signed. Philostorgius, the one friendly historian, whose work survives only in a hostile summary, says he signed. Socrates, a historian of Constantinople a century later, says he was one of five who would not. His own account was written from exile, some three years later, with another bishop: "We signed the creed, but we did not sign the condemnation. We were not finding fault with the creed. We did not believe the accused man was what he was said to be." The condemnation was the council's curse on Arius.

Within three months of the council, Philostorgius says, Eusebius was exiled. Constantine charged him in a letter to the people of Nicomedia: "Who taught these things to so innocent a crowd? Eusebius, plainly: the partner in the tyrant's cruelty." The tyrant was Licinius, the rival emperor who had made war on Constantine. The letter's stated cause was that Eusebius had sheltered men from Alexandria whom Constantine had ordered seized. Socrates gives another reason: Eusebius would not join in condemning Arius.

After three years his plea won his recall, and he took his church back; the bishop of Antioch was removed. A historian of the next century says Eusebius's party bribed the woman who charged him, though Philostorgius tells her charge as true. Athanasius, now bishop of Alexandria, says Eusebius pressed him with threats to receive Arius. Socrates names Eusebius first among the men behind the charges that removed Athanasius by a council at Tyre. Then they told Constantine that Athanasius had threatened Alexandria's grain ships, a charge Socrates calls slander, and the emperor exiled him.

Dying near Nicomedia, Constantine called the bishops and asked for baptism. His biographer, another Eusebius, writes only that "the prelates" gave it. Jerome, writing about forty years later, names Eusebius of Nicomedia. He says that with it Constantine "turned aside" to Arius's teaching, and that seizures of churches and discord across the world followed.

Socrates says Constantine's son Constantius removed the bishop of Constantinople, and moved Eusebius to the emperor's new city. Eusebius had ninety bishops called to a meeting at Antioch, which Socrates says aimed to undo "of one being," Nicaea's word that the Son shares the Father's very being.

Philostorgius says Ulfila, sent by the Goths to Constantine's court, was made their bishop by a bishop named Eusebius. A modern historian takes this to be Eusebius of Nicomedia, and sets it under Constantius instead.

Eusebius died soon after the meeting at Antioch, before he learned the bishop of Rome's ruling on Athanasius. At once the people of Constantinople brought back the bishop he had replaced. The men who had worked with him took up "all his authority." Athanasius had a name for them: "those around Eusebius." After his death, Socrates says, many lives were lost in the fight over the city's bishop.

Philostorgius called him "the Great." Socrates called him "that disturber of the public peace."

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Philostorgius says Ulfila was made bishop of the Goths "by Eusebius and the other prelates that were with him". Louis Duchesne names this Eusebius as Eusebius of Nicomedia.
  • Socrates names Theognis of Nicaea, Maris of Chalcedon, Theodore of Heraclea, Ursacius of Singidunum and Valens of Mursa as those who "had formerly co-operated with Eusebius" and took up his authority after his death.
  • Blomfield Jackson's note to Theodoret says Constantine was baptized "At the hand of Eusebius of Nicomedia".
  • Sozomen says a synod convened in Bithynia wrote to all the bishops, asking them to hold communion with Arius and his followers and to require Alexander to do the same.
  • Prosper's Chronicle repeats Jerome's words that Constantine was baptized by Eusebius of Nicomedia and turned to Arius's teaching.

How they're remembered

  • Athanasius of Alexandria, in his Encyclical Letter: "Eusebius and his fellows have always been the supporters and associates of the impious heresy of the Arian madmen".
  • Constantine, writing to the people of Nicomedia: "the co-operator in the cruelty of the tyrants".
  • Archibald Robertson, editor of Athanasius, calls the Eusebians the "personal entourage, of one man, a master of intrigue".
  • Alexander of Alexandria, in his circular letter kept by Socrates, tells the bishops to pay no attention to anything Eusebius should write to them.
  • Athanasius says Eusebius sent to him urging him to admit Arius and his followers to communion, and threatened him in his spoken messages.
  • Jerome, about forty years after Constantine's death, says Constantine was baptized by Eusebius of Nicomedia and so turned to Arius's teaching, and that seizures of churches and discord across the world followed.

At Major Councils

  • Council of Nicaea, 325: Of Arius's party; exiled after the council and later restored

What Is Uncertain

  • Most accounts of him come from opponents: Alexander of Alexandria, Athanasius, Constantine's letter and the Nicene historians. Philostorgius was on his side, but survives only in the hostile summary of Photius of Constantinople.
  • Whether he signed the creed of Nicaea is told two ways. Philostorgius says he signed and has Secundus of Ptolemais taunt him for it. Socrates says he was one of five who would not accept it. His own plea, in Socrates, says he signed the creed but not the condemnation of Arius. Walford, translating Philostorgius, takes Socrates to be mistaken.
  • Why he was exiled is told two ways. Constantine's letter blames his sheltering of men from Alexandria. Socrates ties it to his support for Arius, and Zenos says Socrates "does not give the correct reason".
  • When he took the see of Constantinople is told two ways. Socrates says Constantius moved him there after deposing Paul, bishop of Constantinople. Theodoret and Philostorgius place it at the death of Alexander of Constantinople, which Walford's note calls a mistake.
  • Theodoret does not say who baptized Constantine. The name is Blomfield Jackson's note.
  • Philostorgius gives Ulfila's consecrator only as "Eusebius". The identification is Duchesne's.
  • Philostorgius's story that Constantine's will was entrusted to him comes only through Photius's summary.
  • The year of his death, 341, is Zenos's reckoning from Socrates's dating of the synod of Antioch.
  • Who baptized Constantine. Eusebius of Caesarea, in the Life of Constantine, writes only that the prelates performed the ceremonies. Jerome's Chronicle names Eusebius of Nicomedia and gives the verdict that Constantine then turned to Arius's teaching; Prosper's Chronicle copies him.
  • Alexander of Alexandria's circular letter charges that Eusebius deserted his church at Berytus for Nicomedia, and the letter of the Egyptian bishops in 338 says the same. The charge is theirs.
  • A woman's charge against Eustathius of Antioch is told two ways. Theodoret says Eusebius's party bribed her; Philostorgius tells the charge as true.
  • Socrates says Eusebius and his allies told Constantine that Athanasius had threatened to stop the corn sent from Alexandria to Constantinople, and he calls the charge a calumny. The charge is theirs as Socrates reports it.
  • Philostorgius sets the embassy on which Ulfila was made bishop at the court of Constantine. Duchesne, who names Eusebius of Nicomedia as the consecrator, sets it at the court of Constantius.
  • Sozomen alone, with "it is said", tells that Eusebius and Theognis bribed the keeper of the Nicene documents to efface their signatures, and that Eusebius answered the charge by pointing to his robe.
  • Ambrose says Eusebius's own letter, read at Nicaea, led the council to put the word homoousios into its creed. The claim rests on Ambrose alone, and his Latin is not held.
  • Philostorgius says Eusebius and other pupils of Lucian yielded to persecutors and sacrificed to the gods, then made amends. It is one line in Photius's summary.
  • Photius's summary says Eusebius and his band accepted the Nicene sentence with a fraudulent purpose, secretly putting homoiousios for homoousios. That is Photius's framing of Philostorgius.
