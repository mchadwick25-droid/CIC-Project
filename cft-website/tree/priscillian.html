Priscillian

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Priscillianist Asceticism.

Known for
Spanish lay teacher of a strict ascetic life, made bishop of Abila, who was condemned by bishops and put to death by the emperor Maximus, and was then honored by his followers as a martyr.
Also called
Priscillian of Abila, Priscillianus
Dates
Sulpicius Severus says he was condemned at Saragossa, which the book to Damasus denies; put to death at Trèves under Maximus; Jerome's modern translator Ernest Cushing Richardson dates him flourished 379, died 385
Place
Spain; bishop of Abila; executed at Trèves
Role
Lay teacher, then bishop of Abila

About This Person

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 4:35

Priscillian's party carried a book to Damasus, bishop of Rome. In it they wrote what they had always urged in the churches. "No one should be forbidden, if he despises parents, children, wealth and rank, even his own life, and would rather love God than the world."

A rich and learned layman of Spain, Priscillian called men and women to that strict life, and became bishop of Abila. Other bishops accused him of heresy, and an emperor who had seized the throne had him tried. He was put to the sword, in 385 by one old chronicle and 387 by another, and his followers honored him as a martyr.

Nearly all we know of him comes from Sulpicius Severus, a writer from Gaul. Sulpicius called the teaching the heresy of the Gnostics, a heresy of secret knowledge, and repeats that Priscillian was believed to have practiced magic from boyhood. Yet he adds: "My own view is that the accused displease me as much as the accusers."

In their book, his party says they gave themselves wholly to God after baptism, because Scripture says that whoever loves anyone more than God cannot be his disciple. The same book gives their creed and curses every heresy, the followers of the Persian teacher Mani above all. Nobles, common people and many women came to him, Sulpicius says. The bishop of Emerita, he adds, harassed them without restraint, and only fanned the fire.

Bishops met at Saragossa. Sulpicius says Priscillian was condemned in his absence as a heretic, still a layman. The book says no charge was laid against his group there, and reminds Damasus that Damasus's own letter forbade judging the absent and unheard.

The bishop of Emerita won an order from the emperor that drove all heretics from the cities and beyond his lands. He asked for it, the book says, against false bishops and Mani's followers. Priscillian went to Rome to clear his name: in a cause of faith, the book says, his party preferred the judgment of holy men to the world's. But Damasus would not even see him, and at Milan Ambrose was just as opposed.

The emperor Maximus came to Trier, where Bishop Ithacius accused Priscillian before him. At a council at Bordeaux, Priscillian appealed from the bishops to the emperor, to escape their judgment, Sulpicius says. Ithacius, he adds, called anyone who loved reading or fasting Priscillian's disciple. Martin, bishop of Tours, begged the emperor not to shed their blood, since the bishops' sentence was enough.

After Martin left, a prefect heard Priscillian twice and found him guilty of sorcery. Sulpicius says Priscillian did not deny studying obscene teachings, holding night meetings of women, and praying naked. These are the court's findings; no account of the trial from his side survives. The emperor ordered death. Priscillian was killed with several companions.

His followers, Sulpicius says, now revered him as a martyr. They carried the bodies home to Spain for grand funerals. In 400, Spanish bishops at Toledo still heard of clergy calling him a holy martyr.

The deaths split the other side. Its bishops above all quarreled for fifteen years, Sulpicius says, and Ithacius, attacked for bringing the case before the courts, lost his bishopric.

Yet sixty years on, Leo, bishop of Rome, wrote against the Priscillianists. Their teaching, he wrote, put each life under the power of demons and stars. He praised the killing. "Even the rulers of the world loathed this unholy folly. So they cut down its founder, with most of his followers, by the sword of the public laws." In 561 a council at Braga still cursed teachings it called his, among them that souls sinned in heaven and were cast down into bodies.

In 1885, a scholar opened an old manuscript at Würzburg and found eleven unsigned treatises. One was a book to Damasus, naming the same bishop of Emerita and the same council at Saragossa as Sulpicius. In it, Priscillian's party could be heard again, in their own words.

Quotations are given in our modern English. The printed wording of each is below.

Quotations and references

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “No one should be forbidden, if he despises parents, children, wealth and rank, even his own life, and would rather love God than the world.”
    As printed
    “nee prohibere si quis contemptis parenti- T)us liberis facultatibus dignitate et adhuc et aiiima sua deum malluerit amare quam saeculum”
    Priscillian, Book to Damasus (Tractate II), Latin, ed. Georg Schepss
    Schepss's print is the only one held, and the scan misreads letters: nee for nec, T)us for the end of parentibus, aiiima for anima, and a stray mark after deum. No second print confirms the reading, so the Latin is given as the scan stands and not as an original; the rendering follows the sense of the sentence. malluerit is the manuscript's spelling.
  • In our modern English
    “My own view is that the accused displease me as much as the accusers.”
    As printed
    “And my feeling indeed is, that the accusers were as distasteful to me as the accused.”
    Sulpicius Severus, Chronicle II.50, tr. Alexander Roberts
    Halm's Latin (lines 5890-5891 of the Halm file) is held only in a damaged scan that reads senteatia and dispUcere, so it is not printed. The story renders the Latin, mea quidem sententia est, mihi tam reos quam accusatores displicere, which puts the accused first; Roberts puts the accusers first.
  • In our modern English
    “Even the rulers of the world loathed this unholy folly. So they cut down its founder, with most of his followers, by the sword of the public laws.”
    As printed
    “for even the leaders of the world so abhorred this profane folly that they laid low its originator, with most of his disciples, by the sword of the public laws.”
    Leo the Great, Letter 15 to Turribius, tr. Charles Lett Feltoe, 1895
    Leo's Latin is not held. The story splits Feltoe's one sentence in two and uses rulers for his leaders, unholy for profane, cut down for laid low, founder for originator and followers for disciples.

Key Works

  • Liber apologeticus (Tractate I): a defense of the faith, cursing the Patripassians, the Manichaeans and other heresies; unsigned, and judged Priscillian's by Georg Schepss, its modern editor
  • Liber ad Damasum episcopum (Tractate II): his party's appeal to Damasus of Rome, saying that at Saragossa none of his group was accused, convicted or condemned; Georg Schepss, its modern editor, judged it Priscillian's own
  • Canons on the letters of Paul: headings for reading Paul's letters, kept in a revision by Peregrinus

Legacy

What traces to them

  • After his death, Sulpicius Severus says, his followers honored him as a martyr, and "it came to be thought the highest exercise of religion to swear by Priscillian."
  • A council at Braga in 561 still cursed teachings it named as his, among them that souls sinned in heaven and were cast down into bodies.
  • Sulpicius says the quarrel that followed was kept up for fifteen years and "could not by any means be set at rest."
  • At the First Council of Toledo, held in about 400, the bishops heard that clergy of the bishop Herenas had cried out, unasked, that Priscillian was a catholic and a holy martyr, and that Herenas held to the end that he was catholic and had suffered persecution from the bishops.
  • Sulpicius says Ithacius, the chief accuser, was the only one of those who had acted against Priscillian who was thrust out of the episcopate, and that Ydacius resigned his bishopric of his own accord.
  • In 447 Leo, bishop of Rome, wrote to a bishop in Spain against the Priscillianists. He said the rulers of the world so abhorred this folly that they laid low its originator, with most of his disciples, "by the sword of the public laws", and that this rigorous treatment was for long a help to the church's law of gentleness.
  • A law of 407 names the Priscillianists, with the Manichaeans and Phrygians, among those the emperors pursue with the most deserved severity.

How they're remembered

  • Sulpicius calls him "a man of noble birth, of great riches, bold, restless, eloquent, learned through much reading."
  • Jerome of Strido says he "published many short writings, some of which have reached us."

What Is Uncertain

  • His teaching comes to us mostly through his enemies. Sulpicius Severus thought him a Gnostic heretic, and was just as hostile to his accusers, Ithacius of Sossuba and Ydacius of Emerita. Orosius, a Spanish priest who wrote to Augustine of Hippo against the Priscillianists, says he taught that souls came down from God through circles of evil powers into bodies. Long after, in 561, the council at Braga cursed like teachings in his name. Jerome of Strido reports that some accused him of Gnosticism and his defenders denied it.
  • Sulpicius says the council at Saragossa condemned Priscillian in his absence. The book to Damasus says no one of his group was accused, convicted or condemned there.
  • The book to Damasus says Hydatius gave the council some memorandum that set down, as it were, a rule for living, and that none of his group was censured under it. The book does not say what it forbade. Karl Josef von Hefele, a modern historian, in Oxenham's English, supposes that eight canons of a Saragossa synod of 380 belong to this council, because they are plainly directed against the Priscillianists. Among them, that all Christian women avoid conventicles, and that no one fast on Sunday. This is his supposition, and Mansi, he notes, dates the synod to 379.
  • Sulpicius says Priscillian did not deny that he had given himself up to lewd teachings, held night meetings with women and prayed naked. These were the findings of the court of Evodius, and Sulpicius gives no account from Priscillian's side.
  • The eleven Würzburg treatises carry no author's name. Georg Schepss, who found them in 1885 and printed them in 1889, judged from the names in the first two that they could only be Priscillian's. This is a modern judgment, and his is the only one held. In his preface he also writes that the judgment on Priscillian had hung on his enemies, chiefly Sulpicius, until the treatises turned up. Schepss attributes the Book to Damasus to Priscillian himself; the story says only that his party wrote it.
  • Alexander Roberts's English Sulpicius says he was made bishop "in the town of Arles". Halm's Latin reads Abilensi oppido, the town of Abila, as Jerome also has it.
  • Ernest Cushing Richardson's English Jerome says Priscillian "belonged to the party of Hydatius and Ithacius". Sulpicius names these two as his accusers.
  • The sources used here give no year of birth, parents or schooling. Sulpicius reports that "it was believed that he had practised magical arts from his boyhood."
  • The year of his death is not fixed. Of the modern editors, Richardson's note to Jerome gives 385. Fremantle's table of dates for Jerome gives 386, and Hydatius places the deaths under a later year, which his editor Mommsen marks 387. The story gives 385 by Prosper's chronicle, which names the consuls Arcadius and Bauto, and 387 by Hydatius's, which names Theodosius's ninth year; the Roman year in each is a margin by their modern editor, Theodor Mommsen.
  • Whether his death was the first execution of a Christian for heresy rests on modern editors alone, and no primary source held says so. The note to Theodoret by Blomfield Jackson speaks of "the first capital punishment of a heretic", and Fremantle's table of dates for Jerome lists "386. Execution of Priscillian for heresy at Treves." Hefele, in Oxenham's English, reports the view of the modern scholar Bernays, who holds against the general view that the charges were sorcery ("maleficium") and night meetings for prayer, and that Priscillian and his followers "were not executed for heresy." In Sulpicius the prefect Evodius convicts him of "evil conduct" in Roberts's English; the Latin word is maleficium, sorcery. The story gives the verdict as the court's.
  • Where Roberts's English and Halm's Latin differ, the story follows the Latin. Roberts says the trial was put off "as long as Martin survived"; the Latin says it was put off while Martin was at Trier. Roberts says a war of quarreling went on "between them and our friends"; the Latin says among our own people (inter nostros), which puts it on Sulpicius's side. Hydatius's Chronicle says Martin and other bishops judged Priscillian a heretic in Gaul, while Sulpicius has Martin beg Maximus not to shed blood.
  • No held source dates Sulpicius's Chronicle.
  • Sulpicius says Ithacius alone was thrust out of the episcopate and that Ydacius resigned. Prosper's Chronicle says that in 389 the bishops Ithacius and Ursacius were cut off from the church's communion for the killing of Priscillian, whose accusers they had been.
  • Sulpicius says that on the way to Rome the party lingered for a little while "in the territory of Euchrotia", and that Euchrotia was beheaded with Priscillian. Prosper calls her the wife of the rhetorician Delphidius.
  • Sulpicius traces the teaching to Marcus of Memphis, through Agape and the rhetorician Helpidius, and says the bishops Instantius and Salvianus bound themselves to Priscillian "with a kind of oath". He says the bishop Ydacius harassed them "without measure" and only fanned the fire. The book to Damasus denies the heresies and curses them. Sulpicius also says that after Damasus and Ambrose turned them away, the party won over Macedonius, master of the offices, "by bribery and flattery" and recovered their churches; The book to Damasus says Ydacius had won his rescript by a false petition that hid their names.
  • The first of the Würzburg tractates, the Liber apologeticus, says whoever uses magic rites with first fruits and an ointment cursed to the Sun and the Moon is to be pursued with the sword, and says that if it could be done the author would send Mani's followers to the underworld by the sword. Schepss's modern attribution of the tractates to Priscillian is the only one held.
  • Jerome, whose Lives of Illustrious Men runs to the fourteenth year of the emperor Theodosius, speaks of him, in a letter that its modern translator Fremantle dates 415, as "condemned by the whole world and put to death by the secular sword". Augustine says that real Priscillianists, posing as Catholics, "execrate the Priscillianists' false martyrdoms".
  • The First Council of Toledo is held in one print only, Mansi's, in a long-s scan. Professions of faith in it, under the names of the bishops Symphosius and Dictinius, condemn Priscillian's books with their author, but an editor's note in Mansi doubts they belong to this council, since they call both men bishops of holy memory.
  • Mansi's note quotes Ambrose as saying that the emperor, seeing him keep away from the bishops in communion with him, or those who sought the death of some, though they had strayed from the faith, was angered and ordered him to leave without delay. The note is the only held place for this, and its scan is damaged.
  • Orosius, in his Commonitorium, says Priscillian held that the soul is caught by evil powers and put into bodies, with a written bond (chirographum) set down against them, and that for this reason astrology (mathesis) prevailed, and that Christ loosed this bond and nailed it to the cross by his passion. Orosius then quotes a letter of Priscillian's.
