The Imperial Church Era · flourished 379; died 385 · Abila, Trèves

Priscillian

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Priscillianist Asceticism.

Known for
Spanish lay teacher of a strict ascetic life, made bishop of Abila, who was condemned by bishops and put to death by the emperor Maximus, and was then honored by his followers as a martyr.
Also called
Priscillian of Abila, Priscillianus
Dates
Sulpicius Severus says he was condemned at Saragossa, which he denied; put to death at Trèves under Maximus; Ernest Cushing Richardson dates him flourished 379, died 385
Place
Spain; bishop of Abila; executed at Trèves
Role
Lay teacher, then bishop of Abila

About This Person

At the court of the emperor Maximus, the prefect Evodius tried a Spanish bishop named Priscillian in two hearings. Sulpicius Severus, a chronicler from Aquitaine, tells it. He says Priscillian did not deny that he had given himself up to lewd teachings, held meetings of women by night, and prayed naked. Evodius found him guilty. The emperor ordered that Priscillian and his friends be put to death.

Priscillian was a layman of Spain, "a man of noble birth, of great riches," Sulpicius says. He could go without sleep, food and drink, and cared little for money. Sulpicius thought his teaching the heresy of the Gnostics. He disliked the bishops who hunted him just as much. "The accusers were as distasteful to me as the accused," he writes. He calls Ithacius of Sossuba, one of the accusers, bold, talkative and given to pleasure.

A council at Saragossa condemned Priscillian in his absence, Sulpicius says. Two bishops who backed him then made him bishop of Abila. Priscillian went to Rome to clear himself before Damasus, bishop of the city, but was turned away.

In 1885 Georg Schepss found eleven treatises in a manuscript at Würzburg. They carry no name, but Schepss judged them Priscillian's. In his book to Damasus, Priscillian says that at Saragossa none of his group was accused, convicted or condemned. He reminds Damasus that his own letter had forbidden judging people absent and unheard. In another treatise he curses anyone who does not condemn Mani, founder of the Manichaeans.

The teaching he was condemned for comes to us through his enemies. Orosius, a Spanish priest, wrote to Augustine of Hippo against the Priscillianists. He says Priscillian taught that souls came down from God through circles of evil powers into bodies. In 561 a council at Braga named him. It cursed the teaching that souls were cast into bodies and bound to the stars. "He is still accused by some, of being tainted with Gnosticism," wrote Jerome of Strido. His defenders, Jerome adds, deny it.

Martin of Tours had begged Maximus not to shed blood, saying a secular judge should not decide a church case. Later Ithacius alone of the bishops was put out of his see, Sulpicius says. The bodies were carried back to Spain and buried "with great pomp." His followers now honored him as a martyr. "It came to be thought the highest exercise of religion to swear by Priscillian."

Key Works

  • Liber apologeticus (Tractate I): his defense of his faith, cursing the Patripassians, the Manichaeans and other heresies
  • Liber ad Damasum episcopum (Tractate II): his appeal to Damasus of Rome, saying that at Saragossa none of his group was accused, convicted or condemned
  • Canons on the letters of Paul: headings for reading Paul's letters, kept in a revision by Peregrinus

Legacy

What traces to them

  • After his death, Sulpicius Severus says, his followers honored him as a martyr, and "it came to be thought the highest exercise of religion to swear by Priscillian."
  • A council at Braga in 561 still cursed teachings it named as his, among them that souls sinned in heaven and were cast down into bodies.
  • Sulpicius says the quarrel that followed was kept up for fifteen years and "could not by any means be set at rest."

How they're remembered

  • Sulpicius calls him "a man of noble birth, of great riches, bold, restless, eloquent, learned through much reading."
  • Jerome of Strido says he "published many short writings, some of which have reached us."

What Is Uncertain

  • His teaching comes to us mostly through his enemies. Sulpicius Severus thought him a Gnostic heretic, and was just as hostile to his accusers, Ithacius of Sossuba and Ydacius of Emerita. Orosius, a Spanish priest who wrote to Augustine of Hippo against the Priscillianists, says he taught that souls came down from God through circles of evil powers into bodies. Long after, in 561, the council at Braga cursed like teachings in his name. Jerome of Strido reports that some accused him of Gnosticism and his defenders denied it.
  • Sulpicius says the council at Saragossa condemned Priscillian in his absence. Priscillian, in his book to Damasus, says no one of his group was accused, convicted or condemned there.
  • Sulpicius says Priscillian did not deny that he had given himself up to lewd teachings, held night meetings with women and prayed naked. These were the findings of the court of Evodius, and Sulpicius gives no account from Priscillian's side.
  • The eleven Würzburg treatises carry no author's name. Georg Schepss, who found them in 1885 and printed them in 1889, judged from the names in the first two that they could only be Priscillian's.
  • Alexander Roberts's English Sulpicius says he was made bishop "in the town of Arles". Halm's Latin reads Abilensi oppido, the town of Abila, as Jerome also has it.
  • Ernest Cushing Richardson's English Jerome says Priscillian "belonged to the party of Hydatius and Ithacius". Sulpicius names these two as his accusers.
  • The sources used here give no year of birth, parents or schooling. Sulpicius reports that "it was believed that he had practised magical arts from his boyhood."
