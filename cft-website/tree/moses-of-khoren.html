About This Person

Moses of Khoren hurried home from Byzantium, hoping to dance at weddings. Instead, he writes, he found himself at a grave, weeping. His teachers, Sahak the Great and Mesrop Mashtots, had died. He had not come in time to close their eyes or hear their last words.

That lament closes his History of Armenia. Its three books tell his nation's story from its first ancestors to the deaths of those two men. He wrote it for Sahak Bagratuni, who had asked for it. Victor Langlois, his 1869 French editor, takes this patron for the Bagratid prince the Armenians made their governor in 481, in a revolt against Persia.

In the History, Moses says Sahak and Mesrop sent him to Alexandria to study. He passed through Edessa and its archives and prayed at the holy places of Palestine. In Alexandria, he says, a new Plato taught, a teacher he was not found worthy to follow. Langlois's note says he surely means Cyril of Alexandria. The Mekhitarist monks of Venice say perhaps. Contrary winds then drove him to Italy, and after a short stay in Athens he made for Byzantium.

Langlois adds what tradition tells of his later life. Moses lived ten years apart and poor. Then the patriarch Kiud made him bishop of Bagrevand after Eznik of Kolb died. Langlois warns that tradition has wrapped his life in details history has not kept. One Armenian historian he cites, Thomas Artsruni, has him living past a hundred, into the reign of Zeno.

Langlois has him born in the later fourth century and draws his life from the History itself, as the Mekhitarist preface does. Modern work argues for a later date, but the Library holds none of it. Frédéric Macler, a French scholar writing in 1917, says almost nothing precise is known of him.

In his lament Moses asks who will now value his teachers' doctrine, and who will be glad of his own progress.

Key Works

  • History of Armenia: three books, from the first ancestors of the Armenians to the deaths of Sahak the Great and Mesrop Mashtots, ending with a lament; Langlois reports that Thomas Artsruni knew a fourth book, of which only fragments are known

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Langlois says the History, with its range of sources and its author's skill as a writer, earned Moses his place at the head of the historians of his nation.
  • Langlois lists translations of the History into Latin, French, Italian and Russian, among them the Whiston brothers' Latin, printed in London, and the Mekhitarist Italian, which he calls excellent.

How they're remembered

  • Victor Langlois places him at the head "des historiens de sa nation".
  • The Mekhitarist monks of Venice: "Pio davvero Mosè: e di cuore umile".

What Is Uncertain

  • Langlois and the Mekhitarist monks of Venice place him in the fifth century and draw his life from his own History. Modern work argues for a later date, but the Library holds none of it. Frédéric Macler, writing in 1917, says almost nothing precise is known of him.
  • Apart from the History itself, his life comes from Langlois's account of tradition: ten years apart and poor, then bishop of Bagrevand under the patriarch Kiud after Eznik of Kolb died. Langlois warns that tradition has wrapped his life in details history has not kept.
  • That his patron was the Bagratid prince made governor in the revolt of 481 is Langlois's identification. The History names him only as Sahak Bagratuni.
  • The new Plato he says taught at Alexandria is Cyril of Alexandria by Langlois's note, which says "sans doute"; the Mekhitarist preface says "forse".
  • Thomas Artsruni's report that he lived past a hundred, into Zeno's reign, comes through Langlois's note; the Mekhitarist preface, citing the same writer, gives him a hundred and twenty years. Langlois says his age cannot be fixed with exactness.
  • Other works under his name are doubted. Langlois reports that some critics deny him the Geography and that the Book of Chreiai has been thought apocryphal, and he defends both. The Mekhitarist preface thinks the rhetorical treatises may be the work of another Moses of the eighth century.
