Auxentius of Durostorum

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Gothic, Vandal & Lombard Homoian Christianity.

Known for
Pupil of Ulfila, bishop of the Goths, and a Homoian ('Arian') bishop at Durostorum, whose letter is the main record of Ulfila's faith, life and death.
Also called
Auxentius Dorostorensis
Dates
Ulfila's pupil from childhood; still living when Maximinus wrote, after Ulfila's death in 383
Place
Durostorum (modern Silistria), in Lower Moesia
Role
Bishop and recorder

About This Person

From his earliest years, Auxentius of Durostorum was Ulfila's pupil. His parents gave him to the bishop of the Goths, who taught him the holy writings and raised him in the faith like a son. So he says in a letter written after Ulfila died. He could not praise his teacher enough, he writes, and he did not dare stay silent.

Auxentius was a bishop of the late fourth century, in the party its foes called Arian. Friedrich Kauffmann, who edited the letter in 1899, places Durostorum in Lower Moesia, at modern Silistria. He thinks Ulfila held that see first. Maximinus, a bishop of the same party writing against Ambrose of Milan, kept him apart from Auxentius of Milan. Ambrose had named an Auxentius, he says, without saying which. One was alive, at Durostorum. The other, at Milan, had died without a successor.

The letter survives because Maximinus copied it into his own book, a defense of two bishops condemned at the Council of Aquileia in 381. It comes down in a fifth-century manuscript now in Paris, in the margins of Ambrose's On the Faith. Kauffmann, after Ludwig Traube, dates that hand probably to the sixth century.

Auxentius tells his teacher's life in the numbers of Scripture. Ulfila became bishop at thirty, like David, Joseph and Christ. Seven years among the Goths and thirty-three in Roman lands make forty, the span of the saints he followed. Kauffmann notes that writers of that time often used numbers so, yet he counts them as real years.

In Kauffmann's day, W. Luft held that only the first part keeps Auxentius's own words, and the rest is Maximinus's summary of another work. Kauffmann answered that the whole fragment has one biblical style, unlike Maximinus's.

Auxentius was still alive when Maximinus wrote. He was ready, Maximinus says, to stand with Palladius of Ratiaria wherever the faith was put to a public test.

Key Works

  • Letter on the Faith, Life and Death of Ulfila: his teacher's faith, his years as bishop among the Goths and in Roman lands, and his death at Constantinople with the creed he left; the title is Kauffmann's

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Wilhelm Streitberg puts the letter first among the sources for Ulfila's life in his edition of the Gothic Bible, 1908.
  • Maximinus, an Arian bishop writing against Ambrose of Milan, built the letter into his own defense of the bishops condemned at Aquileia.

How they're remembered

  • Friedrich Kauffmann, his editor in 1899, says almost nothing else of the Arians' writing about their saints has come down, which makes this fragment all the more valuable.

What Is Uncertain

  • In Kauffmann's day, W. Luft held that only the first part of the letter keeps Auxentius's own words, and the rest is Maximinus's summary of another work by him. Kauffmann answered that the whole fragment has one biblical style, unlike Maximinus's.
  • Bessell, and A. Jülicher after him, thought the letter was written for the emperor Theodosius. Georg Waitz and Kauffmann did not. Kauffmann looked for its readers among the Arians of Moesia. With Bessell, he thought Auxentius sent it from Constantinople soon after Ulfila's death.
  • That Ulfila held the see of Durostorum before Auxentius is Kauffmann's view. Sozomen names Selinus, once Ulfila's secretary, as his successor as bishop of the Goths.
  • Auxentius writes as a pupil and an admirer, and tells Ulfila's faith as his own party held it.
  • He gives Ulfila's life in the numbers of Scripture: bishop at thirty, like David, Joseph and Christ, and forty years in all, the span of the saints he followed. Kauffmann notes that writers of that time often used numbers so, but counts them as real years.
  • The letter survives inside Maximinus's book, written in the margins of Ambrose's On the Faith in a fifth-century Paris manuscript, cod. lat. 8907, which also holds Hilary of Poitiers. Georg Waitz and Pertz dated the writing to 381 to 384; Bessell showed it was written after 438; Kauffmann follows Ludwig Traube's date, probably the sixth century. The letter has lost its address, its opening and its end.
  • No source gives his birth, his death or the years of his time as bishop.
