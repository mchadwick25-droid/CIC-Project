Patrick

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Insular / Irish Monastic Christianity.

Known for
Bishop in Ireland, a captive from Roman Britain who went back of his own will to the people who had enslaved him. Almost all that is known of him comes from his own two short Latin writings, the Confession and the Letter to the soldiers of Coroticus.
Also called
Patricius, Saint Patrick
Dates
Patrick gives no year in either writing. Newport J. D. White, in 1920, gives 389 to 461 as the most recent calculations for his birth and death, and thinks the Confession was written probably not earlier than 454. The Annals of Ulster put his coming to Ireland in 432, record the repose of Old Patrick under 457, and record his death under 461, 491 and 492; the last says he died in the 120th year of his age
Place
Bannavem Taberniae in Roman Britain, where his father had a small farm, a place no held text locates; Ireland, where he gives no place for his slavery; the Wood of Foclut near the western sea, named in his dream, which White places in County Mayo; Ireland, where he says he is a bishop
Role
Missionary bishop in Ireland; author of the Confession and the Letter

About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Near the end of his life, a bishop in Ireland, a senior church leader who could appoint other ministers, began his account of himself: "I am Patrick, a sinner. I am utterly unlearned, and the least of all believers. Very many people look down on me."

He had first come to Ireland as a slave and escaped after six years. Years later, in the fifth century, he went back of his own will, as a bishop, to the people who had enslaved him. Almost all we know of him comes from two short Latin writings of his own. One is a letter against a warlord's soldiers, and the other is the Confession, his account of his life and thanks to God.

He grew up in Roman Britain, then a province of the empire. At about sixteen he was carried off to Ireland. There he tended flocks and prayed, he says, up to a hundred times a day, until a voice in his sleep told him a ship was ready.

Back in Britain, he dreamed of a letter headed The Voice of the Irish, and heard them cry: "We beg you, holy boy, come and walk among us once more." Senior churchmen judged him too unlearned, meaning no malice, he says, but he went.

He says he baptized many thousands, washing them in water to make them Christians, and had church ministers appointed everywhere. Children of Irish chiefs, he writes, became monks, men who left ordinary life for God, and women vowed to stay unmarried for God, some against their fathers' will. The slave women among them suffered most. Kings robbed him and held him in chains for fourteen days, and every day he expected to be killed or made a slave again. At one time, some of his own senior churchmen held against him a sin from his youth, something he had once confided to his dearest friend. That same friend later shamed him in public.

At another time, soldiers of a warlord named Coroticus cut down new converts with the sword the day after their baptism, still in white, the blessing oil on their foreheads. Others they sold, he writes, as if into a brothel. Patrick called the killers fellow-citizens of demons, and told Christians not to eat with them or take their gifts until they showed sorrow and freed the captives. No record says whether any captive came home.

His writings were kept, and Armagh, a church in the north of Ireland that claimed him as founder, copied the Confession. A chronicler of his own century records a bishop sent to Irish believers in 431, and never names Patrick. More than two centuries on, a hymn copied by monks called him the teacher of the Irish. Legends grew up around him centuries later, in the work of writers who came long after.

He had longed to see Britain again, but believed he was bound to stay, praying to shed his blood with his new Christians and captives. Only the gospel, the Christian message, had brought him back, he writes. He signed his Confession as written in Ireland, by Patrick the sinner, unlearned.

Key Works

  • Confession: his account of his life and his thanks to God, written in Ireland late in his life: the capture at about sixteen, six years as a slave, the escape, the dream of the Voice of the Irish, his work as a bishop, and the charge his own senior churchmen brought against him. It ends: this is my confession before I die. White notes that the title is not an admission of sins
  • Letter to the soldiers of Coroticus: written with his own hand against the soldiers of a warlord named Coroticus, who killed his new converts the day after their baptism and sold others as slaves. He tells Christians not to eat with them or take their gifts until they show sorrow and free the captives. No held text says whether any captive was freed

Legacy

What traces to them

  • His writings were kept. An incomplete copy of the Confession stands in the Book of Armagh, written between 807 and 846 by Ferdomnach, the scribe of Armagh, and the earliest title for the two writings together, Libri Sancti Patricii, is found there.
  • The monks of Bangor in the north of Ireland copied a hymn in a manuscript of about 680 to 691, headed the hymn of Saint Patrick, teacher of the Irish. It says the Lord chose him to teach the barbarian nations. The hymn's own date is disputed.
  • Armagh claimed him as its founder. White reports that Tirechan wrote, about 670, to set out the authority of the church of Armagh over rival centers.

How they're remembered

  • Later writers add what his own writings never say. Muirchu, who wrote before 699, tells that Patrick prayed that Coroticus be banished from this world and the next, and that the warlord took the form of a little fox. The Welsh History of the Britons, of about 829, makes him a swineherd for seven years, says Pope Celestine sent him, and gives him 120 years. A Life in the fifteenth-century Book of Lismore calls him chief apostle of the west of the world.

What Is Uncertain

  • Almost everything rests on Patrick himself. No independent fifth-century witness names him. Prosper of Aquitaine, a chronicler of his century, records that Palladius was sent as bishop to the Irish who believe in Christ in 431, and never mentions Patrick. So Patrick was not the first Christian or the first bishop in Ireland.
  • He gives no year. The dates are reckonings by White and the annals, and the annals disagree on his death by more than thirty years. Which of his two writings came first is not known.
  • He says he baptized many thousands and had church ministers appointed everywhere. The numbers are his own, and he is the only witness to them.
  • He says only that God made him a bishop in Ireland. He never says who made him bishop, or when. Muirchu names Germanus of Auxerre and the History of the Britons names Pope Celestine; White finds the early evidence against Celestine and calls Patrick's own silence the most telling fact.
  • He never says what the sin of his youth was, which his senior churchmen brought against him, or how they learned of it. His dearest friend, to whom he had told it, later shamed him in public.
  • He names no place for his slavery. Tradition says Slemish in County Antrim, and Bury argued for County Mayo; White reports both. Neither is Patrick's own word.
  • Who Coroticus was is an editors' reconstruction. White, following Zimmer and Bury, takes him for Ceretic, who ruled in Strathclyde about 420 to 450 or later, with his seat at Dumbarton. Stokes says only that he seems to have been a Briton.
