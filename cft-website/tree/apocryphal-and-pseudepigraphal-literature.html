Apocryphal and Pseudepigraphal Literature in Christian Transmission

From Syria to Carthage, Christians wrote and read stories of Jesus and his apostles under the apostles' own names. Churches read these books aloud, and bishops judged them by what the apostles had handed down.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 4:29

Read along

About the year 200, on a visit to Rhossus, a town on the coast of Syria near Antioch, Bishop Serapion of Antioch found the church quarreling over a gospel that carried Peter's name. He had not read it, and he thought them all sound in faith. So he gave a short answer: "If this is the only thing that seems to be causing bad feeling among you, let it be read."

In the second to fourth centuries, Christians wrote and read gospels about Jesus and tales of his first followers, the apostles, under the apostles' own names. These books never entered the New Testament: they are the Christian Apocrypha. Congregations read them aloud and argued over them, and bishops and teachers had to judge them against what had been handed down, received in the churches from the apostles.

After he left Rhossus, Serapion was told that the book's backers had minds caught up in a teaching he held false. He borrowed the book from the group that had used it from the first, and he read it through. Most of it, he found, agreed with the Savior's true teaching, but some things had been added, and he set those out for them. What he wrote to them, which Eusebius of Caesarea kept in his Church History a century later, lays down his rule: "Brothers, we receive Peter and the other apostles as we receive Christ. But the writings falsely put under their names we refuse, as people with experience. We know that such things were not handed down to us."

In the same years, a book of Paul's travels and deeds told of a girl named Thecla. In its later copies she hears Paul at Iconium, refuses the man she was promised to, and is condemned to the fire for it. The book says a storm of rain and hail puts the fire out, and she is saved. Later a governor sends her to the beasts for shaming a powerful man who had seized her in the street. In the arena she throws herself into a pit of water and baptizes herself in the name of Jesus Christ, on what she calls her last day. Afterward Paul sends her to teach the word of God.

Some Christians cited her example as permission for women to teach and baptize. Tertullian, writing in Carthage in Roman Africa, answered them that Paul did not even let a woman learn too boldly. He also told of the book's writer, a presbyter, an elder of the church: "Let them know that the presbyter in Asia who put that writing together, as if adding to Paul's fame from his own store, was found out. He confessed that he had done it for love of Paul, and he left his office."

Other books told what the Gospels leave untold. One under James's name, kept in many later copies, tells of Mary's childhood. She is born to a childless couple, and from the age of three she lives in the temple, fed, it says, by an angel's hand, until she is betrothed to Joseph.

Early in the fourth century, Eusebius, writing his Church History, set such books in order. The Acts of Paul, Paul's travels and deeds, he counted among books known to most church writers but not genuine. He set lower still the gospel of Peter and other books like it. In his judgment they carried the apostles' names but had not been handed down, and they should be cast aside entirely as absurd and godless.

Late in the fourth century, a pilgrim named Egeria came to Seleucia, on the south coast of today's Turkey. In one form of Thecla's book, this was where she taught many and fell asleep in death. On a low hill outside the city stood her church, with the small houses of men and women around it. There, Egeria writes, prayer was made at the memorial, and then the whole of the Acts of Thecla was read.

Documented Stories

Serapion Reads the Gospel of Peter Properly

Serapion, bishop of Antioch, visited the small church at Rhossus on the coast and found a dispute over a book called the Gospel of Peter. His first ruling, by his own account, was relaxed: ‘If this is the only thing that seems to be causing bad feeling among you, let it be read.’ He assumed the congregation was orthodox and had not read the book himself. Once he learned that the people pressing for it were caught up in some heresy, he obtained a copy from the group that used it, read it through, and wrote back to Rhossus withdrawing his permission. His writing, which Eusebius quotes, is measured: he found ‘many things in accordance with the true doctrine of the Saviour, but some things added to that doctrine’, and he listed them. He also explained his method: ‘we receive Peter and the other apostles as we receive Christ. But the writings falsely put under their names we refuse.’ A gospel fragment in Peter’s voice turned up in a grave at Akhmim in 1886.

The Presbyter Who Wrote the Acts of Paul ‘For Love of Paul’

Tertullian, arguing around 200 that women should not baptize, had to deal with an authority his opponents were citing: the Acts of Paul, in which Thecla, a young woman of Iconium, baptizes herself in a pool of seals and is commissioned by the apostle to teach. He dismissed it in one sentence. The book, he said, was written by a presbyter in Asia who, when convicted of it, ‘confessed that he had done it for love of Paul’ and left his office. Tertullian’s point was that a text composed by a known man in the recent past could not be used to overturn Paul’s own instruction. What the notice records, almost by accident, is a whole culture: a presbyter who admired Paul enough to write a novel about him; congregations that read it eagerly and treated Thecla as a real saint; and a church that, when it found out, disciplined the author but could not recall the book. The Acts of Paul went on circulating for centuries, and the presbyter’s name was never recorded.

In Its Own Record

This is not a community and not a tradition. It is a body of writing that passed among all of them. Its natural link to the other early movements is that many of them read it. Origen, for one, reports what some drew from a Book of James.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced

  • influenced Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition Documented — In his Commentary on Matthew, Origen cites a tradition from the Gospel of Peter and the Book of James. That book is now called the Protevangelium. It says the brothers of Jesus were sons of Joseph by a wife he had before Mary. Origen shares its aim, which is to honor Mary's virginity.
  • influenced The Church of Aquileia Documented — Rufinus translated the Recognitions, a Greek work then read as by Clement of Rome, into Latin. In his preface he says the task had been laid on him to put Clement into 'our language'. Gennadius later lists it among Rufinus's translations.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

For centuries Christians copied and read stories that never entered the Bible. One was a book under James's name, which tells of Mary's parents and childhood. Bishops and teachers judged such books by what the apostles had handed down, and still the books were read.

Legacy

A book under James's name, the Protevangelium of James, tells of Mary's parents and her childhood in the Temple. Anna and Joachim and the presentation in the Temple came from it into the church's calendar and its sacred images. Yet the church never accepted the book as scripture. The ox and the ass at the manger come from Pseudo-Matthew, a later Latin retelling. The apocryphal Acts tell how the apostles died: Peter crucified head down, Philip hung by his ankles. The verdicts went on as well. In 367 Athanasius of Alexandria called such books "an invention of heretics". Jerome still knew the Acts of Paul and Thecla, and counted them "among the apocryphal writings". Later, writing to a mother about her daughter's reading, he said: "Let her avoid all apocryphal writings". If she read them, she should understand "that it requires infinite discretion to look for gold in the midst of dirt."

Open Questions

  • What the teaching at Rhossus was. Serapion was told only that the backers' minds were caught up in some heresy. He writes that he saw what one Marcianus held, a man who contradicted himself, not understanding what he said. He calls those who had used the book from the first, the successors of those who began it, Docetae. He says no more of what either held, and no other writer of his time ties that name to Rhossus.
  • What Serapion did next. He promised the church at Rhossus to come again soon, and the record does not say that he came. He set out for them the things added to the gospel, but Eusebius quotes only the lines about his rule and his reading, so the list is lost.
  • Origen and a Book of James. Origen, in his Commentary on Matthew, reports that some, drawing on the Gospel according to Peter or a Book of James, held that the brothers of Jesus were Joseph's sons by an earlier wife. Those who say so, he writes, wish to keep Mary's virginity honored to the end. Whether his Book of James is the book that survives under James's name, the old sources do not say, and modern editors differ.
  • Joseph in the book under James's name. When Mary is given to him by a sign, Joseph at first refuses, saying that he has children and is an old man. The text printed in English comes from an eleventh-century manuscript, and many other forms of the book exist.
  • Eusebius's reasons. Of the books that heretics cited under the apostles' names, among them the gospels of Peter, Thomas and Matthias and the Acts of Andrew and John, Eusebius says that no church writer in the succession had thought them worth mention. Their style, he says, was unlike the apostles', and their thoughts and purpose were so far from true teaching that they showed themselves to be the fictions of heretics.
  • The presbyter and his judge. Tertullian names no judge, and says that the presbyter left his office. Jerome, nearly two centuries later, says he was convicted by John and resigned his office of presbyter. Jerome also counts the fable of a lion baptized by Paul among the apocryphal writings. In Tertullian's Latin, the words "apud Ioannem", before John, stand only as an early editor's conjecture.
  • Thecla's end. The forms of her book differ. In one, she goes to Seleucia, enlightens many with the word of God and falls asleep there. In the other, she lives in a cave at Seleucia for seventy-two years, and later goes to Rome and is buried near Paul's tomb.
  • Thecla's shrine. Egeria writes of numberless cells of men and women around the church, and of a deaconess named Marthana who ruled the cells of the renouncers and virgins.
  • Egeria. Her name, and the date of her journey in the late fourth century, are editors' reckoning from her own account.
  • The Gospel of Peter found. A fragment of a gospel told in Peter's voice was found in a grave at Akhmim, in Upper Egypt, in 1886, and published in 1892. That it is the book Serapion read at Rhossus is a modern identification.
  • The Apocalypse of Peter. The Muratorian fragment, whose date and place are modern reckonings, says that some would not have it read in the church.

How Sure the Record Is

Large and uneven. Around a dozen works survive - the apocryphal Acts of Andrew, John, Philip, Peter and Paul, the Protevangelium of James, the Infancy Gospel of Thomas, the Gospel and Apocalypse of Peter, the Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs, the Revelation of Moses, the Narrative of Zosimus. Dates and provenance are disputed for nearly all of them, and the received text is often centuries later than the tradition behind it.
