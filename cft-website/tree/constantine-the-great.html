Constantine the Great

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Imperial and Juridical Christianity.

Known for
Roman emperor who, with Licinius, gave Christians freedom of worship, and who called the bishops to the council at Nicaea.

About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Marching on Rome against his rival Maxentius, Constantine prayed, the church historian Eusebius says, that God would show him who he was. Constantine told Eusebius the rest himself, years later and under oath. At midday a cross of light stood above the sun, with the words "Conquer by this," and that night Christ came to him in sleep and told him to make a likeness of the sign. Eusebius admits the story would be hard to believe from anyone else. Lactantius knows only a dream, telling Constantine to mark the sign of Christ on his soldiers' shields.

Maxentius was driven into the Tiber at the broken bridge, Lactantius says. Soon Constantine and Licinius met at Milan and decreed that Christians and all others should be free to follow the religion each thought best. The same decree ordered the places where Christians met to be given back.

At Alexandria the presbyter Arius argued that if the Father begat the Son, there was a time when the Son was not. Constantine wrote to Arius and his bishop that the quarrel was trifling and should stay in private thought. Then he called the bishops to Nicaea and sat among them, "your fellow-servant," as he wrote. Eusebius, the bishop of Caesarea, told his church that the emperor urged one added word, homoousios, "of the same essence," and explained that the Son did not come from the Father by division or cutting off.

Only when he knew he was dying, Eusebius says, did he seek baptism. He had hoped to receive it in the Jordan. Near Nicomedia he told the bishops, "The time is arrived which I have long hoped for, with an earnest desire and prayer that I might obtain the salvation of God." His body was carried to Constantinople and laid in the Church of the Apostles. The church historian Sozomen says later Christian emperors and bishops were buried there too.

Key Works

  • Edict of Milan, with Licinius: Christians and all others free to follow the religion each thought best
  • Letter to Alexander and Arius: to Bishop Alexander of Alexandria and the presbyter Arius; calls their quarrel trifling and asks both to forgive
  • Letter to the churches after Nicaea: one day for Easter everywhere, and obedience to the council

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Nicaea did not end the quarrel. Five bishops would not sign the creed because of the word homoousios, Eusebius of Nicomedia among them. Eusebius of Caesarea signed, he told his church, with peace in view.
  • Constantine also wrote to Eusebius of Caesarea asking for fifty copies of the sacred scriptures, on parchment, for the churches of the city that bore his name.
  • After the council he wrote to the churches that Easter must fall on one day everywhere, so that some would not be fasting while others feasted.

How they're remembered

  • Eusebius of Caesarea honors him as the first sovereign baptized in a church of the martyrs.
  • The church historian Sozomen calls him a powerful protector of the Christian religion and the first emperor zealous for the church.
  • His letter about Easter speaks of the Jews with contempt, and Eusebius of Caesarea copies it whole.

At Major Councils

  • Council of Arles, 314: Called the council and heard the appeal from it
  • Council of Nicaea, 325: Called the council and sat with it; wrote its letter on Easter

What Is Uncertain

  • The two early tellings of the sign do not agree: Eusebius of Caesarea has a cross of light in the sky and then a dream, Lactantius a dream alone. Eusebius adds that at dawn Constantine called in workers in gold and precious stones to make the sign as a standard, and that he saw it with his own eyes.
  • Not everyone accepted Eusebius of Caesarea's account of the baptism. A Syriac chronicle that goes under Zachariah of Mitylene's name says Eusebius got it wrong. The emperor was not baptized at the end of his life, it says, since the story of his conversion by Rome's bishop, Silvester, is kept there in writing and in pictures. Pagans told yet another story, that he turned Christian to be cleansed after the killing of his son Crispus, and Sozomen calls it an invention.
