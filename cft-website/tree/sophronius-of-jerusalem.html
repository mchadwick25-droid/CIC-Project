Damascus, today in Syria · Jerusalem

Sophronius of Jerusalem

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Byzantine Imperial Church (Heraclian & Iconoclast Eras).

Known for
Monk of Theodosius and disciple of John Moschus, then patriarch of Jerusalem in the 630s. He opposed the teaching that Christ acted with one operation, and his synodal letter was received as true to the faith by the Third Council of Constantinople in 680-681. Later chroniclers say he was patriarch when Jerusalem surrendered to the caliph Umar.
Also called
Sophronius the Sophist (John Moschus)
Dates
Daniel Papebroch dates his election to 634 and his death to March 638. A note in J. D. Mansi's collection of the councils says he was still a monk in May 633. Theophanes, writing in the early ninth century, puts the surrender of Jerusalem after a two-year siege, and Eutychius of Alexandria says Sophronius died after four years in office. Angelo Mai gives about 640 for his death. No source used here gives his birth year
Place
Damascus, by his own poem; the monastery of Theodosius, near Jerusalem, where he was a monk; Alexandria, with John Moschus; Jerusalem, as patriarch
Role
Monk and writer; patriarch of Jerusalem

About This Person

Stephen, bishop of Dor, told a synod at Rome in 649 how Sophronius had once led him to Calvary in Jerusalem. There, at the place of the cross, Sophronius bound him with an oath. You will answer to God, crucified here for us, he said, if you hold back while his faith is in danger. He would go himself, he said, but the Saracen invasion hindered him. So Stephen was to go from end to end of the earth until he reached the apostolic see at Rome.

Sophronius of Jerusalem tells his own beginnings in a short poem, printed in Angelo Mai's Latin. He came from Damascus, and his parents were Myro and Plinthas. He never married, and became a monk in the monastery of Theodosius, near Jerusalem. John Moschus, his teacher, calls him the Sophist, a man of learning.

At Alexandria, still a monk, he opposed the patriarch Cyrus's teaching that Christ acted with one operation, or energy. Sergius, patriarch of Constantinople, wrote that Sophronius held two operations. Sergius says Sophronius could not bring texts of the Fathers that spoke of two in those very words. In the end, he writes, it was agreed that Sophronius would speak no more of one or two.

He was then made patriarch of Jerusalem. In the letter he sent out on taking office, he wrote how dear quiet was to him now. The clergy, monks and people of the city, he said, had forced him into office like tyrants. In a Christmas sermon he mourned that Bethlehem lay close by, but fear of the Saracen sword kept him and his people at home.

Theophanes, writing in the early ninth century, says Sophronius won from the caliph Umar a pledge of safety for Palestine. When Umar asked to be shown the temple Solomon built, Theophanes says, Sophronius wept. This is truly the abomination of desolation, he said, standing in the holy place.

The Third Council of Constantinople, in 680 and 681, found his letter true to the faith. It ruled that his name be placed in the diptychs, the lists of names read out in the liturgy.

Key Works

  • Synodal letter: the letter he sent out on taking office as patriarch, setting out the two natures and two operations of Christ. Read out at the Third Council of Constantinople. In Patrologia Graeca 87.3, in Greek with Latin
  • Miracles of Cyrus and John: the healings at the shrine of the martyrs Cyrus and John near Alexandria. He says he wrote them because they cured his eyes. In Angelo Mai's edition, in Greek with Latin
  • Sermons and poems: among them a Christmas sermon given when Bethlehem was cut off by the Saracens, and short poems in the Anacreontic meter, one of them on his longing for the holy places of Jerusalem

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The Third Council of Constantinople, in 680-681, examined "the synodal letter of Sophronius of holy memory" and received it "as orthodox". It decreed that his name be put in the diptychs, in Henry Percival's English of the acts.
  • Stephen, bishop of Dor, told the Lateran synod of 649 that Sophronius had sent him to Rome under oath at Calvary.
  • Symeon of Thessalonica, quoted by Daniel Papebroch, says Sophronius as patriarch restored the order of Saint Sabas's services, lost when barbarians ravaged those places, and that John of Damascus later renewed it.
  • John of Damascus cites his Miracles of Cyrus and John in his defense of icons.

How they're remembered

  • Theophanes calls him a great champion of piety.
  • Maximus the Confessor calls him the divine and great Sophronius, and says Sophronius stayed with him and the other foreign monks in Africa.
  • Leontius of Neapolis, in his Life of John the Almsgiver as Daniel Papebroch quotes it, calls John Moschus and Sophronius wise men and good counselors to that patriarch of Alexandria.
  • Eusèbe Renaudot says the Arab historians also mention him with praise, since the city was taken while he governed its church.

What Is Uncertain

  • Theophanes's account of Umar at Jerusalem, with Sophronius's words on the abomination of desolation, was written in the early ninth century. Theophanes's Greek can be read as saying it of the temple or of Umar; the Latin printed with it takes Umar. No text of Sophronius's own time gives those words.
  • The year he became patriarch. Johann Albert Fabricius, in Harles's edition of his Bibliotheca Graeca, gives 629. A note in J. D. Mansi's collection of the councils says he was still a monk in May 633, and Daniel Papebroch gives 634. Eutychius of Alexandria puts it in the second year of Umar's rule.
  • The year of his death. Papebroch gives March 638, a few months after the city's surrender. Angelo Mai says about 640. A note in the Bibliotheca Patrum gives 14 March 636.
  • What happened at Constantinople. Sergius, patriarch of Constantinople, says Sophronius agreed to keep silent on one or two operations. Eutychius says Sergius turned against him after gifts came from Cyrus, patriarch of Alexandria.
  • Whether the Sophist of John Moschus is the patriarch. Papebroch and Angelo Mai hold that they are one man, and Cyrille Charon calls it hardly open to doubt. No held source says otherwise. The poem and the Miracles give the author as a monk of Theodosius from Damascus.
  • His monastery. His poem and the last of the Miracles say Theodosius. Fabricius says Saint Sabas, without a source.
  • The Life of Mary of Egypt is given to him by Nicephorus Callistus. Papebroch says nothing in it settles whether it is his, though its style is very different from that of the Miracles.
  • The Spiritual Meadow was cited under his name by John of Damascus and the Second Council of Nicaea. It is John Moschus's book, written for him.
