The High Medieval Era · 1113-1153 · Cîteaux and Clairvaux, eastern France

Bernard of Clairvaux

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Cistercian Monasticism.

Known for
Young Burgundian noble who brought his brothers and more than thirty companions into the poor new monastery of Cîteaux in 1113 and two years later became abbot of the new house of Clairvaux. His sermons on the Song of Songs, his letters to popes, his move against Abelard in 1140, his book of advice to Pope Eugenius III and his preaching of the Second Crusade, which turned back from Damascus with nothing done in 1148, made popes and kings ask his word; Geoffrey counts a hundred and sixty monasteries under his care when he died.
Also called
Saint Bernard, Bernardus Clarevallensis
Dates
Born around 1090 by William of Saint-Thierry's reckoning, who says he was about twenty-three when he entered Cîteaux in 1113 and gives a variant reading of twenty-two; in 1091 by Mabillon's chronology, as Samuel Eales translates it. Mabillon puts the founding of Clairvaux in 1115 from the Cistercian Exordium and the tablet on Bernard's tomb, the Song sermons from Advent 1135, the council of Sens in 1140, the crusade preached in 1146, the return of King Louis in 1149, and Bernard's death on 18 August 1153 in his sixty-third year. Jan Hus's book on the Church is dated 6 February 1413 by its editor
Place
Fontaines near Dijon, in Burgundy, his father's castle by Mabillon; Cîteaux; Clairvaux, which William places in the territory of Langres, not far from the river Aube, in a valley once called the Valley of Wormwood; Sens, for the council of 1140; Chartres, where Letter 256 says he was chosen leader of the crusade; Mainz, for the letter on Ralph; Damascus, for the retreat of 1148
Role
Abbot of Clairvaux; preacher and letter writer; adviser of popes; preacher of the Second Crusade

About This Person

In 1113 a young nobleman of Burgundy named Bernard left his father's house to become a monk, and his brothers went with him. The eldest, Guy, saw the youngest, Nivard, playing in the street, and told him that all the family land would now fall to him alone. Nivard answered that heaven was for them and earth for him, and the division was not fair.

Bernard, with more than thirty companions, his friend William, who heard him preach, says, entered Cîteaux, a poor new monastery, where monks kept the old Rule of Saint Benedict strictly. Nivard followed a little later. The house was so small it had almost lost hope of lasting. In time, popes and kings would come to ask Bernard's word.

Two years later, the abbot of Cîteaux sent him with a band of monks to found a new house, and made him their abbot, the head of a monastery. The place was Clairvaux, an old robbers' den. They lived on barley bread and stews of beech leaves.

From 1135 he preached to his monks on the Song of Songs, the Bible's book of love poems, which he read as the love between God and the soul. He said, "Love seeks no cause beyond itself, and no fruit. Its fruit is the loving itself. I love because I love; I love in order to love." His Song sermons were read for centuries.

In 1140 he moved against Peter Abelard, a teacher whose books were read far and wide. Bernard said they let human reason claim everything and leave nothing for faith. At a meeting of bishops at Sens, Bernard writes, he read out the charges, and Abelard walked out and appealed to the pope in Rome, over their heads. A hostile pupil of Abelard's mocked the bishops as drunk and half asleep. Pope Innocent II condemned Abelard and ordered him shut in a monastery and his books burned.

In 1145 a monk Bernard had trained became Pope Eugenius III. The king of France had asked him again and again to speak for a new crusade, a great armed pilgrimage to the East. His monk Geoffrey says he refused until the pope ordered him. In 1146 he preached it, promising pardon for every sin confessed with a contrite heart. That pardon was for all who took the cross, putting its sign on their shoulder as the mark of those going east.

That year a monk of his own order, who had left his cloister, stirred crowds along the Rhine to kill Jews. Bernard wrote that the Jews must not be killed or even driven out. In the same letter he called their scattering a just penalty.

In 1148 the kings turned back from Damascus, the Muslim city of Syria, with nothing done. Bernard wrote that the armies were killed by the sword or by hunger. A historian of the crusader kingdom blamed treachery, but said he could not find out for certain who was guilty. Bernard blamed the crusaders' sins. The blame fell on him, and he wrote to Eugenius, "If one of the two must happen, I would rather people grumble against me than against God. It is good for me if he will use me as a shield. I gladly take on myself the cursing tongues of slanderers and the poisoned darts of blasphemers, so that they do not reach him."

He wrote the pope a book of advice, On Consideration. Peter, whose place the pope held, never rode a white horse or went in gold and silk, he said. In such things the pope followed not Peter but the emperor Constantine. It was a friend's counsel. In 1413 Jan Hus copied those lines into his book on the Church, to argue that the popes of his own day, living in pomp, did not truly hold Peter's place.

He died at Clairvaux in 1153. Geoffrey counts nearly seven hundred monks in that valley, and a hundred and sixty monasteries under his care. His editor notes that the eighty-sixth sermon on the Song breaks off where death stopped him.

Quotations and references

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “Love seeks no cause beyond itself, and no fruit. Its fruit is the loving itself. I love because I love; I love in order to love.”
    As printed
    “It seeks neither cause, nor consequence, beyond itself. It is its own fruit, and its own object and usefulness. I love, because I love ; I love, that I may love.”
    Bernard, Sermon 83 on the Song of Songs, 4, tr. Samuel J. Eales
    The rendering follows the Latin, Amor praeter se non requirit causam, non fructum. Fructus ejus, usus ejus. Amo quia amo; amo, ut amem (PL183 105304-105306), whose scan reads one word of the first clause with a fault, so the Latin is not printed here. Eales renders fructum as consequence in the first clause and then fructus as fruit and usus as object and usefulness; the rendering has fruit for fructum and joins fructus and usus in one sentence, its fruit is the loving itself.
  • In our modern English
    “If one of the two must happen, I would rather people grumble against me than against God. It is good for me if he will use me as a shield. I gladly take on myself the cursing tongues of slanderers and the poisoned darts of blasphemers, so that they do not reach him.”
    As printed
    “malo in nos murmur hominum, quam in Deum esse”
    Bernard, On Consideration II, 1, first clause of the passage, Latin of PL182
    Only the first clause is printed, because the rest of the passage (PL182 75606-75611, and Geoffrey's quotation of it at VP 20932-20937) stands in two scans that each carry small faults, and no English of On Consideration is held. The rendering follows the Latin for the whole passage; where both scans can be read they agree, except that PL182 begins Etsi necesse sit and Geoffrey begins Si necesse sit.

Key Works

  • Sermons on the Song of Songs: eighty-six sermons to his monks on the Bible's love poems, begun in Advent 1135 by Mabillon's reading of the second sermon; the soul recovers its likeness to the Bridegroom, who is Christ, by a chaste and holy love. The eighty-sixth breaks off where death stopped him, by his editor's note. Geoffrey, as Mabillon quotes him, calls them a magnificent search into the mystical sense that also teaches the moral to edification
  • On Consideration (De consideratione): a book of advice to Pope Eugenius III, who had been his monk; its second book answers the blame for the failure of the crusade, and its fourth tells the pope that Peter is not known ever to have gone adorned with gems or silks, covered with gold or riding a white horse, and that in these things Eugenius has succeeded Constantine, not Peter. Latin only is held here: no English of the book is in the Library
  • Letters: Eales translates Mabillon's edition, letters 146 to 380 in his second volume. Those used here: 188 and 189, on Abelard; 237 and 238, on the election of Eugenius; 256, in which he says he was chosen at Chartres as leader of the expedition against his will; 363, which preaches the crusade and says the Jews must not be killed or driven out while calling their scattering a just penalty; and 365, to the archbishop of Mainz, on the monk Ralph. Letter 363 also tells the brave knight that he has a battle he may fight without danger, where it is glory to conquer and gain to die. Mabillon sums up that from 1130 Bernard energetically backed Innocent II for eight years in the papal schism, and a letter of 1140 tells Innocent that the schism of Leo was healed

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Geoffrey counts nearly seven hundred monks at Clairvaux on the day Bernard died, and a hundred and sixty monasteries brought to repentance under his care.
  • Mabillon's chronology lists the houses Clairvaux founded year by year, among them Mellifont in Ireland under 1141, Alvastern in Sweden under 1143 and Alcobaça in Portugal under 1147.
  • In 1413 Jan Hus quoted the lines of On Consideration on Peter and Constantine in his book on the Church, to argue that Eugenius ought to be a vicar in poverty and humility, following Peter; Schaff calls it the most famous book ever written on the papal office and says Hus quoted it much.
  • Calvin quotes Sermon 61 on the Song of Songs on the wounds of the Savior, and Hus cites Sermon 12.
  • The tradition entry for the Cistercians says his Song sermons fed a warm, loving strand of Western prayer and that English writers two hundred years on still quoted him by name.
  • Mabillon says Pope Alexander III, at the Mass of Bernard's canonization, read the Gospel kept for the holy Doctors of the Church; no held text gives the year.

How they're remembered

  • William of Saint-Thierry, his friend, says Bernard kept in his heart and often on his lips the question, Bernard, Bernard, why have you come?
  • Geoffrey says that when the news of the army's ruin reached France a father brought his blind son to Bernard, who prayed that if his preaching had come from God, God would show it, and the boy said that he saw. Geoffrey also says that the eastern church did not win its freedom by that expedition but the heavenly church was filled.
  • Joseph ha-Kohen, a Jewish chronicler of 1575, says God sent Saint Bernard to the people of France, who told them not to speak to the Jews either for good or ill, since to touch them was to touch the apple of Jesus's eye; he says that if God's mercy had not sent this priest, not one of them would have escaped.
  • Luther, as his editor quotes him, says that if a God-fearing and holy monk ever lived on earth, it was Saint Bernard of Clairvaux; the editor cites the Erlangen edition and the line itself is not held.

What Is Uncertain

  • Witnesses. William of Saint-Thierry, his friend, wrote book I of the Life and says he heard Bernard preach; Geoffrey, a monk of Clairvaux, wrote books III to V, and both admire him. Alan's Life of Bernard is a later compilation. Berengar of Poitiers, Abelard's pupil, is hostile. William of Tyre, writing about a generation later, is the crusader kingdom's historian. Hus wrote about 260 years after Bernard, and Calvin and Joseph ha-Kohen about 400. The years given by Mabillon are an editor's.
  • His birth. William's age at entry gives about 1090 and Mabillon says 1091; William's age is twenty-three in the text and twenty-two in a variant. At his death Mabillon counts the sixty-third year.
  • The companions. William says more than thirty entered Cîteaux with him in 1113; Mabillon's chronology says thirty. The Library's scan garbles the house's name in Mabillon's line.
  • Nivard's words. William's Life, in a damaged scan, has Guy tell the youngest brother that all their land now looks to him alone, and Nivard answer that heaven is for them and earth for him, and that the division is not fairly made. Alan's Life has Guy tell him he may play safely, since he will have much land, and Nivard answer: Let this division be cursed, that you have heaven and I earth. The sense is the same and the words differ. William says Nivard stayed at home with his father and followed his brothers a little later, and that neither father, kin nor friends could hold him. William also gives, in a damaged line, that mothers hid their sons, wives held back their husbands and friends turned friends away; only the part about wives and friends is safe to read.
  • His brothers. William says Bernard won his brothers one by one, the youngest alone left at home; Bartholomew, who was younger than the other brothers, and Andrew, who was himself younger than Bernard, went with him, so the brothers who went were not all older.
  • Clairvaux. William says the abbot Stephen sent Bernard's brothers to build the house and set Bernard over them as abbot, to the wonder of older and abler men who feared for his youth and weak body. Mabillon says Clairvaux was founded not by Theobald, as some think who confuse its move in 1135 with its foundation, but by Hugo, Count of Troyes, and that the Exordium and the tomb tablet plainly say 1115.
  • Sens, 1140. Bernard's Letter 189 says he went much against his will, read out articles from Abelard's books, and Abelard left unwilling to listen and appealed. Berengar mocks the bishops as drunk and dozing, with one answer heard as we condemn and another as we swim, and Berengar later asks that anything he said against the man be read as a jest, not in earnest. Mabillon adds that Geoffrey of Auxerre, in his Commentary on the Apocalypse, says Abelard grew confused and could not speak, and that Otto of Freising says he feared the crowd; neither is held. Abelard's own confession of faith to Heloise says logic has made him hateful to the world and that he will not be a philosopher in a way that kicks against Paul. Letter 188 says human reason usurps everything for itself and leaves nothing for faith.
  • The sentence on Abelard. Innocent II's letter, as Mabillon prints it, commands that Peter Abelard and Arnold of Brescia be shut up separately in houses of religion and their books burned wherever found. Mabillon adds, from Peter of Cluny's letters, which are not held, that Abelard gave up his appeal on Peter's advice, was reconciled with Bernard and the pope, and died in 1142 at the monastery of Saint Marcel near Chalon, not at Cluny.
  • The election of Eugenius. Letter 237 tells the cardinals that God forgive them: they had chosen him lord of all who had chosen to be a door-keeper in the house of his God. Letter 238 tells Eugenius that Bernard dares not call him son any longer, since the son has become father and the father son.
  • The crusade's start. Geoffrey says the king of the Franks asked Bernard more than once and papal letters urged him, and he would not speak until the pope ordered him, as the pope's tongue. Mabillon says Louis consulted him and he left the decision to the pope, then that Eugenius had him chosen chief advocate at Chartres in 1146. William of Tyre says he was chosen first of the preachers Eugenius sent out. Mabillon's note says Bernard refused the title of general in chief. Letter 256, which says he was chosen leader against his will, is headed 1146 by its editor, but its own text speaks of the losses of the former army, and Mabillon's chronology says that after his return in 1149 the king was making preparations for a new expedition and the Cistercians dissuaded him, as Abbot Robert's chronicle reports under 1150. The dating of that letter is open.
  • The Rhine, 1146. Letter 365 calls Ralph only a monk and a hermit who claims the right to preach; the editor calls him a certain monk and, from Otto of Freising, who is not held, says Bernard brought him to go back to his monastery, to the people's anger. That he was a monk of Bernard's own order comes from the tradition entry's story of the Rhine, which is marked as from reference summaries, and is not in the held letter. Letter 363 forbids persecuting, killing or driving out the Jews and in the same lines calls their dispersal the just penalty of so great a crime and their state a hard captivity under Christian princes. Joseph ha-Kohen, four centuries later, remembers Bernard as sent by God to save them; both stand. Joseph's account is held in Julien Sée's French and, for the same passage, in Bialloblotzky's English.
  • Why the crusade failed. Bernard's On Consideration says the crusaders were laid low in the desert, killed by the sword or wasted by hunger, that quarreling was poured out on the princes, and that, as Israel fell in the desert for its sins, so did these. William of Tyre says the kings and princes were forced to go back from Damascus with nothing done, our sins requiring it, gives rival rumors of treachery (the jealousy of local barons, the prince of Antioch, a bribe from the enemy) and says he has not found out for certain who was guilty. Geoffrey says the heavenly church was filled though the eastern church was not freed. William of Newburgh and Otto of Freising are cited by Mabillon for other judgments and are not held.
  • The shield. On Consideration II, in the Latin of PL182, has Bernard say that if one of the two must happen he would rather the murmuring of men were against him than against God. Geoffrey quotes the passage from the same book, but where PL182 begins with Etsi, Geoffrey's quotation begins with Si, and Geoffrey's copy has a few small faults of the scan where PL182's has others. Both scans are damaged in the passage, and no English of On Consideration is held.
  • Hus. His book on the Church gives the Peter and Constantine lines as Bernard's, from book IV, chapter 1, and says that Eugenius ought to be a vicar in poverty and humility, following Peter, and that no one can truly rule in Christ's stead or Peter's without following him in his life; Schaff notes that Hus has the words not in this way where Bernard has the word namely, spoken in irony, and neges where Bernard has neget. Schaff, his editor, calls On Consideration the most famous book ever written on the papal office, notes that Eugenius was Bernard's spiritual son whom Bernard addressed as most holy father, and says both Ultramontanes and Gallicans claim the treatise for their side.
  • Not held in the Library: Otto of Freising, Odo of Deuil on the crusade, the account at Vézelay, Geoffrey's Commentary on the Apocalypse, Peter the Venerable's letters, Ephraim of Bonn's own chronicle, the Exordium magnum, the year of Bernard's canonization, and any English of the Lives or of On Consideration.
