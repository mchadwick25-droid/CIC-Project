The Reformation Era · c. 1560-1660 · England, New England

England, today in United Kingdom · New England, today in United States

Puritanism

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Puritans were the English Protestants who thought the Elizabethan settlement had stopped halfway. That a church with bishops, surplices and a set liturgy still carried too much of what the Reformation was meant to clear away. Their real distinctiveness lay less in church furniture than in the inner life. They kept diaries, examined their own hearts in writing, and made the conversion narrative — an account of how God had brought you to faith, told aloud before a congregation. A normal thing for ordinary men and women to produce. Pressed at home, thousands sailed for New England from 1630. Those who stayed came to dominate the Long Parliament and the Westminster Assembly, and after the Restoration of 1660 were ejected from the Church of England altogether.

In Its Own Record

Grew Out Of

  • The English Reformation & Elizabethan Settlement Documented Major — The Admonition to the Parliament of 1572 speaks for ministers forced to sign their assent to the Prayer Book. Its authors, John Field and Thomas Wilcox, were two clergy of the English church. They were sent to Newgate prison that July.

Gave Rise To

  • English Old Dissent — Presbyterians and Congregationalists Documented Major — Richard Baxter grew up in a home that neighbors mocked as Puritan. In 1662 he was silenced with nearly two thousand other ministers. His memoir shows them going on as Nonconformists, and some kept preaching until they were put in prison.
  • The Baptists Widely Accepted Major — John Smyth gave up the orders he had taken from the bishop of Lincoln. In 1911 W. J. McGlothlin wrote that some Puritans left the English church to found their own churches, and that Smyth's flock at Gainsborough was one. In Amsterdam around 1609 it became the first English Baptist church.
  • The Quakers (Religious Society of Friends) Documented Major — George Fox's Journal says James Nayler was put out of an Independent church when he took up Fox's message. Fox also won many from 'a company of shattered Baptists' in Nottinghamshire. William Penn told the Quakers' rise as the next step after the Puritan groups of the English Reformation.

Influenced By

  • The Reformed Cities - Zurich & Geneva Documented — The Admonition to the Parliament of 1572 sends the Archbishop of York to read the 'learned Epistle' Theodore Beza wrote him. Letters from Beza, a minister at Geneva, and from Rudolph Gualter of Zurich were printed with it. They gave the Puritan case the weight of two famous names.

Influenced

  • The Evangelical Awakening in New England Documented — John Winthrop's journal says that Thomas Shepard came to Massachusetts in October 1635, with other ministers. In 1746 Jonathan Edwards quoted from Shepard's book on the ten virgins. He did so in his study of true and false faith.
  • The Methodist Revival Widely Accepted — In 1747 John Wesley read The History of the Puritans. He wrote with anger of the persecution that drove 'those venerable men' out of the Church. Historians of Methodism, Henry Rack among them, see a Puritan strand in the Wesleys' family and faith.

Argued Against

  • The English Reformation & Elizabethan Settlement Documented — The Admonition to the Parliament of 1572 told Parliament that England was still far from 'a church rightly reformed.' It called the Prayer Book an imperfect book, picked out of the Mass book.

Opposed By

  • The English Reformation & Elizabethan Settlement Documented — At Hampton Court in January 1604, King James heard the Puritan case against the Prayer Book. Leaving the room, he said he would make them conform 'or I will harry them out of this Land.' In 1572 the authors of the Admonition had been sent to prison.

Broke With

  • The Restoration and Georgian Church of England Documented Major — The Act of Uniformity of 1662 took away the posts of every minister who would not state his assent to the Prayer Book. Richard Baxter wrote that on St Bartholomew's Day, 24 August 1662, about 1,800 or 2,000 ministers 'were silenced and cast out.'

Sources

  • The diaries: Lady Margaret Hoby (1599-1605, earliest known Englishwoman's diary); Nehemiah Wallington's notebooks (London artisan); Samuel Ward's student diary (primary)
  • The conversion narratives: Thomas Shepard's Cambridge, MA congregation relations (God's Plot, ed. McGiffert; The Confessions, ed. Selement & Woolley) (primary) — Lay men's and women's conversion accounts as delivered for church admission. Worth remembering the setting: these narratives were performed for a gate-keeping audience, which shaped how they were told.
  • Practical divinity as used: Perkins (A Golden Chain; casuistry), Rogers's Seven Treatises, Bayly's Practice of Piety, Dod & Cleaver on household government (primary) — The how-to shelf of godly living - catechesis and conduct rather than system.
  • The church-order record: An Admonition to Parliament (1572), Marprelate tracts (1588-89), subscription/deprivation records, the Dedham classis minutes (1582-89) (primary) — The movement's own semi-clandestine presbytery keeping minutes inside the establishment.
  • Collinson, The Elizabethan Puritan Movement (1967), with Coffey & Lim (eds.), The Cambridge Companion to Puritanism (2008) (secondary) — Field-defining classic and current survey.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 6 (reference)
