Puritanism

England and New England, c. 1560 to 1660 — Protestants who thought the reform had stopped halfway, and who wrote down the state of their own souls in extraordinary detail.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 0:57

Read along

Puritans were the English Protestants who thought the Elizabethan settlement had stopped halfway. That a church with bishops, surplices and a set liturgy still carried too much of what the Reformation was meant to clear away. Their real distinctiveness lay less in church furniture than in the inner life. They kept diaries, examined their own hearts in writing, and made the conversion narrative — an account of how God had brought you to faith, told aloud before a congregation. A normal thing for ordinary men and women to produce. Pressed at home, thousands sailed for New England from 1630. Those who stayed came to dominate the Long Parliament and the Westminster Assembly, and after the Restoration of 1660 were ejected from the Church of England altogether.

Documented Stories

Nehemiah Wallington Tries to Die

Nehemiah Wallington was the son of a turner in Eastcheap and became one himself, and from the age of twenty he wrote: some fifty notebooks over forty years, of which seven survive, recording his sins, his prayers, the sermons he heard, the news, and the state of his soul. The earliest, begun in 1618, is the record of a breakdown. Convinced that he was a reprobate, that his sins were unpardonable and that God had abandoned him, he tried repeatedly to kill himself and wrote each attempt down: he stood at a window meaning to throw himself out and was seen; he took a knife to bed and his maidservant took it away; he drank a cup of what he hoped was poison and it made him sick; he tried to drown himself in the Thames and could not go through with it; he lay down under a cart. He counted eleven attempts. He also recorded what stopped him: a sermon on Christ’s pity, his mother sitting with him, his father taking him to a godly minister, the moment when a text came to him that he could not have been meant to hear if he were damned. He married in 1621 and the fits passed; the later notebooks are about business, his children’s deaths, the news from the Parliament, and his continual accounting of himself before God. He wrote it all, he said, so that his children would know how God had dealt with him.

Lady Hoby Writes Down Her Day

Margaret Hoby was twenty-eight, three times married, and mistress of Hackness near Scarborough when she began, in August 1599, a diary that runs for six years. It is spare. A typical day: ‘After private prayers I went about the house and read of the Bible, then I dressed my patients, and after dinner I talked with some of my neighbours, and then I went to private examination and prayer, and so to supper; after, to public prayers and lecture, and then to bed.’ The ‘patients’ were the sick of the parish, whose wounds and sores she dressed herself, sometimes recording the case; the ‘lecture’ was the evening exposition by her chaplain Richard Rhodes; ‘private examination’ was the Puritan discipline of reviewing the day’s conduct before God, and the diary was its instrument — she notes when she was ‘negligent’, when she talked idly, when she was angry with a servant, and once when she ‘was so ill that I could not pray’. She records reading Perkins and Greenham, hearing sermons at Scarborough and York, and her husband’s lawsuits against Catholic neighbors who had ridden through the house drunk. There is no confession of feeling in the modern sense and almost no description; the entries stop in 1605 without explanation. The diary is the earliest surviving by an Englishwoman, and it is the native genre of her tradition exactly: a life kept as a ledger, so that she and God could see whether the time had been spent.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Shaped by Geneva and grown inside the English church, which it thought only half reformed. Its stress on a personal turn to God later fed the revival in New England.

Descended from

  • grew out of The English Reformation & Elizabethan Settlement Documented Major — The Admonition to the Parliament of 1572 speaks for ministers forced to sign their assent to the Prayer Book. Its authors, John Field and Thomas Wilcox, were two clergy of the English church. They were sent to Newgate prison that July.

Direct descendants

  • gave rise to English Old Dissent — Presbyterians and Congregationalists Documented Major — Richard Baxter grew up in a home that neighbors mocked as Puritan. In 1662 he was silenced with nearly two thousand other ministers. His memoir shows them going on as Nonconformists, and some kept preaching until they were put in prison.
  • gave rise to The Baptists Widely Accepted Major — John Smyth gave up the orders he had taken from the bishop of Lincoln. In 1911 W. J. McGlothlin wrote that some Puritans left the English church to found their own churches, and that Smyth's flock at Gainsborough was one. In Amsterdam around 1609 it became the first English Baptist church.
  • gave rise to The Quakers (Religious Society of Friends) Documented Major — George Fox's Journal says James Nayler was put out of an Independent church when he took up Fox's message. Fox also won many from 'a company of shattered Baptists' in Nottinghamshire. William Penn told the Quakers' rise as the next step after the Puritan groups of the English Reformation.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced The Reformed Cities - Zurich & Geneva Documented — The Admonition to the Parliament of 1572 sends the Archbishop of York to read the 'learned Epistle' Theodore Beza wrote him. Letters from Beza, a minister at Geneva, and from Rudolph Gualter of Zurich were printed with it. They gave the Puritan case the weight of two famous names.

Influenced

  • influenced The Evangelical Awakening in New England Documented — John Winthrop's journal says that Thomas Shepard came to Massachusetts in October 1635, with other ministers. In 1746 Jonathan Edwards quoted from Shepard's book on the ten virgins. He did so in his study of true and false faith.
  • influenced The Methodist Revival Widely Accepted — In 1747 John Wesley read The History of the Puritans. He wrote with anger of the persecution that drove 'those venerable men' out of the Church. Historians of Methodism, Henry Rack among them, see a Puritan strand in the Wesleys' family and faith.

Argued Against

  • argued against The English Reformation & Elizabethan Settlement Documented — The Admonition to the Parliament of 1572 told Parliament that England was still far from 'a church rightly reformed.' It called the Prayer Book an imperfect book, picked out of the Mass book.

Opposed By

  • argued against The English Reformation & Elizabethan Settlement Documented — At Hampton Court in January 1604, King James heard the Puritan case against the Prayer Book. Leaving the room, he said he would make them conform 'or I will harry them out of this Land.' In 1572 the authors of the Admonition had been sent to prison.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Puritans thought the English Reformation had stopped halfway, and their sharpest distinctiveness was interior: they kept diaries examining their own hearts and told conversion narratives — an account of how God had brought them to faith — aloud before the whole congregation. Pressed at home, thousands carried that habit across the Atlantic to New England from 1630.

Legacy

Puritanism did not survive as a church of its own, but it left more behind than most that did. English Congregationalism, Presbyterianism and much of the Baptist tradition came straight out of it. So did the culture of early New England, including Harvard, founded in 1636 to train its ministers. Its books stayed in print. Bunyan's Pilgrim's Progress, by a man formed in that tradition, became one of the most read books in English. And its call to a personal turn to God fed the revivals of the next century: in New England, Jonathan Edwards still quoted the Puritan Thomas Shepard.

Experience It Today

  • The Old Ship Meeting House, Hingham, Massachusetts — built in 1681, the only surviving seventeenth-century Puritan meetinghouse in the United States and still in use

How Sure the Record Is

RICH in first-person interior voice at ordinary levels - diaries and conversion narratives are its native genre

  • The diaries: Lady Margaret Hoby (1599-1605, earliest known Englishwoman's diary); Nehemiah Wallington's notebooks (London artisan); Samuel Ward's student diary (primary)
  • The conversion narratives: Thomas Shepard's Cambridge, MA congregation relations (God's Plot, ed. McGiffert; The Confessions, ed. Selement & Woolley) (primary) — Lay men's and women's conversion accounts as delivered for church admission. Worth remembering the setting: these narratives were performed for a gate-keeping audience, which shaped how they were told.
  • Practical divinity as used: Perkins (A Golden Chain; casuistry), Rogers's Seven Treatises, Bayly's Practice of Piety, Dod & Cleaver on household government (primary) — The how-to shelf of godly living - catechesis and conduct rather than system.
  • The church-order record: An Admonition to Parliament (1572), Marprelate tracts (1588-89), subscription/deprivation records, the Dedham classis minutes (1582-89) (primary) — The movement's own semi-clandestine presbytery keeping minutes inside the establishment.
  • Collinson, The Elizabethan Puritan Movement (1967), with Coffey & Lim (eds.), The Cambridge Companion to Puritanism (2008) (secondary) — Field-defining classic and current survey.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 6 (reference)
