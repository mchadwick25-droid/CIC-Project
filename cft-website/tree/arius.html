Arius

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Homoian ('Arian') Christianity.

Known for
A presbyter of Alexandria who taught that the Son of God was made, and not eternal; his teaching was condemned at the Council of Nicaea in 325.
Dates
In dispute with Bishop Alexander of Alexandria from about 318 or 319; died in Constantinople in 336, both by Archibald Robertson's dating
Place
Alexandria
Role
Presbyter and teacher

About This Person

"We are persecuted because we said: the Son has a beginning, but God has no beginning." Arius wrote that to a friend in the early three-hundreds. Nearly every word of his that survives was copied by men who wanted him condemned.

He was a parish priest of Alexandria in Egypt, and around 318 he clashed with its bishop, Alexander, over the Son of God.

His friend was Eusebius, bishop of Nicomedia, and Arius told him his bishop had driven him out "as godless men". The Son, he wrote, existed before time as perfect God, but before he was begotten, he was not. For Arius, God alone has no beginning, and a Son eternal beside him would make two who had none.

Alexander's council removed him, calling his party "enemies of Christ", and Arius set his teaching in verse. His Thalia praises earlier teachers, wise and taught by God, then turns to himself: "I have walked in their footsteps, sharing their belief. I am the famous one, who has suffered much for the glory of God. Taught by God, I have come to know wisdom and knowledge." We have those lines only because his fiercest opponent, Athanasius, copied them to mock them.

In 325 the emperor Constantine called the bishops to Nicaea, where the council condemned Arius's opinions and the emperor exiled him. Its creed ended by cursing anyone who said "before he was begotten he was not", or that the Son came "of things that were not". Those were his circle's own phrases, written into the creed as what the church denies. Constantine then ordered his books burned, with death for anyone who hid one.

His opponents called everyone who rejected Nicaea "Arian", but the bishops it named refused the name. At Antioch in 341 they wrote: "We have not been followers of Arius. For how could we, who are bishops, follow a priest?" They had examined his faith, they said, and received him rather than followed him. Athanasius answered that they disowned the name because it was hated. Forty years later, at Aquileia, two bishops said they did not know who Arius was, and the council deposed them both.

Arius did not live to see any of that. The emperor took him back, but in the new capital, Constantinople, the bishop refused to admit him to communion. Eusebius's party, as Athanasius tells it, said he would have communion the next day against the bishop's wishes.

The bishop lay on the church floor and prayed that God would take either Arius or himself. Before that day came, Arius withdrew to relieve himself and died. Athanasius, who was not there, told it in the Bible's words for the death of Judas, who betrayed Jesus, and saw God's judgment in it.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The churches later called Arian, which held with him that the Son was not eternal.
  • The creed of Nicaea, which the council wrote to define the faith against his teaching.

How they're remembered

  • Socrates of Constantinople sets the start of the quarrel at his clash with Bishop Alexander, when Arius said "there was a time when the Son was not."
  • Athanasius of Alexandria tells of his sudden death in Constantinople, the day before he was to be received back, as the presbyter Macarius told it to him.

At Major Councils

  • Council of Nicaea, 325: Condemned and sent into exile

What Is Uncertain

  • How the quarrel began is told more than one way. Socrates of Constantinople starts it at Arius's answer to Bishop Alexander. Archibald Robertson, who edited Athanasius in English, calls the origin obscure, and notes that Constantine's own letter has Alexander asking his clergy about a passage of Scripture. Philostorgius, through Photius, says a presbyter nicknamed Baucalis started it.
  • His own words reach us through others. Theodoret of Cyrrhus kept his letter to Eusebius of Nicomedia, and Athanasius quotes the Thalia while mocking it.
  • Philostorgius, a historian on his side, praised Arius but also found fault with him. His history survives only in a hostile summary by Photius, patriarch of Constantinople in the 800s, who calls Philostorgius a liar. So the attack on Arius and the defense of him both come through opponents.
  • Athanasius was not in Constantinople when Arius died, and had the account from the presbyter Macarius. Sozomen gives three views of the death. Those who held Arius's views blamed magic. Others saw God's judgment, and some a sudden disease of the heart.
  • The year of his birth is not given in a held source. Robertson reports that he was said to be a native of Libya, and dates his death to 336.
