Arius

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Homoian ('Arian') Christianity.

Known for
A presbyter of Alexandria who taught that the Son of God was made, and not eternal; his teaching was condemned at the Council of Nicaea in 325.
Dates
In dispute with Bishop Alexander of Alexandria from about 318 or 319; died in Constantinople in 336, both by Archibald Robertson's dating
Place
Alexandria
Role
Presbyter and teacher

About This Person

In Alexandria, Bishop Alexander was teaching his clergy about the Trinity. One of his presbyters spoke up. "If the Father begat the Son," said Arius, "he that was begotten had a beginning of existence: and from this it is evident, that there was a time when the Son was not." Socrates of Constantinople, writing a century later, starts the quarrel here. Archibald Robertson, who edited Athanasius in English, dates it to 318 or 319. He says no one knows for sure how it began. In Constantine's own letter, it began when Alexander asked his clergy about a passage of Scripture.

Arius was a presbyter of Alexandria, in charge of the church of Baucalis. Robertson says he also taught Scripture. His own letters survive in books by his foes. Theodoret of Cyrrhus kept his letter to Eusebius of Nicomedia. In it Arius signs himself "unjustly persecuted by Alexander the Pope." Near the end he writes, "We are persecuted, because we say that the Son has a beginning, but that God is without beginning."

Arius taught that God made the Son from nothing. Yet in the same letter he says the Son "has subsisted before time, and before ages, as perfect God." Philostorgius, a later historian on his side, says Arius wrote songs for sailors, millers and travelers. Arius also wrote a book called the Thalia. Only his foes kept any of it. Athanasius quotes its start, "According to faith of God's elect, God's prudent ones," then calls the lines that follow mockeries.

In 325 the Council of Nicaea condemned Arius and declared the Son to be of one being with the Father. Constantine sent Arius into exile and ordered his books burned, Socrates says.

Years later Arius swore to Constantine that his faith was right. Athanasius tells what came next, as the presbyter Macarius told him. Alexander, bishop of Constantinople, prayed that if Arius came to communion, God would "let me Thy servant depart." If not, he asked God to "take off Arius." Arius died suddenly the day before. Sozomen, the church historian, gives three views. Arius's friends blamed magic. Others saw God's judgment. Some blamed a sudden heart disease.

Philostorgius praised Arius, but found fault with him too. His history survives only in a hostile summary by Photius, a patriarch of Constantinople in the 800s, who calls Philostorgius a liar. So both the attack on Arius and his defense reach us through opponents.

Key Works

  • Letter to Eusebius of Nicomedia: his own statement of belief, kept by Theodoret of Cyrrhus in his Church History, Book 1
  • The Thalia: a book of his teaching in verse, known only from the quotations and mockery of Athanasius

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The churches later called Arian, which held with him that the Son was not eternal.
  • The creed of Nicaea, which the council wrote to define the faith against his teaching.

How they're remembered

  • Socrates of Constantinople sets the start of the quarrel at his clash with Bishop Alexander, when Arius said "there was a time when the Son was not."
  • Athanasius of Alexandria tells of his sudden death in Constantinople, the day before he was to be received back, as the presbyter Macarius told it to him.

At Major Councils

  • Council of Nicaea, 325: Condemned and sent into exile

What Is Uncertain

  • How the quarrel began is told more than one way. Socrates of Constantinople starts it at Arius's answer to Bishop Alexander. Archibald Robertson, who edited Athanasius in English, calls the origin obscure, and notes that Constantine's own letter has Alexander asking his clergy about a passage of Scripture. Philostorgius, through Photius, says a presbyter nicknamed Baucalis started it.
  • His own words reach us through others. Theodoret of Cyrrhus kept his letter to Eusebius of Nicomedia, and Athanasius quotes the Thalia while mocking it.
  • Philostorgius, a historian on his side, praised Arius but also found fault with him. His history survives only in a hostile summary by Photius, patriarch of Constantinople in the 800s, who calls Philostorgius a liar. So the attack on Arius and the defense of him both come through opponents.
  • Athanasius was not in Constantinople when Arius died, and had the account from the presbyter Macarius. Sozomen gives three views of the death. Those who held Arius's views blamed magic. Others saw God's judgment, and some a sudden disease of the heart.
  • The year of his birth is not given in a held source. Robertson reports that he was said to be a native of Libya, and dates his death to 336.
