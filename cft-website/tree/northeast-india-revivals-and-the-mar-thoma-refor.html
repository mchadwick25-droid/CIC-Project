Northeast India Revivals & the Mar Thoma Reform

Kerala and the hill country of northeast India, 1836 to 1906 — an ancient Syriac church reforming its own liturgy at one end of India, and hill villages singing their first hymns in their own languages at the other.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:16

Read along

In 1836 a teacher at the seminary in Kottayam, Abraham Malpan, revised the Syriac liturgy that Kerala's St Thomas Christians had used for centuries — cutting what he judged unscriptural and putting the service into Malayalam so ordinary people could follow it. The reform divided the ancient community, and after decades of argument and litigation a royal court verdict in 1889 separated the two parties for good. The reforming wing carried on as the Mar Thoma Syrian Church, an Eastern church that kept its ancient rite and its evangelical conscience together, while the larger body continued as the Malankara church. A thousand miles to the northeast, in the Khasi, Mizo and Naga hills, small congregations planted by Welsh and American missions caught fire on their own. Village preachers and singers carried revival through the hills, most famously the Khasi awakening of 1905-06, and hill languages received their first written hymnody out of the same process, composed rather than translated.

Documented Stories

The Reformers Send to Mardin

Abraham Malpan's revision of 1836 had removed from the Malankara liturgy the prayers to the saints and for the dead and put the service into Malayalam, and the metropolitan had suspended him. The reformers had no bishop and could not, in the ancient order of their church, make one; bishops came from the Syrian Orthodox patriarch at Mardin, and the patriarch was not a reformer. In 1841 they sent Malpan's nephew, Mathews, a deacon of twenty-three trained at the Anglican-assisted seminary at Kottayam. He traveled to Mardin, presented himself to Patriarch Elias II as the candidate of the Malankara church, and in February 1842, without the patriarch's knowing what he represented, was consecrated Mathews Mar Athanasius. He returned in 1843 with the patriarch's letters, and the reform had its bishop. The other party then did the same in reverse: they appealed to Mardin, and in 1875 Patriarch Peter III came to Kerala himself, held a synod at Mulanthuruthy in 1876 that bound the church to Antioch, excommunicated Athanasius, and consecrated rival bishops. Litigation ran for a generation, and in 1889 the Royal Court of Final Appeal of Travancore ruled that the patriarch's authority was the church's constitution and that the reformers, having departed from it, had no right to its property. They lost every church but a few and walked out with their liturgy, to become the Mar Thoma Syrian Church. The bishop who had made it possible had been consecrated by the man whose successor unmade it.

Mairang, 1905

The Welsh Calvinistic Methodists had worked in the Khasi hills since 1841, and by 1904 had some 20,000 Khasi Christians in congregations governed by their own presbyteries. Through the winter of 1904–05 accounts of the revival in Wales arrived, and Khasi congregations began praying for the same. In March 1905, at a presbytery meeting at Pariong in the western hills, the prayer meetings would not close; people confessed sins aloud, wept, sang the Khasi hymns over and over, and fell; and when the presbytery met at Mairang in April the whole assembly was taken by it, with meetings running through the nights and villagers walking in from miles around. The missionaries' reports describe what they saw with gratitude and alarm: women leading the singing and the prayer in a society where they had not spoken in church, young people dancing, prophecies, and bodily agitations they compared to those of Wales, which some elders wished to stop and could not. The mission counted some eight thousand new members by the end of 1906. Its hymns were Khasi compositions, made in the meetings, not translations; and when delegates from the Lushai hills to the south attended the Khasi assembly at Mairang in March 1906, they carried the revival home to the Mizo, whose own awakening began that year and whose lengkhawm zai, the communal singing with drums that the missionaries had discouraged, became its form. The hill churches were, from then, revival churches, and their theology is in what they sing.

Connections

In Its Own Record

The Mar Thoma Church is an ancient-rite church that added evangelical reform. It carries on the St. Thomas Christian tradition of Kerala. The reform split the community. In 1847 the Patriarch of Antioch condemned its leading bishop. After a royal court verdict in 1889, the larger body, loyal to Antioch, went on as a separate church. Meanwhile, in the Khasi, Mizo and Naga hills, independent revivals made those regions among the most Christian on earth.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 1836 a seminary teacher named Abraham Malpan revised Kerala's ancient Syriac liturgy into Malayalam, keeping the old rite but reforming it from within. A thousand miles away, revival swept the Khasi, Mizo and Naga hills and gave those languages their first written hymns, composed rather than translated.

Legacy

The Mar Thoma Syrian Church is still here, with dioceses in India and among Indian communities abroad, and its annual Maramon Convention on the sandy bed of the Pamba river remains one of Asia's largest Christian gatherings. The hill churches did more than survive. Mizoram and Nagaland are today among the most thoroughly Christian regions on earth, Presbyterian and Baptist respectively, and the revival hymnody composed in those years is still what their congregations sing.
