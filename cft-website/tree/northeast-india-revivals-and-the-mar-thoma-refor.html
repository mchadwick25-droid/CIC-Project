Kerala, today in India · Khasi, Mizo & Naga hills, today in India and Myanmar

Northeast India Revivals & the Mar Thoma Reform

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In 1836 a teacher at the seminary in Kottayam, Abraham Malpan, revised the Syriac liturgy that Kerala's St Thomas Christians had used for centuries — cutting what he judged unscriptural and putting the service into Malayalam so ordinary people could follow it. The reform divided the ancient community, and after decades of argument and litigation a royal court verdict in 1889 separated the two parties for good. The reforming wing carried on as the Mar Thoma Syrian Church, an Eastern church that kept its ancient rite and its evangelical conscience together, while the larger body continued as the Malankara church. A thousand miles to the northeast, in the Khasi, Mizo and Naga hills, small congregations planted by Welsh and American missions caught fire on their own. Village preachers and singers carried revival through the hills, most famously the Khasi awakening of 1905-06, and hill languages received their first written hymnody out of the same process, composed rather than translated.

In Its Own Record

Continued As

  • Northeast India & the Mar Thoma Church — the 20th Century Widely Accepted Major — Thomas Whitehouse, an English clergyman in Kerala, wrote of a bishop from Antioch who lived there in 1853. He had been sent to check the reforming bishop Mar Athanasius. The reform party lived on as the Mar Thoma Church of the next century. So did the hill churches of the northeast. Robert Frykenberg's history of Christianity in India traces both.

Broke With

  • The Malankara Churches — Orthodox & Jacobite Widely Accepted Major — In 1847 the Patriarch of Antioch repeated his predecessor's curse on Mathew Mar Athanasius, the reforming bishop in Kerala. He ordered that 'none shall pray with him'. A rival bishop had already come from Antioch to check the reform. Then a royal court verdict in 1889 left the two sides as separate churches.

Sources

  • The Mar Thoma reform's own documents: Abraham Malpan's revised liturgy (1836ff.); the reformers' 1836 memorial; Mathews Mar Athanasius's records; synod records and service books; the Travancore Royal Court litigation record (to 1889) (primary) — An ancient-rite church's own liturgical and textual act of reform, alongside the adversarial court record of the legal fight that followed.
  • The hill revivals' own record: Khasi revival (1905-06, Mairang) and Mizo revival (1906ff.) accounts; hymnody in local languages (Khasi and Mizo revival hymns; lengkhawm zai); indigenous evangelists' and converts' accounts (primary) — Local-language hymnody — indigenous composition, not translation — carries the revivals' own theological voice; the accounts are partly self-recorded and partly transcribed by missionaries, worth reading with that mixed origin in mind.
  • Church records WITH mission filters: Welsh Calvinistic Methodist records (Khasi/Mizo hills); American Baptist records (Naga hills, Assam); presbytery/assembly and association minutes (primary) — The early layers here come through a missionary's own pen; indigenous-majority governance becomes visible by this window's end. The colonial administration's own frame — the Inner Line, punitive expeditions — surrounds the record throughout.
  • Robert Eric Frykenberg, Christianity in India: From Beginnings to the Present (OUP, 2008), with Frederick S. Downs, History of Christianity in India vol. V pt. 5: North East India (CHAI, 1992) (secondary) — The standard covering both wings and the standard regional study.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 8, ch. 29 'Christians and religious traditions in the Indian empire' (Frykenberg) (reference) — A direct fit for both wings of this story.
