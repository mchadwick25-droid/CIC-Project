The Early Church Era · 248-258 · Carthage

Carthage, today in Tunisia

Cyprian of Carthage

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity.

Also called Cyprianus, Thascius Cyprianus, Cyprian of Africa

Bishop of Carthage from about 248 until he was put to the sword in 258. A teacher of public speaking who became bishop while still a new Christian, he led the church of Roman Africa through the persecution under Decius, the plague, the rival bishop Novatian at Rome and the quarrel with Stephen of Rome over baptism, in some eighty letters and in treatises such as On the Unity of the Catholic Church and On the Mortality. Both the Donatists and Augustine of Hippo later claimed him.

About This Person

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 4:24

Read along

In Carthage, in today's Tunisia, people crowded the doors of a house, waiting for Cyprian. Newly baptized, he had tried to step aside for older men, but they wanted him as bishop, says his deacon Pontius, who wrote his life as an admirer.

Cyprian had taught public speaking, and became bishop about two years after his baptism, by modern reckoning. For ten years he led the church of Roman Africa through persecution, plague and division; some eighty letters show how. He taught that the church is one mother, and died by the sword, the city's first martyr bishop, Pontius says.

In 250 the emperor Decius ordered everyone to sacrifice to the gods. Crowds were shouting for him, he wrote, and to keep the peace he withdrew from the city and hid, leading his church by letter. Clergy at Rome wrote to Carthage of the good shepherd who lays down his life and the hired man who runs. Cyprian replied that his acts had been reported "less than plainly and faithfully", and Pontius later had to argue that fear was not the cause.

When Novatian was set up at Rome as a rival bishop, Cyprian would not receive his envoys as church members. He wrote: "Whoever does not have the Church as mother cannot have God as Father." The church, he meant, bears and feeds Christians like a mother.

When plague struck, about 252, people left their own sick and Cyprian urged care for all, Pontius says. He wrote that it tested everyone: "Do the healthy serve the sick? Do relatives love their kin as they should? Do masters pity their sick slaves? Do doctors stand by the sick who beg for help?"

Cyprian and the African councils held that washing in groups cut off from the church was no baptism, and baptized those who came over. Stephen, bishop of Rome, as Cyprian quotes him, said to keep to what was handed down: hands laid on them as a sign of repentance. Cyprian called his letter proud and his view an error. Eighty-seven bishops met in council, probably in 256. Cyprian opened it: "For none of us sets himself up as a bishop of bishops, or drives his fellow bishops by a tyrant's terror to obey." No bishop, he meant, may force another; each answers to Christ. A bishop in Cappadocia, on Cyprian's side, reported that Stephen refused the African envoys a roof and called Cyprian a false Christ. Stephen died in 257, with no settlement recorded.

That year, says a Christian account built on the court record, he told the Roman governor he was "a Christian and a bishop", and refused the Roman rites. He was sent into exile, and in 258 a harsher law ordered bishops killed. When officers were sent to take him to Utica, he left his gardens on friends' advice, he wrote, to declare his faith before his own people. Pontius says he refused friends' offers of refuge. The account says he stayed at his gardens.

On 14 September 258 he again refused the rites: in so just a cause, he said, there was nothing to think over. The sentence named him an enemy of the Roman gods and leader of a wicked conspiracy, whose blood would be an example to his followers. Sentenced to the sword, he answered, "Thanks be to God," and the crowd cried that they would be beheaded with him. The account says two clergymen tied the cloth over his eyes; Pontius says he bound them himself.

Later, the Donatists, a breakaway African church, quoted his letters and council to defend baptizing again. Augustine of Hippo, about 140 years after Cyprian's death, answered that Cyprian, though wrong on baptism, never left the church's unity and cut off no one who differed. He quoted the "bishop of bishops" line again and again, asking, "What could be more kind? What more humble?" So both sides claimed him.

The night he died, the account says, his body was carried with candles and torches, in prayer and great triumph, to a burial ground outside the city.

Timeline

Dates
Bishop from 248, by the Edinburgh editor of the Ante-Nicene Fathers; from 247 or 248, by the editor of Eusebius's Church History; and by E. W. Benson no earlier than June 248 and no later than April 249, reckoned from Cyprian's own words in 252 that he had been bishop four years. Born probably about 200, by the Edinburgh editor. Converted in 246, by that editor, who has him baptized then; Benson says only that we may perhaps assume he was baptized in 246; no ancient source gives a year, so about two years from baptism to bishop is a modern reckoning, and Pontius says only that he was still a new Christian. The persecution under Decius is dated 250, and On the Unity of the Church 251 by A. Cleveland Coxe, the American editor. The plague is dated about 252, from Eusebius's Chronicle as the Edinburgh editor cites it. The council of eighty-seven bishops on baptism met on 1 September, in 256 by most sources; the heading in the Edinburgh edition gives 258. Stephen of Rome died in 257: Eusebius gives him two years in office, and his editor dates the accession of his successor Xystus to 24 or 31 August 257, while saying the ancient lists differ greatly on how long Xystus held office. The first hearing, before the proconsul Paternus, was on 30 August 257, and it ended in exile to Curubis. The second hearing and the death were on 14 September 258, by the record of his trial, which names the day and the consuls; Theodoret's editor gives 13 August 258 for the death.
Place
Carthage, in Roman Africa, today's Tunisia. The council of 256 gathered bishops from the province of Africa, Numidia and Mauretania. Exiled in 257 to Curubis, which E. W. Benson describes as a small walled coast town about fifty miles from Carthage. In 258 officers were sent to take him to Utica, another city of the province, and he left his gardens for a while. He was tried on 14 September 258 and put to the sword in the field of Sextus, and his body was carried by night to a burial ground on the Mappalian road
Role
Bishop of Carthage; teacher of public speaking before his baptism; letter writer and church leader; martyr

Key Works

  • Letters: some eighty letters from him and to him, numbered 1 to 81 in Hartel's edition, which show how a third-century church was run: his reply from hiding to the clergy at Rome, his refusal of Novatian's envoys, Stephen's letter as he quotes it, a letter from a bishop in Cappadocia on Stephen, and his last letter on where he would meet his arrest. On the Lapsed describes those who gave way under Decius: some ran to the forum of their own accord to sacrifice, and others who kept their hands from sacrifice stained their conscience with certificates
  • On the Unity of the Catholic Church: written at the time of Novatian's break, in 251 by Coxe's dating. The church is one as the sun's rays are one light, a tree's branches one trunk and a spring's streams one source; break a branch from the tree and it cannot bud, cut a stream from its spring and it dries up. She is one mother, from whose womb Christians are born, by whose milk they are fed and by whose spirit they are given life. Chapter 6 says that whoever does not have the Church as mother cannot have God as Father
  • On the Mortality: his own address on the plague of about 252. It names its effects: the bowels loosened into a flow, a fire burning in the throat, constant vomiting, eyes red with blood, and feet or limbs lost to rot. It says the plague searches out how upright each person is, asking whether the healthy serve the sick, relatives love their kin, masters pity their sick slaves and doctors stand by those who beg for help; it also calls these things trainings for believers, not deaths

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • The Donatists of Africa, who had broken from the rest of the church, quoted his letters and his council to defend baptizing again. About 140 years after his death Augustine of Hippo told them to stop citing his authority for repeating baptism and to hold with him to Cyprian's example of keeping unity. He quoted the line about a bishop of bishops again and again, and said that though Cyprian held a different view on baptism, he did not forsake the unity of the church.
  • In one sermon Augustine speaks of the birthday of blessed Cyprian, the day of his death, and in another says that the birthday of Cyprian the martyr is greatly kept through all Africa.
  • Telling of the year 533, Procopius wrote that Cyprian, a holy man, was revered above all others by the people of Carthage, who had built a notable temple to him on the sea-shore and kept a festival there that they called the Cypriana. He adds that the Vandals took the temple from the Christians.
  • A traveler in a work by Sulpicius Severus says he longed to go to Carthage to worship at the tomb of the martyr Cyprian.

How they're remembered

  • Pontius says he was the first bishop of Carthage to die a martyr, and that no earlier bishop of the city is recorded to have come to suffering; Coxe, the American editor, adds that he was the first of the province.
  • Lactantius, about fifty years later, calls him distinguished and renowned and says he had sought great glory from the art of oratory. Jerome says his works are more conspicuous than the sun. Pontius thought he would probably never cease to speak to the end of the world.

Open Questions

  • His death and his hearings are told by a Christian account built on the court record, which survives in Latin in the Library in the printings of Knopf and Krüger and of Gebhardt. His life is told by Pontius, the deacon who shared his exile, as open praise. Jerome says Pontius wrote the Life; the Life itself is unsigned, and Hartel's title page says only that it is commonly attributed to him.
  • His baptism and his election. Pontius says he was chosen to the episcopate while still a new Christian, and that some resisted him. The Edinburgh editor says the older presbyters objected; no ancient source gives the years. Jerome says that after he became a Christian he gave all he had to the poor and was soon made presbyter and bishop; Pontius says he sold whole estates for the poor. In To Donatus, his own words, he says he had once thought it hard that a man could be born again.
  • His name. Jerome says he took his surname from the presbyter Caecilius, who persuaded him to become a Christian. The Edinburgh editor says he took his friend's name at his baptism. Pontius says only that Caecilius converted him.
  • Before he was a Christian. Jerome and Lactantius agree that he was first known as a teacher of rhetoric. Pontius passes over his earlier life. The Edinburgh editor says that all that can be said with any degree of certainty is that he was highly educated for the profession of a rhetorician.
  • His hiding under Decius, and the charge. Cyprian says that when the people had often demanded him with violent clamor he withdrew for a while, thinking less of his own safety than of the public peace of the brothers, and that he sent thirteen letters in that time; he later says he was named in a public notice with his title of bishop added and was called for again and again to the lion in the circus, and Pontius also says he was often demanded for the lions. The clergy at Rome, whose bishop Fabian had just died, wrote to the clergy of Carthage of the good shepherd and the hired man who runs from the wolf. Whether this was a reproach is the editors' reading. The Edinburgh edition's translator calls the passage very obscure, Coxe says it seems in a slight degree to reflect on his withdrawal, and Benson calls it a sarcasm. Cyprian answered that his acts had been reported to Rome less than plainly and faithfully; the Edinburgh English reads somewhat garbled and untruthful. Pontius defends him at length, asking whether the cause of his withdrawal was fear when he suffered later.
  • The lapsed. Cyprian would not settle the return of those who had given way by himself; he promised to come to a meeting of bishops after Easter, and the councils of 251 took the matter up.
  • What the plague address says and Pontius says. Pontius says Cyprian urged care for all, not only for believers. On the Mortality tests whether the healthy serve the sick but does not mention pagans. The care for the pagan sick rests on Pontius.
  • The Unity of the Church, chapter 4, has come down in two forms. Hartel prints a text without the words that primacy is given to Peter and without the chair of Peter, and puts them in his notes as found in some manuscripts. Benson calls them the famous interpolation, and Coxe, the American editor, calls them falsifying interpolations long since exposed. Hugo Koch reports that Chapman argued Cyprian himself wrote both forms, and Koch doubts it.
  • Who first held that baptism must be repeated. Eusebius says Cyprian was the first. Cyprian says an African council under Agrippinus had decided it long before, and Augustine also names Agrippinus. Vincent of Lérins, about 434, writing from Stephen's side, calls Agrippinus the first who held it and gives Stephen's rule as let there be no innovation. An anonymous treatise on re-baptism, printed with his works in the Edinburgh edition, argues against his side; it is by another hand, and the editors think it was aimed at Cyprian.
  • Stephen of Rome. His letter is lost; his words are held only as Cyprian quotes them, and Cyprian calls the letter proud, or beside the point, or self-contradictory, and Stephen's view an error. Eusebius says Stephen was very indignant. What is told of Stephen's treatment of the African envoys comes from Firmilian, bishop of Caesarea in Cappadocia, who was on Cyprian's side: that he would not let them speak with him, told the brothers to give them no roof or lodging, and called Cyprian a false Christ and a false apostle. Firmilian also writes that Stephen boasts of the place of his episcopate and says he holds the succession from Peter. No letter or record of a settlement between the two is held; Eusebius's editor says Xystus seems to have taken a more conciliatory tone.
  • The line about a bishop of bishops, which Cyprian spoke in opening the council of eighty-seven bishops. Benson reads it as a stand against one who wants to make himself a bishop of bishops and force obedience by tyrannical terror. The Edinburgh editor notes that it implies a rebuke to Stephen. Augustine reads it as kindness and humility. Whether it was aimed at Stephen is not settled. The year of the council is 256 in most sources and 258 in the Edinburgh edition's heading.
  • His last hiding. In his last letter Cyprian says that officers were sent to take him to Utica, and that on friends' advice he left his gardens for a while, because a bishop should declare his faith in the city where he leads the church and honor all his people by confessing before them. Pontius says friends offered places to withdraw to, and that he did not consent. The account of his death says that after returning from Curubis he stayed at his gardens, expecting every day that they would come for him. Its phrase for why he stayed, ex sacro praecepto, can be read as by imperial order or as by a holy command, and the sources do not settle which.
  • How he died. The account says he covered his eyes with his own hand, and that when he could not tie the cloths the presbyter Julian and the subdeacon Julian tied them; Pontius says he bound his eyes with his own hands. The account says he had twenty-five gold pieces given to the executioner. Pontius says the executioner's hand shook until it was strengthened from above, which is Pontius's reading. Theodoret says Cyprian found a death in the fire, which his editor corrects from the account, though that editor gives 13 August 258 for the death, against the account's 14 September. Pontius says people climbed into the trees to see.
  • That he read Tertullian every day and called him the master comes from Jerome, who had it from an old man, Paul of Concordia, who said that as a very young man he had met Cyprian's aged secretary at Rome. Jerome's Latin says so; the English translation wrongly makes Paul himself the secretary. It is hearsay at two removes, set down more than 130 years after Cyprian's death.
  • The Cypriana and the shrines. Augustine and Procopius are late witnesses to his feast, about 140 and 275 years after his death. No source held here says who keeps his feast today.
