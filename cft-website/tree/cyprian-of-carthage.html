Cyprian of Carthage

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity.

Also called Cyprianus, Thascius Cyprianus, Cyprian of Africa

Cyprian was bishop of Carthage in Roman Africa in the middle of the third century, a teacher of public speaking chosen bishop while still a new Christian, who led his church through persecution and plague. He taught that the church is one, quarreled with Stephen, bishop of Rome, over whether baptism given outside the church counted, and was put to the sword in 258.

About This Person

His deacon, Pontius, who writes as an admirer, says that soon after Cyprian's baptism the whole people of Carthage wanted him as their bishop. A crowd, he says, besieged the doors of his house, while he stepped back to give place to older men. He was still a new Christian when they made him bishop.

Jerome, writing long after, says he had once been a teacher of public speaking. In the middle of the third century Cyprian led the Latin-speaking church of Carthage, in Roman Africa, and the councils of Africa's bishops through persecution, plague and division. He taught that the church is one, and a mother: no one outside her has God as Father, and each bishop answers to Christ alone. He would not accept the ruling of Stephen, bishop of Rome, on baptism, and in 258 he was put to the sword. Later the Donatists and Augustine both claimed him.

In 250 the emperor Decius ordered Christians before the magistrates to sacrifice to the gods. At Carthage, Cyprian wrote later, the crowds kept demanding him with violent shouting. So he withdrew for a while, thinking less of his own safety than of his fellow Christians' peace, so that his presence would not stir the riot further, and from hiding he led his church by letter.

The clergy at Rome wrote to Carthage's clergy that he had withdrawn, and had done rightly. In the same letter they spoke of the gospel's good shepherd, who lays down his life for the sheep, and the hired man who runs from the wolf. They asked Carthage's clergy not to be hired men. Pontius later wrote that fear was not the cause: he did suffer afterwards, and would have run from that too, had fear made him run before.

At Rome a second bishop, Novatian, was set up against the one already chosen, and as that rival rose, Cyprian wrote a book on the church's unity. In it the church is a single mother, from whose womb Christians are born and by whose milk they are fed. Of anyone who leaves the church of Christ, he writes: "He is a stranger, he is unholy, he is an enemy. The one who does not have the Church as mother cannot have God as Father." By the church he meant the one body joined to its lawful bishops, while Novatian's side held a bishop and a church of its own.

Afterwards a dreadful plague broke out over the whole city. Some Christians were troubled that it struck them as hard as it struck the pagans, and Cyprian answered them in a sermon.

"This plague and pestilence, which seems so dreadful and deadly, searches out how just each person is. It tests the minds of the human race. Do the healthy serve the sick? Do relatives love their kin as they ought? Do masters pity their sick slaves? Do doctors not abandon the sick who plead with them?"

Then came a quarrel with Rome over baptism, the washing that makes a Christian, which is given once. The question was whether a washing given by a group outside the church counted when the one washed came over to it. Cyprian counted as heretics all who stood outside the one church. With councils of African bishops he held that there is one baptism because the church is one, and none outside it, so those who came over were to be baptized in the church.

Stephen, bishop of Rome, answered otherwise. His letter, as Cyprian copies it, said: let nothing new be done beyond what has been handed down. Those who came over were to be received with hands laid on them for repentance, without a new washing, since the heretics themselves do not wash again those who come over to them.

In the months of that quarrel, eighty-seven African bishops met in a council, and Cyprian spoke first. Communion meant belonging to the church and sharing its bread and cup. He said: "It remains for each of us to say what we think on this matter. We judge no one, and we cut no one off from communion if he thinks otherwise. For none of us sets himself up as a bishop of bishops. None of us drives his colleagues to obey by a tyrant's terror."

In 258, at his gardens, he learned that officers were coming to take him to Utica, another city. In his last letter he says he left the gardens for a while, because a bishop should confess his faith in the city where he leads the church, before all his people. He was waiting in hiding, he wrote, for the governor's return to Carthage.

In the end he was taken at his gardens, and next morning, 14 September 258, he stood before the governor. The emperors had ordered him to perform the rites, the sacrifices to Rome's gods, and he said he would not. The sentence, in Rome's own terms, named him an enemy of the Roman gods whose blood would make the law hold, and ordered him put to the sword. Cyprian said, "Thanks be to God." In the field of Sextus he covered his eyes with his own hand. When he could not tie the cloth, a presbyter and a subdeacon, both named Julian, tied it, and there he was put to death.

From the 310s a rival church in Africa, the Donatists, baptized again all who came over to it, and quoted his letters and council in its defense. Augustine of Hippo, a later African bishop, answered them about 140 years after Cyprian's death. Stop citing Cyprian's authority for repeating baptism, he told them, and hold with us to his example of keeping unity.

Augustine also preached on Cyprian's birthday, the martyr's feast on the day he died, kept, he said, through all Africa. Where Cyprian was put to death, he said, a table for God's altar now stood, called Cyprian's table because there he was offered up. Worshippers, he said, now ringed the place where persecutors had ringed him.

    The English print has no longer, which is not in the Latin; the modern English follows the Latin. The Latin profanus is rendered unholy, where the print says profane.

Timeline

Dates
No ancient source gives the year of his baptism, the year he became bishop, the year of the plague or the year of On the Unity of the Church; Pontius says only that he was chosen bishop while still a new Christian. The persecution under Decius is dated 250, which is Widely Accepted; Eusebius tells it in Decius's reign and gives no year. The record of the council of eighty-seven bishops on baptism gives the day, 1 September, and no year. His first hearing, before the proconsul Paternus, was on 30 August, under the consuls Valerian for the fourth time and Gallienus for the third, which is 257; it ended in exile to Curubis. He was taken at his gardens on the Ides of September and tried the next day, 14 September, under the consuls Tuscus and Bassus, which is 258; the record of his trial gives both days. The years follow from the consuls the record names and are Widely Accepted.

Key Works

  • Letters: some eighty letters from him and to him, numbered 1 to 81 in Hartel's edition, which show how a third-century church was run: his reply from hiding to the clergy at Rome, his refusal of Novatian's envoys, Stephen's letter as he quotes it, a letter from a bishop in Cappadocia on Stephen, and his last letter on where he would meet his arrest. On the Lapsed describes those who gave way under Decius: some ran to the forum of their own accord to sacrifice, and others who kept their hands from sacrifice stained their conscience with certificates
  • On the Unity of the Catholic Church: written at the time of Novatian's break, as his own letter to the Roman confessors says. The church is one as the sun's rays are one light, a tree's branches one trunk and a spring's streams one source; break a branch from the tree and it cannot bud, cut a stream from its spring and it dries up. She is one mother, from whose womb Christians are born, by whose milk they are fed and by whose spirit they are given life. Chapter 6 says that whoever does not have the Church as mother cannot have God as Father
  • On the Mortality: his own address on the plague. It names its effects: the bowels loosened into a flow, a fire burning in the throat, constant vomiting, eyes red with blood, and feet or limbs lost to rot. It says the plague searches out how upright each person is, asking whether the healthy serve the sick, relatives love their kin, masters pity their sick slaves and doctors stand by those who beg for help; it also calls these things trainings for believers, not deaths

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • The Donatists of Africa, who had broken from the rest of the church, quoted his letters and his council to defend baptizing again. About 140 years after his death Augustine of Hippo told them to stop citing his authority for repeating baptism and to hold with him to Cyprian's example of keeping unity. He quoted the line about a bishop of bishops again and again, and said that though Cyprian held a different view on baptism, he did not forsake the unity of the church.
  • In 314 the bishops at Arles ruled on the African practice of baptizing again: one who came from heretics and had been baptized in the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit was to have only hands laid on him. The ruling speaks of the Africans and names no one.
  • About 434, Vincent of Lérins wrote of the same quarrel that the authors of the doctrine of baptizing again are judged Catholics and the followers heretics, the teachers absolved and the disciples condemned.
  • In one sermon Augustine speaks of the birthday of blessed Cyprian, the day of his death, and in another says that the birthday of Cyprian the martyr is greatly kept through all Africa.
  • Telling of the year 533, Procopius wrote that Cyprian, a holy man, was revered above all others by the people of Carthage, who had built a notable temple to him on the sea-shore and kept a festival there that they called the Cypriana. He adds that the Vandals took the temple from the Christians.
  • A traveler in a work by Sulpicius Severus says he longed to go to Carthage to worship at the tomb of the martyr Cyprian.

How they're remembered

  • Pontius says he was the first in Africa to stain his priestly crown with the blood of martyrdom, and that no earlier bishop of Carthage is recorded to have come to suffering.
  • Lactantius, about fifty years later, calls him distinguished and renowned and says he had sought great glory from the art of oratory. Jerome says his works are more conspicuous than the sun. Pontius thought he would probably never cease to speak to the end of the world.

Open Questions

  • His death and his hearings are told by a Christian account built on the court record, which survives in Latin in the Library in the printings of Knopf and Krüger and of Gebhardt. His life is told by Pontius, the deacon who shared his exile, as open praise. Jerome says Pontius wrote the Life; the Life itself is unsigned, and Hartel's title page says only that it is commonly attributed to him.
  • His baptism and his election. Pontius says he was chosen to the episcopate while still a new Christian, and that some resisted him. No ancient source gives the years. Jerome says that after he became a Christian he gave all he had to the poor and was soon made presbyter and bishop; Pontius says he sold whole estates for the poor. In To Donatus, his own words, he says he had once thought it hard that a man could be born again.
  • His name. Jerome says he took his surname from the presbyter Caecilius, who persuaded him to become a Christian. Pontius says only that Caecilius converted him.
  • Before he was a Christian. Jerome and Lactantius agree that he was first known as a teacher of rhetoric. Pontius passes over his earlier life.
  • His hiding under Decius, and the charge. Cyprian says that when the people had often demanded him with violent clamor he withdrew for a while, thinking less of his own safety than of the public peace of the brothers, and that he sent thirteen letters in that time; he later says he was named in a public notice with his title of bishop added and was called for again and again to the lion in the circus, and Pontius also says he was often demanded for the lions. The clergy at Rome, whose bishop Fabian had just died, wrote to the clergy of Carthage that he had withdrawn for a certain reason and had acted rightly, and in the same letter spoke of the good shepherd and the hired man who runs from the wolf. Whether the hired man passage was meant as a reproach is not settled. Cyprian answered that his acts had been reported to Rome less than plainly and faithfully; the Edinburgh English reads somewhat garbled and untruthful. Pontius defends him at length, asking whether the cause of his withdrawal was fear when he suffered later.
  • The lapsed. Cyprian would not settle the return of those who had given way by himself; he promised to come to a meeting of bishops after Easter, and the councils of 251 took the matter up.
  • What the plague address says and Pontius says. Pontius says Cyprian urged care for all, not only for believers. On the Mortality tests whether the healthy serve the sick but does not mention pagans. The care for the pagan sick rests on Pontius.
  • The Unity of the Church, chapter 4, has come down in two forms. Hartel prints a text without the words that primacy is given to Peter and without the chair of Peter, and puts them in his notes as found in some manuscripts. Whether Cyprian wrote both forms, or the longer one was added later, is disputed among modern readers.
  • Who first held that baptism must be repeated. Eusebius says Cyprian was the first. Cyprian says an African council under Agrippinus had decided it long before, and Augustine also names Agrippinus. Vincent of Lérins, about 434, writing from Stephen's side, calls Agrippinus the first who held it and gives Stephen's rule as let there be no innovation. An anonymous treatise on re-baptism, printed with his works in the Edinburgh edition, argues against his side; it is by another hand.
  • Stephen of Rome. His letter is lost; his words are held only as Cyprian quotes them, and Cyprian calls the letter proud, or beside the point, or self-contradictory, and Stephen's view an error. Eusebius says Stephen was very indignant, and that Xystus succeeded him after two years. What is told of Stephen's treatment of the African envoys comes from Firmilian, bishop of Caesarea in Cappadocia, who was on Cyprian's side: that he would not let them speak with him, told the brothers to give them no roof or lodging, and called Cyprian a false Christ and a false apostle. Firmilian also writes that Stephen boasts of the place of his episcopate and says he holds the succession from Peter. No letter or record of a settlement between the two is held; Pontius calls Xystus, Stephen's successor at Rome, the good and peace-making priest.
  • The line about a bishop of bishops, which Cyprian spoke in opening the council of eighty-seven bishops. Augustine reads it as kindness and humility. Whether it was aimed at Stephen is not settled. The record of the council gives the day, 1 September, and no year.
  • His last hiding. In his last letter Cyprian says that officers were sent to take him to Utica, and that on friends' advice he left his gardens for a while, because a bishop should declare his faith in the city where he leads the church and honor all his people by confessing before them. Pontius says friends offered places to withdraw to, and that he did not consent. The account of his death says that after returning from Curubis he stayed at his gardens, expecting every day that they would come for him. Its phrase for why he stayed, ex sacro praecepto, can be read as by imperial order or as by a holy command, and the sources do not settle which.
  • How he died. The account says he covered his eyes with his own hand, and that when he could not tie the cloths the presbyter Julian and the subdeacon Julian tied them; Pontius says he bound his eyes with his own hands. The account says he had twenty-five gold pieces given to the executioner. Pontius says the executioner's hand shook until it was strengthened from above, which is Pontius's reading. Theodoret, long after, says Cyprian found a death in the fire; the account of his trial does not say so, and it gives 14 September 258 for the death. Pontius says people climbed into the trees to see.
  • That he read Tertullian every day and called him the master comes from Jerome, who had it from an old man, Paul of Concordia, who said that as a very young man he had met Cyprian's aged secretary at Rome. Jerome's Latin says so; the English translation wrongly makes Paul himself the secretary. It is hearsay at two removes, set down more than 130 years after Cyprian's death.
  • The Cypriana and the shrines. Augustine and Procopius are late witnesses to his feast, about 140 and 275 years after his death. No source held here says who keeps his feast today.
