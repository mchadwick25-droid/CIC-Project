Eunomius of Cyzicus

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Anomoean (Eunomian) Christianity.

Leader of the Anomoeans and for a time bishop of Cyzicus, whose Apology argued that God is the one unbegotten being, so that the Son, begotten by the Father's will, cannot share his substance.

About This Person

Audio Brief 4:33

Read along

Eunomius of Cyzicus wrote a defense of his faith for two sets of readers: those who would hear it in his own day, and those who would find it later. He begged both not to measure the truth by numbers.

His argument turned on one word. "Unbegotten" means having no source, never born and never made. Eunomius taught that it is not only a name for God. It is what God is. So the Son, who is begotten, born of a Father, cannot share the Father's being. That teaching drew answers from Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nyssa. His followers took his name. And under the emperor Theodosius he was banished.

Gregory of Nyssa, his enemy, says his father was a poor farmer who carved letters for schoolboys in winter. The son left the plow, learned shorthand and hoped to be an orator. He went to Alexandria to study under Aetius, says Philostorgius, the one friendly historian. Philostorgius's history survives only in a hostile summary by Photius, a patriarch centuries later. Philostorgius praised him as a teacher with "a style well suited to learners." At Constantinople, Philostorgius says, Eunomius and Aetius led the party that opponents named Anomoean, from the Greek for "unlike." This was about 360.

The bishop of Constantinople made him bishop of Cyzicus. He came with an imperial edict that put out the city's bishop, whom its people wanted to keep. Why he left is told three ways. Philostorgius says he refused to sign what his patron asked. Socrates says the people tired of his showy talk and drove him out. Sozomen says the patron found no fault, but Eunomius would not go back to people who suspected him.

In his Apology he reasoned that nothing was before God. "It has been shown that he was not before himself, nor anything else before him, and that he is before all things. Then 'unbegotten' follows. Or rather, he himself is unbegotten being." To be unbegotten is what God is, and God has no parts, so he cannot share his being. The Son did not exist before he was begotten, and he was begotten before all things, by the Father's will. Eunomius called him the only-begotten God and the Father's image in what he does, not in what he is. He refused the Nicene creed's word that the Son is of one being with the Father, because that would divide God.

Basil quoted it piece by piece. At "or rather" he stopped: it does not agree, he said, with what came before. He answered that he too would say God's being is unbegotten, but not that "unbegotten" is his being. Basil did not deny that God is unbegotten; he denied that the word says what God is. For Basil the Son is like the Father in his very being, and equal in power.

Twice, Philostorgius says, Valens's officials exiled him. After Basil's death, Photius says, Eunomius showed his friends a reply he had worked on, and Gregory of Nyssa answered it. Then Theodosius gave the churches of Constantinople to the party of one being. In June 383 he summoned the head of every party to the palace, each with a written creed. Eunomius came for his own party. The emperor prayed alone, read them all, and kept only the creed of one being. About this time, Sozomen says, Theodosius banished him. Socrates gives the reason: he held meetings in private houses and read them his books. Not long after his banishment, Sozomen says, he died, and was buried at Dacora, his birthplace in Cappadocia.

Sozomen judged that the greater part of the church would have gone over to this teaching, had it not met Basil and Gregory, both from Cappadocia. A law of 398 ordered the books of Eunomian clergy burned, and made hiding one a crime punishable by death.

Later, Philostorgius says, a powerful court official had the body moved to Tyana and ordered his books destroyed by public edict. Yet the Apology he wrote for readers still to come survives.

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • His followers were called by his name, the Eunomians. Sozomen says Eunomius only renewed the teaching of Aetius of Antioch, and spread it more boldly.
  • His circle later split. Sozomen and Socrates of Constantinople tell how Theophronius and Eutychius, once his followers, founded parties of their own.
  • Sozomen says the greater part of the church would have gone over to this teaching "had it not found opponents in Basil and Gregory, the Cappadocians."
  • Philostorgius says the prefect Eutropius ordered his books destroyed by public edict.
  • Basil of Caesarea wrote books against the Apology, and Gregory of Nyssa answered Eunomius's reply to Basil. Jerome says replies were made by Apollinarius, Didymus, Basil, Gregory of Nazianzus and Gregory of Nyssa. Photius read twenty-five books by Theodore of Antioch against Eunomius, and an attack by Sophronius on him, each in defense of Basil. Gregory of Nazianzus's Oration 27 is headed a preliminary discourse against the Eunomians.
  • The First Council of Constantinople, in 381, put his followers under a curse by name in its first canon. A law of 19 July 381 forbade the Eunomians to build churches, and a law of 25 July 383 forbade them to meet.
  • A law of 4 March 398, to the prefect Eutychianus, ordered the clergy of his sect, and the Montanists, out of every city. It ordered their books sought out and burned before the judges, and made hiding one a crime punished by death. Later laws went back and forth on his followers' wills: one of 20 June 394 lets them take and leave property by will, and one of 6 July 399 lifts the penalty on making wills and on gifts.
  • The tree's entry for his tradition says the distinction between God's unknowable being and his knowable acts was worked out to answer him, and that the sect left no continuing church. The first is a modern reconstruction.
  • His Apology survives. Jackson says it is printed in Fabricius and in the appendix to Migne's Basil.

How they're remembered

  • Philostorgius, in Photius's summary, praised his clear teaching and "a style well suited to learners", though he rated Aetius higher in argument.
  • Socrates, an opponent, found in his books "a great scarcity of sense, amidst a profusion of verbiage."
  • William Whiston, who put the Apology into English in 1711, quotes Dr. Cave's verdict that "the Sly Arch-Heretick reasons shrewdly."
  • Gregory of Nyssa says Basil alone, out of the lasting warmth of his love, undertook Eunomius's cure and wrote against his heresy to save its author and bring him back to the church.
  • Socrates says the bishop of Constantinople made him bishop of Cyzicus because his eloquence could win the multitude. An imperial edict ordered Eleusius put out and Eunomius installed in his place, and those who held to Eleusius built a church outside the city and met there with him.
  • Philostorgius says some of the clergy of Cyzicus accused him before Eudoxius of saying the Son was unlike the Father, because he denied he was like him in substance. They also charged him with changing the established rites. Sozomen says his own clergy accused him of introducing new teaching.
  • In the Apology he cites the Lord's saying that the Father is greater, and argues that if the Son is a Son he is not unbegotten. He ends its argument by saying that the supreme dignity and monarchy of God must be kept in all things.
  • Socrates says he broke with Eudoxius and held meetings apart because Eudoxius would not take his teacher Aetius back into communion. Philostorgius says he then lived at Chalcedon, in a garden by the sea walls, and that no bishop of his party acted without asking his advice. He says Eunomius closed Aetius's eyes and mouth and carried out his funeral in splendid style.
  • Philostorgius says Theodosius gave the churches of Constantinople to the party of one being and drove out the party of Arius and Eunomius. Sozomen says multitudes resorted to Eunomius, and that the empress Flacilla kept the emperor from meeting him, for fear his skill in argument would change the emperor's mind.
  • Socrates says that in 383 the most skilled men of each party drew up its creed, that Eunomius took up the cause of his own, and that Theodosius condemned all but the creed of one being. Sozomen says he destroyed the others. Socrates says Theodosius punished only Eunomius of the parties, because he held meetings in private houses in Constantinople and read his works there. Sozomen says he lived in the suburbs and held frequent meetings in private houses.
  • Philostorgius says Theodosius banished him from Chalcedon to Halmyris on the Danube, and that when barbarians took it he was sent to Caesarea in Cappadocia. The people of Caesarea disliked him for having written against their bishop Basil, so he was allowed to live at his country place at Dacora. Philostorgius says the emperor also expelled from his palace chamberlains who held Eunomian teaching.
  • Jerome, writing in the fourteenth year of Theodosius, says Eunomius is said to be still living in Cappadocia and to write much against the church.

Open Questions

  • Most of his life is told by opponents, or by Philostorgius, a partisan who praised him and who survives mainly in the summary of Photius, a hostile patriarch of Constantinople. Gregory of Nyssa's account of his family and his rise is polemic.
  • Philostorgius and Theodoret of Cyrus, another church historian, place his appointment to Cyzicus under Constantius, as A. C. Zenos notes in his translation of Socrates. Socrates and Sozomen place it under Valens.
  • Philostorgius says he won over the clergy of Constantinople, then would not sign what Eudoxius asked and went home to Cappadocia. Socrates says the people drove him out. Sozomen says Eudoxius found no fault in him, but he would not return to people who suspected him.
  • Socrates quotes him as teaching that "God knows no more of his own substance than we do". The words do not appear in the Apology as the Library holds it.
  • Gregory of Nyssa says the Apology answered no real trial. Eunomius, as Gregory quotes him, said "that we were condemned by default" by a hostile panel.
  • Philostorgius says Basil of Caesarea died of remorse after reading the first of Eunomius's five books against him. Photius calls this falsehood.
  • Sozomen reports that some made Eunomius the first to baptize with a single immersion, and thinks it more likely that Theophronius and Eutychius began it.
  • Sozomen says he was buried at Dacora, his birthplace. Philostorgius says Eutropius had him moved from there to Tyana, to be kept by monks, and would not let his body lie in his master's tomb.
  • No ancient source in the Library gives the year of his birth or of his death. Freese, the translator of Photius, gives 393 for his death.
  • When he wrote the Apology. No held source of the time dates it. Jackson says only that it was written somewhere about the time of Basil's books against it, and gives 364 for those, after Maran.
  • The year 360. The tree's entry for his tradition says the break began at Constantinople in 360. Philostorgius gives a council at Constantinople, with Aetius and Eunomius, then deacons, leading the party of unlikeness, but no year in the held text.
  • Theodoret gives a fourth account of why he left Cyzicus. He says Eudoxius urged him to hide his opinions, and when Constantius threatened the party, wrote to Eunomius to escape from Cyzicus.
  • Why Theodosius banished him. Socrates gives meetings in private houses where he read his works. Philostorgius ties the emperor's anger to chamberlains at court who held his teaching. No source says the creed of 383 was the cause.
  • The creed of 383 is held only in quotation. Gregory of Nyssa quotes and answers a Eunomian creed in Book II of Against Eunomius, and the editor says the creed Eunomius presented to Theodosius in 383 is extant. No held line ties that book to 383 by name.
  • Who moved the body. Philostorgius's translator calls Eutropius prefect of the praetorium. The law of 398 is addressed to the prefect Eutychianus. The law and Philostorgius's edict burning his books are not shown to be the same act.
  • The two exiles under Valens rest on Philostorgius alone. He says some censured Eunomius for hiding Procopius at his country place, and that the prefect Auxonius sent him to Mauritania and the bishop Valens of Mursa got him recalled. He says Modestus later condemned him unheard and banished him to Naxos. Zenos, an editor, says Valens banished him because he favored the usurper Procopius. Philostorgius also says Eunomius went on an embassy to Procopius at Cyzicus to free prisoners.
  • Photius says Eunomius kept his reply to Basil hidden while Basil lived, for fear it would fall into his hands, and showed it only to his friends after Basil died. Photius, who is hostile, gives the years Eunomius spent on it as hearsay.
  • Who tells the life. Socrates and Sozomen wrote their church histories about sixty years later, and both were against him. A note in the held edition of Philostorgius, taken from Quasten's Patrology, puts his birth about 368 and his history between 425 and 433, and calls it in reality a late apology for the extreme Arianism of Eunomius.
  • Sozomen says the church would have been subverted had it not found opponents in Basil and Gregory, the Cappadocians. He does not say which Gregory.
