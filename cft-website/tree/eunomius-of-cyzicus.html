Eunomius of Cyzicus

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Anomoean (Eunomian) Christianity.

Known for
Leader of the Anomoeans and for a time bishop of Cyzicus, whose Apology argued that God is the one unbegotten being, so that the Son, begotten by the Father's will, cannot share his substance.
Dates
Bishop of Cyzicus under Constantius (Philostorgius) or Valens (Socrates of Constantinople, Sozomen); gave his creed to Theodosius in 383; died soon after Theodosius banished him (Sozomen)
Place
Cappadocia; Cyzicus; Constantinople
Role
Bishop of Cyzicus and teacher

About This Person

In 383 the emperor Theodosius called the heads of every church party to his palace. Each was to write down its faith. Socrates of Constantinople says "Eunomius himself undertook the cause of the Eunomians." The emperor shut himself up alone, prayed, and read each one. He kept only the creed that held the Son to be of one substance with the Father. Sozomen, a church historian of the next century, says he destroyed the rest.

Eunomius of Cyzicus led the Anomoeans, a name their foes gave them for making the Son unlike the Father. Philostorgius, a historian of his own party who praised him, says he came from Cappadocia and studied under Aetius of Antioch. That history survives mainly in a summary by Photius, a later patriarch of Constantinople. He was hostile to it. Gregory of Nyssa, a foe, says Eunomius's father was a poor farmer. In winter he carved letters of the alphabet for schoolboys.

Philostorgius has him made bishop of Cyzicus under Constantius, and Socrates and Sozomen under Valens. Some of his clergy said he made the Son unlike the Father. Philostorgius says he answered them in Constantinople and won over the clergy there. Socrates, a Nicene opponent, says the people of Cyzicus tired of his showy talk and drove him out. Sozomen says Eudoxius, the bishop in the capital, found no fault in him, but Eunomius would not go back.

His Apology survives in his own words. God, he writes, is "the Unbegotten Substance." God has no parts and cannot share his being. So "the Son, when he was not before, was begotten," by the Father's will. The Son made all else, and is the Father's image in what he does. To share one substance, Eunomius held, would split God. He cites Christ, "The Father, who sent me, is Greater than I."

Basil of Caesarea wrote against the Apology. Gregory of Nyssa answered Eunomius's later reply, and mocked the Apology as a speech "before an imaginary court." To Gregory, God's being lies past all words. "We have learnt to honour in silence what transcends speech and thought," he writes.

Theodosius later banished Eunomius. Sozomen says he died soon after and was buried at Dacora, his birthplace in Cappadocia. Philostorgius says he was later moved to Tyana. Philostorgius had seen him in Constantinople at twenty. Photius says Philostorgius "compares also to pearls the speeches which flowed from his lips," though he admits Eunomius lisped.

Key Works

  • The Apology: his defense and statement of faith, addressed to readers then living and to come; Basil of Caesarea wrote against it
  • The Apology for the Apology: his reply to Basil, known from the passages Gregory of Nyssa quotes to refute it
  • A Confession of Faith: a short Eunomian creed copied after the Apology in its manuscripts

Legacy

What traces to them

  • His followers were called by his name, the Eunomians. Sozomen says Eunomius only renewed the teaching of Aetius of Antioch, and spread it more boldly.
  • His circle later split. Sozomen and Socrates of Constantinople tell how Theophronius and Eutychius, once his followers, founded parties of their own.
  • Sozomen says much of the church would have gone over to this teaching "had it not found opponents in Basil and Gregory, the Cappadocians."
  • Philostorgius says the prefect Eutropius ordered his books destroyed by public edict.

How they're remembered

  • Philostorgius, in Photius's summary, praised his clear teaching and "a style well suited to learners", though he rated Aetius higher in argument.
  • Socrates, an opponent, found in his books "a great scarcity of sense, amidst a profusion of verbiage."
  • William Whiston, who put the Apology into English in 1711, quotes Dr. Cave's verdict that "the Sly Arch-Heretick reasons shrewdly."

At Major Councils

  • First Council of Constantinople, 381: Not present; his followers were condemned in canon 1

What Is Uncertain

  • Most of his life is told by opponents, or by Philostorgius, a partisan who praised him and who survives mainly in the summary of Photius, a hostile patriarch of Constantinople. Gregory of Nyssa's account of his family and his rise is polemic.
  • Philostorgius and Theodoret of Cyrus, another church historian, place his appointment to Cyzicus under Constantius, as A. C. Zenos notes in his translation of Socrates. Socrates and Sozomen place it under Valens.
  • Philostorgius says he won over the clergy of Constantinople, then would not sign what Eudoxius asked and went home to Cappadocia. Socrates says the people drove him out. Sozomen says Eudoxius found no fault in him, but he would not return to people who suspected him.
  • Socrates quotes him as teaching that "God knows no more of his own substance than we do". The words do not appear in the Apology as the Library holds it.
  • Gregory of Nyssa says the Apology answered no real trial. Eunomius, as Gregory quotes him, said "that we were condemned by default" by a hostile panel.
  • Philostorgius says Basil of Caesarea died of remorse after reading the first of Eunomius's five books against him. Photius calls this falsehood.
  • Sozomen reports that some made Eunomius the first to baptize with a single immersion, and thinks it more likely that Theophronius and Eutychius began it.
  • Sozomen says he was buried at Dacora, his birthplace. Philostorgius says Eutropius had him moved from there to Tyana, to be kept by monks, and would not let his body lie in his master's tomb.
  • No source in the Library gives the year of his birth or of his death.
