The Early Medieval Era · died 892 · Baghdad

al-Baladhuri

A person whose story no single tradition tells.

Outside witness

Also called Ahmad ibn Yahya ibn Jabir al-Baladhuri (Philip Hitti)

A Muslim historian of Baghdad whose Futuh al-Buldan tells how the Muslim conquests were won. It records terms made with Christian towns and the fate of churches such as Saint John's at Damascus.

About This Person

The Christians of Damascus still held their church of Saint John. The caliph Mu'awiyah wanted to add it to the city's mosque, and they refused. The caliph 'Abd al-Malik offered them money, and they refused again. Then his son, the caliph al-Walid, threatened to tear it down. Someone warned him that whoever "tears down a church will lose his wits". Angered, al-Walid called for a spade. He began breaking the walls with his own hand, in "a robe of yellow silk", and workmen pulled the rest down.

Al-Baladhuri tells this story in his Futuh al-Buldan. He was a Muslim historian who died in 892, well over a century after Mu'awiyah's reign. Philip Hitti, who put the book into English in 1916, draws on al-Fihrist and the lives by Yakut and al-Kutubi. From these, he writes, al-Baladhuri was Ahmad ibn Yahya ibn Jabir, a native of Baghdad of Persian descent. He died deranged, Hitti writes, from drinking the juice of the anacardia, in Arabic baladhur, which gave him his name.

When 'Umar ibn 'Abd al-'Aziz became caliph, the Christians complained, and he ordered the part taken from their church returned. The Muslims of the city objected. In the end the Christians gave up their claim to Saint John's. In return they received the churches of al-Ghutah, an orchard district of Damascus, that had been taken by force.

During the siege of Damascus, al-Baladhuri reports, the commander Khalid wrote terms at the bishop's request. They promised the people "security for their lives, property and churches". Al-Baladhuri also gives the terms the Christians of Najran made with Muhammad. "No attempt shall be made to turn a bishop from his office as a bishop," they say.

Hitti weighs how far al-Baladhuri can be trusted. He says al-Baladhuri tries, wherever he can, to rest each fact on its own chain of named reporters. Sometimes, as al-Baladhuri says himself, he pieces reports together into one account. The Saint John's story is one he opens only with "It is reported". Hitti finds the book "free from exaggeration", but says the author scarcely judges what he reports.

Hitti quotes the later historian al-Mas'udi: "We know of no better work on the history of the Moslem conquests".

Key Works

  • Futuh al-Buldan: the conquests, land by land, and whether each was taken by force or by terms, with many of the terms written out. Hitti says it is a digest of a larger work that is lost. Held in Hitti's English, volume 1
  • Ansab al-Ashraf (Lineage of Nobles): of which Hitti says two volumes survive. Not held

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • His book keeps terms made with Christian towns, among them Najran and Damascus.
  • Hitti says the geographer Yakut reproduces a great part of the book, and that later historians drew freely on it.

How they're remembered

  • The later historian al-Mas'udi, as Hitti quotes him: "We know of no better work on the history of the Moslem conquests".
  • Richard Gottheil, in his foreword to Hitti, calls the book one of the chief authorities for the forming of the Arab state.

Open Questions

  • He writes as a Muslim historian of the conquests, well over a century after many of the events, from reports passed down to him. His life comes from the Arab lives Hitti draws on, al-Fihrist, Yakut and al-Kutubi.
  • How far to trust him. Hitti finds the book free from exaggeration and sincere in seeking the fact. He also says the author scarcely judges what he reports.
  • His method. Hitti says he rests each fact on its own chain of reporters wherever he can. Sometimes, as al-Baladhuri says himself, he pieces reports together. Where his authorities fail, he writes "they said". The Saint John's story opens only with "It is reported".
  • The shortened book. Hitti and Gottheil say the Futuh is a digest of a larger work, now lost. Hitti says some passages are mutilated and ambiguous.
  • His sources. Hitti says his works rest on the tradition of Medina, which Hitti judges the more reliable. Most of the works of his favorite authorities, Hitti adds, are unknown or lost.
