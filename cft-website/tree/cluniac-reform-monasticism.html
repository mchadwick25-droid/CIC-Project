The Early Medieval Era · 909-1109 · Burgundy, then Europe

Burgundy, today in France and beyond

Cluniac Reform Monasticism

Burgundy and then all of Europe, 909 to 1109 — a monastery deliberately placed beyond the reach of any local lord, which grew into the first continent-wide religious network.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:21

Read along

In 909 or 910 — the foundation charter's own year is disputed — a duke of Aquitaine endowed a monastery at Cluny in Burgundy and did something unusual with the deed: he renounced all rights over it, for himself and his heirs, and placed it directly under the pope, so that no local lord or bishop could appoint its abbot or spend its money. Freed from that grip, and led by a run of unusually long-lived and capable abbots, Cluny grew into a network of hundreds of houses across Europe answering to a single abbot, with a life built around an elaborate round of prayer — the community's chief work was the liturgy, sung at a length no earlier Western monastery had attempted. Its abbots reached outward too: Odilo spread the commemoration of all the dead on 2 November and lent his weight to efforts to limit private warfare, and by the eleventh century Cluny's church was the largest in Christendom. Its records are institutional almost throughout — charters, customaries, letters of business — so its inner life is easier to describe from the outside than from within.

Documented Stories

The Count Who Fought with the Flat of His Sword

Odo of Cluny wrote the Life of Gerald of Aurillac about twenty years after Gerald’s death, from the recollections of people who had known him, and it is an unusual book: a saint’s life whose subject was neither monk, bishop nor martyr but a landowner with soldiers. Odo says he wrote it because people doubted a layman could be holy. Gerald, on his account, wanted to enter a monastery and was told by a bishop that he could do more good ruling his lands justly; so he stayed, wore the tonsure hidden under his cap, said the office privately, fed the poor at his table, and refused to profit from his position — Odo has him overpaying for goods, refusing gifts, returning a stolen horse. He also went to war, because his people had to be defended, and here Odo makes the claim the book is remembered for: Gerald ordered his men to fight with the backs of their swords and the butts of their spears, and God so arranged matters that he always won and never shed blood. Odo does not seem to expect this to be believed literally; he wants a picture of a fighting man restrained by conscience. The Life shows what the reform thought a good Christian noble looked like, in the century before the Peace of God councils tried to legislate it — and it shows, in its anxiety, that most nobles did not look like that.

The Hermit Who Heard the Demons Complain

The commemoration of all the faithful departed on 2 November spread through the Latin church from Cluny, and Cluny’s own account of how it began is told by Jotsald, a monk who wrote the Life of Abbot Odilo shortly after his death in 1049. A monk of Rouergue, returning from Jerusalem, was driven by storm onto an island near Sicily — Jotsald means the volcanic Lipari, whose craters medieval people took for mouths of hell — and found a hermit living there. The hermit asked whether he knew of Cluny and its abbot, and told him what he had heard from the rocks: demons crying out that the souls of sinners in their torment were being taken from them by the prayers and alms of the religious, and by none so much as the monks of Cluny. The pilgrim did, and Odilo, Jotsald says, decreed that in all the houses of Cluny the day after All Saints should be kept as a solemn commemoration of all the dead, with Mass, the office and alms. His statute survives, and it makes no mention of hermits or demons; it speaks of the church’s duty to the departed and of the monastery’s many benefactors. The Cluniac liturgy of the dead was already the most elaborate in the West, and the reform’s wealth rested substantially on gifts from nobles who wanted to be prayed for after death.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Cluny kept the Rule of Benedict. The Carolingian reform had pressed that Rule on Frankish monks. Cluny may have become the establishment that the next century's new orders, the Cistercians above all, set themselves against. It reached its peak in the twelfth century under Peter the Venerable. He led the house until 1156. He had the Qur'an put into Latin for the first time, and he took in Peter Abelard.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced Early Benedictine / Italian Monasticism Inferential-Thin — Oswald Hunter Blair was a Benedictine editor of the Rule. In 1907 he drew on the later customs of Cluny to show how it was lived. Cardinal Gasquet, another Benedictine editor, calls the Rule practically the only rule for monks in the West from the ninth to the twelfth century. He does not name Cluny. This rests on these two editors, not on Cluny's own texts. No modern study of it has been consulted.
  • influenced Carolingian Reform Christianity Inferential-Thin — From 742 the Frankish reformers ordered monks and nuns to live by the Rule of Benedict. Cluny, founded around 910, was a house of Benedictine monks. The tie rests on that shared Rule, not on any named founder who passed from one to the other.

In Contact With

  • in contact with The Latin Papal Church (Gregorian Reform to Innocent III) Documented — In January 1077 Pope Gregory VII lifted his ban on King Henry IV at Canossa. Gregory's own report says the abbot of Cluny, Hugh, helped confirm the king's sworn promises. The record of the oath lists the abbot among those present on the king's side.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 909 or 910 a duke of Aquitaine endowed the monastery of Cluny and renounced all rights over it, placing it directly under the pope so that no local lord could control its abbot or its money. Freed from that grip, Cluny grew into a network of hundreds of houses across Europe built around a liturgy sung at a length no earlier Western monastery had attempted, and its abbot Odilo spread the commemoration of the dead on All Souls' Day and worked to limit private warfare.

Legacy

The order itself dwindled. Its great church was quarried away after the French Revolution, and only a fragment of what was once the largest church in Europe still stands at Cluny. What it left behind is larger than what survives of it. Its rule that a monastery answer to Rome, not to the local lord, may have fed into the papal reform of the eleventh century. Its abbot Hugh was at Canossa in 1077 when Pope Gregory VII lifted his ban on King Henry IV. The feast of All Souls on 2 November, which Odilo did most to spread, is still kept across Western Christianity. And the next century's new orders, the Cistercians above all, may have defined themselves against Cluny's wealth and its long liturgy.
