Socrates of Constantinople

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Novatianism.

Known for
Church historian of Constantinople whose seven-book Church History carries the story on from Eusebius of Caesarea, from Constantine to 439, with many of the emperors' letters and the bishops' decisions set out in full.
Also called
Socrates Scholasticus
Dates
Born at Constantinople, no earlier than 379 by A. C. Zenos's reading; his history ends in the emperor Theodosius's seventeenth consulship, 439; he lived some time after, since he revised his first two books
Place
Constantinople
Role
Church historian and recorder

About This Person

After riots between pagans and Christians at Alexandria, many pagans fled the city. Two of them, the grammarians Helladius and Ammonius, came to Constantinople. Helladius boasted that he had killed nine men in the fighting. Ammonius complained that Theophilus, bishop of Alexandria, had kept one statue only to make the old religion look ridiculous. Socrates of Constantinople tells this in his Church History. He was their pupil, he says, "in my youth at Constantinople."

He lived in that city, "where I was born and educated." He wrote for a man he calls "O holy man of God, Theodore." He carried the story of the church on from where Eusebius of Caesarea left off. His seven books run from Constantine to the year 439.

He wrote his first two books following Rufinus of Aquileia. Then he read the writings of Athanasius of Alexandria, and judged "that more credit was due to him who had suffered." So he rewrote them and added the emperors' letters and the bishops' decisions. He chose a plain style, he says, because ordinary readers want the facts. He expected zealots to blame him for not calling bishops "Most holy."

Auxanon, a very old priest of the Novatian church, had gone to Nicaea as a youth. He told Socrates his stories when Socrates was "a mere youth." A. C. Zenos, who revised the English translation, lists the many places where Socrates writes kindly of the Novatians, a strict church that stood apart. Baronius and others held that Socrates was one of them. Valesius and most later writers put it down to his fairness.

He is called Scholasticus, a word that could mean a lawyer. Zenos and Valesius think he was one. Harnack, Zenos reports, finds no sign of legal knowledge in his book.

He ended with a prayer that the churches "may live in peace; for as long as peace continues, those who desire to write histories will find no materials for their purpose."

Key Works

  • Church History: seven books from the proclamation of Constantine to 439, one to each emperor as A. C. Zenos describes the plan, written for Theodore, whom Socrates calls "O holy man of God"; the first two books were rewritten after he read Athanasius of Alexandria

Legacy

What traces to them

  • A. C. Zenos, who revised the English translation, says the history was used, "according to the best authorities", by Sozomen, and certainly by Liberatus of Carthage and by Theodorus Lector.
  • Zenos reports that Epiphanius Scholasticus put the histories of Socrates, Sozomen and Theodoret of Cyrrhus into Latin under Cassiodorus, as the Tripartite History.
  • Chester Hartranft, in the same volume, says Sozomen never names Socrates, but that the internal evidence makes Sozomen's use of him sure.

How they're remembered

  • Hartranft judges that "Socrates was the finer mind, that he had larger sympathies", and that he followed the church's growth "with a sharper and brighter criticism" than Sozomen.
  • Socrates himself expected that "The zealots of our churches will condemn us for not calling the bishops 'Most dear to God,' 'Most holy,' and such like."

What Is Uncertain

  • His life is known almost only from remarks in his own history. Zenos says that reconstructing it from these scattered items "is a matter of difficult critical investigation."
  • Whether he was a Novatian is disputed. Zenos reports that Baronius, Labbe and others, after Nicephorus Callistus, held that he was, from how closely and kindly he writes of the Novatians. Valesius, and most later writers, put it down to his fairness, and note that he treats the Novatians as apart from the Catholic Church.
  • Whether he was a lawyer is disputed. The name Scholasticus could mean one who had studied for a profession, the advocate's among them. Zenos thinks he was trained as an advocate, and Valesius argued that he was a layman and a lawyer. Harnack, Zenos reports, denies that the History shows any legal knowledge. Hartranft calls it "simply an uncertified tradition".
  • His birth year rests on no held line. Valesius reasoned from his schooling under Helladius and Ammonius that he was born in 379. Zenos says the only certainty is that he was born no earlier than 379.
  • Theodore, to whom the history is written, is not otherwise identified. Zenos infers from the address "O holy man of God" that he was a presbyter, and says he cannot be Theodore of Mopsuestia, who died in 429.
  • No source used here gives his death.
