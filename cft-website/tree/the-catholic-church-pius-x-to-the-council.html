The Catholic Church — Pius X to the Eve of the Council

Rome and the Catholic world, 1906 to 1962 — the modernist crisis and the anti-modernist oath, then Divino Afflante Spiritu reopening what Pascendi had closed, the worker-priests and the nouvelle théologie forming the council's seedbed on the eve of its opening.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:12

Read along

In 1907 Rome moved against what it called modernism: the decree Lamentabili and the encyclical Pascendi condemned it, and in 1910 an anti-modernist oath was required of clergy and teachers. Alfred Loisy and George Tyrrell were at the center of it. The argument was the same one about history, scripture and doctrine that would break American Protestantism apart in the 1920s, running inside Catholicism a generation earlier. Then it turned. Divino Afflante Spiritu, in 1943, reopened the biblical scholarship Pascendi had closed. The worker-priest experiment sent clergy into ordinary workplaces. The nouvelle théologie went back behind the manuals to the fathers and to the liturgy. All of that, contested at the time, became the seedbed of the council. And there were the wars, including the record of Pius XII, which has a historiography of its own and was argued about for decades afterward.

Documented Stories

The Grave at Storrington

George Tyrrell, an Irish convert, a Jesuit and the most read Catholic writer in England, had been dismissed from the Society in 1906 for his published views on doctrine and development, and in 1907, after two letters in The Times attacking the encyclical Pascendi, had been deprived of the sacraments by his bishop. He lived at Storrington in Sussex, in a cottage owned by his friend Maude Petre, writing. In July 1909 his kidneys failed. As he lay dying, mostly unconscious, the local Premonstratensian prior gave him conditional absolution and anointing, and his friend the Abbé Henri Bremond, who had come from France, did the same; Tyrrell had made clear that he wished to die a Catholic, but he had not retracted anything, and could not now be asked to. He died on 15 July, aged forty-eight. Petre and Bremond asked the bishop of Southwark, Peter Amigo, for Catholic burial; Amigo required a public retraction as its price, and since there was none, refused. On 21 July Tyrrell was buried in the churchyard of the Anglican parish of St Mary at Storrington, the only ground open to him. Bremond, in cassock, read the Catholic prayers for the dead over the grave and gave a short address; he was suspended from priestly functions by his own bishop within days and had to apologize to be restored. Petre had a stone carved with a chalice and host, and the words that he had been a Catholic priest.

Out of the Factories by March

In 1943 two young chaplains, Henri Godin and Yvan Daniel, published a study called France, a Mission Country?, arguing that the industrial working class had become as foreign to the church as any people overseas, and Cardinal Suhard of Paris answered by founding the Mission de Paris. Its priests, and others in Lyon, Marseille and the northern coalfields, took ordinary jobs, on the Renault lines, the docks, the building sites, wearing overalls, living in workers' lodgings, celebrating Mass in kitchens at night for whoever came. By 1953 there were about a hundred. They joined the unions, which in France meant the communist-led CGT, and some took office in them. To Rome, and to many French bishops, this was the church's priests becoming indistinguishable from the militants they had gone to convert. In January 1954 the French cardinals published Rome's conditions: by 1 March every worker-priest was to leave full-time employment, limit manual work to three hours a day, hold no union office, and live in community with other priests. The priests met, argued and divided. Roughly half submitted, many in grief; roughly half refused, and were suspended or left the priesthood, some marrying, some remaining at their benches as laymen. Their bishops, who had supported them, obeyed Rome and wept in private, as their letters show. In 1959 the Holy Office forbade the experiment entirely. In 1965 Paul VI permitted it again.

Connections

In Its Own Record

The modernist crisis — condemned in the 1907 decree Lamentabili and encyclical Pascendi, and enforced by an anti-modernist oath from 1910, targeting scholars like Alfred Loisy and George Tyrrell — ran the same argument over history, scripture, and doctrine inside Catholicism a generation before it split American Protestantism. Divino Afflante Spiritu in 1943 reopened the biblical scholarship Pascendi had closed, and the worker-priest movement and the nouvelle théologie became the seedbed for the Second Vatican Council. Opus Dei, founded in 1928, and other new lay movements also trace their origins to this period. The period runs up to the eve of the council in 1962.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 1907 Rome condemned what it called modernism in the decree Lamentabili and the encyclical Pascendi, targeting scholars like Alfred Loisy and George Tyrrell, and required an anti-modernist oath of clergy from 1910 — the same argument over history, scripture and doctrine that would split American Protestantism a generation later. The reversal came slowly: Divino Afflante Spiritu in 1943 reopened the biblical scholarship Pascendi had closed, and the worker-priest experiment and the nouvelle théologie's return to the church fathers became the seedbed the Second Vatican Council would build on.

Legacy

The church is the same church, and this half-century is a chapter in its life rather than a movement that ended: it runs to the eve of the council in 1962 and hands straight on. What this period left behind is the council itself. Almost everything the Second Vatican Council did had been prepared in these decades — the biblical scholarship released in 1943, the liturgical movement, the return to the church fathers, and the questions the worker-priests raised about where the church actually is.
