The Imperial Church Era · c. 360-450 · Tours, Lérins, Marseilles

Tours · Lérins, today in France and beyond

Gallic Monastic-Ascetic Christianity

Meet Renatus on Church in Conversation

About This Movement

About 360, a soldier named Martin told Caesar he was now a soldier of Christ, and asked to leave the army. He gathered a few companions near Poitiers, in western Gaul. Some years later, the people of Tours seized him and made him their bishop, and he kept the habits of a monk even in that office. His story survives because a wealthy Aquitanian lawyer, Sulpitius Severus, gave up his career, attached himself to Martin, and wrote his Life while he was still alive. Two other houses complete the picture. Around 410, Honoratus founded a monastery on a small island off the coast near Cannes, Lérins. Honoratus became bishop of Arles, and Hilary, who succeeded him there, wrote his Life; another monk formed on the island, Faustus, later became bishop of Riez. And around 415, John Cassian, who had spent years among the Egyptian monks, founded two houses at Marseilles and wrote down what he had learned there - the Institutes on how a monastery is run, the Conferences on what the Egyptian elders actually said. It was the monks of Marseilles who resisted Augustine's late teaching on grace and human effort. Augustine answered them in two treatises, after a third one - addressed to a different monastery, in Africa - had first provoked their objection.

In Its Own Record

Influenced By

  • Desert Monasticism Documented — Cassian wrote his Institutes at the request of a Gallic bishop, to set out the monastic customs he had seen throughout Egypt and Palestine. Gennadius says he later founded two monasteries at Marseilles.
  • The Gallic Nicene Episcopate Documented — Sulpicius Severus says Martin sought out Bishop Hilary of Poitiers and lived with him for a time, and that Hilary made him an exorcist. After Hilary's return from exile, Martin set up a monastery near the town. Sulpicius also says Martin had already founded one at Milan.

Influenced

  • Early Benedictine / Italian Monasticism Widely Accepted — Benedict's Rule tells monks to read the 'Collationes' at evening gatherings and names the 'Institutes' as models. Cassian's two works have those titles. Gennadius says Cassian founded two monasteries at Marseilles.
  • Insular / Irish Monastic Christianity Inferential-Thin — A single eighth-century Irish account names Cassian and the monks of Lérins as links in the chain that reached Ireland. Beyond that, there is only Patrick's own wish, in his Confession, to visit the brethren in Gaul. Warren, the 1895 editor of the Bangor Antiphonary, held that Ireland's Eastern features came through Gaul.
  • Merovingian Gallic Christianity Widely Accepted — Venantius Fortunatus, a poet of Merovingian Gaul, praises the Rule of Caesarius of Arles. Caesarius came from Lérins and became bishop of Arles. Gennadius records Faustus, first abbot of Lérins, who became bishop of Riez.

Argued Against

  • Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity Documented — Around 428 Prosper of Aquitaine wrote to Augustine from Gaul. He reported that many servants of Christ at Marseilles thought Augustine's writing on election ran against the Fathers. They thought it ran against the mind of the church as well.

Opposed By

  • Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity Documented — Prosper said the men who raised doubts lived at Marseilles. Augustine answered them in On the Predestination of the Saints. He wrote it in 428 or 429 to Prosper and Hilary, for the brothers they were worried about. Prosper adds that Augustine's earlier book On Rebuke and Grace had met like doubts in Africa.

In Contact With

  • Imperial and Juridical Christianity Documented — Martin of Tours was at the emperor Maximus's court at Trier during the Priscillian affair. Sulpicius Severus says he begged Maximus not to shed the blood of the accused. He called it an unheard-of outrage for a secular ruler to judge a church case.
