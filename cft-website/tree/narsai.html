Narsai of Nisibis

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Church of the East under Persia.

Known for
A Persian Christian poet and teacher of Scripture who led the school of Edessa for twenty years, fled to Nisibis when accused of heresy and of siding with Persia, and founded there, with Barsauma of Nisibis, the school of the Church of the East.
Also called
Narsai, Narses
Dates
Left Edessa in 457 by R. H. Connolly's account, or after 471 if the bishop named by Barhadbeshabba stands, as François Nau notes; died in 502 by Connolly after Alphonsus Mingana, or in 503 or 504 by Nau's reckoning
Place
Ma'alta; Edessa; Nisibis
Role
Poet and head of the schools of Edessa and Nisibis

About This Person

One night at Edessa, a friend warned the teacher Narsai of Nisibis that he would be thrown into the fire unless he fled before morning. Narsai went to the church and found some Persians there. He asked them to carry his books, which were all his treasure, and they took them to Nisibis. Barhadbeshabba 'Arbaia tells it in a history of his church's saints, written near the end of the 500s by François Nau's dating.

Narsai was a Persian Christian, a poet and a teacher of Scripture. Barhadbeshabba says he came from the land of Ma'alta. He went to school at seven and learned the whole Psalter in nine months. He studied at Edessa, in the Roman empire, and led its school for twenty years.

His accusers there said he was a heretic who followed Theodore of Mopsuestia and Nestorius, Barhadbeshabba writes. They also said he sided with Persia and wanted to betray the city. When he left Edessa is disputed. R. H. Connolly, his translator, gives 457. Nau notes that the history names a bishop of Edessa who took office in 471, though Nau thinks it means an earlier bishop.

At Nisibis he meant to go on into Persia. Barsauma of Nisibis, the bishop, persuaded him to stay and bought him a camel stable near the church for a school. Students came from all sides. In Connolly's English of a chronicle of the school, "Edessa was darkened, but Nisibis shone forth". Connolly writes that with this school the church now called Nestorian began to take shape.

Barhadbeshabba says Barsauma's wife grew jealous of the crowds at Narsai's door and turned her husband against him. Narsai withdrew to a monastery and wrote a poem about her. When it was read at Nisibis, the bishop repented and called him back.

Narsai held to Theodore. Connolly reports that in one homily Narsai says all his skill in Scripture came from Theodore's commentaries. Jacob of Sarug, whom Barhadbeshabba calls a heretic, spread his teaching in verse. Narsai answered, Barhadbeshabba says, with a verse homily for every day of the year. Simeon of Beth Arsham, of the opposing side, calls him Narsai the leper.

Barhadbeshabba says he was buried in a good old age. Connolly, following Alphonsus Mingana, puts his death in 502; Nau reckons 503 or 504.

In his homily on baptism he wrote of God, "instead of our clay He has made us spiritual gold."

Key Works

  • Metrical homilies: verse homilies on Scripture and the sacraments; Ebedjesu, a writer of the 1200s, credits him with 360, Connolly reports, and the Library holds four in Connolly's English
  • An Exposition of the Mysteries (Homily XVII): a verse account of the eucharistic liturgy; late writers gave it to a writer of the 1200s, and Connolly argues it is Narsai's
  • On Baptism (Homily XXII): a verse homily on baptism and anointing, in Connolly's English

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The Cause of the Foundation of the Schools, as R. H. Connolly translates it: "Edessa was darkened, but Nisibis shone forth". It names Narsai and his successors as the heads of the school of Nisibis.
  • Connolly writes that with the school of Nisibis began the formation of "what is now known as the Nestorian Church".

How they're remembered

  • Mari ibn Sulayman, writing in the 1100s, reports that Theodulus, a disciple of Mar Diodore, blessed him and called him the tongue of the East and the poet of the Christian religion.
  • Barhadbeshabba 'Arbaia tells his life as a saint's life, with the healing of a boy and a long fight against Satan.
  • Simeon of Beth Arsham, of the opposing side, calls him Narsai the leper, in a letter that Bar Hebraeus's editors quote from Assemani.

What Is Uncertain

  • His life comes almost wholly from Barhadbeshabba 'Arbaia, who wrote in praise of the saints of his own church near the end of the 500s, by François Nau's dating. Mari ibn Sulayman and Bar Hebraeus wrote centuries later. François Nau gives the History to Barhadbeshabba 'Arbaia, and the Cause of the Foundation of the Schools to Barhadbeshabba, bishop of Halwan in 605; he thinks it not impossible that they are one man.
  • When he left Edessa is disputed. Connolly gives 457, the year the followers of Ibas were expelled. Barhadbeshabba names a bishop of Edessa who took office in 471, Nau notes; Nau thinks an earlier bishop is meant, since otherwise Narsai would have left after 471 and died after 512. Addai Scher, editing the Cause, doubts that Narsai and his companions were expelled together in 457, as Simeon of Beth Arsham told it.
  • His years at Nisibis are told three ways: forty in Barhadbeshabba's History, forty-five in the Cause of the Foundation of the Schools, and fifty in Bar Hebraeus. So his death is put in 502 by Connolly after Mingana, in 503 or 504 by Nau, and in 507 by those who follow Bar Hebraeus, as Nau reports.
  • Barhadbeshabba gives the quarrel with Barsauma to the jealousy of Barsauma's wife. Bar Hebraeus, hostile, says Barsauma expelled Narsai over a concubine. All three tellers say the two were reconciled.
  • That all his skill in Scripture came from Theodore of Mopsuestia rests on his homily on the three Doctors, as Connolly reports it; the Library does not hold that homily.
  • Connolly treats three of the four homilies he translates as certainly his. The Exposition of the Mysteries was given by one or two late authorities to a writer of the 1200s; Connolly argues it is Narsai's.
