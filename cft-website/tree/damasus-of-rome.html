Damasus of Rome

About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

A leading pagan of Rome, Praetextatus, used to say to the bishop, Damasus: "Make me bishop of the city of Rome, and I will be a Christian at once." Jerome remembered the joke.

Damasus was bishop of Rome from 366 to 384. He won the office in a fight that left many dead. Then he held his church to the creed agreed at Nicaea, that the Son is of one being with the Father. In 380 a law of the emperors named him as the standard of that faith. He is on the tree because in his years emperors took sides in the church's quarrels and used his name.

When the bishop Liberius died, two parties chose two bishops, the deacons Ursinus and Damasus. Fighting followed in the churches, and the accounts disagree. Ammianus, a former soldier and historian, most likely a pagan, blames both men's ambition. He counts 137 dead in one day in a church. An account written by Ursinus's own party blames Damasus. It says he paid charioteers and arena fighters, and with them and gravediggers stormed a church held by the other side. They used axes, swords and fire, and killed 160 men and women, with no dead on his own side. That is one side's charge. Church writers of his own time and the next blame Ursinus instead.

The city's governor, Praetextatus, calmed the tumult, and Ursinus was expelled.

From about 369, Damasus held the West to Nicaea. A council at Rome under him cut off the bishop of Milan, an Arian, who rejected the creed of Nicaea. His letters went east, offering communion, which meant to count as one church with him. At Alexandria, the bishop there later wrote, the deacon who carried them was led through the town bound and flogged.

In the 370s Apollinaris of Laodicea taught that the Word of God stood in place of a human reasoning mind in Christ. Damasus and the bishop of Alexandria condemned this in council at Rome. After the Arian emperor Valens died, a general enforced an edict handing the churches to those in communion with Damasus. An emperor ruled on bishops whom Damasus and a council of five or seven had condemned. Any who held on to his church was to be brought to Rome by the governors.

In February 380, the emperors issued the law: "It is our will that all the peoples under our merciful rule live in the religion that the divine apostle Peter handed down to the Romans. The faith he brought them, kept to this day, shows that he did. It is plain that Bishop Damasus follows it, and Peter, bishop of Alexandria, a man of an apostle's holiness."

The law did not make Damasus ruler of the church, but it named him, with the bishop of Alexandria, as the measure of the right faith. Everyone else, it said, would bear the shame of heretical teaching, and the punishment of God and then of the emperors.

Two priests of a strict group, followers of Lucifer of Cagliari, petitioned the emperors. They say Damasus's clergy, with officials, broke up a night service held by their priest Macarius and dragged him over the stones. A judge, acting as if under an imperial order, pressed him to join Damasus's communion. He refused, was exiled, and, they say, died of his wound. The petition gives no date and names no law, and we have no answer from Damasus's side.

The book of the popes' lives was written about 150 years later. It says Damasus searched out the graves of the martyrs, in the burial galleries outside the city, and marked them with verses. At his urging, too, Jerome, his secretary, corrected the old Latin Gospels against the Greek.

Damasus died in December 384, almost eighty years old, Jerome says. His epitaph, which its editors take to be his own, praises the one who stilled the sea's bitter waves and gave a brother back to his sister Martha. Then it ends: "I believe that, after the ashes, he will make Damasus rise."

Quotations and references

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “Make me bishop of the city of Rome, and I will be a Christian at once.”
    As printed
    “Make me bishop of Rome, and I will at once be a Christian.”
    Jerome, Against John of Jerusalem 8, tr. W. H. Fremantle, in Nicene and Post-Nicene Fathers, series 2, vol. 6
    Jerome's Latin, in Migne's Patrologia Latina 23 (lines 27958-27960 of its file), is damaged at the name of the city: the scan prints Πο- mans for Romanae. Mansi's print of the same sentence (lines 42058-42060 of its file) reads Romane urbis, so the Latin is bishop of the city of Rome, and the modern wording follows it. The Latin is printed with Romanae as Mansi reads it. Jerome reports the saying as one Praetextatus made in jest, without a date, after Praetextatus had died as consul designate.
  • In our modern English
    “It is our will that all the peoples under our merciful rule live in the religion that the divine apostle Peter handed down to the Romans. The faith he brought them, kept to this day, shows that he did. It is plain that Bishop Damasus follows it, and Peter, bishop of Alexandria, a man of an apostle's holiness.”
    As printed
    “cunctos populos, quos clementiae nostrae regit temperamentum, in tali volumus religione versari, quam divinum petrum apostolum tradidisse romanis religio usque ad nunc ab ipso insinuata declarat quamque pontificem damasum sequi claret et petrum alexandriae episcopum virum apostolicae sanctitatis”
    Edict of Gratian, Valentinian and Theodosius to the people of Constantinople, 27 February 380, Theodosian Code 16.1.2, Latin
    The Latin Library does not say which edition it follows. Mommsen and Meyer's critical text (lines 83597-83601 of its file, whose scan prints Cuncios for Cunctos) reads the same words. The modern wording renders pontificem as bishop. The Latin goes on to say that this is to believe in one Godhead of Father, Son and Holy Spirit; the quotation stops before that.
  • In our modern English
    “I believe that, after the ashes, he will make Damasus rise.”
    As printed
    “post cineres Damasum faciet quia surgere credo.”
    Epitaph of Damasus, verses that Duchesne takes to be his own, from De Rossi, in Duchesne's Liber Pontificalis
    Duchesne's print is the only one the Library holds, and its scan is damaged in the poem's second and fourth lines (128206 and 128210); this last line reads cleanly. Duchesne says the verses were composed by Damasus himself. The one who will make Damasus rise is the one the poem's earlier lines describe: who walked and stilled the sea's bitter waves, who could loose the deadly chains of death, and who gave a brother back to his sister Martha.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • At his request Jerome of Strido revised the old Latin Gospels against the Greek. Jerome's preface begins, "You urge me to revise the old Latin version."
  • Jerome says that he helped Damasus with his letters and wrote his answers to the councils of the East and West.
  • In his preface Jerome says he expects to be called a forger and a profane person for revising the old Latin version. Fremantle dates the preface to 383.
  • A verse of his at the place in the catacombs where Peter and Paul were said to have lain says that the East sent them as disciples, but that Rome deserved rather to defend them as her citizens.
  • In the sixth century Pope Vigilius set up an inscription honoring martyrs whom, it says, Damasus found and marked with a poem.
  • Sozomen, in the next century, retells the law of 380 as bringing all the emperor's subjects to the faith that Peter had preached to the Romans and that Damasus and Peter of Alexandria professed.
  • Theodoret tells that at Antioch, when the general Sapor showed the imperial edict, the priest Flavian told two rival bishops to show that they agreed with Damasus, and told Apollinarius to embrace the teaching of Damasus.
  • About 449, in his own quarrel, Theodoret quotes Damasus's anathema against those who say that pain on the cross was borne by the Godhead and not by the body with the soul. The date is the editor's.
  • Eastern bishops, in a letter to Rufus kept by Theodoret, write that the points they list are those for which "the thrice blessed Damasus deposed the heretics Apollinarius, Vitalius, and Timotheus." The held text gives no date.
  • The Roman Breviary of 1908 keeps his feast on 11 December.

How they're remembered

  • Jerome, writing to him from the East in 376 or 377, said he kept communion with none but Damasus, "that is with the chair of Peter."
  • In his Lives of Illustrious Men, Jerome remembers that he "had a fine talent for making verses and published many brief works in heroic metre."
  • Ammianus Marcellinus, a historian and most likely a pagan, set the riot of his election beside a sharp picture of Rome's bishops, "enriched by offerings from matrons, riding in carriages, dressing splendidly."
  • Blomfield Jackson, who translated Theodoret, quotes Northcote and Brownlow on his work in the catacombs, "to rediscover the tombs which had been blocked up for concealment under Diocletian."
  • The book of the popes' lives says he was buried on the Via Ardeatina, in his own basilica, on 11 December, beside his mother and his sister.

At Major Councils

  • Councils of Ariminum and Seleucia, 359: Not present; his council at Rome said the numbers at Ariminum prejudged nothing
  • First Council of Constantinople, 381: Not present, and not invited by Hefele's reading; the synod of 382 wrote to him

What Is Uncertain

  • Ammianus Marcellinus blames both rivals and counts a hundred and thirty-seven dead in the Basilica of Sicininus in one day. Socrates of Constantinople blames Ursinus, who had himself ordained in secret. Sozomen, a church historian of the fifth century, tells it the same way after him. An anonymous account from Ursinus's own party, kept in the Collectio Avellana, blames Damasus. It says he paid charioteers, arena men and gravediggers, and counts a hundred and sixty dead in a church he besieged, none on his side.
  • The Avellana account dates Liberius's death and the fighting by the consuls of 366, and Chester Hartranft, who translated Sozomen, dates Liberius's death to September 366. A. C. Zenos, who translated Socrates, notes that Jerome's Chronicle puts it in 367.
  • The chronological tables in W. H. Fremantle's translation of Jerome's works date his death to December 384. Ernest Cushing Richardson, who translated Jerome's Lives, gives 380 in a note. Jerome himself says only that he died under Theodosius, at almost eighty.
  • Zenos's note calls him "a Spaniard by race, native of Mantua". No early witness in the sources used here gives his birthplace.
  • Sozomen calls his rival Ursicius. Ammianus, Socrates and the Avellana account call him Ursinus.
  • Ammianus says the city's governor, Juventius, could not stop the disorders and was forced by the violence to withdraw to the suburbs. He says later that Praetextatus, as governor, appeased a tumult that the quarrels of the Christians had raised, and that after Ursinus was expelled calm was restored.
  • Jerome's Chronicle says Ursinus, made bishop by some, seized the Sicininum with his men, and that when Damasus's people flocked there the most cruel killings of both sexes followed. Rufinus says Ursinus, a deacon, had a rustic and unskilled bishop ordain him in the basilica of Sicininus, and that the prefect Maximinus turned the matter against a good and innocent priest, even to the torture of clergy. Socrates says the dispute was not about any article of faith but only about who should be bishop, and that many clergy and laity were punished by the prefect Maximin.
  • The Ursinian account says Damasus paid the judge of the city and the prefect of the grain supply to have Ursinus and two deacons exiled, and that after the exile he fell with armed followers on worshipers gathered at St Agnes and struck down many. It says the crowd chanted that Damasus had made a fifth war, and that the matrons loved him so much that he was called the matrons' ear-tickler. The clubs and swords of the fight are in this account only; Ammianus says only that the partisans carried their violence to actual battle. The account calls the besieged church the basilica of Liberius, while Jerome and Rufinus put the fighting at the Sicininum, and the held texts do not settle whether these are one building.
  • The Liber Pontificalis says a council of priests chose Damasus and that Ursinus was made bishop of Naples. Duchesne judges those lines a later addition modeled on the story of Pope Symmachus, and says Ursinus never held another see. Blomfield Jackson's note calls Damasus the nominee of the Arian party; no held ancient text says so.
  • An order of the emperors to Praetextatus, on the petition of the defenders of the Roman church or of Damasus, has the one church still held by dissenters opened to Damasus. Günther dates it between 16 November 367 and 12 January 368.
  • The council at Rome that cut off Auxentius of Milan also annulled the decrees of Rimini, on the plea that the bishop of Rome had not assented to them. The year 369 is Hartranft's note. Jackson notes that the Latin of the council's letter differs materially from the Greek Theodoret gives.
  • Rufinus says Apollinaris at first taught that the Lord took a body but no soul, and, when pressed, said he had a soul but not its reasoning part, which the Word of God itself filled. Hartranft's note gives the year of the council at Rome that condemned him as 377 by Rade or 374 by Hefele.
  • Basil, in a letter without an address, asks the bishop of Rome to send help to the East; the Benedictine editors say it was obviously addressed to Damasus. In another letter Basil writes of the West: "Really lofty souls, when they are courted, get haughtier than ever." That this is aimed at Damasus is Ceillier's reading, as Jackson reports it, from Basil's wish to write to their Coryphaeus.
  • Günther dates the emperors' rescript on Damasus's court, to the vicar Aquilinus, between 9 August 378 and 19 January 379. It also sends a metropolitan's case to Rome, or to judges the bishop of Rome names. Duchesne dates to 380 the letter of a Roman council to the emperors that asks for such an order.
  • That letter from the council says the party of Ursinus set up Isaac, a Jew, whose accusation sought Damasus's head, and that the emperors, by their judgment, found Damasus innocent. Duchesne judges the Liber Pontificalis's charge of adultery modeled on the story of Symmachus, and says Damasus was then about seventy-five. The nature of the charge is not known.
  • Duchesne says that the tradition of which tomb was whose had already grown obscure or confused by the end of the fourth century, so that the church had to work to establish the identity of the holy tombs. The Liber Pontificalis gives no reason for the searching out of the graves.
