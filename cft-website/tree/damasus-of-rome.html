About This Person

On one day in Rome, a hundred and thirty-seven dead bodies were found in the Basilica of Sicininus, a Christian church. Ammianus Marcellinus, a soldier and historian, most likely a pagan, tells it. Two men wanted to be bishop of the city, Damasus and Ursinus. Their followers fought until men were hurt and killed, and the city's prefect was forced out to the suburbs. In the end, Ammianus says, Damasus won by the hard work of his side.

Damasus of Rome followed Liberius as bishop, in 366 or 367. Ammianus blames both rivals, "both immoderately eager to obtain the bishopric." Socrates of Constantinople blames Ursinus. He says Ursinus was a deacon who could not bear his defeat, and had bishops "of little distinction" ordain him in secret. An account from Ursinus's side tells it the other way. It is kept in Latin in a Roman book of letters, the Collectio Avellana. It says Damasus paid charioteers, arena men and gravediggers, and stormed a church held by the other side. It counts a hundred and sixty dead, men and women, and none on his own side.

As bishop, Damasus held to the creed of the Council of Nicaea. Theodoret of Cyrus kept some of his letters in the history of the church he wrote in the next century. One, from a council at Rome, condemned Auxentius of Milan. It said bishops who thought otherwise should be "deprived of the name of bishop." Another condemned Timothy, a follower of Apollinarius of Laodicea. Damasus wrote that Timothy had been condemned "by the judgment of the apostolic see."

Jerome of Strido worked for him at Rome. He later wrote that he helped Damasus with his letters, and wrote his answers to councils of the East and West. Damasus asked him to correct the Latin Gospels against the Greek. "You urge me to revise the old Latin version," Jerome's preface begins. He knew he would be called a forger for it. One comfort was that "the command is given by you who are the supreme bishop."

Sulpicius Severus, a chronicler from Aquitaine, says Priscillian of Abila and two Spanish bishops came to Rome to clear themselves before Damasus. "They were not even admitted to his presence."

He died under the emperor Theodosius, at almost eighty, Jerome says. Jerome remembered a bishop who "had a fine talent for making verses and published many brief works in heroic metre."

Key Works

  • Synodal letter to the bishops of Illyria: from a council at Rome, condemning Auxentius of Milan and holding to the creed of Nicaea; kept by Theodoret of Cyrus
  • Synodal letter against Apollinarius and Timothy: condemning Timothy, follower of Apollinarius of Laodicea, by the judgment of the apostolic see; kept by Theodoret
  • Confession of faith sent to Paulinus: a list of anathemas against Arius, Eunomius, Sabellius, Photinus and the Macedonians, among others; kept by Theodoret

Legacy

What traces to them

  • At his request Jerome of Strido revised the old Latin Gospels against the Greek. Jerome's preface begins, "You urge me to revise the old Latin version."
  • Jerome says that he helped Damasus with his letters and wrote his answers to the councils of the East and West.

How they're remembered

  • Jerome, writing to him from the East in 376 or 377, said he kept communion with none but Damasus, "that is with the chair of Peter."
  • In his Lives of Illustrious Men, Jerome remembers that he "had a fine talent for making verses and published many brief works in heroic metre."
  • Ammianus Marcellinus, a historian and most likely a pagan, set the riot of his election beside a sharp picture of Rome's bishops, "enriched by offerings from matrons, riding in carriages, dressing splendidly."
  • Blomfield Jackson, who translated Theodoret, quotes Northcote and Brownlow on his work in the catacombs, "to rediscover the tombs which had been blocked up for concealment under Diocletian."

At Major Councils

  • Councils of Ariminum and Seleucia, 359: Not present; his council at Rome said the numbers at Ariminum prejudged nothing
  • First Council of Constantinople, 381: Not present, and not invited by Hefele's reading; the synod of 382 wrote to him

What Is Uncertain

  • Ammianus Marcellinus blames both rivals and counts a hundred and thirty-seven dead in the Basilica of Sicininus in one day. Socrates of Constantinople blames Ursinus, who had himself ordained in secret. Sozomen, a church historian of the fifth century, tells it the same way after him. An anonymous account from Ursinus's own party, kept in the Collectio Avellana, blames Damasus. It says he paid charioteers, arena men and gravediggers, and counts a hundred and sixty dead in a church he besieged, none on his side.
  • The Avellana account dates Liberius's death and the fighting by the consuls of 366, and Chester Hartranft, who translated Sozomen, dates Liberius's death to September 366. A. C. Zenos, who translated Socrates, notes that Jerome's Chronicle puts it in 367.
  • The chronological tables in W. H. Fremantle's translation of Jerome's works date his death to December 384. Ernest Cushing Richardson, who translated Jerome's Lives, gives 380 in a note. Jerome himself says only that he died under Theodosius, at almost eighty.
  • Zenos's note calls him "a Spaniard by race, native of Mantua". No early witness in the sources used here gives his birthplace.
  • Sozomen calls his rival Ursicius. Ammianus, Socrates and the Avellana account call him Ursinus.
