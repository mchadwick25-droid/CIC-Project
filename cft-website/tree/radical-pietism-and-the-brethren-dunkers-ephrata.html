Radical Pietism & the Brethren (Dunkers, Ephrata)

Germany and then Pennsylvania, 1690s to 1815 — separatists who rebaptized one another in a German river in 1708, crossed the Atlantic, and built a singing, manuscript-making cloister in the Pennsylvania woods.

German Pietism - Spener, Francke, Halle→Radical Pietism & the Brethren (Dunkers, Ephrata)→no descendant recorded

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:09

Read along

Where mainstream Pietism stayed inside the Lutheran church, a radical wing walked out of it — separatists who held that the true church had fallen away centuries earlier and that no state establishment could be mended. In 1708, at Schwarzenau in the little county of Wittgenstein — one of the few German territories that would leave separatists alone — eight of them baptized one another in the river Eder and became the Brethren, joining Pietist inwardness to Anabaptist convictions: baptism only for believers, plain living, and refusal of the sword. Persecution pushed them to Pennsylvania from 1719, where they became one of the first German-speaking religious cultures in colonial America; and where one of them, Conrad Beissel, founded the celibate community at Ephrata, which produced its own austere music, its own hand-lettered Fraktur manuscripts, and a sisterhood, the Roses of Saron, who wrote and sang their own hymns.

People and Stories

Voices in the Record

  • Alexander Mack — led the first Brethren baptisms at Schwarzenau in 1708 and wrote the movement's founding tracts
  • Gottfried Arnold — radical Pietist historian whose Impartial History of Churches and Heretics (1699-1700) retold church history from the dissenters' side
  • Ernst Christoph Hochmann von Hochenau — itinerant separatist preacher whose 1702 confession shaped the circle Mack came out of
  • Conrad Beissel — founder of the Ephrata community, composer of its hymnody and author of its rule
  • The Roses of Saron — Ephrata's sisterhood, who produced much of the community's music, illuminated manuscripts and written work

Documented Stories

Eight in the Eder, and the Name Never Told

Alexander Mack was a miller's son from Schriesheim in the Palatinate who had sold his share of the mill and gone to Schwarzenau, in the county of Wittgenstein, where a count who read Gottfried Arnold let separatists live in peace. The circle there, shaped by the preacher Hochmann von Hochenau, read the New Testament, Arnold's history of the church's decline, and the Anabaptist martyr-books, and concluded that the apostolic pattern required believers' baptism by immersion, which none of them had. Early in August 1708 eight of them — Mack and his wife Anna Margaretha, Georg Grebe, Lukas Vetter, Andreas Boni and his wife Johanna, Johannes Kipping and his wife Johanna — went at dawn to the river Eder. They had drawn lots for who should baptize Mack, and had agreed that his name would never be told, so that the church could not be named after any man; it never has been. He baptized Mack three times forward in the name of the Trinity, and Mack then baptized the other seven. Before the day they had written an open letter to the Pietists of the Palatinate explaining why they were doing it and inviting them to come. They kept the love feast with foot-washing, refused oaths and the sword, and held that no one should be baptized who had not counted the cost. By 1720 persecution and the count's death had scattered them; most reached Pennsylvania between 1719 and 1729, and Mack died at Germantown in 1735.

The Printer Marched to Valley Forge

Christopher Sauer the younger was a Germantown printer and a Brethren elder. His father had printed the first Bible in a European language in America, the German Bible of 1743, and the son had printed a second edition in 1763 and a third in 1776, together with almanacs and a newspaper that reached German households through the colonies. When the war came he would not take the oath of allegiance to the new state, because Brethren swore no oaths and took no side, and he went on printing religious books while the British held Philadelphia. His sons were open Loyalists, which did not help him. On the night of 24 May 1778, after the British withdrawal, a party of Continental soldiers took him from his bed at Germantown, stripped him, cut off his hair and beard, painted his face red and black, and marched him barefoot to the camp at Valley Forge, where he was held until General Peter Muhlenberg, a Lutheran pastor's son who knew him, obtained his release. His property had already been seized under the treason laws; his house, press, type, bindery and books were sold at auction that August, and the sheets of the unfinished 1776 Bible were used, tradition says, as wadding for cartridges. He was left with nothing, refused to sue for it, lived on his children and the Brethren, and died in 1784. His petition to the Assembly describes it without complaint.

Ephrata Nurses the Army

The Ephrata community in Lancaster County, founded by Conrad Beissel in 1732, was by the 1770s a small, aging settlement of celibate brothers and sisters and a surrounding congregation of married households, keeping the seventh-day Sabbath, singing Beissel's hymns at midnight services, and lettering their manuscripts in a Fraktur hand that took years to learn. Its members would not fight. After the battle of Brandywine on 11 September 1777 the Continental Army, retreating through the county, requisitioned the community's buildings as a hospital, and wounded and sick men were brought in wagons through the autumn. Camp fever, which was typhus, was among them. The brothers gave up their house, Bethania, and the sisters nursed in it; the community's own later history says that several brothers and sisters caught the fever and died of it, and that the dead soldiers were buried on Mount Zion, above the settlement, in graves the community dug. When the army left in the spring, Bethania was burned as infected. Numbers are uncertain; nineteenth-century accounts gave hundreds of soldiers and a long list of the dead, and modern historians think the figures inflated, but the requisition, the fever, the deaths among the members and the burials are documented. The community never fully recovered its numbers, and the celibate orders died out in the following century. The married congregation became the Seventh Day German Baptists.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Radical Pietism grew out of German Pietism, among people who left the state churches. Out of it came the Brethren. They joined Pietist inward faith to Anabaptist ways: baptism for believers only, plain living and refusal of the sword.

Descended from

  • grew out of German Pietism - Spener, Francke, Halle Widely Accepted Major — Johann Wilhelm Petersen, later a leading radical Pietist, often went to the prayer meeting in Spener's house. There he heard talk of the church's future. Alexander Mack, who founded the Brethren in 1708, wrote that they had once lived 'among the pietists'. A Brethren historian wrote in 1899 that Francke woke Ernst Christoph Hochmann to faith at Halle, and that Hochmann shaped Mack more than anyone. The same historian denied that the founders were Pietists themselves.

Direct descendants

No direct descendant is recorded. Its other links are influence, argument and contact.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced The Anabaptist Movements Documented — The Ephrata chronicle says its first members were "mostly descended from the Mennonites." They kept the plain Mennonite way of life. In 1748 Ephrata printed the Mennonite book of martyrs in German. But the Brethren writer Martin Brumbaugh said his church was not a branch of the Mennonites.

In Contact With

  • in contact with The Mennonites and the Amish Documented — The Mennonites of Pennsylvania agreed that the Ephrata Brethren should put their Dutch martyr book into German and print it. The Brethren spent three years on it and printed 1300 copies. A council with the Mennonites set the price.
  • in contact with The Swedish Lutherans of New Sweden Documented — On 20 August 1753 the Swedish pastor Israel Acrelius came to the cloister at Ephrata. He met its leader, Conrad Beissel. He argued with the brothers about how to baptize.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 1708, eight separatists waded into the river Eder at Schwarzenau in Germany and baptized one another, joining Pietist inward renewal to Anabaptist convictions — baptism only for believing adults, plain living, and refusal to bear arms. Driven to Pennsylvania, one of them, Conrad Beissel, founded the celibate community at Ephrata, where a sisterhood called the Roses of Saron composed austere music and hand-lettered manuscripts unlike anything else made in colonial America.

Legacy

It became an ongoing family of churches: the Brethren bodies of North America — the Church of the Brethren and its relatives. Descend directly from the Schwarzenau baptisms, and they carried the plain-dress, peace-church strand of American Christianity alongside the Mennonites and the Quakers. Ephrata itself died out, but its buildings survive as a Pennsylvania state historic site, along with its Fraktur, its hymnbooks and a body of choral music unlike anything else written in colonial America. The radical Pietist habit of printing — Christopher Sauer's press in Germantown produced the first Bible printed in a European language in North America — left its own long mark on American publishing.

Experience It Today

  • Ephrata Cloister, Ephrata, Pennsylvania — the original eighteenth-century community buildings, preserved as a state historic site and open to visitors
