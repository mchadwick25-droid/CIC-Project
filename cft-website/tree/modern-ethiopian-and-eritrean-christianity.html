Modern Ethiopian & Eritrean Christianity

Ethiopia and Eritrea, 1900 to 2010 — the Tawahedo church carried through empire, occupation and the Derg's persecution, alongside the P'ent'ay evangelical movement, continuing the re-founded empire church of the century before.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:23

Read along

The Ethiopian Tawahedo church entered the twentieth century as an ancient national church under a Coptic hierarchy, and spent the century becoming self-governing under a series of shocks. The first Ethiopian bishops were consecrated in 1929; Italy occupied the country from 1936 to 1941, and massacred the monks of Debre Libanos in 1937; the church received its own patriarch in 1959. The Derg's revolution deposed Patriarch Tewophilos in 1976 and killed him in 1979, and the church's own martyrology from those years is its record of what followed. Alongside all this a Protestant evangelical movement, the P'ent'ay, grew fast — the Kale Heywet and Mekane Yesus churches among them, the latter publishing a much-quoted letter on holistic ministry in 1972 — and grew fastest underground under the Derg. In May 1970 Archimandrite Laike Mariam Mandefro, later Archbishop Yesehaq, established the Ethiopian Orthodox Church in Jamaica. Eritrea's church became autocephalous in 1993; in 2006 its Patriarch Antonios was deposed and placed under house arrest.

Documented Stories

Debre Libanos

Debre Libanos, founded by Tekle Haymanot in the thirteenth century, was the most venerated monastery in Ethiopia, and its abbot held the second place in the church. On 19 February 1937 two young Eritreans threw grenades at the Italian viceroy Rodolfo Graziani in Addis Ababa; he survived, and the reprisals that followed in the capital killed thousands over three days. Graziani then turned on the monastery, which the Italians suspected of sheltering the attackers. On 20 May 1937, the feast of Tekle Haymanot by the Ethiopian calendar, troops under General Pietro Maletti surrounded Debre Libanos as pilgrims filled it. The monks and deacons were separated out, loaded on to lorries, driven to a place called Laga Wolde and shot in groups over the following days; on 26 May the young deacons and students held back at first were taken to Engecha and shot as well. Graziani reported to Mussolini that 297 monks and 23 laymen had been executed and that 'of the monastery of Debre Libanos no trace remains.' The historian Ian Campbell, working from Italian military files and Ethiopian testimony, has put the number of dead, pilgrims included, at between 1,800 and 2,200. The previous July the Italians had shot Abune Petros, bishop of Wollo, in a public square in Addis Ababa for refusing to submit; the church came to count him and the monks of Debre Libanos together among its martyrs.

Two Churchmen the Derg Killed

Abune Tewophilos had been patriarch of the Ethiopian Orthodox Church since 1971 when the Derg, the military council that had deposed Haile Selassie in 1974, removed him in February 1976, charging him with corruption and with having been the emperor's man. He was imprisoned. In his place the government arranged the election of Abune Tekle Haymanot, a hermit-monk of peasant origin from Wollega with no experience of administration, a choice designed to signal that the church would now belong to the poor rather than to the court; the Coptic patriarchate refused to recognize the change. Tewophilos was killed in prison in 1979; his death was not announced, and his remains were identified and reburied with honor only in 1992, after the Derg had fallen. Gudina Tumsa, general secretary of the Ethiopian Evangelical Church Mekane Yesus, had signed the church's 1972 letter insisting that proclaiming the gospel and serving human need could not be separated, and had argued through the revolution's early years that a Christian owed the new order neither flight nor silence. He declined offers to leave the country. He was arrested in 1978 and released, and on 28 July 1979, leaving an evening service at the Urael congregation in Addis Ababa with his wife Tsehay, he was seized by armed men. His body was found in a mass grave and reburied in 1992. After the Derg fell, the Orthodox church counted Tewophilos among its martyrs, and the Lutheran churches counted Gudina Tumsa among theirs.

In Its Own Record

The Tawahedo church lived on through empire, war and the Derg's attacks on it. Beside it grew the P'ent'ay, a fast-growing movement of evangelicals. It carries on the church of Tewodros and Menelik.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Italian occupying forces massacred the monks of Debre Libanos in 1937, and decades later Ethiopia's Communist Derg deposed the country's patriarch, Tewophilos, in 1976 and killed him in 1979 -- a history the Tawahedo church took into its own liturgical memory. Alongside it, a Protestant evangelical movement called the P'ent'ay grew fastest precisely while it was forced underground under the Derg. Eritrea's church, autocephalous since 1993, faced its own severe pressure: in 2006 Patriarch Antonios was deposed and placed under house arrest.

Legacy

In 2010 both churches were living. The Tawahedo was one of the largest ancient churches anywhere. Its own canon, calendar, liturgical language and fasting discipline were intact after a century that took the state away twice. By then the evangelical movement that grew beside it was a large minority in its own right. The relationship between the two was unresolved. The Derg-era martyrology became part of the church's own liturgical memory. Ethiopia's patriarchal disputes ran on after 1991 and were still unresolved in 2010; Eritrea's church was under severe pressure.
