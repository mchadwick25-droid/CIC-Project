The Reformation Era · 1610-1650 · Acadia, Quebec, the Huron country

Mission-Born Christianity in New France

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

On 24 June 1610 a chief named Membertou was baptized at Port Royal in Acadia. About twenty of his family and people were baptized with him. A French priest named Fléché baptized them, about a year before the first Jesuits came. The first report in the Jesuit Relations collection is from that year.

Near Quebec, the Jesuits set up a settlement at Sillery in 1637 and 1638. One evening a man named Negabamat came to hold Father Le Jeune to his word. Had he not promised a house for him and another family? Two Algonquin families settled there, and within a year most of the settlers were Christians. Negabamat took the name Noël.

In 1640 the Christians of Sillery met at night, with no priest present. Negabamat told the others that the French taught that "we all are brothers". An old captain, Jean Baptiste Etinechkavat, said he had weighed the faith well before he took it. One man who was not baptized spoke against them. He would not give up the old right to take more than one wife.

The same Christians agreed among themselves that anyone who sinned should be jailed and made to fast. They told the Jesuits only the next morning. The priests said they were too severe, but the converts kept to their plan. Charles Meiachkawat, just baptized, told them to bind him if he ever lost courage.

Among the Hurons, Joseph Chihwatenhwa was for months the only Christian in his village, with his family. His people spoke hard words against him. The Jesuits called him "Our Christian" and wrote down his speeches. In the summer of 1640 he was killed while he worked in his field. The Jesuit report says two Iroquois killed him.

By 1650, war with the Iroquois had broken the Huron nation. The survivors gathered on the Island of Saint Joseph. There two old captains came in secret to the Jesuit superior. "More than ten thousand have been snatched away by death," they told him. They asked him to lead the rest to Quebec. "There, our faith will not die out," they said. About three hundred went down to Quebec with the Jesuits that year.

Every word of theirs comes to us through the Jesuits. The priests wrote each year for readers in France, to build them up in faith. What the converts meant, and what they doubted, we see only through those reports.
