Vigilius of Rome

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Byzantine Imperial Church (Justinianic).

Known for
Bishop of Rome from 537 to 555. Made pope in place of Silverius, he was held at Constantinople through the quarrel over the Three Chapters and the council of 553, condemning them in 548, defending them in his Constitutum of May 553, and condemning them again that December.
Also called
Pope Vigilius, Vigilius papa
Dates
Made pope in 537, when Belisarius removed Silverius (the continuation of the chronicle of Marcellinus Comes; Louis Duchesne, as Loomis reports, gives 20 March 537; Procopius of Caesarea gives no year). Taken from Rome in 544 or 545, by Louise Loomis's date. Died in Sicily on his way home. A record of the time that Loomis reports gives 7 June in the third indiction, 555 by Louis Duchesne's reckoning. Henry Percival gives the end of 554 or the start of 555
Place
Rome; Constantinople, where he was held after being taken from Rome; Sicily, where he died on his way home
Role
Bishop of Rome; before that a Roman deacon and the pope's envoy at Constantinople

About This Person

On Saint Cecilia's feast, 22 November, an officer sent by the empress Theodora found Pope Vigilius in Cecilia's church in Rome. The officer took him to the Tiber and put him on a ship. As it moved off, the crowd threw stones and sticks and cried, "Your hunger go with you!"

The scene comes from the Book of the Popes, a record of the popes' lives. Its life of Vigilius tells of Romans who called him a murderer. It also shows him stand up to the empress. Louise Loomis, its translator, finds the life full of mistakes and the scene told nowhere else.

Vigilius, a Roman deacon, had been the pope's envoy at Constantinople. In 537 the general Belisarius sent Pope Silverius away, fearing he was plotting with the Goths. The historian Procopius of Caesarea says Belisarius "appointed another man, Vigilius by name, to the office of chief priest."

How he won the post is disputed. Liberatus of Carthage says Theodora had him promise in secret to set aside the Council of Chalcedon, and that she offered him gold. Liberatus and Victor of Tunnuna both copy a letter from Vigilius to three bishops Rome had condemned. In it he says he shares their faith. Theodor Mommsen, who edited Victor, says both men hated Vigilius, and with reason. Loomis, citing the French scholar Louis Duchesne, thinks the letter most likely forged; Mommsen leaves it open.

At Constantinople the emperor Justinian wanted the Three Chapters condemned. These were writings of Theodore of Mopsuestia and Theodoret of Cyrrhus, and a letter of Ibas of Edessa. Loomis says Vigilius condemned them in 548, in a ruling called the Judicatum, but held to Chalcedon.

A council met in the city in May 553 without his consent, Bishop Hefele records. On 14 May Vigilius issued his Constitutum. He did not dare condemn Theodore, he wrote, nor would he let anyone else condemn him. The council condemned them anyway.

That December, writing to Eutychius, bishop of Constantinople, Vigilius gave way and condemned the Three Chapters. Henry Percival, who prints it in English, says some have doubted it is real, but most scholars accept it.

Vigilius died of the stone in Sicily on his way home, the Book of the Popes says.

In that letter to Eutychius, Vigilius pointed to Augustine of Hippo, who had corrected his own writings. When further study shows the truth, he wrote, "there ought to be no shame."

Key Works

  • Constitutum on the Three Chapters: dated at Constantinople on 14 May 553, by Otto Guenther's reading, while the council sat without him. By the account Percival prints from Bossuet, it condemns certain propositions of Theodore of Mopsuestia but spares his person, does the same for Theodoret of Cyrrhus, holds that Chalcedon found the letter of Ibas of Edessa orthodox, and forbids any further dispute over the Three Chapters
  • Letter to Eutychius of Constantinople (the Decretal Letter): dated 8 December, which Henry Percival reckons as 553; it condemns the Three Chapters and cancels whatever Vigilius or others had said in their defense, citing Augustine of Hippo, who corrected his own writings
  • Judicatum: issued in 548, by Loomis's account, condemning the Three Chapters while holding to the Council of Chalcedon, and later withdrawn; the text itself is not in the sources used here

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The council of 553 condemned the Three Chapters in spite of his Constitutum, and he then accepted its ruling. Percival says the council was opposed across the West, with Milan in schism until 571 and a schism in Istria that lasted until 700.
  • Loomis says the quarrel over the Three Chapters brought a new schism between East and West, "which lasted seventy years".

How they're remembered

  • The Book of the Popes gives a mixed life of him. Romans call him a murderer and the crowd throws stones after his ship, but he refuses the empress and, at Constantinople, is dragged from an altar rather than yield. Loomis judges that last account partly legendary.
  • Liberatus of Carthage and Victor of Tunnuna, stern defenders of Chalcedon by Mommsen's account, tell how he won the see through the empress Theodora. Theodor Mommsen says both were embittered against him "iusto odio", with just hatred, and calls him perhaps the worst of the bad popes.

At Major Councils

  • Second Council of Constantinople, 553: In the city but refused to attend; issued the Constitutum on 14 May; gave way in a later letter

What Is Uncertain

  • The scene of his leaving Rome comes from the Book of the Popes, which Loomis finds full of mistakes and calls often partisan in this part; she says the scene is told nowhere else. Louis Duchesne, whom Loomis follows, argues its author wrote no earlier than the time of Pope Pelagius the Second, who began in 579.
  • How he became pope. Procopius of Caesarea says only that Belisarius appointed him after sending Silverius away. Liberatus says the empress Theodora had him promise in secret to set aside Chalcedon and that she offered him money; Victor of Tunnuna says she drew a secret written pledge from him. Liberatus and Victor are both hostile to him, by Mommsen's judgment.
  • The letter in which he tells Theodosius of Alexandria, Anthimus of Constantinople and Severus of Antioch that he shares their faith. Liberatus and Victor copy it. Loomis, citing Duchesne, holds it must be a forgery unless Vigilius was a shameless double-dealer; Mommsen leaves open whether it is his or forged, and says it was taken as genuine at the time.
  • The death of Silverius. Liberatus, as Loomis gives him, says Silverius was handed to Vigilius's guards and starved on the island of Palmaria. The Book of the Popes says Vigilius took him into his own charge, sent him into exile and fed him 'the bread of tribulation', and that he died a confessor. Loomis judges the guilt to rest partly on Antonina, the wife of Belisarius, and partly on Vigilius.
  • Whether the letter to Eutychius is genuine. Percival reports that Harduin held the Jesuit Garnerius had proved it a forgery, and says the learned have generally accepted it as genuine.
  • The year of his death. A record of the time that Loomis reports gives 7 June in the third indiction, 555 by Duchesne's reckoning. A chronicle note printed with Marcellinus Comes gives the second indiction, the thirteenth year after Basil's consulship. Percival gives the end of 554 or the start of 555.
