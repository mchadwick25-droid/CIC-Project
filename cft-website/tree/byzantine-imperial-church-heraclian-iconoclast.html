The Byzantine Imperial Church (Heraclian & Iconoclast Eras)

Constantinople and the Greek East, 622 to 843 — an empire losing half its territory while arguing, twice over, about Christ's will and about whether God can be painted.

About This Movement

This is the imperial church of the empire's worst century and a half. Heraclius recovered the True Cross from Persia in 630 and within a decade had lost Syria, Palestine and Egypt to the Arab armies. His government's attempt to hold the remaining eastern Christians together with a compromise formula — that Christ had two natures but only one will — was pushed as state policy and resisted by a monk, Maximus, who was tried, had his tongue and right hand cut off, and died in exile. In 680 a council at Constantinople condemned the formula and taught that Christ has two wills, though it did not name him. Then came iconoclasm: emperors from Leo III onward removed and destroyed religious images, a council of 338 bishops endorsed the policy in 754, the Council of Nicaea reversed it in 787, a second imperial iconoclasm followed, and only in 843 was the matter closed for good. Through all of it the same institution — palace, patriarchate, councils — kept running, which is why this era looks so different depending on whether you stand with the court or with the monasteries.

Documented Stories

‘No Teaching of My Own’

Maximus was about seventy-five and had spent twenty years arguing, from Africa and Rome, that Christ had two wills as well as two natures, against the imperial doctrine of one will that Heraclius and his grandson Constans II had imposed to reconcile the Miaphysites. Theophanes says the emperor Constans had both him and Pope Martin brought to Constantinople. A record of his first hearing, kept by his own side, survives. The charges were political: that he had handed Egypt, Alexandria and Africa to the Saracens, that he had said the emperor was not a priest, that he had dreamed of a Roman victory over the empire. That evening two officials came to him. He told them he had no teaching of his own, only the common teaching of the catholic church. Would he take communion with the throne of Constantinople, they asked? He would not, he said, for by its decrees it had cast out the four councils. He was told that envoys from Rome had come the day before and would take communion with the patriarch on Sunday. Would he alone be saved, they asked, and all the rest be lost? He answered that the three young men condemned no one when they would not worship the statue that all the rest worshipped. They only took care not to fall from true piety themselves. God forbid, he said, that he should condemn anyone, or claim that he alone would be saved. He was exiled to Bizya in Thrace and tried again. A synod sentenced him to be flogged and to lose his tongue and right hand, and sent him into exile among the Lazi. He died there in 662. The council of 680 taught the two wills he had defended, but it did not name him.

Pope Martin Writes from the Crimea

Martin I was elected pope in 649 without the emperor’s confirmation, and that year he held a synod at the Lateran which condemned the patriarchs who had taught one will in Christ. In June 653 the exarch Theodore Calliopas came to Rome with troops. Martin had been ill since the October before, and in his own letter he says he lay on his bed before the altar of the Lateran church. The soldiers came in with lances, swords and bows, and the church’s candles fell under their weapons like leaves in a winter gale. Rather than see anyone’s blood spilled, he gave himself up. The voyage east took more than a year, a year of it on the island of Naxos. He reached Constantinople on 17 September, spent ninety-three days in prison, and was then carried to his trial in a chair, too sick to stand. The charge was treason: that he had sided with Olympius, a rebel governor of Italy. When he began to speak of the emperor’s decree on the faith, the prefect Troilus cut him off; the faith was not on trial here, he said, but rebellion. One of Martin’s clergy told the rest in a letter sent west. Martin was set on a terrace before the senate, with a crowd packed as far as the hippodrome. Executioners pulled off his priest’s cloak, tore his tunic from top to bottom and put iron chains on his neck, then led him through the city to the prison with a sword carried before him. In the spring of 655 he was shipped to Cherson in the Crimea, and from there he wrote the last letters. Bread, he says, is named there but never seen; grain came only on the rare ships, and he could buy three or four measures for a coin. He wonders that his friends and kin have so forgotten him that they do not care whether he is alive, and he wonders still more at the church of Saint Peter. Then, in the same letter, he asks God to keep that church firm in the true faith, and above all the pastor now set over it. He died in exile the following September.

Connections

In Its Own Record

The church of the crisis centuries. Heraclius fought his wars and sought a compromise on the will of Christ. Maximus the Confessor stood against it, and it later collapsed. Then the iconoclast emperors turned on holy images. Councils backed them, such as Hieria in 754, where 338 bishops met. The monks who defended the icons were the ones they persecuted. It ends with the Triumph of Orthodoxy in 843.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Heraclius recovered the True Cross from Persia in 630 and within a decade had lost Syria, Palestine and Egypt to the Arab armies, and his government's attempt to hold the remaining East together with a compromise on Christ's will was resisted by the monk Maximus the Confessor, who had his tongue and right hand cut off for it and was vindicated by a council only after his death. A century later the same imperial church tried twice to purge religious images from worship, a struggle that ran from Leo III's first iconoclasm through the Council of Nicaea's restoration of icons in 787 to a second round of destruction, closed for good only in 843.

Legacy

The same institution continued as the Byzantine church of the Macedonian era and through that into the Orthodox churches of today. Its decisions are still binding for most of Christianity. The ruling of the Third Council of Constantinople that Christ has a human will as well as a divine one, and the Second Council of Nicaea's defense of religious images, are both counted among the seven ecumenical councils by Orthodox and Catholics alike, and the canons issued at Constantinople in 692 still shape Orthodox church law. The argument is also still visible in the buildings. In Hagia Eirene in Istanbul the apse carries the plain cross that iconoclast-era decorators set where a figure would otherwise have gone.

Experience It Today

  • Hagia Eirene, Istanbul — in the first courtyard of Topkapı Palace, its apse still showing the bare mosaic cross of the iconoclast period
