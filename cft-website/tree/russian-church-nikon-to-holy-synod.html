The Enlightenment & Awakening Era · 1652-1815 · Russia

The Russian Church — Nikon to the Holy Synod

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Patriarch Nikon, elected in 1652, set out to correct Russian service books against Greek models and to assert the priesthood's authority over the tsar's. And lost both fights: the corrections split the church permanently, and Nikon himself was deposed by the council of 1666-67 that condemned his opponents. Half a century later Peter the Great settled the second argument the other way. When the patriarch died in 1700 Peter simply left the office empty, and in 1721 he abolished it, replacing it with a Holy Synod of clergy, watched over by a lay official the tsar appointed. The church became, in effect, a department of the imperial government and stayed one for two hundred years. Catherine II completed the process in 1764 by confiscating the monastic estates. Yet the same church produced Tikhon of Zadonsk's gentle devotional writing, a serious seminary system, and in 1794 sent monks from Valaam across the Pacific to Alaska.

In Its Own Record

Gave Rise To

  • The Alaskan Mission — Herman’s Decades & Veniaminov Dominant Modern Reconstruction Major — By 1842 the Russian church had a diocese of the Aleutian Islands, Kamchatka and Russian America. An English book of that year lists it among the dioceses set up since 1830. That shows the Alaskan church as part of the Russian church. The list says nothing of the first mission of 1794, or of Herman. Modern histories by Michael Oleksa and Lydia Black trace that mission to ten monks of Valaam, in Russia, who reached Kodiak in 1794.
  • The Optina Elders & Russian Ascetic Renewal Documented Major — Leonid, the first Optina elder, joined the Optina monastery in the diocese of Kaluga in 1797. In 1804 the bishop of Orel put him in charge of the Beloberezh hermitage. In 1829 he moved to the new skete at Optina, a small hermitage next to the monastery. There the abbot gave him the care of the brothers' souls.

Continues

  • The Russian Church, Stoglav to the Eve of Nikon Documented Major — Paul of Aleppo went to Moscow with the patriarch of Antioch. He writes that when Patriarch Joseph died, the clergy all voted to make Nikon patriarch. Paisius Ligarides, who later led the case against Nikon, also says Nikon was chosen to follow Joseph.

Continued As

  • The Russian Church — the Synodal Century Documented Major — The Holy Synod that Peter the Great set up in 1721 still ran the church a century later. An account of the church in 1830 says the Synod still worked under Peter's law of 1721, the Spiritual Regulation. Andrei Mouravieff, who wrote his history in 1838, was an official of the same Synod.

Influenced By

  • The Kyiv-Mohyla Orthodox Revival Widely Accepted — Thomas Consett, an English chaplain in Russia, wrote in 1729 that the Russians still used Mohyla's Slavonic service book. A Russian church history says the tsar asked the head of the church in Kyiv for monks from his school. Stephen Yavorsky, sent to Moscow from Kyiv, later led the Russian church under Peter the Great.
  • Orthodoxy under the Ottomans — the Second Act Documented — Nikon's council in Moscow says it wrote to the Eastern patriarchs and to Mount Athos about the service books. It had their answers in writing. It then corrected the book of sacraments from old Greek copies. A history of 1838 says Patriarch Paisius of Constantinople urged Nikon to keep to the rule of the great Church of Constantinople.

Argued Against

  • The Old Believers Documented Major — The council's record says Avvakum and the priest Lazar would not repent and called the council false to the faith. So the council, with the patriarchs of Alexandria, Antioch and Moscow, laid them under anathema once more. A history of 1838 by an official of the Synod says the council threw out their case on the cross and the Creed.Lasting result: A Moscow church council, with two Eastern patriarchs on it, laid a solemn curse on Avvakum and the priest Lazar.
  • The Ruthenian Uniate Church — Zamość to the Partitions Dominant Modern Reconstruction Major — In 1794 Victor Sadkowski, the Orthodox archbishop of Minsk, sent out a letter. It urged the Uniates to come back to the Orthodox church. Priests he sent took over parish churches where even a few people agreed, with Russian officials and soldiers at their side. Two Greek Catholic historians tell this from the Uniate side, in 1862 and 1880.Lasting result: In 1795 Catherine II ruled that all those whose families had joined the union after 1595 must go back to the Russian church.

Opposed By

  • The Old Believers Documented — In prison at Pustozersk, Avvakum and the men held with him wrote out a statement for an officer. It named the visiting Patriarch Paisius and his fellows as cursed, a gathering of heretics. It called Nikon the maker of the heresy. Avvakum also wrote that the ban on him came from heretics.

Broke With

  • The Old Believers Documented Major — Both sides recorded the break. The council's record says Avvakum was stripped of his priesthood and laid under anathema in the cathedral on 13 May. Avvakum wrote that the bishops cursed him and the deacon Fyodor, and that he cursed them back.

In Contact With

  • The Old Believers Inferential-Thin Major — In 1799 Old Believers in Moscow asked the official church for priests and holy oil. Metropolitan Plato looked over their sixteen terms, and in 1800 Tsar Paul I approved them. The terms let them keep the old books under priests of the church. The arrangement came to be called Edinoverie. This rests on one older account, by F. C. Conybeare in 1921. No modern study of it has been consulted.Lasting result: In 1800 Tsar Paul I approved terms that let Old Believers who joined the official church keep using the old books.
  • The Kyiv-Mohyla Orthodox Revival Inferential-Thin Major — In the 1680s the church of Kyiv passed under the patriarch of Moscow. Patriarch Joachim installed Gideon as its metropolitan, or senior bishop. Two Eastern patriarchs then confirmed the change by letter. A Greek Catholic history of 1880 says that after the peace of 1686, Kyiv became a plain diocese of Moscow. This rests on that history and a Russian one of 1838. No modern study of it has been consulted.Lasting result: Two Eastern patriarchs confirmed by letter that the church of Kyiv now came under the patriarch of Moscow.
