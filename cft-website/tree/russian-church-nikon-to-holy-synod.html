The Russian Church — Nikon to the Holy Synod

Russia, 1652 to 1815 — a church that lost its patriarch to a tsar's pen, was run for two centuries as a government department, and still sent monks to Alaska.

About This Movement

Read along

Patriarch Nikon, elected in 1652, set out to correct Russian service books against Greek models and to assert the priesthood's authority over the tsar's. And lost both fights: the corrections split the church permanently, and Nikon himself was deposed by the council of 1666-67 that condemned his opponents. Half a century later Peter the Great settled the second argument the other way. When the patriarch died in 1700 Peter simply left the office empty, and in 1721 he abolished it, replacing it with a Holy Synod of clergy, watched over by a lay official the tsar appointed. The church became, in effect, a department of the imperial government and stayed one for two hundred years. Catherine II completed the process in 1764 by confiscating the monastic estates. Yet the same church produced Tikhon of Zadonsk's gentle devotional writing, a serious seminary system, and in 1794 sent monks from Valaam across the Pacific to Alaska.

The church followed the Russian frontier east. The council of 1666 and 1667 raised Tobolsk, in Siberia, to a metropolitan see. It also proposed new bishoprics at Tomsk and on the Lena. They lay far from Tobolsk. In Peter the Great's reign, Mouravieff says, Theodore of Tobolsk converted 10,000 Ostiaks. The numbers are Mouravieff's, in an English version of 1842.

The church also reached Peking, by way of a lost fort. In 1685 Chinese troops took the Russian stockade of Albazin, on the Amur. The Cossacks taken there were invited to Peking to serve the emperor. Adoratskii says the captives took their priest, Maksim Leontiev, along by force. Father Maksim turned a Buddhist temple the emperor gave them into a chapel of St Nicholas.

In 1695, Adoratskii says, the metropolitan of Tobolsk wrote to him. In English, the letter says: "though you are in captivity, yet you yourself, with God's help, take captive unknowing men into the knowledge of the gospel's truth."

The first permanent Russian mission set out with the emperor's leave in 1715. It reached Peking at the end of that year or early in 1716. Peter the Great had told the metropolitan of Tobolsk to choose its members. A treaty of 1727 named one priest in the Russian house and three more to come. It also said Russians would not be forbidden to pray and to honor their God in their own faith. In 1732 the Albazinians' church was consecrated as the Dormition church, though it was still called St Nicholas's.

Adoratskii says that from 1745 to 1808 the mission was neglected and in a sorry state. He gives three causes: hard dealings with China, the weakness of some members, and too little money. Yet even then, he adds, it held to the Orthodox faith and to its tasks.

The details are his, from the Synod's archives, in a Russian history of 1887. He was an Orthodox churchman.

Documented Stories

Nikon Takes Off His Vestments

Nikon had been patriarch since 1652 and for six years the most powerful man in Russia after, and sometimes beside, Tsar Alexei, who called him Great Sovereign, left him to govern during the Polish war, and let him impose the Greek-corrected books on a resisting church. On 10 July 1658 after a public insult to one of his officers had gone unanswered, Nikon celebrated the liturgy in the Dormition Cathedral, then addressed the people, saying that he was leaving the patriarchal throne because of the tsar's anger, took off the sakkos and mitre in the sanctuary, put on a plain black mantle, and, when the tsar's messengers begged him to stay, walked out and rode to the New Jerusalem monastery he was building at Istra. He left the office vacant without resigning it, and for eight years the Russian church had a patriarch who would not serve and would not release his see, while the tsar sought a way to be rid of him that the canons would allow. In 1666 the patriarchs of Alexandria and Antioch, brought to Moscow at great expense, presided over a council that tried Nikon in his absence and presence, and on 12 December 1666 deposed him for abandoning his flock and for claiming that the priesthood was above the tsardom, and sent him as a simple monk to the Ferapontov monastery in the north. The same council, sitting on into 1667, confirmed his books and anathematized everyone who kept the old ones.

The Seal of Confession, by Imperial Decree

The Spiritual Regulation, drafted by Feofan Prokopovich, archbishop of Pskov, and signed by Peter in January 1721, abolished the patriarchate that had been vacant since 1700 and set in its place a Most Holy Governing Synod of bishops and archimandrites appointed by the tsar and sworn to him, with a lay chief procurator to watch it. The Regulation explained at length why a single head of the church was dangerous to a state, ordered schools for the clergy, forbade the invention of relics and miracles, and regulated preaching. In May 1722 a Supplement was issued on the duties of parish priests. If a penitent confessed an intention to commit treason or rebellion against the sovereign, or a design to spread a false miracle, and would not be dissuaded, the priest was to report him to the authorities, since such a confession was not repentance and was not covered by the seal; a priest who failed to do so was to be deprived and punished as an accomplice. Priests were also required to keep registers of who came to confession and communion each year and to report absentees, a list the police found useful for finding Old Believers. The Synodal church that resulted kept the liturgy, the fasts and the monasteries, produced saints and theologians, sent missionaries across Siberia and to Alaska, and could not, for two centuries, say publicly that any of this was wrong. The Regulation's own text called the church a college of the state.

The Bishop Who Asked Forgiveness for Being Struck

Tikhon of Zadonsk had been bishop of Voronezh for four years when in 1767, at forty-three, exhausted and ill, he resigned the see and withdrew to the monastery of Zadonsk on the Don, where he lived the remaining sixteen years of his life on a pension, writing, receiving the peasants and merchants who came to him, and giving away almost everything he had. His books, On True Christianity and the Spiritual Treasury Gathered from the World, were written there and became the most widely read devotional writing of the century in Russia. His cell-attendants, Vasilii Chebotarev and Ivan Efimov, wrote down after his death what they remembered of him, and one of their stories was retold for a century. A landowner of the district, a man of the new enlightened kind who liked to argue against religion, was visiting the monastery, and Tikhon, who could be sharp, argued back; the landowner, losing his temper, struck him across the face. Tikhon fell at his feet and begged his forgiveness for having provoked him to it. The man, the story says, was so shaken that he became a devout Christian and, in some tellings, freed his serfs. The attendants also recorded Tikhon's rages and his depressions, his fear of death and his habit of lying on the floor of his cell for hours; they were not writing a hagiography and one was made from them. Dostoevsky read the attendants' accounts and put something of Tikhon into Zosima.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Patriarch Nikon's reforms of 1652 and the council of 1666-67 are the establishment side of the story whose other side is the Old Believers, a related but separate tradition born of the same split rather than a successor to this one. Peter the Great left the patriarchal office empty from 1700 and replaced it with the Holy Synod in 1721. The state secularized monastic lands in 1764, and in 1686 the Kyiv metropolia was transferred into this church's jurisdiction. In 1794 it sent the Valaam mission to Alaska, including Herman of Kodiak, and in 1800 it established Edinoverie, a formal arrangement on the shared boundary with the Old Believers.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Patriarch Nikon tried to correct Russia's service books and assert the church's authority over the tsar in the 1650s, and lost both fights — deposed by the same council that condemned the split his reforms caused. Peter the Great settled the larger argument by leaving the patriarchal office empty after 1700 and abolishing it in 1721, replacing it with a Holy Synod watched over by a state official, yet the same church still produced Tikhon of Zadonsk's widely read devotional writing and sent monks from Valaam across the Pacific to Alaska in 1794.

Legacy

The synodal system lasted until 1917, and the church brought back the patriarch almost as soon as the empire fell. The bond between church and state in modern Russia took its shape in this period, and people still argue about it. Its mission to Alaska planted an Orthodox faith that lives on among Native communities there today. It was the only Orthodox church founded in North America before the nineteenth century. The church also lived with a lasting tension. It was an official church tied to the state, with an intense spiritual life running beside and beneath it. The first elders of Optina, in the next century, were trained in this church's own monasteries.
