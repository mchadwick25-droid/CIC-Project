Caesarius of Arles

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Merovingian Gallic Christianity.

Known for
Bishop of Arles in Gaul who gave the church's silver to free captives, signed first at the council of Orange on grace and free will, and wrote rules for monks and for nuns.
Also called
Caesarius Arelatensis
Dates
Died on 27 August, the sixth day before the Kalends of September, as his Life gives it. Bruno Krusch, who edited the Life, gives the year 542, his birth about 469 or 470, and his election between 501 and 503. Friedrich Maassen dates the council of Orange to 529
Place
Born near Chalon, by his Life's account; the island monastery of Lérins; Arles; exile at Bordeaux; Ravenna
Role
Monk of Lérins, then bishop of Arles

About This Person

After a siege of Arles in Gaul, the Goths came back into the city with a great crowd of captives. Caesarius, bishop of Arles, gave the church's silver to buy them free. His disciples report what he often said of clergy who would not do the same. In modern words, he said God would not mind the vessels of his service buying people back, since Christ gave himself to redeem us.

His Life was written by the bishops Cyprian of Toulon, Firminus and Viventius, with the priest Messianus and the deacon Stephen. They wrote for Caesaria, the abbess who then led the convent he founded. It is a saint's Life by men who called him father.

He came from near Chalon, they say. At about eighteen he asked its bishop, Silvester, to take him into the church's service, without his family knowing. Later he joined the monks on the island of Lérins. Fasting wore down his body, and his abbot sent him to Arles to recover. Eonius, bishop of Arles, ordained him and chose him as his successor. The Life says Caesarius hid among the tombs to escape it.

Arles was then under Alaric, the Arian king of the Visigoths. One of Caesarius's own notaries, the Life says, accused him of plotting to give the city to the Burgundians, and he was exiled to Bordeaux. After Arles passed to the Ostrogoths, he was taken under guard to their king, Theodoric, at Ravenna. The king gave him a silver dish, and he sold it to free more captives.

His teaching on grace had rivals in Gaul, the Life says. At a meeting of bishops in Valence, Cyprian of Toulon argued for him. No one can make any progress toward God by himself, Cyprian said, unless God's grace first calls him. Boniface, bishop of Rome, upheld Caesarius. Caesarius signed first at a council in Orange, whose chapters on grace and free will came from Rome. Friedrich Maassen, who edited its acts, dates Orange to 529 and leaves open which council came first.

He wrote rules for monks and for nuns. He sent sermons as far as Italy and Spain for priests to preach, and led the church of Arles for forty years.

His Rule for the nuns opens with a request. When they entered the kingdom with the wise virgins, he asked, they should pray that he would not be left outside with the foolish.

Key Works

  • Rule for Nuns (Regula ad virgines): the rule for the convent he founded at Arles, addressed to its sisters; those who enter are not to leave the convent until death
  • Rule for Monks (Regula ad monachos): his rule for men's communities, printed with the Rule for Nuns in Migne's Latin edition
  • Sermons: preaching he sent out for priests to use, by his Life's account as far as Italy and Spain; many came down under Augustine's name and are given to Caesarius by Migne's editors on grounds of style and content

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The acts of the council of Orange, which he signed first, set out chapters on grace and free will sent from Rome. His own line in the acts says he issued the text and kept the original in the church's archive.
  • His Life says Boniface, bishop of Rome, upheld his teaching on grace against rivals in Gaul.
  • Migne's editors say Radegund brought his Rule for nuns into her convent at Poitiers.

How they're remembered

  • His disciples, in the Life: through the forty years he led the church, whenever he went out to bless the font he seemed new to all who saw him, "semper in oculis omnium novus apparebat".
  • Friedrich Maassen, editor of the Merovingian councils, calls him the bishop "qui concilio Arausicano praesedit", who presided at Orange.
  • Venantius Fortunatus, in verses Bruno Krusch quotes: he "mansit monachus pontificale decus", he stayed a monk and was the glory of bishops.

What Is Uncertain

  • His life rests on the Life his disciples wrote, a saint's Life by men who called him father. The bishops Cyprian of Toulon, Firminus and Viventius wrote book one, and the priest Messianus and the deacon Stephen book two, by Bruno Krusch's reading and a manuscript's closing note. Krusch thinks chapter 60 of book one, which praises Cyprian, is by Firminus or Viventius.
  • The years are editors'. The Life gives the day of his death and says he led the church of Arles for forty years. Krusch gives 542, his birth about 469 or 470 and his election between 501 and 503.
  • The Life tells of a meeting of bishops at Valence on grace and does not name Orange. Maassen leaves open whether Valence came before or after Orange, and Krusch says the acts of Valence are lost.
  • The charge before Alaric, that he meant to hand Arles to the Burgundians, is told only by the Life, which calls it false and the work of the devil.
  • Many of his sermons came down under Augustine's name. Migne's editors give them to him on grounds of style and content.
  • The Life calls the Visigothic king Alaric a barbarian of Arian perversity. That is the Life's polemic.
  • A Testament in his name survives. Krusch holds it forged by a cleric of the cathedral of Arles, to secure the cathedral's freedom from taxes and to place the convent under its bishop.
