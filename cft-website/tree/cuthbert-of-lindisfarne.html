Cuthbert of Lindisfarne

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Anglo-Saxon Christianity.

Also called Cuthbertus

A Northumbrian monk of Melrose and Lindisfarne who lived as a hermit on the island of Farne and was made bishop of Lindisfarne. Bede wrote his Life in verse and in prose, and a monk of Lindisfarne wrote an earlier one. Eleven years after his burial his body was found whole, Bede says.

About This Person

One night a young shepherd kept watch over his flock in the hills. As he prayed, he saw light pour down from the sky, and choirs of angels carry a soul up to heaven. In the morning he learned that Aidan, bishop of Lindisfarne, had died at that hour. He gave the sheep back to their owners and set out for a monastery.

The young man was Cuthbert of Lindisfarne, a monk and bishop in Northumbria, in northern England, in the seventh century. Bede of Jarrow tells this in his Latin prose Life of him. A monk of Lindisfarne wrote an earlier Life, in which the news came some days later. Both are saints' Lives, full of miracles.

He rode up to Melrose, on the river Tweed, with a spear in his hand. The prior Boisil, at the door, said only, in Bede's Latin, "Ecce servus Dei": here is a servant of God.

After the meeting at Whitby in 664, the Scots, as Bede calls the Irish, left Lindisfarne. Eata, abbot of Melrose, became its abbot too, and later moved Cuthbert there to teach the monks' rule. Then Cuthbert went to live alone on the island of Farne. The bank around his cell was so high, Bede says, that he could see nothing but heaven.

A synod at Twyford, under Archbishop Theodore of Tarsus, chose him bishop. When letters failed, King Egfrid of Northumbria crossed to Farne with Bishop Trumwine and the brothers. They knelt and wept until they drew him, weeping too, from his cell.

After two years as bishop he went back to Farne to die. Herefrid, abbot of Lindisfarne, told Bede his last words. Keep peace among yourselves, he said, and have no fellowship with those who keep Easter at the wrong time. He would rather they dug up his bones and left than bowed to schismatics.

Eleven years after his burial, Bede says, the brothers opened his grave. They found his body whole, "more like one asleep than a dead person".

Legacy and Today

What traces to them

  • Bede wrote his Life first in verse and then in prose. He says the prose Life was read for two days before the elders of Lindisfarne, who found no word to change.
  • Bede says Eata, abbot of Melrose, moved him to Lindisfarne to teach the brothers the monks' rule. Some kept to their old custom, and Cuthbert would leave a stormy meeting calmly and give the same counsel next day.
  • Bede says Ethelwald, who became a priest at Ripon, followed him as the hermit of Farne.
  • James Raine says the Life of Wilfrid begins by borrowing the prologue of the Lindisfarne monk's Life of Cuthbert.

How they're remembered

  • Bede says miracles were done at his tomb on Lindisfarne, and tells several.
  • Alcuin of York wrote to Lindisfarne after the pagans sacked it. He asks what trust the churches of Britain can have, if Saint Cuthbert and so many saints did not defend their own.
  • The Roman Breviary, in the Marquess of Bute's English, keeps his feast on 20 March. Its Martyrology for 31 August tells that he saw the soul of Aidan carried into heaven.
  • The York Prymer, which Canon Wordsworth edited, keeps his feast on 20 March and calls on him in its litany.

Open Questions

  • His Lives are saints' Lives, full of miracles. The earliest is by a monk of Lindisfarne, which the Bollandists date about 700 and James Raine between 698 and 708. Bede's prose Life is later.
  • The order and date of Bede's Lives. Bede says verse first, then prose. Alcuin's poem on York says prose first, and Raine's note says the verse is now believed earlier. Charles Plummer dates the verse before 705 and the prose about 720.
  • Charles Plummer judges that Bede did not better the Lindisfarne monk's Life. He says Bede exaggerates the ascetic and miraculous, but that his account of the death, from an eye-witness, has real value.
  • Aidan's death. Bede's prose Life says Cuthbert learned of it the next morning. The Lindisfarne monk says the news came some days later.
  • The consecration. Bede's History says it was at York, with Archbishop Theodore of Tarsus as primate among seven bishops. Bede's prose Life names no city or archbishop.
  • Whitby. No source used here places Cuthbert at the meeting of 664. Bede says only that Eata became abbot of Lindisfarne when the Scots left.
  • Ripon. Bede's prose Life says Eata took Cuthbert to found a monastery at Ripon, and that both were later driven home. Bede's History says the founders left rather than take the Roman Easter. The Bollandists join the two accounts.
