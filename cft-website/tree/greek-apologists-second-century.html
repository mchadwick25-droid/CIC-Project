The Second-Century Greek Apologists

Second-century Christians handing written defenses to emperors who had them killed anyway - and arguing, while they did it, that most Greek poets and philosophers had spoken of one God too.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 4:49

Read along

An Athenian Christian, Athenagoras, addressed his plea to the emperors Marcus Aurelius and his son Commodus, and hailed them, above all, as philosophers. Only Christians, he told them, were hunted for their name alone, and slaughtered on the word of false accusers.

In the second century, from Athens and Rome to Sardis and Antioch, Christians who wrote in Greek put written defenses of their faith into the hands of emperors, and of pagans who asked them about it. Accused of being godless, of eating human flesh and of incest, they answered that they worshipped the one God who made all things and prayed for the emperors, and they asked to be judged by how they lived. Such a defense was called an apology, the kind of speech made in court.

Long before, under Hadrian, certain wicked men were trying to trouble the Christians. So Quadratus addressed a defense to the emperor, arguing that Christ's works were real: some people Christ healed lived to Quadratus's day. Eusebius, a church historian, still had the book in the early 300s.

Under the next emperor, Antoninus Pius, Christians were called atheists, because they refused the gods of the cities and would not sacrifice to them. Justin, a Christian teacher of philosophy at Rome, wrote to the emperor. He granted that they were atheists of such gods, and said they worshipped the most true God. Everywhere, he wrote, they took care to pay their taxes, as Christ had taught them. They worshipped God alone, and gladly served the emperors in all else.

Under Marcus Aurelius, Justin was condemned and put to death for refusing to sacrifice to the gods and obey the emperor's command.

In the Roman province of Asia, in today's western Turkey, Christians were hounded by new decrees, new written orders, as Melito, bishop of Sardis, wrote to Marcus Aurelius. In the passages Eusebius copied, Melito wrote that informers, people who laid charges against others and took their goods, used the decrees to rob the innocent night and day. "If this is done at your command, well and good. For a just ruler will never take unjust measures, and we gladly accept the honor of such a death." But if the decree was not the emperor's, he begged not to be left to the plundering of the crowd.

If the charges were true, Athenagoras said, let the emperors destroy the Christians root and branch. If not, let them look into Christian lives, teaching and loyalty. Poets and philosophers, he said, wrote freely about God, and almost all spoke of one God. No law stood against them. Yet a law stood against Christians, who said the same. As for eating human flesh, he turned to the emperors' own games, the gladiator shows, fights to the death between armed men, or with wild beasts, staged for crowds. "But we hold that to watch a man being killed is close to killing him, so we have given up such shows. We do not even watch, so that we bring no guilt or stain on ourselves. How then could we commit murder?"

At Antioch, in Syria, a bishop, Theophilus, talked about God with a pagan friend, and they parted friends. But the friend believed the rumors, and thought Christian books were new and their teaching nonsense. Theophilus wrote to him, counting back the years to show that the books of Moses, the first in the Bible, were older than the Greeks' own, and so Christian teaching true.

At Carthage, Tertullian took up the same task in Latin. He also wrote against the followers of a teacher, Valentinus. They stacked heaven on heaven for their many gods, he scoffed, and he drew on earlier writers against them, Justin first.

One defense, a letter to a man named Diognetus, has no known writer or date. Of the Christians it says: "They live in their own homelands, but as resident foreigners. They share in everything as citizens, and endure everything as strangers. Every foreign land is a homeland to them, and every homeland is foreign."

Documented Stories

Justin Martyr’s Trial Before the Prefect Rusticus

The record of Justin’s trial is short, and its shape is that of a Roman court transcript. The urban prefect Rusticus — a Stoic, and one of Marcus Aurelius’ teachers — asked what doctrines Justin followed. He answered that he had tried them all and settled on ‘the true doctrines of the Christians’, and gave a brief creed. Asked where they assembled, Justin said that Christians met wherever each could, since the God of the Christians was not confined to a place; as for himself, he lodged ‘above the baths of one Martinus’, and anyone who wished came to him there to hear the word. Rusticus then questioned the others one by one: Chariton, Charito (a woman), Euelpistus — an imperial slave who said he had received the faith from his parents — Hierax, Paeon, Liberianus. Each answered ‘I am a Christian’. Asked whether he expected to go up to heaven if he were scourged and beheaded, Justin said that he hoped so, and that he knew it. The prefect ordered them scourged and beheaded ‘in accordance with the laws’. The document ends with unnamed believers taking away the bodies. It is one of the very few second-century texts in which ordinary Christians — a slave, a woman, men known by one name — speak in the first person, however briefly.

The Woman Who Left Her Husband, and What It Cost Ptolemaeus

Justin opens his Second Apology with a case from his own city that had just happened ‘under Urbicus’, the prefect. A woman who had shared her husband’s dissolute life became a Christian and tried to persuade him to change; when he would not, and grew worse, she decided she could no longer live with him and — against her friends’ advice to wait — sent him a formal notice of divorce. He denounced her as a Christian. She petitioned the emperor for time to settle her affairs and got it, so her husband turned on the man who had instructed her, Ptolemaeus. A centurion he knew arrested Ptolemaeus and asked him one question: was he a Christian? He said yes, was jailed for a long time, and was finally brought before Urbicus, who asked the same question and, on the same answer, ordered him executed. A man named Lucius, watching, asked the prefect aloud why someone convicted of no adultery, theft or crime was being punished for a name; Urbicus asked whether he too was a Christian, and Lucius said he was and was led off. A third man followed. Justin records the whole thing to argue that Christians were being killed for a label, but the episode also shows what conversion did inside a Roman household — and that the church’s teachers, not only its converts, paid for it.

Connections

In Its Own Record

It overlaps the household churches of the same years, in time and in people. Justin Martyr taught at Rome, Theophilus was bishop of Antioch and Melito bishop of Sardis. But it is a different thing. The household churches show the church's inner life. Here the church turns outward, to emperors and an educated public that despised it.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced

  • influenced Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition Documented — Clement of Alexandria praises Tatian's Address to the Greeks. He says it gives an accurate account of the age of Moses. Tatian was one of the Greek apologists of the second century.
  • influenced The Latin Apologists Documented — In Against the Valentinians, Tertullian names 'Justin, philosopher and martyr'. He puts him with the earlier writers who wrote against heresy. So one apologist who wrote in Latin knew the work of one who wrote in Greek.

Argued Against

  • argued against Marcion / Marcionism Documented — Justin Martyr names Marcion of Pontus while Marcion was still alive, and charges him with teaching a god greater than the Creator. In his Dialogue with Trypho he lists the Marcians among groups who wrongly call themselves Christians.
  • argued against Valentinian & other Gnostic Christianities Documented — In his Dialogue with Trypho, Justin Martyr names the Valentinians and the Basilidians. He puts them with the groups who call themselves Christians but are not. Later, Tertullian lists Justin with the writers who had already written against them.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In the second century, Christian writers handed Roman emperors formal defenses of their faith, answering charges of atheism and cannibalism by asking to be judged by how they lived. Justin Martyr ran a school at Rome and was executed under Marcus Aurelius for refusing to sacrifice to the gods and obey the emperor's command. Theophilus of Antioch, writing to a pagan friend, counted back the years to show that the books of Moses were older than the Greeks' own.

Legacy

At Alexandria, Clement praised Tatian, a hearer of Justin's, for his careful account of the age of Moses, the argument Theophilus also made. Theophilus of Antioch called the three days before the lights in the sky were made types of the Trinity, of God, his Word and his Wisdom. And the Epistle to Diognetus calls Christians resident aliens, at home everywhere and strangers everywhere.
