The Restoration and Georgian Church of England

England, Wales, Ireland and the colonies, 1660 to 1815 — the established church of the long eighteenth century, which created Dissent in a single day in 1662 and then grew Methodism inside itself.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:17

Read along

When the monarchy came back in 1660 so did the bishops and the Prayer Book, and the Act of Uniformity of 1662 required every minister to accept both. About two thousand refused and were ejected from their livings on a single day, creating English Dissent as a permanent fact. The church that remained was the established church of the long eighteenth century, and it has had a poor press — pluralism, absentee clergy, comfortable sermons. But it was also the church of the Prayer Book as ordinary people actually knew it, of parish registers and charity schools, and of the first Anglican missionary societies, the SPCK in 1698 and the SPG in 1701. It had its own principled casualties. The nonjurors, several hundred clergy including the Archbishop of Canterbury, who would not swear allegiance to William and Mary in 1689 while James II lived, and walked out rather than break an oath. And it was the church John and Charles Wesley were born in, ordained in, and never meant to leave.

Documented Stories

The Seven Bishops, Acquitted and Then Deprived

In April 1688 James II ordered that his Declaration of Indulgence, suspending the laws against Catholics and Dissenters by royal prerogative, be read in every church on two successive Sundays. Archbishop Sancroft and six bishops, petitioned the king on 18 May that they could not in conscience order it read, since Parliament had declared the suspending power illegal. On 8 June the seven were sent to the Tower, cheered along the river by crowds who knelt for their blessing; on 29 and 30 June they were tried at King's Bench for seditious libel, and the jury, out all night, acquitted them, to shouts that reached the king at Hounslow camp and bonfires in every street. When William of Orange landed in November and James fled, the same men had to decide whether the oath they had sworn to James could be transferred to William and Mary. Sancroft, Ken, Turner, Lake, White and Lloyd of Norwich, with about four hundred clergy, concluded that it could not while James lived, refused the new oath, and were suspended in August 1689 and deprived on 1 February 1690. Sancroft left Lambeth for his brother's farm in Suffolk and consecrated a line of nonjuring bishops before he died; Ken lived thirty years in a friend's house at Longleat, writing hymns. They had defied one king for the church's liberty and lost their sees rather than break faith with him.

SOCIETY, Branded on the Chest

Christopher Codrington, governor of the Leeward Islands and a Fellow of All Souls, died in 1710 and left his two plantations in Barbados, with the roughly three hundred enslaved people who worked them, to the Society for the Propagation of the Gospel in Foreign Parts, founded nine years earlier, on condition that it maintain a college there for the instruction of the enslaved and the training of missionaries and physicians. The Society accepted, and became a slaveholder. Its own records show what that meant. The plantations were run for profit by managers in Barbados under a committee of bishops and clergy in London; the people on them were, in the early decades, branded on the chest with the word SOCIETY, as other planters branded with their initials, to identify them if they ran; they died faster than they were born, and were replaced by purchase, more than four hundred bought between 1712 and 1761; the promised instruction was slight, since the managers thought it made them unruly, and the college that opened in 1745 taught the sons of the white colony. Bishops of London preached anniversary sermons in the Society's favor that defended slavery from Scripture and argued that baptism did not free. The Society freed no one until Parliament's Act of 1833 compelled it, when it received compensation for 411 people. In 2006 the Church of England's General Synod acknowledged the record and apologized, and the papers are open.

Parson Woodforde’s Christmas Dinner

James Woodforde was rector of Weston Longville in Norfolk from 1776 until his death in 1803, and kept a diary every day from his Oxford years in 1758 to a few months before he died. Under Christmas Day, year after year, it records the same things. He read prayers and administered the sacrament at Weston church in the morning, to a small congregation, noting who received. Then, as on every Christmas Day he was at home, the old men of the parish dined at the parsonage: usually six or seven, named in the entry, poor men in their seventies and eighties, who ate roast beef and plum pudding in his kitchen, drank strong beer, and were each given a shilling when they left. He records the same names until they die, and then their replacements. The diary records, alongside this, his tithe audit dinner for the farmers each December, the beggars and the traveling poor he gave sixpence to at the door, the children he catechized in Lent, the sick he visited and the dead he buried, the parish's smallpox inoculations, which he paid toward, his quarrels with the churchwardens, what he ate at every meal, and almost nothing about religion as an inward matter. It is the Prayer Book church as its clergy and people lived it, comfortable, unheroic, resident and kind within its limits, and it is what the awakenings of the same decades were reacting against.

Connections

In Its Own Record

The bishops came back in 1660. Then the Act of Uniformity of 1662 and the Great Ejection of roughly two thousand ministers defined church and dissent in a single stroke. In 1689 several hundred clergy, the nonjurors, refused the new oath to William and Mary and lost their posts. The church founded its first missionary societies, the SPCK in 1698 and the SPG in 1701. This is the church that Methodism grew up inside, and eventually out of.

Descended from

  • continues The English Reformation & Elizabethan Settlement Documented Major — The Act of Uniformity of 1662 begins by recalling the Prayer Book that Parliament made the law in Elizabeth's first year. It put that order back in place after what it called 'the late unhappy Troubles.'

Direct descendants

  • gave rise to The Methodist Revival Documented Major — Methodism began as societies of people inside the Church of England, gathered by John Wesley. In 1766 the preachers' conference said, 'we are not Dissenters from the Church now'. In 1785 Wesley recalled how these 'little companies' had spread through the three kingdoms.
  • gave rise to Early American Unitarians & Universalists Documented Major — King's Chapel in Boston was a Church of England congregation. In 1785, 'torn from that King and Church', it printed its own prayer book. It took most of the text from the Book of Common Prayer. But it left out the Gloria Patri, the old praise of Father, Son and Spirit, and it prayed to God the Father alone, through Christ.
  • continued as The Victorian Church of England Documented Major — The 1662 Prayer Book has a sermon or a homily follow the Creed at communion. In 1859 an Oxford editor said this rule stood 'nearly the same as we have it at the present day.' The same book and church carried on into the 1800s.
  • gave rise to English Unitarianism Documented Major — Theophilus Lindsey was a priest of the Church of England at Catterick. Priestley says he grew uneasy and thought of leaving. In 1774 Lindsey opened a chapel in London. He called it a separate church, apart from the national church.

Broke With

  • broke with The Methodist Revival Widely Accepted Major — In 1785 John Wesley wrote that he had varied from the Church of England's discipline, though not from its doctrines, and only 'by constraint'. He had preached in the fields because the churches were shut to him. If anyone called this separating from the Church, he wrote, 'he may'. In 1795 the Methodist Conference let a chapel hold the Lord's Supper if its trustees, its leaders and the Conference agreed. It was a long step toward a separate church.
  • broke with The Welsh Revival Tradition Documented Major — Many Welsh Calvinistic Methodists did not want their children baptized in the Church of England. So in 1811 the movement ordained its own preachers to give the sacraments. It ordained eight in North Wales in June and thirteen in the South in August.
  • broke with English Old Dissent — Presbyterians and Congregationalists Documented Major — The Act of Uniformity of 1662 set a deadline: St Bartholomew's Day. A minister who had not accepted the Prayer Book and a bishop's ordination by then would lose his post. Richard Baxter wrote that when the day came, about 1,800 or 2,000 ministers were cast out.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

When the monarchy returned in 1660, so did the bishops and the Prayer Book, and the Act of Uniformity of 1662 forced out about two thousand ministers who wouldn't accept both, creating English Dissent as a permanent fact in a single day. The same church founded the first Anglican missionary societies, the SPCK in 1698 and the SPG in 1701, watched several hundred clergy including its own Archbishop of Canterbury walk out rather than break an oath to the old king in 1689, and was the church John and Charles Wesley were born in, ordained in, and never meant to leave.

Legacy

It continues as the Church of England, and a great deal of what people recognize as Anglican comes from this stretch: the 1662 Prayer Book, still the church's legal standard. The parish interiors with their box pews and galleries; and the settled expectation that an established church will contain parties that disagree with each other. Its missionary societies planted congregations across the Atlantic world and became the institutional seed of the worldwide Anglican Communion. And the ejection it caused in 1662 gave England a permanent Nonconformist tradition that shaped its politics for two centuries.
