The Global Church Era · 1933-1945 · Germany

The Confessing Church

Germany, 1933 to 1945 — Lutheran and Reformed Christians who signed the Barmen Declaration against the catastrophe overtaking their church, leaving behind Finkenwalde's records and prison letters, and a record honest enough to name its own silences.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:10

Read along

When the Nazi state and the pro-Nazi 'German Christians' moved to reorganize the German Protestant churches in 1933, a minority refused. Pastors organized an emergency league; a synod at Barmen in May 1934 adopted a declaration, drafted by Karl Barth, that the church has one Word to hear and obey, Jesus Christ, and no other; and the Dahlem synod that October declared itself the lawful church. Dietrich Bonhoeffer ran an illegal seminary at Finkenwalde from 1935 until it was shut down in 1937, and wrote Discipleship and Life Together out of that community. In 1936 the movement sent a memorandum to Hitler. Its own record refuses to flatter it: the church struggle was largely about the church's own freedom, and the near-silence about what was being done to Jews is named by the movement's own historians as part of the story. In October 1945 the survivors issued the Stuttgart Declaration of Guilt.

Documented Stories

Stuttgart, October 1945

The Evangelical Church in Germany had been reconstituted in August 1945 with a council that included Confessing Church leaders, Niemöller among them, and bishops who had kept their sees under the regime; in October the council met at Stuttgart, and a delegation of the World Council of Churches, led by Visser 't Hooft, came to meet them. The visitors held that renewed fellowship required some acknowledgment from the German side, and did not say so; the Germans understood. On 18 October Niemöller, who had spent eight years in the camps, preached that the church had known what was being done and had not spoken; that night a declaration was drafted, and on 19 October the council adopted it and read it to the delegation. It said that infinite suffering had been brought on many peoples and lands, and then: ‘We accuse ourselves for not witnessing more courageously, for not praying more faithfully, for not believing more joyously, and for not loving more ardently.’ It did not mention the Jews, the war's victims by name, or the church's own share in the state's crimes beyond the failure of courage. Visser 't Hooft accepted it as more than he had hoped; much of the German church attacked it as a confession made to foreigners under duress, and Niemöller was shouted down for years for saying the churches were guilty. The declaration reopened the German churches' way into the ecumenical movement. Its silences were named by the church itself only decades later.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

When the Nazi state moved to take over the German Protestant churches in 1933, pastors organized in resistance, and a synod at Barmen in May 1934 declared that the church has one Word to obey, Jesus Christ, and no other. Dietrich Bonhoeffer ran an illegal seminary at Finkenwalde until it was shut down in 1937, writing Discipleship and Life Together out of that community before his own imprisonment. The movement's own historians are honest that its fight was mostly about the church's own freedom, and that its near-silence about what was happening to Jews is part of that story too.

Legacy

The Confessing Church ended with the war, and this map treats it as a closed story rather than a living one. What it left is out of all proportion to its twelve years. The Barmen Declaration was taken up well beyond Germany. The Book of Confessions of the Presbyterian Church (U.S.A.), for one, includes it. It is cited whenever a church has to say what it will not concede to a state. Bonhoeffer's Letters and Papers from Prison, edited by Bethge and published in 1951, came to be read far outside the church struggle it came from. And the Stuttgart Declaration of Guilt set a pattern that other churches used in the decades that followed. It showed a church saying plainly what it had failed to do.
