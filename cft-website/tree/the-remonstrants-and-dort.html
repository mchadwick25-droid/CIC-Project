The Reformation Era · 1610-1650 · Netherlands

The Remonstrants and Dort

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

The Dutch Reformed minister Jacobus Arminius had doubts about how strictly predestination should be taught, and after his death in 1609 his supporters set them out in five articles — the Remonstrance of 1610. Asking the government to allow their view. The quarrel that followed swallowed the young Dutch Republic, mixing theology with politics until it ended on a scaffold. The statesman Johan van Oldenbarnevelt, the Remonstrants' protector, was executed in 1619. The Synod of Dort, meeting through the winter of 1618-19 with delegates from Reformed churches across Europe, condemned the Remonstrance and answered it with five canons of its own. Some two hundred ministers were deposed and their leaders driven into exile — until toleration returned in the 1630s and they came home to build a church of their own.

In Its Own Record

Influenced By

  • Anti-Trinitarian Currents (Servetus; Polish Brethren/Socinians; Transylvanian Unitarians) Inferential-Thin — After Poland expelled the Brethren in 1658, some went to Holland. There, the Unitarian historian Thomas Rees wrote in 1818, they merged into the Remonstrant and Mennonite churches and spread their views through the press. What they changed in Remonstrant thinking rests on that one late account.

Influenced

  • The Methodist Revival Documented — In July 1741 John Wesley picked up the works of Simon Episcopius by mistake and read their account of the Synod of Dort. 'What a scene is here disclosed!' he wrote. Later he drew up plans for a magazine he called The Arminian Magazine.

In Contact With

  • The Reformed Cities - Zurich & Geneva Documented — In 1618 the Dutch government asked Zurich, Bern, Basel, Schaffhausen and Geneva to send men to the Synod of Dort. Geneva sent two, John Diodati and Theodore Tronchin, and the Swiss sent five. Speaking for Geneva, Diodati said the Remonstrants should go.
