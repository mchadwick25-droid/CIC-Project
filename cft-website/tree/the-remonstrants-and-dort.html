The Reformation Era · 1610-1650 · Netherlands

The Remonstrants and Dort

The Netherlands, 1610 to 1650 — five articles about predestination that split a republic, sent a statesman to the scaffold, and produced canons still argued over today.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:02

Read along

The Dutch Reformed minister Jacobus Arminius had doubts about how strictly predestination should be taught, and after his death in 1609 his supporters set them out in five articles — the Remonstrance of 1610. Asking the government to allow their view. The quarrel that followed swallowed the young Dutch Republic, mixing theology with politics until it ended on a scaffold. The statesman Johan van Oldenbarnevelt, the Remonstrants' protector, was executed in 1619. The Synod of Dort, meeting through the winter of 1618-19 with delegates from Reformed churches across Europe, condemned the Remonstrance and answered it with five canons of its own. Some two hundred ministers were deposed and their leaders driven into exile — until toleration returned in the 1630s and they came home to build a church of their own.

Documented Stories

‘Dimittimini, Exite!’

The Synod of Dort opened on 13 November 1618 with delegates from the Dutch provinces and from the Reformed churches of England, Scotland, the Palatinate, Switzerland, Hesse, Bremen, Emden and Geneva; the French delegates had been forbidden by their king to come. The Remonstrants were not delegates. Thirteen of them, led by Simon Episcopius, professor at Leiden, were summoned as parties to answer for their doctrine, and arrived on 6 December. The next day Episcopius delivered an oration of nearly two hours, in Latin, defending the Remonstrants' right to state their whole position and to argue against unconditional reprobation first, since it was what the people feared. The synod's president, Johannes Bogerman, insisted that they answer the synod's questions in the synod's order. For five weeks the two sides argued procedure. On 14 January 1619 Bogerman lost patience and, in a speech that the English observer John Hales reported home, told them that they had begun with a lie and ended with one, that they were unworthy of the synod's patience, and concluded, ‘Dimittimini, exite!’ — ‘You are dismissed, go!’ Hales wrote that the foreign delegates disliked the manner of it. The synod went on to judge the Remonstrant doctrine from their writings, and its Canons were read in the Great Church on 6 May 1619. The Remonstrant ministers who would not sign an Act of Cessation were banished in July; some two hundred were deposed. Episcopius went to Antwerp and wrote the confession of the church they founded there.

A Brotherhood in Exile, and a Church That Could Not Be Seen from the Street

The banished ministers gathered at Antwerp, in the Spanish Netherlands, in the autumn of 1619, and at the end of September constituted themselves as a Brotherhood — not a church, they said, since they did not wish to add to the divisions of the Reformed, but a society of ministers who would serve congregations wherever they could. Johannes Uytenbogaert, the old court preacher who had drafted the Remonstrance of 1610, presided; Episcopius wrote the Confession of 1621, which set out the Remonstrant faith and insisted that no confession should bind consciences. For a decade the Brotherhood's ministers slipped in and out of the Republic under assumed names, preached in barns and private houses, and were fined, imprisoned and, in a few cases, sent to the fortress at Loevestein; congregations were broken up by soldiers and the towns fined them. The death of Prince Maurice in 1625 and the rise of a tolerant party in Amsterdam changed everything. In 1630 the Amsterdam magistrates permitted the Remonstrants to build, provided the building could not be recognized as a church from the street; the meeting-house on the Keizersgracht, opened in September 1630, stood behind ordinary house fronts and seated a thousand. Uytenbogaert came home; Episcopius became the first professor of the Remonstrant seminary at Amsterdam in 1634. The Brotherhood is still called that.

Connections

In Its Own Record

The sharpest internal contest inside Reformed Protestantism in this era: the Five Articles of 1610, judged and condemned by the Synod of Dort in 1618-19, about two hundred ministers deposed, and the Remonstrant Brotherhood driven into exile until toleration returned in the 1630s. The Brotherhood continued afterward with its own seminary in Amsterdam, later associated with the theologian Philipp van Limborch.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced Anti-Trinitarian Currents (Servetus; Polish Brethren/Socinians; Transylvanian Unitarians) Inferential-Thin — After Poland expelled the Brethren in 1658, some went to Holland. There, the Unitarian historian Thomas Rees wrote in 1818, they merged into the Remonstrant and Mennonite churches and spread their views through the press. What they changed in Remonstrant thinking rests on that one late account.

Influenced

  • influenced The Methodist Revival Documented — In July 1741 John Wesley picked up the works of Simon Episcopius by mistake and read their account of the Synod of Dort. 'What a scene is here disclosed!' he wrote. Later he drew up plans for a magazine he called The Arminian Magazine.
  • influenced The Dutch Reformed Church at the Cape Widely Accepted — In 1869 McCarter named the standards of the Dutch Reformed Church in South Africa. They are the Heidelberg Catechism, the Belgic Confession and the Canons of Dort.

In Contact With

  • in contact with The Reformed Cities - Zurich & Geneva Documented — In 1618 the Dutch government asked Zurich, Bern, Basel, Schaffhausen and Geneva to send men to the Synod of Dort. Geneva sent two, John Diodati and Theodore Tronchin, and the Swiss sent five. Speaking for Geneva, Diodati said the Remonstrants should go.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

After Jacobus Arminius raised doubts about how strictly predestination should be taught, his followers set out five articles in the Remonstrance of 1610 — a quarrel that swallowed Dutch politics and sent the statesman Johan van Oldenbarnevelt to the scaffold in 1619. The Synod of Dort answered with five canons of its own, deposing some two hundred ministers, until toleration returned in the 1630s and the exiles came home to build a church of their own.

Legacy

Both sides outlived the quarrel. The Remonstrant Brotherhood is still a small Dutch church, with its own seminary in Amsterdam. The Canons of Dort became one of the three Dutch Reformed standards of faith. Ministers swore to them as far away as North America. 'Arminian' became a lasting label in English theology. John Wesley took it up a century later, and the whole Methodist family took it from him.

Experience It Today

  • The Grote Kerk in Dordrecht — where the Synod of Dort opened and closed, with a permanent exhibition about it in the south aisle chapels

Doctrinal Floor Note

The movement as it stood in this period held the historic creeds without dispute; its quarrel with its opponents was about predestination, not about the doctrine of God. The later drift of some Remonstrant thinkers toward anti-Trinitarian positions belongs to a different story on this map.
