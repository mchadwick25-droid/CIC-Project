Joshua the Stylite

About This Person

In the year Joshua the Stylite counts as 812, famine held Edessa. The poor slept in the porticoes and streets, he writes, wailing night and day from hunger. Their bodies wasted away, and they began to die where they lay. William Wright, his English translator, gives that year as 500 to 501.

Joshua wrote a short Syriac chronicle of those years around Edessa and Amid. He wrote it for Sergius, a priest and abbot near Edessa. Sergius had asked him for a record of the locusts, the famine and plague, and the war between the Romans and the Persians.

Wright says we know little more of Joshua than he chose to tell. His name and title come from a note by a later copyist, Elisha of the convent of Zuknin near Amid. The note asks mercy on "the priest Mar Yêshûa (Joshua) the stylite, from the convent of Züknin." A stylite was a holy man who lived on top of a pillar.

Joshua counts his years in the old Greek era, and Wright adds our own years. By Wright's reckoning the last date in the book is 28 November 506. Wright and the German scholar Theodor Noeldeke place the writing in the months after.

Joshua says plainly why he wrote. The disasters were sent for the people's sins, he argues, and their memory should teach readers to repent. "I am a plain man in speech," he writes, "and I record in this book those things which all men that are in our country can testify to be true."

Wright says the Chronicle is generally counted among the most valuable sources for its time. It survived inside a later Syriac chronicle. Wright took its compiler for the patriarch Dionysius of Tell-Mahre. The French scholar François Nau calls him the Pseudo-Dionysius, writing in 775.

Joshua ends by asking Sergius to pray for him. He promises to send on whatever else is worth recording, "if I remain alive."

Key Works

  • Chronicle (A History of the Time of Affliction at Orhai and Amid and throughout all Mesopotamia): written for Sergius, priest and abbot of a convent near Edessa, as a record of the locusts, famine, plague and the war between the Romans and the Persians, told as chastisements sent for the people's sins

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The Chronicle survives inside a later Syriac chronicle, in a single Vatican manuscript. Wright gives that chronicle to the patriarch Dionysius of Tell-Mahre; François Nau calls its compiler the Pseudo-Dionysius, writing in 775.
  • Wright: it was long known to historians through Joseph Simon Assemani's shortened Latin translation, before Paulin Martin's first full edition of 1876.

How they're remembered

  • Wright: "it is generally acknowledged to be one of the most valuable authorities for the period with which it deals".

What Is Uncertain

  • His name and title rest on one note, by the later copyist Elisha of Zuknin, who filled a gap in the manuscript and asked mercy on "the priest Mar Yêshûa (Joshua) the stylite, from the convent of Züknin". Wright says we know little more of him than he chose to tell.
  • The years are Wright's. Joshua counts in the Greek era, and Wright gives the Christian year beside each one.
  • Paulin Martin, the first editor, titled his edition as written about 515. Wright and Noeldeke place it in the winter of 506 to 507, from the last date and the tone of the final chapter.
  • Wright judges the first sentence of the last chapter a later addition, probably by the compiler he calls Dionysius of Tell-Mahre.
  • Who preserved the Chronicle. Wright, in 1882, names the patriarch Dionysius of Tell-Mahre, who died in 845. François Nau prints an extract from the same Vatican manuscript as the Pseudo-Dionysius and dates its writer to 775; E. W. Brooks calls him "the so-called Dionysius". J.-B. Chabot says the true chronicle of Dionysius of Tell-Mahre is lost but for one chapter.
