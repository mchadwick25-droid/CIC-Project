The Imperial Church Era · c. 360-420 · Constantinople, Asia Minor, Thrace

Constantinople · Asia Minor, today in Turkey and beyond

Pneumatomachian ('Macedonian') Christianity

The party that accepted the Son and stopped at the Spirit - and the reason the creed says anything about the Holy Spirit at all.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:20

Read along

By about 360 the debate had moved on. A body of churchmen in Constantinople and Asia Minor had come to accept, or nearly accept, that the Son is of one being with the Father. But they would not say the same of the Holy Spirit. They held that the Spirit was a creature, or at best a ministering spirit of some third order. Their critics called them Pneumatomachians, Spirit-fighters. The historian Socrates named them after Macedonius, a deposed bishop of Constantinople. But he also says the party was named after Marathonius of Nicomedia. Basil of Caesarea wrote On the Holy Spirit in about 375 to answer them, and he did it with care. He argued the Spirit's divinity mostly from how the church already prayed and baptized, rather than from a bare proof-text. Gregory of Nyssa wrote against them too. The Council of Constantinople in 381 settled the matter. It filled out the creed's third article, which until then had said almost nothing about the Spirit. The result is the clauses still said each week.

Documented Stories

Eleusius Asks His People to Depose Him

Eleusius had been bishop of Cyzicus since 356 and was one of the leaders of the party that accepted the Son’s likeness to the Father in essence but would not call the Holy Spirit God. Under Valens the pressure was on everyone who was not Homoian. Summoned before the emperor and threatened with exile and confiscation, Eleusius ‘yielded to fear’ and signed the Arian formula; then, ‘repenting of his cowardice, he returned to Cyzicus and, standing in the midst of the church, wept, confessed his sin before the whole congregation, and begged them to choose another bishop, since he had betrayed the faith and was unworthy of the priesthood.’ The people would not do it. They loved him, Sozomen says, and refused to let anyone else be ordained in his place; the emperor sent Eunomius, the Anomoean, to take the see, and the city would not receive him. Fifteen years later, in 381, he led the Macedonian party to the council at Constantinople that would write the Spirit into the creed — thirty-six bishops, Socrates says, ‘most of whom came from the cities of the Hellespont’, with Eleusius and Marcian of Lampsacus at their head. They would not sign, ‘chose rather to maintain the Arian dogma than to assent to the homoousian doctrine’, as Socrates puts it, and left the city. The council expanded the third article of the creed without them. The man who had once been too frightened to hold his ground held it this time, and lost.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

By about 360, a body of churchmen in Constantinople and Asia Minor had come to accept the Son's full divinity but stopped short of saying the same of the Holy Spirit, holding him instead to be a creature or a ministering spirit of some lesser order. Basil of Caesarea answered them around 375 in On the Holy Spirit, arguing the Spirit's divinity not from a single proof-text but from how the church already prayed and baptized. The Council of Constantinople settled the question in 381 by expanding the creed's third article into the clauses still recited every week.

Legacy

The third article of the Nicene Creed as it is recited today - Lord and giver of life, worshipped and glorified together with the Father and the Son - is the answer to this party. Basil of Caesarea argued it in a way of his own. He took the church's practice of prayer and baptism as evidence of what it believes.

On the Nicene Creed

They accepted, or came close to accepting, the Son's full divinity while denying it of the Holy Spirit - calling him a creature, or a ministering spirit of a different order from Father and Son. The third article of the creed as Constantinople expanded it in 381, which calls the Spirit Lord and giver of life and worshipped together with the Father and the Son, was written to close exactly that position. "Pneumatomachian" - Spirit-fighter - is their opponents' word; so is the attachment of Macedonius' name, which is later than the party and contested.

How Sure the Record Is

Through opponents almost entirely. Basil of Caesarea's On the Holy Spirit and Gregory of Nyssa's Against the Macedonians are both refutations; no Pneumatomachian text survives in its own right.
