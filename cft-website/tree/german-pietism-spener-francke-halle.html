The Enlightenment & Awakening Era · 1675-1750 · Germany

German Pietism - Spener, Francke, Halle

Germany, 1675 to about 1750 — a renewal inside the Lutheran state church that answered cold orthodoxy with small groups and conversion stories, and built an orphanage in Halle that grew into a world-spanning enterprise.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:29

Read along

In 1675 a Lutheran pastor in Frankfurt, Philipp Jakob Spener, published a short book called Pia Desideria arguing that correct doctrine correctly preached was not enough. Christians needed to meet in small groups, read Scripture together, and expect their lives to change. What followed was less a new church than a renewal movement inside the Lutheran state churches, and its center became Halle, where August Hermann Francke built an orphanage that grew into a complex of schools, a printing house and a Bible institute, funded by donations and tied to correspondents across Europe, Russia, India and North America. Pietism gave German Protestantism the conversion narrative as a genre and a great body of new hymnody, and through Halle's graduates it launched one of the first organized Protestant missions sent out from Europe, when Bartholomäus Ziegenbalg and Heinrich Plütschau sailed for the Danish trading post at Tranquebar in South India in 1706.

The Tamil church they planted went on. Fenger calls Aaron "the first native ordained" to the ministry. That was on 28 December 1733. By 1813 his list counts eleven Indian ministers.

People and Stories

Voices in the Record

  • Philipp Jakob Spener — Frankfurt pastor whose Pia Desideria (1675) set the movement's program and launched its small-group meetings
  • August Hermann Francke — built the Halle orphanage, schools and press that became Pietism's institutional engine
  • Johanna Eleonora Petersen — published her own life story in 1718, one of the first autobiographies written and printed by a German woman
  • Johann Anastasius Freylinghausen — compiled the Halle hymnbook (1704) that carried the movement's piety in its sung form
  • Bartholomäus Ziegenbalg — Halle-trained, sailed in 1706 for Tranquebar in South India, translating the New Testament into Tamil
  • Aaron — the Indian catechist ordained on 28 December 1733, whom Fenger calls the first native ordained to the priesthood

Documented Stories

Four Thalers Sixteen Groschen

August Hermann Francke had been driven out of Leipzig and then Erfurt for holding Bible meetings, and in 1692 was given the poor parish of Glaucha outside the walls of Halle, a suburb of taverns, together with a chair at the new university. Beggars came to the parsonage door on Thursdays for bread, and Francke began catechizing them while they waited, and found that the children could read nothing. He put a box in his front room for alms toward their schooling. Early in 1695, as he tells it, someone left four thalers and sixteen groschen in it at once, and he said, holding the money, ‘That is an honest capital; something proper must be founded with it; I will start a school for the poor.’ He bought two thalers' worth of books and hired a student to teach for two hours a day. Within weeks the townspeople of Glaucha were paying to send their own children; by the end of the year there were orphans lodging with the teacher, and in 1698 Francke laid the foundation stone of an orphan house he had no money to complete, and published, as it rose, accounts of the gifts that arrived unasked, in the conviction that what God had begun God would pay for. By his death in 1727 the Foundations had over two thousand pupils, a Latin school, a printing house, a pharmacy, a Bible institute that sold Scripture cheaply across Europe, and a mission in India.

The Missionary in the Commandant’s Prison

Bartholomäus Ziegenbalg and Heinrich Plütschau landed at Tranquebar on 9 July 1706 and found that no one had been told they were coming. The Danish East India Company's commandant, Johann Sigismund Hassius, regarded a mission to the Tamils as a threat to trade and to the slave-owning habits of the Danish colony, and the Lutheran chaplain regarded two Pietists from Halle as sectarians. Ziegenbalg learned Tamil by sitting on the floor of a village school and tracing letters in the sand with the children, opened schools, and by 1708 had a small Tamil congregation, a church, and a growing quarrel with the fort. In November 1708 a Tamil widow whose husband had been in company service asked him to press her claim against the company; Ziegenbalg wrote to Hassius, was summoned, refused to be silenced, and on 19 November was arrested and put in a cell in the fort, where he was held for four months without pen or paper. He said afterwards that it was the most fruitful time of his ministry, since he could do nothing but pray. Released on 26 March 1709, he went on to finish the Tamil New Testament in 1711, printed at Tranquebar in 1714 on a press sent from London, the first in an Indian language; to compile a Tamil dictionary and a description of Tamil religion that Halle refused to publish because it treated Hinduism too respectfully; and to die at Tranquebar in 1719, aged thirty-six.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Pietism grew out of Lutheran devotion. Spener's Pia Desideria first came out as a preface to a book of sermons by Johann Arndt. Pietism may also owe something to the strict Dutch Reformed piety called precisianism, but how much is not clear. Pietism shaped the Moravian Church at Herrnhut. It also sent some of the first Protestant missions out of Europe. Pietism in Württemberg, linked with Johann Albrecht Bengel, belongs to this same movement.

Direct descendants

  • gave rise to Radical Pietism & the Brethren (Dunkers, Ephrata) Widely Accepted Major — Johann Wilhelm Petersen, later a leading radical Pietist, often went to the prayer meeting in Spener's house. There he heard talk of the church's future. Alexander Mack, who founded the Brethren in 1708, wrote that they had once lived 'among the pietists'. A Brethren historian wrote in 1899 that Francke woke Ernst Christoph Hochmann to faith at Halle, and that Hochmann shaped Mack more than anyone. The same historian denied that the founders were Pietists themselves.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced Dutch Further Reformation & Refuge Culture Documented — In 1717 the German Pietist Johann Henrich Reitz printed the life of a Dutch preacher, Jodocus van Lodenstein of Utrecht. Reitz took it from a Dutch pastor's preface and from Lodenstein's own sermons. It names his teacher, Gisbertus Voetius.
  • influenced Quietist Controversy (Molinos, Guyon) Documented — In 1687 Rome censured Molinos. Soon after, the young August Hermann Francke put his Spiritual Guide into Latin at Leipzig. Critics later said Francke had taken in Molinos's ideas. He replied that reading and translating Molinos had not been the start of his conversion.

Influenced

  • influenced The Moravian Church at Herrnhut Documented — Count Zinzendorf, who let the refugees settle at Herrnhut, had Spener as a godfather and went to school under Francke at Halle. He said the Herrnhut community first grew from 'Spener's idea of planting little churches in the great church'. His colleague August Spangenberg recorded both in his life of the count.
  • influenced The Methodist Revival Documented — In July 1738 John Wesley went to Halle and toured its orphan house. He wrote that August Hermann Francke's 'name is indeed as precious ointment'. George Whitefield read Francke's account of the Halle orphan house and saw it as the pattern for his own orphan house in Georgia.
  • influenced The Haugean Awakening Dominant Modern Reconstruction — Hans Nielsen Hauge began preaching in Norway in 1796, inside the Lutheran state church. His modern biographers place his revival in the line of Lutheran Pietism. This rests on their books.
  • influenced Laestadianism & the Nordic Revivals Inferential-Thin — Lars Levi Laestadius began his revival in Swedish Lapland in 1844, as a Lutheran pastor. It is often counted as a late branch of Pietism in the Lutheran north. The evidence is thin. It rests on how the revival is usually classed, not on a shown debt to Pietist writers.
  • influenced The Reveil & Inner-Mission Awakening Dominant Modern Reconstruction — After 1815 a wave of awakening swept through Protestant churches in Switzerland, France, the Netherlands and Germany. Modern writers such as Nicholas Hope trace it back to older Pietism. This rests on their books.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In 1675 the Lutheran pastor Philipp Jakob Spener argued that correct doctrine preached from the pulpit wasn't enough — Christians needed to meet in small groups, read Scripture together, and expect real change in their lives. That conviction built an orphanage at Halle that grew into schools, a press, and a network reaching across Europe, and sent Bartholomäus Ziegenbalg to Tranquebar in South India in 1706, one of the first organized Protestant missions out of Europe.

Legacy

Pietism never became a church of its own. It stayed inside the Lutheran churches and in time faded as a separate party. But almost everything it touched outlived it. The Moravians at Herrnhut grew up close to it. Their patron, Count Zinzendorf, was Spener's godson and went to school at Halle. John Wesley learned from the Moravians, and in 1738 he went to Halle himself. George Whitefield took Francke's orphan house as the model for his own in Georgia. Halle also chose and backed the first missionaries the king of Denmark sent to India, in 1706. The Halle foundations still stand and still work in Saxony-Anhalt: the orphanage buildings, the library, the cabinet of curiosities and all. Its habits are now so ordinary across evangelical Protestantism that their origin is easy to miss. Small groups meet midweek to read Scripture. A Christian is expected to tell the story of their own conversion.

Experience It Today

  • The Francke Foundations, Halle, Germany — Francke's orphanage complex, library and historic school buildings, still in use and open to visitors
