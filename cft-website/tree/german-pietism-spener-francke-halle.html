The Enlightenment & Awakening Era · 1675-1750 · Germany

German Pietism - Spener, Francke, Halle

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

In 1675 a Lutheran pastor in Frankfurt, Philipp Jakob Spener, published a short book called Pia Desideria arguing that correct doctrine correctly preached was not enough. Christians needed to meet in small groups, read Scripture together, and expect their lives to change. What followed was less a new church than a renewal movement inside the Lutheran state churches, and its center became Halle, where August Hermann Francke built an orphanage that grew into a complex of schools, a printing house and a Bible institute, funded by donations and tied to correspondents across Europe, Russia, India and North America. Pietism gave German Protestantism the conversion narrative as a genre and a great body of new hymnody, and through Halle's graduates it launched one of the first organized Protestant missions sent out from Europe, when Bartholomäus Ziegenbalg and Heinrich Plütschau sailed for the Danish trading post at Tranquebar in South India in 1706.

In Its Own Record

Gave Rise To

  • Radical Pietism & the Brethren (Dunkers, Ephrata) Widely Accepted Major — Johann Wilhelm Petersen, later a leading radical Pietist, often went to the prayer meeting in Spener's house. There he heard talk of the church's future. Alexander Mack, who founded the Brethren in 1708, wrote that they had once lived 'among the pietists'. A Brethren historian wrote in 1899 that Francke woke Ernst Christoph Hochmann to faith at Halle, and that Hochmann shaped Mack more than anyone. The same historian denied that the founders were Pietists themselves.

Influenced By

  • Dutch Further Reformation & Refuge Culture Documented — In 1717 the German Pietist Johann Henrich Reitz printed the life of a Dutch preacher, Jodocus van Lodenstein of Utrecht. Reitz took it from a Dutch pastor's preface and from Lodenstein's own sermons. It names his teacher, Gisbertus Voetius.
  • Quietist Controversy (Molinos, Guyon) Documented — In 1687 Rome censured Molinos. Soon after, the young August Hermann Francke put his Spiritual Guide into Latin at Leipzig. Critics later said Francke had taken in Molinos's ideas. He replied that reading and translating Molinos had not been the start of his conversion.

Influenced

  • The Moravian Church at Herrnhut Documented — Count Zinzendorf, who let the refugees settle at Herrnhut, had Spener as a godfather and went to school under Francke at Halle. He said the Herrnhut community first grew from 'Spener's idea of planting little churches in the great church'. His colleague August Spangenberg recorded both in his life of the count.
  • The Methodist Revival Documented — In July 1738 John Wesley went to Halle and toured its orphan house. He wrote that August Hermann Francke's 'name is indeed as precious ointment'. George Whitefield read Francke's account of the Halle orphan house and saw it as the pattern for his own orphan house in Georgia.
  • The Haugean Awakening Dominant Modern Reconstruction — Hans Nielsen Hauge began preaching in Norway in 1796, inside the Lutheran state church. His modern biographers place his revival in the line of Lutheran Pietism. This rests on their books.
  • Laestadianism & the Nordic Revivals Inferential-Thin — Lars Levi Laestadius began his revival in Swedish Lapland in 1844, as a Lutheran pastor. It is often counted as a late branch of Pietism in the Lutheran north. The evidence is thin. It rests on how the revival is usually classed, not on a shown debt to Pietist writers.
  • The Reveil & Inner-Mission Awakening Dominant Modern Reconstruction — After 1815 a wave of awakening swept through Protestant churches in Switzerland, France, the Netherlands and Germany. Modern writers such as Nicholas Hope trace it back to older Pietism. This rests on their books.

Sources

  • Spener, Pia Desideria (1675; trans. Tappert, Fortress, 1964), with the collegia pietatis practice it launched (primary)
  • Francke's conversion narrative (1690/91 Lebenslauf) and works; the Halle institutional record - orphanage reports (Segensvolle Fusstapfen, 1701ff.), the global correspondence network, Canstein Bible Institute records (primary) — The Halle archives (Franckesche Stiftungen) are largely untranslated from German.
  • Johanna Eleonora Petersen, The Life of Lady Johanna Eleonora Petersen, Written by Herself (1718; trans. Becker-Cantarino, Chicago, 2005); lay conversion-narrative compilations (Reitz, Historie der Wiedergebohrnen, 1698-) (primary) — Women's and lay voices; part of the movement's own conversion-narrative genre.
  • Freylinghausen, Geistreiches Gesangbuch (Halle, 1704; expanded 1714) (primary) — Hymnody in actual use - household and conventicle piety in its own medium. Available only in German.
  • Douglas Shantz, An Introduction to German Pietism (Johns Hopkins, 2013), with W. R. Ward, The Protestant Evangelical Awakening (CUP, 1992) (secondary)
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 7, 'Continental Protestant Europe' (Lehmann), with 'Evangelical awakenings in the North Atlantic world' (Ward) (reference) — Strong fit: Lehmann is a Pietism specialist.
