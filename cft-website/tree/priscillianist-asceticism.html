The Imperial Church Era · c. 370-450 · Spain

Priscillianist Asceticism

Spain and Gaul, c. 370 to 450 — an ascetic movement whose leader was tried and executed at Trier, in what appears to be the first time a Christian state put Christians to death over doctrine.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:29

Read along

In the 370s a Spanish layman named Priscillian began teaching a severe ascetic Christianity in the western provinces. It meant fasting, celibacy, and retreat to the countryside for study and prayer. Lay men and women read Scripture together, outside the bishops' oversight. It spread fast enough to alarm the bishops of Spain and Gaul. They condemned it at a council in Saragossa around 380. His supporters answered by making him bishop of Ávila. He went to Rome and Milan to clear himself, and was turned away by both bishops, Ambrose of Milan among them. His opponents called his teaching the heresy of the Gnostics, accused him of sorcery, and took the case to the emperor's court. Later churchmen likened it to Manichaeism. Around 385, at Trier, the usurping emperor Magnus Maximus had Priscillian and several companions tried and put to death. So far as anyone can tell, it was the first time a Christian state executed Christians over belief. It shocked people at the time. Martin of Tours had begged the emperor not to shed the blood of the accused. A secular ruler, he said, had no place judging a church case. The bishops who had pressed the charges were disgraced for it.

Documented Stories

The Bodies Go Home to Spain

Sulpicius Severus, who thought Priscillian a heretic and his execution a crime, records how the affair ended. At Trier, under the usurper Maximus, Priscillian was convicted of maleficium — sorcery — on the strength of confessions that he had studied obscene doctrines, held nocturnal gatherings of shameful women, and prayed naked; he and four companions, including the wealthy widow Euchrotia, were beheaded, and others were exiled. ‘The heresy was not suppressed by the death of Priscillian but rather spread and strengthened. His followers, who had honoured him before as a saint, began to venerate him as a martyr.’ The bodies of the executed were carried back to Spain and buried ‘with great pomp’, and — the detail Sulpicius finds most telling — ‘it was considered the height of religion to swear by Priscillian.’ Ithacius was deposed and expelled from his see; Idacius resigned; Martin of Tours and Ambrose of Milan broke communion with every bishop who had touched the case. The movement lasted in Galicia into the sixth century, condemned again at council after council, and its founder’s own writings, found in 1885 in a Würzburg manuscript, turned out to be sober tracts on Scripture and asceticism with nothing occult in them. The state had produced a martyr cult by trying to prevent one, and the church that had asked it to act spent the next generation disowning what it had asked for.

Legacy

The movement was suppressed but not immediately extinguished. Priscillian became a martyr in the eyes of his followers, the ascetic current he represented persisted in Galicia in north-western Spain for more than a century, and a council at Braga was still condemning Priscillianism in 561. Its heaviest legacy is the precedent set against it. The execution at Trier is the point at which capital punishment for heresy enters Western Christian practice, and it was cited afterwards both as precedent and as warning. In 1885 a set of eleven anonymous treatises was identified in a Würzburg manuscript, widely thought to come from Priscillian or his circle: the first chance in fourteen centuries to hear this movement in something close to its own voice rather than through the charges laid against it.

Doctrinal Floor Note

Genuinely unclear, and worth saying so. The charges laid against Priscillian included Manichaeism and sorcery, but they were laid by hostile bishops in what became a political prosecution, and what survives of writing attributed to him does not obviously confess anything the creeds rule out. Where this movement actually stood is an open question rather than a settled answer.
