The Reformation Era · 1519-1531 · Zurich

Huldrych Zwingli

A person. The tradition that tells their story: The Reformed Cities - Zurich & Geneva.

Known for
People's priest at the Great Minster in Zurich from 1519, who preached through whole books of the Bible and in 1523 defended that preaching in a public debate before the city council. The council ruled that every preacher must preach only what he could defend from Scripture, and Zurich then removed its images and ended the Mass. He broke with Martin Luther at Marburg in 1529 over the Lord's Supper and died with the Zurich army at Kappel in 1531. The Reformed churches look to him as one of their founders.
Also called
Ulrich Zwingli, Huldreich Zwingli, Zuinglius
Dates
Born 1 January 1484 at Wildhaus, by B. J. Kidd and by Samuel Macauley Jackson, his editors. Elected people's priest at Zurich on 11 December 1518 and admitted on 1 January 1519, by Kidd. His friend Myconius says he began the preaching he had promised on Christmas day, 1519. In the Archeteles of 22-23 August 1522 Zwingli himself wrote that three years before he had preached the whole Gospel of Matthew. On Ash Wednesday, 5 March 1522, some Zurich citizens broke the Lent fast and Zwingli, who ate none of the sausage, came to their aid with a sermon on 23 March; the bishop's commission reached Zurich on 7 April. Two petitions drafted by Zwingli, to the bishop on 2 July and to the Diet on 13 July 1522, asked that the clergy be allowed to marry. The First Debate was held on 29 January 1523, the day of the council's decree, by Kidd's Latin; Jackson's introduction says the day set was 23 January. The Second Debate was held 26 to 28 October 1523. The council ended the Mass on 12 April 1525, passed the drowning law on 7 March 1526 and sentenced Felix Manz to drowning on 5 January 1527. The Marburg Articles were signed on 3 October 1529. He died on 11 October 1531 at Kappel
Place
Wildhaus in the Toggenburg by birth; Glarus, where he was parish priest from 1506 to 1516 and went three times as chaplain with the Glarus soldiers in Italy; Einsiedeln, where he was people's priest from 1516 to 1518; Zurich, the Great Minster; Marburg, in the lands of the prince of Hesse; Kappel, about ten miles south of Zurich
Role
People's priest of the Great Minster in Zurich; preacher and reformer

About This Person

Zurich, January 1523. More than six hundred priests and citizens crowd the hall of the city council. When the bishop's spokesman wants the universities to judge, the preacher, Huldrych Zwingli, answers him.

"I say we have judges here who cannot err and take no side: the Holy Scriptures, which can neither lie nor deceive. We have them here in Hebrew, Greek and Latin. Let both sides take them as fair and just judges."

Since 1519 Zwingli had preached the Bible book by book as people's priest at the Great Minster, the city's main church. He had asked to defend that preaching in public. The council, the two hundred citizens who made Zurich's laws, ruled that every preacher must preach only what he could defend from Scripture. Within two years Zurich removed its images and ended the Mass, the Latin service at the altar. The Reformed churches, the family that grew from Zurich and Geneva, look to him as a founder.

The council that judged by Scripture also used force. Its summons had warned that anyone who opposed it without Scripture would be dealt with more severely, and any preacher who disobeyed its ruling would be restrained. A city council, not a bishop, now decided what its church would do.

Some Zurich men, historians say, pushed the same test further. They would admit nothing without clear warrant in Scripture, and found none for baptizing infants, so they baptized adults again. They were called Anabaptists, or rebaptizers. On March 7, 1526, the council decreed that anyone who baptized another would be drowned without mercy. Zwingli wrote that same day to a friend that patience had at last broken out. He was grieved by their stubborn daring, and wished the new faith had not begun with such examples. But, he went on, it pleases God to guard in this way against evils to come. On January 5, 1527, by the council's sentence, one of their leaders, Felix Manz, was bound and thrown into the water to die.

In May 1529 Zwingli urged Bern, Zurich's ally, to war on the five Catholic cantons, or states, of central Switzerland.

"This peace that some press for so hard is war, not peace. And the war we press for is peace, not war. For we thirst for no one's blood, nor will we drink it in the turmoil. Our aim is to cut the sinews of the rule of the few."

That autumn the prince of Hesse brought Zwingli and Martin Luther to Marburg to settle communion, the shared bread and wine. Luther held that Christ's own body is truly in the bread; Zwingli answered that a body is in one place, and Christ's is in heaven. They signed fifteen articles together, and the last one says where they stopped.

"We have not agreed at this time whether Christ's true body and blood are bodily in the bread and wine. Yet each side shall show the other Christian love, as far as each conscience can bear. And both sides shall pray earnestly to Almighty God to confirm us, by his Spirit, in the right understanding. Amen."

In 1531 Zurich and Bern cut off the cantons' food. The cantons marched, and on October 11 beat Zurich's army at Kappel. His friend Myconius, writing the next year, says Zwingli went as the army's chaplain, though he had said he would not go. He died on the field.

In 1549 the pastors of Zurich signed one agreement on communion with John Calvin of Geneva, who never met him. In 1554 Calvin wrote that if Zwingli were alive, he would not change a word of it.

In March 1532 the records of the Great Minster name Heinrich Bullinger over its teaching in Zwingli's place; he led Zurich's church for four decades. Its overseers gave the house of "Master Ulrich, of blessed memory", as the record calls Zwingli, to the new teacher of the Bible lesson. He agreed to share it with Zwingli's children until the widow found another home.

Key Works

  • The Sixty-Seven Articles: the articles Zwingli issued before the First Debate of 1523. He says he had preached them at Zurich as based on Scripture, offers to defend them, and says that where he has misread Scripture he will let himself be taught better, but only from Scripture
  • Commentary on True and False Religion: his Latin treatise of March 1525, printed at Zurich, which Kidd says he addressed to Francis I of France and in which Kidd says he made a final attack on the Mass
  • An Account of the Faith: his statement of belief, submitted to the emperor Charles V at the Diet of Augsburg on 3 July 1530. In it he says that the true body of Christ is present in the Lord's Supper by the contemplation of faith, and denies that the natural body is present in the supper or chewed with the teeth

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Heinrich Bullinger took his place. The minster's record of March 1532 names him over the teaching in Zwingli's place, and Philip Schaff says he was chosen chief pastor at Zurich on 9 December 1531 and calls him the pupil, friend and successor of Zwingli and the author of the Second Helvetic Confession of 1566. The tree's account of the Reformed cities says he held Zurich's pastorate for four decades after him.
  • On 19 June 1525 the choir office at Zurich gave way to Bible readings, called Prophesyings. Kidd says they were sources of the Reformers' commentaries and of the Puritan prophesyings in England.
  • Marburg's one open point divided the Protestants. Kidd says Luther revised the fifteen articles against the Sacramentaries into the Articles of Schwabach, the first of the Lutheran Symbolical Books, and that Philip of Hesse's wider combination failed.
  • In 1549 the pastors of Zurich and John Calvin of Geneva agreed one statement on the Lord's Supper, the Consensus Tigurinus, published in 1554 with Calvin's exposition.
  • Jackson writes that the Reformed churches, as against the Lutheran churches, look to him as one of their great founders.

How they're remembered

  • His friend Oswald Myconius wrote the first life of him in 1532. He was not at the battle: he says he saw the army go out in the morning and heard the news at night.
  • Calvin wrote in 1554 that if Zwingli and Oecolampadius were still alive they would not change one word in the Consensus Tigurinus, which Calvin calls our doctrine. In 1539 he wrote to Farel that men at Zurich flare up if anyone prefers Luther to Zwingli, and that it would be impiety not to think of such a man with honorable esteem.
  • Luther wrote to Wenzel Link on 3 January 1532 that he was now seeing God's judgments for the second time, first on Münzer and now on Zwingli. In another letter of 1532 he says that his opponents now declare Zwingli one of Christ's martyrs, that they may fill up the measure of their iniquities.
  • John Foxe, an English Protestant writer decades later, calls him the blessed servant and saint of God.
  • Jackson writes that he was a good man and a valiant fighter for the truth as he conceived it.
  • The tree's account of the Reformed cities names the Great Minster, his own church, as still open to visitors in Zurich.

What Is Uncertain

  • The Reformed churches look to him as a founder; that is Jackson's judgment as an editor, not a statement from his own time. The tree's account of the Reformed cities dates their beginning to Zurich in 1519 and says he tested every inherited church practice in public debate. The council's call and decree and Kidd's account show that the debates of 1523 took up his own teaching, and then images and the Mass.
  • When the preaching through Matthew began. Kidd gives his admission as people's priest as 1 January 1519 and says he then began continuous preaching on Matthew, Acts, the letters to Timothy and Galatians, and both letters of Peter. His friend Myconius says he began what he had promised on "Christmas day, 1519". The tree's account of the Reformed cities has him begin on 1 January 1519. Zwingli's own words of August 1522 say only that three years before he had preached the whole of Matthew. Both dates are kept. His claim that he had not even heard the names of the faction he was accused of joining when he preached Matthew is his own, and the passage does not name the faction.
  • The date of the First Debate. Kidd's Latin dates the council's decree 29 January 1523. Jackson's introduction says the day set was 23 January 1523, and his bracket dates the council's call to the debate 3 January 1522, where Kidd's heading has 3 January 1523. The day of the month in Jackson's print of the decree is damaged in the scan, so the 29th rests on Kidd.
  • The First Debate is told in a record kept by a Zurich schoolmaster and edited by Zwingli's own side, in McLouth's English from the Zurich German. The German is not held. The bishop's side printed its own account, which is not held either. Jackson calls the council's decree the official beginning of the Reformation in German Switzerland; that is the editor's verdict.
  • The vote to end the Mass. The council's record of 12 April 1525 says the Lord's Supper is to go on by the printed order and that no Mass is to be held. The narrow vote is the editors': Kidd says the council decreed it by a small majority, and Emil Egli, the editor of the council's records, writes that the majority was a few hands. The Zurich chronicler Edlibach, whom Egli quotes, says only that the last Mass was held, God's table set up, the altars stripped and the choir books destroyed, and that what pleased one man well did not please his neighbor.
  • The Anabaptists. Kidd says the radical party at the Second Debate was led by Conrad Grebel and Felix Manz, both of Zurich, and by Balthasar Hubmaier, and that their aim was to apply the principle of the official reforms and admit nothing without clear warrant of Scripture; that is Kidd's account of their aim, not a statement from the party. Grebel is recorded as speaking at that debate. The Hutterite chronicle says Manz was drowned at Zurich for the true faith and baptism, and the Martyrs Mirror dates his drowning 1526; the council's judgment on him is dated 5 January 1527. Kidd says he was the first to suffer drowning.
  • Zwingli's part in the drowning law. The mandate of 7 March 1526 and the sentence on Manz are the council's acts. Zwingli's held words are his letter of 7 March 1526 to Joachim Watt, called Vadian, burgomaster of St. Gall. He reports the decree, writes that patience had at last broken out, that the daring of the Anabaptists grieved and displeased him, and that he wished the new faith had not begun with such examples. The next clause in Kidd's print reads "Deus non sumus cui sic placet futuris malis prospicere": but we are not God, whom it pleases to guard in this way against evils to come. It goes on to Ananias struck down for lying. The clause is clean in the print; no second print of the letter is held, and Kidd's extract ends there. Kidd's remark that the penalty was decreed as if to suit the crime is the editor's comment.
  • The war letter of 30 May 1529. It is Zwingli's own and is not softened. Kidd calls the seizure of the abbey of St. Gall, which Zwingli had supported, an act of aggression; that is Kidd's verdict. A month later Zwingli wrote to Conrad Sam at Ulm that God had shown they could win even with no killing. The Latin word in the letter is oligarchiae; the letter does not say who the few were.
  • Marburg: what was said and how it ended. Rudolf Collin, a Zurich professor who went with Zwingli, says Luther had written "Hoc est corpus meum" on his table before the debate, and that on the third day articles were drawn up to show that the two sides agreed on every head of faith but the manner of Christ's presence. The summary in the collection of Luther's follower Johann Aurifaber says the talks were broken off by the landgrave because plague had begun, and that they parted amicably. Luther wrote to his wife on 4 October 1529 that they had agreed on nearly all points, that the landgrave was trying to make them consider each other brethren, and that although Luther objected to being brethren, they wished to live at peace. He also wrote that Zwingli's argument was the best, that a body cannot be without place and so Christ's body is not in the bread. Zwingli's own wording of 1530, in the Account of the Faith, is that Christ's true body is present in the supper "by the contemplation of faith" and that the natural body is not.
  • Luther's report to Jacob Probst of 1 June 1530. Luther says Zwingli, weeping before the landgrave and the assembly, asked to be called brother and said there were no people on earth with whom he would rather be at one than with the Wittenbergers, and that Luther kept telling them they had another spirit from his. Only Luther reports the tears. The German words are held only in a damaged scan, so no original is printed for them; the Latin around them is legible.
  • Why he was at Kappel. Myconius, his friend, writing in 1532, says that in the second war he was elected chaplain and, although he told himself and others that he would not go, he went. Foxe, from Sleidan and Oecolampadius, says it was an old custom that the chief minister of Zurich's church goes with its army, that he went as a good shepherd, and that he did not go of his own accord, since the senate pressed him. Jackson says he went as a non-combatant, though armed for defense. Kidd says he was all for renewing the war.
  • Who cut off the food. Kidd says Bern thought it enough to stop the supplies of the five cantons. Foxe says Zurich and Bern together stopped the passes. Myconius says the cutting off of provisions never had Zwingli's approval, and that he did not strenuously oppose it once the reluctant Zurichers had accepted it.
  • His death and the body. That he died on the field at Kappel is stated by Kidd, Jackson, Egli and Foxe. Myconius's account of the fall, his last words and the burning of the body is what he heard, and he says it was reported. Myconius and, after him, Foxe tell that his friends found his heart whole in the ashes; Myconius had it from others and declined to see it. Foxe says he was forty-four and four years younger than Luther, which disagrees with the birth on 1 January 1484 that Kidd and Jackson give.
  • Luther's words of 1532 are hostile and partly wrong: he wrote that Carlstadt had succeeded Zwingli at Zurich, and the council's record names Bullinger.
  • The minster's record of March 1532 names Heinrich Bullinger over the teaching in Zwingli's place. The Bible lesson and Zwingli's house were given to another man, Theodorus Buochmann of Bischofszell, a theologian, who agreed to share the house with Zwingli's children until the widow found another dwelling. The record's printed spelling of the name, Uorichs, reads Uolrichs elsewhere in the same book and in Kidd.
  • Calvin's claim of 1554 is his own, in Beveridge's English from the French; the French is not held. The tree's account of the Reformed cities says Calvin and Zwingli never met.
