The Reformation Era · 1519-1650 · Swiss lands, then Europe

The Reformed Cities - Zurich & Geneva

Meet Theophilus on Church in Conversation

About This Movement

Two Swiss city-states, a generation apart, worked out what a whole town would look like if its church were rebuilt from Scripture: Zurich under Huldrych Zwingli from 1519, which stripped images and the Mass out of its churches. And Geneva after 1536 under John Calvin, whose Ecclesiastical Ordinances of 1541 gave the city pastors, teachers, elders and deacons, and a consistory that met every week. Geneva's consistory registers survive, and they are startlingly ordinary — quarrels between neighbors, absent husbands, people who had not been to church. Which makes this one of the best pictures anywhere of what a church actually did about the daily conduct of ordinary people. From these two cities the Reformed pattern traveled: Scotland, France, the Netherlands and English Puritanism all trace back here.

In Its Own Record

Gave Rise To

  • The Anabaptist Movements Documented Major — In February 1525 a man tried in Zurich said Felix Manz had baptized him. In 1527 Zwingli wrote of the Baptists: 'They have gone out from us.' This is the Swiss branch only. Historians hold that Anabaptists in South Germany, Moravia and the Netherlands had other roots too.

Influenced By

  • Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity Documented — In his Institutes, Calvin calls on Augustine as his guide on grace and on God's choice of the saved. He says he could fill a whole book with Augustine's words alone and need no others.
  • Antiochene Exegetical Christianity (Chrysostom-centered) Documented — Calvin's Institutes quote Chrysostom with delight: 'The foundation of our philosophy is humility.' Yet Calvin also faults him and the other Greek Fathers. He says they gave the human will too much power.

Influenced

  • The Scottish Reformation & Kirk Documented — John Knox studied in Geneva and went to preach to the English exiles at Frankfurt at John Calvin's command. He then went back to Geneva, and from there, by way of Dieppe, to Scotland.
  • The Huguenots Documented — Calvin wrote from Geneva in May 1559, just before the French churches met in synod at Paris. His letter names the ministers Geneva was sending to help them. Two of them were a man called Arnold and Peter Gilbert.
  • The English Reformation & Elizabethan Settlement Documented — In 1548 John Calvin wrote from Geneva to praise the Protector Somerset for his reform of worship in England. In 1562 Bishop John Jewel told Peter Martyr in Zurich that England's teaching did not differ from his 'by a nail's breadth.' In 1566 Archbishop Parker cited Martyr and Bullinger too, to press the clergy to conform.
  • Puritanism Documented — The Admonition to the Parliament of 1572 sends the Archbishop of York to read the 'learned Epistle' Theodore Beza wrote him. Letters from Beza, a minister at Geneva, and from Rudolph Gualter of Zurich were printed with it. They gave the Puritan case the weight of two famous names.
  • Dutch Further Reformation & Refuge Culture Documented — Gisbertus Voetius was professor of theology at Utrecht. His lessons on the Heidelberg Catechism were printed in 1662. Asked if the Dutch Reformed held Calvin's views, he said yes, but Calvin was not their head.
  • The Waldensian-Reformed Union Documented — A man of Angrogne, caught standing guard, said the ministers met at Chanforan included a red-bearded 'M. Farel'. The pastor Pierre Gilles, writing in 1644, calls them pastors who kept coming from Switzerland. In 1535 the valleys paid to print a French Bible.
  • Orthodoxy under the Ottomans - Parish, Millet & Neomartyrs Documented — Cyril Lucaris wrote to the pastors and teachers of Geneva as Patriarch of Constantinople. In that letter he called Calvin a most holy and wise teacher, now with the saints. A Latin copy of his Confession, printed in 1645, said it was so close to Calvin that it could have been copied from him.

Argued Against

  • The Anabaptist Movements Documented Major — In January 1525 Zurich's council told Conrad Grebel and Felix Manz to stop arguing about baptism. Two years later it sentenced Manz to be drowned for baptizing adults again. That same year Zwingli wrote that the Baptists had split and betrayed the church.Lasting result: Zurich's council made a second baptism a crime punished by drowning. On 5 January 1527 it sentenced Felix Manz to die that way.
  • The Latin Papal Church (Gregorian Reform to Innocent III) Documented — In his Institutes, John Calvin of Geneva wrote against Pope Gregory VII by name. He said Gregory, once called Hildebrand, was the one who first won the popes the rank of emperors. He called him 'an impure and wicked man'. The popes after him, Calvin wrote, went on to rule the emperors.
  • Schwenckfelders Documented — Heinrich Bullinger wrote the Second Helvetic Confession in 1566. It rejects the subtle arguments of Schwenckfeld by name. Its Latin adds, "we are not Schwenckfeldians." It also denies that Christ's body was made divine and lost in God.
  • Anti-Trinitarian Currents (Servetus; Polish Brethren/Socinians; Transylvanian Unitarians) Documented Major — In 1546 Calvin wrote to Farel that if Servetus came to Geneva, he would not leave alive. In August 1553 he hoped for a death sentence. On 27 October the city council took advice from Bern, Basel, Zurich and Schaffhausen, then had Servetus burned alive.Lasting result: In 1553 Geneva's council judged Servetus guilty of great errors and blasphemies. It had him and his books burned.

Opposed By

  • Lutheran Wittenberg & Its Congregations Documented — The Lutheran Formula of Concord of 1577 has a chapter on the Lord's Supper. It names the 'Zwinglian teachers' and says they broke away when the Augsburg Confession was first read out. The chapter then condemns the 'Sacramentarians'.
  • Orthodoxy under the Ottomans - Parish, Millet & Neomartyrs Documented Major — Two synods of the Greek church condemned the Confession printed in Cyril Lucaris's name. The first condemned him for saying the whole Eastern Church thought as Calvin did. The second met under Patriarch Parthenius. It condemned 'Calvin's heresy' and every part of the book.Lasting result: Two synods of the Greek church condemned the book in Lucaris's name as Calvin's teaching and put it under a curse.
  • Orthodoxy under the Ottomans — the Second Act Documented Major — In 1672 the Synod of Jerusalem met under Patriarch Dositheus 'against the Calvinists'. Its acts said that as long as they followed Calvin as their leader, they could never share the faith of the Eastern Church.Lasting result: In 1672 the Synod of Jerusalem met against the Calvinists and ruled that their faith could not be the faith of the Eastern Church.

In Contact With

  • Lutheran Wittenberg & Its Congregations Documented — Zwingli made the long journey to Marburg to meet Luther and Melanchthon and talk about the Lord's Supper. Two Zurich writers tell of it: Zwingli himself, who in 1530 recalled the sermon he preached there, and his friend Myconius.
  • The Remonstrants and Dort Documented — In 1618 the Dutch government asked Zurich, Bern, Basel, Schaffhausen and Geneva to send men to the Synod of Dort. Geneva sent two, John Diodati and Theodore Tronchin, and the Swiss sent five. Speaking for Geneva, Diodati said the Remonstrants should go.

Sources

  • The Zurich leg: Zwingli's writings (Sixty-Seven Articles 1523; On True and False Religion 1525; LCC Zwingli and Bullinger), disputation records, the Prophezei and Zurich Bible (1531) (primary)
  • Calvin's corpus: Institutes (1559; trans. Battles, ed. McNeill, LCC 1960), Ecclesiastical Ordinances (1541), Geneva Catechism, sermons and letters (primary) — Teaching-and-order layer, kept distinct from the discipline texture.
  • Registers of the Consistory of Geneva in the Time of Calvin, vol. 1: 1542-1544 (ed. Kingdon, Lambert & Watt, trans. McDonald, Eerdmans, 2000) (primary) — Minutes kept by the very body doing the disciplining — an institutional record, not a neutral one.
  • The Genevan Psalter as lived piety: Marot and Beze metrical psalms (complete 1562), La Forme des prieres (1542), psalm-singing practice (primary) — The movement's ordinary devotional voice.
  • Scott Manetsch, Calvin's Company of Pastors (OUP, 2013), with Bruce Gordon, Calvin (Yale, 2009) (secondary) — Current standards, the first built on the registers.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 6, ch. 6 (Kingdon, Calvin/Geneva), with ch. 5 (Blickle) and ch. 7 (Greengrass) (reference) — Reference author is also the registers' editor.
