The Carmelite Reform and the Spanish Mystics

Castile, 1562 to 1622 — a nun who rebuilt her order from a single small house, and the friar-poet who joined her, between them writing the era's deepest accounts of prayer.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:02

Read along

Teresa of Ávila had spent more than twenty years as a Carmelite nun in a large, comfortable, sociable convent when she concluded that the life she had promised was not the life she was living. In 1562 she founded a small house, St Joseph's in Ávila, under a stricter rule — poverty, enclosure, few sisters, long hours of silent prayer. And spent the rest of her life traveling Spain founding others, arguing with officials, and writing books about prayer she had never intended to publish. In 1568 a young friar, John of the Cross, joined the reform on the men's side and became its greatest poet. The Inquisition examined Teresa's account of her life, and both were opposed from inside their own order. John spent nine months imprisoned by his own brethren. Which is when he wrote some of his finest verse.

Documented Stories

A Princess Denounces a Book

Teresa of Ávila wrote the book she called simply the Book of her Life at her confessors' order, finishing it in 1565, and it circulated in copies among the friends of the reform. One copy went to Ana de Mendoza, Princess of Eboli, whose convent at Pastrana Teresa had founded in 1569 and abandoned in 1574, when the widowed princess moved into it with her servants and made the nuns' life impossible. The princess, who had read the Life aloud to her household as entertainment, denounced it to the Inquisition of Toledo as a book of visions like those of the Franciscan nun Magdalena de la Cruz, exposed as a fraud in 1546. The Inquisition took the manuscript and gave it to the Dominican Domingo Báñez, Teresa's confessor and a professor at Salamanca, to examine. Báñez's report of 7 July 1575 survives in his hand: the book is orthodox and its author sincere; it contains things that would be dangerous in the hands of the ignorant and should not be printed; and it should be shown to no one, but kept. Teresa never saw the manuscript again. It stayed in the Inquisition's keeping until after her death in 1582, when the Empress María, the king's sister, obtained its release; Ana de Jesús had it printed by Fray Luis de León at Salamanca in 1588. The autograph is in the Escorial. In 1576 Teresa herself was denounced at Seville, examined, and cleared.

Nine Months in a Cupboard at Toledo

On the night of 2 December 1577 friars of the unreformed Carmelite observance broke into the chaplain's house at the Convent of the Incarnation in Ávila, where John of the Cross had been confessor to Teresa's nuns for five years, and carried him off blindfolded to their priory in Toledo. The order's general chapter had ruled that the Discalced houses founded without its permission were to be closed and their friars punished as rebels; John, who refused to renounce the reform, was declared contumacious. He was put in a cell some six feet by ten that had been a cupboard, fed bread and water and sardines on the floor of the refectory, and flogged there by the community on Fridays, each friar striking in turn; the scars were seen when he died. Teresa wrote to Philip II on 4 December: ‘I would rather he were among the Moors, who would perhaps show more pity.’ Nothing came of it. In the cell John composed, from memory, the first thirty-one stanzas of the Spiritual Canticle and the poem that begins, ‘I know well the spring that flows and runs, though it is night’; a kinder gaoler gave him paper in the spring. In mid-August 1578, having worked the screws of the lock loose, he let himself down from a window on strips of blanket and found his way to the Discalced nuns of Toledo, who hid him in their church and had him recite what he had written.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced The Carmelites Documented — Teresa of Ávila wrote that her reformed nuns kept 'the rule of our Lady of Carmel, not the rule of the Mitigation'. She meant the rule as it was revised under Pope Innocent IV. Her editor dates his approval to 1247, though she gives 1248.
  • influenced The Tridentine Church Documented — Teresa of Ávila wrote that before each new house she first sought the local bishop's consent, 'according to the decree of the council'. She also tells how Trent's decree on hermits made the hermit Mariano want to appeal to Rome. After she showed him her rule, he joined her reform.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

After more than twenty years in a large, comfortable convent, Teresa of Ávila concluded she wasn't living the life she had promised, and in 1562 founded a small house under a stricter rule of poverty, enclosure and long silent prayer. She wrote books about prayer she never intended to publish, and the friar who joined her reform, John of the Cross, wrote some of his finest poetry during nine months imprisoned by his own brethren.

Legacy

The Discalced Carmelites are still a worldwide order of friars and nuns. Teresa's and John of the Cross's writings became lasting classics of Christian prayer, translated and read far beyond Spain. Both were later named Doctors of the Church. Teresa, named in 1970, was the first woman to hold that title. Their words passed into ordinary English: the 'dark night of the soul', the 'interior castle'.
