Latin American Catholicism in the Twentieth Century

About This Movement

In Mexico the Calles Law took effect on 31 July 1926. That day the bishops suspended public worship. Catholics of the National League for the Defense of Religious Liberty, founded in March 1925, took up arms. So did rural fighters called cristeros. The settlement of June 1929, the arreglos, brought an amnesty and the non-enforcement of the law. Jean Meyer told the war's history in the three volumes of La Cristiada (1973-74). On 21 May 2000 John Paul II canonized 25 of its martyrs, Cristóbal Magallanes and his companions. From 25 July to 4 August 1955 Latin America's bishops held their first General Conference, at Rio de Janeiro. There they set up CELAM, their continental council, under Pius XII. Its seat was in Bogotá. Its first president was Cardinal Jaime de Barros Câmara. The second General Conference met at Medellín, Colombia, in August and September 1968. Its document read the church's task in Latin America in the light of the Council. One chapter was titled 'Poverty of the Church'. The third General Conference met at Puebla, Mexico, in 1979. Leonidas Proaño was bishop of Riobamba, Ecuador, from 1954 to 1985. He came to Riobamba on 29 May 1954 and retired on 29 January 1985. Under him, projects for Indigenous people opened in Chimborazo province. One was the Centro de Estudios y Acción Social ('Center for Social Studies and Action'), in 1960. The Escuelas Radiofónicas Populares del Ecuador ('Popular Radio Schools of Ecuador') began on 19 March 1962. Latin American scholars began to write this church's history themselves. One group of them, CEHILA, was founded in 1973 under Enrique Dussel. From 1981 it published a general history of the church in Latin America.

In Its Own Record

Gave Rise To

  • Latin American Base Communities Dominant Modern Reconstruction Major — At Medellín in 1968, Latin America's Catholic bishops called the base Christian community the first and basic cell of the church. A modern study reads this as their choice to build church life up from these communities.

Continues

  • Nineteenth-Century Latin American Catholicism Widely Accepted Major — In 1999 Pope John Paul II recalled the bishops' council held in Rome in 1899. Since then, he said, the church in Latin America had kept growing. He named its bishops' meetings at Rio de Janeiro in 1955 and Medellín in 1968.

Influenced By

  • The Catholic Church — Pius X to the Eve of the Council Widely Accepted — In 1955 Latin America's bishops met at Rio de Janeiro and asked for a council of their own. Pope Pius XII set it up that year as CELAM, to help the bishops in their work.
  • The Post-Vatican-II Parish Tradition Widely Accepted — In August 1968 Pope Paul VI opened the Latin American bishops' meeting at Bogotá. He pointed them to the council's teaching in Gaudium et Spes. He asked them to give priests' councils and pastoral councils the shape the council wanted.
