Moses the Black

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Desert Monasticism.

Known for
A house-servant driven out for immorality and robbery who became a monk at Scete in Egypt, a priest and a teacher of seventy disciples.

About This Person

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Around the year 400, the desert fathers of Egypt counted Moses among their great ones. He was a priest at Scete, out in the desert, and he left seventy disciples. Yet he began as a household slave, whom Palladius, a bishop writing around 420, calls an Ethiopian by race, and black. His master drove him out for immorality and robbery, and he was even said to have murdered. Palladius tells of these crimes to show what his repentance could do.

People said Moses went on to lead a band of robbers by the Nile. In time, Palladius says only, one of his adventures left him conscience-stricken, and he gave himself to a monastery.

Four robbers once attacked him in his cell, not knowing who he was. Moses tied them up, carried them on his back to the church, and asked the brothers, "I am not allowed to hurt anyone. So what do you tell me to do with these?" When the robbers learned who had caught them, they confessed. "If a man so great and powerful in robbery has feared God," they said, "why should we put off our salvation?" They left the world because of his conversion.

His own fight was long, and by his own account his old lust nearly made him give up. For six years he stood all night in his cell, but still he could not master it. In the end Isidore, the priest of Scete, told him his dreams had ceased and sent him to Communion. Two months later, Moses said he suffered nothing more.

Sayings gathered in the 600s remember the fathers of Scete scorning him. "Why does this Ethiopian come and go among us?" they said. He kept silent, and afterwards said he had been afraid but had not said a word. Once a bishop, wishing to test him, had the clergy drive him out of the sanctuary. Outside, he said to himself, "They have treated you rightly, you whose skin is dark and black. You shall not go back in as if you were a man."

Palladius says he died at Scete, aged seventy-five.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Palladius of Helenopolis says he left seventy disciples.
  • Sayings of an Abba Moses, some of them naming him the Ethiopian, are kept in the Syriac book of the desert fathers translated by Wallis Budge.

How they're remembered

  • Palladius of Helenopolis: "This was the manner of life of Moses the Ethiopian".
  • Sozomen: "he died at the age of seventy-five, leaving behind him numerous eminent disciples."

What Is Uncertain

  • The murders he was said to have done, and his leading a robber band, come to Palladius of Helenopolis as report ("they used to say"). The Lausiac History is a book of holy lives and tells of demons and wonders.
  • Sozomen tells much the same story in much the same order, so he likely depends on Palladius and is no separate witness. He calls Moses "an Egyptian", where Palladius says "an Ethiopian by race and black".
  • There were several monks named Moses. W. K. Lowther Clarke notes that Cuthbert Butler tells them apart, and takes the robber for the Moses of the sayings, but many sayings name only Abba Moses. John Cassian's Abbot Moses spent his boyhood in the Thebaid near Antony, and Edgar Gibson, Cassian's translator, suggests he may be Moses the Libyan.
  • A saying tells that barbarians came to Scete and killed Abba Moses and the brothers who stayed with him. It does not name him the Ethiopian. Palladius says only that he died at Scete at seventy-five.
  • No source used here names his master, his family, or the bishop who made him a priest.
