Moses the Black

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Desert Monasticism.

About This Person

They used to say that Moses the Black once swam the flooded Nile, his sword in his mouth, to kill a shepherd who had foiled him one night.

He lived among the desert fathers of Egypt in the late 300s. Bishop Palladius, his first biographer, calls him an Ethiopian by race, and black. A house-servant driven out by his master, he was said to have killed and led a band of robbers. One of his adventures left him conscience-stricken, and he gave himself to a monastery.

When four robbers attacked his cell, he tied them up and carried them to church. "I am not allowed to hurt anyone," he told the brothers. "So what do you tell me to do with these?" Learning that their captor was Moses, the famous robber, they confessed and became monks. "If a man so great and powerful in robbery has feared God," they said, "why should we put off our salvation?"

At Scete, a desert settlement of monks, he fought lust for years. He stood all night and secretly drew the older monks' water, until, Palladius says, a demon struck him down at the well. Isidore, Scete's priest, sent him to Communion, and two months later Moses said he suffered no more.

The monks' sayings remember contempt as well. The fathers at Scete asked why this Ethiopian came and went among them, and he kept silent. A bishop tested him by having the clergy drive him from the sanctuary: "Get outside, O Ethiopian." They had treated him rightly, Moses told himself. His skin was dark and black, and he must not go back as if he were a white man.

He became a priest, left seventy disciples, and died at Scete at seventy-five, Palladius says. An early saying, which calls him simply Abba Moses, tells more. When barbarians came, he told the brothers to flee and stayed himself. He had long waited, he said, for Christ's words to come true: "Those who take the sword shall perish by the sword." The brothers stayed too, and the barbarians killed them.

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Palladius of Helenopolis says he left seventy disciples.
  • Sayings of an Abba Moses, some of them naming him the Ethiopian, are kept in the Syriac book of the desert fathers translated by Wallis Budge.

How they're remembered

  • Palladius of Helenopolis: "This was the manner of life of Moses the Ethiopian".
  • Sozomen: "he died at the age of seventy-five, leaving behind him numerous eminent disciples."

What Is Uncertain

  • The murders he was said to have done, and his leading a robber band, come to Palladius of Helenopolis as report ("they used to say"). The Lausiac History is a book of holy lives and tells of demons and wonders.
  • Sozomen tells much the same story in much the same order, so he likely depends on Palladius and is no separate witness. He calls Moses "an Egyptian", where Palladius says "an Ethiopian by race and black".
  • There were several monks named Moses. W. K. Lowther Clarke notes that Cuthbert Butler tells them apart, and takes the robber for the Moses of the sayings, but many sayings name only Abba Moses. John Cassian's Abbot Moses spent his boyhood in the Thebaid near Antony, and Edgar Gibson, Cassian's translator, suggests he may be Moses the Libyan.
  • A saying tells that barbarians came to Scete and killed Abba Moses and the brothers who stayed with him. It does not name him the Ethiopian. Palladius says only that he died at Scete at seventy-five.
  • No source used here names his master, his family, or the bishop who made him a priest.
