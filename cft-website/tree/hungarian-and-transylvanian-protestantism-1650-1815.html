Hungarian and Transylvanian Protestantism, 1650 to 1815

About This Movement

Two sources tell this story. Bauhofer, a Protestant, writes of long suffering that ends in freedom. A Catholic work writes of a fight over churches and laws. This entry uses both.

In 1671 the bishop took the Protestant church of Kashaw, after breaking its doors. The government set up a special court at Presburg to try Protestants.

Forty-one prisoners were taken away. Only thirty entered the galleys at Naples, on 7 May. They were sold for fifty Spanish piasters apiece and chained to the benches. Ten others gave up their Protestant faith and joined the Church of Rome on 19 February 1676.

On 11 February 1676 the chaplain of the Dutch fleet came for the galley prisoners. They left their prison singing the 46th, the 114th and the 125th Psalms. Twenty-six of the thirty were still alive.

The Diet of 1681 ordered some churches returned to the Protestants. In 1687 Leopold said that by his own free favor he would keep the liberties granted in 1681. The Peace of Szathmar, signed on 10 May 1711, kept the laws of 1681 and 1687 in force.

On 21 March 1731 Charles VI gave rules the churches had long awaited. Bauhofer says their hopes were badly hurt. Private worship was allowed everywhere. Public worship was allowed only where the law of 1681 named a place.

The two churches did not always act together. In 1732 the Lutherans refused to join the Calvinists in a petition to the king. Soon after, both sent a joint statement to the court. Bauhofer reports a Count Pallfy as saying that the Protestants must have either God or the Devil for their friend. Each time their ruin seemed sure, some untoward event stopped it.

Joseph II's Edict of Toleration went to all the bishops of Hungary on 24 October 1781, in Bauhofer's account. The Catholic Encyclopedia says it was issued on 25 October. Where a hundred families could pay for it, they might build a church, a school and a manse. The churches could have neither bell nor spire, and no door onto the street.

The Diet of 1790 and 1791 gave the churches full freedom to run their own affairs, says the Catholic Encyclopedia. It counts about two million Protestants and five million Catholics in 1805.
