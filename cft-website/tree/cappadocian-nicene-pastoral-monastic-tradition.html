The Imperial Church Era · c. 325-394 CE · Cappadocia / Anatolia

Cappadocia · Anatolia, today in Turkey

Cappadocian Christianity

Meet Chilo on Church in Conversation

About This Movement

In Cappadocia and neighboring Pontus, from the years after the last empire-wide persecutions to the death of the last of a remarkable generation, one extended family and the friends around it did more than most to settle the words Christians still use about God — how settled that settlement really was is itself now a live scholarly question. Basil of Caesarea, his younger brother Gregory of Nyssa, their elder sister Macrina, and Basil's friend Gregory of Nazianzus argued the hardest question of the century — whether the Son and the Spirit are God in the same sense the Father is — while running dioceses, burying relatives, and feeding the hungry through famine.

Basil's own preaching indicted the region's grain-hoarders and moved them to open their stores. After the famine of 368, he built a complex outside Caesarea with a hospital, a hostel, and a workhouse. It was a genuine undertaking, with almost no precedent. But it was not the first of its kind anywhere, as later legend sometimes claims. Even before that, he had spent years answering a more radical ascetic ferment loose in the region. Households were dissolved, slaves were urged to leave their masters, and fasts were kept against the church's own feasts. He did not condemn the impulse. Instead, he gave it a rule, an obedience, and an open door for guests. Macrina turned the family estate into an ascetic household of her own. In her brother's account of her last days, she taught him theology from her deathbed. That account is the only record of her that survives; no words of her own reach us apart from his telling.

Gregory of Nazianzus carried the argument to Constantinople. There, in 381, the creed most Christians still say took its final shape. Those years were not always peaceful. This Christian tradition buried its own martyr, Eupsychius of Caesarea, who was put to death under Julian in 362. Under Valens, a hostile church party had Gregory of Nyssa thrown out of office. He lived in exile until 378. Epistle 38 is the letter most often used to show that the three men agreed on the technical terms. Yet even its author is in dispute. Modern scholars still argue over whether Basil or Gregory of Nyssa wrote it.

In Its Own Record

Influenced By

  • The Anatolian Church in the Third Century Documented — Writing to the Neocaesareans around 375, Basil says his grandmother Macrina, who came from their city, taught him the words of Gregory the Wonderworker. He offers this as proof that his faith is sound. In On the Holy Spirit he defends a form of doxology as one Gregory handed down.
  • Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition Documented — Socrates, a historian of the fifth century, says Basil and Gregory of Nazianzus studied Origen's works. They drew their reading of Scripture from them. Then they used Origen against the Arians. Around 382 Gregory sent a friend 'the volume of the Philocalia of Origen'. The editors of both men's letters credit that book to the two of them.

Influenced

  • Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity Documented — Jerome, writing in the 390s, calls Gregory of Nazianzus his teacher in the Scriptures. He recalls asking Gregory to explain a phrase in Luke. Gregory made a joke and got out of answering.
  • Armenian Christianity (conversion era) Widely Accepted — Moses of Khoren says the young Nerses was then a student at Caesarea in Cappadocia. Nerses later became head of the Armenian church. Ormanian, a modern historian of that church, says only Caesarea and Edessa had schools that Armenians could reach then. When Moses wrote is disputed. Scholars date him anywhere from the fifth to the eighth century.
  • The Church of Aquileia Documented — Gennadius of Marseilles wrote about Rufinus around 480. He calls him a priest of the church at Aquileia. Rufinus, he says, opened up much Greek writing to Latin readers. Among the works that Rufinus put into Latin, he lists books by Basil of Caesarea and by Gregory of Nazianzus as well.

Argued Against

  • Anomoean (Eunomian) Christianity Documented — After Basil died, Gregory of Nyssa took up his quarrel with Eunomius. He wrote a long book to answer him. The first of the Theological Orations of Gregory of Nazianzus is a talk against the Eunomians.
  • Pneumatomachian ('Macedonian') Christianity Documented — Basil wrote On the Holy Spirit. It answers those who say the Spirit may not be ranked with the Father and the Son. Gregory of Nyssa also wrote on the Spirit. His book is titled Against the Followers of Macedonius.
  • Apollinarian Christianity Documented — Gregory of Nazianzus wrote two letters to a priest, Cledonius, against the views of Apollinarius. In the first, he says Christ took on all of human nature. What He has not taken on, Gregory says, He has not healed. He also wrote to Nectarius, bishop of Constantinople, to warn him about a tract by Apollinarius.

Opposed By

  • Homoian ('Arian') Christianity Widely Accepted — Around 375 the emperor's vicar in Pontus ordered Gregory of Nyssa to stand trial. The charge was that he had mishandled church money. Basil then wrote that his foes 'have expelled my brother from Nyssa' and put a rival in his place. The editors of Gregory of Nazianzus's letters, in 1894, read those foes as the party of Valens, which opposed Nicaea.

In Contact With

  • Armenian Christianity (conversion era) Widely Accepted Major — A Life of Nerses says he was made head of his church at Caesarea, in Cappadocia. It says the Armenians went there for this until the Council of Chalcedon. Moses of Khoren says the king later put a new head in Nerses's place without the archbishop of Caesarea. But Malachia Ormanian, a historian of their church, says Caesarea never ruled over it.Lasting result: At Caesarea, many bishops met and made Nerses the head of the church of Great Armenia.
  • Imperial and Juridical Christianity Documented Major — Sozomen says Theodosius put Gregory of Nazianzus in charge of the churches of Constantinople. In July 381 the emperor made a law. It named the bishops whose communion marked a Catholic church. Helladius of Caesarea and Gregory of Nyssa were two of them. Socrates says the council of 381 gave these two men charge of the church in Pontus.Lasting result: A law of July 381 named Helladius of Caesarea, Gregory of Nyssa and others. It said all churches must go to bishops in communion with them.

Sources

  • Basil of Caesarea, Hexaemeron (primary) — Basil's nine surviving homilies on the six days of creation, preached to his congregation at Caesarea.
  • Gregory of Nazianzus, Five Theological Orations (primary) — Preached at Constantinople in 380, these carried the Trinitarian argument into the public square in the year before the creed took its final form.
  • Gregory of Nyssa, Against Eunomius (primary) — Gregory's twelve-book rebuttal — the fullest surviving statement of the Cappadocian case, and the route through which most of Eunomius's own words survive at all.
  • Eunomius, Apology, in Eunomius: The Extant Works, ed. and trans. Richard Paul Vaggione (primary) — Oxford, Clarendon Press, 1987 — the rare case of a condemned opponent's own writing surviving well enough to be read on its own terms, not only through the winning side's rebuttal.
  • The Canons of the Council of Gangra (primary) — Twenty canons censuring "those around Eustathius" — read against the grain, the best surviving evidence for what the radical ascetic ferment Basil answered actually did and taught.
  • Basil of Caesarea, the Eustathius-rupture letters (Epistles 119, 128, 130, 223, 226, 244, 251) (primary) — Basil's own account of his friendship, collaboration, and break with his one-time ascetic mentor — the wounded former disciple's side; Eustathius left no writing of his own to answer it.
  • Anthony Meredith, The Cappadocians (secondary) — St Vladimir's Seminary Press, 1995 — the first English-language study to treat Basil and both Gregorys together.
