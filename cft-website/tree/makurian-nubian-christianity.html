Makurian Nubian Christianity

Makuria on the middle Nile, 697 to 1054 — a Christian kingdom that kept a six-hundred-year treaty with Muslim Egypt and covered its cathedral walls with painted saints.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:11

Read along

Between Egypt and the deserts of the Sudan, the Christian kingdom of Makuria held together for centuries under an arrangement no one else had: the Baqt, a treaty struck with Muslim Egypt in 652 that set terms of exchange rather than conquest and was more or less honored for six hundred years. Under that peace, and especially after Merkurios — whom an Egyptian writer called a new Constantine — took the throne in 697, Nubia built. The cathedral at Faras was rebuilt in 707 and covered, over the following centuries, in wall paintings: enormous saints and archangels, nativities, and portraits of the bishops themselves, several shown with dark skin beside lighter-skinned holy figures. Nubian Christians wrote their own language, Old Nubian, in an alphabet adapted from Coptic. What they did not leave, or what has not survived, is much writing about their inner life; this is a Christianity known mainly by what it built and painted.

Documented Stories

King Kyriakos Marches North for the Patriarch

The History of the Patriarchs tells it in the life of Michael I, patriarch of Alexandria from 744, and its writer says he shared the patriarch’s prison. A new governor of Egypt, Abd al-Malik, son of Musa, son of Nasir, hated the Christians greatly, the History says, and caused them much trouble. He demanded money from the patriarch. Then he heard that letters had gone from Egypt to the king of the Nubians, and he seized Michael and held him. Kyriakos, king of Makuria, was ‘the Great King’, the History says, with thirteen kings under him. He marched north with a great army: a hundred thousand horsemen, with a hundred thousand horses and a hundred thousand camels. A man who saw them told the writer that their horses were small, no higher than asses, and fought with their hooves as their riders fought on their backs. The Nubians plundered the Muslims of Upper Egypt and came on toward Misr, camping at a pool still called the Pool of the Ethiopians. The king had sent his Eparch ahead to demand the patriarch’s release, and the governor jailed the envoy with Michael. Then, afraid and unable to resist, he freed the Eparch to talk the king into going home. The Eparch told Kyriakos that the patriarch had been released and kindly treated. Michael wrote letters of peace to the king, blessing him and his men, and the king went home without a battle. The chronicle presents it as the Nubian church’s loyalty to its mother church in action. Whether the campaign was really about Michael, or about the border and the Muslims’ raids for Nubian slaves, cannot now be separated. What the Coptic church recorded was that when its head was in chains, the only Christian power that could do anything about it was an African kingdom on the upper Nile, and that it did.

Prince Georgios Goes to Baghdad

Under the Baqt, the old agreement between Muslim Egypt and Christian Makuria, Nubia owed the caliph a yearly tribute of slaves. B. T. A. Evetts, in his notes to Abu Salih, gives it as 400 or 360. In the time of the patriarch Joseph, Abu Salih says, Ibrahim, brother of the caliph al-Ma’mun, wrote to King Zacharias of Nubia for the tribute owed for fourteen years. Eusèbe Renaudot, retelling the History of the Patriarchs, has the governor of Egypt write instead. The king could not pay. He feared, Renaudot says, that raiders would overrun his country if he left it, so he sent his son Georgios, his chosen heir. Georgios came to Egypt, where the patriarch Joseph received him kindly, and went on to the caliph at Baghdad. Abu Salih says Ibrahim rejoiced to see him. The king had sent the son he held dearest of all, and the son had gone into exile at his father’s word. In Renaudot the caliph is not named. He was so pleased by the prince’s respect that he remitted the arrears and sent him back to Egypt loaded with honors, gifts and fine robes. Georgios carried a gold cross in his hand, and everyone kissed it. In Egypt he asked the patriarch to consecrate a wooden altar for him, so that the liturgy could be said wherever the king’s camp moved. Bishops from Nubia traveled with him. When they said the liturgy, he had bells rung on the roof of the fortress where he lodged, to the great joy of the Christians. The episode is the one in which a Nubian Christian ruler is seen from outside as a diplomat rather than a raider, and in which the terms under which a Christian kingdom lived beside the caliphate were settled face to face by the people who had to keep them.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Unified Christian Nubia after Merkurios (crowned 697, the 'New Constantine'); Faras cathedral rebuilt 707; the fresco golden age flourished under the peace secured by the 652 Baqt treaty.

Descended from

  • continues Nubian Christianity Widely Accepted Major — John of Biclaro says the Maccurritae took up the Christian faith in the third year of Justin II, about 569; whether he means the Makurians is not settled. Al-Baladhuri shows the Nubians still bound by their treaty in the 770s and 780s, under the caliph al-Mahdi. Modern histories treat Christian Nubia as one church that ran on through these centuries.

Direct descendants

  • continued as Terminal Christian Nubia (Late Makuria and Dotawo) Documented Major — Abu Salih, writing in Egypt around 1200, calls Dongola the seat of the Nubian king, a city with many churches. He says King Raphael built its domed palace in 1002. He also tells of a later king, Solomon, who gave up his throne and died in Egypt after 1078.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced Coptic Christianity under Early Islam Inferential-Thin — Old Nubian was written in the Coptic alphabet, with a few extra letters for Nubian sounds. After the Arab conquest of Egypt, says the scholar F. Ll. Griffith, Coptic monks and Coptic books abounded in Nubia. This rests on Griffith's one account, from 1913.

In Contact With

  • in contact with Coptic Christianity under Early Islam Documented Major — A Coptic history of the 700s describes the king of Makuria in Nubia. It says the patriarch in Egypt ordained his chief bishop, who then ordained the bishops and priests of the land. Whenever a chief bishop died, it says, the patriarch of Alexandria chose and ordained the next.Lasting result: In the mid-700s the patriarch in Egypt chose and ordained each chief bishop of Makuria.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

The Christian kingdom of Makuria held together for centuries under the Baqt, a treaty struck with Muslim Egypt in 652 that was honored for roughly six hundred years, and after Merkurios — called a new Constantine by an Egyptian writer — took the throne in 697, Nubia rebuilt its cathedral at Faras and covered its walls with paintings of saints, archangels and the bishops themselves. Nubian Christians wrote their own language, Old Nubian, in a script adapted from Coptic, though this is a Christianity known mainly by what it built and painted rather than by what it said about itself.

Legacy

Christian Nubia lasted far longer than most people expect, into the fifteenth century in places, before it was worn down and its churches vanished; no Nubian Christian community survives today. What survives is the painting. When the Aswan High Dam was built in the 1960s the cathedral at Faras was about to go under the reservoir, and a Polish archaeological team lifted the murals off its walls. They were divided between Warsaw and Khartoum, and the Warsaw gallery is arranged to reproduce the layout of the church they came from — the only permanent exhibition of medieval Nubian painting in Europe.

Experience It Today

  • The Faras Gallery, National Museum in Warsaw — the wall paintings rescued from the cathedral at Faras, hung in the arrangement they had in the church
