The Holiness Movement

North America and Britain, 1835 to 1906 — a movement launched from a New York parlor, teaching that God could make an ordinary believer holy now, and asking them to say so out loud.

Nineteenth-Century Methodism→The Holiness Movement→Azusa Street & Early Pentecostalism

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:13

Read along

Methodism had always taught that God could make a believer actually holy in this life, and in the 1830s a New York doctor's wife named Phoebe Palmer took that teaching, simplified it into a step anyone could take at once, and began hosting a Tuesday afternoon meeting in her parlor that ran for decades and drew bishops as well as neighbors. From that parlor the Holiness movement spread through camp meetings organized from 1867, through the Guide to Holiness and a flood of cheap devotional print, and across the Atlantic, where it took a quieter and more Anglican form at the Keswick Convention from 1875. Because sanctification had to be testified to out loud, the movement's native literature is ordinary people's accounts of their own experience rather than the writings of its leaders. Women were prominent throughout, and its single most remarkable voice belongs to Amanda Berry Smith, born enslaved, who preached in England, India and West Africa and wrote her own life.

Documented Stories

Hamilton, October 1857

Phoebe Palmer and her husband Walter, a New York physician, had been traveling for twenty years teaching what she called the shorter way to holiness: that a believer who laid everything on the altar could claim entire sanctification at once. In October 1857, returning from a camp meeting in Canada, they missed their boat at Hamilton, Canada West, and stayed over; Methodists there asked her to speak at a prayer meeting, and she agreed on condition that those present go out and bring in the unconverted. Twenty-one professed conversion the first evening. The meetings went on daily; her letters, printed in the New York Christian Advocate and Journal, reported three hundred, then four hundred, then more, and told readers that the same thing could happen in their own churches if laypeople would work for it. They were read in a city that had just entered a financial panic. In September Jeremiah Lanphier, a lay missionary of the Dutch Reformed North Church on Fulton Street in New York, had begun a noon prayer meeting for businessmen; six came the first week. Through the winter, as the Hamilton reports and others like them circulated, the meetings filled churches, theaters and halls across the city and the northern states, without preachers, with laymen praying and five-minute testimonies. Palmer's own account gave the credit to the laity and the Spirit; her biographers have called her the mother of the revival of 1858. She went on to England the following year.

Brighton, and the Fall of Robert Pearsall Smith

Robert Pearsall Smith and his wife Hannah Whitall Smith, Philadelphia Quakers turned Holiness teachers, had come to England in 1873 and within two years had made the doctrine of a second blessing the talk of evangelical Britain and the Continent. The Brighton Convention of 29 May to 7 June 1875 drew some eight thousand, and Robert preached to them on the life of full consecration. Hannah's book The Christian's Secret of a Happy Life appeared that year and never went out of print. Weeks after Brighton the organizing committee learned that Robert had, in a private interview with a young woman seeking counsel, taught a doctrine of physical thrills accompanying the baptism of the Spirit and had behaved toward her in a way the committee would only call unwise and unscriptural. He was asked to cease all public work at once and returned to America; in December the committee published a brief notice that he had withdrawn owing to a doctrinal error and conduct they did not specify, and the religious press speculated for months. He never preached again and lost his faith. Hannah, who had known the pattern from before, stayed in England, raised the children, wrote, and became a suffragist; the movement's British wing regrouped in the same month at a small convention in the Lake District, at Keswick, planned before the fall and held on 28 June, which went on every year and kept the doctrine and dropped the man.

In Its Own Record

Grew out of Methodism's teaching on Christian perfection and became the immediate parent of Pentecostalism. The Keswick Convention, begun in 1875, carried the same current in a quieter, more Anglican form in Britain.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

In the 1830s, a New York doctor's wife named Phoebe Palmer simplified Methodist teaching on holiness into a step any believer could take at once, and her Tuesday afternoon parlor meeting ran for decades. Because believers were expected to testify to their own sanctification out loud, the movement's native literature is ordinary people's own accounts of what happened to them — none more striking than Amanda Berry Smith, born enslaved, who preached across England, India, and West Africa.

Legacy

It became a family of ongoing denominations — the Church of the Nazarene, the Wesleyan Church, the Free Methodist Church, the Church of God (Anderson) and others. And it is the immediate parent of Pentecostalism. The leaders, the vocabulary and the expectation of a second, transforming work of the Spirit all came out of Holiness circles into the Azusa Street revival of 1906. Through Keswick it also shaped a whole strand of evangelical devotional language, the deeper life and the surrendered will, that is still in use by people who have never heard the movement's name.

Experience It Today

  • The Keswick Convention, Cumbria — the Lake District gathering founded in 1875, still held every summer

How Sure the Record Is

RICH, testimony-native, women-prominent (Phoebe Palmer's parlor as a movement's engine)

  • Phoebe Palmer: The Way of Holiness (1843), Entire Devotion to God (1845), Promise of the Father (1859); the Tuesday Meeting records (from 1835); her Guide to Holiness editorship (primary) — Palmer's own writings, including a book-length defense of women's ministry.
  • The testimony literature: Guide to Holiness (1839-); love-feast and camp-meeting testimonies; National Camp Meeting Association records (Vineland 1867, Manheim 1868) (primary) — Testimony was this movement's characteristic genre: ordinary believers testifying to their own experience of sanctification. Some of these editions and archives are not easy to access.
  • Amanda Berry Smith, An Autobiography: The Story of the Lord's Dealings with Mrs. Amanda Smith, the Colored Evangelist (1893) (primary) — A formerly enslaved woman's own account of sanctification, transatlantic evangelism, and the racism she met inside the movement - one of the strongest firsthand voices from inside it.
  • The transatlantic/Keswick wing: William Arthur, The Tongue of Fire (1856); Hannah Whitall Smith, The Christian's Secret of a Happy Life (1875) and letters; Keswick Convention records (1875-) (primary) — This covers the movement's British, Keswick wing. Robert Pearsall Smith's public scandal and withdrawal from ministry is part of that record and worth noting when weighing these sources.
  • Melvin Dieter, The Holiness Revival of the Nineteenth Century (1980; 2nd ed. 1996), with Vinson Synan, The Holiness-Pentecostal Tradition (Eerdmans, 2nd ed. 1997) (secondary)
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 8, 'The growth of voluntary religion' (Bebbington) with 'Women preachers and the new orders' (Holmes & O'Brien); ch. 22 (Noll) beside them (reference) — Exact chapter numbers for the Bebbington and Holmes & O'Brien chapters could not be confirmed; the Noll chapter is chapter 22.
