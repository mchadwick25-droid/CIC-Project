The Early Church Era · 251 · Carthage and Rome

Carthage, today in Tunisia · Rome, today in Italy

Councils of Carthage and Rome on the lapsed

Major council

Known for
Two councils of 251, at Carthage under Cyprian and at Rome under Cornelius, that agreed how to receive back Christians who had given way in the Decian persecution, and cut off Novatian's stricter party.
Dates
251. Carthage probably in May and Rome probably in October, both by Charles Joseph Hefele's dating; no held source of the time gives the month
Rivers
Latin West, Africa
Called by
Cyprian, bishop of Carthage, for the African bishops; Cornelius, bishop of Rome, for the bishops of Italy

About This Council

In the spring of 251, Cyprian, bishop of Carthage, came home after more than a year away. The persecution under the emperor Decius was over. Many Christians had given way under it. Some had offered sacrifice to the gods. Others had paid for a certificate so they would not have to. Now many wanted to come back to the church.

Cyprian had refused to settle their case alone. He would wait, he wrote, until the bishops could "assemble into one place." The clergy of Rome, who had no bishop at the time, agreed. Cyprian says their letter was written by one of their presbyters, Novatian.

The bishops of Africa met at Carthage, probably in May, by the dating of Charles Joseph Hefele, the historian of the councils. The council's own record is lost. What it decided reaches us through Cyprian's letters, and Cyprian called the meeting and defended it. He writes that Scripture was brought forward on both sides. The bishops chose a middle way. Hope was not to be "wholly denied to the lapsed," but their penance would be long, and each case would be examined. Those with certificates could then be received. Those who had sacrificed would be given peace when they were dying.

Rome had split. Cornelius was chosen bishop, and a stricter party set up Novatian against him. The Africans sent their decisions to Cornelius, and he held his own council. Eusebius of Caesarea, drawing on letters of Cornelius that survive only through him, says sixty bishops came. They declared Novatian and his followers "strangers" to the church. Those who had fallen were to be healed "with the medicines of repentance."

Novatian's side reaches us mostly through his opponents. Socrates of Constantinople, writing almost two centuries later, gives his rule. The churches "should not admit to the sacred mysteries those who had sacrificed," but urge them to repent and leave their pardon to God.

The quarrel reached the East. Dionysius of Alexandria wrote to Novatian that "it were better to suffer everything, rather than divide the Church of God." In Antioch, Eusebius says, Bishop Fabius seemed to lean toward Novatian.

The next year, by Hefele's dating, a new persecution seemed near. The African bishops went further and offered peace to all who had kept on repenting. Novatian's church lived on, and in Socrates's day it still kept its strict rule in Phrygia and Paphlagonia.

Why They Met

  • The persecution under the emperor Decius had ended. Many Christians had sacrificed to the gods, and others had paid for certificates saying they had. Now many asked to be taken back (Cyprian, Letter to Antonianus).
  • The question was whether the church could receive them, and on what terms. Some Christians were pressing to receive communion at once. Cyprian refused to decide until the bishops could meet (Cyprian, Letter to Antonianus).
  • At Carthage, the deacon Felicissimus and five presbyters had broken with Cyprian. Charles Joseph Hefele, the historian of the councils, says the party took up the charge that the bishop was too strict with the lapsed.
  • At Rome, the church had two bishops. Cornelius would receive the lapsed after penance. A stricter party set up Novatian, who would not admit those who had sacrificed (Socrates of Constantinople; Cornelius's letter in Eusebius of Caesarea).

Who Came

  • At Carthage, "a large number of bishops," Cyprian writes; Hefele adds some presbyters and deacons. No list of their names is held.
  • At Rome, sixty bishops and a great many more presbyters and deacons, by Eusebius of Caesarea's account of Cornelius's letters. Bishops of other provinces, he says, gave their views where they were.

What They Decided

  • Hope of peace was not to be denied to the lapsed, but their penance would be long and each case examined (Carthage).
  • Those who had taken certificates could be received after their case was examined; those who had sacrificed would be given peace at the point of death (Carthage).
  • The deacon Felicissimus and five presbyters who had opposed Cyprian were excommunicated (Carthage, by Cyprian's later letter to Cornelius).
  • Rome agreed with Carthage. Novatian and those who held with him were declared strangers to the church, and the fallen were to be healed with the medicines of repentance (Rome).

What Came of It

  • The next year, by Hefele's dating, the African bishops offered peace to all the lapsed who had kept on repenting, because a new persecution seemed near.
  • Novatian's church lived on. Socrates of Constantinople says that in his day it still kept its strict rule in Phrygia and Paphlagonia.

Traditions It Touched

Gathered

  • Cyprian of Carthage: The African bishops met at Carthage under Cyprian.
  • The Roman Church in the Third Century: Cornelius held the second council at Rome and agreed with Carthage.

Condemned

  • Novatianism: The Rome council declared Novatian and his followers strangers to the church.

Who

  • Cyprian of Carthage · Called the Carthage council and wrote the account of it
  • Cornelius of RomeTold in a story · Called the Rome council
  • Novatian · Rival bishop at Rome; he and his followers were condemned at Rome
  • Dionysius of Alexandria · Not present; wrote to Novatian urging him to step down

Whose Account We Have

  • The council's book of decisions (acts): Cyprian says the heads of the decisions were collected in a little book. It is lost. Henri Leclercq, in his edition of Hefele: nothing of this council remains.
  • Cyprian, Letter to Antonianus (participant): Written by the bishop who called the Carthage council, to defend it and himself against Novatian's letters. On Cyprian's side.
  • Eusebius of Caesarea, Church History VI.43 to 46 (later narrative): Written later, from letters of Cornelius and Dionysius of Alexandria, which Eusebius quotes or summarizes and which survive only through him. Cornelius wrote as Novatian's enemy.
  • Socrates of Constantinople, Church History IV.28 (later narrative): Written by about 439. The fullest statement of Novatian's own rule, which he reports from Novatian's letters. A. C. Zenos, who edited him in English, finds him favorable to the Novatians.

What Is Uncertain

  • The council's own record is lost. What Carthage decided reaches us through Cyprian, who called it and defended it. What Rome decided reaches us through Eusebius, who summarizes Cornelius's letters.
  • Novatian's view reaches us through his opponents, and through Socrates of Constantinople almost two centuries later.
  • The months are Hefele's reconstruction. No held source of the time names them.
  • Hefele says some older editors and historians turned these two councils into four. He also doubts a council at Antioch on the Novatians that the Libellus Synodicus reports. Eusebius says Dionysius was invited to a synod at Antioch where some were trying to strengthen Novatian's party; Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius, thinks it probably met, while Hefele and others doubt it.
  • Whether the presbyter Novatus of Carthage was one of the five presbyters cut off with Felicissimus is disputed. Hefele says some assert it and others deny it. Leclercq, following Paul Monceaux, says he was.
