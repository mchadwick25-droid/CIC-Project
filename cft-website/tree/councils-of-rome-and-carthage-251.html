Councils of Carthage and Rome on the lapsed

Major council

Bishops at Carthage and then at Rome agreed that Christians who had given way under the emperor Decius could come back to the church after long penance. The bishops at Rome put Novatian, a rival bishop, and his followers outside the church.

About This Council

Audio Brief 4:26

Read along

In 250 the emperor Decius ordered everyone to sacrifice to the gods before the officials. At Carthage, their bishop Cyprian wrote later, many Christians did not wait for arrest but ran to the forum, the main square where the officials sat. As evening came and officials put some off, they begged not to be.

When the persecution ended, many of the lapsed, those who had given way, asked to come back. In 251, councils at Carthage, in what is now Tunisia, and then at Rome chose a middle way. The fallen could return after long penance, a time of public sorrow and prayer, with each case examined. A stricter party refused them all and set up Novatian as a rival bishop of Rome, whose church lasted for centuries.

Some had sacrificed, while others had bought a certificate, a paper from an official saying they had. Their hands were clean, Cyprian held, their consciences stained.

Honored voices pressed to take them back at once. Confessors, jailed or tortured for the faith without giving way, were handing out written pardons granting peace, the church's word for sharing the Lord's Supper again. Cyprian says thousands went out a day, none examined. A deacon, Felicissimus, and five elders offered peace without penance: "another persecution", Cyprian called it.

Cyprian, leading from hiding by letter, would not decide alone. The bishops must first meet and fix a sentence "tempered with discipline and mercy". Meanwhile the dying who repented would receive peace, and Rome's clergy agreed.

The African bishops met at Carthage, while Rome already had two bishops, Cornelius and Novatian. Cyprian, who called the council and defended it, is our only witness. "We weighed out a balance, with healing moderation," he wrote. "Hope of communion and peace was not to be wholly denied to the lapsed, so that despair would not make them fall away further." Yet none would rush straight back, and penance would be long. "And the cases, wishes and needs of each person would be examined."

Certificate holders were taken back case by case, and the sacrificed received peace at death, but those who first asked on their sickbed, never having repented, were refused. Felicissimus and the five elders were cut off from the church's fellowship. The council's own book is lost.

Socrates, writing two centuries later and kind to Novatian's church, reports his rule: "They should not admit those who had sacrificed to the sacred rites. They should urge them to repent, but leave their pardon to God, who is able, and has the power, to forgive sins." The sacred rites meant the Lord's Supper. Socrates says Novatian broke away because Cornelius took back those who had sacrificed, but Cyprian calls that a false rumor.

Later that year sixty bishops under Cornelius at Rome, by Eusebius's account of his letters, agreed with Carthage. Novatian's followers were "strangers", outsiders, and the fallen would be healed by penance, "with the medicines of repentance".

Novatian's church kept its rule: in Socrates's day, Phrygia and Paphlagonia held it "to the present day". At Nicaea in 325, its clergy who came over had to share communion with the lapsed who did penance.

In 252, by a historian's reckoning, with a new persecution expected, the African bishops gave peace to all who had kept repenting, so they could stand firm. "In this time we give peace not to those who sleep, but to those who keep watch. We give peace not for luxury, but for arms. We give peace not for rest, but for the battle line."

People and Stories

Who Came

  • At Carthage, "a large number of bishops," Cyprian writes; Hefele adds some presbyters and deacons. No list of their names is held.
  • At Rome, sixty bishops and a great many more presbyters and deacons, by Eusebius of Caesarea's account of Cornelius's letters. Bishops of other provinces, he says, gave their views where they were.

Who

  • Cyprian of Carthage · Called the Carthage council and wrote the account of it
  • Cornelius of RomeTold in a story · Called the Rome council
  • Novatian · Rival bishop at Rome; he and his followers were condemned at Rome
  • Dionysius of Alexandria · Not present; wrote to Novatian urging him to step down

What Happened

Called by

Cyprian, bishop of Carthage, for the African bishops; Cornelius, bishop of Rome, for the bishops of Italy

Why They Met

  • The persecution under the emperor Decius had ended. Many Christians had sacrificed to the gods, and others had paid for certificates saying they had. Now many asked to be taken back (Cyprian, Letter to Antonianus).
  • The question was whether the church could receive them, and on what terms. Some Christians were pressing to receive communion at once. Cyprian refused to decide until the bishops could meet (Cyprian, Letter to Antonianus).
  • At Carthage, the deacon Felicissimus and five presbyters had broken with Cyprian. Charles Joseph Hefele, the historian of the councils, says the party took up the charge that the bishop was too strict with the lapsed.
  • At Rome, the church had two bishops. Cornelius would receive the lapsed after penance. A stricter party set up Novatian, who would not admit those who had sacrificed (Socrates of Constantinople; Cornelius's letter in Eusebius of Caesarea).
  • Confessors, Christians jailed or tortured for the faith, were handing out written pardons. Cyprian says that in 250 thousands of these papers were being given each day, with no one's case examined. The confessors' own short letter to him grants peace to all whose conduct since their fall satisfied him (Cyprian, Letter to the clergy at Rome; Letter of the confessors to Cyprian).
  • A holder of a certificate, Cyprian says, might plead that he never sacrificed: he had gone to the magistrate, or sent someone, to say he was a Christian and could not sacrifice, and had paid a price so as not to. Cyprian held that his hand might be clean but his conscience was stained (Cyprian, Letter to Antonianus).
  • Cyprian had agreed with the confessors, the clergy at Rome and the bishops of Africa and overseas that nothing new would be done about the lapsed until all could meet and fix a sentence tempered alike with discipline and with mercy. He warned that anyone who gave communion to the lapsed before then would himself be kept from communion. The Roman clergy's letter, written by Novatian, added that peace should be granted to the sick lapsed at the point of death (Cyprian, Letter to the people of Carthage; Letter to Antonianus).
  • Novatian sent envoys to Carthage. Cyprian and the several bishops who had come together to him held that they must be kept from communion (Cyprian, Letter to Cornelius).

What They Decided

  • Hope of peace was not to be denied to the lapsed, but their penance would be long and each case examined (Carthage).
  • Those who had taken certificates could be received after their case was examined; those who had sacrificed would be given peace at the point of death (Carthage).
  • The deacon Felicissimus and five presbyters who had opposed Cyprian were excommunicated (Carthage, by Cyprian's later letter to Cornelius).
  • Rome agreed with Carthage. Novatian and those who held with him were declared strangers to the church, and the fallen were to be healed with the medicines of repentance (Rome).
  • Cyprian gives two reasons for the middle way: that the lapsed, shut out of the church, not fall further in despair and live as pagans; and that the discipline of the gospel not be loosened so that they rushed into communion (Carthage, by Cyprian's Letter to Antonianus).
  • Cyprian gives the reason for peace at death: there is no confession in the place of the departed (Carthage, by Cyprian's Letter to Antonianus).
  • Those who did not repent, and began to ask for communion and peace only when sickness and danger came, were refused. Cyprian says it was the warning of death, not repentance, that drove them to ask (Carthage, by Cyprian's Letter to Antonianus).

Legacy and Today

What Came of It

  • The next year, by Hefele's dating, the African bishops offered peace to all the lapsed who had kept on repenting, because a new persecution seemed near.
  • Novatian's church lived on. Socrates of Constantinople says that in his day it still kept its strict rule in Phrygia and Paphlagonia.
  • Socrates of Constantinople says Novatian's rule seemed to some a cruel and merciless course, and to others just and a help to discipline.
  • At Nicaea in 325, the bishops set terms for clergy of those who call themselves Cathari if they came over: they were to agree to communicate with those who had lapsed in persecution and had a period of penance laid on them (Council of Nicaea, canon 8).
  • By Hefele's account, Novatian's party at Carthage then put a bishop, Maximus, at its head, and the lax party was given the presbyter Fortunatus as its bishop. Cyprian, who is our witness, calls Fortunatus a false bishop set up by a few (Cyprian, Letter to Cornelius on Fortunatus and Felicissimus).

Open Questions

What Is Uncertain

  • The council's own record is lost. What Carthage decided reaches us through Cyprian, who called it and defended it. What Rome decided reaches us through Eusebius, who summarizes Cornelius's letters.
  • Novatian's view reaches us through his opponents, and through Socrates of Constantinople almost two centuries later.
  • The months are Hefele's reconstruction. No held source of the time names them.
  • Hefele says some older editors and historians turned these two councils into four. He also doubts a council at Antioch on the Novatians that the Libellus Synodicus reports. Eusebius says Dionysius was invited to a synod at Antioch where some were trying to strengthen Novatian's party; Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius, thinks it probably met, while Hefele and others doubt it.
  • Whether the presbyter Novatus of Carthage was one of the five presbyters cut off with Felicissimus is disputed. Hefele says some assert it and others deny it. Leclercq, following Paul Monceaux, says he was.
  • Whether Cornelius took back those who had sacrificed. Socrates of Constantinople says Novatian broke away because Cornelius received into communion believers who had sacrificed. Cyprian calls the report that Cornelius communicates everywhere with those who sacrificed a false rumor of the apostates, and says Cornelius had only joined with us those whose cause was heard and whose innocence was approved.
  • The terms for those who had taken certificates. Hefele sums them up as reconciled at once. Cyprian says only that they were admitted in the meantime, after each case was examined.
  • What Novatian's rule meant. Socrates reports it as leaving the pardon of those who had sacrificed to God. Eusebius, who calls him Novatus, says he acted as if there was no hope of salvation left for them. In McGiffert's English, the Rome decree calls his opinion brother-hating and inhuman.
  • Whether the quarrel at Carthage began over the lapsed. Hefele, citing Cyprian's letters, says it began over Cyprian's commissioners who carried help to the poor, and over Felicissimus's ordination as deacon by the presbyter Novatus without Cyprian's leave. Only afterward did the party take up the severity of the bishop toward the lapsed as its war-cry.
  • Cyprian answers a charge. A bishop in Africa, Antonianus, had been shaken by Novatian's letters, and Cyprian writes to clear himself of the thought that he had turned from his early severity to make peace easy for the fallen. He pictures a wounded brother lying stricken on the field of battle, whom Christ urges that he may not wholly perish.
  • Nicaea's canon 13 keeps what it calls the ancient canonical law, that a man at the point of death must not be deprived of the last Viaticum. Hefele calls the Carthage book the first penitential book of the church. No held source says Nicaea's rules came from these councils.
  • Alexandria in the same years. Dionysius says the martyrs there received some of the fallen who had sacrificed, once they saw their repentance was enough, and had fellowship with them in prayers and feasts. He had also ordered that the dying who asked for remission should receive it, and tells of the old man Serapion, who had sacrificed and was given a little of the eucharist as he died (Dionysius of Alexandria, in Eusebius, Church History VI.42 and VI.44).
  • The year after. The African bishops' letter granting peace to all who kept repenting gives no year. Hefele dates its council to May 252, and the brief tells it as a historian's reckoning.

Whose Account We Have

  • The council's book of decisions (acts): Cyprian says the heads of the decisions were collected in a little book. It is lost. Henri Leclercq, in his edition of Hefele: nothing of this council remains.
  • Cyprian, Letter to Antonianus (participant): Written by the bishop who called the Carthage council, to defend it and himself against Novatian's letters. On Cyprian's side.
  • Eusebius of Caesarea, Church History VI.43 to 46 (later narrative): Written later, from letters of Cornelius and Dionysius of Alexandria, which Eusebius quotes or summarizes and which survive only through him. Cornelius wrote as Novatian's enemy.
  • Socrates of Constantinople, Church History IV.28 (later narrative): Written by about 439. The fullest statement of Novatian's own rule, which he reports from Novatian's letters. A. C. Zenos, who edited him in English, finds him favorable to the Novatians.
