Nineteenth-Century Methodism

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

A movement that began as a society inside the Church of England spent the nineteenth century becoming a worldwide family of churches, and dividing as it grew. In Britain the Wesleyan connexion consolidated into a respectable denomination, and those who found it too respectable went their own way: the Primitive Methodists, born at an open-air camp meeting on Mow Cop in 1807 and a settled connexion by 1811-12, took the gospel into the pit villages behind working-class preachers, a striking number of them women. In America, Methodism became the largest Protestant body in the country and then split down the middle over slavery in 1844 into northern and southern churches that would not reunite for nearly a century. Black Methodists, refused equal standing, had already built churches of their own — the African Methodist Episcopal Church in 1816, the AME Zion Church in 1821. By 1881 the family was scattered enough that it needed a world conference simply to speak to itself.

In Its Own Record

Gave Rise To

  • The Holiness Movement Dominant Modern Reconstruction Major — Historians trace the Holiness movement to New York Methodists of the 1830s, above all Phoebe Palmer. She made the old Methodist hope of a holy life into a step that any believer could take at once.
  • The Salvation Army Dominant Modern Reconstruction Major — William Booth was a Methodist preacher until 1861, when he gave up his pulpit. In 1865 he began to preach in the streets of East London. His mission there took the name of an Army in 1878. Historians Norman Murdoch and Pamela Walker trace its methods back to Methodist revival preaching.
  • African-Initiated Churches (Harrist; Aladura; Zionist/ZCC; Kimbanguist) Dominant Modern Reconstruction Major — Nehemiah Tile worked for the Methodist mission among the Thembu people from the early 1870s. He broke with his mission leaders. In 1884 he founded the Thembu National Church. Historians call it the first church in South Africa that Africans ran on their own.

Continues

  • The Methodist Revival Widely Accepted Major — Wesley died in 1791. The Methodist Conference he had built kept meeting and ruling its preachers. In America the Methodists had already formed their own church at Baltimore in 1784. They called it the Methodist Episcopal Church, and Jesse Lee carried its story on to 1809.

Broke With

  • The Black Church in America Documented Major — Richard Allen tells how white Methodist elders fought his Bethel church for its pulpit. One elder sent out a letter that cast its members out. Another took Bethel to court and lost. In April 1816 Black Methodists from Bethel and from Baltimore formed a church of their own, the African Methodist Episcopal Church.
