Early American Unitarians & Universalists

New England and the mid-Atlantic, 1770 to 1815 — two liberal movements, one holding that God saves everyone and one that God is one person, both organizing churches before either had a denomination.

About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 1:04

Read along

Two separate movements, often confused with one another, converged in the new American republic. The Universalists came first in organized form. John Murray, an English preacher who had absorbed the doctrine that God will finally save everyone, arrived in America in 1770 and gathered a congregation at Gloucester, Massachusetts in 1779, and a general convention adopted the brief Winchester Profession in 1803. The Unitarian thread came from inside New England Congregationalism and from English precedent — Theophilus Lindsey opened the Essex Street Chapel in London in 1774, and in 1785 King's Chapel in Boston, an Anglican congregation left leaderless by the Revolution, revised its Book of Common Prayer to remove the Trinity. Neither had a denomination yet; those came in the 1820s, after this period closes.

Documented Stories

The Parish Sells the Universalists’ Silver

John Murray, an English Methodist-turned-Universalist who had landed in New Jersey in 1770 and preached up and down the coast, settled in Gloucester, Massachusetts, in 1774 at the invitation of a group of families, the Sargents chief among them, who had been reading the English Universalist James Relly. They withdrew from the First Parish, and on 1 January 1779 fifteen of them signed the articles of association of the Independent Christian Church, the first Universalist congregation in America, and in 1780 built a meeting-house. Massachusetts law still required every inhabitant to pay for the support of public worship, and allowed the money to go to a Dissenting minister only if he belonged to a recognized sect; the First Parish, and the town, did not recognize Universalists as anything but seceders from Congregationalism. In 1782 the parish collector seized goods from the Universalists for the unpaid rates, silver plate from Epes Sargent, anchors and merchandise from others, and sold them at auction. The case went through the county court to the Supreme Judicial Court of the Commonwealth, and in June 1786 the court held that the Universalists were a religious society within the meaning of the constitution's article on religion, that their teacher was a public teacher of piety, religion and morality, and that their rates must go to him. The decision put the first Universalist church on the same footing as the Baptists and Quakers, and Murray was ordained by his own congregation in 1788 and served it until 1793.

King’s Chapel Ordains Its Own Minister

King's Chapel had been the church of the royal governors of Massachusetts, and in 1776 its rector and most of its Loyalist congregation left Boston with the British fleet. The families who remained kept the building and in 1782 engaged James Freeman, a Harvard graduate of twenty-three, as lay reader. Freeman had read the English Unitarians, Lindsey and Priestley, and came to hold that the doctrine of the Trinity was not in Scripture; he told the congregation so from the reading desk, and in 1785 proposed that the Book of Common Prayer be revised to remove it. The proprietors voted, twenty to seven, to adopt the revision, which followed Lindsey's Essex Street liturgy of 1774, dropped the Nicene and Athanasian Creeds, and addressed every prayer to the Father alone, and the book was printed that year. Freeman then asked for ordination. Bishop Seabury of Connecticut, the first Anglican bishop in America, declined; Bishop Provoost of New York declined after examining him and finding his views what they were. On 18 November 1787 the congregation, at a meeting of the proprietors, voted that it had the right to ordain its own minister, and at a service that evening the senior warden, Thomas Bulfinch, laid hands on Freeman and declared him rector, teacher and pastor of the church. The Episcopal churches of Boston refused communion with the chapel from that day, and King's Chapel, keeping its Prayer Book, its box pews, its Anglican architecture and its name, has been Unitarian since.

Connections

In Its Own Record

In 1785 King's Chapel in Boston revised its prayer book to remove the Trinity. English Unitarian precedent came earlier still, with the opening of the Essex Street Chapel in London in 1774. Separately, John Murray organized the Universalists at Gloucester in 1779, and they adopted the Winchester Profession in 1803. Both threads only became formal denominations later, with the American Unitarian Association founded in 1825.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Two distinct movements converged in the new American republic. John Murray, an English preacher who believed God would finally save everyone, gathered a Universalist congregation at Gloucester, Massachusetts in 1779, while in 1785 King's Chapel in Boston — an Anglican congregation left leaderless by the Revolution — revised its own prayer book to remove the Trinity, years before either movement had an actual denomination.

Legacy

Both became denominations in the following decade — the American Unitarian Association in 1825, the Universalists consolidating their conventions. And in 1961 the two merged into the Unitarian Universalist Association, which continues. Their cultural influence in nineteenth-century New England was far larger than their numbers. Harvard, the Transcendentalists, and a great deal of American reform work in education, prisons and abolition came out of this milieu. King's Chapel in Boston still uses a descendant of its 1785 prayer book.

Experience It Today

  • King's Chapel, Boston — the 1754 stone church that became America's first Unitarian congregation in 1785, on the Freedom Trail and open for tours

Doctrinal Floor Note

Two different questions need to be kept separate here. The Unitarian thread states plainly, in its own confessions, that it does not hold the Trinity — a clear divergence from the historic creeds, on its own account. The Universalist thread is a different matter: its claim is that God will finally save everyone, which is an argument about salvation rather than about the creed, and may not touch the creed at all. Neither is treated as a fully settled question.
