Hebrew Roots & Torah-Observance Movements

North America and digital-native, c. 1990s to 2010 — a largely gentile Torah-observant current of Sabbath- and feast-keeping congregations and digital teaching ministries, deliberately distinct from Messianic Judaism, whose own publications draw the line.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 1:31

Read along

From the 1990s a largely gentile current of Christians took up Torah observance. They kept the Sabbath and the biblical feasts in congregations, in Torah study groups and, above all, online. It was not Messianic Judaism, a movement of Jewish believers, and both traditions drew that line in their own writing. Its best-documented body was First Fruits of Zion, founded in 1992, and its own teaching changed in public. In 2009 Boaz Michael and D. Thomas Lancaster set out a shift in Messiah Journal. They moved away from 'One Law' toward what they called Divine Invitation. The One-Law side answered in print. That exchange is the clearest dated record of the argument inside this current. Digital ministries followed, 119 Ministries among them from around 2010. Founding dates for many of them simply cannot be established. An older current kept the Sabbath and the feasts as well. This was the Sacred Name movement of the 1930s. It began among members of the Church of God (Seventh Day), a Sabbath-keeping body with Adventist roots. Its magazine The Faith dates from 1937. How far the newer current drew on it is not clear.

Documented Stories

One Law, Reconsidered

First Fruits of Zion had been founded in 1992 by Boaz Michael, and for seventeen years had taught what the Hebrew Roots current called One Law: that since there is one Torah for the native-born and the stranger, gentile believers in Jesus were bound to the same commandments as Jews, Sabbath, festivals and food laws included. It was the ministry's reason for existing and the conviction of most of its readers. In the spring 2009 issue of its Messiah Journal, Michael and its senior teacher D. Thomas Lancaster published an article stating that they had changed their minds. Reading the apostolic decision of Acts 15 and Paul's letters again, they had concluded that the Torah's covenant obligations fell on Israel, that gentile believers were invited to keep them as a gift and not commanded to as a debt, and that treating gentiles as bound had produced legalism and erased the distinction between Israel and the nations. They called the alternative Divine Invitation. They said they knew what it would cost. It cost what they expected: subscribers canceled, donors withdrew, and the ministry's income fell sharply over the following year, which it reported. Tim Hegg and other One-Law teachers published detailed rebuttals within months, arguing the exegesis point by point, and the exchange is the clearest record of an argument in a current with almost no institutions to have arguments in. The ministry did not reverse itself, and by 2010 the two positions marked the current's two wings.

The Faith, 1937

Clarence Orvil Dodd was a steelworker and an elder in the Church of God (Seventh Day) in Warren, Ohio, a small Sabbath-keeping body that shared roots with the Adventists but had never accepted Ellen White. In the early 1930s he came to hold that the biblical feasts of Leviticus 23 were binding on Christians, and then, through correspondence and his own study of the Hebrew, that the divine name represented by the four letters, which English Bibles rendered as LORD, was meant to be spoken, and that its concealment had been a loss. His church did not agree. In 1937 he began publishing a magazine, The Faith, from Warren, printing the name in its pages as Yahweh, later using Yahshua for Jesus, teaching the feasts and the Sabbath, and carrying letters from scattered readers who had reached similar conclusions in Michigan, Missouri and the South. It was the first periodical of what became the Sacred Name movement. Small assemblies formed around its readership, the Assembly of Yahweh at Holt, Michigan among the earliest, and later bodies trace themselves to that mailing list. Dodd died in 1955 and the magazine continued for decades under others. The Hebrew Roots current of the 1990s, which arose largely from evangelical and Messianic soil, did not come from Dodd's assemblies, but it reached, by another road, a good deal of the same ground: the feasts, the Sabbath, the name, and the conviction that the church had left something behind.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

From the 1990s a largely gentile current of Christians took up Torah observance. They kept the Sabbath and the biblical feasts in congregations and, more and more, through teaching online. Its own writers took care to set it apart from Messianic Judaism, a movement of Jewish believers. Its best-documented body, First Fruits of Zion, was founded in 1992. In 2009 it published a public shift away from teaching 'One Law' toward what it called Divine Invitation. The movement's One-Law side answered in print. An older current, the Sacred Name movement of the 1930s, also kept the Sabbath and the feasts. Its magazine The Faith dates from 1937. How far the newer current drew on it is not clear.

Legacy

In 2010 this was a current in progress rather than a settled tradition, and this map says so. Institutionally it was thin — a handful of publishers and congregational networks, and a great deal of video — which makes it genuinely hard to describe: no central body, no shared confession, and no independent count of any kind, so every figure in circulation comes from self-report or journalism. What it did was put a question back into ordinary Christian conversation that most churches had long considered settled: what, if anything, the Torah asks of gentile believers. Its individual bodies differ widely from one another, and the map assesses them one at a time.

How Sure the Record Is

Thin institutionally but abundant online: First Fruits of Zion, founded in 1992, whose 2009 shift from 'One Law' to 'Divine Invitation' teaching is documented on both sides; digital ministries like 119 Ministries from around 2010; and the older Sacred Name movement's periodical The Faith, dating from 1937. Much of the record was kept on fragile platforms.
