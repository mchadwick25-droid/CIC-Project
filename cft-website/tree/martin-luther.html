The Reformation Era · 1517-1546 · Wittenberg, Saxony

Saxony, today in Germany

Martin Luther

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Lutheran Wittenberg & Its Congregations.

Known for
Augustinian friar and teacher at Wittenberg whose letter of 31 October 1517 against the sale of pardons began the movement that bears his name. He taught that forgiveness is God's free gift, received by trusting God's promise. At Worms in 1521 he would not take back his books unless Scripture or clear reason proved him wrong, and, outlawed and hidden, he put the New Testament into German. He wrote hymns for his congregations to sing. In 1525 he first warned both rulers and peasants, then urged the rulers to strike down the armed bands.
Also called
Martinus Lutherus, Brother Martin Luther, Dr. Luther
Dates
Born at Eisleben in 1483, as the editor Henry Morley gives it; the year is the editor's. The letter and theses are dated the vigil of All Saints, 1517, which is 31 October. The pope's decree Exsurge Domine was issued on 15 June 1520, by Kidd, and a second decree, Decet Romanum Pontificem, followed on 3 January 1521, by the editor of the Philadelphia edition. The editors Currie and Kidd date the burning of the decree and the church law at Wittenberg to 10 December 1520. Luther answered at Worms on 18 April 1521, and the imperial law against him dates from May 1521. Jacobs dates the New Testament to September 1522 and the whole Bible to 1534; the first Wittenberg hymnbook is of 1524. By Currie, his marriage to Katharina von Bora took place on 13 June 1525. Bacon dates the hymn Ein feste Burg to 1529, and Kidd prints it undated. A letter of 1 February 1546 to his wife is held
Place
Eisleben, his birthplace by Morley's note, where Currie's headnote has him preaching at the end of January 1546; Wittenberg, in Saxony, where he taught; Augsburg, where he answered Cardinal Cajetan in October 1518; Worms, on the Rhine, where the emperor met the princes of the empire; the Wartburg, where editors say he was taken on 4 May 1521
Role
Augustinian friar and teacher of theology at Wittenberg; preacher, translator of the Bible into German and writer of hymns

About This Person

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 4:23

On 31 October 1517, a friar, living under religious vows, wrote to the archbishop of Mainz, a great prince of the church. Pardons, letters said to cancel punishment for sin, were being sold in the archbishop's name for the pope's new church in Rome. People believed, he warned, that a pardon made them sure of heaven.

The friar was Martin Luther, who taught at Wittenberg, a university town in eastern Germany. He taught that forgiveness is grace, God's free gift, received by faith, by trusting what God promises, so it could not be bought. Condemned by the pope, he would not take back his books before the emperor, ruler of the German lands and more, unless Scripture, the Bible, or clear reason proved him wrong. Made an outlaw, whom anyone could seize, he was hidden away and put the New Testament, the gospels and letters of the early church, into German. The Lutheran churches, grown from his teaching, carry his name.

With his letter he sent theses, short statements for a university debate. One put his teaching in a line: "Every true Christian, living or dead, has a share in all the good things of Christ and the church. God gives it to him, even without letters of pardon."

In June 1520 the pope's decree condemned his books, ordered them burned and gave him sixty days to take them back. In December 1520 he burned the popes' book of church law, and editors say the pope's decree against him burned with it. In January the pope cut him off from the church.

In April 1521, under the emperor's promise of safe passage there and back, he came to Worms. His friends printed his answer: "Unless I am convinced by the witness of the Scriptures or by clear reason … I cannot and will not take back anything." It ends, "God help me. Amen." "Here I stand, I cannot do otherwise" appears only in prints from one Wittenberg press.

In May the emperor, Charles V, made him an outlaw: no one was to house or feed him. Hidden on his friends' advice, he made that German New Testament, printed in 1522. He wrote that it must be in the simplest language, so that all could understand it. He wanted the people to sing in their own language, not only to hear trained singers. By one editor's count, four of the eight hymns in Wittenberg's first hymnbook, in 1524, were his.

In 1525 peasants across the German lands rose against their lords. Luther first warned both sides. The rulers, and above all the bishops, had only themselves to thank for the rising, he wrote, for they did nothing but rob their people. To the peasants he said that whoever takes the sword will perish by the sword. Then armed bands began robbing and burning. He urged rulers, and anyone who could, to strike them down: "So let everyone who can strike, kill and stab, in secret or in the open. Remember that nothing can be more poisonous, harmful or devilish than a rebel."

One friend wrote to him that people who favored him found it strange that he let the tyrants kill without mercy. In July he held to his call: rulers should strike quickly, guilty or innocent. But once they had won, he said, they should show mercy even to the guilty, and he condemned the "furious, raving, senseless tyrants" who could not get their fill of blood. A later, hostile chronicle of the Anabaptists, a church of believers baptized as adults, said he had leaned on worldly power.

The whole Bible followed in German by 1534, finished with his colleagues, editors say. His churches kept his songs, and two centuries later the hymnbook of Halle, a Lutheran town in Germany, still printed them.

In 1539 he looked back on putting the Bible into German. He had hoped for less writing and more reading of Scripture, so that each might "drink for himself from the fresh spring".

Quotations and references

Quotations

  • In our modern English
    “Every true Christian, living or dead, has a share in all the good things of Christ and the church. God gives it to him, even without letters of pardon.”
    As printed
    “Quilibet verus Christianus, sive vivus sive mortuus, habet participationem omnium bonorum Christi et ecclesiae, etiam sine literis veniarum, a Deo sibi datam.”
    Luther, Ninety-five Theses, Thesis 37, 1517, in Kidd, Documents Illustrative of the Continental Reformation
  • In our modern English
    “Unless I am convinced by the witness of the Scriptures or by clear reason … I cannot and will not take back anything.”
    As printed
    “Nisi convictus fuero testimoniis Scripturarum aut ratione evidente (nam neque Papae neque Conciliis solis credo, cum constet eos errasse saepius et sibi ipsis contradixisse), victus sum Scripturis a me adductis captaque est conscientia in verbis Dei : revocare neque possum neque volo quidquam,”
    Luther's answer before the emperor and the Diet, 18 April 1521, in Kidd's Latin
    The brief leaves out, without marking it, the words between: the aside that he trusts neither pope nor councils alone, since they have often erred, and the clause that he is bound by the Scriptures he has cited and his conscience is captive to the words of God.
  • In our modern English
    “God help me. Amen.”
    As printed
    “Gott helff mir. Amen.”
    Luther's answer before the emperor and the Diet, 18 April 1521, in Kidd's Latin
  • In our modern English
    “Here I stand, I cannot do otherwise”
    As printed
    “Ich kann nicht änderst, hie stehe ich”
    the closing words as given in two prints of 1521, in Wrede's note, Deutsche Reichstagsakten, volume 2
    The two prints give the clauses the other way round: I cannot otherwise, here I stand. Kidd prints them in the brief's order, Hie stehe ich. Ich kan nicht anders.

    Luther, Against the Robbing and Murdering Hordes of Peasants, May 1525, English of the Philadelphia edition, volume 4

Key Works

  • The Ninety-five Theses, with his letter to the archbishop of Mainz (1517): short statements for a university debate on indulgences, sent with a letter that asks the archbishop to stop the preaching of pardons. Thesis 37 puts his teaching in one line: a true Christian, living or dead, shares in all the good things of Christ and the church, given by God even without letters of pardon. C. M. Jacobs, the editor, notes that the word faith does not occur in the theses, that penance is not disputed and that indulgences are admitted within limits. The Latin is in Kidd and the English in the Philadelphia edition
  • The German New Testament (1522) and the German Bible (1534): not held in the Library, which has no copy of the German text. His letter to Spalatin of 30 March 1522 says he has translated the whole New Testament in his Patmos and is correcting it with Philip, and that it is to be written in the simplest language so that all may understand it. Jacobs dates the printing to September 1522 and the complete Bible to 1534, made with his Wittenberg colleagues
  • Hymns and the Wittenberg hymnbook of 1524: by Leonard Bacon's count the first Wittenberg hymnbook held eight hymns, four of them Luther's. His own preface says the songs are set in four parts so that the young may be weaned from love ballads and worldly songs. German texts with English in Bacon's edition; which tunes are his own is disputed

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Within two months of 31 October 1517, Jacobs says, three editions of the Latin theses were printed, at Wittenberg, Nuremberg and Basel. In February 1519 the Basel printer Froben wrote to Luther that he had sent six hundred copies of his writings to France and Spain, and had sold all his copies but ten.
  • The archbishop of Mainz wrote to Pope Leo X in July 1521 that the number of Lutherans was growing, that hardly a layman could be found who favored the clergy without reserve, and that a good part of the priests sided with Luther.
  • Cyriacus Spangenberg, in his preface to the Cithara Lutheri of 1545, in Luther's lifetime, called Luther the best and most accomplished of all master-singers since the apostles, in whose hymns "one does not find a vain or needless word".
  • The sixteenth edition of the Halle hymnbook, of 1730, lists his hymns Aus tiefer Not, Nun freut euch, lieben Christen and Vom Himmel hoch in its index, and its preface says that at the Reformation the gift of building up God's congregation with strong songs came forth in the blessed Luther and other pious men of that time.
  • The English Moravian hymnal of 1754 prints an English hymn on the Ten Commandments headed as a parody of his hymn; a note in the book calls its author the translator of Luther's hymn on the Ten Commandments.
  • The church order of 1537 for Denmark and Norway says its draft was sent to Doctor Martin Luther, who approved it with the other Wittenberg theologians, and urges that the Lord's Prayer, Christ's words of institution and the last collect with the blessing be said in Danish, for the sake of those simple and untaught in the faith.
  • The Formula of Concord of 1577 acknowledges his Small and Large Catechisms as "the Bible of the laity".

How they're remembered

  • Charles V, in his French declaration of 19 April 1521, said that a single friar errs against all Christendom, of the past thousand years and more and of the present, and that he was ready to stake his realms, his friends, his body, his blood, his life and his soul against him.
  • Aleander, the pope's envoy and hostile to him, wrote in February 1521 that all Germany was in commotion, nine in ten crying Luther and the tenth crying death to the court of Rome. The Venetian envoy Contarini wrote in April 1521 that his books were sold publicly in Worms although the pope and the emperor had forbidden them.
  • John Rühel, a friend, wrote to him on 26 May 1525 that it seemed strange to many who favored him that he let the tyrants kill without mercy. Jacobs says his utterances gave great offense to many of his own friends and followers.
  • A later chronicle of the Hutterite Brethren says Luther and Zwingli exposed all the pope's tricks but set up nothing better, hanging at once on worldly power.
  • Johann Walter, court composer, wrote some forty years on that Luther himself made the notes over the Epistles, the Gospels and the Words of Institution for the first German Mass and sang them over to him.
  • Bacon judges that Luther's Bible and Luther's hymns gave life not only to the churches of the Reformation but to German nationality and the German language.

What Is Uncertain

  • Words of his own and later accounts. His theses, letters, treatises, prefaces and hymns are his words at the time. His preface of 1545, written twenty-eight years on, is his own later account. The Table Talk was written down by others at table and printed after his death; the editor Wrede says the Table Talk's story of Worms is to be used only with the greatest caution, so its details are left out. His letters, prefaces, German Mass and Small Catechism are held only in English, and his German Bible is not held.
  • Here I stand. Kidd prints Hie stehe ich. Ich kan nicht anders as the end of the answer at Worms. Wrede finds the words Ich kann nicht änderst, hie stehe ich only in two prints of the time, one Latin and one German, from the same Wittenberg printer, so one source and not two. Every translation of the speech, in manuscript and in print, ends only with Gott helf mir. Amen, and Wrede holds that the longer ending was added in Wittenberg. Wrede reported in 1896 that it was mainly church historians who kept the longer form, and secular historians who accepted only the short cry. Which ending he spoke is not settled.
  • The church door. Kidd, Jacobs and the editor of the Table Talk say the theses were posted on the door of the Castle Church at Wittenberg. No witness of 1517 is held, and the earliest account, Melanchthon's, is not held. The brief says he sent them to the archbishop, as his letter shows.
  • What he expected. His own later account, in the 1545 preface, says he was sure the pope would side with him against the preachers of pardons. Jacobs says the theses did not deny the pope's power to forgive and that Luther was then a devoted son of the church.
  • The decree and the law book. His own words, in 1520 and 1521, say he meant to burn the whole papal code and that he burned the decrees. That the pope's decree burned with them, at the Elster gate before students, is the editors' account (Currie, Kidd and the editor of volume 3), not his. That editor adds that the decree of June 1520 was not properly a bull of excommunication but an admonition; the second decree, of January 1521, launched the curse of Rome upon him, in the editor's words.
  • The safe-conduct. The Table Talk says it was soon broken. Charles V's declaration says Luther is to be sent home under it, and Luther's letter of May 1521 says he left Worms with a safe-conduct of twenty-one days.
  • His hiding. Luther wrote that he would let himself be hidden away on the advice of friends. That his prince arranged it, and that he was taken to the Wartburg, is the editors' account.
  • Ein feste Burg. Bacon dates it to 1529 and says the story that it was written or sung on the way to Worms comes from a likeness to a saying about devils on the roofs; Kidd prints it undated among the hymns of 1523 and 1524. The year is not settled, and 1521 is excluded.
  • The tunes. Bacon says some tunes come from older hymns, some are thought to have been secular airs, and many were probably Luther's own. Walter says Luther made the chant notes of the German Mass himself. Sleidan, by Bacon, says he made the tune of Ein feste Burg. Who wrote which tune is not settled.
  • 1525. Of his three writings of that year, the Admonition to Peace, the tract Against the Robbing and Murdering Hordes of Peasants and the Open Letter on it, the German texts are not held and the English is Jacobs's. Rühel's letter is known only as Jacobs quotes it. The Hutterite chronicle is a later and hostile witness. No figure for the dead and no link between the tract and any change in his churches is held. Jacobs says the war was nearly over when the tract spread and that it seemed a defense of the rulers' cruelty. The peasants' Twelve Articles cited the Gospel; Luther wrote that the pretenses made in them under the name of the Gospel were nothing but lies. In July he held that the rulers should strike the rebel bands, guilty or innocent, but that the winners should show grace even to the guilty, and he condemned the furious, raving, senseless tyrants who could not get their fill of blood.
  • The marriage. Luther's letter to Koppe says the nun whom Koppe carried off from the nunnery two years before is returning to the cloister, not to take the veil but as the honored wife of Dr. Luther; another letter says the change seemed very strange to himself. Currie names Koppe as the man who took nine nuns from the cloister near Grimma, Katharina among them. No detail of the escape is held. Melanchthon's Greek letter on the marriage is too garbled in the scan to quote.
  • His late writings against the Jews. His treatises against the Jews of 1543 are not held in the Library, so this card cannot say from his own words what they teach. The brief does not tell this. One letter is held: Luther wrote to his wife, Katharina, on 1 February 1546, in Currie's English, a letter that Currie's headnote calls jocular. He writes that he grew very weak near Eisleben, and that it was his own fault. But she would have said, he writes, that the Jews or their God were behind it. His carriage had passed a village near Eisleben where many Jews lived, and perhaps they blew on him. Then he writes: "When the principal matters are arranged, I must endeavour to banish the Jews." He adds that Count Albrecht does not like them and has tried to expose them, and that, if God will, he will help the count and speak about them from the pulpit.
