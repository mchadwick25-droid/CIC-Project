The Imperial Church Era · 5th c. · Armenia

Koriun

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Armenian Christianity (conversion era).

Known for
Armenian pupil of Mesrop Mashtots and of Sahak, patriarch of the Armenians, whose Life of Mesrop is the main account of how the Armenian letters were made and the Scriptures first put into Armenian.
Also called
Gorioun
Dates
Fifth century; at Constantinople with Eznik of Goghp before the return that brought home the canons of the Council of Ephesus, by the Life of Mesrop; no source used here gives his birth or death
Place
Armenia; Constantinople; Georgia, by Victor Langlois's account
Role
Translator and recorder

About This Person

Two Armenians named Leon and Koriun arrived at Constantinople. There they joined their companion Eznik of Goghp, who had been raised with them like a brother. Together they finished their work. Then they went home to Armenia. They took true copies of the Scriptures and the Church Fathers, with the canons of the councils of Nicaea and Ephesus.

This scene comes from the Life of Mesrop Mashtots, the monk who made the Armenian letters, and of Sahak, patriarch of the Armenians. The Life names Koriun only this once, in the third person. A note in Victor Langlois's 1869 French edition takes him for its author. So does Lazar of Pharp, a later Armenian historian, who calls Koriun a disciple of Mashtots. Langlois calls the Life the main source for Armenian literary history from the late fourth century into the fifth.

Koriun says he wrote of Sahak and Mesrop, men he saw with his own eyes. He writes of himself as an attentive witness of their preaching and a servant of these teachers. He left out many of their good deeds, he adds, and told only the chief things worth knowing. His Life is also the work of a disciple honoring a saint. It has demons put to flight and a cross of light over the house where Mesrop died.

Who asked him to write it is told two ways. Lazar says it was King Vramshapuh. Langlois says it was Joseph, who led the Armenian church after Sahak. The held French text names no patron.

Langlois also tells what is said of Koriun's later life. Sahak, he says, made him a bishop of the Georgians and sent him to their land. Koriun is believed to have died there, Langlois adds. But nothing certain can be said, since writers of his time give no details.

Koriun says he wrote as an example for the spiritual children of Sahak and Mesrop, and for all who would teach their nation the truth.

Key Works

  • Life of Mesrop: the lives and deaths of Mesrop Mashtots and Sahak, the making of the Armenian letters and the first translations, written, Koriun says, of men he saw himself

Legacy

What traces to them

  • Lazar of Pharp sends readers to Koriun's Life to learn when, where and through whom the Armenian letters were found.
  • Langlois calls the Life the main source for Armenian literary history from the late fourth century into the fifth.
  • Langlois reports that some critics have thought Koriun may have put Agathangelos's History of Tiridates into Armenian. He calls the question far from settled.

How they're remembered

  • Lazar of Pharp: "Cette histoire a été écrite véridiquement par le célèbre Gorioun".
  • Langlois finds Koriun's style "fort élevé" but often obscure, and blames cuts made by copyists.

What Is Uncertain

  • The Life names Koriun once, in the third person, as one of two companions who came to Constantinople. That this Koriun wrote it rests on a note in Langlois's edition and on Lazar of Pharp, who calls Koriun a disciple of Mashtots.
  • Who asked him to write is told two ways. Lazar of Pharp says King Vramshapuh. Langlois says Joseph, who led the Armenian church after Sahak. The held French text names no patron.
  • His later life is known only from Langlois's introduction: sent to Byzantium with Ghevont, the Leon of the Life by a note in the edition, then to Jerusalem, then made bishop of the Georgians by Sahak. Langlois says he is believed to have died in Georgia, and that nothing certain can be said, since writers of his time give no details.
  • Moses of Khoren also names Leon and Koriun among the pupils who went to Byzantium. He says they went of their own accord, moved by envy of those already there; the held scan reads "TeuTie", most likely "l'envie".
  • The held text is J.-Raphaël Emine's French, made from the Mekhitarist edition of 1854. Langlois says the copyists' cuts make parts of it obscure. No Armenian text or English translation of the Life is held.
