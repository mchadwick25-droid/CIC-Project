The Imperial Church Era · 428-c. 451 · Constantinople, Antioch

Constantinople · Antioch, today in Turkey

Nestorius

A person. The tradition that tells their story: Antiochene Exegetical Christianity (Chrysostom-centered).

Known for
Bishop of Constantinople from 428, who taught that Christ is God and man, the two natures kept distinct and worshiped as one Christ, and who would not simply call Mary Mother of God. A council at Ephesus in 431, led by Cyril of Alexandria, judged that his teaching split Christ in two and removed him from office; he denied it and died in exile. His defense, written in Greek near the end of his life under the name of Heracleides, survives only in Syriac, kept by the Church of the East.
Also called
Mar Nestorius
Dates
Ordained bishop of Constantinople on 10 April 428; removed from office at Ephesus in June 431. The editors of his book date the emperor's order to burn his writings to August 435 and his banishment to 436. He was still living in Egypt in 439, when Socrates wrote. When and where he died is not known; Nau reckons about June 451, from stories that he died as he set out for Chalcedon. Bethune-Baker, as the editors of his book report him, thinks he wrote it in 451 or 452
Place
A native of Germanicia; brought from Antioch to Constantinople in 428; after Ephesus allowed, at his own request, to retire to his monastery outside Antioch, said to be that of Euprepius; banished to the Oasis in Egypt, then taken to Panopolis, toward Elephantine, and back
Role
Bishop of Constantinople, 428 to 431; preacher; author of a late defense

About This Person

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 3:39

A book about Christ came down to us under the name of Heracleides, a man who lived near Damascus. Its translator into Syriac, the Aramaic language of a church in Persia, explains why: "The writer chose to give the book this man's name. He feared that his own name, which many hated, would stop them from reading it and turning to the truth." The writer was Nestorius.

From 428 he was bishop, the leader of the churches, in Constantinople, the empire's capital. He taught that Christ is God and man, the two kept distinct yet worshiped as one Christ. So he would not simply call Mary "Mother of God", the one who gave birth to God. In 431 a council at Ephesus, a meeting of bishops called to settle teaching, was led by his enemy, Cyril of Alexandria. It judged that he split Christ in two and removed him from office. He denied it, and died in exile, forced to live far away under guard. Historians still argue over what he really taught.

Socrates, a historian of the city in his lifetime, tells of the first sermon this newcomer from Antioch preached. In it he asked the emperor for "the earth purged of heretics", people the church judged to teach falsely. Later he had the emperor take churches from a rival Christian group.

Socrates also says he rejected the name "Mother of God" outright. Nestorius says he offered a name drawn from the Gospel's own words, "Mother of Christ".

In a sermon kept by his opponents, he said: "God is not separated from the one who is seen. So I do not separate the honor of the one who is not separated. I separate the natures, but I unite the worship." The natures are what Christ is as God and as man, two, yet Christians bow in worship to one Christ. Socrates, who blames his temper, says he did not deny that Christ was God, but feared the name "as though it were some terrible phantom".

In 435, four years after the council, the emperor's law ordered his writings burned. Banished the next year to southern Egypt, he was dragged to and fro by soldiers, old and sick, with "a mangled hand and side", his own letters say.

Near the end, perhaps in 451, he wrote his defense in Greek under the borrowed name. In it he offered his own name to be cut off under the church's solemn curse: "So let Nestorius be anathema. Only let them say of God what I beg them to say."

His Greek is lost. The book survives because the Church of the East, the church of Persia beyond the Roman empire, put it into Syriac, perhaps a century later. Its rites, its set services, call him "the persecuted martyr", one who suffered for the faith. Yet that church would not be called his. In 1298 its chief bishop of Nisibis wrote that Nestorius had never been its patriarch, its head. They had held the faith in two natures already, he wrote, and they refused to cut him off: "Nestorius, then, followed them, and not they him."

When and how he died is not known. In his last book he bids the desert rejoice for him. He calls the land of his exile his mother, which will guard his body until the resurrection, when the dead rise at the end of time.

Key Works

  • Sermons at Constantinople: kept only in fragments by his opponents, in Greek (edited by Loofs) and in the Latin of Marius Mercator. In one he says he honors the man who is seen for the sake of the God hidden in him, and that he separates the natures but unites the worship
  • Letters from exile: two letters to the governor of the Thebaid, kept by the historian Evagrius, who reads his sufferings as God's vengeance. In them the Oasis falls to desert raiders and he is let go; at Panopolis he shows himself in public; and soldiers drag him toward Elephantine and back, worn, sick and old, with a mangled hand and side
  • The Book of Heracleides (the Bazaar of Heracleides): his defense, written in Greek in exile and lost in that language; it survives only in Syriac. Its Syriac translator says the writer took the name of Heracleides, a man who lived near Damascus, because his own name was hated, and that he set the book as a judge between himself and Cyril, each speaking in his own defense. In it he tells how he offered the title Mother of Christ; says Christ is immortal and unable to suffer as God, mortal and able to suffer as man; praises Leo of Rome while saying Leo agreed to his condemnation unheard; offers his own name to the church's curse if they will say of God what he asks; and ends by bidding the desert rejoice for him

Legacy

What traces to them

  • The emperor's law of 435 ordered his books sought out and publicly burned, and the editors of his book add that his followers' meetings were forbidden. His Greek is lost; only a few sermons and letters survive in Greek and Latin.
  • The Church of the East put his last book into Syriac: about 535 under the patriarch Paul, according to Bedjan; between 525 and 533 under the catholicos Maraba, Bethune-Baker conjectures, with no certainty; after 638, one editor's note allows, if the name Aleppo is the translator's own.
  • One mutilated copy is left, in the library of the patriarch of the Church of the East at Kotchanes in Kurdistan. It was damaged in 1843, chiefly by Kurds, in the massacre of Christians by Bedr Khan Bey. Two German scholars noticed it in the 1890s, and in 1910 Bedjan published the Syriac in Leipzig and Nau the French in Paris.
  • Badger reported in 1852 that the Church of the East kept three liturgies, among them the Liturgy of Nestorius, directed to be used five times a year. Abdisho's catalog of 1298 says he wrote a large liturgy, translated into Syriac by Thomas and Mar Awa.
  • The rites of the Church of the East name him: the ordination rite calls him Mar Nestorius the persecuted martyr, and a marriage rite names him as one whom the enemy strove to hinder.

How they're remembered

  • Narsai, a teacher of the Church of the East at Nisibis who died about 502, says that the priest at the liturgy makes mention of all these before God, imitating Mar Nestorius in his supplication.
  • About 680 the catholicos George I wrote that the faith he set out was not the invention of Nestorius and Theodore, whom the multitude of the impious slander and whose names scandalize the simple.
  • In 1298 Abdisho, Nestorian metropolitan of Nisibis, wrote that the Easterns were unjustly called Nestorians, since Nestorius was not their patriarch and they did not understand his language; they held the faith in two natures already, he says, so that Nestorius followed them and not they him, and when called on to cut him off they refused.
  • Barhadbeshabba, about 600, told his exile as a saint's life: called back, Nestorius refuses and says the desert pleases him; brought by force, he goes to Christ on a litter. A legend, as Nau's note on a neighboring story calls it.
  • In the empire, Evagrius, a hostile historian of the late sixth century, writes that he learned from a writer on his death that Nestorius's tongue had been eaten through with worms.

At Major Councils

  • Council of Ephesus, 431: Bishop of Constantinople; refused to appear and was deposed; wrote his own account in exile

What Is Uncertain

  • What he really taught is contested. Cyril and the council of Ephesus judged that his teaching split Christ in two; he denied it, saying that Christ is God and man in two natures and one Christ. Socrates, who read his writings, says he was an unlearned man who did not deny that Christ was God, and that he was scared of the word Theotokos as though it were some terrible phantom. A 1908 study by Bethune-Baker reopened the question.
  • Who began the quarrel over Mary's name. Socrates says his priest Anastasius, whom he had brought from Antioch, preached against the title Theotokos and Nestorius backed him and rejected it outright. Nestorius says in his book that he found two parties, one for Mother of God and one for Mother of man, who differed only in name, and offered them Mother of Christ. Evagrius's summary follows Nestorius; Hefele sides with Socrates.
  • The recantation. Socrates alone says that, as communion broke apart, he cried out that Mary might be called Theotokos if they wished, but no notice was taken. No other witness tells it, and bitter regret is Socrates's own reading.
  • When his book was written and put into Syriac. Bethune-Baker, as the editors report him, reckons from references to later events that he wrote it in 451 or 452, though he notes that Nestorius seems not to know the decisions of Chalcedon. The Syriac date is Bedjan's about 535, Bethune-Baker's 525 to 533 as a conjecture, or later than 638 on one editor's reading of the name Aleppo. Bazaar is held to be the translator's slip for a Greek word meaning treatise.
  • When and how he died. Hefele says it is unknown. Nau reckons about June 451 from stories that he died on his way to Chalcedon. Evagrius has worms from hearsay; Barhadbeshabba has a litter, in a legendary telling. Any date is Inferential-Thin; about 451 is the dominant modern reconstruction.
  • Who held him captive. His letter says barbarians and nomads, with the Mazices coming after; Evagrius's summary names the Blemmyes.
  • His own letters come to us through Evagrius, a hostile later writer, and his last book through a translator who calls Cyril impious. The brief's modern English for the book's words follows Driver and Hodgson's English, checked against Nau's French; the Syriac is not held. The sermon's modern English follows the Greek, which the Library's text gives with damaged letters.
