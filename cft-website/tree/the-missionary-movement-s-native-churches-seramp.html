About This Movement

Audio Brief · Narrated Edition 4:06

Read along

Between 1793 and 1906 European and American missionary societies planted churches across India, West Africa, the Pacific and the China coast. And almost immediately those churches began to be run, staffed and spread by local Christians the societies had not planned for. Samuel Ajayi Crowther, enslaved as a boy in Yorubaland and freed by an anti-slavery patrol, translated the Bible into Yoruba and in 1864 became the first African Anglican bishop, only for the next generation of the same society to force him aside in 1890. In the Pacific it was hundreds of Polynesian teachers, not Europeans, who carried Christianity from island to island, often at the cost of their lives. In China the preacher Liang Fa wrote a tract in 1832 whose stray reading by Hong Xiuquan helped set the Taiping upheaval in motion, and the former opium addict known as Pastor Xi built a network of his own converts inland. What holds these four wings together is a single problem. Nearly everything written about them was written from the mission's side, and the native voices that do survive are what any honest telling has to be built on.

Liberia's settler church belongs to the West African wing. It came from America. Lott Cary was born a slave near Richmond, Virginia, about 1780, and the church there encouraged him to preach. About 1815 he helped rouse the Black Christians of Richmond to take an interest in Africa. The Richmond African Missionary Society that followed gave between one hundred and one hundred and fifty dollars to the African mission for several years. In 1821 Cary sailed for West Africa. In a letter from Free Town he wrote, 'Before we left the United States, we formed ourselves into a church'. On 16 August 1823 he wrote from Monrovia, 'Our little church has been wonderfully blessed of late.' He had baptized two the day before. Cary's words reach us through a biography by J. B. Taylor, a Richmond Baptist pastor, printed in 1837.

Korea lies outside the four wings, but its Protestant church began in these same years. Before the first missionaries came, two Scots at Mukden, Ross and McIntyre, had done introductory work in China among Koreans. Some Koreans there took up the faith. They began work in Korean villages on the China side of the Yalu. Ross and McIntyre translated a number of the Gospels, and later the whole New Testament.

Underwood, who reached Korea in 1885, says in his book of 1908 that Dr. Allen settled in Seoul in September 1884. He adds that the Methodists sent Dr. McClay from Japan to look over the field in that same year.

The first converts, in July and the fall of 1886, were baptized behind closed doors. The old law of death to Christians had never been repealed. It seemed plain that it would not be enforced, but no one knew how far to venture.

A Korean colporteur, Soh Sang Ryun, told Underwood of 'a small company of Christians' at Sorai. By 1890 the two churches had over one hundred converts in all Korea. In 1896 the missionaries' monthly in Seoul spoke of inquirers who had 'finished the study of the Ross catechism'. It was The Korean Repository. All of this comes to us in the missionaries' own telling.

People and Stories

Voices in the Record

  • Samuel Ajayi Crowther — freed from a slave ship as a boy, translator of the Bible into Yoruba, and in 1864 the first African bishop in the Anglican Communion
  • Krishna Pal — the first Indian baptized at Serampore, in 1800, and a Bengali hymn-writer
  • Ta'unga — Cook Islands teacher and missionary to New Caledonia, one of the few Pacific evangelists whose own writings survive
  • Joeli Bulu — Tongan missionary to Fiji, whose autobiography is a rare first-person account from an islander evangelist
  • Liang Fa — the first ordained Chinese Protestant preacher, whose 1832 tract passed into hands he never intended
  • Xi Shengmo (Pastor Hsi) — Confucian scholar and former opium addict who founded refuges and led a Chinese-run network of congregations in Shanxi
  • William Carey and Hudson Taylor — the English missionaries whose Serampore mission and China Inland Mission set the pattern for the wider movement

Documented Stories

Krishna Pal Goes into the Hooghly

William Carey had been in Bengal since 1793 and had baptized no one. In November 1800 Krishna Pal, a carpenter of Serampore, the Danish settlement where the Baptist mission had settled that year, fell and dislocated his shoulder, and was treated by John Thomas, the surgeon who had brought Carey to India. Thomas set the arm and preached to him; Krishna came back to the mission house, brought his family and his friend Gokul, and within weeks asked for baptism. On 22 December he and Gokul ate a meal with the missionaries, in public, which broke their caste, and the town rioted; Krishna was seized and beaten and released by the Danish governor. On Sunday 28 December 1800, before the governor and a crowd on the riverbank, Carey went down into the Hooghly at Serampore and baptized first his own son Felix and then Krishna Pal, in Bengali. Thomas, who had waited longest, had broken down in the days before and was confined during the service. Krishna Pal became a preacher, planted congregations in Calcutta and among the Khasi hills, and wrote hymns in Bengali, one of which, in Marshman's translation, English congregations sang for a century: ‘O thou, my soul, forget no more the Friend who all thy misery bore.’ He died of cholera in 1822. The mission's account of his baptism went to England in its Periodical Accounts and was read as the first fruit of the whole enterprise, which, after seven years, it was.

Onitsha, August 1890

Samuel Ajayi Crowther had been consecrated bishop of the Niger territories in Canterbury Cathedral in 1864, at Henry Venn's insistence, and had built up, with African clergy and almost no Europeans, a chain of stations along the Niger. By the late 1880s the Society's new generation, formed by Keswick and by the confidence of empire, had concluded that the mission was lax, that its African agents were worldly and some corrupt, and that Crowther, old and gentle, could not govern them. Graham Wilmot Brooke, a layman of twenty-four, and J. A. Robinson, the mission's secretary, arrived in 1889 with powers from London. At the Finance Committee meeting at Onitsha in August 1890 they and their allies, in Crowther's presence, suspended, dismissed or disconnected most of the African clergy and agents on charges the bishop had not been allowed to examine, and struck his own son, Archdeacon Dandeson Crowther, from the roll. Crowther rose, said that he could no longer act as chairman, and withdrew. The Society's committee partly repudiated the young men's methods, confirmed their policy, and appointed European bishops. Crowther suffered a stroke and died at Lagos on 31 December 1891. Robinson was dead of fever before the year was out, and Brooke within another. In Lagos, Yoruba Christians who had watched founded in 1891 the United Native African Church, the first of the independent African churches, on the principle that Africa must be evangelized by Africans, which had been Venn's principle and Crowther's life.

The Boxer Summer, and a Mission That Refused Its Damages

The Boxer rising of 1900, a peasant movement against foreigners and Christians that the Qing court briefly endorsed, killed some 189 Protestant missionaries and their children, 47 Catholic priests and nuns, and around 30,000 Chinese Catholics and 2,000 Chinese Protestants, most of them in their own villages. In Shanxi the governor, Yuxian, invited the missionaries of Taiyuan to his yamen for protection on 9 July and had forty-four of them beheaded in the courtyard. The China Inland Mission, which had more workers in the interior than any other, lost fifty-eight adults and twenty-one children. When the allied armies had taken Beijing and the court had agreed to pay an indemnity to the powers, the missions were invited to file claims for lives, property and their converts' losses, and most did. The CIM's council in Shanghai, under Dixon Hoste, with Hudson Taylor's agreement from his sickbed in Switzerland, resolved in 1901 to claim nothing, for lives or for buildings, and to ask its Chinese Christians to forgo their own claims where they could, so as to show the meekness and gentleness of Christ; where officials insisted on paying, the money was refused or given to relief. The governor of Shanxi who succeeded Yuxian had a proclamation posted in the province recording the refusal and commending it, and it was read in the villages where the killing had been. Yuxian was executed by his own government in 1901 at the powers' demand. The mission's Chinese churches in Shanxi grew through the following decade.

Connections

In Its Own Record

These churches grew out of Protestant mission societies. The societies learned from earlier Pietist, Moravian and Methodist missions. In 1792 William Carey wrote the appeal that led to the Baptist mission in India. In it he held up the Moravians as the model, and praised the Methodists' work. The churches the societies planted became parents of the faith that now grows fast across the global South. Their own converts soon went out to spread it too. Teachers from Polynesia carried it from island to island.

Descended from

  • grew out of The Baptists Inferential-Thin Major — In 1792 Baptist ministers at Kettering formed a society to spread the gospel among the heathen. It sent William Carey and John Thomas to India, with a letter to Indian believers Thomas had known. No record from those first years shows the churches that grew from the mission, so the tie is thin.

Direct descendants

  • gave rise to The Chinese Church - Indigenous & Suffering Dominant Modern Reconstruction Major — China's independent churches of the 1910s and 1920s, such as the True Jesus Church and Watchman Nee's assemblies, grew up beside the older mission churches. Historians Daniel Bays and Lian Xi trace them to Chinese believers first formed in that mission world. The new churches' own founders stressed their freedom from the foreign missions.
  • gave rise to Ratana Church & Pacific Adjustment Movements Widely Accepted Major — Tahupōtiki Wiremu Rātana was baptized in the Methodist church as a baby. At first he told his followers to stay in their own churches. When those churches turned them away, his movement registered as a church of its own in 1925.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced The Moravian Church at Herrnhut Documented — William Carey's 1792 'Enquiry' led to the founding of the Baptist Missionary Society, and Carey went on to lead its mission at Serampore in India. In the 'Enquiry' he wrote that 'none of the moderns have equalled the Moravian Brethren' in mission work. He held up their missions in Greenland and Labrador as proof that the work could be done.
  • influenced The Methodist Revival Documented — In his 1792 appeal for a mission society, William Carey pointed to John Wesley's mission in the West Indies. He wrote that he had 'seen pleasing accounts of their success' among the Caribs and the Black people of the islands. Carey went on to lead the Baptist mission at Serampore in India.

Influenced

  • influenced The Taiping Heavenly Kingdom Dominant Modern Reconstruction — In 1832 the Chinese preacher Liang Fa published a Christian tract. Years later Hong Xiuquan reread it and decided that his visions meant he was God's second son. Jonathan Spence's history of the Taiping tells the story this way.
  • influenced African-Initiated Churches (Harrist; Aladura; Zionist/ZCC; Kimbanguist) Dominant Modern Reconstruction — In 1890 his own mission society pushed Samuel Ajayi Crowther aside. He had been the first African Anglican bishop since 1864. Andrew Walls and Lamin Sanneh see that as a lesson African Christians did not forget. In their view it is one root of the churches Africans later founded for themselves.

In Contact With

  • in contact with The Black Church in America Inferential-Thin — About 1815 Lott Cary helped rouse the Black Christians of Richmond to care for Africa. Taylor's biography of 1837 says a society grew from it and gave money for several years. Cary himself sailed to West Africa in 1821. This rests on one pastor's book.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

Samuel Ajayi Crowther, freed as a boy from a slave ship, translated the Bible into Yoruba and in 1864 became the first African bishop in the Anglican Communion, only to be forced aside by his own missionary society in 1890. In the Pacific it was Polynesian teachers, not Europeans, who carried the faith from island to island, often at the cost of their lives, while in China the former opium addict known as Pastor Xi built a network of congregations he led himself. Across India, West Africa, the Pacific, and the China coast, churches planted by foreign missions were run and spread almost immediately by converts the missions had not planned for.

Legacy

Much of the Christianity of Africa, Asia and the Pacific comes from these churches. The global South is now the faith's center by numbers. The Anglican Church of Nigeria, the churches of South India, the Pacific island churches and the Chinese church all trace their roots to these years. The translation work left a second legacy nobody planned. Hundreds of languages got a written form, a grammar and printed books for the first time. That kept them alive as much as it kept any teaching alive. And the way Crowther was treated in his last years became a lesson African Christians learned well. It is one of the roots of the independent African churches of the next century.

Experience It Today

  • Serampore College, West Bengal — founded in 1818 by William Carey, Joshua Marshman and William Ward, and still teaching

How Sure the Record Is

Rich but missionary-pen-weighted; real native inside voices exist and must anchor any build (Crowther's journals, Pastor Xi, island teachers' narratives)

  • Samuel Ajayi Crowther: journals (incl. the 1841 Niger Expedition journal), his 1837 enslavement-and-rescue narrative, letters, Yoruba translation work; WITH the 1890-91 Niger crisis record in the CMS archives (primary) — A Black bishop's own pen — the century's fullest African episcopal self-record. It also documents, in his own words, the Church Missionary Society's own late-century betrayal of him under real colonial and racial pressure.
