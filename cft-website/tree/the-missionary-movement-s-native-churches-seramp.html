The Missionary Era · 1793-1906 · India, W. Africa, Pacific, China

The Missionary Movement's Native Churches (Serampore India; Sierra Leone & the Niger; Pacific Islands; China Coast & CIM)

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Between 1793 and 1906 European and American missionary societies planted churches across India, West Africa, the Pacific and the China coast. And almost immediately those churches began to be run, staffed and spread by local Christians the societies had not planned for. Samuel Ajayi Crowther, enslaved as a boy in Yorubaland and freed by an anti-slavery patrol, translated the Bible into Yoruba and in 1864 became the first African Anglican bishop, only for the next generation of the same society to force him aside in 1890. In the Pacific it was hundreds of Polynesian teachers, not Europeans, who carried Christianity from island to island, often at the cost of their lives. In China the preacher Liang Fa wrote a tract in 1832 whose stray reading by Hong Xiuquan helped set the Taiping upheaval in motion, and the former opium addict known as Pastor Xi built a network of his own converts inland. What holds these four wings together is a single problem. Nearly everything written about them was written from the mission's side, and the native voices that do survive are what any honest telling has to be built on.

Liberia's settler church belongs to the West African wing. It came from America. Lott Cary was born a slave near Richmond, Virginia, about 1780, and the church there encouraged him to preach. About 1815 he helped rouse the Black Christians of Richmond to take an interest in Africa. The Richmond African Missionary Society that followed gave between one hundred and one hundred and fifty dollars to the African mission for several years. In 1821 Cary sailed for West Africa. In a letter from Free Town he wrote, 'Before we left the United States, we formed ourselves into a church'. On 16 August 1823 he wrote from Monrovia, 'Our little church has been wonderfully blessed of late.' He had baptized two the day before. Cary's words reach us through a biography by J. B. Taylor, a Richmond Baptist pastor, printed in 1837.

Korea lies outside the four wings, but its Protestant church began in these same years. Before the first missionaries came, two Scots at Mukden, Ross and McIntyre, had done introductory work in China among Koreans. Some Koreans there took up the faith. They began work in Korean villages on the China side of the Yalu. Ross and McIntyre translated a number of the Gospels, and later the whole New Testament.

Underwood, who reached Korea in 1885, says in his book of 1908 that Dr. Allen settled in Seoul in September 1884. He adds that the Methodists sent Dr. McClay from Japan to look over the field in that same year.

The first converts, in July and the fall of 1886, were baptized behind closed doors. The old law of death to Christians had never been repealed. It seemed plain that it would not be enforced, but no one knew how far to venture.

A Korean colporteur, Soh Sang Ryun, told Underwood of 'a small company of Christians' at Sorai. By 1890 the two churches had over one hundred converts in all Korea. In 1896 the missionaries' monthly in Seoul spoke of inquirers who had 'finished the study of the Ross catechism'. It was The Korean Repository. All of this comes to us in the missionaries' own telling.

In Its Own Record

Grew Out Of

  • The Baptists Inferential-Thin Major — In 1792 Baptist ministers at Kettering formed a society to spread the gospel among the heathen. It sent William Carey and John Thomas to India, with a letter to Indian believers Thomas had known. No record from those first years shows the churches that grew from the mission, so the tie is thin.

Gave Rise To

  • The Chinese Church - Indigenous & Suffering Dominant Modern Reconstruction Major — China's independent churches of the 1910s and 1920s, such as the True Jesus Church and Watchman Nee's assemblies, grew up beside the older mission churches. Historians Daniel Bays and Lian Xi trace them to Chinese believers first formed in that mission world. The new churches' own founders stressed their freedom from the foreign missions.
  • Ratana Church & Pacific Adjustment Movements Widely Accepted Major — Tahupōtiki Wiremu Rātana was baptized in the Methodist church as a baby. At first he told his followers to stay in their own churches. When those churches turned them away, his movement registered as a church of its own in 1925.

Influenced By

  • The Moravian Church at Herrnhut Documented — William Carey's 1792 'Enquiry' led to the founding of the Baptist Missionary Society, and Carey went on to lead its mission at Serampore in India. In the 'Enquiry' he wrote that 'none of the moderns have equalled the Moravian Brethren' in mission work. He held up their missions in Greenland and Labrador as proof that the work could be done.
  • The Methodist Revival Documented — In his 1792 appeal for a mission society, William Carey pointed to John Wesley's mission in the West Indies. He wrote that he had 'seen pleasing accounts of their success' among the Caribs and the Black people of the islands. Carey went on to lead the Baptist mission at Serampore in India.

Influenced

  • The Taiping Heavenly Kingdom Dominant Modern Reconstruction — In 1832 the Chinese preacher Liang Fa published a Christian tract. Years later Hong Xiuquan reread it and decided that his visions meant he was God's second son. Jonathan Spence's history of the Taiping tells the story this way.
  • African-Initiated Churches (Harrist; Aladura; Zionist/ZCC; Kimbanguist) Dominant Modern Reconstruction — In 1890 his own mission society pushed Samuel Ajayi Crowther aside. He had been the first African Anglican bishop since 1864. Andrew Walls and Lamin Sanneh see that as a lesson African Christians did not forget. In their view it is one root of the churches Africans later founded for themselves.

In Contact With

  • The Black Church in America Inferential-Thin — About 1815 Lott Cary helped rouse the Black Christians of Richmond to care for Africa. Taylor's biography of 1837 says a society grew from it and gave money for several years. Cary himself sailed to West Africa in 1821. This rests on one pastor's book.

Sources

  • Samuel Ajayi Crowther: journals (incl. the 1841 Niger Expedition journal), his 1837 enslavement-and-rescue narrative, letters, Yoruba translation work; WITH the 1890-91 Niger crisis record in the CMS archives (primary) — A Black bishop's own pen — the century's fullest African episcopal self-record. It also documents, in his own words, the Church Missionary Society's own late-century betrayal of him under real colonial and racial pressure.
  • Pacific island teachers' narratives: The Works of Ta'unga (ed. Crocombe, 1968); Joeli Bulu, The Autobiography of a Native Minister in the South Seas (1871); the islander-teacher corpus as a class (primary)
  • India and China inside voices: Krishna Pal's hymns and letters (Serampore, 1800-); Liang Fa, Good Words to Admonish the Age (1832); Pastor Hsi (Xi Shengmo) - hymns and accounts via Mrs Howard Taylor's CIM biography (primary)
  • The societies' archives WITH flags: Carey's Enquiry (1792) and Serampore Form of Agreement (1805); Sierra Leone Native Pastorate records; CMS/LMS/WMMS station diaries; CIM records and China's Spiritual Need and Claims (1865); women's missionary society records (zenana missions, women's boards) (primary) — Worth noting plainly: this material is weighted toward the missionary's own pen, and entangled with empire. Women's missionary societies are represented here too, not just the men's.
  • Lamin Sanneh, Translating the Message (Orbis, 1989; 2nd ed. 2009), with Brian Stanley, The Bible and the Flag (1990) and Andrew Porter, Religion versus Empire? (Manchester, 2004) (secondary) — The translation-agency thesis (native appropriation) plus the current mission-empire standards.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 8, chs. 27 & 34 (Stanley), with regional chs. 29 (Frykenberg), 30 (Bays & Grayson), 33 (Piggin & Davidson), 35 (Kalu) (reference)
  • J. B. Taylor, Biography of Elder Lott Cary, Late Missionary to Africa (Baltimore, 1837) (secondary) — By the pastor of Second Baptist Church, Richmond. It carries Cary's own letters from West Africa.
  • Horace G. Underwood, The Call of Korea: Political, Social, Religious (New York, 1908) (primary) — By the founding Presbyterian missionary, written in 1908. Held in the Library. Used here only for events up to 1890. It is one missionary's account.
  • The Korean Repository, volume 3 (Seoul, 1896) (primary) — A monthly of the Protestant missionaries in Seoul. Held in the Library. The scan is rough in places, and only clear passages are used.
