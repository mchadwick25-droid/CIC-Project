About This Movement

In November 201, after a great rain in the night, the river rose against Edessa and broke the city's western wall. The flood killed more than two thousand people, and destroyed the Christians' church. The Chronicle of Edessa, put together some 340 years later, says the city's scribes recorded the flood at the time.

Those Christians spoke Syriac, and with the churches of Nisibis they made up Syriac Christianity, in two frontier cities between the Roman and Persian empires, from the flood to the year 410. In the 300s they sang their teaching in hymns and counted among them men and women vowed to God for life. Edessa's later founding story pictures its people hearing the Diatessaron read: the one story Tatian wove from the four Gospels.

Across the border in Persia, a teacher named Aphrahat wrote letters. Some men and women took a vow, before baptism, to live unmarried and holy for God. They were called the sons and daughters of the covenant. One of his letters asks them to keep watch at night, to sing psalms and pray, to give their goods to the poor, and not to live with the other sex, so lust will not draw them back.

If a vowed man asks a daughter of the covenant to live with him and serve him, Aphrahat gives her the words to answer. "I am promised in marriage to a royal husband, and I serve him. If I leave his service and serve you, my bridegroom will be angry with me. He will write me a letter of divorce and send me away from his house." The husband she means is Christ.

In Nisibis, Ephrem taught in hymns, each with a refrain, a line that came back after every verse. In his hymn for a Persian siege, the river threatens the city, and Ephrem sets that flood beside Noah's. After Noah's flood, God set his bow, the rainbow, over the earth as his promise. So the refrain runs: "Stretch out your bow against the flood, for see, it has lifted up its waves against our walls!"

In Persia, the Christians had grown in number, which angered the Magi, the priests who guarded the Persian religion. Sozomen, a Greek church historian of the next century, says the king believed a charge that their bishop was a friend of the Roman emperor. The king taxed the Christians heavily, hoping that want would make them give up their faith. Then he ordered their priests killed and their churches pulled down. Aphrahat, writing in 344, said that in his day many declared their faith and were killed.

In 363, to save its starving army inside Persia, Rome handed Nisibis to Persia, and Ephrem moved west to Edessa. In prose, Ephrem answered three rival teachers by name. Marcion taught a Stranger God, apart from the Maker of the world; Mani taught two first Roots, Light and Darkness; and Bardaisan said the world was made from five first beings. Of their followers Ephrem wrote: "Their works are like our works, just as their fast is like our fast, but their faith is not like our faith." He warned that whoever prayed with Mani's followers "prays with Satan."

Ephrem died at Edessa in 373, and the Syrians went on singing his hymns.

A Roman envoy, a bishop who made peace between East and West, came to a later Persian king. Through his work, the king ordered the churches his fathers had torn down to be rebuilt. He let "the priests, the leaders and all the holy covenant" go about freely and without fear. In 410, at the king's order, forty bishops of the Persian church met in a synod, a meeting of bishops to set rules for the church. Among the forty was the bishop of Nisibis, Ephrem's old city, now inside Persia. The day they met, the bishops said, was for them like seeing the dead rise.

Voices in the Record

  • Ephrem was a deacon, first at Nisibis and then, after 363, at Edessa, and this tradition's most prolific poet: hundreds of hymns carried its doctrine in verse a congregation could sing. He spent his last year running famine relief in the city and died within the month the crisis ended.
  • Aphrahat, remembered simply as 'the Persian sage,' wrote twenty-three dated letters from inside the Persian empire between 336 and 345, the last of them composed deep inside Shapur's persecution. He once drafted a circular letter in a synod of bishops' own name, yet whether he held that office himself is a question his own tradition never answers.
  • Tatian wove the four Gospels into one continuous story a generation before this Christian tradition's own window opens, and Edessa's later founding story pictures its people hearing his harmony read. Greek church history also remembers him breaking with Rome and founding a rigorist sect of his own—a controversy that belongs to his life outside this Syriac tradition, not inside it.
  • Bardaisan was a philosopher and poet at the court of King Abgar the Great. His one surviving dialogue argues that free will, not the stars, decides how people live—Christians the world over follow one law, whatever horoscope they were born under. His own later tradition rejected him over a separate question, whether the body itself is raised, and exactly where he stood on it is a question the record leaves open.
  • Jacob, bishop of Nisibis from about 309 and a signatory at the Council of Nicaea in 325, was remembered as the city's protector through Persian siege — though even his own people could not agree on when he died: the Chronicle of Edessa places it in 337 or 338, while Theodoret's history has him still at his post through a siege.
  • The daughters of the covenant were women vowed to God for life. Not one line composed by one of them survives; everything known of them, even the words Aphrahat gives one of them to say, was written by men.
  • Simeon bar Sabbae, bishop of Seleucia-Ctesiphon, was ordered to collect a crushing new tax on Persia's Christians and refused. Arrested and brought before Shapur II, tradition says he was offered gifts, then threatened, and each time refused to worship the sun. He was executed with his own clergy — tradition remembers him as the first of the persecution's bishops to die for his faith. His death opened the twenty-year vacancy the Persian church endured.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Its see of Nisibis passed into the Church of the East. Syriac translators later carried Greek philosophy and medicine into Arabic. From there, that learning came back to Europe.

Direct descendants

That year the city passed to Persia, and Ephrem moved to Edessa. In 410 the Persian church held a synod. Osee of Nisibis signed it. The synod made him the second of its chief bishops.
  • gave rise to Syriac Orthodox (West Syriac) Christianity Inferential-Thin Major — The Chronicle of Edessa says Paul, the city's bishop, would not accept the synod of Chalcedon. He was removed in 522. That December the new bishop drove out the monks who also refused it. Later Jacob Baradaeus was made bishop for Edessa. But these few notes are thin evidence for a line of descent.

Influence, argument and contact

Influenced By

  • influenced Bardaisan and the Bardaisanites Documented — Ephraim complained that Bardaisan 'fashioned hymns, and joined them with tunes'. Sozomen and Theodoret wrote in the 400s. They say Harmonius, the son of Bardaisan, wrote songs. Ephraim answered them with orthodox songs to the same music.

Influenced

  • influenced The Church of the East under Persia Documented Major — Barhadbeshabba wrote about the year 600. He says Ephrem set up a school at Edessa after Nisibis passed to Persia in 363. Later, he says, Narsai set up the school at Nisibis, and many came to it from Edessa to learn. Sources differ on when Narsai left Edessa.Lasting result: Narsai founded a new school at Nisibis in Persian lands, and most of the students from the school of Edessa came to him.
  • influenced Armenian Christianity (conversion era) Documented — Koriun was one of the translators who worked with Mesrop Mashtots. He says King Vramshapuh sent Mashtots to Syrian Mesopotamia to look for an alphabet. Later Sahak and Mashtots sent two of their pupils to Edessa. There the two were to put the Scriptures into Armenian from the Syriac.

Argued Against

  • argued against Bardaisan and the Bardaisanites Documented — Ephraim the Syrian wrote Prose Refutations against Mani, Marcion and Bardaisan. In them he names Bardaisan. He attacks what Bardaisan taught on the soul, on space and on matter.
  • argued against Marcion / Marcionism Documented — Ephraim the Syrian wrote prose discourses against Marcion and his followers. He names Marcion and attacks his teaching that a 'Stranger' God stands apart from the Maker.
  • argued against Manichaeism Documented — Ephraim the Syrian wrote prose discourses to Hypatius against Mani, Marcion and Bardaisan. In one he says he has 'opposed Mani also with a true refutation'. He attacks Mani's picture of God as a land of light.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

This is the church that sang its teaching in hymns - Ephrem's hymns carried its doctrine, and men and women bound themselves to a covenant to live unmarried and holy for God.

Legacy

Syriac is still a language of worship in the Assyrian Church of the East, the Syriac Orthodox Church, and the Eastern Catholic churches in union with Rome. All of them still sing Ephrem's hymns.

Within a generation of this tradition's own close, the Gospel harmony Edessa's later founding story pictures these communities reading gave way to a new translation, the Peshitta, and churches began reading the four Gospels separately again. Tradition credits Bishop Rabbula of Edessa (411–435) with the change.

Missionaries carried the faith east along the trade routes into Persia and India, and by the seventh century as far as Tang-dynasty China. Centuries later, Syriac translators became the channel through which Greek philosophy and medicine reached the Arabic-speaking world, and from there passed back into Europe.

A separate school of formal theological training later grew up at Nisibis—founded around 490, two generations after this tradition's own record closes—and became one of the ancient world's most important centers of Christian education, distinct from the teaching Jacob and Ephrem had given the same city a century earlier.

The name of the covenant lasted. The acts of the synod of 410 use it in the king's grant, and in the sixth century John of Ephesus still writes of women becoming "readers and daughters of the covenant." Edessa also kept the memory of Persia's martyrs: Sozomen says some sixteen thousand were known by name, with a multitude beyond counting, and that the people of Edessa took great care over their names.

Experience It Today

  • Mor Gabriel Monastery, Tur Abdin, southeastern Turkey — founded in 397 and still a working monastery and the seat of the metropolitan of Tur Abdin, one of the oldest continuously inhabited Christian monasteries on earth

Sources

Doctrinal Floor Note

This tradition holds the doctrine the Nicene Creed states. One figure inside it, the philosopher-poet Bardaisan, taught cosmological ideas his own later tradition repudiated; exactly where he stands relative to that doctrine remains an open question.

How Sure the Record Is

Rich in hymns and doctrinal teaching (Ephrem, Aphrahat's twenty-three Demonstrations); thin on ordinary believers, the daughters of the covenant's own words, and the years after Ephrem's death in 373.

  • Aphrahat, Select Demonstrations (primary) — Twenty-three dated letters written from inside the Persian empire, 336–345; the earliest firmly dated source for the covenant order and, in Demonstration 21, for the persecution itself.
  • Ephrem the Syrian, Nisibene Hymns (primary) — Hymns composed for the city Ephrem served until its surrender to Persia in 363, and for Edessa after his move there.
  • Tatian, The Diatessaron (primary) — A single continuous gospel woven from all four, used as the standard gospel text in Syriac churches for centuries.
  • Bardaisan of Edessa, The Book of the Laws of Divers Countries (primary) — The free-will dialogue, recorded by his student Philip; the one near-primary witness to the court philosopher Ephrem's own hymns were written to answer.
  • Eusebius, Church History I.13, IV.29, IV.30 (primary) — The earliest datable witness to the Abgar legend (as a document Eusebius himself accepted, which this Christian tradition's own record does not); also the earliest Greek notices of Tatian's harmony and Bardaisan's dialogues.
  • The Chronicle of Edessa (primary) — A sober civic-and-church record, compiled c. 540s from archives; its year-201 flood entry is the earliest hard evidence of a Christian community at Edessa.
  • The Doctrine of Addai (tradition) — This Christian tradition's own fuller telling of its Abgar-and-Addai foundation story — its self-understanding, not a historical record.
  • Theodoret, Ecclesiastical History II.26 (primary) — The remembered story of Bishop Jacob's prayer and the siege of Nisibis, written a century after the event.
  • Sozomen, Ecclesiastical History II.9–14, III.16 (primary) — The fullest surviving narrative of Shapur II's persecution of the Persian church, and an independent notice of Ephrem's famine relief in his last year.
  • Palladius, Lausiac History, ch. 40 (Ephraim) (primary) — The fullest account of Ephrem's famine relief and death, gathered about fifty years after the fact.
  • "Bardesanes and Bardesanites", Catholic Encyclopedia (reference) — Background on the Edessan philosopher-poet whose cosmology Ephrem's own hymns were written to refute.
  • Ute Possekel, "Bardaisan of Edessa: Philosopher or Theologian?", Zeitschrift für Antikes Christentum 10.3 (2006) (reference) — The strongest non-hostile modern reconstruction; even on this reading, Bardaisan's own cosmology likely diverges from bodily resurrection specifically.
  • Sebastian Brock, The Luminous Eye: The Spiritual World Vision of Saint Ephrem the Syrian (secondary) — Cistercian Studies 124, 1985 — by the scholar generally regarded as the leading authority on Ephrem and Syriac Christianity writing in English.
