The High Medieval Era · c. 1137-1270 · Ethiopian highlands

Ethiopian highlands, today in Ethiopia

Zagwe Ethiopian Christianity

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

After the old kingdom of Aksum faded, a new line of kings, the Zagwe, ruled the Ethiopian highlands from the twelfth century. Under them the church went on as it had for eight hundred years, with one odd feature. Its head bishop, the abun, was not Ethiopian but a monk sent from the Coptic patriarch in Egypt. So a free kingdom kept its senior churchman on loan from another land. The Zagwe are best known for eleven churches cut downward into solid rock. They stand at the town now called Lalibela, after the king who had them made. Several bear the names of holy places, such as Golgotha and Bethlehem. A Portuguese visitor in the 1520s was shown a tomb there said to copy Christ's tomb in Jerusalem. In 1270 Yekuno Amlak pushed the Zagwe aside, and his line claimed descent from Solomon and the Queen of Sheba. The Kebra Nagast, put together in the generations after he took power, set out that claim. Ethiopian kings leaned on it for the next seven centuries.

In Its Own Record

Continues

  • Post-Aksumite Ethiopian Christianity Documented Major — Abu Salih wrote in Egypt around 1200. He says the head of the Abyssinian church is still sent from Alexandria. He also tells how an earlier patriarch had that bishop stop the kings taking more than one wife. His editor dates this to about 1086.

Continued As

  • The Ethiopian Monastic Flowering Documented Major — The Life of the monk Takla Haymanot says that at fifteen he was made a deacon by Bishop Gerlos, 'in the kingdom of Zaga'. The same Life says Yekuno Amlak 'brought back the kingdom from the Zague'. The Life was written down after the saint's time, and some of its dates do not fit. It still shows one church carrying its saint across the change of kings.

In Contact With

  • Coptic Christianity, Fatimid to Mamluk Documented — Around 1200 a Coptic survey of Egypt's churches says the head bishop of Abyssinia is sent 'from the patriarch of Alexandria'. 'Abd al-Latif, writing in the same years, tells of an envoy from the Abyssinian king. The envoy reported the bishop's death and asked for a new one. A note in the Kebra Nagast names a Coptic book of 'the throne of Mark' in King Lalibela's time.

Sources

  • Kebra Nagast (The Glory of Kings) (trans. E. A. Wallis Budge, 1922; free online) (primary) — The Solomonic dynasty's charter myth, compiled and redacted in the early 14th century - royal ideology from just after this period, not the Zagwe's own self-description. Budge's translation is dated but remains the standard available edition.
  • Gadla Lalibela (Life of King Lalibela) (ed./French trans. Perruchon, 1892) (primary) — Zagwe royal hagiography. No English translation exists, and the Life was written after the king it praises died.
  • Lalibela's rock-hewn churches (material culture) + land grants and marginalia in gospel books (e.g. the Golden Gospel of Dabra Libanos) (primary) — The era's most eloquent contemporary witnesses are carved and written into stone and manuscript margins, not narrated. Covered mainly in specialist publications.
  • Taddesse Tamrat, Church and State in Ethiopia, 1270-1527 (Oxford, 1972) (secondary) — Available on archive.org. Still the field's standard frame, by an Ethiopian historian.
  • Marie-Laure Derat, L'enigme d'une dynastie sainte et usurpatrice: les rois zagwe (2018) (secondary) — Current Zagwe scholarship revising the Solomonic-propaganda picture. French only; no English translation.
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 5, 'Church and nation: the Ethiopian Orthodox Tawahedo church (from the thirteenth to the twentieth century)' (Crummey) (reference)
