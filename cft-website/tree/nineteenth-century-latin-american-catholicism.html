Nineteenth-Century Latin American Catholicism

Spanish America and Brazil, 1810 to 1906 — a church that lost its king, waited a generation for Rome to recognize its bishops, and was fought over by every new republic.

About This Tradition

Audio Brief 2:46

Read along

The Spanish crown had run the church in its American empire under a papal grant, appointing the bishops and collecting the tithes. When the colonies won independence the new republics claimed exactly the same right, and Rome — which had backed the crown — took decades to recognize either the governments or their nominees, leaving dioceses vacant and seminaries shut for a generation. What followed was a long fight between liberals and clericals over land, schools, cemeteries and marriage. Sharpest in Mexico, where the Reform laws of the 1850s stripped the church of its property and drove it out of public life, and loudest in Brazil, where two bishops were tried and imprisoned in the 1870s. Rome answered by tightening its grip, importing European clergy and religious orders, rebuilding seminaries on Roman lines, and in 1899 summoning the bishops of the whole continent to Rome for a first Latin American Plenary Council. Underneath all of it the devotional life of ordinary Catholics — confraternities, patronal feasts, pilgrimage shrines, household saints — carried on much as before, often with very few priests.

The rising of 1810 in Mexico began with a priest. He was Miguel Hidalgo, the priest of Dolores. A history printed in 1843 says he began the revolution on 15 September 1810. José María Morelos was the priest of Carácuaro. He left his parish on 25 October 1810. The Inquisition of Mexico later charged that the rebel priest of Dolores, Hidalgo, had sent him to raise troops in the south.

In November 1815 the Inquisition tried Morelos. It called him a degraded priest. It said he had joined «el hereje Cura de Dolores», the heretic priest of Dolores. It said this brought on him the excommunications of some bishops, and its own edicts of 1810 and 1811.

Another popular movement of the 1890s is known from a novel. Heriberto Frías's Tomóchic, in its edition of 1906, tells of a village in Chihuahua. Its people said the name of the Santa de Cabora, Teresa Urrea, with veneration. The novel follows a young officer in the army sent against them, and dates its scenes to October 1892. A novel is not a record of events.

Documented Stories

The Bishop of Olinda Goes to Prison

The church of imperial Brazil lived under the padroado: the emperor appointed the bishops and no papal document had force without his placet, and its parishes were run by lay brotherhoods that owned the churches and paid the priests, whose leading members were often Freemasons; Masonry in Brazil was respectable and the emperor's own ministers belonged to it. Dom Vital, a Capuchin trained in France, became bishop of Olinda in 1872 at twenty-eight, and set out to apply the papal condemnations of Masonry the placet had never admitted. He ordered the brotherhoods to expel their Masonic members; when they refused, he placed them under interdict, and Dom Antônio de Macedo Costa of Pará did the same. The brotherhoods appealed to the crown. The cabinet, whose head was grand master of the Grand Orient, ruled the interdicts void without the placet and ordered them lifted; both bishops refused. In January 1874 Dom Vital was arrested and taken to Rio de Janeiro; in February the Supreme Tribunal sentenced him to four years' hard labor, commuted by the emperor to fortress imprisonment; Macedo Costa followed in July. The emperor would not yield, and the bishops sat in the fortress nineteen months, until a new cabinet amnestied them in September 1875. Dom Vital, his health broken, went to Europe and died in Paris in 1878, aged thirty-three. The religious question, as Brazil called it, cost the empire the church's loyalty; when the republic came in 1889, the bishops did not mourn the emperor.

‘God Does Not Die’

Gabriel García Moreno had governed Ecuador since 1860 and built a state on principles no other ruler of the century attempted: a concordat of 1862 that gave the church control of education; a constitution of 1869 that made Catholicism a condition of citizenship; European religious orders to run schools and hospitals; roads and a treasury in order; and, in 1873, the consecration of the republic to the Sacred Heart of Jesus by act of congress. He also exiled, imprisoned and on occasion shot his opponents, and the continent's liberals regarded him as the clerical tyrant of the age. In 1875 he was re-elected for a third term. On the morning of 6 August 1875, having heard Mass in the cathedral of Quito as he did daily, he walked across the plaza to the government palace, and on its steps a Colombian named Faustino Rayo attacked him with a machete while three young liberals fired revolvers. He fell down the steps and was carried into the cathedral, where he died within the hour at the foot of an altar; tradition gives his last words as ‘Dios no muere’, God does not die. Rayo was shot by a soldier; the others fled. Pius IX, who had called him the model of a Catholic ruler, called him a martyr. Juan Montalvo, the liberal writer, said that his pen had killed him. Ecuador's liberals took power in 1895 and undid the rest; the cause for his beatification, opened and stalled, is still a quarrel.

Connections

In Its Own Record

Independence brought the patronato fights and Rome's slow recognition of the new governments. Liberal and clerical factions clashed, most sharply in Mexico's Reforma. Rome pursued the Romanization of the continent's church, culminating in the 1899 Latin American Plenary Council. Brazil's imperial church faced its own conflicts with the state.

Legacy and Today

Why It Is on the Tree

When Spanish America's new republics claimed the same right to appoint bishops the Spanish crown once held, Rome took a generation to recognize either the governments or their nominees, leaving dioceses vacant and seminaries shut. Mexico's Reform laws stripped the church of its property in the 1850s and Brazil imprisoned two of its own bishops in the 1870s, while ordinary Catholics kept their confraternities, patronal feasts and household saints going with far fewer priests than Europe had.

Legacy

This is the century that made Latin America's church what it is: a Roman-aligned hierarchy laid over a much older popular Catholicism, with far fewer priests per head than Europe had, and a long habit of arguing with the state. The 1899 council in Rome was the first time the continent's bishops met as a single body. They met again as a continent at Rio in 1955 and at Medellin in 1968. Latin America is still home to the largest share of the world's Catholics.

The evidence is moderate: patronato dossiers, records from the Reforma era, the acts of the 1899 Plenary Council, and documents from Brazil's religious question.
