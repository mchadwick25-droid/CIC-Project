The Missionary Era · c. 1770s-1906 · United States

The Black Church in America

About This Movement

Synthesized voice — not a recording.

Before the Civil War most Black Christians in America worshipped under white supervision. And made something else of it anyway, in praise houses, brush arbors and night meetings where the preaching, the singing and the theology were their own. Independent congregations appear from the 1770s (Silver Bluff in South Carolina, First African Baptist in Savannah in 1788), and in 1816 Richard Allen, who had walked out of a Philadelphia Methodist church with the rest of its Black members after a trustee pulled Absalom Jones up off his knees in the segregated gallery, organized the African Methodist Episcopal Church — the first Black denomination of national reach; AME Zion followed in 1821. Because the law in much of the South forbade the enslaved to read, the tradition's theology comes down to us largely in what it sang and told: the spirituals, conversion accounts, and self-published narratives like Jarena Lee's. By 1906 it was a whole world of its own. Denominations, colleges, newspapers and a worship life that was about to reshape global Christianity from a mission on Azusa Street.

In 1874 a church in the United States made a bishop for Haiti, and its mission board asked that he be of the African race. The Spirit of Missions for December 1874, the journal of the Protestant Episcopal Church's mission board, prints a triennial report on the Haitian church. It is signed by J. Theodore Holly as Dean, at Port-au-Prince on 30 June 1874, but Holly's own part is a table of districts and his signature. The story around it is the board's.

The board also wanted him to be a Haitian citizen. The Dean of the Haitian Convocation came to make a covenant with the American church. By it, the Church in Haiti was a foreign church, still aided by the American one.

The Haitian church promised to keep the doctrine, worship and discipline of the American church. The House of Bishops chose Holly as the first bishop. He was consecrated in Grace Church, New York, on 8 November 1874.

In Its Own Record

Grew Out Of

  • The Baptists Widely Accepted Major — George Liele was baptized in a Baptist church in Georgia, and its white members called him to preach. David George recalled that "Brother Palmer formed us into a church" at Silver Bluff. Historians treat such Black Baptist congregations as one root of the Black Church.
  • The Methodist Revival Documented Major — Richard Allen joined a Methodist society in Delaware. Later his Black church in Philadelphia fell out with the white elders sent to lead it. In Baltimore, too, white preachers and trustees drove many Black members away. In April 1816 Black delegates from both towns and others met. They agreed to form one body of their own. They named it the African Methodist Episcopal Church.

Continued As

  • The Black Church in the Twentieth Century Dominant Modern Reconstruction Major — In 1816 Black Methodists in Philadelphia and Baltimore formed a church of their own. It was the African Methodist Episcopal Church. It lived on in the next century, as did the AME Zion Church and the Black Baptist churches. Historians see the Black church of that century as the same tradition carried on. That church led much of the civil rights movement.

Influenced

  • Azusa Street & Early Pentecostalism Dominant Modern Reconstruction — The Azusa Street revival began in Los Angeles in 1906. Its leader, William J. Seymour, was a Black Holiness preacher. Historians trace much of its worship, its songs and its testimony to the Black church.

Broke With

  • Nineteenth-Century Methodism Documented Major — Richard Allen tells how white Methodist elders fought his Bethel church for its pulpit. One elder sent out a letter that cast its members out. Another took Bethel to court and lost. In April 1816 Black Methodists from Bethel and from Baltimore formed a church of their own, the African Methodist Episcopal Church.

In Contact With

  • The Missionary Movement's Native Churches (Serampore India; Sierra Leone & the Niger; Pacific Islands; China Coast & CIM) Inferential-Thin — About 1815 Lott Cary helped rouse the Black Christians of Richmond to care for Africa. Taylor's biography of 1837 says a society grew from it and gave money for several years. Cary himself sailed to West Africa in 1821. This rests on one pastor's book.

Sources

  • Richard Allen, The Life Experience and Gospel Labors (pub. 1833); the 1794 yellow-fever vindication pamphlet (with Absalom Jones); AME Doctrines and Discipline (1817) and General Conference minutes; Daniel Coker, A Dialogue between a Virginian and an African Minister (1810) (primary) — The founding bishop's own narrative and the church's own records from the start.
  • Jarena Lee, The Life and Religious Experience of Jarena Lee (1836; expanded 1849); Zilpha Elaw, Memoirs (1846); Julia Foote, A Brand Plucked from the Fire (1879) (primary) — Black women preachers' own narratives (Lee self-published, with Allen's authorization recorded in her own words), collected in Andrews's Sisters of the Spirit.
  • Slave narratives as primary religious texts: Douglass, Narrative (1845, with the appendix on slaveholding religion); Sojourner Truth, Narrative (1850 - dictated); God Struck Me Dead (Fisk interviews, 1920s); WPA ex-slave narratives (1930s) (primary) — These narratives carry real editorial filters: abolitionist framing, dictation rather than self-authorship in some cases, and interviewer bias. The Fisk and WPA interviews were recorded in the 1920s and 1930s, decades after the events they describe.
  • Spirituals as communal theology: Slave Songs of the United States (1867) and the Fisk Jubilee Singers' repertoire; with David Walker, Appeal (1829) and The Confessions of Nat Turner (1831, recorded by Thomas R. Gray) (primary) — The spirituals survive here through white transcription and concert-stage arrangement, both real filters on the original oral form. Walker's Appeal is a direct work of Black prophetic writing. Turner's Confessions were recorded by Thomas R. Gray, a white lawyer, from a captive man facing execution - a hostile and coercive context for the text.
  • Albert J. Raboteau, Slave Religion (OUP, 1978; updated 2004), with Milton Sernett (ed.), African American Religious History: A Documentary Witness (Duke, 2nd ed. 1999) (secondary)
  • Cambridge History of Christianity vol. 8, ch. 22 'Christian America' and 'Christian Canada' (Noll) (reference) — This reference volume has no dedicated chapter on the Black church; chapter 35 (Kalu) touches on the Atlantic diaspora as related context.
  • The Spirit of Missions, vol. 39 (December 1874): the Triennial Report of the Missionary Jurisdiction of Haiti for 1872-74, signed by J. Theodore Holly as Dean, and the Board's account of the Haitien Episcopate (primary) — Holly's own part is a table and his signature; the narrative is the Mission Board's, an American Episcopal view of the Haitian church.
