claim: "Theophilus of Antioch's triad of \"God, and His Word, and His wisdom\" (c. 169-181 CE per the edition's own dating, with an alternative tradition the same edition also records extending to 188 - the earliest surviving use of the Greek word Τριάδος/\"Trinity\") is the same referent as the developed Trinitarian doctrine later formalized at Nicaea (325) and after - three co-equal, co-eternal persons in one being."
held_against:
- "The edition's own footnote on Theophilus II.15 reads his usage as unremarkable to him (\"illustrating an accepted word, not introducing a new one\"), not as a first statement of a developed doctrine; the same footnote also notes that Theophilus's title for the third member is not fixed - here he calls it \"wisdom\" (σοφία) and assigns it to the Holy Spirit, but elsewhere in his own work (Book II, chapter x) he gives that same title to the Son - which is not how the later formula's fixed, co-equal, co-eternal persons are distinguished."
- "Tertullian's own Against Praxeas (a.d. 208 or later, per the edition's own floor date) is closer in time to Theophilus than to Nicaea, and is itself still arguing the question - one substance, three Persons, against a rival Monarchian reading - rather than reporting a settled formula; a doctrine still being argued in the early third century is evidence that Theophilus's triad, decades earlier still, cannot simply be read as the same settled referent Nicaea later formalizes."
- "Reading the word's early appearances as continuous with the later doctrine risks projecting the fourth-century formula backward onto a second-century author who never states co-equality or co-eternity as such."
concedes: "The word's continuity is not in dispute: Theophilus, Tertullian, and Nicaea are all naming a threefold subject - Father/God, Son/Word, Spirit/Wisdom - and each later use draws on the same word Theophilus's own edition calls the earliest surviving one, already, in the editor's own reading, familiar rather than new (\"illustrating an accepted word, not introducing a new one\") - not a word he coined for the occasion. The same footnote quotes an eminent authority (Kaye) stating the claim's own strongest case directly: \"it is certain, that, according to the notions of Theophilus, God, His Word, and His wisdom constitute a Trinity; and it should seem a Trinity of persons.\" What is contested is only whether the referent named stayed constant from that reading to Nicaea, or whether the word survived while the doctrine it names developed substantially underneath it. This record does not resolve that question; _fleet.modern.trinity's own distinguishing_claim states both the word's early date and the doctrine's later formation without asserting either reading of the referent question."

Carries the scholarly question _fleet.modern.trinity's own
distinguishing_claim deliberately leaves open: the word "Trinity" is
attested well before 325, but whether Theophilus's own second-century
triad is the same referent as the doctrine Nicaea later formalizes is
not settled by that fact alone, and this record does not settle it
either - it holds the case each way, from the same edition's own
footnote, so neither an "always meant the same thing" reading nor a
"never related" reading is asserted where the sources themselves
disagree. formation_confidence: Contested because the dispute is over
interpretation of textual evidence both sides agree on, not over the
evidence itself.
