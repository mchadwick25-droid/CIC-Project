# Blind packet for the world: Latin North African church, c. 200-430 (Cyprian to Augustine), pastoral-congregational tradition

## Item 1
**Question put to the representative:** Explain how leadership and authority were established within your community.

**Answer given:**
We were held by one office, the office of bishop, and that office was understood as a charge to keep the particular people placed in a man's care. Authority among us ran two ways at once. A bishop answered for his flock, and his flock could hold him to it.

A bishop was put into office by two things together: the people's own voice, and God's judgment made visible through it. When Cyprian was elected at Carthage, he had been a Christian only a short time. Five presbyters opposed him. The people chose him anyway. Pontius, who knew him, said that God's judgment and the people's favour made him bishop. Cyprian himself later called it suffrage. This was not a ballot with a fixed procedure, but neither was it only consent afterward. The people could stand in front of a bishop's own seat and refuse to leave until he heard them.

When Augustine became bishop at Hippo, the people's voice figured in it too, though we do not claim the same word or form held in both phases.

For us, the episcopate is one office, shared by every bishop. Each one holds his part on behalf of the whole. A bishop is not an officer of some wider institution, dropped into a posting. He is given a people, in one place, and he answers for them. He preaches to them and baptizes them, and when they fail it is not a problem he manages but a wound he carries.

Augustine traced the office back to the apostles by naming the line of Roman bishops from Peter to the present, and Cyprian gave the principle that carries that line forward.

Among bishops themselves, we hold two rulings, and they are not the same. Cyprian, opening the council of 256, said no bishop sets himself up as a bishop of bishops. None forces a colleague to obey. Every bishop has his own proper right of judgment, and only Christ judges him. At that same council, he said we judge no one, and reject no one from communion, for thinking differently from us.

Augustine held a different rule. Small councils yield to wider ones. Even the widest council can later be corrected.

These are real, substantial differences. We do not consider them fully settled. The one bond both bishops answer to holds the disagreement inside it, but we do not pretend the disagreement is not there.

The church Cyprian led was organized enough to hold real factions and to call councils of dozens of bishops. It could run a ruled process of penance, which a loose community could not have done. And because it was organized, bishops who disagreed could still keep communion.

One bishop could write to eight others and send them a hundred thousand sesterces to ransom captives.

Authority was also checked by a claim that pressed it hard. In Cyprian's time, those who had confessed Christ and suffered for it held that their suffering gave them standing to ask peace for some who had failed. Celerinus, a confessor, wrote to another confessor, Lucian, who was in prison. He asked Lucian to help his own sister, and two other women, Numeria and Candida. Lucian granted the peace they asked for, on one condition: each case had to be set before the bishop.

We took the confessors' claim in earnest, and we also answered it by something steadier: a name set down, then examined before the whole congregation. We have never found the place where these become one settled rule, and we do not expect to.

The main way our teaching reached an ordinary believer was through preaching. You were in the room, baptized or still learning, and again and again the same voice worked on you. It was not a lecture and not a show. It was the man in charge of you, telling you to your face what he thought you needed.

Augustine once told the people who came to hear him: “But now I imagine that no one has come here except those who want to hear. So I am not speaking to deaf hearts, or to minds that will look down on the word. Instead, this longing expectation of yours is a prayer for me.”

You were also taught before you were washed. We told you what you were joining before you joined it. You said the creed back, and then we baptized you. Augustine traced infant baptism and the refusal to rebaptize back to the apostles, though no apostle wrote it down. A custom this old and this universal, he held, is rightly believed to have come from the apostles.

Nearly our whole record comes from two bishops, across 184 years. Women appear throughout it, but almost always in someone else's words. No text a woman wrote herself survives from us. One martyr account gives a woman's vision in her own first person: Quartillosia, held in prison, told what she saw. That is one voice, kept inside a text men wrote, and not a body of women's writing.

## Item 2
**Question put to the representative:** Did your community hold that a person enters the world already carrying guilt?

**Answer given:**
We held that a newborn does not carry guilt for his own sins, since he has not yet done any. What he carries is something older. Cyprian and sixty-five other bishops met in council and wrote to Fidus about it. Augustine went on to the hard end of this. What the child carries is not his own doing. It is the old death's contagion, passed to him at birth.

We did not make a newborn wait. We brought them to the water as soon as we could.

## Item 3
**Question put to the representative:** Describe the character and nature of God as your community understood it.

**Answer given:**
We understand God as the one who holds everything together, not by pretending our failures do not exist, but by making a road back through them. He is the one who placed these particular people in our care and will ask us what became of them. That is not an abstraction to us. It is the fact our whole office stands on.

God's mercy, we taught, is not a refusal to judge. It is the making of a road where there was none. They said this about newborns, who carry no sin of their own but only what they inherited. Augustine taught the same from Paul's own words. Paul asked God three times to take away a thorn in his flesh. God did not take it away. Paul was heard for his salvation, Augustine taught, even though he was not heard according to his wish. That became a rule for all the saints: they are always heard in what concerns their salvation, even when they are not heard as they wished.

What God gives is never ours to claim as though we earned it. Augustine taught that no one has the love of God from himself: “Indeed, he had said, ‘The love of God has been poured out in our hearts.’ Then he added at once, ‘through the Holy Spirit, who has been given to us.’ He did this so that no one would think his love for God came from himself. So if you are to love God, let God live in you and love Himself through you. That is, let Him move you to His love. Let Him kindle you, give you light and stir you up.”

Whatever good a person does was given to them before they did it. That is what we mean by grace: no effort of ours is enough on its own.

And God judges. He did not flinch from what was coming. He had taught his people that God would hold them to account, and he let God hold him to the same measure.

## Item 4
**Question put to the representative:** Was there space in your community for someone who struggled to believe?

**Answer given:**
There was, and we knew that struggle from the inside. One of our own bishops told it himself, years later, when he wrote the story of his life. Years later, close to his own conversion, the truth had become certain to him, yet the old burden had not lifted. We do not promise you that struggle resolves on any set schedule. We tell you it was real, in one of our own, and that it was not the end of his story.

He told them their longing expectation was itself a prayer for him. That restlessness was not something we judged out of existence. It was something we named, plainly, as part of being formed.

## Item 5
**Question put to the representative:** Every community has failed people at times - how did yours respond when it failed someone?

**Answer given:**
We did fail people, and more than once. Twice in our earliest years a rival bishop appeared in the same city, under our own bishop's nose. In Carthage, a deacon named Felicissimus gathered a faction apart while Cyprian was away in hiding. At Rome, Novatian was set up as a rival bishop over a disputed election. Both were separations from the inside, not arguments that held. We do not pretend they never happened.

How did we respond? Not always well, and not always in time. But we built something out of what those breaks taught us. The road back for a failed member became a public, examined thing -- not a formality, not a closed door. Name set down, waiting that was real, restoration where the same people who watched the failure received the person home. That came, in part, from having watched the separations break what the easy grant could not hold together.

And we held one rule over everything else, even across the hardest disagreements. At the council of 256, Cyprian said we judge no one, and reject no one from communion, for thinking differently from us. A century and a half later, Augustine argued at book length that the ruling made that day was wrong. He never once put Cyprian outside our communion.

Those failures -- the separations, the too-easy grants that let too many in at once without examination -- they are in our record because we could not make them go away. What we made instead was the discipline, and the refusal to break over a disagreement. That is our response.

## Item 6
**Question put to the representative:** How did your community understand and mark marriage?

**Answer given:**
We understood marriage as carrying a real threefold good, and we marked it as a bond that lasts. Augustine named the three goods directly: offspring, faithfulness between the two, and what he called its sacrament. Of that third he wrote this: the marriage compact is entered into as a matter of a certain sacrament, and is not made void even by separation. So long as her husband lives, a wife who marries another while separated from him commits adultery.

For us, marriage was not dissolved by the partners' separation. The bond held as long as both lived. That is what we meant when we said it carried something of a sacrament.

What we cannot tell you is how the wedding itself was performed, or what words were said at it. Augustine's treatise defends marriage as a real good. It names what marriage is and what it binds. It does not describe the rite that makes it.

Among us, some were rich. Cyprian wrote a whole treatise on almsgiving, and much of it speaks to believers who had money. That much we know was said to married believers of means. But how courtship was conducted, or what made a match fitting, the record does not say.

## Item 7
**Question put to the representative:** What physical evidence would remain of your community's gathering place?

**Answer given:**
We can describe what one of our own bishops saw and named, but not what a trench would find.

Augustine wrote to the people of Fussala about a basilica there. He described an apse, a raised place where the clergy sat, steps, and a floor where the congregation stood. That comes from a letter, not from the ground itself.

We cannot tell you what an excavation would show. No site report or inscription catalogue has been read to answer that. Real material evidence exists in this region, but it has not been weighed for what it would say about our own worship.

The one thing we can say is this: what survives as evidence is document-borne, carried in a pastor's own words about a building he saw, not recovered from stone.

## Item 8
**Question put to the representative:** If a modern scholar examined your claims, how much would survive rigorous scrutiny?

**Answer given:**
We hold ourselves to a standard most scholarship does not reach, and we name our limits plainly where the record runs thin. A scholar examining our claims would find this:

Every sentence that makes a specific claim carries the record it draws on, inline, before the period. We copy each record's own id exactly, from the section heading or this turn's evidence block. We never assemble an address from what a heading seems to say. Where no id fits, the sentence carries no tag - an untagged sentence is our own standing instruction or an ordinary connection, not a claim about the world.

What would survive scrutiny is this. We rest on two bishops' own words, written at enormous length, across 184 years. Both speak as bishops of this tradition, not as quotes in an opponent's book. That is not a hostile-source problem. What is thin is not the tradition's own record but the record of anyone who was not a bishop. We say so, openly.

We name nine places where the ground is thin and will not claim past them. We have no ordinary believer's own account of an ordinary week. We have no woman's writing in her own hand. We have no excavation report verified in this build. And we carry forward one unresolved question: what councils can decide. Cyprian and Augustine disagreed, substantially, and we do not settle which was right.

A scholar would also find this. We refuse to borrow vividness from outside our own formation. Not Tertullian's words, though he forged the Latin we argue in. Not the Scillitan martyrs, though they come from the same region. Not the Donatist sources that fill the century we go silent in. If the fitting image does not come from what actually formed us, we do not reach past it.

What a rigorous examination would test is not whether we have everything, but whether we honestly name what we lack. We do. We name it nine ways, and we speak our limits in voice, at the point in the answer where they bear - never announced ahead, and never apologized for as though honesty were failure.

## Item 9
**Question put to the representative:** Would your community describe conversion using language like being 'born again'?

**Answer given:**
We would not use those exact words to name what happened, no. That is not the way our own record speaks of it.

What we say is this: you are washed, and you walk in a life made new. You are buried with Christ in the water, and his rising becomes the shape your own life takes. That is not a single moment you name and set aside. It is the pattern you are cut to, and it goes on working itself out in how you live.

Before the water, we teach you the creed. Augustine told those preparing for baptism to take the creed as a mirror: “Call your faith to mind. Look into yourself. Let your Creed be like a mirror to you. See yourself in it, and see whether you believe all that you declare you believe. And rejoice in your faith every day. Let it be your wealth. Let it be, in a way, the daily clothing of your mind.”

That is the work done before you come to the font, not after it.

One of our own bishops did write of his own conversion at length, years afterward. Augustine told of hearing a voice in a garden, taking up Scripture, and weeping greatly in the hymns and canticles at his baptism. But he was already a trained teacher when it happened, and he wrote it down as a bishop, examining at book length what had brought him there. That is not an ordinary believer's own week. It is one uncommon life, told by the man himself after he had become a bishop.

We cannot tell you what an ordinary person felt walking up to the font, or sitting in the congregation on a plain Sunday. Both of our voices are bishops. What reaches us is mostly what a bishop saw and decided, not what someone unremarkable in front of him was feeling.

## Item 10
**Question put to the representative:** What, if anything, did people give up to belong to your community?

**Answer given:**
The question presses on what the certificate forced a believer to choose. We will answer from what the demand itself was, and from what one of our own bishops said about those who gave in to it.

The Decian edict ordered a sacrifice to the Roman gods, done in the open and recorded on paper. The demand reached each person alone. You could perform it, or you could pay someone to record that you had performed it. Either way, you walked away with the certificate in hand, and the whole congregation knew which people did and which did not.

Cyprian called the paper itself a denial, even when no sacrifice was made. He wrote that their hands were clean, but their conscience was not. So the certificate alone made a person one of the lapsed. What a person gave up was not a private conviction. It was visible membership in the body that would have to decide whether to receive them back.

The suffering of those who stood was real. So was the grief of those who gave way. Celerinus, a confessor, wrote to another confessor, Lucian, and told him he was in great trouble. His own sister had sacrificed, and he grieved for her as for a death, a death of the spirit.

The table was emptied one certificate at a time, and those who remained had to decide what to do with those who came back asking to be received.
