# Blind packet for the world: Lutheran Wittenberg and its congregations, c. 1517-1580

## Item 1
**Question put to the representative:** What, if anything, did people give up to belong to your community?

**Answer given:**
What a person gave up to belong to us depended on where they stood before they came.

If they held a conscience bound by traditions that claimed to earn grace, they gave up those bonds, though the tradition itself might still be kept freely. If they came from a monastic vow they believed they could not keep, they gave up that vow, since we held that marriage was the noblest estate for nearly everyone and that the monastic claim to perfection was false. A priest who married gave up his place under the old order, and in some places gave up his life — our one martyrology remembers two young monks burned at Brussels for refusing to take back what they had come to believe.

They gave up praying to the saints, though the saints were still remembered and imitated. They gave up the claim that their own works could earn grace, though good works still followed faith and were commanded by God. They gave up private masses, which we ended, though we kept public worship with great reverence.

What they did not give up was harder to see from the outside. They gave up certainty that came from their own effort, and learned instead to rest on a promise spoken from outside them. They gave up the right to judge Scripture by tradition, and held instead that Scripture judged every other voice.

And every household took up what had not been asked before. A father questioned his family and servants at least once a week on the three parts. Morning, table, and night, the catechism was said. Whether every household actually kept that program, we cannot say from the inside. We hold only the program itself, and our founder's own complaint that people held the Gospel too lightly.

## Item 2
**Question put to the representative:** What physical evidence would remain of your community's gathering place?

**Answer given:**
We cannot say what someone would find if they dug up the place we met. None of our texts describes a building, a site, or an object recovered from the ground. We are not the right voice to guess at what an excavation would turn up.

What we hold instead is what we did with our hands, our print, and our song. We printed books and catechisms. We sang hymns in German inside the Mass, with Latin kept beside it. We kept the altar and the font. But we hold no object and no outside witness to confirm any of it beyond our own claim.

The texts themselves are what survived, and that survival was shaped by selection, not suppression. No force in the evidence threatened the survival of our writings. The printers of Wittenberg and other towns passed them on, as did the hymnal compilers. Our founder himself was a reluctant collector, and wished his books forgotten. He hoped the Bible would have kept its place in the pulpit.

So the physical evidence, if any remains, is not something we can describe. What we can tell you is that the Word was printed, the hymns were sung, and the catechism was taught in households. That is what our record holds.

## Item 3
**Question put to the representative:** If a modern scholar examined your claims, how much would survive rigorous scrutiny?

**Answer given:**
We would expect three parts of what we say to survive rigorous scrutiny, one part to face real questions, and one gap to remain exactly where we name it.

What would survive: First, the texts themselves. We cite only what is vendored and checked. Where we quote, we mark the record by its id, and the system places its modern rendering word for word. A scholar could verify that the text we cite says what we claim it says. Second, the household program. We can show you exactly what a father was told to ask his children each week, at what hour, with what words. That prescription is fully documented. Third, our honest limits. We state plainly that the Saxon visitation reports exist but are not held here. We say no woman's own writing survives. We do not smooth over the 1525 writings we hold or the 1543 treatise we do not. A scholar examining our limits would find we named them accurately.

What would face questions: our reconstructed scenes. When we walk you through a morning in a household, we build it entirely from the catechism's own prescription. Every piece is documented. But no diary, no letter from inside a home, and no visitation report in our record confirms that any household kept it this way. A scholar could challenge whether the scene represents lived reality or only an ideal. We hold it as prescription, never as verified practice. The open question is real.

What would remain contested among scholars: two doctrinal readings we do not settle. Whether "accounted righteous" and "made righteous" are two claims or one claim said two ways, our text states both and ranks neither. Whether the two-kingdoms language names one doctrine or two distinct pairings, our founder's own words leave open. We do not resolve these. A scholar examining them would find us holding the question rather than answering it.

The gap that would remain: whether the household program was received. We hold the program in full. We hold our founder's testimony that people were cold and pastors negligent. We do not hold any village's own report of itself. A historian could not settle from our record whether households kept what they were told to do. Neither can we.

## Item 4
**Question put to the representative:** Every community has failed people at times - how did yours respond when it failed someone?

**Answer given:**
We do not claim we never failed anyone, and we will not pretend our record shows us answering that failure well. What we can tell you is what our own founder said about it, more than once, in his own voice. He did not speak of strangers. He spoke of pastors and preachers of our own. In 1522 he told his own Wittenberg congregation that an ass can almost intone the lessons, because God wants doers, not repeaters of words. In 1529, in his Large Catechism, he said plainly that many pastors were negligent, and that ordinary people held the Gospel far too lightly. That is not a stranger's accusation. That is our own founder's own complaint, said in his own voice, more than once.

At his own table, his wife Katharina asked why some prayed with such fervor under the old system while we, under the new one, prayed so coldly and carelessly. Our founder answered her plainly: the devil drives his own servants hard, and they take great pains at their false worship, while we, at the true one, grow ice-cold and negligent.

That testimony is real, and it came more than once. But it is testimony. No report from an actual parish stands beside it to check it against. The Saxon church inspected its own parishes from 1527 and wrote down what it found. Those reports exist. We do not hold them. So we cannot tell you whether he was right about how bad it really was, only that he believed it and said so.

What we do not hold is any account of a household or a congregation responding to that failure, admitting it, and mending it. We hold only the complaint itself. Whether anyone among us heard it and changed, our record does not say.

## Item 5
**Question put to the representative:** Given that a key outside record of your worship was obtained under torture, how reliable is that source?

**Answer given:**
We must be honest: no record we hold says that any account of our worship was obtained under torture. What we have is this. Rome replied in writing to our own confession, and that reply reaches us only at one remove, quoted and argued against inside our own Apology. We never have it in its own words. That is the nearest thing we hold to an outsider's account of our worship.

Two young monks, John and Henry, were burned at Brussels in 1523 after theologians from Louvain pressed them to take back a teaching they had come to hold, and they refused. Our founder wrote a ballad about it within months. That ballad is our only martyrology. It says they were stripped of their monks' habits and became, by grace, true priests of God's own making. The ballad itself is not an independent witness. It is our founder's own commemorative voice, not a transcript of the burning or the questioning before it.

Whether torture was used to press them, or to extract any other account of what we believed or how we worshipped, no text we hold says. We simply do not have evidence of it, in either direction.

## Item 6
**Question put to the representative:** When your people disagreed about doctrine, who settled it, and what evidence tells us that process actually worked that way?

**Answer given:**
When belief was disputed among us, the form the dispute took may surprise you. No council of bishops sat and voted on it the way you may be picturing. What we held instead was this: confession, written reply, and written defense - all of it argued from Scripture, none of it settled by a show of hands.

The clearest case we hold comes from 1530, at Augsburg before the Emperor himself. A confession of our teaching, written by Melanchthon, was read aloud and signed. It was not signed by one preacher alone. An Elector, princes, and two city councils signed it together. Theologians speaking for Rome answered it in writing. We answered that answer in turn, point by point, in a second document we call the Apology. That is how the dispute moved: written confession, written reply, written defense. Argued it was. Voted on it was not.

Now, the harder part of your question: what evidence shows us this process actually worked? We do not hold the evidence that would answer that the way you are asking it. We hold the confessional documents themselves. The Saxon church inspected its own parishes from 1527 and wrote down what it found. Those reports exist. We do not hold them. We can tell you what form the doctrinal dispute took. We cannot show you from the inside that it settled anything for any actual congregation.

## Item 7
**Question put to the representative:** For someone whose mind won't settle, does your community's practice offer anything?

**Answer given:**
We would not say we offer a technique for quieting a restless mind. What we carried was a promise spoken plainly into a person's ears, and a daily round that returned to it.

The catechism was said at rising, at meals, and at night. The same three parts — the Commandments, the Creed, the Lord's Prayer — were said every day, for a whole life. No one outgrew them, not even our founder. A father asked his household once a week what they meant, not only whether they could be repeated back. That rhythm was the daily shape we prescribed, whether any household kept it exactly that way or let it slide.

Repentance, for us, was not one ritual handled by a priest. It was a whole life turned toward God, in two parts: fear under the Law, then trust in the promise. The promise itself was held outside the self — in the Word, in the water, in the bread, in the absolution heard as though from God himself. We simply received it. We never offered it back as a sacrifice.

Good works, for us, were ordinary things done in faith: working, walking, eating, sleeping. We did them because God commands them, never to earn anything. That made a whole life holy, not only the hour set aside for prayer.

What we cannot tell you is whether any of this actually settled a restless mind. We hold the program. We do not hold a single believer's own testimony that it worked for them the way we hoped.

## Item 8
**Question put to the representative:** Would your community describe conversion using language like being 'born again'?

**Answer given:**
We would, and we would not. Both are true, and the difference matters.

We locate new birth differently than you may expect: at the font, not only at a later, felt moment. The same sentence in our confession that names us "born with sin" names the remedy. So baptism itself is where we are born again, and we baptized children. A child offered to God through baptism was received into his grace on exactly that ground.

But that new birth is not one moment only. Our founder wrote that baptism is a death begun that lasts a whole life. Our catechism says the old self is drowned and a new self rises, not once only, but daily. Every morning a household was taught to bless itself with the sign of the cross and pray. The drowning and the rising, begun at the font, continued every day.

So yes, we speak of being born again. It happens at baptism, given to children and not only adults, and it happens daily. It is not one felt conversion moment you look back on. It is a death and a rising that lasts a life.

## Item 9
**Question put to the representative:** What sources, if any, connected your community to Jesus, and by what path did they arrive?

**Answer given:**
What we have of Jesus is the Word — his own speech, the good news of Christ, carried in Scripture. We did not invent it and we did not discover it freshly ourselves. It reached us the way the Word always reaches anyone, our own founder says: preached, taught, and written down, not argued into being by our own effort. Before him, that same Word had lain, in his own words, forgotten in the dust under the bench — covered by teaching that was not its own. We believed it came back because Scripture's own authority was heard again over every other voice, ours included.

None of us knew anyone who saw him. We do not pretend otherwise. More than fourteen hundred years lie between his days and ours, and none of us claimed a living chain of witnesses back to that time. What we have instead is the Word itself, and our own confession of what it says.

That Word was carried to us through preaching and through print. Every household was to teach its own the three parts. The catechism itself says the three parts contain fully and completely everything that is in the Scriptures. A father questioned his children and servants at least once a week on what they knew. After every part he asked the catechism's own question, what does this mean, so a truth was explained and not merely repeated back. The promise itself could not be forced on a conscience. It was carried to the ears where it could be heard.

## Item 10
**Question put to the representative:** Is it accurate that believers regularly concealed themselves underground?

**Answer given:**
No. That question belongs to a much earlier age of the Church, many centuries before our own founder was born. It is not ours to answer from inside. Nothing in our own record touches it.

We stood openly. Our founder preached from a pulpit. Our confession was read aloud before the Emperor himself at Augsburg in 1530, and an elector, princes, and two city councils signed it together. They called themselves his faithful subjects. Our founder returned from the Wartburg against his prince's own wishes and preached eight days running to his own congregation at Wittenberg. That was open defiance, not concealment. Our teaching was printed, our hymns were sung in the language of the people, and our catechism was taught in households where a father questioned his children and servants each week. None of that was done in hiding.

We did face real danger. Our founder was warned on the road that he was already condemned, and he went to Worms anyway. Two young monks were burned at Brussels in 1523 for refusing to take back what they had come to believe. But that danger was not met by going underground. It was met by standing openly on the Word, at great cost when the cost came.
