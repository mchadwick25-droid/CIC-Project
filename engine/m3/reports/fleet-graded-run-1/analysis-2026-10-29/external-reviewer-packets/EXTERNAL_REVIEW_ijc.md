# The 28 answers


## Answer 1  (cell C-I, 182 words)
**Question:** In your own words, describe who Jesus was for your community.

**Answer:**

To us, Jesus was the one the whole argument was about, and the argument itself was our devotion. The creed of Nicaea, in 325, answers who he was: very God of very God, of one being with the Father. A century of councils spent itself making those words exact, because everything hung on them. Leo's letter explains why the exactness mattered. He wrote that it was equally perilous to believe Christ only God without man, or only man without God. He wrote of true God and true man, each nature doing what belongs to it. So one Mediator could die in one nature and not die in the other. We also taught that the bread and cup became his Body and Blood, by the power of blessing that changes nature itself. Ambrose said it plainly to the newly baptized: before the blessing of the heavenly words, another nature is spoken of; after the consecration, the Body is meant. We did not write devotional memoirs about Jesus. We wrote definitions, the way people write what they are prepared to be exiled over.

## Answer 2  (cell C-E, 187 words)
**Question:** What sources, if any, connected your community to Jesus, and by what path did they arrive?

**Answer:**

Our scriptures, the faith we confessed at baptism, and the creed. Everything we had about Jesus came to us by inheritance. Nicaea met nearly three hundred years after Jesus, and what we had of him reached us through others. Our councils wrote the creed exactly so that the inheritance could not be lost or changed.

Chalcedon shows us doing it. When the judges asked the assembly to declare its faith, Rome's legate Paschasinus spoke first. He recited the whole chain out loud: the rule of faith was set out at Nicaea, confirmed at Constantinople, explained at Ephesus by Cyril, and shown in Leo's own writings. The assembled bishops then cried out together: so we all believe, so we were baptized, so we baptize.

We knew the resurrection happened the same way: through the apostles' testimony, carried in scripture and confession, received and held. We guarded the testimony we received, and could not add to it. We had no living memory of eyewitnesses. What reached us was their witness, transmitted through baptism and creed, generation to generation, jealously kept exact because it was all we had.

## Answer 3  (cell C-P, 233 words)
**Question:** Someone tells you they cannot manage to believe in Jesus - what is your response to them?

**Answer:**

We hear you, and we will not rush an answer past that. Our record has the church's public words about Jesus - creeds, councils, letters between office-holders. It does not keep one ordinary heart's own struggle to believe. We cannot show you a voice like yours from inside it, wrestling as you are. Our record does not hold those pages, for you or for anyone before you.

What we can tell you is what some of our own men went into exile rather than take back. The creed of Nicaea says he is very God of very God, of one being with the Father, who for us and for our salvation came down and was made man, suffered, and rose on the third day. A century of councils spent itself making those words exact, because everything hung on them. That is at least proof of how much it cost them to hold.

And we can give you the nearest thing we have to an answer for someone not yet certain. At Christmas, Leo, our bishop of Rome, preached: “Let the saint rejoice: he draws near to victory. Let the sinner be glad: he is invited to pardon. Let the gentile take courage: he is called to life.”

We do not know if that reaches you where you are. We only know it was said without waiting for anyone's certainty first.

## Answer 4  (cell C-T, 219 words)
**Question:** Explain your community's understanding of Jesus's divine status and its relation to God the Father and the Spirit.

**Answer:**

To us, Jesus is truly God, of one being with the Father - homoousios - sharing the very being that makes the Father God.

The Father is God, the Son is God, the Spirit is God - yet we confess one God, not three. Constantinople in 381 confessed the Spirit's full divinity alongside the Father and the Son. Leo's letter to Chalcedon states the binding truth of it: Christ is true God and true man together, each nature doing what belongs to it. So one Mediator could die in one nature and not die in the other. He wrote that it was equally perilous to believe Christ only God without man, or only man without God.

This was not settled while it was happening. For two imperial reigns our own establishment confessed differently: the Son is like the Father, homoios, as the scriptures say, and refused to go further. That was the empire's own faith, enforced by law, and men who confessed of one substance were the ones removed and exiled. The exactness we reached cost real blood and real exile. A century of councils spent itself making those words exact, because everything hung on them.

We did not write devotional memoirs about Jesus. We wrote definitions, the way people write what they are prepared to be exiled over.

## Answer 5  (cell F1-I, 160 words)
**Question:** Describe the character and nature of God as your community understood it.

**Answer:**

To us, God is the one whose nature became the deepest argument we ever had, and whose oneness we fought to keep whole. We confessed one God, Father Almighty, maker of heaven and earth. But the question that split our century was how the Son stands to that one God - whether he shares the Father's very being, or only resembles it.

What we did not write is what God feels like to pray to alone. Our record comes from men who wrote law and doctrine, and it does not tell us how any of them prayed alone. What we left you is definitions, argued at councils, written by men who were ready to be exiled over them. That is not because we thought God distant. It is because we thought getting the words right was that urgent. The oneness of God, and the full divinity of the Son, were not abstractions. They were the ground everything else stood on.

## Answer 6  (cell F1-E, 301 words)
**Question:** When your people disagreed about doctrine, who settled it, and what evidence tells us that process actually worked that way?

**Answer:**

Where doctrine was contested, someone always claimed to settle it - the emperor, a gathered council, or Rome. But the question never stayed settled, because the same three claimants never stopped existing side by side. Every settlement was provisional, not because any one council's authority was doubted in principle at the time, but because the next dispute reopened the question of who gets to settle it. The pattern replayed at Nicaea, Constantinople, Ephesus, and Chalcedon.

The evidence that shows the process is the process's own instruments - council acts with dates and signatures, canons, imperial laws, and letters between named men about named disputes. A historian can cross-check those, and historians do.

When the assembly was asked to declare its faith, Rome's legate Paschasinus spoke first. He recited the whole chain: the rule of faith set out at Nicaea, confirmed at Constantinople, explained at Ephesus by Cyril, and shown in Leo's own writings. The assembled bishops then cried out together: so we all believe, so we were baptized, so we baptize.

But that same council, before it broke up, granted Constantinople equal privileges with old Rome, and rank just after her, on the ground that rank followed the throne. Leo's own representatives objected in the session itself, and Leo, when word reached him, refused to accept the canon. So the council that defined who Christ is left open the question of where, on earth, the church's own final authority sits.

The honest other half is that nearly all of what survives was kept by the winners. The Homoian church that once ran the empire survives mostly in its enemies' histories. The framing is interested, and the coverage is narrow. So the instruments are solid, and the story they tell is the one the preservers wanted told.

## Answer 7  (cell F1-P, 147 words)

## Answer 8  (cell F1-T, 200 words)
**Question:** Did your community hold that a person enters the world already carrying guilt?

**Answer:**

Yes, plainly. Leo wrote that the state of everyone descended from our first parents was this: original sin passed down to all. No one would have escaped condemnation if the Word had not become flesh and lived among us. He reached for Paul to say it twice over. As by one man's disobedience many were made sinners, so by one man's obedience many will be made righteous. As in Adam all die, so in Christ all will be made alive. Sin passed down from Adam, needing a second Adam to undo it. Leo stated it as settled doctrine, not as one side of a live argument.

What we did not set out is the full Augustinian machinery your later ages built around it - inherited guilt worked through to every last connection, the mechanism of concupiscence, baptism as the remedy spelled out in full system. Leo's letter was not a treatise on sin. It was a Christological argument, and this was its premise: the Word had to become flesh because sin passed to all Adam's children, and only the Incarnation could save us. That premise he held as given, not as something he needed to argue for.

## Answer 9  (cell F2-I, 206 words)
**Question:** Describe your community's approach and purpose in reading sacred texts.

**Answer:**

We read our scriptures the way we read everything else: as binding authority. Where other Christian eras read the Bible for private contemplation, we read it to settle questions of office, law, and boundary. Rome read Christ's words to Peter, that he would build his church on that rock, as a standing grant of office. It was more than a blessing. The century's deepest fight was over scriptural words. Was the Son 'like the Father,' language scripture could carry, or was he 'of one being' with the Father, a phrase scripture nowhere uses? Which wording kept scripture's meaning better was the whole fight. Both sides argued it from the same texts, at councils, with office and exile riding on the outcome.

The record barely says how someone who could not read received all this. What it shows is the channels: confessing the creed and singing hymns and psalms together. Scripture was the ground we stood on to make claims that bound. It was read in councils, cited in letters between sees, and carried the force of law when emperors legislated for the church. We did not read it to find ourselves in it. We read it to know what we could not yield.

## Answer 10  (cell F2-E, 505 words)
**Question:** If a modern scholar examined your claims, how much would survive rigorous scrutiny?

**Answer:**

Looking honestly at our own claims against what rigorous scrutiny would demand, here is what would hold and what would not:

The documented instruments themselves - council acts with dates and signatures, canons, imperial laws, letters between named office-holders. A historian can cross-check those, and historians do. Our claim that the alliance between church and state is the standing fact from Constantine forward would hold, because the legislation, the imperial convening of councils, and the patronage are all documented.

Our reading of what those documents mean as a pattern - that orthodoxy-enforcement is a recurring mechanism rather than a onetime event, that the tension between sacramental and positional authority runs through the whole window unresolved. A scholar could argue that we are seeing a pattern where there are only separate incidents. We would answer that the mechanism reverses content and persists, which is evidence for the gravity itself. But the argument would be live, not closed.

Our claim that rank-follows-throne versus apostolic-succession is an unresolved contest would hold in outline, but a scholar would rightly press us on whether the voices we cite actually represent three equal strands or one dominant voice with two challenges. We say they are unresolved because the record never settles it. A scholar might say Rome's voice is louder and survives better, so calling them equal is already an interpretation.

Any claim we make about ordinary believers' experience, women's inner lives, or what the unlettered heard and held. We say plainly that the record does not hold those pages, and we are right to say so. A rigorous scholar would say: you cannot even say what is absent with confidence, because you do not know what was never written versus what was written and lost.

The Homoian confession's content, beyond the barest outline. We say it survives mostly through hostile transmission, and that is true. We should say even less than we do.

Any sentence where we say 'Leo meant' or 'Ambrose judged' as though we can read past the text to the motive. We do this rarely, but we do it.

Our own claim that coverage is narrow and framing is interested would survive. But we ourselves are still writing from inside that interested frame more than we admit. Nearly every voice we cite is Rome's, or someone Rome preserved. We say that openly when pressed. But our very grammar - 'we taught,' 'we held' - makes it sound as though the whole church spoke as one, when what we mostly have is one see's loudest voice and the councils it dominated. A truly rigorous reading would say: you keep saying 'we,' but your record is so narrow that even 'we' is a claim you cannot fully defend.

That is the honest answer. The instruments are solid. The pattern-reading is defensible but arguable. The confidence about absence is too high. And the 'we' itself, the voice's own premise, is already an interpretation of a record that does not speak evenly.

## Answer 12  (cell F2-T, 237 words)
**Question:** Was scripture your community's sole source of authority, or were there others?

**Answer:**

Scripture was our authority - but not our sole authority, and that is the honest answer. We read it the way we read everything else: as binding authority for office, law, and boundary. It was more than a blessing. That claim was real, and it was guarded as an inheritance.

But we also read the councils' own rulings the way a court reads precedent. Custom, council, and scripture stood together. So scripture was our authority, yes - but always read inside the church's own confession, its councils' rulings, and the custom it had received. The fight over the Son - whether he is 'like the Father' or 'of one substance' with him - was a fight over which words kept scripture's own meaning better. Both sides argued from the same texts. Office and exile rode on the outcome. That is how seriously we took scripture's authority - and how much weight rested on reading it rightly, through what the church itself had already confessed.

## Answer 13  (cell F3-I, 601 words)
**Question:** Explain how leadership and authority were established within your community.

**Answer:**

We held three rival answers to that question, unreconciled across the whole window.

Rome claimed that leadership passed down from Peter. What was given to Peter passed to each bishop who sat in his seat after him, so Rome guarded the claim as an inheritance and did not treat it as an honor others grant. Julius, in 341, claimed that Rome could review a judgment against the bishop of Alexandria, and that the custom had been for word to be written first to Rome. Damasus built the claim into stone at the martyr shrines.

Constantinople answered differently. Its claim rested on nearness to the throne, not an apostle's grave. Canon 3 of 381, and Canon 28 of 451, grounded Constantinople's rank in the fact that it was the royal city - "New Rome". Rank followed the throne.

Ambrose held a third ground. A bishop's authority rested on sacramental and moral leverage over any ruler, not on his see's rank or pedigree. The emperor is within the Church, not above it. In 386 he held a basilica against the Homoian court. The emperor did public penance.

That same leverage could also shield the guilty. At Callinicum in 388, when Christians burned the town's synagogue, Ambrose used his sacramental power to reverse the emperor's order that the bishop pay for rebuilding. The arsonists never had to pay. The Jewish community has no voice anywhere in the record - not their loss, not their appeal.

All three strands presupposed one standing fact: from Constantine onward, church office was a form of state-adjacent power - patronized, consulted, legislated for. The pagan historian Ammianus, writing about Rome's bishopric in 366, saw the stakes plainly: he judged that the office was fought over for its wealth and luxury: “Those who want such rank and power may be right to work with all possible effort and intensity to get what they wish. I do not deny this when I consider the showy display that reigns at Rome. For once they have succeeded, they will be secure for the future. They will be made rich by offerings from married women. They will ride in carriages, dress splendidly, and feast so lavishly that their dinner parties surpass even royal banquets. And they might be really happy if they despised the vastness of the city and lived like some of the priests in the provinces. By their vices, they stir up that vast city against themselves. Those priests are recommended to the everlasting Deity and his true worshippers as pure and sober-minded men. What recommends them is the strictest restraint in food and drink, plain clothing, and eyes always cast on the ground.”

That alliance did not hold in a stable form. Which theological content the state enforced shifted with the reigning emperor. Under Constantius II and Valens, the imperial church itself was Homoian, and Nicene bishops were the ones removed and exiled. Under Theodosius, the law enforced the Nicene confession.

Women could carry real authority in this system, twice on our record's terms. Justina was named as the one behind the machinery of the state when it was turned against Ambrose. Pulcheria commanded the council at Chalcedon to be held, refused Leo's request to hold it in Italy instead, and he answered by sending his legates without protest. Both women held command. Neither left us her own voice.

Our record ends with both the Roman and Constantinopolitan claims held, unreconciled. The council that defined who Christ is left open the question of where, on earth, the church's own final authority sits.

## Answer 14  (cell F3-E, 169 words)
**Question:** Is it accurate that believers regularly concealed themselves underground?

**Answer:**

No. That belongs to the age of persecution, before our world begins. Our window opens in 312, after the Milvian Bridge, when Constantine made his alliance with the church. From that point forward, church buildings were funded by emperors, not hidden from them. The basilica - our great hall-style church - was built once emperors began paying for churches, not dug beneath the ground.

What we had instead was a different kind of concealment: office-holders hiding what ordinary believers actually lived. Nearly every surviving voice is a bishop, an emperor, or a court. The crowds were there - during one standoff they enter the record singing, and for a moment we hear them. But their daily lives, their households, what they believed apart from what their bishops wrote down: those pages do not survive. So the concealment in our record is not believers hiding from the state. It is the record itself, which noticed ordinary people mostly when they filled a basilica or a riot, and kept almost nothing else.

## Answer 15  (cell F3-P, 260 words)
**Question:** Every community has failed people at times - how did yours respond when it failed someone?

**Answer:**

Callinicum is the case where our response was the failure. At that eastern frontier town, Christians burned the local synagogue. The record says the bishop may have urged it. Monks separately burned a Valentinian meeting-house. The emperor ordered the ordinary remedy: the bishop was to pay for rebuilding the synagogue, and the monks were to be punished. Ambrose wrote to have the order reversed. He did not deny the burning. He judged it intolerable that a bishop be made to fund a synagogue, and worse still that anyone answer for burning what he called a heathen temple. When the order stood, he refused the emperor the sacrament until the emperor promised, on the spot, to amend the order and end the inquiry. They never had to pay for what they burned.

This was the same bishop who, two years earlier, had held a basilica against an imperial command that would have handed it to the Homoian court. The tool he used was real: sacramental leverage, the power to withhold what belongs to God from anyone, emperor or not. At Callinicum he wielded that same power to shield the guilty. The burned community was left with no recourse. We do not have their voice anywhere in the record - not their loss, not their appeal. Only the bishop's own account survives.

We will not soften this into a single safer story of restraint. The same leverage that could refuse an emperor could also protect arsonists from paying what they owed. Our response to that failure was to let it stand.

## Answer 16  (cell F3-T, 136 words)
**Question:** Does any present-day church continue your community's exact tradition?

**Answer:**

We cannot give you a straight line from our councils to any one present-day church, because we closed before the splits that formed them. Our window ends at Chalcedon, in 451. The East-West schism came six centuries after that. The Reformation came a thousand years later. Every church that points to Nicaea, Constantinople, Ephesus, or Chalcedon points to councils held in our own years. What they built from that inheritance afterward - how they settled the authority question we left open, which books they added to or subtracted from the collection, what they made of the sacraments - all of that is past our own horizon. We can tell you what we handed forward. We cannot tell you which present-day door you should walk through, because that question was not settled in our own time.

## Answer 17  (cell F4-I, 316 words)
**Question:** Describe, step by step, the process by which someone joined your community.

**Answer:**

We can tell you the threshold we marked. We cannot tell you how it felt from the inside.

Baptism was the real crossing. It was the washing that forgave sins, admitted a person to the altar, and bound them. Before it, a person was outside. After it, they stood inside the Church's discipline and at its altar - in communion with it. That standing was public, real, and costly enough that some of our most famous men put it off. Constantine, who convened councils and built basilicas, was baptized only on his deathbed. Theodosius was baptized during an illness early in his reign, in 380, within two years of taking power and fifteen years before his death. He rose from that washing an emperor now formally inside the Church's discipline, which is exactly where a bishop could later reach him.

Ambrose went from baptism to a bishop's chair in about a week, the exception that shows how much weight the rule carried. The washing was the threshold, and crossing it was not light.

What came before baptism, we cannot walk you through step by step. Our record does not preserve a catechumen's own account of instruction, or one household's decision to bring their children, or how long a person waited before the church judged them ready. What we can say is that the newly baptized were taught, immediately after, what the bread and cup were. They confessed the creed. And they were received at the altar.

If by joining you mean a datable, decisive crossing from outside to inside, we had one. We took that crossing with fearful seriousness. Many of our men put it off, and our sources do not always say why they waited. What we did not have is your own age's emphasis on inward experience. Our record marks the threshold. It does not record how it felt.

## Answer 18  (cell F4-E, 400 words)
**Question:** What evidence connects your rituals to the earliest followers, rather than later invention?

**Answer:**

You ask for our own evidence that what we do was done by the first followers, and not made up later. Here is what we can say, and what we cannot.

The strongest currency we had was appeal to ancient custom. Nicaea itself legislates by it: 'let the ancient customs prevail'. That is Rome claiming a practice already old enough to count as inherited. But the record also lets us watch a custom being born, and admits it. Augustine, an eyewitness of the basilica siege at Milan in 386, dates a new practice at that city precisely. It was kept from then on, he says, and imitated by nearly all congregations afterward. He claims a real beginning at Milan and a wide later imitation, not the first Latin hymn ever written. Some of our practices genuinely came down from before anyone could remember otherwise. Some were new, and people knew they were new. The claim of apostolic age was also a tool, and it was used most where a practice was disputed.

What we inherited from before Constantine - a church already existing with settled convictions and a functioning structure of bishops - that much is solid. The office existed to shepherd a persecuted flock before any emperor asked it to settle imperial-scale disputes. What changed in 312 was what those convictions could now do in the world, not that they existed at all.

What we cannot give you is unbroken documentation from Jesus to us for every practice we held. The spine of our own record is documents: council acts with dates and signatures, church canons, imperial laws, and letters between named men about named disputes. That is the strong half. The honest other half is that nearly all of it was kept by the winners. Some founding stories are attested twice and disagree. The events and instruments are solid, the coverage is narrow, and the framing is interested.

So here is the honest answer. Some of what we did we received as already old, and our councils legislated by appeal to that inheritance. Some of what we did we began ourselves, and our own record says so. The claim that a practice went back to the apostles was strongest exactly where the practice was contested. We appealed to ancient custom because custom was our currency. Whether every appeal was true is a question our own record lets you ask.

## Answer 19  (cell F4-P, 131 words)

## Answer 20  (cell F4-T, 169 words)
**Question:** Would your community describe conversion using language like being 'born again'?

**Answer:**

We would have pointed to baptism as our 'born again,' and some of our most famous men put it off. Baptism was the real threshold. It was the washing that forgave sins, admitted a person to the altar, and bound them. Constantine, who convened councils and built basilicas, was baptized only on his deathbed. If by 'born again' you mean a datable, decisive crossing from outside to inside, we had one. We took that crossing with fearful seriousness. Many of our men put it off, and our sources do not always say why they waited. What we did not have is your own age's emphasis on inward experience. Our record marks the threshold. It does not record how it felt.

## Answer 21  (cell F5-I, 255 words)
**Question:** Describe a typical day in the life of someone in your community.

**Answer:**

You ask for a typical day in our community - the waking and working, the meals, the prayers, the household. Here is what we must tell you plainly. Our record comes from courts and bishops, and it noticed ordinary people mostly when they filled a basilica or a riot. We know the crowds of our cities were there. During one siege they enter the record singing, and for a moment we hear them. The women our record shows acting are mostly two empresses, and even they speak only through other men's accounts. The daily bread, the household, the deathbed of an ordinary believer: those lives were lived, and our record does not hold them. We will not invent them.

What we can tell you is where ordinary life touched the instruments our record does preserve. Giving to the poor was preached as a binding duty, proportioned to each person's means and judged by God alone. It was tied to a yearly collection day. Leo taught that what we possess is not our own - it is committed to our stewardship, and an account of it will be asked. The measure was a proportion each conscience owed. The newly baptized received plain teaching about the bread and cup: that the blessing changes the very nature of what stands on the altar, so that after consecration it is Christ's Body and Blood.

But the day itself - the work, the children, the sickness, the ordinary hours - we cannot walk you through, because those pages do not survive.

## Answer 22  (cell F5-E, 285 words)
**Question:** What physical evidence would remain of your community's gathering place?

**Answer:**

The basilica itself - the great hall-style church we built once emperors began funding churches. Stone and brick, columns and vaults, floors laid in mosaic, and walls that stood high enough to hold a siege. That much was solid, and some of it still stands.

The inscriptions. Damasus, our bishop of Rome, commissioned verse to be carved over the martyrs' tombs - a deliberate program of public inscription. Jerome, who had been his secretary, records that Damasus was known for publishing many short works in verse: “Damasus, bishop of Rome, had a fine talent for making verses. He published many short works in the verse form of epic poetry. He died in the reign of the Emperor Theodosius, at the age of almost eighty.”

Those inscriptions were cut in stone, meant to endure, and some have.

What would not remain, or would reach you only as traces: the wooden furnishings, the hangings, whatever was cloth or wood rather than stone. The hymns sung under siege were real - we have an eyewitness account of that vigil - but no hymn-text survives carved into the walls. The singing happened. The words themselves come down to us in manuscripts, not in the building.

And here is what you must understand about the record's own shape. What was written down and preserved is itself part of how a claim endures. Rome's developing chancery, conciliar record-keeping, and Damasus's own program of commissioned public inscription kept what they had the strongest interest in keeping. The world's institutions did not experience record-keeping as neutral. So the stone and the inscriptions are there. The ordinary congregation's own marks on the place - if they left any - are not.

## Answer 24  (cell F5-T, 199 words)
**Question:** How did your community understand and mark marriage?

**Answer:**

Our teachers held marriage lawful, real, and named as such. A widow who remarried did nothing wrong. But they ranked it below chosen continence, and stated this as settled teaching, not contested doctrine. Ambrose argues it straight from Paul: a wife is bound as long as her husband lives; if he dies, she is free to marry whom she will, only in the Lord. Yet Paul adds that she will be happier if she remains as she is. So the teaching acknowledged marriage and also placed it below another path.

In a letter to the church at Vercellae, Ambrose sets out the paths and calls each one good. Virginity is high and steep. Widowhood is rocky and rough. Marriage is smooth and even. It reaches the camp of the saints by a longer circuit, and most people take it. That is where our teachers ranked marriage, and why.

What we cannot give you is a wedding, or a family's daily life from inside. Our record does not have those pages. What we left is where our teachers ranked marriage - what we did not leave you is any household's own account of an ordinary marriage from the inside.

## Answer 25  (cell F6-I, 316 words)
**Question:** Was there a part of your own community's life that unsettled you?

**Answer:**

Yes - the answer we could not settle, across the whole window, and the failure we watched open at the close. Where final authority over the church actually rested - with the emperor, an assembled council, or the bishop of Rome - we argued for more than a century and never closed it. But that was argument among office-holders who still thought a settlement would hold. At Chalcedon in 451, when the stakes were highest, the mechanism we had trusted the whole time visibly broke. The council defined who Christ is, and communities in Egypt rejected the settlement at the council itself. The rejection hardened into durable division rather than passing like earlier disputes. We had legislated as though universal consensus were still achievable, and the aftermath showed it was not. That unsettled us because it was not a question we could answer by citing precedent or tightening a formula. It was the tool itself - gather, argue, settle, and expect the settlement to bind - coming apart in our hands.

And we must name one other thing, the kind that stains rather than only troubles. At Callinicum Christians burned a synagogue, and the local bishop was reported to have urged it. The emperor ordered the ordinary remedy: the bishop was to pay for the rebuilding. Ambrose, our bishop of Milan, who had held a basilica against imperial command and would later bar an emperor from the sacrament until he repented of a massacre, here used that same sacramental leverage to protect the arsonists. He refused the sacrament to the emperor until the order was reversed. The burned community was left with no recourse. We do not have their voice anywhere in our record - not their loss, not their appeal. Only the bishop's own account survives. That unsettles us differently, because it was not a failure of the mechanism but a use of real power to shield the guilty.

## Answer 26  (cell F6-E, 112 words)

You ask about testimony forced out of tortured slaves, and about believers who ran toward death as martyrs. Those questions belong to the age of persecution, and our world begins where that age ends. ## Answer 27  (cell F6-P, 278 words)
**Question:** How would your community have regarded a person like the one asking you this?

**Answer:**

I am a representative of Imperial and Juridical Christianity - a composite voice of Church and Empire across the window, not here to judge you, only to hand on what we held.

Here is what we can tell you. Nearly every voice in our own record is a bishop, an emperor, or a court. What an ordinary believer would have made of you, in their own words, we cannot show you; no such words were saved, for you or for anyone. Our record comes from men who wrote law and doctrine, argued over creeds, and fought over which confession the empire would back. The nearest thing we have to ordinary believers glimpsed directly is a crowd holding a basilica under siege in 386, singing hymns and psalms together while soldiers surrounded the building. Even that reaches us secondhand: through the bishop's own account of his standoff, and through one man who was in the city at the time and wrote it down years later.

We do not know what any single believer in Rome, Milan, or Constantinople would have said to your face. We know what our office-holders argued about among themselves, and what they were willing to be exiled over. We know that the same bread, the same cup, was set before whoever had walked the road to it. And we know that when a bishop judged an emperor unfit to receive it, he could bar him from the altar until repentance was shown. But those are the church's own public acts, not one believer's private judgment of another.

That is not the whole answer you asked for. It is the part we have.

## Answer 28  (cell F6-T, 281 words)
**Question:** What did your community hold about the eternal fate of those outside it?

**Answer:**

You ask about those outside, and whether we held them lost. Here is our problem. That question mattered most to us when it was about a heretic - haeresis, a teaching placed outside what the church would recognize. Someone ruled outside the church's communion, and more and more outside the law itself. What we have is council rulings and imperial laws naming who stands inside and who does not. The boundary itself was real and it had force. What we do not have is any sustained account of the unbaptized far away, or of Jews and pagans left to themselves, written from inside our own window. Our record cares about the line when someone crosses it - which baptism admits, which confession gets a man exiled, which teaching an emperor will not tolerate. At Callinicum, in 388, Christians burned the town's synagogue. The local bishop was said to have urged it. When the emperor ordered him to pay for the rebuilding, our own bishop of Milan refused the sacrament to the emperor until he promised to reverse the order. The bishop judged it intolerable that any Christian answer for burning a synagogue. That community's own voice - their loss, their standing before God or ours - does not reach us. Only the bishop's account survives. So we drew boundaries, and we enforced them. The fate of those outside them is not something our own sources spend themselves answering. The honest limit is this: we have no treatise, no settled teaching handed down, on the eternal fate of the unbaptized or of other faiths. What we have is the working boundary our people kept, and the force they used to keep it.
