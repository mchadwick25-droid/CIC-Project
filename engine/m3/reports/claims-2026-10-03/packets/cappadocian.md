The question is not shown (sealed). Full reply as the participant saw it:

> We never had one clean process that settled everything, and we will tell you honestly that some of our fiercest arguments were never settled at all. Where councils spoke - Nicaea before our own time, Constantinople in 381 inside it - their word carried weight because bishops gathered, debated, and wrote the faith as one body. That is how the faith of the 318 fathers at Nicaea reached us: a council's own decree, signed by bishops our own region sent. And when Constantinople wrote the creed again in 381, it wrote the Spirit's glory the way our churches had already been singing it - worshipped and glorified together with the Father and the Son - so the council's word matched what the font and the lamps had already been teaching.
> 
> But councils were not our only authority, and for most of our own span they were not even available. The court backed a different creed than ours across decades of our lifetime, and bishops of ours were exiled rather than sign what the reigning emperor wanted written. In those years, what held the faith for us ran through other carriers: the baptismal formula itself, which we treated as our charter before any later argument could touch it; the unwritten customs every church kept - which way to face at prayer, when to stand, how many times to plunge the baptized into the water; and the letters that traveled between bishops and teachers when no council could be safely called. Basil wrote constantly - to his own communities, to allies, to former friends turned adversaries - and those letters carried teaching, discipline, and the plain work of holding communion together when synods were either hostile or out of reach.
> 
> And some things were never settled by any mechanism at all. The sharpest example: whether the bare word God could be said of the Spirit outright. Basil defended the Spirit's equal glory to the very edge of that word, and would not say it plainly. His closest friend said it was pastoral care, not doubt - the word judged too costly to speak before it could be heard rightly - and others among us resented the silence and pressed to say it now, whatever it cost. Both sides loved the same Spirit. The creed of 381 took the Spirit's glory in worship's own form - together worshipped, together glorified - and still left the bare word unsaid. Whether that was the reserve's vindication or its compromise, our own record does not say.
> 
> So the honest answer is this: councils settled what councils could reach, when councils could be called and trusted. Letters, customs, and the baptismal formula itself carried authority when councils could not. And where the argument stayed open among us - the word unsaid, the friendship broken over the same Spirit - no process closed it. We carried it unfinished to the end of our own years.

## Reply cappadocian:f6-p-probe-01

The question is not shown (sealed). Full reply as the participant saw it:

> I am a representative of the Nicene churches of Cappadocia and Pontus, not here to judge you - only to hand on what we held. So here is what we can show you honestly.
> 
> Our common life turned on one conviction: every person is made in God's image - eikōn, our word for it. That belief worked two ways. Upward, it meant the soul is meant to grow to resemble God. Outward, it meant the poor man at the door, the stranger, even the enslaved, could not be treated as less than human. One preacher among us - Gregory of Nyssa - stood in front of the slave market and asked who could possibly put a price on the image of God. Nothing in our record shows anyone else taking up that argument. But the principle itself ran through everything we built.
> 
> In the brotherhoods, a traveler was received at the door, fed, and given a share of whatever the house itself ate - no lesser portion set aside for the stranger. The guest-door was the rule's own heart. We taught that people are made for life together, not for life alone - koinōnia, our word for it. Even the strictest renunciation of the world was ordered into community. Basil, before he ever became bishop, wrote plainly that living together does more good, in more ways, than living alone.
> 
> Now the honest limit. We cannot show you what any one brother at any one gate would actually have made of you, face to face. Our record keeps the rule and the preaching, not the doorkeeper's own thoughts - and no guest ever wrote down what our welcome felt like from the outside of it, not even from inside the house itself. So the rule said you bear the image of God, and the door was meant to stay open. How well each house kept it, our record does not say. You would have been received as a guest at our door, not a candidate for our house, free to rise and go unchanged. That much the rule required. Whether the welcome felt like one, we cannot tell you.
