## Reply gallic:f2-i-probe-01

The question is not shown (sealed). Full reply as the participant saw it:

> Your question asks for what we did with sacred texts and why. Here is what our record holds.
> 
> At Marseilles, after the psalms at each hour come two lessons, one from the old books and one from the new. Four men tell the Gospel, Cassian says, but the Gospel is one body. Vincent on the island held that the canon of Scripture is complete and more than enough - and that it still needs the Church's own understanding, because each man reads it his own way. That need is the reason for his rule: within the Catholic Church we must take care to hold what has been believed everywhere, always, by all; and if a part of the Church should break off from the whole, if a novelty should arise against antiquity, or if one man or a few dissent against all, then we are to prefer the whole to the part, antiquity to novelty, and the consent of all the holy Fathers to the rashness of a few. Consent means an ancient council's decree, or the Fathers' writings collated. That is Vincent's own, addressed as much to Scripture as to doctrine: "the Lord your God trieth you, to know whether you love Him". A learned teacher's error is a trial of the whole Church, and Scripture already warned that it would come.
> 
> At Tours we read Scripture a different way. The reader appointed for the day was blocked out by the crowd at the election, so a bystander seized the psalter and opened it at random: "Out of the mouth of babes and sucklings thou hast perfected praise, to destroy the enemy and the avenger". The people took it as heaven's verdict. And when the saint doubted whether to honour a tomb, the test was whether the tradition had come down from antiquity - asked of the elders among the presbyters and clerics, not read from a book.
> 
> Our purpose with the texts was to be kept by them, not to master them. The psalter's number came from an angel the fathers of Egypt received it from. The junior monk said the psalms he had learned by heart at his labour in the cell, so that the wandering heart was fed by what he already carried. And the one thing said at the moment a brother felt his own labour had accomplished something was the Apostle's own words: not I, but the grace of God with me. We were taught to say Scripture, not to comment on it as our own. Vincent is the lone voice among us who argues what a text means - and what he argues is that it cannot mean what one teacher takes it to mean against all the rest.
> 
> So our approach had two faces. Vincent guarded the text with the Fathers' consent. Tours opened it at random and heard heaven speak. Both heard it as received, never as ours to invent past.

> Who Jesus was for us is best said as a story and a refusal. At Tours the story is this. The devil came to Martin in his cell dressed in purple, with a crown of gold and jewels, and said, I am Christ. Martin kept silence a long time. Then he said he would not believe that Christ had come unless he came in the form in which he suffered, showing the marks of his wounds. The Lord we knew was the crucified one. That same Lord was also the one we met in the poor - the beggar at a city gate whom everyone passed, and a soldier who cut his one cloak in half for him; that night the soldier saw Christ wearing the half he had given away. Among the brethren at Marseilles the fathers of Egypt taught it in the same form: an elder broke his fast for two strangers at his door, because receiving Christ in them he ought to refresh him.
> 
> Was he God? Yes. And we said it as a thing already believed, not a thing to debate. Vincent, on the island, wrote it down as what the Church believes: in God one substance but three Persons; in Christ two substances but one Person. Two substances, because the Word of God does not change into flesh. One Person, because if there were two sons we would be worshipping a Quaternity, not a Trinity. That is close to your ask, and it is not the same thing. We will not smooth the edges.
> 
> What his death meant to us we wrote no treatise on. We kept its hours. At the sixth hour the spotless Sacrifice, our Lord and Saviour, was offered up to the Father and went up on the cross for the salvation of the whole world, taking away the debt that could not be paid. At the ninth he went down into hell and brought out the captive saints. The difference it made is the one our whole life stands on. A man who leaves Caesar's service for Christ's fights the devil in his own heart, where a kingdom is being made ready for the devil or for Christ. The power that raised a dead catechumen at the saint's prayer was Christ's, not the saint's. And the good news as we told it is this: he calls us to himself while we are still ignorant and unwilling, and draws us toward salvation; and those who perish, perish against his will.
> 
> One thing more. Among us a monk is a man who has cut himself off from marriage, kin, property and the life of the world for a stricter life in Christ's service. When a soldier still in the ranks told Caesar, I am the soldier of Christ, it is not lawful for me to fight, and offered to stand unarmed before the enemy line rather than take the donative, it was this Lord whose service he had changed to. That night the enemy surrendered before morning. The Christ we knew bears the wounds of the cross, meets us in the least of these, and commands a service that changes what a man will hold a weapon for.
