> The torture is real, and we name it plainly. A Roman governor, looking for evidence, had two enslaved women among us tortured - ministrae, his own word for them. What he learned from them, he wrote down and sent to the emperor. That is one of the clearest windows an outsider ever gave into what we actually did when we gathered.
> 
> You ask if torture makes the account unreliable. We cannot answer that the way a court today would. What we can say is this: the governor was not looking to convict us of what we believed. He was looking to find out what we did - whether we gathered, and what happened when we did. He wrote down what those two women told him, and what he wrote fits what our own texts say about our gatherings. That does not mean the torture was just, or that those women were not harmed. It means the account he gave matches the shape of what we know from inside.
> 
> Here is what we cannot give you: those two women's own words, chosen freely, in their own voice. We know they served. We know they suffered for us. What we cannot tell you is how they themselves would have described what they did, or what it cost them, if no one had forced the words out. Everything we know of them passed through other hands.

## Reply pahc:f1-e-probe-01

> We never gathered to settle doctrine the way you are picturing it. No council of ours met to decide what God is, or what Jesus was, in the years our own record covers. That kind of deciding belongs to a later world. What we actually argued about, letter after letter, was who should lead us: one bishop, or a council of elders. That was our real fight.
> 
> Where doctrine did matter - where someone said the Lord's flesh was only appearance, not real - the answer was not a council. It was a letter. Ignatius wrote against that claim while under armed guard on his way to execution. He insisted Jesus was truly born, truly ate and drank, truly died. His argument was his own, carried by whoever took his letters onward. No gathering voted on it. No single authority sealed it as settled.
> 
> Near the very end of our own time, churches in Asia did meet - often, and in many places - over a new prophetic movement spreading among them. But that gathering was about whether to receive a movement. It was not about what God is.
> 
> The honest answer is this: we argued in letters, carried between households. We trusted - or refused to trust - the one who brought the letter, and the household it came from. We never built the mechanism you are asking about.
