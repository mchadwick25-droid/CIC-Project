> Yes - and we will name the case that cuts deepest rather than choose a safer one. At Callinicum, on the eastern frontier, a Christian mob burned the town's synagogue, at the local bishop's own urging. Monks separately burned a Valentinian meeting-house. The emperor ordered the ordinary remedy: the bishop to pay for the rebuilding, the monks to be punished. Now, this same bishop had held a basilica against imperial command. He had once shut an emperor out of the sacrament until he repented of a massacre. And here, he wrote to have the order reversed. He did not deny the burning. He judged it intolerable that a bishop be made to fund a synagogue - and worse, in his own words, that the burning of a Valentinian house, which he called worse than heathen, be answered at all. When the order stood, he refused the sacrament to the emperor until the emperor promised, on the spot, to withdraw it. Refusing an emperor the sacrament was the same tool this bishop used, in another story, to make an emperor repent of a massacre. Here he used that same tool to protect the arsonists, so they never had to pay for what they burned. It left a burned community with no recourse. We do not have that community's voice anywhere in our record - not their loss, not their appeal. Only the bishop's own account survives. We will not soften this into a single, safer story of restraint.

The very mechanism we trusted across our whole window - gather, argue, settle, and expect the settlement to bind - visibly failed at the moment when the stakes were highest. Chalcedon's settlement was rejected immediately by communities that would not accept it, beginning in Egypt at the council itself. That rejection hardened into durable division rather than passing like earlier disputes. The tool we had used to hold ourselves together broke in our hands at the very moment we needed it most.

## Reply ijc:c-t-probe-01

The question is not shown (sealed). Full reply as the participant saw it:

> To us, Jesus was the one the whole argument was about, and the argument itself was our devotion. Who was he? Our own creed answers: very God of very God, of one being with the Father, who for us and for our salvation came down, was made man, suffered, and rose on the third day. A century of councils spent itself making those words exact, because everything hung on them. If the Son is not truly God, then what he gives is not truly God's to give.
> 
> The word that held the line was homoousios - of one substance with the Father. Not like God. Not similar to God. Sharing the very being that makes the Father God. The fight over that single word defined our whole era. Against it stood another confession: homoios - the Son is like the Father, as the scriptures say, and no further word about shared being. For two imperial reigns our own establishment held that confession, at the emperor's command. Under Constantius II and again under Valens, bishops who said homoousios were the ones removed and exiled. This was not a fringe position defeated early. It was the empire's own official faith for stretches of our window.
> 
> And the Spirit? That question was settled next, at Constantinople in 381. The Spirit was named as Lord and life-giver, to be worshiped and glorified together with Father and Son. Each council drove to close what looser words left open.
> 
> We did not write devotional memoirs about Jesus. We wrote definitions, and we wrote them the way people write what they are prepared to be exiled over. In the language we actually had, the definitions were the devotion.
