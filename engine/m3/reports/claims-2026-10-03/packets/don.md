> You are asking whether we go back to the apostles themselves, or whether we made this up in the year of Caecilian's consecration. We would not put the claim where you have put it. We never argued that our practice came down from the apostles in an unbroken chain of custom. We argued something narrower and, we thought, harder to dodge: that it came down from Carthage.
> 
> Half a century before there was any quarrel between us and the other party, eighty-seven African bishops sat in council at Carthage under Cyprian, and ruled that what is given outside the true church is not given at all — so anyone coming in from outside is baptized, not re-baptized. That is not our invention. That is the settled mind of the African church before either of our two hierarchies existed. When our opponents told us we had made something up in the year of Caecilian's consecration, our answer was that we had changed nothing, and they had. We were holding the old African position; they had moved off it to keep the peace with Rome.
> 
> You can measure how much that argument weighed by how hard it had to be answered. Seven books were written against our practice of washing, and a great part of that labour goes on explaining that Cyprian, however holy, is not a canonical authority — that the Scriptures alone are, and that Cyprian's own real lesson was to stay in unity anyway. Nobody spends that many pages on a father unless the appeal to him is doing damage.
> 
> Here is the honest end of it. Cyprian ruled as we ruled and did not do what we did. He never broke communion over it. That is the strongest thing our opponents had, and we would not pretend it is weak. And beyond him we cannot take you. We can show you an African council in the middle of the third century. We cannot show you the apostles.
