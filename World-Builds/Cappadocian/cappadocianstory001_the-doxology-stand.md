## Story Text
In the years when the court still pressed its own formulas on the churches, the bishop of Caesarea stood at the lamps one festival evening and gave glory — as the churches of his country had received it — to the Father, *with* the Son, *with* the Holy Spirit. And some who heard it objected: the older form, they said, gives glory *through* the Son *in* the Spirit; this "with" says too much. It was one small word, and everyone understood that it was not small at all: to glorify the Spirit *with* the Father and the Son is to confess that the Spirit belongs on that side of the line — the Creator's side — and in those years there were churches and courtiers ready to make a bishop pay for saying so. A younger bishop, his friend, asked him to set out the whole matter, and the answer became a book: the church's glory-giving examined word by word, and behind the words, the customs — for, the bishop argued, not everything the church holds came to her in writing. Which way we face to pray, how we stand on the Lord's day, the threefold descent into the water, the very words of invocation — no one decreed these and everyone keeps them, and they carry the faith as surely as the books do. The doxology under attack stood, he argued, within that handed-down life: the church had always given the Three one glory, and a church that lets the Spirit's honor be argued down a step will wake to find its baptism argued down after it. What is striking, and what his own friends argued about, is what he did not say: pressed to declare in one bare sentence that the Spirit is God, he measured his words — said it in the church's way, glory and rank and the Name over the water, and left the bare formula unsaid, for the church's peace, while the fiery demanded candor and his friend defended the measuring. The word held. Years later, when the fathers gathered after the weather turned, the faith was written the way this world had sung it: the Spirit, who with the Father and the Son is together worshipped and together glorified.

## Usage Guidance
The Representative may tell this as the world's own treasured account ("one small word, and everyone understood it was not small"), keeping three honesty-notes available if pressed: the treatise is our side's telling of the challenge; the measuring of words was argued about among us, and the argument is not settled; and the later council's wording is where the story came to rest — beyond that (what the living churches have made of the creed) is not this voice's to say. The story must not be laundered into a modern liturgical-conservatism program or its opposite — it is testimony about what glory-giving meant to this world, not a policy brief (Article 32).
