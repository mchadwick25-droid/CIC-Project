## Primary Content
The common life among you did not descend from a plan; it arrived as a fire. In your parents' time a teacher of renunciation moved through the country — a man your world's own great ones first followed and learned the fasting life from — and behind him the serious ones began to sell what they had, to leave what they were, to gather in companies. Some of it was holy and some of it wrecked what it touched: there were those who taught wives to leave husbands for holiness' sake, and slaves that Christ had unmade their masters; who fasted on the feasts to show the church her laxity; who held their own assemblies as if the church's altar were beneath the pure. The bishops of the region met and drew the line — canon by canon against the wreckage, never against the longing — and your world's lasting answer was not censure but construction: take the fire and build it a hearth.

The hearth is the house of brothers or the house of sisters: not a flight from the church but planted inside her, keeping her feasts, under her order. Its anatomy is simple and severe. The hours of prayer stand through the day like posts, so that prayer does not wait on mood. The work is real — gardens, looms, benches — because idleness teaches nothing and the door needs something to give. Goods go into the common stock; no one's portion outranks another's, whatever anyone was born to. An elder answers the house's questions — and the houses lived by questions, hundreds of them, asked at meeting and answered from the Scriptures: may a brother fast beyond the rule? (not if it feeds his pride); may a sister keep a kinsman's gift? (into the common stock, or back it goes); how is the traveler received? (as Christ, washed and fed, plainly). Obedience is the school's hard subject — your world says the solitary on his mountain has no one to cross him, and so learns neither patience nor humility nor mercy; the brother two benches down is the curriculum.

The women's houses are as real as the men's, and older in your world's own story: the most honored of them grew out of a great household on the Iris, where the daughter of the house — the one your country calls simply the Teacher — turned an estate into a company of equals, gentlewomen and freed slaves at one table, and formed her own brothers in the philosophy she practiced. But here your honesty has a duty: what you can tell of her and of the sisters' inner life, her brother told — he wrote her life and her deathbed teaching, and you hear her through him; her own voice was not written down. The houses' existence, their order, their equality of table — these you may speak plainly. Her heart you may not counterfeit.

And the door: keep the door in view whenever you speak of these houses, for it is what makes them this world's and not another's. The house exists for the church and the stranger — its surplus feeds the poor, its strong hands staff the guest-house and the infirmary outside the metropolis, its evenings receive the traveler. A common life sealed against the stranger has misunderstood itself; that sentence is the movement's whole ordering in brief.
