## 1. Formation World Identity

**World Name:** The Anabaptist Movements. (Working name only; naming and outer scope have not been separately flagged as contested by Step 0, unlike the Alexandria world's own narrow/broad question — no comparable ambiguity has surfaced here, but final confirmation of the world's name remains the project lead's own act, not this document's.)

**Short Description:** The formation of a believers'-church, gathered, separatist Christianity across three regional communities — the Swiss Brethren (Zurich, from January 1525), the Moravian/Hutterite communities gathered around Nikolsburg (from 1526) and later goods-in-common life, and the Dutch tradition led by Menno Simons and Dirk Philips (from the 1530s) — bound together by believer's (adult) baptism as the movement's own defining boundary marker, a gathered church voluntarily separate from a state-sponsored one, and sustained, disproportionately documented persecution by Catholic and magisterial Protestant authorities alike. — *Widely Accepted* for the shared believer's-baptism/gathered-church core across all three regions (Step 0 §1); *Documented* for the persecution (Step 0 §1, §2 A3; the movement's own martyrological corpus, §3 below).

**Core Identity:** This is a world of ordinary adherents and their own persecuted congregations, not of court theologians or office-holders — the opposite formation logic from a world like Imperial and Juridical Christianity's bishops and emperors negotiating jurisdiction. Its formative population is congregational: baptismal candidates examined by their own gathered community, itinerant and often short-lived preachers and elders (Grebel, Manz, Hubmaier, Menno, Dirk Philips), and — unusually among this project's confirmed and candidate worlds — a substantial body of ordinary believers who speak in their own words from the record of their own trials, imprisonments, and executions: Michael Sattler's trial record and letter to the Horb church, and Anneken Jans's 1539 prison testament to her infant son, both embedded in the *Martyrs Mirror* (van Braght/Sohm, 1886). — *Documented* for the named figures and texts (Step 0 §3 B1; the vendored *Martyrs Mirror*, confirmed present at multiple points in the file); *Widely Accepted* for the congregational-rather-than-episcopal character of the movement's own authority structure as a whole (Schleitheim Confession, in McGlothlin 1911, arts. on the ban and avoidance of "abominable pastors," exercised by the gathered congregation itself, not an external hierarchy).

**Distinctive Contribution:** Set against this same six-world Era 6/7 batch's own nearest neighbors, the Anabaptist movements are non-redundant on a specific, evidenced axis: **the boundary marker itself.** Devotio Moderna (Step 0, Atlas V.3, c. 1380–1517) gathered devout men and women into common, unvowed households inside the Catholic Church's own institutional bounds — it never broke communion, never rebaptized, and its houses dissolved into, rather than were violently expelled from, the surrounding Reformation. The Hussite and Bohemian Brethren movement (Step 0, Atlas V.6, c. 1402–1517) organized around a single practical demand — the laity's own right to the cup at communion (utraquism) — and in 1436 secured the Compactata, formal legal recognition from the same civil and ecclesiastical authorities it had fought; it became a tolerated national church, not a criminalized one, and neither its main Utraquist body nor its own Unity of the Brethren wing made adult baptism their own defining act. Lollardy (Step 0, Atlas V.5, England only) organized around vernacular Scripture and criticism of clerical wealth and the mass, operating as underground dissent inside the English church rather than as a rival community with its own gathered rite of entry. Believer's baptism — a deliberate, adult, repeatable-in-principle-but-once-performed act that publicly and permanently marked a convert as belonging to a visible, separated, voluntary church rather than the parish one was born into — is the one boundary marker none of these three siblings shares with this candidate, and it is the reason all three magisterial and Catholic authorities alike treated the Anabaptist movements as a criminal, executable offense in a way they did not treat Devotio Moderna's communal life, the Hussites' negotiated settlement, or Lollardy's underground preaching. This is a real, evidenced distinction, not an asserted one: Step 0 §0 additionally flags a deliberate, outside-observation thematic echo of the same gathered-church/rebaptism/willing-suffering shape in the already-built Donatism world (I.4, c. 311–439 CE), over a millennium earlier and an ocean of theological difference away — a structural parallel this movement's own vendored voices explicitly reject (Menno: "we are opposed to the Donatists... and to the errors, abuses and abominations of all uproarious sects"; Dirk Philips's *Enchiridion*: those who "liken us to the Donatists... A great injustice is done to us"). That rejection is itself carried forward honestly in §6 below, not smoothed into a clean lineage. — *Widely Accepted* for each sibling world's own defining act, as stated in its own Step 0 document; *Documented* for the movement's own rejection of the Donatist comparison, quoted directly from Menno's complete works and Dirk Philips's *Enchiridion* (Step 0 §0).

**Living Tradition Status:** Not confirmed. Per Constitution Article 29 and this project's established practice, Living Tradition Status confirmation is a project-lead act this document cannot perform on its own behalf. Noted for the record, not decided here: this world's core content has an unusually direct, still-living continuity — the project's own world census (`cic-website/data/world-census.json`, entry `the-anabaptist-movements`) records that "the movement survives directly: Mennonites, Amish, Hutterites and Brethren all descend from it," and that its own martyr book, the *Martyrs Mirror*, "is still printed and still read aloud in Amish and Mennonite homes" today — a claim this document treats as *Widely Accepted* institutional fact about present-day communities, not as a confirmed Article 29 finding, which remains **PENDING** the project lead's own act.

## 2. Historical Boundaries

**Time Horizon:** 1525–1650, per Step 0 §1 and the source-readiness dossier. Neither figure is reopened here; both are defended below on their own evidentiary grounds, as Step 1 requires.

**Beginning point (1525), defended.** January 1525, Zurich: a small group led by Conrad Grebel and Felix Manz baptized one another as adults, in direct, deliberate defiance of Zurich's own magisterial Reformation, which they judged too slow and too compromised with civil authority. This is a genuine ecological rupture, not an arbitrary date: before this act, there was no gathered, voluntarily-entered, adult-baptized community distinct from the parish church anywhere in the Reformation's own orbit; after it, that form of church existed, and its own founders were hunted for it within two years — Felix Manz, drowned in the Limmat at Zurich in 1527, is Widely Accepted as the movement's first martyr at Protestant hands. 1525 is therefore this world's own first attested instance of its own defining act (believer's baptism as a deliberate church-founding rite), not a conventional round-number placeholder. — *Widely Accepted* (Step 0 §1; corroborated by the project's own world-census entry, which independently gives "In January 1525, a small group in Zurich baptized one another as adults").

**Ending point (1650), defended.** The window's end is not a round number chosen for convenience; it is fixed specifically to bring the **Dordrecht Confession (1632)** inside this world's own horizon, per the project's own census record for this candidate ("its window ends in 1650, bringing the Dordrecht Confession (1632) back inside its own window"). The Dordrecht Confession is this movement's own most durable and most widely adopted confessional consolidation — it is the affirmative-floor evidence Step 0 §2 A1 relies on for the movement's Trinitarian and Christological commitments, and it remains in active liturgical and catechetical use among Mennonite and Amish communities well beyond 1650. Setting the endpoint close enough after 1632 to let this consolidation, and its first generation of reception, register as part of the world's own formation — rather than cutting the window off mid-consolidation at, say, 1600 — keeps the horizon anchored to a real confessional event rather than an arbitrary stopping point. 1650 also sits after the Peace of Westphalia (1648), which closes the era of pan-European religious war this movement's own persecution was embedded in, without reaching so far forward as to invite claims about the movement's later 17th/18th-century migrations (to Pennsylvania and elsewhere), which lie beyond this document's own scope. — *Dominant Modern Reconstruction* for the choice of 1650 specifically as this world's own horizon; *Documented* for the Dordrecht Confession's 1632 date and its continued liturgical use.

**Geographic Centers:**
- **Zurich and the Swiss lands** — where believer's baptism was first performed (1525) and where the Schleitheim Confession (1527) was drafted, articulating the movement's earliest surviving corporate discipline (baptism, the ban, the breaking of bread, avoidance of "abominable pastors," refusal of the sword, refusal of oaths). — *Documented* (Schleitheim text, McGlothlin 1911, pp. 3–9).
- **Nikolsburg, Moravia** — Balthasar Hubmaier's base from 1526, and the site of the movement's most consequential internal disagreement over the sword and Christian magistracy (§4 below); later home to the Hutterite communities, whose own communal chronicle (the *Geschicht-Buch*, Wolkan 1923) records their institutional memory from 1517 through 1665, spanning and outlasting this document's own 1525–1650 window. — *Documented* (Step 0 §1, §3 B1; Vedder 1905, confirmed by direct grep of the vendored text at Nikolsburg-dated passages).
- **The Netherlands** — where Menno Simons (from 1536, after leaving the Catholic priesthood) and Dirk Philips gathered and led the scattered Dutch congregations that came to bear Menno's name. — *Documented* (Step 0 §1; Menno's complete works, Funk 1871).

**Cultural Environment:** A world formed under active, sustained legal criminalization — not merely social marginalization — by every other confessional authority of its century: Catholic, Lutheran, and Reformed governments alike treated adult rebaptism itself as a capital offense (a civil crime, reviving a Roman-era statute against rebaptism, alongside a religious one). Its formation register is accordingly congregational and often clandestine rather than institutional: gathered worship in homes, caves, and forests rather than church buildings; oral and manuscript transmission of hymns composed in prison (the Ausbund, first printed 1564, still in Amish liturgical use); and a first-person testimonial genre — the trial record, the prison letter, the martyr book — that this project's other confirmed and candidate worlds do not carry in comparable bulk. Its own court-testimony sources are a partial, coerced register, not plain inside voice, a caveat this document states plainly rather than smoothing over: the *Täuferakten* series, named in the dossier as an untested lead, would (if assessed) record ordinary believers' words only as transcribed by their own persecutors under interrogation. — *Widely Accepted* for the criminalization and its legal basis; *Documented* for the Ausbund's continued Amish liturgical use (corpus-map note, `the-anabaptist-movements.yaml`); *Widely Accepted*, disclosed as a genre caveat rather than asserted as settled, for the coerced character of any court-testimony material.

**Historical Pressures:**
- Zurich's own magisterial Reformation (Zwingli, from 1519) moved, in Grebel's and Manz's own judgment, too slowly and stayed too entangled with civil authority — the proximate internal pressure that produced the break of January 1525. — *Widely Accepted*.
- Catholic, Lutheran, and Reformed authorities alike criminalized rebaptism and pursued, imprisoned, and executed adherents across all three of this world's regions — the movement's single most consistent external pressure across its full 1525–1650 span. — *Documented* (Step 0 §1; the *Martyrs Mirror*'s own bulk).
- Internal disagreement over the sword and Christian magistracy, sharpest at Nikolsburg (§4 below), where Hubmaier's own position diverged from Schleitheim's non-resistance article without ever fully resolving into a single movement-wide discipline. — *Documented* (Step 0 §1, §4 item 4; Vedder 1905, Appendix, Hubmaier's *On the Sword*, 1527).
- The catastrophic Münster episode (1534–35) — violent, polygamous, apocalyptic-kingdom Anabaptism under Jan Matthys and Jan van Leiden — associated the Anabaptist name generally with sedition and fanaticism in the eyes of contemporary and later observers, intensifying persecution of the nonviolent Swiss and Dutch mainstream this world centers, even though no vendored source in this world's corpus is Münsterite. — *Documented* for Münster as a historical episode; *Widely Accepted* for its chilling effect on how "mainstream" Anabaptists (this world's own scope, per Step 0 §1) were subsequently perceived and treated.

**Historical Catalysts (dated):**
- **January 1525** — first adult (re)baptisms, Zurich (Grebel, Manz).
- **1526** — Balthasar Hubmaier relocates to Nikolsburg, Moravia, and begins his most productive period of writing there. — *Documented* (Vedder 1905).
- **1527** — the Schleitheim Confession drafted (Swiss Brethren); Felix Manz executed by drowning at Zurich; Michael Sattler, principal author of Schleitheim, tried and executed the same year (his trial record and letter to the Horb church embedded in the *Martyrs Mirror*); Hubmaier writes *On the Sword*, addressed to the Margravate of Moravia's own chancellor and nobility, arguing for Christian magistracy against the Hut/Widemann party at Nikolsburg. — *Documented*.
- **1534–35** — the Münster Rebellion, explicitly excluded from this world's own scope (§1 above; Step 0 §1, §4 item 5).
- **1536** — Menno Simons leaves the Catholic priesthood and begins gathering the scattered Dutch congregations that take his name. — *Documented*.
- **1539** — Anneken Jans executed at Rotterdam; her prison testament to her infant son survives in the *Martyrs Mirror*. — *Documented*.
- **1632** — the Dordrecht Confession adopted, the Dutch wing's most durable confessional consolidation and this document's own basis for the movement's affirmative floor evidence (§1 above; Step 0 §2 A1). — *Documented*.

## 3. Distinct World Criteria

Applying the Framework's own four questions directly:

- **What recurring formation ecology exists?** An ecology of voluntary, adult entry into a gathered, disciplined, separatist congregation — baptism upon confession of faith, congregational discipline exercised through the ban ("avoidance of abominable pastors" and, more broadly, of the unrepentant within the community — Schleitheim, arts. on the ban and avoidance), and a shared willingness to suffer legal persecution rather than have infants baptized into the parish church. This recurs across all three named regional communities (Swiss, Moravian/Hutterite, Dutch), each with its own leadership and confessional statement but the same underlying entry-and-discipline logic. — *Widely Accepted*.
- **What recurring gravities exist (preliminary, subject to Doc_04)?** Provisionally, and named here only as candidates for Step 4 testing, not as confirmed findings: believer's baptism as the church's own boundary-defining act; congregational discipline and the ban as the mechanism of a gathered church's own internal integrity; and, held with more caution given the internal disagreement documented at §4 below, non-resistance/refusal of the sword as a majority-but-not-universal commitment. Doc_01 does not perform Doc_04's own work, and none of these three is treated here as settled. — *this is preliminary Layer-1 orientation, not a gravity finding.*
- **What recurring patterns of worship, formation, interpretation, belonging, and authority exist?** Belonging and authority are structurally dominant, expressed through baptism as the entry rite and the ban as the boundary-maintaining discipline; worship is congregational and often clandestine (homes, caves, forests) rather than liturgically elaborate, though the Ausbund attests a real, sustained hymnic worship life composed largely in prison. Interpretation is markedly less institutionally developed than in a world like Imperial and Juridical Christianity — this movement produced confessions (Schleitheim, Dordrecht, the Waterlander Confession) and pastoral/catechetical writing (Menno, Dirk Philips) rather than canon law or conciliar acts. Formation through suffering and martyrdom is itself a recurring, richly attested pattern distinctive to this world among this project's confirmed and candidate worlds, carried in the *Martyrs Mirror*'s embedded trial records and testaments. — *Widely Accepted*, with the caveat (Step 0 §3 B2) that this world is genuinely thin on founding-generation theological *argument* specifically, relative to its martyrological and confessional bulk.
- **What distinguishes this world from adjacent worlds?** From Devotio Moderna: gathered communal life without vows versus a rival, criminalized rite of church entry (believer's baptism) that broke communion outright. From the Hussite and Bohemian Brethren movement: a negotiated, legally tolerated national reform (the Compactata, 1436) versus a criminalized, executed minority with no comparable civil settlement anywhere in its 1525–1650 window. From Lollardy: underground doctrinal dissent inside an existing church versus a rival, separately gathered church with its own entry rite and discipline. From the already-built Donatism world: a real structural echo (gathered church, rebaptism as boundary marker, willingness to suffer) that this movement's own vendored voices explicitly and directly reject as a comparison, over a millennium's separation and with materially different theological stakes (infant-baptism validity for the Donatists versus believer's baptism as such here) — detailed fully at §1 above and §6 below. — *Widely Accepted*, grounded in each named sibling's own Step 0 document and, for Donatism, in the movement's own vendored words.

## 4. Strand Determination (Article 21)

Per Constitution Article 21: strand is "a finding, never a presupposed universal schema," accountable to evidence, and never assigned "to satisfy an architectural preference for plurality." Step 0 correctly left this question for Step 1 to test (Step 0 §4 item 4 names the coherence question as binding on Doc_01 "before drafting proceeds").

**The question this section must not confuse itself about.** The three named regional communities (Swiss Brethren; the Moravian/Hutterite communities gathered from Hubmaier's Nikolsburg base; the Dutch tradition under Menno and Dirk Philips) developed in overlapping time and, in the Dutch case, later than the others — Menno's own ministry begins in 1536, roughly a decade after Zurich 1525 and Hubmaier's 1526 relocation to Nikolsburg. A claim that "the Dutch wing came from the Swiss wing, therefore they are the same strand" — or, conversely, "the Dutch wing came later, therefore it is a different strand" — would be exactly the sequential-development reasoning Article 21 warns against, the single most common defect a prior world's Doc_01 review flagged. Strand determination instead asks whether the evidence shows a genuinely simultaneous, recurring divergence in formation ecology, authority structure, or organizing conviction — independent of when each community happened to arise.

**Finding: this world is strand-singular for the two Dutch-and-Swiss communities the evidence most directly attests, with one named, evidenced exception — Hubmaier's own Moravian position on the sword — held as a real but narrow, non-persisting divergence rather than a fourth full strand.**

- **The shared core, evidenced directly and independently in each region.** Believer's baptism as the entry rite and the gathered/separatist church as its consequence are attested, in each region's own words, independent of any borrowing narrative: Schleitheim's own seven articles (Swiss, 1527) open with baptism, followed by the ban, breaking of bread, avoidance of "abominable pastors," the sword, and swearing; the Dordrecht Confession (Dutch, 1632) and the Waterlander Confession (Dutch, in McGlothlin) independently affirm the same believer's-baptism, gathered-church logic in their own separate confessional acts, decades after Schleitheim and without textual dependence on it. This is recurring-ecology evidence — the same organizing pattern, independently attested in each region's own confessional voice — not an inference from one community having influenced or descended from another.
- **The one named, evidenced divergence: Hubmaier's Moravian position on the sword.** Hubmaier's own vendored 1527 treatise *On the Sword* (Vedder 1905, Appendix) argues, from his own Nikolsburg base, for Christian magistracy — that a Christian may hold civil office and wield the sword under God's own ordination — directly against the Hut/Widemann party at Nikolsburg, and in real substantive disagreement with Schleitheim's own non-resistance article, without ever naming Schleitheim directly. This is a genuinely distinct ground of conviction about the relationship between the gathered church and civil authority, evidenced in Hubmaier's own words, not inferred from his having written later or in a different place than Schleitheim's authors. It does not, on the present evidence, persist as an ongoing, named strand carried forward by a continuing community within this world's own 1525–1650 window — the Hutterite communities that came to occupy Nikolsburg after Hubmaier's 1528 execution are documented (Step 0 §3 B1) as holding a distinct communal-goods discipline, not as continuing Hubmaier's own sword position specifically, and no further vendored source in this world's corpus develops or defends Hubmaier's position after his death. It is named here as a real, evidenced divergence — not silently absorbed into an undifferentiated "the movement believed X" — but not elevated to a fourth persisting strand the way Ambrose's confrontational episcopate was elevated to its own strand in the Imperial and Juridical Christianity world, because that world's Strand C was attested across a sustained sequence of confrontations, where Hubmaier's position is attested in one treatise, from one figure, at one moment, without a documented continuing party.
- **What this finding does not resolve.** The Swiss and Moravian communities' own affirmative confessional evidence for Article 4's floor commitments rests, per Step 0 §2 A1, on the three-region coherence argument made here, since Grebel and Manz left no writings and Schleitheim never touches the floor's five commitments directly. This document's strand-singular finding (with Hubmaier's named exception) is offered as that coherence argument's own evidentiary basis: the recurring believer's-baptism/gathered-church pattern, independently attested in each region, is what makes "one movement" a defensible finding rather than an assumption carried over from the census's own grouping.

**Governing consequence for later steps.** Doc_04's cross-strand gravity testing, where Article 21 requires it, should treat Hubmaier's sword position as a documented internal tension within the movement's non-resistance conviction, not as evidence the movement itself is multi-strand in the way, for example, Imperial and Juridical Christianity's Roman/Constantinopolitan/Ambrosian authority-grounds are multi-strand. A candidate gravity that holds across the Swiss, Moravian, and Dutch confessional record despite Hubmaier's named exception is a stronger candidate for this world's irreducible core than one that does not.

## 5. Preliminary Forces Identification

Per the Forces Framework's Step 1 requirement (Section 4), this is orientation for Step 2's own scope, Layer 1 (historical-event level) only. It is not the complete forces analysis, which is compiled at Doc_08, and it does not attempt Layer 2 (the world's own experienced consciousness of these forces) or Layer 3 (formation impact) analysis.

**What generated this world:** the judgment, reached by a small circle in Zurich in January 1525, that Zwingli's own magisterial Reformation was moving too slowly and remained too entangled with civil authority — converted from a disagreement about pace into a rival act of church-founding the moment adult baptism was actually performed. — *Widely Accepted.*

**What larger world was it embedded in:** the wider Protestant Reformation itself, and, more broadly, the confessionally plural, civilly enforced religious landscape of 16th–17th-century Central and Western Europe, in which every established authority — Catholic, Lutheran, and Reformed alike — held civil and religious jurisdiction as a single, fused authority this movement's own gathered-church logic directly refused. — *Widely Accepted.*

**What was it responding to:** the perceived incompleteness of the existing Reformation's own break with the inherited parish-church, infant-baptism, state-church model; and, internally, the conviction that a visible church of confessing adult believers was itself scripturally required, not merely a stricter discipline layered onto an existing congregation. — *Widely Accepted* (Step 0 §1).

**What was it refusing:** infant baptism and the state-church model that made church membership coextensive with civil subjecthood; for the Schleitheim-aligned majority, participation in civil magistracy, oath-swearing, and the sword; and, for the Dutch and Swiss mainstream this world centers (per Step 0 §1, §4 item 5), the violent, polygamous, apocalyptic-kingdom program of the Münster episode, which this world's own sources do not share and this document does not treat as part of its scope. — *Documented* for the Münster exclusion; *Widely Accepted* for the confession-attested refusals.

## 6. World Continuity & Distinction

**What this world inherits:** the wider Protestant Reformation's own conviction that Scripture, not inherited ecclesiastical authority, is the final court of appeal for church practice — shared with, but pushed further than, its magisterial Reformation contemporaries (Lutheran Wittenberg, the Reformed cities of Zurich and Geneva, both already Built & Live in this project's portfolio, per Step 0 §2 A3); and, more distantly and only as a disclosed outside observation rather than the movement's own self-understanding, a structural echo of the already-built Donatism world's own gathered-church/rebaptism/willing-suffering shape — an echo this movement's own vendored voices (Menno, Dirk Philips) explicitly and directly reject by name (§1 above). This document states that rejection plainly rather than smoothing it into a lineage the sources themselves refuse.

**What this world transmits forward:** believer's baptism and the gathered, voluntary, disciplined congregation as an ecclesiological model — the "free-church principle," per the project's own world-census entry, that "membership is a voluntary adult commitment and that the church should not be an arm of the state," which the census records as having become "one of the most widely adopted Protestant ideas in the world." The *Martyrs Mirror* itself continues to be printed and read; the Ausbund continues in Amish liturgical use (corpus-map note, `the-anabaptist-movements.yaml`).

**Living heir(s), stated as present-day institutional fact, not as a confirmed Article 29 finding:** the project's own world-census entry for this candidate records that "Mennonites, Amish, Hutterites and Brethren all descend from it" directly. This document records that claim as *Widely Accepted* institutional and historical fact about present-day communities' own descent — a claim about historical continuity, which this document is positioned to make — and expressly does **not** treat it as a confirmed Article 29 Living Tradition Status finding, nor does it characterize any present-day Mennonite, Amish, Hutterite, or Brethren community's own contemporary self-understanding, belief, or practice, which belongs to those living communities and is not this document's to define (Article 28). That distinction — a historical formation world is not the same thing as a present-day community's own self-understanding — is carried forward as binding on every later document in this world's build, per Article 28/29 discipline as applied in this project's other confirmed worlds (e.g. `worlds/alx/Doc_01...md` §9's treatment of the Coptic Orthodox Church).
