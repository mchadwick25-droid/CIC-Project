## 1. Formation World Identity

**World Name:** The Anabaptist Movements. (Working name only; naming and outer scope have not been separately flagged as contested by Step 0, unlike the Alexandria world's own narrow/broad question — no comparable ambiguity has surfaced here, but final confirmation of the world's name remains the project lead's own act, not this document's.)

**Short Description:** The formation of a believers'-church, gathered, separatist Christianity in three regional communities — the Swiss Brethren (Zurich, from January 1525, non-resistant and Schleitheim-aligned), the Moravian/Hutterite communities (whose community-of-goods tradition took shape at Austerlitz from 1528, after a division at Hubmaier's own Nikolsburg base — §4 below), and the Dutch tradition led by Menno Simons and Dirk Philips (from the 1530s) — sharing one recurring formation ecology, believer's (adult) baptism as the entry rite into a gathered church voluntarily separate from a state-sponsored one. Within that shared ecology, the evidence supports two organizing-conviction strands (§4 below), not three: a Congregational strand spanning the Swiss and Dutch regions together, holding discipline through the ban within private household property, and a Communal-Property strand specific to the Moravian/Hutterite communities, holding the same entry rite but organized instead around community of goods. The world is marked throughout by sustained, disproportionately documented persecution by Catholic and magisterial Protestant authorities alike, with real but local exceptions of toleration in both the Dutch and Moravian cases (§2 below). — *Widely Accepted* for the shared believer's-baptism/gathered-church ecology across all three regions (Step 0 §1); *Documented* for the persecution and its exceptions (Step 0 §1, §2 A3; Vedder 1905; *Martyrs Mirror*; §2, §4 below).

**Core Identity:** This is a world of ordinary adherents and their own persecuted congregations, not of court theologians or office-holders — the opposite formation logic from a world like Imperial and Juridical Christianity's bishops and emperors negotiating jurisdiction. Its formative population is congregational: baptismal candidates examined by their own gathered community, itinerant and often short-lived preachers and elders (Grebel, Manz, Hubmaier, Menno, Dirk Philips), and — unusually among this project's confirmed and candidate worlds — a substantial body of ordinary believers who speak in their own words from the record of their own trials, imprisonments, and executions: Michael Sattler's trial record and letter to the Horb church, and Anneken Jans's 1539 prison testament to her infant son, both embedded in the *Martyrs Mirror* (van Braght/Sohm, 1886). — *Documented* for the named figures and texts (Step 0 §3 B1; the vendored *Martyrs Mirror*, confirmed present at multiple points in the file); *Widely Accepted* for the congregational-rather-than-episcopal character of the movement's own authority structure as a whole (Schleitheim's own Second Article: those who fall into sin are to be "admonished twice in secret; the third time... corrected publicly before the church," McGlothlin 1911, p. 4 — a translation of Zwingli's own Latin translation of the German original, disclosed here as at every point this Confession bears weight in this document, not McGlothlin's own translator's-summary heading line, which compresses the document's seven articles into six headings and is not itself evidence of how the ban was administered).

**Distinctive Contribution:** Set against the six-candidate batch this world was itself selected alongside on 2026-09-15 — Lutheran Wittenberg, the Reformed cities of Zurich and Geneva, the Society of Jesus, the Tridentine Church, and Lollardy — the Anabaptist movements are non-redundant on a specific, evidenced axis: **the boundary marker itself, and the recurring ecology it founds.** (Devotio Moderna and the Hussite and Bohemian Brethren movement, engaged below, belong to a separate, later three-candidate Era 6 batch assembled 2026-09-25, not this world's own 2026-09-15 batch; only Lollardy is shared between the two.) Devotio Moderna (Step 0, Atlas V.3, c. 1380–1517) gathered devout men and women into common, unvowed households inside the Catholic Church's own institutional bounds — it never broke communion, never rebaptized, and its houses dissolved into, rather than were violently expelled from, the surrounding Reformation. The Hussite and Bohemian Brethren movement (Step 0, Atlas V.6, c. 1402–1517) organized around a single practical demand — the laity's own right to the cup at communion (utraquism) — and in 1436 secured the Compactata, a durable, negotiated, movement-wide legal settlement from the same civil and ecclesiastical authorities it had fought; its main Utraquist body became a tolerated national church on that constitutional footing. Whether its own Unity of the Brethren wing made adult (re)baptism a defining act of its own is not settled here: standard scholarship holds the Unity rebaptized adult entrants from 1467 to about 1534, but no vendored source for either world confirms or disputes this, so this document tags that comparison *Contested* rather than treating it as a boundary marker this candidate holds uniquely against the Hussite world's own dissenting wing. Lollardy (Step 0, Atlas V.5, c. 1380–1520, England only) organized around vernacular Scripture and criticism of clerical wealth and the mass, operating as underground dissent inside the English church, under a real, executable legal threat of its own — English statute made burning for heresy law in 1401, and Oldcastle's failed 1414 rising made Lollardy treason as well as heresy, per Lollardy's own Step 0 §1 — but never as a rival community with its own gathered rite of entry. Lollardy's own criminalization does not distinguish it from this world; the absence of any rival baptismal or entry rite does. Believer's baptism — a deliberate, adult, repeatable-in-principle-but-once-performed act that publicly and permanently marked a convert as belonging to a visible, separated, voluntary church rather than the parish one was born into — is the boundary marker genuinely absent from Devotio Moderna and Lollardy alike, whatever else does or does not distinguish this candidate from each of them, and its criminalization by every other confessional authority of its century is real, though with real, local exceptions, disclosed rather than smoothed over (§2, §3 below).

This candidate's nearest built neighbors are themselves batch-mates from its own 2026-09-15 batch, already Built & Live, and this document engages them directly rather than naming only its own more distant Era 6/7 counterparts (§3 below gives the systematic version of this same argument). Reformed Zurich and Geneva (`rzg`, Built & Live) is the closest sibling of all: its own Doc_01 names the 21 January 1525 Zurich baptisms as its own catalyst and calls the Swiss Brethren's origin "an internal schism from this world's own Zurich strand" — a claim this document does not dispute (both worlds may cite the same event from their own side, per Step 0 §2 A3) but does not accept as making the two worlds redundant. `rzg`'s own recurring ecology is a city's magisterial reform, carried out in cooperation with, and continuous with, existing civil authority; this world's own recurring ecology is exactly the rejection of that cooperative model — a gathered, voluntary, separatist church that broke communion with the magisterial reform precisely over church-state entanglement, from the moment believer's baptism was first performed. A shared originating event and, in the Swiss case, a shared set of founders who once belonged to Zwingli's own circle do not make one world's own strand a subset of the other's; Article 21's own warning against sequential-development reasoning applies across worlds, not only within one. Lutheran Wittenberg (`witt`, Built & Live) is distinguished on the same axis: it retained infant baptism and a territorial, magisterial church-state model throughout its own window, which this world's believer's-baptism/voluntary-church logic directly rejects. The anti-Trinitarian currents (VI.14, a separate, already-registered census entry) are distinguished on a different axis entirely, already established at Step 0 §2 A1 and not reopened here: VI.14 is a floor-level question, a plain denial of the Trinity, while this movement's own confessions (Dordrecht, Waterlander, Dirk Philips's *Enchiridion*) affirm the Trinity directly, and its own distinctive Christological complication (§7 item 1 below) disputes the *mode* of Christ's incarnation, not his deity or the Trinity itself.

Step 0 §0 additionally flags a deliberate, outside-observation thematic echo of the same gathered-church/rebaptism/willing-suffering shape in the already-built Donatism world (I.4, c. 311–439 CE), over a millennium earlier and an ocean of theological difference away — a structural parallel this movement's own vendored voices explicitly reject (Menno: "we are opposed to the Donatists... and to the errors, abuses and abominations of all uproarious sects"; Dirk Philips's *Enchiridion*: those who "liken us to the Donatists... A great injustice is done to us"). That rejection is itself carried forward honestly in §6 below, not smoothed into a clean lineage. — *Widely Accepted* for each sibling world's own defining act, as stated in its own Step 0 or Doc_01 document; *Contested* for the Unity of the Brethren rebaptism comparison, disclosed rather than asserted as settled; *Documented* for the movement's own rejection of the Donatist comparison, quoted directly from Menno's complete works and Dirk Philips's *Enchiridion* (Step 0 §0); *Documented* for `rzg`'s own Doc_01 framing and for Lollardy's own Step 0 record of its own criminalization.

**Living Tradition Status:** Not confirmed. Per Constitution Article 29 and this project's established practice, Living Tradition Status confirmation is a project-lead act this document cannot perform on its own behalf. Noted for the record, not decided here: this world's core content has an unusually direct, still-living continuity — the project's own world census (`cic-website/data/world-census.json`, entry `the-anabaptist-movements`) records that "the movement survives directly: Mennonites, Amish, Hutterites and Brethren all descend from it," and that its own martyr book, the *Martyrs Mirror*, "is still printed and still read aloud in Amish and Mennonite homes" today. This document treats the Mennonite, Amish, and Hutterite lines as *Widely Accepted* direct institutional descent; the census's own "directly" wording is carried forward for the Brethren as attributed wording only, not as independently verified fact (§6 below states why). None of this is treated as a confirmed Article 29 finding, which remains **PENDING** the project lead's own act.

## 2. Historical Boundaries

**Time Horizon:** 1525–1650, per Step 0 §1 and the source-readiness dossier. Neither figure is reopened here; both are defended below on their own evidentiary grounds, as Step 1 requires.

**Beginning point (1525), defended.** January 1525, Zurich: a small group in direct, deliberate defiance of Zurich's own magisterial Reformation, which they judged too slow and too compromised with civil authority, baptized one another as adults — Conrad Grebel baptizing Georg Blaurock, who then baptized the others present, including Felix Manz (the precise sequence, per `rzg`'s own Doc_01 — Historical Catalysts below). This is a genuine ecological rupture, not an arbitrary date: before this act, there was no gathered, voluntarily-entered, adult-baptized community distinct from the parish church anywhere in the Reformation's own orbit; after it, that form of church existed, and its own founders were hunted for it within two years — Felix Manz, drowned in the Limmat at Zurich in 1527, is Widely Accepted as the movement's first martyr at Protestant hands. 1525 is therefore this world's own first attested instance of its own defining act (believer's baptism as a deliberate church-founding rite), not a conventional round-number placeholder. — *Widely Accepted* (Step 0 §1; corroborated by the project's own world-census entry, which independently gives "In January 1525, a small group in Zurich baptized one another as adults").

**Ending point (1650), defended.** 1650 is a project-level era-boundary convention, not a scholarly-consensus finding about this movement specifically: it is the same close date the census assigns to essentially every other candidate and confirmed world in this project's own "Reformation Era" cohort (Lutheran Wittenberg, the Reformed cities, the Society of Jesus, the Tridentine Church, Lollardy, and others), marking where the project's own next era ("The Enlightenment & Awakening Era") begins. Labeling this a *Dominant Modern Reconstruction* would overstate it as a scholarly claim about this movement's own history; it is more accurately labeled a project design choice.

Within that shared boundary, 1650 does real, disclosed work for this candidate specifically, and the defense is not Dutch-only. It brings the **Dordrecht Confession (1632)** inside the window, per the project's own census record ("its window ends in 1650, bringing the Dordrecht Confession (1632) back inside its own window") — the movement's own most durable and most widely adopted confessional consolidation, and the affirmative-floor evidence Step 0 §2 A1 relies on for the Dutch wing's own Trinitarian commitments (Article 4's commitments (1) and (5)). The same confession's Christological "in Mary" phrasing is corroborating evidence only on the Dutch wing's own celestial-flesh complication (§7 item 1 below); it is not itself load-bearing floor evidence on Article 4's third commitment (Step 0 §2 A1). For the Swiss region, 1650 closes the window on persecution that continued, not on mid-suffering: the *Martyrs Mirror* records that Felix Landis died of hunger and neglect in the Othenbach prison, about 1642, after being denied food for many days — more than a century after Manz's 1527 drowning, showing Swiss persecution had not ended by the time this window closes, though the same source also records that the magistrates of Amsterdam, at the Amsterdam Anabaptists' own urgent request (made on behalf of the Swiss brethren's distress), sent Zurich a supplication that same year, February 1642, "for mitigation of the persecution" — a step that met only "an unfriendly and irritating reply," disclosed here rather than omitted. For the Moravian region, 1650 falls after the near-total destruction of the Hutterite communities that followed the 1623 royal decree (§4 below; Vedder 1905, pp. 267–270) — the window closes on the aftermath of that collapse, not on a settled mid-century steady state, and it does not reach forward into the scattered communities' later survival in Bohemia, Hungary, and Russia, which lies beyond this document's own scope.

This document also discloses, rather than leaves implicit, that the movement's two richest vendored sources post-date the window they document: the *Martyrs Mirror* itself was compiled in 1660, and the Hutterite *Geschicht-Buch* runs to 1665 — both compiled after 1650 closes. The census's own record calls the *Mirror* "not a contemporaneous archive." Doc_02 should read both as retrospective compilations whose own compiling framework lies outside this document's window, even where the events and testimonies they preserve fall inside it.

No vendored or census source attests Dordrecht's own continuous liturgical use specifically. What the corpus-map and census do attest, and what this document relies on instead, is the Ausbund's own continued Amish liturgical use and the *Martyrs Mirror*'s own continued printing and reading in Amish and Mennonite homes (§6 below) — both *Documented* directly. — *Documented* for 1650 as the shared project-level era boundary (`world-census.json`, cross-world); *Documented* for the Dordrecht Confession's 1632 date; *Documented* for Swiss persecution continuing into the 1640s and the Moravian collapse after 1623 (Vedder 1905; *Martyrs Mirror*); *Documented* for the *Martyrs Mirror*'s and *Geschicht-Buch*'s own post-1650 compilation dates.

**Geographic Centers:**
- **Zurich and the Swiss lands** — where believer's baptism was first performed (1525) and where the Schleitheim Confession (1527) was drafted, articulating the movement's earliest surviving corporate discipline (baptism, the ban, the breaking of bread, avoidance of "abominable pastors," refusal of the sword, refusal of oaths) — McGlothlin's own English text is a translation of Zwingli's own Latin translation of the German original, not a direct German-to-English rendering, disclosed here as at every point this Confession bears weight in this document (§7 item 3 below). — *Documented* (Schleitheim text, McGlothlin 1911, pp. 3–9).
- **Nikolsburg, Moravia, and Austerlitz** — Balthasar Hubmaier's own base from 1526, and the site of the movement's most consequential internal disagreement over the sword and Christian magistracy (§4 below). In the summer of 1528, months after Hubmaier's own execution, this disagreement split the Nikolsburg brethren into two named parties: the Schwertler ("men of the sword"), who held Hubmaier's own position, and the Stäbler ("men of the staff," non-resistant), led by Jacob Widemann, for whom community of goods became "the doctrine of a standing or falling Church." Lord Lichtenstein pressed the Stäbler to moderate or leave; they chose to leave, and, in Vedder's own words, "went their way to Austerlitz," about thirty miles north — where the movement's own community-of-goods tradition was actually founded, not at Nikolsburg and not by Hubmaier's own party. Jacob Hutter organized these Austerlitz-descended communities from soon after their founding; by 1536 they numbered eighty-six settlements. The Hutterite communities therefore descend from Hubmaier's own opponents, not from Hubmaier himself, and their own communal chronicle (the *Geschicht-Buch*, Wolkan 1923) records their institutional memory from 1517 through 1665, spanning and outlasting this document's own 1525–1650 window. The Schwertler, Hubmaier's own named continuing party, are not attested again in this world's vendored corpus after this same 1528 dispute. — *Documented* (Vedder 1905, pp. 248–250).
- **The Netherlands** — where Menno Simons (from 1536, after leaving the Catholic priesthood) and Dirk Philips gathered and led the scattered Dutch congregations that came to bear Menno's name. — *Documented* (Step 0 §1; Menno's complete works, Funk 1871).

**Cultural Environment:** A world formed predominantly under active, sustained legal criminalization — not merely social marginalization — by every other confessional authority of its century: Catholic, Lutheran, and Reformed governments alike treated adult rebaptism itself as a capital offense, a civil crime reviving a Roman-era statute against rebaptism (vendored directly, Codex Theodosianus 16.6) alongside a religious one — Menno's own words for it: "the law of Theodosius... and in our times the imperial mandate and the condemnation of the empire." This pattern is not universal across the whole window and every place, and this document discloses the real exceptions rather than presenting a uniform picture, though the two do not resolve alike: William of Orange granted liberty of conscience to the Anabaptists of Middelburg, Zealand, over the objection of the town's own magistrates, and commanded twice, by separate written charges, that their consciences not be burdened (*Martyrs Mirror*); harassment resumed there only after his death, and was itself ended, in the Anabaptists' own favor, "by a third prohibition, by his son" — a lapse that was corrected, not a toleration finally withdrawn. Moravia's own outcome was different: the Moravian nobility protected these communities during what the Anabaptist chronicles themselves call "the good time of the church" — 1564 to the first half of Rudolf II's reign, c. 1594 — until the royal decree of 1623 decisively ended that protection and dispersed or destroyed the communities that survived (Vedder 1905, pp. 264–270), a genuine, unreversed end to the toleration (the Hutterite communities themselves were mostly settled at Austerlitz, Auspitz, and other sympathetic estates, not at Nikolsburg specifically, which passed to a hostile lord in 1576). Both episodes were local and dependent on a particular noble or magistrate, not a formal, movement-wide legal settlement — but only the Moravian one proved reversible against the movement; the Dutch one did not (§1 above). Its formation register is accordingly congregational and often clandestine rather than institutional: gathered worship in homes, caves, and forests rather than church buildings; oral and manuscript transmission of hymns composed in prison (the Ausbund, first printed 1564 per the project's own census record, still in Amish liturgical use); and a first-person testimonial genre — the trial record, the prison letter, the martyr book — that this project's other confirmed and candidate worlds do not carry in comparable bulk. Its own court-testimony sources are a partial, coerced register, not plain inside voice, a caveat this document states plainly rather than smoothing over: the *Täuferakten* series, named in the dossier as an untested lead, would (if assessed) record ordinary believers' words only as transcribed by their own persecutors under interrogation. — *Widely Accepted* for the criminalization and its legal basis; *Documented* for the Middelburg toleration and its own restoration by William's son, and for the Moravian toleration and its own 1623 reversal (*Martyrs Mirror*; Vedder 1905); *Documented* for the Ausbund's continued Amish liturgical use, attributed to the project's own census record (corpus-map note, `the-anabaptist-movements.yaml`); *Widely Accepted*, disclosed as a genre caveat rather than asserted as settled, for the coerced character of any court-testimony material.

**Historical Pressures:**
- Zurich's own magisterial Reformation (Zwingli, from 1519) moved, in Grebel's and Manz's own judgment, too slowly and stayed too entangled with civil authority — the proximate internal pressure that produced the break of January 1525. — *Widely Accepted*.
- Catholic, Lutheran, and Reformed authorities alike criminalized rebaptism and pursued, imprisoned, and executed adherents across all three of this world's regions — the movement's single most consistent external pressure across its full 1525–1650 span, though not without real, local exceptions (William of Orange's Middelburg grant, restored after a lapse by his son; Moravian noble protection, genuinely ended in 1623 — §2 above). — *Documented* (Step 0 §1; the *Martyrs Mirror*'s own bulk; Vedder 1905).
- Internal disagreement over the sword and Christian magistracy, sharpest at Nikolsburg in the summer of 1528 (§2, §4 below), where Hubmaier's own Schwertler party held his position for Christian magistracy against Schleitheim's own non-resistance article, while the Stäbler majority left Nikolsburg for Austerlitz and organized the movement's own community-of-goods tradition there instead — never resolving into a single movement-wide discipline on the sword, and producing, on the Stäbler/Hutterite side, a distinct organizing conviction of its own (§4 below). — *Documented* (Vedder 1905, pp. 248–250; Hubmaier's *On the Sword*, 1527).
- The catastrophic Münster episode (1534–35) — violent, polygamous, apocalyptic-kingdom Anabaptism under Jan Matthys and Jan van Leiden — associated the Anabaptist name generally with sedition and fanaticism in the eyes of contemporary and later observers, intensifying persecution of the nonviolent Swiss and Dutch mainstream this world centers, even though no vendored source in this world's corpus is Münsterite. The movement's own vendored voices state the repudiation directly: Menno declares "we are opposed to the Donatists, Circumcelliones, Munsterites... and to the errors, abuses and abominations of all uproarious sects"; Dirk Philips's own *Enchiridion* rejects those who "liken us to the Donatists, Munsterites, and other turbulent, erring spirits, saying we are of the same kind," calling this "a great injustice." Münster itself arose from the same Melchiorite Dutch/North German milieu from which Menno Simons later gathered his own congregations — a real historical proximity this document discloses rather than treats as a lineage claim (§7 item 5 below). — *Documented* for Münster as a historical episode, for the Menno and Dirk Philips repudiations quoted directly above, and for the shared Melchiorite milieu with the early Dutch congregations; *Widely Accepted* for its chilling effect on how "mainstream" Anabaptists (this world's own scope, per Step 0 §1) were subsequently perceived and treated.

## 3. Distinct World Criteria

Applying the Framework's own four questions directly:

- **What recurring formation ecology exists?** An ecology of voluntary, adult entry into a gathered, disciplined, separatist congregation — baptism upon confession of faith, congregational discipline exercised through the ban (Schleitheim's own Second Article, excommunication administered by the gathered church, once-removed through Zwingli's own Latin translation of the German original, disclosed here as at every point this Confession bears weight in this document; its own, distinct Fourth Article separates the church from "abomination" and its own Fifth Article addresses the church's own pastors — three distinct provisions, not one), and a shared willingness to suffer legal persecution rather than have infants baptized into the parish church. This recurs across all three named regional communities (Swiss, Moravian/Hutterite, Dutch — §4 below). Of the two Article-21 strands the evidence there supports, one — the Congregational strand — spans the Swiss and Dutch regions together on this same entry-and-discipline ecology; the other — the Communal-Property strand — is specific to the Moravian/Hutterite communities, which hold the same entry ecology but organize around community of goods instead of private household property. — *Widely Accepted*.
- **What recurring gravities exist (preliminary, subject to Doc_04)?** Provisionally, and named here only as candidates for Step 4 testing, not as confirmed findings: believer's baptism as the church's own boundary-defining act; congregational discipline and the ban as the mechanism of a gathered church's own internal integrity; and, held with more caution given the internal disagreement documented at §4 below, non-resistance/refusal of the sword as a majority-but-not-universal commitment. Doc_01 does not perform Doc_04's own work, and none of these three is treated here as settled. — *this is preliminary Layer-1 orientation, not a gravity finding.*
- **What recurring patterns of worship, formation, interpretation, belonging, and authority exist?** Belonging and authority are structurally dominant, expressed through baptism as the entry rite and the ban as the boundary-maintaining discipline; worship is congregational and often clandestine (homes, caves, forests) rather than liturgically elaborate, though the Ausbund attests a real, sustained hymnic worship life composed largely in prison. Interpretation is markedly less institutionally developed than in a world like Imperial and Juridical Christianity — this movement produced confessions (Schleitheim, Dordrecht, the Waterlander Confession) and pastoral/catechetical writing (Menno, Dirk Philips) rather than canon law or conciliar acts. Formation through suffering and martyrdom is itself a recurring, richly attested pattern distinctive to this world among this project's confirmed and candidate worlds, carried in the *Martyrs Mirror*'s embedded trial records and testaments. — *Widely Accepted*, with the caveat (Step 0 §3 B2) that this world is genuinely thin on founding-generation theological *argument* specifically, relative to its martyrological and confessional bulk.
- **What distinguishes this world from adjacent worlds?** From Devotio Moderna: gathered communal life without vows versus a rival, criminalized rite of church entry (believer's baptism) that broke communion outright. From the Hussite and Bohemian Brethren movement: a durable, negotiated, movement-wide national legal settlement (the Compactata, 1436) versus scattered, local episodes of noble or magisterial toleration — one (Moravia) genuinely reversed, one (Middelburg) a lapse that was itself corrected — that never rose to a formal, movement-wide settlement of this movement's own distinct church order (§1, §2 above). From Lollardy: underground doctrinal dissent inside an existing church, under its own real threat of criminal prosecution (statute since 1401), versus a rival, separately gathered church with its own entry rite and discipline — criminalization is shared with Lollardy and does not distinguish the two; the rival entry rite does. From the nearest built worlds, engaged fully at §1 above: Reformed Zurich and Geneva's own magisterial, civil-cooperative reform ecology versus this world's own voluntary, separatist rejection of that model; Lutheran Wittenberg's retained infant baptism and territorial church-state model versus believer's baptism and a gathered, voluntary church; the anti-Trinitarian currents' (VI.14) floor-level denial of the Trinity versus this movement's own direct Trinitarian affirmation, on a different axis of disagreement entirely. From the already-built Donatism world: a real structural echo (gathered church, rebaptism as boundary marker, willingness to suffer) that this movement's own vendored voices explicitly and directly reject as a comparison, over a millennium's separation and with materially different theological stakes (infant-baptism validity for the Donatists versus believer's baptism as such here) — detailed fully at §1 above and §6 below. — *Widely Accepted*, grounded in each named sibling's own Step 0 or Doc_01 document and, for Donatism, in the movement's own vendored words; *Contested* for the Unity of the Brethren rebaptism comparison, per §1 above.

## 4. Strand Determination (Article 21)

Per Constitution Article 21: strand is "a finding, never a presupposed universal schema," accountable to evidence, and never assigned "to satisfy an architectural preference for plurality." Step 0 correctly left this question for Step 1 to test (Step 0 §4 item 4 names the coherence question as binding on Doc_01 "before drafting proceeds").

**The question this section must not confuse itself about.** The three named regional communities (Swiss Brethren; the Moravian/Hutterite communities; the Dutch tradition under Menno and Dirk Philips) developed in overlapping time and, in the Dutch case, later than the others — Menno's own ministry begins in 1536, roughly a decade after Zurich 1525 and Hubmaier's 1526 relocation to Nikolsburg. A claim that "the Dutch wing came from the Swiss wing, therefore they are the same strand" — or, conversely, "the Dutch wing came later, therefore it is a different strand" — would be exactly the sequential-development reasoning Article 21 warns against. The same warning applies to the movement's own polygenesis scholarship, which is a claim about geographic and historical *origin*: "these communities arose independently, therefore they are different strands" is the identical forbidden move, run in the direction opposite the more familiar "same origin, therefore same strand" error. Strand determination instead asks whether the evidence shows a genuinely simultaneous, recurring divergence in formation ecology, authority structure, or organizing conviction — independent of when or where each community happened to arise.

**Testing the Swiss/Dutch division directly against the vendored confessions.** The Swiss Brethren's own Schleitheim Confession (1527) and the Dutch wing's own Dordrecht Confession (1632) do not, on direct comparison, evidence a distinct recurring practice between the two regions. Dordrecht's Article XIV rejects, for "revenge, whereby we resist our enemies with the sword," "all revenge and resistance" — the same non-resistant conviction Schleitheim's own Fourth Article holds ("by the vigor of this regulation there fall away from us the diabolical arms of violence, such as swords... in virtue of that word of Christ 'You ought not to resist evil'"). Dordrecht's Article XV forbids "all oaths, high and low." Dordrecht's Article XVI institutes "the ban, or excommunication," "a separation... by the church," administered "for the amendment, and not for the destruction, of offenders" — the same discipline Schleitheim's own Second Article administers. Dordrecht's Article XVII requires "the shunning of those who are expelled." Non-resistance, oath-refusal, the ban, and shunning are all present, in the Dutch wing's own confessional voice, in its most durable and widely-adopted confession — not a distinctively Swiss set of practices at all. The Dutch wing's own internal divisions, per McGlothlin's introduction (pp. 24–25), concern "questions of discipline and the proper view of the person of Christ": a variation in degree within one shared practice, not a different practice. No vendored source describes a Dutch community that lacked believer's baptism, the ban, oath-refusal, or non-resistance; no vendored source describes a Swiss community practicing something different from these in their place. The evidence does not support treating "Swiss" and "Dutch" as two Article-21 strands: what actually divides them is geography and named leadership (Grebel, Manz, and Sattler for the Swiss; Menno Simons and Dirk Philips for the Dutch) — a basis Article 21 excludes on its own.

**Testing the Hutterite/Moravian community of goods directly.** The evidence here is different in kind. Vedder's own historical narrative (1905, pp. 248–249) records that the Stäbler, once settled at Austerlitz under Jacob Widemann and organized from soon after by Jacob Hutter, held community of goods as "the doctrine of a standing or falling Church," convinced "that a true Christian brotherhood could exist on no other basis" than shared property. This is not a claim about origin; it is a claim about a sustained, recurring, structurally distinct pattern of communal life — collective rather than private household ownership of property — practiced continuously across eighty-six settlements by 1536, carried forward in the communities' own chronicle (the *Geschicht-Buch*, spanning 1517–1665), and surviving as an organizing structure through the Moravian nobility's protection to 1594 and the destruction that followed the 1623 decree. Non-resistance itself does not distinguish this community from the Swiss or Dutch: the Stäbler were "men of the staff, i. e., of peace and non-resistance" (Vedder, p. 248), the same conviction Schleitheim and Dordrecht both hold. What distinguishes the Moravian/Hutterite communities is the additional, sustained structural commitment to communal rather than private property — a genuinely different, evidenced, recurring pattern of ecological orientation within the shared believer's-baptism ecology, not a variation in degree of an existing one.

**Finding: this world is strand-plural, but the evidence supports two strands, not three.**

- **The Congregational strand (Swiss and Dutch).** Believer's baptism as the entry rite; discipline through the ban, oath-refusal, and non-resistance; private household property. Evidenced directly in Schleitheim's own Second Article (excommunication, administered by the gathered church itself) and Fourth Article (separation from "abomination," which itself includes the sword renunciation — "the diabolical arms of violence, such as swords... fall away from us") for the Swiss Brethren, and in Dordrecht's Articles XIV–XVII for the Dutch wing — two regionally and historically distinct bodies practicing the same recurring discipline, led respectively by Grebel, Manz, and Sattler among the Swiss and by Menno Simons and Dirk Philips among the Dutch. Real internal disagreement exists within this strand — chiefly questions of discipline and, in the Dutch wing specifically, a celestial-flesh Christological complication (§7 item 1 below) — but no vendored source describes either regional body practicing a fundamentally different discipline, entry rite, or property structure than the other. — *Widely Accepted* as this strand's own defining shape, on Schleitheim's and Dordrecht's own direct textual evidence.
- **The Communal-Property strand (Moravian/Hutterite).** Community of goods as the church's own "standing or falling" test, held alongside the same believer's-baptism entry rite and, per Vedder, the same non-resistant conviction as the Congregational strand — the divergence is specifically, and only, the property structure, sustained for a century as an organizing pattern of daily communal life, not a momentary position in a single dispute. This document discloses, rather than smooths over, a real evidentiary asymmetry here: no vendored primary Hutterite confession exists — Riedemann's own *Rechenschaft* (c. 1545) is known only through McGlothlin's editorial summary, not his own text (Step 0 §2 A1) — so this strand's own evidentiary basis is a documented historical narrative (Vedder), not, as for the Congregational strand, the movement's own confessional voice. This asymmetry is carried forward as an open item for Doc_02 (§7 item 2 below), not treated as a reason to omit the strand or to force it into the Congregational pattern it does not share.
- **Hubmaier's own sword position (the Schwertler), tested against Article 21's own standard, excluded.** Article 21 requires "a meaningfully distinct pattern... within a single world" — a pattern, which by its nature requires more than one documented instance. Hubmaier's own sword position is attested twice as his own individual view — his 1527 treatise "On the Sword" (vendored as an appendix to Vedder's biography) and the summer 1528 Nikolsburg dispute (Vedder, pp. 248–250) — but the Schwertler as a named, organized party are attested only in that one local Nikolsburg controversy. No chronicle, confession, communal structure, or later reference of any kind attests a continuing Schwertler party, community, or teaching anywhere else in the corpus. This is a stricter and different failure than non-persistence: one man's individually-held position, asserted in his own writing and then taken up by one named party in one local dispute, is not yet a documented recurring *pattern* at all, whatever its own later fate. `worlds/ijc/Doc_01…` §4 correctly holds that Article 21 does not test for persistence through a world's full temporal span — its own Strand C, Ambrose's sacramental-independence claim, does not survive past ijc's 451 close but is retained because it is evidenced across three separate, dated confrontations over several years (Callinicum, 388; the Thessalonica response, 390; the 386 basilica standoff), a genuine recurring pattern even though the strand itself does not continue as a claim-making stream. The Schwertler fail a prior and different test: no second instance of the Schwertler as an organized party or practice is attested anywhere in the corpus. Hubmaier's own sword position is accordingly named here as a real, disclosed, non-recurring internal tension within the Communal-Property strand's earliest history (Nikolsburg, to 1528) — the Schwertler held his position after his 1528 execution, while the Stäbler, his own opponents under Jacob Widemann, left Nikolsburg for Austerlitz and organized, under Jacob Hutter, the century-long community-of-goods tradition this strand rests on — not a third strand of its own.
- **Polygenesis, engaged as context only, never as corroboration.** The movement's diverse, multiple geographic origins (Roth & Stayer's polygenesis scholarship, named in the census; also relied on by `rzg`'s own Doc_01, §1 above) explain why this world's communities arose independently in Zurich, Moravia, and the Netherlands. That is a claim about *where and when* each community began. It has no bearing on how many Article-21 strands the evidence supports, and this document does not treat it as corroborating the two-strand finding above, or any strand count — doing so would be the "different origins, therefore different strands" reasoning the framing paragraph above already excludes.
- **What this finding does not resolve.** Step 0 §2 A1 flagged that the Swiss and Moravian wings have no affirmative confessional evidence of their own for Article 4's five floor commitments — Grebel and Manz left no writings, Schleitheim never touches the floor, and Hubmaier's own vendored treatise argues a different question. That floor question was already settled at Step 0's own Section A conclusion (Approved to proceed), on the strength of the shared recurring ecology across all three regions, not on a claim that the three regions form a single undifferentiated strand. This document's own strand-plural finding does not reopen that floor clearance: the world clears the floor as a whole, on the Dutch wing's own affirmative evidence extended by the recurring-ecology argument above, exactly as Step 0 anticipated; strand-plurality is a separate, internal-structure question about how the world's own communities differ from one another — analogous to how Imperial and Juridical Christianity is one floor-cleared world with multiple, internally differentiated strands.

**Governing consequence for later steps.** Doc_04's own gravity testing should test candidate gravities across both strands, not assume either strand's own organizing conviction (the ban, held within private property, or community of goods) automatically holds for the other. A candidate gravity that holds across the Congregational and Communal-Property strands despite their differing property structures is a stronger candidate for this world's own irreducible core than one that does not; Hubmaier's own sword position, since it is not attested as a recurring pattern at all, is a documented internal tension to disclose within the Communal-Property strand's early history, not a candidate gravity or a strand in its own right.

## 5. Preliminary Forces Identification

**What larger world was it embedded in:** the wider Protestant Reformation itself, and, more broadly, the confessionally plural, civilly enforced religious landscape of 16th–17th-century Central and Western Europe, in which every established authority — Catholic, Lutheran, and Reformed alike — held civil and religious jurisdiction as a single, fused authority this movement's own gathered-church logic directly refused. — *Widely Accepted.*

## 6. World Continuity & Distinction

**What this world inherits:** the wider Protestant Reformation's own conviction that Scripture, not inherited ecclesiastical authority, is the final court of appeal for church practice — shared with, but pushed further than, its magisterial Reformation contemporaries (Lutheran Wittenberg, the Reformed cities of Zurich and Geneva, both already Built & Live in this project's portfolio, per Step 0 §2 A3); and, more distantly and only as a disclosed outside observation rather than the movement's own self-understanding, a structural echo of the already-built Donatism world's own gathered-church/rebaptism/willing-suffering shape — an echo this movement's own vendored voices (Menno, Dirk Philips) explicitly and directly reject by name (§1 above). This document states that rejection plainly rather than smoothing it into a lineage the sources themselves refuse.

**What this world transmits forward:** believer's baptism and the gathered, voluntary, disciplined congregation as an ecclesiological model — the "free-church principle," per the project's own world-census entry, that "membership is a voluntary adult commitment and that the church should not be an arm of the state," which the census records as having become "one of the most widely adopted Protestant ideas in the world." The *Martyrs Mirror* itself continues to be printed and read; the Ausbund continues in Amish liturgical use (corpus-map note, `the-anabaptist-movements.yaml`).

**Living heir(s), stated as present-day institutional fact, not as a confirmed Article 29 finding:** the project's own world-census entry for this candidate records that "Mennonites, Amish, Hutterites and Brethren all descend from it" directly. This document records the Mennonite, Amish, and Hutterite lines as *Widely Accepted* direct descent. The Brethren (the Schwarzenau Brethren, 1708) are, on standard scholarship, a Pietist–Anabaptist hybrid rather than an unmixed continuation; this document carries the census's own "directly" wording forward for the Brethren as attributed wording, not as independently verified fact. This is a claim about historical continuity, which this document is positioned to make — and it expressly does **not** treat any of this as a confirmed Article 29 Living Tradition Status finding, nor does it characterize any present-day Mennonite, Amish, Hutterite, or Brethren community's own contemporary self-understanding, belief, or practice, which belongs to those living communities and is not this document's to define (Article 28). That distinction — a historical formation world is not the same thing as a present-day community's own self-understanding — is carried forward as binding on every later document in this world's build, per Article 28/29 discipline as applied in this project's other confirmed worlds (e.g. `worlds/alx/Doc_01...md` §9's treatment of the Coptic Orthodox Church).
