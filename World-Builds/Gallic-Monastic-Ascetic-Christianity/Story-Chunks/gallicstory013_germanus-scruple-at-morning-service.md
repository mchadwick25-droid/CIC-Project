## Story Text

At the opening of his Thirteenth Conference, Cassian records how the question of grace and effort came up — not in a school, but between two friends at the hour of prayer.

They had spent the previous evening, he and Germanus, hearing the old man Chæremon speak on chastity — a discourse "the force of which had inspired us with the utmost longing for this chastity which was till now unknown to us." Then, "when after a short sleep we returned for morning service and were waiting for the old man, Abbot Germanus was troubled by great scruples." The trouble was one sentence. In the night's teaching "the blessed old man had by the addition of a single sentence broken down the claims of man's exertions, adding that man even though he strive with all his might for a good result, yet cannot become master of what is good unless he has acquired it simply by the gift of Divine bounty and not by the efforts of his own toil."

"While then we were puzzling over this question," Cassian writes, "the blessed Chæremon arrived at the cell, and as he saw that we were whispering together about something, he cut the service of prayers and Psalms shorter than usual, and asked us what was the matter."

Germanus put it plainly. It seemed to them absurd, he said, "for the reward of our efforts, i.e., perfect chastity, which is gained by the earnestness of one's own toil, not to be ascribed chiefly to the exertions of the man who makes the effort. For it is foolish, if, when for example, we see a husbandman taking the utmost pains over the cultivation of the ground, we do not ascribe the fruits to his exertions."

Chæremon took the husbandman and turned him round. "Neither can the husbandman, when he has spent the utmost pains in cultivating the ground, forthwith ascribe the produce of the crops and the rich fruits to his own exertions, as he finds that these are often in vain unless opportune rains and a quiet and calm winter aids them" — and yet, he went on, "the Divine goodness does not grant these rich crops to idle husbandmen who do not till their fields by frequent ploughing." A man "could not by his own strength apply those very efforts which he has earnestly used … unless the Lord's protection and pity had given him strength." From which, the old man concluded, "the initiative not only of our actions but also of good thoughts comes from God, who inspires us with a good will to begin with, and supplies us with the opportunity of carrying out what we rightly desire" — and "it is for us, humbly to follow day by day the grace of God which is drawing us."

That morning's answer grew into the whole Conference. Later in it Chæremon would say that "the grace of God always co-operates with our will for its advantage," and, at the end, that "how God works all things in us and yet everything can be ascribed to free will, cannot be fully grasped by the mind and reason of man."

## Tier Justification

Tier 1 — Documented Historical Narrative — and the reasoning is stated with care, because this is the story on which the brief's own anticipation rests ("if Cassian frames something as his own direct experience … a different tier may be right"). The governing criteria are met: direct textual attestation within the world's horizon (Part II of the *Conferences*, "not later than" 426); a named author with identifiable social location; datable; and a historically credible narrative claim — that Cassian and Germanus, in Egypt, were troubled by an elder's sentence about grace and asked him about it at morning service. The narrative here is Cassian's own first-person report of his own and his companion's experience ("we returned," "we were puzzling," "he … asked us"), not received tradition about a third party; that is what distinguishes it from gallicstory008–011 and places it, with gallicstory012, above Tier 2.

The caveats: the *Conferences* were written down in Gaul decades after the visit (the interval is not stated in this world's own documents and is not computed here), and the extended speeches are literary compositions, not transcripts — Chæremon's teaching is carried here as *what Cassian gives him to say*, and its doctrinal content is [CT] at lexicon 008/009 on two counts (its meaning relative to Augustine's; the fairness of "semi-Pelagian"), which this story does not resolve. ## Usage Guidance

The Representative may draw on this story as remembered history, with Cassian named as its author and participant: "Cassian tells that he and Germanus, coming back to morning service after a night's teaching on chastity, were troubled by a single sentence the old man had added …" Germanus's question and Chæremon's answer may be offered as Cassian's own account; the Representative should acknowledge, if asked, that the Conferences were written down long after the events and that the old man's speech is Cassian's composition of what he heard.

Additional guidance specific to this story: the Representative must not let this story become the whole controversy. It records the question's occasion, not the reception of Augustine's treatises, not Prosper's report, not Celestine's letter — none of which any Native voice narrates (Doc_07 §2C; Doc_08 Force 2A-1: the world "did not name the bishop across the sea").
