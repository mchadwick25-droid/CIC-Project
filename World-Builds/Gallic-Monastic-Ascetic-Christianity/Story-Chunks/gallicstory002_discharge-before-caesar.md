## Story Text

In his *Life of St. Martin*, Sulpitius Severus records how Martin left the army — the scene the whole of Tours's memory of him turns on.

The barbarians, Sulpitius writes, "were rushing within the two divisions of Gaul," and Julian Cæsar, gathering an army at the city of the Vaugiones, "began to distribute a donative to the soldiers." The men were called forward one by one. When it came to Martin's turn he judged it the moment to ask for his discharge, because "he did not think it would be proper for him, if he were not to continue in the service, to receive a donative," and said to Cæsar: "Hitherto I have served you as a soldier: allow me now to become a soldier to God: let the man who is to serve thee receive thy donative: I am the soldier of Christ: it is not lawful for me to fight."

Julian — "the tyrant," in Sulpitius's word — stormed, and said the man was withdrawing "from fear of the battle, which was to take place on the morrow, and not from any religious feeling." Martin, "all the more resolute from the danger that had been set before him," answered: "If this conduct of mine is ascribed to cowardice, and not to faith, I will take my stand unarmed before the line of battle tomorrow, and in the name of the Lord Jesus, protected by the sign of the cross, and not by shield or helmet, I will safely penetrate the ranks of the enemy." He was put back in prison, "determined on proving his words true by exposing himself unarmed to the barbarians."

"But, on the following day," Sulpitius writes, "the enemy sent ambassadors to treat about peace and surrendered both themselves and all their possessions." There was no battle. Sulpitius does not leave the reader to draw the conclusion: "who can doubt that this victory was due to the saintly man?" — it was granted him that he should not be sent unarmed to the fight, and the good Lord, "that his blessed eyes might not be pained by witnessing the death of others," removed all necessity for fighting, so that "the enemy being subdued without bloodshed, no one should suffer death."

## Tier Justification

Tier 1 — Documented Historical Narrative — with its caveats stated. The governing criteria are met: direct textual attestation within the world's horizon (the *Vita*, c. 397, of a man who died in 397 — Doc_01 §10, §2.4); a named author with identifiable social location (Sulpitius, of an "illustrious family," a personal acquaintance of Martin who says he "obtained my information partly from himself" — *Vita* ch. XXV, re-read at Doc_09; Doc_02 §1.1); datable with reasonable confidence (the text places the scene under "Julian Cæsar" gathering an army in Gaul against the barbarians — its own internal anchor; the identification with Julian the Apostate is Roberts's editorial note, and the text gives no year; the *Vita*'s own date is Widely Accepted, Doc_02 §12); and a historically credible narrative claim — a Christian soldier seeking discharge at a donative and being accused of cowardice is an ordinary, non-miraculous event of a kind the period knew.

The caveats Tier 1 itself requires "for the author's perspective and genre": Sulpitius was not present (the event predates his acquaintance with Martin by decades); no independent witness to it survives in this world's Native corpus (Gennadius, row 30, ch. XIX corroborates only that Sulpitius wrote "a Life of the holy Martin, monk and bishop"); and the enemy's surrender the next morning is presented by Sulpitius as a providential victory "due to the saintly man." The surrender is carried here as what Sulpitius reports; the reading of it as Martin's victory is carried as Sulpitius's own, not as fact. This is the same discipline Donatism's own Story Inventory applied to the omen and vision in Macrobius's letter (donstory003): a Tier 1 narrative may carry its author's interpretive framing at Contested strength without the whole account dropping to Tier 3, so long as the framing is named. The Confidence rating is Widely Accepted rather than Documented for the reason Doc_01 §2.2 already gave for the Hilary–Martin link: one ancient witness, however close, is not the "multiple independent sources" Documented requires.

Construction note: the whole scene rests on the identification of the emperor as Julian and the city as Worms only through Roberts's editorial notes; Sulpitius's own text says "Julian Cæsar" and "the city of the Vaugiones." The Story Text uses the ancient text's own words.

## Usage Guidance

The Representative may draw on this story as remembered history, with Sulpitius named as its source and his distance stated where it matters: "In his *Life of Martin*, Sulpitius records that Martin said to Cæsar …" The words of Martin's refusal and his offer to stand unarmed may be offered as the tradition's remembered words; the Representative should not claim more certainty than one hagiographer supplies, and should present the barbarians' surrender as what Sulpitius reports and its meaning ("this victory was due to the saintly man") as Sulpitius's own reading.

Additional guidance specific to this story: if a participant reads the scene as pacifism or conscientious objection in a modern sense, the Representative should let the world's own words correct the frame — Martin does not say war is wrong; he says he is now the soldier of Christ and it is "not lawful for me" to fight, and he offers to walk into the enemy's line. The world's category is a change of service, not a refusal of service (Doc_08 1A-2's Layer 2). If asked what the south made of this scene, the honest answer is that Cassian never mentions Martin (Doc_05 §7.3), though his *Institutes* open with the same idiom.
