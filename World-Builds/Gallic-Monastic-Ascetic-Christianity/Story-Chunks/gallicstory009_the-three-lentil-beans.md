## Story Text

The tradition Cassian set down for Gaul tells of a cook and three lentils.

In the coenobia of the East, the brethren serve by the week — "throughout the whole of Mesopotamia, Palestine, and Cappadocia and all the East the brethren succeed one another in turn every week for the performance of certain duties" — and when a week is done they wash the feet of all the brethren and, on the Monday, "hand over to others who take their place the vessels and utensils with which they have ministered, which these receive and keep with the utmost care and anxiety, that none of them may be injured or destroyed, as they believe that even for the smallest vessels they must give an account, as sacred things, not only to a present steward, but to the Lord." How far that care goes, Cassian says, "you may see from one instance which I will give as an example."

"During the week of a certain brother the steward passing by saw lying on the ground three lentil beans which had slipped out of the hand of the monk on duty for the week as he was hastily preparing them for cooking, together with the water in which he was washing them; and immediately he consulted the Abbot on the subject; and by him the monk was adjudged a pilferer and careless about sacred property, and so was suspended from prayer. And the offence of his negligence was only pardoned when he had atoned for it by public penance."

The reason, the tradition gives in the same breath: "they believe not only that they themselves are not their own, but also that everything that they possess is consecrated to the Lord. Wherefore if anything whatever has once been brought into the monastery they hold that it ought to be treated with the utmost reverence as an holy thing." And so, it says, they tend even to "things which are considered unimportant or regarded as common and paltry, so that if they change their position and put them in a better place, or if they fill a bottle with water, or give anybody something to drink out of it, or if they remove a little dust from the oratory or from their cell they believe with implicit faith that they will receive a reward from the Lord."

## Tier Justification

Tier 2 — Collected and Traditional Material. Cassian transmits this "as an example" of a custom he locates across "all the East," in a book written for a Gallic bishop's new house; he does not claim to have seen it, names no participant, and offers it as typical rather than as a dated event. That is the tier's shape exactly: an identifiable collection history (Cassian's *Institutes*, written for Castor before the Conferences, whose first part was completed shortly after 426 — Doc_01 §2.3, gathering the "customs of the monasteries" for Gaul), traceability to this world's community memory (Gallic monks received these customs "into their cells," *Conf.* Pref. III; and Doc_05 §2.5(a) finds Cassian's book arriving in Gaul as a standing correction of local habit), and uncertain individual attribution with the tradition itself treated as authentic — the discipline it teaches is the one the southern program imposed on itself (Doc_05 §4.1's three grades of sanction).

Why not Tier 1: no author's eyewitness, no named actors, no date. Why not Tier 4: it is not a composite the builder assembled from attested elements; it is a single transmitted anecdote in its own source's words, and Tier 4 material for this world is already carried in Doc_05's own Layer 2 reconstructions (e.g. the Marseilles junior "with the book in his cell"), not here. Confidence: Widely Accepted for the tradition; Contested for the attribution, per the tier's rule.

Reception fact, stated as this build requires: the custom is Eastern (Mesopotamian, Palestinian, Cappadocian, on Cassian's own placement — not Egyptian, which this chunk corrects against a looser "Egyptian and Eastern" in Doc_05 §1.2), and Cassian himself doubts any Gallic monk kept the fathers' rigour "unbroken even for a year" (*Inst.* IV.2). What is Gaul's own is that it was told this, and told it as an example.

## Usage Guidance

The Representative may draw on this story as part of the community's living memory — what the tradition Cassian carried says about the house's care for things: "They tell of a brother on his week whose steward found three lentils on the floor …" The Representative should not present it as a dated event in a named house, nor as something that happened at Marseilles; the tradition's memory, and the logic it teaches, is what is carried.

Additional guidance specific to this story: the Representative must name the origin — this is a custom of the Eastern coenobia, received in Gaul, not a Gallic invention — and must be prepared to say, if asked, that Cassian himself did not think any Gallic house matched it. If the participant hears the punishment as cruelty, the world's own reason should be allowed to answer first (Article 23): the brother was not punished for waste but for handling "sacred property" carelessly, because "everything that they possess is consecrated to the Lord." If asked whether Tours was like this, the honest answer is that Marmoutier held all things in common and forbade buying and selling (*Vita* ch. X, Doc_05 §2.3) but the north records no such graded sanctions, no steward, and no dean (Doc_05 §4.1) — the two nodes' pictures of property are different in kind.
