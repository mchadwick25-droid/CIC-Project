## Story Text

The tradition tells — as Abbot Piamun told it to Cassian and Germanus, and as Cassian wrote it down for Gaul — of the patience of Paphnutius, when he was young.

Paphnutius, Piamun said, loved the desert of Scete so much that the other anchorites called him "Bubalis" "because he always delighted in dwelling in the desert as if with a sort of innate liking." Even as a boy he was so grave and steadfast that the great men of the time "put him on a level with the Elders out of regard for his virtues, and thought fit to admit him to their order." And that was the trouble: "the same envy, which formerly excited the minds of his brethren against the patriarch Joseph, inflamed one out of the number of his brethren with a burning and consuming jealousy."

So this brother, wanting "to mar his beauty by some blemish or spot," waited until Paphnutius had gone to church on a Sunday, slipped into his cell, and "slyly hid his own book among the boughs which he used to weave of palm branches," and went off to church himself "as if with a pure and clean conscience." When the service ended, "in the presence of all the brethren he brought his complaint to S. Isidore who was Presbyter of this desert before this same Paphnutius, and declared that his book had been stolen from his cell." Nobody knew what to think — no theft had ever been heard of in that desert before, or since. The accuser himself proposed the remedy: keep everyone in church and send chosen men to search the cells one by one. Three elders searched, "and at last found the book hidden in the cell of Paphnutius among the boughs of the palms … just as the plotter had hidden it."

Then, the tradition says, Paphnutius — "although he was perfectly clear in the sincerity of his conscience, yet like one who acknowledged the guilt of thieving, gave himself up entirely to make amends and humbly asked for a plan of repentance, as he was so careful of his shame and modesty (and feared) lest if he tried to remove the stain of the theft by words, he might further be branded as a liar, as no one would believe anything but what had been found out." He left the church "not cast down in mind but rather trusting to the judgment of God," wept at his prayers, fasted three times as often, and for almost a fortnight came early on Saturday and Sunday "not to receive the Holy Communion but to prostrate himself on the threshold of the Church and humbly ask for pardon."

And then God, "Who is the witness of all secret things," would not let him be tried longer: the true thief, "possessed by a most fierce demon, he made known all the craft of his secret plot, and the same man who had conceived the accusation and the cheat betrayed it." No one could free him — not the saints of that desert, not Isidore himself, whose gift was such that "no one who was possessed was ever brought to his doors without being at once healed" — "for Christ was reserving this glory for the young Paphnutius, that the man should be cleansed only by the prayers of him against whom he had plotted." Piamun ends: "If then we want to attain to his height of virtue, we must lay the same foundation to begin with."

## Formation Ecology Connection

This is the south's own story of **G7 — the interior road** at the virtue Cassian's fathers rank highest — "Humility therefore is the mistress of all virtues" (*Conf.* XV.7, Doc_04 G6) — and Doc_05 §6A names it first among the south's *exempla*, quoting the very sentence that makes the story: he "gave himself up entirely to make amends … lest if he tried to remove the stain of the theft by words, he might further be branded as a liar." Doc_05 §8 reads it as "a story about envy," the fault weighted as "a food of the mind" (*Inst.* V.21). It is also, in its resolution, the south's one story that *uses* an exorcism — and uses it exactly as *Conf.* XV.7 allows: not as the good monk's boast but as the vindication God reserves. Set beside the north's Brictio (gallicstory006), it is the cross-node pair Doc_08 Force 2B-4 makes visible — a community wronged from within, answered at Tours by the master's patience with the accuser and at Scete by the accused's silence before the community — the two economies of power displayed and power disowned.

What it does that a lexicon entry cannot: it gives the Representative the south's own picture of what a formed person does when innocent and disbelieved — and the tradition's answer is not to argue.

## Tier Justification

Tier 2 — Collected and Traditional Material — and the case is worth stating, since this story comes closer to two other tiers than any other southern chunk. It is *not* Tier 1, though Cassian was present when Piamun told it: what Cassian attests at first hand is the *telling*; the events are of Paphnutius's youth, decades earlier, reported by Piamun as received desert memory (introduced, at *Conf.* XVIII.15, as "the other instance of Abbot Paphnutius" — corrected, Round 1 review finding M6: the prior draft's "as we promised to do … let us give the other instance" spliced in a phrase from a different chapter, *Conf.* II.5, not this locus), and the resolution rests on a possession and a divinely reserved cure that are the tradition's own claims, not an eyewitness's. It is *not* Tier 3, though it has a hagiographic shape (the boy admired by the elders; the reserved glory), because it is not a *Life* of a saint but an *exemplum* on one virtue, attached to a named elder still living at the time of telling ("in which he is now Presbyter"), inside an identifiable collection (Cassian's Eighteenth Conference, the third part of the *Conferences*, dedicated to named Gallic recipients — Registry row 10). The governing text's Tier 2 profile fits: collected form, identifiable collection history (Scete → Piamun → Cassian → Gaul), traceable to this world's community memory by reception into Gallic cells, attribution uncertain in its details, the tradition treated as authentic — Piamun offers it as "the example of patience" to be imitated.

Genuine uncertainty at construction: the demonic resolution is the same kind of element that is caveated inside this repository's Tier 1 Tours stories (the angel in gallicstory005, the demons in gallicstory006). It is not caveated here because the tier's own register already carries it — "the tradition tells" — and because the possession is the story's climax, not a frame around a documentary core. Confidence: Widely Accepted for the tradition; Contested for the attribution.

Reception fact: a story of Scete; a Gallic reading. What is this world's own is only the reception — and the fact that the Gallic dedicatees of this Conference were told, in the same sentence, "we must lay the same foundation."

## Usage Guidance

The Representative may draw on this story as part of the community's living memory — "As Abbot Piamun told it, and Cassian handed it on to Gaul, they say of Paphnutius that …" The Representative should not claim the events, and especially the possession and the reserved healing, as historically verified; the tradition's memory and the virtue it teaches are what is carried.

Additional guidance specific to this story: name the origin — this is Scete's story, received at Marseilles. If a participant asks whether the story teaches that the falsely accused should not defend themselves, the Representative should let the world's own reason stand as the world gave it (Article 23) — Paphnutius feared that words would only add "liar" to "thief" — and then say honestly that this is the tradition's ideal, and that the north told a story in the same situation with the opposite shape (Martin *did* reprove Brictio; the community's own text calls Brictio's charges "reproaches"). If asked whether the exorcism at the end contradicts Cassian's fathers' warning against exorcists, the honest answer is that the story is careful on exactly that point: the cure is reserved to the wronged man's *prayers*, and he never "professed himself" anything.
