## 1. Human Ecology — who was formed, in what body, under what sky

*(Forces integration: §1.4, labeled, required by Forces Fw §4 Step 5.)*

### 1.1 Who the participants were

**Tours.** The founding participant is a discharged soldier: Martin, catechumen from the age of ten (Doc_03 8.3), who before Caesar "as the barbarians were rushing within the two divisions of Gaul" says "Hitherto I have served you as a soldier: allow me now to become a soldier to God … I am the soldier of Christ: it is not lawful for me to fight" (*Vita* ch. IV, `ii.ii.v`; G8's literal origin, Doc_04). Around him at Marmoutier: "eighty disciples, who were being disciplined after the example of the saintly master," of whom "many … were such as are deemed of noble rank" who "though far differently brought up, had forced themselves down to this degree of humility and patient endurance" (*Vita* ch. X, `ii.ii.xi`, re-read this pass); "the brethren of younger years" who transcribe and "the elders" who pray (same chapter); the presbyters and clerics who are Martin's "elders" in matters of tradition (*Vita* ch. XI, Doc_04 G4); a soldier who "had renounced the military life in the Church, having professed himself a monk" and his wife, whom "Martin had ordered to have a place in the nunnery [Roberts's fn.: *monasterio*] of the young women" (*Dial.* II.11, `ii.iv.ii.xi`, re-read); the crowds — "the whole city poured forth to meet his body; all the inhabitants of the district and villages," monks "almost to the number of two thousand," "bands arrayed in cloaks, either old men whose life-labor was finished, or young soldiers who had just taken the oath of allegiance to Christ. Then, too, there was the choir of virgins" (*Ep.* III, `ii.iii.iii`, file lines 2032–2044, re-read); and, as the object of the northern ministry, "the Gallic rustics in their wretched folly" of the unconverted countryside (Doc_03 6.8; *Vita* chs. 12–15). Martin's own mother, whom he "set free … from the errors of heathenism, though his father continued to cleave to its evils" (*Vita* ch. VI, `ii.ii.vii`, read this pass), and Sulpitius's sister, to whom he "wrote … many Letters exhorting to love of God and contempt of the world" (Gennadius ch. XIX, row 30 ancient text, re-verified at `npnf203` file line 35502), complete the northern cast that the sources let us see.

**Lérins–Marseilles.** The participant is a man come in from "the manifold and deplorable tempests of secular warfare" to "cast anchor in the harbour of religion" (Vincent, *Comm.* ch. 1 [2], Doc_03 1.12) — or, in the founding narrative's own Latin, a man whom nobility and wealth would not let escape notice: of Honoratus and his brother Venantius in their first ascetic years, Hilary of Arles says (my rendering of rough OCR, wording Inferential/Thin, presence checkable at file lines 447–462) that "to reach obscurity and poverty was not permitted them: the more their life was hidden, the more their fame shone out" (*"Pervenire illis ad ignobilitatem et paupertatem non licebat: quanto magis eorum vita abscondebatur, tanto magis fama emicabat"*). The Marseilles junior whom Cassian writes for is a man who must be searched for "a single coin from his former possessions clinging to him" (*Inst.* IV.3, `iv.iii.iv.iii`, re-read) and who, if noble, must "come down to the want and poverty of Christ" and "not be ashamed to be on a level with the poor, that is with the body of the brethren" (*Inst.* IV.5, `iv.iii.iv.v`, re-read). Eucherius "came to Lérins with his wife Galla and his two sons," Salonius and Veranus, who "were put under the successive tutelage of Honoratus, Hilary, Salvian and Vincent" — **Sanford's own 1930 Introduction** (p. 12, file lines 852–857), editorial and without a Registry row of its own (Doc_03 §10 item 8), paraphrasing Eucherius's letter to Salonius that Doc_02 §13A quotes in Sanford's translation; carried at Widely Accepted for the tutelage chain and at editorial strength for Galla. Cassian's own house is two: Gennadius's "two monasteries, that is to say one for men and one for women, which are still standing" (ch. LXII, ancient text, Doc_02 §1.2). Salvian's participant is "the religious" — "lowly — they wish to be so; poor — they delight in poverty" (*Gov.* I.2, Doc_03 1.13) — and the half-hearted rich who "make a show of renouncing their wealth" (III.3, Doc_03 1.11).

**The Article 20 finding, stated before anything is built on it.** Every voice in this world's Native corpus is a literate, Latin, male member of the provincial elite: Sulpitius "distinguished by his birth" (Gennadius ch. XIX), Cassian, Vincent, Salvian, Hilary of Arles, Eucherius. The ordinary Gallic Christian is visible only as a crowd (the funeral; the election, *Vita* ch. IX), as a mission-field (the rustics), as a body to be exorcised (the energumens, Doc_03 6.5), or — in Salvian alone — as the poor "murdered" by the rich man's taxes (§1.4). Ordinary believer psychology is not visible and is not reconstructed here. Women's presence is treated at §1.3 as a separate, affirmative duty.

### 1.2 The body: dress, food, sleep, cell, labor, climate, sexuality

This world thought about the body in G8's idiom — dress as armour, the day as a campaign — and disagreed about it across nodes on exactly one garment.

**Dress.** In the south the monk is "girt … with this twofold girdle" before he learns anything else: *Institutes* II opens with the words "Girt, therefore, with this twofold girdle of which we have spoken, the soldier of Christ should next learn the system of the canonical prayers and Psalms" (*Inst.* II.1, `iv.iii.ii.i`, **new this pass**) — the office is the second lesson, dress the first. Each Egyptian garment is a "mystery" of the inner life, and the whole kit is then explicitly refitted for Gaul: "the severity of the winter does not allow us to be satisfied with slippers or tunics or a single frock; and the covering of tiny hoods or the wearing of a sheepskin would afford a subject for derision instead of edifying the spectators" (*Inst.* I.10, Doc_03 8.6; G2's climate clause). At Marmoutier "most of them were clothed in garments of camels' hair. Any dress approaching to softness was there deemed criminal" (*Vita* ch. X, re-read; Roberts's fn. reads the phrase as Matt. iii.4, editorial — and Doc_04's S1 correction stands: this is not Egyptian-comparative evidence). The one documented divergence is sackcloth: Martin's "weapon," worn to the deathbed ("It is not fitting that a Christian should die except among ashes," *Ep.* III, file line 2011, re-read), against Cassian's Egyptians who "utterly disapproved of a robe of sackcloth as being visible to all and conspicuous" (*Inst.* I.2, Doc_04 §2.2, §6 point 3). The northern body is displayed as a sign; the southern body is hidden as a discipline — the same opposition G6/G7 stage at the level of narrative.

**Food.** Tours: "They all took their food together, after the hour of fasting was past. No one used wine, except when illness compelled them to do so" (*Vita* ch. X); Martin "did not indulge either in food or sleep, except in so far as the necessities of nature required" (*Vita* ch. XXVI, `ii.ii.xxvii`, read this pass). Marseilles (Cassian's Egyptian rule, prescriptive for Gaul): one goal for all — "that no one may be overburdened beyond the measure of his appetite, by gluttony" — but "a difference of time, manner, and quality of the refreshment in proportion to the difference of condition of the body, the age, **and sex**" (*Inst.* V.5, `iv.iii.v.v`, **new this pass**; the word "sex" is one of the few places Cassian's rule visibly contemplates women's houses, and is carried to §1.3); "a reasonable supply of food partaken of daily with moderation, is better than a severe and long fast at intervals" (V.9); common bread, no private diet, because anything beyond "the common use of all, is branded by the ancient tradition of the fathers as defiled with the sin of vanity" (V.23) — G4 applied to the plate; five fast days a week with "an interval of at least two days" (*Inst.* III.9, `iv.iii.iii.ix`, new); and the fast broken for a guest, because "a fast, although it is useful and advisable, is yet a free-will offering. But the exigencies of a command require the fulfilment of a work of charity" (V.24, `iv.iii.v.xxiv`, new). Salvian's religious find "toil, fasting, poverty, humility and weakness … not burdensome" (I.2, Doc_03 1.13).

**Sleep.** The south regulates it by the office: no sleep after the night office "lest our envious adversary … should by some illusion in a dream pollute the purity which has been gained by the Psalms and prayers of the night" (*Inst.* II.13, `iv.iii.ii.xiii`, new); the winter Saturday vigil "till the fourth cock-crowing" and then "at least an hour's sleep … snatched before daybreak," since "a man will certainly have to give up everything to this flesh if he tries, not in a rational manner to withhold a part only, but to refuse the whole" (III.8, `iv.iii.iii.viii`, new); the new Mattins instituted precisely against those who "prolonged their time for sleep too long" (III.4). Tours has Martin's "watchings" and "whole nights" (*Vita* ch. XXVI; *Ep.* III) and no rule.

**The cell.** Marmoutier: a wooden cell for the master, caves "hollowed" from the rock for most of the brethren, "the place could be approached only by one, and that a very narrow passage," "Rarely did any one of them go beyond the cell, unless when they assembled at the place of prayer" (*Vita* ch. X). Egypt-as-written-for-Gaul: a cell "which he inhabits alone, or is allowed to share with only one other whom partnership in work or training in discipleship and learning has joined with him" (*Inst.* II.12, `iv.iii.ii.xii`, new); after the office "none of them dares to loiter ever so little or to gossip with another: nor does he presume even to leave his cell throughout the whole day" (II.15); the cell as "some perfectly secure harbour" in which "the changeableness and wanderings of the heart" are "fastened within the barriers" by labour "like some strong and immoveable anchor" (II.14, `iv.iii.ii.xiv`, new) — the same harbour image Vincent and Eucherius use of Lérins itself (Doc_04 G2). Accidie is the cell's disease (Doc_03 4.6; *Inst.* X.2, re-read).

**Labor.** Marmoutier: "No art was practiced there, except that of transcribers, and even this was assigned to the brethren of younger years, while the elders spent their time in prayer" (*Vita* ch. X) — the north's only named craft is copying, which is also its transmission mechanism (§6A). The south: manual labour incessant, "practising equally the virtues of the body and of the soul," so that "it is not easy to say … whether they practise their incessant manual labour for the sake of spiritual meditation, or whether it is for the sake of their continuous labours that they acquire such remarkable spiritual proficiency" (*Inst.* II.14); the weekly hebdomadaries who serve and then "wash the feet of all in turn" on Sunday evening (IV.19, `iv.iii.iv.xix`, new); the steward who finds three lentils on the floor and has the cook "suspended from prayer" because "everything that they possess is consecrated to the Lord" (IV.20, re-read). Cassian names this as *Egyptian and Eastern* practice; whether Marseilles kept the hebdomadary week is not documented.

**Climate.** "This province that is frozen as it were with the cold of Gaul" (*Conf.* Pref. II, Doc_03 8.8) is a force, not a figure — it rewrites dress (I.10), the hours ("in this country neither the climate nor the weakness of our constitution would permit," IV.10–11, Doc_04 G2), and the vigils (III.8's "winter time, when the nights are longer"); it is the one force Cassian names in his own voice as changing what he transmits (§1.4).

**Sexuality and the excised body.** What this world's own text says about the sexual body reaches this build through a translator who removed most of it. Doc_01 §4 and Registry row 11 record two excisions (*Conf.* XII "On Chastity," "Not translated"; *Conf.* XXII "On Nocturnal Illusions," "This Conference is omitted"). **This pass found a third, not previously recorded anywhere in this build:** *Institutes* Book VI, "On the Spirit of Fornication," carries in this edition only the sentence "We have thought best to omit altogether the translation of this book" (`iv.iii.vi`, file lines 18889–18893). Doc_03 §10 item 3 listed *Inst.* VI among sections "not read," assuming it was present; it is not. Three body/sexuality texts are therefore absent from this world's English evidence, all removed by the same nineteenth-century hand (Gibson, row 17), all of the same content-type, none explained — the pattern Doc_02 §10 already called "plausibly motivated in exactly the way Article 20's own distinction between evidential and ecological absence anticipates," now with a third instance. A Registry extension is proposed at §10D. What survives is the *edge* of the subject: the dream-illusion (II.13; III.5); "familiarity with women" as a fault "atoned for by expulsion" or "stripes," not by the ordinary penance (IV.16, `iv.iii.iv.xvi`, re-read); "the fiery motions of the body" that "can[not] be extinguished, before the incentives of the other chief vices are utterly rooted out" (V.11, new); accidie's pretext of "that religious woman, devoted to the service of God, who is deprived of all support of kindred" (X.2, re-read); Martin's "Let not a woman enter the camp of men" (*Dial.* II.11); and *Conf.* XIII, which opens on the morning after the excised chastity conference (§2.4). The sexual body is thus visible in this world's text mainly as a danger to be guarded and a purity to be kept; whether it was also a subject of teaching in its own right — as three whole excised texts suggest — this edition does not let us see.

### 1.3 Women's presence — the Article 20 affirmative duty, discharged rather than deferred

Doc_01 §11 item 4 and Doc_04 §10 item 9 carried this forward twice. The Constitution's order of operations is: *first* make structural absence visible; *second*, and only on independent positive evidence, reconstruct within bounds, at Inferential confidence, never from silence.

**(a) The absence, named.** No woman's own voice survives in this world's Native corpus. No woman ascetic is named who lived at Tours, Lérins, or Marseilles. No superior of either women's house is named. No rule for a women's house is preserved. The letters Sulpitius wrote to his sister are attested (Gennadius ch. XIX) and lost. Of the men's literature that survives, the three texts most likely to have said most about the body, chastity, and (possibly) women's houses were removed by a Victorian translator (§1.2). The structural cause is double: the ancient sources are male monastic literature addressed to men and bishops (Cassian to Castor, Leontius, Honoratus; Sulpitius to Desiderius and to Bassula — a woman, his mother-in-law, addressee of *Ep.* III per its own title, `ii.iii.iii`); and the modern edition subtracted body-material on top of that. **Facilitator-Governance hand-off (per Doc_02 §13 item 10's pattern):** whoever builds this world's Facilitator apparatus should receive this paragraph directly. A participant asking this world's Representative about women's formation must be told that the world had women's houses and consecrated virgins and that the sources let us hear none of them.

**(b) The positive traces, every one, with its layer and strength.** These are the independent positive evidence Article 20 requires before any reconstruction; they are listed so a reviewer can check that nothing below exceeds them.

1. **Saint-Sauveur, Marseilles** — "one for men and one for women, which are still standing" (Gennadius ch. LXII, ancient text, c. 495). Documented.
2. **A women's house under Martin's jurisdiction at Tours** — the soldier's wife "whom Martin had ordered to have a place in the nunnery [*monasterio*] of the young women" (*Dial.* II.11, `ii.iv.ii.xi`). Widely Accepted (Sulpitius's own report; one voice).
3. **Consecrated virginity conferred by the bishop, with its own dress** — Arborius "consecrated the girl to God, and devoted her to perpetual virginity … he would not suffer her to be consecrated by any other than Martin, through his placing upon her the dress characteristic of virginity" (*Vita* ch. XIX, `ii.ii.xx`, read this pass). Widely Accepted.
4. **The enclosed virgin who refused Martin** — "that virgin who had so completely withdrawn herself from the eyes of all men, that she did not admit to her presence Martin himself"; "an example only too rare in that locality"; Martin accepted her gift "declaring that her blessing was by no means to be rejected by a priest, since she was indeed to be placed before many priests" (*Dial.* II.12, `ii.iv.ii.xii`, read this pass). Widely Accepted. This is the closest the northern corpus comes to a woman's own act of formation — and it is her *refusal to be seen*.
5. **The choir of virgins** at Martin's funeral, "abstaining out of modesty from weeping" (*Ep.* III), and "one of the virgins" among the five who saw the globe of fire at the altar (*Dial.* II.2, `ii.iv.ii.ii`, read this pass) — virgins present at the liturgy and at the burial as a recognized body. Widely Accepted.
6. **Martin's mother**, converted by him (*Vita* ch. VI). Widely Accepted.
7. **Sulpitius's sister** and **Bassula** as addressees (Gennadius ch. XIX; *Ep.* III title). Documented as addressees; content lost or male-authored.
8. **Claudia** (row 5, the *Doubtful Letters*, pseudepigraphal, elite — Doc_02 §6, S19): a male-authored letter *to* a woman on virginity, "the brides of Christ" (Doc_03 8.4). Attribution-doubtful; not a woman's voice.
9. **Cassian's dietary rule contemplating "sex"** (*Inst.* V.5, new) and his fault-list's "familiarity with women" (IV.16): the rule knows women exist near the men's house. Documented as text; Egyptian in frame.
10. **Cassian's "religious woman … deprived of all support of kindred"** (*Inst.* X.2): a category of vowed woman living alone and dependent, offered as accidie's pretext — Egyptian frame, written for Gaul. Documented as text; whether it describes Gallic reality is Inferential.
11. **Hilary of Arles on Honoratus and Venantius**: "how rare the visiting of women, even of near kin" (*"quam rara feminarum visitatio etiam proximarum,"* file line 458, my rendering, Inferential/Thin for wording) — women named only as the visitors the brothers kept rare. Presence of the phrase checkable by grep.
12. **Galla**, Eucherius's wife, who "came to Lérins" with him — Sanford's Introduction (editorial, no Registry row). Editorial strength only.

**(c) Bounded reconstruction, at Inferential confidence, not exceeding (b).** Women's houses existed at both nodes within this world's window, each under a man's jurisdiction — a bishop's at Tours (trace 2), a presbyter-founder's at Marseilles (trace 1); no woman's own authority within them is attested. Consecrated virginity was a recognized, dressed, episcopally-conferred state in the north (trace 3), and virgins acted as a corporate body at worship and burial (trace 5). The ideal held out for women by the men who wrote was enclosure and invisibility: Martin's own rule for the soldier's wife — "let the woman keep herself within the protection of the walls. She, too, certainly has her own glory, if, when her husband is absent, she maintains her chastity; and the first excellence, as well as completed victory of that, is, that she should not be seen" (*Dial.* II.11) — and his praise of the virgin who would not see him (trace 4). Article 23 requires this to be rendered as the world held it: not as a deprivation the world recognized, but as the woman's own "glory" and "victory" in the world's own words; the analyst's judgment that this is also the mechanism of her silence belongs here, in the analytical layer, and nowhere in inhabited prose. What cannot be said: anything about women's formation from within — their prayer, their rule, their elders, their disclosure, their reading. That silence is the finding.

### 1.4 Forces integration — how the outside world entered the body and the house

*Connects to Doc_01 §7, Doc_02 §11/§13A, Doc_04 forces notations at G1, G2, G8, G10.*

**The initiating setting (Doc_01 §7) as the participants lived it.** The Roman army and the late-Roman civil career are not background: they are the *before* of every founding biography. Martin's renunciation is a discharge scene before Caesar with barbarians across the Rhine (*Vita* ch. IV); Vincent's is a retreat from "secular warfare" (*Comm.* ch. 1 [2]); Honoratus's is a flight from a nobility that "would not permit" obscurity (Hilary, above); Cassian's junior arrives with worldly clothes that the steward keeps "until by different sorts of temptations and trials they can recognize the excellence of his progress" and gives back to him if he "grow[s] cold" (*Inst.* IV.6, `iv.iii.iv.vi`, re-read) — the world is kept in a cupboard against the day of failure. G8 is this force's interior form: the soldier does not stop being a soldier; he changes service.

**Arianism and Hilary's exile** enter Martin's body directly: after Milan, "when the Arian heresy had spread through the whole world … and when he, almost single-handed, was fighting most strenuously against the treachery of the priests, and had been subjected to many punishments (for he was publicly scourged, and at last was compelled to leave the city)," he found "the Church in the two divisions of Gaul in a distracted condition through the departure also of the holy Hilarius, whom the violence of the heretics had driven into exile," was expelled again by Auxentius, and "withdrew to the island Gallinaria," where he ate hellebore and nearly died (*Vita* ch. VI, `ii.ii.vii`, read this pass). This is the one place this world's own text touches the world Registry row 33 excludes (Hilary of Poitiers's Nicene episcopate); it is used here only as the force that scattered Martin before Ligugé, per Doc_01 §2.2/§8.1, and nothing of Hilary's own teaching is imported.

**Climate** is the force Cassian names in his own voice as changing what he transmits (§1.2). It is experienced from within not as weather but as *incapacity* — "the weakness of our constitution" (IV.10–11) — and as a mild shame: Gaul is the "frozen" province a monk wants to leave for Egypt (*Conf.* Pref. II). G2's receptive mode is felt in the body as being the colder, weaker, later people.

**Fiscal and civic ruin (row 43, forces-licensed; Marseilles, 439–450).** Salvian is the only Native voice that lets the Roman economy into the text, and he lets it in as the world's own moral catastrophe. The commonwealth is "strangled by the cords of taxation as if by the hands of brigands," and "very many rich men are found whose taxes are murdering the poor" (*Gov.* IV.6, Sanford p. 109, file lines 5014–5020, **new this pass**); "what cities are there, or even what municipalities and villages, in which there are not as many tyrants as curials?" (V.4, p. 139, file line 6317, new); "you find men passing over everywhere, now to the Goths, now to the Bagaudae … for they would rather live as free men, though in seeming captivity, than as captives in seeming liberty" (V.5, p. 142, file lines 6455–6463, new); and of the Bagaudae themselves, "despoiled, afflicted, and murdered by wicked and bloodthirsty magistrates … We call those men rebels and utterly abandoned, whom we ourselves have forced into crime" (V.6, p. 143, file lines 6475–6505, new). *Named Tension (Forces Fw §5.3):* Salvian's picture is a preacher's indictment, not a survey — Sanford's own Introduction (editorial) treats his hyperbole as a critical problem, and Doc_03 §10 item 2 already found his genre to be "moral-societal judgment," not description. It is carried here at the strength of one Native, polemical, in-window witness. **How it penetrated the house, and how the house received it:** the monastery's poverty is a *chosen* inversion of the province's imposed one. Salvian's religious are "poor — they delight in poverty" (I.2) in a city where, on his own account, the poor are being killed by other men's taxes; Cassian's junior is searched for a single coin (IV.3) and told that "even for the smallest vessels they must give an account, as sacred things" (IV.20) in a province where the curial is a tyrant. This document does not claim the *Institutes* (c. 420s) were written in answer to the fiscal ruin Salvian describes (439–450); it claims only that the two texts sit in the same city within thirty years and that, from within, the monk's voluntary poverty and the province's involuntary ruin were the same fact seen from opposite sides. G10 is the frame in which the second entered the text at all (Doc_04 G10 forces notation: "the only gravity the barbarian force attaches to").

**Barbarian pressure** enters the body only through Salvian — "the country of the Belgae burst into flames, then the rich estates of the luxurious Aquitanians, and after these the whole body of the Gallic provinces" (VII.12, Doc_04 G10) — and, a generation earlier and far more lightly, as the occasion of Martin's discharge (*Vita* ch. IV). In Cassian, Vincent, Hilary, and Eucherius as read, no barbarian appears. This is a proportionality finding (Forces Fw §5.2): the force that historians treat as the period's dominant one left, in this world's formation literature, one indignant book and one discharge scene.

## 2. Community Ecology — how a person was actually formed, node by node

*(Forces integration: §2.5, labeled, required by Forces Fw §4 Step 5.)*

### 2.1 The formative relationship (G5, G1, G7)

**Tours: formation by proximity to one master.** The mechanism is stated once and repeated: disciples are "disciplined after the example of the saintly master" (*Vita* ch. X); the *Life* is written "which shall serve in future as an example to others" (*Vita* ch. I, Doc_04 G5); at the end, "through his example so numerous plants had sprung up for the service of the Lord" (*Ep.* III, file line 2039, re-read). There is no probation, no dean, no disclosure of thoughts, no named rule; there is a wooden cell in the middle and eighty caves around it, "the place of prayer," and a man who, when asked for straw on his deathbed, answers "I have sinned if I leave you a different example" (*Ep.* III, line 2012, re-read). Formation is *seeing* — which is why the north's literature is made of *virtutes* (G6): the disciple is formed by what the master's power does before his eyes (Gallus: "I myself am a witness," *Dial.* II.4, Doc_04 G5), and the community's own failure-mode is the disciple who stops believing what he sees — Brictio's "ridiculous fancies about visions" (*Dial.* III.15, Doc_03 6.7). The relationship runs downward into the countryside (§5) and upward into the episcopate: "we have seen numbers of these afterwards made bishops" (*Vita* ch. X).

**Marseilles: formation by an ordered sequence, received from Egypt and written for a bishop's new house.** The sequence Cassian prescribes (*Inst.* IV.3–9, all re-read this pass) is: ten days or more "lying outside the doors … prostrate at the feet of all the brethren that pass by, and of set purpose repelled and scorned by all of them" — "an evidence of his perseverance and desire, as well as of humility and patience" (IV.3); a search for money (IV.3–4); stripping "in the council of the brethren … and clad by the Abbot's hands in the dress of the monastery" (IV.5); his old clothes kept by the steward against failure (IV.6); a year in the guest-house "given into the charge of an Elder, who lodges apart not far from the entrance of the monastery, and is entrusted with the care of strangers and guests" (IV.7); then a dean "placed over ten of the juniors … in accordance with the arrangement which we read of in Exodus as made by Moses" (IV.7); whose "chief part of … instruction" is "to teach him first to conquer his own wishes; and … he will of set purpose contrive to give him such orders as he knows to be contrary to his liking" (IV.8); and disclosure — "the alphabet, as it were, and first syllables in the direction of perfection": "not to conceal by a false shame any itching thoughts in their hearts, but, as soon as ever such arise, to lay them bare to the senior … For they lay it down as an universal and clear proof that a thought is from the devil if we are ashamed to disclose it to the senior" (IV.9). **The from-within fact that governs everything else in this lens:** this is Egypt, written *for* Gaul, and Cassian says so — "we cannot recollect any one who joined our monasteries keeping it up unbroken even for a year" (IV.2, Doc_04 G2). What a Gallic junior in the 420s actually underwent is not documented; what he was *told Egypt did*, in a book meant to be received "into their cells" (*Conf.* Pref. III, Doc_04 G7), is documented in detail. The southern formative relationship is therefore two relationships at once: to a present elder whose Gallic practice we cannot see, and to an absent Egyptian father whose words are on the page — G5 already shifting from *heard* to *read* (Doc_04 G4×G5), a generation before Vincent makes the shift explicit.

**Lérins: formation by a founder, a succession of teachers, and a household.** The bounded evidence: Honoratus's "anxious guardianship over the salvation of those who had bound themselves over to their teaching" (*"sollicita custodia erga eorum salutem qui se doctrinae eorum mancipaverant,"* Hilary, file line 461, my rendering, Inferential/Thin) — the disciple as one who has *mancipated* himself; the tutelage chain Honoratus → Hilary → Salvian and Vincent for Eucherius's sons (Doc_02 §13A; Sanford p. 12, editorial paraphrase of Eucherius's own letter) — a documented, named, multi-generation *sequence* of teachers, the only one in the build, and its pupils were a bishop's children, later bishops themselves (G1 in the second generation); and Eucherius's Lérins as the harbour that "receives with most loving arms those cast out by the shipwrecks of the stormy world" (Doc_04 G2, row 26, Inferential/Thin wording). Whether Lérins kept Cassian's entrance sequence is unknown; the *Conferences* XI–XVII were dedicated to its abbot (row 9), which is the only documented traffic in formation-content between the two southern houses.

### 2.3 Communal expectations

What a brother owed the house, in the world's words. **Common property** — "No one there had anything which was called his own; all things were possessed in common. It was not allowed either to buy or to sell anything, as is the custom among most monks" (*Vita* ch. X) — the phrase "as is the custom among most monks" is Sulpitius's own admission that Marmoutier was stricter than the Gallic norm; and in the south, "everything that they possess is consecrated to the Lord … if anything whatever has once been brought into the monastery they hold that it ought to be treated with the utmost reverence as an holy thing" (*Inst.* IV.20). **Silence** — at the synaxis "you would not think a single person was present except the one who stands up and chants the Psalm" (II.10, `iv.iii.ii.x`, new); at meals "with their hoods drawn down over their eyelids … no one notices what another is eating" (IV.17, new); no gossip after the office (II.15). **Enclosure of correspondence** — a fault "if he tries to receive a letter from any one or to write back without his Abbot's leave," or "sees any of his relations or friends in the world and talks with them without his senior" (IV.16). **Hospitality** as an overriding duty — the guest-Elder (IV.7), the fast broken for a guest (V.24), the Sunday supper "only for strangers who have come to see the brethren, and those whom bodily weakness or their own inclination invites" (III.12, `iv.iii.iii.xii`, new). **Mutual correction under the elder**: the senior "clapping his hands where he sits in his stall" to stop a too-long psalm (II.11); "no one has any liberty of praying with him" who is suspended (II.16). **Not growing cold** — the expectation named most often: the novice who "grow[s] cold in regard to the rule of their institution" is stripped and sent away (IV.6); the monastery must not become "lukewarm by being dispersed among the many" (II.5, `iv.iii.ii.v`, new; Doc_03 3.11). At Tours the communal expectation is stated once and negatively: "Any dress approaching to softness was there deemed criminal" (*Vita* ch. X) — and once positively in Martin's own person, who as bishop "allowed himself to be wronged by the lowest clerics with impunity" (*Vita* ch. XXVI, read this pass).

### 2.4 Formation Logic — the world's own account of why this works

The Framework asks for the world's reasoning, not the analyst's. Five pieces of it are stated in the texts.

**(i) Aids are not the goal (G7).** "Fastings, vigils, meditation on the Scriptures, self-denial, and the abnegation of all possessions are not perfection, but aids to perfection" (*Conf.* I.7, Doc_04 G7); "It is like the case when one endeavours to strike some mighty prize of virtue on high, pointed out by some very small mark; with the keenest eyesight he points the aim of his dart" (*Inst.* V.15, `iv.iii.v.xv`, new — the same archer as *Conf.* I.5). This is the south's whole logic: every practice in §2.2 is ranked by whether it produces purity of heart. Cassian frames it as Abbot Moses's, received (Doc_03 4.1, [SC] "received") — and the inhabited voice must say so.

**(ii) The order of combats (G8).** "First then we must trample under foot gluttonous desires"; "it is impossible for a full belly to make trial of the combat of the inner man: nor is he worthy to be tried in harder battles, who can be overcome in a slight skirmish" (*Inst.* V.13–14, new); the Olympic scrutiny in which a youth "first … give[s] evidence of his abilities" before "the most illustrious conflict of the games" (V.12, new). The faults are fought in sequence because a city "is laid waste by the giving up of one postern however small" (V.11, new). Formation is a campaign with an order of battle.

**(iii) Take your model from one or two (G5).** "The examples of the perfect life … should be sought from a very few or indeed from one or two only" (*Inst.* IV.40, Doc_04 G5) — and yet, at the anchorite's summit, "the monk who desires to gather spiritual honey, ought like a most careful bee, to suck out virtue from those who specially possess it" (V.4, `iv.iii.v.iv`, new; Antony's saying, received). The two rules are not in tension for the world: the junior imitates one; the perfect gathers from many. The north's logic is the first rule only, and its one exemplar is a bishop.

**(iv) Received, not invented (G4).** The angel's twelve psalms were "no appointment of man's invention, but … brought down from heaven to the fathers by the ministry of an angel" (*Inst.* II.4, `iv.iii.ii.iv`, new); monasteries stand "not … at the fancy of every man who renounces the world, but through a succession of fathers and their traditions" (II.3, new); "we often have the audacity to preside over a monastery without even having learnt the system of the Elders, and appoint ourselves Abbots before we have, as we ought, professed ourselves disciples, and are readier to require the observance of our own inventions than to preserve the well-tried teaching of our predecessors" (II.3). Formation works because it is not one's own — the same logic Vincent applies to doctrine ("not an author but a keeper," *Comm.* 22 [53]) and Martin to a cult (*Vita* ch. XI).

**(v) Effort is required and cannot suffice (G3) — and this sits at the top of the formation manual, not only in Conference XIII.** This is the finding that answers Doc_04 §10 item 2's revisit criterion ("how pervasively the argument shaped southern formation practice"), and it is stated in full here and dispositioned at §10C. *Institutes* XII, the last book, on pride, turns at ch. IX into a treatise on grace inside the treatise on formation (chs. IX–XIX, `iv.iii.xii.ix`–`xix`, **all new this pass**): "we can escape the snare of this most evil spirit, if in the case of every virtue in which we feel that we make progress, we say these words of the Apostle: 'Not I, but the grace of God with me'" (XII.9); "no tortures of this body, and no contrition of heart, can be sufficient for the acquisition of that true chastity of the inner man … merely by human efforts, i.e., without the aid of God" (XII.11); "no one can be altogether cleansed from carnal sins, unless he has realized that all his labours and efforts are insufficient" — "taught, not by the system handed down to him, but by his feelings and virtues and his own experience" (XII.13); "Nor do I say this to cast a slight on human efforts … But clearly and most earnestly do I lay down, not giving my own opinion, but that of the elders, that perfection cannot possibly be gained without these, but that by these only without the grace of God nobody can ever attain it. For when we say that human efforts cannot of themselves secure it without the aid of God, we thus insist that God's mercy and grace are bestowed only upon those who labour and exert themselves" (XII.14 — Gibson's headnote, editorial: "The language in this chapter is perilously near semi-Pelagianism"); "we cannot even make the effort to obtain perfection without the mercy and inspiration of God" (XII.16, title and text); "this very free will of ours, which is more readily inclined to sin, is turned by Him to a better purpose" (XII.18); and the closing frame — "This then is that humility towards God, this is that genuine faith of the ancient fathers which still remains intact among their successors" (XII.19). Two things follow for the ecology. First, the grace-and-effort teaching is not a doctrinal appendix but the *remedy for the eighth and last fault*: the formation program ends by teaching the monk to attribute his own formation to God — G3 is the formation logic's final move, and Cassian ties it to G4 in the same breath ("the genuine faith of the ancient fathers"). Second, *Conf.* XIII itself arises as a formation problem: "When after a short sleep we returned for morning service … Abbot Germanus was troubled by great scruples because in the previous discussion [the excised chastity conference] … the blessed old man had by the addition of a single sentence broken down the claims of man's exertions" (XIII.1, `iv.v.iv.i`, read this pass) — the question is whether chastity is the husbandman's crop or God's rain (XIII.2–3). The argument this world became known for is, from within, an argument about *whether the practices of §2.2 accomplish anything* — and the answer given is the one the formation program itself already taught at its summit.

### Inhabited passage (Layer 2) — Marmoutier, a younger brother

> I am one of the young ones, so the copying is mine; the elders keep to prayer. We came in by the one narrow way between the rock and the river, and I have not been past it since, except to the place of prayer. My cell is a hollow in the mountain; his is wood. We eat once, together, when the hour of fasting is done, and no one takes wine unless he is sick. If a man's garment is soft here, that is his crime. There are men among us who were born to rank, and they have forced themselves down to this. The master was a soldier before he was anything, and he is the bishop across the river now, and the bishops who did not want him said his hair was disgusting and his clothes were mean — and he kept both. He wears the sackcloth as a weapon. What we know, we know because we have seen it done in front of us: the power present, the dead raised, the demon named. He says the man who imitates Paulinus imitates the right man. Some of us will be taken from here and made bishops; what church would not want its priests from among Martin's? I do not know which of us. Sackcloth, ashes, fasting, prayer without a gap — as the smith beats his anvil between strokes, so he prays between everything.

### Inhabited passage (Layer 2) — Marseilles, a junior with the book in his cell

> The fathers of Egypt lay ten days at the door and were spat on, and in the end they were stripped before the brethren and clothed by the abbot's hands. We in this country do not keep even the year in the guest-house; Cassian says he cannot remember one of us who did. So I have the book instead, and I talk with the fathers in it by questions and answers in my cell, as he told us to. First the girdle; then the psalms — twelve at evening and twelve at night, because an angel sang eleven and the twelfth with Alleluia and vanished, and that was no man's invention. We stand and sing "Glory be to the Father" together after every psalm; he says the East never heard it. He says we hurry to fall on our faces to get the service over, and that some in this province go back to bed after the morning psalms, and that the cold here forbids the little hoods and the sheepskin. It is true; we are the frozen province, and one of the holy brothers wanted to go to Egypt to see the fathers in the flesh. When a thought itches I am to tell it to the senior at once; the ones I am ashamed to tell are from the devil. And when I have fasted and watched and kept the cell and feel I have got somewhere, I am to say, not I, but the grace of God with me — because that, he says, is the faith of the ancient fathers, and God gives to the man who labours, and yet the labouring itself is not mine.

### 2.5 Forces integration — how outside pressure entered communal formation, and how the community received it

*Connects to Doc_01 §7; Doc_02 §11, §13A; Doc_04 forces notations at G2, G3, G7, G9, G1.*

**(a) Egypt as an external standard pressing on Gallic habit (G2, receptive mode) — felt from within as rebuke.** The clearest new evidence this pass is Cassian's running critique of *Gallic* worship-practice from inside the office: "as some of us do in this country, who, before the Psalm is fairly ended, make haste to prostrate themselves for prayer, in their hurry to finish the service" (*Inst.* II.7, `iv.iii.ii.vii`); "That practice too which we have observed in this country — viz., that while one sings to the end of the Psalm, all standing up sing together with a loud voice, 'Glory be to the Father …' — we have never heard anywhere throughout the East" (II.8, `iv.iii.ii.viii`); "some in this province, not knowing the reason why this office was appointed and introduced, go back again to bed after their Mattin prayers are finished" (III.5, `iv.iii.iii.v`); "we cannot recollect any one who joined our monasteries keeping it up unbroken even for a year" (IV.2). These are ancient, first-person statements about Gallic monastic practice c. 420 — the first such direct evidence this build has found outside Sulpitius — and every one of them is framed as a falling-short of Egypt. The receptive mode is not a neutral inheritance; it is a standing correction of local custom by a foreign one, received *as authority* (G4: "the most ancient institutions of the earliest fathers," II.2). Doc_04's G2 forces notation ("a rule to receive") gains its texture here: the rule arrived as a critique.

**(b) The grace controversy as a force on the formation program (G3, Doc_02 §11).** Because the grace teaching sits at the summit of the *Institutes* (§2.4 v) and arises from a formation question (*Conf.* XIII.1), the pressure Prosper and Celestine brought (Gibson's prolegomena, editorial; Doc_02 §1.2, §11) bore on the *formation manual itself*, not on a separable doctrine. Cassian's own defence is G4's: "not giving my own opinion, but that of the elders" (XII.14); "the genuine faith of the ancient fathers" (XII.19). Under that force, per Doc_04 G3's notation, the position intensified and its carriers relocated to a bishop at synodal commission; and in transmission (Forces Fw §5.5), a Carthusian later cut from *Conf.* XIII what "savours of Semi-Pelagianism" (Doc_02 §1.2, editorial). *Institutes* XII.9–19 survives uncut in this edition with only Gibson's warning headnote — a smaller selection effect than *Conf.* XIII suffered, recorded here.

**(c) Episcopal recruitment as a force on the community (G1 × G9).** The community was a source the churches drew on: "what city or church would there be that would not desire to have its priests from among those in the monastery of Martin?" (*Vita* ch. X); Honoratus was "bound to the long-avoided office" and "the dignity came to him" (Hilary, Doc_04 G1); Archebius "complained that he had been expelled from the monastic system" (*Conf.* XI.2, Doc_04 G1); the *Conferences*' own dedicatee turns from "holy brother" to "Bishop" between two prefaces (Doc_04 G1). The internal counter-force is written into the formation program as a fault: vainglory "creates a wish to take holy orders" (*Inst.* XI.14) and "when a man has grown cold through pride he wants to be put to rule other people" (XII.30, title, `iv.iii.xii.xxx`, seen this pass in the div index; text not read). The community received the drain by making the desire for office a sin and the office itself a destiny — G9's opposed valences inside one house.

**(d) Political violence at Trier (G9, northern).** After the Priscillianist executions Martin "maintained that it was quite sufficient punishment that, having been declared heretics by a sentence of the bishops, they should have been expelled from the churches" (*SH* II.50, Doc_04 G9) and "never again did he attend a synod, and kept carefully aloof from all assemblies of bishops" (*Dial.* III.13); after a coerced communion with the Ithacians "he felt a diminution of his power" (III.13, Doc_04 G6). The northern community learned, from its master's body, to keep away from bishops — the force reshaped G1's own product into a monk-bishop who shunned his colleagues.

**(e) Cross-Cell Connection (Forces Fw §5.4).** The initiating force (Egypt received as the standard, under a bishop's request) produced the ongoing internal response (defence of the received program as antiquity when challenged from Africa and Rome), which in turn shaped the ending (the defenders become bishops at synodal commission; the network becomes the episcopate — Doc_01 §2.4, Doc_04 G9). The barbarian and fiscal forces do not enter this chain in the formation literature; they enter through Salvian and attach to G10 only.

## 3. Worship Ecology — Worship Integration Analysis

Doc_01 §5 asked this document to treat worship as "a load-bearing, if hedged, inheritance," and Doc_04 §10 item 8 handed it the unread *Institutes* II–III. Both books were read in full this pass (`iv.iii.ii.i`–`xviii`, `iv.iii.iii.i`–`xii`). The Framework's seven questions are answered in order; the eighth heading records what the two nodes do *not* share. Throughout, "the office" means Cassian's "canonical system of the … prayers and Psalms" (Doc_03 4.11; his own phrase, *Inst.* II.1), and "communion" carries Doc_03 7.11's double sense.

**How worship functions.** In the south the office is the skeleton of the day and the week — Vespers and Nocturns of twelve psalms each with two lessons, "one from the Old and the other from the New Testament" (*Inst.* II.4, II.6); Tierce, Sext, None of three psalms each "so that constant prayers may be offered to God at the appointed times, and yet, the spiritual duties being completed with due moderation, the necessary offices of work may not be in any way interfered with" (III.3, `iv.iii.iii.iii`); the new morning office of three psalms (III.4); and "the Lord's communion" on Sunday in "a more solemn and a longer service of Psalms and prayers and lessons" that absorbs Tierce and Sext (III.11, `iv.iii.iii.xi`), preceded in Egypt by the Saturday and Sunday meeting "at the third hour for the purpose of Holy Communion" (III.2, `iv.iii.iii.ii`). Cassian's own ranking is stated with the office: the Egyptians' *continuous* prayer-with-work is "more than what is rendered at intervals of time; and more acceptable as a free gift than the duties which are performed by the compulsion of a rule" (III.2) — the fixed hours exist for "us, who, unless we are compelled, as it were, by some rule to discharge these pious offices at least at stated times, either through sloth or through forgetfulness, or being absorbed in business, spend the whole day without engaging in prayer" (III.3). The office is a scaffold for the weak, and the goal it scaffolds is G7's "continual and unbroken perseverance in prayer" (*Conf.* IX.2, Doc_03 4.11). **In the north** the same goal is reached without the scaffold, or with a scaffold the sources never describe: Martin at "the place of prayer" (*Vita* ch. X), Martin whose every hour is prayer as the smith beats his anvil (ch. XXVI), Martin "offering the sacrifice" at "a very famous day in the midst of a great multitude of people" while a globe of fire rises from his head (*Dial.* II.2, `ii.iv.ii.ii`, read this pass), Martin whose hand at the altar Arborius "testifies that he saw … clothed, as it seemed, with the noblest gems" (*Dial.* III.10, `ii.iv.iii.x`, read this pass). What Marmoutier sang, at what hours, is not documented. **Communion as sanction** functions at both nodes (Doc_03 7.11): in the south "no one has any liberty of praying with him" who is suspended, because he "is, as the Apostle says, 'delivered unto Satan'" (*Inst.* II.16, `iv.iii.ii.xvi`); in the north Martin's one coerced communion with the Ithacian bishops diminishes his power (*Dial.* III.13, Doc_04 G6/G9).

**How worship forms participants.** Silence and brevity: at the synaxis "no spitting, no clearing of the throat, or noise of coughing, no sleepy yawning," and prayers "short and offered up very frequently … that when the devil is lying in wait for us, we may by their terse brevity avoid the darts with which he endeavours to wound us" (II.10); understanding over quantity: "ten verses … sung with understanding and thought" are better "than for a whole Psalm to be poured forth with a bewildered mind" (II.11, `iv.iii.ii.xi`); posture as formation: standing with "outspread hands," a brief prostration "as if but adoring the Divine Mercy," and up again, because "when you lie prostrate for any length of time upon the ground you are more open to an attack … not only of wandering thoughts but also slumber" (II.7); the vigil continued privately "as their special private sacrifice" (II.12); the office as the guard of chastity — the night's psalms yield a "purity" the dream must not spoil (II.13, III.5). The person the office forms is the one G7 describes: attentive, brief, unsleeping, unashamed to be seen kneeling briefly and ashamed to be heard groaning. At Tours the liturgy forms by *what is seen at it* — the fire, the gems (G6) — and by the master's own posture in it.

**How worship shapes belonging.** Membership is the right to pray with the brethren; its suspension is the house's sharpest sanction short of expulsion (II.16; IV.16; III.7 — the latecomer "does penance lying on the ground, and obtains absolution … knowing that he can in no other way expiate the fault"). The Sunday supper distinguishes guests and the weak from the rest (III.12). At Martin's funeral belonging is displayed in the procession's order — monks in cloaks, old men, "young soldiers who had just taken the oath," "the choir of virgins" (*Ep.* III) — and in the exclusion that made the cortege: "Let your worldly great men lead before their chariots captives … Those accompanied the body of Martin who, under his guidance, had overcome the world" (*Ep.* III, file lines 2058–2061, read this pass).

**How worship shapes memory.** The hours are memorials: Tierce because "at the third hour the Holy Spirit … descended," Sext because "at the sixth hour the spotless Sacrifice, our Lord and Saviour, was offered up," None because "at the ninth hour, penetrating to hades, He there by the brightness of His splendour extinguished the indescribable darkness of hell" and Cornelius prayed; Vespers because of "that true evening sacrifice which was given by the Lord our Saviour in the evening to the Apostles at the Supper"; Mattins because "I prevented the dawning of the day and cried" (III.3, all `iv.iii.iii.iii`). Each day re-walks the saving events in order. The office's own origin is a memory too — the angel who sang twelve and vanished (II.5–6) — and its rationale is the memory of the primitive church, "the multitude of believers had one heart and one soul … they had all things common" (II.5, quoting Acts iv), received from "the Evangelist Mark." In the north, memory of Martin himself begins liturgically within his own generation: "Martin is praised with the divine psalms, Martin is honored in heavenly hymns" (*Ep.* III, file line 2062, read this pass) — the saint enters the psalmody that formed him (§6B).

**How worship shapes theology.** Three ways, all traceable. (i) The hours carry a whole soteriology in miniature (III.3, above — descent into hell included). (ii) The Gallic Gloria — sung "with a loud voice" by all after every psalm, a practice Cassian says the East never heard (II.8; Gibson's note that it "seems to have originated in Gaul" is editorial) — is a Trinitarian confession repeated dozens of times a day in this province specifically; and the three-psalm offices are kept "to signify the confession of the Trinity" (III.4, *"trinae confessionis exemplo"* — Gibson's fn. supplies the Latin, Doc_03 3.2). (iii) The grace doctrine is prayed before it is argued: *Inst.* XII.17 is a catena of Psalm verses to be said "as we feel our own weakness, and at the same time His help" — "It is God who girded me with strength"; "Take hold of arms and shield: and rise up to help me" (`iv.iii.xii.xvii`) — the psalter supplying the vocabulary of G3 in G8's idiom. And *Conf.* XIII opens "when after a short sleep we returned for morning service" (XIII.1): the question of grace is raised at the hour of prayer.

**How worship shapes authority.** Presiding is earned by obedience: "no one is allowed to preside over the assembly of the brethren, or even over himself, before he has … learnt the fact that he is not his own maker" (II.3, `iv.iii.ii.iii`). The one who summons must read "the course of the stars" rather than "the accident of his own sleep" (II.17). The senior stops a psalm by clapping (II.11); the Abbot's command raises the penitent (IV.16); "the priest concluding the prayer" is the only voice heard (II.10). Above all, the office's authority is that it is *not anyone's* — "no appointment of man's invention" (II.4) — so that G4 is most literal here: the legitimate leader is the one who transmits the angel's number. In the north, the psalter judges the bishops: at Martin's election, when the reader "failed to appear," a bystander "laying hold of the Psalter, seized upon the first verse which presented itself to him," which named Defensor (*Vita* ch. IX, `ii.ii.x`, file lines 770–787, read this pass) — worship as the people's instrument against episcopal opposition, G9 enacted at the lectern.

**How worship shapes time.** The day: seven times (III.4, "Seven times a day do I praise Thee"), with Nocturns, Mattins, Tierce, Sext, None, Vespers named and Cassian's second "Mattins" instituted "in our own day, and also in our own monastery, where our Lord Jesus Christ was born of a Virgin" (III.4; the name Bethlehem is Gibson's, editorial) — an office Cassian says is *new*, argued for on the same "received" ground (the elders decided it "after long discussion"). The week: five fast days and two of relief (III.9); the Saturday vigil "till the fourth cock-crowing" in winter (III.8); no kneeling "from the evening of Saturday which precedes the Sunday, up to the following evening" (II.18); Sunday's single, longer office and supper (III.11–12); the hebdomadary's week ending at Sunday supper with foot-washing (IV.19). The year: "from Easter to Whitsuntide" no kneeling and no fasting (II.18), lessons from the New Testament only in those fifty days (II.6) — and nothing else, because *Conf.* XXI (the Quinquagesima relaxation) and the Lenten material remain unread (Doc_03 §10 item 3). **Finding:** in what was read, worship shapes the day and the week far more visibly than the year; the liturgical year is a coverage limit, not an ecological absence.

**What the nodes do not share — a finding.** The south has a documented office; the north has "the place of prayer," unceasing prayer, a bishop at the altar, and a funeral. Doc_01 §5's hedged inheritance — Gibson's "the rule that prayers should be intermingled with Psalms which was perhaps introduced into the West by Cassian, was widely adopted both in Gaul and in Spain" (II.11 fn., editorial) — stays hedged; what this pass adds is *ancient* evidence, in Cassian's own voice, of Gallic monastic worship c. 420: the Gloria (II.8), the hurried prostration (II.7), the Mattins "generally celebrated … in the monasteries of Gaul" (III.4), the return to bed (III.5), the unkept year (IV.2). These are the first direct statements about Gallic monastic practice this build has found outside Sulpitius, and they describe a province that already had its own habits before Cassian's book told it what Egypt did. Whether Marmoutier's "place of prayer" kept anything like them is not documented, and this document does not infer it.

**Name-the-layer note.** Gibson's footnote at *Conf.* XVIII.15 — "In Gaul it was apparently received daily: Institutes VI. viii." (`iv.vi.ii.xv`, file lines 37389–37390) — cites, for daily communion in Gaul, a chapter of the book his own edition omits (§1.2). The claim is therefore an editor's, resting on a Latin text this build has not read; it is carried as editorial, Inferential/Thin, and as a Doc_06/Latin-pass item (§10D).

**Input to the deferred candidate (Doc_04 §2.2, "Unceasing prayer / the canonical office").** Doc_04 stated what would change its outcome: "whether those books show the office organizing *other* practices (dependency) or merely regulating time within a formation program G7 already covers." They show dependency: the office organizes sleep (II.13, III.4–5, III.8), labour (II.14–15, III.2), penance and belonging (II.16, III.7, IV.16), fasting and meals (III.9–12), the weekly service (IV.19), authority (II.3, II.17), and the day's memory (III.3). But it is single-voice (Cassian), Egyptian in content by Cassian's own framing, and absent as a program from Tours — the same profile that held G7 at Supporting and node-bound. This document's input, for Doc_04's owner and not applied here: the office should be recorded as the *temporal skeleton of G7* with the dependency finding attached, not advanced as an independent gravity; unceasing prayer, cross-node and Supporting-grade as a motif, organizes as G7's stated aim (*Conf.* IX.2) rather than on its own.

### Inhabited passage (Layer 2) — the night office, in the words the south used for it

> He who has the summoning has watched the stars and not his own sleep, and calls us at the hour. One stands in the midst; the rest of us sit low, because we have fasted and worked all day and could not stand through twelve. He sings, and no one coughs, no one sighs aloud — if a groan escapes a man it is because his heart could not hold it, and that is forgiven; a yawn is not. Between the verses we rise and pray standing, hands out, and go down to the ground only for a breath, and up when he who collects the prayer rises, not before and not after. Eleven psalms and the twelfth with Alleluia, as the angel sang them before he vanished. Then Glory be to the Father — all of us, loud; they do not do that in the East, but we do. Then a lesson from the old books and one from the new. Then to the cell, and no lingering at the door, and no sleep, because what the psalms have cleaned the dream would dirty. He who came late stands outside till we are dismissed and lies on the ground for it. Whoever is suspended prays with no one; if I pray with him I go where he has been sent.

*Anchors:* *Inst.* II.17 (the summoner and the stars); II.12 (low stalls, fasting and work); II.10 (silence; the escaped groan; the yawn as fault); II.7 (standing prayer, brief prostration, rising with the one who "collects" the prayer); II.5–6 (eleven psalms, the twelfth with Alleluia, the angel); II.8 (the Gloria "in this country," "never heard … throughout the East"); II.4, II.6 (two lessons); II.15 (no lingering; back to the cell); II.13 (no sleep after the night office; the dream); III.7 (the latecomer outside); II.16 (the suspended; "partaker of his damnation"). Cross-world check: the Egyptian content is what Cassian prescribes; the only Gallic-owned element is the Gloria, which Cassian himself assigns to "this country." Containment: no "Lauds," "Prime," "Compline," or later office-names (those are Gibson's, editorial); no Benedict.

## 4. Organizational Ecology — Authority Structures

Built, as the brief requires, around G9's opposed valences and G4's criterion of legitimacy, with G1 as the structure's characteristic output.

### 4.1 Inside the house

**South (Cassian's prescription for Castor's "new monastery").** A superior over the house, whom Cassian calls the Abbot in Egypt and "presiding … over a large monastery of the brethren" when the house is Gallic (Doc_03 1.7's lexical gap on the title, unchanged); a guest-Elder at the gate (IV.7); deans, "one … placed over ten of the juniors" (IV.7); a steward who keeps the renunciants' clothes (IV.6) and the house's vessels (IV.20); hebdomadaries who serve by the week (IV.19); a summoner (II.17); seniors in stalls who can stop a psalm (II.11). Authority runs down through obedience learned in ascent: "no one is chosen to be set over a congregation of brethren before that he who is to be placed in authority has learnt by obedience what he ought to enjoin on those who are to submit to him" (II.3). Sanctions in three grades — public penance until the Abbot bids rise, suspension from prayer, and, for the graver faults including "familiarity with women," "stripes; or … expulsion" (IV.16). The whole apparatus is prescriptive; how much of it any Gallic house kept, Cassian himself doubts (IV.2).

**North.** One man. "The saintly master" (*Vita* ch. X) who consults "those who were his elders, whether among the presbyters or clerics" on tradition (ch. XI) but is not subject to them; Clarus, "a disciple of Martin's," who gathers his own brethren (Doc_03 3.1); no dean, no steward, no grade of penance — Brictio is "brought back to repentance" by Martin's own patience (*Dial.* III.15, Doc_03 8.7). The house's authority *is* the founder's *virtus* (G6), which is why the north's account of office is an account of what office does to power (§4.2).

**Lérins (bounded).** Honoratus as founder, "master of bishops, doctor of the churches" (Eucherius's phrase, quoted by Sanford — editorial paraphrase, p. 12); a named succession of teachers (§2.1); Vincent "presbyter in the Monastery on the Island of Lerins" (Gennadius ch. LXV, Doc_02 §1.3). Internal grades: not attested.

### 4.2 Above the house — the four authorities and their opposed valences (G9)

**The bishop (G1 × G9).** The office is both the ecology's destination and, in its own formation program, a temptation: vainglory "creates a wish to take holy orders, and a desire for the priesthood or diaconate" (*Inst.* XI.14, Doc_04 G7/G9); the devil "incites a man to desire the holy office of the clergy under the pretext of edifying many people" (*Conf.* I.20). Yet Cassian dedicates everything to bishops and calls two of them "Pope" (Doc_03 7.12), and Honoratus is caught by "the priestly fillet" (Doc_04 G1). In the north, the bishop is made *against* bishops: at Martin's election "some of the bishops, who had been summoned to appoint a chief priest, were impiously offering resistance" (*Vita* ch. IX, Doc_04 G1), Defensor is confounded by the psalter (§3), and as bishop Martin "allowed himself to be wronged by the lowest clerics with impunity; nor did he either remove them from the office on account of such conduct, or … repel them from a place in his affection" (*Vita* ch. XXVI, read this pass) — episcopal authority exercised, from within, as the refusal to use it. And the office costs the saint his power: "such an abundance of power was by no means granted him while he was a bishop, as he remembered to have possessed before he obtained that office" (*Dial.* II.4, Doc_04 G6).

**Councils and synods.** Vincent: "prefer the decrees, if such there be, of an ancient General Council to the rashness and ignorance of a few" (*Comm.* ch. 3 [8]); Ephesus as the model in which the bishops "innovated nothing, presumed nothing" (Doc_03 7.8). Sulpitius: synods are where the Priscillianists were handed to the sword, and "never again did he attend a synod" (*Dial.* III.13). Doc_03's [PV] tag at 7.8 is the organizational fact: the same institution guarantees truth in the south and is fled in the north.

**The Apostolic See.** Vincent's "twofold authority of the Apostolic See" (*Comm.* ch. 32 [84], Doc_04 G9); Cassian's "most blessed Pope Castor" — the title belonging to Gallic bishops (Gibson's fn., editorial; Doc_03 7.12); and the Massilians who "clung to their views in spite of the authority of the Pope" (Gibson's prolegomena, editorial, Doc_02 §1.2). Rome is invoked as the guardian of antiquity by the one Lérins voice and, on the editor's account, resisted by the Marseilles party in the same decade — G9's two valences within one node.

**The emperor and the state.** Martin before Maximus, where "the priestly dignity had, with degenerate submissiveness, taken a second place to the royal retinue," while "in Martin alone, apostolic authority continued to assert itself" (*Vita* ch. XX, Doc_04 G9); "a foul and unheard-of indignity, that a secular ruler should be judge in an ecclesiastical cause" (*SH* II.50). In the south, the state appears only in Salvian, as the tax-collector and the magistrate who "devoured" those under him (V.6, §1.4); Cassian's and Vincent's texts, as read, do not address the emperor at all — a real absence, since Cassian's Rome-connection runs through an archdeacon (Doc_02 §1.2), not a court.

### 4.3 The criterion of legitimacy (G4)

Authority is legitimate insofar as it transmits and illegitimate insofar as it invents. The criterion is stated in the same three-part form for monastic custom and for doctrine: "not to those customs and rules which the will of a few have introduced, but to those which a long standing antiquity and numbers of the holy fathers have passed on by an unanimous decision" (*Inst.* I.2, Doc_04 G4); "universality, antiquity, consent" (*Comm.* ch. 2 [6]); and at the office, "no appointment of man's invention" (II.4). Its negative is the self-appointed superior — "we … appoint ourselves Abbots before we have, as we ought, professed ourselves disciples" (II.3) — and, for Vincent, the singular teacher: "whatsoever a teacher holds, other than all, or contrary to all, be he holy and learned, be he a bishop, be he a Confessor, be he a martyr, let that be regarded as a private fancy of his own" (*Comm.* ch. 28 [72], `iii.xxix`, re-verified at file lines 12564–12568). Martin applies it to a cult (*Vita* ch. XI). The organizational consequence is that *no office confers authority by itself*: not the abbacy (II.3), not the episcopate (Comm. 28; Vita XXVII's calumniating bishops), not even a Pope's letter, on the editor's account of the Massilians. What confers it is being a keeper of what was handed down — which is why this ecology can produce bishops and distrust them in the same breath (Doc_04 §5).

### 4.4 From within

Authority in this world is felt as something one flees and is overtaken by. Honoratus "who had refused to go to the dignity — the dignity came to him"; Archebius "carried off from the assembly of anchorites"; Martin, who "could not easily be drawn forth from his monastery," lured out by "a certain Ruricius, one of the citizens, pretending that his wife was ill," and "under a kind of guard escorted to the city" by "multitudes of the citizens … previously … posted by the road" (*Vita* ch. IX, `ii.ii.x`, file lines 742–747, read this pass), then made bishop over the objections of bishops. The monk who *wants* to rule has "grown cold through pride" (*Inst.* XII.30, title). The legitimate leader is the one who did not seek it and, having it, keeps "a monk's humility as entire in the priesthood as, when a monk, he had fully possessed the merits of the priesthood" (Hilary on Honoratus, Doc_04 G1). This is not a paradox to the world; it is the shape of G1.

*Forces note (brief; full treatment at §2.5(c), §7.3).* The consolidation Doc_01 §2.4 records from Mathisen (row 20, thesis-level only, not read) and the register shift to synodal commission (Faustus, c. 473–475) are the organizational ecology's exit: the men formed to flee office fill the sees of southern Gaul, and the argument formed in cells is carried by a bishop for two synods. G9's fracture "is the world's boundary" (Doc_04).

## 5. Ministry Ecology — what the formed person did for others

The one named lens (CF line 665) without a line-658 counterpart. Defined here as the outward work of the formed person, which in this world is the work of the monk-bishop (G1) and of the writer who stands in for an absent elder (G5). The lens is *node-divided more sharply than any other.*

**North: power exercised for the countryside (G6, G1).** The bishop's ministry is exorcism and healing — "he touched no one with his hands, and reproached no one in words, as a multitude of expressions is generally rolled forth by the clerics," but prayed in sackcloth (Doc_03 6.5) — and it is mission: "wherever he destroyed heathen temples, there he used immediately to build either churches or monasteries" (*Vita* ch. XIII, Doc_03 6.8); a whole heathen crowd made catechumens "by placing his hand upon the whole of them" (ch. XVII, Doc_03 8.3); "before the times of Martin, very few, nay, almost none, in those regions had received the name of Christ" (Doc_03 6.8). It is intercession before power — Maximus, the Ithacians, the Priscillianists' lives (§4.2). It is pastoral visitation "as a bishop … in the discharge of duty" (*Dial.* II.12), consecration of virgins (*Vita* ch. XIX), and a letter that cures a fever when placed "in the bosom of the girl" (ch. XIX) — the bishop's ministry reaching where his body does not, by *virtus* in an object (Doc_03 6.3). The community's ministry is the master's; the disciples' own ministry is to become bishops elsewhere (*Vita* ch. X). **Article 20 note:** the rustics are the ministry's *object* throughout — converted, catechized, exorcised — and never its subject; no rustic speaks.

**South: teaching by writing, hospitality, and the refusal of wonders (G5, G7, G2).** Cassian's ministry is a book written so that "those who perhaps may meet only with this book, and be unable to procure the other, may find that they are supplied with some sort of information" (*Inst.* II.9, `iv.iii.ii.ix`, new) — a text designed to work in the teacher's absence, for a bishop's "new monastery in its untrained infancy in Christ" (II.2). Its interior counterpart is the refusal to minister by miracle: "our predecessors never reckoned those as good monks or free from the fault of vainglory, who professed themselves exorcists among men" (*Conf.* XV.7, Doc_04 G6) — Egyptian counter-teaching, not a reply to Tours, but it defines what a Marseilles monk's ministry is *not*. Hospitality is a ministry with its own officer and its own suspension of the rule (IV.7; V.24: "receiving Christ in you I ought to refresh Him"). Lérins's ministry is the one the case document named: it formed bishops — and, per the tutelage chain (§2.1), formed a bishop's sons into bishops. Vincent's ministry is to memory (§6B); Salvian's is the preacher's, "to the Roman" — the one Native voice whose whole work is addressed outward to the lapsed Christian rather than inward to the monk (Doc_03 §10 item 2's genre finding).

**The shared form: ministry by example, and the drain it causes.** In both nodes the formed person's first ministry is to be imitable (G5): Castor's "life alone is amply sufficient for an example" (*Inst.* Pref.); Martin's disciples are "plants" from his example (*Ep.* III). And in both nodes the ministry empties the house upward: Martin's monks become bishops; Honoratus's monks become bishops of Arles and Riez (Doc_04 G1). The ecology's most characteristic ministry is therefore one the formation program teaches its members to dread (§4.2) — which is Doc_04's G1×G9 competition seen as a ministry pattern.

## 6. Meaning Transmission, Memory Structures, and Interpretive Ecology

### 6A. Meaning Transmission (teaching, memory, storytelling, apprenticeship, worship, authority)

**The governing shift: from heard to read (G5 reshaped by G4, Doc_04 §4).** Cassian's own hierarchy is explicit: "one who has never heard anything from one who is at home in such things, may learn from ecclesiastical history" (*Inst.* II.5, `iv.iii.ii.v`) — hearing first, reading as the fallback; and his own books are the fallback institutionalized: Gallic monks are to receive the fathers "into their cells … talking with them after a fashion by daily questions and answers" (*Conf.* Pref. III, Doc_04 G2/G7). Sulpitius's *Dialogues* are, in Gennadius's word, a "Conference" (Doc_03 3.5) — the north's teaching cast in the south's genre, though the two never cite each other. Vincent completes the shift: "the things which I have truthfully received from the holy Fathers" are writings to be collated, and the *Commonitory* itself exists because memory fails (§6B). Transmission in this world therefore has three attested channels, in descending order of the world's own trust: the master seen (Tours; Egypt as Cassian saw it), the elder heard (Cassian's conferences; disclosure to the senior), the father read (the *Institutes* in the cell; Vincent's collation).

**Teaching.** The dean "teaches and governs" ten (IV.7); the senior's judgment replaces the junior's (IV.9; Doc_03 3.8); the elder's authority "will never be strong unless he fixes it in the heart of his hearer by the actual performance of his duty" (*Conf.* XI.4, Doc_03 3.2). Two lessons at every office (II.4, II.6). Reading at meals is Cappadocian, not Egyptian — introduced "not so much for the sake of the spiritual exercise as for the sake of putting a stop to unnecessary and idle conversation" (IV.17). At Tours teaching is Martin's speech, reported verbatim ("I am going to repeat his very words to you," *Dial.* II.11) — G5's eyewitness guarantee applied to teaching.

**Storytelling.** The north's meaning is carried in *virtutes* narrated (Doc_03 6.1; Doc_09's future inventory); the south's in *exempla* narrated by the elders — Paphnutius, falsely accused of theft, who "gave himself up entirely to make amends and humbly asked for a plan of repentance … lest if he tried to remove the stain of the theft by words, he might further be branded as a liar" (*Conf.* XVIII.15, `iv.vi.ii.xv`, read this pass); the three lentils (IV.20); the two old men Pæsius and John, asked what forty years apart had yielded — "Never," said the one, "has the sun seen me eating," "nor me angry," said the other (V.27, `iv.iii.v.xxvii`, new); the abbot who fed six guests and "am still hungry" (V.25, new). Cassian's stories teach a virtue; Sulpitius's display a power. Both nodes use the same *kind* of story of the devil's illusion (Doc_03 6.7).

**Apprenticeship.** The year in the guest-house, the dean's ten, contrary orders, disclosure (§2.1); and in the north, the copying — "the brethren of younger years" transcribe (*Vita* ch. X), which makes the north's only craft its only textual transmission mechanism, and makes the *Vita* itself the product of a Marmoutier apprenticeship in letters.

**Worship** as transmission: the psalter by heart during work (II.15); the hours as daily catechesis in salvation history (III.3); the Gloria as Trinitarian confession (II.8) — §3.

**Authority structures** as transmission: §4.3 — what may be transmitted is what was received, and the one who transmits his "own inventions" (II.3) is the ecology's own definition of false teaching.

### 6B. Memory Structures

Memory in this world is an *institution against forgetting*, and the world says so. Vincent's book is "a Commonitory (or Remembrancer) for myself, such as may aid my memory, or rather, provide against my forgetfulness" (*Comm.* ch. 1, Doc_03 7.13), and its rule is a memory-test — what "all, or the more part, have supported and confirmed manifestly, frequently, persistently, in one and the same sense, forming, as it were, a consentient council of doctors, all receiving, holding, handing on the same doctrine" (ch. 28 [72], file lines 12561–12565, read this pass). The deposit is "a matter brought to thee, not put forth by thee" (ch. 22 [53], Doc_04 G4). The south's founding memories are Egypt's and are received as such: the angel's twelve (II.5), the apostolic community of Acts iv under "the Evangelist Mark" (II.5), the "succession of fathers and their traditions" (II.3). The north's memory is Martin's, and it is built in his own generation and in the medium that formed him: "Martin is praised with the divine psalms" (*Ep.* III); threads from his garment and straw from his bed "wrought frequent miracles" (Doc_03 6.3) — memory in objects that carry power; a *Life* written "which shall serve in future as an example" (*Vita* ch. I). Lérins's memory is Hilary's *Life of Saint Honoratus, his predecessor* (Gennadius ch. LXX, Doc_04 G5), the disciple's *Life* of his master, and the funeral sermon that names Salvian as one of Honoratus's "own dear associates" (Doc_02 §13A). The *Sacred History* (row 6, Priscillianist chapters only read) frames the north's memory in a chronicle of the whole world. **A memory structure this world could not keep:** Vincent's second book was stolen (Gennadius ch. LXV, Doc_02 §1.3) — the *Commonitory* against forgetting lost half of itself, and Vincent wrote a summary against the loss. Memory as authority is G4; memory as power is G6; memory as text is G5's reading-half.

### 6C. Interpretive Ecology — how Scripture and the world were read

Seven attested modes, each with its node and gravity.

1. **The psalter as the day's substance and the mind's food** — sung "with the spirit … also with the understanding," in sections "with prayers in between," ten verses with understanding over a whole psalm "with a bewildered mind" (II.11); repeated "by heart" at work (II.15). South; G7's temporal skeleton.
2. **Typological reading of the hours** (III.3): Pentecost at the third hour, the crucifixion at the sixth, the harrowing of hell at the ninth, the Supper at evening, the vineyard's hours as the offices; Peter's sheet "let down from heaven by the four corners" as "nothing else than the Gospel … divided by the fourfold narrative of the Evangelists" (III.3, `iv.iii.iii.iii`, new). South; received.
3. **The garment as mystery** — each item of dress a "mystery" (*sacramentum*, Gibson's fn., Doc_03 8.6) of the inner life; the girdle as Elijah's and John's (*Inst.* I.1). South.
4. **The fourfold sense** (*Conf.* XIV.8, Doc_03 4.10) — attested, Cassian-only, held at Tier 3 by Doc_03 pending this lens: this document finds it *present but not organizing* in what was read; the office's typology (mode 2) does the interpretive work in practice. South.
5. **The rule of faith as the rule of reading** (G4) — Vincent's whole method, with Scripture read "according to the Tradition" because heretics quote it too (Doc_03 7.2, 3.4); the devil quotes Scripture (*Comm.* ch. 26, Doc_03 6.4); Deut. 13 read of the *church's* trial by an erring teacher (Vincent) and of the *individual will's* trial (Cassian, *Conf.* XIII.14) — one proof-text, two ecologies (Doc_03 7.6). South, both houses.
6. **Scripture as oracle** — the psalter opened at random names Defensor (*Vita* ch. IX); "It was believed that this Psalm had been chosen by Divine ordination." North; G6's logic applied to a book.
7. **The psalter as the vocabulary of grace** — XII.17's catena, in which the monk learns to *say* what he cannot *reason* (XIII.18's "cannot be fully grasped by the mind and reason of man," Doc_04 G3). South; G3 in G8's idiom.

**The interpretive ecology's own boundary:** in both nodes, a text's authority is tied to a *person's* — the elder who heard, the disciple who saw (G5) — and Vincent's rule is the one place where authority is transferred from persons to a *consensus of texts*. That transfer, made in 434 at Lérins, is the interpretive ecology's most consequential single move, and it is single-voice (Doc_03 7.2, [AS], single-voice Vincent).

### 7.1 The boundaries the world drew

- **House / world.** The renunciant's clothes in the steward's cupboard (IV.6); leaving is possible only "like a runaway slave by taking advantage of the thickest shades of night" (IV.6); the cell as harbour with the heart "fastened within the barriers" (II.14). Marmoutier's "one, and that a very narrow passage" (*Vita* ch. X); Lérins an island that "receives … those cast out by the shipwrecks of the stormy world" (Eucherius, Doc_04 G2). The boundary's failure-category is the Sarabaite, who makes "renunciation only as a public profession" and lives "two or three together," "their own masters" (Doc_03 1.4) — the world's own name for a monk without a boundary.
- **Catholic / heretic.** Vincent's rule; Pelagians as "frogs, fleas, and flies" (Doc_03 5.9); Arians in Martin's youth (*Vita* ch. VI); Priscillianists at Trier — with the northern insistence that the boundary is drawn by "a sentence of the bishops" and expulsion "from the churches," not by the sword (*SH* II.50). ## 8. Emotional and Affective Reconstruction

CF asks for hopes, fears, emotional weighting, and emotional patterns. The world's own affective vocabulary (Doc_03: compunction 4.8, lukewarmness 3.11, accidie 4.6, fear→hope→love 4.9, "terror of judgment" 6.9) is used; the analyst's terms stay outside the quotation marks.

**Hopes.** The south's hope has a name and a picture: "purity of heart, without which no one can gain that end … fixing our gaze then steadily on this goal, as if on a definite mark" (*Conf.* I.4, Doc_04 G7), the archer's mark (*Inst.* V.15), the heavenly Jerusalem toward which the athlete "does not run uncertainly" (V.17, `iv.iii.v.xvii`, new); and it is weighed against the present with a physical image — "all the long years of this present life disappear when you have regard to the eternity of the future glory: and all our sorrows vanish away in the contemplation of that vast bliss, and like smoke melt away, and come to nothing, and like ashes are no more seen" (XII.12, `iv.iii.xii.xii`, new). Lérins's hope is a harbour after shipwreck (Vincent, Eucherius). The north's hope is Martin's last sentence — "Abraham's bosom is about to receive me" (*Ep.* III, file line 2022, read this pass) — and the funeral's "holy joy" that "faith would prevent the shedding of tears" (line 2045).

**Fears.** The fear this world names most is not damnation but *cooling*: "lukewarm by being dispersed among the many" (II.5); "grow cold" (IV.6); "coldness of mind" that "prays out loud" (II.10); the deserter (X.3); and, at the affective level, accidie's noon — "frequently gazes up at the sun, as if it was too slow in setting … a kind of unreasonable confusion of mind takes possession of him like some foul darkness" (X.2, re-read). The second fear is the illusion — the dream (II.13), the false Christ (Doc_03 6.7), "an angel of Satan as an angel of light" (Doc_03 6.4). The third is shame, which the program turns into a diagnostic: "a thought is from the devil if we are ashamed to disclose it" (IV.9). The north fears differently: "a weariness of the present world, a terror of judgment, a fear of punishment" (*Ep.* II, Doc_04 G10), the devil at the deathbed ("Why do you stand here, thou bloody monster?", *Ep.* III, line 2020), and — Sulpitius's own — the fear of not being believed (*Ep.* I, Doc_04 G6). Vincent's is "a certain awful expectation of the approach of the divine judgment" (*Comm.* ch. 1 [2]); Salvian's is already realized.

**Emotional weighting.** *Compunction* is weighted as the sign of progress, not of failure: "their compunction for their faults increases day by day in proportion as their purity of soul advances" (XII.15, `iv.iii.xii.xv`, new); the monk fasts "with horror at sin" and "inflamed with the desire of perfection" by turns, and "by the copiousness of our tears and the weeping of our heart we shall succeed in extinguishing the fiery furnace of our body" (V.14, new). *Anger* and *envy* are weighted as the soul's food: "Slander is its food … A burst of anger also is its food, even if it be a very slight one … Envy is a food of the mind" (V.21, `iv.iii.v.xxi`, new); Paphnutius's story is a story about envy (XVIII.15). *Astonishment* is weighted oppositely at the two nodes: Cassian will leave out "all these things which minister to the reader nothing but astonishment and no instruction in the perfect life" (*Inst.* Pref., Doc_04 G6); Sulpitius writes so that readers "shall be roused" (*Vita* ch. I) and reports that Martin's face at death was "as if it had been the face of an angel" (*Ep.* III, line 2026). *Grief* is permitted and policed at once: "one would have been inclined to pardon those who wept, as well as to congratulate those who rejoiced, while each single person preferred that he himself should grieve, but that another should rejoice" (*Ep.* III, lines 2049–2052).

**Emotional patterns.** (i) *Fervour, then cooling* is the world's affective plot: the renunciant "aglow with spiritual fervour" who "afterwards, when he has cooled down," wants his money back (IV.4, re-read); "the early days of their conversion were so bright" (Doc_03 1.10); Cassian's climate pun on the "cold of Gaul" (Doc_03 3.11, C4) sits on top of it. (ii) *Fear → hope → love* as the sanctioned ascent (Doc_03 4.9), with fear of hell as the beginner's restraint (*Conf.* XI.6, Doc_04 G10) — an emotional program, not only a doctrinal one. (iii) *Two affective economies*: the south trains the monk out of wonder and into compunction; the north forms the disciple through wonder and into imitation. This is Doc_04's G6 × G7 competition felt from within: Brictio's sneer at "ridiculous fancies about visions" is the southern economy speaking, uninvited, inside the northern one. (iv) *Tonal signature by voice*: Sulpitius exuberant and defensive; Cassian sober and exact; Vincent anxious ("awful expectation," "no ordinary care"); Salvian indignant. A Representative that smooths these into one temperament has left the ecology.

### Inhabited passage (Layer 2) — Lérins, a man newly come from the world

## 9. Representative Theological Patterns, and Power, Influence & Historical Dynamics

### 9A. Representative Theological Patterns

Stated as the world held them, each with its gravity, node, and confidence, so Doc_06/Doc_10 inherit the shape rather than a modern summary.

1. **Grace and effort co-operate, and the relation cannot be reasoned to the bottom** (G3; south; Documented for Cassian's text, Contested for its relation to Augustine). The world's own two sentences: "the grace of God always co-operates with our will for its advantage" (*Conf.* XIII.13) and "how God works all things in us and yet everything can be ascribed to free will, cannot be fully grasped by the mind and reason of man" (XIII.18, Doc_04 G3); its formation-manual form: "perfection cannot possibly be gained without these, but … by these only without the grace of God nobody can ever attain it" (*Inst.* XII.14). What it refused, in its own words: that God "does not generally will all men … to be saved" (XIII.7), and, on the other side, "the profane notion of some who attribute everything to free will" (XIII.16). The Massilian side's own best later witness is grace-prevenient: "the grace of God always invites, precedes and helps our will" (Gennadius on Faustus, ch. LXXXVI, ancient; Doc_02 §1.4). The pattern is *two-sided by design*, and a Representative that resolves it either way has left the world.
2. **Received, not invented** (G4; both nodes; Documented). Monastic custom, doctrine, and cult all tested by antiquity, numbers/universality, and consent (§4.3). Progress is allowed as "enlargement," never "alteration" (Doc_03 7.5).
3. **The monk-bishop is no contradiction** (G1; both nodes; Documented) — "kept up the position of a bishop properly, yet in such a way as not to lay aside the objects and virtues of a monk" (*Vita* ch. X); with the standing counter-pattern that office is a temptation and power is diminished by it (G9, G6).
4. **Power and humility** (G6 vs. G7; opposed by node; Documented for each). North: the saint is "deemed powerful and truly apostolical" (Doc_03 8.2); south: "Humility therefore is the mistress of all virtues" and "nor should we ask whether the devils are subject to him" (*Conf.* XV.7, Doc_04 G6). Cassian awards "mother/mistress of all virtues" both to discretion and to humility (Doc_04 §9).
5. **The devil is real, present, and sometimes visible** (folded into G8/G6; all three voices; Documented) — Doc_03 6.4.
