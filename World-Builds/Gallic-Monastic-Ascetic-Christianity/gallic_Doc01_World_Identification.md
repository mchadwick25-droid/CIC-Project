## 1. Distinct World Criteria

What makes this a distinct formation world, rather than an undifferentiated extension of Egyptian desert monasticism transplanted westward, or a mere biographical footnote to Hilary of Poitiers' Nicene episcopate, is a recurring, evidenced cluster of features. **This section is written more cautiously than its first draft** (Round 1 findings S1, S2, S10): it separates what is evidenced from what is this world's own working hypothesis, and attributes the case document's own language to the case document rather than to "the secondary literature."

- **Formation ecology — evidenced for one node, hypothesised for the whole.** This world's case rests on a real, evidenced circle at its southern end: Cassian dedicated the second set of *Conferences* (XI–XVII) jointly to **Honoratus, abbot of Lérins, and Eucherius** (later bishop of Lyons) — a documented, personal, dated link between the Marseilles house and Lérins, confirmed directly in this world's own vendored text (see §2.3). Martin's own household at Ligugé and then Marmoutier is this world's earliest and best-attested node, but — as §2.3 finds directly from the sources rather than assumes — **no documented link between it and the Lérins–Marseilles pair has been found.** This document therefore treats "a lived circle of institutions and people moving between them" as demonstrated for Lérins–Marseilles and as this world's own working hypothesis, not yet evidence, for the connection to Tours. The case document's own G0 framing (case §4) states the whole-ecology version; this document is the place that document's own reservation ("Whether that is one formation world or a lineage of two is a Step 0 question this document cannot settle," case §5) actually gets tested, and it is tested honestly in §2.3 and §6 below, not assumed here.
- **Recurring candidate gravities** (preliminary only; formal Gravity Discovery is reserved for Doc_04): ascetic withdrawal that nonetheless produces bishops rather than permanent hermits — the case document's own phrase for this, worth quoting exactly rather than treating as established scholarly language, is that Lérins was "not a withdrawal world; it is a seminary that looked like a monastery" (case document §4, its own coinage, corrected here from the first draft's misattribution to "the secondary literature," Round 1 finding S10); the transmission of Egyptian coenobitic teaching into a specifically Latin, Gallic idiom, chiefly through Cassian's *Institutes* and *Conferences*; a rule for discerning catholic tradition against novelty, articulated from within this circle (Vincent's *Commonitory*, 434); and a distinctive, actively defended position on grace and human effort, held under direct theological pressure from Augustine's own authority.
- **Recurring patterns:** formation through a named teacher-to-disciple relationship inside an institution (Martin's own disciples at Marmoutier; Cassian addressing named abbots at Marseilles and Lérins), rather than the more diffuse *geron*-and-wandering-visitor pattern of the Egyptian desert; a self-conscious relationship to Egyptian monastic authority as *received and adapted* rather than invented locally — Cassian's own Institutes Preface states he will supplement what the Gallic climate makes impossible to copy from Egypt "by the customs of the monasteries which are found throughout Pontus and Mesopotamia" (quoted exactly, Round 1 finding S11 corrected — see §2.3 for the fuller passage); direct participation, as monks rather than as spectators, in a live doctrinal controversy over grace, argued in writing against the most authoritative living theologian in the West.
- **What distinguishes this world from its nearest neighbors** (argued fully under World Continuity & Distinction, §8): from Egyptian desert monasticism, this world is a *reception and adaptation* culture, not an originating one. From the (unbuilt) Gallic Nicene Episcopate (I.37, Hilary of Poitiers' world), this world is a monastic and ascetic ecology, not an episcopate defending a creed against imperial pressure. From Merovingian Gallic Christianity (also unbuilt, c. 480–620), this world is late-Roman and provincial, not post-Roman, Frankish, and royal-episcopal.

## 2. Temporal Scope

### 2.1 Beginning point: conditional on §6's still-open question, not asserted as settled

The case document's own window opens "c. 360." Independent verification against this world's own vendored source confirms Martin, after leaving military service and seeking out Hilary of Poitiers, was ordained exorcist by Hilary and — with Hilary's protection, at Poitiers, Hilary's own see — founded a small ascetic community at **Ligugé**. This world's own vendored NPNF introduction states plainly: "Ligugé was founded shortly after 360, and Marmoutier rather later, after 371" — independently corroborating the c. 360–361 date this document's own research separately reached [Widely Accepted — the fact and date are well attested across the source tradition Sulpicius himself founds; see §9 on why this is not rated *Documented*].

**This document does not, and cannot honestly, give this world a single floor**, because §2.3 and §6 below find that this world's southern node (Lérins–Marseilles, evidenced as linked to each other) has no documented link to Martin's Tours. Two floors follow, held apart deliberately rather than blended into one:

- **If Doc_04 finds one continuous formation world:** the floor is **c. 360** (Ligugé), the earliest node, on the reasoning that Ligugé is where the formation pattern this world is built around — ascetic withdrawal organized as a replicable community, not removed from episcopal life — first appears.
- **If Doc_04 finds a lineage of two, or two strands with genuinely separate origins:** the Loire node's own floor stays c. 360, but the southern node's own floor is **c. 400–410** (Honoratus's founding of Lérins, §2.3), a full four decades later, and this document's own "distinct world criteria" (§1) and "entry gravity" (§5) would need to be re-argued from the southern node alone.

This document proceeds using c. 360 as its working floor **because the current Atlas entry is a single entry (I.27) and Doc_01 has no authority to split it** — but it names the conditional nature of that choice explicitly here rather than letting §5's later "has a new gravity emerged" answer read as though the question were already closed (the failure Round 1 finding S1 identified).

### 2.3 The southern node: a documented pair, not an assumed one

Honoratus founded the cenobitic community on the island of Lérins. This world's own vendored source states the date without hedging: **"In 410 S. Honoratus founded the monastery ... in the island of Lérins"** [Widely Accepted — this document's independent research found the same c. 400–410 range and treats 410 as the working figure, following the world's own vendored source rather than narrowing an otherwise-open scholarly range on its own authority]. Cassian arrived in Marseilles and founded the twin houses of **Saint-Victor** for men and **Saint-Sauveur** for women (named correctly here; the first draft left the women's house unnamed, Round 1 finding C4) **c. 415** [Widely Accepted]. Vincent joined the community at Lérins; this document's independent research found no dominant scholarly date for his entry — reference accounts state only that he arrived after a period of secular pursuits, with no date Gennadius himself supplies — so **"c. 425" is corrected here from Widely Accepted to Inferential/Thin** (Round 1 finding S13c), a floating convention rather than a documented or dominant position. Vincent wrote the *Commonitory* in **434**, [**Documented** — this label is fully earned: Vincent states in his own text that he is writing three years after the Council of Ephesus (431), internal dating this world's own vendored source and this document's independent research both confirm].

**What this document did not check in its first draft, and does now:** this world's own vendored volume states directly, in its own editorial introduction to the Institutes, that Cassian's second set of *Conferences* (XI–XVII) — completed shortly after Castor's death in 426 — **"is dedicated to Honoratus and Eucherius, who are styled 'fratres.' Eucherius did not become Bishop of Lyons till 434; but, as Honoratus was raised to the see of Arles in 426, the volume must have been published not later than that year."** This is a documented, dated, personal-and-institutional link between the Marseilles house and the abbot of Lérins (Round 1 finding S5) — real evidence that Lérins and Marseilles are one linked node, not an assumed unity.

**What this document searched for and could not find, and says so rather than assuming otherwise:** no documented direct institutional or personal link between Martin's Tours and this Lérins–Marseilles pair. The first draft offered Sulpicius's *Dialogues* as cross-node evidence — Dialogue I carries Postumianus's own first-hand travel account, which the first draft read as connecting the Tours-descended Martin material to the southern circle. **This was wrong, and reading the passage directly in this world's own vendored file corrects it** (Round 1 finding S4): Postumianus sails "after setting sail from Narbonne, on the fifth day we entered a port of Africa" and, on the return, "landing on the thirtieth day at Marseilles, I came on from that and arrived here." Marseilles appears once, as a port of disembarkation, in a work written c. 404–406 — a decade before Cassian arrived there and founded anything. Lérins does not appear at all. Postumianus's own destination and subject is **Egypt**, not the Lérins–Marseilles circle. Sulpicius's corpus attests Martin and Egypt; it does not attest this world's own southern node in any form.

**The honest shape this leaves:** a well-evidenced southern pair (Lérins–Marseilles) and an isolated, earlier, better-known northern node (Tours) with no documented traffic between them — not "three institutions moving between each other" as the case document's own G0 framing puts it, but two clusters whose unity is this document's own live, unresolved question, tested properly in §6.

### 2.4 Ending point: c. 450 argued against its strongest counter-evidence, not around it

Vincent's death is placed "before 450, and probably shortly after 434" [Widely Accepted — independently verified: it follows from Gennadius placing his death in the reigns of Theodosius II and Valentinian III]. The first draft proposed c. 450 as a directional closing marker without engaging the single figure who bears most directly on whether that boundary holds. **This revision engages him directly** (Round 1 finding S8):

**Faustus of Riez** was a monk, then the **third abbot of Lérins from c. 433** — inside this world's own window — and later bishop of Riez. He is named in this world's own vendored source's introduction to the *Commonitory*, in the same sentence as Honoratus and Hilary of Arles, as one of the Lérins circle "opposed to St. Augustine's later teaching, and holding what was afterwards called Semipelagian doctrine" (§2.5, §7 below). He wrote ***De Gratia*** (two books) **c. 475**, at the express request of the synods of Arles and Lyons (475), against the predestinarian presbyter Lucidus — the direct institutional and doctrinal continuation of this world's own defining argument, conducted from inside its own institutional network, a full quarter-century past the c. 450 figure this document proposes. **This document has independently located and verified the Latin text**: archive.org item `corpusscriptorum21fausuoft` (*Fausti Reiensis et Ruricii Opera*, ed. August Engelbrecht, CSEL vol. 21, Vienna: F. Tempsky, 1891; confirmed `NOT_IN_COPYRIGHT (US)`; table of contents independently fetched and confirmed to contain "FAVSTI DE GRATIA LIBRI II"). It is public domain, genuinely findable, and **not yet vendored into this project's library** — a concrete, ready-to-execute G1 follow-up (second-witness rule, original-language only — no public-domain English translation exists, per this world's own G1 manifest), not a speculative lead. This document does not complete that intake here, to keep this revision scoped to what Doc_01 itself requires, but names it as the immediate next G1 action rather than a someday item.

**Does Faustus break the c. 450 boundary, or confirm what kind of boundary it is?** This document argues the latter, but states the argument rather than asserting the conclusion: by 475, the controversy is being conducted **by a bishop, writing at the express commission of two episcopal synods**, not by a monk within a still-forming ascetic community addressing named brother-monks the way Cassian's *Conferences* prefaces do (§2.3). That is a change in the *institutional register* of the argument — from formation-community literature to synodal commission — even though the underlying question and the personnel (Faustus was formed at Lérins well inside this world's own window) are continuous. **This document treats that register change, not the argument's content, as the boundary** — which means the c. 450 date is itself approximate and the real marker is closer to the mid-450s to 460s, when Faustus's own role shifts from monk-abbot to bishop. This document does not narrow the date further without more direct evidence and flags the imprecision rather than manufacturing false precision.

**The corrected Mathisen argument.** The first draft used Mathisen's finding that the Lérins "power elite" — the network of Lérins-trained bishops occupying sees across southern Gaul — "peaked around 450" as evidence the boundary sits "before" that consolidation. **This inverted the finding** (Round 1 finding S8's second problem): a boundary cannot sit *before* a process that peaks *at* it. Mathisen's own account has this consolidation rising through Hilary of Arles's own tenure (bishop 430–449) — meaning the process the first draft used as an *exit* marker actually *begins* two decades **inside** the proposed window, not after it. This document corrects the claim rather than repeating it: the shift from formation community to ecclesiastical-political network is gradual and already underway well before 450, not a clean transition at that date. What actually changes in kind, per the Faustus reasoning above, is closer to the 460s–470s.

### 2.5 The interval this world's boundary opens onto is not undocumented — it is a housekeeping gap between two Atlas entries, not a gap in history

The first draft called the 450–480 interval between this world's proposed close and `merovingian-gallic-christianity`'s proposed c. 480 opening "undocumented" and said this document "does not know ... what fills that gap." **This was wrong and is corrected** (Round 1 finding S9): the interval is one of the better-attested stretches of the period. It contains Faustus of Riez's episcopate and *De Gratia* (§2.4); the synods of Arles and Lyons (475); and Sidonius Apollinaris's letter corpus, among the fullest surviving windows onto Gallic episcopal and aristocratic life in exactly these decades. The gap is between two **census entries' proposed date ranges**, a housekeeping question this document should name plainly as such, not an evidentiary void.

**This document also failed to engage the census's own continuity claims**, corrected here: this world's own census `relationsSummary` calls it "the seedbed of the Merovingian Gallic church that follows it," and Merovingian Gallic Christianity's own summary says it "receives Martin of Tours's line." Both entries already assert continuity across the interval this document called a gap. This document does not resolve the tension between "genuine transition in political frame" (§8.5) and "seedbed/receives" continuity — both may be true at once, a political-frame change riding on top of real institutional continuity — but names both claims and leaves the reconciliation to Doc_02.

### 2.6 What remains internal vs. what indicates transition

Internal to this world: ascetic withdrawal that continues to produce bishops and remains in active, direct correspondence with the wider Latin church; the live, unsettled argument over grace and human effort, carried by named living participants; direct transmission of Egyptian teaching by a participant (Cassian) still explicitly adapting it to Gallic conditions, not yet codified into a fixed regional Rule. Indicates transition out of this world: the argument being carried forward by bishops writing at synodal commission (Faustus, §2.4) rather than monks addressing brother-monks; Lérins's alumni network consolidating into the ecclesiastical-political structure Mathisen documents; the region's political frame shifting from late-Roman provincial administration toward the post-Roman, Frankish, royal-episcopal world Merovingian Gallic Christianity is built to hold.

## 3. Geographic Scope

**Core sites:** Ligugé and Marmoutier, both near Tours, in Roman Gaul's Loire valley; the island of Lérins (modern Île Saint-Honorat), off the Mediterranean coast opposite Cannes; and Marseilles (Massilia), where Cassian founded Saint-Victor (men) and Saint-Sauveur (women). These sites are geographically dispersed — Tours to Marseilles is on the order of 600 km straight-line, materially more by any actual route available in the period [corrected from the first draft's "roughly 700 km by the routes available," which understated a by-road figure relative to the straight-line distance, Round 1 finding C5] — connected, per §2.3, not by a documented three-way circuit but by a real, evidenced southern pair (Lérins–Marseilles) and a separate, earlier northern node (Tours) whose link to the south this document could not establish.

## 4. Cultural Scope

**Linguistic:** Latin throughout — unlike Egyptian desert monasticism's predominantly Coptic-speaking base reached mainly through Greek-literate outside observers, every primary voice in this world writes in Latin. Cassian's own project is one of linguistic and cultural translation — rendering Greek-mediated Egyptian teaching into Latin for a Gallic audience.

**Social:** the case document's own §4 names this world's distinctive social function in its own words: "Lérins formed bishops. It is not a withdrawal world; it is a seminary that looked like a monastery" (attributed correctly here to the case document, not to secondary scholarship — see §1). This world's own vendored *Commonitory* introduction independently corroborates the pattern by name: Honoratus and Hilary "afterwards successively bishops of Arles," and Faustus "afterwards bishop of Riez" — three Lérins-formed men who became bishops, confirmed directly in this world's own primary source rather than only asserted from the case document.

**Women's presence — a real Missing Voices concern (Constitution Article 20).** Cassian's foundation at Marseilles explicitly included a women's house, **Saint-Sauveur**, alongside the men's Saint-Victor [Widely Accepted]. No named woman ascetic comparable to the desert's *ammas* has surfaced in this document's research for Tours, Lérins, or Marseilles specifically. **A second, edition-level absence compounds this, and this document did not previously connect the two** (Round 1 finding S17): this world's own vendored NPNF *Conferences* translation does not render **Conference XII ("On Chastity")** — marked "Not translated" in the text itself — or **Conference XXII ("On Nocturnal Illusions")** — marked "This Conference is omitted." Twenty-two of twenty-four conferences carry text in this edition; these two, both sexuality-and-body material, do not. This is a **Victorian-era translator's excision**, not an absence in the ancient record — an evidential-visibility gap layered directly on top of the ecological-visibility question this paragraph opens with, and the two should not be allowed to blur into one another: the record may or may not preserve women's own voice well; this specific edition demonstrably suppresses body-and-chastity material regardless of whose voice it belonged to. Both gaps are named here and carried to Doc_02's own Missing Voices Assessment, not resolved.

**Political:** late-Roman provincial Gaul. This world's monks were in direct, functioning correspondence with the highest ecclesiastical authority in the Latin West (Augustine; and, per Prosper's and his correspondent's 431 journey, Pope Celestine I — the journey itself independently verified, only the correspondent's identity is in question, §7).

**Institutional:** authority ran through named abbots addressing named monks and brother-abbots in writing (Cassian's *Institutes* is addressed to one named bishop; the *Conferences* to named recipients including Honoratus and Eucherius, §2.3), and through a bishop who was himself the community's founder and head (Martin at Marmoutier).

## 5. World Separation Criteria

Framework Part I requires all six questions answered, not only the first. The first draft answered two (Round 1 finding S15); this revision answers all six, and — per §2.1's own conditional floor — answers the entry question in the same conditional form.

**Has a new gravity emerged marking entry into this world?** Conditionally yes, on two possible readings (§2.1): if one world, Martin's founding of Ligugé under Hilary's patronage (c. 360) marks a formation practice — replicable ascetic community, not a bishop's individual household — distinct from Hilary's own creedal-episcopal world (§2.2, §8.1). If a lineage of two, the southern node's own entry gravity is Honoratus's founding of Lérins (c. 410, §2.3), a different gravity at a different date, and the Ligugé founding would belong to a separate, earlier world this document does not construct.

**Has a major gravity disappeared?** Not clearly, inside this world's own window. The clearest candidate — direct personal transmission from participants who had themselves lived in Egypt (Cassian) — persists at least through his death (c. 435) and, per §2.4, arguably survives in attenuated, institutionally-mediated form through Faustus.

**Has formation changed substantially?** Yes, in one traceable respect: from Martin's single-household model (disciples gathered directly around a founder-bishop) to Cassian's *written*, codified formation literature (the *Institutes*, explicitly designed to be portable to "a new establishment in the West," per the Preface quoted in §2.3) — a shift from person-mediated to text-mediated formation that is itself part of what this world transmits onward (§8.3).

**Has worship changed substantially?** This is not a thin question for this world specifically. Cassian's *Institutes* Books II–III concern the liturgical office at length, and this world's own vendored text's own editorial apparatus traces the practice of interspersing psalms with set prayers as a specific Egyptian-to-Gallic (and Spanish) transmission this world's own literature carries — worship as an explicit object of the same East-to-Gaul adaptation project that structures the *Institutes* as a whole. Doc_05's Formation Ecology work should treat this as a load-bearing, not incidental, inheritance.

**Has authority changed substantially?** Yes, in the same direction as the formation question: from Martin's own direct, personal, bishop-founder authority to Cassian's named-abbot-addressing-named-monks written correspondence (§4) to, by the 470s, bishops writing at the express commission of episcopal synods (Faustus, §2.4) — a real, traceable shift in where formation authority actually sits, running through and past this world's own proposed window.

**Has interpretation changed substantially?** Yes — Vincent's *Commonitory* (434) supplies this world's own explicit interpretive rule ("quod ubique, quod semper, quod ab omnibus"), a criterion for what counts as legitimate doctrinal development that this world's own literature did not have and needed precisely because it was living through a live, contested theological argument (§7). This is itself a candidate gravity (§1, §6) as much as a separation criterion.

## 7. Preliminary Forces Identification

**What generated this world:** the felt inadequacy, for a subset of committed Latin-West Christians, of ordinary institutional Christian life as a sufficient arena for radical formation, combined with a specifically Gallic-Roman political and ecclesiastical setting in which formation could be pursued without severing a monk from institutional authority or even from eventual episcopal office. Where Egyptian withdrawal moved bodies into literal desert wilderness, this world's withdrawal (an island; a cross-river retreat from a city) is geographically modest and does not remove its practitioners from ordinary ecclesiastical reach — a difference this document treats as structurally significant, not merely a matter of degree.

**What larger world it was embedded in:** late Roman, increasingly institutionally Christian Gaul, itself embedded in a Latin West whose most authoritative living theological voice (Augustine of Hippo) sat in North Africa, and whose highest ecclesiastical authority (the bishop of Rome) this world's own monks travelled to consult directly.

**What it was responding to:** the practical and pastoral problem of how to teach grace, human effort, and perseverance to monks actually living the ascetic life. Egyptian teaching, carried west by Cassian, supplied a vocabulary — synergy between divine grace and human will — this world drew on directly. **This document corrects, rather than silently resolves, the relative dating of that response** (Round 1 finding S7): the first draft treated Cassian's Conference XIII as straightforwardly preceding Augustine's 428/429 replies. This world's own vendored source dates the Conferences XI–XVII collection (which contains Conference XIII) to "not later than" 426 — before *even* Augustine's 426/427 *De correptione et gratia*, on that dating. But the relative chronology is **Contested**, not settled in either direction: Owen Chadwick argued for redating Honoratus's episcopate specifically to make room for Conference XIII to respond to Augustine; Augustine Casiday (*Tradition and Theology in St John Cassian*, Oxford University Press, 2007) rejects Chadwick's redating and separately argues Conference XIII shows little direct anti-Augustinian polemic at all. This document names both positions and does not adopt either — which means this world's "what it was refusing" cannot be stated as a direct rebuttal-in-sequence to a specific Augustine text, only as a shared engagement with the same live question, argued from Scripture and Egyptian precedent rather than as a citation-level reply.

**What it was refusing:** neither Pelagius's own position (already condemned) nor Augustine's authority as such — Conference XIII argues from Scripture and the Egyptian abbas' own teaching, not from an explicit rejection of Augustine by name, whichever way the dating question above resolves. What this world's participants refused, more precisely and per their own later correspondence (§2.3, §2.4), was the implication some read out of *On Rebuke and Grace* — that human effort and moral responsibility become close to meaningless under a strong doctrine of unconditional predestination. **This document avoids the retrospective label "semi-Pelagian"** for this refusal — a 16th-century coinage (Theodore Beza, c. 1556, applied originally to contemporary Roman Catholic teaching, not retrospectively; the backward application to fifth-century Gaul is usually credited to the Roman Catholic controversialist Nicholas Sanders, 1571 — corrected from the first draft's conflation of these two distinct moves, Round 1 finding C2). **This document's own vendored source supplies the period-contemporary alternative, and states plainly whose word it is** (corrected from the first draft's error, Round 1 finding S18): "Massilian" (*Massilienses*) is **Prosper of Aquitaine's own label for the group he was reporting to Augustine**, not the group's self-designation — a hostile outsider's geographical label, genuinely period-contemporary rather than a later retrojection, but not, as the first draft wrongly stated, "this world's own name for itself." It is also, by its own etymology, a Marseilles-specific term that does not naturally extend to cover Vincent or the Lérins figures — a limitation this document notes rather than smooths over when using the term as this build's own standing vocabulary going forward (a question for Doc_03/Doc_06 to resolve with a fuller term inventory).

## 8. World Continuity & Distinction

### 8.1 vs. `gallic-nicene-episcopate` (Atlas I.37, Hilary of Poitiers' world, unbuilt)

The two worlds touch at exactly one documented point (§2.2): Martin's formation as Hilary's disciple and Hilary's patronage of Martin's first foundation at Ligugé. This document treats that point as a genuine inheritance without collapsing the two worlds into one — they differ in kind, not merely in period. The census entry's own `relationsSummary` states this same judgment independently.

### 8.2 vs. Egyptian desert monasticism (`desert-monasticism`, built and live)

This world is a *reception* culture for desert teaching, not an alternative or rival origin. Cassian's own *Institutes* and *Conferences* are simultaneously desert-monasticism's own primary-source Latin transmission and this world's founding literature — the same texts doing genuinely different work for each world. What is distinctive to this world is a live, two-way written theological argument with the wider Latin church's most authoritative living voice — desert monasticism, per its own Doc_01, produced terse oral sayings; this world produced treatises addressed to, and answered by, Augustine of Hippo by name.

### 8.3 vs. `early-benedictine-italian-monasticism` (Atlas era 3, Pre-Survey Candidate, unbuilt)

The census's own `why` field names this future entry as where "what Cassian carried west from the desert takes durable institutional form ... in a Rule that later Western monasticism kept returning to" (quoted in full here, correcting the first draft's mid-sentence truncation, Round 1 finding C8). This world is upstream of that one: **corrected from the first draft** (Round 1 finding C10), Rule of Benedict ch. 73 commends "the Conferences of the Fathers, their Institutes and their Lives" together — not the Institutes alone — and RB 42 separately prescribes the *Conferences* specifically as daily reading, an equally strong and previously under-credited half of the same documented transmission line.

### 8.4 vs. `priscillianist-asceticism` (Atlas era 2, Possible Future World, unbuilt) and `pelagianism` (Atlas era 2, Possible Future World, unbuilt)

Priscillianist asceticism is geographically distinct (Spain, with Gallic entanglement via the Trier trial Sulpicius's own *Sacred History* documents) and theologically distinct (a controversy over a movement's own orthodoxy, ending in the first state execution for heresy, rather than a controversy over the mechanics of grace). Pelagianism is the doctrine this world's own participants explicitly did not want to be mistaken for holding (§7) — the census's own entry notes it "clears the floor but its surviving ecology is too thin for a lived tradition," which this document reads as corroborating why the Massilian side of the controversy belongs here rather than there.

### 8.5 vs. `merovingian-gallic-christianity` (Atlas era 3, Pre-Survey Candidate, unbuilt)

Post-Roman, Frankish, royal-episcopal, and ordinary-parish in character against this world's late-Roman, provincial, and monastic-elite character. **Corrected from the first draft** (§2.5, Round 1 finding S9): the interval between the two entries' proposed date ranges is not an evidentiary void, and both entries' own census summaries assert direct continuity across it ("seedbed"; "receives Martin of Tours's line") that this document names rather than either accepts or resolves.
