## 1. Distinct World Criteria

What makes this a distinct formation world, rather than an undifferentiated extension of Egyptian desert monasticism transplanted westward, or a mere biographical footnote to Hilary of Poitiers' Nicene episcopate, is a recurring, evidenced cluster of features:

- **Formation ecology:** a lived circle of institutions and people moving between them, not a reading list assembled after the fact — Martin's ascetic household at Ligugé and then Marmoutier near Tours; the island cenobium at Lérins; Cassian's twin houses (men's and women's) at Marseilles. Sulpitius Severus knew Martin directly and wrote of him within Martin's own lifetime; Vincent of Lérins and Cassian both belonged to the Lérins–Marseilles circle in the same generation. [Documented — case document §4, corroborated by this document's own research below.]
- **Recurring candidate gravities** (preliminary only; formal Gravity Discovery is reserved for Doc_04): ascetic withdrawal that nonetheless produces bishops rather than permanent hermits — Lérins is repeatedly described in the secondary literature as "a seminary that looked like a monastery" (case document §4); the transmission of Egyptian coenobitic teaching into a specifically Latin, Gallic idiom, chiefly through Cassian's *Institutes* and *Conferences*; a rule for discerning catholic tradition against novelty, articulated from within this circle (Vincent's *Commonitory*, 434); and a distinctive, actively defended position on grace and human effort, held under direct theological pressure from Augustine's own authority.
- **Recurring patterns:** formation through a named teacher-to-disciple relationship inside an institution (Martin's own disciples at Marmoutier; Cassian addressing named abbots at Marseilles), rather than the more diffuse *geron*-and-wandering-visitor pattern of the Egyptian desert; a self-conscious relationship to Egyptian monastic authority as *received and adapted* rather than invented locally — Cassian's own stated purpose in the *Institutes* is to carry Egyptian practice to Gallic houses "so far as the nature of the climate would allow," not to reproduce it unchanged; direct participation, as monks rather than as spectators, in a live doctrinal controversy over grace, argued in writing against the most authoritative living theologian in the West.
- **What distinguishes this world from its nearest neighbors** (argued fully under World Continuity & Distinction, §8): from Egyptian desert monasticism, this world is a *reception and adaptation* culture, not an originating one — every node in it (Martin, Cassian, Vincent) stands in an acknowledged debtor relationship to Egypt, and the transmission itself, in a specifically Gallic idiom, is part of what this world *is*. From the (unbuilt) Gallic Nicene Episcopate (I.37, Hilary of Poitiers' world), this world is a monastic and ascetic ecology, not an episcopate defending a creed against imperial pressure — even though the two touch at one real, documented point (§8.1). From Merovingian Gallic Christianity (also unbuilt, c. 480–620), this world is late-Roman and provincial, not post-Roman, Frankish, and royal-episcopal (case document §5, "just widen the Merovingian entry" objection, rejected there on the same grounds restated here).

## 2. Temporal Scope

### 2.1 Beginning point: c. 360, refined from the case document's own window by this document's research

The case document's own window opens "c. 360." Independent research this session sharpens the case for a beginning point close to that figure, on stronger evidentiary ground than the case document itself supplies: Martin, after leaving military service and seeking out Hilary of Poitiers, was ordained exorcist by Hilary and — with Hilary's protection, at Poitiers, Hilary's own see — founded a small ascetic community at **Ligugé, conventionally dated c. 360–361**, arguably the first organized monastic settlement in Gaul [Widely Accepted; Ligugé Abbey's own historical summary and multiple encyclopedia accounts converge on 360–361, though none of this document's sources claims documentary-level precision for the exact year]. Martin's better-known foundation, **Marmoutier**, across the Loire from Tours, follows only after his acclamation as bishop of Tours in 371 and his own withdrawal from the city in 372 [Widely Accepted].

**This document proposes c. 360 (Ligugé), not 372 (Marmoutier), as the working floor**, on the reasoning that Ligugé is where the formation pattern this world is built around — ascetic withdrawal that does not remove its practitioner from institutional and even episcopal life — first appears, a full decade before Marmoutier. Marmoutier is this world's best-attested and most influential node, not necessarily its earliest.

### 2.2 A genealogical root this world does not itself hold, and should not silently absorb

**Martin's own formation begins under Hilary of Poitiers** — the very figure the case document deliberately excludes from this world (case document §4: "a Nicene controversialist whose Gallic location is close to incidental to what he wrote"), and who is now the founding figure of a separate, adjacent, still-unbuilt census entry, `gallic-nicene-episcopate` (Atlas I.37, c. 350–400). That entry's own `relationsSummary` names the connection directly and in these terms: *"The two touch at one point that matters — Martin of Tours was Hilary's disciple, and the monastic world grows out of the ground this one defended."* [Documented — Martin's ordination as exorcist under Hilary and his founding of Ligugé under Hilary's protection are reported consistently across the secondary literature this document consulted.]

This document treats that inheritance as real and worth naming plainly (per Framework Part I's World Continuity & Distinction requirement, §8 below) without importing Hilary himself, or the Nicene-Arian creedal struggle he embodies, into this world's own core. Martin's formation under Hilary is background this world inherits; it is not this world's own gravity, which is ascetic-monastic rather than creedal-episcopal.

### 2.3 The rest of the ecology: Lérins and Marseilles, a full generation later

Honoratus founded the cenobitic community on the island of Lérins **c. 400–410** (this document uses c. 410 as its working figure, following the balance of the sources consulted, while noting the range is itself the scholarly position, not an artificially narrowed one) [Widely Accepted]. Cassian arrived in Marseilles and founded the twin houses of St. Victor (one for men, one for women — a detail worth carrying forward to the Missing Voices assessment, §4 below) **c. 415** [Widely Accepted, converging across multiple sources]. Vincent joined the community at Lérins **c. 425** [Widely Accepted] and wrote the *Commonitory* in **434**, datable with unusual precision because Vincent himself states he is writing three years after the Council of Ephesus (431) [Documented — internal dating from the text itself].

**This document does not smooth over what this means for the world's own internal shape:** Martin died in 397. Vincent was still alive and writing in 434, and the controversy this world holds under pressure (§6, §7) runs into 428–429 at Augustine's own hand and later still in Cassian's and Prosper's exchange. The distance between Ligugé (c. 360) and Vincent's *Commonitory* (434) is not a single working lifetime but **most of two generations**. The case document itself names this as the world's most serious open question (case document §5, "It is three things, not one") and explicitly declines to resolve it, reserving it for Doc_04's own six-test assessment. This document does not resolve it either. What this document adds, from its own research, is that **no documented direct institutional or personal link between Martin's Tours and the Lérins–Marseilles circle was found** — unlike, for instance, Desert Monasticism's own Antony-to-Amoun link (Amoun founded Kellia "on Antony's own advice"), nothing in the sources this document consulted shows Honoratus, Cassian, or Vincent citing Martin, visiting Marmoutier, or claiming Martin as a model, and nothing shows Martin's own community at Tours engaging with Lérins or Marseilles. The two nodes share a region, a broad ascetic culture, and (per Cassian's own dedication of the *Institutes* "for Gallic monasteries" generally) a common addressee class — but the absence of a demonstrated direct connection is itself a finding, not a gap this document should paper over, and it sharpens rather than settles the one-world-or-two-worlds question for Doc_04.

### 2.4 Ending point: c. 450, offered as directional, with an honest gap named rather than hidden

Vincent's death is placed "before 450, and probably shortly after 434" [Widely Accepted]. This document proposes **c. 450** as a directional closing marker: it is close to the death of the last of this world's own primary voices (Vincent), and it sits before the mid-to-late 5th-century consolidation of Lérins into what Mathisen's scholarship (§8, §10) describes as a factional "power elite" whose influence peaked closer to 450 and after — a shift in the community's character from a formation ecology to an ecclesiastical-political one that this document treats as indicating transition rather than continuity, on the same logic Desert Monasticism's own Doc_01 applied to Palladius's and Cassian's own retrospective, curatorial relationship to a substantially completed body of teaching.

**A gap this document names rather than hides:** `merovingian-gallic-christianity`, the next Gallic census entry, opens c. 480 — a full **thirty years after** this world's proposed c. 450 close. This document does not know, and does not attempt to invent, what fills that gap; it is flagged as an open item (§11) rather than either extended backward into Merovingian territory (which the case document's own §5 argues against on independent grounds — "backdating one entry to cover both would blur the transition that makes each interesting") or silently ignored.

### 2.5 What remains internal vs. what indicates transition

Internal to this world: ascetic withdrawal that continues to produce bishops and remains in active, direct correspondence with the wider Latin church (Prosper and Hilary of Arles writing to Augustine; Augustine writing back); the live, unsettled argument over grace and human effort, carried by named living participants rather than inherited as settled doctrine; direct transmission of Egyptian teaching by a participant (Cassian) still explicitly adapting it "so far as the climate allows," not yet codified into a fixed regional Rule. Indicates transition out of this world: the controversy settling into an inherited, no-longer-live theological position (the process the later, anachronistically named "semi-Pelagian" label itself represents, imposed retrospectively by 16th-century controversialists — see §10); Lérins consolidating from a formation community into the "power elite" ecclesiastical-political network Mathisen's scholarship documents; the region's political frame shifting from late-Roman provincial administration toward the post-Roman, Frankish, royal-episcopal world Merovingian Gallic Christianity is built to hold.

## 3. Geographic Scope

**Core sites:** Ligugé and Marmoutier, both near Tours, in Roman Gaul's Loire valley; the island of Lérins (modern Île Saint-Honorat), off the Mediterranean coast opposite Cannes; and Marseilles (Massilia), the Mediterranean port city where Cassian founded St. Victor. These sites are geographically dispersed — Tours sits roughly 700 km from Marseilles by the routes available in this period — connected not by administrative unity but, per §2.3's honest finding, by shared regional culture, a common addressee class for exported teaching (Cassian's *Institutes*, dedicated broadly to Gallic monasteries), and this world's own internal generational depth rather than by any documented direct traffic between Tours and the south.

**A further site named in this world's own vendored sources, not yet weighed for inclusion:** Hadrumetum, in North Africa, is where Augustine's *On Rebuke and Grace* was addressed (this world's own corpus-map, gal-src-10) — outside this world's geographic core, but the treatise's *reception* by the Gallic monks at Marseilles (via Prosper and Hilary of Arles) is squarely inside it. This document treats Hadrumetum itself as outside scope while treating the controversy it provoked as core.

## 4. Cultural Scope

**Linguistic:** Latin throughout — unlike Egyptian desert monasticism's predominantly Coptic-speaking base reached mainly through Greek-literate outside observers (Desert Monasticism Doc_01 §4), every primary voice in this world (Sulpitius, Cassian, Vincent, and Augustine on the opponent's side) writes in Latin, addressed to a Latin-literate audience. Cassian's own project is explicitly one of *linguistic and cultural translation* — rendering Greek-mediated Egyptian teaching (he himself had spent years in Egypt and at Bethlehem, and knew Greek) into Latin for a Gallic audience, a mechanism this world's Author Gravity work (Doc_02) should treat as load-bearing: this world's own primary sources are already once-translated before this project translates them again.

**Social:** the case document's own §4 names this world's distinctive social function plainly — "Lérins formed bishops. It is not a withdrawal world; it is a seminary that looked like a monastery." This document's research corroborates the shape without yet independently verifying a specific roster: Hilary of Arles (Honoratus's own successor and biographer) and other Lérins-trained figures went on to hold sees across southern Gaul, a pattern the Mathisen scholarship (§8, §10) treats as central to the Lérins circle's later ecclesiastical-political weight. This is a research lead for Doc_02, not fully adjudicated here.

**Women's presence — a real Missing Voices concern, named rather than passed over (Constitution Article 20):** Cassian's foundation at Marseilles explicitly included a women's house alongside the men's [Widely Accepted — reported consistently, though this document has not yet located a named abbess or named individual sister for St. Victor's women's community]. No named woman ascetic comparable to the desert's *ammas* (Syncletica, Theodora, Sarah) has surfaced in this document's research for Tours, Lérins, or Marseilles specifically. This is a structural absence this document names now, per the Framework's affirmative duty, rather than deferring silently to Doc_02: whether it reflects genuine near-total absence of women's voice in the surviving record, or simply that this document's own research pass has not yet found what exists, is unresolved and flagged for Doc_02's own Missing Voices Assessment.

**Political:** late-Roman provincial Gaul, not yet the post-Roman Frankish world Merovingian Gallic Christianity holds. This world's monks were in direct, functioning correspondence with the highest ecclesiastical authority in the Latin West (Augustine, and — per Prosper and Hilary of Arles's 431 journey to Rome — Pope Celestine I), a political-ecclesiastical reach this document treats as evidence against reading this world as a peripheral or marginal one, notwithstanding its physical distance from Rome, Carthage, or Constantinople.

**Institutional:** authority ran through named abbots addressing named monks in writing (Cassian's *Institutes* and *Conferences* are addressed to specific named recipients — Castor of Apt for the *Institutes*; various named abbots and bishops for the *Conferences*), and through a bishop who was himself the community's founder and head (Martin at Marmoutier). This is a text-mediated, named-correspondent authority structure, closer in shape to Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity's letter-and-treatise culture than to Desert Monasticism's largely oral, terse-saying transmission — a comparison Doc_02 should develop rather than this document asserting further.

## 5. World Separation Criteria

**Has a new gravity emerged marking entry into this world, distinct from the Nicene-episcopate world it grows out of?** Yes, on the reasoning in §2.1–2.2: Martin's founding of Ligugé under Hilary's patronage (c. 360) marks a formation practice — ascetic withdrawal organized as a replicable community, rather than a bishop's individual ascetic household — that is not itself part of what made Hilary's own world what it is (a creedal controversialist resisting imperial pressure). The genealogical debt is real and named (§2.2); the gravity is new.

**Has a new gravity emerged marking exit from this world by c. 450?** On the reasoning in §2.4: yes, provisionally — the shift from a live, still-forming formation ecology with directly-participating named voices to a settled inherited theological position (the eventual "semi-Pelagian" retrojection) and an ecclesiastical-political "power elite" network (Mathisen) reads as a genuine change in kind, not merely continued activity by the same kind of community. This document holds the c. 450 closing boundary with markedly less confidence than the c. 360 opening one, for the same reason Desert Monasticism's own Doc_01 flagged its own closing marker as directional rather than settled — and additionally flags the undocumented thirty-year gap to Merovingian Gallic Christianity (§2.4) as the single largest open item this document carries forward.

## 6. Strand Determination (Constitution Article 21)

**Finding: this document does not resolve strand-singular vs. multi-strand status here, and states plainly why.**

The Framework's own Strand Determination test asks whether *within* a confirmed single world there are meaningfully distinct internal patterns of formation emphasis, practice, authority structure, or ecological orientation. What this world actually presents, per §2.3's honest finding, is a harder and logically prior question: **whether Martin's Tours and the Lérins–Marseilles circle are one world with (at most) internal strand variation, or whether they are two worlds in a lineage** — a World Separation Criteria question (§5), not a Strand Determination question, and one the case document explicitly reserves for Doc_04's real six-test assessment (Repetition, Dependency, Formation, Explanatory, Persistence, Interaction) rather than an assumed answer either way.

**What this document can say now, to give Doc_04 real material rather than a bare instruction to go find out:**

- **For "one world, two strands":** both nodes share a region, a language, a common relationship of debtor-adaptation to Egyptian monastic teaching (Cassian explicitly, Martin's own formation less directly attested on this specific point), and — per the case document's own count — both are counted inside the same 366,371-word figure that first surfaced this world as a gap in the census.
- **For "a lineage of two":** no documented direct link between the two nodes was found (§2.3); the generational gap is substantial (Martin d. 397; Vincent still writing in 434); and the two nodes differ in one respect this document considers real rather than superficial — Martin's Tours is built around a single, monumentalized founder-bishop and his hagiographer, while Lérins–Marseilles is a multi-voiced circle (Honoratus, Cassian, Vincent, and later Hilary of Arles) with no single dominant founder-figure of Martin's stature.
- **Cross-strand (or cross-world) candidate gravities worth testing at Doc_04:** ascetic withdrawal that produces or supports bishops rather than removing its practitioners permanently from institutional life; a self-conscious relationship of transmission-and-adaptation from Egyptian monastic authority; and — testable across both nodes because both are attested in Sulpitius's own corpus (Dialogues, carrying both the Martin material and Postumianus's Egyptian travel account) — a shared value placed on direct, named eyewitness or near-eyewitness testimony as the guarantee of a text's authority, a pattern Vincent's own *Commonitory* criterion ("quod ubique, quod semper, quod ab omnibus") arguably generalizes into an explicit rule.

**If Doc_04 finds these do not hold across both nodes, or finds the absence of a documented link decisive, this document's own framing above should be read as the record of what was tested and why it was inconclusive at Doc_01 — not as an argument this document is trying to win in advance.**

## 7. Preliminary Forces Identification

**What generated this world:** the felt inadequacy, for a subset of committed Latin-West Christians, of ordinary institutional Christian life as a sufficient arena for radical formation — the same generative pressure named in Desert Monasticism's own Doc_01 §7 — combined here with a specifically Gallic-Roman political and ecclesiastical setting in which formation could be pursued without severing a monk from institutional authority or even from eventual episcopal office. Where Egyptian withdrawal moved bodies into literal desert wilderness, this world's withdrawal (an island; a cross-river retreat from a city) is geographically modest and does not remove its practitioners from ordinary ecclesiastical reach — a difference this document considers structurally significant, not merely a matter of degree.

**What larger world it was embedded in:** late Roman, increasingly institutionally Christian Gaul, itself embedded in a Latin West whose most authoritative living theological voice (Augustine of Hippo) sat in North Africa, and whose highest ecclesiastical authority (the bishop of Rome) this world's own monks travelled to consult directly (§4). This world was not isolated from the wider Latin church's own live controversies; it was an active, corresponding party to the largest one of its generation.

**What it was responding to:** the practical and pastoral problem of how to teach grace, human effort, and perseverance to monks actually living the ascetic life, without either denying the real moral weight of ascetic striving (the risk this world's own monks perceived in Augustine's later formulations) or reopening Pelagius's own condemned position (which this world's own participants, per Prosper's testimony, did not want to be mistaken for). Egyptian teaching, carried west by Cassian, supplied a vocabulary — synergy between divine grace and human will — this world drew on directly in framing its own answer (Conference XIII).

**What it was refusing:** neither Pelagius's position (already condemned) nor, straightforwardly, Augustine's own authority — this world's own primary text on the question, Cassian's Conference XIII, argues from Scripture and from the Egyptian abbas' own teaching, not from a rejection of Augustine as such, and this world's monks pursued Augustine's own engagement rather than simply ignoring him. What it refused, more precisely, was the specific implication its own monks read out of *On Rebuke and Grace* — that human effort and moral responsibility become close to meaningless under an strong doctrine of unconditional predestination. **This document deliberately avoids the retrospective label "semi-Pelagian"** for this refusal: the term is a 16th-century Protestant polemical coinage (Theodore Beza, c. 1556), applied backward to this world's own participants who could not have recognized it, and modern scholarship increasingly treats its retrospective application as anachronistic and, per at least one specialist assessment, "inappropriate, ambiguous and unjust." **This world's own name for itself, where the sources supply one, is "Massilian"** (from Massilia, Marseilles) — the term this document and all subsequent documents in this build should prefer, reserving "semi-Pelagian" only where explicitly marking it as a later, external label being discussed as such.

This preliminary work frames, but does not substitute for, the complete forces analysis compiled at Doc_08 (Framework Step 8).

## 8. World Continuity & Distinction

### 8.1 vs. `gallic-nicene-episcopate` (Atlas I.37, Hilary of Poitiers' world, unbuilt)

Per §2.2 above: the two worlds touch at exactly one documented point — Martin's formation as Hilary's disciple and Hilary's patronage of Martin's first foundation at Ligugé. This document treats that point as a genuine inheritance (this world owes its earliest institutional protection to Hilary's own episcopal authority) without collapsing the two worlds into one. They differ in kind, not merely in period: Hilary's world is a creedal-episcopal one, organized around resisting an imperial Arian/Homoian settlement through councils and exile; this world is a monastic-ascetic one, organized around formation practice and, a generation later, a live argument over grace rather than Christology. The census entry's own `relationsSummary` states this same judgment independently, which this document treats as corroboration rather than as this document's own invention.

### 8.2 vs. Egyptian desert monasticism (`desert-monasticism`, built and live)

This world is a *reception* culture for desert teaching, not an alternative or rival origin. Cassian's own *Institutes* and *Conferences* are simultaneously desert-monasticism's own primary-source Latin transmission (that world's srcDES026, load-bearing for four of its own documents) and this world's founding literature — the same texts, doing genuinely different work for each world, exactly the pattern the corpus-map's own re-pointing notes already describe (this world's G1 manifest, Part C). This document does not need to re-argue desert monasticism's own Doc_01; it names the relationship as debtor-to-source and moves on. What is distinctive to this world, and not shared with desert monasticism, is precisely what desert monasticism's own Doc_01 (§8.1) already names as absent from *its* comparison to Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity: a live, two-way written theological argument with the wider Latin church's most authoritative living voice. Desert monasticism, per its own Doc_01, developed largely informally and produced terse oral sayings; this world produced treatises addressed to, and answered by, Augustine of Hippo by name.

### 8.3 vs. `early-benedictine-italian-monasticism` (Atlas era 3, Pre-Survey Candidate, unbuilt)

The census's own `why` field for this future entry names it as "the parent of the whole Latin monastic line — what Cassian carried west from the desert takes durable institutional form here, in a Rule." This document names the relationship plainly and leaves it there: this world is upstream of that one. Cassian's own *Institutes* is "commended by the Rule of Benedict ch. 73" (per this world's own corpus-map note) — a documented line of transmission, not an inference. Whether or how directly Lérins-trained figures fed into that later institutional consolidation is a research lead for Doc_02, not resolved here.

### 8.4 vs. `priscillianist-asceticism` (Atlas era 2, Possible Future World, unbuilt) and `pelagianism` (Atlas era 2, Possible Future World, unbuilt)

Both are adjacent Gallic/Latin-West era-2 ascetic-or-doctrinal entries this world could be mistaken for. Priscillianist asceticism is geographically distinct (Spain, with Gallic entanglement via the Trier trial Sulpitius's own *Sacred History* documents — this world's gal-src-08) and theologically distinct (a controversy over the propriety of a specific ascetic movement's own orthodoxy, ending in the first state execution for heresy, rather than a controversy over the mechanics of grace). Pelagianism is the doctrine this world's own participants explicitly did not want to be mistaken for holding (§7) — the census's own entry for `pelagianism` notes it "clears the floor but its surviving ecology is too thin for a lived tradition," which this document reads as corroborating why this world, and not a Pelagianism world, is where the Massilian side of the controversy actually belongs: this world has the lived ecology pelagianism itself lacks.

### 8.5 vs. `merovingian-gallic-christianity` (Atlas era 3, Pre-Survey Candidate, unbuilt)

Addressed at §2.4 and in the case document's own §5: post-Roman, Frankish, royal-episcopal, and ordinary-parish in character (per that entry's own census `why` field, "the era's only ordinary-parish window") against this world's late-Roman, provincial, and monastic-elite character. The case document's own rejection of "just widen the Merovingian entry back to c. 360" is affirmed here on independent grounds: that move would require pretending the documented thirty-year gap (§2.4) does not exist, and would blur a genuine transition in political frame (imperial-provincial to Frankish-royal) that a single entry cannot hold without flattening it.

## 10. Sourcing and confidence notes carried forward to Doc_02 and Doc_04

- **Sulpitius Severus, *Life of Martin*, *Dialogues*, *Sacred History*, and Letters** (c. 397 onward). The founding hagiographic and near-contemporary narrative source for Martin. **Clare Stancliffe, *St. Martin and His Hagiographer: History and Miracle in Sulpicius Severus*** (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 1983) is the standard critical study and should anchor Doc_02's treatment of the *Life*'s reliability — Stancliffe places Sulpicius's work in both its literary and its ecclesiastical-transformation context and treats Martin as having made "a powerful impact on his hagiographer" without treating the *Life* as neutral biography. [This document has verified the work's existence, general critical stance, and publication details; has not yet read it directly — a research lead for Doc_02, not a finding asserted here.]
- **John Cassian, *Institutes*, *Conferences* I–III, *De Incarnatione*.** **Conrad Leyser, *Authority and Asceticism from Augustine to Gregory the Great*** (Oxford: Clarendon Press, 2000) devotes sustained attention to Cassian's own "language of moral authority" and his close association with the Lérins circle, including his standing with Pope Celestine I. **Owen Chadwick's *John Cassian*** is the older standard monograph on Cassian specifically and should be checked directly at Doc_02 (this document has verified its existence and general reputation but has not independently confirmed edition/date details beyond the widely-cited original 1950 publication with a revised 1968 second edition — flagged for Doc_02 verification rather than asserted here at a confidence this document cannot yet support).
- **Vincent of Lérins, *Commonitory* (434).** Dated with unusual internal precision (§2.3). The traditional claim that Vincent was the brother of Lupus of Troyes is **Contested — carried at that confidence level, not higher**: it circulates widely in hagiographical and popular sources but is not corroborated by Gennadius's contemporary *De Viris Illustribus*, per this document's own research. Doc_02 should not upgrade this claim without independent verification this document has not found.
- **Ralph W. Mathisen, *Ecclesiastical Factionalism and Religious Controversy in Fifth-Century Gaul*** (Washington, DC: Catholic University of America Press, 1989). The standard study of the Lérins circle's ecclesiastical-political dimension — argues the fifth-century Gallic church was factionalized rather than unified, that the "Lérins faction" built a real power elite whose influence peaked around 450, and that charges of Pelagianism were frequently deployed as factional weapons rather than as neutral theological description. **Load-bearing for §5's closing-boundary argument and for Doc_08's Forces work** — this document has verified the work's existence, general thesis, and publication details via secondary description, not yet read it directly.

- **Confidence note carried to Doc_02 and Doc_04, in the same register as Desert Monasticism's own standing note:** this world's evidentiary base is genuinely strong relative to most of the Atlas's era-2 entries (case document §6) — near-contemporary, insider-authored, and multi-voiced rather than dependent on a single surviving witness.
