## 1. Primary Voices

### 1.1 Sulpitius Severus (dates contested — see below)

**What survives in this world's library:** *On the Life of St. Martin*, the *Letters* (three, undisputed), the *Dialogues* (three), the *Doubtful Letters* (seven — corrected below to at least eight known, see §6), and the *Sacred History*.

**Who he was — now on two independent ancient authorities, not one, and they disagree on his dates.** `npnf211`'s own introduction gives "born ... about a.d. 363 ... died, **as is generally supposed**, in a.d. 420." Gennadius's own chapter (`npnf203`, ch. XIX, read directly this revision) gives **"born after 353, died about 410."** Neither source is this world's own primary voice describing himself; both are near-contemporary or later biographical notices, and they disagree by roughly a decade at both ends. **This document does not pick one** — the dates are carried as a genuine, disclosed discrepancy, not resolved silently.

**Independent corroboration, now from two ancient sources rather than one, for the core biographical facts:** Jerome (*Comm. Ezech.* 36, "our friend Severus"); Augustine (*Ep.* 205, "a man excelling in learning and wisdom"); and now Gennadius directly (not only via `npnf211`'s own quotation of him): "a man distinguished by his birth, by his excellent literary work, by his devotion to poverty and by his humility, beloved also of the sainted men Martin bishop of Tours and Paulinus Nolanus." **[Corrected confidence, Round 1 finding S5: Widely Accepted, not Documented, for the composite biographical picture.]** Only Gennadius, of the four ancient references now in hand (Jerome, Augustine, and two separate readings of Gennadius — `npnf211`'s quotation and this revision's own direct read of `npnf203`), actually attests the Martin relationship and any biographical particular beyond acquaintance or reputation. Jerome and Augustine attest only that Sulpitius was known to them and well regarded. **Article 17's *Documented* is not earned by this evidence for anything beyond "a real, respected contemporary of Jerome, Augustine, and Gennadius who knew Martin"** — the specific facts (birth date, family, oratory training, the bar, his wife's death, 392, ordination) rest on the `npnf211` editor's own account, itself hedged ("as is generally supposed").

**A genuine, disclosed tension, now sharpened rather than merely gestured at (Round 1 finding S7 — the prior Contested rating was unearned; this one is):** `npnf211`'s editor reports, as a rumor he rebuts, that "some ancient writers" charged Sulpitius with lapsing toward Pelagian opinion. **Gennadius states it as settled fact, not rumor, in his own voice**: *"In his old age, he was led astray by the Pelagians, and recognizing the guilt of much speaking, kept silent until his death, in order that by penitent silence he might atone for the sin which he had contracted by speaking."* This is a near-contemporary (Gennadius wrote c. 495, within a century) direct claim, not a report of what others said. **This now genuinely meets Article 17's *Contested* bar** — two ancient sources in actual disagreement, one flatly asserting the charge, one reporting and rejecting it — where the prior draft's rating rested on an editor's own unaided rebuttal of a claim from unnamed "ancient writers." This document does not adjudicate it (Stancliffe, Registry row 17, is the right instrument and has not been read), but it is flagged for Doc_04's gravity work with real weight behind it now, not an unearned label.

**Author Gravity Assessment (five dimensions):**

- **Visibility:** Very high for Martin specifically, and now independently corroborated: Gennadius's own chapter separately confirms Sulpitius "wrote also, to the profit of many, a Life of the holy Martin" and describes the *Dialogues* as "a Conference between Postumianus and Gallus" — Gennadius's own name for the frame narrator, not found under that name in `npnf211`'s own text (a discrepancy worth noting, not resolved: whether "Gallus" was ever the narrator's actual name, or is Gennadius's own loose paraphrase, is not determinable from what this document has read).
- **Representativeness:** Low, deliberately — unchanged from the prior draft's finding, which holds up under Gennadius's independent account.
- **Influence:** Very high and structurally asymmetric — this world's entire knowledge of Martin's Tours still runs through essentially one author (Gennadius corroborates rather than supplements — he read Sulpitius's own works, he was not himself present).
- **Limitations:** unchanged — Sulpitius's own stated hagiographic purpose (Doc_01 §10 and §1.1 above).
- **Transmission History** — corrected to actually answer the Framework's question, not a restatement of composition dating (Round 1 finding S9): this world reads Sulpitius entirely through **Alexander Roberts's** translation in the 1894 NPNF Series II vol. 11 (translator credited on the volume's own title page), itself made from a specific underlying critical text this document has not identified by editor or edition. No independent modern check on Roberts's translation is vendored in this project's library. Gennadius's own summary (§1.1 above, `npnf203`, Ernest Cushing Richardson's translation, a different translator in a different volume) functions as the one available cross-check this world's library actually holds, and it already surfaced one live disagreement (the dates) — a concrete demonstration of why Transmission History matters here, not an abstract requirement.

**Confidence:** the composite biographical picture — **Widely Accepted**, corrected down from the prior draft's unearned Documented (S5); the Pelagianism charge — **Contested**, now genuinely earned (S7); the two date ranges — carried as an open discrepancy, not resolved.

### 1.2 John Cassian (dates contested — see below)

**What survives:** the *Institutes*, the *Conferences* (twenty-four, in three dedicated parts), and *De Incarnatione*.

**Now independently corroborated by a second ancient source, read directly this revision:** Gennadius (`npnf203` ch. LXII) confirms Cassian was "ordained deacon by bishop John the Great, at Constantinople, and a presbyter at Marseilles," founded "two monasteries, that is to say one for men and one for women, **which are still standing**" (an independent witness to Saint-Victor and Saint-Sauveur, corroborating rather than merely repeating Doc_01 §2.3's finding), lists all twenty-four Conferences by subject in Gennadius's own order, and states directly that Cassian wrote *De Incarnatione* "at the request of Leo the archdeacon, afterwards bishop of Rome" — independently confirming Doc_01 §10's Leyser-sourced Leo detail from a source outside Leyser entirely. Gennadius also gives a specific death date, **"died 450,"** not previously in hand, and states Cassian "made an end, both of writing and living, at Marseilles" — Cassian died where he had founded his houses, a fact worth carrying to Doc_08's forces work on this world's own geographic center of gravity.

**Author Gravity Assessment:**

- **Visibility, Representativeness, Influence, Limitations:** unchanged in substance from the prior draft, now with Gennadius as independent corroboration for the institutional facts rather than sole-source assertion.
- **Transmission History** — corrected to answer the actual question (Round 1 finding S9): translated by **Edgar C. S. Gibson** (credited on the volume's title page). **This world's own vendored text is missing two of Cassian's twenty-four Conferences in this translation** — Conference XII ("On Chastity," marked "Not translated") and Conference XXII ("On Nocturnal Illusions," "This Conference is omitted") — a Victorian editorial excision of specifically sexuality-and-body material, verified directly in the file (Registry row 10). This is transmission history in the Framework's actual sense: an identifiable intermediary, with an identifiable agenda, shaping what of Cassian's own corpus this world can draw on at all. Institutes I, chapter X (**corrected from the prior draft's "I.11," Round 1 finding S21**) carries its own such note: an editorial footnote records that "this and the following chapter are altogether omitted in the edition of Gazæus" — a second, independent instance of the same phenomenon (a different editor, a different omission, the same pattern of selective transmission) inside the very same book.

**Confidence, corrected (Round 1 finding S6):** authorship and the Institutes' addressee — **Widely Accepted**. **The Conferences' three-part chronology is downgraded from the prior draft's "Widely Accepted to Documented" to Widely Accepted at most, with the Conference XIII/Augustine relative-dating question carried at full Contested strength (Chadwick vs. Casiday, per Doc_01 §7) — the prior rating and this document's own §7 below were reporting two different confidence levels for the same disputed chronology inside one document, and Article 17 requires naming a live tension, not scoring it twice.**

### 1.3 Vincent of Lérins (fl. 425–434, d. before 450)

**What survives:** the *Commonitory*, 33 chapters plus three appendices — corrected structural note (Round 1 finding C3): chs. 8–9 expound Gal. 1:8; chs. 21–24 separately expound 1 Tim. 6:20 — two distinct Pauline expositions in two distinct locations, not "a close exposition of two Pauline texts" read as one continuous block.

**Now independently corroborated and extended by Gennadius (`npnf203` ch. LXV), read directly this revision:** Vincent was ordained **presbyter specifically in 434** — the same year as the *Commonitory* itself, a precise new datum. Gennadius independently confirms the *Peregrinus* pseudonym and adds a genuine Transmission History fact the prior draft did not have: **"the greater part of the second book of this work having been stolen, he composed a brief reproduction of the substance of the original work, and published in one [book]."** The *Commonitory* as this world possesses it is, by its own ancient biographer's account, a reconstruction of a lost original for at least part of its length — a transmission fact, not a composition fact, and exactly the kind of thing the Framework's Transmission History dimension asks for.

**Author Gravity Assessment:**

- **Visibility, Representativeness, Influence:** unchanged in substance.
- **Limitations — corrected, Round 1 finding S28.** The prior draft stated Vincent's silence on Augustine as "directly verified this session," "a documented fact, not speculation," and "textually observable." **What was verified is that the volume's editor asserts it**; Registry row 12 itself records that the *Commonitory*'s own chapters, beyond the Introduction, were not read this session. For not only does he omit the name of St. Augustine ... but he denounces his doctrine..."* — offered as one leg of a probability argument, not a free-standing established fact. **This document restates the finding at the strength the evidence actually supports:** the editor argues, from Vincent's own omission pattern, that his sympathies likely lay with the Massilian side — a real and interesting reading, resting on the editor's own analysis of the text, not yet on this document's own direct verification of the omission pattern across all 33 chapters.

Heurtley** (credited on the volume's title page); and, per Gennadius above, the work's own second book is itself a reconstruction of stolen material — a transmission event inside the ancient tradition, prior to and independent of the modern translation layer.

**Confidence:** the 434 date, **now doubly anchored** (internal Ephesus dating and Gennadius's independent presbyter-ordination date, the same year) — **Documented**, the one label in this document fully earned on convergent independent evidence. ### 1.4 Augustine of Hippo, as opponent-side witness (three treatises)

**Two claims withdrawn from this document's prior draft, in full, per Round 1 findings S1 and S2 — stated here as corrections, not silently replaced.**

**Withdrawn: the "Massilian is Prosper's own coinage" finding (S1).** The prior draft quoted `npnf105`'s own footnote with its first sentence elided and read the remainder as confirming that "Massilian" was Prosper's word. **The footnote, read in full, says the opposite of what was claimed**: *"The party which Augustin is here opposing had its chief centre in Marseilles, and hence is called 'Massilians.' Prosper in his letter called them* reliquiæ Pelagianorum*... They are now most commonly called 'Semi-Pelagians.'"* The footnote gives an etymological derivation (from the city, no author named) and then states that **Prosper's own actual label was different** — *reliquiæ Pelagianorum*, "the remnants of the Pelagians," not "Massilian." This document does not carry the withdrawn finding forward, and does not treat Doc_01 §7's own independently-argued position (that "Massilian" is on other historical grounds a period-contemporary, Prosper-associated label, per Doc_01's own separate research) as corroborated by this passage — it is not. **What this world's library actually shows, and what is worth carrying to Doc_03/Doc_06 instead, is that three distinct labels compete for the same group in the sources this project holds**: "Massilians" (geographic, unattributed derivation), *reliquiæ Pelagianorum* (Prosper's own words, per this footnote), and "Semi-Pelagians" (the retrospective label, per Doc_01 §7's Beza/Sanders research) — a genuinely richer lexicon finding than the single-label claim it replaces.

**Withdrawn: the "three-way Hilary tangle" (S2).** The prior draft manufactured a third candidate ("Hilary of Norbonne") from a passage that concerns a different letter (*Epistle* 178, dated **417**, eleven years before the Prosper/Hilary correspondence) and presented the volume's actual proposal as one option among three rather than as its own considered position. **What `npnf105` actually contains, read again directly this revision:** the endnote attached to "two laymen" reads *"Compare Epistles 225, 1, and 156. It is, of course, not certain that this is the same Hilary that wrote to Augustin from Sicily, but it seems probable."* The volume's own **Index of Subjects** (checked directly this revision) lists exactly three men named Hilary — "Hilary the commentator," "Hilary of Poitiers," and **"Hilary of Sicily,"** the last indexed to the precise pages where "my dearest sons, Prosper and Hilary" appears — with no index entry anywhere for "Hilary of Arles" or "Hilary of Norbonne." **This world's second vendored volume makes exactly one identification of the 428 correspondent: Hilary of Sicily.** That is a real, named, third position — genuinely competing with both Doc_01 §10's preferred Gallic-lay-monk reading and the "Hilary of Arles" reading Doc_01 §10 rejected — not a corroboration of either. This document carries it as a **rival candidate**, and the corpus-map's own stale "Hilary of Arles" references (found still present despite G1's claim of correction) have been fixed at the root as a direct consequence of catching this error (see the staging-file correction note, 2026-09-09).

A weaker point in the same subsection, also corrected: "two laymen" excludes a *bishop*, but Hilary of Arles was still a monk, not yet bishop, in 428 (he succeeded Honoratus at Arles in **429**, per Gennadius ch. LXX's own date — one year earlier than Doc_01 had it). An unordained monk is technically a layman. "Two laymen" therefore does not, by itself, rule out Hilary of Arles as cleanly as the prior draft implied; the stronger argument against him remains what it always was — `npnf211`'s own Commonitory introduction places him among the Massilian party being reported on, not among the reporters.

**A live edition-level gap, found only this revision (Round 1 finding S20, PRESS-2):** `cic/texts/npnf101_augustine-confessions-letters.xml`, this project's own Augustine letters volume, **omits Letters 221 through 226 entirely** — its sequence runs directly from Letter CCXX to Letter CCXXVII, verified directly in the file. Letters 225 and 226 are, per `npnf105`'s own repeated citation ("Compare *Epistles* 225, 1, and 156"), Prosper's and Hilary's own letters to Augustine — the actual reports, in the reporters' own words, that provoked the two treatises this world's library holds Augustine's replies to. **This is an edition-level absence of exactly the same shape as the Conference XII/XXII excision** (Registry row 10), now given its own row (Registry row 30). Consequently, **this document withdraws the prior draft's claim that Augustine's own summary is "the fullest surviving statement of what they held"** (Round 1 finding S20): it is the fullest statement *in this project's current library*, not the fullest surviving statement — the actual fuller reports exist (per `npnf105`'s own citations to their content) and are simply not vendored, an important and different fact.

**What holds up, verified again directly this revision:** Augustine's own summary of the disputed position, in *De praedestinatione sanctorum* ch. 2 — accepting original sin, the necessity of grace, and that no one suffices to begin or complete a good work unaided, while disputing predestination specifically — remains an accurate, faithful paraphrase of what Augustine's own text says, correctly framed as Augustine's characterization rather than the Massilians' own words.

**Author Gravity Assessment (brief, unchanged in scope from the prior draft's declared limitation):** Augustine writes as the most authoritative living theologian in the Latin West, replying to a report about Gaul. Whether the report itself (Letters 225/226) will ever be vendored, or whether the Massilian side's own direct reply exists at all beyond Faustus's later *De gratia* (Registry row 22, Contra Collatorem now also identified via Gennadius, Registry row 31), remains this world's central sourcing asymmetry.

## 6. Ordinary Participant Evidence

**Corrected in three respects (Round 1 finding S19).** The prior draft offered Claudia, Sulpitius's sister, as "the one concrete trace" of ordinary, non-elite, non-monastic participation, on the strength of one letter. Checked again directly this revision:

1. **There are at least two letters to Claudia, not one** — Letter I ("Concerning the Last Judgment") and **Letter II, "Concerning Virginity"** (`npnf211`, Doubtful Letters), both read directly. Gennadius (ch. XIX) independently states Sulpitius "wrote to his sister **many** Letters."
2. **Claudia is not evidence of non-elite participation.** Sulpitius himself "belonged to an illustrious family" (`npnf211` line 366); his sister is a member of the same family. She is this world's own literate provincial aristocracy, not a counterexample to it.
3. **"Non-monastic" is not supported and is likely wrong.** Letter II, read directly this revision, opens with a treatise-length meditation on the special sanctity of consecrated virginity — "those who are virgins possess something above the rest, since ... they are chosen by the Holy Spirit, and are presented by the bishop ... at the altar of God, as if being more holy and pure sacrifices." A letter in this specific genre, addressed to a named woman, is the standard form for correspondence with a woman already under, or being urged toward, a vow of virginity — not neutral family correspondence to a laywoman outside any ascetic commitment.

**Corrected finding:** Claudia is real, textually present, and worth keeping in view — but as a second data point on this world's own elite, and very possibly ascetically-committed, women (alongside the Saint-Sauveur community, Doc_01 §4), not as this library's one thread to ordinary, non-elite, non-monastic life. **That thread remains genuinely absent**, and §10 below restates the Missing Voices finding accordingly, without Claudia doing work she cannot do.
