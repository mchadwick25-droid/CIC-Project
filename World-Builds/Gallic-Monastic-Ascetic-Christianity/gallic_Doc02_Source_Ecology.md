## 1. Primary Voices

### 1.1 Sulpitius Severus (c. 363 – c. 420)

**What survives in this world's library:** *On the Life of St. Martin* (the *Vita Martini*), the *Letters* (three, undisputed), the *Dialogues* (three), the *Doubtful Letters* (seven, disputed), and the *Sacred History* (*Chronica*, two books) — the whole of Sulpitius's surviving corpus, per this world's own vendored volume's editorial ordering.

**Who he was, per the volume's own introduction (read directly):** born in Aquitania c. 363, of an illustrious family, trained in oratory, prominent at the bar; abandoned secular life c. 392 after his young wife's death; became a Presbyter and "attached himself to St. Martin of Tours, for whom he ever afterwards cherished the profoundest admiration and affection." Jerome calls him "our friend Severus" (*Commentary on Ezekiel* 36); Augustine, in his own *Epistle* 205, calls him "a man excelling in learning and wisdom" — two independent contemporary attestations of his standing, not merely the volume's own editorial praise. Gennadius's *De Viris Illustribus* (c. 495) independently confirms the same picture: "distinguished both for his family and learning... a great friend of some holy men, such as Martin, bishop of Tours, and Paulinus, bishop of Nola." **[Documented — three independent ancient sources: Jerome, Augustine, Gennadius, none of them Sulpitius's own testimony about himself.]**

**A genuine, disclosed tension worth carrying forward rather than smoothing over:** the volume's own introduction records that "it is stated by some ancient writers that Sulpitius ultimately incurred the charge of heresy, having, to some extent, embraced Pelagian opinions," though the editor argues against the charge, citing Sulpitius's own repeated self-presentation as a strict upholder of "catholic" doctrine. **[Contested — the charge is ancient but the editor's rebuttal is also substantive; this document does not adjudicate it, and flags it as directly relevant to Doc_04's gravity work on the Massilian question, since a real ancient tradition already associates Martin's own circle, however loosely, with exactly the theological current this world's southern node argues over.]**

**Author Gravity Assessment (five dimensions):**

- **Visibility:** Very high for Martin specifically — Sulpitius is Martin's near-exclusive biographer, writing within Martin's own lifetime (the *Vita*'s preface addresses Desiderius while its subject "still lived," per Doc_01 §10) and continuing to write about him for decades after (the *Dialogues*, later; the *Letters*, spanning Martin's death). Archdeacon Farrar's estimate, quoted in the volume's own introduction, that Martin's fame reached "from Armenia to Egypt more widely than that of any other monk or bishop of his day" is itself an estimate of Sulpitius's own literary effect, not an independent fact about Martin.
- **Representativeness:** Low, deliberately. Sulpitius writes as an admiring disciple constructing a case for Martin's sanctity, not as a neutral chronicler of Gallic monasticism broadly. He represents Martin's own circle's self-understanding, not the wider ascetic movement's.
- **Influence:** Very high and structurally asymmetric — this world's entire knowledge of Martin's Tours runs through one author. There is no second independent witness to Martin's own career in this library.
- **Limitations, per Sulpitius's own stated purpose (read directly in the *Vita*'s Chapter I):** he states explicitly he writes to produce a moral exemplar ("a most holy man, which shall serve in future as an example to others"), not a historical record for its own sake, and explicitly disclaims literary polish, asking readers to "attend to the facts related, rather than the language in which they are set forth." This is a stated hagiographic purpose, not an incidental genre drift — Doc_09's tier discipline should treat this as load-bearing.
- **Transmission History:** The *Vita*'s preface itself records an anxiety about publication — Sulpitius asks his addressee Desiderius to keep the work private, even suppress his own name, and predicts (correctly, per the volume's later fame) that it will spread beyond his control regardless. The work's transmission was, by its own author's account, never fully in his hands.

**Confidence:** the fact of Sulpitius's authorship, dates, and relationship to Martin — **Widely Accepted** (single-author-tradition, per Doc_01 §2.2's own S13a correction); the *content* of his portrait of Martin — genre-bound, treated at the tier discipline Doc_09 will apply, not asserted as documentary fact here.

### 1.2 John Cassian (c. 360 – c. 435)

**What survives:** the *Institutes* (twelve books), the *Conferences* (twenty-four, in three dedicated parts — I–X to Leontius, XI–XVII to Honoratus and Eucherius, XVIII–XXIV to Jovinian, Minervius, and others), and *De Incarnatione* (seven books, against Nestorius).

**Author Gravity Assessment:**

- **Visibility:** Extremely high, and doubly so — Cassian is both desert-monasticism's primary Latin transmission voice (per that world's own srcDES026 assignment) and this world's own founding literary voice. His visibility is not organic to this world alone; it is inherited from, and shared with, a different world's own evidentiary base.
- **Representativeness:** Deliberately partial by his own stated design (§1.3 below revisits this at the Institutes Preface). He writes as an outsider-turned-Gallic-resident transmitting Egyptian practice, explicitly adapting rather than reporting neutrally — "where I find anything in the rule of the Egyptians which... is impossible in these countries... I shall to some extent balance it by the customs of the monasteries which are found throughout Pontus and Mesopotamia" (Institutes Preface, quoted in full at Doc_01 §2.3). He represents his own synthesis, not a transparent record of either Egyptian or Gallic practice alone.
- **Influence:** Immense and multi-generational — commended by name in Rule of Benedict ch. 42 (daily reading) and ch. 73 (per Doc_01 §8.3's C10 correction), making him a documented hinge figure for the whole later Western monastic line, not only for this world.
- **Limitations:** Cassian's own Conferences are themselves already a literary construction — presented as transcribed conversations with named Egyptian abbas, but composed years after the fact, in Latin, for a Gallic audience, by a participant reconstructing his own memory and framing it pedagogically. The genre convention (the "conference" as a teaching dialogue) shapes what can be shown as much as what was actually said.
- **Transmission History:** Documented and dated with unusual precision from within this world's own vendored volume itself — Part I (Conferences I–X) completed shortly after Castor's death in 426; Part II (XI–XVII) published not later than 426, dedicated to Honoratus while still "frater," not yet "Episcopus" (Doc_01 §2.3); Part III (XVIII–XXIV) later still. This is a rare case of a work's own publication history being independently reconstructable from internal textual evidence (the *frater*/*Episcopus* address) rather than asserted from outside.

**Confidence:** authorship, chronology of the three Conferences parts, and the Institutes' addressee — **Widely Accepted to Documented** (the *frater*/*Episcopus* internal-evidence dating is about as close to Documented as ancient chronology gets); the content and accuracy of what Cassian reports the Egyptian abbas as having said — genre-bound, not independently verifiable, and this document does not claim otherwise.

### 1.3 Vincent of Lérins (fl. 425–434, d. before 450)

**What survives:** the *Commonitory* (*Commonitorium*), one work, 33 chapters plus three appendices — a substantial doctrinal-methodological treatise, not a short pamphlet, ranging in subject from a general rule for distinguishing catholic truth from heresy (chs. 1–10) through historical case studies (Donatists, Arians, Nestorius, Photinus, Apollinaris, Origen, Tertullian: chs. 4, 11–19) to a close exposition of two Pauline texts and the proper interpretation of doctrinal development (chs. 20–29) and a closing treatment of the Council of Ephesus (chs. 30–33) — read in full table-of-contents form directly from this world's own vendored volume.

**Author Gravity Assessment:**

- **Visibility:** Moderate but sharply concentrated — Vincent survives through exactly one text, written under a pseudonym ("Peregrinus"), whose authorship is itself a later attribution (Gennadius, c. 495, "some sixty years after its date").
- **Representativeness:** High for the southern node's own self-understanding — the Commonitory is a genuine insider document of the Lérins circle (Doc_01 §1), not an outside report about it.
- **Influence:** Durable and specific — the "quod ubique, quod semper, quod ab omnibus" formula (the Vincentian Canon) became a standing reference point in later Western ecclesiology, independent of this world's own window.
- **Limitations, directly verified this session:** Vincent's own rhetorical strategy is itself a documented fact, not speculation — this world's own vendored introduction states plainly that he "omits the name of St. Augustine... when making honorable mention at any time of the champions of the faith," while denouncing Augustine's doctrine "under a misrepresentation of it" as one of the novel errors the treatise opposes, without naming him. This is a textually observable silence-as-argument, the strongest kind of "against the grain" evidence the Framework's Missing Voices discipline asks for (Framework Part I §Affirmative Duty) — though here it evidences a doctrinal position, not a marginalized voice.
- **Transmission History:** Internally dateable with unusual precision (434, three years after Ephesus 431 — Doc_01 §2.3, **Documented**). The pseudonymous publication itself is a transmission fact worth carrying forward: Vincent chose not to publish under his own name, for reasons the text itself does not state.

**Confidence:** the 434 date — **Documented**; the Vincentius attribution (via Gennadius) — **Widely Accepted**; whether Vincent held the full Massilian position or only sympathized with it — genuinely **Contested**, and this world's own vendored introduction says so explicitly rather than asserting either reading: "whether he actually held the doctrine which was afterwards called by that name is not clear," while noting Cardinal Noris's own contrary judgment that Vincent "betrays himself as not merely a Semipelagian" ("non modo Semipelagianum se prodit"). This document carries the tension rather than resolving it, per Article 17.

### 1.4 Augustine of Hippo, as opponent-side witness (three treatises)

**What survives, and the corrected scope (per G1 Part C and Doc_01 §10):** *On the Predestination of the Saints* and *On the Gift of Perseverance*, both addressed directly to Prosper and a correspondent named Hilary (428/429); *On Rebuke and Grace*, addressed to Hadrumetum, carried `provisional` as the provoking text rather than a Gallic-addressed one.

**Read directly this session, opening chapters of *De praedestinatione sanctorum*:** Augustine addresses "my dearest sons, Prosper and Hilary," describing their letters as reporting brethren who hold a specific, narrower position than full Pelagianism — accepting original sin, the necessity of grace, and that no one suffices to begin or complete a good work unaided, but disputing specifically *predestination itself*. **This is a precise, primary-source-grounded statement of exactly what the controversy was and was not about**, worth carrying forward to Doc_04's gravity work rather than relying on the retrospective "semi-Pelagian" label's own imprecision.

**A finding that directly strengthens Doc_01 §7's terminology argument, found only in this later reading pass:** this world's own vendored Augustine volume's own editorial footnote, attached to Augustine's own Chapter 2 heading ("To What Extent the Massilians Withdraw from the Pelagians"), states without qualification: **"Prosper in his letter called them *reliquiæ Pelagianorum*... They are now most commonly called 'Semi-Pelagians.'"** This is independent, direct confirmation — from this world's own second vendored volume, not only from the first — that "Massilian" is Prosper's own coinage. Doc_01 is already Approved to Proceed and this document does not reopen it; this is recorded here as reinforcing evidence for Doc_03/Doc_06's lexicon work.

**A second finding bearing on Doc_01's still-open Hilary identification (§7, §10, §11 item 2), also found only in this reading pass:** the same volume's editorial introduction states plainly, twice, that Prosper and Hilary were "**two laymen**" ("We first hear of them in 428, through letters from two laymen, Prosper and Hilary, to Augustin"). This is independent textual support, from this world's own second vendored file, for the lay-monk identification Doc_01 already favored over Hilary of Arles — not decisive on its own (a note elsewhere in the same volume calls the identification with "Hilary of Norbonne" only "probable," and separately flags a *third* candidate, "Hilary of Sicily," as "not certain... but seems probable" to be the same man), but a real, independently-arrived-at corroboration. **This document carries the Contested rating forward unchanged** (three named candidates now on record — a Gallic lay monk, "Hilary of Norbonne," and a Sicilian correspondent possibly conflated with him — genuinely more tangled than a two-option Contested, not less), flagged for Doc_04 or a dedicated prosopographical pass to resolve if it becomes load-bearing.

**Author Gravity Assessment (brief, since Augustine is not this world's own voice but its opponent's-side witness — full Author Gravity treatment belongs to whichever world Augustine is primary for):** Augustine writes as the most authoritative living theologian in the Latin West, replying to a report *about* Gaul, not (so far as this library shows) to a text the Gallic monks addressed to him directly (Doc_01 §8.2). His own treatises are the fullest surviving primary evidence for what the controversy's opposing side actually argued — genuinely valuable for this world's own ecology precisely because they preserve, in Augustine's own summary, positions the Massilian monks held that no surviving Massilian-authored text in this library states directly (Doc_01 Open Item 6).

## 3. Institutional Evidence

Drawn entirely from the primary voices above, since no independent institutional record (charters, conciliar acts naming these houses, archaeological reports) is vendored in this library:

- **Ligugé and Marmoutier** (Martin's foundations, Doc_01 §2.1) — known only through Sulpitius.

- **Saint-Victor and Saint-Sauveur, Marseilles** (Cassian's foundations, Doc_01 §2.3) — known through Cassian's own dedicatory framing and the volume's editorial apparatus; no independent Marseilles-side record.

**Honest limitation, carried forward rather than implied away:** every institutional fact in this world's library passes through exactly one of three authorial voices. There is no institution in this world attested by two independent authors.

## 8. Formation Narrative Sources

Sulpitius's *Vita Martini*, *Dialogues*, and *Letters* are this world's own formation-narrative sources in the Framework's specific sense (§1.1 above covers authorship, proximity, and genre in full). One further note the Framework requires here specifically: **what formation ecology the narrative reveals.** Read together, Sulpitius's corpus reveals a formation pattern organized around direct, personally-witnessed contact with a single exemplary figure (Sulpitius insists throughout on his own acquaintance with Martin, and the *Dialogues*' frame narrative — a Gallic friend "who had been one of [Martin's] disciples," reunited with the returning traveller Postumianus — stages eyewitness testimony as a structural device, not incidental color) rather than through a written rule or codified curriculum. This is the same "named, direct testimony as the guarantee of a text's authority" pattern Doc_01 §6 flags as a candidate cross-node gravity for Doc_04 to test — this document supplies the Tours-side textual grounding for that candidate more fully than Doc_01 had space to.

## 10. Source Asymmetry and Missing Voices Assessment

Building directly on Doc_01 §4's own treatment (not repeating it, extending it):

**Overrepresented:** Martin (through Sulpitius's singular devotion), Cassian (through his own prolific authorship), the Lérins circle's later bishops (Honoratus, Hilary of Arles, Faustus — all visible precisely because they became bishops, whose sees generated further textual traces).

**Underrepresented, evidenced concretely this session:**
- **Women:** Saint-Sauveur (Doc_01 §4) has no named individual; Claudia (§6 above) is addressed but not heard. Evidential visibility here is near-zero; ecological visibility (whether women's presence was formatively significant to this world's own life) cannot be assessed from this library at all — the honest position, per the Framework's own evidential/ecological distinction, is not "thin" but **absent data**, which this document states plainly rather than rounding up to "thin."
- **Ordinary, non-elite participants:** near-total absence beyond Claudia's single letter. This world's textual record is a record of founders, abbots, and bishops-in-formation, not of the wider lay or peasant population these institutions must have interacted with economically and socially (labor, land, almsgiving — all implied by the Institutes' own account of manual work, never described from the receiving end).
- **The Massilian monks' own direct voice:** already named at Doc_01 Open Item 6 and §11.4-5 — this document confirms, after direct reading of Augustine's own treatises, that the asymmetry is real and specific: Augustine's own summary of the Massilian position (§1.4 above) is the fullest surviving statement of what they held, in his words, not theirs.

**Affirmative Duty (Constitution Article 20), stated plainly rather than left implicit:** this world's sources structurally omit ordinary participants, women specifically, and the Massilian monks' own unmediated voice on the controversy that is one of this world's own candidate gravities. The reasons are structural, not incidental: monastic literary production in this period was produced by and for an elite, literate, male, increasingly episcopal circle, and what survives is what that circle chose to write, copy, and preserve. **This is a participant-facing obligation, not only a backstage note** — per the Framework, the Facilitator-Governance function must be able to name this absence plainly when a participant asks after these voices, not only when a builder happens to record it here.

**Bounded reconstruction:** this document does not attempt one. No comparative evidence, material evidence, or against-the-grain textual trace strong enough to meet the Framework's three-condition test has surfaced this session for any of the missing voices above. This is stated as a finding, not a failure to look — Vincent's own silence-as-argument about Augustine (§1.3) is the one genuine against-the-grain reading this session's work supports, and it evidences a doctrinal position, not a marginalized voice, so it does not satisfy this section's own test.

## 11. Forces Lens Applied to Source Ecology (Forces Framework Step 2)

**Which sources speak to external forces?** Sulpitius's *Sacred History* directly (its own subject is world-historical narrative reaching into contemporary events, including — per Doc_01 §8.4 — the Priscillianist affair and the first state execution for heresy); Vincent's *Commonitory* indirectly, in its sustained engagement with imperial-era councils (Ephesus 431) and named heresies as the pressures orthodoxy must be defended against.

**What do the silences reveal?** Vincent's silence on Augustine (§1.3) is itself the clearest forces-signal in this library: a text that can denounce a position without naming its author reveals a live, asymmetric power relationship — Augustine's authority was too significant to attack by name directly, even by a writer whose own sympathies (per Cardinal Noris's reading) lay against him.

**Survivorship pattern:** the near-total absence of a Massilian-authored direct reply to Augustine's 428/429 treatises (Doc_01 Open Item 6) is itself informative about which side of this controversy's literature survived transmission best — Augustine's own authority, and the copying institutions that preserved his works, evidently outlasted whatever the Gallic monks themselves circulated in direct response. Faustus's later *De gratia* (c. 473–475, per Doc_01 §2.4, located but not yet vendored) is the nearest surviving Massilian-side answer, and even it postdates the original exchange by roughly half a century.
