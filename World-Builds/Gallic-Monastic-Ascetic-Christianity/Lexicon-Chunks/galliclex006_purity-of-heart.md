## Quick Meaning

In Cassian's Marseilles, the immediate goal of the whole monastic life — as distinct from its end, the kingdom of heaven — the "definite mark" at which every fast, vigil, reading and renunciation is aimed, "which is charity," and the standard by which all of these are ranked as aids to perfection and never as perfection itself.

---

## World Meaning

The teaching arrives as Abbot Moses's, in an Egyptian cell, and Cassian sets it first among all the conferences. Every art, the old man says, "has some goal or mark; and end or aim of their own." The monk's profession has both: "The end of our profession indeed, as I said, is the kingdom of God or the kingdom of heaven: but the immediate aim or goal, is purity of heart, without which no one can gain that end: fixing our gaze then steadily on this goal, as if on a definite mark, let us direct our course as straight towards it as possible." The Apostle names it too — "the immediate goal is purity of heart, which he not unfairly terms 'sanctification,' without which the afore-mentioned end cannot be gained." An archer with no target cannot correct his aim; a monk with no goal cannot tell whether his fasting is doing anything.

Everything else in the life is then ranked by it. "Fastings, vigils, meditation on the Scriptures, self-denial, and the abnegation of all possessions are not perfection, but aids to perfection: because the end of that science does not lie in these, but by means of these we arrive at the end." Fasts, vigils, withdrawal, reading — all "we ought to practise with a view to our main object, i.e., purity of heart, which is charity, and we ought not on their account to drive away this main virtue." The formation manual closes its book on the renunciant with the same ladder, rung by rung: by driving out faults virtues grow, "by the budding of virtues purity of heart is gained. By purity of heart the perfection of apostolic love is acquired." The Gallic junior with the book in his cell is therefore told to weigh every practice he is given — the office, the fast, the labour — by whether it clears the heart, and to drop none of them for the sake of the very thing they serve.

Prayer is where the goal shows itself. "The aim of every monk and the perfection of his heart tends to continual and unbroken perseverance in prayer," toward "an immovable tranquillity of mind and a perpetual purity"; the canonical hours exist as a scaffold for that, and the night's psalms yield a purity the dream must not spoil. And the argument about grace is fought inside this goal: when the question is put whether effort accomplishes anything, the answer is laid down by "all the Catholic fathers who have taught perfection of heart not by empty disputes of words, but in deed and act," and the manual says plainly that no bodily labour "can be sufficient for the acquisition of that true chastity of the inner man" without God's help. Purity of heart is what the monk works toward and cannot make.

At Tours the words are not used. Martin refuses a robe said to have come from heaven and will not believe a purple-robed Christ "unless he appears with that appearance and form in which he suffered" — the thing itself, a heart that tests what it is offered — but nothing at Marmoutier forms disciples *by* purity of heart, and no one there names it. The phrase belongs to the south, and the south says it belongs to Egypt: the Gallic monk who speaks of it speaks of "what the fathers handed down and Cassian brought to us," not of what Gaul devised.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
Sexual purity or moral spotlessness; "a pure heart" as sincerity or good intentions; "perfection" as flawlessness or perfectionism.

**World Hearing:**
The *scopos* — a state of undistracted charity, "tranquillity of mind," from which the kingdom is reached and by which every ascetic practice is measured and ranked; sexual chastity is one element within it, and this world's own conference on chastity (*Conf.* XII) is missing from its English text; Tours has the thing without the word.
