## Quick Meaning

In Cassian's Marseilles, the sixth fault, "which we may term weariness or distress of heart," "especially trying to solitaries" — the noon assault that "produces dislike of the place, disgust with the cell, and disdain and contempt of the brethren," makes the monk gaze at the sun "as if it was too slow in setting," and dresses his escape as pious duty until "the soldier of Christ becomes a runaway from His service, and a deserter."

---

## World Meaning

"Our sixth combat is with what the Greeks call ἀκηδία, which we may term weariness or distress of heart. This is akin to dejection, and is especially trying to solitaries, and a dangerous and frequent foe to dwellers in the desert"; it attacks "about the sixth hour," so that "some of the elders ... declare that this is the 'midday demon' spoken of in the ninetieth Psalm." Cassian describes it from inside the cell. "When this has taken possession of some unhappy soul, it produces dislike of the place, disgust with the cell, and disdain and contempt of the brethren who dwell with him or at a little distance, as if they were careless or unspiritual." The monk thinks other monasteries better and his own useless; he "often goes in and out of his cell, and frequently gazes up at the sun, as if it was too slow in setting, and so a kind of unreasonable confusion of mind takes possession of him like some foul darkness." The escape it offers is dressed as charity — visiting the sick, or "that religious woman, devoted to the service of God, who is deprived of all support of kindred" — "until little by little he is drawn away from his cell, and begins to forget the object of his profession, which is nothing but meditation and contemplation of that divine purity," "and so the soldier of Christ becomes a runaway from His service, and a deserter, and 'entangles himself in secular business.'" The attack is twofold — sleep or flight — and "the true Christian athlete who desires to strive lawfully in the lists of perfection, should hasten to expel this disease also from the recesses of his soul." The remedy is the cell, held by labour.

For a Gallic monk with the book in his cell, accidie is the name of what he feels at noon and the diagnosis of every good reason he has for leaving. Tours has no word for it; its failure is Brictio's sneer, not the sun watched.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
"Sloth" as laziness; depression or burnout as a clinical condition to be treated; boredom; or a romantic "noonday demon" detached from the cell.

**World Hearing:**
A specifically monastic, cell-bound, hour-bound assault by a spirit — the sixth of eight — that makes the cell hateful and the brethren contemptible, offers pious pretexts for leaving, and ends in desertion; fought like a combat by staying, and named as Egypt's elders named it.
