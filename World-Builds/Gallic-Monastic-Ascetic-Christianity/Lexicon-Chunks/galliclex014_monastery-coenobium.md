## Quick Meaning

For this world a monastery is simply the place where monks dwell — Martin's wooden cell and the caves around it, a bishop's "new monastery" in a province "at present without monasteries," a house "for women" at Marseilles — while *coenobium*, the word Cassian brings from Egypt, names not the place but the kind of life kept in it: a congregation "governed by the direction of a single Elder."

---

## World Meaning

At Tours the word names wherever Martin stops. Driven from Milan by the Arians, "he established a monastery for himself at Milan"; received by Hilary at Poitiers, "he established for himself a monastery not far from the town"; made bishop of Tours, unable "to tolerate the disturbance caused by the numbers of those visiting" his cell by the church, "he established a monastery for himself about two miles outside the city," a spot "so secret and retired that he enjoyed in it the solitude of a hermit," shut in by a precipice on one side and the river on the other, "approached only by one, and that a very narrow passage." There "he possessed a cell constructed of wood," and "many also of the brethren had, in the same manner, fashioned retreats for themselves, but most of them had formed these out of the rock of the overhanging mountain, hollowed into caves." Eighty disciples live there under one master. And the word covers a women's house too: the soldier's wife "whom Martin had ordered to have a place in the nunnery of the young women." A monastery at Tours is a place with a master in it, and the churches come to it for their priests; where Martin pulls down a temple, "there he used immediately to build either churches or monasteries."

At Marseilles the word opens a book written for a bishop's house that does not yet exist — "the brethren in your new monastery," in a province "at present without monasteries" — and the book is careful to say that the word by itself guarantees nothing. Abbot Piamun, explaining the kinds of monk to two visiting Gauls, draws the line: "monastery is the title of the dwelling, and means nothing more than the place, i.e., the habitation of monks, while Cœnobium describes the character of the life and its system: and monastery may mean the dwelling of a single monk, while a Cœnobium cannot." The coenobites are those "who live together in a congregation and are governed by the direction of a single Elder"; the anchorites are those "who were first trained in the Cœnobium and then being made perfect in practical life chose the recesses of the desert"; and the Sarabaites, who build "monasteries" of their own and remain "perfectly free and their own masters," are the proof that a monastery can house no rule at all. What a Gallic house is measured by, then, is not the building but whether one elder governs a congregation in it. Cassian's own two houses at Marseilles — "one for men and one for women," as Gennadius remembers them, "still standing" a lifetime later — are what the word points to in that city; and to a Christian outside them, in the same city, monasteries are where the "servants of God" live whom Carthage jeers at: "they in evil dens, these in monasteries."

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
An abbey or cloister — a walled precinct with a church, a written Rule, an abbot, enclosure, and a charter; "convent" for the women's version; an institution with a constitution.

**World Hearing:**
Nothing more than "the habitation of monks": a wooden cell and hollowed caves, or a bishop's new foundation where none existed before; a word that guarantees no rule (Sarabaites have "monasteries" too), so that the south reaches for *coenobium* — one elder over a congregation — when it wants to say what matters; women's houses under a bishop's or a founder's jurisdiction, present in the record and silent in it.
