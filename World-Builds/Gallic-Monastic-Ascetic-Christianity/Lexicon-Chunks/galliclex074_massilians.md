## Quick Meaning

"Massilians" is what a hostile outsider called the monks and clergy of Marseilles who held that grace and effort co-operate — Prosper's geographical label, taken up in Augustine's treatise ("To What Extent the Massilians Withdraw from the Pelagians") — used by this build as shorthand for a position also held at Lérins, and never a name the Marseilles monk used of himself.

---

## World Meaning

No one at Marseilles or Lérins calls himself a Massilian. The word belongs to the reporters: it is the party "which Augustin is here opposing" and which "had its chief centre in Marseilles," so that Prosper "in his letter called them *reliquiæ Pelagianorum*, i.e., 'the remnants of the Pelagians'" — a hostile phrase, and a geographical one that by its own letters does not reach Lérins, let alone Tours. What the label points at, in the reporters' own admission, is a party that has "attained to the confession that men's wills are anticipated by God's grace." "Semi-Pelagian," the word later ages put beside it, appears in this world's vendored files only where the nineteenth-century editors write it — a headnote "On the Semi-Pelagianism of this Conference," a footnote on "those who were afterwards called semi-Pelagians (they belonged chiefly to the churches of Southern Gaul)," a bracketed remark that "they are now most commonly called 'Semi-Pelagians.'" It is never in the ancient text. A Marseilles monk of the 420s knows himself as a brother of the house, a keeper of "the genuine faith of the ancient fathers"; the names he has for the argument are grace, free will, and the beginning of a good will.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
"Semi-Pelagian" as a settled label for a heresy this world taught; "Massilians" as a self-designation or a sect.

**World Hearing:**
An opponent's word for the brethren of one city, repeated by editors, that names a position from outside; from inside, the only names are the faith of the fathers and the argument's own terms.
