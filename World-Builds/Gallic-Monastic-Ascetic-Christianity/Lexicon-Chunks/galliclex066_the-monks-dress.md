## Quick Meaning

For this world the monk's dress is, in Cassian's book, a lexicon of meanings — each Egyptian garment "a mystery" of the inner life, the girdle for mortified loins, the sleeveless tunic for being "dead to all earthly conversation," the hood for "the simplicity and innocence of little children," the staff against "invisible beasts" — refitted for Gaul because "the severity of the winter does not allow us to be satisfied with slippers or tunics or a single frock"; at Marmoutier, "garments of camels' hair" where "any dress approaching to softness was there deemed criminal."

## World Meaning

Cassian begins the whole formation with clothes. "A monk, then, as a soldier of Christ ever ready for battle, ought always to walk with his loins girded," and the second book opens "Girt, therefore, with this twofold girdle." Each garment is read: the small hood, "in order that they may constantly be moved to preserve the simplicity and innocence of little children by imitating their actual dress"; the linen sleeveless tunic, which teaches "that they are dead to all earthly conversation"; the cords; the cape; the sheepskin; the staff, because the monk must "never ... walk unarmed among so many barking dogs of faults and invisible beasts of spiritual wickedness"; the sandals; and the girdle of dead skin that "signifies that he bears about the mortification of those members in which are contained the seeds of lust." The dress is to be "commonplace, so that it may not be thought remarkable for novelty of colour or fashion among other men of the same profession." Then the adaptation, in Cassian's own voice: "the severity of the winter does not allow us to be satisfied with slippers or tunics or a single frock; and the covering of tiny hoods or the wearing of a sheepskin would afford a subject for derision instead of edifying the spectators. Wherefore we hold that we ought to introduce only those things which we have described above, and which are adapted to the humble character of our profession and the nature of the climate, that the chief thing about our dress may be not the novelty of the garb, which might give some offence to men of the world, but its honourable simplicity." The Gallic monk is dressed in Egypt's meanings and Gaul's wool.

At Marmoutier the dress is plain and the judgment plainer: "most of them were clothed in garments of camels' hair. Any dress approaching to softness was there deemed criminal." Martin goes about "in his shaggy garment, with a long black cloak over it," and the bishops who opposed his election said "his clothing was mean." No one at Tours reads a garment as a mystery; the dress there is a mark of severance and a scandal to bishops.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
"The habit" as a uniform of an order, fixed by rule, worn for identification; or dress as incidental to formation.

**World Hearing:**
A set of received Egyptian garments each read as a mystery of the inner life, explicitly altered for Gaul's winter and for the eyes of "men of the world," chosen for simplicity against novelty; at Tours, camel's hair and a shaggy cloak that make softness a crime and a bishop's opponents sneer.
