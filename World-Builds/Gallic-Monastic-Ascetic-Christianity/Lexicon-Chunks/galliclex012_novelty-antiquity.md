## Quick Meaning

The axis on which all three of this world's founding voices judge legitimacy: whatever is new — a doctrine, a monastic custom, a fashion of dress, a cult, an opinion of one's own — is suspect for being new, and whatever "has been handed down" from antiquity is to be held fast; "if novelty is profane, antiquity is sacred."

---

## World Meaning

Vincent puts it in his title: the *Commonitory* is written "For the Antiquity and Universality of the Catholic Faith Against the Profane Novelties of All Heresies." The Apostle's charge to Timothy — "keep the deposit, shunning profane novelties of words" — is turned into a rule of contraries: "For if novelty is to be shunned, antiquity is to be held fast; if novelty is profane, antiquity is sacred." Two popes are quoted for it. Stephen: "Let there be no innovation — nothing but what has been handed down." Celestine: "let novelty cease to assail antiquity." The occasion of the whole book is that "the subtle craftiness of new heretics calls for no ordinary care." And yet Vincent asks the question a reader will ask — "Shall there, then, be no progress in Christ's Church?" — and answers that there shall be "all possible progress," "on condition that it be real progress, not alteration of the faith. For progress requires that the subject be enlarged in itself, alteration, that it be transformed into something else." A child grows into a man with the same limbs; wheat sown is wheat reaped. The old may grow; it may not become other.

At Marseilles the same axis runs through the monk's clothes and his thoughts. His dress is to be "commonplace, so that it may not be thought remarkable for novelty of colour or fashion among other men of the same profession," and, refitted for the Gallic winter, is to be such "that the chief thing about our dress may be not the novelty of the garb, which might give some offence to men of the world, but its honourable simplicity." A thought that arises is to be weighed "whether it be light with human display or some conceit of novelty." True discretion is reached "by following the steps of the Elders never to do anything novel nor to decide anything by or on our own responsibility, but to walk in all things as we are taught by their tradition and upright life." The false superior is the one "readier to require the observance of our own inventions than to preserve the well-tried teaching of our predecessors." Even the argument about grace is closed on this ground: it is "the genuine faith of the ancient fathers which still remains intact among their successors." And the axis has a way of admitting the new: a whole morning office was added "in our own day," but by the elders, "after long discussion and anxious consideration" — a novelty made lawful by the consent of the old.

At Tours the axis is a bishop's scruple. Asked to bless a tomb the crowd honoured as a martyr's, Martin asks his elders for the martyr's name and the time of his suffering, "because he had great scruples on these points, inasmuch as no steady tradition respecting them had come down from antiquity," and, until he knows, keeps away, "lest a mere superstition should obtain a firmer footing." What the crowd has newly taken up, the bishop will not authorize without antiquity behind it.

So one instrument serves three uses: it tests doctrine, it tests custom, and it tests a cult. What counts as old differs by house — at Marseilles, Egypt's fathers; at Lérins, the consent of the ancients; at Tours, the clergy's memory — but the direction of the judgment is the same everywhere, and it is what makes a thing not one's own. The word for what goes wrong, in each house, is invention.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
"Novelty" as harmless newness or as a virtue (innovation, creativity); "conservative" as a political posture; "tradition" as sentiment or custom for its own sake; "progress" as change.

**World Hearing:**
Novelty is a diagnostic of error and of pride — in a doctrine, a garment, a thought, a cult — and antiquity is what makes a thing not one's own; progress exists only as the growth of the same thing; and the new can be made lawful only by the consent of the old.
