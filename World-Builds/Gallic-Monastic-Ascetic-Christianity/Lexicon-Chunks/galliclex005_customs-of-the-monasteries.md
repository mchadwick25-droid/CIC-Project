## Quick Meaning

What Cassian carried from Egypt and Palestine to a Gallic bishop's "new monastery": not a written Rule of his own devising but "the customs of the monasteries which we have seen observed" there, "as they were there delivered to us by the Fathers" — to be kept where Gaul's cold and "the weakness of our constitution" allow, and balanced from Pontus and Mesopotamia where they do not.

---

## World Meaning

A bishop asks for them. Castor of Apta Julia is "anxious that the institutions of the East and especially of Egypt should be established in your province, which is at present without monasteries," and Cassian undertakes "to declare, although with inexpert pen, the customs of the monasteries which we have seen observed throughout Egypt and Palestine, as they were there delivered to us by the Fathers." He states the rule by which he will judge whatever he finds already in place: anything "withdrawn or added in those countries not in accordance with the example of the elders established by ancient custom, but according to the fancy of any one who has founded a monastery," he will add or omit "in accordance with the rule which I have seen followed in the monasteries anciently founded throughout Egypt and Palestine, as I do not believe that a new establishment in the West, in the parts of Gaul could find anything more reasonable or more perfect than are those customs." The customs are not his; that is their whole authority. A monastery in Egypt stands not "at the fancy of every man who renounces the world, but through a succession of fathers and their traditions," and a Gallic house "in its untrained infancy in Christ" is to "be instructed in the most ancient institutions of the earliest fathers."

What the customs are is the content of the twelve books: the dress, garment by garment, each with its hidden meaning; the canonical system of psalms and prayers; the receiving and training of those who renounce the world; and the eight principal faults with "the remedies for them according to their traditions." Their test is the one Cassian states over sackcloth: allegiance is owed "not to those customs and rules which the will of a few have introduced, but to those which a long standing antiquity and numbers of the holy fathers have passed on by an unanimous decision to those that come after." That is why the dress is judged by "the Catholic rule" and why a self-made abbot who prefers "our own inventions" to "the well-tried teaching of our predecessors" is the customs' own definition of a false superior.

And they are adapted, in Cassian's own voice, because Gaul is not Egypt. "Where I find anything in the rule of the Egyptians which, either because of the severity of the climate, or owing to some difficulty or diversity of habits, is impossible in these countries, or hard and difficult, I shall to some extent balance it by the customs of the monasteries which are found throughout Pontus and Mesopotamia." So: "the severity of the winter does not allow us to be satisfied with slippers or tunics or a single frock; and the covering of tiny hoods or the wearing of a sheepskin would afford a subject for derision instead of edifying the spectators." Much of the Egyptian diet is passed over, since "in this country neither the climate nor the weakness of our constitution would permit." And the customs arrive as a standing correction of what Gaul already does: some "in this country ... before the Psalm is fairly ended, make haste to prostrate themselves for prayer, in their hurry to finish the service"; all here sing "Glory be to the Father" aloud at the end of the psalmody, a thing "we have never heard anywhere throughout the East"; some "in this province ... go back again to bed after their Mattin prayers are finished"; and of the Egyptians' lifelong perseverance, "we cannot recollect any one who joined our monasteries keeping it up unbroken even for a year." A Gallic monk who keeps the customs keeps them as a debtor, in a "province that is frozen as it were with the cold of Gaul," and is told to receive the fathers into his cell as a book so as not to be "left to their own resources to find that way which is difficult and almost unknown in this country."

At Tours the customs exist without the word. Marmoutier has common property, no buying or selling "as is the custom among most monks," one meal after the fast, no wine, camel's hair, and the young copying while the old pray — a house with a discipline and no named rule, ordered by a master's example. Sulpitius does not call any of it an institute, and Cassian never mentions Martin. Both houses, though, would say the same thing about what a custom is for: it is what keeps a monk from being his own master, which is the Sarabaite's fault.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
"Rule" as a codified legal document — the Rule of Benedict, with a canonical abbot, a habit, and a novitiate; "Institutes" as a theology textbook (Calvin's); "customs" as trivial house habits.

**World Hearing:**
A received, oral-then-written body of Egyptian practice whose authority is precisely that it is *not* one's own, explicitly incomplete in Gaul, adapted for the winter and the Gallic constitution, and read in the cell as a correction of what Gaul already does. Gibson's cross-references to the Rule of Benedict in this volume's footnotes are a nineteenth-century editor's, not this world's.
