## Quick Meaning

In Cassian's Marseilles, the exact point at issue in the argument about grace: "does God have compassion upon us because we have shown the beginning of a good will, or does the beginning of a good will follow because God has had compassion upon us?" — answered with cases on both sides and declared beyond "the mind and reason of man"; and, in Augustine's report, the one thing the Massilians were said to claim for themselves — "the beginning of faith."

---

## World Meaning

Abbot Chaeremon puts the question in a single sentence, and says at once that both answers, pressed, are errors: "does God have compassion upon us because we have shown the beginning of a good will, or does the beginning of a good will follow because God has had compassion upon us? For many believing each of these and asserting them more widely than is right are entangled in all kinds of opposite errors. For if we say that the beginning of free will is in our own power, what about Paul the persecutor, what about Matthew the publican, of whom the one was drawn to salvation while eager for bloodshed and the punishment of the innocent, the other for violence and rapine? But if we say that the beginning of our free will is always due to the inspiration of the grace of God, what about the faith of Zaccheus, or what are we to say of the goodness of the thief on the cross, who by their own desires brought violence to bear on the kingdom of heaven and so prevented the special leadings of their vocation?" Elsewhere the same teacher leans one way — "the initiative not only of our actions but also of good thoughts comes from God, who inspires us with a good will to begin with"; "when His goodness sees in us even the very smallest spark of good will shining forth, which He Himself has struck as it were out of the hard flints of our hearts, He fans and fosters it" — and elsewhere the other: "when He sees in us some beginnings of a good will, He at once enlightens it and strengthens it and urges it on towards salvation, increasing that which He Himself implanted or which He sees to have arisen from our own efforts"; "sometimes first beginnings of a good will arise, which however cannot attain to the complete performance of what is good unless it is guided by the Lord." Abbot Paphnutius, in an earlier conference, says simply that "the beginning of our good will is given to us by the inspiration of the Lord, when He draws us towards the way of salvation." The conclusion is not a formula: "how God works all things in us and yet everything can be ascribed to free will, cannot be fully grasped by the mind and reason of man."

This is the sentence the reporters carried to Hippo. Augustine's treatise is written to show that "even the beginning of faith is of God's gift," against brethren said to hold "that we have faith itself of ourselves, but that its increase is of God," so that a man "compounds as it were with God to claim a portion of faith for himself, and to leave a portion for Him." The Marseilles monk who reads his own teacher finds the beginning given by God and struck from his flint, and finds Zaccheus running ahead — and is told that no one can grasp how both are true.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
A settled "semi-Pelagian" error — man makes the first move, God does the rest — read backwards from later confessional verdicts; or a philosophical question about causation.

**World Hearing:**
A question the world's own teacher answers on both sides with Scripture's cases and declares beyond reason's grasp — the beginning as God's spark from the flint *and* as Zaccheus running ahead — held together as "the genuine faith of the ancient fathers," and reported to Africa by men who heard only one side of it.

## CT Contest Type

**Meaning:** which of Cassian's own two phrasings is his position — that the beginning of a good will comes from God who "inspires us with a good will to begin with" (*Conf.* XIII.3, XIII.7; III.19), or that it can "have arisen from our own efforts" (XIII.8; XIII.9's "first beginnings of a good will arise") — is the precise question Prosper's *Contra Collatorem* (row 32, unvendored) was written to settle, extracting propositions from this Conference and judging all but the first erroneous (Gibson's prolegomena, editorial). The contest is live in the field as the Chadwick (row 21) / Casiday (row 22) disagreement over whether *Conf.* XIII answers Augustine at all (Doc_01 §7, §11 item 5; see 008's CT section for the fuller statement); on this term specifically it is a contest over the *meaning* of Cassian's sentence, not over its application.
