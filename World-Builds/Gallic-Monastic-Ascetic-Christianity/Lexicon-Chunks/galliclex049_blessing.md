*Voice note:* a northern (Tours) term, Sulpitius's only in the power sense; Cassian's uses are ordinary or liturgical. Written in close third person as Tours's usage; the south is named only to say the sense is absent there.

---

## Quick Meaning

At Tours a blessing is a transferable power residing in the saint's touch, word, and belongings — oil he blesses "such is the custom" that multiplies and heals, threads "plucked from the sackcloth which he wore" that "wrought frequent miracles," straw the virgins divide, a letter laid in a fevered girl's bosom — so that "the blessing of God, bestowed on the vessel by Martin ... could not possibly perish."

---

## World Meaning

The blessing is asked for on the knees and felt on the head. Sulpitius, visiting, "embraced his sacred knees, and begged for his blessing according to custom. Upon this, I felt his hand placed on my head with the sweetest touch, while, amid the solemn words of benediction, he repeated again and again the name of the cross." It passes into things. Avitianus "had sent some oil to Martin, that he might bless it (such is the custom) so as to be ready when needful to meet different causes of disease," and the presbyter "saw the oil increase under the blessing of Martin, so much that, the abundance of it overflowing the jar, it ran down from the top in every direction"; another vessel of oil "blessed by Martin," knocked from "a pretty high window" onto "the marble pavement," "was found as safe as ever, just as if it had fallen on the softest feathers" — "the power of Martin, whose blessing could not possibly perish." "Threads from Martin's garment, or such as had been plucked from the sackcloth which he wore, wrought frequent miracles upon those who were sick"; Arborius "inserted in the bosom of the girl, at the very paroxysm of the heat, a letter of Martin," and the fever left her. Where Martin had lodged, "all the virgins used to rush into that retirement: they kiss every place where the blessed man had either sat or stood, and distribute among themselves the very straw on which he had lain." Even his name carries it: a man commands a mad dog "in the name of Martin, to be quiet," and "a certain Egyptian merchant, who was not yet a Christian, cried out, 'Save us, O God of Martin,' upon which the tempest was immediately stilled." And the blessing can run the other way: when a virgin who will not see even Martin sends him a gift, he receives it, "declaring that her blessing was by no means to be rejected by a priest, since she was indeed to be placed before many priests."

At Marseilles the word means only what it ordinarily means — a reward, or the elder's closing of the prayer — and no oil, thread, or straw is blessed in what Cassian writes for Gaul. The south's counter-teaching on wonders has no room for a saint's straw.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
"Blessing" as a wish, a prayer, or a kindly word; relics as later medieval superstition detachable from the world's real concerns; "sacramentals" as a later category.

**World Hearing:**
A power that passes from the saint into oil, thread, straw, a letter, and a name, asked for on the knees "according to custom," measured by what it does, and never able to "perish"; the memory of the saint in things, begun while he lived; and, in the south, a sense the teaching on wonders leaves no place for.
