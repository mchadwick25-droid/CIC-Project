## Quick Meaning

In Cassian's Marseilles, the help of God without which no effort reaches its goal — grace that "always co-operates with our will," that goes before the will and follows it, that inspires the first desire and crowns the last perseverance — held together, deliberately, with a will that remains free, in a relation "that cannot be fully grasped by the mind and reason of man."

---

## World Meaning

A monk meets the word as a sentence to be said. At the end of the formation manual, in the book on pride, Cassian tells him that "in the case of every virtue in which we feel that we make progress, we say these words of the Apostle: 'Not I, but the grace of God with me.'" The teaching is stated on both sides at once, and on purpose: "clearly and most earnestly do I lay down, not giving my own opinion, but that of the elders, that perfection cannot possibly be gained without these [efforts], but that by these only without the grace of God nobody can ever attain it. For when we say that human efforts cannot of themselves secure it without the aid of God, we thus insist that God's mercy and grace are bestowed only upon those who labour and exert themselves." The chapter after that says "we cannot even make the effort to obtain perfection without the mercy and inspiration of God"; and the manual's last sentence says the monk must "not only ... acknowledge that we cannot possibly perform anything connected with the attainment of perfect virtue without His assistance and grace, but also truly to believe that this very fact that we can understand this, is His own gift." This, Cassian says, "is that genuine faith of the ancient fathers."

The fuller argument is Abbot Chaeremon's, in a conference that begins the morning after a talk on chastity, because Germanus is troubled that "the blessed old man had by the addition of a single sentence broken down the claims of man's exertions." The answer: "the initiative not only of our actions but also of good thoughts comes from God, who inspires us with a good will to begin with." And yet "the grace of God always co-operates with our will for its advantage, and in all things assists, protects, and defends it, in such a way as sometimes even to require and look for some efforts of good will from it that it may not appear to confer its gifts on one who is asleep." The hard question is put in the old man's own words: "does God have compassion upon us because we have shown the beginning of a good will, or does the beginning of a good will follow because God has had compassion upon us? For many believing each of these and asserting them more widely than is right are entangled in all kinds of opposite errors." Cases stand on both sides — Paul and Matthew drawn unwilling, Zacchaeus and the thief coming first — and the conclusion is not a formula but a limit: "how God works all things in us and yet everything can be ascribed to free will, cannot be fully grasped by the mind and reason of man." What "all the Catholic fathers" hold is that God works in us "so as to incite, protect, and strengthen, but not to take away the freedom of the will which He Himself has once given." Abbot Paphnutius says the same in his own conference: "the beginning of our good will is given to us by the inspiration of the Lord," and "it is in our own power to follow up the encouragement and assistance of God with more or less zeal"; "we have no wish to do away with man's free will ... but only to establish the fact that the assistance and grace of God are necessary to it every day and hour."

Two things are refused. On one side, "the profane notion of some who attribute everything to free will and lay down that the grace of God is dispensed in accordance with the desert of each man." On the other, the thought that God "does not generally will all men, but only some instead of all to be saved" — "how can we imagine [that] without grievous blasphemy? Those then who perish, perish against His will." In prayer the monk names God on both sides at once: "not only our Protector and Saviour, but actually as our Helper and Sponsor. For whereas He first calls us to Him, and while we are still ignorant and unwilling, draws us towards salvation, He is our Protector and Saviour, but whereas when we are already striving, He is wont to bring us help," He is Helper and Sponsor.

This teaching was reported to Africa and to Rome by men who did not share it, and the brethren of Marseilles held to it. What the reporters themselves conceded: these brethren "have attained to the confession that men's wills are anticipated by God's grace; and to the agreement that no one can suffice to himself either for beginning or for completing any good work" — and are said to be "as yet ... in darkness" only on a question, predestination, that they did not put in their own words. Vincent, at Lérins, names only Pelagius as the one who "attributed so much antecedent strength to Free-will, as to deny the necessity of God's grace." A generation later a Lérins-formed bishop, Faustus, is remembered as teaching that "the grace of God always invites, precedes and helps our will, and whatever gain that freedom of will may attain for its pious effect, is not its own desert, but the gift of grace."

The same word, at Tours, means something else, and the two must not be run together. There, "the gift of accomplishing cures was so largely possessed by Martin," and the "abundance" of power he had as a monk and lost as a bishop is an abundance *of grace* — a saint's power, diminishable by a bad communion. Martin's grace is a thing he has more or less of; the grace argued over at Marseilles is the initiative of salvation itself.

## Ecological Function

This is the term through which G3 (Supporting at world level; Primary within the southern node) is spoken, and — after Doc_05 §2.4(v) — the formation program's own terminus rather than a doctrinal appendix: it occupies *Inst.* XII.9–19 as the remedy for pride and supplies the *Institutes*' actual closing sentence (XII.33), and *Conf.* XIII arises as a formation question at morning service (XIII.1). It is defended as G4 (the fathers' faith against novelty), argued inside G7 (purity/perfection of heart), carried in G8's idiom (Job the athlete; the psalter's "God who girded me with strength," *Inst.* XII.17), and authorized by G5 (Cassian *heard* Chaeremon). Dependencies: free will (5.2), beginning of a good will (5.3), co-operation (5.6), merit (5.7), perseverance (5.4), predestination (5.5, the opponent's word), Pelagians as foil (5.9), Massilians (5.8); grace as charism (6.2) is the northern sense held apart. Doc_05 §9A.1 states the pattern as "two-sided by design," and warns that "a Representative that resolves it either way has left the world." A participant who understands this term understands why the Massilian monks could confess that grace anticipates the will and still insist that fasting is required.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
A binary — "grace alone" versus "works," Augustine versus Pelagius — in which this world must be placed on one side; "semi-Pelagian" as a settled verdict; grace as a feeling or a favour; or the whole matter as abstract theology unrelated to how anyone lived.

**World Hearing:**
Help that co-operates, precedes, and *requires* labour, held on both sides at once as "the genuine faith of the ancient fathers"; a teaching that closes a formation manual and is raised at the hour of prayer; a limit — "cannot be fully grasped" — rather than a system; and, at Tours, a different word entirely: a saint's healing power.

## CT Contest Type

**Meaning:** whether Cassian's "co-operates," "precedes," and "inspires us with a good will to begin with" amount to what Augustine means by prevenient grace, or to the position Prosper's *Contra Collatorem* attacked — a live dispute in the field between the older reading (Chadwick, row 21, who redated Honoratus's episcopate to let *Conf.* XIII answer Augustine) and Casiday (row 22, who rejects the redating and finds little direct anti-Augustinian polemic); bound up with the Contested relative chronology of *Conf.* XIII and Augustine's Gallic treatises (Doc_01 §7, §11 item 5). Cassian's own text says the beginning comes from God in some places and can arise "from our own efforts" in others (*Conf.* XIII.8, XIII.12) — the contest is over which is his position.

**Application to this world:** whether the label "semi-Pelagian" — a sixteenth-century coinage (Beza; retrojected by Sanders), present in this world's vendored files only in the editors' apparatus (Gibson's headnotes; Heurtley's footnote at *Comm.* ch. 24 [62]; Warfield's Argument) — fairly describes the Massilian position, given Cassian's own two-sidedness, Augustine's own concession that these brethren hold wills to be anticipated by grace, and Gennadius's report of Faustus as grace-prevenient (Doc_02 §1.4). Doc_01 §7 rules the label anachronistic; the build's own shorthand, "Massilian," is itself a hostile outsider's geographical term (Doc_03 5.8). A second application question: whether Vincent and Lérins held the position at all (Heurtley's editorial reading, Contested, Doc_02 §1.3).
