## Quick Meaning

For this world "predestination" is a word its own voices do not use: Cassian's Chaeremon calls it "grievous blasphemy" to imagine that God "does not generally will all men, but only some instead of all to be saved," and says "those then who perish, perish against His will" — while Augustine, reporting from Africa, names the same brethren "as yet ... in darkness on the question concerning the predestination of the saints."

---

## World Meaning

Chaeremon never says the word. What he says is what the Scripture says of God's will: since "He willeth all men to be saved and to come to the knowledge of the truth," and since it is not His will "that one of His little ones should perish, how can we imagine without grievous blasphemy that He does not generally will all men, but only some instead of all to be saved? Those then who perish, perish against His will, as He testifies against each one of them day by day." The whole conference is built to keep two things standing — that God's grace precedes, accompanies, and crowns every good, and that "the choice of free will is open to either side," God working "so as to incite, protect, and strengthen, but not to take away the freedom of the will which He Himself has once given." Whatever can be said of God's foreknowledge or choosing is left where Chaeremon leaves the whole question: it "cannot be fully grasped by the mind and reason of man." No Gallic monk in what survives argues *about* predestination; the argument is about the beginning of a good will and its end.

The word arrives from outside. Augustine, told by Prosper and Hilary what the brethren of Marseilles hold, concedes much — "they have attained to the confession that men's wills are anticipated by God's grace; and to the agreement that no one can suffice to himself either for beginning or for completing any good work" — and then names their lack: "for as yet they are in darkness on the question concerning the predestination of the saints." His second treatise answers their further objection, as his editor summarizes it, "that the definition of predestination is opposed to the usefulness of exhortation and rebuke," and bids "that predestination should be preached in a harmonious manner." The Massilian monk hears in that word the thing his own teacher had called blasphemy — a God who wills only some to be saved — and refuses it, not as a doctrine he has weighed and rejected, but as a thing outside the faith of the fathers he received. A generation later Faustus, Lérins-formed, is remembered as teaching that "the grace of God always invites, precedes and helps our will" — the same two-sidedness, still without the opponent's word.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
A doctrine this world "opposed" — Calvinism versus Arminianism, determinism versus free will — with the Massilians as the anti-predestinarian party; or predestination as a metaphysical puzzle about foreknowledge.

**World Hearing:**
An outsider's word for a question the world's own voices left "beyond the mind and reason of man," refusing only what it could name in its own terms — that God wills "only some instead of all to be saved" — while confessing that grace anticipates every will; heard by the Massilian monk as a stranger's vocabulary, not as a position of his own.

## CT Contest Type

**Application to this world:** the contest is whether this term applies to this world at all — it is the opponent's word (Augustine's, *Praed.* ch. 2: what "those brethren" are "in darkness" on), not this world's own vocabulary in anything read; and whether the Massilian side's own doctrine amounts to a *denial* of predestination is complicated by Gennadius's report of Faustus as teaching that grace "always invites, precedes and helps our will" (Doc_02 §1.4) — a grace-prevenient position that does not fit the "anti-predestinarian" label the term's later use assigns. The contest is therefore about application, not meaning: whether a Representative may be said to have held, denied, or simply lacked a doctrine named only by its critic.
