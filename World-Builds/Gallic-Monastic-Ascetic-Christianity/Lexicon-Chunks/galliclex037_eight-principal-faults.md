## Quick Meaning

In Cassian's Marseilles, the fixed list of the monk's enemies — "first, Gluttony or the pleasures of the palate; secondly, Fornication; thirdly, Covetousness," then anger, dejection, accidie, vainglory, pride — "of which they reckon eight," each a "spirit" fought in order with "the remedies for them according to their traditions," and the subject of a book apiece.

---

## World Meaning

Cassian promises Castor the whole system: "the origin and causes of the principal faults, of which they reckon eight, and the remedies for them according to their traditions." The fifth book opens the campaign — "the struggle against the eight principal faults, i.e. first, Gluttony or the pleasures of the palate; secondly, Fornication; thirdly, Covetousness, which means Avarice," then anger, dejection, accidie, vainglory, and pride — and gives each its book and its number: "the first conflict we must enter upon is that against gluttony"; "our fourth conflict is against the sin of" anger; "our fifth combat ... against the spirit of dejection"; "our sixth combat is with what the Greeks call ἀκηδία"; "our seventh combat is against the spirit of κενοδοξία"; and last, pride, whose remedy is the confession "Not I, but the grace of God with me." The order is the order of battle: no one "is worthy to be tried in harder battles, who can be overcome in a slight skirmish," and a city "is laid waste by the giving up of one postern however small." The faults are not a catalogue of sins but a sequence of spirits, each with its own remedy handed down, and the last two are the ones a good monk fears most — vainglory that "creates a wish to take holy orders, and a desire for the priesthood or diaconate," and pride, by which a man "grown cold ... wants to be put to rule other people." Gennadius remembers the eight books precisely: "On the origin, nature and remedies for the eight principal sins, a book on each sin."

A Gallic reader receives the list as Egypt's — "they reckon eight," "their traditions" — and reads it toward a purity of heart the faults obstruct. Nothing at Tours names the list; Martin fights the devil, not eight spirits in order.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
"The seven deadly sins" — a later, different, seven-item list treated as a moral catalogue; "vices" as bad habits; the faults as psychology rather than as spirits to be fought.

**World Hearing:**
Eight named spirits in a fixed order of battle, each with a book and a handed-down remedy, beginning with the belly and ending with pride's wish to rule — Egypt's reckoning, received; and with the second fault's own book missing from this world's English text.
