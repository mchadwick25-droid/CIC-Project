## Quick Meaning

For this world humility is the root from which discretion and patience grow and the only ground on which power may be held — "attested by these signs," ten of them, in Cassian; "the true nobility" a count's son wins by hawking baskets; at Tours "the same humility in his heart" that a bishop keeps from his cell, sitting on a "rude little stool, such as those in use by the lowest of servants."

---

## World Meaning

Cassian makes it measurable. "Humility is attested by these signs: First of all if a man has all his desires mortified; secondly, if he conceals none of his actions or even of his thoughts from his senior," and so on through ten, ending in "love which knows no fear." It is what obedience buys — the count's son "was aiming at gaining through the grace of obedience that humility of Christ which is the true nobility" — and what a famous abbot goes looking for when honour has found him: Pinufius "sought a distant monastery where he could be received as a novice." It is the one source of discretion: "True discretion ... is only secured by true humility," and its first proof is to reserve every thought "for the scrutiny of the elders." Abbot Piamun: "True patience and tranquillity is neither gained nor retained without profound humility of heart." And Abbot Nesteros sets it above every wonder: since miracles are "not always necessary, nor good for all, nor granted to all," "Humility therefore is the mistress of all virtues, it is the surest foundation of the heavenly building." At the summit of the whole program the last book on pride teaches the monk to say of every virtue "Not I, but the grace of God with me" — "this then is that humility towards God."

At Tours humility is what does not change when a monk is made bishop. Martin "continued with the utmost constancy, the same as he had been before. There was the same humility in his heart, and the same homeliness in his garments." "When sitting in his retirement, he never used a chair; and, as to the church, no one ever saw him sitting there" — unlike a bishop Gallus had lately seen "seated on a lofty throne, yea, in its elevation, a kind of royal tribunal" — "but Martin might be seen sitting on a rude little stool, such as those in use by the lowest of servants, which we Gallic country-people call tripets"; "at eventide he himself washed" his guest's feet; the bishops who opposed him said "his clothing was mean, and his hair disgusting," and he kept both. Vincent names it as the offering he brings to the harbour: "propitiating God by the sacrifice of Christian humility." Gennadius remembers Sulpitius himself "by his devotion to poverty and by his humility." Across every voice it is the same thing: the one virtue that survives elevation.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
Modesty, self-deprecation, or low self-esteem; a temperament; or a virtue opposed to confidence and leadership.

**World Hearing:**
A virtue with ten checkable signs, won by obedience, that is the sole source of discretion and the "mistress" set above miracles; the thing a famous abbot flees his own house to recover; and at Tours the unchanged heart and garments of a bishop on a servant's stool.
