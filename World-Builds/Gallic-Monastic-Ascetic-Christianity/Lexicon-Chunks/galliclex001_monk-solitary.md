## Quick Meaning

For this world, a monk is a man who has cut himself off from marriage, kin, property and "the life of this world" for a stricter life in Christ's service — a severance that shows in his body and his dwelling, but that at Tours can be worn by a soldier still in the ranks and by a bishop in his see, who is yet "regarded ... as a monk."

## World Meaning

The word names a cut before it names a place. When the old man Piamun explains to two visiting Gauls where the name comes from, he says the first monks were those who "abstained from marriage and cut themselves off from intercourse with their kinsmen and the life of this world," and "were termed monks or solitaries from the strictness of their lonely and solitary life." That is Egypt's own account of the word, and Cassian carries it to Gaul as Egypt's. A monk is therefore recognizable not by where he sleeps but by what he has severed and how strictly he keeps the severance. His mark is that nothing is "called his own."

At Tours the word is worn by a man who has not yet left the army. Martin, still a soldier, shows such self-denial that "he was regarded not so much as being a soldier as a monk"; and when the people of Tours drag him out of his monastery and make him their bishop, he "kept up the position of a bishop properly, yet in such a way as not to lay aside the objects and virtues of a monk." He goes on using the cell by the church, then builds Marmoutier two miles out, between the river and the rock, where "there were altogether eighty disciples, who were being disciplined after the example of the saintly master." What a monk looks like there is plain: common property, no buying or selling, a single meal after the hour of fasting, no wine except in sickness, garments of camel's hair, and the judgment that "any dress approaching to softness was there deemed criminal." The young copy books; the old pray; almost no one leaves his cell except for the place of prayer. And the churches come to this place for their priests: "we have seen numbers of these afterwards made bishops." A monk at Tours is a severed man whom the Church has not let go of.

At Marseilles the word opens a book. Cassian's first sentence about the monk's life is that "a monk, then, as a soldier of Christ ever ready for battle, ought always to walk with his loins girded" — the monk is first dressed, then taught the psalms, then taught to lay bare his thoughts to a senior. The kinds of monk are Egypt's kinds, received: the coenobites "who live together in a congregation and are governed by the direction of a single Elder"; the anchorites, trained first in the coenobium and then gone out to the desert; and the Sarabaites, the "reprehensible" third kind who make "renunciation only as a public profession" and are their own masters. Piamun's taxonomy gives the south its word for a false monk. And the Gallic monk who reads all this knows he is not what Egypt is: of the Egyptians' lifelong perseverance Cassian says, "we cannot recollect any one who joined our monasteries keeping it up unbroken even for a year." To be a monk in Gaul is to keep a standard one already knows one falls short of, and to receive the fathers "into their cells" as a book, "talking with them after a fashion by daily questions and answers."

In the same city, Salvian — a presbyter who taught at Lérins and wrote for the lapsed Roman, not for the cell — hardly uses the word at all. His name for the same people is "the religious": "Religious men are lowly — they wish to be so; poor — they delight in poverty." When he does say "monks," he glosses it at once, describing the hatred of Africa "for the monks — that is, for the servants of God," a class the crowd at Carthage jeers at for their pallor and shaven heads. That is what the word sounds like to a Christian outside the monastery in Cassian's own Marseilles: a shorthand for God's servants, and a target.

What holds the word together across these usages is the severance, the strictness, and the fact that in this world the severed man is never out of the Church's reach. Vincent, who calls himself a "Peregrinus" dwelling "in the seclusion of a Monastery," does not use the word of himself in what he writes; the two men who do use it, Sulpitius and Cassian, agree without ever citing each other that a monk is made by what he gives up and known by how he keeps it. Neither of them has a written Rule to define him by.

## Ecological Function

This is the subject-term of the whole ecology: every other lexicon entry presupposes it. It carries the first half of G1 (Primary — the monk-bishop) as the thing G1 acts on, and it is the word in which G2's received kinds (coenobite / anchorite / Sarabaite), G7's aims ("the aim of every monk"), and G8's idiom ("a monk, as a soldier of Christ") are all spoken. Doc_05 §1.1 builds its Human Ecology on who this word names at each node, and §7.1 uses the Sarabaite as the world's own name for a monk without a boundary. A participant who understands "monk" here understands why "monk-bishop" is not a contradiction (G1), why "renunciation" is an entry and not an exit, why the Gallic monk speaks of Egypt as a standard rather than as himself, and why no one in this world can point to a Rule that defines him.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
A cloistered, celibate, professional religious under a written Rule and an abbot, withdrawn from public and civic life, medieval in dress and discipline — someone who by definition is not a soldier, not a bishop, and not to be found in a city.

**World Hearing:**
A severance visible in the body and the dwelling, compatible with army rank (Martin "regarded ... as a monk" while a soldier) and with the episcopate (a bishop who keeps "the objects and virtues of a monk"); a life whose kinds are named by Egypt and whose standard the Gallic monk knows he does not meet; no written Rule of this world's own; and, to a non-monk in the same city, simply "the servants of God."
