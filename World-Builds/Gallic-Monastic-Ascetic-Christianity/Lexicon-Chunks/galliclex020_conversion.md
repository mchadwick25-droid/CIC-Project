## Quick Meaning

In Cassian's Marseilles, "conversion" is not a change of religion but the act of turning to the monastic life — "the early days of their conversion were so bright," a murderer's "compulsory conversion" turned "into a voluntary one" — while at Tours the same English word names what a robber, a rustic, or a heathen household does when it turns to Christ.

---

## World Meaning

In the south the word marks the beginning of a monk. Abbot Paphnutius warns that a bright start guarantees nothing: to some "it was of no avail that the early days of their conversion were so bright, because they were not careful to bring the remainder of their life to a suitable end"; and Abbot Moses of Calamus, who fled to the desert to escape death for a murder, "made such use of his compulsory conversion that with ready zeal he turned it into a voluntary one and climbed the topmost heights of perfection." Germanus confesses that "in the fervour of the early days of my conversion I made light of the mere worldly substance" while the inner man stayed entangled. The formation manual's ladder puts it first: through the fear of the Lord "those who are trained in the way of perfection can gain a start in conversion as well as purification from vices and security in virtue." A conversion here is what a man does with his feet and his goods; its test is whether he keeps it warm. Salvian, in the same city, knows the same use and its failure: "the religious who have gained some reputation by a general repentance and now seek after new honors" — "do they not repent of their conversion?"; and of the man "professing physical continence" who runs "riot in incontinence of spirit": "this is not conversion to God but aversion from him." Salvian also uses the plain moral sense — "the conversion of one man does not atone for the sins of the many" — and the two lie side by side without confusion.

At Tours the word is what happens to others. Martin preaches to the robber who has captured him, and "the robber believed"; after the temples fall, "through his virtues and example that name has prevailed" where "very few, nay, almost none ... had received the name of Christ"; Martin "could not visit the house of an unconverted heathen." No one at Tours calls a man's entry to Marmoutier a conversion; the eighty are "disciples," and Martin's own turning is a discharge before Caesar. The south's word for the monastic beginning is simply not the north's word for it.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
A private inner event of coming to faith — evangelical conversion, "born again," a change of religion; and therefore a monk's "conversion" read as his becoming a Christian.

**World Hearing:**
In the south, the act of entering the monastic life, whether voluntary or "compulsory," judged by whether its early fervour is kept; in Salvian, the same act and its lapse ("aversion from him"); at Tours, a different referent altogether — a robber, a rustic, a household turning to Christ under Martin's preaching and power.
