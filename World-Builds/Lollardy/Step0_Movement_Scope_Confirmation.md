## 1. Candidate identification

Atlas V.5, "Lollardy," c. 1380–1520, England. Grew from the teaching of the Oxford theologian John Wycliffe — who attacked church wealth and papal authority, denied transubstantiation, and argued scripture should be available in English — but, per the census's own already-cleared floorNote, quickly outgrew him: its adherents were mostly ordinary people, artisans and household readers, who owned and shared vernacular scripture and met to read it aloud. Burning for heresy was made law in England in 1401; Archbishop Arundel's 1409 constitutions forbade unlicensed English scripture translation; Sir John Oldcastle's failed 1414 rising made Lollardy treason as well as heresy. The movement survived underground as scattered household networks into the 1520s.

Whatever their tag, none of these — the two genuinely "Verified," the one `[S]`-tagged, and the one uncited — have been checked for **accessibility**, only ever for content accuracy or not checked at all, and none of the four are, per direct host-checking this pass, freely available anywhere (archive.org, HathiTrust) — not usable as direct vendoring targets.

The census already names "the Latin works in the Wyclif Society editions"; Buddensieg's *Polemical Works* (1883) and Forshall & Madden's Wycliffite Bible (1850) **confirm** that existing census lead and its Bible lead respectively, rather than adding anything new. Only two of five candidate PD leads — Arnold's *Select English Works of John Wyclif* (1869–71, archive.org) and Vaughan's *Tracts and Treatises* (1845, `NOT_IN_COPYRIGHT`) — are genuinely not named in the census at all. Matthew's *English Works of Wyclif Hitherto Unprinted* (EETS 1880, archive.org) stands as a PD substitute for the in-copyright modern Wyclif corpus the census cited generally, not as a wholly new lead either. Vaughan belongs to an earlier, separate 1840s "Wycliffe Society," not the same body as the 1880s–1920s "Wyclif Society" the census names. **Same methodological caveat as every sibling document in this batch: no figure here is word-extracted from vendored XML.**

### B2 — Ecology

Strong on primary vernacular text (the Wycliffite Bible itself, ~250 surviving manuscripts, more than any other Middle English text). Arnold Vols. I–II are in fact the Sunday-Gospels/Proprium/Commune-Sanctorum/Ferial-Gospels/Sunday-Epistles preaching cycle — the same material Hudson & Gradon later edited separately as the collective *English Wycliffite Sermons* — and Arnold's own preface sets aside a "spurious and doubtful writings" section; modern scholarship generally treats this cycle, and much of Matthew's collection, as Wycliffite (collective) rather than Wyclif's own personal writing. The "movement's own later collective voice" this document treats as a still-open gap (the Twelve Conclusions, the anonymous Bible tracts the census names as "real internal texts") is narrower than it first appears — a substantial share of the already-vendored corpus is itself that collective voice, not Wyclif's own academic writing. None of the Twelve Conclusions/Bible-tracts material was independently confirmed for accessibility this pass, since the research brief focused on the four explicitly-flagged sources and the Wyclif-attributed corpus specifically; that gap stands as stated in §4 below.
