## 1. Candidate identification

Atlas V.5, "Lollardy," c. 1380–1520, England. Grew from the teaching of the Oxford theologian John Wycliffe — who attacked church wealth and papal authority, denied transubstantiation, and argued scripture should be available in English — but, per the census's own already-cleared floorNote, quickly outgrew him: its adherents were mostly ordinary people, artisans and household readers, who owned and shared vernacular scripture and met to read it aloud. Burning for heresy was made law in England in 1401; Archbishop Arundel's 1409 constitutions forbade unlicensed English scripture translation; Sir John Oldcastle's failed 1414 rising made Lollardy treason as well as heresy. The movement survived underground as scattered household networks into the 1520s.

## 3. Section B — Seed Screening and Tiering

**Framing note, adjusted as for Section A above:** the underlying Tier (Viable, secondary — Tier 2) is already on record from the Era 6 Step 0 run; what follows is this pass's own contribution to the sourcing half of that assessment, corrected against what the census actually verified versus merely cited.

### B1 — Sourcing: a real correction to the census's own prior record, disclosed in full

**The census's own citation practice conflated two different things, and this pass separates them.** Four sources the census marked "Verified" — Hudson's *Selections from English Wycliffite Writings* (1978), Hudson & Gradon's *English Wycliffite Sermons* (1983–96), Tanner's *Heresy Trials in the Diocese of Norwich* (1977), and Hudson's *Two Wycliffite Texts* (1993) — were verified only for **content accuracy** (that they accurately represent what the census says they contain), never for **actual accessibility**. All four are, per direct host-checking this pass, genuinely in copyright and not freely available anywhere (archive.org, HathiTrust) — not usable as direct vendoring targets as the census's own "Verified" tag might suggest to a reader who didn't check further.

**In their place, this pass found five genuinely PD, host-confirmed leads the census never named at all:** Arnold's *Select English Works of John Wyclif* (1869–71, archive.org), Matthew's *English Works of Wyclif Hitherto Unprinted* (EETS 1880, archive.org), Forshall & Madden's complete Wycliffite Bible (1850, archive.org, "unaware of any copyright restrictions"), the Wyclif Society's *Polemical Works* (Buddensieg, 1883, Latin, `NOT_IN_COPYRIGHT`), and Vaughan's *Tracts and Treatises* (1845, `NOT_IN_COPYRIGHT`) — the last a correction in its own right, since it belongs to an earlier, separate 1840s "Wycliffe Society," not the same body as the 1880s–1920s "Wyclif Society" the census names. **Same methodological caveat as every sibling document in this batch: no figure here is word-extracted from vendored XML.**

### B2 — Ecology

Strong on primary vernacular text (the Wycliffite Bible itself, ~250 surviving manuscripts, more than any other Middle English text) and on Wyclif's own academic corpus (Arnold, Matthew); genuinely uncertain, pending the acquisition items in §4, on the movement's own later collective voice (the Twelve Conclusions, the anonymous Bible tracts the census names as "real internal texts") — none of these were independently re-confirmed for accessibility this pass, since the research brief focused on the four explicitly-flagged sources and Wyclif's own corpus specifically.

### B3 — Uniqueness

**Versus the six already-built patristic worlds:** no overlap, over a millennium's separation. **Versus this batch's five Era VII candidates:** no figure or source overlap found — different century, different country, and (per the census's own already-cleared A5 note) only a contested, unresolved influence-line toward a world (the English Reformation) not currently part of this batch at all.

### B4 — Audiences

The double-witness structure the census itself already names as this candidate's own distinctive appeal: hostile trial records forced, by their own prosecutorial purpose, to write down what ordinary artisans and women actually believed, alongside the movement's own internal texts — a rarer combination than most candidates in this project offer, and the census's own `why` field already states this plainly as the reason the entry is "worth an entry on its own."
