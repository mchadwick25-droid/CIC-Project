## 1. Formation World Identity

**World Name:** Lollardy.

**Short Description:** An English vernacular reform movement, c. 1380–1520, that grew out of the Oxford theologian John Wyclif's attack on clerical wealth, papal and ecclesiastical authority, and the doctrine of transubstantiation, and his insistence that Scripture be available in English — but which, within a decade of its own origin, became something larger and more durable than its founder: a lay and artisan movement of household Bible-reading and mutual instruction, documented across a century and a half chiefly through the hostile trial records of the bishops who prosecuted it.

**Core Identity:** Lollardy is a movement rooted in one man's Oxford theology but not, by its own later period, organized or sustained by him or by any comparable clerical-academic authority. The census's own floor finding states this plainly: "the movement outlasted Wycliffe by well over a century, and what the trial records preserve are ordinary believers' own scripture-centred convictions rather than an Oxford theologian's system" (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §2, A3, quoting the census `floorNote`). The vendored corpus corroborates this at the level of primary evidence: the hostile trial record directly names, and lets speak, a tailor (John Badby), a layman the record itself calls "learned" — Walter Brute, who describes himself as "sinner, lay-man, husbandman, and a Christian ... of the Britons" (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, lines 11162–63; the bishop's own process calls him "a lay person, learned, of our diocese," line 10722) — and household networks of readers and hearers reaching into the 1520s (Foxe's account of "the good martyrs of Amersham," martyred "in the beginning of king Henry VIII" under Bishop Longland of Lincoln — `foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, line 32323 ff.) — **Confidence: Documented** for the vendored attestation; **Widely Accepted** for the broader scholarly characterization of Lollardy as predominantly a lay/artisan phenomenon by its later period.

**Distinctive Contribution — engaging the Hussite relationship directly, not deferring it.** `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §3 B3 discloses, honestly and without resolving it, that this world sits in a real, direct textual-dependency relationship with a separate, later-batch world — Step 0's own language (§3 B3, B5) is explicit that the Hussite and Bohemian Brethren candidate (Atlas V.6, drafted 2026-09-25) belongs to a different, later batch, not this document's own 2026-09-15 batch, so it is not a batch-mate — and not yet built at this document's own drafting date: the Hussite and Bohemian Brethren world's own Step 0 records that Jan Hus's *De Ecclesia* draws substantially on Wyclif's own writing, quoting Schaff's translator introduction that "Huss appropriated paragraph after paragraph from his predecessor and transferred them often with little verbal change to his own pages." This document does not minimize that dependency or attempt to resolve the cross-world corpus-map question it raises (§7 item 7 below carries that question forward, undecided, as Step 0 requires). What this document does argue is that direct textual dependency of one figure on another's writing is not the same claim as two formation worlds being ecologically redundant. Three features keep Lollardy its own distinct formation ecology despite the dependency:

- **A different, England-specific persecution regime, not merely a different regional flavor of the same repression.** Bohemia's Hussite crisis produced open war (the Hussite Wars, from 1420) and, eventually, a negotiated toleration (the Compacts of Basel/Prague) for a plural ecclesial settlement. England's own regime is structurally different: a *statutory* fusion of heresy and capital punishment (the 1401 *De heretico comburendo*, making burning for heresy the law of the land — `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §1), reinforced by Archbishop Arundel's 1409 Constitutions, which forbade both unlicensed translation of Scripture into English and unlicensed preaching generally (ibid.), and then — after Sir John Oldcastle's failed 1414 rising — a further fusion with the law of *treason* specifically, so that Lollardy is prosecuted simultaneously as heresy against the church and sedition against the crown in a way Hussite Bohemia's own trajectory (toward civil war and eventual negotiated plurality) does not parallel. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**
- **The vernacular Bible project's own manuscript-survival scale, held open rather than compared.** The Wycliffite Bible — the complete translation project vendored here in the Forshall & Madden (1850) edition — survives in roughly 250 manuscripts, more than any other Middle English text (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §3 B2, citing the census). This document does not claim nothing comparable exists in the Hussite world's own corpus: the Hussite world's own Step 0 records Czech-vernacular preaching from 1402 and a vernacular hymnbook by 1501, so a vernacular-access tradition of some kind is on record there too, and this document has not checked whether a comparably scaled manuscript tradition also stands behind it. What is documented, and narrower than a cross-world comparison, is the scale of the Wycliffite Bible's own English manuscript survival on its own terms, and that Hus's own vendored textual dependency runs specifically through a Latin ecclesiological treatise (*De Ecclesia*), not through the vernacular-Scripture project this document is describing. — **Confidence: Documented** for the manuscript-survival claim as already established in Step 0; **held open** for any comparative claim about the Hussite world's own corpus, which this document does not have in hand to check.
- **A social composition the record itself, not inference, establishes as different in kind.** The Twelve Conclusions of the Lollards (1395), vendored here in their Latin trial-record form inside *Fasciculi Zizaniorum*, are addressed to "the full parliament of the realm of England" in a collective, unnamed voice — "*nos procuratores Dei*," "we, God's proctors" (`fasciculi-zizaniorum_shirley1858.txt`, heading at line 28381 ff.; corollary at line 28603) — not circulated as an academic disputation. The heading names no individual author, and whether the Conclusions' own authors were lay or clerical is not itself settled by the vendored text; any public posting, and any link to the "Lollard knights," rests on chronicle tradition outside this document's own vendored corpus. The trial records of Thorpe (1407), Badby (1410), and the Amersham household networks (1500s–1520s) are, by contrast, the movement's own primary surviving evidentiary mode — a household and artisan ecology visible because it was prosecuted, not because it left behind a clerical or princely archive of its own. This is a different social shape from the Hussite movement's own university-and-noble-led trajectory into open war. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** at most that the Conclusions were addressed to Parliament in this collective form; **Contested/held open** whether their own authorship was lay, clerical, or mixed, and whether this comparative claim about the Hussite world's own social composition holds, since this document does not have that world's own Doc_01 in hand to check against — both named as limits on this comparison, not resolved here.

**Living Tradition Status.** **PENDING.** Per Constitution Article 29, Living Tradition Status confirmation is a project-lead act, not something this document can perform on its own behalf. Unlike the Coptic Orthodox Church's direct institutional descent from Alexandrian Christianity, or the papacy's and Eastern Orthodoxy's own traceable continuity from Imperial and Juridical Christianity's two primacy-claiming strands, Lollardy left no comparable surviving ecclesial body of its own — its household networks were suppressed, not institutionally transformed. The only continuity claim on record is the census's own, carefully unresolved one: a "Contested influence line to the English Reformation" (`relationsSummary`, quoted at `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §2, A5). Whether that contested line to the (much later, and not-yet-built) English Reformation world is itself the kind of contact with a "living tradition" Article 29 contemplates — as opposed to a contact with another *historical* formation world whose own living-tradition question is separately its own to answer — is not obvious, and is flagged here as its own open question for the project lead, not assumed either way. **Status: PENDING project-lead determination, including of whether Article 29 squarely applies to this world at all.**

## 2. Historical Boundaries

**Time Horizon:** c. 1380–1520 CE, carried forward exactly as `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §1 and the Source Readiness Dossier both already fix it (Atlas V.5: "c. 1380–1520, England"). This document does not reopen that window; it argues why it is a defensible one.

**Beginning point (c. 1380), defended.** The defensible beginning is not Wyclif's own earlier Oxford career as an academic critic of ecclesiastical wealth and papal authority (active from the 1360s, sharpened in works like *De Civili Dominio* from the mid-1370s) — that phase belongs to the larger world Lollardy emerged from, not to Lollardy itself (§5 below). The ecological rupture that actually opens this world falls in a tight cluster, c. 1379–1382: Wyclif's own turn to the specific, sacrament-centered doctrine that defines Lollard dissent going forward — denial of transubstantiation, argued in his own *De Eucharistia* and *Confessio* (traditionally dated 1379–1381) — the beginning of the vernacular Bible translation project his circle undertook (the Earlier Version, conventionally dated from the early-to-mid 1380s), and the Blackfriars ("Earthquake") Council of May 1382, at which Archbishop Courtenay's council condemned twenty-four conclusions drawn from Wyclif's teaching and moved the controversy out of Oxford's own academic confines into a matter of parliamentary and national concern. This last event is independently corroborated in this world's own vendored corpus, in two separate editorial notes, each dating a different tract: Arnold's headnote to the "Seven Werkys of Mercy Bodyly" tract states it was "composed, it would seem, not long after the holding of the council convened at London by Archbishop Courtenay in May, 1382" (`wyclif_select-english-works-v3_arnold1871.txt`, lines 10741–42); and a separate footnote to a tract on the friars' denial that tithes are pure alms states that "this allusion to the earthquake 1382, on May 19 (*Fasc. Zizaniorum*, p. 272), fixes so far the date of this treatise" (ibid., lines 11189–91). Neither note says the council condemned anything; what each supports is a dating of its own tract to the months around the May 1382 council and earthquake. The condemnation of the twenty-four conclusions at that council stands on its own as **Widely Accepted**, not as something these two notes corroborate. Before this cluster, what existed was an Oxford academic's escalating critique of ecclesiastical wealth and authority; after it, a doctrinally distinct, publicly condemned, and increasingly lay-transmitted movement with its own defining artifact (the vernacular Bible) underway. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the cluster of dates; **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for treating this cluster, rather than a single event, as the defensible ecological beginning.

**Ending point (c. 1520), defended.** Step 0 itself states that "the movement survived underground as scattered household networks into the 1520s" (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §1). This document's own vendored corpus reaches into that period, though only as a forward reference rather than a trial record of its own: Foxe, arguing against Cope elsewhere in the text, mentions in passing that "the good martyrs of Amersham" suffered death "in the beginning of king Henry VIII" (whose reign began in 1509), naming Longland as "then bishop of Lincoln" (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, lines 32322–29) — Longland held that see only from 1521, which dates this attestation to 1521–22, not earlier. Foxe himself defers the full account to a later volume not vendored here: "as when we come to that time ... shall hereafter more amply and notoriously appear" (ibid., lines 32331–33). Ending the window at c. 1520, close to but just before this vendored attestation's own 1521–22 date, keeps this world upstream of the census's own named, unresolved contest: "Contested influence line to the English Reformation" (`relationsSummary`, `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §2, A5). This document, like the census before it, does not decide, here or anywhere in this document, whether or how far Lollard household networks fed directly into the early English Reformation; it ends the world's own window at the point where that question becomes live, rather than assuming an answer by where the window is drawn. — **Confidence: Documented** for the vendored sentence itself, as a forward reference rather than a trial record; **Contested** for any claim about what, if anything, connects it forward to the Reformation proper.

**Geographic Centers:** Oxford, London, East Anglia, and the Chilterns.
- **Oxford:** the movement's own point of origin — Wyclif's academic base, and the site of his condemnation and the expulsion of his Oxford circle (Nicholas Hereford, Philip Repingdon, John Aston) following the 1382 Blackfriars Council. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**
- **London:** the seat of Parliament, to which the Twelve Conclusions were addressed in 1395 (`fasciculi-zizaniorum_shirley1858.txt`, line 28381 ff.: *pleno parliamento regni Anglie*, "the full parliament of the realm of England" — the heading itself does not name London specifically), and the seat of the 1401 parliament at which Sautre was tried, per Foxe's own account of "a parliament holden at Westminster" (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, line 18217 ff.). The 1414 statute, following Oldcastle's rising, was enacted at a parliament held at Leicester, not London. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** that Parliament sat at Westminster/London for the 1395 and 1401 proceedings; **Documented** only that the Twelve Conclusions were addressed to "the full parliament," per the vendored heading itself.
- **East Anglia:** the site of the Norwich diocesan heresy trials of 1428–31. Step 0 §3 B1 corrects the census's own prior sourcing tags directly: Tanner's *Heresy Trials in the Diocese of Norwich, 1428-31* was one of only two Lollardy sources ever carrying the census's own "Verified" tag, but the volume itself remains borrow-only and has not been vendored or independently content-checked by this build; what is independently on record is the trials' existence and date range, not the volume's own content (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §3 B1, §4.1). — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the trials' existence and date range; the volume's own content is not independently checked here.
- **The Chilterns:** the site of the Amersham household networks Foxe records as suffering "in the beginning of king Henry VIII" for possessing and reading vernacular Scripture (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, line 32323 ff.) — this world's own latest directly-vendored geographic attestation. — **Confidence: Documented**, per the vendored citation.

**Cultural Environment:** A late-medieval English Church operating in Latin liturgy and Latin/scholastic academic argument at Oxford, against a lay world of growing, though still limited, vernacular literacy. Household reading circles — families and neighbors gathering to hear vernacular Scripture read aloud, sometimes led by literate laity, sometimes by a single trained reader for an otherwise unlettered group — are this world's own most distinctive site of formation, attested directly by the trial records (the Amersham prosecutions; Thorpe's own Examination attests individual preaching and priesthood, not household reading circles specifically, and is not cited for that claim here). Trade and craft networks recur in the record as a social substrate for the movement's transmission (Badby is recorded as a tailor by trade — `foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, line 19229 ff.). The Twelve Conclusions' own ninth conclusion, on confession, has lords and ladies ("*domini et domi[n]ae*," rendered in the vendored OCR as "domiui et domiure," `fasciculi-zizaniorum_shirley1858.txt`, line 28735) attest that they dare not tell their confessors the truth for fear of them — evidence of penitents generally resisting confession, not, on its own, evidence of women's participation in Lollardy specifically. — **Confidence: Documented** for the named individuals and their trades; **Inferential-Thin** for the interior experience of ordinary household participants, who are visible only through the shaping lens of prosecutorial record (Article 20 concern, carried forward at §3 and §7 below).

**Historical Pressures:** The 1401 statute *De heretico comburendo*, making burning for heresy the law of the land (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §1); Archbishop Arundel's 1409 Constitutions, forbidding unlicensed translation of Scripture into English and unlicensed preaching generally (ibid.); and, after 1414, the fusion of heresy with treason following Oldcastle's rising, which made the movement's own survival a matter of political as well as ecclesiastical danger. Foxe's own text paraphrases the constitution's prohibition rather than quoting it directly, in his polemic against Cope: "the constitution provincial of Thomas Arundel above-mentioned, where it was decreed, that the text of holy Scripture should not be had, or read in the vulgar tongue, from the time of Master John WicklifF for cvcr after, unless the said translation be approved first by the ordinary, or by provincial council, under pain and punishment of heresy" (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, lines 32299–304; the vendored OCR itself renders "ever" as "cvcr," reproduced here rather than silently corrected). — **Confidence: Documented** that this is Foxe's own paraphrase of the constitution's content, independently verified against the vendored text; **Widely Accepted** for the statutes' dates and general effect.

**Historical Catalysts** (dated):
- **1377** — Pope Gregory XI's bulls condemning propositions drawn from Wyclif's own writing on dominion and church authority. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**
- **1382 (May)** — The Blackfriars ("Earthquake") Council, under Archbishop Courtenay, condemns twenty-four conclusions drawn from Wyclif's teaching and expels his Oxford adherents. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted**, independently corroborated in Arnold's own vendored editorial apparatus (see beginning-point discussion above).
- **1384** — Wyclif's death, at Lutterworth. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**
- **1391–93** — The trial of Walter Brute, whom the bishop of Hereford's own process calls "a lay person, learned, of our diocese," and who describes himself as "sinner, lay-man, husbandman, and a Christian ... of the Britons"; required by the bishop to answer in Latin, Brute writes that he "was required that I should write an answer in Latin to all those matters" (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, lines 10690–91, 10722, 11162–66). Foxe also records "a Letter of the King against Walter Brute" dated 1392 (index at line 327). — **Confidence: Documented.**
- **1395** — The Twelve Conclusions of the Lollards posted publicly and addressed to Parliament. — **Confidence: Documented**, per the vendored Latin text.
- **1401** — William Sautre, tried before Convocation and degraded, was burned by royal writ ("Teste Rege, apud Westm. 26th Feb.," `foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, line 18837) in the same parliament that went on to enact the statute *De heretico comburendo* — the burning itself precedes the statute's own enactment, not the reverse, so Sautre was not executed under it. He is nonetheless the first Lollard burned as a heretic in this world's own window. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the sequence (writ before statute); the Foxe account itself carries an old-style regnal date ("1400," itself an OCR-garbled marginal reading, `a.d.hoo`, for what modern reckoning gives as 1401) for the trial proceedings that immediately precede the execution (line 18218 ff.), a discrepancy of calendar convention and OCR rather than of fact, noted here rather than silently reconciled.
- **1407** — William Thorpe's Examination before Archbishop Arundel, "penned with his own hand" (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, line 20623). — **Confidence: Documented.**
- **1409** — Archbishop Arundel's Constitutions take effect. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**
- **1410** — John Badby, a tailor, burned as a heretic for denying transubstantiation (Foxe's own heading dates the proceeding "1409," an old-style-calendar discrepancy of the same kind noted above for Sautre). — **Confidence: Documented** for the trial and its content; **Widely Accepted** for the conventional modern date of the execution itself.
- **1414** — Sir John Oldcastle's failed revolt, after which Lollardy is prosecuted as treason as well as heresy. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**
- **1428–31** — The Norwich diocesan heresy trials. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted**, per the already-verified bibliographic record (§ Geographic Centers, above).
- **c. 1521–22** — The Amersham (Chiltern) household-network prosecutions, Foxe's own forward reference to "the good martyrs of Amersham" under Bishop Longland of Lincoln, who held that see only from 1521; this world's own latest directly-vendored attestation, though as a passing mention rather than a trial record (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, lines 32322–29; see the Ending point discussion above). — **Confidence: Documented** for the vendored sentence itself.

## 3. Distinct World Criteria

Applying the Framework's own four questions directly:

- **What recurring formation ecology exists?** A household and lay-network ecology of vernacular Scripture reading, mutual instruction, and itinerant or informal teaching, recurring across the full 1380–1520 window and documented almost entirely through the paradox of hostile process: the same bishops' courts that prosecuted this world's own participants are also the reason their words, and something of their own convictions, survive at all. A second, narrower recurring ecology — an individually-argued, personally-authored register of engagement with authority, distinct from the household register above — also recurs: public doctrinal proclamation addressed to Parliament (the Twelve Conclusions, 1395) and formal personal examination before ecclesiastical authority (Thorpe's own Examination before Archbishop Arundel, 1407, not itself addressed to Parliament) both sit alongside the household ecology as a distinct, more articulate register of the same underlying movement (§4 below develops this as a strand question).
- **What recurring gravities exist (preliminary, subject to Doc_04)?** Provisionally, and named here only as candidates for Step 4 testing, not as confirmed findings: scriptural sufficiency and lay access to Scripture in the vernacular; a reinterpreted eucharistic theology (Conclusion IV holds that the sacrament of the altar is "habitually" (*habitualiter*) Christ's body rather than transformed by "a feigned miracle," reporting — not quoting — what it calls "the Gospel doctor's" own *Trialogus* on this point — `fasciculi-zizaniorum_shirley1858.txt`, lines 28485–28523); and anticlerical suspicion of clerical wealth, temporal office, and sacramental gatekeeping (confession, indulgences, and the "power of the keys" are all three named together within Conclusion IX of the same text). These are named as candidates only; Doc_04 performs the six-test assessment Doc_01 does not.
- **What recurring patterns of worship, formation, interpretation, belonging, and authority exist?** Vernacular Scripture access is this world's own single most recurring and best-evidenced pattern, running from the translation project itself through the trial records' own repeated emphasis on what the accused had read, owned, or heard read aloud. Rejection of transubstantiation recurs as a specific, repeatedly prosecuted doctrinal marker (Sautre, Badby, and the Twelve Conclusions' own Conclusion IV all name it directly). Anticlericalism recurs across register — the Twelve Conclusions' own opening conclusions against church endowments in "temporalities" and against a priesthood "feigned with power higher than the angels'" (*fictum potestate angelis altiori*), a power not warranted by the New Testament (`fasciculi-zizaniorum_shirley1858.txt`, lines 28391 ff., 28415 ff.) — and, in a more diffuse form, across the trial records' own repeated emphasis on lay suspicion of priestly authority over confession, penance, and the dead. Rejection of pilgrimage and image veneration also recurs, at least in the Twelve Conclusions' own articulation (Conclusion VIII: pilgrimages and offerings to "dumb images of wood or stone" are "near akin to idolatry," and an image of the Trinity specifically "most abominable" — ibid., lines 28674 ff.), though this document does not assume every Lollard defendant across the full window held this specific position with equal emphasis; that proportionality question belongs to Doc_04/Doc_05. — **Confidence: Documented** for each cited textual instance; **Contested/held open** for how uniformly any one of these held across the movement's full social and geographic range.
- **What distinguishes this world from adjacent worlds?** From the Hussite and Bohemian Brethren world specifically: see §1 above (Distinctive Contribution) and §6 below. From this project's other confirmed and built worlds — none of which (per a direct check of `worlds/`: `alx`, `cappadocian`, `desert`, `don`, `gallic`, `grkap`, `hal`, `ijc`, `latap`, `lpc`, `pahc`, `rzg`, `syr`) is a vernacular-reform, trial-record-anchored English movement of this shape — there is no overlap risk requiring vigilance. The nearest built comparison in this project's current portfolio is Lutheran Wittenberg (`witt`), a batch-mate whose own Doc_01 grounds its distinctiveness in confessional/catechetical doctrine and a territorial, princely-sponsored magisterial Reformation (`witt_Doc_01_World_Identification_Boundaries_Orientation.md` §1). Lollardy differs from it on the same three grounds already argued for the Hussite comparison in §1 — a domestic statutory persecution regime rather than territorial establishment, a manuscript vernacular Bible rather than a confessional-document tradition as its defining artifact, and a lay/artisan household social base rather than a princely-and-university-led one — and, additionally, on simple chronology: this world's own 1380–1520 window precedes Wittenberg's 1517–1580 window by well over a century, overlapping only in its final years and only as the still-unresolved influence-line question named throughout this document.

## 4. Strand Determination (Article 21)

Per Constitution Article 21, strand is "a finding, never a presupposed universal schema," accountable to evidence and "never assigned to satisfy an architectural preference for plurality." The census's own floor finding — "the movement outlasted Wycliffe by well over a century" — is a temporal-development finding (Article 15, Historical Development and Instability), not by itself a strand finding, and this document does not treat it as one. Simply noting that Lollardy's later, more socially diffuse lay/artisan form came *after* Wyclif's own Oxford phase would be exactly the sequential-development error Article 21 warns against; it would show only that the movement developed over time, which every formation world does. Strand determination instead asks whether the evidence shows a genuinely *simultaneous* divergence in authority structure or ecological orientation, not a mere succession of one phase by another.

**Finding: two strands identified, on evidence drawn from inside this world's own window, attested simultaneously rather than sequentially.** The distinction is not "early Oxford academic phase" followed by "later lay phase," and it is not a distinction of clerical versus lay status: it is a distinction in how authority is grounded and defended when challenged — by one person's own sustained, individually-argued mastery of doctrine, or by a household's or company's shared, communal access to Scripture in English. Both registers are attested within the window at overlapping dates, not one following the other's close.

- **Strand A — Individually-argued strand.** Authority grounded in one named person's own sustained, personally-authored argument, defended directly before ecclesiastical authority under formal process. This mode of argument originates in Wyclif's own pre-window Latin ecclesiology (§5 below), but this strand's own within-window evidence is independent of that origin and independent of clerical or lay status: Walter Brute, a layman the bishop's own process calls "learned," was required by the bishop of Hereford to "write an answer in Latin to all those matters" and did so, over his own name, in 1391–93 (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, lines 10690–91, 11162–66); John Purvey, a priest who led the Wycliffite Bible's own revision through the 1390s, personally composed his own Latin confession and recantation before Archbishop Arundel and his provincial council, dated by the vendored *Fasciculi* to "Feb. 29," under the marginal old-style year "1400" (so early 1401 by modern reckoning, the same old-style/new-style discrepancy already noted for Sautre) — "*Ego Johannes Purvey, presbyter indignus* ..." (`fasciculi-zizaniorum_shirley1858.txt`, lines 30600–17); Foxe separately records that Purvey "recanted at Paul's cross" (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, line 20316 ff.); and William Thorpe, also a priest, personally composed his own first-person account of his 1407 examination before Archbishop Arundel, defending "the office of priesthood" he says he has "taken upon" himself (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, lines 20623, 21340). A lay landholder and two priests all fall in this strand together, because what unites them is the mode of argument — personal, sustained, textually reasoned, staked on that one person's own authority — not their status.
- **Strand B — Communal-access strand.** Authority grounded not in any one person's argued mastery but in a household's or company's shared, direct access to Scripture in English, defended, where defended at all, collectively rather than through sustained individual argument. The Twelve Conclusions (1395) speak throughout in a collective, anonymous voice — "*nos procuratores Dei*," "we, God's proctors" (`fasciculi-zizaniorum_shirley1858.txt`, line 28603) — naming no individual author; John Badby, a tailor, was briefly examined and burned in 1410 without leaving a sustained personal composition of his own; the Norwich diocesan trials (1428–31) and the Amersham household reading-networks (1521–22, corrected at §2 above) both proceed against households and companies as such, examined and prosecuted collectively, in the fullest and latest-attested register of this world's own vendored corpus.

**Simultaneity, not sequence.** Brute's own sustained Latin defense (1391–93) predates the Twelve Conclusions' own collective voice (1395) by only a few years, and both continue to be prosecuted well into the fifteenth century's first decade: Thorpe's own individually-argued Examination (1407) falls three years before Badby's collective-mode trial (1410), not after a completed transition from one register to the other. What this document discloses rather than conceals is that the individually-argued strand's own directly-vendored instances thin out after Thorpe (1407) — this corpus does not preserve a comparably sustained, personally-authored individual defense later in the window — while the communal strand's own attestation continues unbroken to the window's 1521–22 close. This is a fact about which kind of record the trial process happened to produce and preserve at each later date, not a claim that one strand caused, produced, or absorbed the other; it is carried at Confidence: Contested, not asserted as settled. Middle English homiletic material (Arnold's Vols. I–II, Matthew's collection) and the Wycliffite Bible translation project's own eventual communal use both belong to the communal strand's own resources, whatever the status of their individual compilers or revisers — the strand distinction turns on how authority is grounded and defended, not on who performed the underlying labor of composition or translation. — **Confidence: Documented** for each named figure's own vendored self-description and mode of engagement with authority; **Contested** for the asymmetry in how long each strand's own direct attestation happens to run within the window.

**Governing consequence for later steps:** cross-strand gravity testing occurs formally at Step 4. A candidate gravity holding across both strands (for example, the shared conviction that Scripture's authority is supreme over ecclesiastical mediation, however differently each strand argues for or lives out that conviction) is a stronger candidate for this world's irreducible core than one attested only in one strand's own register.

## 6. World Continuity & Distinction

**What this world inherits:** Wyclif's own Oxford theology of dominion-by-grace, his denial of transubstantiation, and his conviction that Scripture's own authority is supreme over ecclesiastical tradition — all already developed, in nascent form, before this world's own window opens, and taken up, transformed, and carried forward by a movement that in time exceeded him. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**

**What this world transmits forward:** Two things, held at different confidence levels and not conflated. First, and with direct, named textual evidence, outward rather than forward in time: Wyclif's own Latin ecclesiology feeds directly into Jan Hus's *De Ecclesia*, per the Hussite world's own Step 0 record (§1 above). This is transmission *outward*, to a contemporary, geographically separate movement — not this world's own later inheritance, and not something this document resolves as a matter of corpus-map assignment (§7 below carries that question forward). Second, and far more cautiously: the census's own `relationsSummary` names a "Contested influence line to the English Reformation" — a claim this document states in the census's own careful language rather than its own. This document does not assert, anywhere above, that Lollard household networks caused, seeded, or substantially shaped the English Reformation; it names that as a live, unresolved historiographical question, carried forward rather than answered. — **Confidence: Documented** for the Wyclif-to-Hus textual dependency (per the Hussite world's own already-cited record); **Contested**, explicitly, for the English-Reformation influence line.

**Relationship to the Hussite and Bohemian Brethren world:** transmission outward, not inheritance received. Lollardy does not derive from the Hussite movement; Wyclif's own writing predates and partly generates Hus's. This document does not import Hussite formation logic, vocabulary, or ecological findings into Lollardy's own construction, nor does it treat the shared textual root as license to do so — per Constitution Article 3's own governing distinction, a genuine historical transmission relationship between two worlds is not the same thing as "cross-world contamination" in the constitutional sense, which concerns a *builder* importing another world's own vocabulary or formation logic into a world it does not belong to. The transmission here is a fact about the fifteenth century, not a construction shortcut available to whoever builds either world.

**Living heir(s):** none mapped. Per Article 28/29 discipline (§1 above), this document names no living institutional heir for Lollardy — unlike Alexandria's Coptic Orthodox Church or Imperial and Juridical Christianity's papal and Orthodox continuations, no comparable surviving ecclesial body traces its own institutional descent to this world. The only continuity claim on record is the contested Reformation influence-line named above, itself unconfirmed and not self-confirmed here.
