## 1. Formation World Identity

**World Name:** Lollardy.

**Short Description:** An English vernacular reform movement, c. 1380–1520, that grew out of the Oxford theologian John Wyclif's attack on clerical wealth, papal and ecclesiastical authority, and the doctrine of transubstantiation, and his insistence that Scripture be available in English — but which, within a decade of its own origin, became something larger and more durable than its founder: a lay and artisan movement of household Bible-reading and mutual instruction, documented across a century and a half chiefly through the hostile trial records of the bishops who prosecuted it.

**Core Identity:** Lollardy is a movement rooted in one man's Oxford theology but not, by its own later period, organized or sustained by him or by any comparable clerical-academic authority. The census's own floor finding states this plainly: "the movement outlasted Wycliffe by well over a century, and what the trial records preserve are ordinary believers' own scripture-centred convictions rather than an Oxford theologian's system" (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §2, A3, quoting the census `floorNote`). The vendored corpus corroborates this at the level of primary evidence: the hostile trial record directly names, and lets speak, a tailor (John Badby), a lay Herefordshire landholder (Walter Brute), and household networks of readers and hearers reaching into the 1520s (Foxe's account of "the good martyrs of Amersham," martyred "in the beginning of king Henry VIII" under Bishop Longland of Lincoln — `foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, line 32323 ff.) — **Confidence: Documented** for the vendored attestation; **Widely Accepted** for the broader scholarly characterization of Lollardy as predominantly a lay/artisan phenomenon by its later period.

**Distinctive Contribution — engaging the Hussite relationship directly, not deferring it.** `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §3 B3 discloses, honestly and without resolving it, that this world sits in a real, direct textual-dependency relationship with a batch-mate world not yet built at this document's own drafting date: the Hussite and Bohemian Brethren world's own Step 0 records that Jan Hus's *De Ecclesia* draws substantially on Wyclif's own writing, quoting Schaff's translator introduction that "Huss appropriated paragraph after paragraph from his predecessor and transferred them often with little verbal change to his own pages." This document does not minimize that dependency or attempt to resolve the cross-world corpus-map question it raises (§7 item 7 below carries that question forward, undecided, as Step 0 requires). What this document does argue is that direct textual dependency of one figure on another's writing is not the same claim as two formation worlds being ecologically redundant. Three features keep Lollardy its own distinct formation ecology despite the dependency:

- **A different, England-specific persecution regime, not merely a different regional flavor of the same repression.** Bohemia's Hussite crisis produced open war (the Hussite Wars, from 1420) and, eventually, a negotiated toleration (the Compacts of Basel/Prague) for a plural ecclesial settlement. England's own regime is structurally different: a *statutory* fusion of heresy and capital punishment (the 1401 *De heretico comburendo*, making burning for heresy the law of the land — `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §1), reinforced by Archbishop Arundel's 1409 Constitutions forbidding unlicensed English Scripture translation and preaching (ibid.), and then — after Sir John Oldcastle's failed 1414 rising — a further fusion with the law of *treason* specifically, so that Lollardy is prosecuted simultaneously as heresy against the church and sedition against the crown in a way Hussite Bohemia's own trajectory (toward civil war and eventual negotiated plurality) does not parallel. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**
- **The vernacular Bible project as this world's own defining artifact, not a shared one.** The Wycliffite Bible — the complete translation project vendored here in the Forshall & Madden (1850) edition — survives in roughly 250 manuscripts, more than any other Middle English text (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §3 B2, citing the census). Nothing comparable anchors the Hussite world's own corpus; Hus's own textual dependency runs through a Latin ecclesiological treatise (*De Ecclesia*), not a vernacular Scripture-translation project of this scale. The vernacular Bible, not the Latin ecclesiology it shares a debt to, is Lollardy's own most distinctive surviving artifact. — **Confidence: Documented** for the manuscript-survival claim as already established in Step 0; **Widely Accepted** for its significance as this world's defining feature.
- **A social composition the record itself, not inference, establishes as different in kind.** The Twelve Conclusions of the Lollards (1395), vendored here in their Latin trial-record form inside *Fasciculi Zizaniorum*, were posted publicly and addressed to Parliament by named lay adherents, not circulated as an academic disputation (`fasciculi-zizaniorum_shirley1858.txt`, heading at line 28381 ff.: "SEQUUNTUR CONCLUSIONES LOLLARDORUM IN QUODAM LIBELLO PORRECTAE PLENO PARLIAMENTO REGNI ANGLIE... ANNO EJUS CIRCITER XVIII" — roughly the eighteenth year of Richard II, c. 1394–95). The trial records of Thorpe (1407), Badby (1410), and the Amersham household networks (1500s–1520s) are, by contrast, the movement's own primary surviving evidentiary mode — a lay, artisan, household ecology visible because it was prosecuted, not because it left behind a clerical or princely archive of its own. This is a different social shape from the Hussite movement's own university-and-noble-led trajectory into open war. — **Confidence: Documented** for the vendored trial evidence; **Contested/held open** whether this comparative claim about the Hussite world's own social composition holds, since this document does not have that world's own Doc_01 in hand to check against — named as a limit on this comparison, not resolved here.

**Living Tradition Status.** **PENDING.** Per Constitution Article 29, Living Tradition Status confirmation is a project-lead act, not something this document can perform on its own behalf. Unlike the Coptic Orthodox Church's direct institutional descent from Alexandrian Christianity, or the papacy's and Eastern Orthodoxy's own traceable continuity from Imperial and Juridical Christianity's two primacy-claiming strands, Lollardy left no comparable surviving ecclesial body of its own — its household networks were suppressed, not institutionally transformed. The only continuity claim on record is the census's own, carefully unresolved one: a "Contested influence line to the English Reformation" (`relationsSummary`, quoted at `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §2, A5). Whether that contested, many-times-removed line to the (much later, and not-yet-built) English Reformation world is itself the kind of contact with a "living tradition" Article 29 contemplates — as opposed to a contact with another *historical* formation world whose own living-tradition question is separately its own to answer — is not obvious, and is flagged here as its own open question for the project lead, not assumed either way. **Status: PENDING project-lead determination, including of whether Article 29 squarely applies to this world at all.**

## 2. Historical Boundaries

**Time Horizon:** c. 1380–1520 CE, carried forward exactly as `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §1 and the Source Readiness Dossier both already fix it (Atlas V.5: "c. 1380–1520, England"). This document does not reopen that window; it argues why it is a defensible one.

**Beginning point (c. 1380), defended.** The defensible beginning is not Wyclif's own earlier Oxford career as an academic critic of ecclesiastical wealth and papal authority (active from the 1360s, sharpened in works like *De Civili Dominio* from the mid-1370s) — that phase belongs to the larger world Lollardy emerged from, not to Lollardy itself (§5 below). The ecological rupture that actually opens this world falls in a tight cluster, c. 1379–1382: Wyclif's own turn to the specific, sacrament-centered doctrine that defines Lollard dissent going forward — denial of transubstantiation, argued in his own *De Eucharistia* and *Confessio* (traditionally dated 1379–1381) — the beginning of the vernacular Bible translation project his circle undertook (the Earlier Version, conventionally dated from the early-to-mid 1380s), and the Blackfriars ("Earthquake") Council of May 1382, at which Archbishop Courtenay's council condemned twenty-four conclusions drawn from Wyclif's teaching and moved the controversy out of Oxford's own academic confines into a matter of parliamentary and national concern. This last event is independently corroborated in this world's own vendored corpus: Arnold's editorial apparatus to Wyclif's Middle English sermons dates several to the same moment and names the same council directly — "This allusion to the earthquake 1382 ... [condemned] at London by Archbishop Courtenay in May, 1382" (`wyclif_select-english-works-v3_arnold1871.txt`, lines 10742 and 11189 ff.). Before this cluster, what existed was an Oxford academic's escalating critique of ecclesiastical wealth and authority; after it, a doctrinally distinct, publicly condemned, and increasingly lay-transmitted movement with its own defining artifact (the vernacular Bible) underway. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the cluster of dates; **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for treating this cluster, rather than a single event, as the defensible ecological beginning.

**Ending point (c. 1520), defended.** Step 0 itself states that "the movement survived underground as scattered household networks into the 1520s" (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §1). This document's own vendored corpus reaches exactly that far and no further on its own evidence: Foxe records the prosecution of household Lollard reading-networks at Amersham, in the Chilterns, "in the beginning of king Henry VIII" — that is, in the first two decades of the sixteenth century — under Bishop Longland of Lincoln (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, line 32323 ff.). Ending the window here, rather than reaching forward into the 1520s' own Lutheran-influenced controversies or the Henrician Reformation proper, keeps this world upstream of the census's own named, unresolved contest: "Contested influence line to the English Reformation" (`relationsSummary`, `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §2, A5). This document, like the census before it, "carries the contest rather than the conclusion" (dossier §6) — it does not decide, here or anywhere in this document, whether or how far Lollard household networks fed directly into the early English Reformation; it ends the world's own window at the point where that question becomes live, rather than assuming an answer by where the window is drawn. — **Confidence: Documented** for the Amersham evidence as the vendored corpus's own closing attestation; **Contested** for any claim about what, if anything, connects it forward to the Reformation proper.

**Geographic Centers:** Oxford, London, East Anglia, and the Chilterns.
- **Oxford:** the movement's own point of origin — Wyclif's academic base, and the site of his condemnation and the expulsion of his Oxford circle (Nicholas Hereford, Philip Repingdon, John Aston) following the 1382 Blackfriars Council. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**
- **London:** the seat of Parliament, to which the Twelve Conclusions were directly addressed in 1395 (`fasciculi-zizaniorum_shirley1858.txt`, line 28381 ff.), and the political and legal center from which the 1401 and 1414 statutes were enacted. — **Confidence: Documented**, per the vendored Twelve Conclusions heading.
- **East Anglia:** the site of the Norwich diocesan heresy trials of 1428–31, named directly in this world's own already-verified sourcing record (Tanner's *Heresy Trials in the Diocese of Norwich, 1428-31* — `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §3 B1; the volume itself is borrow-only, not vendored, but its existence and date range are independently on record). — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the trials' existence and date range; the volume's own content is not independently checked here.
- **The Chilterns:** the site of the Amersham household networks Foxe records as suffering "in the beginning of king Henry VIII" for possessing and reading vernacular Scripture (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, line 32323 ff.) — this world's own latest directly-vendored geographic attestation. — **Confidence: Documented**, per the vendored citation.

**Cultural Environment:** A late-medieval English Church operating in Latin liturgy and Latin/scholastic academic argument at Oxford, against a lay world of growing, though still limited, vernacular literacy. Household reading circles — families and neighbors gathering to hear vernacular Scripture read aloud, sometimes led by literate laity, sometimes by a single trained reader for an otherwise unlettered group — are this world's own most distinctive site of formation, attested directly by the trial records (Thorpe's Examination; the Amersham prosecutions). Trade and craft networks recur in the record as a social substrate for the movement's transmission (Badby is recorded as a tailor by trade — `foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, line 19229 ff.), and women appear in the record as participants, not merely bystanders, though always as reported by hostile ecclesiastical process rather than in their own unmediated words (the Twelve Conclusions' own appeal to "domini et dominae" — lords and ladies, i.e. laypeople of both sexes — as witnesses against corrupt confession, `fasciculi-zizaniorum_shirley1858.txt`, line 28735). — **Confidence: Documented** for the named individuals and their trades; **Inferential-Thin** for the interior experience of ordinary household participants, who are visible only through the shaping lens of prosecutorial record (Article 20 concern, carried forward at §3 and §7 below).

**Historical Pressures:** The 1401 statute *De heretico comburendo*, making burning for heresy the law of the land (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §1); Archbishop Arundel's 1409 Constitutions, forbidding unlicensed translation of, or preaching from, English Scripture (ibid.; independently attested in this world's own vendored Foxe, which quotes the constitution's own prohibition — "that the text of holy Scripture should not be had, or read in the vulgar tongue, from the time of Master John Wickliff for ever after, unless the said translation be approved" — `foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, line 32299 ff.); and, after 1414, the fusion of heresy with treason following Oldcastle's rising, which made the movement's own survival a matter of political as well as ecclesiastical danger. — **Confidence: Documented**, the Arundel citation independently verified against the vendored text; **Widely Accepted** for the statutes' dates and general effect.

**Historical Catalysts** (dated):
- **1377** — Pope Gregory XI's bulls condemning propositions drawn from Wyclif's own writing on dominion and church authority. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**
- **1382 (May)** — The Blackfriars ("Earthquake") Council, under Archbishop Courtenay, condemns twenty-four conclusions drawn from Wyclif's teaching and expels his Oxford adherents. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted**, independently corroborated in Arnold's own vendored editorial apparatus (see beginning-point discussion above).
- **1384** — Wyclif's death, at Lutterworth. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**
- **1391–93** — The trial of Walter Brute, a lay Herefordshire landholder, before the Bishop of Hereford; Foxe records "a Letter of the King against Walter Brute" dated 1392 (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, index at line 327). — **Confidence: Documented.**
- **1395** — The Twelve Conclusions of the Lollards posted publicly and addressed to Parliament. — **Confidence: Documented**, per the vendored Latin text.
- **1401** — William Sautre burned as a heretic — the first Lollard executed under the new statute — and the statute *De heretico comburendo* itself enacted. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted**; the Foxe account itself carries an old-style regnal date ("1400") for the trial proceedings that immediately precede the execution (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, line 18218 ff.), a discrepancy of calendar convention rather than of fact, noted here rather than silently reconciled.
- **1407** — William Thorpe's Examination before Archbishop Arundel, "penned with his own hand" (`foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3_cattley-townsend1837.txt`, line 20623). — **Confidence: Documented.**
- **1409** — Archbishop Arundel's Constitutions take effect. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**
- **1410** — John Badby, a tailor, burned as a heretic for denying transubstantiation (Foxe's own heading dates the proceeding "1409," an old-style-calendar discrepancy of the same kind noted above for Sautre). — **Confidence: Documented** for the trial and its content; **Widely Accepted** for the conventional modern date of the execution itself.
- **1414** — Sir John Oldcastle's failed revolt, after which Lollardy is prosecuted as treason as well as heresy. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**
- **1428–31** — The Norwich diocesan heresy trials. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted**, per the already-verified bibliographic record (§ Geographic Centers, above).
- **c. 1506–1522** — The Amersham (Chiltern) household-network prosecutions under Bishop Longland of Lincoln, this world's own latest directly-vendored attestation. — **Confidence: Documented.**

## 3. Distinct World Criteria

Applying the Framework's own four questions directly:

- **What recurring formation ecology exists?** A household and lay-network ecology of vernacular Scripture reading, mutual instruction, and itinerant or informal teaching, recurring across the full 1380–1520 window and documented almost entirely through the paradox of hostile process: the same bishops' courts that prosecuted this world's own participants are also the reason their words, and something of their own convictions, survive at all. A second, narrower recurring ecology — public or quasi-public doctrinal proclamation directed at Parliament and at ecclesiastical authority itself (the Twelve Conclusions, Thorpe's Examination) — sits alongside the household ecology as a distinct, more articulate register of the same underlying movement (§4 below develops this as a strand question).
- **What recurring gravities exist (preliminary, subject to Doc_04)?** Provisionally, and named here only as candidates for Step 4 testing, not as confirmed findings: scriptural sufficiency and lay access to Scripture in the vernacular; a reinterpreted eucharistic theology (the sacrament of the altar as, in Wyclif's own words per the Twelve Conclusions, "habitually" (*habitualiter*) Christ's body rather than transformed by "a feigned miracle," per Conclusion IV's own appeal to "the Gospel doctor's" *Trialogus* — `fasciculi-zizaniorum_shirley1858.txt`, lines 28603 ff.); and anticlerical suspicion of clerical wealth, temporal office, and sacramental gatekeeping (confession, indulgences, and the "power of the keys," per Conclusions I, VI, and IX of the same text). These are named as candidates only; Doc_04 performs the six-test assessment Doc_01 does not.
- **What recurring patterns of worship, formation, interpretation, belonging, and authority exist?** Vernacular Scripture access is this world's own single most recurring and best-evidenced pattern, running from the translation project itself through the trial records' own repeated emphasis on what the accused had read, owned, or heard read aloud. Rejection of transubstantiation recurs as a specific, repeatedly prosecuted doctrinal marker (Sautre, Badby, and the Twelve Conclusions' own Conclusion IV all name it directly). Anticlericalism recurs across register — the Twelve Conclusions' own opening conclusions against church endowments in "temporalities" and against a priesthood "feigned with a higher angelic power" not warranted by the New Testament (`fasciculi-zizaniorum_shirley1858.txt`, lines 28391 ff., 28415 ff.) — and, in a more diffuse form, across the trial records' own repeated emphasis on lay suspicion of priestly authority over confession, penance, and the dead. Rejection of pilgrimage and image veneration also recurs, at least in the Twelve Conclusions' own articulation (Conclusion VIII: pilgrimages and offerings to "dumb images of wood or stone" are "near akin to idolatry," and an image of the Trinity specifically "most abominable" — ibid., lines 28674 ff.), though this document does not assume every Lollard defendant across the full window held this specific position with equal emphasis; that proportionality question belongs to Doc_04/Doc_05. — **Confidence: Documented** for each cited textual instance; **Contested/held open** for how uniformly any one of these held across the movement's full social and geographic range.
- **What distinguishes this world from adjacent worlds?** From the Hussite and Bohemian Brethren world specifically: see §1 above (Distinctive Contribution) and §6 below. From this project's other confirmed and built worlds — none of which (per a direct check of `worlds/`: `alx`, `cappadocian`, `desert`, `don`, `gallic`, `grkap`, `hal`, `ijc`, `latap`, `lpc`, `pahc`, `rzg`, `syr`) is a vernacular-reform, trial-record-anchored English movement of this shape — there is no overlap risk requiring vigilance. The nearest built comparison in this project's current portfolio is Lutheran Wittenberg (`witt`), a batch-mate whose own Doc_01 grounds its distinctiveness in confessional/catechetical doctrine and a territorial, princely-sponsored magisterial Reformation (`witt_Doc_01_World_Identification_Boundaries_Orientation.md` §1). Lollardy differs from it on the same three grounds already argued for the Hussite comparison in §1 — a domestic statutory persecution regime rather than territorial establishment, a manuscript vernacular Bible rather than a confessional-document tradition as its defining artifact, and a lay/artisan household social base rather than a princely-and-university-led one — and, additionally, on simple chronology: this world's own 1380–1520 window precedes Wittenberg's 1517–1580 window by well over a century, overlapping only in its final years and only as the still-unresolved influence-line question named throughout this document.

## 4. Strand Determination (Article 21)

Per Constitution Article 21, strand is "a finding, never a presupposed universal schema," accountable to evidence and "never assigned to satisfy an architectural preference for plurality." The census's own floor finding — "the movement outlasted Wycliffe by well over a century" — is a temporal-development finding (Article 15, Historical Development and Instability), not by itself a strand finding, and this document does not treat it as one. Simply noting that Lollardy's later, more socially diffuse lay/artisan form came *after* Wyclif's own Oxford phase would be exactly the sequential-development error Article 21 warns against; it would show only that the movement developed over time, which every formation world does. Strand determination instead asks whether the evidence shows a genuinely *simultaneous* divergence in authority structure or ecological orientation, not a mere succession of one phase by another.

**Finding: two strands identified, with named evidence, on non-sequential grounds.** The two candidate registers are not "early Oxford academic phase" followed by "later lay phase" — they are attested as running alongside one another from the very outset of this world's own window, and diverge in what each treats as the ground of religious authority, not merely in which decade each happens to fall.

- **Strand A — Academic/Scholastic strand.** Authority grounded in Latin scholastic disputation and university doctoral standing, addressed to an audience of fellow clergy and academics. Evidenced directly in this world's own vendored Latin corpus: Wyclif's *Tractatus de Ecclesia* (Loserth 1886) and *De Veritate Sacrae Scripturae* (Buddensieg 1907), both argued in full scholastic apparatus — sustained *quaestio*-form argument citing patristic and canon-law authority — and both addressed, by genre and language, to Oxford's own theological and ecclesiastical readership rather than to lay hearers. This strand does not persist as an independent claim-making stream past Wyclif's own circle's silencing: the Blackfriars Council's 1382 condemnation and the expulsion of Hereford, Repingdon (who recants), and Aston from Oxford (§2 above) end this register's own institutional home within this world's window. No later actor in this window continues to argue Lollard doctrine in this specific Latin-academic, disputation-grounded form.
- **Strand B — Vernacular Lay-Access strand.** Authority grounded not in scholastic argument but in direct, unmediated lay access to Scripture in English, addressed to a household and artisan audience without requiring Latin literacy or university training. Evidenced in the Middle English sermon and tract corpus (Arnold's three volumes and Matthew's *English Works of Wyclif Hitherto Unprinted*) — homiletic material addressed to lay hearers rather than argued in scholastic form — and, decisively, in the Wycliffite Bible translation project itself (Forshall & Madden), a collective undertaking that continued well beyond Wyclif's own death. This strand is the one independently attested continuously across the full window: the Twelve Conclusions (1395, a public lay articulation addressed to Parliament, more than a decade after Wyclif's death), Thorpe's Examination (1407), Badby's trial (1410), the Norwich trials (1428–31), and the Amersham household networks (1500s–1520s) are all, in different ways, this same register — lay conviction grounded in Scripture directly available in English, defended before hostile authority without recourse to scholastic disputation.

Both strands share a common root in Wyclif's own initiating theological conviction that Scripture's own authority is supreme over ecclesiastical tradition and institutional office (§5 below). Their coexistence is not sequential: Wyclif's own Latin ecclesiological treatises and the vernacular Middle English sermon material both derive from broadly the same years of his active career (the later 1370s to 1384), addressed simultaneously to two different audiences in two different registers of argument, not one succeeding the other. What is genuinely sequential — and is not, on that basis alone, treated as evidence of strand-hood here — is that Strand A's own institutional home closed in 1382 while Strand B continued for another century and more; that is Strand A's own historical limit (a Doc_08 Cell 3A/3B matter), not the reasoning that established the strand distinction in the first place. — **Confidence: Documented** for the generic and audience differences between the cited Latin and Middle English corpora; **Contested** for the precise personal-authorship attribution of much of the Middle English material to Wyclif himself, per Step 0's own disclosure (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §2 A1, §3 B2) that modern scholarship increasingly treats Arnold's volumes as Wycliffite-collective preaching material rather than Wyclif's own personal composition — a finding that, if anything, strengthens rather than weakens Strand B's own case for persisting past Wyclif's death as the movement's own collective vernacular voice, distinct from his own personal Latin-academic one.

**Governing consequence for later steps:** cross-strand gravity testing occurs formally at Step 4. A candidate gravity holding across both strands (for example, the shared conviction that Scripture's authority is supreme over ecclesiastical mediation, however differently each strand argues for it) is a stronger candidate for this world's irreducible core than one attested only in Strand A's own closed, shorter-lived register.

## 5. Preliminary Forces Identification

Per the Forces Framework's Step 1 requirement, this is orientation for Step 2's scope, not the complete forces analysis compiled at Step 8.

**What generated this world:** *External:* the late-medieval English Church's own accumulated temporal wealth and political entanglement — the very condition the Twelve Conclusions' own first article names directly, dating the church's own decline to "when the church of England began to endow itself in temporalities, after its great step-mother, the great Roman Church" (`fasciculi-zizaniorum_shirley1858.txt`, lines 28391 ff., paraphrased from the Latin) — together with the wider disruption of papal authority's own credibility during the Great Western Schism (from 1378), a background condition this document notes as Widely Accepted in general historiography without a specific vendored citation pinning Wyclif's own reaction to it. *Internal:* Wyclif's own prior academic conviction, already developed in his writing on civil and ecclesiastical dominion before this world's own window opens, that true lordship and authority depend on a state of grace rather than on office as such — a conviction that, applied to the sacrament of the altar and to papal claims alike, generates this world's own defining doctrinal departures. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the Schism's background role; **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for characterizing Wyclif's dominion theology as this world's own specific internal generative seed.

**What larger world was it embedded in:** the late-medieval English Church itself — its parochial and monastic structure, Oxford University as its own intellectual center, and the English crown's own uneasy, shifting patronage of and hostility toward reform, oscillating across the reigns of Richard II, Henry IV, and Henry V. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**

**What was it responding to:** clerical wealth (the Twelve Conclusions' own recurring target, from Conclusion I onward) and the lack of vernacular Scripture access for ordinary lay believers — the two forces this document's own task explicitly names, and the two most directly and repeatedly attested in the vendored corpus itself. — **Confidence: Documented**, per the cited Twelve Conclusions text.

**What was it refusing:** transubstantiation, named directly in Conclusion IV ("Quod fictum miraculum sacramenti panis inducit omnes homines... in idolatriam" — "that the feigned miracle of the sacrament of bread leads all people... into idolatry," arguing instead that the bread of the altar is "habitually" Christ's body, per Wyclif's own *Trialogus* — `fasciculi-zizaniorum_shirley1858.txt`, lines 28603 ff.); indulgences, named directly in Conclusion IX's own corollary, condemning the pope's "feigned indulgence from penalty and guilt" (*fictam indulgentiam a poena et a culpa* — ibid., lines 28777 ff.); and images and pilgrimage, named directly in Conclusion VIII, which calls offerings made to "dumb images of wood or stone" "near akin to idolatry" and singles out an image of the Trinity as "most abominable" (ibid., lines 28674 ff.) — three refusals this document's own task explicitly asked to be checked against the vendored text, and all three independently confirmed there, in a single 1395 lay document rather than scattered across unrelated sources. — **Confidence: Documented**, all three verified directly against the vendored Latin text.

## 6. World Continuity & Distinction

**What this world inherits:** Wyclif's own Oxford theology of dominion-by-grace, his denial of transubstantiation, and his conviction that Scripture's own authority is supreme over ecclesiastical tradition — all already developed, in nascent form, before this world's own window opens, and taken up, transformed, and carried forward by a movement that in time exceeded him. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**

**What this world transmits forward:** Two things, held at different confidence levels and not conflated. First, and with direct, named textual evidence, outward rather than forward in time: Wyclif's own Latin ecclesiology feeds directly into Jan Hus's *De Ecclesia*, per the Hussite world's own Step 0 record (§1 above). This is transmission *outward*, to a contemporary, geographically separate movement — not this world's own later inheritance, and not something this document resolves as a matter of corpus-map assignment (§7 below carries that question forward). Second, and far more cautiously: the census's own `relationsSummary` names a "Contested influence line to the English Reformation" — a claim this document states in the census's own careful language rather than its own, because that is the discipline the census itself models: it "carries the contest rather than the conclusion" (dossier §6). This document does the same. It does not assert, anywhere above, that Lollard household networks caused, seeded, or substantially shaped the English Reformation; it names that as a live, unresolved historiographical question, carried forward rather than answered. — **Confidence: Documented** for the Wyclif-to-Hus textual dependency (per the Hussite world's own already-cited record); **Contested**, explicitly, for the English-Reformation influence line.

**Relationship to the Hussite and Bohemian Brethren world:** transmission outward, not inheritance received. Lollardy does not derive from the Hussite movement; Wyclif's own writing predates and partly generates Hus's. This document does not import Hussite formation logic, vocabulary, or ecological findings into Lollardy's own construction, nor does it treat the shared textual root as license to do so — per Constitution Article 3's own governing distinction, a genuine historical transmission relationship between two worlds is not the same thing as "cross-world contamination" in the constitutional sense, which concerns a *builder* importing another world's own vocabulary or formation logic into a world it does not belong to. The transmission here is a fact about the fifteenth century, not a construction shortcut available to whoever builds either world.

**Living heir(s):** none mapped. Per Article 28/29 discipline (§1 above), this document names no living institutional heir for Lollardy — unlike Alexandria's Coptic Orthodox Church or Imperial and Juridical Christianity's papal and Orthodox continuations, no comparable surviving ecclesial body traces its own institutional descent to this world. The only continuity claim on record is the contested Reformation influence-line named above, itself unconfirmed and not self-confirmed here.

## 9. Mission Alignment (world-level)

The project's mission is to help people experience Jesus in new ways through engagement with the living traditions and practices of His Church (Vision V1.1; Constitution Preamble).
