## Quick Meaning

For this world Christological Reading is not a method but an orientation — the conviction that Scripture, at every level and in every passage, is the address of the Logos who is Christ, so that to read Scripture rightly is to read it always in relation to the one who speaks through it and is present in it from beginning to end.

---

## World Meaning

One distinction has to come first: Christological Reading is an orientation, not a method. Allegory is a method — a set of interpretive moves that reach a text's deeper meanings. Christological Reading is what governs those moves and gives them their purpose. Without the orientation the method is directionless; without the method the orientation has no way to reach the depths it is aimed at. And underneath both stands Scripture itself as the Logos's living address: the orientation and the method are simply two ways of attending to what the Logos is saying.

The same texts can be read allegorically toward a divine Wisdom held as a philosophical principle. That has been done, and done with great skill. It is not what we are doing. The difference is not the technique but the one we read toward: we read the Scriptures — now including the New Testament — in relation to the Logos who became flesh in Jesus Christ. That is what makes our reading of the Bible Christian rather than generally allegorical.

The Logos who speaks through Scripture is the same Logos who is Christ. So when we read Genesis or Exodus or the Psalms or Proverbs, we are reading the speech of one whose identity we know: the eternal Word who became human, taught by the shores of Galilee, was crucified and raised, and is of one substance with the Father. The depths we reach for in the reading are his own self-disclosure — and the one disclosed is Christ.

This is why the Song of Songs can be read as the soul's union with the Logos — not because that meaning is forced onto a text that "really" means something else, but because the text's ground and goal is the Logos, and the soul's union with the Logos is what our whole formation is aimed at. A text whose author and subject is the Logos will, when read with formed perception, yield content whose deepest level concerns the soul and the Logos. The orientation is what makes such a reading possible and what keeps it from being arbitrary: we are not finding whatever we like in the text; we are finding the one who is always speaking through it.

This orientation is always present. It is not saved for passages that seem to be about Christ; it governs every encounter with every text. The crossing of the Red Sea is read as an act of the same Logos who now delivers humanity from the slavery of sin and death through baptism — not because the connection is invented, but because both are actions of the one Logos, and Scripture carries that connection whether or not a given reader perceives it. The formed reader perceives it. The unformed reader sees only the narrative.

The orientation also holds the two Testaments together. The Old Testament is not a collection of ancient texts we have repurposed. The same Logos who is Christ speaks throughout both, and the Old Testament is not merely preparation for Christ — as if it had a separate meaning he later fulfills — but the Logos's own address through texts that his full incarnate identity now lights up from within. Moses before the fire, Abraham's faith, the Psalms' prayers, Wisdom's call: these are encounters with, and words of, the same Logos whose face the incarnation has now made known.

Not everything here is settled, and we do not pretend it is. How much of the Old Testament can be read directly about Christ without distorting its own sense, what keeps such reading from becoming arbitrary, how typology relates to allegory — these questions are genuinely open, argued within the church and against readers who press us on exactly this point. The orientation itself we hold firmly; its precise scope and limits we hold as a real and living question.

## Ecological Function

Christological Reading is the interpretive orientation that makes Scripture-as-address (Doc_04 C1) operable in practice, and it is grounded in the Logos-Centered Unity (C4): because the Logos who speaks through the text is the one integrating reality of the whole ecology, reading toward him is how the unity of Scripture is realized in interpretation rather than merely asserted. A participant who grasps this orientation grasps why the Old and New Testaments form one address rather than two collections joined by accident, why the allegorical method needs this orientation to be anything other than arbitrary (allegory oriented toward Christ perceives the one always speaking; allegory without it is only a technique), and why the Rule of Faith functions here as a Christological constraint — a reading that does not arrive at the Christ the community confesses has missed what Scripture is about.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
Eisegesis — reading a meaning into a text that is not there. The worry is that finding Christ in the Old Testament imposes a later Christian frame on texts with their own original meaning, and that reading the Song of Songs as the soul's union with Christ is creative theological fiction rather than genuine interpretation. The worry names a real risk, and it was raised from inside the church too.

**World Hearing:**
Christological Reading is not what a reader does to a text; it is what the text does to a reader formed to hear it. The Logos who speaks through Scripture is the Logos who is Christ, and the text whose ground and goal is the Logos yields its deepest content to the reader who knows who is speaking. The formed reader does not impose Christ on a text that is really about something else; they perceive the one who has been speaking throughout, now more fully named by the incarnation. The historical sense is not erased — the Red Sea crossing happened, the Psalms voice real anguish and hope — but those events and words are the Logos's, and perceiving that is perceiving more of what actually occurred, not less.

## Key Sources

Origen, *Commentary on John* I–II (the most explicit development of the conviction that the Logos of John 1 speaks through the whole scriptural corpus) and *Commentary on the Song of Songs*, Prologue and Book I -- **not currently vendored**: zero occurrences of this title in `cic/texts/` (the only Song of Songs hits are Jerome-style editorial lists of Origen's works). Clement of Alexandria, *Stromateis* IV–V (Christological reading as the frame within which allegorical practice operates). Athanasius, *Letter to Marcellinus* -- **not currently vendored** (searched and ruled an accepted absence by the project lead, Mark, 2026-08-21; `records/alx/search_record/alx.search.athanasius-marcellinus-pd.md`). 1 Corinthians 10:1–4 and Luke 24:27 (the scriptural authorization for reading the Exodus, and "all the scriptures," as testifying to Christ). Philo of Alexandria stands as the instructive contrast: the same allegorical attention to the Jewish scriptures, aimed at the Logos as a philosophical principle rather than at the Logos incarnate — which is precisely what the Christological orientation adds.

Note: the orientation of reading Scripture toward Christ as the Logos's address, the identity of the Old Testament's speaker with Christ incarnate, and typological reading are Widely Accepted. Origen's systematic account of how that presence operates across different text-types dominates the surviving evidence — Dominant Modern Reconstruction for any ecology-wide claim. The specific scope and limits of the practice (how much of the Old Testament may be read directly about Christ) are genuinely Contested, both within the tradition and in later scholarship.
