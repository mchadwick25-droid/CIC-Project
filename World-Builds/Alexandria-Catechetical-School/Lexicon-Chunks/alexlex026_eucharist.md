## Quick Meaning

For this world the Eucharist is not a memorial of something Christ did once — it is the community's recurring, bodily, shared enactment of what he is still doing: giving himself to be received, so that every gathering at the table is a real participation in the divine life the resurrection opened and the whole of formation is reaching toward.

---

## World Meaning

The Eucharist tempts us to reach for the right words — "real presence," "the body and blood of Christ," "the sacrament of the altar" — and to believe the words have carried the meaning. They have not. The words are not wrong. But held on their own they say what the Eucharist is called without beginning to say what it does to the soul that receives it, week upon week, across a whole life.

What it does begins with what it is: the community gathered around the one who gives himself to be shared. Not the memory of that giving. Not the doctrine that it once happened. The giving itself, again — as the one who gave himself in the incarnation and the cross and the rising gives himself still, in the bread and cup we receive together. We do not gather to recall a sharing that happened somewhere else. We gather to share — to take part, in the body, in the life of the one who offers himself to be taken part in. The bread broken is the Logos giving himself; the cup received is his life held out for us.

This makes the table the most concentrated single act of formation in our recurring life. Every other practice we keep — Scripture, prayer, fasting, belonging, catechesis — is aimed at deepening the soul's share in the divine life. The Eucharist enacts that share directly, in the gathered body's act of receiving. The soul that has received a hundred times is not repeating a ceremony but going deeper each time into the participation the receiving is. And the table asks no literacy and no schooling of us; it asks the baptism that made a person a full member, and it asks genuine attention and desire. The believer who receives with real attention shares in the divine life as truly as the teacher grown old in Scripture's depths — this is one of the main ways the life at the center of our world reaches the whole community, including those the school tradition cannot reach. The bishop who presides is doing what is most fundamental to that office: gathering us around the one who gives himself.

The table also points forward. What it enacts is real but not yet full. We gather in hope as much as in sharing — receiving now what is given now, and knowing that the fullness of it is the horizon theosis names. Every Eucharist is the life of formation already enacted; every Eucharist waits for what formation is still reaching toward. We receive and hope, take part and wait, are formed and are not yet fully formed.

---

## Ecological Function

The Eucharist is the recurring enacted center of the community's life — the practice that most directly does what every other practice is preparing for. A participant who grasps it grasps why participation is not metaphor but something enacted weekly in the body, why the bishop's authority here is liturgical before it is administrative or doctrinal (his primary formation act is presiding at the table), why the gathered assembly is not a meeting of people who share opinions but the community becoming what it is, and how the divine life reaches those the school tradition's modes cannot. The Eucharist and the school are complementary channels through which formation reaches different people in different modes, not rivals.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
Either a memorial meal — a backward-looking act of piety commemorating the Last Supper, carrying the significance that remembering carries but no additional formation power — or the correct labels (the Body and Blood of Christ, real presence, the sacrament of the altar) held abstractly, describing what is present in the elements without addressing what receiving them week after week does to the soul.

**World Hearing:**
The community's present sharing in the life of the one who is always giving himself — not a memorialized Christ but a present Christ, whose life is genuinely available to be genuinely shared by those who gather and receive. It forms the soul because in it the soul takes part, bodily, in the life it was made for — not by conveying spiritual nutrition through an agreed symbol, and not merely by applying the correct label, but by genuine participation.

## Key Sources

Clement of Alexandria, *Paedagogus* II.2 and *Quis Dives Salvetur* (the Eucharist as the community's participation in the Logos's life; feeding on the Logos through sacramental reception). **Not currently vendored, flagged 2026-09-08, Round 5 Opus review (same defect class as `alexlex028_prayer.md`'s multi-round correction):** this entry previously cited Origen, *Commentary on Matthew* on ch. 26 and *Homilies on Numbers* 7 for the Eucharist as genuine participation and a lifetime of reception forming the soul. Neither is vendored: the vendored Commentary on Matthew (`anf09`) covers Books 1–2 and 10–14, ending well short of ch. 26 (the Last Supper); *Homilies on Numbers* survives only as Rufinus's preface (`npnf203` apparatus), per `Ministry/Technology/table_phase0/Texts_Scrub_alexandria.md`'s own confirmation. Both remain live acquisition candidates. Athanasius, *Festal Letters* (the gathering as the community's ongoing formation act, the bishop mediating the participation). 1 Corinthians 10:16–17 and 11:23–26; John 6:51–58 (the participation character of the act — one bread, one body; those who eat and drink abide in Christ).

Note: Eucharist as present participation rather than past memorial, its reach to the whole community including the non-literate, and the bishop's Eucharistic presidency as the episcopate's primary formation function are Widely Accepted; the practice is broadly attested across liturgical sources and Clement independent of Origen. Origen's systematic account of the formation mechanics is the most developed and carries ecology-wide claims at Dominant Modern Reconstruction. The account here is the literate tradition's understanding of the practice; how the non-literate majority interiorly appropriated the gathering is thinner than the practice's own broad attestation — Inferential/Thin for the majority's inner experience.
