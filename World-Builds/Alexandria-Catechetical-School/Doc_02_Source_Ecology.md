## 0. Purpose and Scope of This Document

This document performs Step 2: it establishes the evidential foundations of the Alexandria world before gravity discovery. It maps the surviving evidence through which this world becomes visible; assesses the major voices for Author Gravity (five dimensions including Transmission History); names the secondary scholarship through which the primary sources are read; assesses formation-narrative, hagiographic, and material-culture sources; names source asymmetry and the structurally missing voices (Constitution Article 20); and applies the forces lens (Forces Framework Step 2) to ask which sources speak to external forces and what the silences reveal.

It does **not** discover gravities, classify them, or reconstruct the ecology — those are Doc_04 and Doc_05. Its outputs are evidence and its honest limits. Confidence levels use the fixed five-level vocabulary (Documented / Widely Accepted / Dominant Modern Reconstruction / Contested / Inferential-Thin) per Constitution Article 17.

**Consistency with Doc_01 (carried, not re-argued):** the world is the Alexandrian Christian formation ecology c. 150–400 CE, anchored on its catechetical-formation tradition (broad reading, pending project-lead confirmation, OG-3); strand-singular for internal construction, with the learned-vs-desert gravity question and the desert world-attribution question held open for Doc_04; Clement and Origen carry the highest Author Gravity risk; the Greek/Coptic and elite/ordinary evidentiary asymmetries are defining source problems; ordinary-believer interior formation is not confidently recoverable and is bounded downstream.

## 1. Source Ecology Map

The evidence is organized below into **twelve evidence streams** — not twelve gravities (a stream is a channel of surviving evidence; a gravity is an organizing force, discovered only at Doc_04). Each stream carries a visibility estimate (how well the surviving record attests it) and a confidence level (how securely reconstruction can rest on it, and for which population/period). The overarching pattern the map reveals is stated up front so it governs everything below: **the record is heavily weighted toward the literate, Greek-speaking, educated stratum of the first century-and-a-half of the horizon (Clement and Origen above all), and thin-to-silent for Coptic-speaking, rural, non-elite, and female participants across the whole span.** Evidential visibility is not ecological visibility (Article 17); this distinction is an actively-maintained discipline, not a one-time caveat.

## 2. The Twelve Evidence Streams

### Stream 1 — Scriptural Interpretation
*Visibility: Very High (in the literate tradition).* The distinctive multilevel, allegorical-spiritual reading of Scripture. Primary sources: **Clement** (*Stromateis*, *Paedagogus*, *Protreptikos*); **Origen** (biblical commentaries and homilies — e.g. on John, Matthew, Romans, the Song of Songs; *On First Principles* Book IV on scriptural senses; the *Philocalia* anthology); **Didymus the Blind** (commentaries on Psalms, Job, Zechariah, recovered at Tura in 1941); **Athanasius** (*Letter to Marcellinus*; *Festal Letters*). Four partly-independent witnesses across the horizon support the pattern. — **Confidence: Dominant Modern Reconstruction** — Widely Accepted that this mode characterizes the educated tradition; provisional for the broad ecology, because the four witnesses are all learned voices. **Author Gravity note:** heavily Origen-weighted; the pattern's reach beyond the literate stratum is a Doc_04 test, not a settled finding.

### Stream 2 — Catechetical Formation
*Visibility: High.* Formation understood as gradual transformation (becoming, not only believing), and the catechumenate as a staged path. Sources: Clement's *Paedagogus* (explicitly catechetical); Origen's homilies functioning as catechetical instruction and *Contra Celsum* (apologetic); Athanasius's *Festal Letters* as annual formation documents for the whole Egyptian church. — **Confidence: Dominant Modern Reconstruction.**

### Stream 3 — Worship and Prayer
*Visibility: Moderate–High for the theology of worship; thinner for lived liturgical experience.* Baptism as illumination (*photismos*); Eucharistic participation; the Paschal cycle; fasting, vigils, penitential practice. Sources: Athanasius's *Festal Letters* (the paschal-dating letters); Eucharistic/baptismal references distributed across the corpus; homilies as themselves liturgical acts; **Origen's *On Prayer*** (*De Oratione* — widely characterized in general scholarship as one of the fullest surviving early Christian treatments of what prayer is and how it develops, though this world's own build does not independently re-assert that characterization as a grounded claim — see `Lexicon-Chunks/alexlex028_prayer.md`, which struck the equivalent claim there as not independently re-derivable from a vendored text, **corrected 2026-09-08, Round 2 Opus review, from this line's own unreconciled overclaim**; **not currently vendored into this corpus in English** — a candidate public-domain English translation exists but its provenance is unresolved and it was accepted as an absence by the project lead, `records/alx/search_record/alx.search.origen-on-prayer-curtis.md`, Mark, 2026-08-21; corrected 2026-09-08, Round 1 Opus review, from an intervening correction pass's false claim that no such edition exists at all — this Stream line is a historical evidence-stream inventory, like Stream 4's own listing of unvendored Origen homilies below, not a corpus-holdings claim, so the work belongs here regardless of vendoring status. **The Greek original is now vendored** (`origen_de-oratione-grc_koetschau1899.txt`, Koetschau's 1899 critical edition, GCS 3, all 34 chapters complete) as an original-language witness per `cic/texts/INTAKE.md`'s own discipline — legitimate, rights-clean material for cross-checking, but never primary evidence for a Representative (this project's evidence language is English), so it does not by itself restore Origen's *English* content to this world's citable evidence base; see `Lexicon-Chunks/alexlex028_prayer.md` for the full citation-integrity account and what this world's built claims can currently rest on). — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the theology of worship in the learned sources; **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for how worship was lived across the community. Note: literary near-silence about ordinary worship is not evidence of worship's low ecological weight — worship is precisely the kind of pervasive practice a literate record under-documents.

### Stream 4 — Spiritual Formation / Transformation
*Visibility: High (in the literate school-and-monastic tradition).* A progression often described as purification → illumination → union, and the ideal of the fully-formed Christian (Clement's "true gnostic"). Sources: Clement (*Stromateis*); Origen (*On First Principles*, *Commentary on the Song of Songs*, homilies); emerging Egyptian ascetic literature (late horizon); Athanasius's *Life of Antony* as a paradigmatic formation portrait. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** — but strictly scoped to the literate school-and-monastic tradition. Ordinary non-literate believers are governed by the Doc_01 binding constraint: this stream is **not** license for confident ordinary-believer interior reconstruction.

### Stream 5 — Educational Ecology (the catechetical-teaching tradition)
*Visibility: High for the fact of advanced Christian teaching; Contested for its institutional form.* The tradition of learned Christian instruction traditionally listed as Pantaenus → Clement → Origen → Heraclas → Dionysius → … → Didymus. Sources: teaching embedded in the corpus; Gregory Thaumaturgus's *Address of Thanksgiving to Origen* (a student's-eye account of Origen's pedagogy); **Eusebius**, *Ecclesiastical History* (the scaffolding for the personnel list). — **Confidence: Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for the teaching tradition; **Contested** (carried from Doc_01 §1.2) for whether it was a *formal institution with a continuous head-succession* before c. 215–230 — the orderly succession derives largely from Eusebius, who carries **HIGH Author Gravity risk for institutional/biographical claims** specifically (§4 below).

### Stream 6 — Philosophical Engagement
*Visibility: High (in the educated stratum).* Confident synthesis with, and critical refusal of, Greek philosophy (Middle Platonism, Stoic logos-theory, Pythagorean currents), with the Logos as the point of both contact and refusal. Sources: Clement (*Stromateis*); Origen (*Contra Celsum*, *On First Principles*); apologetic writings throughout. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted for the educated stratum; Dominant Modern Reconstruction for the broader ecology** (the split is load-bearing and carried forward — most Alexandrian Christians were not doing philosophy).

### Stream 7 — Community and Ecclesial Life
*Visibility: Moderate.* Episcopal governance, the teacher-to-bishop relationship, discipline and the reintegration of the lapsed. Sources: **Dionysius of Alexandria's** correspondence (surviving largely via Eusebius's quotation); Athanasius's *Festal Letters*; ecclesiastical histories; synodal materials. — **Confidence: Dominant Modern Reconstruction**; note the Origen–Demetrius conflict and much pre-4th-c. institutional detail reach us mediated through Eusebius (HIGH risk).

### Stream 8 — Martyr Memory
*Visibility: Moderate.* The Decian (249–251 CE) and Diocletianic/Great (303–311 CE, toleration confirmed 313) persecutions fell heavily on Egypt and anchor Coptic identity (the *Anno Martyrum* era, reckoned from Diocletian's 284 accession). Sources: martyr acts; persecution records in the ecclesiastical histories; hagiographic literature. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted / Documented** for the persecutions as events; **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for how martyr memory functioned formatively in ongoing community life.

### Stream 9 — Monastic Emergence
*Visibility: Moderate–High for the fact of emergence; the world-attribution is Contested.* The Egyptian desert tradition emerging c. 270–400. Sources: Athanasius's *Life of Antony*; **Pachomian rule literature** (*cross-build provisional*); the *Apophthegmata Patrum* (*cross-build provisional*); **Evagrius Ponticus** (*Praktikos*, *Chapters on Prayer*; *cross-build provisional*); Egyptian ascetic literature broadly. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the emergence and intensification of ascetic practice across c. 270–400; **Contested** for the world-attribution question — whether desert formation belongs to Alexandria's ecology, to a distinct Desert Christianity world, or to both by discovered convergence (Doc_01 §3.3, §6). The no-strand-split working assumption is a construction-continuity posture, **not** a finding; both the intensification and distinct-world readings remain live pending Doc_04. The *Apophthegmata*, Pachomian, and Evagrian material is held open for world-attribution and must not be claimed as constitutive of Alexandrian formation logic here (Constitution Article 3).

### Stream 10 — Ordinary Believer Visibility
*Visibility: Low.* Dockworkers, artisans, merchants, fishers, household members — accessible only through others' concerns (pastoral references, indirect homily evidence, implied liturgical participation). **No first-person ordinary-believer source exists.** — **Confidence: Inferential-Thin.**

### Stream 11 — Women and Household Visibility
*Visibility: Low.* Women appear as martyrs, as recipients of teaching, and as consecrated virgins and widows; household references appear in pastoral letters. Scholarly consensus holds that women participated fully in liturgy and were likely primary transmitters of formation within the household — but that household formation is largely unrecoverable, and women's own theological voice is almost absent from the literary record. — **Confidence: Inferential-Thin.**

### Stream 12 — Doctrinal Controversy and Boundary Formation
*Visibility: High (late horizon).* Anti-Gnostic and anti-Manichaean boundary-drawing across the horizon; the Arian controversy and the Nicene/post-Nicene settlement dominating the late horizon. Sources: Athanasius (anti-Arian corpus; the dominant late-horizon voice); conciliar and synodal records; episcopal correspondence on boundaries; anti-heretical writings. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted for the post-325 period; Dominant Modern Reconstruction across the full 150–400 horizon.** **Author Gravity note:** Athanasius's post-Nicene concentration risks making doctrinal conflict appear more central to *ordinary* formation than it was; this stream must be temporally located (post-325 for the Arian material) rather than smeared across the whole span.

## 3. Author Gravity Assessment

Per Constitution Article 26 and Framework Part II, each major voice is assessed on five dimensions — visibility, representativeness, influence, limitations, and transmission history — and the highest-risk voices are flagged so gravity discovery does not mistake source-concentration for ecological centrality.

### 3.1 Origen (c. 185–254 CE) — SYSTEMIC Author Gravity risk (the overriding flag)
- **Visibility:** Extremely high. The most prolific writer of the early Church; his surviving output is a large fraction of *all* surviving Alexandrian Christian literature. No other single voice comes close.
- **Representativeness:** Partial, with critical qualification. He represents one specific mode — intellectually intensive, allegorically ambitious, philosophically sophisticated — and he has substantially *shaped* how the ecology appears in the record, not merely reflected it.
- **Influence:** Systemic and immense. He shapes Streams 1, 2, 4, 5, and 6 simultaneously. His students carry the method outward (Dionysius; Gregory Thaumaturgus to Cappadocia; later Didymus). The tradition reveres, inherits, cautions about, and in part condemns him.
- **Limitations:** His theological adventurousness (pre-existence of souls, *apokatastasis*, certain subordinationist Christological formulations) was later condemned in part (§3.5, §5); his intellectual emphasis risks portraying the whole ecology as primarily intellectual; his later work was done in Caesarea (c. 231–234 onward), a different context (§3.6).
- **Transmission history:** A large part of his corpus survives only in Latin translation — Rufinus, who softened doctrinally suspect passages, and Jerome, who (after turning against him) quoted him to discredit him. Fine-grained claims about Origen's *precise* positions must be held with this adversarial/edited transmission in view (§7).
- **Risk classification: SYSTEMIC.** Multiple candidate gravities are attested primarily through Origen. The Author-Gravity screening question must stay active throughout Doc_04: is this the ecology's pattern, or Origen's? His evidence also clusters mid-horizon, compounding author-dominance with period-dominance.

### 3.2 Clement of Alexandria (c. 150–215 CE)
- **Visibility:** Significant (Protreptikos, Paedagogus, Stromateis survive), but much catechetical/homiletical work is lost; surviving output is a fraction of probable production.
- **Representativeness:** Partial; writes as an educated teacher for the formation of the Christian intellectual (the "true gnostic"); non-elite, women, and household Christians are largely invisible as subjects, though addressed pastorally in the *Paedagogus*.
- **Influence:** Formative for Origen; establishes the Christianity-and-philosophy synthesis template and the faith → knowledge → wisdom progression.
- **Limitations:** Systematic under-representation of the majority of Alexandrian Christian life; may overstate ordinary comfort with philosophy.
- **Transmission history:** Greek text survives reasonably directly; the *Stromateis* is textually difficult but not adversarially transmitted.
- **Risk:** High for Streams 5 and 6 specifically (his concentration there can make them look more ecologically central than they were).

### 3.3 Athanasius (c. 296–373 CE)
- **Visibility:** High but temporally concentrated (post-Nicene): anti-Arian corpus, *Festal Letters*, *Life of Antony*, apologetic and pastoral works.
- **Representativeness:** Partial; a bishop addressing his community — the perspective is episcopal, not the community describing itself.
- **Influence:** Defines the Nicene Christological settlement for the Eastern church; links Alexandrian theology to monasticism through the *Life of Antony*.
- **Limitations:** His Christological-controversy concentration risks over-representing doctrinal conflict relative to ongoing formative life; must **not** be used to characterize the pre-Nicene period.
- **Transmission history:** Well-transmitted in Greek, with significant Coptic and Syriac witnesses expanding the corpus (Brakke).
- **Risk:** Stream 12 over-representation for the late horizon.

### 3.4 Didymus the Blind (c. 313–398 CE)
- **Added 2026-09-08, independent maturity-gap audit; corrected 2026-09-08, Round 1 Opus review, from this note's own overclaim.** This section previously discussed Didymus at length without this world's vendored corpus actually holding any of his own primary text. That gap is now partly closed: Mingarelli's 1769 editio princeps of Didymus's *De Trinitate* is vendored as `didymus-alexandria_de-trinitate-lat-grc_mingarelli1769.txt` and corpus-mapped to this world — but what is actually vendored, verified directly (a full Unicode census of the file's 3.29 MB body), is Mingarelli's Latin translation, not Didymus's Greek: the file contains zero recoverable Greek characters anywhere. The 1769 title page describes a Greek-and-Latin bilingual edition; the surviving OCR scan of it does not deliver the Greek half as usable text. This is still this world's first primary-text contribution from Didymus himself — in Latin translation, not in the "sole surviving Greek witness" this note originally claimed — a narrower but still real gap-closure. The Tura commentaries below remain correctly disclosed as unvendorable (their 1941 discovery postdates any possible pre-1929 public-domain edition by definition); *De Trinitate* is a different, independently-attested work, not affected by that constraint. Its own specific content has not yet been read for any claim below — this is a discovery-layer/library integration only, consistent with this project's own "vendor and map now, integrate later" discipline; a future revision pass may draw on it directly.
- **Visibility:** Moderate; the Tura commentaries (Psalms, Job, Zechariah) recovered in 1941; output far smaller than Origen's.
- **Representativeness / social location:** Head of the Alexandrian teaching tradition for roughly half a century; blind from early childhood, he engaged Scripture through hearing and oral memory — a particularizing (not distorting) factor, and not representative of the tradition generally.
- **Influence:** Carries Origenian exegesis into the late 4th century; had study contact with Jerome (briefly), before Jerome's anti-Origenist turn.
- **Limitations:** Similar elite-scholarly limitations to Origen.
- **Transmission history:** *De Trinitate* is now vendored (Latin translation; see above). The Tura commentaries specifically were lost for centuries after his posthumous association with condemned Origenism and recovered only in the 20th century (1941) — that fate does not extend to *De Trinitate*, which survived instead through the single Passionei manuscript witness and reached print in 1769.
- **Risk:** Moderate — confirms and extends Origen's patterns without introducing new ones (which can create an illusion of independent corroboration where it is really the same tradition).

### 3.5 Philo of Alexandria (c. 20 BCE – c. 50 CE) — diagnostic, not a source *within* the world
- Jewish, pre-horizon, and **absent from the evidence streams**: Philo is not evidence for Christian formation practice. He is used **diagnostically** — to distinguish what Alexandrian Christians *inherited* (allegorical reading of Scripture as philosophy; the Logos as divine intermediary; scriptural strangeness as pedagogy) from what they *transformed or invented*. His pervasive background presence does not make Alexandrian Christianity a form of Judaism; the transformation is real, but so is the inheritance (Runia 1993; van den Hoek 1988). — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the Philonic inheritance as inherited grammar.

### 3.6 Narrative-source and boundary voices (five-dimension entries)

Framework Part II requires that historians and hagiographers who function as *secondary narrative sources* for the world — accounts *of* the world rather than primary voices *within* it — receive their own Author Gravity entries applying all five dimensions, because a narrative source's interpretive agenda, temporal distance, and institutional location distort as much as any primary author's. Eusebius and Palladius are such sources and are given full five-dimension entries; Dionysius (a thinly-attested primary voice, surviving mediated) and Gregory (a first-person formation-narrative source, cross-referenced to §5) are given the same structured treatment for parity.

**Eusebius of Caesarea (c. 260–339 CE) — HIGH Author Gravity risk for institutional/biographical claims.**
- **Visibility:** Very high *as a secondary narrative source*: the *Ecclesiastical History* is the primary — often sole — scaffolding for pre-4th-century Alexandrian institutional history and school personnel (Pantaenus, Clement, Origen, Heraclas, Dionysius).
- **Representativeness:** Not an Alexandrian voice at all; writes from Caesarea with a Constantinian-apologetic project (Christianity's orderly, providential unfolding), which predisposes him toward tidy successions.
- **Influence:** Enormous and lens-shaping — the pre-4th-century institutional picture is received almost entirely through his selection and framing.
- **Limitations:** Writes ~a century after Clement and decades after Origen; his account of an orderly head-succession has been questioned by modern scholars as more institutionally orderly than the independent evidence supports — the direct basis of the Doc_01 §1.2 **Contested** finding on the didaskaleion's institutional form. His apologetic aim compounds this.
- **Transmission history:** The Greek text survives reasonably directly; the risk is **interpretive, not textual** — the danger is trusting his *construction* of institutional order, not a corrupted text.
- **Risk classification: HIGH for institutional/succession/biographical claims specifically** (lower for his verbatim quotation of documents, e.g. Dionysius's letters). Any Doc_04 gravity resting on the school-as-institution must keep this screen active.

**Palladius (c. 363 – c. 420s CE) — hagiographic/travel narrative source.**
- **Visibility:** Moderate; the *Lausiac History* is a major witness to late-4th-century Egyptian ascetic communities.
- **Representativeness:** An outsider-visitor's perspective (a Galatian writing later for a court official), not a desert-insider voice; foundational nonetheless for how desert formation was received.
- **Influence:** Substantial for later understanding of Egyptian asceticism; sits at the very edge of this world's horizon.
- **Limitations:** Mixes direct personal testimony with received report and edifying convention; the two must be distinguished per instance.
- **Transmission history:** Survives in multiple Greek recensions with live textual scholarship and later editorial accretions — attribution of specific material to Palladius's own witness is not always secure.
- **Risk classification: Moderate** — high value for personally-witnessed material (notably his meeting with Didymus), lower for received accounts; the desert-formation content additionally carries the **cross-build provisional** flag (world-attribution held open, Doc_01 §3.3).

**Dionysius of Alexandria (bishop c. 248–264 CE) — primary voice, thinly and mediately attested.**
- **Visibility:** Low as an independent corpus; survives largely through Eusebius's quotation.
- **Representativeness:** Significant for the teacher-to-bishop transition (he headed the teaching tradition before the episcopate) and for crisis governance (the Decian persecution and the reintegration of the lapsed).
- **Influence:** Real for Streams 7 and 8, but reaches us pre-selected.
- **Limitations:** Concentrates on discipline, authority, and crisis rather than ordinary formation.
- **Transmission history:** **Doubly mediated** — his own selection of what to write, then Eusebius's selection of what to preserve; almost nothing survives unmediated.
- **Risk classification: Moderate**, inheriting Eusebius's transmission risk on top of his own.

**Gregory Thaumaturgus (c. 213 – c. 270 CE) — first-person formation-narrative source.**
- **Visibility:** Low total output, but the *Address of Thanksgiving to Origen* (delivered c. 238 CE) is uniquely valuable.
- **Representativeness:** A single student's-eye view of Origen's pedagogy and its sequence (logic and natural science → ethics → theology) — one gifted pupil, not the school's ordinary student.
- **Influence:** Carried Origen's method toward Cappadocia; the Address is the best single witness to the teacher-student formation relationship (§5).
- **Limitations:** Epideictic genre idealizes (though it does not fabricate); a single text.
- **Transmission history:** Direct Greek manuscript transmission, no adversarial translation layer — but the *authenticity and dating* were questioned by Nautin and reaffirmed by later scholarship (Markschies, Mitchell), so the c. 238 dating is carried with that named caveat.
- **Risk classification: Low**, with the Nautin authenticity/dating caveat attached wherever the Address is dated or relied on.

## 4. Secondary Scholarship Assessment

Every formation world's primary sources reach us through modern scholarly interpretation; naming that layer prevents treating modern consensus as ancient evidence (Framework Part II; Constitution Article 28). The works below were independently verified for this draft (author, title, year); each is named for what it is genuinely used for, not decoratively.

- **Frances M. Young**, *Biblical Exegesis and the Formation of Christian Culture* (Cambridge UP, 1997) — the leading treatment of patristic exegesis as *formation* within Graeco-Roman literary-educational culture, and of the Alexandria/Antioch exegetical contrast. Shapes how Streams 1, 2, 5 are read.
- **Annewies van den Hoek**, *Clement of Alexandria and His Use of Philo in the Stromateis* (Brill, 1988) — the specialist demonstration of Clement's dependence on and reshaping of Philo; grounds the Philonic-inheritance claim (Stream 6; §3.5).
- **Henry Chadwick**, *Early Christian Thought and the Classical Tradition* (Clarendon, 1966) and his edition of *Origen: Contra Celsum* (CUP, 1953) — the standard framing of Justin/Clement/Origen's engagement with Greek philosophy, and the standard English *Contra Celsum*.
- **Andrew Louth**, *The Origins of the Christian Mystical Tradition: From Plato to Denys* (Clarendon, 1981; 2nd ed. 2007) — traces the Platonic shaping of Christian contemplative theology, with Clement and Origen pivotal; informs Stream 4.
- **Rowan Williams**, *Arius: Heresy and Tradition* (DLT 1987; rev. SCM 2001) — situates Arius *within* the Origenian Alexandrian theological tradition (a "school" mode of authority), which reframes Stream 12 as internal to this world's own intellectual descent rather than an external intrusion.
- **Samuel Rubenson**, *The Letters of St. Antony* (Lund 1990; Fortress 1995) — argues from the authentic Letters that Antony was literate and an Origenist/Platonizing thinker, against Athanasius's unlettered-monk portrait. Directly bears on the desert world-attribution question (Stream 9; §6) — and cuts *against* any simple reading of Antony as proof that desert formation diverged from the learned gravity.
- **David Brakke**, *Athanasius and the Politics of Asceticism* (Clarendon, 1995) and *Demons and the Making of the Monk* (Harvard, 2006) — reads the *Life of Antony* and Athanasian asceticism as instruments of episcopal politics, and demonic combat as constitutive of monastic self-formation; a caution for Streams 4, 9, 12.
- **Birger A. Pearson**, *Gnosticism and Christianity in Roman and Coptic Egypt* (T&T Clark, 2004) — on the diversity of Egyptian Christian textual culture (including the Nag Hammadi material) that the Gnostic-refusal streams (1, 12) were drawn against.
- **Ewa Wipszycka**, *The Alexandrian Church: People and Institutions* (JJP Suppl. 25, 2015) and *Moines et communautés monastiques en Égypte* (JJP Suppl. 11, 2009) — the leading papyrological/documentary reconstruction of the institutions and personnel of the Egyptian church; a primary corrective to the literary sources' elite bias.
- **Roger S. Bagnall**, *Egypt in Late Antiquity* (Princeton UP, 1993) — the standard reconstruction of society, economy, and literacy in late-antique Egypt from documentary evidence; grounds the Material Culture and Missing-Voices assessments (§5, §6).
- **Peter Brown**, *The World of Late Antiquity* (1971) and *The Body and Society* (1988) — the social-religious world of late antiquity and the meaning of asceticism/renunciation.
- **David T. Runia**, *Philo in Early Christian Literature: A Survey* (Van Gorcum/Fortress, 1993) — the standard survey of Philo's Christian reception, with major attention to Clement, Origen, and Didymus (§3.5).

**Named live debate (carried at full strength, not resolved):** the relationship between Origen's own theology and the "Origenism" condemned at the Second Council of Constantinople (553 CE). Some scholars read the condemned propositions as later distorting systematizations of Origen; others read meaningful continuity with his own thought. This document does **not** adjudicate it (Article 17: named scholarly disagreement is carried, not resolved); it bears on how confidently any specific Origen doctrine may be attributed to Origen himself (§3.1, §7).

## 5. Formation Narrative and Hagiographic Sources

This source type carries formation ecology from inside individual lives and teacher-student relationships and requires its own assessment and tier estimate (Framework Part II; four-tier story scheme). Tiers here are **provisional** and finalized at Doc_09.

1. **Gregory Thaumaturgus, *Address of Thanksgiving to Origen*** (c. 238 CE — carrying the Nautin authenticity/dating caveat, §3.6) — direct first-person student testimony; epideictic genre idealizes but does not fabricate. Primary evidence for the teacher-student formation relationship. — **Tier 1 (Documented Historical Narrative)** at the narrative level.
2. **Athanasius, *Life of Antony*** (c. 356–362 CE, within living memory) — hagiographic conventions (call narrative, temptation sequence, exemplary death). The formation *ideal* it communicates is credible evidence; the specific episodes (visions, demonic combat, miracles) are not historical reporting. — **Tier 3 (Attributed/Hagiographic Tradition).** **Cross-build note:** where used as evidence of the *desert tradition's own* formation logic, or to argue continuity/intensification between the learned tradition and the desert, the cross-build constraint applies regardless of framing (Doc_01 §3.3); and Rubenson's literate-Antony argument means even the "unlettered" premise is contested.
3. **Palladius, *Lausiac History*** (c. 419–420 CE) — **Tier 1** for Palladius's own direct encounters (including Didymus); **Tier 3** for received accounts. Cross-build note applies to the desert-formation content.
4. **Apophthegmata Patrum** (collected late 4th–5th c.; attributed to Antony, Macarius, Poemen, and others, plus anonymous material) — the collection tradition is well-attested; individual attributions carry only moderate confidence. — **Tier 2 (Collected Traditional Material).** **Cross-build provisional** (world-attribution held open).
5. **Jerome, *On Illustrious Men* and Letters** (late 4th c.) — **Tier 1** for his own direct testimony (including brief study with Didymus), with the explicit caveat that his later **anti-Origenist** position colors his retrospective treatment of Origen-associated figures.
6. **Eusebius, *Ecclesiastical History*** — scaffolding for the school personnel; full assessment at §3.6 (HIGH risk for institutional/biographical claims).

## 6. Source Asymmetry, Missing Voices, and the Article 20 Affirmative Duty

**Overrepresented:** bishops and episcopal leaders; teachers and theological scholars; educated Greek-speaking elites capable of literary production.

**Underrepresented / structurally absent:** women (present as martyrs, teaching-recipients, consecrated virgins/widows — rarely as literary voices); laborers, artisans, merchants; rural Egyptian Christians; **Coptic-speaking communities**; enslaved persons in Christian households; catechumens (present only as recipients, never as authors).

**The Greek–Coptic asymmetry (the defining source problem).** Nearly all literary evidence is Greek; the Coptic-speaking majority is substantially invisible in the literary record, so the record may systematically overrepresent urban, learned, Alexandrian concerns relative to the broader Egyptian ecology. **Deliberately-engageable Coptic evidence** (underrepresented in standard patristics because it requires Coptic access): Coptic Scripture; Coptic martyr acts; Pachomian rule literature (*cross-build provisional*); the Nag Hammadi texts (evidence for the diversity of Egyptian Christian textual culture); and, just past the horizon, Shenoute of Atripe's Coptic corpus (indicative of Coptic formation concerns). Papyrology (Bagnall; Wipszycka) partially reaches non-elite practice where literary sources cannot.

**The Article 20 Affirmative Duty — named explicitly.** The architecture carries an *active obligation* regarding voices the record does not naturally amplify, not merely awareness of asymmetry (Constitution Article 20).
- **Primary duty (making structural absence visible):** name whose voices the sources structurally suppress, why, and what that omission means for the reconstruction — and carry that naming *into the encounter* (a participant-facing obligation, not a backstage note). For this world, the structurally suppressed within the community are: **women** (especially as household formation-transmitters and as theological voices); **ordinary non-literate believers**; **rural and Coptic-speaking Christians**; **catechumens**; and **enslaved persons in Christian households**. They are suppressed because literary production required Greek literacy, leisure, and (usually) male ecclesial standing — conditions most of the community did not meet.
- **Secondary duty (bounded reconstruction):** where evidence permits, marginalized voices may be reconstructed — but only on independent positive evidence (comparative evidence from similar communities; material/documentary evidence; or the world's own sources read against the grain via a *specific textual trace*, never inference from silence alone), presented at Inferential-Thin confidence and never as equivalent to documented material. **Absence of evidence is never evidence of content.**
- **Scope of the duty (Article 20 vs. Article 23):** this duty covers the marginalized *within* the community. It does **not** extend to the community's external opponents (Gnostics, Manichaeans, Arians as the tradition combated them) — those are governed by the Writing-From-Inside Principle (Article 23), not the marginalized-voices duty. The two must not be conflated.

**The governing warning (repeated because it is load-bearing):** evidential visibility must not be silently converted into ecological visibility. A practice frequent in the surviving authors may be genuinely central, central only to those authors' concerns, or both — and distinguishing these is Doc_04's work, not a presumption this document may make.

## 7. Forces-Lens Reading (Forces Framework, Step 2)

This applies the forces lens to the *sources*: which sources can speak to which forces (identified in Doc_01 §7), and what the silences and survivorship patterns reveal. **Framing rule (Forces Framework):** confidence levels in this section describe *a source's capacity to attest a named force*, not the truth of the force-claim itself.

**Initiating forces.** The primary initiating force is the **Philonic inheritance** — the pre-existing Jewish-scripture/Greek-philosophy synthesis this world *entered* rather than built. Best-attesting sources: Clement's *Stromateis* (most directly addresses the inherited grammar; capacity **Widely Accepted**) and, diagnostically, Philo himself (§3.5). Early apostolic/catechetical inheritance is attested via the *Paedagogus* (capacity **Widely Accepted** for presence; **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for how it functioned for non-elite participants). Initiating-condition sources cluster early-to-mid horizon.

**Ongoing external forces.** *Philosophical (Neoplatonic) competition* — best attested by Clement and Origen (*Contra Celsum* is the clearest single witness of apologetic pressure); capacity **Widely Accepted** early-to-mid horizon, **Inferential-Thin** for its effect on ordinary believers. *Gnostic challenge* — Clement's "true gnostic," Origen's counter-account, and the anti-heretical writings (Streams 1, 12); capacity **Widely Accepted** for the school tradition early-to-mid, **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** at best for the broad ecology. *Persecution* — Stream 8; the Decian (249–251) and Diocletianic (303–311) persecutions are **Documented/Widely Accepted** as events, but the sources' capacity to attest *memory between episodes* is **Dominant Modern Reconstruction**. *Egyptian indigenous religious environment* — **no literary source names it explicitly**; capacity for specific claims is **Inferential-Thin**, reachable only obliquely through material culture (§8).

**Ongoing internal forces / transmission.** The learned tradition transmitted itself through teacher-student relationships (Gregory's *Address*), commentary and homily, and the copying of Origen's vast corpus — a transmission that was itself contested (§3.1, and the Origen Latin-transmission note below). The episcopal consolidation across the horizon (Stream 7, 12) is an internal force whose late-horizon intensification is well attested (Athanasius) but whose earlier form is thinly and Eusebius-mediated.

**Constitutive refusals** (Doc_01 §7 Q4): Gnostic esotericism (school tradition **Widely Accepted**; broad ecology **Dominant Modern Reconstruction**); the purely literal reader (**Widely Accepted** for the school tradition, **Inferential-Thin** for the broader ecology, since it presupposes literacy); philosophical systems that cannot include the incarnate Logos (**Widely Accepted** early-to-mid); Arianism (**Widely Accepted**, but strictly located post-325).

**Forces silences (predicted by the frame, and themselves evidence).** Three forces operated on most of the population but left the thinnest traces: (1) whatever pressed on **Coptic-speaking Christians** from Roman administration and indigenous religion — named by Streams 10/11, filled by none; (2) the formation logic of **rural Egyptian Christianity** — partial access via the Pachomian material (cross-build provisional), otherwise near-silence; (3) **domestic/household formation** of ordinary believers, especially by women — only documentary-papyri traces. That the forces bearing on the *majority* left the faintest record is itself a forces finding (survivorship favored the literate, urban, Greek-speaking, male, and ecclesially-positioned), carried to Doc_08.

**Transmission-history note — Origen's Latin corpus.** A significant part of Origen survives only through Rufinus (who softened doctrinally suspect passages in translation) and Jerome (who, post-rupture, quoted him adversarially). This is a transmission *force* (Forces Framework, Transmission dimension) shaping what can be known: precise attributions of Origen's most contested positions (pre-existence of souls, *apokatastasis*, subordinationist Christology) are refracted through interested intermediaries and must be held at correspondingly reduced confidence. Full treatment in Doc_08, Cell 2B/3B.

## 8. Material Culture and Daily Life Sources

- **Documentary (papyri).** Egypt is uniquely rich: the Oxyrhynchus papyri (early gospel fragments, private letters with Christian content, community receipts), and Fayum/Delta documentary material naming Christian individuals and communities. This is often the *only* direct, non-elite-authored, unmediated trace of ordinary Christian life. — **Confidence: Moderate** (fragmentary, frequently decontextualized, but direct where Christian content is identifiable).
- **Archaeological.** Alexandria's own remains are fragmentary (continuous rebuilding, coastal subsidence). Available: baptistery and church-building archaeology elsewhere in Egypt (spatial ordering of worship — where catechumens stood versus the faithful); funerary archaeology (Christian–indigenous Egyptian mortuary interaction). — **Confidence: Limited for Alexandria specifically; Moderate for broader Egyptian Christian archaeology.**
- **Social-historical background.** Literacy in Roman Egypt was a small minority (urban, male, economically secure); most of the population lived in agricultural labor and subsistence tenancy. Alexandria itself was exceptional — multilingual, cosmopolitan, higher literacy and mobility than the hinterland — and this urban exceptionalism must be held in view when the learned streams tempt over-generalization (Bagnall). — **Confidence: Moderate–Strong** (rests on general Roman-Egypt scholarship, not Christian sources).
- **Surrounding cultural/religious environment.** The continuous indigenous Egyptian religious environment (the human-divine relationship, death and transformation, sacred text and priestly interpretation) likely shaped Alexandrian Christian formation at the level of cultural atmosphere, but is hard to trace with specific textual evidence — treated as background atmosphere, not direct source material. — **Confidence: Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for influence-in-general; **Inferential-Thin** for any specific mechanism.

Where material-culture evidence is sparse, that is acknowledged rather than treated as evidence the conditions were absent (Framework Part II).
