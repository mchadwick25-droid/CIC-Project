## Story Text

A visitor to Alexandria, later in this world's life, tells us of going to see the blind teacher four times over ten years, seeking him out the way one seeks out someone whose sight has gone but whose seeing has not. He tells us Didymus had lost his eyes as a small child, before he had learned even his letters, and so had never read a word with his own eyes in his whole life — and that this had not closed a single door his formation needed open. He heard the man discourse from memory on Scripture with the same command another might bring to a scroll open on the table before him.

It is said, elsewhere and differently, that the desert father who once came to Alexandria on account of the Arians, perceiving Didymus's learning, told him not to grieve the loss of eyes that even gnats and flies possess, since he had instead eyes such as the angels see with, by which God himself is discerned.

## Formation Ecology Connection

Illustrates C1 (Scripture as Deep Formative Reality) at its sharpest edge: the deep reading this world prizes is shown here to be a capacity of the soul, not merely a skill of the eyes — the text's depths are perceived, not decoded. It equally illustrates the accompaniment mode of formation (Doc_05 §5) continuing across the whole span into the late horizon, and C5's own documented late-horizon attenuation (Doc_04 §3.5; the C5↔T2 interaction, §6): the story attests that even at the school tradition's late edge, when C5's living force was documented to be attenuating, the teacher-student encounter it names remained sought out and real.

## Tier Justification

Direct first-person testimony: the author records his own repeated visits over a decade, not a report of someone else's encounter. Palladius elsewhere in the same work relays received desert traditions at one or more removes (those passages are Tier 3); this passage is explicitly his own meeting, which the tier criteria distinguish as Tier 1 even within a single author's larger work. The account is internally consistent with the independently attested fact of Didymus's blindness and his standing as a teacher in Alexandria in this period.

The second Story Text paragraph above is Tier 3, Contested/hearsay, not Tier 1 — a materially different evidentiary weight than the encounter itself, which remains genuinely Tier 1. Direct inspection of the vendored Lausiac History (both the Clarke 1918 English and the Budge 1907 Syriac recension) found no version of this saying anywhere in it, in either recension. The saying does survive, but differently sourced and differently spoken: Socrates Scholasticus's *Historia Ecclesiastica* IV.25 (`npnf202`) reports it as words **Antony spoke to Didymus**, not words Didymus spoke about himself, and reports it at one remove even there ("It is said that..."). Sozomen III.15 also carries a version of the same saying, but is not independent corroboration of Socrates — NPNF's own apparatus lists Socrates as a source for this chapter of Sozomen, and the two accounts differ in exactly the way a derivative retelling would (Sozomen: Antony visits "to give his testimony in favor of the doctrines of Athanasius," and the animals are "rats, mice, and the lowest animals," not "gnats and flies"). This remains a single reported tradition, differently worded in its two survivals, not two agreeing witnesses.

---

## Usage Guidance

May be offered as remembered history, with Palladius named as the visitor and firsthand witness — the encounter paragraph only. The Antony anecdote, if used at all, must be offered as a separately-sourced, later-mediated tradition ("it is said that..."), never folded into Palladius's own testimony or attributed to Didymus's own self-account, since it is neither. This story belongs to the late horizon (Didymus's active teaching years run into the 380s–390s CE) — it should not be used to characterize the school tradition's earlier, mid-horizon peak, where the evidentiary base is different (Origen-concentrated rather than Didymus-concentrated). The story is silent on Didymus's specific theological positions in their later, contested reception; it attests only the encounter and the capacity it demonstrates.
