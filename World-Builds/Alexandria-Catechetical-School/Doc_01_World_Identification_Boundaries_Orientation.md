## 1. Candidate World Name and Scope Determination

### 1.1 The naming/scope question, stated plainly

The build folder for this world is `Alexandria-Catechetical-School`. That label can carry either of two materially different scopes, and Step 1 must not assume one silently (an unargued scope decision is a named failure mode in this project's history):

- **Narrow reading:** the world *is* the Alexandrian *didaskaleion* as an institution — the succession of Christian teachers traditionally listed as Pantaenus → Clement → Origen → Heraclas → Dionysius → … → Didymus the Blind — treated as its own bounded formation world, distinct from a broader "Alexandrian Christianity."
- **Broad reading:** the world is the **Alexandrian Christian formation ecology** of roughly 150–400 CE (the scope the Alexandria-v7 source built), whose single most identity-defining feature is a distinctive tradition of *learned, philosophically-engaged, allegorical-scriptural catechetical formation* — the didaskaleion tradition as its anchoring formation gravity candidate, not as the whole of the world.

### 1.2 Why the narrow (institution-only) reading is not adopted as the world's definition

A formation world, constitutionally, is "a historically situated communal interpretive ecology" (Constitution Article 3), not an institution or a school. Naming *the institution itself* as the world runs into a specific evidentiary problem: the very character of the didaskaleion *as a formal institution with a continuous teacher-succession* is **Contested** in the scholarship, and the disagreement runs in more than one direction. R. van den Broek argues that "the whole idea of a Christian school with a succession of teachers handing down a fixed tradition of learning to their pupil successors is completely false, at least until the second decade of the third century" — i.e., until roughly Origen's mature period (van den Broek, "The Christian 'School' of Alexandria in the Second and Third Centuries," in *Centres of Learning*, Brill, 1995, 39–47). Annewies van den Hoek, who quotes that judgment approvingly, likewise challenges the traditional formal-institution-with-succession picture and reconstructs a more informal teaching milieu before Origen (van den Hoek, "The 'Catechetical' School of Early Christian Alexandria and Its Philonic Heritage," *Harvard Theological Review* 90.1 (1997): 59–87). Clemens Scholten, by contrast, holds that there *was* an institutional school but that "catechetical school" is a misnomer for it — it was the Alexandrian church's advanced *theological* school, not a school for the instruction of catechumens (Scholten, "Die alexandrinische Katechetenschule," *Jahrbuch für Antike und Christentum* 38 (1995): 16–37). The traditional organized-school-with-head-succession picture derives largely from Eusebius (*Historia Ecclesiastica* 5.10–6.30), writing in the early 4th century and reconstructing origins from incomplete sources. — **Confidence: Contested** — scholars genuinely disagree both about whether the didaskaleion was a formal institution before c. 215–230 CE and about what kind of institution it was; **Widely Accepted** that identifiable Christian teachers (Clement, then Origen) were conducting advanced Christian instruction in Alexandria across this period, whatever its institutional form.

To define the *world* as the institution would therefore be to build the world's identity on one of its most contested factual claims. That is the opposite of the discipline Step 1 exists to enforce.

### 1.3 Working determination (proposed name), and what is flagged for the project lead

**Working determination:** this world is the **Alexandrian Christian formation ecology, c. 150–400 CE**, anchored and named by its distinctive catechetical-formation tradition — a milieu of learned Christian teaching, allegorical-spiritual reading of Scripture, and confident synthesis with Greek intellectual culture — construed as the world's anchoring *formation gravity candidate* rather than as a bounded institution. Proposed world name: **"Alexandrian Christianity (Catechetical-Formation World)."** The catechetical tradition gives the world its name and its sharpest distinction from adjacent worlds (Section 4) and from the separately-planned Desert Christianity world (Section 3); it does not narrow the world to the *didaskaleion*'s attested membership.

**Flagged for the project lead (Open_Gaps OG-3):** the world's *name* and *outer scope* are foundational, and the folder label `Alexandria-Catechetical-School` is a real signal that the intended scope might be narrower than the broad reading adopted here. This document argues the broad reading (Sections 2–8 below) rather than assuming it, but the final name and scope are Mark's to confirm. Three clean options for that confirmation: (a) **broad, as proposed here** — "Alexandrian Christianity," catechetical tradition as anchor; (b) **narrow** — a bounded "Alexandrian Catechetical School" world (which would require re-arguing the temporal/geographic boundaries in Sections 2–3 around the teaching tradition specifically and excising most of the Nicene/episcopal/monastic material as belonging to a different world); (c) **broad ecology, name emphasizing the school** — e.g., "Alexandria: The School and Its World." The downstream cascade (Doc_02 onward) is built on option (a) unless redirected; the cost of a later redirect grows with each document, so this is surfaced now.

> **OG-3 RESOLVED — confirmed by the project lead (Mark Chadwick), in session, 2026-07-17:** **option (a), the broad reading as proposed.** The world's confirmed name is **"Alexandrian Christianity (Catechetical-Formation World)"**; the catechetical-formation tradition is its anchor and namesake, not its boundary. The whole cleared cascade (Doc_02–09) and the Representative build stand on this reading with no rework required.

This name designates a formation ecology, not a denomination, a theological school in the modern sense, or a doctrinal system. — **Confidence: Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for the scope determination itself (a defensible, consistently-argued periodization and framing; not a claim of scholarly unanimity on exactly where "this world" begins and ends).

## 2. Temporal Scope

### 2.1 Candidate horizon: c. 150–400 CE

This horizon captures: the maturation of Alexandrian Christian learned formation from Clement of Alexandria (c. 150–215 CE) through Origen (c. 185–254 CE) and the post-Origen teachers; Didymus the Blind (c. 313–398 CE); Athanasius (c. 296/298–373 CE) and the Nicene settlement; the major Trinitarian and Christological controversies of the 4th century as they played out in Egypt; and the emergence of Egyptian monasticism across the latter portion of the horizon. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the figures' approximate dates (all conventionally given with "c."); **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for the choice of this span as one world's horizon.

### 2.2 Beginning point

The defensible beginning point is the **latter 2nd century**, with Clement's Alexandrian activity (c. 180s–202 CE, until he left during the persecution under Septimius Severus) as the earliest richly-attested formation activity in this world's own learned register, and Pantaenus (fl. c. 180, traditionally Clement's teacher) as an earlier but far more thinly-attested figure treated as boundary-adjacent rather than as the world's own securely-documented starting point. The traditional claim of an apostolic foundation by **Mark the Evangelist** is treated the same way the Syriac build treated the Addai/Abgar legend: as a later foundation-narrative carrying real formational and identity weight for the living tradition, **not** as a historical beginning point. The Markan foundation is first clearly asserted by Eusebius (*HE* 2.16, early 4th century) with no contemporary 1st–2nd-century corroboration. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** that the Markan foundation is a later apostolic-foundation claim rather than documented 1st-century history; **Widely Accepted** for Clement's floruit and departure.

### 2.3 Ending point, and the two internal-vs-boundary questions

The horizon ends at **c. 400 CE**, intentionally *before* the genuine world-boundary at 451 CE. Two internal questions govern this:

**(a) Is Nicaea (325 CE) a world boundary or an internal development?** Working determination: **internal**. The Arian controversy and Nicaea sharpen and reconfigure existing organizing forces — especially scriptural authority and episcopal order — without generating a new ecology with distinct organizing gravities. The catechetical-formation logic (transformation through Scripture read at depth, worship, teaching, and philosophical engagement) persists across 325 CE. What changes is addressed precisely in Section 5 (World Separation Criteria) rather than smoothed over: episcopal authority does not merely intensify, it takes on a qualitatively new *function* (doctrinal enforcement at scale) after 325. That is a substantial internal development gravity discovery (Doc_04) must track with temporal precision, not a world boundary. — **Confidence: Dominant Modern Reconstruction.**

**(b) Is the 451 boundary real, and does it sit outside this horizon?** Yes. The Council of Chalcedon (451 CE) generated a permanent communal fracture: the Coptic (miaphysite) and Chalcedonian (Greek/"Melkite") streams of Alexandrian Christianity became distinct communities. That is a genuine world-ending/transforming force (a Doc_08 Cell 3 matter) and it lies deliberately beyond this horizon. Ending at c. 400 keeps this world upstream of the fracture. — **Confidence: Documented** for the Chalcedonian schism as a permanent fracture; **Widely Accepted** that it constitutes a world boundary for reconstruction purposes.

**(c) Monastic emergence within the horizon.** Egyptian monasticism (Antony, c. 251–356; Pachomius, c. 292–348) emerges across the latter half of this horizon. Whether it is an *intensification* of this world's existing formation concerns or a *distinct daughter ecology* is **not resolved here** — it is held open pending Doc_04 gravity discovery, and it interacts with the Desert Christianity cross-build boundary (Section 3.3). This document does not pre-decide it. — **Confidence: held open; see Sections 3.3 and 6.**

### 2.4 What the endpoint precision leaves open

Setting the endpoint at "c. 400" rather than a specific event is deliberate: unlike the Syriac world's 410 Synod, Alexandria's late-horizon does not present a single crisp institutional hinge at the century's end — Didymus dies c. 398, the anti-Origenist controversy erupts around 399–400 (Theophilus of Alexandria), and the Chalcedonian boundary is still 50 years off. **Flag for Doc_02/Doc_04:** whether the endpoint should be tightened to a specific marker (e.g., the outbreak of the first Origenist controversy c. 399–400, which directly concerns this world's own defining inheritance) should be revisited once source ecology and gravity discovery show whether anything in the world's *formation logic* shifts at that point. Named here, not resolved. — **Confidence: Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for "c. 400" as a working endpoint.

## 3. Geographic Scope

### 3.1 Primary centers

- **Alexandria** — the intellectual, institutional, and episcopal center; a Greek-speaking Mediterranean metropolis and one of the empire's great cities.
- **The Nile Delta and the broader Egyptian Christian urban network.**
- **The Egyptian hinterland (chora)** — rural communities and, increasingly across the horizon, monastic settlements.

— **Confidence: Documented** for Alexandria's centrality; **Widely Accepted** for the Delta/hinterland reach of Egyptian Christianity in this period.

### 3.2 Greek and Coptic Egypt within one world

The ecology's reach spans, with sharply uneven evidence: **Greek-speaking Alexandrian communities** (by far the best-documented, and the source of nearly all the learned-formation material this world is named for); **Coptic-speaking communities across Egypt** (substantially less documented but ecologically significant, and increasingly visible from the later 3rd century as Coptic becomes a literary Christian language); and communities across the Delta and hinterland. The Greek/Coptic evidentiary asymmetry is one of this world's defining source problems and is carried forward as a Doc_02 Author Gravity and Missing-Voices matter (Section 7 below flags it as a meaningful silence). — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**

### 3.3 Note on Desert Christianity and the cross-build boundary (required before the Desert build)

This note names a boundary Alexandria's construction must observe and cannot resolve on its own terms. It is required by Constitution Article 3 (no cross-world contamination; convergences between worlds must be *discovered*, not engineered).

- **Geographic fact:** the desert communities of the Thebaid, Nitria, Kellia, and Scetis lie geographically within Alexandria's horizon, and their formation concerns emerged from the same broader Egyptian Christian world. The desert tradition is not a foreign body inserted from outside; it emerged within the Egyptian ecology and was transmitted partly through figures deeply embedded in Alexandrian learned Christianity (Athanasius, who wrote the *Life of Antony*; Evagrius Ponticus, whose ascetic theology is demonstrably Origenian in structure).
- **The tension the evidence presents, stated honestly on both sides.** The claim that desert formation is simply an *intensification* of Alexandrian learned-catechetical formation rests most securely on the educated desert strand (Evagrius; the more literate abbas). But the desert tradition's founding portrait includes practitioners for whom learned allegorical Scripture-reading operated differently or not at all. Antony is portrayed as illiterate in Athanasius's *Life of Antony* (the *Life* is Widely Accepted as a text and as broadly historical background; the specific illiteracy claim is hagiographic and Athanasius-shaped). **But that portrait is itself contested by modern scholarship:** Samuel Rubenson argues from the *Letters of St. Antony* that Antony was in fact literate and philosophically formed, an Origenist thinker, and that Athanasius's illiterate-holy-man portrait is a deliberate construction (Rubenson, *The Letters of St. Antony* — 1990; Eng. rev. ed. 1995 — cited here as the standard statement of the literate-Antony case; exact edition/subtitle to be pinned at Doc_02, and the case engaged fully there). The tension therefore cuts **both** ways and cannot be resolved at Step 1: neither "illiterate Antony proves desert formation diverges from the school gravity" nor "Origenist Antony proves it is simply the school gravity intensified" is a Step 1 finding. Its resolution belongs to Doc_04 gravity discovery.
- **The cross-build constraint.** A Desert Christianity formation world is planned as a distinct build. Alexandria's construction **must not** claim desert formation as constitutive of the Alexandrian ecology in ways that preempt what Desert Christianity will discover about itself. Specifically: the *Apophthegmata Patrum*, the Pachomian *koinonia* and its rule literature, and the ascetic theology of Evagrius are held as **shared evidence whose world-attribution is determined by gravity discovery**, not pre-assigned to Alexandria here. Alexandria may reference these as evidence of what its ecology produced within its own horizon; it may not claim them as expressions of Alexandrian formation logic without the demonstration that belongs to Doc_04.
- **Governing principle:** if Alexandria constructs desert formation as an expression of its own ecology before Desert Christianity is built, any convergence found later when the two worlds meet is an artifact of shared construction assumptions, not a genuine finding. Holding the shared evidence open protects the value of a genuine convergence if gravity discovery confirms one.

— **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the geographic and transmission facts; **Contested** for Antony's literacy and for the intensification-vs-distinct-ecology question, both explicitly held open.

### 3.4 Adjacent worlds — exchange partners, not part of this ecology

The following are more accurately treated as distinct formation worlds with significant exchange relationships to Alexandria, not as secondary zones of this world (treating them as internal would blur the differentiation that makes each world distinct):

- **Palestine / Caesarea** — where Origen relocated c. 231–234 and did much of his later work; a distinct world with its own ecology that received and transmitted Alexandrian influence.
- **Cappadocia** — received Origenian influence (through Gregory Thaumaturgus and the Cappadocians' Origen reception) and developed its own synthesis.
- **Antioch** — a distinct world with systematically different hermeneutical (more literal-historical) and Christological tendencies, in ongoing productive tension with Alexandria.

These exchange relationships belong to later external-world analysis, not to scope determination here. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**

## 4. Cultural Scope

**Language and Scripture.** The learned tradition operated in **Greek**, reading the **Septuagint** as inspired Scripture (Alexandria being the Septuagint's own city of origin) alongside the emerging New Testament writings; Coptic rises as a Christian literary language across the horizon, especially in monastic and non-elite settings. — **Confidence: Documented** for Greek/Septuagint centrality; **Widely Accepted** for Coptic's rise.

**Interpretive and theological method.** The distinctive Alexandrian mode is **allegorical and spiritual reading of Scripture at multiple levels**, and **confident synthesis with Greek philosophy** (Middle Platonism, Stoic logos-theory, Pythagorean-mathematical currents), inherited and transformed from the Jewish-Alexandrian synthesis of **Philo** (c. 20 BCE – c. 50 CE), whose allegorical method and Logos-as-intermediary theology were a living intellectual inheritance for the first Christian Alexandrians (Philo's Christian reception is treated in David T. Runia, *Philo in Early Christian Literature: A Survey*, 1993 — the standard reference; precise engagement deferred to Doc_02). This is the world's signature and its sharpest contrast with Antioch. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the allegorical-philosophical character of the Alexandrian learned tradition and its Philonic debt; the claim that this character defines the *whole* ecology (not only its educated Greek stratum) is **explicitly not yet established** — see the Author Gravity discipline in Section 7.

**Intellectual-religious milieu.** Alexandria was a cosmopolitan hub where Neoplatonism, the mystery religions, indigenous Egyptian priestly-interpretive traditions, a large and ancient Jewish community (devastated in the 115–117 CE revolt but formatively present in memory and inheritance), and various Gnostic movements (Valentinian, Basilidean) were in visible, competitive relationship. This plural milieu is treated as constitutive of the world's formation, not background (Section 7, Preliminary Forces). — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**

**Institutional/social scope.** Beyond the learned teaching tradition: the Alexandrian episcopate (which became, across the horizon, unusually powerful — the "pharaonic" bishop of later characterization); catechumenal formation for ordinary believers; the martyr tradition (the Great Persecution under Diocletian, 303–313, fell heavily on Egypt and is central to Coptic identity; the Coptic calendar's "Era of the Martyrs" / *Anno Martyrum* is reckoned from Diocletian's accession in 284 CE, though associated in Coptic memory with the later persecution); and the emerging monastic settlements. The relative weight of these across the world — how much the learned-catechetical gravity actually organized the formation of ordinary, non-elite, Coptic-speaking believers — is the central open question this document hands to gravity discovery. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the existence and significance of each; **Inferential-Thin** for ordinary-believer interior formation, which the sources barely reach.

## 5. World Separation Criteria

The Framework requires formal engagement with: when does this world become another?

- **Has a new gravity emerged?** No *new* organizing force appears post-325 that had no prior form. But a precise answer is required, not a blanket "no": what changed at and after Nicaea was not the *existence* of episcopal authority (bishops governed, taught, and adjudicated in Alexandria well before 325 — the Demetrius–Origen conflict of the early 3rd century and the episcopate of Dionysius of Alexandria, c. 248–264, both attest this) but its *configuration*. Post-325, the bishop of Alexandria functions as singular doctrinal arbiter enforcing conciliar Trinitarian boundaries across all Egypt, with exile, letter-networks, and synodal appeal as instruments (most visible in Athanasius). This is a substantial intensification-and-functional-transformation of an existing force, not the emergence of a new one and not mere continuity. Gravity discovery (Doc_04) must treat "episcopal authority" with temporal precision across the 250-year span, not as one undifferentiated feature. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the pre- and post-325 authority facts; **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for the intensification-not-emergence reading.
- **Has a major gravity disappeared?** No. Scriptural interpretation, learned formation, worship, and authority all persist across 150–400, though they develop. — **Assessment: no world boundary.**
- **Has formation changed substantially?** Formation deepens and becomes more institutionally elaborated (catechumenal formalization; monastic emergence), but the underlying logic — gradual transformation through Scripture, worship, community, and teaching — persists. — **Assessment: internal development, not boundary.**
- **Has authority / worship / interpretation changed substantially?** Authority: substantially developed (above), not replaced. Interpretation: the allegorical-spiritual mode persists and is transmitted onward. Worship: continuity across the horizon (the Chalcedonian liturgical divergence is post-451). — **Assessment: internal development, not boundary.**

**Boundary determination:** one world across c. 150–400 CE. The internal transition at Nicaea (325) is a significant development gravity discovery must account for; the genuine world fracture is Chalcedon (451), deliberately outside the horizon. — **Confidence: Dominant Modern Reconstruction.**

## 6. Strand Determination (Constitution Article 21)

**Question:** does the evidence support distinct internal strands (sub-ecologies with genuinely different organizing gravities, authority structures, or formation logics requiring separate tracking), or is the world strand-singular?

**Finding: strand-singular — as a Step 1 working determination, with one candidate split named and held open.**

Strand is a finding, never a presupposed schema (Article 21). The candidate internal split most worth naming is **learned-urban Alexandrian formation** (Clement, Origen, the didaskaleion milieu, the episcopal city) versus **desert/monastic formation** (the Nitrian/Scetiote and Pachomian communities). At Step 1 this is treated as strand-singular for internal-construction purposes, on the following honest footing:

- What is *shared* across the urban-learned and desert material — christological-transformational formation, allegorical/spiritual reading (in the learned strand and, per Rubenson, arguably in at least part of the desert strand), prayer, ascetic seriousness, and a common Egyptian ecclesial matrix under the Alexandrian episcopate — is real and does the actual work of the strand-singular finding.
- What is *not yet resolvable* — whether desert formation runs on the *same* organizing gravity as learned-catechetical formation or a genuinely different one (the Antony tension, Section 3.3) — is **not** used to prop up the finding in either direction. It is exactly the question held open for Doc_04 and for the Desert Christianity cross-build boundary.

**Crucial distinction (carried forward):** the *internal* strand determination (is Alexandria strand-singular for its own gravity discovery, lexicon, and ecological reconstruction?) is separate from the *cross-world* attribution question (does desert formation belong to Alexandria's ecology, to a distinct Desert Christianity world, or to both by discovered convergence?). Alexandria may be strand-singular internally while the desert-attribution question remains open. This document resolves the first (strand-singular, provisionally) and explicitly holds the second open. — **Confidence: Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for the strand-singular internal disposition; the desert-attribution question is **held open**, not assigned a confidence.

**Revision trigger, named not obscured:** if Doc_04 gravity discovery reveals the desert/monastic tradition generating organizing gravities *absent* from the urban-learned tradition, both this strand-singular determination and the monastic-emergence classification (Section 2.3) require revision. No such finding has been made yet, because gravity discovery has not yet run.

## 7. Preliminary Forces Identification (Forces Framework Step 1)

This is not the full forces analysis (that is Doc_08). It is the Step 1 orientation that governs what Doc_02 source-ecology work looks for, and which silences are expected to be meaningful. Four questions:

**Q1 — What generated this world?** *External:* a city where the synthesis of Jewish Scripture and Greek philosophy was already institutionally underway before Christianity arrived — Philo had already shown, within living cultural memory of the first Christian Alexandrians, that the Septuagint could be read allegorically as philosophy, that a Logos could mediate between the transcendent God and the world, and that scriptural strangeness was pedagogical rather than embarrassing. This was the intellectual grammar of the city's educated class. *Internal:* the inherited conviction that all genuine truth participates in the divine Logos — taken up and christologically transformed — which made philosophical engagement a *preparation* for Christian faith rather than a threat to it. That initiating conviction is among the most consequential generative forces the ecology carries. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the Philonic-milieu inheritance; **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for its characterization as a specifically *generative* force.

**Q2 — What larger world was it embedded in?** A metropolis that was itself a meeting-point of worlds: African, Greek, Jewish, Roman, and Egyptian; urban intellectual life and desert hinterland; local community and imperial network. Forces should therefore be expected from several directions simultaneously — Neoplatonic and Gnostic intellectual competition (sharing the same city and sometimes the same teacher-student networks), a large Jewish community and its scriptural inheritance, indigenous Egyptian religious culture (death, transformation, sacred text, priestly interpretation), and Roman imperial administration and its persecutions. The **near-invisibility of non-Greek, non-elite Egyptian Christian experience** in the surviving literary record is itself a force-relevant silence, predicted by this frame. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**

**Q3 — What was it responding to?** *Philosophical competition* — Alexandria could not concede that Christianity was for the uneducated; Clement's "philosophy as schoolmaster to Christ" and Origen's *Contra Celsum* (a point-by-point answer to a sustained pagan critique) are responses to real competitive pressure. *Gnostic claims* to a superior *gnosis* — Clement's portrait of the "true gnostic" (the fully-formed Christian whose knowledge comes through faith, Scripture, and transformation, not esoteric initiation) is a direct counter to Valentinian and Basilidean claims, and it pressed the tradition to develop a positive account of formation *available to the whole community*, not a spiritual elite. *The Jewish hermeneutical inheritance* — which had to be taken up and christologically transformed rather than rejected. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the documented Gnostic/pagan-philosophical engagement (Clement and Origen attest it directly).

**Q4 — What was it refusing?** The refusal of **Gnostic esotericism** (truth is for the whole community, not a secret elite) — the most explicitly attested refusal in the early-horizon learned tradition. The refusal of the **purely literal reader** (a reader who stops at the surface has missed what God placed in the depths) — a claim about what faithful reading *is*, at least within the educated tradition where such reading was possible. The refusal of **philosophical systems that could not include the Logos-become-flesh** (Platonism's account of reality's depth was kept; its inability to accommodate incarnation was refused). And, crystallizing later in the horizon, the refusal of the **Arian account** of the Logos as highest creature — a refusal that sharpens and defends commitments already present rather than generating the ecology. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the Gnostic and Arian refusals; **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for the structural framing of the literal-reader and philosophy refusals.

**Author Gravity discipline (binding on downstream construction).** The generative forces, responses, and refusals in Q1–Q4 are constructed **primarily from Clement (c. 150–215) and Origen (c. 185–254)** — the two figures carrying the highest Author Gravity risk in this world's source ecology (to be assessed formally in Doc_02). ## 8. World Continuity & Distinction

- *Greek philosophical traditions* — Platonism (layered reality, the soul's ascent), Stoicism (Logos as rational principle; virtue-knowledge link), Pythagorean instincts — as intellectual resources and cultural grammar, not authorities on a par with Scripture. — *Widely Accepted.*
- *Early apostolic and catechetical tradition* — the creation/fall/incarnation/death/resurrection narrative; baptism and Eucharist; recognized teachers; the memory of martyrs — shared with other early Christian worlds but received through distinctively Alexandrian lenses. — *Widely Accepted.*

**Where continuity masks change:** the vocabulary — Logos, theosis, participation, gnosis — stays remarkably stable across 150–400, which can obscure real shifts in how the terms function. ## 9. Living Tradition Status (Constitution Articles 28 and 29)

**This world corresponds to a living tradition with present-day adherents.** Constitution Article 28 (Scholarly Integrity and Representational Accountability) applies throughout: all downstream reconstruction must maintain a clear distinction between the *historical formation world* (what Alexandrian Christianity was and believed, c. 150–400 CE) and any living tradition's *present-day self-understanding*, which belongs to living communities and is not ours to define.

**Primary living heir:** the **Coptic Orthodox Church** — the direct institutional and liturgical inheritor, with continuous communal existence from this period to the present (widely estimated at roughly 10 million or more adherents; exact figures contested and not load-bearing here). It carries liturgical, theological, and formational traditions from this period, though its contemporary self-understanding is its own and cannot be read off historical reconstruction. Fifteen centuries, the 451 Chalcedonian split, and the Arab conquest of Egypt (641 CE) separate the reconstructed world from the present-day Church. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the Coptic Orthodox descent; **Documented** that the reconstructed world is chronologically and institutionally distinct from the present-day Church.

**Secondary inheritors:** Eastern Orthodoxy (via the Cappadocian synthesis and Nicaea); the Catholic tradition (via Jerome, Ambrose, Augustine's Platonist formation, and later Origen reception); and Alexandrian-influenced hermeneutics across various later traditions, usually mediated through the West. — *Widely Accepted.*

**A contested figure at the world's own center — Origen.** Origen's influence on this world is immense; arguably no individual more profoundly shaped how Alexandrian Christianity read, thought, and prayed. Yet specific teachings *associated with his name* (pre-existence of souls, certain *apokatastasis* formulations, aspects of subordinationist Christology) were anathematized at the Second Council of Constantinople (553 CE), nearly three centuries after his death — and *which* of those anathemas target Origen himself versus a later 6th-century "Origenism" is itself a live scholarly question. Within this world's own horizon (ending c. 400), Origen's standing is genuinely unresolved: the first Origenist controversy erupts only c. 399–400, at the very edge of the horizon, and the definitive institutional judgment against him lies a century and a half beyond it. **This is an ecologically real feature of the world — Origen held as formative inheritance and as a locus of unease simultaneously — and it is recorded here as a world-level fact, not as a Representative-construction instruction.** How a future Representative should carry this tension is a Step 10 question, deliberately out of this thread's scope (see Open_Gaps OG-2); this document neither answers it nor presupposes the figure who would. — **Confidence: Documented** for the 553 condemnation as an event; **Contested** for the precise attribution of the anathemas to Origen personally; **Widely Accepted** that Origen's standing was unresolved within this world's own horizon.
