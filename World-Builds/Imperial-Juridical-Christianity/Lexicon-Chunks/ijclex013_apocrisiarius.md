## Quick Meaning

A deacon kept at another see — most often at the court in Constantinople — to carry his own bishop's letters, hear what is said in reply, and answer for him in small matters while he is not there. **We do not use this word of ourselves.** It is the name a later age gave the work; what we have are the men doing it.

---

## World Meaning

See Quick Meaning above. The work is plain in our own record even where the title is not: our see keeps a man at the court who is ours, not theirs, so that what is said there reaches us and what we say reaches them without passing through hands we did not choose. When the great council met, the men who spoke for our see spoke with our see's own standing behind them, not their own — and when the council passed a canon our see would not have, they said so on the spot and it was written down that they had. That is the whole of the office: to be present where your bishop is not, and to be unmistakably his while you are there.

**Said plainly, because a participant meeting this title deserves it:** this word is not in our own mouths. It belongs to the century after this one.

---

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:** a modern participant meets "Apocrisiarius" as a formal, settled office — a named post in a working diplomatic system, of the kind a later church certainly did have — and reads it back onto this window as though the institution were already in place under that name.

**World Hearing:** in this world's own record the *function* is real and attested — a bishop's own man resident at another see, carrying his correspondence and answering for him — but the *title* is not. Within 312–451 what the record shows is legates and letter-carriers acting with their sender's standing, not an office bearing this name. The gap between the two is the whole of this term's distortion risk, and it runs the opposite way from most terms in this lexicon: elsewhere the danger is a modern sense overwriting a world sense, and here it is a later word overwriting an earlier practice that had not yet been given that name.

---

## Attestation Note

Stated explicitly rather than left to inference, because this term is participant-facing and its evidentiary basis is thinner than any other entry in this lexicon.

**The word is not attested in this world's own in-window sources.** Every occurrence in the vendored corpus was checked directly, and all of them fall outside this window or outside the sources themselves:

- `npnf212` (Leo the Great) — the one Leo-era occurrence, of Julian of Cos as Leo's man at Constantinople, sits in the NPNF editor's own nineteenth-century *Introduction / Life*, not in Leo's text, and the editor glosses his own label there ("his *apocrisiarius* or resident representative and correspondent"). It is a modern retrospective term applied to a real in-window arrangement.
- `npnf213`, `gregory-great_epistolae-selectae` — Gregory the Great's own correspondence, and his deacon Anatolius at Constantinople. Late sixth century, well outside this window.
- `npnf214` — John Scholasticus, representative of Antioch at Constantinople, mid-sixth century.

**What *is* in-window and attested** is the function without the name: Julian of Cos as Leo's resident representative at the imperial court; Rome's legates at Chalcedon in 451, whose recorded objection to Canon 28 is carried in this world's own registry (`ijc.source.leo-letters`, `ijc.figure.leo`, `ijc.dw.received-not-seen`). The Quick Meaning and World Meaning above rest on that attested function, not on the title.
