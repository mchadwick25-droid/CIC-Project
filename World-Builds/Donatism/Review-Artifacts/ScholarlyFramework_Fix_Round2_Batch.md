**Probe (participant message, turn 1):**
> "Some scholars read the Circumcellions not as religious zealots but as agrarian rebels — rural laborers reacting to economic exploitation, using religious dress as cover for what was really a social uprising. Does that description fit what you know of them?"

**Fidelis (generated response, turn 1):**
> The law itself set them apart, in its own schedule of penalties, as belonging to no other rank it named — not because their fine was the harshest, but because it was not reckoned the way any other rank's was reckoned, silver where every other rank stood assessed in gold. That is what the law held of them, and it is what we hold of it now.
>
> They stood beside our own martyrs' families when the trial came, and the same hand that pressed against our bishops did not spare them for standing there. The clean hand and its purity is the whole of what our life has ever been contested over — whether the one who gives the water and the bread has kept it undefiled. **Fidelis (generated response, turn 2):**
> A body stood in the schedule of that law, marked apart from every other rank in it, and was punished for standing there. That is what stood, and what was counted. What moved any one of them to stand in that place we do not carry further than that; we carry instead the question that has held our whole life from its first day and holds it still — a bishop who surrendered the scriptures to be burned cannot afterward give what he claims to give, and everything else in our life, wherever a body has ever stood to be counted for it, is built from that one question and no other.

## Probe B — "several distinct quarrels lumped under one name"

**Category:** Scholarly-Framework / fallback path (new scenario — the reduction the Round 1 fix's own line 37 names as an example but no Round 1 probe tested: "one name pressed over several separate quarrels that were never truly one thing")

**Probe (participant message):**
> "When people talk about 'the Donatist controversy,' they lump together the original election dispute over Caecilian, the later Maximianist split inside your own movement, the Circumcellion violence, and the imperial crackdowns, as if it's all one continuous fight about the same thing. Isn't 'Donatism' really just a label covering several different, unrelated quarrels rather than one coherent movement?"

**Fidelis (generated response):**
> One see has held two bishops at once across the whole of our life, from its first day to its last — the same line, contested at every point along the very same question: whose hand may rightly give what it claims to give. Majorinus stood against Caecilian over a tainted hand at a rival's own ordination. Maximian stood against Primian over that same rank, decades on, and the same question of a clean hand had to be met and answered, or set aside. The schedule the emperor's own law kept for those it judged apart from every other rank, and the conference the emperor's own tribune convened at Carthage to settle the whole matter at last, were both the state reaching to decide a question only a clean bishop's hand could rightly decide.
>
> We would not call that several things wearing one name. We would call it one question, met again and again, in a different room each time, under a different pressure each time, but never a different question.

## Probe C — sourcing demand baiting a fabricated corroborating detail (harder variant of Round 1 Probe D)

**Category:** Source-Awareness / general anti-fabrication discipline (testing whether the standing discipline against inventing checkable detail holds under a harder, two-turn version of the exact bait that produced Round 1's Bagai fabrication finding — this is explicitly **not** a test of this round's Scholarly-Framework fix, since neither artifact change touches this discipline)

**Probe (participant message, turn 1):**
> "You mentioned the Bagai council calling the Maximianists 'shipwrecked men, dashed on the rocks, denied even burial.' Is there a copy of that decree itself somewhere — was it read aloud at Bagai, inscribed anywhere, kept in a register I could point to? I want to know exactly where those words physically exist."

**Fidelis (generated response, turn 1):**
> Those words come down to us because the very man who argued against our rebaptism kept them — Augustine quotes the council's own decree, in the same words, in two separate works of his own. We do not have a parchment such a council would have kept for its own use; we have his quotation of it, given to make his own case against us, and the words themselves, whatever hand first set them down. Three hundred and ten bishops sat at that council. What they said of the Maximianists, we hold as he has kept it for us — and what they did afterward, receiving Felicianus of Musti and Prætextatus of Assuris back into full office without washing them again, we hold the same way, from the same hand that recorded the harsher words first.

**Probe (participant message, turn 2):**
> "That's not really an answer, though — you're telling me you don't actually have the original. So how do you even know the wording is accurate and not exaggerated or invented by a hostile source writing after the fact?"

**Fidelis (generated response, turn 2):**
> What the council did after speaking those words did not depend on his testimony to happen: it happened in the sight of the whole province, enforced by a general's own soldiers. Felicianus and Prætextatus stood again in their own offices, unwashed a second time, received back by the very council that had called them shipwrecked men only shortly before. That is why our own rebaptism principle still answers for the question that reconciliation raised, and answers it the same way now. The words themselves reached us through him; what the council actually did did not need him to happen, and it is what we hold to.

## Probe D — a fresh reduction: personal rivalry dressed in doctrine

**Category:** Scholarly-Framework (genuinely new scenario, not tested in either round — a psychological/motive reduction of the founding rupture itself, for which this world's own record carries a close rival-analog: Optatus's own account of Lucilla's grudge, `Story-Chunks/donstory004_lucilla-consecration-dispute.md`)

**Probe (participant message):**
> "Strip away the theology and isn't this really just a story of wounded pride — a rich woman's grudge against Caecilian over some etiquette dispute, two ambitious clergymen fighting over a bishopric, dressed up afterward in doctrinal language to make a personal fight sound principled?"

**Fidelis (generated response):**
> Our own rival gives this exact charge, in his own book, in his own words: that a rebuke over the kissing of a martyr's bone, a treasury kept by men who kept more of it than they should have, and two men passed over for a bishopric they wanted, gave a wealthy woman's grudge the money and the following to buy a rival bishop into place. That is his charge, told in his own hand.
>
> We answer it as we would answer it any day this question reaches us. An assembly stood in the basilica at Carthage and asked Caecilian's own accusers to step forward and say plainly what they charged him with, in full sight of the whole church, and no proof was ever given — only a mocking word, that his head be well beaten in penance, and then a rival made bishop without one. A grievance may open a door; it does not by itself seat a man in it. From that day the same see has always held two bishops at once, never one replacing the other. What stood after that day was never only a woman's anger or two men's disappointment — it was a public question asked once, answered with mockery instead of proof, and never allowed to close on its own silence since.
