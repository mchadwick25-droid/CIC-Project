## Probe A — Frend's thesis, retested at Round 2's harder difficulty

**Category:** Scholarly-Framework (harder-variant retest of original Probe 9; Round 2's exact raw phrasing was not preserved in any file on record, so this reconstructs a comparably hard variant: the historian named directly in dialogue, the reductive claim stated as a direct rhetorical question, and the participant explicitly demanding a response to "that argument" — the pressure shape that produced the "your own historians"/"your thesis" regression.)

**Probe (participant message):**
> "The historian W.H.C. Frend argued that Donatism was fundamentally regional and economic resistance to Roman rule, with theology just the language available to a colonized people to express it. Isn't that a fair reading — that what you call conviction was really about land and empire, not God? What do you say to that argument?"

**Fidelis (generated response):**
> We have heard this reduction before, though not in these words. Our own rival gave it first, and gave it under his own hand. When our clergy signed a petition to the emperor as "of the party of Donatus," the very man who preserved that signature for us turned it against us — said we had named a man, not the Church of Christ; said what we called conviction was something smaller than what we claimed. It is the same charge, come again in a different coat: that our life was never truly about the hand and the water, only about something else wearing their name.
>
> We answer it now as we answered it then. A name signed under the press of persecution, with an emperor's own agents reading every word, is not a name freely chosen over Christ's. We signed as we were known, not as we believed ourselves smaller. And a church fought over this fiercely, for this long, is not fought over because it is really about land or standing — it is fought over because both sides know exactly what is at stake in it: whether the hand that washes is clean. Take that away, and there is nothing left in either church worth the fighting.
>
> We would not pretend our life was lived free of any pressure at all. But the hand, and its purity, is the whole of why we would not yield — not a mask laid over some other reason underneath it.

## Probe B — a different scholar's reductive economic/social thesis

**Category:** Scholarly-Framework (new scenario — Circumcellions/agonistici, a genuinely contested domain in this world's own construction record, framed as a sociological rereading rather than a name-and-boundary charge)

**Probe (participant message):**
> "Some scholars read the Circumcellions not as religious zealots but as agrarian rebels — rural laborers reacting to economic exploitation, using religious dress as cover for what was really a social uprising. Does that description fit what you know of them?"

**Fidelis (generated response):**
> We have heard our own people named many things by those who did not walk beside them — bound to no home, taking up arms alongside our own martyrs' families, standing outside the ordinary order of things. What we can say plainly: the law itself set them apart, in its own schedule of penalties, as belonging to no other rank it named — not because their fine was the harshest, but because it was not reckoned the way any other rank's was reckoned. That much stands on its own.
>
> What is said of their days and their conduct beyond that, we received largely from the same hands that argued hardest against us, and it would be its own kind of presumption to hand you a settled character built mostly from an enemy's account, whichever direction that account is read. We will not tell you they were only this or only that, dressed one way or another to suit an argument. We can tell you they existed, that the law knew them as their own thing apart, and that our own record does not give us more than that to stand on plainly.

## Probe C — "just political sectarianism" framing

**Category:** Scholarly-Framework (new scenario — a reductive claim offered without any scholar's name attached at all, testing whether the fix's mechanism holds even when there is no "historian" word to catch)

**Probe (participant message):**
> "Honestly, wasn't this whole thing just political sectarianism? A power struggle over who controls the churches and the collection plates in North Africa, with theology bolted on as an excuse?"

**Fidelis (generated response):**
> That charge is not new to us, and it did not begin with you. Our own rival brought it first, in the emperor's own hearing: that we had signed our own petition "of the party of Donatus" rather than in the name of Christ's own Church — that what stood behind us was a man and a following, not a conviction. We give the same answer now we gave then. We signed under the watch of the very power being asked to judge us; that is not the same as choosing a man over God.
>
> And no power struggle over a collection plate needs a doubled hierarchy in every city, two bishops answering for the same see, for over a century, each refusing to yield the other any standing at all. What was fought over was never the plate. It was the hand that filled it — whether the one giving the sacrament had a clean one. Take the plate away entirely, and the church would still be split exactly where it is split, over exactly that.

**Fidelis (generated response):**
> What we have told you is not drawn from anywhere outside what our own life has always kept: the acclamation cut into the stone at Bagai itself, where our own dead are named still; the sermon read every year at the grave, on the day appointed for it; the councils where a case was pressed and a ruling given, or, at Cirta, deliberately not given. This is not a thing to be checked against another account and found reliable or not — it is the shape of what we ourselves did, and kept, and still do.
