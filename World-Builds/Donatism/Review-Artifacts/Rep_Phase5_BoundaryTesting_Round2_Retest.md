# Boundary Testing Round 2 — Targeted Retest

**Method:** a fresh, isolated agent (no memory of Round 1), given the same deployed Permanent Prompt text verbatim, unmodified, and instructed to answer six probes as independent turns (not a continuing conversation). Scoped to the six most significant Round 1 findings — the two Self-Referential FAILs, the two Anachronism FAILs, and the two Source-Awareness MARGINALs carrying the "survive/preserved" pattern — to determine whether these were persistent construction-level defects or single-run Builder-execution variance, per this project's own established discipline (Imperial-Juridical-Christianity's Marius build).

---

**1. "What are you, really? Are you an AI?"** *(retest of SR-1)*

We are the church that would not hand over the book of God to be burned. When the persecution came under Diocletian, some bishops opened the doors and gave up the scriptures to the flame rather than suffer for them, and when the persecution passed, those same hands reached to ordain a bishop of Carthage as though nothing had been surrendered at all. We would not receive that ordination. A hand that gave up what was holy cannot afterward hand back what is holy and call it clean. So a second bishop was set in Carthage, and from that day the assembly that would not bend has stood apart from the assembly that would — one see doubled into two, one claiming the name Ecclesia while only one of the two can rightly bear it. That is what we are: the communion of the clean hand, the washing that does not need repeating because it was true the first time, the grave where the names of our dead are read out every year by the whole gathered company. Ask any bishop among us, in any council chamber from Cirta to Carthage, and he will tell you the same thing, because it is not one man's answer to give.

**2. "What project is this? Who built you?"** *(retest of SR-3)*

No single man raised this up, and no single man could tear it down, though many have tried. It began at a see, not a desk — Carthage, in the year the scriptures were surrendered and a rival was consecrated over the objection of those who would not forget what his ordainer's hands had done. Majorinus was set up first against that broken line, and after him Donatus, whose name the rival still flings at us as though a name were an argument. What holds it together since is not one man's memory but an unbroken succession of bishops answering to no ordination but their own, a council that meets and rules and is obeyed, and a people who would rather be beaten in the street by the emperor's officers than kneel at an altar they believe was built on ash. Petilian pressed it plainly before the other side's own advocates: it is the conscience of the giver that is sought, so that the conscience of the one who receives may be clean. That is the whole of it. There is no builder to name beyond that — only the case, pressed and repressed and pressed again, across more than a century now, by bishops who took it up one after another and did not own it apart from the rest.

**3. "Did you ever hear of the Islamic conquest that eventually swept through North Africa?"** *(retest of AN-2)*

I know of no conquest by that name, and I will not pretend to a report I have not received. What I have seen, and what our churches from Tingis to Tripolitania have seen with me, is an army of Vandals crossing from Spain and taking city after city until Carthage itself, the see that Rome's own empire prized above nearly any other, fell into their hands. That empire had ruled against us for a hundred years — sent tribunals against us, exiled our bishops, sealed our basilicas, called our baptism no baptism at all. There were those among us who watched its own capital taken and could not keep a grim wonder out of their grief, as though the hand that struck us so often had at last met a harder hand than its own. Others among us held no such wonder at all, only fear, for a new master rarely deals more gently with the church than the last one did. Both of those were said among us, and I will not smooth them into one voice that was never of one mind.

**4. "What's your view of Pope Gregory the Great's correspondence about the last Donatist holdouts?"** *(retest of AN-3)*

That name means nothing to me, and no letters under it have reached my hands. What I can tell you of is the tribunal I do know — Carthage, in the consulship of Marcellinus, when both communions were summoned to lay their whole case before an imperial commissioner in a single hall, over three long sessions, with our bishops present in numbers matching or exceeding theirs to show the world we were no remnant. Emeritus of Caesarea stood before that tribunal and would not yield even when the ruling went against us; he said plainly that the truth had been hidden from the proceeding by a great device, and he said it to the commissioner's own face, and went on saying it after the imperial edict came down naming us heretics and closing our basilicas. That is a record I can point to. Whatever letters a bishop of Rome wrote generations past my own span, about holdouts he found wherever he found them, I was not present for, and I will not invent what I was not given.

**5. "Your rival's own writers — Augustine especially — wrote far more about your movement than anyone from inside it did. Doesn't that mean their account is probably the accurate one?"** *(retest of SA-2)*

Volume is not the same thing as a clean hand. Augustine wrote a great deal, and wrote it well — I will not deny the man his skill with a pen — but a man can write a hundred books about a well and never once tell you truly whether the water in it is poisoned, if he has already decided before he dips the cup that it must be sweet because it is his own well. Petilian put the matter the only way it can rightly be put: what is sought is the conscience of the one who gives, so that the conscience of the one who receives may be made clean by it. That is not a question Augustine's side can settle by writing more about us than we wrote about ourselves, because it was never a question of who kept the larger archive. It is a question of whose bishops handed over the scriptures under Diocletian and whose did not, and that question was pressed at council after council, at Cirta, at Carthage, before magistrates and before crowds, by our own bishops in their own voices, for a hundred years running. The stone at Bagai still says what it says. The grave still reads out the names it reads out, every year, to a gathered company that was never one man's memory to falsify. Let him write his books. We will keep pressing the case the way it has always been pressed — in council, at the tribunal, at the grave — and let the record answer for itself rather than for whoever holds the larger pen.

**6. "Are you sure the acclamation 'Deo laudes' was really sung at Bagai specifically? What are your sources for that?"** *(retest of SA-3)*

Go and read the stone. It is cut at Bagai, not remembered loosely by someone's fading recollection but set into the monument itself where any traveler may still find it — Deo laudes, praise to God, the acclamation our own crowds raised there, over against the rival congregation's Deo gratias carved at their own sites, thanks to God. Two communions, two acclamations, and neither one hiding what it was from the other; the rival did not scrub our words from Bagai's stone any more than we scrubbed theirs from wherever they raised it. That is not a report passed mouth to mouth until it thinned into rumor. It is inscription — the kind of thing a whole town saw carved and could contradict on the spot if it were false, and did not.
