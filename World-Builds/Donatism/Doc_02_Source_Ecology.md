## 1. Primary Sources

This world's evidentiary situation is the inverse of a typical Author Gravity problem: the volume of surviving material is large, but it is concentrated almost entirely on one side of the schism it describes (Doc_01 §7, item 1). The following are Native per the Source Registry's Boundary Check; full classification detail (Type, Confidence, Licensed-For target) lives in `Source_Registry.md`.

**Optatus of Milevis, *Against the Donatists* (Books I–VII, c. 366–367, revised c. 385).** The earliest substantial narrative source on the schism, and the primary anti-Donatist source outside Augustine (`cic/corpus-map/donatism.yaml`). Contemporary and primary in composition date, but hostile and external in perspective — Optatus describes the Donatists rather than speaking as one, "usable for Donatism only etically" (corpus map, verbatim). Transmits, in its own appendix, a body of contemporary documentary material of a different character: the *Acta Purgationis Felicis* (314), the *Gesta apud Zenophilum* (320), Constantine's own letters, the Council of Arles' 314 letter to Pope Silvester, the Acts of the Council of Cirta (305 — a date genuinely disputed in the scholarship, with 307 and later argued by some; `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §3 B1), and Anulinus's *relatio* (313). This appendix's *contents* are court acts and imperial correspondence; its *selection, assembly, and transmission* are still Optatus's own act, so it sits inside this world's Author Gravity concentration rather than outside it, even though it is a comparatively less rhetorically shaped layer than Optatus's own argumentative prose.

**Augustine of Hippo's anti-Donatist corpus.** By far the largest body of surviving evidence, and the dominant weight in this world's own Author Gravity concentration: *On Baptism, Against the Donatists* (7 books, c. 400); *Answer to the Letters of Petilian* (3 books) — quoting the Donatist bishop Petilian of Constantina's own letters clause by clause, and therefore the single most extensive surviving Donatist voice in the vendored corpus, though preserved entirely inside its own refutation; *The Correction of the Donatists* (Letter 185, c. 417, to the tribune Boniface, defending imperial coercion after the 411 Conference); and a cluster of eleven letters (XXIII, XLIII, XLIV, LIII, LXXVI, LXXXVII, LXXXVIII, LXXXIX, XCIII, CXXXIX, CLXXIII), split from the wider Augustine correspondence on Mark's 2026-08-26 ruling by the volume's own div3 markup (168 letters, cut at eight or more occurrences of Donatist vocabulary, plus Letter LXXVI by its title's own address to the Donatists as a body — `cic/corpus-map/donatism.yaml`). This letters cluster is classified `role: context` in the corpus map, distinct from the `role: tradition` classification carried by the three works named just above it. The corpus map's own stated warrant for that distinction is internally inconsistent — it grounds the letters' `context` classification in "the role normalisation already applied to his anti-Donatist treatises," but those treatises are themselves classified `tradition`, not `context` — an inconsistency this build thread cannot resolve, since the corpus map is generated from `_staging/` and sits outside this build's own editing authority. It is named here for census-level correction rather than smoothed over; this document continues to use the letters cluster at the narrow licensing the Registry already sets for it (row 6), regardless of which role label eventually prevails.

**The Cyprianic antecedent corpus.** Cyprian's *Epistles*, *On the Lapsed* (*De Lapsis*), *On the Unity of the Church* (*De Unitate*), and the acts of the Seventh Council of Carthage (256, on the baptism of heretics) are classified `role: antecedent` in the vendored corpus map, on Mark's 2026-08-26 ruling: Cyprian is neither a Donatist (he predates the schism by half a century) nor merely `context` describing it from outside — he is "the authority both sides argued FROM," a status measured rather than asserted — the corpus map records that Augustine's *On Baptism, Against the Donatists* names Cyprian 306 times and Agrippinus 28 times in roughly 92,000 words. **The 256 council is independently attested twice, from two different vendored volumes with two different classifications, and this document keeps the two distinct rather than treating them as one entry:** the Cyprianic corpus's own record of the council (`anf05`, `role: antecedent`, confidence: assigned) and a separate telling within the conciliar-history volume, *The Acts of the Council of Carthage under Cyprian* (`npnf214`, `role: tradition`, confidence: **provisional**). The provisional flag on the second is carried forward, not silently resolved, per this document's own confidence-sensitive test below.

**Conciliar and institutional record.** The Code of Canons of the African Church (Council of Carthage, 419), collecting canons on the reception of Donatist clergy and congregations that mostly originate in the African councils of c. 397–407, is vendored at `confidence: provisional` in the corpus map — carried forward here, not upgraded without independent verification. **Not currently vendored, and load-bearing for this world's own institutional self-understanding:** the *Gesta Collationis Carthaginiensis*, the acts of the imperially-convened 411 Conference of Carthage, at which Donatist bishops — Emeritus of Caesarea prominent among them — are recorded speaking at length on their own side of the exchange. The *Gesta* remains confirmed unavailable in the public domain and not requested — no edition of it, in any language, was identified as clearing this project's own vendoring rule, though this session's own network access could not verify every possible lead (Manifest §1's closing note). Until that changes, Emeritus's own recorded words remain referenced-only, not quotable from a vendored text. The *Codex Theodosianus* Book 16 material's own status is more mixed after the G1–G7 acquisition round: the actual critical Latin edition (Mommsen and Meyer, CSEL) remains unvendored after five separate acquisition attempts, all resolving to the wrong part of that edition or rejected on source-integrity grounds (`don_Decision_Log.md`), so the specific law this world's own Circumcellion/*agonistici* attestation depends on (16.5.52, §6 below) is still not held in any vendored text. A substitute secondary source, William K. Boyd's *The Ecclesiastical Edicts of the Theodosian Code* (1905, Registry row 49), is now vendored and corroborates the surrounding legislative sequence (C. Th. xvi.5.3–58, including the 405 Edict of Unity at xvi.5.38–39) with several individual laws quoted verbatim in Latin — but it does not itself quote 16.5.52, and the term *agonistici* does not appear in it at all.

**The Maximianist schism's rebaptism-theology evidence.** Augustine's own *On Baptism, Against the Donatists* (Book I, c. 400 — vendored, Registry row 3) makes this world's central rebaptism-consistency argument directly, in his own words. Felicianus of Musti and Prætextatus of Assuris were among the Maximianist bishops who, after the mainstream (Primianist) party's council at Bagai (394) condemned the Maximianist schism, were received back into Primianist communion. This is not merely asserted here: Augustine's own corpus attests it repeatedly, every time in his own voice, and every attestation sits inside this world's own Author Gravity concentration rather than outside it (Doc_01 §7, item 1). *Answer to the Letters of Petilian* describes the Donatist bishop of Thamugadi, Optatus Gildonianus — "one of your colleagues," Gildo's ally, whom the Donatists themselves "were afraid to condemn though they had long known his wickedness" — "advancing with a military force" and "sucking back Felicianus and Prætextatus once again within their pale" (Registry row 4). *On Baptism* itself independently names him the same way: "they pressed heavily on them through the agency of Optatus the Gildonian," glossed by the volume's own editorial note as "Optatus, a Donatist bishop of Thamogade in Numidia... not to be confounded with Optatus, Bishop of Milevis, the strenuous opponent of the Donatists" (*On Baptism* II.11.16, row 3). Augustine's own Letter LI to the Donatist bishop Crispinus makes the same point directly: "why have you harshly persecuted the Maximianists by the help of judges... [and driven] them, by the din of controversy, the authority of edicts, and the violence of soldiery, from those buildings for worship which they possessed" (Registry row 47) — the same letter that states outright, of the reception itself, "you restored some of them without re-ordination, and accepted their baptism as valid." Augustine states plainly that this reception carried no repetition of either sacrament: "For Felicianus... when he separated himself from them with Maximianus, was not held by the Donatists themselves to have lost either the sacrament of baptism or the sacrament of conferring baptism. For now he is a recognized member of their own body, in company with those very men whom he baptized while he was separated from them in the schism of Maximianus" (*On Baptism* I.1.2). He returns to the same fact as decisive: "the condemnation of the party of Maximianus, and their restoration after they had been condemned... settles the whole question in dispute, and removes all controversy... they themselves are obliged to confess that those whom Felicianus baptized in schism received true baptism, inasmuch as they now acknowledge them as members of their own body" (*On Baptism* I.5.7). If the validity of baptism and ordination depended on the minister's own standing outside schism — the Donatists' own founding logic against the Caecilianists — the Maximianists, themselves schismatics from the Primianist party's own point of view, should have needed reordination and rebaptism on their return. On Augustine's own direct testimony, quoted above from the treatise's own text, they did not receive it, and the mainstream Donatist party is reported acknowledging as much.

This reception was not uncontested within the exchange, and the contest is itself evidence of the fact's weight rather than against it. A later Donatist apologetic account of the reception — reported in a scholarly summary of Augustine's *Contra Cresconium* (four books, c. 406, "some say as late as 409"; Registry rows 17 and 31) — held that "the Synod" (almost certainly the same Bagai council, on the vendored apparatus's own dating) "had granted a season of delay during which all who returned should be held innocent," and that "the baptism of these" — those who returned, not only their clergy specifically — "was valid" by that Council's own dispensation rather than by any general principle; Augustine's own reply, per the same summary, "refutes the statement from its inherent contradictions and from the language of the Synod against the Maximianists." The dispute between the parties is therefore over how to justify the reception, not over whether it happened — the reception itself is the common ground *On Baptism* states outright — and Augustine used exactly this fact as an argument against the Donatists' own rebaptism logic, a use already visible earlier still in his rhetorical question from the *Psalmus contra Partem Donati* (per the same summary): "Why rebaptize us... when you do not repeat the rite upon your once expelled but now restored Maximianists?" In its Cresconius-account half, this remains Augustine-mediated evidence of a Donatist justification — no Maximianist or mainstream-Donatist first-hand account of that justification survives independent of his report and refutation of it — but the reception itself is attested in Augustine's own direct argument in a vendored primary text, not only in his report of his opponent's account of it. Consistent with Doc_01 §4's own reasoning, it is also evidence against Strand status for the Maximianist episode (a shared formation pattern under internal contest, not two structurally distinct traditions), a finding not reopened here. **Both *Contra Cresconium* and *Contra epistulam Parmeniani* are now vendored** (Petschenig's CSEL critical edition, Registry rows 17, 18, and 39; Manifest G5) — the paragraph above still rests on row 31's scholarly summary rather than a direct reading of the newly-available primary Latin text, and re-verifying the Cresconius-account material directly against it is named as an open item (§9 below) rather than silently upgraded here.

**Tyconius's *Liber Regularum* (Book of Rules).** A substantially complete, independently-authored hermeneutical treatise, later influential on Augustine's own *De doctrina christiana* III — **now vendored** (F.C. Burkitt's 1894 critical edition, Registry rows 15 and 41; Manifest G1), the single largest available correction to this world's own Author Gravity concentration realized rather than merely anticipated, since it is Donatist-affiliated material surviving in its own right rather than through hostile quotation. Doc_01 §4 found that Tyconius's own dissent does not rise to strand status (no attested following, parallel authority structure, or distinct communal practice); that finding does not depend on this acquisition and is not reopened here. Paul Monceaux's own literary history (*Histoire littéraire de l'Afrique chrétienne*, Tome V, 1920, Registry row 40; Manifest G6) independently cites the same Burkitt 1894 edition by name as its own source for Tyconius's text — a direct cross-validation that this is the edition the field treats as standard. Monceaux's own account (a dedicated chapter, "Tyconius," not independently re-verified against the primary Latin text this session) characterizes Tyconius as distinctive on several counts: a layman writing theology in a movement where "only the primate spoke in the party's name" and laypeople otherwise had little standing but to obey; a polemicist "like every Donatist" but one who pursued truth in argument even against his own side's interest, provoking "scandal and irritation" among fellow Donatists for it; and the author of an exegetical system original enough to "impose itself on both rival Churches" and to inspire Augustine directly. Monceaux also records that Tyconius was African by birth, of Proconsular Africa specifically (this document's own gloss, not Monceaux's, notes that this is distinct from Numidia); and that he was "certainly raised in the Donatist faith," an inference Monceaux draws from silence (his opponents' failure to accuse him of having changed sides) rather than a direct statement. Even his name's spelling is not settled with certainty across the manuscript tradition — Monceaux lists six attested forms (Thiconius, Thyconius, Tichonius, Ticonius, Tychonius, Tyconius), noting that "Tichonius" was formerly the near-consensus spelling — with Monceaux himself following the oldest *Regulae* manuscript's own "Tyconius," the spelling this document already uses.

## 2. Author Gravity Assessment

Per the Construction Framework's own five-dimension author-gravity discipline, governed at the constitutional level by Article 26's delegation of "the dimensions of source assessment, including transmission history, and the construction stages at which the discipline is enforced" to the Framework, every major voice is assessed across all five dimensions: Visibility, Representativeness, Influence, Limitations, and Transmission History.

**Optatus of Milevis.**
- *Visibility:* Over-visible for the schism's own documentary record — his appendix is this world's single richest concentration of contemporary legal and institutional material, and his own narrative frames how that material is read.
- *Representativeness:* Represents the Caecilianist/Catholic party's own case against the schism, not any Donatist perspective — he writes explicitly against "my brother Parmenian," the Donatist bishop of Carthage, a rhetorical fraternity that does not extend to representing Parmenian's own view.
- *Influence:* Foundational — Augustine's own later anti-Donatist corpus draws on and extends arguments Optatus first assembled, and Optatus's appendix is the earliest surviving collection of the schism's own founding documents.
- *Limitations:* A polemicist's own selection and framing govern which documents survive in the appendix and how they are read; the work's second edition (after 383, on the evidence of the added name of Pope Siricius to the papal list, and — per the apparatus's own hedge, "evidently added" rather than stated outright — of Lucian and Claudian to the Donatist episcopal list) shows Optatus's own argument extended after the fact rather than fixed at the first edition, though the extent of that second edition beyond these list updates is not settled from the vendored text alone (its translator's preface, where the fuller account would sit, is not part of the vendored file). Karl Ziwsa's critical Latin edition (CSEL 26, 1893) is now vendored (Registry row 38; Manifest G2) — the specific instrument row 1's own Verification Note already named as able to settle this question from the primary apparatus rather than the 1917 translator's own footnotes — but its own apparatus has not yet been read for that purpose this session; this limitation is named as open, not resolved.
- *Transmission History:* Preserved through the Caecilianist/Catholic side's own manuscript tradition, as the eventually-victorious party's literature; no adversarial-quotation problem of the kind affecting Petilian's letters below, since Optatus's own words are not filtered through a hostile source, but his selection of *what to include from the other side* is itself the transmission-shaping act this world's central evidentiary problem depends on.

**Augustine of Hippo.**
- *Visibility:* Overwhelmingly over-visible — the largest single body of evidence for this entire world, spanning three decades of his own episcopate (post-411 material comes disproportionately from the period after his own side's decisive institutional victory).
- *Representativeness:* Represents the Caecilianist/Catholic party at its most intellectually developed and most institutionally confident, writing after Rome, Arles, and (for his later works) the 411 Conference had already ruled against the Donatist party — not a contemporary, undecided voice.
- *Influence:* Enormous and lasting, both for how this schism is known at all and for the sacramental theology (objective validity of sacraments independent of the minister's worthiness) his engagement with Donatism substantially forged — a theological legacy that belongs to World #8's own future reconstruction (Doc_01 §6), not developed further here.
- *Limitations:* Every quotation of a Donatist voice in this corpus — Petilian's letters above all — survives only as much of it as Augustine chose to quote, in the order and selection he chose, for the purpose of refuting it clause by clause. This is not merely a general caution; it is the specific mechanism by which this world's own central evidentiary problem operates.
- *Transmission History:* Preserved through the Catholic institutional tradition's own manuscript transmission — the eventually-establishment side's literature, copied and circulated by the institutions that had every reason to preserve it. The letters cluster's own survival, split out of a 168-letter volume by markup rather than by a Donatism-specific manuscript tradition, is itself evidence of how thoroughly this world's own material is embedded inside a larger Catholic corpus rather than transmitted as its own body of literature.

**Petilian of Constantina.** Treated separately from the general Augustine entry because Petilian is, on the current vendored corpus, the fullest surviving *Donatist* voice — and because his own Author Gravity profile is therefore not Augustine's, even though his words reach us entirely through Augustine's own text.
- *Visibility:* Visible only to the extent Augustine's *Answer to the Letters of Petilian* chose to quote him, clause by clause, for refutation — a visibility entirely conditioned by his opponent's own selection.
- *Representativeness:* Represents a Donatist bishop's own articulate defense of the movement's rebaptism theology and ecclesiology, from letters spanning the years around 400 — the first answered by Augustine's *Answer* Book I (c. 400), and a further reply from Petilian himself that occasioned Augustine's Book III (c. 401–402) — a period of renewed Caecilianist/imperial pressure in the run-up to the 405 Edict of Unity, not, as the schism's own origin might suggest, within personal living memory of the Macarian martyrs of 347–348, over fifty years earlier. Where Petilian's own argument invokes that martyrdom, it draws on the movement's cultic and communal memory of it (§4 below), not a claim to personal recollection.
- *Influence:* Substantial within this world's own reconstruction specifically, precisely because so little independent Donatist material survives — Petilian's quoted words carry disproportionate evidentiary weight for want of an alternative, a fact this document names rather than treats as a strength.
- *Limitations:* Everything about this entry is downstream of the same limitation: no direct manuscript of Petilian's own letters survives independent of Augustine's quotation. Where Augustine paraphrases rather than quotes, or characterizes rather than reproduces, this document treats the material as Augustine's own characterization, not Petilian's words, and the Registry's own entry marks this distinction row by row where it can be checked.
- *Transmission History:* The single sharpest instance of this world's central evidentiary problem: transmission and refutation are, for this figure, the same act. Petilian's words survive *because* Augustine needed them in front of a reader in order to refute them — a preservation mechanism that guarantees selection for refutability, not for completeness or fairness to Petilian's own case.

**Tyconius.** Treated separately from Petilian because his profile is structurally different in the one way that matters most for this world's own central evidentiary problem: his own words survive in a text transmitted in its own right (§1 above), not preserved only inside a hostile refutation. This entry is deliberately modest — the vendored *Liber Regularum* has not itself been read in depth this session, so the dimensions below draw on Monceaux's own secondary characterization (§1 above) and Doc_01 §4's contested-standing finding, not on independent analysis of the primary Latin text.
- *Visibility:* Uniquely visible among this world's Donatist-side figures in one respect — an independently-transmitted work, not a hostile quotation — but thin in another: what survives is "only in part" (Monceaux), with the *Liber Regularum* itself substantially complete while the rest of his output is known only through fragments and through others' analyses or refutations (Parmenian's, Augustine's, Gennadius's).
- *Representativeness:* Represents a genuinely atypical position within the Donatist party rather than its center — a lay theologian in a movement Monceaux describes as one "where only the primate spoke in the party's name," whose own polemical instincts nonetheless followed truth-seeking over party loyalty closely enough to provoke his own side's "scandal and irritation," and whose exegetical method Monceaux reports "imposed itself on both rival Churches" rather than remaining Donatist-specific.
- *Influence:* Substantial on both sides of the schism specifically because his work was not confined to one — his influence on Augustine's own *De doctrina christiana* III (§1 above) is the standard-attested case, and Monceaux frames this as continuous with a broader cross-party reception of his exegetical rules, not an isolated instance.
- *Limitations:* The same atypicality that makes him valuable limits what he can be made to represent — a single, unusually independent lay voice does not stand in for the Donatist episcopate's own mainstream theology, and Doc_01 §4's own finding that his dissent never rose to strand status (no attested following, parallel authority structure, or distinct communal practice) means this document does not treat him as representative of any organized internal current. Monceaux's own account, checked directly this session against that finding, corroborates rather than complicates it: he reports that after his condemnation Tyconius, unable to rejoin the Catholics either, "*devint le chef d'une petite Église schismatique, où peut-être il était le seul fidèle*" — "became the head of a small schismatic church, where perhaps he was its only member." A congregation of one is not the attested following, parallel authority structure, or distinct communal practice Doc_01 §4's own test requires; if anything it names the negative case directly.
- *Transmission History:* Preserved and condemned by different mechanisms at different points — a Donatist council reportedly condemned him (c. 380, on the standard account, `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §3 B1) without his ever joining the Catholic church, yet his exegetical work survived well enough to shape Augustine and to reach the modern critical edition (Burkitt 1894) both this Registry and Monceaux cite independently. This document does not resolve, and does not need to resolve, exactly how a work by a figure his own party condemned came to survive as well as it did; that transmission puzzle is named rather than explained.

**Cyprian of Carthage (antecedent, not a Donatist voice).** Given a full entry despite predating the schism by half a century, because his material is load-bearing for this world's own A1/A2 reasoning (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §2) and B3 boundary with World #8 (Doc_01 §6).
- *Visibility:* Very high within his own third-century context; his visibility *for this world specifically* is a function of how heavily both the Donatist and Caecilianist parties argued from him, not of anything Cyprian himself said about a schism he did not live to see.
- *Representativeness:* Represents the third-century North African church's own settled position on rebaptism (baptism outside the true church is no baptism) at a moment when that position was itself contested within the wider church (his dispute with Pope Stephen) but not yet a basis for schism within Africa itself.
- *Influence:* Decisive for this world's own founding logic — the Donatist position is a direct continuation and sharpening of Cyprian's own view, not a departure from it (Doc_01 §5).
- *Limitations:* Cyprian cannot be read as though he anticipated or endorsed the Donatist schism specifically; the corpus map's own antecedent classification exists precisely to prevent that anachronism, and this document does not use Cyprian to characterize any specific Donatist claim beyond what the schism's own participants (via Optatus's and Augustine's quotation of them) can be shown to have actually argued from him.
- *Transmission History:* Preserved through the same African, later broader Latin, manuscript tradition both the Donatist and Caecilianist parties drew on — a shared inheritance, not a source either side transmitted exclusively, which is itself the evidentiary basis for treating him as antecedent rather than as either side's own tradition (corpus map ruling, 2026-08-26).

## 4. Formation Narrative Sources

Per Construction Framework Part II, hagiographic and collected-narrative sources are assessed separately from theological treatises and institutional records, since they carry formation content — how the community understood itself — differently than argument does. Each source below is evaluated for authorship and date, proximity to the events or persons described, genre conventions that shape the account, the formation ecology it reveals, and its Author Gravity risk.

**The martyr and confessor Passiones.** Step 0's own disclosure (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §1) names martyr-cult and confessor memory as this world's own distinctive strength among the nine confirmed worlds — the community's self-understanding as the "Church of the Martyrs" is not peripheral color but this world's own central formation narrative.

- **An anonymous Donatist-authored sermon commemorating an earlier, less-attested persecution — the *Passio Donati* under its standard scholarly name (found incidentally while acquiring the Passiones below, not itself requested; Registry row 50; same vendored Migne file).** Paul Monceaux (Registry row 40, §3 above) treats this text at chapter length as one of a deliberate trio he groups together — "Un sermon: la *Passio Donati*. Un récit: la *Passio Marculi*. Une lettre: la *Passio Maximiani et Isaac*" — and this document follows him in naming it that way rather than leaving it an unnamed curiosity, since Monceaux's analysis is the fuller and more reliable treatment of it, against Jean Mabillon's own narrower 17th-century apparatus. The manuscript's own title reads "*De passione sanctorum Donati et Advocati*," and Mabillon's own admonitio records that the sermon "was delivered on the fourth of the Ides of March, at the solemn and annual commemoration of certain Donatists held by their own people to be martyrs" ("*in solemni et anniversaria commemoratione*") — the liturgical-reading practice Monceaux's own analysis of the sermon's exordium independently confirms. *Authorship and date:* **Contested between two vendored authorities.** Mabillon dates the persecution described to *circa* 340, or a little after, reasoning externally from the imperial agents named in the text (Leontius and Ursacius) against Optatus's own chronology and the Council of Sardica's 347 date for Caecilian's death. Monceaux dates the same events more precisely to **12 March 317**, and the sermon's own composition to *circa* 320, reasoning from the text's own reference to a "persecution of Caecilianus" and Caecilian's death shortly after 325, tied to Constantine's 316 edict of union ordering dissidents' basilicas seized. On authorship, Monceaux argues from the Donatist rule that only a bishop preached, in his own diocese, that the sermon's author was the Donatist bishop of Carthage in 320 — **possibly Donatus the Great himself**, primate of the party from 313–347 — while candidly noting that the sermon's own tone does not obviously match the "haughty eloquence" Optatus attributes to Donatus in his later years, and that Donatus's own character may have differed earlier. Monceaux also judges the preacher an eyewitness, from "the vividness of the orator's own impressions" and his live polemic against a still-living Caecilianus. This document does not adopt either dating or the authorship hypothesis as settled — both are named, with their competing reasoning, in §8 below — but Monceaux's account is markedly stronger than Mabillon's alone, and would make this, on Monceaux's own dating, the earliest Donatist-authored text in this world's entire vendored corpus, roughly contemporary with the *Gesta apud Zenophilum* (320, §1 above) and forty-five years earlier than Optatus. *Proximity:* on Monceaux's dating and eyewitness finding, a first-hand, near-contemporary composition; on Mabillon's alone, a sermon delivered ("*sermonem habitum*") while "the sect of the Donatists still flourished in Africa," with no eyewitness claim established. *Genre conventions:* homiletic, not the passio narrative form of the texts below — a sermon meant for liturgical reading at a martyrs' anniversary, built around a narrated episode framed by an exordium and peroration, rather than a single confessor's structured interrogation-and-execution account; Mabillon himself judges the manuscript's title "alien to the sermon itself," and Monceaux independently proposes it is a corruption of *Sermo de Passione Donati episcopi Advocatensis* (or *Avioccalensis*) — the bishop of Advocata/Avioccala killed in the narrative, not a martyr literally named Donatus and Advocatus, and possibly the preacher's own name attached to that bishop's death. Its content, per Monceaux, describes fighting across two or three Carthage basilicas after the 316 edict: a bishop of Sicilibba gravely wounded — named by Mabillon's own apparatus as Honoratus, glossing the sermon's "memoratus episcopus" as "Honoratus scilicet episcopus Sicilibensis" — "many Donatists clubbed to death," and the bishop of Advocata killed "with numerous faithful," their bodies buried near the altar — a multi-victim episode substantially larger than a single death. *Formation ecology revealed:* Monceaux notes this liturgical commemoration of sectarian martyrs was itself a novelty when the sermon was composed ("*c'était alors une nouveauté*"), meaning this text most plausibly documents the practice's own invention in the movement's first years, rather than establishing a deeper root beneath the later, better-attested Macarian-repression commemorations. *Author Gravity risk:* a genuinely Donatist-authored (and, on Monceaux's account, possibly eyewitness and episcopally-authored) primary voice, independent of Optatus's or Augustine's hostile mediation — a real correction to this world's central evidentiary problem (§6 below), though it reaches this document through two editors' own mediation (Mabillon's 17th-century apparatus and Monceaux's 1920 analysis) rather than as an unmediated text, and its own full Latin content beyond the passages checked this session has not been independently analyzed.
- **The *Passio Marculi*.** **Now vendored** (Migne *Patrologia Latina* vol. 8, Registry row 19; Manifest G4). Its own heading, further into the vendored text than the volume's editorial rubric, reads "INCIPIT PASSIO BENEDICTI MARTYRIS MARCULI. 8 (al. die 5) kal. decembris" — a day only, no year. The preceding editorial catalogue entry ("PASSIO MARCULI SACERDOTIS DONATISTAE, QUI SUB MACARIO INTERFECTUS A DONATISTIS PRO MARTYRE HABEBATUR" — "...who, killed under Macarius, was held by the Donatists to be a martyr") is Mabillon's and Migne's own descriptive rubric, not the Passio's self-description, and "ANNO DOMINI 348" above it is the volume's own dated section marker (per this file's own EXCERPT NOTE), not a date the Passio itself states. *Authorship and date:* anonymous; composed close to the martyrdom it narrates (Marculus's death during the Macarian repression, 347–348), on the dating given in the standard field literature (Frend, row 23) — the text's own heading does not itself supply a year. *Proximity:* a first-generation, near-contemporary account on that dating, not a later legendary elaboration. *Genre conventions:* the passio form — structured around the confessor's steadfastness under interrogation and the manner of death, conventions that idealize the subject's resolve and de-emphasize ambiguity in the events leading to arrest. *Formation ecology revealed:* this world's own "Church of the Martyrs" self-understanding in its most direct textual form — a community narrating its own persecution as vindication, not merely as suffering. *Author Gravity risk:* hagiographic idealization is structural to the genre, not incidental; this document does not treat its account of Marculus's own conduct or motivations as a neutral historical record on that basis. Its own text has not yet been read in depth beyond confirming its heading and identity this session; Monceaux's own dedicated section ("La *Passio Marculi*. — Date de l'ouvrage") has not yet been checked against it either, and both remain for future construction stages.
- **The *Passio Isaac et Maximiani*.** (This Maximian is a martyr of the Macarian repression, distinct from Maximian the deposed deacon whose 393 consecration as rival bishop gives the later Maximianist schism its name, discussed above and in Doc_01 §4 — a homonym worth flagging once, since both figures are prominent in this same document.) **Now vendored** (same file as the *Passio Marculi* above, Registry row 20; Manifest G4). *Authorship:* **not anonymous** — the work's own rubric reads "PASSIO MAXIMIANI ET ISAAC DONATISTARUM AUCTORE MACROBIO," and it is, per its own explicit ("*Explicit epistola beatissimi martyris Macrobi ad plebem Karthaginis de passione martyrum Isaac et Maximiani*"), Macrobius's own letter to the Carthage congregation, not a separate passio with an epistle appended to it. Mabillon's own note identifies Macrobius further as "the Donatists' own hidden bishop in the city of Rome" — a specific, checkable geographic fact about the movement's reach not otherwise present in this document. *Date:* the same Macarian-repression context (347–348). *Proximity:* contemporary, and — unlike the *Passio Marculi* — a first-person account by a named author writing to his own congregation, not a third-person narrative. *Genre conventions:* an episcopal letter cast partly in narrative form, addressed directly to the Carthage congregation ("Opportunus me, fratres, et laetus scribendi ad vos... ardor accendit"). *Formation ecology revealed:* a second, independently-authored Donatist voice from the same repression, corroborating the martyr-cult self-understanding through a source that — unlike the *Passio Marculi* — is not anonymous and speaks in its own author's first person throughout. *Author Gravity risk:* as a named author's own direct address to his community, this carries less of the passio genre's idealizing convention than an anonymous third-person narrative would, though it remains a partisan account written for exhortation, not neutral reportage; not yet read in depth beyond confirming its heading, authorship, and identity this session.
- **The *Acts of the Abitinian Martyrs* (*Acta Saturnini*).** *Authorship and date:* anonymous, set during the 304 Diocletianic persecution — earlier than the schism itself, transmitted forward by both parties as a shared pre-schism inheritance rather than composed from within an already-divided community. *Proximity:* contemporary or near-contemporary to 304 on its own account, though its transmission history is genuinely contested between Donatist and Catholic custody (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §3, B2), which bears on how far that proximity can be trusted as continuous rather than as later redaction. *Genre conventions:* the passio form, with the added complication that a text transmitted through a contested chain of custody may carry shaping specific to whichever party's manuscript tradition preserved it, on top of the genre's own idealizing conventions. *Formation ecology revealed:* what a faithfully formed life looks like under ultimate pressure, per the Framework's own Martyrology assessment category — the model of a faithful confessor this world's other formation narratives assume. *Author Gravity risk:* doubled relative to the Macarian-repression texts above — genre idealization plus a contested transmission history whose direction is not resolved in the vendored scholarship consulted this session.

**Tyconius's own genre.** The *Liber Regularum* (§1 above) is a hermeneutical treatise, not narrative material, but its author's own contested standing — the standard account (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §3, B1) holds that Parmenian and a Donatist council condemned Tyconius c. 380, and he never joined the Catholic church, with his formal status left open in Doc_01 §4 — makes it a formation-narrative-adjacent question in its own right: what a movement does with a dissenting intellectual figure it produces but does not fully embrace is itself formation evidence, flagged here for Doc_09's own Story Inventory work rather than resolved. Monceaux's now-vendored chapter (§1, §3 above) gives this question a fuller secondary treatment than this document has otherwise assembled — his own chapter outline names "Tyconius's condemnation by a council" and "his final years" as sub-topics within its own single argument-summary line, suggesting the primary source material for narrating this episode is more developed than this document's own present, brief treatment reflects; that fuller narration is left for Doc_09 rather than attempted here.

**No Tier 5 material identified.** Consistent with the Framework's prohibition, this document identifies no generated or illustrative narrative for this world; where the evidence is thin (ordinary lay experience of the schism, specifically), that thinness is named as a finding (§6 below), not filled.

## 5. Material Culture and Daily Life Sources

- **Epigraphic evidence:** the *Deo laudes* acclamation, attested in inscriptions as the Donatist party's own liturgical marker, set against the Caecilianist *Deo gratias*. **Resolved to specific catalogued inscriptions** (Confidence A, Registry row 27; the *Corpus Inscriptionum Latinarum* VIII Numidia supplement, Registry row 48, Manifest G7). The primary witness is inscription *CIL* VIII **17732**, found on two pillars near Bagai itself (now preserved at Khenchela) and reading "DEO LAVDES" twice; the editors' own note reads, in full: "*Uti [Deo] gratias... catholicorum, ita [Deo] laudes signum ac tessera fuit Donatistarum, quorum sedes primariae erant Bagai et Thamugadi*" — "just as 'Deo gratias' [was the sign] of Catholics, so 'Deo laudes' was the sign and token of the Donatists, whose primary seats were Bagai and Thamugadi." A second inscription, *CIL* VIII 20482 ("DEO LAVDES SVPER AQVAS...," an architrave fragment 1.80m long, found at Beni Fouda in the same volume's *Mauretania Sitifensis* supplement, not the Numidian material proper), carries an editorial note that is a direct cross-reference to 17732's own identification, not a second, independent attestation — "*Deo laudes (de hoc signo Donatistarum cf. supra ad n. 17732)*." Two further inscriptions in the same volume also carry the acclamation: no. 17368 ("DEO LAVDES") and no. 18669 ("DEO LAVDES DICAMVS"). This is a genuine field-recognized category resting on four catalogued attestations, the strongest of which is from Bagai itself, one of the two Donatist seats it names.
- **Basilica archaeology across Numidia:** physical, still-partially-recoverable evidence of the parallel institutional life this world's own Doc_01 names as its defining Authority Structures lens content (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §3, B2 discussion — this document does not repeat that reasoning, only its evidentiary basis). Confidence C on the same basis as the epigraphic material above — this is a real, recognized category of evidence in the field, not yet independently verified against specific site reports this session.
- **Social-historical background:** Donatism's own regional and social distribution — heavily rural and Numidian relative to the more urban, Proconsular-Africa-centered Caecilianist strength — bears directly on §6 below's own claim that "ordinary believer" experience constitutes the bulk of the movement rather than a separate, thin population. This distribution is uncontroversial; the fuller account of *why* it holds is not. Frend (row 23) reads it, and the Circumcellion movement associated with it, substantially through a native-social-protest thesis tied to a Punic/Libyan-speaking rural substrate resisting a Latinized, Romanized urban Church; Shaw (row 24) and, on this document's own field knowledge rather than a re-read this session, Brown (row 26) are the standard correctives to that reading, treating the ethnic-substrate frame as considerably overstated against the actual textual evidence. This document does not adjudicate the dispute; it names it, at Confidence C, pending independent verification of the underlying distributional data against a primary source.
- **Surrounding cultural and religious environment:** the Punic- and Libyan-speaking substrate of rural Numidia, and the region's own pre-Christian religious practice, are named in the field literature (again substantially in Frend, and again contested on the same basis as the bullet above) as part of the standard context both for the Circumcellion question (§6 below) and for Donatism's own rural strength more generally — a context this document names rather than exploits, at the same Confidence C and with the same not-yet-independently-verified caution.
- **Imperial legislation as material-institutional evidence:** the *Codex Theodosianus* Book 16 material (§1 above) would function partly as this category — state documentation of a specific, dated institutional reality (property confiscation, clerical exile) rather than either polemicist's own narrative framing of it. The critical Latin edition itself remains unvendored (§1 above); Boyd's now-vendored corroborating history (§1, §3 above) gives partial access to this material — the legislative sequence and several individual laws quoted verbatim — without itself being the primary legal text.

**Honest limitation, not treated as absence of the underlying conditions:** the *Deo laudes* acclamation is now independently verified against a specific catalogued inscription (above); the remaining material/epigraphic evidence named in this section — basilica archaeology, the social-historical and cultural-environment categories — is not yet independently verified against a specific site report, excavation record, or primary demographic source this session. This document names those categories and their evidentiary promise; it does not yet claim to have exploited them. ## 6. Source Asymmetries and Missing Voices

Per Step 0 §4, item 1 (binding on this document): this world's Author Gravity concentration on the hostile (Optatus/Augustine) side is severe and unusually direct, and is named here as this world's central evidentiary problem, not a background caveat.

**A constitutional routing note, following the discipline `Imperial-Juridical-Christianity/Doc_02_Source_Ecology.md` §6 already applied to an analogous case, with one difference from that case named below.** Article 20's Affirmative Duty is scoped to voices marginalized within the community. Article 23, the Writing-From-Inside Principle, governs something different: how this world's eventual Representative speaks about the world's own opponents and combatants — "as the world understood them... without rehabilitating them into modern equals and without modern editorializing" — not a general rule about how this document itself handles sourcing. The bullets below sort accordingly: a bullet about a voice missing from the community's own internal record belongs to Article 20; a bullet about how the Caecilianist/Catholic opponent is characterized belongs to Article 23's future application, not to this section's own Missing Voices work. One difference from IJC's precedent is worth naming rather than leaving implicit: IJC routed to Article 23 an opponent whose voice is nearly absent from its own vendored record (Homoian Christianity, surviving as an erased layer) — a genuine missing voice, discussed in a Missing Voices section for exactly that reason. The routing conclusion holds regardless of which direction the imbalance runs: Article 23, not Article 20, governs how that opponent is eventually characterized. This document names the inversion rather than letting the precedent's shape imply a parallel that is not there.

- **A genuine, and larger than first assessed, correction to the Author Gravity concentration itself: the G4 acquisition and Tyconius (G1) together.** This document's own central evidentiary problem, stated at the top of this section, is that the record is "concentrated almost entirely on one side of the schism it describes" (§1 above). The single Migne file vendored for G4 in fact delivers **four** texts that speak as Donatists rather than being spoken about or through by one — the *Passio Donati* sermon, the *Passio Marculi* (headed in Migne's own catalogue rubric "PASSIO MARCULI SACERDOTIS DONATISTAE"), and Macrobius's own letter to the Carthage congregation, present in the file both as the *Passio Isaac et Maximiani* and as a separate *Fragmentum epistolae Macrobii Donatistae* — alongside Tyconius's own *Liber Regularum* (§1, §2 above) from G1. The correction is bounded, and unevenly so across the four: Macrobius's letter and the *Passio Marculi*'s own Donatist self-identification are directly legible from the vendored text itself; the *Passio Donati* sermon is, by contrast, independently checkable against Paul Monceaux's 1920 analysis (§4 above), which dates it, proposes an author, and resolves its title question. This document treats the four together as a real, not merely token, addition to the non-hostile-voice side of the ledger, while still not treating it as resolving the concentration §1 describes: every one of the four remains a comparatively short, occasional text against the scale of Optatus's and Augustine's own corpus.
- **Elite/literate/male/urban skew (Article 20), with a genuine partial mitigation this world's own B5 finding already names (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §3, B5):** this world's sourcing is not merely elite-skewed in the way several other confirmed worlds are — Donatism was, for substantial stretches and regions, the numerically dominant church, not a small elite current. The access problem is source-mediation (nearly everything currently vendored passes through hostile hands), not scale (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §3, B2/B5). This does not eliminate the skew; it changes its character — "ordinary believer" experience here is the bulk of the movement itself, currently unreachable in its own words, rather than a genuinely separate, thin population.
- **Gender (Article 20):** the vendored corpus's founding narrative names one woman at the center of the schism's own origin. Optatus's *Against the Donatists* I.16 ("The quarrel of Lucilla against Caecilian") describes Lucilla, a wealthy Carthaginian laywoman, as a proximate cause of the schism: "the Schism, after the consecration of Caecilian, was effected at Carthage through a certain mischief-making woman named Lucilla," and Optatus reports that "Majorinus, a member of the household of Lucilla — at her instigation, and through her bribes — was consecrated Bishop by Betrayers," the rival consecration that gave the emerging Donatist party its first bishop of Carthage. This is the single most consequential female role attested anywhere in this world's vendored corpus, and it survives entirely inside the founding hostile narrative's own explanation for why the schism happened at all — Optatus's own reason for including it is to locate the schism's cause in a private grievance and a wealthy patron's bribery rather than in any principled Donatist case against Caecilian. That is itself the Author Gravity point: a hostile source that needs a woman's real agency and standing — wealth, a household, the capacity to make or unmake a bishop — in order to make its own accusation land is a specific against-the-grain textual trace, not an absence. The vendored edition's own translator's apparatus records a second lead, from outside Optatus's own text: it reports Augustine (citing an Epistle numbered "clxii," very likely under an older, pre-standard citation scheme) as saying "that a woman like Lucilla was subsequently the cause of a schism within a schism" — a later schism among the Donatists themselves. That content is vendored, and this is the Maximianist schism: Augustine's Letter XLIII, §26 (Registry row 6), asks his correspondents to "ask by what woman's instigation Maximianus... withdrew himself from the communion of Primianus... precisely as Majorinus, under the influence of Lucilla, assembled a faction of bishops" against Caecilian, and states directly, a few lines later, that the Council of Secundus of Tigisis "which Lucilla stirred up against Cæcilianus" is to be weighed no differently than the Council of Maximianus, "which in like manner some other woman stirred up against Primianus." Augustine draws the Lucilla parallel himself, in Lucilla's own name, in the same paragraph — but the second woman he leaves unnamed, unlike the schism's own founding actor. That asymmetry is itself the finding this bullet reports: a hostile source names Lucilla in the very act of comparing her to a second woman whose own name the source does not give. Letter XLIII is dated a.d. 397 in the vendored volume, consistent with (though not itself dating) the Maximianist reception §1 above reconstructs. No comparably individually-attested Caecilianist-side or ordinary-believer woman has been identified this pass; the asymmetry this bullet reports is Lucilla's presence, named, at the schism's founding moment, against a second woman's presence, unnamed, at the schism's own internal repetition of it — not against a continued absence.
- **The Circumcellion/*agonistici* question (Article 20, internal to this world, per `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §2, A5 and §4 item 2).** Discharged in full here, not as a footnote. The characterization the vendored sources give this group — itinerant, rural, associated with episodes of violence — is substantially shaped by hostile Catholic polemic (Augustine, Optatus), and Shaw's *Sacred Violence* is the standard treatment of that shaping. But the group's *existence* is independently attested outside hostile polemic — imperial legislation names them directly (*Codex Theodosianus* 16.5.52, 412, still not vendored in any critical Latin edition after five acquisition attempts; §1 above) — and Augustine himself reports their own self-designation, *agonistici* ("contestants"), a term of the group's own rather than merely an outsiders' label. Boyd's now-vendored corroborating history (§1, §3 above) discusses the surrounding legislative sequence (C. Th. xvi.5.3–58) extensively but does not itself quote 16.5.52, and never uses the term *agonistici* — Boyd, writing in 1905, uses "Circumcellions" in his one sentence naming the group at all, itself a small data point on how the field's own terminology has shifted since, though too thin a sample to generalize from. §3 above flags Boyd's own characterization of the group in that same sentence as an uncritical restatement of Augustine's and Optatus's hostile framing, not itself corroborating evidence of the group's character. This document distinguishes the two claims throughout: the group's existence and self-designation are treated at the confidence the independent attestation supports; the group's *character* (their scale, their typical conduct, their relationship to the wider Donatist hierarchy) is treated as substantially Augustine's and Optatus's own framing, not independently checkable at present. The Maximianist suppression (Doc_01 §4) reports Circumcellion involvement through exactly this same channel — Augustine's own polemic — and is marked accordingly wherever it appears (Doc_01 §5, Cell 2B).
- **The Caecilianist/Catholic party's own voice (Article 23's future concern, not Article 20's present one):** per `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §2, A5, this world's defining opponent is reconstructed here from inside Donatism's own perspective — a persecuted "Church of the Martyrs" facing a state-favored rival — not cited as a neutral external source. Optatus and Augustine are used throughout as *this world's own opponents*, whose framing must be marked as such, consistent with the Writing-From-Inside Principle's own future application to how the Representative speaks of them.

**Affirmative Duty (Article 20), applied to the genuinely internal-marginalization cases above:** the primary duty — naming structural absence — is discharged throughout this section, including where the record's own limits (source-mediation, elite skew) apply. The secondary duty — bounded reconstruction — is exercised once, for Lucilla, under the Framework's third bounding condition: Optatus I.16's need to name her real agency and standing in order to blame her for the schism is a specific against-the-grain textual trace, and the reconstruction above stays inside what that trace actually supports — a named woman of means with the standing to make a rival consecration happen — rather than extending to anything Optatus does not say. No further case meeting any of the three bounding conditions has been identified this pass for any other specific ordinary believer's or marginalized voice's perspective. If later construction work (Doc_05, Doc_07) surfaces one, it is added here under the Registry's own living-document discipline.

**Forces lens on the source ecology (Construction Framework V7.4 Step 2; Forces Framework V1.1 Section 4).** Which sources speak to external forces: Optatus's appendix and Augustine's letters and treatises are themselves the record of the Caecilianist/imperial alliance's own actions against this world — the Rome and Arles rulings, Constantine's coercion, the Macarian repression, the 405 Edict, the 411 Conference — so this world's own external-force record survives almost entirely through the acting or allied party's own documentation of it, not through this world's own account of experiencing it.
