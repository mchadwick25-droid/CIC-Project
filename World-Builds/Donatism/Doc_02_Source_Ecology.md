## 1. Primary Sources

This world's evidentiary situation is the inverse of a typical Author Gravity problem: the volume of surviving material is large, but it is concentrated almost entirely on one side of the schism it describes (Doc_01 §7, item 1). The following are Native per the Source Registry's Boundary Check; full classification detail (Type, Confidence, Licensed-For target) lives in `Source_Registry.md`.

**Optatus of Milevis, *Against the Donatists* (Books I–VII, c. 366–367, revised c. 385).** The earliest substantial narrative source on the schism, and the primary anti-Donatist source outside Augustine (`cic/corpus-map/donatism.yaml`). Contemporary and primary in composition date, but hostile and external in perspective — Optatus describes the Donatists rather than speaking as one, "usable for Donatism only etically" (corpus map, verbatim). Transmits, in its own appendix, a body of contemporary documentary material of a different character: the *Acta Purgationis Felicis* (314), the *Gesta apud Zenophilum* (320), Constantine's own letters, the Council of Arles' 314 letter to Pope Silvester, the Acts of the Council of Cirta (305 — a date genuinely disputed in the scholarship, with 307 and later argued by some; `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §3 B1), and Anulinus's *relatio* (313). This appendix's *contents* are court acts and imperial correspondence; its *selection, assembly, and transmission* are still Optatus's own act, so it sits inside this world's Author Gravity concentration rather than outside it, even though it is a comparatively less rhetorically shaped layer than Optatus's own argumentative prose.

**Augustine of Hippo's anti-Donatist corpus.** By far the largest body of surviving evidence, and the dominant weight in this world's own Author Gravity concentration: *On Baptism, Against the Donatists* (7 books, c. 400); *Answer to the Letters of Petilian* (3 books) — quoting the Donatist bishop Petilian of Constantina's own letters clause by clause, and therefore the single most extensive surviving Donatist voice in the vendored corpus, though preserved entirely inside its own refutation; *The Correction of the Donatists* (Letter 185, c. 417, to the tribune Boniface, defending imperial coercion after the 411 Conference); and a cluster of eleven letters (XXIII, XLIII, XLIV, LIII, LXXVI, LXXXVII, LXXXVIII, LXXXIX, XCIII, CXXXIX, CLXXIII), split from the wider Augustine correspondence on Mark's 2026-08-26 ruling by the volume's own div3 markup (168 letters, cut at eight or more occurrences of Donatist vocabulary, plus Letter LXXVI by its title's own address to the Donatists as a body — `cic/corpus-map/donatism.yaml`). This letters cluster is classified `role: context` in the corpus map, distinct from the `role: tradition` classification carried by the three works named just above it. The corpus map's own stated warrant for that distinction is internally inconsistent — it grounds the letters' `context` classification in "the role normalisation already applied to his anti-Donatist treatises," but those treatises are themselves classified `tradition`, not `context` — an inconsistency this build thread cannot resolve, since the corpus map is generated from `_staging/` and sits outside this build's own editing authority. It is named here for census-level correction rather than smoothed over; this document continues to use the letters cluster at the narrow licensing the Registry already sets for it (row 6), regardless of which role label eventually prevails.

**The Cyprianic antecedent corpus.** Cyprian's *Epistles*, *On the Lapsed* (*De Lapsis*), *On the Unity of the Church* (*De Unitate*), and the acts of the Seventh Council of Carthage (256, on the baptism of heretics) are classified `role: antecedent` in the vendored corpus map, on Mark's 2026-08-26 ruling: Cyprian is neither a Donatist (he predates the schism by half a century) nor merely `context` describing it from outside — he is "the authority both sides argued FROM," a status measured rather than asserted — the corpus map records that Augustine's *On Baptism, Against the Donatists* names Cyprian 306 times and Agrippinus 28 times in roughly 92,000 words. **The 256 council is independently attested twice, from two different vendored volumes with two different classifications, and this document keeps the two distinct rather than treating them as one entry:** the Cyprianic corpus's own record of the council (`anf05`, `role: antecedent`, confidence: assigned) and a separate telling within the conciliar-history volume, *The Acts of the Council of Carthage under Cyprian* (`npnf214`, `role: tradition`, confidence: **provisional**). The provisional flag on the second is carried forward, not silently resolved, per this document's own confidence-sensitive test below.

**Conciliar and institutional record.** The Code of Canons of the African Church (Council of Carthage, 419), collecting canons on the reception of Donatist clergy and congregations that mostly originate in the African councils of c. 397–407, is vendored at `confidence: provisional` in the corpus map — carried forward here, not upgraded without independent verification. **Not currently vendored, and load-bearing for this world's own institutional self-understanding:** the *Gesta Collationis Carthaginiensis*, the acts of the imperially-convened 411 Conference of Carthage, at which Donatist bishops — Emeritus of Caesarea prominent among them — are recorded speaking at length on their own side of the exchange; and the *Codex Theodosianus* Book 16, the imperial legal record of the movement's suppression, independent of either polemicist's own selection. Both are flagged for Source Acquisition in the accompanying manifest.

**The Maximianist schism's rebaptism-theology evidence.** Augustine's *Contra Cresconium* (four books, c. 406, "some say as late as 409") and *Contra epistulam Parmeniani* (three books, c. 400) are this world's own primary sources for how the Maximianist affair was used in rebaptism argument, and neither survives in a vendored full text. A detailed scholarly summary of both works, sufficient to put their content to evidentiary use at this document's own confidence level, is already vendored inside `npnf104_augustine-anti-manichaean-anti-donatist.xml`'s own Prolegomena ("Chapter II.—An Analysis of Augustin's Writings Against the Donatists") and is rowed in the Registry as its own entry. That summary reports the decisive fact Doc_01 §4 identifies: when the mainstream (Primianist) Donatist party suppressed the Maximianist schism with state assistance (the Bagai sentences, 394–398) and its bishops and clergy sought return, "the Synod had granted a season of delay during which all who returned should be held innocent. Of this very many availed themselves; the baptism of these was valid; those who remained outside lost both baptism and the church" — a decision Augustine's Donatist interlocutor Cresconius does not deny but engages with directly, professing (per the same summary) to "have made special inquiry into the whole history." Returning Maximianist clergy and their baptisms were received without reordination or rebaptism. Augustine used exactly this fact as an argument against the Donatists' own rebaptism logic — a use already visible in his earlier rhetorical question, from the same Prolegomena's account of his *Psalmus contra Partem Donati*: "Why rebaptize us... when you do not repeat the rite upon your once expelled but now restored Maximianists?" If the validity of baptism and ordination depended on the minister's own standing outside schism — the Donatists' own founding logic against the Caecilianists — the Maximianists, themselves schismatics from the Primianist party's own point of view, should have needed reordination and rebaptism on their return. They did not receive it. This is evidence that the Donatist rebaptism logic was not applied consistently even by its own adherents when the schismatic in question was their own, not merely a debating point scored by a hostile source: the underlying practice is reported as a fact about the Donatist party's own conduct, contested by a Donatist interlocutor on its interpretation rather than on its occurrence. It remains Augustine-mediated evidence — no Maximianist or mainstream-Donatist first-hand account of the reception decision survives — and is treated at that confidence. Consistent with Doc_01 §4's own reasoning, it is also evidence against Strand status for the Maximianist episode (a shared formation pattern under internal contest, not two structurally distinct traditions), a finding not reopened here.

**Tyconius's *Liber Regularum* (Book of Rules).** A substantially complete, independently-authored hermeneutical treatise, later influential on Augustine's own *De doctrina christiana* III — not currently vendored. If acquired, this is the single largest available correction to this world's own Author Gravity concentration, since it is Donatist-affiliated material surviving in its own right rather than through hostile quotation. Doc_01 §4 found that Tyconius's own dissent does not rise to strand status (no attested following, parallel authority structure, or distinct communal practice); that finding does not depend on this acquisition and is not reopened here.

## 2. Author Gravity Assessment

Per the Construction Framework's own five-dimension author-gravity discipline, governed at the constitutional level by Article 26's delegation of "the dimensions of source assessment, including transmission history, and the construction stages at which the discipline is enforced" to the Framework, every major voice is assessed across all five dimensions: Visibility, Representativeness, Influence, Limitations, and Transmission History.

**Optatus of Milevis.**
- *Visibility:* Over-visible for the schism's own documentary record — his appendix is this world's single richest concentration of contemporary legal and institutional material, and his own narrative frames how that material is read.
- *Representativeness:* Represents the Caecilianist/Catholic party's own case against the schism, not any Donatist perspective — he writes explicitly against "my brother Parmenian," the Donatist bishop of Carthage, a rhetorical fraternity that does not extend to representing Parmenian's own view.
- *Influence:* Foundational — Augustine's own later anti-Donatist corpus draws on and extends arguments Optatus first assembled, and Optatus's appendix is the earliest surviving collection of the schism's own founding documents.
- *Limitations:* A polemicist's own selection and framing govern which documents survive in the appendix and how they are read; the work's second edition (after 383, on the evidence of the added name of Pope Siricius) updates the papal and Donatist episcopal lists and carries Book VII, showing Optatus's own argument extended after the fact rather than fixed at the first edition.
- *Transmission History:* Preserved through the Caecilianist/Catholic side's own manuscript tradition, as the eventually-victorious party's literature; no adversarial-quotation problem of the kind affecting Petilian's letters below, since Optatus's own words are not filtered through a hostile source, but his selection of *what to include from the other side* is itself the transmission-shaping act this world's central evidentiary problem depends on.

**Augustine of Hippo.**
- *Visibility:* Overwhelmingly over-visible — the largest single body of evidence for this entire world, spanning three decades of his own episcopate (post-411 material comes disproportionately from the period after his own side's decisive institutional victory).
- *Representativeness:* Represents the Caecilianist/Catholic party at its most intellectually developed and most institutionally confident, writing after Rome, Arles, and (for his later works) the 411 Conference had already ruled against the Donatist party — not a contemporary, undecided voice.
- *Influence:* Enormous and lasting, both for how this schism is known at all and for the sacramental theology (objective validity of sacraments independent of the minister's worthiness) his engagement with Donatism substantially forged — a theological legacy that belongs to World #8's own future reconstruction (Doc_01 §6), not developed further here.
- *Limitations:* Every quotation of a Donatist voice in this corpus — Petilian's letters above all — survives only as much of it as Augustine chose to quote, in the order and selection he chose, for the purpose of refuting it clause by clause. This is not merely a general caution; it is the specific mechanism by which this world's own central evidentiary problem operates.
- *Transmission History:* Preserved through the Catholic institutional tradition's own manuscript transmission — the eventually-establishment side's literature, copied and circulated by the institutions that had every reason to preserve it. The letters cluster's own survival, split out of a 168-letter volume by markup rather than by a Donatism-specific manuscript tradition, is itself evidence of how thoroughly this world's own material is embedded inside a larger Catholic corpus rather than transmitted as its own body of literature.

**Petilian of Constantina.** Treated separately from the general Augustine entry because Petilian is, on the current vendored corpus, the fullest surviving *Donatist* voice — and because his own Author Gravity profile is therefore not Augustine's, even though his words reach us entirely through Augustine's own text.
- *Visibility:* Visible only to the extent Augustine's *Answer to the Letters of Petilian* chose to quote him, clause by clause, for refutation — a visibility entirely conditioned by his opponent's own selection.
- *Representativeness:* Represents a Donatist bishop's own articulate defense of the movement's rebaptism theology and ecclesiology, at a moment specifically dated to c. 398–400 — contemporaneous with Augustine's *Answer* (Book I c. 400, Book III c. 401–402) and with *On Baptism* (c. 400) — a moment of renewed Caecilianist/imperial pressure in the run-up to the 405 Edict of Unity, not, as the schism's own origin might suggest, within personal living memory of the Macarian martyrs of 347–348, over fifty years earlier. Where Petilian's own argument invokes that martyrdom, it draws on the movement's cultic and communal memory of it (§4 below), not a claim to personal recollection.
- *Influence:* Substantial within this world's own reconstruction specifically, precisely because so little independent Donatist material survives — Petilian's quoted words carry disproportionate evidentiary weight for want of an alternative, a fact this document names rather than treats as a strength.
- *Limitations:* Everything about this entry is downstream of the same limitation: no direct manuscript of Petilian's own letters survives independent of Augustine's quotation. Where Augustine paraphrases rather than quotes, or characterizes rather than reproduces, this document treats the material as Augustine's own characterization, not Petilian's words, and the Registry's own entry marks this distinction row by row where it can be checked.
- *Transmission History:* The single sharpest instance of this world's central evidentiary problem: transmission and refutation are, for this figure, the same act. Petilian's words survive *because* Augustine needed them in front of a reader in order to refute them — a preservation mechanism that guarantees selection for refutability, not for completeness or fairness to Petilian's own case.

**Cyprian of Carthage (antecedent, not a Donatist voice).** Given a full entry despite predating the schism by half a century, because his material is load-bearing for this world's own A1/A2 reasoning (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §2) and B3 boundary with World #8 (Doc_01 §6).
- *Visibility:* Very high within his own third-century context; his visibility *for this world specifically* is a function of how heavily both the Donatist and Caecilianist parties argued from him, not of anything Cyprian himself said about a schism he did not live to see.
- *Representativeness:* Represents the third-century North African church's own settled position on rebaptism (baptism outside the true church is no baptism) at a moment when that position was itself contested within the wider church (his dispute with Pope Stephen) but not yet a basis for schism within Africa itself.
- *Influence:* Decisive for this world's own founding logic — the Donatist position is a direct continuation and sharpening of Cyprian's own view, not a departure from it (Doc_01 §5).
- *Limitations:* Cyprian cannot be read as though he anticipated or endorsed the Donatist schism specifically; the corpus map's own antecedent classification exists precisely to prevent that anachronism, and this document does not use Cyprian to characterize any specific Donatist claim beyond what the schism's own participants (via Optatus's and Augustine's quotation of them) can be shown to have actually argued from him.
- *Transmission History:* Preserved through the same African, later broader Latin, manuscript tradition both the Donatist and Caecilianist parties drew on — a shared inheritance, not a source either side transmitted exclusively, which is itself the evidentiary basis for treating him as antecedent rather than as either side's own tradition (corpus map ruling, 2026-08-26).

## 4. Formation Narrative Sources

Per Construction Framework Part II, hagiographic and collected-narrative sources are assessed separately from theological treatises and institutional records, since they carry formation content — how the community understood itself — differently than argument does. Each source below is evaluated for authorship and date, proximity to the events or persons described, genre conventions that shape the account, the formation ecology it reveals, and its Author Gravity risk.

**The martyr and confessor Passiones.** Step 0's own disclosure (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §1) names martyr-cult and confessor memory as this world's own distinctive strength among the nine confirmed worlds — the community's self-understanding as the "Church of the Martyrs" is not peripheral color but this world's own central formation narrative.

- **The *Passio Marculi*.** *Authorship and date:* anonymous; composed close to the martyrdom it narrates (Marculus's death during the Macarian repression, 347–348), on the scholarship's own dating — not yet independently vendored or verified this session. *Proximity:* a first-generation, near-contemporary account on that dating, not a later legendary elaboration. *Genre conventions:* the passio form — structured around the confessor's steadfastness under interrogation and the manner of death, conventions that idealize the subject's resolve and de-emphasize ambiguity in the events leading to arrest. *Formation ecology revealed:* this world's own "Church of the Martyrs" self-understanding in its most direct textual form — a community narrating its own persecution as vindication, not merely as suffering. *Author Gravity risk:* hagiographic idealization is structural to the genre, not incidental; this document does not treat its account of Marculus's own conduct or motivations as a neutral historical record on that basis.
- **The *Passio Isaac et Maximiani*.** *Authorship and date:* anonymous, from the same Macarian-repression context (347–348). *Proximity:* contemporary or near-contemporary, on the same basis as the *Passio Marculi*. *Genre conventions:* the passio form, as above. *Formation ecology revealed:* the same martyr-cult self-understanding, reinforced through a second, distinct pair of confessors from the same repression — evidence that this identity rested on more than one narrated case, not a single isolated story. *Author Gravity risk:* as above.
- **The *Acts of the Abitinian Martyrs* (*Acta Saturnini*).** *Authorship and date:* anonymous, set during the 304 Diocletianic persecution — earlier than the schism itself, transmitted forward by both parties as a shared pre-schism inheritance rather than composed from within an already-divided community. *Proximity:* contemporary or near-contemporary to 304 on its own account, though its transmission history is genuinely contested between Donatist and Catholic custody (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §2, A5), which bears on how far that proximity can be trusted as continuous rather than as later redaction. *Genre conventions:* the passio form, with the added complication that a text transmitted through a contested chain of custody may carry shaping specific to whichever party's manuscript tradition preserved it, on top of the genre's own idealizing conventions. *Formation ecology revealed:* what a faithfully formed life looks like under ultimate pressure, per the Framework's own Martyrology assessment category — the model of a faithful confessor this world's other formation narratives assume. *Author Gravity risk:* doubled relative to the Macarian-repression texts above — genre idealization plus a contested transmission history whose direction is not resolved in the vendored scholarship consulted this session.

**Tyconius's own genre.** The *Liber Regularum* (§1 above) is a hermeneutical treatise, not narrative material, but its author's own contested standing — the standard account (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §3, B1) holds that Parmenian and a Donatist council condemned Tyconius c. 380, and he never joined the Catholic church, with his formal status left open in Doc_01 §4 — makes it a formation-narrative-adjacent question in its own right: what a movement does with a dissenting intellectual figure it produces but does not fully embrace is itself formation evidence, flagged here for Doc_09's own Story Inventory work rather than resolved.

**No Tier 5 material identified.** Consistent with the Framework's prohibition, this document identifies no generated or illustrative narrative for this world; where the evidence is thin (ordinary lay experience of the schism, specifically), that thinness is named as a finding (§6 below), not filled.

## 6. Source Asymmetries and Missing Voices

Per Step 0 §4, item 1 (binding on this document): this world's Author Gravity concentration on the hostile (Optatus/Augustine) side is severe and unusually direct, and is named here as this world's central evidentiary problem, not a background caveat.

**A constitutional routing note, following the discipline `Imperial-Juridical-Christianity/Doc_02_Source_Ecology.md` §6 already applied to an analogous case, with one difference from that case named below.** Article 20's Affirmative Duty is scoped to voices marginalized within the community. Article 23, the Writing-From-Inside Principle, governs something different: how this world's eventual Representative speaks about the world's own opponents and combatants — "as the world understood them... without rehabilitating them into modern equals and without modern editorializing" — not a general rule about how this document itself handles sourcing. The bullets below sort accordingly: a bullet about a voice missing from the community's own internal record belongs to Article 20; a bullet about how the Caecilianist/Catholic opponent is characterized belongs to Article 23's future application, not to this section's own Missing Voices work. One difference from IJC's precedent is worth naming rather than leaving implicit: IJC routed to Article 23 an opponent whose voice is nearly absent from its own vendored record (Homoian Christianity, surviving as an erased layer) — a genuine missing voice, discussed in a Missing Voices section for exactly that reason. Donatism's own opponent, the Caecilianist/Catholic party voiced through Optatus and Augustine, is the overwhelming majority of this world's surviving corpus — the inverse case. The routing conclusion holds regardless of which direction the imbalance runs: Article 23, not Article 20, governs how that opponent is eventually characterized. This document names the inversion rather than letting the precedent's shape imply a parallel that is not there.

- **Elite/literate/male/urban skew (Article 20), with a genuine partial mitigation this world's own B5 finding already names (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §3, B5):** this world's sourcing is not merely elite-skewed in the way several other confirmed worlds are — Donatism was, for substantial stretches and regions, the numerically dominant church, not a small elite current. The access problem is source-mediation (nearly everything currently vendored passes through hostile hands), not scale (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §3, B2/B5). This does not eliminate the skew; it changes its character — "ordinary believer" experience here is the bulk of the movement itself, currently unreachable in its own words, rather than a genuinely separate, thin population.
- **Gender (Article 20):** the vendored corpus's founding narrative names one woman at the center of the schism's own origin. Optatus's *Against the Donatists* I.16 ("The quarrel of Lucilla against Caecilian") describes Lucilla, a wealthy Carthaginian laywoman, as a proximate cause of the schism: "the Schism, after the consecration of Caecilian, was effected at Carthage through a certain mischief-making woman named Lucilla," and Optatus reports that "Majorinus, a member of the household of Lucilla — at her instigation, and through her bribes — was consecrated Bishop by Betrayers," the rival consecration that gave the emerging Donatist party its first bishop of Carthage. This is the single most consequential female role attested anywhere in this world's vendored corpus, and it survives entirely inside the founding hostile narrative's own explanation for why the schism happened at all — Optatus's own reason for including it is to locate the schism's cause in a private grievance and a wealthy patron's bribery rather than in any principled Donatist case against Caecilian. That is itself the Author Gravity point: a hostile source that needs a woman's real agency and standing — wealth, a household, the capacity to make or unmake a bishop — in order to make its own accusation land is a specific against-the-grain textual trace, not an absence. Optatus's own apparatus also records a second lead: Augustine's Letter 162 reports "that a woman like Lucilla was subsequently the cause of a schism within a schism" — a later split among the Maximianists themselves — naming a second, currently unidentified female actor inside this world's own internal history, flagged here for Doc_09's Story Inventory rather than pursued further in this pass. No comparably individually-attested Caecilianist-side or ordinary-believer woman has been identified this pass; the asymmetry this bullet reports is Lucilla's presence at the schism's founding moment against a continued absence everywhere else.
- **The Circumcellion/*agonistici* question (Article 20, internal to this world, per `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §2, A5 and §4 item 2).** Discharged in full here, not as a footnote. The characterization the vendored sources give this group — itinerant, rural, associated with episodes of violence — is substantially shaped by hostile Catholic polemic (Augustine, Optatus), and Shaw's *Sacred Violence* is the standard treatment of that shaping. But the group's *existence* is independently attested outside hostile polemic — imperial legislation names them directly (*Codex Theodosianus* 16.5.52, 412, not yet vendored) — and Augustine himself reports their own self-designation, *agonistici* ("contestants"), a term of the group's own rather than merely an outsiders' label. This document distinguishes the two claims throughout: the group's existence and self-designation are treated at the confidence the independent attestation supports; the group's *character* (their scale, their typical conduct, their relationship to the wider Donatist hierarchy) is treated as substantially Augustine's and Optatus's own framing, not independently checkable at present. The Maximianist suppression (Doc_01 §4) reports Circumcellion involvement through exactly this same channel — Augustine's own polemic — and is marked accordingly wherever it appears (§1 above; Doc_01 §5, Cell 2B).
- **The Caecilianist/Catholic party's own voice (Article 23's future concern, not Article 20's present one):** per `Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §2, A5, this world's defining opponent is reconstructed here from inside Donatism's own perspective — a persecuted "Church of the Martyrs" facing a state-favored rival — not cited as a neutral external source.
