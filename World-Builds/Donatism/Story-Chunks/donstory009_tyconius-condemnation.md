## Story Text

Tyconius made an argument from Scripture too well-supported to simply dismiss, and it undid him from the inside.

He argued, at length and with abundant scriptural support, that the Christians of Africa belonged to a church spread across the whole world — connected, not by anything peculiar to Africa, but by communion with that same worldwide body. Anyone actually listening to the case he built from the text could see where it led: if that was true, the African churches still in communion with the wider world had not lost anything by staying there, and those who had cut themselves off from that communion — Tyconius's own party among them — were the ones who had actually separated themselves from something real.

Parmenian, the movement's own bishop at Carthage, and the other leaders around him saw exactly where this argument pointed, and they did not follow it. Parmenian wrote to Tyconius directly, rebuking him for preaching that the church extended across the whole world, and warning him not to dare do it again. Tyconius did not recant, and did not leave. He kept arguing what he had argued, from inside the party that told him to stop.

What came after the letter is less certain, and the tradition itself marks it as less certain: it is reported — not witnessed outright, but reported — that Tyconius was afterward condemned by a council of his own communion. He was never received back. He did not go over to the other side either. He simply remained, for whatever years he had left, the head of a schismatic church so small that he may have been the only one left in it.

## Tier Justification

This is Tier 1 material, correcting an earlier judgment in this document's own construction: an initial pass concluded no primary attestation of this condemnation survived, resting only on Monceaux's 1920 secondary synthesis. That conclusion was wrong. Monceaux's own footnote for this exact claim cites, and directly quotes, Augustine's *Contra Epistulam Parmeniani* I.1 — a work fully vendored in a Latin critical edition (Source_Registry.md row 39) — and the cited sentence is directly locatable there: Augustine himself reports that Parmenian first tried to correct Tyconius by letter, and that afterward, "it is reported" ("*perhibent*"), he was condemned by a council. This is a near-contemporary primary source (Augustine writing *c.* 400, roughly two decades after the events of *c.* 380 that Doc_02 §4 already dates), squarely within this world's own construction-window horizon — not "fifteen centuries removed," as this document's own earlier draft incorrectly stated.

The tier assignment carries its own internal calibration worth stating plainly: Augustine's report of Parmenian's letter and its rebuke is stated directly, without a reporting hedge, and is treated here as Documented at that level of confidence. The specific mechanism of what followed — a conciliar condemnation, rather than some other form of exclusion — carries Augustine's own explicit hedge ("*perhibent*," "it is reported," not "I know" or "it happened"), and this chunk preserves that hedge rather than smoothing it into unqualified fact, the same discipline donstory007 applies to the Council of Cirta's own disputed date. Monceaux's own secondary account, quoting the same Latin and adding the aftermath (Tyconius becoming head of a schismatic remnant so small he may have been its only member), is used here as corroborating context for that aftermath, not as the primary evidentiary basis the earlier draft mistakenly treated it as being on its own.

## Usage Guidance

The Representative may draw on this story as documented history, with Augustine named as the source through whom Parmenian's own rebuke survives, and the hedge on what followed carried into the telling: "Augustine tells us that Parmenian wrote to Tyconius directly, rebuking him — and that afterward, it is reported, he was condemned by a council." The argument Tyconius made, Parmenian's refusal to accept its conclusion, and Tyconius's own refusal either to recant or to leave, may all be offered with confidence; the specific mechanism of the condemnation itself should be offered with the same "it is reported" quality Augustine's own text carries.

Additional guidance specific to this story: this is this world's clearest documented instance of its own leadership choosing not to follow an argument to its logical end when that end threatened the movement's own separatist self-understanding — a genuinely uncomfortable story for a Representative to carry, and one that should not be softened into a story about legitimate doctrinal boundary-keeping without acknowledging what it actually cost: a serious, scripturally-grounded internal voice, permanently cut off, whose own writing nonetheless proved durable enough to shape a Catholic theologian's method for reading Scripture for centuries after. The Representative should be prepared to sit with that irony rather than resolve it in either direction.
