## Story Text

The tradition remembers Marculus as a man who had already given up what the world offers before persecution ever reached him — a life of unusual virtue, a refusal of worldly advancement, given instead to the church.

When Macarius's persecution came into Numidia, ten bishops were sent either to persuade Marculus's own community to submit or — as the tradition tells us happened — to join the resistance themselves instead. Marculus himself was seized at a place called Vegesela. He was bound to columns and flogged, and — so the tradition insists — bore it without visible pain, praising God the whole time as though the whips could not reach the part of him that mattered. He was paraded through several Numidian towns as a spectacle, then held for four days at a cliff called Novapetra.

In that waiting, the tradition says, he fasted, and he was given a vision: a cup, a crown, and a palm, shown to him together, the way the community remembers such things being shown to those about to complete a formed life. Before dawn, he was thrown from the cliff. The tradition holds that his body did not break on the fall — that it descended gently, as though something were carrying it down rather than dropping it — and that a light settled over the place afterward, bright enough that the brethren could find him and take him for burial before anyone could stop them and deny it.

This is how the tradition remembers Marculus: not as a man who died, but as a man shown, in advance, what completing his own formation would look like — and then given exactly that.

## Formation Ecology Connection

This story is direct evidence for **G3 — Martyr-Cult Identity** (Doc_04 §3.3) in its fullest hagiographic register, and it belongs to the same Cell 2A (Ongoing/External) persecution cluster Doc_08 names as "the later, better-attested Macarian repression" (Force 2A-2). Where donstory001 (the Passio Donati sermon) shows martyrdom kept through annual liturgical commemoration, this story shows martyrdom rendered through the full visionary and miraculous vocabulary of the genre — the cup/crown/palm triad, the incorruptible body, the guiding light — which is itself evidence of what this community believed a completed formation under ultimate pressure was supposed to look like, independent of whether every element happened as described.

It also stands in a documented, named tension with the Catholic side's own account, in two separate forms: Optatus, arguing the deaths were deserved punishment for schism, still concedes the bare fact of the killing; Augustine, separately, disputes whether the manner of death (self-inflicted or imposed) even qualifies for the martyrdom label at all. Neither writer accepts this world's own account of what happened — a genuine cross-voice contest over the very meaning of this world's central formation category, worth naming rather than smoothing over.

## Tier Justification

Tier 3 is the clear fit, and this is the closest thing in this world's own corpus to the "classic case" Tier 3's own definition anticipates: "hagiographic narrative is a specific type within this tier... the idealized portrait of a saint's life, the miracle sequence, the death as completion of a formed life." Every one of those three named markers is present here in textbook form: the miracle sequence (the visionary cup/crown/palm triad, the unbroken fall, the guiding light), the idealized portrait (a man who has already renounced worldly advancement before persecution even arrives), and the death as completion (the vision shown *before* the death, then delivered exactly as shown).

The account is anonymous and near-contemporary rather than by a securely identified eyewitness, which rules out Tier 1's own "named author with identifiable social location" requirement. Per Tier 3's own confidence rule, the general portrait — that Marculus died at Macarius's hands, resisting a persecution operation — carries Contested confidence, corroborated even by the hostile Optatus/Augustine material; the specific visionary and miraculous details carry Inferential/Thin confidence, exactly as Tier 3 specifies for genre-shaped detail. The formation ideal this story communicates (what a completed, faithful death looks like) is the evidence; the cliff-top vision and the unbroken fall are not offered as verified events.

## Usage Guidance

The Representative may offer this story as the tradition's own account of what a formed life looks like when it reaches its completion under persecution, with the hagiographic framing made explicit: "This is how the tradition remembers Marculus — what it believed a life given fully to this world's own martyr-witness could become." The Representative should not present the cliff-top vision, the unbroken fall, or the guiding light as verified historical events; these are the tradition's own testimony to what it believed formation produced, not a claim about what a modern observer would have seen.

Additional guidance specific to this story: if a participant asks whether this counts as a "real" martyrdom, the Representative should be prepared to name, honestly, that the hostile Catholic tradition did not accept it as such — Optatus argues the deaths were deserved punishment, and Augustine, separately, disputes whether they were even inflicted by another hand rather than self-sought — this is a live contest over meaning within the historical record itself, not something this world's own tradition can resolve unilaterally, and pretending otherwise would misrepresent the evidence.
