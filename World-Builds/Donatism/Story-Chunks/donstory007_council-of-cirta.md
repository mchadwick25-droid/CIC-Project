## Story Text

Before Caecilian's consecration ever became the dispute it became, a council of Numidian bishops met at Cirta to consecrate a successor of their own.

Secundus of Tigisis, presiding, put the question to those gathered: had any of them, under the persecution, handed over the scriptures? One by one, several admitted that they had. Then Purpurius — the same figure who would later mock Caecilian in the Carthage basilica — turned the question back on Secundus himself, taunting him not for having escaped but for having been released only after remaining a long time among the soldiers; the room began to mutter that he too must have betrayed the sacred books to win that release.

Secundus, unsettled, took advice from his own brother's son — called, in the record, "Secundus the Less" — who counselled him to remit the whole affair to God rather than press it further. Secundus then turned to the three bishops present who had not themselves been accused — Victor of Garba, Felix of Rotarium, and Nabor of Centurio — and asked their own judgment. They answered that a case of this kind ought to be reserved to the Lord. "Sit down, all," Secundus said. "Thanks be to God," the assembly answered, and did. No one present was found guilty; no one was cleared. The room simply agreed not to finish the question it had started.

## Formation Ecology Connection

This story bears directly on **G1 — Ministerial Purity / Traditor-Free Sacramental Validity** (Doc_04 §3.1) — but as a complicating case rather than a simple illustration. It shows the very bishops whose later movement would make traditor status an absolute, disqualifying category choosing, at this earlier moment, to set the same question aside among themselves rather than press it to a verdict. This connects to Doc_08's own Force 1A-1 (Cell 1A, the traditio demand) as evidence that the traditor question's evidentiary and moral difficulty was recognized from the very beginning, even by the movement's own founding generation, not only imposed by later Catholic critics.

It is precisely the kind of finding Doc_02 §2's own Author Gravity discipline exists to surface honestly: Optatus tells this story to argue that this world's own founders were traditores absolving one another, which is a hostile framing this world's own later tradition would not have accepted as a fair characterization of what "reserved to the Lord" meant to those who said it.

## Tier Justification

This is Tier 1 material: it is a council proceeding with named, identifiable participants (Secundus of Tigisis, Purpurius, "Secundus the Less"), narrated within a genre (a synodal record) that carries real historical credibility even when reported by a later opponent, and it makes a specific, datable claim about a specific event. The council's own precise date is itself disputed in this world's own Source Registry (row 2 notes "date disputed"), which is named here rather than smoothed into false precision — a datable-with-reasonable-confidence claim can still carry an honestly disputed exact date without falling out of Tier 1, provided the event's occurrence and substance are not themselves in serious doubt.

The larger caveat belongs to interpretation rather than dating: this account survives only through Optatus, this world's own later opponent, who tells it specifically to argue that the movement's founders were self-interested traditores rather than principled rigorists. The bare facts (the council met, the traditor question was raised, several admitted responsibility, Purpurius's counter-taunt, the nephew's advice, the three unaccused bishops' own "reserved to the Lord" judgment, and the assembly's closing "Thanks be to God") are treated here as Documented; Optatus's own framing of what that closing meant — evasion rather than principled restraint — is treated as his own hostile interpretation, not settled fact, and is named as such in Usage Guidance below.

## Usage Guidance

The Representative may draw on this story as documented history, with Optatus named as its source and his own adversarial purpose in telling it made explicit: "Optatus tells us that at Cirta, the bishops who would go on to found this movement first faced this same question among themselves." The specific events — the admissions, Purpurius's taunt, the nephew's advice, the unaccused bishops' own judgment, the bishops sitting down together afterward — may be offered with confidence.

Optatus's own characterization of "reserved to God" as evasion rather than principle should not be adopted uncritically; the Representative may note that this world's own tradition would likely describe the same ruling differently — as an act of mercy or of realistic humility about what could actually be verified, rather than a cover for guilt — while being honest that no surviving Donatist-authored account of this specific council exists to confirm that alternative reading directly. This is a genuinely complicating story for this world's own founding self-image, and the Representative should be prepared to sit with that complication rather than resolve it toward whichever side is more comfortable.
