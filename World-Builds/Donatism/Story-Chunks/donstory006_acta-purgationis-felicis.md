## Story Text

The whole schism turned, at its root, on a single accusation: that Felix, the bishop of Aptungi who had consecrated Caecilian as bishop of Carthage, had himself been a traditor — that he had handed over the scriptures during the persecution, and so had no standing to consecrate anyone, which would make Caecilian's own office invalid from its very first day.

Constantine himself ordered the matter investigated. The proconsul Aelianus opened a formal inquiry and took testimony under record from named witnesses: Alfius Caecilianus, Maximus, Apronianus, Claudius Saturianus, Superius, and a notary named Ingentius. At the center of the case was a letter — supposedly written by Felix himself, and read aloud in the proceeding — that seemed to prove the accusation.

Under questioning, and under the threat of torture, Ingentius broke. He confessed that he himself had forged the letter, and that he had done so as an agent working for the Donatist party, moving through Numidia and Mauritania in that service. With the forgery exposed, the case against Felix collapsed. The proceeding records his vindication as complete — "finally and triumphantly," in the text's own words.

## Tier Justification

This is Tier 1 material of the strongest kind available in this world's corpus: a formal judicial proceeding, ordered by the emperor himself, conducted by a named Roman official, with named deponents giving testimony under record, resulting in a documented confession and a stated verdict. Every element of Tier 1's own definition — direct textual attestation, named participants with identifiable social locations, a precise datable proceeding, a historically credible claim — is satisfied at the fullest strength this corpus offers.

The one caveat Tier 1's own definition asks for — "appropriate caveats for the author's perspective and genre" — applies here in a specific way worth naming plainly: this proceeding is preserved because it was included in Optatus's own Appendix of Documents, and its outcome (Felix vindicated, a Donatist agent exposed as a forger) is unfavorable to this world's own founding accusation. This does not make the proceeding's own record less credible as a judicial document — a forged-letter confession under threat of torture is not the kind of detail a hostile compiler would need to invent, and its presence in the schism's own opponent's appendix argues, if anything, for its reliability rather than against it — but the Representative should be prepared to acknowledge, honestly, that this is a documented setback for this world's own founding claim, not evidence favorable to it.

## Usage Guidance

The Representative may draw on this story as documented history with high confidence: "An imperially-ordered inquiry, with named witnesses giving testimony under record, found that the letter accusing Felix had been forged — by an agent of this world's own party." This should be offered honestly as an unfavorable finding for the traditio accusation against Felix specifically, not minimized or reframed as a victory.

Additional guidance specific to this story: because this proceeding cuts against this world's own founding accusation rather than supporting it, the Representative should not avoid it when a participant asks directly about the strength of the case against Felix — Constitution-level honesty about historically documented setbacks is part of what this project understands as trustworthy formation representation, not a weakness to be managed around. The Representative may note, if helpful, that the broader traditio dispute involved many more figures and incidents across this world's founding decade than this one proceeding settles, and that this world's own founders held the underlying principle (that a traditor could not validly ordain) even where this specific case against Felix did not hold up.
