## Story Text

Macrobius wrote to his own congregation at Carthage as their bishop, in the aftermath of what he had witnessed, to tell them what had happened to two of their own.

Maximianus, a soldier of Christ in the tradition's own description — robust, unflinching — was chosen to face the Roman proconsul first. The night before, at a shared meal, wine in his cup seemed to take the shape of a ring, shining with the mingled colors of blood and light — an omen, Macrobius wrote, of what the next day would ask of him. He was beaten with leaded whips, then with rods. Isaac, whose very name the letter reads as a sign — a victim's name, given to a victim — was flogged alongside him, and in the middle of it he called out to the men doing the flogging, naming them *traditores* and telling them to come and see what their so-called unity's madness had brought them to. He did not survive the beating.

Maximianus, still living, had his own vision that night: he saw himself in combat, first against the emperor's own ministers, and then against the emperor himself, who in the dream struck out one of his eyes — and then a bright youth appeared and crowned him. The Roman proconsul, wanting neither of them venerated as relics afterward, ordered both bodies — Isaac's corpse and Maximianus, who by the letter's account was not yet dead — thrown into the sea, weighted down with jars filled with sand so they would not float or wash ashore.

The sea would not keep them. Macrobius's letter says the bodies rose and were driven back toward shore again and again, for six days, until finally both were delivered up whole to the congregation waiting for them, who took them and buried them with the same rites and the same joy they would have given the living.

Macrobius closes his letter by turning to his own congregation directly: what happened to Isaac and Maximianus, he tells them, is what may yet be asked of any of them.

## Tier Justification

Tier 1 is the correct classification, and it is worth stating explicitly why this account earns a different tier than the outwardly similar donstory002, since the skill governing this inventory specifically warns against tiering by genre label rather than evidentiary character. Tier 1 requires "direct textual attestation within or close to the world's horizon, named author with identifiable social location, datable with reasonable confidence" — every element is present here that was absent or contested for donstory001 and donstory002: Macrobius is named, he identifies himself as bishop to the specific congregation he is addressing, the letter is datable to 347-348 with the persecution it describes, and it is a real occasional letter to a real audience rather than a composition built from the outset for liturgical or hagiographic performance.

This does not mean the letter is free of interpretive framing. The wine-cup omen and Maximianus's combat vision are Macrobius's own reporting of what he understood these events to signify, and Tier 1's own definition explicitly allows for this: the narrative may "carry Widely Accepted or Contested confidence for specific details within the narrative depending on the author's access and perspective." The bare facts — that Isaac and Maximianus were tortured and killed, that their bodies were thrown into the sea to prevent veneration, that the community recovered and buried them — carry the story's own narrative-level confidence; the omen and the vision carry the author's own interpretive perspective on those facts, appropriately caveated rather than treated as reducing the whole account to Tier 3.

A further, source-level caveat: this letter survives only in raw, uncorrected Latin OCR (the vendored file's own header flags this), and no established published English translation of it was consulted for this chunk. Isaac's own cry to his tormentors is rendered above as indirect speech rather than a quoted translation for exactly this reason — the Latin itself ("*Traditores... venite, salvate vestram unitatis insaniam*") was checked directly against the source, but this builder is not a certified translator, and presenting an improvised rendering in quotation marks as though it were a verified verbatim quotation would overstate what this document can responsibly claim.

## Usage Guidance

The Representative may draw on this story as remembered history, with Macrobius named as its author and his own position (bishop, writing to his own flock, in the immediate aftermath) made explicit: "Macrobius, writing to his own congregation at Carthage, tells us that..."
