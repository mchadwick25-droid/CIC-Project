## Quick Meaning

Baptism given outside the one true church is no baptism at all, so anyone received from the rival communion must be baptized again — not a repetition of something already valid, but the first real baptism that person has ever received.

---

## World Meaning

We do not think of this as baptizing someone twice. From where we stand, nothing happened the first time. A rite performed by a minister descended from a tainted line, inside a communion that has made its peace with that taint, does not confer what baptism is supposed to confer — so when someone comes to us from the other church, we are not repeating a sacrament; we are giving the first one. This is the same logic that makes *traditor* the schism's own founding wound: if the minister's own hand cannot be trusted, nothing that flows through it can be trusted either, and baptism is the sharpest place that trust either holds or fails, because baptism is what makes a person part of the church at all.

This is why rebaptism is not a private theological opinion held by a few rigorists among us. It is the single most concretely enacted, individually experienced act of belonging this world has. A believer does not merely agree with our teaching about *traditor*-tainted ordination in the abstract; a believer is washed, bodily, into this communion and out of the other one, and that washing is the whole argument made real in one person's own life. Every rebaptism performed is this world's own doctrine, lived rather than merely stated.

We know what this costs us in the eyes of the empire and of our rival. Successive imperial edicts name this practice specifically, because rebaptizing a Catholic is, to the state that favors our rival, the plainest possible sign that we do not accept the settlement it has tried to impose. We accept that cost. To stop rebaptizing would be to concede that the other church's own baptisms are real after all — and if theirs are real, ours were never necessary, and the whole reason this church exists collapses with it.

---

## Ecological Function

Rebaptism is the enacted rite of the purity doctrine (*Traditor*/Purity) — the concrete threshold a person crosses, not merely the belief a person holds. It is what makes membership in this world a specific, datable, individually experienced act rather than an inherited label. It is also the single most direct target of imperial legislation against this world, and the specific practice whose one recorded exception — the Maximianist clergy received back without repeating either rite — is this world's own sharpest internal tension (Reception without Reordination), tested against the same doctrine rebaptism otherwise enacts without qualification.

---

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
A strange or excessive ritual scruple — repeating a sacrament that, by most Christian traditions' own later consensus, only needs to happen once, suggesting a movement preoccupied with technicalities.

**World Hearing:**
Not a repetition at all, but the first true baptism — the correction of an act that, whatever it looked like, conferred nothing the first time, because it came from a hand this world does not recognize as capable of giving it. To decline rebaptism would be to accept that the rival church's own ministers can validly baptize regardless of their own line's own purity — which is precisely the claim this world was founded to refuse.

## Key Sources

Augustine of Hippo, *On Baptism, Against the Donatists* (7 books) and *Answer to the Letters of Petilian* (3 books) — both devoted substantially to this practice, quoting Petilian of Constantina's own argument for it clause by clause. Petilian's own quoted proposition, independently verified against `npnf104_augustine-anti-manichaean-anti-donatist.xml`: "What we look for is the conscience of the giver, to cleanse that of the recipient" — a direct Donatist theological voice arguing for the practice, not merely characterized by an opponent.

Note: Augustine dominates the surviving evidence for this practice's own specific argumentative texture, but the bare fact of the practice, and Petilian's own argument for it, are attested directly and repeatedly in Augustine's primary text rather than merely summarized by him (Doc_04 §3.2).
