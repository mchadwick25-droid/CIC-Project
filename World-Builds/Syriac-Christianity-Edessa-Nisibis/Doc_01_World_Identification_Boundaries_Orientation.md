## 1. Distinct World Criteria

**What recurring formation ecology exists?** A hymnic, symbolic, and exegetical mode of Christian formation, carried in Syriac (a dialect of Aramaic), organized institutionally around a pre-monastic covenantal ascetic order (the bnay qyama / bnat qyama, "sons/daughters of the covenant") rather than around emerging monarchical episcopal office as the primary formation structure, and oriented scripturally around a harmonized single-narrative Gospel text (the Diatessaron) rather than the four discrete Gospels of Greek-speaking Christianity.

**What recurring gravities exist?** (Provisional — full classification is Doc_04's job, but the candidates visible even at this orientation stage are:) poetic/typological theological method as the primary vehicle of doctrinal argument (most visible in Ephrem, who argues theology through hymns rather than treatises); the covenant-order (qyama) as an organizing ascetic-liturgical institution; direct engagement with a religiously plural, contested milieu (Marcionite Christianity, Bardaisan's following, later Manichaeism) as a constitutive condition of the tradition's own self-definition, not background noise.

**What distinguishes this world from adjacent worlds?** Most sharply from World #1 (Post-Apostolic/Sub-Apostolic House-Church Christianity): different language (Aramaic/Syriac vs. Greek), different expressive genre (hymnic-symbolic poetry vs. community letters and emerging conciliar-adjacent office), different authority structure (the qyama covenant order vs. plural presbyters), and a materially different — though not simply "outside Roman" — political frame (see Section 3). See Section 6 for the full continuity-and-distinction argument.

## 2. Temporal Scope

**Beginning point.** The Step 0 Conclusion's working label ("c. 2nd–4th century") is retained, but the actual beginning point requires more precision than the traditional foundation legend supplies. The *Doctrina Addai* / Abgar-correspondence tradition (King Abgar V allegedly corresponding with Jesus and being converted by the apostle Addai in the mid-1st century) is not treated as a historical beginning point. It is first attested by Eusebius in the early 4th century, drawing on claimed Edessene archives, and is developed further in the *Doctrina Addai* itself — a text whose own scholarly function is understood as retrojecting apostolic-era origins onto a community that in fact Christianized considerably later. There is no contemporary 1st- or 2nd-century corroboration (Abgar V's own coinage remains pagan in iconography). — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** that the legend is a later apostolic-foundation claim, not history (Drijvers; the Abgar-legend literature generally).

The defensible beginning point for actually attested Christian community life in Edessa is the turn of the 2nd/3rd century: a church building is attested at Edessa by 201 CE (recorded as destroyed in that year's flood, per the *Chronicle of Edessa*), and Bardaisan (154–222), a prominent figure moving in Christian-adjacent circles at the court of Abgar VIII "the Great" (r. 177–212), is the earliest individually attested figure from this milieu. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.** This document's working beginning point is therefore **c. 200 CE** (with Bardaisan's own career, from the 170s–180s onward, as the earliest individually attested activity in the world's own religiously plural milieu, addressed as boundary-adjacent rather than as the world's own starting figure — see Section 5).

**Ending point — revised in Round 2.** Three candidate markers were evaluated:

- **373 CE — death of Ephrem the Syrian.** The last major individual voice shaped by the pre-institutional, literary-hymnic formation logic this world is built around. Round 1 set the world's end point here, but on review this is a biographical marker, not itself evidence that the formation ecology changed structurally on that date — nothing about Syriac Christianity's own formation logic shifts the day Ephrem dies. It is retained below as a significant internal hinge, not the boundary.
- **410 CE — Synod of Seleucia-Ctesiphon.** Formally organized Persian Christianity into the structured Church of the East under Catholicos Isaac, with metropolitan provinces and Sasanian-recognized episcopal order. This is the actual point of institutional-structural change — the criterion this document itself treats as decisive for a "genuine transition" everywhere else it applies that test (see the Nisibis-school discussion below). It is also the marker the Step 0 Conclusion itself invokes when distinguishing World #7 from the deferred Persian Church of the East candidate ("distinct from the 410-Synod institutional church").
- **424 CE — Synod of Dadisho/Markabta.** The point at which the Church of the East declared full ecclesiastical independence from Antioch — arguably the truer independence marker, with 410 as a way station. Not adopted as this world's own boundary: this document's own criterion is institutional-structural change, and the qualitative break is the 410 Synod's act of organizing a previously unformalized (if internally contested — see Section 6) set of Mesopotamian and Persian bishoprics into a single metropolitan structure with a recognized head. 424's further declaration of independence from Antioch is a political-jurisdictional refinement of an already-institutionalized church, not the formation-logic-level shift itself; it belongs to the successor world's own history, not to marking where that successor world begins.

**Working decision (revised):** this document sets the world's own working end point at **410 CE (Synod of Seleucia-Ctesiphon)**, not 373. This removes the circularity a reviewer correctly identified in Round 1 (justifying 373 mainly by appeal to matching Step 0's own prior framing rather than independent evidence of a structural shift) and instead applies this document's own stated criterion — institutional-structural change — consistently. Ephrem's death in 373 is retained as a significant internal marker: the last major individually-attested literary-hymnic voice, occurring within this world's own final, still-pre-institutional phase, but not itself the boundary.

**What the 373–410 expansion actually adds to this world's scope.** Extending the boundary from 373 to 410 is not merely a label change; it brings roughly 37 years of continued, less individually-documented community life (the relocated Edessa school, the qyama-organized community) inside this world's own window, and it brings at least one additional named figure into scope who Round 1/2 did not account for: **Marutha of Maiperqat**, the Roman-side bishop who brokered and helped convene the 410 Synod itself, acting as an intermediary between the Roman and Persian churches. His role is itself a data point for this world's own Roman/Persian frontier character (Section 3) and should be developed at Doc_02, not merely gestured at here. This document does not claim to have fully inventoried the 373–410 period's content — that is Doc_02's job — but flags explicitly that the period is not empty, and that failing to inventory it would leave the temporal-scope expansion under-supported. — **Confidence: Dominant Modern Reconstruction** (a defensible periodization choice, consistently argued; not a claim of scholarly unanimity on where exactly "this world" ends).

**Internal developments vs. transition.** The Nisibis school history illustrates the difference. Jacob of Nisibis (bishop from c. 309, signatory at Nicaea in 325, traditionally Ephrem's own mentor and baptizer, died during a Persian siege — c. 338 per the most commonly cited tradition, though c. 350 is also found in the literature and should not be treated as settled) anchors a Roman-Nisibene phase of this world's own formation. The 363 cession of Nisibis to Persia and the associated relocation of Ephrem and other teachers to Edessa is treated as an internal transition (same tradition, same personnel, new political jurisdiction) rather than a world-ending change, since the formation logic (hymnic-exegetical, qyama-organized) continues unbroken across it — though this document notes explicitly that a full state-jurisdiction change accompanied by population evacuation is a real discontinuity worth weighing carefully, not a trivial one, and flags it for reconsideration if Doc_02's fuller evidence suggests the political rupture mattered more to lived formation than currently assessed. By contrast, the 410 Synod's creation of a metropolitan episcopal structure under explicit Sasanian state recognition is treated here as the genuine transition out of this world's own formation logic. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the events themselves; **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for the internal/transition classification.

**Flag for Doc_02:** exact founding date for the School of Nisibis (commonly cited as 350) and Jacob of Nisibis's exact death year (338 vs. 350, both attested) rest partly on later hagiographic tradition rather than contemporary documentation and should receive a closer primary-source pass (Theodoret's *Historia Religiosa*, the Julian-era Nisibene Hymns) before either date is treated as fixed. **The School of Nisibis founding-date half of this flag is now resolved — see Section 10's retroactive resolution note below; 350 is not this institution's founding date. Jacob of Nisibis's death-date question remains open as originally flagged here.**

## 3. Geographic Scope

**Core geography:** Edessa (Urhoy), capital of the former client kingdom of Osroene, and Nisibis, a fortress-capital on the Roman-Persian frontier — both in upper Mesopotamia, in the Aramaic/Syriac-speaking zone.

**Political status — Edessa.** Osroene was a Roman client/vassal kingdom under the Abgarid dynasty from c. 132 BCE, functioning as a buffer state through the 1st and early 3rd centuries, with intermittent direct Roman intervention (Trajan's and Lucius Verus's campaigns). Annexation was staged rather than a single event: Abgar IX was summoned to Rome by Caracalla around 213, where most secondary sources report he and his son were executed rather than merely detained, with Osroene's formal provincialization following in 214 CE and Edessa elevated to *colonia* status; nominal Abgarid kingship was briefly restored under Gordian III (c. 242 CE) before final absorption. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted**, with the exact sequencing of the 213/214 events varying across sources and treated here as two distinguishable steps (summons/execution, then formal annexation) rather than one event.

**Political status — Nisibis.** Captured by Trajan in 115 CE, contested repeatedly through the 3rd-century Roman-Persian wars, fixed as a Roman fortress-capital by the Peace of Nisibis (298/299) after Galerius's victory over Narses, and held by Rome until the **363 CE Perso-Roman peace settlement** (this document uses the standard scholarly label, the Peace Treaty of 363, rather than "Treaty of Dura" — an occasional alternate label whose prevalence in the literature this document could not confirm, and which risks confusion with the much earlier, unrelated site of Dura-Europos) — negotiated by Jovian after Julian's death during the retreat from his failed Persian campaign — which ceded Nisibis (along with Singara and other trans-Tigritane territory) to Shapur II, with the Christian population evacuating to Roman territory. — **Confidence: Documented.**

**Critical boundary correction to the Step 0 Conclusion's working characterization.** The Step 0 Conclusion describes this world as formation "outside the Roman imperial frame." Research conducted for this document does not support that as a precise characterization for Edessa, and this document revises it accordingly — while naming clearly where the revision does and does not extend. For nearly the entire period usually cited as this world's formative core — Bardaisan (late 2nd–early 3rd century), the *Doctrina Addai* legend's own composition, and Ephrem's Edessa years (363–373) — Edessa was **inside** the Roman provincial system, not outside it (post-214 CE). Fergus Millar's *The Roman Near East, 31 BC–AD 337* treats Osroene/Edessa explicitly as part of the Roman East's provincial fabric, and Sidney Griffith's own framing of Edessa as a "frontier of contested allegiance... on the Aramean frontier" (*Journal of the Canadian Society for Syriac Studies* 2, 2002, 5–20 — a directly on-point source this document initially left under-verified and has since confirmed) situates Edessa's Christianity as a struggle for allegiance occurring within, not outside, an evolving imperial-religious landscape. The historically precise characterization for Edessa specifically is: **a Roman-administered but linguistically and culturally distinct periphery.**

This revision does **not**, however, produce one clean label for the whole world. It holds for Edessa across nearly this entire period. It holds for Nisibis only from 298 (Peace of Nisibis) through 363 — before 298 Nisibis's status was genuinely unstable and contested, and after 363 it is factually outside Roman control altogether, the opposite of the revised characterization. And it does not apply to Aphrahat's Persian-Empire context at all (see below). The Step 0 Conclusion's phrase and this document's replacement phrase are both, in their own way, oversimplifications of a genuinely three-part picture (Roman Edessa throughout; Roman-then-Persian Nisibis; Persian Aphrahat throughout) — this document names that complexity explicitly rather than trading one clean label for another. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the political facts; **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** that "Roman-administered periphery" is the more precise characterization for Edessa specifically, with the limits of that characterization's reach stated as part of the finding, not left implicit.

**Aphrahat and the question of a two-empire geographic scope.** Aphrahat wrote his *Demonstrations* from within the Sasanian Persian Empire, traditionally associated with the Adiabene region near Mosul — a distinct polity from Roman Osroene, under Parthian and then Sasanian rule from 224 CE. Grouping Aphrahat with Ephrem and Edessene material in one formation world is defensible on **linguistic-cultural** grounds (shared Syriac idiom, overlapping ascetic institutions including the qyama, a shared exegetical mode) but is **not** defensible on grounds of shared polity. This document's working position: **this world's geographic scope is the Syriac-speaking Christian ecology across the Roman-Persian Mesopotamian frontier zone**, not "Edessa" narrowly construed — with an explicit, carried-forward caveat that Ephrem and Aphrahat sit under materially different political jurisdictions and, as Section 6 discusses, clearly different intensities of state pressure. Whether they also sit under genuinely different authority structures is an open question this document does not resolve in either direction — see Section 6, which explains why the strand-singular finding does not depend on resolving it. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the political facts; **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for the geographic-scope decision itself.

## 4. Cultural Scope

**Language and scripture.** Syriac, a literary dialect of Aramaic. The dominant Gospel text throughout this world's core period was not the four discrete Gospels but Tatian's **Diatessaron** (a single continuous-narrative Gospel harmony, composed c. 172 CE), which functioned as the standard lectionary text in Syriac-speaking churches until displaced by the Peshitta in the early 5th century — traditionally credited to Bishop Rabbula of Edessa (411–435), the same bishop who separately, and within the same tenure, moved against the residual Bardaisanite and Marcionite communities in Edessa (see Section 5.5) — a genuine two-century span in which the normative devotional experience of "the Gospel" for this world was narrative unity, not four-witness plurality. (William L. Petersen's *Tatian's Diatessaron: Its Creation, Dissemination, Significance, and History in Scholarship*, Brill, 1994, is the standard critical treatment.) The Syriac Diatessaron itself does not survive except in fragments and citations. Rabbula's personal causal role in the displacement is the traditional attribution but is not uncontested: Arthur Vööbus has argued the Peshitta's own composition predates Rabbula's episcopate, which would make him a promoter rather than the originator of the shift. This document treats the traditional attribution as the working assumption, flagged as contested rather than settled. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the Diatessaron-to-Peshitta timeline; **Contested** for Rabbula's specific personal causal role; the interpretive claim that the Diatessaron's centrality shaped a distinctive narrative-unity orientation to scripture is this document's own synthesis — **Confidence: Dominant Modern Reconstruction.**

**Theological method.** Syriac theological expression in this period is poetic, exegetical, and symbolic rather than the scholastic/philosophical-categorical mode of Greek and Latin patristic writing — most visible in Ephrem, whose primary vehicle of theological argument is hymnody (madrashe) rather than treatise. This characterization is broadly supported in the Syriac-studies literature (Sebastian Brock's work is the standard reference point), though a precise, quotable citation was not independently verified in this research pass and should be treated as a well-supported synthetic characterization pending direct citation-check at Doc_02. — **Confidence: Dominant Modern Reconstruction.**

**Institutional/social scope.** The bnay qyama and bnat qyama ("sons/daughters of the covenant") — celibate ascetics, male and female, bound by a vowed "covenant" (qyama), living communally or semi-communally, serving liturgical functions (including, per Ephrem's own corpus, performing his hymns as women's choirs) — are first clearly attested in the 4th century and are a genuinely distinctive Syriac institution predating and differing from Egyptian desert monasticism. Sidney Griffith and Robert Murray (*Symbols of Church and Kingdom*, Cambridge, 1975) are the standard cited authorities; recent scholarship continues actively revisiting the institution's precise internal structure, including Malatius Malki Malki's 2024 article "Revisiting the Institution of Bnay and Bnoth Qyōmo in the Syriac Tradition" (*Religions* 15(6): 686) — corrected in Round 3 from a Round 2 citation that misattributed this article's author, exact title, year, and volume. This document flags explicitly that the institution's **formal organizational structure** (rule, enclosure, hierarchy) is thinly and contestedly documented and should not be presented as settled — only its existence, its vowed-celibacy/communal-liturgical character, and its 4th-century attestation are solidly evidenced. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for existence and basic character; **Contested/Inferential-Thin** for internal structural detail.

## 5. Required Section A Task: Bardaisan of Edessa and the Nicene-Trajectory Eligibility Floor

Per the Step 0 Conclusion's explicit, non-generic requirement: this world's own Step 1/2 work must show, not assume, that its founding sources clear the Nicene-trajectory eligibility floor (Constitution Article 4, A2 continuity test), with Bardaisan of Edessa as the specific test case. This section discharges that requirement.

**5.1 The source problem.** Bardaisan (c. 154–222 CE) left almost nothing indisputably in his own hand. The *Book of the Laws of Countries* — the closest thing to a Bardaisanite primary text — is a Syriac dialogue in which Bardaisan is the principal speaker but which was recorded by his student Philip; everything in it is therefore mediated at one remove. Eusebius (*Praeparatio Evangelica* 6.10; *Historia Ecclesiastica* 4.30), writing decades later but well before Ephrem and with no local Edessene rivalry to prosecute, is the most comparatively reliable ancient witness, confirming the work's title and anti-fatalist purpose. Julius Africanus independently corroborates Bardaisan's standing as a courtier at Abgar VIII's court (195 CE) but says nothing of his theology. The hostile record is dominant and much later: Ephrem (d. 373, writing roughly 150 years after Bardaisan's death) lumps Bardaisan with Marcion and Mani as a rhetorical triad rather than offering individualized doctrinal analysis; Epiphanius (*Panarion* 56, c. 374–377) reports a cosmology of God/Darkness/four elements and attributes to Bardaisan the view that bodily resurrection is "absurd blasphemy" — but Epiphanius's information is explicitly filtered through later followers' writings, and Epiphanius and Eusebius directly contradict each other on which direction Bardaisan's theology moved. Modern methodological scholarship on heresiological genre generally (Alain Le Boulluec's account of how the very category of "heresy" was constructed in this period; Walter Bauer's thesis, applied specifically to Edessa) treats this kind of hostile, decades-or-centuries-later characterization with structural suspicion. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** that Bardaisan's own theology is known to us only through mediated and often hostile sources, with the two earliest heresiological witnesses disagreeing on basic trajectory.

**5.2 Astrological/fate material.** Verified: the *Book of the Laws of Countries* argues **against** strict astrological determinism, not for it. Its scheme is tripartite — Nature (universal human traits, created by God), Fate (planetary/astrological influence, real but not absolute), and Free Will (a God-given faculty that neither nature nor fate can fully compel) — argued ethnographically: peoples under similar horoscopes follow starkly different customs, which the text treats as proof that the stars do not finally determine behavior, culminating in the claim that Christians, dispersed under every possible horoscope, nonetheless observe one shared law. Recent scholarship (Ilaria Ramelli; Izabela Jurasz) situates this as a serious contribution to the era's broader anti-determinist current, structurally comparable to the pagan philosophical case against Stoic determinism and to Origen's roughly contemporary Christian free-will arguments. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**

**5.3 Christology / the docetism charge.** The evidence for a hostile gloss here is unusually strong. The clearest ancient evidence for docetism in this milieu belongs not to Bardaisan but to **Marinus**, a named 4th-century Bardaisanite refuted in the *Dialogue of Adamantius*, who explicitly denied Christ was born of a woman — a harder dualism than anything securely attributable to Bardaisan himself. No text in Bardaisan's own voice addresses Christ's nature at all. H.J.W. Drijvers's foundational monograph (*Bardaiṣan of Edessa*, 1966) argued the "Gnostic" label was a later catch-all applied to Bardaisan by heresiologists rather than a precise description, and that his system lacks the structural hallmark of classical Gnosticism (an ignorant, hostile Demiurge opposed to a higher unknown God). Ute Possekel ("Bardaisan of Edessa: Philosopher or Theologian?", *Zeitschrift für Antikes Christentum* 10.3, 2006) substantially engages and refines rather than overturns this, concluding it is "highly doubtful" Bardaisan considered Darkness an evil entity opposed to God and that he "firmly rejects dualist notions." Ilaria Ramelli's *Bardaisan of Edessa: A Reassessment of the Evidence and a New Interpretation* (Gorgias, 2009) extends this line. Not all scholarship agrees — the Encyclopaedia Iranica entry (Prods Oktor Skjærvø, 1988) situates Bardesanes closer to Syriac-Gnostic and Manichaean systems — so the rehabilitation is not universal, but it is the better-evidenced position specifically on docetism. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** that the docetism charge specifically does not stick to Bardaisan himself, as distinct from his later school.

**5.4 Resurrection of the body — the genuinely unfavorable finding.** This is the one place where the evidence does not lean toward rehabilitation. Even Possekel's more sympathetic reading treats Bardaisan's primordial elements (light, wind, fire, water, darkness) as **pre-existent rather than created ex nihilo** by God — a real, substantive divergence from "maker of all things visible and invisible," independent of the separate question of whether Darkness is an evil rival principle. More directly: Possekel's own scholarly reconstruction concludes that Bardaisan's cosmology of an accidental primordial mixture plausibly entails, and that Bardaisan argued exegetically for, a doctrine of the soul's purification **apart from** bodily resurrection. This is a modern, non-hostile scholar's own conclusion, even though the specific exegetical fragments it rests on survive only embedded within Ephrem's refutation. Eusebius's own testimony, independent of Ephrem, also places Bardaisan inside the Valentinian orbit at some stage of his life. — **Confidence: Contested**, but leaning toward non-clearance on this specific clause.

**5.5 Relationship to organized Christianity — revised in Round 2 to engage Ramelli's positive-evidence argument directly.** Round 1 stated that Bardaisan's baptism and ordination "could not be corroborated against a primary source." This understated the record. Ramelli's own 2009 reassessment — already cited above for the docetism question — argues, drawing on Didymus the Blind and Julius Africanus and situating Bardaisan within the wider Origenian tradition, that the most favorable ancient sources on Bardaisan present him as a deacon or even presbyter who remained within the church until his death, and even, in some readings, as a confessor of the faith during persecution; Ramelli further notes that the most favorable sources on Bardaisan are consistently Origenian in provenance, and that none of them characterizes him as a heretic. This is a real, citable, positive-evidence case for institutional standing, and it should have been engaged in Round 1 rather than passed over. It is engaged directly here: **ecclesial standing and doctrinal-content clearance are separate questions under the Constitution's own A2 test.** The A2 test asks whether a figure's theology, followed to its own conclusion, lands within Nicene-Constantinopolitan content — not whether the figure held clerical office or died in communion with a church. A Bardaisan who was genuinely a presbyter in good standing, and even a confessor under persecution, can still fail the A2 test on a specific doctrinal clause if the best reconstruction of his own theology diverges there — the two facts are not in tension and do not cancel each other out. What this correction does change is the picture of Bardaisan's overall standing: he should not be described, as Round 1's phrasing risked implying, as merely "a religiously engaged court intellectual with an unclear relationship to the church." The more accurate summary is: a figure with a real, positively-attested claim to institutional Christian standing (per the Origenian-sourced tradition Ramelli surveys), whose specific theological content nonetheless appears, on independent modern reconstruction, to diverge from the Creed on one specific clause. Separately, "the church" as a single bounded institution was in any case not yet fully consolidated in Edessa during Bardaisan's lifetime (154–222) — the milieu included paganism, Judaism, Marcionite Christianity, Bardaisan's own following, and what only later consolidated as "Palutian" proto-orthodoxy, an identity substantially constructed retrospectively through the *Doctrina Addai*'s own apostolic-succession legend (Section 2). Bardaisanism persisted as a distinct religious faction in Edessa for roughly two centuries after Bardaisan's death; Bishop Rabbula (411–435) actively moved against it, though Bardaisanite adherents are still attested encountering Jacob of Edessa in the 7th–8th century, indicating the suppression was real but not total. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** that a positive, Origenian-sourced tradition presents Bardaisan as institutionally Christian in standing; **Contested** whether that standing is best read as full ecclesial incorporation given the milieu's own institutional immaturity in his lifetime; **Widely Accepted** that Rabbula's actions against Bardaisanism were real but not fully successful.

**5.6 Determination.** Weighing 5.1–5.5 together: Bardaisan is substantially less "Gnostic" and closer to orthodox on creation, fate, and free will than the heresiological tradition suggests; the docetism charge specifically does not hold against him personally; and — per the Round 2 correction — there is a real, positively-attested tradition of his institutional Christian standing that should not be minimized. None of this, however, changes the resurrection-of-the-body finding: tested via a modern, non-Ephrem-dependent scholarly reconstruction (Possekel), the best current scholarship leans toward a genuine, non-trivial divergence from Nicene-Constantinopolitan content on that specific clause, independent of his ecclesial standing. Applying the Constitution's A2 continuity test honestly — theology, not institutional membership, followed to its own conclusion — this does not confidently land within that content on this specific point, at **Contested** confidence.

**This determination directly shapes World #7's own boundary, and is treated as decided in this document rather than deferred:** Bardaisan is **not** included in this world's founding-source set as a floor-cleared, native theological voice — a determination that holds whether or not he was institutionally Christian in standing, since the A2 test is about theological content, not membership. He is instead named as a **documented, contemporary, contested figure within this world's own milieu whom its actual primary voice explicitly defines against** — structurally the same treatment the Step 0 Conclusion requires for Marcion, Valentinian Christianity, and Montanism relative to World #1. This is in fact the historically accurate relationship: Ephrem's own literary and hymnic program is constituted substantially *by* refuting Bardaisan (alongside Marcion and Mani), using hymnody deliberately because Bardaisan had already made hymnody the register of popular theological persuasion in Edessa. Bardaisan therefore belongs in this world's Doc_02 Source Ecology as a named boundary case / contested-neighbor entry (Boundary Status: Excluded; Exclusion Reason: Named Comparandum), not as a Native founding source whose theology the Representative may draw on as this world's own voice. This resolves the required Section A task at the boundary-setting stage, now having engaged the strongest available counter-evidence rather than proceeding around it.

## 6. Strand Determination (Constitution Article 21)

**Question:** does the evidence support distinct internal strands within World #7, or does the world present as strand-singular?

**Candidate strand split considered:** an "Osroene/Roman-frontier strand" (Bardaisan-adjacent milieu, Ephrem, Edessa) versus a "Persian/Adiabene strand" (Aphrahat, Sasanian jurisdiction, state persecution under Shapur II).

**Finding: strand-singular — reasoning rebuilt in Round 3 on a more honest evidentiary footing.** Round 1 argued strand-singular partly on the grounds that the authority-structure difference between Ephrem's and Aphrahat's contexts was attested through only a single comparison. Round 2 attempted to correct this by claiming Persia had a stable, parallel episcopal hierarchy (Papa bar Aggai, then Simeon bar Sabbae) running alongside Aphrahat, concluding his own non-episcopal role reflected personal vocation rather than systemic divergence. An independent reviewer found this correction itself overstated: Aphrahat's non-episcopal status is not a settled fact in the secondary literature (William Wright and others have argued from Demonstration 14 — a circular letter Aphrahat drafted on behalf of a synod of bishops and clergy — that he may himself have been a bishop), and Papa bar Aggai's own claimed primacy over other Persian bishops was itself fiercely contested in his own lifetime by Miles of Susa and by Aqib-Alaha, bishop of Karka d'Baith Slok, at a synod c. 315 — meaning "Persia had its own settled episcopal hierarchy, symmetrical to Rome's" overstates how uncontested that hierarchy actually was in exactly this period. Round 2's specific evidentiary chain does not hold up as confidently as it claimed.

**The honest position, on the evidence actually available:** whether Aphrahat's own leadership role was episcopal or not is itself unresolved in the scholarship, and whether Persia's episcopal hierarchy in this period functioned as a stable, uncontested parallel to Rome's is also unresolved (it was, at minimum, actively contested internally within living memory of Aphrahat's own career). Unresolved, contested evidence cannot establish a strand-distinguishing authority-structure difference — Article 21 requires evidence that establishes a strand as "distinct rather than merely variant," and genuine scholarly uncertainty in both directions does not meet that bar, whichever way the uncertainty is eventually resolved. This is a materially different, and more defensible, basis for strand-singular than either Round 1's "insufficient sample size" framing or Round 2's "corrected, parallel-hierarchy" framing: it rests on naming the uncertainty honestly rather than resolving it prematurely in either direction. What is not in question is that formation emphasis, practice, scriptural tradition (the Diatessaron-era text), and the qyama institution are shared across Ephrem's and Aphrahat's contexts — that continuity is solidly evidenced and does the actual work of the strand-singular finding, independent of how the authority-structure question eventually resolves.

The genuinely well-documented systemic difference that remains is **external pressure intensity**: Simeon bar Sabbae's own martyrdom, the "Great Massacre" of Persian Christians (c. 344–345), and the broader Shapur II persecution are all independently attested well beyond Aphrahat's own text (and well beyond the contested Papa bar Aggai material), and represent a real, systemic difference in state pressure between the Roman and Persian sides of this world in this period. This is correctly a Forces question, not a Strand question — Article 21 strands are about formation emphasis, practice, authority structure, or ecological orientation actually differing, and external force intensity is a distinct analytical category the Framework itself assigns to Preliminary Forces Identification (Section 7, below) and to the full Forces compilation at Doc_08, not to Strand Determination.

**Disposition:** this world is treated as **strand-singular**, on the strength of clearly-evidenced continuity in formation emphasis, practice, and scriptural/institutional tradition — not on a claimed (and, on scrutiny, unsupportable) resolution of the authority-structure question in either direction. The Roman/Persian persecution-intensity asymmetry is named explicitly and carried into Section 7 and flagged for Doc_08. The authority-structure question itself (was Aphrahat a bishop; how contested was the Persian episcopal hierarchy) is named as a genuinely open item, not resolved, and carried to Doc_02 for fuller investigation — this document does not need to resolve it to support strand-singular, since the finding rests on the evidence that is solid, not the evidence that remains contested. — **Confidence: Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for the strand-singular disposition; **Contested/Inferential-Thin**, explicitly, for the authority-structure question itself, which should not be cited elsewhere in this document (or in later documents) as settled in either direction.

## 7. Preliminary Forces Identification

**What generated this world?** The spread of Christianity into Aramaic-speaking upper Mesopotamia via trade-route contact and a religiously plural local milieu (paganism, a significant Jewish community, and later Marcionite and Bardaisanite currents), taking root as a genuinely non-Greek theological and literary expression of the faith rather than a provincial extension of Greek patristic Christianity.

**What larger world was it embedded in?** The contested Roman-Persian Mesopotamian frontier zone; the broader pre-Christian Aramaic literary-wisdom tradition (e.g., Ahiqar); and a locally plural religious ecology in Edessa specifically that included paganism, Judaism, Marcionism, and Bardaisan's own following before any single "orthodox" identity had institutionally consolidated.

**What was it responding to?** Rival claimants to authentic Christian teaching circulating in the same Aramaic-speaking milieu and contested by this world's own primary voice directly: Marcion's rejection of the God of the Old Testament as maker of all things; Bardaisan's Nature/Fate/Free-Will cosmology and his popular hymnic mode of persuasion (Section 5); and, later, Mani's fully independent universal religion, which itself emerged from a Jewish-Christian baptismal sect in nearby Mesopotamia and spread through this same zone in the 3rd century. Ephrem's own corpus explicitly triangulates against exactly this Marcion/Bardaisan/Mani triad.

**What was it refusing?** Greek philosophical-categorical method as the necessary vehicle for theological legitimacy, in favor of a Semitic poetic-symbolic mode; and full assimilation into the Greek-language imperial-ecclesiastical apparatus centered at Antioch and Constantinople, in favor of a vernacular Aramaic register and locally rooted institutions (the qyama).

**Carried forward from Section 6:** the differential intensity of state pressure between Roman Osroene and Sasanian Persia — most sharply the Shapur II persecution and Simeon bar Sabbae's martyrdom — is a real, independently-documented systemic force distinguishing this world's Persian-side experience from its Roman-side experience, properly a matter for the full Forces compilation at Doc_08, not for Strand Determination.

This preliminary forces work frames Doc_02's scope: sources should be sought that illuminate this world's actual engagement with Marcion, Bardaisan, and Mani as real contemporaries, the differential persecution experience across the Roman/Persian frontier, and the silences around non-elite, non-literate qyama members' own voices, treated as a meaningful evidentiary gap to name, not fill by invention. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the documented Marcion/Bardaisan/Mani triangulation and the Shapur II persecution; **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for the broader framing of what the world was "refusing" structurally.

## 8. World Continuity & Distinction

**What does this world inherit from prior worlds?** A claimed (not demonstrated) apostolic-foundation narrative (the Addai/Abgar legend), structurally similar in rhetorical function to Rome's Peter-Paul claim or Alexandria's Mark claim. Shared scripture and an inherited concern (visible already in Bardaisan's own generation) with defining authentic Christian teaching against Marcionite currents also actively contested by World #1's own milieu.

**What does it transmit to subsequent worlds?** To the successor formation this world's own end point (410 CE) opens onto — the institutionally organized Church of the East: Ephrem's hymnic corpus and exegetical method (carried into and remaining authoritative within the later East Syriac tradition), the Nisibis/Edessa school lineage (which relocates again, after this world's own end point, back to Persian-controlled Nisibis following Edessa's school's 489 closure), and the bnay/bnat qyama institution as a forerunner within the wider Syriac ascetic tradition.

**Where does continuity mask genuine ecological change?** The name "School of Nisibis" itself risks masking a real rupture: a pre-363 Roman-Nisibene institution under Jacob, a post-363 relocated Edessene institution carrying the same personnel and tradition into Roman Edessa, and (beyond this world's own end point) a post-489 institution under Persian control reusing the name after Edessa's school was closed. These are three related but distinct institutional moments sharing a lineage-claim, not one continuous institution.

**Where does apparent discontinuity mask deeper structural continuity?** The 363 Roman-to-Persian cession of Nisibis looks like a sharp political rupture, but Ephrem's own personal continuity and the shared Aramaic linguistic-literary culture spanning both the Roman and Sasanian sides of the frontier show the formation logic itself persisting underneath the political break — relevant to why this document treats the world's geographic scope as spanning both empires (Section 3), and why 363 is treated as internal while 410 is not (Section 2): the former changes jurisdiction without changing formation logic or ecclesial structure; the latter changes ecclesial structure itself.
