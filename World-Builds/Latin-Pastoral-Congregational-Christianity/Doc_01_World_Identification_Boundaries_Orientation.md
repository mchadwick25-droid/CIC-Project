## 1. Formation World Identity

**World Name:** Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity.

**Short Description:** The formation of ordinary Latin North African Christianity as territorial, congregational, pastoral life under episcopal office — Cyprian of Carthage navigating the Decian persecution, plague, and schism as a working bishop (248/249–258), and Augustine of Hippo preaching, catechizing, and administering the sacraments for his own congregation a century later (391–430) — bounded by two bishops' ordinary care of an entire local flock, not by a single continuous institutional narrative across the century between them.

**Core Identity:** This is a world of ordinary pastors and their own congregations, not of courts, councils convened to settle empire-wide jurisdiction, or ascetic withdrawal from congregational life. Its formation logic is pastoral and sacramental before it is juridical (contrast World #6) or philosophical-catechetical (contrast World #2) or ascetic-literary and patronage-based (contrast World #9): preaching to the same gathered congregation week after week, catechizing new believers, administering baptism and the Eucharist, and disciplining and readmitting those who fail — under recurring external pressure (persecution, epidemic, schism, invasion) that repeatedly tests the bond between a bishop and the flock actually in front of him.

**Distinctive Contribution:** No other confirmed world in this project's portfolio reconstructs ordinary congregational and lay formation — preaching, catechesis, sacramental life, and pastoral care of a bounded local community — as richly as this one; Step 0 §3 B2 calls it "*plausibly* the richest ordinary-believer, ordinary-worship, ordinary-formation world in the confirmed portfolio to date," a hedge this document keeps rather than sharpens, since Step 0 §3 B3 also flags a live, unresolved Antiochene contrast against exactly this claim (§7 below). Where World #6 asks how the church governed itself relative to the state, and World #9 asks how a network of ascetic scholars and their aristocratic patrons pursued textual and spiritual formation, this world asks the plainer question underneath both: what did it look like for an ordinary bishop to hold together, teach, and discipline the actual people in his own church, under real and recurring pressure to fail at it.

**Living Tradition Status:** Not confirmed. Per Constitution Article 29 — which makes confirmation a freeze-eligibility gate while leaving "the method of confirmation... governed by the build documents" — and this build's own process document (`Ministry/Technology/CiC_Record_Native_World_Build_Process_V1_2.md`), Living Tradition Status confirmation is a project-lead act (that document's checkpoint **M2**, alongside the freeze declaration's own Article 29 listing), not performable by this document. Noted for the record, not decided here: this world's core content — an ordinary bishop's territorial, sacramental, congregational care of a local flock — is, if anything, more diffusely and more widely continuous into the present than any single-see primacy claim: it is close to the default self-understanding of the parish or diocesan ministry of most historic Christian communions that have retained the office of bishop or pastor at all, not a claim particular to one see's own succession. **Status: PENDING.**

## 2. Historical Boundaries

**Approximate Time Horizon:** c. 246–430 CE.

- **Beginning point (c. 246):** Cyprian's own conversion, a trained rhetorician turning from a public career to Christian life, ordained and elected bishop of Carthage between roughly July 248 and April 249 by the acclamation of the Carthaginian people (over the recorded opposition of five presbyters) — the year is not fixed by ancient testimony itself and modern reconstructions differ on it by a matter of months; the difference is not load-bearing here. This is a defensible beginning point under the Framework's Temporal Scope test for a reason distinct from mere biography: Cyprian's own formation pathway — an adult, rhetorically-trained convert rising to episcopal office by popular election — is not incidental color but a pathway Augustine's own formation will independently, if differently, recapitulate a century later (§5 below).
- **Ending point (430):** Augustine's death at Hippo, 28 August 430, during the Vandal siege of the city. Defensible on two grounds: (1) it is Augustine's own life-close, and the Representative construction discipline this project follows (RCF Part Four) grounds a world's outer temporal edge in its own anchor figures' documented lives, not an arbitrary round date; (2) it closes the world on a real ecological rupture of the same kind that opens it — the Decian persecution (250) is external, state-organized pressure on this world's own congregational life at its beginning, and the Vandal invasion (crossing from Spain in 429, besieging Hippo as Augustine lay dying) is external, violent pressure on this world's own congregational life at its end. Both bookends are pressures the flock itself lived through, not events belonging to a different register of history. 430 sits comfortably inside the broader Phase One ceiling (451, Chalcedon) with no tension.
- **What developments remain internal, and what would indicate transition (the Framework's own remaining Temporal Scope questions, not run at Step 0 or elsewhere in this document until now):** the full argument is at §4 below (World Separation Criteria, "Has authority changed substantially?"), which this note anchors rather than duplicates. Stated in summary: the change in external political status between Cyprian's phase (an illegal, periodically persecuted religion) and Augustine's phase (an established religion whose bishops can solicit state coercion against a rival communion) is real and substantial, and this document does not treat it as internal by assertion — §4 argues it directly, on the evidence, and concludes that the *ground* of episcopal office (ordination, territorial responsibility for one flock, collegial non-jurisdictional standing among fellow bishops) remains constant across the change, which is what keeps the development internal to one world rather than indicating a transition to a different one. If Doc_04's later, formal gravity testing finds otherwise, that finding supersedes this one, per Article 21's own iterative-revision discipline.

**Geographic Centers:** Carthage and Hippo Regius.

- **Carthage:** the metropolitan see of Africa Proconsularis and, through most of this world's own span, the most populous and consequential Latin Christian city outside Rome itself — the natural convening point for the African episcopate's own councils (Cyprian's own councils on the lapsed and on rebaptism; the councils of Carthage that Augustine's own generation continues to hold, including the 419 council that codified the African church's own canon law in the context of the Apiarius appeal to Rome — a jurisdictional dispute in its own right, flagged here rather than smoothed into an uncomplicated pastoral example, and returned to at §6 below). Cyprian's own see, and a city where Augustine preached frequently across his own episcopate and where a real part of his surviving sermon corpus was delivered, alongside Hippo (Step 0 §3 B1: "~97 sermons preached to his own congregations at Hippo and Carthage").
- **Hippo Regius:** a substantial port city on the North African coast, west of Carthage — Augustine's own see from 395/396 (bishop), after ordination to the presbyterate there in 391 by Valerius, bishop of Hippo, who also gave Augustine leave to preach in his own presence as presbyter, a concession the sources record as contrary to African custom. Civilly, Hippo sat in the province of Africa Proconsularis; ecclesiastically, it sat in the province of Numidia — Augustine was consecrated by the primate of Numidia and sat in Numidian provincial councils, distinct from Carthage's own metropolitan authority. This is itself part of this world's own character: an ordinary provincial bishop's flock, ecclesiastically answerable within a different provincial structure than the primatial see whose wider African councils he nonetheless attended.

**Cultural Environment:** Latin-speaking Roman North Africa, a provincial cluster (Africa Proconsularis and Numidia together, loosely) with its own markedly vigorous Latin Christian literary and rhetorical culture — the tradition that produced Tertullian's own forged theological vocabulary (§6 below), Cyprian's episcopal letters, and Augustine's vast corpus, none of it derivative of the Greek East's own idiom. A Romanized provincial society with real underlying Punic and, further inland, Berber populations; how deeply that substrate culture shaped ordinary congregational life specifically (as against the literate, Latin-trained episcopal voice this world's surviving sources overwhelmingly preserve) is a genuine open question for Doc_02's own Source Asymmetry work, not resolved or assumed here. Predominantly urban in its surviving evidence (Carthage and Hippo both were significant cities), with a rural hinterland whose own Christianization this world's sources attest unevenly.

**Historical Pressures:**
- The Decian persecution (250 CE) — the first systematically enforced, empire-wide persecution, administered through the *libelli* (sacrifice-certificate) system, producing the lapsed crisis that defines much of Cyprian's own surviving correspondence.
- Recurring later persecution: the Valerianic persecution (257–258), under which Cyprian was martyred; the Great (Diocletianic) persecution, whose active African phase falls c. 303–305 (the empire-wide legal state of persecution ran longer, to 311/313) and which falls within this world's own documentary gap (§4 below) — more directly the founding crisis of Donatism (World #4) than of this world's own surviving voice.
- Epidemic disease: the so-called Plague of Cyprian (c. 249–262), a severe, well-attested pandemic Cyprian himself addresses directly in *De Mortalitate* — a real, lived pressure on ordinary congregational life, not merely a literary occasion.
- The Donatist schism, dominant across large parts of North African Christian life for most of the century between Cyprian and Augustine and a continuing, live pastoral problem throughout Augustine's own episcopate — this world's own defining external rival in the Forces-Framework sense (§5 below) and its own internal rival in the Article 23 reconstruction sense (§6 below) — two different axes, not a contradiction; per Step 0's boundary and the reciprocal Article 23 obligation established there.
- Manichaeism, a live, organized rival religious system Augustine personally belonged to for roughly nine years before his conversion, and a recurring target of his own pastoral and polemical writing.
- The Pelagian controversy (410s–420s), a live doctrinal dispute over grace, sin, and human capacity that occupied a substantial share of Augustine's own later pastoral and theological output.
- The Vandal invasion of Roman North Africa (from 429), reaching Hippo itself as a besieging army in the final months of Augustine's life.

**Historical Catalysts:** Cyprian's conversion and rise to the episcopate (c. 246–249); the Decian persecution and the lapsed crisis (250–251); the Felicissimus schism opening during Cyprian's own absence in hiding and condemned at the council he convened after his return, and the roughly contemporaneous Novatianist rival consecration at Rome (both 251); the Carthage plague and *De Mortalitate* (c. 252–253); Cyprian's councils on rebaptism (255–256) and his martyrdom under Valerian (258); [this world's own documentary gap, §4 below]; Augustine's conversion (386) and baptism (387); his ordination as presbyter (391) and bishop (395/396) at Hippo; the sack of Rome (410) and the composition of *City of God*; the Pelagian controversy (410s–420s); Augustine's death during the siege of Hippo (430).

## 3. Distinct World Criteria

Applying the Framework's own four questions directly:

- **What recurring formation ecology exists?** Ordinary territorial-congregational pastoral care, exercised through preaching, catechesis, penitential discipline, and sacramental administration to the same gathered flock — under recurring external crisis-pressure (persecution, epidemic, schism, invasion) that repeatedly tests whether that bond between bishop and congregation holds.
- **What recurring gravities exist (preliminary, subject to Doc_04's own formal six-test assessment — this document establishes the primary characterization on this world's own ecological terms first, per Step 0 §4 item 8, which Doc_04 then formally tests rather than re-derives)?** Provisionally: pastoral office as flock-keeping (a bishop's authority exercised as care for and responsibility toward a bounded local community, not as jurisdiction over other sees — see §4 and §6 below); penitential discipline and the reintegration of the failed (how a community that has suffered real, visible collapse — the lapsed under Decius, ordinary sin under Augustine's own catechetical care — is received back rather than permanently excluded or indiscriminately readmitted); preaching and catechesis as the primary mode of ordinary formation, rather than ascetic discipline (World #9) or philosophical schooling (World #2) or conciliar/juridical process (World #6).
- **What recurring patterns of worship, formation, interpretation, belonging, and authority exist?** Formation and belonging dominate this world's own surviving evidence — the ordinary catechumen's and believer's own journey into and within the community. Authority is real and exercised throughout (both anchor figures are bishops), but its characteristic mode is pastoral — preaching, personal correspondence, penitential process — rather than juridical decree (World #6), ascetic exemplarity (World #9), or philosophical instruction (World #2). Worship is present but recovered mostly through what preaching and catechesis presuppose about it (baptismal practice, the Eucharist, the Creed taught to catechumens), not through separate liturgical treatises the way, for instance, World #9's ascetic literature more directly documents monastic horaria.
- **What distinguishes this world from adjacent worlds?** See §6 below.

## 4. World Separation Criteria

Per Construction Framework V7.4 Part I, this section asks whether the developments already named (§2, §5) mark this world becoming, at some point in its own span, a *different* world — not whether it is distinct from its neighbors (§6's question). Run directly, honestly, and not assumed in either direction:

- **Has a new gravity emerged?** Possibly not, on a preliminary reading Doc_04 must actually test: the anti-Manichaean and anti-Pelagian material that occupies much of Augustine's own later output is new *subject matter*, but §5's own preliminary forces observation is that the underlying pattern — resisting a purity- or sufficiency-test that would exclude the compromised from the community — may be the same recurring concern in new clothing (the lapsed's own readmission under Cyprian; grace's own necessity against Pelagian self-sufficiency under Augustine), not a genuinely new gravity. Not resolved here.
- **Has a major gravity disappeared?** The *acute, empire-wide, state-organized* form of the persecution-and-lapsed-reconciliation gravity recedes after Cyprian's own era — no comparable systematic persecution falls within Augustine's own episcopate — but the underlying concern (how the community holds and reintegrates its own failed members) persists in a different register (ordinary sin and schism-temptation rather than persecution-lapse specifically). A real question for Doc_04, not assumed settled by this document either way.
- **Has formation changed substantially?** No clear evidence of this within this world's own boundary: preaching, catechesis, and sacramental administration remain the mode of ordinary formation throughout, for both anchor figures.
- **Has worship changed substantially?** No clear evidence of substantial change is attested within this world's own boundary — though worship is itself thin evidence for both phases (§3 above), and an absence of attested change is not the same claim as attested continuity; Doc_02/Doc_05 must hold this distinction rather than collapse it.
- **Has authority changed substantially? — the hard question, engaged directly rather than deferred.** Yes, in one real and checkable respect, and no, in the respect that actually determines world-identity under this test. What changes: Cyprian holds episcopal office within an illegal, periodically persecuted religion, with no coercive backing whatsoever — his only tools against internal failure (the lapsed) or a rival consecration (Novatian's, at Rome, in the same year as the Felicissimus schism) are persuasion, excommunication, and conciliar consensus among fellow African bishops. Augustine holds episcopal office within an established, imperially-patronized religion, and — this must be engaged directly, not elided — he actively solicits and defends the Roman state's coercive power against the Donatist rival communion: *The Correction of the Donatists* (Letter 185, `confidence: assigned` to this world in its own corpus-map) argues explicitly from Luke 14:23, "compel them to come in," for imperial law's role in bringing Donatists back into communion. That is a real, substantial difference in what an African bishop of this world could actually do with his office between the mid-3rd and early 5th centuries, and it is not minimized here.

  What stays constant, and why it keeps this development internal to one world rather than marking a transition to a different one: the theological *judgment* Augustine applies to the Donatists is structurally the same one Cyprian applies to Novatian a century earlier — a rival consecration outside the one episcopate is null, not a legitimate parallel jurisdiction to be out-ranked or negotiated with (Cyprian's own *episcopatus unus est*, "the episcopate is one," held in common by all its bishops rather than owned by any one see — the same anti-primacy principle that, applied in the other direction, also holds against Stephen of Rome's own claim over Cyprian, §6 below). What is new in Augustine's phase is not the *judgment* but the *instrument available to enforce it* — the state, now Christian, where none was available to a bishop of an illegal religion. Augustine's own argument for coercion is framed throughout in pastoral-corrective terms (bringing a wayward member of the one family back), continuous with the logic Cyprian applies to the lapsed — that the community's own duty is to work for the return of the compromised or estranged, not simply to write them off — extended to a tool that only exists once the surrounding state is itself Christian. The *ground* of episcopal authority — ordination, territorial responsibility for one see's own flock, collegial standing among fellow African bishops answerable to one another in council, no jurisdiction claimed over other sees — is unchanged across the shift; only the external political capacity available to defend and enforce it changed, and that capacity is not itself part of what this world's own recurring gravities (§3 above) are about. This finding is preliminary, in the same sense every finding in this document is preliminary ahead of Doc_04's formal six-test assessment, and Doc_08's Forces analysis must take the illegal-to-established shift fully seriously as a major force, not a footnote, precisely because this section has just found that it does not rise to a different world.
- **Has interpretation changed substantially?** No clear evidence of this within this world's own boundary: both figures read Scripture within the developing common exegetical and typological tradition of the Latin West; no marked break is attested.

**Conclusion:** on balance, the developments across this world's own span are real, substantial in places (authority's external capacity, above), and honestly named rather than minimized — but none of them, singly or together, indicates a transition to a different world under this test's own six questions. This finding governs §5's Strand Determination below, which asks a related but distinct question under Article 21.

## 5. Strand Determination

Per Constitution Article 21: strand is "a finding, never a presupposed universal schema," accountable to evidence, and never assigned to satisfy an architectural preference for plurality. This determination is made here, for the first time — Step 0 correctly deferred it as Step 1's own work (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §2, the century-gap discussion, and A5), while flagging that Doc_01 must make an actual, reasoned determination rather than silently assume continuity or silently treat the century between Cyprian and Augustine as immaterial.

**What Article 21 actually asks, read against Article 15.** A century of real historical development separating two figures is not, by itself, evidence of internal strand plurality — Article 15 (Historical Development & Instability) already covers formation worlds as "historically developing ecclesial realities shaped, to the degree the evidence attests, through instability, adaptation, and incomplete continuity" (quoted exactly; this document reads it as covering temporal development within a world's own bounded timeframe). Article 21's own test is narrower and different: does the evidence show *meaningfully distinct patterns of formation emphasis, practice, authority structure, or ecological orientation* — this document reads that test as reaching successive as well as coexisting patterns, since neither Article 21 nor CF V7.4's own Strand Determination questions impose a simultaneity requirement (unlike IJC's own Doc_01 §4, which glossed the test as requiring "genuinely simultaneous" divergence — a reasonable but narrower reading than the text itself requires, not adopted here).

**Finding: strand-singular**, reached only after engaging the strongest available strand-plural candidate directly, not by omitting it. That candidate is exactly the authority-structure question §4 above just resolved: the shift from an illegal religion's bishop with no coercive backing to an established religion's bishop willing to solicit state coercion against a rival communion. Tested against Article 21's own named criteria:

- **Authority structure:** §4 above found the *ground* of episcopal authority constant — ordination, territorial responsibility for one flock, collegial non-jurisdictional standing among fellow African bishops — with only the external political instrument available to defend it changing. That finding is not re-derived here; it is applied. Under Article 21's own criterion, a change in external instrument without a change in the ground of the office is not a distinct authority structure.
- **Formation emphasis and practice:** the same in both phases — preaching, catechesis, penitential discipline, and sacramental administration directed at the ordinary believer and catechumen in front of the bishop, not doctrinal argument for its own sake (contrast World #2), ascetic formation of a scholarly-patronage network (contrast World #9), or consolidation of jurisdiction over other bishops (contrast World #6).
- **Ecological orientation:** the same in both phases — a bishop's authority is legitimated by, and exercised as, care for a bounded local community he is answerable to and for, visible in Cyprian's own agonized correspondence about the lapsed and in Augustine's own catechetical manual and sermons to his own congregation alike.

**This finding does not by itself settle the harder, adjacent question Step 0 flagged and left open — Article 3's "sufficient historical coherence" test for the world as a whole, given the century's real gap and Donatism's own rival claim to the interval. Article 21 and Article 3 are different tests, and strand-singularity is not an answer to Article 3; it is the finding that clears the way to actually answer it, which this document now does.**

**The Article 3 question, addressed directly, and corrected here from an error in this document's own earlier draft.** This world's "sufficient historical coherence" does not rest on continuous surviving self-documentation across the full span — none exists, and none is manufactured to supply it (Tier 5 is prohibited regardless). It rests on a checkable, in-world fact this world's own surviving corpus actually attests: Augustine's own church understood and documented itself as the same catholic communion Cyprian had led, not as a new community starting over. This is not a continuity claim this project imports from outside; it is Augustine's own, extensively documented, in his own corpus — but stated precisely, correcting a real error in how this document first characterized it.

An earlier draft of this document claimed that Augustine's central anti-Donatist treatise, *On Baptism, Against the Donatists*, treats Cyprian's "authority, teaching, and conciliar procedure as directly binding precedent." Checked directly against the text, that is false, and the truth is more interesting. Augustine argues at length that Cyprian's specific ruling — that baptism outside the unity of the church must be repeated — was *not* binding and was *wrong*: Cyprian's own position, Augustine writes, "was contrary to that which was afterwards brought to light... by the authority of a plenary Council" (Book VI), and Cyprian and his colleagues did not act "by the authority of any plenary or regionary Council" that could bind the whole church (Book II). What Augustine treats as normative is not Cyprian's conclusion but Cyprian's own conduct: his charity, and above all his refusal to break communion over the very question on which he and Rome disagreed. Augustine argues *with* Cyprian, disputing his ruling while claiming his communion — and that is a stronger continuity datum than deference would have been, precisely because a bishop only argues this way with his own tradition's own father, not with a stranger's. The Donatists themselves are the proximate occasion for this engagement: they cited Cyprian's own conciliar acts against Augustine's church, and Augustine's detailed argument through those acts is his answer to that citation, not an independent act of homage.

**Honestly named, not smoothed over, and — corrected here from a second error in this document's own earlier draft — not quietly resolved either:** the Donatists also claimed Cyprian as their own precedent, and on the narrow question of baptismal rigor specifically, Cyprian's own historical position sits closer to the Donatist position than to Augustine's own eventual, more accommodating one. An earlier draft of this document conceded this and then, in the same breath, resolved it anyway — distinguishing this world from Donatism by "which *mode* of pastoral life recurs: ordinary, territorial, sacramental care of an entire local flock... versus a rigorist-separatist alternative that broke communion." That distinction does not actually hold: Donatism was not a communion that declined ordinary territorial pastoral care. For much of the fourth century it was the majority church across large parts of Numidia, with its own full parallel episcopate holding territorial sees, catechizing, baptizing, and caring for entire local flocks — 279 Donatist bishops attended the 411 Conference of Carthage, against 286 Catholic. On the axis actually named, both sides qualify; the only thing that separated them in the sentence as first drafted was "in communion with the wider catholic world," which is precisely the contested question of who inherited Cyprian's own legitimacy — the same question this document had just said it would not adjudicate.

**Corrected finding:** this document does not adjudicate which communion was legitimately the church, and does not need to in order to satisfy Article 3. The mode of pastoral life this world reconstructs — territorial episcopal care of an entire local flock, exercised through preaching, catechesis, and sacrament — genuinely recurs in both the Caecilianist/Catholic and the Donatist communions; that shared recurrence is exactly *why* the two are separate worlds distinguished by their own primary gravities rather than by a legitimacy verdict this project does not hand down. Donatism's own defining gravity is the rigorist maintenance of a purity boundary against a state-favored rival — its own self-understanding, on record in its own Step 0, is "a persecuted 'Church of the Martyrs' facing a state-favored rival." This world's own defining gravity, on the preliminary reading at §3 and §4 above, is closer to the opposite: a readiness to readmit the compromised and to remain in communion with those one disagrees with (Cyprian's own conduct toward Rome, argued above) rather than to break communion over a purity test. That is an ecology-grounded distinction, available from each world's own documented self-understanding, not an adjudication of which church was real — and it is a genuinely more interesting and more defensible finding than the one it replaces, because it is not circular.

**What the gap itself is, named precisely for Doc_02's own sake, not resolved here:** the interval between Cyprian's death (258) and Augustine's own arrival in ordained ministry (391) is not undocumented history in general — it includes the Great Persecution, the *traditio* crisis, and the rise of Donatism, all richly attested, but overwhelmingly through sources that are Donatism's own territory (World #4), not this world's. What is genuinely missing is a surviving voice, continuous with Cyprian's own ordinary-pastoral mode, from within this world's own boundary, for roughly 133 years. Doc_02's Source Ecology must name this precisely — an honest silence in *this world's own record*, not a general absence of evidence about the period — and Doc_05/the Representative's own eventual construction must hold it the way RCF Part Four requires: as natural quiet, never as meta-commentary about what survived to be documented.

**The reading divergence with Donatism's own Step 0, resolved here rather than deferred, per Step 0 §4 item 2(e)'s own instruction that Doc_01 must resolve it explicitly and not inherit either side's reading by default.** The Step 0 Conclusion's World #8 entry reads: "Cyprian as working pastor navigating the Decian persecution and its aftermath (not the schism-crisis angle, which belongs to world #4), Augustine's preaching, catechesis, and ordinary sacramental administration for his own congregation at Hippo." Donatism's own Step 0 reads the "not the schism-crisis angle" parenthetical as qualifying both anchor figures together; this document's own Step 0 read it as qualifying Cyprian specifically. **Finding: the Cyprian-scoped reading is correct**, on the sentence's own grammar — the parenthetical closes before the comma that opens the independent Augustine clause — and this document adopts it as its own, on the record, rather than leaving the question open while acting on it anyway (as an earlier draft of this document in fact did: naming the Donatist schism among this world's own Historical Pressures at §2, carrying it in the Forces sketch at §5, and building the Article 3 argument through *On Baptism, Against the Donatists* — none of which the world-level reading would permit). Donatism's own Doc_01 may reach a different reading for its own construction; that is a live, currently-unreconciled matter between two independently-drafted, unmerged branches, not a category-4 escalation (§8 below) — flagged for reconciliation when the branches merge, with only the concrete downstream consequence (the Optatus placement question) routed to Doc_02, per Step 0 §4 item 2(a).

## 6. Preliminary Forces Identification

Per the Forces Framework's Step 1 requirement, this is orientation for Step 2's scope, not the complete forces analysis compiled at Step 8.

**What generated this world:** the maturation of North African Latin Christianity, by the mid-3rd century, into a settled, numerous, institutionally real communion — Tertullian's own generation had already forged the Latin theological vocabulary this world inherits (§6 below) and established a vigorous local Christian literary culture; Cyprian inherits a community large and organized enough to have real internal factions (the Felicissimus party) and enough clergy and structure to convene councils of dozens of bishops on short notice.

**What larger world was it embedded in:** two genuinely different larger worlds, which is itself the century's real content, engaged directly rather than set aside at §4 above. Cyprian's own phase is embedded in a pre-Constantinian Roman North Africa under recurring, escalating state suspicion of an unlicensed religion. Augustine's own phase is embedded in a post-Constantinian, officially Christianized Roman North Africa whose own political and military integrity is visibly failing by his final years — a Christianized empire that can no longer reliably protect the very congregations it now formally recognizes, and that Augustine's own church can, by then, ask to act against a rival communion in a way Cyprian's church never could.

**What was it responding to:** for Cyprian, the practical, unprecedented pastoral crisis of empire-wide, systematically enforced persecution testing whether the church could survive its own members' failure under pressure and still reconstitute itself; for Augustine, the practical pastoral crisis of a rival communion (Donatism) claiming to be the only true church, and the intellectual-pastoral crisis of Manichaean dualism and Pelagian anthropology, each requiring sustained catechetical and polemical response from the ordinary work of a bishop's own pulpit.

**What was it refusing:** for Cyprian, both extremes of the lapsed controversy at once — Novatianist rigorism, which refused any reconciliation to those who had sacrificed under persecution, and an opposite, undisciplined laxity that would readmit without any real penitential process; a refusal, in both directions, of letting a single test of purity decide who remains within the community. For Augustine, Donatist separatism's claim that a sacrament's validity depends on the personal purity of the minister administering it, and, on a different axis, Pelagian anthropology's claim that a person's own moral effort is sufficient without grace — in both cases a refusal to let one factor (a minister's own purity; a believer's own unaided will) become the sole determinant of the church's or the soul's own standing. Named here as a preliminary observation worth Doc_04's own testing, not asserted as a confirmed gravity: across the century's real gap, both anchor figures resist a purity-test that would exclude the compromised or the imperfect from the community's own ongoing life, in favor of a more patient, grace-dependent, communally-mediated model — a genuine thematic resonance that would, if it holds under Doc_04's six tests, be a strong candidate for this world's own cross-figure Primary gravity, and the same resonance §5 above draws on for its own corrected Article 3 finding.
