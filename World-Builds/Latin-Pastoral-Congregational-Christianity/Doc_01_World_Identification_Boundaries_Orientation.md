## 1. Formation World Identity

**World Name:** Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity.

**Short Description:** The formation of ordinary Latin North African Christianity as territorial, congregational, pastoral life under episcopal office — Cyprian of Carthage navigating the Decian persecution, plague, and schism as a working bishop (248/249–258), and Augustine of Hippo preaching, catechizing, and administering the sacraments for his own congregation a century later (391–430) — bounded by two bishops' ordinary care of an entire local flock, not by a single continuous institutional narrative across the century between them.

**Core Identity:** This is a world of ordinary pastors and their own congregations, not of courts, councils convened to settle empire-wide jurisdiction, or ascetic withdrawal from congregational life. Its formation logic is pastoral and sacramental before it is juridical (contrast World #6) or philosophical-catechetical (contrast World #2) or ascetic-literary and patronage-based (contrast World #9): preaching to the same gathered congregation week after week, catechizing new believers, administering baptism and the Eucharist, and disciplining and readmitting those who fail — under recurring external pressure (persecution, epidemic, schism, invasion) that repeatedly tests the bond between a bishop and the flock actually in front of him.

**Distinctive Contribution:** Among this project's confirmed worlds, none appears to reconstruct ordinary congregational and lay formation — preaching, catechesis, sacramental life, and pastoral care of a bounded local community — as richly as this one; Step 0 §3 B2 calls it "*plausibly* the richest ordinary-believer, ordinary-worship, ordinary-formation world in the confirmed portfolio to date," a hedge this document keeps rather than sharpens, since Step 0 §3 B3 also flags a live, unresolved Antiochene contrast against exactly this claim (§7 below). Where World #6 asks how the church governed itself relative to the state, and World #9 asks how a network of ascetic scholars and their aristocratic patrons pursued textual and spiritual formation, this world asks the plainer question underneath both: what did it look like for an ordinary bishop to hold together, teach, and discipline the actual people in his own church, under real and recurring pressure to fail at it.

**Living Tradition Status:** Not confirmed. Per Constitution Article 29 — which makes confirmation a freeze-eligibility gate while leaving "the method of confirmation... governed by the build documents" — and this build's own process document (`Ministry/Technology/CiC_Record_Native_World_Build_Process_V1_2.md`), Living Tradition Status confirmation is a project-lead act (that document's checkpoint **M2**, alongside the freeze declaration's own Article 29 listing), not performable by this document. (Article 29's actual text: "The method of confirmation is governed by the build documents," quoted here in full rather than elided across its own verb.) Noted for the record, not decided here: this world's core content — an ordinary bishop's territorial, sacramental, congregational care of a local flock — is, if anything, more diffusely and more widely continuous into the present than any single-see primacy claim: it is close to the default self-understanding of the parish or diocesan ministry of most historic Christian communions that have retained the office of bishop or pastor at all, not a claim particular to one see's own succession. **Status: PENDING.**

## 2. Historical Boundaries

**Approximate Time Horizon:** c. 246–430 CE.

- **Beginning point (c. 246):** Cyprian's own conversion, a trained rhetorician turning from a public career to Christian life, ordained and elected bishop of Carthage between roughly July 248 and April 249 by the acclamation of the Carthaginian people (over the recorded opposition of five presbyters) — roughly two to three years from conversion to the episcopate; the exact year is not fixed by ancient testimony itself and modern reconstructions differ on it by a matter of months, a difference not load-bearing here. This is a defensible beginning point under the Framework's Temporal Scope test for a reason distinct from mere biography, stated here precisely rather than as an unqualified parallel: an adult, rhetorically-trained convert compelled into clerical office by congregational acclamation, against his own preference, is a pathway this world's own formation pattern recurs on, though not identically, in Augustine's own case — baptized in 387, seized by the Hippo congregation and ordained presbyter, against his own wishes, in 391 (roughly five years after his own conversion); his own later rise to the episcopate, in 395/396, came by a different mechanism — Valerius's own designation as coadjutor and consecration by Megalius, primate of Numidia — not a second popular acclamation. The recurring element is congregational demand overriding an initially reluctant convert's own preference at the point of entry into clerical office, not an identical career shape end to end.
- **Ending point (430):** Augustine's death at Hippo, 28 August 430, during the Vandal siege of the city. Defensible on two grounds: (1) it is Augustine's own life-close — the natural terminus of this world's own last-attested pastoral witness, argued here as this document's own Step 1 reasoning rather than by appeal to Representative-construction methodology, which properly runs the other direction (Constitution Article 3: the Representative "influences nothing above it," so a world's own temporal boundary is not grounded in a downstream document's own rule); (2) it closes the world on a real ecological rupture of the same kind that opens it — the Decian persecution (250) is external, state-organized pressure on this world's own congregational life at its beginning, and the Vandal invasion (crossing from Spain in 429, besieging Hippo as Augustine lay dying) is external, violent pressure on this world's own congregational life at its end. Both bookends are pressures the flock itself lived through, not events belonging to a different register of history. 430 sits comfortably inside the broader Phase One ceiling (451, Chalcedon) with no tension.
- **What developments remain internal, and what would indicate transition (the Framework's own remaining Temporal Scope questions, not run at Step 0 or elsewhere in this document until now):** the full argument is at §4 below (World Separation Criteria, "Has authority changed substantially?"), which this note anchors rather than duplicates. Stated in summary: the change in external political status between Cyprian's phase (an illegal, periodically persecuted religion) and Augustine's phase (an established religion whose bishops can solicit state coercion against a rival communion) is real and substantial, and this document does not treat it as internal by assertion — §4 argues it directly, on the evidence, naming three real differences rather than one, and finds that two of them do not touch — and the third does not clearly touch — the axis this world's own recurring gravities are actually about, which is what this document finds keeps the development internal to one world rather than indicating a transition to a different one. That finding is held provisionally, as this document's own discipline for every preliminary reading ahead of Doc_04's formal six-test assessment — not because Article 21 itself states an iterative-revision rule, which it does not.

**Geographic Centers:** Carthage and Hippo Regius.

- **Carthage:** the metropolitan see of Africa Proconsularis and, through most of this world's own span, the most populous and consequential Latin Christian city outside Rome itself — the natural convening point for the African episcopate's own councils (Cyprian's own councils on the lapsed and on rebaptism; the councils of Carthage that Augustine's own generation continues to hold, including the 419 council that codified the African church's own canon law in the context of the Apiarius appeal to Rome — a jurisdictional dispute in its own right, flagged here rather than smoothed into an uncomplicated pastoral example, and returned to at §7 below). Cyprian's own see, and a city where Augustine preached frequently across his own episcopate and where a real part of his surviving sermon corpus was delivered, alongside Hippo (Step 0 §3 B1: "~97 sermons preached to his own congregations at Hippo and Carthage").
- **Hippo Regius:** a substantial port city on the North African coast, west of Carthage — Augustine's own see from 395/396 (bishop), after ordination to the presbyterate there in 391 by Valerius, bishop of Hippo, who also gave Augustine leave to preach in his own presence as presbyter, a concession the sources record as contrary to African custom. Civilly, Hippo is usually placed in the province of Africa Proconsularis, though the late-imperial provincial boundary near the coast is not uniformly drawn the same way by every authority and this document does not treat the point as settled beyond dispute; ecclesiastically, its provincial placement is secure — Numidia — Augustine was consecrated by the primate of Numidia and sat in Numidian provincial councils, distinct from Carthage's own metropolitan authority. This is itself part of this world's own character: an ordinary provincial bishop's flock, ecclesiastically answerable within a different provincial structure than the primatial see whose wider African councils he nonetheless attended.

**Cultural Environment:** Latin-speaking Roman North Africa, a provincial cluster (Africa Proconsularis and Numidia together, loosely) with its own markedly vigorous Latin Christian literary and rhetorical culture — the tradition that produced the Latin theological vocabulary Tertullian himself is credited with forging (§7 below), Cyprian's episcopal letters, and Augustine's vast corpus, none of it derivative of the Greek East's own idiom. A Romanized provincial society with real underlying Punic and, further inland, Berber populations; how deeply that substrate culture shaped ordinary congregational life specifically (as against the literate, Latin-trained episcopal voice this world's surviving sources overwhelmingly preserve) is a genuine open question for Doc_02's own Source Asymmetry work, not resolved or assumed here. Predominantly urban in its surviving evidence (Carthage and Hippo both were significant cities), with a rural hinterland whose own Christianization this world's sources attest unevenly.

**Historical Pressures:**
- The Decian persecution (250 CE) — the first systematically enforced, empire-wide persecution, administered through the *libelli* (sacrifice-certificate) system, producing the lapsed crisis that defines much of Cyprian's own surviving correspondence.
- Recurring later persecution: the Valerianic persecution (257–258), under which Cyprian was martyred; the Great (Diocletianic) persecution, whose active African phase falls c. 303–305 (the empire-wide legal state of persecution ran longer, to 311/313) and which falls within this world's own documentary gap (§5 below) — more directly the founding crisis of Donatism (World #4) than of this world's own surviving voice.
- Epidemic disease: the so-called Plague of Cyprian (c. 249–262), a severe, well-attested pandemic Cyprian himself addresses directly in *De Mortalitate* — a real, lived pressure on ordinary congregational life, not merely a literary occasion.
- The Donatist schism, dominant across large parts of North African Christian life for most of the century between Cyprian and Augustine and a continuing, live pastoral problem throughout Augustine's own episcopate — this world's own defining external rival in the Forces-Framework sense (§6 below) and its own internal rival in the Article 23 reconstruction sense (§7 below) — two different axes, not a contradiction; per Step 0's boundary and the reciprocal Article 23 obligation established there.
- Manichaeism, a live, organized rival religious system Augustine personally belonged to for roughly nine years before his conversion, and a recurring target of his own pastoral and polemical writing.
- The Pelagian controversy (410s–420s), a live doctrinal dispute over grace, sin, and human capacity that occupied a substantial share of Augustine's own later pastoral and theological output.
- The Vandal invasion of Roman North Africa (from 429), reaching Hippo itself as a besieging army in the final months of Augustine's life.

**Historical Catalysts:** Cyprian's conversion and rise to the episcopate (c. 246–249); the Decian persecution and the lapsed crisis (250–251); the Felicissimus schism opening during Cyprian's own absence in hiding and condemned at the council he convened after his return, and the roughly contemporaneous Novatianist rival consecration at Rome (both 251); the Carthage plague and *De Mortalitate* (c. 252–253); Cyprian's councils on rebaptism (255–256) and his martyrdom under Valerian (258); [this world's own documentary gap, §5 below]; Augustine's conversion (386) and baptism (387); his ordination as presbyter (391) and bishop (395/396) at Hippo; the sack of Rome (410) and the composition of *City of God*; the Pelagian controversy (410s–420s); Augustine's death during the siege of Hippo (430).

## 3. Distinct World Criteria

Applying the Framework's own four questions directly:

- **What recurring formation ecology exists?** Ordinary territorial-congregational pastoral care, exercised through preaching, catechesis, penitential discipline, and sacramental administration to the same gathered flock — under recurring external crisis-pressure (persecution, epidemic, schism, invasion) that repeatedly tests whether that bond between bishop and congregation holds.
- **What recurring gravities exist (preliminary, subject to Doc_04's own formal six-test assessment — this document establishes the primary characterization on this world's own ecological terms first, per Step 0 §4 item 8; Doc_04 generates its own candidates independently from Doc_02's Source Ecology, per CF V7.4 Part III's own Candidate Gravity Generation discipline, and states explicitly whether this document's preliminary reading survives that independent derivation)?** Provisionally: pastoral office as flock-keeping (a bishop's authority exercised as care for and responsibility toward a bounded local community, not as jurisdiction over other sees — see §4 and §7 below); penitential discipline and the reintegration of the failed (how a community that has suffered real, visible collapse — the lapsed under Decius, ordinary sin under Augustine's own catechetical care — is received back rather than permanently excluded or indiscriminately readmitted); preaching and catechesis as the primary mode of ordinary formation, rather than ascetic discipline (World #9) or philosophical schooling (World #2) or conciliar/juridical process (World #6); and collegial communion maintained despite real disagreement — a bishop working to preserve fellowship with those he sharply disagrees with, rather than break it, exercised most visibly in Cyprian's own conduct toward Rome across the rebaptism dispute and in Augustine's own conduct toward Cyprian's memory over the same question. This fourth candidate is stated here, from this world's own ecology, before §5 below applies it against World #4's own boundary — not derived there for the first time in the act of distinguishing from a neighbor.
- **What recurring patterns of worship, formation, interpretation, belonging, and authority exist?** Formation and belonging dominate this world's own surviving evidence — the ordinary catechumen's and believer's own journey into and within the community. Authority is real and exercised throughout (both anchor figures are bishops), but its characteristic mode is pastoral — preaching, personal correspondence, penitential process — rather than juridical decree (World #6), ascetic exemplarity (World #9), or philosophical instruction (World #2). Worship is present but recovered mostly through what preaching and catechesis presuppose about it (baptismal practice, the Eucharist, the Creed taught to catechumens), not through separate liturgical treatises the way, for instance, World #9's ascetic literature more directly documents monastic horaria.
- **What distinguishes this world from adjacent worlds?** See §7 below.

## 4. World Separation Criteria

Per Construction Framework V7.4 Part I, this section asks whether the developments already named (§2, §5) mark this world becoming, at some point in its own span, a *different* world — not whether it is distinct from its neighbors (§7's question). Run directly, honestly, and not assumed in either direction:

- **Has a new gravity emerged?** Not resolved at this step, and not assumed either way. The anti-Manichaean and anti-Pelagian material that occupies much of Augustine's own later output is new *subject matter*; whether it represents a genuinely new gravity or the same recurring concern in new clothing — resisting a purity- or sufficiency-test that would exclude the compromised from the community, already present in the lapsed's own readmission under Cyprian — is Doc_04's own question. This document's own reading, offered but not asserted as settled, favors the latter.
- **Has a major gravity disappeared?** Not resolved at this step, and not assumed either way. The *acute, empire-wide, state-organized* form of the persecution-and-lapsed-reconciliation gravity recedes after Cyprian's own era — no comparable systematic persecution falls within Augustine's own episcopate — but whether the underlying concern (how the community holds and reintegrates its own failed members) persists in a different register (ordinary sin and schism-temptation) or has genuinely lapsed is, again, Doc_04's own question, on evidence this document does not consider decisive either way.
- **Has formation changed substantially?** No clear evidence of this within this world's own boundary: preaching, catechesis, and sacramental administration remain the mode of ordinary formation throughout, for both anchor figures.
- **Has worship changed substantially?** No clear evidence of substantial change is attested within this world's own boundary — though worship is itself thin evidence for both phases (§3 above), and an absence of attested change is not the same claim as attested continuity; Doc_02/Doc_05 must hold this distinction rather than collapse it.
- **Has authority changed substantially? — the hard question, engaged directly rather than deferred, and revised here from a false claim in an earlier draft of this document.** Yes, in three real and checkable respects; what does not change is narrower than an earlier draft of this document claimed, and is argued rather than asserted below.

  **What changes, named without minimizing it.** *First:* Cyprian holds episcopal office within an illegal, periodically persecuted religion, with no coercive backing whatsoever — his only tools against internal failure (the lapsed) or a rival consecration (Novatian's, at Rome, in the same year as the Felicissimus schism) are persuasion, excommunication, and conciliar consensus among fellow African bishops. Augustine holds episcopal office within an established, imperially-patronized religion, and actively solicits and defends the Roman state's coercive power against the Donatist rival communion. *The Correction of the Donatists* (Letter 185, `confidence: assigned` to this world in its own corpus-map, and separately double-placed with World #6's own corpus-map) argues on two registers, not one, corrected here from an earlier draft's claim that it is framed "throughout" in a single register: a pastoral-corrective register (bringing a wayward member of the one family back, continuous with the logic Cyprian applies to the lapsed), and a second, non-pastoral register arguing from the Christian ruler's own religious duty to legislate against error — Old Testament kings censured for failing to annul ordinances contrary to God's commands, and Nebuchadnezzar's own law "enacted... on behalf of the truth" after his conversion held up as a positive model. The corpus-map's own double placement of this letter with Imperial and Juridical Christianity (World #6) is the signal that this second register is real, not a rhetorical flourish inside an otherwise-pastoral argument.

  *Second — corrected here from a false claim in an earlier draft of this document:* the two bishops do not reach the same theological judgment about a rival consecration's validity. Augustine's central argument in *On Baptism, Against the Donatists* is that a rival consecration retains its sacramental reality outside the one communion — "he who is ordained, if he depart from the unity of the Church, does not lose the sacrament of conferring baptism" (I.1.2) — valid but unfruitful, which is why the African church could and did receive Donatist clergy back in their orders rather than re-ordaining them. Cyprian's own position is the opposite: no valid baptism, and by the same logic no valid ordination, outside the one church — which is why he required rebaptism of converts from schism at all. An earlier draft of this document asserted these were "structurally the same" judgment, and that a rival consecration is "null" on both men's view; neither claim survives a direct check against the text, and Augustine's own treatise argues at length that Cyprian's specific ruling on this exact point was wrong (§5 below).

  *Third:* the two bishops do not share the same theory of conciliar authority. Cyprian, opening the 256 Council of Carthage (a text Augustine himself quotes, and this document relies on again at §5 below): *"neither does any of us set himself up as a bishop of bishops, nor by tyrannical terror does any compel his colleague to the necessity of obedience; since every bishop, according to the allowance of his liberty and power, has his own proper right of judgment, and can no more be judged by another than he himself can judge another. But let us all wait for the judgment of our Lord Jesus Christ."* Augustine, arguing the opposite structure in the same treatise: *"all the letters of bishops... are liable to be refuted... by the authority of Councils; and further, that the Councils themselves, which are held in the several districts and provinces, must yield, beyond all possibility of doubt, to the authority of plenary Councils which are formed for the whole Christian world; and that even of the plenary Councils, the earlier are often corrected by those which follow them"* (II.3). Cyprian's ground is egalitarian and non-coercive — each bishop answerable to Christ alone, no council empowered to compel a dissenting colleague. Augustine's ground is hierarchical and correctable — provincial councils yield to plenary councils, and even plenary councils yield to later ones. This is a real difference in the theorized *ground* of episcopal authority, not merely in the external instrument available to defend it, and it is not resolved by relabeling it a Forces question (§5 below engages it directly under Article 21).

  **What this document finds nonetheless stays constant, and why that keeps the development internal to one world rather than marking a transition — argued, not asserted.** None of the three differences above touches the axis this world's own recurring gravities (§3 above) are actually about: a bishop's *ordinary* exercise of authority toward the flock he is personally responsible for — ordination into a territorial see, preaching, catechesis, penitential discipline, and sacramental administration to the congregation in front of him. All three differences instead concern a bishop's authority *relative to other bishops and to a rival, competing hierarchy*: whether a rival's sacraments are void, whether a plenary council can correct a provincial one or an individual bishop, and what tools exist against a rival communion claiming the same territory. In both phases, for both figures, the ordinary, day-to-day exercise of episcopal office toward one's own congregation is preaching, teaching, and pastoral correction — Augustine governs his own flock at Hippo by the same means Cyprian governed his at Carthage; state coercion is invoked specifically against the *separate* Donatist hierarchy, not as how either bishop disciplines his own people. This document does not treat the three named differences as unimportant on that account: Doc_04's formal six-test assessment must weigh all three directly, with particular weight on the third (conciliar-authority theory), which this document finds the closest call and does not consider fully closed by the argument just given.
- **Has interpretation changed substantially?** No clear evidence of this within this world's own boundary: both figures read Scripture within the developing common exegetical and typological tradition of the Latin West; no marked break is attested.

**Conclusion:** the developments across this world's own span are real and substantial on three separate axes (the sacramental status of a rival consecration; the theory of conciliar authority; coercive capacity against a rival communion, and the theological register available to defend it) and are named here without minimizing any of them. On the first and third of these, this document finds the development does not indicate a transition to a different world, because neither axis touches this world's own recurring gravities as established at §3. On the second — conciliar-authority theory — this document reaches the same finding but with real, disclosed uncertainty, and identifies it as the single question most in need of Doc_04's own independent testing (carried forward at §8 item 10). On the two remaining separation questions above (new/disappeared gravity), the evidence available at Step 1 does not, on its most transition-favoring reading, describe an emergent or a vanished gravity so much as the same underlying concern in different circumstantial clothing — but both are Doc_04's own questions to close, not this document's to assert as settled. This finding governs §5's Strand Determination below, which asks a related but distinct question under Article 21, and which must actually engage — not merely inherit — the conciliar-authority axis named here.

## 5. Strand Determination

Per Constitution Article 21: strand is "a finding, never a presupposed universal schema," accountable to evidence, and never assigned to satisfy an architectural preference for plurality. This determination is made here, for the first time — Step 0 correctly deferred it as Step 1's own work (`Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §2, the century-gap discussion, and A5), while flagging that Doc_01 must make an actual, reasoned determination rather than silently assume continuity or silently treat the century between Cyprian and Augustine as immaterial.

**What Article 21 actually asks, read against Article 15.** A century of real historical development separating two figures is not, by itself, evidence of internal strand plurality — Article 15 (Historical Development & Instability) already covers formation worlds as "historically developing ecclesial realities shaped, to the degree the evidence attests, through instability, adaptation, and incomplete continuity" (quoted exactly; this document reads it as covering temporal development within a world's own bounded timeframe). Article 21's own test is narrower and different: does the evidence show *meaningfully distinct patterns of formation emphasis, practice, authority structure, or ecological orientation* — this document reads that test as reaching successive as well as coexisting patterns, since neither Article 21 nor CF V7.4's own Strand Determination questions impose a simultaneity requirement (unlike IJC's own Doc_01 §4, which glossed the test as requiring "genuinely simultaneous" divergence — a reasonable but narrower reading than the text itself requires, not adopted here).

**Finding: strand-singular, qualified**, reached only after engaging the strongest available strand-plural candidates directly, not by omitting them. §4 above tests three such candidates on the authority-structure axis — coercive capacity against a rival communion, a rival consecration's sacramental validity, and the theory of conciliar authority — rather than the single candidate an earlier draft of this document engaged. Tested against Article 21's own named criteria:

- **Authority structure:** §4 above, revised there from an earlier draft's false claim of an unchanged theory of authority, found three real differences (rival-consecration validity, conciliar-authority theory, and coercive capacity against a rival communion) and argued that the first and third do not touch, and the second does not clearly touch, the axis this world's own recurring gravities (§3 above) are actually about — a bishop's ordinary exercise of authority toward his own flock. That argument is applied, not re-derived, here. It is weakest on the conciliar-authority axis, which §4 flags as the closest call and does not consider fully closed; this document's strand-singular finding below is reached with that qualification stated on the record, not concealed, and carried forward at §8 item 10 for Doc_04's own independent test to resolve with the weight the evidence deserves.
- **Formation emphasis and practice:** the same in both phases — preaching, catechesis, penitential discipline, and sacramental administration directed at the ordinary believer and catechumen in front of the bishop, not doctrinal argument for its own sake (contrast World #2), ascetic formation of a scholarly-patronage network (contrast World #9), or consolidation of jurisdiction over other bishops (contrast World #6).
- **Ecological orientation:** the same in both phases — a bishop's authority is legitimated by, and exercised as, care for a bounded local community he is answerable to and for, visible in Cyprian's own agonized correspondence about the lapsed and in Augustine's own catechetical manual and sermons to his own congregation alike.

**This finding does not by itself settle the harder, adjacent question Step 0 flagged and left open — Article 3's "sufficient historical coherence" test for the world as a whole, given the century's real gap and Donatism's own rival claim to the interval. Article 21 and Article 3 are different tests, and strand-singularity is not an answer to Article 3; it is the finding that clears the way to actually answer it, which this document now does.**

**The Article 3 question, addressed directly, and corrected here from an error in this document's own earlier draft.** This world's "sufficient historical coherence" does not rest on continuous surviving self-documentation across the full span — none exists, and none is manufactured to supply it (Tier 5 is prohibited regardless). It rests on a checkable, in-world fact this world's own surviving corpus actually attests: Augustine's own church understood and documented itself as the same catholic communion Cyprian had led, not as a new community starting over. This is not a continuity claim this project imports from outside; it is Augustine's own, extensively documented, in his own corpus — but stated precisely, correcting a real error in how this document first characterized it.

An earlier draft of this document claimed that Augustine's central anti-Donatist treatise, *On Baptism, Against the Donatists*, treats Cyprian's "authority, teaching, and conciliar procedure as directly binding precedent." Checked directly against the text, that is false, and the truth is more interesting. Augustine argues at length that Cyprian's specific ruling — that baptism outside the unity of the church must be repeated — was *not* binding and was *wrong*: Cyprian's own position, Augustine writes, "was contrary to that which was afterwards brought to light... by the authority of a plenary Council" (Book VI), and Cyprian and his colleagues did not act "by the authority of any plenary or even regionary Council" that could bind the whole church (Book III). What Augustine treats as normative is not Cyprian's conclusion but Cyprian's own conduct: his charity, and above all his refusal to break communion over the very question on which he and Rome disagreed. Augustine argues *with* Cyprian, disputing his ruling while claiming his communion — and that is a stronger continuity datum than deference would have been, precisely because a bishop only argues this way with his own tradition's own father, not with a stranger's. The Donatists themselves are the proximate occasion for this engagement: they cited Cyprian's own conciliar acts against Augustine's church, and Augustine's detailed argument through those acts is his answer to that citation, not an independent act of homage.

**Honestly named, not smoothed over, and — corrected here from a second error in this document's own earlier draft — not quietly resolved either:** the Donatists also claimed Cyprian as their own precedent, and on the narrow question of how to treat converts arriving from *outside* the church, Cyprian's own historical position sits closer to the Donatist position than to Augustine's own eventual, more accommodating one — Cyprian required rebaptism of converts from schism or heresy, the same rigor Donatism itself later revived (Donatism's own Step 0, §2 A2: "the Donatists revived Cyprian's own third-century position against Pope Stephen"). An earlier draft of this document conceded this and then, in the same breath, resolved it anyway — distinguishing this world from Donatism by "which *mode* of pastoral life recurs: ordinary, territorial, sacramental care of an entire local flock... versus a rigorist-separatist alternative that broke communion." That distinction does not actually hold as stated: Donatism was not a communion that declined ordinary territorial pastoral care. For much of the fourth century it was the majority church across large parts of Numidia, with its own full parallel episcopate holding territorial sees, catechizing, baptizing, and caring for entire local flocks — 279 Donatist bishops attended the 411 Conference of Carthage, against 286 Catholic.

**Corrected finding, on an axis this world's own evidence supports and a sibling world-build has already adopted on the record, rather than this document inventing or asserting one about a world it does not own.** This document does not adjudicate which communion was legitimately the church, and does not need to in order to satisfy Article 3. The distinguishing axis is not the presence or absence of ordinary pastoral office — both communions had that — nor a general "purity" orientation Cyprian himself does not cleanly hold, on the rebaptism concession just above. It is a different question: what a bishop of this world does when he disagrees, sharply, with a fellow bishop or with the wider church's own discipline — work to preserve communion despite the disagreement, or break communion and build a rival, parallel hierarchy over it. Cyprian disagreed with Stephen of Rome to the point of open conflict over the rebaptism question and never broke communion with Rome over it (§4 above); Augustine argues at length *against* Cyprian's own specific ruling while explicitly refusing to let that disagreement touch Cyprian's own standing or his own communion with him (§4 above, and §7 below). Donatism's own defining act, by contrast, was the opposite: a permanent, parallel episcopate formed over the *traditio* charge, refusing communion with the other side rather than remaining within it and disputing. This is not this document's own characterization of Donatism's own defining gravity — that is not this document's to make.
