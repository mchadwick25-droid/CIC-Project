## 2. Author Gravity Assessment

Per the Construction Framework's five-dimension author-gravity discipline (Constitution Article 26), both anchor figures are assessed across Visibility, Representativeness, Influence, Limitations, and Transmission History.

**Cyprian of Carthage.**
- *Visibility:* High but narrow in duration — a ten-year episcopate (248/249–258), concentrated almost entirely on two acute crises (the Decian persecution and its lapsed-reconciliation aftermath, and the rebaptism dispute with Stephen), against which his own ordinary, non-crisis pastoral routine is comparatively under-visible in the surviving letters, which are disproportionately occasional (written to address a specific crisis) rather than routine.
- *Representativeness:* Represents an African metropolitan bishop's own voice specifically, at the head of a large, organized see capable of convening councils of dozens of bishops on short notice (Doc_01 §6) — not a small or marginal congregation, and not, on the rebaptism question, the eventual position of the wider church (Rome's own view prevailed on that specific point, per Doc_01 §5's own account of Augustine's later argument that Cyprian's specific ruling "was contrary to that which was afterwards brought to light... by the authority of a plenary Council"). Nor is Cyprian's own voice free-standing: at least four of his own minor treatises (Registry row 5) directly rework, follow, or depend on Tertullian — a real, non-Native influence-source Doc_01 §8 item 5 binds this document to name here rather than only at §1's own Named Comparandum treatment of Tertullian (row 29), per that item's own instruction not to "let it pass silently a second time."
- *Influence:* Foundational for this world's own ecclesiology of the one episcopate and, on the point Doc_01 §8 item 10 holds open rather than settled, real and substantial but *contested* on conciliar authority specifically: Doc_01 §5 records Augustine "arguing *with* Cyprian, disputing his ruling while claiming his communion" on the rebaptism question — a stronger continuity datum than deference would have been, since a bishop argues this way only with his own tradition's own father — but Doc_01 §8 item 10 itself, not yet closed, names Cyprian's own egalitarian, non-coercive conciliar-authority theory and Augustine's own hierarchical, correctable one as "real, substantial differences" not "fully settled" to touch this world's own recurring gravities. This document's own account states the dispute rather than compressing it into unqualified continuity, an overclaim an earlier draft of this entry made. Also foundational for the Latin theological vocabulary itself, at one remove: Cyprian works directly within the idiom Tertullian is credited with forging (Doc_01 §7), so Cyprian's own influence on this world is itself partly the transmission of an influence he received.
- *Limitations:* The corpus embeds letters by others (Cornelius, the Roman clergy, Firmilian of Caesarea, the confessors) under Cyprian's own name, per the corpus map's own note on CCEL's whole-body attribution — a transmission fact this Registry (row 1) discloses rather than treats as though every letter in the body is demonstrably Cyprian's own composition. The corpus is also occasional rather than systematic: it documents what crises provoked correspondence, not Cyprian's ordinary, undocumented weekly pastoral routine.
- *Transmission History:* Preserved through the Latin Western manuscript tradition as a canonical Father of the church on both sides of the later rebaptism-doctrine dispute his own letters record — his conciliar authority theory was cited by Augustine against the Donatists (this world's own boundary work, Doc_01 §7), and the Donatists themselves appealed to Cyprian's own authority and to the 256 council's own judgments for their rebaptism doctrine — grounded on this world's own vendored primary corpus rather than on a corpus-map annotation or an unverified cross-world citation (Registry row 13, Confidence A). Augustine's own *On Baptism, Against the Donatists* states this directly, in his own continuous words, at Book III ch. 2 §2: what the Donatists bring forward is "from the Council and the epistles of Cyprian, to the effect that Christ's baptism may not be given by the hands of heretics." The surrounding volume corroborates the same claim through its own editorial apparatus rather than through further first-person Augustine text — the NPNF preface's own quotation of Augustine's *Retractations* II.18 ("who strive to defend themselves by the authority of the most blessed bishop and martyr Cyprian") — the work Registry rows 67 (Mutzenbecher's CCSL 57, in copyright) and 78 (Knöll's CSEL 36, public domain) both now carry, corrected here from an earlier draft's own gap (Round 6's own L5) that named the quotation without citing either row, and the vendored edition's own editorial summaries of Books II, VI and VII (**corrected here, Round 4's own H1, from an earlier draft that presented these editorial summaries as the work's own titles and as independently "directly re-verified" Augustine's-own-words loci alongside Book III's** — they are the edition's third-person *argumenta*, not Augustine's text, the same editorial-voice distinction this document already draws for row 42's Zonaras quotation and for Letter CCXI at §6 below). This is Augustine's own testimony about the Donatist appeal, not an independent Donatist-authored source making it in their own words — a distinction worth stating plainly rather than blurring. Three earlier drafts of this sentence got the grounding or its description wrong: the first cited only Registry row 42's own corpus-map annotation as though that settled it; the second, correctly rejecting that as insufficient, asserted that the claim "is not independently documented" anywhere in this world's own corpus — false, reached by searching the sibling Donatism build's own record rather than `cic/texts/`; the third, correcting that, mischaracterized which of its own supporting loci were Augustine's own words and which were the volume's editorial apparatus. The underlying claim has been right, and has rested on Book III ch. 2 §2, since the first correction; only its own description has taken three further passes to state accurately. A figure preserved and argued from by parties who reached opposite later conclusions is not a transmission-selection risk in the way a one-sided hostile-quotation corpus is, but it does mean his own words are read by later readers already embedded in disputes he did not himself anticipate.

**Augustine of Hippo.**
- *Visibility:* Overwhelmingly high and unusually well-documented across his own thirty-five-year episcopate (395/396–430) — the largest surviving body of evidence for any figure in this project's own portfolio, spanning sermons, letters, treatises, and a first-person spiritual autobiography (the *Confessions*) with no real analogue elsewhere in this world's own corpus.
- *Representativeness:* Represents an African bishop writing from a position of increasing institutional confidence relative to Donatism specifically (post-405 Edict of Unity, post-411 Conference) — his own later anti-Donatist and anti-Pelagian works are not written from a contested, undecided position the way Cyprian's mid-crisis letters often are.
- *Influence:* Immense and lasting, both for how this world's own theology of grace, original sin, and the two cities becomes foundational for the entire subsequent Western tradition (Doc_01 §7), and — the specific risk this dimension exists to flag — for how disproportionately his own voice, rather than any other North African bishop's, has come to stand for "what the ordinary Latin pastoral bishop of this period was like." This is a claim about *episcopal* voice specifically, not a claim that no other individual is named or heard from in this world's own corpus at all — §6 below documents real, if narrower, non-episcopal and lay first-person material (Pontius, Epistles XX–XXI, several of Augustine's own women correspondents), and this dimension's own risk is precisely that a reader could mistake Augustine's episcopal dominance for the whole of this world's own voice, which it is not.
- *Limitations:* The great majority of ordinary-believer and ordinary-congregant experience of this world survives only as it is refracted through one or the other bishop's own preaching and correspondence, not in the believer's own words — a real, load-bearing asymmetry this document names directly at §6 below, alongside the narrower, real exceptions §6 also documents.
- *Transmission History:* Preserved through the Catholic institutional tradition's own manuscript transmission, as the doctrinally and institutionally victorious party's literature on every dispute he engaged (Donatism, Manichaeism, Pelagianism) — a transmission advantage this world shares with, and inherits in part from, the same institutional continuity the sibling Donatism build's own Author Gravity work names as working systematically against its own preservation. More specifically: the vendored NPNF Series I volumes are 19th-century English translations (Series editor Philip Schaff), a further translating hand between Augustine's own Latin and this document's own citations, distinct from and later than the Latin manuscript transmission itself — a fact this document has not yet traced to a specific base Latin edition per volume, unlike the Cyprian corpus (below), and named here as an open gap rather than assumed innocuous.

**Pontius the Deacon**, treated separately as a distinct formation-narrative voice rather than folded into the Cyprian entry above, since his own perspective (an eyewitness deacon writing after his bishop's martyrdom) is not the same as Cyprian's own first-person letters.
- *Visibility:* Visible only through the one work attributed to him, *The Life and Passion of Cyprian* — no other writing by Pontius survives in this world's own corpus.
- *Representativeness:* Represents a close subordinate's own retrospective, hagiographically-shaped account of his bishop, not a peer bishop's or an ordinary congregant's perspective. He is nonetheless this world's own clearest non-episcopal, extended first-person clerical voice (a deacon, not a bishop) — a real, if narrow, exception to the two-bishops-dominate-everything pattern named throughout this section, and named again at §6 below where the Article 20 discharge depends on it.
- *Influence:* Substantial for how Cyprian's own conversion and election are known at all — the acclamation account this document and Doc_01 both draw on (§1 above) has no surviving parallel in Cyprian's own letters.
- *Limitations:* Written after Cyprian's own martyrdom, by a deacon with every institutional and personal reason to idealize his bishop; the genre-level Author Gravity risk this fact creates is assessed directly at §4 below.
- *Transmission History:* The ANF05 volume's own introductory notice states its base Latin text for the Cyprianic corpus is Migne's, with the Oxford edition's own letter-numbering appended in notes for reference (its translator for the Cyprian material is the Rev. Ernest Wallis) — the specific transmission fact whose absence from an earlier draft of this Registry (row 1's own citation) let a letter-numbering error stand uncaught; recorded here rather than only as a bibliographic footnote, since it is the mechanism, not merely the provenance. Pontius's own *Life* transmits inside this same volume and edition, "often counted the earliest Christian biography" per the corpus map's own note.

## 4. Formation Narrative Sources

Per Construction Framework Part II, hagiographic and narrative sources are assessed separately from theological treatises and institutional records, since they carry formation content differently than argument does.

**Pontius the Deacon, *The Life and Passion of Cyprian, Bishop and Martyr*.** *Authorship and date:* attributed to Pontius, Cyprian's own deacon; composed after Cyprian's martyrdom (258), within living memory of the events it narrates. *Proximity:* a first-generation, eyewitness-adjacent account — Pontius accompanied Cyprian into exile at Curubis, per the work's own narrative — not a later legendary elaboration. *Genre conventions:* early Christian biography with hagiographic shaping already present (the martyr's steadfastness, the manner of death as the completion of a formed life), though earlier and less developed in convention than the fuller passio form that follows it. *Formation ecology revealed:* how a Carthaginian congregation and its clergy understood their bishop's own path into office (the acclamation account, §1 and §2 above) and his conduct under persecution — a close subordinate's own testimony to what formation under this world's founding crisis looked like from inside the bishop's own household. *Author Gravity risk:* real and named directly — written by a deacon with every personal and institutional reason to idealize his own bishop, after that bishop's own martyrdom had already begun to be venerated (the *Life* itself does not state that Cyprian ordained Pontius, only that Pontius was one of his "household companions" chosen to share his exile — checked directly this session, not asserted); this document does not treat its account of Cyprian's own interior motivations as a neutral historical record on that basis, while treating its narration of external, checkable events (the exile to Curubis, the manner and circumstances of the martyrdom) as more reliable than its characterization of Cyprian's own inner disposition.

**Cyprian's own letters as formation-narrative-adjacent material.** Several of Cyprian's own letters on the lapsed and on Felicissimus's schism (Registry row 1) function partly as narrative — first-person accounts of a community's crisis and its resolution — rather than purely as doctrinal argument, though they remain classified as primary correspondence rather than as a separate narrative genre, consistent with how the corpus map itself treats them.

**No Tier 5 material identified.** Consistent with the Framework's prohibition, this document identifies no generated or illustrative narrative for this world. Where the evidence is thin — ordinary lay believers' own first-person experience of either crisis phase, specifically — that thinness is named as a finding at §6 below, not filled.

## 6. Source Asymmetries and Missing Voices

This world's own evidentiary asymmetry is not, primarily, a hostile-source problem in the way the sibling Donatism build's own is — both anchor voices speak in their own words, as bishops of the tradition this world's own construction centers on, not as an opponent's quotation. The asymmetry here is narrower and different in kind: **two named, elite, male, clerical voices carry nearly this entire world's own surviving record across a 184-year span.**

- **Elite/literate/male/episcopal skew (Article 20), corrected here from an earlier draft's own overstatement of it.** The great majority of this world's own surviving record is authored by, or organized around, one of two bishops — but not all of it, and an earlier draft of this section asserted three blanket negatives that the corpus this document's own Registry licenses directly falsifies. **The non-episcopal clergyman is Pontius**, a deacon, whose extended first-person account (Registry row 7) is the whole subject of §1, §2, and §4 above — named in the very sentence before this section's own prior negative denied any such voice existed. **Two lay believers' own letters survive in their own words:** Epistles XX and XXI, "Celerinus to Lucian" and "Lucian Replies to Celerinus" (Registry row 1) — two confessors, writing to each other in the first person about the reconciliation of Celerinus's lapsed sisters at Rome, neither yet ordained at the time of writing. **Named women appear, in real narrative weight, inside Registry row 11's own licensed body:** Letter CXXVI, to Albina (a.d. 411) — Augustine's own first-person account of the riot in his basilica at Hippo when the congregation demanded Pinianus's ordination, written to answer Albina's own charge that Pinianus had been coerced under threat of death; and Letter CCXI, to the Nuns of Hippo (a.d. 423) — addressing the revolt in the women's monastery his own sister had led as prioress; **the vendored volume's own editorial framing**, not Augustine's text itself, characterizes the letter as embodying the rule of the Augustinian orders ("of historical value, as embodying the rules of nunneries belonging to the Augustinian orders"), a textual relationship between this letter and the *Praeceptum* that is a live question in the scholarship and is attributed to the editorial hand here rather than absorbed as Augustine's own statement, per the discipline Doc_01 established for this exact shape of claim. Both independently re-verified against source this revision (Registry row 11). What remains true, and is not softened by these corrections: no source anywhere in this world's own vendored corpus is authored by an ordinary lay believer *writing about ordinary congregational life as such*, rather than about a specific crisis (the lapsed reconciliation, a riot, a monastic revolt) that drew a bishop's own attention and thereby survival. Doc_01 §2's own open question about how deeply the Punic/Berber substrate culture shaped ordinary congregational life (§5 above) is one direct consequence of the remaining, narrower asymmetry, not a separate finding.
