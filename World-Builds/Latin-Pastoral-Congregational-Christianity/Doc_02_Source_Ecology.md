## 1. Primary Sources

This world's evidentiary situation is close to the inverse of the sibling Donatism build's own: rather than a narrow, hostile-side concentration, this world holds the largest and most direct primary-source base of any confirmed world in the portfolio to date — both anchor figures' own surviving corpora, in their own words, not filtered through an opponent's quotation. The following groups the corpus-map's 40-plus Native entries thematically; full row-by-row classification (Type, Confidence, Licensed-For) lives in `Source_Registry.md`.

**Cyprian of Carthage (bishop 248/249–258).** The *Epistles* (82 letters, Registry row 1) are this world's central documentary corpus for its Cyprian phase: the Decian persecution and the lapsed crisis, the Felicissimus schism, the Novatianist rival consecration at Rome, and the rebaptism controversy with Pope Stephen. Doc_01's own nine review rounds directly re-verified this corpus's own account of Cyprian's election (Ep. XL, "your suffrage and God's judgment," against the "ancient venom" of a rival faction) and his uniform position as *object*, never solicitor, of state power — a corpus-wide negative check across every occurrence of "proconsul," "magistrate," "emperor," and "imperial" in the vendored volume, re-run independently across three separate review rounds (Doc_01 §7). *On the Unity of the Church* and the acts of the *Seventh Council of Carthage* (256, on rebaptism; Registry rows 3–4) carry Cyprian's own egalitarian, non-coercive conciliar-authority theory — "no bishop sets himself up as a bishop of bishops... every bishop has his own proper right of judgment" — directly quoted and re-verified across Doc_01 Rounds 3–9. A cluster of pastoral treatises (Registry row 5) — *On the Mortality*, *On the Lord's Prayer*, *On Works and Alms*, and others — carries the ordinary congregational content: catechesis, almsgiving, and, in *De Mortalitate*'s case, lived pastoral response to the plague of the 250s.

**Augustine of Hippo (bishop 395/396–430).** The vendored corpus is the largest in the portfolio for any single figure: the *Confessions*; ~292 letters, split by the corpus map into a 17-letter Jerome cluster (deliberately double-placed with the Hieronymian world, since the exchange has voice on both sides) and a 138-letter general-correspondence body (Registry rows 9–11); the central anti-Donatist works, *On Baptism, Against the Donatists* and *The Correction of the Donatists* (Letter 185), both directly and repeatedly re-verified at source across Doc_01's nine review rounds (Registry rows 12–13); ~97 sermons and ~695,000 words of Psalm exposition, both preached to his own congregations at Hippo and Carthage; 124 Tractates on John; the catechetical works (*On the Catechising of the Uninstructed*, *On Christian Doctrine*, *The Enchiridion*); the anti-Manichaean and anti-Pelagian corpora, each occasioned by a live controversy Doc_01 §6 names as a recurring gravity candidate (resisting a purity- or sufficiency-test that would exclude the compromised); and *City of God*. Doc_01's own construction record directly quotes and re-verifies Augustine's own account of his ordination — presbyter in 391 "against his own wishes," by congregational seizure, and bishop in 395/396 by Valerius's own designation as coadjutor and Megalius's consecration (Letters XXXI §4 and CCXIII §4, Registry row 11) — and his own three-phase, textured relationship to state coercion of the Donatists: an early opinion against any coercion (Letter XCIII §17), a real but narrow solicitation of legal protection argued for, but in the event not granted, at the African council of 401 (Letter 185 §§25–26), and a later, sustained defense of compulsion already in force.

**Institutional and conciliar record.** *The Code of Canons of the African Church* (Council of Carthage, 419; Registry row 26) is the collected conciliar law of the African church under Aurelius and Augustine — the institutional skeleton behind the Apiarius affair Doc_01 §2 and §7 discuss (419, running to c. 426, one recurring episode within Augustine's own thirty-five-year episcopate, not a defining thread through it).

**Formation-narrative evidence.** Pontius the Deacon's *Life and Passion of Cyprian* (Registry row 7) — Cyprian's own deacon, often counted the earliest Christian biography — carries the acclamation account Doc_01's own Round 9 review directly re-verified: "by the judgment of God and the favour of the people, he was chosen to the office of the priesthood and the degree of the episcopate while still a neophyte." Assessed further at §4 below.

**Adjacent, non-Native or partially-Native material, named for completeness.** Optatus of Milevis's *Against the Donatists* (Registry row 27) is provisionally homed on the sibling Donatism build rather than this one, per the corpus map's own note; Augustine's anti-Donatist *Answer to the Letters of Petilian* (Registry row 14) is Native here but not independently drawn on for a specific claim in this world's own construction, since it is Doc_01's own boundary that this world's material does not depend on characterizing the Donatist side of that dispute. Two exclusions are recorded explicitly rather than left implicit: *The Passion of the Scillitan Martyrs* (180 CE, Registry row 28) predates this world's own c. 246 beginning by 66 years and is Excluded as Out-of-Boundary — a real ancestor of this world's Latin-African Christian culture, per the corpus map's own note, but outside Doc_01's own evidentiary window; and Tertullian's own corpus (Registry row 29) is Excluded as a Named Comparandum, since Doc_01 §2, §6, and §7 all independently establish that Tertullian forged the Latin theological vocabulary Cyprian works within and Augustine inherits at one further remove, "without Tertullian himself being this world's own voice" — the exact shape of temptation a Named Comparandum exists to flag, since a builder reaching for Tertullian's own words to characterize Cyprian's or Augustine's preaching would be borrowing a different world's primary voice.

## 2. Author Gravity Assessment

Per the Construction Framework's five-dimension author-gravity discipline (Constitution Article 26), both anchor figures are assessed across Visibility, Representativeness, Influence, Limitations, and Transmission History.

**Cyprian of Carthage.**
- *Visibility:* High but narrow in duration — a ten-year episcopate (248/249–258), concentrated almost entirely on two acute crises (the Decian persecution and its lapsed-reconciliation aftermath, and the rebaptism dispute with Stephen), against which his own ordinary, non-crisis pastoral routine is comparatively under-visible in the surviving letters, which are disproportionately occasional (written to address a specific crisis) rather than routine.
- *Representativeness:* Represents an African metropolitan bishop's own voice specifically, at the head of a large, organized see capable of convening councils of dozens of bishops on short notice (Doc_01 §6) — not a small or marginal congregation, and not, on the rebaptism question, the eventual position of the wider church (Rome's own view prevailed on that specific point, per Doc_01 §5's own account of Augustine's later argument that Cyprian's specific ruling "was contrary to that which was afterwards brought to light... by the authority of a plenary Council").
- *Influence:* Foundational for this world's own ecclesiology of the one episcopate and its conciliar procedure on readmitting the lapsed (Doc_01 §7) — a formation pattern this document's own construction record finds continues, argued with rather than against, in Augustine's own later engagement with Cyprian's conciliar acts.
- *Limitations:* The corpus embeds letters by others (Cornelius, the Roman clergy, Firmilian of Caesarea, the confessors) under Cyprian's own name, per the corpus map's own note on CCEL's whole-body attribution — a transmission fact this Registry (row 1) discloses rather than treats as though every letter in the body is demonstrably Cyprian's own composition. The corpus is also occasional rather than systematic: it documents what crises provoked correspondence, not Cyprian's ordinary, undocumented weekly pastoral routine.
- *Transmission History:* Preserved through the Latin Western manuscript tradition as a canonical Father of the church on both sides of the later rebaptism-doctrine dispute his own letters record — his conciliar authority theory was cited by Augustine against the Donatists (this world's own boundary work, Doc_01 §7) and, per the sibling Donatism build's own Registry, was independently claimed by the Donatists themselves as their own third-century precedent. A figure preserved and argued from by parties who reached opposite later conclusions is not a transmission-selection risk in the way a one-sided hostile-quotation corpus is, but it does mean his own words are read by later readers already embedded in disputes he did not himself anticipate.

**Augustine of Hippo.**
- *Visibility:* Overwhelmingly high and unusually well-documented across his own thirty-five-year episcopate (395/396–430) — the largest surviving body of evidence for any figure in this project's own portfolio, spanning sermons, letters, treatises, and a first-person spiritual autobiography (the *Confessions*) with no real analogue elsewhere in this world's own corpus.
- *Representativeness:* Represents an African bishop writing from a position of increasing institutional confidence relative to Donatism specifically (post-405 Edict of Unity, post-411 Conference) — his own later anti-Donatist and anti-Pelagian works are not written from a contested, undecided position the way Cyprian's mid-crisis letters often are.
- *Influence:* Immense and lasting, both for how this world's own theology of grace, original sin, and the two cities becomes foundational for the entire subsequent Western tradition (Doc_01 §7), and — the specific risk this dimension exists to flag — for how disproportionately his own voice, rather than any other North African bishop's, has come to stand for "what the ordinary Latin pastoral bishop of this period was like," when the vendored corpus in fact preserves only two named individuals across a 184-year span.
- *Limitations:* Nearly the entire ordinary-believer and ordinary-congregant experience of this world survives only as it is refracted through one or the other bishop's own preaching and correspondence, not in any lay voice's own words — the missing-voices problem this document names directly at §6 below, not smoothed over here.
- *Transmission History:* Preserved through the Catholic institutional tradition's own manuscript transmission, as the doctrinally and institutionally victorious party's literature on every dispute he engaged (Donatism, Manichaeism, Pelagianism) — a transmission advantage this world shares with, and inherits in part from, the same institutional continuity the sibling Donatism build's own Author Gravity work names as working systematically against its own preservation.

**Pontius the Deacon**, treated separately as a distinct formation-narrative voice rather than folded into the Cyprian entry above, since his own perspective (an eyewitness deacon writing after his bishop's martyrdom) is not the same as Cyprian's own first-person letters.
- *Visibility:* Visible only through the one work attributed to him, *The Life and Passion of Cyprian* — no other writing by Pontius survives in this world's own corpus.
- *Representativeness:* Represents a close subordinate's own retrospective, hagiographically-shaped account of his bishop, not a peer bishop's or an ordinary congregant's perspective.
- *Influence:* Substantial for how Cyprian's own conversion and election are known at all — the acclamation account this document and Doc_01 both draw on (§1 above) has no surviving parallel in Cyprian's own letters.
- *Limitations:* Written after Cyprian's own martyrdom, by a deacon with every institutional and personal reason to idealize his bishop; the genre-level Author Gravity risk this fact creates is assessed directly at §4 below.
- *Transmission History:* Preserved and vendored inside the same ANF05 volume as Cyprian's own corpus, "often counted the earliest Christian biography" per the corpus map's own note — a transmission history bound up with, rather than independent of, Cyprian's own canonical status.

## 4. Formation Narrative Sources

Per Construction Framework Part II, hagiographic and narrative sources are assessed separately from theological treatises and institutional records, since they carry formation content differently than argument does.

**Pontius the Deacon, *The Life and Passion of Cyprian, Bishop and Martyr*.** *Authorship and date:* attributed to Pontius, Cyprian's own deacon; composed after Cyprian's martyrdom (258), within living memory of the events it narrates. *Proximity:* a first-generation, eyewitness-adjacent account — Pontius accompanied Cyprian into exile at Curubis, per the work's own narrative — not a later legendary elaboration. *Genre conventions:* early Christian biography with hagiographic shaping already present (the martyr's steadfastness, the manner of death as the completion of a formed life), though earlier and less developed in convention than the fuller passio form that follows it. *Formation ecology revealed:* how a Carthaginian congregation and its clergy understood their bishop's own path into office (the acclamation account, §1 and §2 above) and his conduct under persecution — a close subordinate's own testimony to what formation under this world's founding crisis looked like from inside the bishop's own household. *Author Gravity risk:* real and named directly — written by a deacon with every personal and institutional reason to idealize the bishop who ordained him, after that bishop's own martyrdom had already begun to be venerated; this document does not treat its account of Cyprian's own interior motivations as a neutral historical record on that basis, while treating its narration of external, checkable events (the exile to Curubis, the manner and circumstances of the martyrdom) as more reliable than its characterization of Cyprian's own inner disposition.

**Cyprian's own letters as formation-narrative-adjacent material.** Several of Cyprian's own letters on the lapsed and on Felicissimus's schism (Registry row 1) function partly as narrative — first-person accounts of a community's crisis and its resolution — rather than purely as doctrinal argument, though they remain classified as primary correspondence rather than as a separate narrative genre, consistent with how the corpus map itself treats them.

**No Tier 5 material identified.** Consistent with the Framework's prohibition, this document identifies no generated or illustrative narrative for this world. Where the evidence is thin — ordinary lay believers' own first-person experience of either crisis phase, specifically — that thinness is named as a finding at §6 below, not filled.

## 6. Source Asymmetries and Missing Voices

This world's own evidentiary asymmetry is not, primarily, a hostile-source problem in the way the sibling Donatism build's own is — both anchor voices speak in their own words, as bishops of the tradition this world's own construction centers on, not as an opponent's quotation. The asymmetry here is narrower and different in kind: **two named, elite, male, clerical voices carry nearly this entire world's own surviving record across a 184-year span.**

- **Elite/literate/male/clerical skew (Article 20), stated at its full weight rather than softened.** Every primary source named at §1 above is authored by, or preserved because of, one of two bishops or their own immediate circle (Pontius, Cyprian's deacon). No ordinary lay believer's own letter, no woman's own voice, and no non-episcopal clergy member's own extended first-person account survives anywhere in this world's own vendored corpus. This is a narrower version of the affirmative duty the sibling Donatism build discharges for its own community: there, the movement itself was numerically dominant and the access problem was source-mediation through a hostile filter; here, the access problem is that almost no one besides the bishop wrote, or had their writing preserved, at all. Doc_01 §2's own open question about how deeply the Punic/Berber substrate culture shaped ordinary congregational life (§5 above) is one direct consequence of this asymmetry, not a separate finding.
- **Gender (Article 20).** No named woman's own words or actions comparable in scale to the sibling Donatism build's own Lucilla finding have been identified in this world's own vendored corpus this session. This is stated as a genuine gap in this pass's own research, not as a claim that no such material exists — Cyprian's letters address consecrated virgins as a class (*On the Dress of Virgins*, Registry row 5) and Augustine's own correspondence includes several women by name (his mother Monica appears throughout the *Confessions*, though that reflects Augustine's own formation before this world's own boundary opens rather than a Carthage/Hippo congregant's), but no comparably load-bearing, individually-named female actor within this world's own 246–430 congregational life has been surfaced and verified this session. Flagged at §9 below as an open item for Doc_05/Doc_07's own further work, not closed here.
- **Ordinary believers, specifically.** Both bishops preach and write *to* their congregations constantly, but almost never *record* an ordinary believer speaking in their own voice, at length, in a way that survives independent of the bishop's own framing — the same structural problem Doc_01 §6's own Forces sketch names for the century-gap discussion, applied here across the whole 184-year span rather than only the gap between the two phases.
- **The Punic- and Berber-speaking rural substrate**, named but not resolved, per Doc_01 §2's own disclosure quoted at §5 above: this world's surviving sources overwhelmingly preserve the literate, Latin-trained episcopal voice, and how deeply the underlying substrate culture shaped ordinary congregational life specifically is not answered by anything identified in this pass.

**Affirmative Duty (Article 20).** The primary duty — naming structural absence — is discharged throughout this section. The secondary duty — bounded reconstruction, per the Framework's own three conditions (comparative evidence from similar communities, material evidence, or the world's own sources read against the grain) — is not exercised in this pass: no specific textual trace meeting any of the three bounding conditions for a marginalized voice's own perspective has been identified and verified this session, unlike the sibling Donatism build's own Lucilla case. This is disclosed as an open item at §9 below, to be revisited at Doc_05 and Doc_07 rather than filled here without a genuine trace to ground it.

**A routing note, following the discipline the sibling Donatism build's own §6 and `Imperial-Juridical-Christianity/Doc_02_Source_Ecology.md` §6 both already apply.** Article 20's Affirmative Duty is scoped to voices marginalized within this world's own community. How this world's eventual Representative characterizes this world's own opponents — the Donatists, the Manichaeans, the Pelagians, each already engaged as a live controversy in the primary sources at §1 above — is Article 23's future concern, not this section's.

**Forces lens on the source ecology (Construction Framework V7.4 Step 2; Forces Framework V1.1 Section 4).** Which sources speak to external forces: Cyprian's own letters are themselves the direct record of the Decian and Valerianic persecutions bearing on his own congregation, in his own voice, not mediated through an opponent's account — a genuinely different evidentiary situation from a world whose external-force record survives mainly through an acting party's own hostile documentation. Augustine's own anti-Donatist and anti-Manichaean corpora are the record of his own side actively engaging external rival systems, again in his own voice. What the silences reveal: the near-total absence of any surviving account of how an ordinary believer experienced either crisis phase, as distinct from how the bishop who led them through it experienced and narrated it. What survivorship patterns indicate about which forces were most threatening: both bishops' own writing survives in unusually full form precisely because each was, by the time of writing or by the time of later transmission, on the institutionally dominant and eventually canonized side of every dispute he engaged — a transmission advantage this world shares with the wider Catholic institutional tradition generally, distinct from the erasure or hostile-mediation patterns other confirmed worlds in this portfolio document for themselves.
