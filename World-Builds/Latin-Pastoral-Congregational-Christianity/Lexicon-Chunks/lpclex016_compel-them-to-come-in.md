## Quick Meaning

For us, this is the argument — reached late, and against an earlier opinion of his own — that a Christian ruler may rightly use compulsion to bring a separated party back into the one communion.

---

## World Meaning

The text is a parable: a householder's feast, the invited guests excuse themselves, and the servant is sent out to the highways and hedges to *compel them to come in, that my house may be filled.*

The argument turns on a distinction inside the parable itself. *Mark, now, how it was said in regard to those who came first, "bring them in;" it was not said, "compel them to come in,"—by which was signified the incipient condition of the Church, when it was only growing towards the position in which it would have* strength. The early church could not compel; it was small. The later church can; therefore the later church may. And the answer to the obvious objection — *you are of opinion that no one should be compelled to follow righteousness* — is that correction is what love does when it is serious.

**This is the end of a three-phase development, and this world's own record does not permit compressing it into one position.** An early opinion, by his own later account, against any coercion at all; then a real but narrow solicitation of legal protection, argued and in the event not granted; and only later the sustained defence of broader compulsion, after an argued change of mind. Cyprian never solicits state power at all, and nothing in his phase corresponds to this.

---

## Ecological Function

This term is where this world's second phase meets the power available to it, and it is the sharpest test of the claim that communion is preserved rather than broken: the coercion is argued as recovering a separated party rather than expelling one still inside. It bears directly on schism and on the boundary questions in heresy.

**It is not an organizing force of this ecology, and that is a finding rather than an omission.** Doc_04 §2 tested the illegal-to-established shift as a candidate gravity and **declined to advance it**, finding that nothing in this world's ecology organizes *around* it — the shift changes the instruments available to a bishop, not the thing a bishop is.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing:**
A modern participant is likely to hear a straightforward endorsement of religious persecution and to attach it to inquisitions and wars of religion — and to assume it was this world's settled position throughout.

**World Hearing:**
A late, argued position reached against the author's own earlier opinion, defended in the idiom of correction and recovery rather than punishment, in a world where the same author's predecessor a century earlier had no access to state power at all and never sought it.

## Key Sources

Letter 185 §§25–26 (Registry row 12), the imperial-coercion defence; Letter XCIII §17 (row 43), the retrospective account of the earlier contrary opinion — both directly quoted and re-verified across Doc_01's review rounds. The parable exegesis quoted above is Augustine's own, re-verified at source in `npnf101`.

**Author Gravity note, high and specific.** This doctrine is known in this world's corpus **only through Augustine's own advocacy, in his own defence, with no Donatist first-person answer surviving in this world's Native record.** Doc_02 §2 records that he writes from a position of increasing institutional confidence relative to Donatism.

**Editorial-apparatus warning, and this one is a theological verdict, not a gloss.** The vendored `npnf101` volume's own 19th-century preface says Augustine defended coercion *"by a false exegesis"* of this text, and calls the position *"least satisfactory to Protestant readers."* **That is Schaff's judgement, not Augustine's and not this world's**, and it sits in the same volume as the primary text. It is excluded from this entry entirely and must not be absorbed into any rendering of this world's own voice.

## CT Contest Type

**Relationship to present-day traditions**, and secondarily **Meaning**.

The contest is live and substantial. This text is the single most-cited patristic warrant in the modern historiography of religious coercion, and the argument runs over how far Augustine's position is the ancestor of later inquisitorial practice, how far his own framing as pastoral correction should be credited, and whether the three-phase development in his own writing is a genuine change of mind on the evidence or a retrospective self-presentation. Serious readers differ on all three.

Secondarily a **Meaning** contest: what *cogite intrare* licenses in Augustine's own terms — the scope, the agent, and the severity he actually contemplates — is itself argued.

**What this entry therefore does not do:** it does not adjudicate the historiographical question, does not endorse or condemn the position, and does not characterize the Donatists. Doc_01 binds this build explicitly against compressing the three-phase sequence, and that binding is honoured in the World Meaning above.
