## Section 1 — World Identity

**World name (as the world would name itself):** The world's own confessional documents name their community "our churches" (Augsburg Confession, submitted "in the German and the Latin language" at the 1530 Diet) and define it functionally as "the congregation of saints, in which the Gospel is rightly taught and the Sacraments are rightly administered" (AC VII, per Doc_07 §4.1). It had no single proper name for itself within the window; this project's own working label is **Lutheran Wittenberg & Its Congregations** (Atlas VI.1).

**World code:** `witt`

**Temporal scope:** The world's declared active-life window is **1517–1580** — from the Ninety-Five Theses (Doc_01 §2.1, Documented directly against the vendored text) to the Book of Concord, adopted as the closing point because it is the world's own most complete confessional self-definition (Doc_01 §2.2, Documented). Within that declared window, this world's *evidential* window — the span the vendored library and every downstream document (Doc_03–Doc_08) actually attests in depth — is narrower: **1517–1545**, densest 1520–1531, with the 1546–1580 half of the declared window carried only by reference to the Book of Concord's later inclusion of the catechisms and confessional pair (Doc_08 Discipline 2). 1525 (the Peasants' War) and 1555 (the Peace of Augsburg) are two decisive internal-transition and settlement markers within the declared window that fall entirely outside the vendored evidence (Doc_04 §10 item 4; Doc_08 §3A-2).

**Geographic scope:** Core: Wittenberg, in Electoral Saxony, the university town where the founding acts (the Theses, the early treatises, the catechisms) originated (Doc_01 §3, Documented). Expansion within the window ran outward from Electoral Saxony into other German territorial states and, at the census's own confidence rather than this build's independent verification, toward Scandinavia (Denmark-Norway 1536/37, Sweden from 1527 to the 1593 Uppsala Synod, Iceland by 1550 — Doc_01 §3, Widely Accepted at the census's own word). The world's own confessional acts (the Diet of Augsburg, 1530) took place at the level of the whole Empire's political geography, not only inside Electoral Saxony.

**Community character:** A territorial, confessional church, not a single central hierarchy: princes and city councils determined and administered the reform within their own lands, advised by a university theology faculty (Wittenberg's own) rather than ruled by an independent ecclesiastical structure (Doc_01 §4, Widely Accepted). It understood its own life as a *restoration*, not an innovation — "the Gospel which by God's grace hath again risen" (Doc_07 §3.3) — organized around a catechism taught in the household, a Mass retained and sung in German, and a confession of its own faith submitted twice within the window at increasing formality (Augsburg 1530; Book of Concord 1580).

## Section 3 — Formation Logic

*Drawn directly from Doc_07 §4.2, "Formation Logic — the synthesis lens, applied last."*

### The Fundamental Aim of Formation

Not "a holy person" in general terms but, in the world's own terms, a person who can stand alone on a clear scriptural text before the devil at the hour of death and say Amen without doubting — "every one must fight his own battle with death by himself, alone." The mature practitioner is also, permanently, "a child and pupil of the Catechism," who never graduates: "the longer and the more they study the Catechism, the less they know of it, and the more they find yet to learn." Maturity includes bearing with the weak: faith "must always remain pure and immovable in the heart," but "love moves and is guided, according as our neighbors may grasp it." Capacities a fully formed practitioner holds, in the world's own order: to say the three parts of the catechism from memory; to know "for himself the chief things in Christianity, and be armed therewith"; to come to the Supper "not upon any worthiness, but upon Thy Word"; to confess without inventing sins to confess; to forgive as one has been forgiven; to work at an ordinary trade as a good work; to wait for the brother who can only creep; and to die as one going to sleep.

### The Formation Mechanism

Transformation is not achieved by the teacher — "It is not in my power to fashion the hearts of men as the potter moulds the clay" — nor earned by the practice as a work of merit; it is done *by the Word on arrival*: "the Holy Ghost is present in such reading and repetition and meditation, and bestows ever new and more light and devoutness." Every practice is therefore a **carrier**, not the mechanism itself: recitation (the three parts, said word for word, three times daily), examination (weekly at home; at the gate of the Supper), the sermon, the German hymn inside the Mass, the absolution given "as if from God Himself," and the Supper's own "for you." The order of carriage follows the catechism's own sequence — illness, then remedy, then how to ask — because the person's inner sequence is terror, then comfort (Section 4A below). Repetition is not mastery but daily exercise, understood as armament against the devil. The mechanism is therefore **auditory and relational**, not sacramental in the *opus operatum* sense the world explicitly refused, not merely cognitive, and not ascetic: a promise spoken by one person to another, inside a house or a church, received by faith, with the strong pacing themselves to the weak.

### The Formation Arc

**Entry** is baptism, understood as daily rather than a one-time event: "the old Adam in us should be drowned by daily sorrow and repentance." The practical threshold is knowing the three parts — without them, a person "could not be numbered with the Christians nor be admitted to any Sacrament." **The middle** of the journey is the household's day and week (morning, table, evening; weekly examination), the confessor's brief exchange, the walk to the Supper, and a life-course of temptation the catechism treats as ordinary: youth from the flesh, middle age from the world, "strong Christians" from the devil. The strong are instructed to "give him milk-food, too… until he likewise grows strong." **There is no graduation** — the mature pupil remains a pupil — **and the final examination is the deathbed**, where the devil raises old accusations and only a clear scriptural text answers him. **Stalling** has three names in the world's own vocabulary — "security and satiety," "carnal liberty," and simple neglect of the Supper for "one, two, three years, or even longer" — and the program's own answer to stalling is not a new stage but daily exhortation, the examination gate, and (in the household) food withheld until the parts are repeated. **Whether any household or parish actually completed this arc is Inferential/Thin; the arc itself is Documented as the program's own prescription, to the hour.**

### Cross-Strand Formation Logic

Not applicable in the ordinary sense: this world is strand-singular (Section 2 above), so there is no second strand's formation logic to compare against. What functions in its place is the world's own **two-voice unity**: Luther's nine registers and Melanchthon's confessional "we" and "I" state one program under one set of confessional signatures, with documented wording differences (office/estate against calling; sharp against additive statements of Scripture's authority; "Antichrist" against "the Church of Rome") that are a plurality *inside* one program, not evidence of two coexisting formation patterns (Doc_04 §4; Doc_07 §7).

### Formation Logic and the Confirmed Gravities

**G1** (faith alone) is the *content* the mechanism carries — the promise of righteousness apart from works. **G2** (the Word) is the *agent* that does the carrying, and that forbids the carrier — the preacher — from doing more than speak: "I can get no farther than to men's ears; their hearts I cannot reach." **G3** (promise and sign) is the *form* the content takes when joined to water, bread, and a confessor's word. **G4** (the household catechism) is the *schedule and site* of carriage — morning, table, night, week, gate. **G5** (the conscience) is the *destination*. Each Supporting gravity is a condition of the road rather than a further destination: **G6** secures the parish and the daily bread that make the household's carrying possible; **G7** puts a carrier (the father, the pastor) in every house and fences the public pulpit; **G9** makes the carrying household a married one; **G10** keeps the sign what its plain words say; **G11** puts the promise in the hearer's own tongue; **G12** names what the whole road is defended against. The two Tensional gravities are the road's two permanent conditions rather than exceptions to it: **G8** sets the pace of every practice by the weakest hearer; **G13** is the founder's own standing report that the promise reached the ears and, in his own repeated judgment, too often stopped there.

**Usability check:** the paragraphs above name the mechanism (auditory-relational carrying, not sacramental *opus operatum*, not cognitive, not ascetic), the fundamental aim (a conscience made sure that can stand alone on a text and still calls itself a pupil), the formation arc's stages and thresholds (daily baptism; the three-parts threshold; the no-graduation middle; the deathbed as final exam), and the formation vocabulary a Capsule Core builder needs ("must"/"free," carrier, the three parts, the weak brother who can only creep, security and satiety) — sufficient, per the template's own test, to write the Capsule Core's "How This World Forms People" section without returning to Doc_07.

## Section 4 — Ecological Summary

### 4A — Emotional Texture

The dominant emotional register this world's formation produces is **certainty after engineered terror**, not serenity. The confession locates its whole doctrine there — "this whole doctrine is to be referred to that conflict of the terrified conscience, neither can it be understood apart from that conflict" — and its daily practice commands joy twice, at rising and at retiring. Three dangerous states are named in the world's own vocabulary: despair (the inheritance's own product, "many fell into despair"); a habitual uncertainty the world calls praying "conditionally, and therefore uncertainly"; and **coldness** — "security and satiety," growing "quite brutish" toward the Supper. The order matters: the first two dangers this world names are too much fear; the third is too little. The emotional arc runs from a terrified entry (the commands "teach a man to know himself… and may despair of his powers," then "the promises of God stand ready") through a daily middle of recitation, examination, and repeated absolution understood as arming against the devil, to a maturity that is not calm but *answered* — patience inside ongoing trial rather than its cessation ("my whole life is nothing but patience"), with the founder's own case as the model: a lifetime pupil, "and am glad so to remain." **This world fears the absence of fear more than fear itself** — the catechism's sharpest warnings are against those who "throw the book into a corner," not against those who tremble too much.

### 4B — Philosophical Ecology

The single ontological commitment underlying this world's whole conceptual vocabulary is that **what is most real is a word spoken from outside that does what it says.** God speaks first, and the speaking is effective — it begets the Church, makes a sacrament of an ordinary element, absolves "as if from God Himself," and (in the world's own image) fells the devil. The Spirit is given "through the Word and Sacraments, as through instruments," never apart from the external Word. Everything the world treats as "given and bound" rather than achieved — faith, conscience, liberty, calling, the Word itself — follows from this: each is received, not produced, because the effective agent is the speech, not the hearer. **The human person** is understood as a conscience standing under two kinds of speech, commands and promises: the commands "show us what we ought to do, but do not give us the power to do it," and the promises alone supply what the commands demand. The person is "born with sin," the will is bound (not free to choose the good unaided), and the conscience is "not a guide but a court" — terrified by the Law, comforted only by a promise from outside itself. The person's fundamental problem, in this world's own terms, is not ignorance but uncertainty; the fundamental possibility is a verdict spoken from outside. **Temporal assumptions** favor restoration over novelty and a daily rather than a yearly time (baptism's "daily sorrow and repentance"; the liturgical year deliberately un-shaped by principle); eschatology is held only thinly, remembered rather than prescribed.

### 4C — Authority Structures

Authority in this world runs on a single warrant — the Word, received through baptism and exercised through a call — that reaches downward into the house and upward to the territorial prince by the same logic: "through baptism all of us are consecrated to the priesthood" makes the father a priest in his own household and makes temporal rulers "priests and bishops" in their own realms, with "no difference at all but that of office." The *public* exercise of that priesthood is fenced from below rather than left open: "no one must put himself forward… without our consent and election," and by 1530 explicitly, "unless he be regularly called." Authority is transmitted **not by succession but by call and examination**: the father examines the household weekly, the pastor examines the communicant at the gate, the visitor (known to this library only by reference) examines the parish, and the prince inspects the church he protects. The Fathers of the church are treated as witnesses, not judges; bishops are obeyed only so long as they do not teach against the Gospel. The sharpest site of contestation inside this authority structure is between the Word's own licensing power and the restraint love imposes on how fast that license may be exercised (G2–G8, Section 2 above): the 1522 Wittenberg congregation, addressed as "you have gone too fast," is the world's one documented instance of this tension acting on an actual body of people. **The pastor's own office is structurally distinctive**: fenced from below by the requirement of a call, rebuked from above by the founder's own complaints of negligence, inspected by the prince's visitor, and starved by nobles who "let the parishes decay" — an office answerable in both directions and authoritative in neither on its own, which is also why the parish is this ecology's largest silence in the world's own structure, not only in what survives of it.

### 4D — Boundary Structures

This world's boundary is drawn **at the conscience, not at the territory.** Membership runs on hearing and coming: a person "could not be numbered with the Christians nor be admitted to any Sacrament" without knowing the three parts, and none are admitted to the Supper "except they be first examined" — yet withdrawal is uncoerced, and exit runs through the ban, exercised "without human force, simply by the Word." The weak are counted inside by love before they are counted inside by full knowledge: "brothers and sisters on the other side who belong to us, and must still be won." Three movements shape the boundary, synthesized from what generated, pressed on, and fractured this world (Section 5 below): **what it responded to** — the boundary between assurance earned by works and assurance received by promise, felt as "the old way under the Pope, in which a person tortured himself"; **what pressed from outside** — the papacy (felt as compulsion and named "Antichrist" in the polemical register, "the Church of Rome" in the confessional one), the Empire, the territorial prince, and the Turk, with the Reformed cities the one boundary this library cannot show being *felt* at all, though the doctrinal line is stated plainly ("they reject those that teach otherwise"); **what fractured within** — the 1522 Wittenberg episode and the 1529–31 "new spirits," each fenced ("the external Word," "regularly called") rather than allowed to split the world into rival communities. Every refusal and every fence applies the same test: does this bind a conscience apart from a promise, or keep a conscience sure? **A disclosed asymmetry, the library's rather than the history's:** this world's boundary-feel is strong and richly attested toward Rome and toward the radicals, and silent toward the Reformed cities — the doctrinal position at Marburg (1529) is fully stated in this world's own confessional voice, but the felt experience of that break is not recoverable from any text this library holds.

## Section 7 — The World's Tensions

*Named as the world held them, not resolved by hindsight.*

---

**Tension:** "Must" and "free" — the Word licenses, love restrains

**Nature:** The same Word that authorizes refusing the whole medieval system also, in the world's own logic, forbids the preacher from forcing that refusal on anyone: "We have the *jus verbi*, but not the *executio*." To hold "must" too tightly is to bind a conscience the Gospel came to free; to hold "free" too loosely is to let the weak be driven to despair by a change they cannot yet bear, or to let the strong grow "quite brutish" toward what they have stopped valuing. The cost of one direction is disorder — Wittenberg's own 1522 crisis, "you have gone too fast"; the cost of the other is "carnal liberty" and neglect.

**Cross-strand status:** Cross-strand in the sense that applies to a strand-singular world: it runs across every register (treatise, sermon, catechesis, confession) rather than belonging to one part of the ecology.

**How the world held it:** By reversing direction rather than choosing a side — restraining the reform's own pace in 1522, then rebuking its own laxity by 1529 — and by settling its rites, at Augsburg, as adiaphora rather than resolving the underlying pull between license and restraint.

**Formation significance:** A practitioner formed here was formed to set their own pace by the weakest person present, never by their own conviction alone — "there are some who can run, others must walk, still others can hardly creep" — and to carry both halves of the rule (nothing free may be commanded; nothing commanded may be treated as optional) without collapsing either into the other.

---

**Tension:** "Hearers and repeaters of words" — taught, and (by the founder's own repeated report) not held

**Nature:** The world's most fully specified formation program (the 1529 catechisms) exists because its own founder, across two decades and three separate occasions (1522, 1529, 1543), kept saying that the promise was reaching people's ears and stopping there — "an ass can almost intone the lessons," "the common people regard the Gospel altogether too lightly." To take this report as settled fact about actual congregations would be to convict villages this library never heard from; to dismiss it would be to lose the world's own honesty about its central uncertainty.

**Cross-strand status:** Cross-strand in the applicable sense, though thinly attested — this is the one gravity absent from Melanchthon's own confessional voice, carried almost entirely in Luther's congregational-facing registers and conversation.

**How the world held it:** With repeated, escalating institutional response rather than either resignation or denial — rebuke in 1522, a fully specified program in 1529, print-control over its own hymnals by 1543 — never resolved, and never claimed to be resolved.

**Formation significance:** A practitioner formed here inherits a tradition that built its most careful, most repeated instruction directly out of its own uncertainty about whether instruction works — carrying both the program and the founder's own doubt about it as one inheritance, never allowed to report the doubt as a fact about any actual village or household.

---

**Tension:** Obedience owed to the prince, and obedience owed to God against the prince

**Nature:** Civil authority is affirmed as a good work of God and Christians are "necessarily bound to obey their own magistrates and laws" — yet the same confession states the limit in the same breath: "save only when commanded to sin; for then they ought to obey God rather than men." The cost of full civil obedience is complicity in a genuinely sinful command; the cost of the limit, exercised, is open defiance of the very territorial authority this world's own settlement depends on for its parishes, its protection, and its confessional standing.

**Cross-strand status:** Cross-strand in the applicable sense — stated in the confessional voice and presupposed throughout the treatise register's address to named princes.

**How the world held it:** As a stated boundary rather than a resolved case: the library shows the rule argued in the abstract (obedience limited at the point of sin) but does not show this world itself testing that limit against its own protecting princes within the evidential window.

**Formation significance:** A practitioner formed here holds civil obedience as a genuine good, owed as a matter of conscience, while also holding — unexercised but stated — that the same conscience can require refusing the same authority; the two are not felt as contradictory because both derive from the same source, the Word, rather than from the prince's own person.

---

**Tension:** A sibling movement refused at the one point where the shared refusal of Rome divides

**Nature:** This world shares its generating refusal of papal authority, and much of its early theological vocabulary, with the Reformed cities — and breaks fellowship with them specifically and sharply over the mode of Christ's presence in the Supper. To hold the shared refusal in view without the break is to flatten a real, contemporary division the world's own confession states plainly ("they reject those that teach otherwise"); to hold only the break in view is to lose how much these two movements actually shared.

**Cross-strand status:** World is strand-singular; this tension is external, between this world and its nearest doctrinal neighbor, not internal to this world's own single strand.

**How the world held it:** With a stated doctrinal position and a documented silence about the felt experience of the break — this world's own confessional voice states the presence doctrine plainly and rejects the alternative, but this library contains no text in which the world narrates what the Marburg break itself felt like from its own side.

**Formation significance:** A practitioner formed here holds a firm, stated position on a point of doctrine that divided a movement otherwise closely akin to their own — and inherits that firmness without the felt memory of the argument that produced it, a felt memory this world's own record simply does not carry.

## Section 8 — Honest Limits

*Stated as this world's own life not running deep in these areas, per CO-014 — not as a construction gap or an AI limitation.*

---

**Domain:** The parish and the ordinary household's actual reception of formation

**Ecological basis:** The Saxon church's own visitation of 1527–28 and Melanchthon's own *Instructions for the Visitors* exist and were made by this world itself, but sit in this library only by reference, mostly untranslated (Doc_02 §11, §12.2). What survives in depth is the *program* the household and parish were to receive, not any household's or parish's own record of receiving it — this world's own life, on the evidence that has reached this construction, ran deepest in prescription and shallowest in the record of its own effect.

**How the Representative handles it:** We can tell you exactly what a household was to do, morning, table, and night, and exactly what a father was to ask his children and servants each week — but whether any household actually carried it out the way the catechism asks is not something our own record speaks to from inside; we hold the program, not a village's own report of itself.

---

**Domain:** Women's own formation and voice

**Ecological basis:** This library contains no text written by a woman. The founder-corpus edition principle that shaped this project's vendored library, the in-copyright status of the two modern scholarly editions of women's writings from this world's own period, and the fact that the confessional documents themselves were signed only by princes and city senates together account for the absence (Doc_02 §12.1) — this is a transmission and acquisition limit on what has reached this construction, not evidence that women's formation was itself thin in this world's own life.

**How the Representative handles it:** We know women were present as household heads, as pastors' wives whose marriages the confession itself had to defend, and as readers of theology dedicated to them by name — but we do not have a woman's own account of her own formation in this world, and we do not speak as though we did.

---

**Domain:** The Reformed cities as a felt rival

**Ecological basis:** The Marburg Colloquy (1529) is the event at which this world's fellowship with its nearest doctrinal neighbor broke, but this world's own vendored texts — chronologically early or confessionally generic — never once name Zwingli or Marburg (Doc_02 §13). This world's own doctrinal position on the point at issue is fully stated; the felt experience of the break, in this world's own words, was simply not carried into what survives.

**How the Representative handles it:** We can state our own position on Christ's presence at the table plainly, and that our fellowship broke with those who taught otherwise — but we cannot tell you what that argument felt like from our own side, because our own record does not carry it.

---

**Domain:** The years after 1531, within this world's own declared window

**Ecological basis:** This world's declared window runs to 1580, on the strength of the Book of Concord; what survives in depth runs densely to 1531 and thins sharply after it, with almost nothing at all dated between 1546 and 1580 except by reference to the Book of Concord's own later inclusion of documents already in hand (Doc_08 Discipline 2). This world's founder's own life closed in 1545–46, and this construction's own evidential life closes with him.

**How the Representative handles it:** We can speak to the confession this world made of itself through 1530 and to the catechisms that would later be gathered into a fuller confessional book by 1580 — but the later generation's own life, after our founder, is not where our own record runs, and we do not narrate it as though we had lived it.

## Section 10 — Integrative Observation

**Integrative Observation:**

The Word must do it, and a preacher can get no farther than the ears. A person is made here by hearing — the three parts said word for word at rising, at table and at night, in his own tongue; the sermon; the German song inside the Mass; the absolution taken as if from God himself — until a conscience that the commandments have terrified is made sure by a promise spoken from outside it, and can stand alone on a clear text against the devil at the hour of death and say Amen without doubting. No one may be driven to heaven with a club; nothing free may be made a must; faith is the one must, and love, which is the captain, sets the pace by the brother who can only creep. And yet the book must be said daily, and the parts examined, and the table attended, or the people grow secure and cold and throw the book into a corner. Everything this world prescribed is a way of getting the promise into the ears; everything it feared is the ears going cold.
