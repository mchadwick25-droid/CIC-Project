### 1.1 Who the participants were, as the library shows them

**The Article 20 finding, stated before anything is built on it.** No text in this library is by a woman, a servant, a peasant, a village pastor, or a visitor. The people this world was formed *in* are visible only as the founder, the confessor and the princes addressed them: as "the ordinary Christian, who cannot read the Scriptures" (v2 13182, per Doc_04 §3 G4), as "children and the simple-minded" (LC 230–231), as "the uneducated" who must be taught to confess (SC 410), as "children and servants" who "should come to the table modestly and with folded hands" (SC 583–584), as "a servant, maid, etc." who "complained about my master's wife and sworn at her" (SC 445–451), as "a master or a lady of the house" who has "not faithfully led my children, servants or wife to God's glory" and has "charged him too much, cheated him and sold him badly made goods" (SC 454–459), as "rude, old persons who knew nothing and still know nothing of these things, and who, nevertheless, go to Baptism and the Lord's Supper" (LC 245–247), and as the "dear friends" of one congregation who "have gone too fast" (v2 14676, 14727–14728). Doc_02 §7 ranked the ordinary-participant evidence from least to most mediated and found it "thin, and almost entirely mediated by the founder"; nothing read this pass changes that, and no ordinary believer's psychology is reconstructed here.

**The founder, as a formed person.** The one participant whose formation the library narrates from inside is Luther's own, and only as he chose to tell it: a friar "so full and drunken, yea, so besotted in papal doctrine that, out of my great zeal, I would have been ready to do murder" who "shrank from the Last Day with great anxiety and fear and terror, and yet from the depths of my heart desired to be saved" (v1 358–360, 373–375, the 1545 preface — Contested as self-characterization, per Doc_02 §9); who "have grown by writing and by teaching others, and not one of those who, starting with nothing, have in a trice become the most exalted and most learned doctors" (v1 395–398); who in 1529 still "do as a child who is being taught the Catechism, and every morning, and whenever I have time, I read and say, word for word, the Ten Commandments, the Creed, the Lord's Prayer, the Psalms, etc." and "must remain a child and pupil of the Catechism, and am glad so to remain" (LC 102–107); who says "I, said Luther, have every day enough to do to pray. And when I lay me down to rest, I pray the Lord's Prayer, and afterwards take hold on two or three sentences out of the Bible, and so betake myself to sleep" (TT 3120–3123, Bell's English, Contested as verbatim; corrected this revision, Round 1 finding C4); and who, in the household register, must "have patience with Kate my wife" (TT 3013–3015, per Doc_04 §3 G9). The conversion narrative proper is not in the library (Doc_02 §0).

**The pastor.** Addressed, rebuked and prescribed for, never heard: "All Pastors and Preachers, that They Should Daily Exercise Themselves in the Catechism" (LC 44–46); "very negligent… some from great and high art… but others from sheer laziness and care for their paunches" (LC 51–54); freed "from the unprofitable and burdensome babbling of the Seven Canonical Hours" and asked instead to "morning, noon, and evening, read a page or two in the Catechism, the Prayer-book, the New Testament, or elsewhere in the Bible, and pray the Lord's Prayer for themselves and their parishioners" (LC 71–76); to be honored by "what Hearers owe their Pastors" (SC 619); to hear an accusation "before the whole congregation" and "exclude him and put him under the ban before the whole assembly" (v2 15639–15646); and, in the confession's voice, married — "our priests were desirous to avoid these open scandals, they married wives" (AC 713–715, per Doc_04 §3 G9). Whether any pastor did any of this is not in the library (Discipline 5).

**The household: father, lady of the house, children, servants, maids.** The Small Catechism's every part is headed "The Simple Way a Father Should Present [it] to His Household" (SC 45, 166, 227, 357, 494) and its Home Chart lists the estates it presupposes: bishops, pastors and preachers; hearers; earthly authorities; those under authority; husbands; wives; parents; children; "Servants, Maids, Hired Hands and Workers"; "the Man and Woman of the House"; young people; widows; "Everyone in General" (SC 615–665). The household the catechisms address is stratified and served — "his domestics, ma-servants and maid-servants" (LC 335–336, typo as found) — which Doc_02 §6 already read as Documented presupposition and Inferential/Thin as to how widely such households existed outside towns. The father's authority is stated as God's own ordering: "among us there must necessarily be such inequality and ordered difference, and therefore God commands it to be observed, that you obey me as your father, and that I have the supremacy" (LC 1072–1075). Women's presence is treated separately at §1.3.

**The nobleman and the prince.** Present as addressees of every register (Doc_02 §1.1), as signatories (AC 1557–1567 [R37]), as the "louts and scrimps, who declare that there is no longer any need either of pastors or preachers… and so they are content to let the parishes decay and become desolate, and pastors and preachers to suffer distress and hunger a plenty, just as it becomes crazy Germans to do" (LC 92–97), and — the world's own emblem for them — as the ones on whose coat-of-arms "it would be very proper to place… a loaf of bread instead of a lion, or a wreath of rue, or to stamp it upon the coin, to remind both them and their subjects that by their office we have protection and peace, and that without them we could not eat and retain our daily bread" (LC 3480–3484).

**The student and the charity-scholar.** "Our young people (who both will and ought to be instructed in music and other sciences)" (Hy 919–920); Walter's "charity-scholars, collecting their bread," who "should sing from door to door Latin Hymns, Anthems and Responses, appropriate to the season" (Hy 775–777 [R45], late, three hands); "the students and boys" for whom Vespers were re-established (Hy 772–774). The university itself is a register, not a documented formation site (Doc_04 §2.2).

**The monk and the nun, leaving or staying.** The 1522 sermons address them as the congregation's own question: "Any priest, monk or nun who cannot restrain the desires of the flesh, should marry, and thus relieve the burden of conscience. But see to it that you be well-armed and fortified, so that you can stand before God and the world when you are assailed, and especially when the devil attacks you in the hour of death. It is not enough to say: This man or that has done the same, I followed the example of the crowd" (v2 14990–14997); "He who can do so without harm… may wear a cowl or a tonsure, since it will not injure his faith; wearing a cowl will not kill him" (v2 15024–15026); "Would to God all monks and nuns could hear this sermon" (v2 15013–15014). The Teutonic knights and the Brussels martyrs are the other two monastic figures (Doc_04 §3 G9, G7); the founder himself is the third.

**The dying.** The one participant every register reaches: "The challenge of death comes to us all, and no one can die for another. Every one must fight his own battle with death by himself, alone. We can shout into one another's ears, but every one must be prepared finally to meet death alone. I will not be with you then, nor you with me" (v2 14641–14644); the one who "on his deathbed… thinks, Woe is me, for I have eaten meat and I am lost!" (v2 14799–14801); the one for whom "the devil attacks… in the hour of death" (v2 14994–14995); the household praying "that when at last our final hour has come, He will grant us a blessed death, and, in His grace, bring us to Himself from this valley of tears" (SC 345–347); and, in 1542, the one buried with "no dirges nor lamentations, but comforting songs" (Hy 983–984).

### 1.2 The body and the day: rising, work, table, bed, food, sex, sickness, death

This world thought about the body in the catechism's idiom — a creature "not because I've earned it or deserved it" (SC 182–183) and a battlefield "where the evil enemy" seeks to "gain power over me" (SC 555) — and it prescribed the body's day with a precision no other dimension of the ecology has.

**Rising.** "As soon as you get out of bed in the morning, you should bless yourself with the sign of the Holy Cross and say: May the will of God, the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit be done! Amen. Then, kneeling or standing, say the creed and pray the Lord's Prayer" (SC 543–548), then, "if you wish," the little prayer — "Let Your holy Angels be with me, so that the evil enemy will not gain power over me" (SC 549–556) — and then: "After that, with joy go about your work and perhaps sing a song inspired by the Ten Commandments or your own thoughts" (SC 557–558). The sign of the cross, kneeling, and the creed are kept from the inheritance; the joy and the song are the prescription's own.

**Work.** Not a formation site of its own but the place good works are done: "when they work at their trade, walk, stand, eat, drink, sleep" (v1 6863–6865, per Doc_04 §3 G7); "God careth and provideth for us, but we must labour" (TT 2407, Bell's English, one of the "Brief Sentences of the Catechism, according as Luther used to teach and instruct his family at home," TT 2394–2395). The fourth petition's "daily bread" is glossed to include "Food, drink, clothing, shoes, house, yard, fields, cattle, money, possessions, a devout spouse, devout children, devout employees, devout and faithful rulers, good government, good weather, peace, health, discipline, honor, good friends, faithful neighbors" (SC 303–308) — a household economy, not an individual's meal.

**Table.** "The children and servants should come to the table modestly and with folded hands and say: All eyes look to you, O Lord…" (SC 583–587), then the Lord's Prayer and a blessing; after eating, "they should modestly fold their hands" again, then the Lord's Prayer and thanks (SC 597–607). The meal is also the catechism's sanction: the three parts are to be recited "when they arise in the morning when they sit down to their meals, and when they retire at night; and until they repeat them, they should be given neither food nor drink" (LC 331–334) — a rule the world states as a father's plain duty, which Article 23 requires be rendered as such and not as the analyst would judge it.

**Bed.** The evening blessing mirrors the morning's — the sign of the cross, the creed, the Lord's Prayer, the little prayer with its angels (SC 562–573) — and closes: "After this, go to sleep immediately with joy" (SC 575). Luther's own version: the Lord's Prayer and "two or three sentences out of the Bible" (TT 3121–3122).

**Food and fasting.** Free: "it is allowable to eat milk, butter, eggs, cheese and meat every day, whether it be Sunday or Friday, Lent or Advent" (v2 15963–15969, per Doc_03 8.6); "fasting and other physical preparations are excellent disciplines for the body" but do not make one worthy (SC 530–532); "If you cannot give up meat without harm to yourself, or if you are sick, you may eat whatever you like, and if any one takes offence, let him be offended" (v2 15229–15231) — but "if you should be pressed to eat fish instead of meat on Friday… as the pope has done with his fools' laws… I will eat meat in spite of you" (v2 15238–15244), and, toward "some who are still weak in faith," "we must bear patiently with them and not use our liberty" (v2 15253–15259). The body's food is the "must"/"free" distinction (G8) applied at the table.

**Sex and marriage.** The world states it as nature: "where nature has its course, as it is implanted by God, it is not possible to remain chaste without marriage. For flesh and blood remain flesh and blood, and the natural inclination and excitement have their course without let or hindrance, as everybody sees and feels" (LC 1726–1729); God "created man and woman separately (as is evident), not for lewdness, but that they should [legitimately] live together, be fruitful, beget children, and nourish and train them to the honor of God" (LC 1693–1696, bracket Bente/Dau's); marriage is "not a peculiar estate, but the most common and noblest estate, which pervades all Christendom" (LC 1714–1716), with "some exceptions (although few) whom God has especially excepted… or whom He has released by a high, supernatural gift" (LC 1722–1725). The Sixth Commandment's explanation for the household is "so that our words and actions will be clean and decent and so that everyone will love and honor their spouses" (SC 101–102). The monastic body is the counter-case (G9), and it is felt from within as *conscience*, not appetite: the monk "too weak to keep the vow of chastity, should conscientiously examine himself; if heart and conscience are strong… let him marry" (v2 15010–15013).

**Sickness, plague, and the list of calamities.** The Fifth Commandment's household gloss: "help him and care for him when he is ill" (SC 93). The fourth petition's catalogue: "lightning, hail, fire, flood, poison, pestilence, cattle-plague, war and bloodshed, famine, destructive beasts, wicked men" (LC 3507–3509). Prayer "availeth more to health, than the physician's physic" (TT 3036–3037, Bell's English, quoting Ecclesiasticus). Epidemic disease is, as Doc_02 §6 found, a stock item in lists; the 1527 plague tract is not vendored, and nothing read this pass adds a plague *event* in the world's own voice.

**Death and burial.** The body's end is prescribed as sleep: "we Christians… should exercise and wont ourselves in faith to despise death, to look on it as a deep, sound, sweet sleep, the coffin no other than the bosom of our Lord Christ, or paradise, the grave nought but a soft couch of rest" (Hy 957–962, the 1542 preface, Bacon's English); the churchyard is again what "the primitive Fathers called them, 'Cemeteries,' that is, resting and sleeping places" (Hy 980–981); "wakes, masses for the soul, obsequies, purgatory, and all other mummeries for the dead" are "out-and-out made an end of" (Hy 975–978); and yet "it is meet and right to give care and honor to the burial of the dead… decorating them, singing beside them and adorning them with monuments" (Hy 989–990, 1004–1005). The old tunes are kept and re-worded: "The notes and melodies are of great price; it were pity to let them perish; but the words to them were unchristian and uncouth, so let these perish" (Hy 1017–1019). This is the movement's own claimed practice (Widely Accepted, Doc_02 §5); no burial is recorded.

### 1.3 Women's presence — the Article 20 affirmative duty, discharged within the limits Doc_02 §12.1 set

Doc_01 §4 named it, Doc_02 §12.1 discharged the naming and attempted no reconstruction, Doc_04 §10 item 10 carried it forward "unchanged" with the finding that "no gravity here centres a woman's own voice." The Constitution's order of operations — absence first; then, only on independent positive evidence, bounded reconstruction at Inferential confidence, never from silence — is followed exactly, and this pass adds traces the earlier documents did not have because the sermons that carry them were unread.

**(a) The absence, named.** No woman's own voice is in this library. The one reported sentence (Katharina von Bora's, TT 3146–3148) is triple-filtered and Contested as verbatim; it concerns prayer, and it is answered by her husband. The two named women who did publish are unvendored (Grumbach, R54, Native) and Excluded on a disclosed conservative default (Zell, R55; OG-1). The household — the ecology's prescribed formation site — is documented from the father's side only. The structural causes are Doc_02 §12.1's: the edition principle, the rights barrier, the confessional documents signed by princes and senates.

**(b) The positive traces, every one, with its layer and strength.** Listed so a reviewer can check that (c) exceeds none of them (Gallic S9 and S10 are the error classes: no upgrade, no over-reading of one word).

1. **Katharina von Bora's question** — "Sir! how is it, that in Popedom they pray so often with great vehemence, but we are very cold and careless in praying?" (TT 3146–3148, Bell's English) [R31]. Contested as verbatim; Documented as Aurifaber's report. The only woman's speech in the library, and it is about the household's own prayer, in the negative.
2. **"A master or a lady of the house should speak this way"** (SC 454) — a woman heading a household at confession, scripted by the founder. Documented as prescription.

4. **The servant's confession, "I have complained about my master's wife and sworn at her"** (SC 450–451) — the mistress as the object of a servant's sin. Documented as prescription.
5. **"Nuns" and "the nuns who have taken husbands"** in the Third Sermon (v2 14983–14984, 15002–15003, 15013) — women as agents of a decision the founder calls "a matter of liberty," to be made "conscientiously" by "heart and conscience" (v2 15010–15013). Documented as address; nothing about any nun's own reasoning.
6. **The altar-linen and "a pure nun"** — the pope's precept "that no woman shall wash the altar-linen on which the body of Christ has lain, even if it be a pure nun, except it first be washed by a pure priest" (v2 15300–15302) — the inherited rule the founder mocks. Documented as the world's report of its inheritance.
7. **"If the woman bears a child, he lets that pass, too"** (v2 15305–15306) — the priest's concubine, present only as the pope's tolerated scandal.
8. **"Brothers and sisters on the other side who belong to us, and must still be won"** (v2 14728–14729) and "our weak brothers and sisters who are still without the knowledge of this liberty" (v2 15281–15282) — women counted among the weak the strong must bear with. Documented as address.
9. **Marriage as God's creation of "man and woman separately"** and the Sixth Commandment's "everyone will love and honor their spouses" (LC 1693; SC 102). Documented as doctrine.
10. **AC XXIII's pastors' wives** — "it is to be expected that the churches shall at some time lack pastors if marriage is any longer forbidden" (AC 754–756 [R37], per Doc_02 §12.1). Widely Accepted as to the practice presupposed.

12. **Luther's prayer "when my wife… was sick"** (TT 3143–3144, Bell's English) — Kate as the object of a household prayer. Contested as verbatim.

**(c) Bounded reconstruction, at Inferential/Thin, not exceeding (b).** Women were present in this world's formation as heads of households who confessed (trace 2), as wives and widows the Home Chart addressed (3), as the mistresses servants were taught to confess against (4), as nuns whom the founder told to decide marriage by their own consciences (5), as the "sisters" among the weak (8), and as pastors' wives in numbers sufficient to be a confessional issue by 1530 (10). Article 23 requires the world's own framing: the woman heading a house has "not faithfully led my children, servants or wife to God's glory" (SC 455–456 — the script is the master's and says "wife"; the lady is told to "speak this way," and the script is not re-gendered for her), and the nun's leaving is her "liberty" and her "conscience," not a liberation the world recognized as such. What cannot be said: anything about a woman's formation from within — her prayer, her reading, her examination of her children, her confession's content beyond the master's script. That silence is the finding, and it is unchanged from Doc_02 §12.1 in substance; what this pass adds is that the 1522 sermons, unread until now, address nuns and "sisters" directly and so give the absence three more edges.

### 1.4 Forces integration — how the outside world entered the body and the house

*Connects to Doc_01 §7 (all four preliminary questions), Doc_02 §13, and Doc_04's forces notations at G4, G5, G9, G12, G13. Layer 1 names the force in the analyst's code; Layer 2 renders it in the world's own words.*

**[F-resp] — what the world was responding to — is felt in the body as the terrified conscience and the tortured preparation.** Doc_01 §7's "laity taught to rely on indulgences, private confession… and the church's own sacramental system for assurance" is, from within, a memory of self-torture: "the old way under the Pope, in which a person tortured himself to be so perfectly pure that God could not find the least blemish in us. On this account we became so timid that every one was instantly thrown into consternation and said to himself: Alas! you are unworthy!… one week trails another, and one half year the other" (LC 4327–4336); "it is easily seen what sort of Christians we were under the Papacy, namely, that we went from mere compulsion and fear of human commandments, without inclination and love" (LC 4304–4307); "we always prayed in Popedom conditionaliter, conditionally, and therefore uncertainly" (TT 3063–3064, Bell's English); confessionally, "Some persons were driven by conscience into the desert, into monasteries hoping there to merit grace by a monastic life" (AC 559–561). The force is not remembered as an institution; it is remembered as a bodily habit of fear, and the household's prescribed joy (SC 557, 575) is its answer.

**[F-inh] — the inheritance refused — is felt in the body as one's own former vow and cowl.** The founder was a friar; the sermons speak to monks and nuns as to people whose bodies are the question ("cannot restrain the desires of the flesh," v2 14991); "wearing a cowl will not kill him" (v2 15026); the pope's rule about who may wash the altar-linen (v2 15300–15307) is the inheritance at its most bodily and most mocked; the Seven Canonical Hours are "babbling" from which pastors "have now… been delivered" (LC 71–72); the "prayers of monks and priests, who howl and growl day and night like fiends" (LC 3148–3149) are what household prayer is defined against. The saints' functional specialisms — Lawrence against fire, Sebastian against pestilence (LC 451–455, per Doc_02 §6) — are the inherited body-protections the catechism replaces with the Word as "holy water" (LC 137). Doc_04 §3 G9 and §8 carry the seedbed question (is this vocabulary the Augustinian inheritance's own?) unanswered; this document carries it unchanged (§10C).

**[F-rad] — the internal-radical force of 1522 — penetrated the body of one congregation, and the world received it as endangered deathbeds and scandalized neighbours.** Meat on Friday, the sacrament in the hands, the cup, images: each is rendered as a bodily act that a weak person might die frightened of — "if you entice any one to eat meat on Friday, and he is troubled about it on his deathbed, and thinks, Woe is me, for I have eaten meat and I am lost! God will call you to account for that soul" (v2 14798–14801) — and as a public scandal: "men say: 'At Wittenberg there are very good Christians, for they take the sacrament with the hands and handle the cup, and then they go to their brandy and drink until they are drunken'" (v2 15404–15407). Received, resisted, interpreted: as the devil's "flank attack" (v2 14755), as "the devil… after us, through his apostles, with all his craft and cunning" (v2 15173–15174), as a game in which "you wanted to paint the devil black, but forgot the charcoal and used chalk" (v2 15215–15216). By 1529 the founder reports the same force felt the other way — bodies that "go one, two, three years, or even longer without the Sacrament" and "become quite brutish" (LC 4240–4247, the founder's testimony, Discipline 5 — corrected this revision, Round 1 finding C8) — Doc_04 §3 G8's documented reversal, here at the level of the body.

**[F-pop] — the popular-insurrectionary force — is felt, in 1529, as a bloodshed prayer prevented.** "What do you think has hitherto accomplished such great things, has checked or quelled the counsels, purposes, murder, and riot of our enemies, whereby the devil thought to crush us, together with the Gospel, except that the prayer of a few godly men intervened like a wall of iron on our side? They should else have witnessed a far different tragedy, namely, how the devil would have destroyed all Germany in its own blood" (LC 3190–3196). The text names no event, and this document supplies none: 1525 is a named absence [R48/R94], and the Large Catechism's "riot" is left as the world's own unspecified memory of "murder, and riot" answered by prayer. In 1522 the same force is felt as the Word's alternative to force: "I could have brought great bloodshed upon Germany, Yea, I could have started such a little game at Worms that even the emperor would not have been safe. But what would it have been? A fool's play. I did nothing; I left it to the Word" (v2 14932–14935).

**[F-ter] and [F-emb] — the territorial-princely force in the imperial frame — enter the body through the daily bread.** The prince is prayed for in the fourth petition as the one "by which most of all God preserves to us our daily bread and all the comforts of this life… For where there are dissension, strife, and war, there the daily bread is already taken away, or at least checked" (LC 3471–3478), hence the loaf on the coat-of-arms (LC 3480–3484) and the household's prayer for "devout and faithful rulers, good government" (SC 305–306). The same force enters the pastor's body as hunger when nobles "let the parishes decay… and pastors and preachers to suffer distress and hunger a plenty" (LC 95–97).

**[F-turk] — the military-external force — is a petition, not a fear the body records.** "Vanquish the Turks and all enemies" (LC 3504) sits inside the prayer for rulers; "I will pray against the Pope and the Turk as long as I live" (TT 3134–3135, Bell's English). Proportionality (Forces Fw Principle 2): the Turk left, in the formation literature read, a standing referent in prayer and no bodily trace — no flight, no levy, no household instruction — and this document gives it that much and no more.

**Economic pressure — the one economic force the library carries in the world's own voice.** Doc_02 §13 did not list an economic force because no source spoke to one; the Large Catechism's fourth petition does: "How much trouble there is now in the world only on account of bad coin, yea, on account of daily oppression and raising of prices in common trade, bargaining and labor on the part of those who wantonly oppress the poor and deprive them of their daily bread! This we must suffer indeed; but let them take care that they do not lose the common intercession, and beware lest this petition in the Lord's Prayer be against them" (LC 3535–3541). From within: not a market but a sin against the neighbour's bread, answered by withholding him from the household's prayer. The servant's and master's confessions carry the same economy at the household scale — "neglected my duty and allowed damage to be done"; "charged him too much, cheated him and sold him badly made goods" (SC 448–449, 458–459). This is a Layer-2 datum for Doc_08 rather than a new force code; Doc_04's codes are not extended here (Discipline 7).

**[F-print] — print — enters the house as the book itself, and the world already feared what the book would do.** The catechism is "a poor, mean teaching, which they can read through at one time, and then immediately know it, throw the book into a corner" (LC 87–89); the nobles say "we have everything in books, and every one can easily learn it by himself" (LC 94–95); the founder wants his own books read "as I read the orders and the ordures of the pope and the books of the sophists" (v1 316–317) and hopes they "will yet be forgotten in the dust" (v1 299–300). The medium that carried the household catechism is, in the world's own consciousness, a danger to the household's diligence — the same edge Doc_04 §3 G11 and G13 found at the hymnal (Hy 1067–1069).

**[F-pap] — the papal force — is felt in the body as the Easter law, the altar-linen, the fast, the seven hours, and "under the bench."** Its Layer 2 name shifts by register (Doc_03 6.7 [PV]): "the pope… the Antichrist" in the sermons (v2 15024), "the old way under the Pope" in the catechism (LC 4327), "the Church of Rome" in the confession (AC 631–633 per Doc_04 §3 G2). The body remembers it as compulsion: "the most holy father, the pope, has done with his fools' commandment: All Christians must go to the sacrament at the holy Eastertide, and he who does not go shall not be buried in consecrated ground" (v2 15468–15470).

**Cross-cell connection (Forces Fw Principle 4).** The initiating force ([F-resp], the tortured conscience) produced the household program ([F-resp] → G4); the internal force of 1522 ([F-rad]) reversed by 1529 into the bodily neglect G13 documents; the territorial force entered the house as bread and left the parish as hunger. The forces the library cannot show entering the body at all — the Reformed rival [F-ref], the visitation's inspecting arm — are the two Doc_02 §13 said Doc_08 "cannot be built from this library," and §1.4 does not pretend otherwise.

### Inhabited passage (Layer 2) — a household father, in the morning, 1529

*Register: the household catechism's own — the father as the Small Catechism scripts him. Tier 4 reconstruction per Doc_02 §10 (every element cites R25, R26 or R31; no named individual).*

> Out of bed, and the sign of the cross, and the words: the will of God, Father, Son and Holy Ghost, be done. Then on my knees, the creed and the Our Father, and the little prayer for the day, that His holy angels be with me so the evil enemy gets no power over me. Then to work, with joy — that is how it is set down — and a song if one comes, on the commandments or on whatever is in my head. The children and the servants come to the table with their hands folded and say the psalm about all eyes looking to Him, and the Our Father, and the blessing; and after, the thanks, and the Our Father again. They say the three parts word for word at rising and at table and at night, and whoever cannot say them does not eat, and once in the week I am to hear them and ask what they know of it. What does this mean? — the children and the servants must say it: we must fear, love, and trust God more than anything else. When I go to confess I do not hunt for sins or invent them, for that turns confession into torture; I say that I have not led my children and servants and wife to God's glory as I should, that I have cursed, that I sold badly made goods and charged too much, and I ask for grace. At night the cross again, the creed, the Our Father, the angels, and then straight to sleep, with joy. We pray for a devout wife and devout children and devout servants and for the prince, for without the prince we could not keep our bread; we are told that a few godly men's prayer has stood like a wall of iron for us.

## 2. Community Ecology — how a person was formed, and by whom

*(Forces integration: §2.5, labeled, required by Forces Fw para 182.)*

### 2.1 The formative relationships

This world names five relationships in which formation happens, and the library documents them very unevenly.

**Father → household (G4, G7).** The only relationship the library specifies to the hour: the father presents each part "the simple way" (SC 45), examines weekly (LC 241–243), withholds food (LC 333–334), leads morning, table and evening (SC 537–607), and is told at the Large Catechism's last page that "it is his duty by the injunction and command of God, to teach these things to his children, or have them learn what they ought to know… for they must all indeed help us to believe, love, pray, and fight against the devil" (LC 4497–4503). The father is a priest in his house because "through baptism all of us are consecrated to the priesthood" (v2 2176–2177, per Doc_04 §3 G7), and his honor is God's: "regard their parents as in God's stead, and remember that however lowly, poor, frail, and queer they may be, nevertheless they are father and mother given them by God" (LC 1066–1069). Prescribed, Documented; held, absent (Discipline 5).

**Pastor → parishioners (G7, G3).** Preaches "the doctrine which is preached to you" (v2 14682), examines and absolves before the Supper (AC 797; Ap 8493–8494), catechizes the youth "publicly [and privately]" (Ap 6897–6899, per Doc_04 §3 G7), receives the accusation "Dear pastor, this man has done this and that" and excludes "before the whole assembly" (v2 15642–15646), and is owed honor and support (SC 619–623; LC 3485–3489). The Large Catechism is itself this relationship written down: a "sermon… designed and undertaken that it might be an instruction for children and the simple-minded" (LC 230–231), addressed to the pastors who must give it.

**Confessor → penitent (G3, G5).** "A person receives absolution or forgiveness from the confessor, as if from God Himself, without doubting it" (SC 414–417); the confessor asks, "Do you also believe that the forgiveness I give is God's forgiveness?" and the penitent says "Yes, dear sir" (SC 480–483); the confessor "will know how to use additional passages to comfort and to encourage the faith of those who sorrow, are troubled or whose conscience is greatly burdened" (SC 488–490). Not compelled — "I refuse to go to confession just because the pope wishes it" — and not surrendered: "I will let no man take private confession away from me, and I would not give it up for all the treasures in the world, since I know what comfort and strength it has given me… the devil would have slain me long ago, if the confession had not sustained me" (v2 15675–15683).

**Brother → brother (G8, G5).** "When one takes another aside, and tells him what troubles him, so that he may hear from him a word of comfort… What harm is there, if he humbles himself a little before his neighbor, puts himself to shame, looks for a word of comfort from him, and takes it to himself and believes it, as if he heard it from God himself" (v2 15671–15673, 15685–15688); and the strong toward the weak: "have patience with him for a time, suffer his weakness and help him bear it; we should give him milk-food, too, as was done with us, until he likewise grows strong" (v2 14719–14722). Mutual correction by Matthew 18 is prescribed and, by the founder's own admission, absent: "Of this confession there is no longer even a trace to be found, and in this particular the Gospel is put aside in this place. He who could reestablish it would perform a good work" (v2 15629–15631).

**Founder → congregation (G8, G13).** The one documented instance of the relationship acting: eight sermons in eight days to a congregation that "have gone too fast" (v2 14727–14728), from a preacher who insists "I was regularly called by the Council to preach in this place" (v2 14778–14779) and who threatens to withdraw — "If you will not heed me, no one need drive me away from you — I will leave you unbidden, and I shall regret that I ever preached so much as one sermon in this place" (v2 15400–15403). The relationship is felt as love — "I love you even as I love my own soul" (v2 14751–14752) — and as wound: "none of my enemies, although they have caused me much sorrow, have wounded me as you have wounded me in this matter" (v2 15324–15326). The outcome is Steimle's editorial account (Doc_01 §2.3, Widely Accepted), not the sermons'.

**Prince → pastor; visitor → parish.** The first is documented at court level (Doc_04 §3 G6) and as rebuke (LC 92–97); the second only by reference [R51–R52] — "the movement's own self-checking mechanism… is known to this library only by reference" (Doc_02 §4). ### 2.3 Communal expectations, in the world's words

What a person owed the community, as the community's own texts state it:

- **To know the three parts, or not to be counted.** "He who does not know this could not be numbered with the Christians nor be admitted to any Sacrament, just as a mechanic who does not understand the rules and customs of his trade is expelled and considered incapable" (LC 233–236); "a person who is so rude and unruly as to be unwilling to learn these things is not to be tolerated" (LC 337–339).
- **To come to the Supper, uncompelled.** "No one should by any means be coerced or compelled, lest we institute a new murdering of souls. Nevertheless… such people as deprive themselves of, and withdraw from, the Sacrament so long a time are not to be considered Christians" (LC 4250–4254); "If you wish such liberty, you may just as well have the liberty to be no Christian" (LC 4295–4296). The 1522 form: "this sacrament must not be made a law" (v2 15467).
- **To love, or the rest is nothing.** "There must also be love, and through love we must do unto one another as God has done unto us through faith. For without love faith is nothing" (v2 14672–14674); "If you do not want to show yourselves Christians by your love, then leave the other things undone, too" (v2 15574–15575); "No one looks after the poor or seeks how to help them. It is pitiful" (v2 15593–15594).
- **To bear with the weak and not make a "must" of what is "free."** "In all these matters love is the captain" (v2 15142–15143); "treat the weak brother differently from the stubborn" (v2 15225–15226); "no new practices should be introduced, unless the Gospel has first been thoroughly preached and understood" (v2 15368–15369).
- **To forgive as forgiven.** "If, therefore you do not forgive, then do not think that God forgives you; but if you forgive, you have this consolation and assurance, that you are forgiven in heaven" (LC 3597–3599) — and the sign is to be used "every hour, as a thing that we have with us at all times" (LC 3616–3617).
- **To pray, as one obeys a father.** "It is our duty and obligation to pray if we would be Christians, as much as it is our duty and obligation to obey our parents and the government" (LC 3032–3034); "every one of us should accustom himself from his youth daily to pray for all his wants… as for preachers, the government, neighbors, domestics" (LC 3174–3177).
- **To honor pastors and rulers, and to give them their due.** "What Hearers owe their Pastors" (SC 619); rulers "worthy of all honor, that we give to them for their office what we ought and can" (LC 3485–3486); "All that govern are called Fathers" (TT 2422, Bell's English).
- **Not to invent one's own holiness.** "there would have been no need of inventing monasticism nor spiritual orders, but every child would have abided by this commandment" (LC 1096–1097); "no good works except those which God has commanded" (v1 6817–6818, per Doc_03 2.3).
- **To stand alone before death, on a text.** "every one must stand on his own feet and be prepared to give battle to the devil. You must rest upon a strong and clear text of Scripture if you would stand the test" (v2 14998–15000).

### 2.4 Formation Logic — the world's own account of why this works

The Framework asks for the world's reasoning, not the analyst's. Seven pieces of it are stated in the texts.

**(i) The Word does it; we only get as far as the ears (G2).** "It is not in my power to fashion the hearts of men as the potter moulds the clay… I can get no farther than to men's ears; their hearts I cannot reach. And since I cannot pour faith into their hearts, I cannot, nor should I, force any one to have faith. That is God's work alone… We have the *jus verbi*, but not the *executio*" (v2 14857–14866); "the Word must first capture the hearts of men and enlighten them, — we cannot do it. Therefore the apostles gloried in their service, *ministerium*, and not in its effect, *executio*" (v2 15125–15128). Formation is not something the teacher achieves; it is something the Word does while the teacher "drank Wittenberg beer with my Philip and with Amsdor[7]" (v2 14928–14929, bracket the file's own). This is the logic behind every refusal of compulsion in §2.3 and behind the founder's own report that reception is not his to secure (G13).

**(ii) Illness, then remedy (the Law/Gospel fold, Doc_04 §2.2).** The three parts are ordered so that the Commandments "teach a man to know his illness" and the Creed "where he may find the remedy" (v2 13202–13210, per Doc_03 2.6); "the Creed is a doctrine quite different from the Ten Commandments; for the latter teaches indeed what we ought to do, but the former tells what God does for us" (LC 2967–2969, per Doc_04 §3 G1). Formation follows the catechism's order because the person's own inner sequence — terror, then comfort — follows it (G5).

**(iii) Repetition is not mastery; the pupil never graduates (G4, G13).** "Whoever knows the Ten Commandments perfectly must know all the Scriptures" (LC 194–195), and no one does: "though they should know and understand it perfectly (which, however, is impossible in this life), yet there are manifold benefits and fruits still to be obtained, if it be daily read and practiced in thought and speech; namely, that the Holy Ghost is present in such reading and repetition and meditation, and bestows ever new and more light and devoutness" (LC 120–125); God "always keeps teaching the same thing, and does not take up anything new or different" (LC 183–184); "the longer and the more they study the Catechism, the less they know of it, and the more they find yet to learn" (LC 220–221). The failure mode has a name — "the shameful vice and secret infection of security and satiety" (LC 86–87) — and the program's answer is daily exhortation: "it is not enough to teach and instruct only, but there is need also of daily exhortation" (LC 4262–4265).

**(iv) The Word is a weapon, and repeating it drives the devil off (G12).** "Undoubtedly, you will not start a stronger incense or other fumigation against the devil than by being engaged upon God's commandments and words, and speaking, singing, or thinking of them. For this is indeed the true holy water and holy sign from which he flees" (LC 134–138); God "wishes to warn, equip, and preserve us against them, as with a good armor against their fiery darts and with good medicine against their evil infection" (LC 170–173); "all our shelter and protection rest in prayer alone" (LC 3185–3186). Formation is arming.

**(v) Command plus promise, and the conscience made sure (G3, G5).** Every practice is grounded the same way: prayer "at Thy commandment and promise, which cannot fail or deceive me" (LC 3122–3123); the Supper's "command and… promise" (LC 4407–4408) with "your own distress which is about your neck" as the third ground (LC 4408–4409); forgiveness with a "seal… that I should be as sure as though I had absolution pronounced by Thyself" (LC 3611–3613). The person is formed toward certainty — "That I should be certain that such prayers are acceptable" (SC 351) — and the world's diagnosis of its inheritance is that it prayed "at a venture" (LC 3083, 3119, 3741).

**(vi) Liberty bound by love sets the pace (G8).** "There are some who can run, others must walk, still others can hardly creep. Therefore we must not look upon our own, but upon our brother's powers, so that he who is weak in faith, and attempts to follow the strong, may not be destroyed of the devil" (v2 14737–14740); "the faith must always remain pure and immovable in the heart, never wavering; but love moves and is guided, according as our neighbors may grasp it" (v2 14734–14736). Formation is corporate and staggered; the strong are formed by waiting.

**(vii) Need teaches; trial is the school (G5, G12).** "Without trouble, trials, and vexations, prayer cannot rightly be made… the common saying is 'Need teacheth to pray'" (TT 3076–3079, Bell's English); "the young suffer especially from the flesh, afterwards, they that attain to middle life and old age, from the world, but others who are occupied with spiritual matters, that is, strong Christians, from the devil" (LC 3677–3680) — a life-course of temptation the catechism states as the ordinary Christian's expected shape; "no one can escape temptation and allurements… but we pray for this, that we may not fall and be drowned in them" (LC 3669–3672). The person is formed *in* assault, not by being kept from it.

### 2.6 The deferred prayer fold (Doc_04 §10 item 5), tested on the Large Catechism's Third Part, read in full this pass

Doc_04 §2.2 folded prayer "provisionally into G4 (household devotions) and G12 (prayer against the Turk)" on a coverage limit — LC 3006–3759 unread — and flagged it "the fold most likely to be wrong," asking for a re-test "at Doc_05's request once read." It has now been read (LC 3006–3759 entire, §11), together with the Table Talk's four prayer paragraphs (TT 3021–3161) and the Small Catechism's Third Part and devotions. This document runs the test Doc_04 asked for, in Doc_04's own terms, and reports the result as input, not as a reclassification (Discipline 8).

**What the Third Part shows prayer to be.** Three grounds, each stated at length: **commandment** — "Prayer is therefore as strictly and earnestly commanded as all other commandments: to have no other God, not to kill, not to steal, etc." (LC 3015–3017), derived from the Second Commandment (LC 3011–3014, 3030–3031); **promise** — "Call upon Me in the day of trouble: I will deliver thee… Ask, and it shall be given you" (LC 3110–3115), so that "Here I come, dear Father, and pray, not of my own purpose nor upon my own worthiness, but at Thy commandment and promise, which cannot fail or deceive me" (LC 3121–3123); **form** — "God anticipates us, and Himself arranges the words and form of prayer for us, and places them upon our lips… which [the Lord's Prayer] is a great advantage indeed over all other prayers that we might compose ourselves. For in them the conscience would ever be in doubt" (LC 3128–3136, bracket Bente/Dau's). The seven petitions are then the world's own map of "every need which never ceases to relate to us" (LC 3210–3211): the Word taught purely against "bishops, tyrants, enthusiasts" (LC 3276–3277); the kingdom "through the Word and faith" (LC 3320); the will done against "the devil, Pope, bishops, tyrants, and heretics" (LC 3433); the bread, the prince, the coin (LC 3449–3541); forgiveness with its sign (LC 3591–3617); temptation "of the flesh, of the world and of the devil" (LC 3633–3634); and deliverance from "our chief enemy… he who hinders among us everything that we pray for" (LC 3710–3711). Its close is the conscience's certainty: "all depends upon this, that we learn also to say Amen, that is, that we do not doubt that our prayer is surely heard" (LC 3738–3739), against "a pernicious delusion of those who pray in such a manner that they dare not from the heart say yea… For I am but a poor sinner" (LC 3745–3749).

**The six tests, run as Doc_04 ran them.** *Repetition* — **P**: catechesis (LC, SC, *Kurze Form* by its third part [R14], unread past the preface), sermon (v2 14764, 14916–14917, 14818), conversation (TT 3021–3161), and the confessional voice, where prayer is the mark of faith itself: "he that knows that he has a Father gracious to him through Christ… calls upon God… For devils and the ungodly are not able to believe this Article… call not upon Him" (AC 575–579); hymnal preface only as song's neighbour (Hy 1231–1233, Winkworth's English). On the content-narrowed criterion, every one of these is prayer *serving* another gravity's content — the reform's order (G8/G6), the household program (G4), the promise-logic (G3), the conscience's certainty (G5), the adversary (G12) — and no practice was found that depends on prayer for content of its own; scored **p**, not **F**, because the Framework's own wider criterion is not met by a fail. *Formation* — **P** as prescribed daily; reception: one filtered household sentence, negative. *Explanatory* — **P** for one thing the folds did not cover: it explains the refusal of the Seven Hours and of "the prayers of monks and priests, who howl and growl day and night like fiends" (LC 3148–3149) — an [F-inh] datum — and it explains why Kate's question is the household's own. *Persistence* — **P** 1522–1545 across registers, both voices. *Interaction* — **P**, on this build's own established reading of the polarity, stated rather than applied silently: the row is uniformly reinforcing — G1 ("not… upon my own worthiness"), G2 (the form and the Word's purity), G3 (promise; the sign), G4 (the third part), G5 (the whole argument on doubt), G6 (the loaf on the prince's arms), G12 (every petition "against our chief enemy"), G13 (prayer's own coldness), thinly G11 (in German, "in our mother-tongue we would say," LC 3218–3219), no competing cell, no reshaping cell — and Doc_04 §5 read an identically-shaped uniformly-R row (G12's) as "characteristic of a frame rather than a generator" rather than as a pass toward gravity status, following the Gallic precedent for that polarity; this document applies the same reading, named as a reading rather than the Framework's own default (CF paras 301–302 make a fully-related row a pass on its own terms), because it applies here with more force still, since prayer generates none of its own content on either criterion.

Prayer sits inside G4 and G12 only as its location and its enemy; its logic is G3's (command and promise; a sign that makes the conscience sure) and its whole argument is G5's (against praying "at a venture," "conditionally," in doubt). The recommended annotation to Doc_04 §2.2's prayer entry: "folded into G4 (as the third part and the household's daily frame), G12 (as the weapon), **and G3 and G5** (as the promise-logic applied to asking, and as the daily exercise of the conscience's certainty); the LC's Third Part, read in full at Doc_05, shows prayer reinforcing eight gravities and organizing no content of its own"; and the "most likely to be wrong" flag can be lowered to "tested at Doc_05 and confirmed as a fold." Two further things for Doc_06: prayer scores 4/4 on Doc_04 §4's register-and-voice substitute, with the fourth quarter — for once — being a non-founder voice on exactly this term; and the affective vocabulary of G13 ("cold," "coldly," "ice cold") is prayer's own vocabulary in both the founder's and the household's mouths (LC 3181, 4316–4324; TT 3148–3151) — though Bell's "cold" and Bente/Dau's "coldly" are two translators' English and the match is not claimed at the German level from this library. **What would change this result:** the *Kurze Form*'s own Lord's Prayer section (v2 13342–14402, unread) and *Good Works*' Second Commandment (v1 6883–10599, unread) could show a 1520 form of the same argument; neither would make prayer a generator.

## 3. Worship Ecology — Worship Integration Analysis

Doc_01 §5 instructed this document to treat worship "as argued-for-in-doctrine **and** claimed-in-practice-by-the-movement-itself, with the gap being independent (non-self-reporting) confirmation"; Doc_02 §5 narrowed the gap ("the library can now show what was sung and what the movement said it did on Sunday; it cannot show what any parish outside Wittenberg did"); Doc_03 5.6 [PV] asked that the mass's 1520/1522/1530 arc be treated "as real change." All three are observed. The Framework's seven questions are answered in its order; an eighth heading records what the library cannot answer. "The goal is not to elevate worship artificially" (para 373): the parish column of §2.2 is the movement's word, and every sentence below that describes a Sunday is Widely Accepted as the movement's claim, Inferential/Thin as any parish's practice, and Wittenberg's alone where a place is named.

**How worship functions.** As the Word's delivery and the promise's sign, in that order. The Word: "ceremonies are needed to this end alone that the unlearned be taught" (AC 791–792); ceremonies "ought to be observed both to teach men Scripture, and that those admonished by the Word may conceive faith and fear [of God, and obtain comfort] and thus also may pray (for these are the designs of ceremonies)" (Ap 8509–8512, bracket Bente/Dau's); "It has, however, nowhere been written or represented that the act of hearing lessons not understood profits men, or that ceremonies profit, not because they teach or admonish, but *ex opere operato*" (Ap 8520–8523). The sign: the Supper as "a pure wholesome, comforting remedy imparting salvation and comfort, which will cure you and give you life both in soul and body" (LC 4389–4390), received "as an assurance, or seal, or sign to assure him of God's promises and grace" (v2 15494–15496). The Mass "is retained among us, and celebrated with the highest reverence. Nearly all the usual ceremonies are also preserved" (AC 787–789); "we hold only Public or Common Mass [at which the people also commune, not Private Mass]" (Ap 8526–8527); "the private mass must be abolished, as I have said in my writings. And I heartily wish it would be abolished everywhere and only the evangelical mass for all the people be" observed (v2 14850–14852) — private masses, not the Mass itself, are the thing abolished (corrected this revision from a misattributed phrase, Round 1 finding S1). The arc Doc_03 5.6 names is real: 1520's "the mass is an evil thing… Therefore it must be abolished" (v2 14846–14848, restating 1520 in 1522), 1522's "I let it live in God's name" (v2 14893), 1530's "retained" (AC 787). Worship's function did not change across the arc; its *public form* did, under [F-rad] and [F-imp] (Doc_04 §3 G3).

**How worship forms participants.** By the vernacular hymn as catechesis — "German hymns, which have been added to teach the people" (AC 790–791); "we mingle with it German hymns, in order that the people also may have something to learn, and by which faith and fear may be called forth" (Ap 8514–8515); "some psalms or hymns, which have been composed on these parts" (LC 376–378, per Doc_03 4.9) — and by examination before the Supper, which makes the household's weekly test the parish's gate (AC 797; Ap 8494); by the confessor's question and the penitent's "Yes, dear sir" (SC 480–483); and by the funeral, where the songs are "for the strengthening of our faith, and the stirring up of the people to a true devotion" (Hy 986–987). The person worship forms is the one who "believes these words, 'Given for you'" (SC 531–532) and who comes "not upon any worthiness, but upon Thy Word" (LC 4361–4362). Latin is kept "on account of those who are learning and understand Latin" (Ap 8512–8513) — worship forms the learned and the unlearned differently, by the movement's own two-tier rule (Doc_04 §3 G11).

**How worship shapes belonging.** Membership is knowing the three parts and being admitted — "he who does not know this could not be numbered with the Christians nor be admitted to any Sacrament" (LC 233–234) — and coming: "such people as deprive themselves of, and withdraw from, the Sacrament so long a time are not to be considered Christians" (LC 4252–4254), though "no one should by any means be coerced or compelled" (LC 4250–4251). "We Christians have no other outward sign by which we may be distinguished from others than this sacrament and baptism" (v2 15446–15447). Belonging is also communal reception: "The people are accustomed to partake of the Sacrament together, if any be fit for it, and this also increases the reverence and devotion of public worship" (AC 795–797). Its negative is the ban, prescribed "before the whole assembly" (v2 15646) and, at the confessional level, "without human force, simply by the Word" (AC 1339–1340, per Doc_04 §3 G2). The weak belong provisionally — "brothers and sisters on the other side who belong to us" (v2 14728–14729) — and worship is where the strong must not drive them away (v2 15363–15366).

**How worship shapes memory.** Three ways, all traceable. (i) The hymn as the memory-carrier of the three parts (LC 376–378 per Doc_03 4.9; SC 557–558) and of the Gospel "which by God's grace hath again risen" (Hy 912) — the movement remembers itself in song as a restoration, not a founding. (ii) The old melodies kept and re-worded: "we have taken the sweet music or melodies which under popish rule are in use at wakes, funerals and masses for the dead… But we have set other words thereto, such as shall adorn our doctrine of the resurrection, not that of purgatory" (Hy 1009–1016); "Let each church keep its own notes according to its book and use. For I myself do not listen with pleasure in cases where the notes to a hymn or a *responsorium* have been changed, and it is sung amongst us in a different way from what I have been used to from my youth. The main point is the correcting of the words, not of the music" (Hy 1047–1052) — the inheritance's *sound* is deliberately kept as memory while its words are replaced. (iii) The hymnal "with name attached" (Hy 1084), guarding the community's memory of its own songs against "strange and unsuitable songs… sold under our name" (Hy 1086–1087). The saints are memory, not intercession: "the memory of saints may be set before us, that we may follow their faith and good works" (AC 620–626, per Doc_03 8.7). And the churchyard is renamed as memory's own place: "Cemeteries" (Hy 980).

**How worship shapes theology.** (i) The Mass as promise, not sacrifice — the confessional voice's single reply: "this line of authorities, reasons and testimonies, however long, does not prove that the Mass confers grace *ex opere operato*" (Ap 8551–8553). (ii) The "for you" — "put yourself into this YOU, that He may not speak to you in vain" (LC 4376–4377) — is theology learned by hearing one's own address. (iii) The Supper's boundary ("truly present… and they reject those that teach otherwise," AC 322–324, per Doc_04 §3 G10) is stated in worship's own article; G10's organizing weight in *this library* is thin (Doc_04 §3 G10, §6.3), and this document does not read "Supporting" as "minor" (Doc_04 §10 item 3) — it records only that the library shows the doctrine taught in the catechism and confessed, not the controversy that made it a boundary. (iv) Music itself is theologized: "I would fain see all the arts, and music in particular, used in the service of Him who hath given and created them" (Hy 928–930); "the beautiful ornament of music, brought back to its right use, may serve its blessed Maker" (Hy 1039–1040); and, in a verse piece this pass read for the first time in this build, music "pleaseth God far more, we know, / Than any joys the world can show; / The Devil's work it doth impede, / And hinders many a deadly deed" (Hy 1203–1206, "Lady Musick Speaketh," Catharine Winkworth's English, Hy 1236 — a translator's hand Doc_02 §2 did not name; §11).

**How worship shapes authority.** The preacher must be "regularly called" (v2 14778–14779; AC 383–384 per Doc_04 §3 G7); the pastor excludes "before the whole assembly" (v2 15646); the Council calls; "the aid of the authorities" makes a change of worship "from God" (v2 14764–14766); the vernacular in church is "so ordained by man's law" as well as by Paul (AC 793–795). The founder's own musical authority is remembered, at one remove, as personal — he "himself decided to appropriate the Eighth Tone to the Epistle and the Sixth Tone to the Gospel… So he himself made the notes over the Epistles, and the Gospels, and the Words of Institution" (Hy 757–765, Walter [R45], late, three hands) — and the 1543 hymnal makes authorship a guard: "henceforth without our knowledge and consent no further additions or alterations be made in this book of ours" (Hy 1090–1092). Worship's authority is the Word's — "we must let the Word of God alone do the work" (v2 15293–15294) — exercised through a called office and a printed name.

**How worship shapes time.** The day: the household's morning, table and evening (SC 537–607); the pastor's "morning, noon, and evening" (LC 73). The week: "every Lord's Day and on the other festivals" (Ap 8492–8493); Vespers "re-establish[ed]" (Hy 772). The year: made *free*. "Sunday or Friday, Lent or Advent" (v2 15963–15969 per Doc_03 8.6); the Supper "bound to no place or appointed time," against "the Passover of the Jews, which they were obliged to eat only once a year… although the Pope afterwards perverted it, and again made a Jewish feast of it" (LC 4282–4290); the Easter law refused (v2 15467–15470); holy-days kept "without sin" but not as necessary (AC 391–398 per Doc_04 §3 G8). **Finding:** in what was read, worship shapes the day and the week by prescription and un-shapes the year by principle — the liturgical year survives in this library only as the thing the movement refuses to make a "must" of, and as the festivals the Apology mentions in passing. Whether any parish kept a calendar is not in the library.

**What the library cannot answer — a finding, not a gap to fill.** No service order [R59]; no tune (present in Bacon's 1883 print, absent from the text file, Doc_02 §5); no order of baptism; no parish but Wittenberg's, and Wittenberg's only as the movement described it; no Sunday attended by anyone whose account survives except Walter's, forty years on, "in his old age" (Hy 740). The one dated congregational scene in the library outside 1522 is Aurifaber's: "In the year 1532, throughout all Germany was a great drought… On the ninth of June the same year, Luther called together the whole assembly into the church, and directed his prayer, with deep sighs, to God" (TT 3157–3160, Bell's English) — reported, arranged, translated, revised; Contested as to wording, uncorroborated in this library as to the event, and used here only as what it is: the Table Talk's picture of a parish at prayer. Doc_01 open item 3 stays where Doc_02 §15 left it: narrowed, not closed.

### No inhabited passage for the parish's Sunday

The draft of this document carried a fourth inhabited passage here, in the first person of an anonymous Wittenberg congregant, built from the movement's own self-description of its worship (AC/Ap XXIV, the catechism's exhortation) and from Walter's late reminiscence (Hy 752–778 [R45]). Round 1 (finding S4) held that this is the parish-practice reconstruction Doc_02 §14 bars categorically ("Doc_05 may reconstruct household practice at Tier 4 and may not reconstruct parish practice at all") whatever the passage's label claimed about its own evidential status, and that it compounded the problem two further ways: it put Walter's privileged, forty-years-later private testimony to Luther in an anonymous congregant's mouth, and it silently composited material from six different dates (1526, 1529, 1530–31, 1532, 1542, 1543) into one Sunday. **The passage is cut, not rebuilt.** §3's analytical treatment of worship already carries everything the passage would have shown — how it functioned, formed, bounded, remembered, theologized, authorized and timed a Sunday, each sentence marked Widely Accepted as the movement's claim and Inferential/Thin as any parish's fact — and does so without manufacturing a speaker the library does not license. This document therefore contains **three** inhabited passages, not four, and reconstructs no parish scene anywhere; §10C's discharge line for Doc_02 §14 and §12's review-requirement description are both corrected to match.

## 4. Organizational Ecology — Authority Structures

Built around Doc_04's G6 (the two governments), G7 (estate, office, calling — and the "regularly called" fence), and G2's *jus verbi*/*executio* as the criterion of what any authority may do; with G8 as the rule that governs the pace of all of it.

### 4.1 Inside the house and the parish

**The house.** One head, by God's ordering: "among us there must necessarily be such inequality and ordered difference, and therefore God commands it to be observed, that you obey me as your father, and that I have the supremacy" (LC 1072–1075); parents "at His side" (LC 1056–1057), honored "as to a majesty there hidden" (LC 1059), even when "lowly, poor, frail, and queer" (LC 1067). Under him: the lady of the house (SC 454), children, "domestics, ma-servants and maid-servants" (LC 335–336), whom he may keep or not "if they do not know these things and are unwilling to learn them" (LC 336–337). The Home Chart's estates are the house's own constitution (SC 615–668). The house is the parish's gate — the three parts learned there admit one to the Sacrament there (LC 233–234) — and the parish's seedbed: "we train the people who are to come after us and succeed us in our office and work, in order that they also may bring up their children" (LC 4493–4495).

**The parish.** The pastor, "regularly called" (v2 14778–14779; AC 383–384 per Doc_04 §3 G7), who preaches, examines, absolves, catechizes, and excludes; owed his living, and starving where nobles let parishes decay (LC 95–97); rebuked by the founder in the same breath — "swineherds and dog-tenders" (LC 68) — and honored by the same catechism, "worthy of double honour" (TT 2424, Bell's English). The congregation as a body: prescribed to accuse "before the whole congregation" and to "intercede for him with God" (v2 15622–15624), and admitted by the founder to do neither (v2 15629–15631). The Council that calls (v2 14779). The visitor: by reference only [R51].

### 4.2 Above the parish — the four authorities as the world held them

**The prince (G6).** Doc_04 §3 G6 documents him at court level and not at the parish; this document adds how the household was taught to *feel* him: as the loaf on the coat-of-arms (LC 3480–3484), as "devout and faithful rulers, good government" in the daily bread (SC 305–306), as "Fathers" (TT 2422, Bell's English), as the one who is "worthy of all honor, that we give to them for their office what we ought and can" (LC 3485–3486), and — in the fifth commandment's household gloss — as the exception to the rule against wrath: "Wrath is forbidden in every man, except in the magistrates" (TT 2428, Bell's English). Obedience "save only when commanded to sin" (AC 426–428 per Doc_04 §3 G6). The prince's *church* authority — the visitation, the ordinances — is Layer 1 only, by reference, and Doc_04 §10 item 4's coverage gap (1525, 1555) is not narrowed here.

**The pope (G2, G12).** The authority refused, in three registers' names (Doc_03 6.7 [PV]): the sermons' "the pope… the Antichrist" (v2 15024), whose "fools' laws" made the year and the sacrament a compulsion (v2 15240, 15468); the catechism's "the old way under the Pope" (LC 4327) and "the Papacy, where faith was entirely put under the bench" (LC 2807–2808); the confession's "the Church of Rome as known from its writers" (AC 631–633 per Doc_04 §3 G2). Under Article 23 the opponent is rendered as the world saw him; this document does not rehabilitate him and does not editorialize.

**The bishops and the Fathers.** The bishops obeyed "But when they teach or ordain anything against the Gospel, then the congregations have a commandment of God prohibiting obedience" (AC 1342–1344 per Doc_04 §3 G2); the Fathers as witnesses, not judges — "lest any one should craftily say that a new interpretation of Paul has been devised by us, this entire matter is supported by the testimonies of the Fathers. For Augustine, in many volumes…" (AC 534–537) — and, in the founder's sharper voice, "we must let the Prophets and Apostles sit at the desk, while we sit at their feet and listen to what they say. It is not for us to say what they must hear" (v1 292–294). Two voices, one rule, differently pitched (Discipline 4).

**The founder himself.** The world's texts refuse him as an authority in his own person (Doc_04 §2.2's Article 26 trap): "I would gladly have seen all my books forgotten and destroyed" (v1 260); "I did nothing; the Word did it all" (v2 14931); "not one of those who, starting with nothing, have in a trice become the most exalted and most learned doctors" (v1 397–398). What he claims is a *call* — "regularly called by the Council" — and a *text* — "You must rest upon a strong and clear text of Scripture if you would stand the test" (v2 14999–15000). The one place his authority is exercised as personal is the threat to leave (v2 15400–15403), and even that is framed as what the Word requires of him.

### 4.3 The criterion of legitimacy

Authority is legitimate insofar as it is the Word's, exercised through a call, and illegitimate insofar as it is a "must" made of what is "free" or a doctrine of men bound on the conscience. Stated once for each authority: the preacher — "I was regularly called" (v2 14778–14779); the reformer of worship — "called upon God in earnest prayer, and… obtained the aid of the authorities, one could be certain that it had come from God" (v2 14764–14766); the monk who marries — "a clear text of Scripture" (v2 15004) and not "the example of the crowd, according to the preaching of the provost or Dr. Carlstadt, or Gabriel, or Michael" (v2 14996–14998); the bishop — "without human force, simply by the Word" (AC 1339–1340 per Doc_04 §3 G2); the prince — "save only when commanded to sin" (AC 427). Its negative is precisely stated: "they wished to make a 'must' out of that which is free, and that God cannot tolerate. Do you wish to change the ordering of the Majesty on high?" (v2 15070–15072). The organizational consequence is that *no one may compel faith* — "I can drive no man to heaven with a club" (v2 14974); "faith must not be chained and imprisoned, nor bound by an ordinance to any work" (v2 14893–14894) — and that *everyone must know for himself*: "every one must know for himself the chief things in Christianity, and be armed therewith" (v2 14645–14646). This is why the household father is the ecology's first officer: he is the one authority whose whole work is to make each person able to stand alone on a text.

### 4.4 From within

Authority in this world is felt as *being called and being answerable*, not as rank: the father answers to God for "his duty by the injunction and command of God" (LC 4497–4498); the pastor answers for souls, "care-takers of souls" (LC 68–69); the reformer answers at the deathbed to the devil's texts, "'I have not sent them, yet they ran'" (v2 14775); the strong answer "for those who were led astray by your exercise of liberty without love" (v2 14797–14798). Rank is real — "inequality and ordered difference" (LC 1073–1074) — but it is felt as a *place in life* one confesses from: "Consider here your place in life according to the Ten Commandments. Are you a father? A mother? A son? A daughter? A husband? A wife? A servant?" (SC 428–430). And the office is not the man: "when deposed, he is a peasant or a townsman like the rest" (v2 2223–2226 per Doc_04 §3 G7).

*Forces note (brief; full treatment at §1.4, §2.5, §7.3).* [F-ter] in both directions (Doc_04 §3 G6); [F-rad] as the fence "regularly called" (Doc_04 §3 G7); [F-imp] as the register in which the princes become confessors (AC 1557–1567 [R37]). The two moments that decided this authority structure's history — 1525 and 1555 — are outside the library, and this lens is a 1520–1531 lens (Discipline 9).

## 5. Ministry Ecology — what the formed person did for others

The one named lens (para 664) without a Part III definition; defined here as the outward work of the formed person, which in this world is stated estate by estate, because the world's own answer to "what is a good work?" is "when they work at their trade, walk, stand, eat, drink, sleep" (v1 6863–6865 per Doc_04 §3 G7) and not "praying in church, fasting, and almsgiving" (v1 6866–6870 per Doc_03 2.3). Ministry is what each "place in life" does for the neighbour.

**The pastor's.** To preach the Word "with tongue and pen" and leave the *executio* to God (v2 14854–14857, 14865–14866); to catechize — "the pastors and ministers of the churches are compelled publicly [and privately] to instruct and hear the youth; and this ceremony produces the best fruits" (Ap 6897–6899 per Doc_04 §3 G4); to examine and absolve (AC 797); to pray "for themselves and their parishioners" (LC 75–76); to comfort with "additional passages" the one "whose conscience is greatly burdened" (SC 488–490); to exclude, "for the sake of the congregation, until he comes to himself and is received back again" (v2 15646–15648).

**The father's.** To teach and examine (LC 241–243, 4497–4499); to lead prayer (SC 537–607); to be the house's priest by baptism (Doc_04 §3 G7) — and to answer for it: "I have not faithfully led my children, servants or wife to God's glory" (SC 455–456).

**The hearer's, the neighbour's.** To love — the fruit of the Sacrament is "that we should treat our neighbor even as God has treated us" (v2 15559–15560); to "give a poor man a gold-piece" rather than "God a golden image" (v2 15165–15166); to bear with the weak (v2 15258–15259); to forgive (LC 3594–3596); to admonish a brother "in secret" and then "with two others" (v2 15635–15638); to honor and support the pastor (SC 619–623); to help the sick neighbour (SC 93); to pray "for preachers, the government, neighbors, domestics" (LC 3177). The Seventh Sermon's charge is the world's own measure of this ministry's failure at Wittenberg: "no one as yet has given his goods to the poor, no one has yet been burned" (v2 15586–15587) — a founder's complaint (G13), not a fact about the town (Discipline 5).

**The prince's.** To protect the bread (LC 3471–3489); to "vanquish the Turks and all enemies" (LC 3504) — the library's own image for this ministry is the loaf on the arms (LC 3480–3484), and no other is imported. To keep the parishes from decaying (LC 92–97). To confess: the AC is "the Confession of our preachers and of ourselves" (AC 81–82 [R37], per Doc_02 §3.1).

**The founder's and the movement's.** To write hymns "to make a good beginning and to encourage others who can do it better" (Hy 909–910); to set the German Mass (Hy 752–770 [R45]); to put the Testament into German "that there would be less writing, and more studying and reading of the Scriptures" (v1 280–282; the Testament itself unvendored [R60]); to print — twelve known editions of one treatise recorded by the Philadelphia editors (v1 12304–12309 [R85], editorial — corrected this revision, Round 1 finding S6); to stand before the Emperor (AC preface [R37]); and, to the north, the one outward trace: "the Gospel hastens to Prussia" (v3 20802–20803, Luther as quoted by Lambert, editorial [R24]). The movement's ministry to the Turk is prayer, and to the Turk's victims nothing the library records.

**What ministry is *not*, in this world.** Not the monastic list — "particular holy-days, particular fasts, brotherhoods, pilgrimages, services in honor of saints, the use of rosaries, monasticism, and such like" (AC 501–504) — and not the mass "as a sacrifice and work of merit" (v2 14847). The confessional voice's own summary: "our teachers… have taught to good purpose concerning all estates and duties of life, as to what estates of life and what works in every calling be pleasing to God" (AC 497–500). Ministry is redefined as vocation (Doc_04 §3 G7), and the redefinition is felt from within as *relief*: "Christian perfection is to fear God from the heart, and yet to conceive great faith… and meanwhile, to be diligent in outward good works, and to serve our calling" (AC 1233–1239 per Doc_03 8.5).

**Article 20 note.** The ministry's objects — "the uneducated" (SC 410), "the simple" (LC 3510), "the common people" (LC 80, 251), "the young" (LC 236), "the weak" (v2 15253) — never speak. The one text that gives an ordinary person's ministry in his own scripted voice is the servant's confession, and its content is failure: "I have neglected my duty and allowed damage to be done" (SC 448–449). This document does not reconstruct what any servant did well.

**Forces note (added this revision, Round 1 finding S5).** This lens carries forces already coded elsewhere without having said so. The prince's ministry is felt under [F-ter] (the loaf, LC 3480–3484) and [F-turk] (the petition, LC 3504); the founder's and the movement's ministry of the printed word is [F-print] (twelve editions, the German Testament, the hymnal — the same medium §1.4 and §2.5(d) already code, here as an outward work rather than a pressure received); the Gospel's one outward trace toward Prussia is territorial expansion beyond the parish, cross-referencing §2.5(b)'s [F-ter]; and "what ministry is *not*" — the monastic list refused — is [F-inh] stated as a negative, the same inheritance §1.4, §2.5(a) and §7.3 document being refused elsewhere. No new force code is needed; this lens simply had not named the ones its own material already carries.

## 6. Meaning Transmission, Memory Structures, and Interpretive Ecology

### 6A. Meaning Transmission (teaching, memory, storytelling, apprenticeship, worship, authority structures — paras 337–344)

**The governing shift: from the priest's Latin heard to the father's German recited (G11 reshaping G4).** Doc_04 §3 G4 recorded the audience shift 1520 → 1529 (the illiterate Christian → the negligent pastor → the household father); this document adds the *mode* shift the same texts state. The inheritance transmitted meaning by a work of hearing — "They evidently imagine that the mere work of hearing is a service, that it profits without being understood" (Ap 8502–8503); "babbling and bawling in the churches" that "may be called singing or reading, but not really praying" (LC 3026–3029). The world's own mode is repetition with understanding, in the mother tongue, by mouth: "word for word" (LC 103, 331), "well and fluently" (LC 238), "in our mother-tongue we would say" (LC 3219). The catechism is the whole of Scripture made repeatable: "in these three parts everything that we have in the Scriptures is comprehended in short, plain, and simple terms" (LC 339–340, "pain" in the file); "the holy Fathers or apostles (whoever they were) have thus embraced in a summary the doctrine, life, wisdom, and art of Christians" (LC 341–342). Transmission has three attested channels, in the world's own descending order of trust: the Word preached (the sermon, which the founder says has reached Wittenberg: "you have the pure Word of God," v2 14744–14745); the catechism recited and examined (the household); the hymn sung (the parish and the street). A fourth channel — the printed book — is the one the founder trusts least (§1.4, [F-print]).

**Teaching.** Three stages of one text (Doc_02 §1.1): the *Kurze Form* for "the ordinary Christian, who cannot read" (v2 13182 per Doc_04 §3 G4), the Large Catechism as a sermon for pastors (LC 230), the Small for fathers (SC 45). The form is question and answer — "What does this mean?" (SC 51 and passim; Doc_03 4.3) — and the Large Catechism stages the father's own voice: "As if you were to ask a little child: My dear, what sort of a God have you?" (LC 2605–2609 per Doc_03 4.3). Teaching is examined: "to question and examine his children and servants… and to ascertain what they know of it" (LC 241–243). The teacher is himself a pupil: "I do as a child who is being taught the Catechism" (LC 102). The pastor's teaching is prescribed as reading aloud, "a page or two" (LC 73) — meaning transmitted by a man reading a book to himself before he teaches it.

### 6B. Memory Structures

Memory in this world is an *institution of restoration*, and the world says so: the Gospel "which by God's grace hath again risen" (Hy 912); "the blessed light of Christian truth, suppressed for a time by the pope and his followers, has again risen" (v3 10653–10654 per Doc_03 3.2); the three parts "which have remained in Christendom from of old, though little of it has been taught and treated correctly" (LC 251–253); the Fathers cited as witnesses that "a new interpretation of Paul has [not] been devised by us" (AC 534–535); the German hymn "not such a new departure," since "the people almost everywhere sang something in their own tongue" (Ap 8518–8519, bracket Bente/Dau's); the Greek churches' single public Mass as "the traces of former customs" (Ap 8531). The movement remembers itself as recovering, and its memory-institutions are built to show it: the confession's preface narrating the Diets (AC 116–161 [R37]); the 1545 preface's self-portrait as a warning to readers of the early books (v1 354–357); the hymnal "with name attached" (Hy 1084); the funeral songs on the old tunes (Hy 1009–1019). ### 6C. Interpretive Ecology — how Scripture and the world were read

Eight attested modes, each with its voice and gravity.

1. **Law and Gospel, commands and promises** (the fold Doc_04 §2.2 argued) — "all the Scriptures of God are divided into two parts — commands and promises" (v2 11782–11783 per Doc_04 §2.2); the catechism's illness/remedy order (v2 13202–13210 per Doc_03 2.6). Both voices (Ap 579–580 per Doc_04 §2.2).
2. **The catechism as the key to the whole** — "whoever knows the Ten Commandments perfectly must know all the Scriptures, so that, in all affairs and cases, he can advise, help, comfort, judge, and decide both spiritual and temporal matters and is qualified to sit in judgment upon all doctrines, estates, spirits, laws, and whatever else is in the world" (LC 194–198); "the entire Psalter [is] but thoughts and exercises upon the First Commandment" (LC 199–200). Luther, catechesis.
3. **The clear text, on which one stands alone** — "You must rest upon a strong and clear text of Scripture if you would stand the test. If you cannot do that, you will never withstand, — the devil will pluck you like a withered leaf" (v2 15000–15002); and its limit, argued against the congregation's own proof-text: "when the devil… argues, Where have you read in the Scriptures that 'take' means 'seizing with the hands'? — how shall I prove or defend it?… Thus I am driven into a corner and must concede, even against my will, that 'take' means not only to receive with the hands" (v2 15343–15358). The literal sense (Doc_03 3.5) is the rule, and the devil is a reader of Scripture too — which is why a text must be "clear."
4. **Scripture against Fathers, Councils, pope — in two pitches** — "the Bible has come to lie forgotten in the dust under the bench" (v1 268–269) and "let the Prophets and Apostles sit at the desk" (v1 292–293); confessionally, Scripture with "the Church Catholic" and the Fathers as corroborating witnesses (AC 534–544; 631–633 per Doc_04 §3 G2). Carried as two registers (Discipline 4).
5. **"Hear ye Him"** — Christ alone as teacher (Doc_03 3.6); the Gospel's tone is Christ's friendship: "Our Lord Christ is a good Friend, and his words are full of love; so we will take the Sixth Tone for the Gospel" (Hy 759–761, Walter's report [R45]).
6. **The "for you"** — reading as being addressed: "These words… are not preached to wood and stone, but to me and you… put yourself into this YOU, that He may not speak to you in vain" (LC 4374–4377); "the words, 'for you' demand a heart that fully believes" (SC 534–535).
7. **Scripture as diagnosis when feeling fails** — "if you cannot feel it, at least believe the Scriptures, they will not lie to you and they know your flesh better than you yourself" (LC 4435–4436); "look about you and see whether you are also in the world, or if you do not know it, ask your neighbors about it" (LC 4448–4449). The text reads the reader.
8. **Music as reading's ally** — "The heart grows still when I am heard, / And opens to God's Truth and Word" (Hy 1213–1214, "Lady Musick," Winkworth's English); "we by his holy word impressed upon the heart with sweet songs, be builded up" (Hy 1041–1043).

**The interpretive ecology's own boundary:** every mode above lands in the conscience (G5) and is tested there — the monk's text, the communicant's "for you," the pray-er's Amen — and the one authority that may not interpret for the conscience is the one that binds it apart from Scripture (G2 against [F-pap]; "consciences are not to be burdened," AC 396–398 per Doc_04 §3 G5). The Apology's own hermeneutic — patristic citation, "Epiphanius writes" (Ap 8542) — is the confessional voice's, and Doc_03 §11 item 1's unread Apology is where Doc_06 will find how far it differs from the founder's.

## 8. Emotional and Affective Reconstruction

The Framework asks for hopes, fears, emotional weighting, and emotional patterns (paras 359–363). The world's own affective vocabulary (Doc_03: comfort 9.1, temptation/trial 9.2, assurance 9.3, patience 9.7, the terrified/anxious/captive conscience 2.8, the devil 4.7) is used; the analyst's terms stay outside the quotation marks.

**Hopes.** The world's hope has one sentence: "I cannot doubt I have a gracious God" (v2 15724 per Doc_04 §3 G5). Its household form is joy commanded twice a day — "with joy go about your work" (SC 557), "go to sleep immediately with joy" (SC 575) — and its singing form is joy as the condition of song: "Cheerful and merry must we be in heart and mind, when we would sing" (Hy 1116–1117). Its hope for the neighbour is that "the Word would sink into the heart and perform its work… to-morrow another would do the same" (v2 14883–14886). Its hope in death is sleep and paradise (Hy 959–961). Its hope for the movement is the Word's own future: "Pray that the Word may be further spread abroad, and may be strong against the miserable devil" (v1 404–405). And its hope is God's generosity beyond the asking: "when we truly pray for a piece of bread, so giveth God a whole acre of land" (TT 3142–3143, Bell's English) — against the "fool [who] would beg only for a dish of gruel" (LC 3346).

**Fears.** Despair, named as the sin the inheritance produced — "many fell into despair" (AC 991–992 per Doc_03 9.3), "Despair maketh Priests and Friars" (TT 2405, Bell's English) — and as the devil's target: "to despair, denial of God, blasphemy" (LC 3654); the deathbed, where "the devil attacks you" (v2 14994–14995) and reminds "those who began this affair of texts" (v2 14772–14773); unworthiness — "Alas! you are unworthy!" (LC 4330–4331); being unheard — "how should I be so bold as to boast that God hears my prayer?" (LC 3747–3748); praying "at a venture" (LC 3083); the devil who "breaks men's necks or drives them to insanity, drowns some, and incites many to commit suicide" (LC 3721–3723); persecution — "he must risk what whatever he has upon earth -- possessions, honor. house and estate, wife and children, body and life" (LC 3406–3407, the file's own doubled "what" and stray period as found); the plague God will send on a loveless congregation (v2 15598–15599); and, the founder's own, that the people are cold: "we are very cold and careless in praying" (TT 3148). The fears are age-graded by the catechism: "the young suffer especially from the flesh, afterwards, they that attain to middle life and old age, from the world, but others who are occupied with spiritual matters, that is, strong Christians, from the devil" (LC 3677–3680).

**Emotional weighting.** *Terror* is weighted as the site of everything — "This whole doctrine is to be referred to that conflict of the terrified conscience, neither can it be understood apart from that conflict" (AC 552–553); "in such terrified and trembling hearts alone God desires to dwell" (v2 15504–15505); the "most worthy" are "those who are constantly vexed by death and the devil" (v2 15521–15522). *Coldness* is weighted as the named failure, in both voices of the household — "cold and careless" (TT 3148, Bell), "ice cold therein, and negligent" (TT 3151, Bell), "coldly and indifferently" (LC 3181, Bente/Dau), "callous and cold" (LC 4320–4321), "quite cold and hardened" (LC 4386) — and its opposite is *warmth* the Sacrament gives: "the more will the heart be warmed and enkindled" (LC 4324). *Security and satiety* are weighted as vice (LC 86–87), as is walking "so securely and heedlessly" (LC 4466–4467). *Anger* is the founder's own register in the sermons — "none of my enemies… have wounded me as you have wounded me" (v2 15325–15326) — and the polemical register's whole texture (Doc_02 §1.1); it is refused to everyone but the magistrate (TT 2428). *Patience* is weighted as the whole of a life: "the patiences are so many, that my whole life is nothing but patience" (TT 3014–3015 per Doc_04 §3 G9). *Compunction* is not weighted as progress; the goal is that the compunction *stop* — "labor to have no compunctions, you must never approach" (LC 4337–4338) is the error the catechism warns against.

**Emotional patterns.** (i) *Terror, then comfort* is the world's affective plot, and it is the Law/Gospel fold felt: "the commands… teach a man to know himself… and may despair of his powers," then "the promises of God" (v2 11786–11807 per Doc_04 §3 G5); the disciples "made… all tremble with fear and sorrow, then only did He institute the blessed sacrament as a comfort" (v2 15533–15535). (ii) *Fervour, then cooling* is the plot the founder reports of the congregations: 1522's hearers who "have a great deal to say of the doctrine" (v2 14681–14682) become 1529's who "throw the book into a corner" (LC 89) and "go one, two, three years" (LC 4240) — G13's arc, reported not as fact about any parish but as the founder's consistent complaint (Discipline 5). (iii) *Embattled* is the constant frame: "we Christians must be armed and daily expect to be incessantly attacked" (LC 3685–3686); the Sacrament as "protection, shelter, and power against death and the devil and all misfortune" (LC 4404–4405); "If you could see how many knives, darts, and arrows are every moment aimed at you, you would be glad to come to the Sacrament as often as possible" (LC 4464–4466). (iv) *Tonal signature by register* — the disputation's cool theses; the polemic's "goat," "ass," "jackanapes" (Doc_02 §1.1); the sermons' wounded love; the catechism's patient repetition and sudden vehemence ("swineherds and dog-tenders," LC 68); the hymn's defiance (Doc_04 §3 G12); the confession's measured "our churches"; the Table Talk's household humor — Luther "laughed at my simplicity" (Hy 791, Walter). A Representative that smooths these into one temperament has left the ecology (Discipline 4).

**Forces note (added this revision, Round 1 finding S5).** CF para 384 names emotional texture directly among what forces produce, and this lens's own material already shows it: [F-rad] is the texture of *Fears* (the deathbed accusation, the devil's flank attack) and of terror's weighting in 1522's aftermath; [F-pap] is the texture of *Fears*' first entry (the old way that "tortured himself," §1.4) and of the "wall of iron" hope (LC 3193–3194, §2.5b); [F-print] is the texture behind the anger of the 1543 preface's guardedness (§2.5d, §7.1); and G13's force — the founder's own report of coldness — is this lens's own named pattern (ii), *fervour, then cooling*. No candidate force in this world's library was found to produce a distinct emotional texture not already carried by one of these; the lens's emotional vocabulary and Doc_04's force codes describe the same material from two directions.

### Inhabited passage (Layer 2) — a terrified conscience on the way to the Sacrament

*Register: the catechism's exhortation and the Sixth Sermon — the founder's pastoral voice, as a hearer would carry it inside. Not a named person; the "I" is the one the Large Catechism's "Answer" addresses.*

> I feel that I am not prepared. That is the old scruple, from the way under the pope, when a man tortured himself to be so clean that God could find no spot on him, and grew so timid that he cried at once, Alas, you are unworthy! — and one week trailed another and one half-year the other, and he never went. If I wait till I am rid of my burdens and can come pure and worthy, I shall stay away forever; and then He says: if you are pure and godly, you have no need of me, nor I of you. So I do not go because I am worthy. We are not baptized because we are holy, nor go to confession because we are without sin, but the other way, because we are poor miserable men. I would gladly be worthy; but I come upon His Word, because He commanded it, and let my worthiness be what it will. The words are for you — not preached to wood and stone but to me — and I am to put myself into that "you." And if I cannot feel my distress? Then I put my hand into my bosom to see whether I have flesh and blood, and I look about me to see whether I am in the world, and if I do not know it I ask my neighbours; and I have the devil about me, a liar and a murderer, who cannot bear to see me live one hour. If I could see how many knives and darts and arrows are aimed at me every moment I would run to the Sacrament as often as I could. It is not a poison; it is a remedy, an antidote against the poison I have in me. We are told God will dwell only in terrified and trembling hearts, that this bread is a comfort for the sorrowing, a healing for the sick, a life for the dying; that the most worthy are those the devil and death vex without ceasing. ## 9. Representative Theological Patterns, and Power, Influence & Historical Dynamics

### 9A. Representative Theological Patterns

Stated as the world held them — the HAL Doc_05's Round 1 S1 is the error class this section exists to avoid — each with its gravity, its voice(s), and its confidence, so Doc_06 and Doc_10 inherit the shape and not a modern summary. Where the two voices word a pattern differently, both wordings are given.

1. **Justified freely, by faith alone, for Christ's sake; works follow and are commanded, but merit nothing** (G1; both voices; Documented). "men cannot be justified before God by their own strength, merits, or works, but are freely justified for Christ's sake, through faith" (AC 234–239 per Doc_04 §3 G1); "it is necessary to do good works, not that we should trust to merit grace by them, but because it is the will of God" (AC 585–587); and the founder's inner-Lutheran caveat that faith "must have something which it believes" (LC 3916–3921 per Doc_04 §3 G1). Its affective form: "confidence which consoles and encourages the terrified mind" (AC 582–583).
2. **The Word is the instrument through which the Spirit works, and it is external** (G2; both voices; Documented). "through the Word and Sacraments, as through instruments, the Holy Ghost is given" (AC 247–248 per Doc_03 3.1); "the Word must do this thing, and not we poor sinners" (v2 14917–14918). The sharp form (Scripture "under the bench," the Fathers at our feet) is Luther's; the additive form (Scripture and the Church Catholic and the Fathers as witnesses) is the confession's — one rule in two pitches (Discipline 4).
3. **A sacrament is God's promise joined to a sign and received by faith; the body and blood are truly present, "in and under"** (G3, G10; both voices; Documented for the doctrine, thin for the controversy). "when the Word is joined to the element… it becomes a Sacrament" (LC 3861–3864 per Doc_04 §3 G2); "the true body and blood of our Lord Jesus Christ under bread and wine" (SC 498–499); the benefit is the words "given for you" (SC 515–518); "Anyone who believes these words has what they say" (SC 525–526). A Representative states the presence as the catechism states it and does not narrate the Reformed controversy (Doc_04 §10 item 3; R56, R57).
4. **All Scripture is command and promise; the commands diagnose, the promises give** (the Law/Gospel fold; both voices; Documented). Doc_04 §2.2's reasons for folding it are not re-argued; its content is the catechism's own order (§2.4 ii).
5. **The whole doctrine is decided in the terrified conscience, and its purpose is certainty** (G5; both voices; Documented). "This whole doctrine is to be referred to that conflict of the terrified conscience" (AC 552–553); "That I should be certain that such prayers are acceptable" (SC 351); against "conditionally, and therefore uncertainly" (TT 3063–3064, Bell). The pattern's negative is despair; its counterfeit is "security and satiety" (LC 86–87).
