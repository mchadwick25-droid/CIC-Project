## 1. Distinct World Criteria

What makes this a distinct formation world is a cluster of recurring, evidenced features, not merely "the Reformation in general":

- **Recurring formation ecology, in two registers the census itself keeps deliberately distinct.** One register is Luther's and Melanchthon's own written corpus — treatises, catechisms, confessional documents — produced from inside the Wittenberg theological faculty and addressed outward to princes, clergy, and the wider church. The second register is the territorial churches' own lived, administered record: the Saxon visitation records of 1527–28 (Melanchthon's own *Instructions for the Visitors* governing them), church ordinances, and named lay voices publishing under their own names (Argula von Grumbach, Katharina Schütz Zell) — none of which are yet vendored into this project's library (see §10). The census's own source notes state the reason for holding these apart explicitly: **"Founder corpus kept distinct from the congregations' record so founder-gravity discipline can run"** — this is this world's own methodological self-awareness that a founder's stated program and a movement's actual lived reception are different evidentiary classes, not a claim that they are two strands (§6 below addresses why this is not a strand question).
- **Recurring candidate gravities** (preliminary only; Doc_04 is the formal test): justification by faith apart from meritorious works, argued repeatedly and centrally rather than as one doctrine among many (*A Treatise on Christian Liberty*'s "simul justus et peccator" paradox; Augsburg Confession Article IV); Scripture's own authority set directly against an intervening institutional hierarchy (*To the Christian Nobility*, *To the Papacy at Rome*); the sacraments reduced from seven to two or three and re-argued from Scripture directly (*A Prelude on the Babylonian Captivity of the Church*); and — the congregational register's own candidate, distinct from the founder's — catechesis as the actual mechanism by which ordinary households, not only clergy, were expected to hold the new doctrine (the Small Catechism's own household framing, §4 below).
- **Recurring patterns:** doctrine argued in writing, in German as much as Latin, addressed to specific named readers (the Archbishop of Mainz in 1517, the German nobility in 1520, Pope Leo X, brother-monks and princes) rather than settled first in council; a movement that produced its own confessional self-definition twice within its own window, at increasing formality (Augsburg Confession, 1530; Book of Concord, 1580); and — per the census's own visitation-record leg — a self-conscious institutional practice of *checking* whether the doctrine had actually reached ordinary believers, with the checking itself part of the historical record rather than a modern historian's retrospective addition.
- **What distinguishes this world from its nearest neighbors** (argued fully under World Continuity & Distinction, §8): from the Reformed cities (VI.2, same batch), this world is Lutheran specifically on the point that actually broke fellowship between them — the Eucharist (§7, §8.1). From the Anabaptist movements (VI.3, same batch), this world is a territorial, state-recognized magisterial Reformation, not a movement whose adherents this world's own authority structures helped suppress (§8.2). From the Tridentine Church (VI.22, same batch), this world is the doctrinal position Trent's own canons were convened to answer, not the answering body (§8.3).

## 2. Temporal Scope

### 2.1 Beginning point: 1517, on the movement's own founding document

**Documented, verified directly against this world's own vendored file, not inferred.** `luther_works-v1-selected_jacobs-spaeth1915.txt` carries, at its own head, the covering letter Luther sent to Albrecht, Archbishop of Magdeburg and Mainz, dated in the file itself **"OCTOBER 31, 1517"** (line 993), followed immediately by the Ninety-Five Theses themselves (marked in the file at line 436 as a heading reached earlier in a different arrangement of the same volume's front matter — the letter and the Theses travel together in this edition, the letter as covering correspondence for the Theses it encloses). This is the conventional founding date of the Lutheran Reformation and this document finds no reason in the vendored text to disturb it: **[Documented]**.

### 2.2 Ending point: 1580, matching the census, with the real alternative named rather than collapsed into it

The census's own `dates` field gives 1580 as this world's closing year — the **Book of Concord**, the Lutheran churches' own definitive collection of confessional documents (the Augsburg Confession and its Apology, both vendored here; the Small and Large Catechisms, both vendored here; plus documents not yet vendored — the Smalcald Articles, the Formula of Concord). This document adopts 1580 as the working ceiling, matching the census and Step 0 exactly, rather than proposing an unauthorized boundary change.

**The real alternative, named rather than smoothed over:** Step 0's own §1 candidate-identification line describes the window as running through "the consolidation of the Lutheran territorial churches after the **Peace of Augsburg (1555)**." The Peace of Augsburg is a different kind of marker than the Book of Concord — a political and legal settlement (imperial recognition of Lutheran princes' right to determine their territories' confession, under *cuius regio, eius religio*) rather than a doctrinal one. Both are real, well-attested closing candidates for different reasons: 1555 marks when this world's territorial-church structure gained durable legal standing within the Empire; 1580 marks when its own confessional self-definition reached its most complete and final form. **[Both Widely Accepted as dates and as events; which one better marks this world's own "ending" is a judgment this document does not need to resolve, since the census's own 1580 figure is not in tension with either — it is simply the later and more doctrinally complete of the two, and using it does not require treating 1555 as unimportant.]** Doc_02 and Doc_04 should treat 1555 as a real internal-transition marker (§2.4) rather than a rejected alternative.

### 2.3 What remains internal vs. what indicates transition

**Internal to this world, across the whole 1517–1580 span:** the argument for justification by faith and Scripture's authority against the papacy, continuously restated rather than abandoned; catechesis as the practical mechanism for lay formation, present from the earliest treatises (*A Treatise on the Holy Sacrament of Baptism*, 1519) through the 1529 catechisms to the Book of Concord's own inclusion of both catechisms as confessional documents in 1580; the territorial-church structure (visitation, ordinances) that begins forming in the 1520s and is still the operative structure in 1580.

**Indicates transition, within the window, disclosed as real internal change rather than treated as a single flat continuity:**

- **1521–1522 — the Wartburg exile and the Wittenberg unrest.** `luther_works-v2-selected_jacobs-spaeth1916.txt` carries **The Eight Wittenberg Sermons**, preached, per the corpus-map's own note and the file's own framing, on Luther's return from the Wartburg "to check the Karlstadt-led unrest" (1522) — direct evidence that Wittenberg's own reform was not monolithic even in its first years, and that Luther's own authority was exercised, in this instance, to check a more rapid or disorderly reform led by one of his own Wittenberg colleagues (Andreas Karlstadt). This document does not treat this as a strand within this world (§6) — Karlstadt's own subsequent trajectory moves him outside Wittenberg's own institutional authority, which is precisely why this is a transition-indicating event rather than an internal-variance one.
- **1524–1525 — the Peasants' War.** Named directly as a binding disclosure obligation by Step 0 (§4 item 2, binding on Doc_02): Luther's *Against the Murderous, Thieving Hordes of Peasants* (1525) is not in this world's vendored library yet (§10). This document does not have the text in hand to characterize it beyond what Step 0 already names, and defers full disclosure to Doc_02 as Step 0 requires — but marks the event itself as a real internal transition point: after 1525, this world's own relationship to political authority (siding with territorial princes against a popular uprising with real economic and religious grievances) is settled in a way it was not before.
- **1529 — Marburg.** Addressed in full in §7 and §8.1 below, since Step 0 (§4 item 3) makes this binding specifically on Doc_01.
- **1530 and 1580 — the confessional documents themselves**, already treated in §2.2 as closing-boundary candidates, are also internal developments in their own right: a movement that argued its case in occasional treatises in 1517–1520 had, by 1530, produced a single formal confession subscribed by named princes, and by 1580 a complete confessional corpus — a real change in institutional register, from protest literature to settled confession, that Doc_05 and Doc_07 should treat as a genuine ecological development rather than assume was already true from the start.

## 3. Geographic Scope

**Core:** Wittenberg, in Electoral Saxony — the university town where Luther held his professorship and where the movement's own founding acts (the Theses, the early treatises, the catechisms) originated. **[Documented]**, confirmed directly by every vendored file's own place of composition or address.

**Expansion within window:** the census's own `region` field states "Saxony, then N. Europe" and its `regions` field gives "North Europe." This document treats the *direction* of this spread — outward from Electoral Saxony into other German territorial states, and from there toward Scandinavia — as **Widely Accepted**, on uncontested general history this document has not independently re-verified against a dedicated secondary source this pass (a gap named for Doc_02, not concealed here): several German princes had adopted the Lutheran confession by the 1530s, and the Scandinavian kingdoms' own state churches (Denmark-Norway, then Sweden) moved toward Lutheran establishment within or just after this world's own window. **This document does not commit to specific Scandinavian conversion dates** — they are well short of a claim this document can support from the vendored library alone, and are logged as an open item for Doc_02 (§11) rather than stated here as fact.

**A named site this world's own vendored library speaks to directly but that is not itself a separate geographic node:** the Diet of Augsburg (1530), where the Augsburg Confession was submitted to Emperor Charles V, took place in Augsburg, not Wittenberg — a reminder that this world's own confessional acts happened at the level of the whole Empire's own political geography, not only inside Electoral Saxony.

## 4. Cultural Scope

**Linguistic:** German and Latin both, deliberately and simultaneously. The vendored corpus itself shows this directly — treatises addressed to a general German readership (*To the Christian Nobility*) sit alongside confessional Latin documents meant for an imperial and international audience (the Augsburg Confession's own submission to Charles V). **This is a distinguishing feature worth naming plainly**, not a footnote: Luther's own catechisms and hymns (not yet vendored, see §10) were written in German specifically so that lay households, not only clergy, could use them — the census's own `why` field names this directly ("put the Bible, a catechism and a hymnbook into ordinary German households").

**Social — the founder/congregation asymmetry named here, not only in §1.** The vendored library currently gives this document Luther's and Melanchthon's own voices in full, and gives it nothing yet in the ordinary believer's own voice or in the visitation record's own words (§10). This document does not know, from what it has actually read, how well Wittenberg's doctrine reached an ordinary Saxon villager by, say, 1550 — only that the project's own census entry states this is a live, documented, and disillusioning question ("what villagers actually knew... considerably less than the pamphlets would suggest," citing the Saxon visitation records) and a live scholarly debate (Strauss's "failure" reading versus Scribner's and Kittelson's critiques, per the census). **This document treats that gap as Doc_02's to close with the visitation records and Sehling's *Kirchenordnungen*, not something to answer from the founder corpus alone.**

**Women's presence — a real Missing Voices matter, named rather than deferred to silence (Constitution Article 20).** The census names two women publishing under their own names in the 1520s and after — **Argula von Grumbach** (Bavarian noblewoman, pamphlets defending the evangelical cause) and **Katharina Schütz Zell** (Strasbourg pastor's wife, devotional writing, hymn collections, open letters) — plus **Katharina von Bora**, Luther's own wife, whose household the census calls "the movement's most-watched model of a pastor's family." None of the three has a vendored primary text in this world's library yet (§10); this document cannot verify any claim about their voices beyond what the census itself states, and does not attempt to. **A further placement question, flagged rather than resolved:** Katharina Schütz Zell writes from Strasbourg, which the census's own note calls "wider evangelical orbit" — Strasbourg's own reformers (Bucer, Capito) sit closer to the Reformed tradition in some respects than to Wittenberg specifically, and Doc_02 should settle whether Zell belongs to this world's own congregational record or is better treated as an adjacent-world witness, rather than this document assuming the placement.

**Political:** the territorial-church model — princes and city councils, not a single central Lutheran hierarchy, determined and administered the reform within their own lands, backed after 1555 by imperial legal recognition (§2.2). Authority ran through named theologians (Luther, Melanchthon) advising and instructing territorial rulers and their visitors, not through an independent ecclesiastical structure parallel to the state.

**Institutional:** a university faculty (Wittenberg's own theology faculty) functioning as the movement's doctrinal center, producing both occasional polemical treatises and settled catechetical and confessional documents — the same two-register structure named in §1.

## 5. World Separation Criteria

**Has a new gravity emerged marking entry into this world?** Yes: Luther's 1517 protest against indulgence sales, escalating within three years (1520) into a full argument against the papacy's own sacramental and institutional authority, marks a clear entry point — a formation practice (doctrine argued from Scripture against an intervening hierarchy, addressed to specific named readers) distinct from the late-medieval piety this world's own census `relationsSummary` names as its parent ("child of late-medieval piety and its protest").

**Has a major gravity disappeared?** The medieval sacramental system's own seven-sacrament structure and the papacy's own claimed authority over doctrine are both explicitly and repeatedly refused within this world's own vendored texts (*A Prelude on the Babylonian Captivity of the Church*; *To the Papacy at Rome*) — not merely absent, but argued against by name.

**Has formation changed substantially?** Yes: from late-medieval sacramental and penitential practice to catechetical instruction grounded directly in Scripture and in a small set of confessional documents, with the household (not only the monastery or the confessional) as a formation site the movement explicitly targets (§4).

**Has worship changed substantially?** This document cannot yet answer this with the depth Doc_05 will need — the vendored library's *A Treatise on the New Testament, That Is the Holy Mass* (1520) and the Babylonian Captivity's own sacramental sections argue for a changed theology of the Mass (testament/promise rather than sacrifice), but the actual reformed liturgical practice as lived (German-language services, congregational hymn-singing) rests on texts — hymns, service orders — not yet vendored (§10). Doc_05 should treat this as argued-for-in-doctrine but not yet demonstrated-in-lived-practice from this world's own current library.

**Has authority changed substantially?** Yes, and in a specific, evidenced direction: from papal and episcopal authority to a settlement in which territorial princes hold legal authority over their own churches' confession (§2.2, §3), advised by a university theological faculty rather than ruled by an independent church hierarchy.

**Has interpretation changed substantially?** Yes: *sola scriptura* — Scripture's own authority set against councils, popes, and canon law where they conflict with it — is argued directly and repeatedly across the vendored treatises, a genuinely new interpretive rule for this world rather than an inherited one.

## 6. Strand Determination (Constitution Article 21)

**Finding: provisionally strand-singular, per the Framework's own stated default.**

This document considered, and rejects, one candidate strand division before recording the default: the founder-corpus/congregation-record asymmetry named in §1 and §4. **This is not a strand in Article 21's own sense.** A strand is "meaningfully distinct patterns of formation emphasis, practice, authority structure, or ecological orientation within a single world" — evidenced by, for example, two coexisting communities with different practices. What the census's own source notes describe instead is an *evidentiary* asymmetry (one register documented by its own authors' treatises, the other documented by administrators observing lay reception) internal to a single, unified formation ecology — the same distinction Author Gravity Assessment (Constitution Article 26, Doc_02) exists to handle. Treating it as a strand would misfile a sourcing problem as a formation-pattern finding.

A second candidate this document also considered and rejects as a strand, for the same reason as Gallic Monastic-Ascetic Christianity's own Doc_01 rejected a comparable temptation: the 1522 Wittenberg unrest (§2.3) shows real internal disagreement (Luther versus Karlstadt) but resolves within the window, under Luther's own continued authority, rather than persisting as two coexisting patterns — a transition event, not a strand.

**No positive evidence for genuinely distinct internal strands has surfaced in this document's own reading of the vendored library.** This world is recorded provisionally strand-singular, per the Framework's own default rule, pending whatever Doc_04's cross-strand gravity testing may find.

## 7. Preliminary Forces Identification

Quoting the Forces Framework directly (V1.1 §4, Step 1): *"What generated this world? What larger world was it embedded in? What was it responding to? What was it refusing?"*

**What generated this world:** an Augustinian friar and university theology professor's own developing conviction — argued first over the specific, bounded question of indulgence sales, then rapidly generalized — that the practice of the late-medieval Western church had come to rest on an authority (the papacy's own claimed power to bind and loose, extended into the sale of indulgences) that Scripture itself does not support. The vendored 1517 letter to Albrecht of Mainz shows this beginning as a bounded, respectful protest to a specific archbishop about a specific abuse, not yet a movement — the escalation to a full institutional break happens across the following three years, evidenced directly in the 1520 treatises' own widening scope.

**What larger world it was embedded in:** late-medieval Western European Christendom under a single, if already contested, papal authority, with the Holy Roman Empire as its immediate political frame — an empire of numerous semi-autonomous princes and cities whose own political fragmentation is part of why a territorial, prince-by-prince settlement (rather than a single empire-wide resolution) became this world's own eventual legal shape (§2.2, §3).

**What it was responding to:** the practical and pastoral problem, argued directly in the vendored texts, of a laity taught to rely on indulgences, private confession to a priest, and the church's own sacramental system for assurance of salvation, rather than on direct trust in Scripture's own promise. *A Treatise on Christian Liberty* and the Babylonian Captivity both argue this is a pastoral failure with real consequences for ordinary believers' own spiritual confidence, not only an abstract doctrinal dispute.

**What it was refusing:** most directly and by name, the papacy's own claimed authority to define doctrine and administer the sacraments as a matter that binds the conscience apart from Scripture (*To the Papacy at Rome*; the Babylonian Captivity's reduction of the sacraments) — refused in writing, addressed to named readers, rather than argued only within a closed circle of sympathizers. **A second, later refusal, real and disclosed rather than smoothed into a single undifferentiated "Reformation":** the Eucharistic theology of the Reformed tradition, refused directly at the **Marburg Colloquy (1529)**. This world's own Step 0 (§2 A3) and the Reformed cities' own sibling Step 0 (§2 A3, "the Marburg break with Luther") both name this as a real, sharp, contemporary disagreement between two movements this project is building in the same batch — Luther's own insistence on Christ's bodily presence in the Eucharist against Zwingli's own symbolic reading broke fellowship between Wittenberg and the Swiss Reformed cities, and **this document states that plainly, as this world's own unresolved theological difference with a genuine contemporary, not a settled question either world's own Doc_01 can close unilaterally.** This document does not yet have a vendored primary source describing Luther's own side of the Marburg exchange directly (a gap logged at §11 for Doc_02) and does not overstate what it can currently source.

This preliminary work frames, but does not substitute for, the complete forces analysis at Doc_08.

## 8. World Continuity & Distinction

### 8.1 vs. the Reformed cities (`the-reformed-cities` / Atlas VI.2, same batch, unbuilt)

**Sibling-rival**, per this world's own census `relationsSummary`. The two movements share the same generating refusal of papal authority and much of the same early theological vocabulary (justification by faith, Scripture's authority), and diverge sharply and specifically at the Eucharist (§7). This document treats the two as genuine contemporaries in real, disclosed tension — neither this document nor VI.2's own Doc_01, when it exists, should present Marburg as resolved in its own favor.

### 8.2 vs. the Anabaptist movements (`the-anabaptist-movements` / Atlas VI.3, same batch, unbuilt)

The Anabaptist movements' own Step 0 (§2 A3) states the relationship from its own side plainly: it was "persecuted by, and in real tension with, both Lutheran Wittenberg and the Reformed cities... from the establishment side." This document holds the other side of that same disclosure: this world's own territorial-church settlement (§3, §5) was itself part of the state authority that suppressed the more radical Reformation, and Luther's own writings against the Peasants' War (§2.3) sit on this same axis, even though this document does not yet have that text vendored to characterize it in Luther's own words. Doc_02 should not let this world's own account of its formation stand in for the Anabaptists' own account of what they suffered from it.

### 8.3 vs. the Tridentine Church (`the-tridentine-church` / Atlas VI.22, same batch, unbuilt)

**Rival of Rome**, per this world's own census `relationsSummary`, and named directly and specifically from the Tridentine side: that world's own Step 0 (§2 A3) states "Lutheran Wittenberg and the Reformed cities are this candidate's own named, direct doctrinal targets — Trent's own canons anathematize positions each of those two candidates' own confessional documents affirm," and (§4 item 5) names this relationship binding on both worlds' Doc_01s as "primary structural material, not incidental." This document affirms that framing from this world's own side: the Augsburg Confession and its Apology (both vendored here) are exactly the kind of confessional text Trent's own canons would later be convened, in part, to answer. Doc_02 for both worlds should expect to cite each other as primary named rivals, per both Step 0 documents' own instruction.

### 8.4 vs. the Society of Jesus (`the-society-of-jesus` / Atlas VI.11, same batch, unbuilt)

Unlike this world's relationship to the three movements above, this is not a rival-by-direct-argument relationship. The Society was founded in 1540, during Luther's own lifetime (he died in 1546), but in Rome and Paris, not in the German lands, and its own vigorous implementation of Trent's program in German-speaking territory (e.g., Peter Canisius's later mission) falls institutionally under the Tridentine Church's own program (§8.3) rather than under any direct Luther-Jesuit contact. This document finds no documented direct link and does not manufacture one.

### 8.5 vs. Lollardy (`lollardy` / Atlas V.5, same batch, unbuilt, and an Era VI world despite the batch's own Era VII framing)

Geographically and institutionally distinct — England, and per that movement's own stated window (1380–1520, per the batch materials this document has seen), a movement that had already gone underground well before Luther's own protest began. This document finds no documented direct contact and, per the same discipline as §8.4, does not assert a "forerunner" relationship it cannot source; if one exists in the secondary literature, it is Doc_02's to establish, not this document's to assume.

### 8.6 vs. the Augustinian Hermits (unbuilt, no separate Atlas entry — routed into this world's own record as its seedbed)

This world's own census `relationsSummary` states this directly: **"the Augustinian Hermits are its seedbed (Luther's own order [S]) — their Reformation-lands houses dissolved into this story, the Catholic remainder continuing in the Tridentine orbit."** Luther was himself an Augustinian friar before and during the early years of this world's own formation; this document treats that inheritance as a genuine and direct continuity, not a mere biographical detail, and notes — per the same census note — that the order itself did not survive as a single, undivided institution: its houses in Reformation territory became part of this world's own story, while its remainder continued within the Catholic Church, now within the Tridentine Church's own orbit (§8.3). This is this world's one clear predecessor-institution relationship, distinct from the papacy-in-general it came to refuse.
