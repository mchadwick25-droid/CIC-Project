## 0. What this document is, and three disciplines it applies

**What it is.** A working candidate list, organized by this world's own conceptual clusters, with short source-grounded preliminary definitions, Origin and Risk/Function tags, provisional tier estimates, and Author Gravity flags — so that Doc_04's gravity discovery can be conducted in this world's own words rather than in modern analytical vocabulary. **What it is not:** it is not Doc_06. No entry below carries a World Meaning, an Ecological Function, or a paired Modern-Hearing/World-Hearing Distortion Risk at Tier 1 depth; the Lexicon Framework (Part I) warns that "premature development at the candidate stage risks anchoring later full entries to an early, less-considered framing," and this document takes that literally. Where an entry notes a distortion, it is one clause flagging the gap for Doc_06, not the Doc_06 writeup.

**Discipline 1 — read, don't infer.** Every term below was found in a passage read in a vendored file during this pass, and every quoted phrase is given with the file and line(s) where it begins. The Document Log (§13) records exactly which sections were read and which were not. Where a term is prominent in a section *not* read this pass (e.g., the *Fourteen of Consolation*, *The Magnificat*'s body, the Third through Seventh Wittenberg Sermons, the Apology beyond Articles IV, XII and the passages the Registry already verified, *Bondage* beyond its opening sections), that is stated as a coverage limit, not filled in from general knowledge. Nothing is drawn from *On the Jews and Their Lies* (1543) or *Against the Murderous, Thieving Hordes of Peasants* (1525) — neither is vendored, and R94 bars their language; where the peasants' unrest surfaces below, it is the 1522 *Earnest Exhortation* [R19], a different text, and is named as such. Nothing is drawn from Katharina Schütz Zell [R55, Excluded].

**Discipline 2 — name the layer.** This world's vocabulary reaches this document through named modern hands: the Philadelphia Edition translators (C. M. Jacobs, Steinhaeuser, Lambert, Schindel, Steimle, Schmauk, Neve — 1915, 1916, 1930) with their sidenotes, introductions and footnotes; Bente and Dau (*Concordia Triglotta*, 1921) for the Large Catechism and the Apology, with bracketed German-text insertions; Robert E. Smith (1994/2004) for the Small Catechism, in modern idiom; Leonard Woolsey Bacon (1883) for the hymns, whose English is a composite rendering by his own account; Captain Henry Bell (1646) via Morley (1886) for the Table Talk; Henry Cole (1823) for *The Bondage of the Will*, in badly degraded OCR; and the anonymous Gutenberg translator of the Augsburg Confession. **Where a term's English wording is the translator's choice, the entry says so** — an editor's sidenote or introduction is never treated as Luther's own text (the phrase "The Priesthood of Believers," for instance, is Jacobs's sidenote at v2 2171; Luther's own words are "we are all priests"). **Original-language headwords are recorded only where the vendored English itself quotes or glosses them** — Luther's own German in the text (*Taufe*/*tief*, v1 2256–2257; *Gemeinde*, *Christenheit*, *Versammlung*, LC 2822–2858), a translator's bracketed or footnoted gloss (*Gemeinden*, v1 10944; *Christenheit* and *Gemeinde*, v1 14739–14793; *Geysterey*, v3 21027; *Bussetun*, v1 1523; *Gewissen*, v3 7180; *Mein Wort*, v3 11232), or Latin quoted in the text (*Poenitentiam agite*, *opus operatum*, *communio*/*excommunicare*, *Accedat verbum ad elementum*, *meritum congrui*/*condigni*, *prima gratia*, *Ascensus mentis ad Deum*, *jus verbi*/*executio*). **No German or Latin lemma is supplied from general knowledge**, and no entry claims a lemma the vendored text does not show. In particular, the Latin sola-formulas (*sola fide*, *sola scriptura*, *sola gratia*) do not occur anywhere in the vendored English; what the texts say, in English, is recorded at 2.1 and 3.3.

**Discipline 3 — one founder's voice in many registers, and a second voice under princes' signatures.** Doc_02 §1.1 establishes that Luther reaches this library in nine distinct registers — academic disputation, vernacular/Latin treatise, polemic, devotional-exegetical writing for a prince, spoken pastoral address to his own congregation, catechesis, hymnody, conversation, and sustained argument against a learned opponent — and that the Author Gravity problem is precisely that all nine are his. Doc_02 §1.2 establishes that the Augsburg Confession is a corporate princely "we" drafted by Melanchthon, and the Apology is Melanchthon in his own name. CF V7.4 Step 3 requires flagging "terms that appear to carry unusual weight from specific voices." This document therefore records, for every term, **which registers and which of the two voices actually use it as read**, and flags Author Gravity on a scale fitted to this world's actual shape: **none** (Luther in two or more registers *and* Melanchthon); **Luther-only, cross-register**; **Luther-only, single-register** (the register named); **Melanchthon-only**; **layer-only** (a translator's or editor's wording, not either voice). Non-founder voices reach this library only at one remove (Doc_02 §1.4) and are named where they bear on a term. This is the classification most likely to be wrong in detail and most consequential for Doc_04; it is stated per term so a reviewer can check it against the files.

**Strand attribution.** Doc_01 §6 records this world as **strand-singular**, as its own considered finding under Constitution Article 21's definition, having considered and rejected both the founder/congregation evidentiary asymmetry and the 1522 Wittenberg unrest as strand candidates. This document therefore does **not** split any term by strand, and — unlike the fleet's Gallic Doc_03, which records a node-attestation pattern because its Doc_01 left a two-worlds question open — records no node either, since Doc_01 left no such question open here. ## 1. Pardon, penalty, and the treasure — the 1517 vocabulary

### 1.1 indulgence / pardon / "letters of pardon"
- **Preliminary definition.** The papal remission of *penalty* (not guilt) that the 1517 Theses dispute, which Jacobs's footnote states Luther "uses... interchangeably" with "pardon"; by 1520 named in capitals as "a knavish trick of the Roman sycophants," and in 1529 the standing contrast-case for what the Word makes precious.
- **Evidence.** v1 1230–1232 (Th. 21: "those preachers of indulgences are in error, who say that by the pope's indulgences a man is freed from every penalty, and saved"); 1253–1254 (Th. 27: "so soon as the penny jingles into the money-box, the soul flies out"); 1285–1286 (Th. 36: "Every truly repentant Christian has a right to full remission of penalty and guilt, even without letters of pardon"); 1348–1349 (Th. 52: "The assurance of salvation by letters of pardon is vain"); 1553–1554 (Jacobs's fn. [9]: "Luther uses the terms 'pardon' and 'indulgence' interchangeably"); v2 6499 ("INDULGENCES ARE A KNAVISH TRICK OF THE ROMAN SYCOPHANTS"); LC 3805–3807 ("when the Pope with his letters and bulls dispensed indulgences"); v1 357–358 (1545: "When I took up this matter against Indulgences").
- **Voices.** Luther — disputation, treatise, catechesis, 1545 retrospect. Melanchthon: the Apology's fourteen hits (term count) were not read this pass. "Pardon" as the English of the Theses is Jacobs's rendering, glossed by his own footnote.
- **Tags.** [SC] [DR] [TC] [RT]. Distortion flag: modern "indulgence" (self-gratification) vs. a canonical remission of penalty.
- **Tier (est.).** 1. **AG.** Luther-only, cross-register (Melanchthon's Apology not read for it).
- **Registry.** 1, 2, 12, 25.

### 1.2 repentance / penance (*Poenitentiam agite*)
- **Preliminary definition.** The first Thesis's whole-life "repentance," explicitly *not* "sacramental penance, i. e., confession and satisfaction"; by 1530 confessionally defined as two parts, "contrition... [and] faith," the Roman three-part "penance" (contrition, confession, satisfaction) being the thing refused.
- **Evidence.** v1 1154–1156 (Th. 1: Christ "when He said _Poenitentiam agite_... willed that the whole life of believers should be repentance"); 1158–1160 (Th. 2: "This word cannot be understood to mean sacramental penance, i. e., confession and satisfaction, which is administered by the priests"); 1522–1524 (Jacobs's fn. [1]: "English 'repent'; German _Bussetun_. The Latin and German versions may also be rendered, 'Do penance'; the Greek, on the other hand, can only mean 'Repent'"); v2 8953–8954 ("Three parts have they made of penance,--contrition, confession, and satisfaction"); AC 344–349 ("repentance consists properly of these two parts: One is contrition, that is, terrors smiting the conscience through the knowledge of sin; the other is faith, which is born of the Gospel, or of absolution"); Ap 4900–4901 ("the parts of repentance are contrition and faith"); SC 398–399 (baptism means "the old Adam in us should be drowned by daily sorrow and repentance").
- **Voices.** Luther — disputation, treatise, catechesis; Melanchthon — AC and Apology. The English opposition "repentance"/"penance" is the translators' way of carrying the Latin/German ambiguity Jacobs's footnote itself explains; a Representative should not assume the two English words were two words for Luther.
- **Tags.** [SC] [DR] [TC] [RT]. Distortion flag: "penance" heard as a punishment; "repentance" heard as a feeling — the world's word is a whole-life turning with faith as its second part.
- **Tier (est.).** 1. **AG.** none.
- **Registry.** 2, 12, 26, 37, 38.

### 1.3 contrition / attrition
- **Preliminary definition.** Sorrow for sin: in 1517 "True contrition seeks and loves penalties"; in 1520 a thing found "only where there is a lively faith in the promises and the threats of God," against a scholastic "attrition" that the keys were said to convert into contrition; in 1530 "terrors smiting the conscience."
- **Evidence.** v1 1264–1265 (Th. 30: "No one is sure that his own contrition is sincere"); 1301–1303 (Th. 40); v2 8960–8962 ("they teach that contrition precedes faith in the promise; they hold it much too cheap"); 8968–8971 ("invented a so-called 'attrition,' which is converted into contrition by virtue of the power of the keys"); 8976–8977 ("A contrite heart is a precious thing, but it is found only where there is a lively faith in the promises and the threats of God"); AC 344–345.
- **Voices.** Luther — disputation, treatise; Melanchthon — AC. 
- **Tags.** [SC] [TC] [DR].
- **Tier (est.).** 2. **AG.** none.
- **Registry.** 2, 12, 37.

### 1.4 satisfaction — two referents
- **Preliminary definition.** (a) The penitent's own "satisfaction," the third part of Roman penance, which indulgences relax and which "human traditions" were "instituted... to make"; (b) Christ's own "satisfaction for our sins," the ground of justification. The same English word does opposite work in the same confession.
- **Evidence.** (a) v1 1158–1160 (Th. 2); 1278–1279 (Th. 34: "these 'graces of pardon' concern only the penalties of sacramental satisfaction, and these are appointed by man"); 1556–1557 (fn. [10]); AC 400–404 ("human traditions instituted to propitiate God, to merit grace, and to make satisfaction for sins, are opposed to the Gospel"); AC 916–917 ("Aforetime satisfactions were immoderately extolled"). (b) AC 237–238 ("who, by His death, has made satisfaction for our sins"); AC 847 ("Christ's passion was an oblation and satisfaction").
- **Voices.** Luther — disputation; Melanchthon — AC in both senses. The Apology's 122 hits (term count) were not read.
- **Tags.** [SC] [TC] [DR]. Distortion flag: the two referents collapse in modern hearing.
- **Tier (est.).** 2. **AG.** cross-voice, same word, two referents — flagged for Doc_06 as an alias problem, not a plurality of voices.
- **Registry.** 2, 37.

### 1.5 purgatory
- **Preliminary definition.** In 1517 a state the Theses reason about ("Hell, purgatory, and heaven seem to differ as do despair, almost-despair, and the assurance of safety"); by 1542 one of the "popish horrors... for the dead" abolished from the movement's own burial practice.
- **Evidence.** v1 1213–1214 (Th. 16); 1191–1192 (Th. 10: priests who "reserve canonical penances for purgatory"); 1457–1461 (Th. 82); Hy 975–978 ("abolished, done away, and out-and-out made an end of the popish horrors, such as wakes, masses for the soul, obsequies, purgatory, and all other mummeries for the dead"); Hy 1015–1017 ("our doctrine of the resurrection, not that of purgatory with its pains and expiations").
- **Voices.** Luther — disputation (1517), hymnal preface (1542, Bacon's English). Melanchthon: the Apology's thirty hits (term count) were not read.
- **Tags.** [SC] [RT] [DR].
- **Tier (est.).** 2. **AG.** Luther-only, cross-register.
- **Registry.** 2, 28.

### 1.6 the keys / power of the keys
- **Preliminary definition.** Christ's word "Whatsoever thou shalt bind," read in this world as a *promise* that "calls forth the faith of the penitent," not a *power*; given "not... to Peter alone, but to the whole community"; confessionally, "a power or commandment of God, to preach the Gospel, to remit and retain sins, and to administer Sacraments" — and nothing more.
- **Evidence.** v1 1249–1251 (Th. 26: "not by the power of the keys (which he does not possess)") with Jacobs's fn. [5] at 1538–1540 ("not a denial of the power of the keys, i. e., the power to forgive and retain sin, but merely that the power of the keys extends to purgatory"); v2 2388–2392 ("the keys were not given to Peter alone, but to the whole community... the keys were not ordained for doctrine or government, but only for the binding and loosing of sin"); v2 8917–8921 ("He calls forth the faith of the penitent... Here there is no mention at all of power, but of the ministry of him that absolves"); v2 8927–8928 ("a wicked usurpation of power to claim the keys for the pope alone"); AC 912 ("The power of the Keys is set forth in its beauty"); AC 1287–1290.
- **Voices.** Luther — disputation, treatise; Melanchthon — AC.
- **Tags.** [SC] [TC] [DR] [RT].
- **Tier (est.).** 1. **AG.** none.
- **Registry.** 2, 11, 12, 37.

### 1.7 the cross — "Cross, cross"; bearing affliction
- **Preliminary definition.** The Theses' closing antithesis to the preachers of "Peace, peace"; the Christian's "banner" in baptism; confessionally, "the true, earnest, and unfeigned mortification, to wit, to be exercised with divers afflictions, and to be crucified with Christ" — against monastic self-chosen discipline.
- **Evidence.** v1 1506–1510 (Th. 92–93: "Blessed be all those prophets who say to the people of Christ, 'Cross, cross,' and there is no cross!"); 1512–1518 (Th. 94–95); 2269–2270 ("Christ, our Captain, under Whose banner (i. e., the Holy Cross) we continually fight against sin"); AC 1043–1046 ("they have always taught concerning the cross that it behooves Christians to bear afflictions. This is the true, earnest, and unfeigned mortification").
- **Voices.** Luther — disputation, treatise; Melanchthon — AC. The phrase "theology of the cross" occurs nowhere in the vendored English (checked).
- **Tags.** [SC] [DR] [RT].
- **Tier (est.).** 2. **AG.** none.
- **Registry.** 2, 3, 37.

## 2. Faith, works, and righteousness — the justification cluster

### 2.1 faith / "faith alone" / "by faith alone"
- **Preliminary definition.** The trust that "clings to the word of the promise made by God"; "the saving and efficacious use of the Word of God"; "the first and highest, the most precious of all good works"; confessionally, not "merely the knowledge of the history" but "confidence which consoles and encourages the terrified mind." The English formula is "faith alone"/"by faith alone"; no Latin *sola fide* occurs in the vendored files.
- **Evidence.** AC 234–239 ("men cannot be justified before God by their own strength, merits, or works, but are freely justified for Christ's sake, through faith"); AC 268–270 (Ambrose quoted: "without works, by faith alone"); Ap 1112 (heading: "_That We Obtain Remission of Sins by Faith Alone in Christ._"), 1118–1120 ("By faith alone in Christ, not through love, not because of love or works, do we acquire the remission of sins, although love follows faith"); v2 11713–11714 ("faith alone is the saving and efficacious use of the Word of God"); 11720–11722 ("justified by faith alone and not by any works"); 7341–7343 (the mass "is to be approached, not with any work or strength or merit, but with faith alone"); v1 6834–6835 ("The first and highest, the most precious of all good works is faith in Christ"); AC 568–573, 581–583 (faith as "confidence which consoles and encourages the terrified mind"); v2 14689–14690 ("a faith without love is not enough--rather it is not faith at all"); **LC 3916–3917 (the formula in an opponent's mouth: "our would-be wise, new spirits assert that faith alone saves, and that works and external things avail nothing")**.
- **Voices.** Luther — treatise, sermon, catechesis; Melanchthon — AC, Apology. Note for Doc_04: in the Large Catechism the *same* formula is quoted as the sectarians' slogan against baptism — this world's own "faith alone" is a faith that "must have something which it believes... upon which it stands and rests" (LC 3920–3921).
- **Tags.** [SC] [DR] [TC] [RT]. Distortion flag: "faith" heard as belief-that; the world's sense is trust-in-a-promise, with a real inner-Lutheran caveat against the slogan's misuse.
- **Tier (est.).** 1. **AG.** none.
- **Registry.** 6, 12, 13, 15, 25, 37, 38.

### 2.2 justification / to justify / "the righteousness of faith"
- **Preliminary definition.** God's act of accounting or making a sinner righteous "for Christ's sake, through faith": AC IV says "This faith God imputes for righteousness in His sight"; the Apology says "by faith alone we are justified, understanding justification as the making of a righteous man out of an unrighteous, or that he be regenerated," and calls the whole matter "the chief topic of Christian doctrine."
- **Evidence.** AC 234–239; AC 515–516 ("the deepest silence in their sermons concerning the righteousness of faith"); Ap 561–562 ("in this controversy the chief topic of Christian doctrine is treated"); Ap 1120–1122; v2 11737–11738 ("justified by the merits of another, namely, of Christ alone"); 11744–11745 ("since faith alone justifies, it is clear that the inward man cannot be justified, made free and be saved by any outward work"); Co 15139–15141 (Luther's concession to Erasmus on "the grand hinge," per R34's recovered reading — OCR-degraded, not re-read this pass).
- **Voices.** Luther — treatise; Melanchthon — AC, Apology (the Apology's 376 hits make it the term's home; only Article IV's opening and the "faith alone" passages were read).
- **Tags.** [SC] [TC] [DR] [RT] **[CT]**. **Contest type: the term's meaning within its historical context, and its relationship to present-day traditions that use the term differently.** The vendored texts themselves carry both an imputing wording (AC 238) and a "making of a righteous man" wording (Ap 1121); how these relate — and how present-day Lutheran, Catholic and Reformed traditions read the same word — is a live specialist question. **Disclosed:** no secondary source is rowed in the Registry for this contest; the tag rests on the in-text tension and on this drafting pass's judgment, and Doc_06 owes a rowed source before the tag is carried into a Tier 1 entry.
- **Tier (est.).** 1. **AG.** none — but weighted to Melanchthon's Apology for the technical development.
- **Registry.** 13, 34, 37, 38.

### 2.3 good works / "works"
- **Preliminary definition.** Not the medieval short list ("praying in church, fasting, and almsgiving") but "no good works except those which God has commanded" — so that working at a trade, walking, eating, sleeping are good works when done in faith; confessionally "necessary... because it is the will of God," never "to merit justification."
- **Evidence.** v1 6817–6818 ("there are no good works except those which God has commanded"); 6863–6870 ("when they work at their trade, walk, stand, eat, drink, sleep... they define good works so narrowly that they are made to consist only of praying in church, fasting, and almsgiving"); AC 496 ("Our teachers are falsely accused of forbidding good Works"); AC 262–265; AC 585–587; v1 1312–1313 (Th. 43: "he who gives to the poor or lends to the needy does a better work than buying pardons"); v2 11749–11751 ("lay aside all trust in works").
- **Voices.** Luther — disputation, treatise; Melanchthon — AC.
- **Tags.** [SC] [DR] [RT]. Distortion flag: "works" heard as charity or ritual; the world's list includes the whole of ordinary life.
- **Tier (est.).** 1. **AG.** none.
- **Registry.** 2, 6, 13, 37.

### 2.4 merit (*meritum congrui* / *meritum condigni*)
- **Preliminary definition.** What this world denies to human works and reserves to Christ: the Theses deny that indulgences are "the merits of Christ and the Saints"; the Apology names and rejects the scholastic distinction in its own Latin; the penitential hymn sings "Naught building on my merit."
- **Evidence.** v1 1373–1375 (Th. 58); AC 234–235 ("their own strength, merits, or works"); Ap 681–686 ("Their feigning a distinction between _meritum congrui_ and _meritum condigni_ [due merit and true, complete merit] is only an artifice in order not to appear openly to Pelagianize" — the bracket is Bente/Dau's); Ap 659–660 ("_prima gratia_, 'first grace,' which they understand as a habit"); v2 11737 ("the merits of another"); Hy 1671–1672 (hymn IV, Bacon's English: "my hope in God shall rest, / Naught building on my merit").
- **Voices.** Luther — disputation, treatise, hymn (Bacon's rendering); Melanchthon — AC, Apology. The Latin terms are quoted in Melanchthon's own text as the adversaries' vocabulary.
- **Tags.** [SC] [TC] [DR].
- **Tier (est.).** 2. **AG.** none.
- **Registry.** 2, 13, 27, 37, 38.

### 2.5 grace / "gracious God"
- **Preliminary definition.** God's free favour, given "not because I've earned it or deserved it"; what the Creed "brings" as against the Commandments; the thing the sacrament makes a Christian "sure" of — "I cannot doubt I have a gracious God."
- **Evidence.** SC 181–183 ("because of His pure, fatherly and divine goodness and His mercy, not because I've earned it or deserved it"); SC 161 ("He promises grace and all good things"); LC 2973–2975 ("this [namely, the doctrine of faith] brings pure grace, and makes us godly and acceptable to God" — the bracket is Bente/Dau's); v2 7346–7347 ("in free and unmerited mercy makes a beginning and offers us the word of His promise"); v2 15723–15724 ("I cannot doubt I have a gracious God"); Hy 1292–1293 (hymn I: "God saw, in his eternal grace, / My sorrow out of measure"); v1 6855–6856 ("no good conscience toward God").
- **Voices.** Luther — treatise, sermon, catechesis, hymn; Melanchthon — AC ("through Baptism is offered the grace of God," 310).
- **Tags.** [SC] [DR] [RT].
- **Tier (est.).** 1. **AG.** none.
- **Registry.** 12, 15, 25, 26, 27, 37.

### 2.6 Law and Gospel — "commands and promises"
- **Preliminary definition.** The world's own division of all Scripture into two parts: "the commands show us what we ought to do, but do not give us the power to do it... the promises of God give what the commands of God ask"; the Apology: "All Scripture ought to be distributed into these two principal topics, the Law and the promises." Catechetically, the Commandments diagnose the "illness," the Creed shows the "remedy."
- **Evidence.** v2 11782–11788 ("all the Scriptures of God are divided into two parts--commands and promises... the commands show us what we ought to do, but do not give us the power to do it"); 11817–11822 ("the promises of God give what the commands of God ask... the promises of God belong to the New Testament, nay, they are the New Testament"); Ap 579–580; v2 13202–13210 (the _Commandments_ "teach a man to know his illness"; the _Creed_ "teaches him where he may find the remedy"); LC 2967–2969 ("the Creed is a doctrine quite different from the Ten Commandments; for the latter teaches indeed what we ought to do, but the former tells what God does for us"); v3 12239–12241 (the law's "another function... to teach men to recognize sin, that they may be made humble unto grace"); v1 10917–10918 ("the less law, the more justice; the fewer commandments, the more good works").
- **Voices.** Luther — treatise, catechesis (1520 and 1529); Melanchthon — Apology.
- **Tags.** [AS] [TC] [DR] [RT]. AS on the comparative test: the Reformed cities share Scripture's authority (Doc_01 §8.1) but this two-part *hermeneutic* is the organizing device of this world's own catechesis and confession as read; Doc_06 should confirm against VI.2's own Doc_03 when it exists.
- **Tier (est.).** 1. **AG.** none.
- **Registry.** 13, 14, 20, 25, 38.

### 2.7 promise / testament
- **Preliminary definition.** God's word that "must first come and give him a promise" before any human work; a *testament* is "a promise made by one about to die," so that "what we call the mass is the promise of remission of sins made to us by God"; confessionally the sacraments are "signs and testimonies of the will of God toward us" to which "faith be added to believe the promises."

### 2.8 conscience — "terrified," "anxious," "captive" consciences
- **Preliminary definition.** The inner court where the whole doctrine is decided: AC XX says the doctrine of faith "is to be referred to that conflict of the terrified conscience, neither can it be understood apart from that conflict"; it is what human laws "burden," what the pope "torments," what the sermons must not "lead astray," and what "many absolutions" strengthen.

- **Registry.** 4, 15, 16, 18, 25, 31, 37, 38.

### 2.9 free will / bondage / "assertion"
- **Preliminary definition.** Confessionally, a will with "some liberty to choose civil righteousness" but "no power, without the Holy Ghost, to work the righteousness of God"; in *Bondage* the thing Luther denies against Erasmus while defending the very posture of *asserting* — "The Holy Spirit is not a sceptic"; in the 1523 hymn, "Free-will against God's judgment fought, / And dead to good remained."

## 3. The Word, the Scriptures, and the doctrines of men — the interpretation cluster

### 3.1 the Word / Word of God — "the Word must do it"
- **Preliminary definition.** "One thing and one only is necessary for Christian life, righteousness and liberty. That one thing is the most holy Word of God, the Gospel of Christ"; the thing that, "joined to the element," makes a sacrament; the "mother that begets and bears every Christian"; and — in the 1522 sermons — the agent of reform itself: "I did nothing; the Word did it all."

## 4. Catechesis and the household — the formation cluster

### 4.1 catechism / "the three parts" / "the five parts"
- **Preliminary definition.** "Instruction for children, what every Christian must needs know" — the Ten Commandments, the Creed, and the Lord's Prayer, which "contain fully and completely everything that is in the Scriptures" (1520), "a compend and brief summary of all the Holy Scriptures" (1529), with the two sacraments' words added to make "five parts."

### 4.2 household / "father of a family" / "head of a household"

### 4.3 "What does this mean?" — the question-and-answer form
- **Preliminary definition.** The catechetical device that structures every Small Catechism part and the Large Catechism's own "Answer:" paragraphs — the form in which a child or "the simple-minded" is expected to hold doctrine.

### 4.4 "to have a god is to trust" — god, idol, Mammon

## 5. Word, sign, and faith — the sacramental cluster

### 5.1 sacrament — "sign, significance, and faith"; "the Word joined to the element"

### 5.6 the mass — sacrifice, "good work," private mass, "evangelical mass"

## 6. Priest, office, estate, calling — the priesthood-of-all-believers cluster

### 6.1 spiritual estate / temporal estate — "the Christian Estate"
- **Preliminary definition.** The medieval division Luther calls "pure invention": "all Christians are truly of the 'spiritual estate,' and there is among them no difference at all but that of office"; the "temporal power has become a member of the body of Christendom, and is of the 'spiritual estate,' though its work is of a temporal nature." The same estate-vocabulary is then kept for marriage ("the most common and noblest estate") and parenthood.
- **Evidence.** v2 2159–2169; 2233–2238 ("no difference between laymen and priests, princes and bishops, 'spirituals' and 'temporals,' as they call them, except that of office and work, but not of 'estate'"); 2199–2200 ("the Christian Estate"); 2298–2300; 1195–1197; v3 10658–10659 ("the entire spiritual estate"); v3 20897–20900 (the Teutonic Order "must therefore wield the worldly sword and be a secular order at the same time that it is to be a spiritual order"); v2 13272–13273 ("spiritual and temporal lords"); LC 1713–1716 ("both ecclesiastical and civil estates must humble themselves and all be found in this estate"); LC 1051–1052 ("this estate of fatherhood and motherhood").

### 6.2 "we are all priests" — the royal priesthood
- **Preliminary definition.** "Through baptism all of us are consecrated to the priesthood"; "whoever comes out of the water of baptism can boast that he is already consecrated priest, bishop and pope, though it is not seemly that every one should exercise the office"; hence "in cases of necessity any one can baptise and give absolution." - **Evidence.** v2 2176–2180; 2191–2199; 2213–2216; 2413–2416 ("if we are all priests, as was said above, and all have one faith, one Gospel, one sacrament, why should we not also have the power to test and judge what is correct or incorrect in matters of faith?"); v2 8908–8909 ("Christ hath not ordained principalities or powers or lordships, but ministries, in the Church"); Hy 1799–1802 (hymn V, Bacon's English: "Thus by the power of grace they were / True priests of God's own making").

### 6.7 pope / papacy / papist / Romanist / "Antichrist" — and "the Church of Rome"

## 7. The two governments — sword, obedience, liberty

### 7.1 the two governments / "kingdom of God" and "kingdom of the world" — and *Bondage*'s "two kingdoms"
- **Preliminary definition.** In *Secular Authority*: "We must divide all the children of Adam into two classes; the first belong to the kingdom of God, the second to the kingdom of the world"; "God has ordained the two governments; the spiritual, which by the Holy Spirit under Christ makes Christians and pious people, and the secular, which restrains the unchristian and wicked." Confessionally: "the difference between the power of the Church and the power of the sword," which "must not be confounded." **Sense caveat:** in Cole's *Bondage* Luther also says "there are two kingdoms in the world mutually militating against each other" — Satan's and Christ's — a spiritual-warfare pairing, not the church/civil pairing; the same English phrase carries two referents in this library.
- **Evidence.** v3 12139–12141; 12318–12323; 12188–12191 ("these people need no secular sword or law. And if all the world were composed of real Christians... no prince, king, lord, sword, or law would be needed"); 12371–12374 (OCR-damaged: "these two kin ee must be sharpl distinguished, and both be permitted to remain; the one to produce piety, the other to bring about external peace" — recovered "kingdoms... sharply"); AC 1281–1285; 1311–1312; v2 15334–15335 ("the kingdom of God consists not in outward things"); Hy 3705 (hymn XXVI, Bacon's English: "The kingdom ours remaineth"); Co 14748–14750 (OCR as found: "two kingdoms in the world mutually miUtatingagainst each other. — That Satan reigns in the one"), 14769 ("these two kingdoms"). **Layer note:** the running marginal "The Two Kingdoms" (v3 12123–12127) and "Spiritual and Secular Government" (12276–12280) are the 1930 editor's; Luther's text at these loci says "two classes," "two governments," "the kingdom of God... the kingdom of the world."
- **Voices.** Luther — treatise (1523), sustained argument (1525, different referent), sermon, hymn; Melanchthon — AC.
- **Tags.** [AS] [TC] [DR] [RT] **[CT]**. AS: the two-governments formulation is the Wittenberg form of the church/state answer that Doc_01 §8.0 finds each neighbour gives differently. **Contest type: the term's historical scope — whether the later systematic label ("two kingdoms doctrine") applies as broadly to Luther's own usage as claimed, given that the vendored texts use "governments," "classes," and, in *Bondage*, "kingdoms" in a different sense.** Disclosed: the contest is signalled here from the in-text evidence and the editorial marginal; no secondary source is rowed for it, and Doc_06 owes one before carrying the tag.
- **Tier (est.).** 1. **AG.** none.
- **Registry.** 15, 20, 29, 34, 37.

### 7.2 the sword / temporal sword / secular authority
- **Preliminary definition.** The power that "bear[s] sword and rod with which to punish the evil and to protect the good," "ordained of God," reaching "pope, bishops, priests, monks, nuns or anybody else"; a sword "the spiritual estate" may not wield, "whose sword is not to be of iron, but the sword of the Spirit"; confessionally, "lawful civil ordinances are good works of God," and Christians may "serve as soldiers... make oath... marry a wife."
- **Evidence.** v2 2250–2251; 2265–2269; 1195–1199; v3 12300–12306; 12323–12326 ("So Paul interprets the secular sword, Romans xiii, and says it is not a terror to good works, but to the evil"); AC 411–416; 1306–1309 ("restrain men with the sword and bodily punishments in order to preserve civil justice and peace"). "Secular" is Schindel's 1930 English (v3, 85 hits); the 1915–16 volumes say "temporal."
- **Voices.** Luther — treatise (1520, 1523); Melanchthon — AC. Doc_01 §8.2: the Anabaptist movements' own Step 0 names this world "from the establishment side."
- **Tags.** [SC] [TC] [DR] [RT].
- **Tier (est.).** 1. **AG.** none.
- **Registry.** 10, 11, 20, 37.

### 7.3 obedience / "obey God rather than men" / honor
- **Preliminary definition.** Owed to "parents or superiors," to "every ordinance of man for the Lord's sake," to magistrates "save only when commanded to sin"; the Fourth Commandment's "honor" being "a far higher thing... than to love one," extended to "All that govern."
- **Evidence.** SC 82–84; 625–633 (Home Chart: "For Earthly Authorities," "For those Under Authority"); LC 1051–1060; AC 426–428; v2 2288–2294; TT 2422 (Bell's English: "All that govern are called Fathers"); AC 1340–1344 ("the congregations of necessity and by divine right must obey them... ### 7.4 insurrection / rebellion / "the common man" (1522 only)
- **Preliminary definition.** The unrest Luther addresses in December 1521/January 1522: "the common man has been brooding over the injury he has suffered in property, in body and in soul, and has become provoked... and would indeed have good reason to lay about him with flails and cudgels, as the peasants are threatening to do" — met with "no fear whatever that there will be an insurrection" because "God will watch over His Word," and with the 1522 sermons' refusal of force: "I could have brought great bloodshed upon Germany... I did nothing; I left it to the Word."
- **Evidence.** v3 10641–10643 (title); 10665–10672; 10696–10702; Lambert's fn. at 10680–10684 (identifying "the peasants" with the *Karsthans* pamphlet — editorial; the pamphlet's own words at 10554–10594 are quoted in Lambert's introduction, R44, not re-read); v2 14932–14935; v2 2135 ("our three rods"). **Bar, stated:** the 1525 tract and its language ("smite, slay and stab" etc.) are not in this library and are barred by R94; nothing here characterizes 1525.

### 7.5 "must" and "free" — Christian liberty, the weak in faith, offence
- **Preliminary definition.** The 1522 sermons' governing distinction — "The 'must' is that which necessity requires... as, for instance, the faith... But 'free' is that in which I have choice, and may use or not, yet in such wise that it profit my brother" — applied to mass, images, both kinds, handling the sacrament; grounded in the 1520 paradox ("A Christian man is a perfectly free lord of all, subject to none. - **Tags.** [AS] [DR] [TC] [RT]. Distortion flag: liberty as autonomy vs. this world's liberty bound by love to the weak.

## 8. Vows, chastity, marriage — the monastic and marital cluster

### 8.1 vow / monastic vows
- **Preliminary definition.** The binding promise this world declares void: "no man's law, no vow, can annul the commandment and ordinance of God"; vows once taught to be "equal to Baptism," a "state of perfection" that "merited forgiveness of sins," now "wicked services, and, consequently... void"; "such a vow is nothing at all and is not to be kept, unless a man have God's special grace."

## 9. Comfort, trial, and death — the pastoral register

### 9.1 comfort / consolation
- **Preliminary definition.** What the doctrine of faith brings to "anxious consciences" — "the greatest consolation, because consciences cannot be set at rest through any works"; what the sacrament, absolution and baptism are for ("comfort and strengthening for our conscience... many absolutions"); what burial hymns do ("comfort one another with God's word"); what baptism is, "a gracious covenant of comfort."

### 9.3 assurance / "sure" / "certain" — against despair and "conditionally"
