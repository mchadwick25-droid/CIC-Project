## 1. Primary Voices

### 1.1 Martin Luther (1483–1546) — nine registers, not one voice

The library holds Luther in more distinct registers than any other single-author corpus this project has built, and the Author Gravity problem (§3) is precisely that all nine are *his*. Each register is characterized here from the text itself, with the specific work that carries it.

- **Academic disputation** — the Ninety-Five Theses (1517), with the covering letter to Albrecht of Mainz and three further explanatory letters [R2]. The volume's own editor (C. M. Jacobs) insists these are an academic document in medieval form and warns the modern reader that "They are not 'a trumpet-blast of reform'; that title must be reserved for the great works of 1520" (v1 line 498–499) — an editorial judgment this document agrees with on reading the Theses, and tags **[Widely Accepted]**.
- **The vernacular and Latin treatise of 1519–1520**, addressed to named readers — the sacramental treatises of 1519, *Good Works* (to Duke John), *Fourteen of Consolation* (to Elector Frederick), *To the Christian Nobility* (to Amsdorf and the German estates), the *Babylonian Captivity* (Latin, to Tulich), *Christian Liberty* (with the letter to Leo X), *The Papacy at Rome*, *The Ban*, *The New Testament, That Is the Holy Mass* [R3–R13]. Doc_01 §1 and §4 already characterized this register; nothing found this pass disturbs that characterization **[Documented]**.
- **Polemic against a named opponent** — *The Papacy at Rome* against Alveld; the three Emser writings; the *Argument in Defense* against the bull *Exsurge Domine* [R8, R17, R21–R23]. The editorial introduction to the Emser writings calls this "a faithful portrait, with well-defined lights and shadows, of the controversial Luther" (v3 line 14640–14642) — and it is in this register, not the catechetical one, that the "goat," "ass," and "jackanapes" vocabulary lives. The Representative-facing consequence is real: this register is Documented and Native, and it is also the register most easily mistaken for the whole man.
- **Devotional-exegetical writing for a prince** — *The Magnificat* (1520–21), dedicated to the seventeen-year-old John Frederick, composed in two instalments around the Diet of Worms and the Wartburg [R18]. The editor (Steinhaeuser) reads it as "a true 'mirror for princes'" and quotes Luther's own Mary who "goes about her wonted household tasks, milking the cows, cooking the meals, washing pots and kettles, sweeping out the rooms" (v3 lines 6396–6397; the editor is quoting Luther's text, and this document has not itself located that sentence in the OCR-degraded body — cited at the editor's word) **[Widely Accepted]** for the characterization. This is the one vendored text in which Luther writes at length about a woman, and the woman is Mary; §12 notes what that means.
- **Spoken pastoral address to his own congregation** — the Eight Wittenberg Sermons (March 1522) [R15]. Luther's own voice, from the text: "Let us beware lest Wittenberg become Capernaum" (v2 line 14681); "And here, dear friends, have you not grievously failed? I see no signs of love among you" (lines 14676–14677). This is the only place in the library where the founder addresses a specific congregation about its own conduct, and the congregation is Wittenberg's **[Documented]**.
- **Catechesis, in three stages** — the 1520 *Kurze Form* (whose preface opens: "The ordinary Christian, who cannot read the Scriptures, is required to learn and know the Ten Commandments, the Creed, and the Lord's Prayer," v2 lines 13182–13183); the Large Catechism (1529), addressed to pastors; the Small Catechism (1529), each part headed "The Simple Way a Father Should Present Them to His Household" [R14, R25, R26]. Doc_01 already established catechesis as a candidate gravity; what this pass adds is that the *audience* named in each is different (the illiterate "ordinary Christian," the negligent pastor, the household father), which is exactly the kind of internal distinction Doc_04 will need **[Documented]**.
- **Hymnody** — thirty-six hymn texts in German with English versions, from the 1524 *Eight Songs* through the 1543 Klug hymnal, plus four of Luther's own hymnal prefaces (1525, 1542, 1543, 1545) [R27–R30]. The 1525 preface states the purpose in Luther's own words: "to provide our young people (who both will and ought to be instructed in music and other sciences) with something whereby they might rid themselves of amorous and carnal songs" (hymns file lines 919–922) **[Documented]**. This register was not in the library when Doc_01 was written and directly answers its §5/§11 item 3 (see §5 below).
- **Conversation** — *Table Talk*, a selection [R31]. Triple-filtered (Luther's speech → Lauterbach's and Aurifaber's notes → Aurifaber's 1566 arrangement by topic → Bell's 1646 English → Morley's 1886 selection), and §3 treats it accordingly. It is nevertheless the only place in the library where Luther's household is audible at all: "I must have patience with Kate my wife; to conclude, the patiences are so many, that my whole life is nothing but patience" (TT lines 3013–3015).
- **Sustained theological argument against a single learned opponent** — *The Bondage of the Will* (1525), against Erasmus [R34]. Luther's own concession to Erasmus, which the OCR preserves legibly enough to recover: "You, and you alone saw, what was the grand hinge, upon which the whole turned, and therefore you attacked the vital part at once; for which, from my heart, I thank you" (Cole lines 15139–15142, corrected from "^^nd hinge" and "iwhole" — the file's own reading is shown in the Registry) **[Documented]** as to content, with the OCR caveat carried into the Registry's Confidence tier.

Two further Luther voices exist in the library only at second hand, quoted by editors from letters not themselves vendored: the 1525 letter to Georg von Polentz, "Behold the wonder! In rapid course, with full sails, the Gospel hastens to Prussia" (v3 lines 20802–20803, in Lambert's introduction), and the 1521 letters to Spalatin about Hutten ("I would not have the Gospel defended by violence and murder," v3 line 10525, in Lambert's introduction). Both are cited only as the editor's quotations **[Widely Accepted]**, since the letters themselves are not in hand.

### 1.2 Philipp Melanchthon (1497–1560) — a drafter's voice under princes' signatures

The Augsburg Confession (1530) [R37] and its Apology (1531) [R38] are Melanchthon's composition, but their author-gravity shape is unusual and Doc_04 should not flatten it. The Confession is submitted in the first person plural of "the undersigned Elector and Princes" and closes with nine signatures — John of Saxony, George of Brandenburg, Ernest and Francis of Lüneburg, Philip of Hesse, John Frederick of Saxony, Wolfgang of Anhalt, and the senates of Nuremberg and Reutlingen (AC lines 1557–1567) — so its "we" is a corporate political-confessional voice, not Melanchthon's own **[Documented]**. The Apology, by contrast, opens in Melanchthon's own name ("Philip Melanchthon Presents His Greeting to the Reader," Apology line 97) and is openly polemical against "the writers of the *Confutation*," who "were deficient not only in judgment, but also in candor" (lines 155–157), addressing Cardinal Campeggio by name ("It was particularly becoming for you, O Campegius," lines 5915–5916) **[Documented]**. The Confession's own preface also carries the movement's own account of its political situation — the Diet called "to deliberate concerning measures against the Turk, that most atrocious, hereditary, and ancient enemy of the Christian name and religion" (AC lines 49–51), the appeal to a "general, free Christian Council" (line 149) — which §13 uses.

Doc_01 §10's two rights/translator flags on these files are carried forward unchanged: the Confession's translator remains unconfirmed, and the two files are not cited here as a matched translation pair.

### 1.3 Opponent voices that reach this library only in adversarial quotation

This is the register Doc_01 §7 asked Doc_02 to seek — "Roman Catholic responses contemporary with the treatises themselves" — and the honest finding is that the library holds them **only as quoted by the side that answered them**, never in their own right. Named and located:

- **Erasmus, *Diatribe on Free Will* (1524)**, quoted section by section throughout *Bondage* [R36]. Cole's preface states his typographic rule: "where Erasmus' own words are quoted in the original, the commas are double; but single, where the substance of his sentiments only is quoted" — while admitting the rule "was not adopted till after the first three sheets were printed" (Cole lines 218–230, verified directly — corrected from 218–227, Round 2 N8). So even the quoted Erasmus is filtered twice (by Luther's selection and Cole's typography) and, for the first three sheets, indistinguishably **[Documented]** as to the filter.
- **The Roman *Confutation* (1530)**, cited by name at least eleven times in the Apology (lines 156, 2359, 4145, 4909–4910, 5171, 5407, 5778, 5867, 6333, 8557 and elsewhere), always as "the adversaries" say or condemn [R39]. The Confutation itself was withheld from the Lutheran party in writing — and the vendored Apology says so, in Melanchthon's own voice: "Because they are not ignorant of this, they were unwilling to exhibit to us a copy of their Apology, lest this falsehood and these reproaches might be exposed" (Apology lines 8447–8449; "their Apology" is the adversaries' own defence, named as "the *Confutation*" at 8442–8443 two sentences earlier). **[Documented]** as Melanchthon's own complaint, from the primary text in hand. Revision 0 stated the opposite ("not from the vendored text, which does not say so") and tagged the point Widely Accepted from secondary literature; Round 1 (S3) located the passage, and this revision confirmed it by direct reading — the same asserted-absence defect class Doc_01's Round 1 caught twice.
- **The bull *Exsurge Domine* (1520)**, whose forty-one condemned articles structure the *Argument in Defense* article by article; the editor notes the bull's full text is translated in "Jacobs, *Luther*, Appendix I" (v3 line 360–361), not vendored [R40].
- **Jerome Emser, Thomas Murner, Augustin von Alveld** — known here through Luther's replies and through the editors' introductions, which quote Emser's own words in places ("Three times I gave you a brotherly warning…," v3 lines 14553–14557, Emser as quoted by Steimle) and report Alveld's "seven swords" (v1 lines 12283–12288) [R41, R42].
- **Andreas Karlstadt** — quoted in Steimle's introduction to the Wittenberg Sermons from his own theses ("In my opinion, he who partakes only of the bread, sins," v2 line 14446) [R43].

**Boundary finding, carried into the Registry:** these are recorded as **Native (context)** — the same handling the Gallic Registry gave Augustine's anti-Massilian treatises — because each was actually drawn on in this world's own formation (Luther and Melanchthon wrote *against* them, by name), but each is licensed only for "the opponent's position as this world's own texts represent it," never as the opponent's own voice. A Representative for this world may say what Luther said Erasmus said; it may not speak as Erasmus. The marker is confined to opponent texts: the Marburg Articles [R56], which Luther and Melanchthon themselves signed, are not an answered opponent text and are rowed as a plain Native lead, not "(context)" (Round 1 S9; §13 item 1).

### 1.4 Lay and non-founder voices present only at one remove

Four sixteenth-century voices that are not Luther's or Melanchthon's exist in the library, all embedded in editorial apparatus, and each is the kind of trace Article 20's against-the-grain rule is looking for:

- **The *Karsthans* pamphlet (1521)** — a popular dialogue in which a peasant is made to say "Dear Luther, write the divine truth in our language, in German, that we simple laymen also may read it… Where is my flail?" (v3 lines 10556–10586, quoted in Lambert's introduction from Burckhardt's edition) [R44]. A fictional peasant, written for the people, in ten editions per the editor — the closest thing to a lay reception voice in the library, and it is a literary construction **[Widely Accepted]** as to the pamphlet's existence and popularity; **[Contested]** as to what it evidences about actual peasant opinion.
- **Johann Walter's reminiscence** of preparing the German Mass with Luther ("He kept me three weeks long at Wittenberg… until the first German Mass was sung in the parish church," hymns file lines 766–768), written in Walter's old age, printed by Praetorius, taken by Bacon from Rambach (1813) [R45]. A participant's testimony, late and through three hands **[Widely Accepted]**.
- **Johann Kessler's eyewitness note** on Luther's first 1522 sermon, cited by Steimle (v2 lines 14507–14510) [R46] **[Widely Accepted]**, cited at the editor's word.
- **Spangenberg's 1545 preface to the *Cithara Lutheri***, quoted by Bacon (hymns lines 556–571) [R47] — an in-window, non-founder assessment of the hymns' reception, laudatory and partisan.

## 2. Secondary Voices — the editorial apparatus, in six layers

Every sixteenth-century text in this library reaches it through a named modern hand with a discernible agenda, and those hands are recorded here as secondary voices with their own gravity (§3.4), because the Gallic Doc_02's Round 2 diagnosis — an editor's note attributed to the ancient author — is the single most likely error class in a corpus this heavily introduced.

1. **The Philadelphia Edition committee (Holman 1915, 1916; Muhlenberg/ULCA 1930)** [R63]. Self-described in Jacobs's introduction: "The work has been done by a small group of scholarly Lutheran pastors, residing near each other, and jointly preparing the copy for the printer" (v1 lines 138–140), and prepared, in the general introduction's own words, "with especial reference to the discussions which, we have every reason to believe, will then occur" — "then" being "the approaching jubilee of the Reformation in 1917" (lines 171–175; Revision 0 spliced the two phrases into "with especial reference to the jubilee," which the text does not say — Round 1 C8). Translating from the Weimar edition "so far as this is completed," with Erlangen, Berlin (Buchwald), Walch/St. Louis and Clemen as helps (lines 225–244). Their introductions are substantial historical essays — Steimle's account of the 1521–22 Wittenberg unrest, Lambert's of the Teutonic Order, Jacobs's of the excommunication process — and they are the library's *fullest* connected narrative of events (Bacon's and Morley's introductions narrate too, more narrowly — §3.4; "only" was overstated, Round 1 C13). They are confessional-Lutheran, jubilee-motivated, and in places openly apologetic (Steinhaeuser's footnote defending the *Magnificat* against Grisar's Catholic reading, v3 lines 6464–6475). **[Documented]** as to their own stated method and situation.
2. **F. Bente and W. H. T. Dau, *Concordia Triglotta* (1921)** [R64] — the Missouri Synod's confessional edition, the source of the Large Catechism and the Apology, with bracketed insertions from the German text that the vendored files preserve (e.g., Apology lines 108–109, 119–129). Confessional-Lutheran in a stricter sense than the Philadelphia committee.
3. **Robert E. Smith, Project Wittenberg (1994/2002/2004)** [R65] — the Small Catechism, in a modern translation "under continuous revision" by the translator's own note, released by him to the public domain (SC lines 672–674). Modern idiom ("Yes, this is true!"); no preface, no critical apparatus.
4. **Leonard Woolsey Bacon and Nathan H. Allen (Scribner, 1883)** [R66] — the hymns. **The intake header flagged the edition year as unconfirmed; this pass closes that flag:** Bacon's own Introduction calls the volume "this Birth-day Edition of Luther's Hymns and Tunes" (hymns line 819–820) and "the commemorative character of the present edition" (line 711), i.e. the 1483 quatercentenary, and external catalogue records (HathiTrust, Valparaiso University archives) give 1883 and Charles Scribner's Sons. **1883 is more than 95 years before 2026, so the file now clears this project's own by-date rule, not only Gutenberg's determination** — a G1-level improvement recorded here for `REGISTRY.yaml` [Documented as to the catalogue record; the file's own text supplies the inference]. Bacon is a nineteenth-century American Protestant editor whose Introduction quotes Coleridge, Heine, Carlyle and Merle d'Aubigné at length (§3.4 on why that matters) and who states he "reluctantly attempted a wholly new version" where existing translations would not fit the music (lines 831–832) — so the English hymn texts are composite and are cited here as Bacon's renderings, never as "Luther's words."
5. **Henry Morley (Cassell's National Library, 1886) over Captain Henry Bell (1646) over Aurifaber (1566)** [R67, R32, R33] — the Table Talk. Morley states his own selection rule: "every paragraph stands unabridged, exactly as it was translated by Captain Bell; and there has been no other purpose governing the choice of matter than a resolve to make it as true a presentment as possible of Luther's mind and character" (TT lines 87–90), while conceding that even Bell's folio "has been prepared for reprint by some preceding editor, whose pen has been busy in revision" (lines 84–86). Aurifaber's own 1569 testimony describes the method — notes "which that Reverend Father Anthony Lauterbach himself noted and wrote out of the holy mouth of Luther, and afterwards the same by me were collected into sure and certain *Loci Communes*" (lines 135–138). Bell's narrative of the manuscript's rescue is treated at §9 as a transmission legend, not history.
6. **Henry Cole (1823)** [R68] — *Bondage*. Cole states his principle — "to deliver faithfully the mind of Luther; retaining literally, as much of his own wording, phraseology, and expression, as could be admitted into the English version" (lines 186–189, capitals in the original normalized) — and his basis, "translated from Melancthon's Edition, which he published immediately after Luther's death" (lines 200–202, "Melkncthon's" in the OCR). His theological location is stated in his own preface: the project was urged by friends impressed by "the unqualified encomium bestowed upon it by… the late Reverend Augustus Montague Toplady" (lines 152–155), i.e. the Calvinistic wing of the Church of England, and the closing advertisement announces companion tracts on "Popery" with woodcuts of "the Whore of Babylon" and "Monkery" (lines 16259–16272). This is the library's one edition whose translator's agenda is not Lutheran but Reformed-Anglican — a fact that bears directly on which register of *Bondage* got emphasized in English, and §3.1 treats it as a transmission-history datum.

## 3. Author Gravity Assessment (five dimensions, per the Expanded requirement)

### 3.1 Luther

**Visibility.** Overwhelming. Eight of the ten files are Luther; the other two (AC/Apology) are his colleague defending his doctrine (Round 1 C1 corrected Revision 0's "nine… the tenth pair"). Within the library the *most* visible Luther is the 1519–1523 polemicist and treatise-writer (v1–v3, ~470,000 words), then the 1525 disputant against Erasmus (~117,000), then the catechist (~53,000), then the confessor's colleague; the hymnist and the conversationalist are the least visible by volume (~58,000 together) and are the two registers ordinary congregants actually encountered. **This inversion — the registers with the widest ecological reach are the thinnest in the library — is the first Source Asymmetry finding (§11)** **[Documented]** as to word counts.

**Representativeness.** Low, and in two directions. Luther is a university professor and Augustinian friar writing from the doctrinal center; he is not a village pastor, a visitor, a layman, or a woman. And within his own output the library over-samples 1517–1525 (all of v1–v3, *Bondage*, the *Eight Songs*) and under-samples 1530–1546 (only the catechisms of 1529, the later hymnal prefaces, and whatever Table Talk selection Morley made): the mature territorial-church Luther of the 1530s–40s is barely present **[Documented]** as to the library's own dates.

**Influence.** On the ecology, total by the movement's own account — the AC preface speaks of "the Confession of our preachers and of ourselves, showing what manner of doctrine… has been up to this time set forth in our lands" (AC lines 81–83). On the *historiography*, equally total: every secondary work in §8 is organized around him. The census's own instruction — "Founder corpus kept distinct from the congregations' record so founder-gravity discipline can run" — names the risk exactly.

**Limitations.** Three, each documented from the texts: (i) he reports reception only in his own voice, and always as a complaint about others' negligence (Large Catechism preface, LC lines 60–98; the 1543 hymnal preface's fear of hymns "sold under our name," hymns lines 1086–1087, verified directly — corrected from 1087 alone, Round 2 N8); (ii) he characterizes opponents only in the act of refuting them (§1.3); (iii) his own retrospective on his beginnings is written in 1545 with an explicit interpretive frame — "Such a Saul was I at that time" (v1 line 365) — that the Theses' own editor already declines to take at face value (v1 lines 510–513).

**Transmission history.** Luther's works reached English through a chain with a stated theological interest at every link: the Weimar critical edition (from 1883) as base; a Lutheran pastoral committee for the 1917 jubilee (v1–v3); the Missouri Synod's confessional *Triglotta* (LC, Apology); a Calvinistic Anglican admirer of Toplady (*Bondage*); an American Protestant quatercentenary editor working "within the very scanty time allotted" (hymns, line 823–824); and, for the Table Talk, a Stuart-era soldier translating in prison over five years from a manuscript with a miracle story attached, reprinted by a Victorian popular-library editor who admits an intervening editor's revisions. Luther himself, in the 1539 preface, opposed the collecting of his works at all: "I would gladly have seen all my books forgotten and destroyed" (v1 lines 260–261) [R1]. **Effect on confidence:** the doctrinal content of the treatises and confessions is **[Documented]** through multiple editions and is not seriously at issue; the *wording* of the Table Talk is **[Contested]** at the level of any individual saying; the *emphasis* of *Bondage* in English is shaped by a Reformed-Anglican editorial interest and is **[Widely Accepted]** to be so; and the two OCR-degraded files carry a further, purely mechanical transmission loss that the Registry tracks per quotation.

### 3.2 Melanchthon

**Visibility:** two files, ~126,000 words, both confessional. **Representativeness:** the AC is signed by princes and city senates, so it represents the movement's political-confessional leadership, not its pastors or laity; the Apology represents Melanchthon's own scholastic-humanist method (patristic citation, "Epiphanius writes…," Apology line 8542). **Influence:** the AC is the document later worlds inherit (Doc_01 §8.0 on VI.27). **Limitations:** neither file carries the *Instructions for the Visitors* (1528) — the one Melanchthon text the census names for the congregational register — so the library holds Melanchthon only as confessor, never as administrator **[Documented]** as to the library. **Transmission:** Bente/Dau for the Apology; an unconfirmed translator for the AC (Doc_01 §10 flag stands).

### 3.3 What becomes over-visible because these sources survive; what becomes under-visible

Over-visible: doctrine, argued; the papacy as the movement's antagonist; Wittenberg and the Saxon court; the years 1517–1525; the founder's own assessment of everything, including of his congregation's failings. Under-visible: the parish; the visitation; women (§12); the Reformed as a rival rather than a footnote (§13); the Anabaptists as anything but a condemned name (AC lines 253, 314, 351, 418, 440 — five condemnations "of the Anabaptists" by name, verified); Jews as anything but a category in exegesis (§12); the north and east beyond Saxony (the only vendored text about the Baltic is a treatise urging the Teutonic Knights to marry, §11); the 1530s and 1540s; and the *sound* of any of it — the hymns are the one exception, and even there the tunes are printed in the source edition but not in this text file.

### 3.4 Narrative Source Author Gravity — editors and collectors functioning as secondary accounts

- **The Philadelphia Edition introducers (Jacobs, Steinhaeuser, Lambert, Schindel, Steimle, Schmauk, Neve)** [R63]. Visibility: every work is prefaced by one. Representativeness: American Lutheran pastors and seminary professors, 1915–1930. Influence: their framing (e.g., Steimle's "guiding the turbulent waves of popular uprising into the channels marked by faith and love," v2 lines 14425–14426) is the frame Doc_01 §2.3 already adopted for the 1522 unrest. Limitations: confessional, jubilee-motivated, occasionally polemical against Catholic historiography (Grisar). Transmission: they cite Weimar, Enders, De Wette, Köstlin-Kawerau, Smith's *Correspondence* — a real scholarly apparatus of its generation, now a century old. **Confidence effect:** their narrative claims are **[Widely Accepted]** where they report dated documents (letters, editions, edicts) and **[Dominant Modern Reconstruction — of 1915–1930]** where they characterize motives.
- **Johann Aurifaber (1519–1575), collector, and Anton Lauterbach, note-taker** [R32]. Visibility: the Table Talk exists because of them. Representativeness: Aurifaber was Luther's *famulus* only in 1545–46 (Morley, TT lines 108–112); most of what he printed he collected from others. Influence: the Table Talk is the source of most of the "human Luther" anecdote tradition. Limitations: arranged by topic, not chronology; a disciple's edition dedicated to princes as "fragments that fell from Luther's Table" (line 156–157). Visibility: every Table Talk sentence in the library is in Bell's English, and his own *Narrative* (TT lines 171–330) is the first voice a reader of the file meets after Morley. Representativeness: a Stuart-era English soldier and Protestant layman, translating in prison — he represents seventeenth-century English reception of Luther, not the sixteenth-century Saxon household he transmits. Influence: on the library, total for the Table Talk's wording; on the ecology, none — his narrative of the book's survival (book-burning under Rudolf II at Gregory XIII's instigation, the buried copy found in 1626, a white-bearded apparition, ten years in the Gatehouse, Laud's borrowing of the manuscript, lines 171–330) is a transmission legend, and the claim that the Table Talk was suppressed by imperial edict is **[Inferential/Thin]** on Bell's word alone (no corroboration found; none searched for). Limitations: no German-side control on his rendering; the miracle frame; and Morley's own admission that Bell's folio "has been prepared for reprint by some preceding editor, whose pen has been busy in revision" (lines 84–86). Transmission: Aurifaber's 1566 print → the manuscript Bell says came from Kaspar von Sparr → Bell's 1646 folio → an unnamed reviser → Morley 1886. **Boundary effect: his *Narrative* is Excluded in the Registry (Out-of-Boundary — its subject is 1580s–1640s Germany and England)**, recorded because it is physically inseparable from the vendored file and because a Representative must never repeat it as this world's own story; his *translation* is the Native text of R31.

Visibility: high and structural — the hymn chronology by first printing (§5), the Walter and Spangenberg testimonies [R45, R47], the Speratus anecdote (§10), and every English hymn text pass through him; his Introduction (lines 521–887) is the library's only account of the hymnals' publication history 1524–1545. Representativeness: a nineteenth-century American Protestant editor writing for the 1883 quatercentenary ("this Birth-day Edition," 819–820), not a Lutheran confessional voice — he narrates the Reformation through Coleridge, Heine ("the Marseillaise of the Reformation," 601), Carlyle, and Merle d'Aubigné (573–682). Influence: on the ecology, none; on this build, decisive for §5 and §10 — the hymn dates and the Walter reminiscence are accepted at his scholarship, not re-verified. Transmission: Weimar-era German hymnology and Rambach (1813) for the Walter letter (874–881) → Bacon's English → Gutenberg text without the tunes. Bacon's own footnote correctly separates the "tiles" saying from *Ein feste Burg* (856–866). **Confidence effect:** hymn attributions and dates **[Widely Accepted]**; anything Bacon *relates* rather than documents (Speratus under the window, 105–110, 690–692) **[Inferential/Thin]**.
- **Henry Morley (1886), selector and introducer of the Table Talk** [R67]. Visibility: he chose which of Aurifaber's thousands of paragraphs the library holds; the selection *is* the file. Representativeness: a Victorian popular-library editor (Cassell's National Library) writing for a general English readership, with no confessional stake. Influence: on the ecology, none; on this build, direct — his stated rule, "no other purpose governing the choice of matter than a resolve to make it as true a presentment as possible of Luther's mind and character" (lines 87–90), is the reason §12.3 treats the absence of a chapter on the Jews as a selection effect rather than evidence about Luther: a selection made for "mind and character" in 1886 will omit what an 1886 editor did not wish to present. Limitations: he prints "every paragraph… unabridged, exactly as it was translated by Captain Bell" (87–88) but concedes the intervening reviser (84–86); his Aurifaber biography (94–120) is his own summary, cited here at his word. Transmission: Aurifaber → Bell → reviser → Morley → Gutenberg. **Confidence effect:** the selection's *silences* are never evidence; its *contents* inherit R31's Contested-as-verbatim status.
- **Henry Cole (1823), translator of *Bondage*** [R68]. Visibility: the whole of the library's *Bondage* and the appended Amsdorf letter [R34, R35] are his English, in an OCR-degraded scan. Representativeness: a Calvinistic Anglican, urged to the work by the "unqualified encomium" of "the late Reverend Augustus Montague Toplady" (lines 152–155) — he represents the Reformed-evangelical wing of the Church of England's interest in Luther on grace, not a Lutheran reading. Influence: on the ecology, none; on the English reception of *Bondage*, large — his was the standard nineteenth-century English text, and his emphasis (§3.1) is the one this library inherits. Limitations: his stated principle, "to deliver faithfully the mind of Luther; retaining literally, as much of his own wording, phraseology, and expression, as could be admitted into the English version" (186–189), is a claim, not a control; his quoting rule for Erasmus "was not adopted till after the first three sheets were printed" (218–230, verified directly — corrected from 218–227, Round 2 N8); the closing Advertisement's woodcut tracts (16259–16272) show his polemical programme. Transmission: "Melancthon's Edition, which he published immediately after Luther's death" (200–202) → Cole 1823 → a degraded scan whose every quotation needs character-level re-check (R34). **Confidence effect:** doctrinal content **[Documented]**; wording, per quotation, only as the Registry clears it.

## 4. Institutional Evidence

**In the library:** the Augsburg Confession as a submitted imperial document with named signatories and its own account of the Diets of Speyer (1526, 1529) and the appeal to a council (AC lines 116–161) [R37] **[Documented]**; the Apology's account of the Confutation's reading before the Emperor [R38]; the *Argument in Defense*'s editorial reconstruction of the excommunication process 1517–1521, with the bull's forty-one articles [R17, R40] **[Widely Accepted]**; the Wittenberg city council's "Worthy Ordinance for the princely City of Wittenberg" of January 24, 1522, described (not reproduced) by Steimle (v2 lines 14481–14487) **[Widely Accepted]** at the editor's word; Duke George's bans on Luther's New Testament (September 1522; itself unvendored [R60]) and complaints to the Elector, described by Schindel (v3 lines 11685–11688, 11744–11745) **[Widely Accepted]**; the Council of Regency's edict of January 20, 1522 (v3 line 11681) **[Widely Accepted]**; the conversion of the Teutonic Order's Prussia "into a duchy held as fief of the king of Poland and hereditary in the family of Albert," and Albert's marriage on "July 1, 1526," narrated by Lambert (v3 lines 20784–20790 — the editor gives no year for the conversion; the customary 1525 is the builder's, from prior knowledge, not Lambert's: Round 1 C4) **[Widely Accepted]**; and *Secular Authority* itself as the movement's own theory of the state, dedicated to Duke John [R20] **[Documented]**.

**Not in the library, and decisive:** the Saxon visitation records (1527–28) and Melanchthon's *Instructions for the Visitors* [R51, R52]; the church ordinances (Sehling) [R52]; the Book of Concord (1580) with the Smalcald Articles and Formula of Concord [R53]; parish registers. III" — both in copyright; Sehling remains untranslated. **The institutional register is therefore documented at the level of the Empire (Diets, bull, edicts) and of the Saxon court, and undocumented at the level of the parish. The movement's own self-checking mechanism — the thing the census calls this world's distinctive evidential feature — is known to this library only by reference** **[Documented]** as to the absence.

## 5. Liturgical Evidence — Doc_01 open item 3, partly closed

Doc_01 §5 had only AC Article XXIV's apologetic self-description of worship and flagged "no service order or hymn text" as its gap. The G1 pass closed the hymn-text half; this section states exactly what the library now holds and what it still does not.

**Now in hand:** thirty-six hymn texts arranged by first printing — the Wittenberg *Eight Songs* (1524), the Erfurt *Enchiridion* (1524), Walter's *Gesangbuch* (1525), the German Mass (1526; only the German Sanctus, hymn XXV), the Augsburg *Form und Ordnung* (1529, *Ein feste Burg*), and the Klug hymnals (1535, 1543) [R27–R30] **[Documented]** as to the edition's own attributions, **[Widely Accepted]** as to the attributions' correctness (Bacon's 1883 scholarship, not re-verified). Luther's four hymnal prefaces, including the 1542 funeral-hymns preface, which is the library's only text on burial practice: "we, in our churches, [have] abolished, done away, and out-and-out made an end of the popish horrors, such as wakes, masses for the soul, obsequies, purgatory… We sing, withal, beside our dead and over their graves, no dirges nor lamentations, but comforting songs" (hymns lines 975–985) [R28] **[Documented]** as the movement's own claimed practice. The Apology's own account of Sunday practice, which is fuller than the AC's: "among us masses are celebrated every Lord's Day and on the other festivals, in which the Sacrament is offered to those who wish to use it, after they have been examined and absolved. And the usual public ceremonies are observed, the series of lessons of prayers, vestments, and other like things" and "we retain the Latin language on account of those who are learning and understand Latin, and we mingle with it German hymns" (Apology lines 8490–8496, 8512–8514) [R38] **[Documented]** as claimed practice. Household devotion: the Small Catechism's morning and evening blessings, table graces, and the instruction to "perhaps sing a song inspired by the Ten Commandments or your own thoughts" on going to work (SC lines 557–558) [R26]. And Walter's testimony (§1.4) that Luther set the tones for Epistle and Gospel himself and "kept me three weeks long at Wittenberg… until the first German Mass was sung in the parish church" [R45].

**Still not in hand:** the *Formula Missae* (1523) and *Deutsche Messe* (1526) as service orders [R59]; the *Order of Baptism* (1523); any parish's actual usage as distinct from Wittenberg's; the tunes (present in the 1883 print edition, absent from the text file). **What this means for Doc_01 open item 3:** the request was for "independent (non-self-reporting) confirmation of lived worship practice." The hymns are the movement's own texts, and the Apology is its own apologetic; neither is *independent*. Walter's reminiscence is the nearest thing to a non-founder participant's confirmation, and it is late, laudatory, and thrice-transmitted. **The item is narrowed, not closed: the library can now show what was sung and what the movement said it did on Sunday; it cannot show what any parish outside Wittenberg did** **[Documented]** as to the state of the evidence.

## 6. Material Evidence

None is vendored, and the Framework's rule applies: "The ecology existed in material conditions whether or not those conditions are recoverable." What the library does carry is material culture *by report*, and one class of material evidence that literary sources happen to preserve well:

- **Print as the movement's own medium — documented inside the editorial apparatus.** The Theses in "three editions of the Latin text… one at Wittenberg, one at Nürnberg, and one as far away as Basel" within two months (v1 lines 488–491); *The Papacy at Rome* in "twelve known editions… all quartos… from twenty-two to thirty-two leaves," with the printers named (Lotther, Peypus, Otmar, Nadler, Petri) (v1 lines 12304–12309); *Karsthans* in ten editions (v3 line 10574–10575); the Grunenberg press of Wittenberg reaching Duke George within days (v2 lines 107–109); Luther's own 1543 complaint that "the earliest of our hymns are more perverted the more they are printed" (hymns lines 1068–1069) [R85] **[Widely Accepted]** as to the editors' bibliographical data. This is the one material register this library documents richly, and Doc_05 should use it: the ecology's material substrate was the printed quarto and the hymn-sheet.
- **Woodcut polemic**, glimpsed only in Cole's 1823 advertisement of "two very remarkable tracts on Popery… Each contains an extraordinary Wood-cut Figure… That by Melancthon, is a representation of the Whore op [sic] Babylon : that by Luther, of Monkery" (Cole lines 16259–16272, OCR as found) [R86] — the two figures of the 1523 joint pamphlet *Deuttung der zwo grewlichen Figuren, Bapstesels zu Rom und Munchkalbs zu Freyberg in Meyssen funden* (Wittenberg and reprints, 1523), with Cranach-workshop woodcuts: Melanchthon's essay interprets the *Papal Ass*, Luther's the *Monk Calf* — which is exactly Cole's "That by Luther, of Monkery." The identification is **[Widely Accepted]** (verified this revision against the SLUB Dresden / Deutsche Digitale Bibliothek catalogue record of the 1523 print and the Luther museums' description; Round 1 C7 raised the tag from Revision 0's Inferential/Thin, which contradicted its own "almost certainly"). The one loose end is that Cole calls Melanchthon's figure "the Whore of Babylon" rather than the Papal Ass — a nineteenth-century gloss on the same image, not a different one. **[Widely Accepted]** that such image-polemic was a major register of the movement (Scribner, §8).
- **Places.** The census names the Luther and Melanchthon houses in Lutherstadt Wittenberg (UNESCO) as present-day sites [R84]; the library names the Castle Church door (v1 lines 467–474, with the editor's insistence that it "was not night, but mid-day when the Theses were nailed up") and the Wartburg. The door-posting itself is **[Contested]** since Iserloh (1961) [R76] — the editor's confident narrative predates that debate by half a century.
- **Social-historical background, recoverable from the catechisms against the grain.** Both catechisms presuppose households with servants: the Large Catechism's short preface obliges "every head of a household… with respect to his domestics, ma-servants [sic] and maid-servants" (LC lines 334–336); the Small Catechism scripts a confession for "a servant, maid, etc." who has "complained about my master's wife and sworn at her" (SC lines 445–451) and a separate one for "a master or a lady of the house" (line 454), and its "Home Chart" lists "Servants, Maids, Hired Hands and Workers" as a standing estate (line 649) [R25, R26]. Luther's 1525 hymnal preface addresses "our young people (who both will and ought to be instructed in music and other sciences)" (hymns line 919–920); Walter describes "charity-scholars, collecting their bread," singing "from door to door Latin Hymns" (lines 775–776). So the catechetical ecology assumes a stratified household (master, lady, children, servants), schooling for boys, and an economy of alms-singing — all **[Documented]** as presupposition, **[Inferential/Thin]** as to how widely such households existed outside towns. Literacy is presupposed *not* to be general: the *Kurze Form* is written for "the ordinary Christian, who cannot read the Scriptures" (v2 line 13182).
- **Surrounding religious environment.** The Turk as standing external threat (AC preface, LC petition "vanquish the Turks and all enemies," LC line 3504; Table Talk "I will pray against the Pope and the Turk as long as I live," line 3134–3135); the relic cult at All Saints', Wittenberg (v1 lines 478–483); saints' cults named by Luther by their functional specialisms — "If any one had toothache, he fasted and honored St. Apollonia…; if he was afraid of fire, he chose St. Lawrence as his helper in need; if he dreaded pestilence, he made a vow to St. Sebastian or Rochio" (LC lines 451–455; the ellipsis stands for the bracketed German-text insertion), matched by the Apology's "Sebastian keeps off pestilence" (Apology line 7595) — Revision 0 spliced this line backwards and made Lawrence the patron against pestilence, which Luther does not say (Round 1 S1; corrected from direct re-reading); and Jews, present in the library only as an exegetical and homiletic category (§12) **[Documented]** as to what the texts say.

Epidemic disease — a force the Forces Framework names — appears only as a stock item in lists ("pestilences wars," LC line 755; "war, pestilence, and famine," line 1363). Luther's own plague tract of 1527 (*Whether One May Flee from a Deadly Plague*) is not vendored, and the one plague *event* this pass touched is secondary: Karlstadt's death of plague at Basel in 1541 (§15) **[Widely Accepted]**.

## 7. Ordinary Participant Evidence

Thin, and almost entirely mediated by the founder — stated plainly as the Framework's Affirmative Duty requires (§12). What exists, ranked from least to most mediated:

1. **The Brussels martyrs (1523)** — two Augustinian friars, "John" and "Henry" in Luther's ballad (hymns lines 1759–1760), burned at Brussels by "the Sophists of Louvain" [R30]. Non-founder participants whose deaths are narrated within weeks, in verse, by the founder. The nearest thing to a Tier 1 formation narrative about non-elite believers the library holds (§10).
2. **Katharina von Bora** — one reported sentence in her own voice, via Table Talk: "Sir! how is it, that in Popedom they pray so often with great vehemence, but we are very cold and careless in praying?" (TT lines 3147–3148) [R31]. Triple-filtered; Tier 2; **[Contested]** as verbatim. Beyond this, "Kate" appears as the object of Luther's patience (line 3014) and prayer (lines 3143–3146).
3. **The Wittenberg congregation of March 1522** — addressed, never speaking: "I see no signs of love among you" [R15].
4. **The negligent pastors, "louts and scrimps" among the nobility, and "rude, old persons who knew nothing and still know nothing of these things, and who, nevertheless, go to Baptism and the Lord's Supper"** (LC lines 92–98, 245–247) [R25] — Luther's 1529 assessment, already used by Doc_01 §4, extended here by the short preface's disciplinary instruction that children "until they repeat them… should be given neither food nor drink" (LC lines 333–334).
5. **The scripted servant and the scripted master of the Small Catechism** (SC lines 444–459) — Luther writing the words an ordinary person should say; evidence of what he expected them to have done wrong ("neglected my duty and allowed damage to be done"; "charged him too much, cheated him and sold him badly made goods"), which is against-the-grain evidence of ordinary economic life **[Documented]** as prescription, **[Inferential/Thin]** as description.
6. **Karsthans** — a fictional peasant, in a pamphlet, quoted by an editor (§1.4).
7. **"Brief Sentences of the Catechism, according as Luther used to teach and instruct his family at home"** (TT lines 2394–2395) — the household as a formation site, reported by Aurifaber.

**Not present:** any visitation protocol; any parish register; any lay letter, testament, or court record; any woman writing under her own name; any Scandinavian, Baltic, or North German congregation's own record. The census's "what villagers actually knew" is not in this library at all.

**Significant debates that affect how this library's evidence is read — each tagged:**

1. **Did the Reformation "fail" at the parish level?** Strauss (1978), reading the visitation records, argued that catechetical indoctrination largely failed; Scribner, Kittelson and later Karant-Nunn argued the records measure the wrong thing, or a different thing. **[Contested]** — and, per Constitution Article 17, carried at full strength. **What this library contributes:** Luther's own 1529 complaints (LC preface) are *consistent with* Strauss's reading but are the founder's rhetoric, not a visitor's protocol; nothing in the library can adjudicate. Doc_05 must not narrate village ignorance as fact on the strength of the LC preface alone.
2. **Were the Theses posted?** Iserloh (1961) argued the door-posting is legend and the Theses were only sent to the bishops. The Philadelphia editor's confident "mid-day" narrative (v1 line 474; Round 1 C3) predates the debate. **[Contested]**; Doc_09 should carry the story with the debate attached, not settle it.
3. **Continuity or rupture in Luther's attitude to Jews?** Kaufmann argues religious and non-religious antisemitism are "inseparably united" in Luther and that he "took pre-modern anti-Semitism for granted, adopted it, and helped to spread it" (as summarized in reviews located this session, not from the book itself); the older view treated 1523 and 1543 as a reversal; and on *reception*, Wallmann (1987) [R92] argued the treatise was largely ignored in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries, against the continuity-of-influence reading. **[Contested]** as to explanation and later effect; the content of the 1543 text is **[Widely Accepted]** as reported by the tertiary and secondary literature this document could reach (§12.3 — not Documented, since the basis is one unread tertiary article and reviews of an unread monograph; Round 1 S6).
4. **The Peasants' War as "the revolution of the common man."** Blickle's reframing is the **[Dominant Modern Reconstruction]**; its bearing here is that the 1522 *Exhortation*'s "the common man has been brooding over the injury he has suffered in property, in body and in soul" (v3 lines 10665–10667) reads very differently against it than the 1525 tract does.
5. **The dating of Luther's "Reformation breakthrough"** (1513–1519) — **[Contested]**, and moot for this library, which lacks the 1545 tower passage (§0).

**Synthesis versus specialist scholarship.** The synthesis picture (the census's own `longDescription`: a professor argues in public, a movement "put the Bible, a catechism and a hymnbook into ordinary German households," the visitors are "frank about how little many villagers actually knew") is fully consistent with what the vendored founder-corpus shows was *intended*. The specialist work complicates it at exactly one point — what the visitation records mean — and that is the point this library cannot see. The relationship between the two layers is therefore not a disagreement but a division of evidence: the synthesis rests on the founder corpus and reports the debate; the specialist work rests on the congregational record this library lacks.

## 11. Source Asymmetry Assessment — the founder/congregation case

**Evidential visibility versus ecological visibility, stated as the Framework requires.** What *survives* in this library is the founder's argued doctrine (≈ 90% of the words). What was *formatively significant* for the congregations the world is named for was, by the movement's own account, the catechism learned at home, the hymn sung in church, the sermon heard weekly, the examination before communion, and — for the parishes — the visitor's inspection. Of these five, the library holds the first two as texts, the third once (Wittenberg, 1522), the fourth as a claim (Apology XXIV), and the fifth not at all. **The asymmetry is not that the congregational register is thin; it is that it is inverted — the register with the largest ecological role has the smallest evidential footprint** **[Documented]** as to the library.

- **Overrepresented:** Luther; Wittenberg and the Saxon court; 1517–1525; doctrine and polemic; the papacy as antagonist; the founder's own account of reception.
- **Underrepresented:** Melanchthon as administrator; 1530–1580 (the whole second half of Doc_01's window is represented by the 1529 catechisms, the 1530–31 confessional pair, three hymnal prefaces, and undated table talk — nothing at all from 1546–1580 except by the AC's later reception); parish clergy; women; servants and peasants as speakers rather than addressees; the Reformed as interlocutors; the Anabaptists as anything but condemned; Jews as anything but a category; Roman Catholic contemporaries in their own words.
- **Lost voices:** the visited villagers of 1527–28 (recorded, but not here); Grumbach (published, but not here — §12.1, §15 item 4); the Brussels friars (only in Luther's ballad); Katharina (one sentence). **Not Zell:** §15 item 4 and Registry R55 determine her Excluded / Named Comparandum — her own corpus speaks for Strasbourg's church, not this world's — so she is not counted among this world's own lost voices; see §12.1 and §15 for the argued determination, not repeated here.
- **Missing regions:** everything the census's "then N. Europe" covers. Prussia appears once, as the object of a treatise on clerical marriage and an editor's narrative; Scandinavia not at all; the North German cities only as Hamburg's superintendent Aepinus visiting Luther (TT lines 3129–3136) and Nuremberg and Reutlingen as AC signatories. Doc_01 open item 2 (Scandinavian dates) is untouched by this pass and stays open.
- **Missing social classes:** rural laity; urban artisans; women of every rank except one noblewoman's dedication (Duchess Margaret of Braunschweig-Lüneburg, dedicatee of the 1519 sacramental trilogy, v2 lines 92–93, editor) and one prince's mother (Mary, in the *Magnificat*).

**Per Framework para 170–174, this document accordingly:** acknowledges the gap explicitly (this section); assesses that the household, the parish, and women are the dimensions most underrepresented relative to their probable ecological role; marks every reconstruction extending beyond the founder's prescription as **[Inferential/Thin]** (§6, §10); and instructs Doc_05 and Doc_07 not to let the founder corpus's evidential weight stand in for ecological proportion.

## 12. Missing Voices Assessment and the Affirmative Duty (Constitution Article 20)

**The primary duty is naming structural absence, and this section is participant-facing material by the Framework's own rule** (para 184): "not a backstage construction note — it becomes a participant-facing obligation in the encounter." Each absence below is stated so the Facilitator function can carry it.

### 12.1 Women

**Whose voices the sources omit:** every woman's. The library contains no text written by a woman. **Why:** the founder-corpus edition principle (the Philadelphia committee selected Luther's "treatises… of most permanent value," v1 lines 132–134); the rights barrier on the two modern editions of women's writings (G1 Part B); and the historical fact that the movement's confessional documents were signed by princes and senates. **What the omission means:** the household — which the catechisms make a formation site — is documented from the father's side only.

**What the library does hold, against the grain (Article 20's permitted route, each with its specific textual trace):**
- Katharina von Bora's one quoted question (§7) [R31] — a *speaking* trace, triple-filtered.
- The Small Catechism's separate confession script for "a master or a lady of the house" (SC line 454) — presupposes women heading households at confession.
- AC Article XXIII's defense of clerical marriage, which argues from consequence — "it is to be expected that the churches shall at some time lack pastors if marriage is any longer forbidden" (AC lines 754–756) — and reports that "men, and that, priests, are cruelly put to death… for no other cause than marriage" (lines 765–766) [R37]. A polemic presupposing a practice: by 1530 pastors' wives existed in numbers sufficient to be a confessional issue **[Documented]** as to the argument, **[Widely Accepted]** as to the practice.
- *To the Knights of the Teutonic Order* (1523), an exhortation "that they lay aside false chastity and take upon them the true chastity of wedlock" (v3 lines 20634–20635, verified directly — Round 1's C3 claimed 20632–20633, which Round 2 found to be two blank lines; Revision 0's citation was correct) [R24] — the same presupposition from the male-religious side.
- The *Magnificat*'s Mary at household tasks (§1.1) — the founder's ideal of a woman's life, not a woman's.
- Duchess Margaret of Braunschweig-Lüneburg as dedicatee of the 1519 trilogy — a woman as *reader* of sacramental theology, per the editor [R9].

**Bounded reconstruction:** none attempted. The traces above support the *claim* that women were present as household heads, pastors' wives, and noble readers **[Widely Accepted]**; they do not support narrating any woman's formation. **The named, unvendored voice that answers this gap is Argula von Grumbach** [R54] — Native, since her 1523 pamphlets defended a Wittenberg-trained student and the evangelical cause by name, and she corresponded with Luther (existence and edition verified; content unread; **[Widely Accepted]** from prior knowledge). **Katharina Schütz Zell does not** — §15 item 4 and Registry R55, resolving the placement question Doc_01 open item 4 raised, determine her Excluded / Named Comparandum: her own corpus, on the argument made there, speaks for Strasbourg's own church rather than for this world. ### 12.3 Minority groups: Jews — binding disclosure (Step 0 item 1; Doc_01 §9 item 1)

**Named plainly, as this world's own historical record.** In 1543 Luther published *Von den Juden und ihren Lügen* (*On the Jews and Their Lies*), a treatise of some 65,000 words [R49]. **The text is not in this library**; G1 Part B records why (the standard English translation, in *Luther's Works* vol. 47, 1971, is in copyright; the only free copies found online are hosted by antisemitic sites and are unusable on that ground independent of copyright). **What follows is therefore tertiary- and secondary-sourced — from the Wikipedia article "On the Jews and Their Lies" [R83], read this session, and from published reviews of Kaufmann's monograph [R75], which itself was not read — not from the primary text, and is labeled as such.** (Revision 0 called R83 "the reference article" throughout; it is Wikipedia, and this document now says so wherever it is used — Round 1 S6.) The treatise recommends, in its own closing section, a programme of seven measures: that Jewish synagogues and schools be burned; that Jews be forbidden to own houses among Christians; that their religious writings be confiscated; that rabbis be forbidden to teach; that they be denied safe-conduct on the roads; that usury be prohibited and their wealth confiscated; and that they be put to forced manual labour. **[Widely Accepted]** as to the treatise's content — not Documented: one unread tertiary article plus reviews of one unread monograph is not "multiple independent sources," and the tag now says what the sourcing supports; **[Contested]** as to its later historical effect — the Wikipedia article reports the disagreement between scholars who see the treatise as a direct contributor to modern German antisemitism and Johannes Wallmann's view (*Lutheran Quarterly*, 1987 [R92]) that it "was in fact largely ignored during the 18th and 19th centuries." Kaufmann's 2017 study is the current specialist treatment and reads the 1543 text as continuous with, not a reversal of, Luther's earlier views [R75]. The 1523 treatise *That Jesus Christ Was Born a Jew*, which urged gentle treatment, is likewise unvendored [R50]. **The vendored library's own Jewish references** — searched across all ten files (198 occurrences of the string "Jew," case-sensitive, recounted this revision; Revision 0's "190" did not reproduce — Round 1 C2) — are exegetical (Paul on Israel in *Bondage*, Romans 9–11), homiletic ("The Turks, the Jews, and the ungodly may rehearse and speak the words of prayer… but they cannot pray," TT lines 3100–3102), and in one Table Talk heading positively admiring ("That the Jews have better Teachers and Writers of the Holy Scriptures than the Gentiles," TT lines 1040–1041). **None of this is the 1543 text, and its mildness must not be read as representative:** Morley's selection contains no chapter on the Jews, and the absence is a selection effect of an 1886 popular edition, not evidence about Luther **[Documented]** as to the library, **[Widely Accepted]** that the full Aurifaber collection contains anti-Jewish table talk (from prior knowledge, not verified this session). **This is a disclosed limitation of the build:** a construction document discussing a text it cannot vendor. A Representative for this world must be able to acknowledge this treatise's existence and content when asked, from this secondary characterization, and must never be built so as to smooth it into "Luther's later career."

### 12.4 The peasants — binding disclosure (Step 0 item 2; Doc_01 §9 item 2)

**Two distinct texts, not conflated, per Doc_01 §2.3 and the corpus-map note.** *An Earnest Exhortation for All Christians, Warning Them Against Insurrection and Rebellion* (written December 1521, published January 1522) **is vendored** [R19], and its own opening is sympathetic to the "common man" who "has been brooding over the injury he has suffered in property, in body and in soul, and has become provoked… and would indeed have good reason to lay about him with flails and cudgels, as the peasants are threatening to do" (v3 lines 10665–10672, verbatim, OCR clean at this passage) — before arguing that insurrection is nonetheless forbidden to Christians **[Documented]**. *Against the Murderous, Thieving Hordes of Peasants* (May 1525) **is not vendored** [R48]; G1 Part B records that the 1931 Philadelphia Edition vol. 4 sits at this project's PD threshold pending a renewal check. From the Wikipedia article "Against the Murderous, Thieving Hordes of Peasants" [R83], read this session, and from the scholarship whose existence was verified but which was not read [R48, R77] — tertiary- and secondary-sourced and labeled as such: it followed an *Admonition to Peace* (April 1525) within weeks; it charged the rebels with three sins against God and man (breaking their oaths of obedience, robbery and murder, and cloaking rebellion with the gospel), and called on the princes to put them down by force, holding that a man killed fighting on the rulers' side could count as a martyr before God; a defensive *Open Letter on the Harsh Book Against the Peasants* followed the same summer; all three are in *Luther's Works* vol. 46 (1967). **[Widely Accepted]** as to content (one unread tertiary article; not Documented — Round 1 S6); **[Widely Accepted]** that the tract's timing — appearing as the princes' armies were already winning — made its harshness politically consequential. **No phrase from the 1525 tract is placed in quotation marks in this document**, because the project cannot open the text: the most-quoted line ("smite, slay and stab"), the "three terrible sins," and the "true martyr" sentence all reach this document through the same tertiary article and are equally unverified against any edition; Revision 0 quoted two of the three directly while quarantining the first, and Round 1 S6 rightly asked that all three be treated alike [R94]. Doc_01 §8.2's point stands and is sharpened: the Anabaptists' Step 0 names this world as a persecutor "from the establishment side," and the 1525 tract is the primary document of that side, absent from this library.

## 13. Forces Lens Applied to Source Ecology (Forces Framework V1.1 §4, Step 2)

**Quoted precisely from the Forces Framework itself:** *"Sources are assessed not only for what they reveal about the world's internal life but for what they reveal about the forces acting on it. The forces lens at Step 2 asks: which sources speak to external forces? What do the silences reveal? What survivorship patterns indicate which forces were most threatening to the community's literary output? A source ecology that does not explicitly address forces is a source ecology that has missed a central question. Author Gravity assessment (Article 26) must include transmission history as a named dimension at Step 2."* Transmission history is at §3; this section answers the three questions, and then honors Doc_01 §7's three specific pointers by name.

**Which sources speak to external forces.** *Papal:* the *Argument in Defense* (the bull's forty-one articles) [R17, R40]; *The Papacy at Rome* [R8]; the 1545 preface's "the kingdom of his Vicar, the Antichrist in Rome… is sore beset" (v1 lines 408–409; the ellipsis stands for the editor's footnote marker). *Imperial:* the AC preface (the Diet's twin agenda — "measures against the Turk" and "dissensions in the matter of our holy religion," AC lines 49–55), the appeal to a council, the Table Talk's Worms narrative; Schindel's account of the Edict of Worms and the Council of Regency [R20]. *Territorial-princely:* Frederick's protection ("the Prince Elector well marked the Pope's unaccustomed humility," TT lines 3473–3474), Duke George's bans, the Teutonic Order's secularization, *Secular Authority* as the movement's own doctrine of the state — dedicated to Duke John and preached before him at Weimar (v3 lines 11732–11741, editor). *Popular-insurrectionary:* the *Exhortation*, the Sermons, *Karsthans*; the 1525 tract's absence (§12.4). *Military-external (the Turk):* AC preface; LC's fourth petition; Table Talk. *Print:* §6. *Epidemic:* only stock references (§6).

**What the silences reveal.** Three, each tested by search across all ten files this pass rather than inherited from Doc_01:

1. **The Reformed rival.** "Zwingli" and "Marburg" return zero hits in all ten files (Doc_01 checked six). "Oecolampadius" appears once, in an editor's footnote (v3 line 14540). **"Sacramentarians" appears in Luther's own voice exactly once** — in *Bondage* (1525): "when Sacramentarians, Donatists, Arians, Anabaptists, Epicureans, &c. are springing up, we ask — How is it that our Lord's field hath tares?" (Cole lines 16090–16092, "sprjing" in the OCR) [R34] — and three times in the Philadelphia apparatus of v1: twice in the editor's commentary on the 1520 eucharistic treatises (lines 10661, 10727) and once as an entry in the volume index (line 15611; Revision 0 counted two — Round 1 C9). **Finding:** the library's founder corpus predates the Eucharistic controversy almost entirely (the 1519–1523 treatises were written, as the editor says, when "the opposition of the Sacramentarians… had not yet arisen," v1 lines 10661–10662), and the 1529 catechisms and 1530 confession state the Lutheran position without naming the opponent — the Large Catechism's "enthusiasts," "new spirits," and "fanatics" (LC 3277, 3841, 3916, 4069, 4095) are generic labels, not names (§12.5; Round 1 C10). The silence is therefore *chronological and generic*, not suppressive: the rival is absent because the vendored texts are early or confessional, not because anyone removed it. **Doc_01 §7's pointer is honored and extended:** an English text of the Marburg Articles (3 October 1529, signed by Luther, Melanchthon, Zwingli and Oecolampadius among others) exists [R56] — in the German History in Documents and Images collection and in *Luther's Works* vol. 38 — both in copyright; the 1529 German original is public domain. Because this world's own leaders signed the Articles, the Registry rows them as a plain Native lead — a partly-native primary document, not an answered opponent text — and not under the "(context)" marker Revision 0 gave them (Round 1 S9); when read, they are licensed for the Eucharistic break as the signed text states it, never for the Reformed side's account of the Colloquy. Zwingli's own report of the Colloquy [R57] is Excluded here as a Named Comparandum: it is VI.2's evidence, not this world's. **The silence remains a real gap, now with a located lead** **[Documented]** as to the searches.
2. **The Roman Catholic contemporary.** Doc_01 §7 asked for Roman responses "as a check on this world's own one-sided account." §1.3 is the answer: they are in the library only as quoted, and the *Confutation*'s English text exists in Kolb–Nestingen (2001), in copyright [R39, R80]. The one-sidedness is structural to a founder-corpus library and cannot be corrected from inside it.
3. **The congregational register.** Doc_01 §7 asked that the visitation records and women's writings be weighted "at least as heavily as any further founder-corpus material." This pass found the G1 weighting went the other way — the four newly vendored files are all founder corpus (hymns, Table Talk, v3, *Bondage*), each valuable, none congregational — and states that plainly. The reason is rights, not neglect (§4, §12), and the leads are now specific: LW 40 for the *Instructions*, Matheson for Grumbach, both in copyright **[Documented]** as to the library's state.

**Survivorship patterns.** Which forces threatened the community's literary output? On the evidence in hand, *none did, and that is itself the finding.* Luther's corpus survived in twelve, ten, and three editions within months of writing (§6); the movement had territorial protection and the press. What shaped survival was *selection*, not suppression: Luther's own reluctance to be collected (1539 preface); Aurifaber's topical rearrangement; the Philadelphia committee's principle of "permanent value" and its exclusion of *Monastic Vows* "because of its size" (v3 lines 20823–20824) [R61]; Morley's selection for "Luther's mind and character"; and — the one modern force acting on *this* library — rights and hosting: the two texts most damaging to the founder's reputation (1525, 1543) are the two this project could not responsibly vendor, for reasons of copyright and of who hosts the free copies. **A survivorship pattern in reverse: the texts survive abundantly, and the library's gaps track a twentieth-century translation economy and a twenty-first-century hosting problem, not sixteenth-century suppression** **[Documented]**. Bell's story of imperial book-burning is the only claim of suppression in the library and is unverified (§3.4).

**What this means for Doc_04, Doc_05 and Doc_08.** Every candidate gravity Doc_01 §1 named (justification, Scripture against hierarchy, sacramental reduction, catechesis) is documented in texts that were written *under* a named external force — the bull, the Diet, the Duke's ban, the threat of insurrection — and the forces analysis at Doc_08 can be built from dated documents for the papal, imperial and territorial forces. It cannot be built from this library for the Reformed rivalry or for the parish's experience of the visitation; those are inputs Doc_08 will have to mark as absent.
