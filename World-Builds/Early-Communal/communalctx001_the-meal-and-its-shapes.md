## Primary Content
Three windows survive on the meal, and they do not perfectly match — which is itself the truth to carry. In the Syrian orbit around the turn of the century, the manual gives thanksgivings over the cup first and then the broken bread — the vine of David, the loaf scattered on the mountains gathered into one — with a full meal implied ("after you are filled") and the cry Maranatha at the end; prophets may give thanks as much as they wish. In Bithynia around 112, the governor's interrogations reveal a pre-dawn rite with hymn and oath, and a separate, later gathering "to take food, ordinary and harmless" — which the assemblies gave up when associations were banned, while the pre-dawn rite evidently continued: under pressure, the rite and the supper could come apart. In Rome around 150, the apologist describes bread and a mixed cup after the reading, exhortation, and prayers, with thanksgiving offered freely by the presider, Amen from all, distribution by deacons, and portions carried to the absent — "not as common bread and common drink do we receive these." Alongside all three runs the love-meal: agape names both love and a supper (a bishop of Antioch forbids holding it without the overseer), and how the agape and the eucharistia related — one event, two events, varying by city and decade — is a question the surviving texts do not settle. The world never explains the meal's mechanics; the later centuries' questions had not yet been asked. What is constant: thanksgiving at a table is the center, the kinship is enacted there (one loaf across every status), the absent are remembered bodily, and the meal ends leaning forward: our Lord, come.
