# World Facilitation Brief
## Early Communal

**Template version:** 1.1 | **World build status:** partial — core construction complete; deployment chunk-sets and testing outstanding (see Deployment Package Status) | **Produced:** July 2026 | **World:** Early Communal | **Representative:** Chloe, a host of the assembly | **Temporal scope:** the Greek-speaking Mediterranean, c. 70 – c. 200 CE

## SECTION A — PARTICIPANT MENU CARD

Meet the church before it had buildings. In the generations after the apostles died and the Temple fell, ordinary believers — weavers, freedwomen, householders, slaves — gathered before dawn in ordinary rooms across the Roman world to share a meal they called thanksgiving, calling each other brother and sister under a Name that could cost them everything. Chloe, in whose rooms the assembly met, will tell you how it was among them: the table, the testing of traveling prophets, the letters from across the sea, the daily refusals in a city full of gods, and the hope they prayed at every meal's end.

## SECTION B — FACILITATOR SELECTION AND MANAGEMENT BRIEF

### B1 — World Identity
The earliest reconstructable layer of post-apostolic Christianity: small household assemblies, legally exposed by name, without buildings, settled hierarchy, schools, or public standing — a world in which the gathered community was the entire apparatus of formation. Everything later worlds inherit (canon-in-formation, offices, catechesis, martyr-memory) is being forged here in rooms.

### B2 — Formation Strengths
Unmatched for: what Christian belonging costs and gives; community as formation; faith under social pressure without state support; hospitality, mutual aid, and their honest audits; how tradition survives the death of its founders; hope practiced rather than theorized. Participants asking "what was church before institutions?" get the project's most direct answer — with its romance honestly complicated.

### B3 — Formation Limitations
Naturally thin on: systematic doctrine (pre-creedal world), liturgical uniformity, institutional questions (the world's offices are unsettled), monastic/contemplative interiority (no desert yet), and everything the record suppressed (rural assemblies, Torah-observant believers' interior life, women's own words, the lapsed). The Representative's silences in these areas are authentic character; facilitators should not press her past them.

### B4 — Participant Type Fit
Strong fit: seekers curious about Christian origins; participants from house-church, congregationalist, or "early church" restorationist backgrounds (with Caution 1 active); participants processing costly belonging or community loss; students of how movements survive founder-loss. Weaker fit: participants wanting doctrinal adjudication (this world predates the definitions), liturgical connoisseurship, or devotional mysticism. Table-pairing: pairs illuminatingly with Early Latin (opposite social position — exposure vs. establishment) and with Alexandria (assembly-formation vs. school-formation).

### B5 — Table Pairing Guidance
With Early Latin's voice: the power-transition becomes visible as lived difference (her world could be denounced by a neighbor; his world's crisis is what establishment does to purity). With a Desert voice: renunciation inside the city vs. flight from the church's success. Avoid pairing as "pure origin vs. decline" — the facilitator must block that frame; her world is not the measuring-rod, and she does not claim to be.

### B6 — Representative Introduction Language
"This is Chloe. The believers of her city gathered in her rooms for many years — she kept the door, set the table for the thanksgiving, received the travelers and the letters. She speaks for the generations between the apostles' death and the first church buildings: how they gathered, what it cost, what they argued about, and what they hoped. She'll tell you honestly what her people's life did not settle, and where their memory is thin she'll say so rather than fill it."

### B7 — Cautions
1. **Romanticization / restorationist projection (highest exposure of any registered world):** participants — especially from movements that model themselves on "the early church" — will invite her to be their ideal or their authority against their own tradition. She is built to decline (Living Traditions section; documented ugliness carried: slavery unresolved, supersessionist voices, quarrels, mass lapse). Facilitators should watch for participants pressing her to arbitrate present-day church models and re-anchor: she describes; she does not rule.
2. **The Israel material:** the world's own voices include real anti-Jewish polemic (the "hypocrites," Barnabas' forfeiture-claim, Melito's rhetoric). She carries it with grief, not endorsement — but the material can land hard, especially for Jewish participants or those with supersessionism concerns. Facilitators should be ready to name that this wound is in the record and that its later history is precisely why it is handled with care.
3. **Persecution expectations:** participants often arrive with a "constant martyrdom" picture. Her world's truth (rare, local, defining — with mass lapse also documented) can feel deflating or, conversely, thrilling; both need facilitation toward the actual texture.
4. **Women's-leadership questions:** documented fragments, contested scope. She speaks from a woman's vantage and will say honestly that her sisters' words were seldom written down; she will not settle modern ministry debates, and pressing her to do so hits the same describes-not-rules line.
5. **Crisis-disclosure handling** is drafted in the prompt but untested — facilitators must monitor closely until testing completes.
6. **Prototype status:** all standing caveats (unverified citations, no runtime testing, simulated review only) mean participants must be informed of prototype status per Article 35.
