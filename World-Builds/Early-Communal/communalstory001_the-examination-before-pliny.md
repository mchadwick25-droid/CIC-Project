## Story Text
Around the year 112, in Bithynia on the Black Sea coast, the governor Pliny wrote to the emperor Trajan because accusations against Christians were multiplying and he had never attended such a trial. His procedure: ask three times whether the person is a Christian; execute the obstinate; release any who deny it, invoke the gods, offer incense and wine to the emperor's image, and curse Christ — "things which, it is said, those who are really Christians cannot be made to do." Some who had been accused said they had left the faith years before — some twenty years. From these deserters, and from two enslaved women "who were called ministrae," examined under torture, Pliny learned what the Christians actually did: they met on a fixed day before dawn, sang a hymn to Christ "as to a god," bound themselves by oath to commit no theft, adultery, or breach of trust, and met again later to share ordinary, harmless food — a meeting they had given up after his edict against associations. Trajan's reply set the empire's course for a century: they are not to be hunted; anonymous pamphlets have no place in our age; but the confessed and obstinate are punished.

## Formation Ecology Connection
The world's legal weather in one document: the Name itself the charge; enforcement local and accusation-driven; the gathering as the identifying act; the meal suspended under pressure while the pre-dawn rite endured; mass lapse as documented reality; enslaved women as officeholders — and as the only members whose testimony reaches us, extracted under torture, their names unrecorded.

## Tier Justification
Contemporary, datable, named-author official correspondence with no motive to flatter. Tier 1 at the narrative level; details filtered through Roman administrative categories (noted at use).

## Usage Guidance
The Representative may tell this as documented history — what the assemblies knew such examinations to be. She must keep its unheroic textures: the deserters, the ban's effect on the meal, and above all the two ministrae, of whom she should say what the record enforces: their names, words, and fates were not written down; that silence is part of the story.
