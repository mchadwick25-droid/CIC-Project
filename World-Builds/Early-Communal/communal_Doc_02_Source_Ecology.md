## 1. The Shape of the Evidence — Read This First

This world's evidence has a character unlike any other registered world, and the character itself is a primary finding:

1. **The world rarely describes itself.** Its writings are almost all *occasional* — letters answering crises, manuals prescribing (not describing) practice, visions demanding repentance, defenses addressed to outsiders. The two fullest descriptions of its central act of worship come from (a) a Roman governor interrogating deserters and torturing two enslaved women (Pliny), and (b) an apologist explaining the assembly to a hostile emperor (Justin). Almost nothing was written *by the world, for the world, about what the world was like* — because everyone inside already knew.
2. **Nearly all narrative arrives through a fourth-century filter.** Papias, the Lyons letter, the Quartodeciman correspondence, most of what we know about second-century persons and events, survives because Eusebius of Caesarea chose to quote it c. 320s. Eusebius is this world's single greatest Author Gravity risk and receives a full secondary-narrative entry below (§3.8).
3. **The material record is close to empty, and the emptiness is data.** No church buildings, no securely dated Christian inscriptions until the very edge of the span, no identifiable Christian art or burial until c. 200. The world met in space that left no trace because leaving no trace is how it inhabited space.
4. **The losers were not preserved.** Torah-observant assemblies, Marcionite churches (numerous enough that Justin says Marcion's teaching had spread "to every race of men"), Valentinian circles, the New Prophecy — all survive chiefly in their opponents' quotations. The surviving corpus is the proto-orthodox stream's self-selection, made mostly in the following two centuries.

Consequence, stated once and inherited by every later document: **evidential visibility and ecological visibility diverge more widely in this world than in any other registered world.** The build compensates by elevating outside witnesses and against-the-grain readings to first-class status and by refusing to let the prescriptive manuals masquerade as description.

## 2. Primary Voices (in-world, roughly chronological)

For each: date and provenance (with contest noted), genre, what it makes visible, and its distortion.

**2.1 The Didache ("The Teaching of the Lord through the Twelve Apostles to the Nations").** Composite community manual; final form c. 100–120 on the dominant reconstruction, with layers possibly decades older; Syria or its orbit (Dominant Modern Reconstruction; Egypt argued by a minority). Rediscovered 1873 (Bryennios) — a reminder of how contingent this world's visibility is. Makes visible: the Two Ways moral catechesis (chs. 1–6), baptism practice with its pragmatic concessions (ch. 7: running water preferred, other water permitted, affusion if necessary), the Wednesday/Friday fasts and thrice-daily Lord's Prayer set explicitly against "the hypocrites" (ch. 8), meal prayers of startling beauty and un-Pauline shape (chs. 9–10: cup before bread; "as this broken bread was scattered upon the mountains and gathered became one"), the itinerant economy and its tests (chs. 11–13: an apostle who stays three days is a false prophet; whoever asks for money in the Spirit is a false prophet; the christemporos, "Christ-trafficker"), the instruction to appoint episkopoi and diakonoi "worthy of the Lord" who must not be despised beside the prophets (ch. 15 — the settling of offices caught mid-motion), and the eschatological conclusion (ch. 16). Distortion: prescriptive, not descriptive — it legislates precisely where practice was contested; unknown how typical its community was (Doc_01 open question); its closeness to Jewish practice may make it an outlier or may make it a window into the world's suppressed plurality. Both possibilities carried.

**2.2 1 Clement.** Letter of the Roman assembly to the Corinthian, occasioned by Corinth's deposition of its presbyters; traditionally c. 96 (Domitianic), with a live minority range c. 70–140 — the traditional date rests substantially on reading the opening's "sudden misfortunes" as Domitianic persecution, itself contested. Anonymous and corporate ("The church of God sojourning in Rome to the church of God sojourning in Corinth" — the *paroikousa* address that gives the world one of its deepest self-descriptions); attributed to Clement by later tradition. Makes visible: trans-local intervention without command authority (Rome exhorts, cites scripture and example, sends envoys — it cannot order); succession reasoning one generation deep ("the apostles appointed... and afterwards gave the rule that if these fall asleep, other approved men should succeed" — 1 Clem. 42, 44); the liturgical-cosmic order argument (chs. 20, 40–41); the world's prayer for rulers *under whom it is vulnerable* (ch. 60–61). Distortion: the earliest surviving voice of order speaking against disorder — the deposed presbyters' side, and the Corinthian majority's actual reasoning, are unrecoverable; over-weighting 1 Clement makes the world look more office-settled than the Didache and Hermas show it was.

**2.3 The letters of Ignatius of Antioch** (middle recension, seven letters: Ephesians, Magnesians, Trallians, Romans, Philadelphians, Smyrnaeans, Polycarp). Written en route to execution in Rome; traditional date c. 107–110 under Trajan; **authenticity and date are live scholarly contest** — the middle recension's authenticity was the consensus settlement (Lightfoot/Zahn), but a serious revisionist line (Hübner, Barnes, and others) redates to the 130s–140s or questions authenticity; the contest is carried at full strength, and every Ignatian claim in this build is dated "early-to-mid second century" rather than 107. Makes visible: the threefold office *being urged* (one bishop, presbyters, deacons — "do nothing without the bishop"), which is against-the-grain evidence that it was not yet the norm; eucharistic realism against docetists ("the medicine of immortality," Eph. 20.2; those who "abstain from eucharist and prayer because they do not confess that the eucharist is the flesh of our Savior," Smyrn. 6–7); the first surviving use of "the catholic church" (Smyrn. 8.2) and of *Christianismos* set against *Ioudaismos* (Magn. 10); the theology of martyrdom from inside a condemned man ("God's wheat, ground by the teeth of beasts," Rom. 4); the network in motion (assemblies sending envoys and money along his route); slavery inside the assembly (Pol. 4.3: slaves are not to be haughty, nor to demand manumission from the common fund — documented, uncomfortable, carried). Distortion: the highest-voltage voice in the corpus, writing under a death sentence — his intensity is not the world's baseline temperature; and his bishop-centered order is his *program*, not the world's settled state. Over-weighting Ignatius produces both an anachronistically episcopal world and an anachronistically martyrdom-saturated one. This build treats him as the world's most vivid voice and its second-greatest internal Author Gravity risk (after Eusebius among transmitters).

**2.4 Polycarp of Smyrna, To the Philippians.** c. 110–140 (possibly two letters combined — Harrison's partition thesis; Contested but widely entertained). Makes visible: the ordinary pastoral register the corpus otherwise lacks — duties of wives, widows, deacons, young men, presbyters; the presbyter Valens fallen through avarice, and the instruction to treat him and his wife not as enemies but "as suffering and straying members" (restoration practice, Documented); heavy reuse of earlier Christian writings (the deposit already functioning as a working library).

**2.5 The Shepherd of Hermas.** Rome; composite, layers plausibly spanning c. 90–150 (the Muratorian fragment's claim that Hermas wrote it recently, in the episcopate of his brother Pius, c. 140s, is itself part of the dating argument). The longest surviving text of this world, and its single best window into ordinary-believer texture: Hermas is a freedman with a business, a wife, and failed children, not an officeholder. Makes visible: post-baptismal sin and the one repentance (Mand. 4.3 — the world's purity anxiety and its pastoral loosening, caught mid-negotiation); wealth and commerce as the chief spiritual dangers of a comfortable decade (the rich are round stones that must be squared; the elm-and-vine of rich and poor, Sim. 2); double-mindedness (*dipsychia* — his signature diagnostic term); prophets tested in the assembly by conduct (Mand. 11); the tower still building (the church's incompleteness as lived eschatology); church order in Rome as plural presbyters-and-leaders, with Grapte instructed to teach the widows and orphans and Clement to write to the cities abroad (Vis. 2.4.3) — a named woman with a named teaching charge, and the fractionated-Rome picture (Lampe) in miniature. Distortion: one anxious conscience's Rome; visionary genre; his rigorism-vs-mercy struggle must not be generalized as the whole world's temperature.

**2.6 Justin Martyr.** Samaria-born philosopher-convert teaching in Rome; 1 Apology (c. 153–155), Dialogue with Trypho (c. 155–160); executed with six companions c. 165 (Acts of Justin — see §5). The world's indispensable descriptive witness: 1 Apol. 61 (baptism as *photismos*, "illumination," after instruction, prayer, and fasting "with the community praying and fasting with them"); 65–66 (post-baptismal incorporation: the kiss, the bread and mixed cup, "not as common bread and common drink do we receive these"); 67 (the Sunday assembly entire: city and country gather in one place; the memoirs of the apostles and the writings of the prophets read "as long as time permits"; the proestos exhorts; all stand and pray; the eucharist with distribution by deacons to the absent; the free-will collection deposited with the proestos "who succors the orphans and widows, and those who through sickness or any other cause are in want, and those who are in bonds, and the strangers sojourning among us"; the day named: first day, creation and resurrection). Distortion: apologetic idealization for an outside audience; Rome-specific (generalization beyond Rome is a reconstruction step, per Bradshaw — flagged wherever Doc_05 uses him); philosophically trained voice atypical of the assemblies he describes.

**2.7 The Martyrdom of Polycarp.** Letter of the Smyrnaean assembly to Philomelium; death dated c. 155–156 (or 167; Contested), text substantially contemporary with later editorial touches (Contested in degree). The world's first martyr-narrative as a genre-birth: "eighty-six years I have served him"; the deliberate imitatio Christi patterning; the refusal to praise Quintus the volunteer (ch. 4); the bones "more precious than costly stones and finer than gold," gathered for annual commemoration of his "birthday" (ch. 18) — the martyr-cult documented at its moment of origin. Distortion: genre patterning shapes details (Doc_09 handles tier discipline); Smyrna's crisis is not every year's weather.

**2.8 2 Clement.** Anonymous sermon, c. 140–160, provenance unknown (Corinth and Rome both argued). The earliest surviving Christian homily: repentance while there is time, the church as prior reality, "almsgiving is good as repentance for sin... almsgiving lifts the burden of sin" (16.4), the ordinary moral exhortation register.

**2.9 The Epistle of Barnabas.** c. 70–135, Alexandria on the dominant guess. The world's supersessionist edge in full voice: Israel's covenant forfeited, the scriptures allegorized wholly to the church. Included as primary evidence *of the world's internal range on the Israel question*, not as its consensus — Justin's Dialogue (Trypho treated as an interlocutor, Torah-observant believers acknowledged) and the Didache (Jewish-adjacent practice) mark the other poles.

**2.10 The Epistle to Diognetus.** Later second century (Contested; some place it beyond span). The world's most polished self-description at its edge: Christians "dwell in their own countries, but as sojourners... every foreign land is their fatherland, and every fatherland foreign" (ch. 5); "what the soul is in the body, Christians are in the world" (ch. 6). Used with its idealizing genre named.

**2.11 Papias of Hierapolis (fragments, via Eusebius and Irenaeus).** Early second century (date contested c. 95–130). The living-voice preference ("I did not suppose that things from books would profit me as much as things from a living and abiding voice," HE 3.39) — the memory crisis articulated from inside; traditions about Mark's and Matthew's origins (foundational and endlessly contested); chiliastic expectation Eusebius scorns (the filter visible in the act of transmission: Eusebius calls him "a man of very little intelligence" — we see the selector's hand).

**2.12 Fragments and edge voices:** Quadratus and Aristides (earliest apologies, c. 125 — Aristides on the Christians' practical ethics: care of orphans, hospitality to strangers, fasting to feed the poor); Melito of Sardis, Peri Pascha (c. 160–170; Quartodeciman paschal celebration Documented; the anti-Judaic rhetoric at full, disturbing strength — carried, not sanitized); the anonymous anti-Montanist and Quartodeciman correspondence preserved in Eusebius (Polycrates' letter with its list of Asian luminaries; Ammia of Philadelphia named as a recognized prophetess — HE 5.17); Dionysius of Corinth's letter network (via Eusebius); Hegesippus (via Eusebius; succession-list making at the span's end); Irenaeus of Lyons (c. 180) **used as a doorway voice**: his memories of Polycarp, the Quartodeciman irenics with Victor, and his witness to assembly practice are in-span evidence; his systematic heresiology and canon-consolidation belong to the successor world.
**New Testament texts composed within the span** (on dominant datings: the Gospels in their final forms, Acts, the Pastorals, 1 Peter, Hebrews, the Johannine epistles, Revelation, others) are used **strictly as evidence of assembly life in their composition milieux** — Revelation for Asia Minor's assemblies under pressure and its liturgical imagination; 1 Peter for the "sojourners" self-understanding and household tensions; the Pastorals for the office-and-widow trajectory; Hebrews 10:25 for the "not neglecting to gather" exhortation — with each dating's contest acknowledged and **no canonical-theological adjudication of any kind** (Doc_01 constitutional boundary). This build never treats the NT as "this world's literature to interpret"; it treats specific documents as datable artifacts of the world's decades where dominant scholarship so dates them.

## 3. Outside and Secondary-Narrative Voices

**3.1 Pliny the Younger, Ep. 10.96–97 (with Trajan's rescript).** Bithynia-Pontus, c. 111–113. **The single most important external document this world possesses** — Documented, contemporary, administratively disinterested in flattery. Yields: the fixed-day pre-dawn gathering; the hymn "to Christ as to a god" (carmen... quasi deo); the oath (sacramentum) binding members against theft, adultery, and breach of trust; the separate later meal of "ordinary and harmless food," abandoned after Pliny's ban on associations (hetaeriae); interrogation procedure and the test of cursing Christ and sacrificing; the two enslaved women "called ministrae" tortured for information; lapsed members who had left "some three years ago, some many years, one even twenty"; the movement's spread through "cities, villages, and farms" and "every age, every rank, both sexes"; Trajan's rescript establishing the accusation regime: *conquirendi non sunt* — they are not to be sought out; anonymous pamphlets have no place "in our age." Author Gravity: a governor's interrogation summary — everything is filtered through Roman associational categories and extracted under threat; the two ministrae, this world's only enslaved female voices, speak only through his one clause about them.

**3.2 Tacitus, Annals 15.44.** c. 115–120, on the events of 64: Nero's scapegoating, the "vast multitude," the *odium humani generis* charge, deaths as spectacle. Documented for the event's memory-shape by Tacitus' time; details contested.

**3.3 Suetonius** (Nero 16; Claudius 25 "impulsore Chresto") — brief, contested in reference (the Claudius notice especially).

**3.4 Lucian of Samosata, The Passing of Peregrinus.** c. 165–170. Satirical, therefore precious: what Christian charity looked like to a mocking outsider — the imprisoned Peregrinus attended from dawn by widows and orphans, officials sleeping inside the prison with him, meals brought in, money streaming from assemblies as far as Asia, "their first lawgiver persuaded them that they are all brothers of one another." The kinship-and-aid ecology confirmed by hostile testimony.

**3.5 Celsus, The True Word** (c. 175–180, surviving in Origen's quotation c. 248). The educated pagan indictment: Christians as wool-workers, cobblers, and fullers who win over children and "stupid women" in private houses and workshops, telling them to leave their teachers and come "to the women's quarters, or the cobbler's shop, or the fuller's shop, to learn perfection" (c. Cels. 3.55). Against-the-grain gold: the assemblies' social texture, domestic recruitment, women's and children's agency, workshop religion — visible precisely in the sneer. Transmission caveat: reaches us only through Origen's selective rebuttal.

**3.6 Galen** (fragments, some via Arabic transmission): Christians' contempt of death and sexual restraint noted with a physician's puzzlement; their reliance on "parables" rather than demonstration. Small, but a rare neutral-to-grudging register.

**3.7 Rabbinic notices** (birkat ha-minim and related): late-dated, redaction-critical minefield; used only to attest that separation was felt on both sides, at Inferential/Thin.

**3.8 Eusebius of Caesarea — secondary narrative source, full five-dimension Author Gravity entry (the transmission filter itself):**
- *Visibility:* overwhelming — the majority of this world's narrative and much of its fragmentary literature exists only as his quotations (Papias, Hegesippus, the Lyons letter, Polycrates, Dionysius, the anti-Montanist sources).
- *Representativeness:* systematically skewed — a fourth-century imperial-church historian selecting a pedigree for Constantinian orthodoxy; succession lists, anti-heretical continuity, and respectable order are what he needs this world to show.
- *Influence:* near-total on how this world was known for seventeen centuries; the very picture of an unbroken episcopal early church is substantially his artifact (Dominant Modern Reconstruction as a critique; the lists' data quality is Contested case-by-case).
- *Limitations:* 150–250 years distant; no access to most of what he did not quote; demonstrable editorial contempt for what he disliked (Papias' chiliasm).
- *Transmission history:* his own text is well-transmitted; the sources he quotes are, for us, only what he excerpted — the excerpting *is* the loss.
Disposition: every Eusebian-mediated datum in this build carries a "via Eusebius" marker and drops one confidence level from what a direct source would earn, unless independently corroborated.

## 4. Secondary Scholarship Assessment (the interpretive layer named)

The reconstruction reads its primary sources through, and against, the following named frameworks — acknowledged here so that modern consensus is not laundered into ancient evidence:

- **Walter Bauer, Orthodoxy and Heresy in Earliest Christianity (1934)** and its long critical reception: the thesis that "heresy" was often regionally first and "orthodoxy" the later Roman-inflected winner. Not adopted; not dismissed; its permanent legacy adopted — *diversity first, uniformity later is the default expectation, and every uniformity claim needs positive evidence.*
- **Wayne Meeks, The First Urban Christians (1983)** and the social-history tradition: urban, socially mixed, status-inconsistent membership. Dominant Modern Reconstruction; adopted with its Pauline-era center of gravity noted (extension to 70–200 is inference).
- **Peter Lampe, From Paul to Valentinus:** Rome as fractionated immigrant house-congregations without monarchical government through most of the span. Dominant Modern Reconstruction; load-bearing for this build's order-picture; flagged as such.
- **Paul Bradshaw, The Search for the Origins of Christian Worship:** liturgical minimalism — early diversity, late uniformity; harmonizing reconstructions of "the early liturgy" are modern artifacts. Adopted as a governing discipline for every worship claim in Doc_05.
- **Edward Adams, The Earliest Christian Meeting Places (2013):** direct challenge to the "house church" consensus — shops, workshops, warehouses, rented dining rooms, gardens were plausibly common venues. **Carried as a named tension against this build's own label** ("household churches"): the domestic register is secure as the dominant *social template*; the archaeology of venues is genuinely open. The world's self-description in Doc_01 survives, but the Facilitation Brief must not let participants picture only villas.
- **Candida Moss, The Myth of Persecution (2013)** with Ste. Croix's and Barnes' older juridical work: persecution as local, sporadic, accusation-driven; the martyr-literature's later inflation. Adopted for the legal-regime picture (it matches Pliny/Trajan exactly); its stronger deflationary claims about martyr-memory carried as Contested against the documented in-span cult (Mart. Pol. 18).
- **The "Parting of the Ways" debate** (Dunn versus Becker/Reed's The Ways That Never Parted; Boyarin): whether Judaism and Christianity were distinct by this span's end or entangled for centuries more. Carried as a full-strength named tension; this build's Israel-question gravity (Doc_04) is written to survive either resolution.
- **Larry Hurtado (Lord Jesus Christ; Destroyer of the Gods)** on early devotional practice and Christian distinctiveness; **Rodney Stark (The Rise of Christianity)** whose demographic and epidemic-care arguments are stimulating and methodologically Contested (retrojection of modern sociology; used only with flags); **Robert Wilken (The Christians as the Romans Saw Them)** for the outside view; **Carolyn Osiek & David Balch (Families in the New Testament World)** for household structure; **Margaret Y. MacDonald (Early Christian Women and Pagan Opinion)** for the women's-history lens on Pliny/Celsus; **Helmut Koester/James Robinson (Trajectories)** as the named-and-declined strand framework (Doc_01 §3).
- **The Ignatian question** (Lightfoot/Zahn settlement vs. Hübner/Barnes revision) and **the Didache's dating and milieu** (Niederwimmer, Milavec, van de Sandt/Flusser): both carried at Contested wherever they matter.

Where accessible synthesis histories (e.g., standard one-volume histories of early Christianity) smooth these debates into narrative, this build follows the specialist contest, not the synthesis.

## 5. Formation Narrative Sources (named assessment)

- **Martyrdom of Polycarp** (§2.7): the genre's birth; Tier discipline at Doc_09 (core event and date-memory vs. genre-patterned details).
- **Acts of Justin and Companions** (c. 165; recension A close to a court record): Justin and six companions — including Euelpistus, a slave of Caesar's household, and the woman Charito — interrogated by the prefect Rusticus; Justin's lodging "above the bath" where "those who wished came to me and I shared with them the words of truth"; their refusals plain and unadorned. The closest this world comes to hearing ordinary members answer for themselves (even here, through a court genre).
- **Letter of the Churches of Lyons and Vienne** (177, via Eusebius HE 5.1): Blandina the slave woman outlasting torturers, "I am a Christian, and among us nothing evil is done"; the ninety-year-old bishop Pothinus; Sanctus answering every question "Christianus sum"; the confessors refusing the title "martyrs" as belonging to Christ and the already-dead (HE 5.2). Immense formation content; double filter (communal letter genre + Eusebius) named.
- **Acts of the Scillitan Martyrs** (180, Carthage): the earliest dated Latin Christian document, at the world's outer geographic and temporal edge; the capsa containing "books and letters of Paul, a just man" — the deposit physically present in court.
- **Apocryphal acts beginning at span's edge** (Acts of Paul (and Thecla), c. 160–190): Tier 3 at best; used only as evidence of what stories the world's own people wanted told (celibacy, women's agency, apostolic power) — the Thecla material's popularity is itself formation-ecology evidence; its events are not treated as history.
- **Hermas** functions secondarily as formation narrative (a conscience's journey rendered in vision).

## 6. Material Culture and Daily Life Sources

- **Built space:** nothing in-span. The earliest identified Christian meeting structure anywhere (Dura-Europos renovation, c. 232–256) postdates the world and is cited only as the *terminus* of this world's invisibility. Assessment: the assemblies met in spaces archaeologically indistinguishable from ordinary life — houses, apartments (the insulae of Rome make "house church" itself too grand for many), workshops, rented rooms (Adams' challenge, §4). Acknowledged explicitly: the material conditions existed; they are unrecoverable in specifics.
- **Papyri:** the earliest Christian literary papyri (P52 traditionally c. 125, now dated more broadly c. 125–175+ — Nongbri's caution adopted; P90, P104 similarly ranged) attest Gospel texts circulating in Egypt within the span but yield no community detail. Documentary papyri identifiably Christian begin only after the span. Stated plainly rather than padded.
- **Inscriptions:** the Abercius inscription (Hieropolis, c. 190s) — "disciple of the holy shepherd... everywhere faith led the way and set before me for food the fish from the spring, immense, pure, which the pure virgin caught" — the earliest arguably Christian epitaph, its Christian reading dominant though famously contested; at the span's edge and treated as such.
- **Social-historical background:** literacy plausibly 10–20% in the urban East (standard estimates; Contested in precision) — the assemblies were therefore *hearing* communities; reading was performance (Justin's "as long as time permits"). Household economics: patronage, trade, slavery structuring daily life; the assembly meeting inside, not apart from, these structures. Urban density: privacy scarce; the pre-dawn hour of Pliny's report is working people's only free hour (inference, flagged). Meals: the era's ordinary association banquet is the formal comparandum for the agape (Documented as comparandum; the inference discipline governs details).
- **Surrounding religious environment:** civic cults, mysteries, household gods, the imperial cult's pervasive presence in Asia Minor especially — the refusal-scape the world lived inside. Documented richly from the non-Christian side.

## 7. Source Asymmetry, Missing Voices, and the Affirmative Duty (Article 20)

**Whose voices the record structurally suppresses, why, and what that means:**

1. **The Torah-observant believers.** Suppressed twice: by gentile-majority polemic in-span (Ignatius, Barnabas) and by later heresiology's classification of them as sects (Ebionites, Nazoraeans). Their own account of Jesus-faithfulness inside Israel's practice does not survive in a single first-person line. Meaning: the world's deepest internal plurality is reconstructible only against the grain; every claim about them carries Contested or worse; and the encounter must say so (participant-facing obligation).
2. **The enslaved.** Present everywhere (Pliny's ministrae; Euelpistus; Blandina; Ignatius' instructions; the ordinary texture of every household) and voiced nowhere except through interrogators and hagiographers. Meaning: when this world says "brothers and sisters," the record cannot tell us how it sounded from below the master's table — an absence the Representative must carry honestly.
3. **Women.** Named at moments of crisis or as objects of instruction (Grapte teaching widows and orphans; Ammia the prophetess remembered; the ministrae tortured; Blandina; the widows as an emerging order in Polycarp/Ignatius; Celsus' sneer at women's agency as recruitment) — with the constraining trajectory (Pastorals' restrictions) documented within the span, and the scholarship on the whole arc Contested. Meaning: women's leadership is documented in fragments and contested in scope; both the fragments and the contest are carried; the Facilitation Brief must state that this world's women are heard mostly through men and enemies.
4. **The rural majority-of-the-future.** Pliny attests village and farm Christians in 112; not one rural assembly's practice is described anywhere in the span. Named as pure absence.
5. **The "heretics" as they saw themselves.** Marcion's own Antitheses, Valentinian texts in their own hands (Nag Hammadi's copies are later and their compositional dates contested), the New Prophecy's own oracles beyond hostile quotation — lost. Meaning: the world's internal argument survives as a one-sided transcript; the build renders opponents' positions from hostile evidence with that filter always named.
6. **The lapsed and the left.** Pliny's deserters of twenty years' standing are a documented mass phenomenon with zero surviving self-description. The world's back door is dark.

**Bounded reconstruction permitted under the three conditions:** against-the-grain readings used in this build are enumerated at their sites (e.g., Ignatius' anti-sabbath polemic → live sabbath practice; Didache's prophet-tests → real prophet abuses; the Pastorals' restrictions → the practices restricted; Celsus' sneer → domestic recruitment agency). Each requires a specific textual trace; none proceeds from silence alone.

## 8. Forces Lens on the Source Ecology (Forces Framework Step 2)

- **Which sources speak to external force:** Pliny/Trajan (the legal regime, definitive); Tacitus (the Neronian precedent's memory); the martyr-acts (the regime's sharp end); Revelation and 1 Peter (pressure as lived atmosphere in Asia); the apologists (the cultural indictment answered).
- **What the silences reveal:** no in-span source describes a normal year without crisis — the occasional character of the corpus means the ordinary is systematically under-documented relative to the emergency (evidential visibility vs. ecological visibility, worst case).
- **Survivorship pattern:** what the proto-orthodox stream preserved is what its third- and fourth-century heirs found usable — order (1 Clement, Ignatius), moral seriousness (Didache absorbed into later manuals, Hermas widely copied then dropped), martyr-glory (Mart. Pol., Lyons), apology (Justin). What it found threatening or embarrassing it let go: the losers' own texts, the prophets' oracles, the ordinary. The preservation pattern is itself a map of which internal forces the successors feared: disorder, impurity, false teaching.

## 9. Confidence Map (summary; five-level vocabulary throughout)

- **Documented:** existence, rough dates, and content of the named primary texts; Pliny–Trajan exchange and the accusation regime; Justin's execution; the Sunday assembly with reading, exhortation, prayer, meal, and collection (Justin, for Rome, c. 150); the fictive-kinship address; Wednesday/Friday fasting and thrice-daily Lord's Prayer (Didache's community); mutual-aid economy (triangulated in-world and outside); presence of slaves, women officeholders (ministrae), and mixed status; the Quartodeciman practice and dispute; existence and spread of Marcionite and Valentinian movements; the martyr-commemoration at Smyrna.
- **Widely Accepted:** the c. 70 ecological break; the trans-local network self-understanding; the urban mixed-status social profile; the office-settling trajectory across the span; the Two Ways as common catechetical pattern; continued Torah-observant communities.
