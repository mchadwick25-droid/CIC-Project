# Guided Starters — The Bethlehem Circle (Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity) (DRAFT V0.1)

**Status:** DRAFT — awaiting Mark's review. Not deployed.
**World:** Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity — the Bethlehem Circle, c. 382–420 CE, Rome (the Aventine Hill) and Bethlehem (the double monastery). Representative: Albina (*vidua* / ascetic widow of the household), community "we"-voice.
**Purpose:** Entry points for participants who don't know what to ask. Questions open doors; they never walk the participant through them (Encounter Over Persuasion applies to curricula).
**Grounding:** Every topic below traces to this world's approved build documents (Doc_01 World Identification, Doc_04 confirmed gravities, Doc_06 Full Lexicon + Lexicon-Chunks, Doc_07 Integrated Ecology Analysis, Doc_08 Forces Document, Doc_09a Story Inventory + Absent Stories, the World Profile, the World Capsule Core, and Albina's Permanent Prompt). Nothing is invented.

---

**Read this first — the caveat that governs the whole list.** Per `CiC_World_Coverage_Cards_V0_1.md`, starter questions are **largely role-generic**: the Representative handles per-world variation, and the live calibration proved a generic question meeting a thin record produces an honest calibrated answer rather than a failure. The premise that starters must be authored per world was cancelled. So the openers below are **illustrative routing seeds, not per-world authored gates** — they show the *kinds* of doors this world opens especially well and where its honest silences lie, to help a participant who doesn't yet know what to ask. A participant is never confined to them, and Albina will meet a question this list never anticipated on the same terms — answering, as her household always does, in the first-person plural: "we," not "I."

## First Visit (start here)

**Two labors, one discipline** — This household never treated scholarship and asceticism as separate things; a day spent testing a Hebrew word against its source and a day spent fasting were, for them, the same labor worked at from different ends. [Doc_01 §5.1 Recurring Formation Ecology; Doc_07 §4 Formation Logic; Capsule "How This World Forms People"]
*Opening question:* "What was daily life actually like for you? Was it more about study, or more about giving things up?"
Follow-ups: "Why weren't those two things separate for you?" · "Which one came first for most people who joined you?" · "Did anyone ever feel torn between the two?"

**The Hebrew truth** — At the center of everything sits one costly conviction: that the Hebrew text of scripture carries the Lord's own word nearer to how it was first given than the Greek the wider church had prayed from for generations. [Doc_04 G1 (Primary); hal_lex01 Hebraica veritas; Capsule "What Organizes Everything"]
*Opening question:* "Why did it matter so much to you whether a translation came from Hebrew or from Greek?"
Follow-ups: "Did you ever worry you were tampering with something people already trusted?" · "What did you lose by insisting on the Hebrew?" · "Did the Greek text get anything right that the Hebrew didn't?"

**Giving it all away** — Renunciation here was never quiet or private; it was public, family-disrupting, and paid for in front of a whole watching city. [Doc_04 G2 (Primary); hal_lex03 Renuntiatio; hal_story06]
*Opening question:* "You gave up wealth, family, everything — what actually made you do that?"
Follow-ups: "What did your own family say when you did it?" · "Did you ever regret it?" · "Was it as total as it sounds, or did some of you keep more than you let on?"

**The letter as a life, not a record of one** — Rome and Bethlehem never shared a roof, so nearly everything this household was — argument, grief, spiritual direction — happened on the page, carried across real distance by letter. [Doc_04 G4 (Supporting); hal_lex07 Epistula; Doc_08 force 2B-2]
*Opening question:* "How did you stay one household when you were split between two cities?"
Follow-ups: "What did a letter from you actually contain — news, or something more?" · "Did letters ever arrive too late to matter?" · "Who wrote to whom the most?"

**Trust, not an office** — No bishop's seat organized this household; authority ran instead through demonstrated learning and freely-given wealth, trusted and re-trusted rather than simply conferred. [Doc_04 G3 (Primary, strongest bipolar-holding candidate); hal_lex06 Patrocinium; Doc_01 §3.3]
*Opening question:* "If there was no bishop over you, who was actually in charge?"
Follow-ups: "How did someone earn that kind of trust?" · "Could it be lost?" · "Did that ever cause confusion about who really spoke for you?"

**Leaving Rome for Bethlehem** — A household of Roman scholars and aristocratic women left the city they knew, crossed through Cyprus and Nitria's monks, and settled at last by the cave where the Lord was born. [hal_story01; Doc_01 §1.1, §2]
*Opening question:* "Why did you actually leave Rome? What were you looking for at Bethlehem?"
Follow-ups: "What was the hardest part of that journey?" · "Did you consider staying in Egypt among the monks there instead?" · "Did you ever miss Rome?"

## Going Deeper

**The word that unsettled a congregation** — A single translation choice — "ivy" instead of "gourd" in Jonah — was small on the page and enormous in the pew, reaching all the way to a respected bishop in Africa who would not simply concede the point. [hal_story03; hal_lex01 Distortion Risk; Doc_08 force 2A-2]
*Opening question:* "Can you give me one real example of what this Hebrew-versus-Greek argument actually cost you?"
Follow-ups: "What happened when a congregation heard a familiar word changed?" · "What did Augustine say to you about it?" · "Did you ever back down on a word, once the argument got heated?"

**The widow clergy came to** — Among this household, one voice — a widow who never left Rome and never needed anyone's patronage — was trusted by visiting clergy with the same hard scriptural questions the traveling scholar once answered. [Doc_04 G5(Marcella), Tensional; hal_lex11 Exegesis-as-practiced-authority; hal_story07]
*Opening question:* "Was there a woman among you who was actually trusted as a scriptural authority in her own right?"
Follow-ups: "How was her standing different from the traveling scholar's?" · "Did anyone question whether a woman could hold that kind of trust?" · "Why didn't her example become the pattern for the rest of you?"

**Two ways of vowing a life** — Consecrated virginity and ascetic widowhood were not the same vocation here, and this household never blurred the difference between a life that never began one way and a life turned decisively away from another. [hal_lex04 Virginitas; hal_lex05 Vidua]
*Opening question:* "What's the difference between the virgins among you and the widows?"
Follow-ups: "Which one carried more weight in your household?" · "Could a widow choose virginity's path instead, or was that closed to her?" · "Was the choice ever actually free, or was it expected?"

**Wealth enough to do this at all** — Every renunciation this household made — every hostel and hospital and translation — depended on a specific kind of Roman aristocratic standing most people never had to begin with. [hal_lex10 Matrona; Doc_07 §5 Material Culture]
*Opening question:* "Could an ordinary person have lived the way you did, or did it take real money?"
Follow-ups: "Where did that wealth actually come from?" · "Did you ever think about what your renunciation cost people who had nothing to renounce?" · "Was there ever tension between you and less wealthy Christians?"

**Arguing in the open, book by book** — Nearly every book this household's scholar translated arrived with its own combative preface — defending the rendering, naming the objector, arguing the case in public before anyone else could raise it. [hal_lex13 Praefatio; Doc_07 §1 Intellectual and Conceptual Structures]
*Opening question:* "Why did you feel you had to defend every single translation choice in writing?"
Follow-ups: "Did you ever regret how sharply one of those prefaces was written?" · "Who read these — just scholars, or ordinary believers too?" · "What happened when someone pushed back on a preface?"

**How a book actually got translated** — Consultation with a Hebrew teacher, rendering the word against both the Greek and the Hebrew, then a defensive preface naming the dedicatee and anticipating the objection — the whole shape of the labor, from question to finished page. [hal_story11]
*Opening question:* "Walk me through it — how did a translation actually get made, step by step?"
Follow-ups: "How long did one book usually take?" · "What happened when the Hebrew teacher and the Greek text disagreed?" · "Who decided which book got translated next?"

**An ordinary day at the double monastery** — Prayer in common, Hebrew study under teachers who did not share the household's own faith, manual labor, and the correspondence that kept Rome and Bethlehem one project — the typical shape of a day, not any single remembered one. [hal_story10; Doc_01 §5.3]
*Opening question:* "What did a normal day actually look like for you at Bethlehem?"
Follow-ups: "How much of the day was prayer, and how much was study?" · "Did the men's and women's communities ever share the same space?" · "Was there ever a day with nothing scheduled at all?"

**Favor that could vanish in a season** — When the pope who had protected the scholar in Rome died, and rumor turned against him, he had no office to fall back on — only reputation, and a patron's wealth, which held even when a protector's favor did not. [hal_story02; Doc_08 force 2A-4; Doc_04 §5 G3 "shifted in form"]
*Opening question:* "You said your whole authority rested on trust, not office. What happened when that trust broke down?"
Follow-ups: "Did that actually happen to one of you?" · "What did you have to do to rebuild it?" · "Did it change how carefully you guarded your own reputation afterward?"

## For the Wrestling (hard questions welcome here)

**The friend who became the fiercest opponent** — A former friend, once close enough to translate the same Greek texts side by side, became the harshest critic this household ever faced, once a shared theological inheritance had to be urgently and publicly renounced. [hal_story03a; hal_lex08 Origenism, CT-tagged; Doc_08 force 2B-1]
*Opening question:* "You had a close friend who turned into your worst enemy over theology. What actually happened there?"
Follow-ups: "Was it really about doctrine, or was it personal by then?" · "Do you regret how that friendship ended?" · "Could two people who once agreed on everything really end up that far apart?"

**Violence at the door** — An argument that had stayed on paper for years — over grace, and whether a person could live without sin unaided — arrived one year as a mob, burning buildings, and, by report, killing one of the household's own. [hal_story04; hal_lex09 Pelagianism; Doc_08 force 3A-2]
*Opening question:* "Did your theological arguments ever turn violent?"
Follow-ups: "Were you afraid, living with that kind of danger?" · "Did the violence change what you were willing to say publicly?" · "Who was actually responsible — the mob, or the argument that provoked it?"

**Only one man's hand** — Everything anyone knows of these women's own reasoning and conviction survives only because one man chose to write it down, after they could no longer speak for themselves. A participant can fairly press whether that's really their story at all, or his. [Doc_01 §0; Doc_09a Absent Story #4; hal_lex11 Reported-Experience Status]
*Opening question:* "If everything about these women comes from one man's letters, how do I know I'm hearing them and not just him?"
Follow-ups: "Did he ever get one of them wrong, that you know of?" · "Would they have told their own story differently?" · "Why should I trust his account of women he clearly admired — isn't that exactly when a writer flatters?"

**Was the giving-up as total as you claim?** — This household's own record cannot fully settle whether its women's self-impoverishment was as complete as the letters describe, or whether a household that could still fund a monastery, a hostel, and a hospital never actually gave up as much as it says. [hal_lex03 Renuntiatio, Key Sources note; hal_story06; Doc_02 §1.2, §6]
*Opening question:* "You say you gave up everything, but you were still funding hospitals and monasteries. Which is it?"
Follow-ups: "Isn't 'renouncing wealth' easier when you still control what's left of it?" · "Did anyone among you ever call this out as inconsistent?" · "What would it have actually looked like to give up *everything*?"

**Wasn't this all just for the educated rich?** — This household's core practice — Hebrew study, textual correction, sustained correspondence — was structurally closed to anyone without wealth and literacy, and this world's own record never named that as a boundary because, from inside, it never had to. A participant can fairly press whether that silence is itself telling. [Doc_07 §3 Boundary Structures; Doc_01 §8.2]
*Opening question:* "Be honest — wasn't this whole way of life only available to the rich and educated?"
Follow-ups: "What about someone who couldn't read?" · "Did you ever notice that yourselves, or did it take an outsider to point it out?" · "Is a life like this really for everyone, or just for people like you?"

## Questions This World Answers Honestly With Its Limits

*These are good questions to ask precisely because this world will not pretend to more than its own record holds. Its own build documents (the World Profile's Honest Limits; Doc_09a's Absent Stories) name where the record runs thin, and Albina is built to say so plainly and turn back to what she can speak of well.*

**"What did your daily prayer actually sound like — the hours, the psalms?"** — This world can attest the bare fact of a structured common life at Bethlehem, but not its timetable: no source gives the specific hours or psalms used, only the general fact of a discipline. Albina will say the shape of the life, not the schedule of a day. [World Profile §8; Doc_01 §9 Open Issue #5; hal_story10 Absent Story Note]

**"What did Paula, Eustochium, or Marcella actually say, in their own words?"** — No text composed independently by any of these three women survives at all. Every account of their reasoning and courage reaches a participant through Jerome's hand alone — a structural fact this world's own record names plainly rather than filling with an invented voice. [Doc_01 §0; Doc_09a Absent Story #4; World Profile §8]

**"What about the ordinary monks and household servants who lived alongside you — what were their lives like?"** — The "countless multitudes" at Nitria and the unnamed members of Bethlehem's own communities are never individuated in any surviving source. This world names that silence rather than inventing a name or a story to fill it. [Doc_09a Absent Stories #1, #5; World Profile §8]

**"Did your family ever forgive you for leaving?"** — Family resistance to renunciation is attested only through Jerome's own account of the grief it caused — never in the resisting relative's own words. This world cannot tell that story from the inside, and does not attempt to. [Doc_01 §6; Doc_09a Absent Story #2]

**"Was Jerome actually fluent in Hebrew, the way he claimed?"** — This is genuinely contested in the scholarship this build draws on: some readings treat his self-presentation as a later, more polished construction than his own earlier, more modest claims support. This world will not claim a fluency more complete than the record can bear out. [Doc_01 §3.1, §9 Open Issue #2; hal_lex12 Grammaticus Distortion Risk]

**"Is this your tradition today? Is there a church I could visit that still lives this out?"** — No. This household's own institutional life ended with the deaths of everyone who built it, within a few years of one another; no successor to its central transmission mechanism is named anywhere in its own record, and this world's construction actively considered and rejected two candidate living-tradition links before concluding there is none. [World Profile §9; Doc_08 force 3B-2; Doc_09c §12]
