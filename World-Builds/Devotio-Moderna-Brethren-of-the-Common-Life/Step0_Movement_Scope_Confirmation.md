## 1. Candidate identification

Atlas V.3, "Devotio Moderna / Brethren of the Common Life," c. 1380–1517, Low Countries. Geert Groote gave up a wealthy career to gather devout men and women into households that lived in common without ever taking vows, supporting themselves by copying manuscripts. Their practice of keeping a personal notebook of gathered readings (a *rapiarium*) produced Thomas à Kempis's *Imitation of Christ*, the most copied Christian book after the Bible. Their schools shaped Erasmus (educated at Deventer) and touched a year of Luther's own schooling (Magdeburg), and the same disciplined, interior devotion fed into Jesuit spiritual formation a century later. The houses themselves mostly dissolved during the Reformation; the *Imitation* never stopped circulating.

## 2. Section A — Movement-Scope Eligibility

**Framing note**, as for every candidate in this project's Step 0 documents: this document's "clears" are a considered signal, not a self-administered disposition.

### A1 — The floor: clears, on positive textual evidence, with a correction to the movement's own relationship to authority

Quoted directly: floorNote "No question." *The Imitation of Christ* and Kempis's *Founders of the New Devotion* raise no Trinitarian or Christological complication. Positive evidence, not merely absence: Groote's own *Publica protestatio* (Ch. XVIII of *Founders*, in his own first-person voice) is a direct profession of faith — "in regard to those things that are of faith, I have..." — offered specifically to answer the movement against unorthodoxy; this is affirmative floor evidence, not simply a clean read finding nothing.

**Correction (Revision 2):** Revision 1 said the movement "never challenged church authority at all" and framed this as *why* no floor complication was found — a non sequitur (Article 4 concerns Trinitarian/Christological commitments, not ecclesiastical authority) and factually wrong besides. Groote's own *Founders* records "many prelates of the Church were opposed to him... he was forbidden to preach by an edict craftily obtained" — Ch. XVIII is itself a defensive profession of faith written against accusations of unorthodoxy, and an appendix letter pleads for his suspended preaching license to be restored. The census confirms this: Groote was "forbidden to preach the year before for attacking the clergy's concubines." The movement submitted to church authority rather than breaking from it — a real distinction from Lollardy and the Hussites — but its relationship to that authority was genuinely contested, not absent of friction.

**Correction (Revision 2), overstated reading claim:** "both vendored and read this pass" overstates what the file headers themselves disclose — sampling ("the opening pages and a mid-document sample"), not a full reading, across roughly 1 million combined characters. Directly relevant passages (Groote's Protestatio, Imitation Book IV) went uncited in Revision 1 despite being available. This pass also checked Groote and Kempis only — the movement's own women's side (the Sisters' houses at Deventer and Diepenveen, named as its own voice by the census) was not checked at all, and has zero vendored representation (see §3 B1).

## 3. Section B — Seed Screening and Tiering

**Framing note**, as for every candidate in this project's Step 0 documents: Tier below is a considered signal, not a self-administered disposition.

### B1 — Sourcing: a genuine three-pillar base, with several corrections to what Revision 1 claimed about it

Per the dossier, directly host-verified: *The Imitation of Christ* (Benham, 1886), Kempis's own *Founders of the New Devotion* — his account of the movement's founder Gerard Groote and successor Florentius Radewin (Arthur, 1905), and an edition of Groote's own letters, Latin original (Acquoy, 1857).

**Correction (Revision 2), the letter count.** Revision 1 called this "Groote's own fourteen surviving letters." Acquoy's own introduction (c. line 891) describes the source codex as containing 66 letters, 64 attributable to Groote — Acquoy's own edition covers 14 of them. "Epistolae XIV" names the scope of this particular edition, not the number of letters that survive; the vendored file, with its 14 EPISTOLA headings, is complete as vendored, but the document's own characterization of total survival was wrong.

**Correction (Revision 2), Groote's own voice is not Latin-only.** Revision 1 framed the missing PD English translation of Groote's letters as the one real gap, with his voice available "only in Latin." This is wrong — the vendored *Founders* volume already contains his own writing in public-domain English: his *Publica protestatio* (Ch. XVIII, first person), his "Resolutions and Intentions... not confirmed by vows" (the census's own named self-rule text), excerpted letters, and an appendix letter to the Bishop of Utrecht. Acquoy's own introduction confirms these were originally printed in Kempis's Vita. This improves the actual sourcing picture; it means *Founders* was not read closely enough at Revision 1 to catch it.

**Flagged for Mark directly — a methodology question, not something this revision resolves on its own:** Revision 1 also filed the Groote Latin letters as "PRIMARY content rather than a second witness," citing the Jesuit Constitutions and Nadal's *Adnotationes* as precedent. Independent review found this conflicts with this project's own written rule — `cic/texts/INTAKE.md` states original-language files are "second witnesses — never primary evidence for a Representative... this project's evidence language is English," citable where no English rendering exists (as here) but still carrying that label; LPC's own build documents apply "second witness, second limb" consistently to comparable Latin-only material. The "PRIMARY" framing for the Jesuit precedent this document leaned on exists only in informal REGISTRY/dossier notes, not in any actual ruling — and per §3 B1's own correction above, it may not even be needed here, since Groote's own English voice already exists in *Founders*. **This document does not change the corpus-map's own "PRIMARY" label unilaterally — that would be a quiet methodology change, which CLAUDE.md reserves for the project lead's own decision. Flagged here for Mark to rule on** (apply the written second-witness rule as stated, or treat this as a deliberate, disclosed exception) rather than resolved by this revision.

**Correction (Revision 2), the Imitation's own authorship is disputed in its own front matter.** Revision 1 treated Kempis's authorship as settled. The vendored Benham preface itself (c. lines 490–640) calls Kempis's authorship "the erroneous notion that he was its author" and argues instead for a 13th-c. "Abbot John Gersen." Kempis authorship remains the mainstream modern view, but this world's own central vendored edition disputes the attribution in its own introduction — worth a `formation_confidence` tag at Doc_02, not silence.

**Correction (Revision 2), the "documented" influence claims need separate confidence tags.** The Luther/Erasmus schooling connection (§1, §3 B3) is contested in scholarship — R. R. Post's 1968 study rebutted Hyma's earlier thesis, arguing the Brethren mostly ran student hostels rather than schools and that the Reformation link is weak; the census's own wording is already careful ("a household connected with the Brethren"), and "documented" overstates it. The Jesuit direction has a real vendored anchor Revision 1 missed: Xavier's *Letters* Vol. 1 (lines ~3509, 4535) records the early companions reading the Imitation and Ignatius distributing *De Contemptu Mundi*. Same methodological note as every sibling document: figures below are raw word counts from vendored .txt, not extracted from XML markup.

The interior, devotional register this Era 6 batch otherwise lacks.
