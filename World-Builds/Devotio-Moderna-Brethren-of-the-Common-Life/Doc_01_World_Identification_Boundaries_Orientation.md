## 1. Formation World Identity

**World Name:** Devotio Moderna / Brethren of the Common Life.

**Short Description:** A lay-centered spiritual reform movement of the Low Countries, c. 1374–1517, organized around households of devout men and women who lived a common life of prayer, mutual correction, and manual work — chiefly manuscript copying — without taking religious vows, alongside a formally professed canons-and-canonesses-regular wing that grew out of the same founding circle.

**Core Identity:** This is a world of households, not an institution or a single rule of life: laypeople and secular clergy gathered under a house Rector or Mistress, supporting themselves by copying manuscripts and other ordinary work, bound by custom and mutual correction rather than by canon law or profession (Step 0 §1; census `longDescription`). Its formative population is deliberately unglamorous — Deventer and Zwolle townspeople, schoolboys boarded for a Latin education, copyists, a few former university men (Groote himself resigned two prebends) — not a scholarly elite and not a persecuted minority. Its formation logic is interior and methodical before it is doctrinal or institutional: the *rapiarium* (a personal notebook of gathered readings, returned to and reworked in prayer), graded interior "ascent" (Zerbolt of Zutphen's method text, vendored as *The Spiritual Ascent*), and communal correction are this world's characteristic formative practices, in a way shared by none of the strictly monastic or juridical worlds already in this project's portfolio. Running alongside these unvowed households, and drawing recruits from them, is a second wing: canons and canonesses regular under the Rule of St. Augustine at Windesheim and its affiliated houses (including the women's house at Diepenveen), formally professed and monastically enclosed — Section 4 below gives this its own Strand Determination treatment rather than assuming either that it is the same thing as the unvowed households or that it is a separate world.

**Distinctive Contribution:** Against this same Era 6 batch's other two candidates — Lollardy and the Hussite and Bohemian Brethren Movement — this world shares no figure or text (Step 0 §3 B3) and offers the batch's one interior-devotional register: Lollardy and the Hussite movement are, at root, protest movements against ecclesiastical authority, while this movement submitted to that authority under real pressure (Groote's own suspended preaching license) rather than breaking from it (Step 0 §2 A1, §3 B4) — a genuine distinction in kind, though not an absence of friction. Its sharper, more consequential distinctiveness is the relationship the census's own `relationsSummary` names directly: **a documented both-directions ancestor relationship** — this world's disciplined, interior devotional formation is claimed as a parent-influence on both Luther's schooling and Jesuit spiritual formation, in two different confessional directions from the same source. The Jesuit direction has a real vendored anchor, outside this world's own corpus: Coleridge's 1872 *Life and Letters of St. Francis Xavier* records the early Jesuit companions' spiritual reading including "the Imitation of Christ," and a footnote there (citing Alcazar) records Ignatius giving away copies of it (Step 0 §3 B1, B3). The Society of Jesus is itself a source-ready candidate, not yet built — no world folder for it exists in this portfolio as of this drafting — so this relationship is presently one-directional in what can be checked: this document names it; the Jesuit world's own future Doc_01/Doc_02, whenever built, is where it would be named from that side. The Luther/Erasmus schooling direction is claimed by the census but **contested in the wider scholarship** — R. R. Post's 1968 study argues the Brethren mostly ran student hostels rather than schools and that the Reformation-link thesis (Hyma's) is weak (Step 0 §3 B1) — so "documented ancestor" is too strong for that direction specifically; "claimed by the census, contested in the literature" is the accurate framing, carried forward rather than upgraded here. **Lutheran Wittenberg is a Built & Live world in this portfolio** (`worlds/witt`); as of this drafting, neither Wittenberg's own corpus-map entry nor this world's names the other, and no shared vendored text links them — a real, disclosed gap this document does not attempt to close on its own (Step 0 §3 B3, §5). Beyond the Era 6 batch and these two influence relationships, this world's own recurring formation ecology — see Section 3 below — occupies medieval Low Countries lay-communal space that no already-built world in this portfolio reconstructs; the clearest already-built analogue worth naming for contrast rather than overlap is Desert Monasticism (`worlds/desert`, c. 320s onward, Egypt) — a communal ascetic-withdrawal world separated from this one by thirteen centuries, a different continent, and, most importantly, a vow structure: desert monasticism is built on renunciatory withdrawal and (for its Pachomian wing) formal cenobitic rule from the outset, where this world's most distinctive wing is lay households that explicitly refuse vows while remaining in ordinary town life (Section 3 below).

**Living Tradition Status:** Not confirmed. Per Constitution Article 29 and this project's established practice, Living Tradition Status confirmation is a project-lead act this document cannot perform on its own behalf. Noted for the record, not decided here: this world does not present the same kind of single, continuously governing institutional heir that (for example) the Roman episcopate or the Coptic Orthodox Church give other worlds in this portfolio — its own houses "did not survive the century they helped bring about; most dissolved during the Reformation" (census `legacy`; Step 0 §3 B2 names this same institutional-decline gap as undocumented in the vendored corpus). What does continue is textual and formational rather than institutional: *The Imitation of Christ* "never stopped circulating" (Step 0 §1; census `legacy`) and remains in active devotional use across multiple living Christian traditions, Catholic and Protestant alike, without any one of them being this movement's institutional continuation. Whether that textual/formational continuity is the kind of thing Article 29 confirmation is meant to govern, and if so which living tradition(s) it should be checked against, is a question for the project lead, not a finding this document makes. **Status: PENDING.**

## 2. Historical Boundaries

**Time Horizon: c. 1374–1517.**

- **Beginning point.** The census's own window gives "c. 1380" as a round-number label, and Step 0 §2 A3 already established that 1380 is not itself a founding date. The defensible beginning point is **20 September 1374** — the day Geert Groote executed the deed of gift turning the house his parents had left him on the Bagijnenstraat in Deventer into a dwelling for "poor persons who wish to serve God," under the oversight of the city magistrates rather than any religious order, with residents lay, taking no vows, wearing no habit, and free to leave (census `documentedStories`, "Geert Grote Gives His House Away"; this specific deed text is drawn from Van Engen's *Basic Writings*, a source the vendored corpus does not currently hold — Step 0 §4 item 5 — so this date is carried at **Documented** confidence for the date itself, on the strength of the census's own dated-document citation, while the deed's own text is not independently checked against a vendored file here). This is a genuine ecological rupture, not an arbitrary date: before it, no household anywhere lived this movement's own characteristic form (common life, self-support through manuscript work, deliberate non-monastic status) at all; after it, that form existed and began to be copied. It sits only six years inside the census's own "c. 1380" label, which this document treats as a rounding of the same underlying event, not a conflicting date requiring reconciliation. **Confidence: Documented** for the date and the deed's substance (per the census's own citation); the surrounding conversion narrative — Groote's illness, his turn from a career including "astrology and magic" (vendored, Founders, c. line 955), and the Carthusian influence at Monnikhuizen — is itself Kempis's own devotional biography (vendored, *Founders*, Chs. III–VI) rather than an independent contemporary record, so it is carried at **Widely Accepted** rather than Documented.
- **Ending point.** 1517. Defensible on the same "real ecological event, not a round number" test the beginning point applies: the census itself states the window "ends in 1517, not the round number 1500, since its houses ran into the sixteenth century and dissolved under the Reformation" (census `statusDescription`). 1517 is the conventional onset of the Reformation, and it is inside, not before, the unraveling this world's own houses underwent — the census's own `legacy` field states plainly that "the houses themselves did not survive the century they helped bring about; most dissolved during the Reformation," a process that continued for decades after 1517 rather than concluding on that date. Ending the window there keeps the boundary anchored to the real hinge event (the onset of the Reformation that dissolved these houses) rather than reaching for either the too-early round number 1500 or a later date tied to any one house's own final closure, which the vendored corpus does not document house-by-house (Step 0 §3 B2). **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for 1517 as the Reformation's conventional onset; **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for treating it as this world's own defensible closing hinge given the century-long dissolution it opens.

**Geographic Centers:** The Low Countries, above all the towns of the IJssel valley — **Deventer** (Groote's own city, site of Master Geert's House and, later, one of the region's leading Latin schools), **Zwolle** (site of the Zwolle Latin school and, nearby, Mount St. Agnes, Kempis's own monastery), and **Windesheim** itself, described in the vendored *Founders* introduction as "a desolate place between Zwolle and Deventer" chosen as the site of the movement's first canons-regular house. **Diepenveen**, near Deventer, is the corresponding center for the women's canonesses-regular branch: the vendored *Chronicle of Mount St. Agnes* records that in 1408, on the Feast of St. Agnes, forty-three women "of the Order of Canons Regular" were invested there under the Rule of St. Augustine, "many of these Sisters... gathered and brought from Deventer from the house of Master Gerard Groote" (Kempis, *Chronicle of Mount St. Agnes*, directly vendored). The movement's own reach extended beyond this core: the vendored *Founders* records communities spreading "to the more remote regions of Flanders, Frisia, Westphalia and Saxony" after Groote's death, and this document's own §1 above places Luther's Brethren-linked household at Magdeburg, in Saxony. **Confidence: Documented** for Deventer, Zwolle, Windesheim, and Diepenveen as centers; **Documented** for the Westphalia/Saxony spread, on direct vendored textual evidence.

**Cultural Environment:** Urban, vernacular-adjacent lay piety in the Latin-liturgical Low Countries — a milieu of merchant and artisan townspeople, schoolboys boarded for a Latin education, and secular clergy, rather than a court, a university theology faculty, or a monastery in the older sense. Manuscript copying was not incidental labor but this world's own characteristic form of self-support and, per its own devotional logic, a spiritual discipline in its own right (rapiarium-keeping and manuscript work share the same underlying practice of slow, repeated engagement with a written text). The movement operated in a bilingual devotional culture — Latin for its formal method texts and (per Step 0 §3 B1) its founder's own letters, Middle Dutch for much of the women's houses' own record-keeping, which the vendored corpus does not currently hold (Step 0 §3 B2, §4 item 5). **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**

**Historical Pressures:**
- **The Western Schism (1378–1417).** The vendored *Founders* introduction narrates the schism directly and at length — the double papal election after Gregory XI's death, the Avignon/Roman split, Urban VI's Italian line, the continuing "strife" after "Alexander died in 1410" — placing this movement's entire founding and early-growth period inside a Western church whose own highest authority was genuinely, visibly divided. **Confidence: Documented** (directly vendored narrative).
- **Institutional and clerical laxity.** Groote's own preaching campaign targeted clerical concubinage directly — the vendored *Founders* preserves a friend's letter pleading to the Bishop of Utrecht for the restoration of Groote's suspended preaching license, naming "Clerks who live in concubinage" as among those who benefited from his silencing (vendored, *Founders*, c. line 5140). The census corroborates: Groote was "forbidden to preach the year before [his death] for attacking the clergy's concubines." **Confidence: Documented.**
- **Recurring plague.** The vendored *Founders* introduction discusses plague mortality directly ("five millions died in France alone during the first plague"), and Groote himself died of plague in 1384 (census `documentedStories`). Plague recurrence is part of this world's own lived environment throughout its window, not only at its founding. **Confidence: Documented** for plague as a recurring pressure; **Widely Accepted** that Groote's own death was plague-caused (census attribution; not independently checked here against a vendored primary account of his death, which gives the date and his age but not the cause).
- **Mendicant friction and the pressure toward institutional legitimacy.** The vendored *Founders* introduction records the deliberate reasoning behind founding the Windesheim canons-regular house: adopting an established Rule (Augustine's) so as "to overcome or avoid the opposition of the Mendicants, who would certainly do their utmost to crush an entirely new society." An unrecognized new form of religious life was a real institutional vulnerability this movement had to manage, not a background detail. **Confidence: Documented** (directly vendored).

**Historical Catalysts** (dated, so far as the evidence allows):
- **1372/1374** — Groote's own conversion, following an illness and the influence of a Carthusian friend (census `documentedStories`; corroborated in outline by Kempis's own *Founders*, Chs. III–VI, though the exact year is debated within a two-year span per the census's own caveat).
- **20 September 1374** — Groote's deed of gift for the house on the Bagijnenstraat, Deventer: the movement's first house, for women, without vows (census `documentedStories`; see Beginning point above).
- **1383** — Groote forbidden to preach, "for attacking the clergy's concubines" (census; corroborated by the vendored friend's letter to the Bishop of Utrecht, above).
- **20 August 1384** — Groote's death, "in the forty-fourth year of his age," during the reign of Urban VI and the episcopate of Florentius de Wevelichoven, Bishop of Utrecht (directly vendored, Kempis, *Founders*, Ch. XVI, "Of his happy death and burial" — the vendored text gives the date as the feast of St. Bernard following the Assumption and "the thirteen hundred and eighty-fourth year"; the census gives his age as forty-three at death, a minor discrepancy this document does not attempt to resolve).
- **1386** — Mount St. Agnes, near Zwolle, founded "a year and a half after the death of the aforesaid Master Gerard" (directly vendored, Kempis, *Chronicle of Mount St. Agnes*, Ch. II). The Windesheim house itself was built under Florentius Radewijns's direction in this same period, shortly after Groote's death and Florentius's naming as his successor (vendored, *Founders* introduction); this document does not assert a more precise year for Windesheim's own founding than "the mid-1380s," since none is independently verified here.
- **1408** — First investiture of the Sisters of the Order of Canons Regular at Diepenveen, near Deventer, under the Rule of St. Augustine — forty-three women, several transferred from Master Geert's House at Deventer (directly vendored, Kempis, *Chronicle of Mount St. Agnes*, Ch. XIV).
- **Progressive spread** to Flanders, Frisia, Westphalia, and Saxony after Groote's death (directly vendored, *Founders*).
- **Early-to-mid 16th century** — Erasmus's education at Deventer and Luther's schooling connection at Magdeburg both fall within this window's later reach (census `legacy`; the Luther connection contested per Step 0 §3 B1, above).
- **1517** — Conventional Reformation onset; this world's own closing hinge (see Ending point above).

## 3. Distinct World Criteria

Applying the Framework's four questions directly:

- **What recurring formation ecology exists?** A household-centered ecology of common life without (for one wing) or with (for the other) formal vows, organized around manual work — chiefly manuscript copying — as shared support and spiritual discipline; the *rapiarium* practice (a personal notebook of gathered devotional readings, kept and reworked over years) as the movement's characteristic interior-formation tool, directly attested as the practice out of which the Imitation of Christ itself grew (Step 0 §1; census); a graded, methodical "ascent" toward interior devotion, set out as an explicit method in Zerbolt of Zutphen's vendored *Spiritual Ascent*; mutual correction and communal accountability within each house under a Rector or Mistress; and attached schools for boys, which is how this world's own formation reached "far more people than ever joined it" (census `longDescription`). This recurs across the whole documented span — Groote's own founding households, the Windesheim/Diepenveen professed houses, and the movement's spread into Westphalia and Saxony all show the same basic formative cluster, not a feature confined to one phase or one house.
- **What recurring gravities exist?** **Preliminary only — full gravity analysis is explicitly deferred to Doc_04, per Forces Framework V1.1 §4 (Step 4 is where confirmed gravities are tested, not Step 1).** Candidates worth naming for Doc_02's scoping, not confirmed here: interior, methodical devotion prioritized over scholastic or purely institutional religious formation; communal mutual correction as a substitute for (or supplement to) canonical discipline; manuscript labor as simultaneously economic self-support and spiritual practice; and — a candidate specifically flagged by the Strand Determination below — a shared commitment to disciplined interior formation that appears to hold across both this world's vowed and unvowed wings despite their differing authority structures. None of these is asserted as a confirmed gravity by this document.
- **What recurring patterns of worship, formation, interpretation, belonging, and authority exist?** *Worship*: canonical hours, documented directly in the vendored *Chronicle* for the professed houses ("the Canonical Hours were sung in our church after the monastic manner"); the unvowed households' own worship practice is less directly attested in what's vendored and is a matter for Doc_02/Doc_05, not asserted here. *Formation*: the rapiarium and graded-ascent practices above, recurring across both wings. *Interpretation*: devotional and practical rather than exegetical-scholarly — this movement's own characteristic mode is excerpting and returning to short passages for spiritual use (the rapiarium), not producing biblical commentary or systematic theology; it is a sharp contrast with a world like Alexandria's learned catechetical tradition (`worlds/alx`), and this document names that contrast explicitly rather than leaving interpretation unaddressed. *Belonging*: household membership, dissolvable and vow-free for the Common Life households proper, formally professed and enclosed for the Windesheim/Diepenveen wing (Section 4 below). *Authority*: a Rector or Mistress within each house; for the professed wing, a General Chapter across the Windesheim congregation, directly attested in the vendored *Chronicle* ("their General Chapter was held at Windesem"); for the unvowed wing, custom and mutual correction rather than a canonical superior, per Groote's own "Resolutions and Intentions... not confirmed by vows" (directly vendored, *Founders*).
- **What distinguishes this world from adjacent worlds?** From this same Era 6 batch's Lollardy and the Hussite and Bohemian Brethren Movement: no figure or text overlap, and a difference in kind in relationship to church authority — submission under real pressure rather than open breach (Step 0 §2 A1, §3 B4). From Desert Monasticism (`worlds/desert`, already built): different era (this world's 1374 start is roughly thirteen centuries after Desert Monasticism's c. 320s beginning), different region, and — most importantly — a different relationship to vows: Desert Monasticism is built on renunciatory withdrawal and, for its Pachomian wing, formal cenobitic rule from its own outset, where this world's most historically distinctive contribution is a wing that explicitly refuses vows while remaining inside ordinary town life. From the two worlds this movement is itself claimed as an ancestor of: Lutheran Wittenberg (`worlds/witt`, Built & Live) and the Society of Jesus (source-ready, not yet built) — see the Distinctive Contribution discussion in Section 1 above; this document does not re-argue that relationship here, only flags that no corpus-map cross-reference or shared vendored text currently represents it from any of the three sides (Step 0 §3 B3, §4 item 4).

## 4. Strand Determination (Constitution Article 21)

Per Article 21, strand is "a finding, never a presupposed universal schema," accountable to evidence, and never assigned "to satisfy an architectural preference for plurality." This determination is made here, for the first time — Step 0 correctly left it to Step 1 (Step 0 §2 A5: "not applicable in the developmental-tradition sense... the real relationships... are documented-influence questions, not developmental-tradition ones," which is a different question from strand-hood).

**The question this section must not confuse itself about.** Windesheim's canons-regular wing grew out of the same founding circle as the unvowed Common Life households, and grew later — Groote's own household (1374) predates Florentius's building of Windesheim (mid-1380s) by roughly a decade. That sequence, on its own, is not evidence of a strand: it would be sequential-development reasoning of exactly the kind Article 21 warns against, treating "happened later" as though it meant "is therefore a separate stream." What this section asks instead is whether the evidence shows a genuinely **recurring, simultaneous** divergence in authority structure that persists across the remainder of this world's own window, not merely a one-time developmental step.

**Finding: two strands identified, on recurring-ecology evidence, with an explicit finding that gender is not itself a separate strand axis.**

- **Strand A — Common Life (unvowed).** Households of laypeople and secular clergy living communally, self-supporting through manuscript copying and other work, explicitly without vows. Evidenced directly: Groote's own founding deed specifies residents "were to be lay, take no vows, wear no habit, and be free to leave" (census `documentedStories`, citing Van Engen's edition of the deed); Groote's own personal rule of life, vendored in full, is headed "Resolutions and Intentions set forth by Master Gerard in the name of the Lord, **but not confirmed by vows**" (*Founders*, directly vendored) — a first-person statement of the same principle from the founder himself, not merely a later summary of it.
- **Strand B — Windesheim Canons and Canonesses Regular.** Formally professed religious life under the Rule of St. Augustine, at Windesheim (men) and its affiliated houses including Diepenveen (women), with monastic habit, perpetual vows, and General Chapter governance. Evidenced directly: the vendored *Founders* introduction describes the habit distinction ("dark grey cloth, and when they became Canons Regular they wore a white rochet with a black hood") and records that the Rule adopted was summarized from an established source (Kettlewell's summary of the adopted Rule, reproduced in the same introduction); the vendored *Chronicle of Mount St. Agnes* records the 1408 Diepenveen investiture in full — "the Order of Canons Regular... the habit and the order that followeth the rule of the Blessed Augustine... This investiture, with indelible and perpetual vows" — and records a General Chapter convened at Windesheim.
- **These two strands are evidenced as recurring and simultaneous, not merely sequential.** Both operated concurrently for the greater part of this world's own window: Master Geert's House at Deventer (Strand A) continued operating for at least two decades after Windesheim's founding, and was still the source of new members recruited into Diepenveen (Strand B) as late as 1408 — the vendored *Chronicle* records that "many of these Sisters were gathered and brought from Deventer from the house of Master Gerard Groote" at the very moment of Diepenveen's own founding investiture. This is evidence of two authority structures coexisting and exchanging members across decades, not of one phase replacing another.
- **What is shared across both strands, and does the actual work of not simply dissolving Strand B into "the same thing, later."** Zerbolt of Zutphen — a Brother of the Common Life, not a professed canon — wrote the movement's own method text for graded interior devotion (*The Spiritual Ascent*, vendored), a text whose formation logic the professed Windesheim/Diepenveen houses shared rather than replaced; the rapiarium practice and its most famous product, the Imitation of Christ, came from Kempis, himself a professed canon regular at Mount St. Agnes, working in a genre and practice that originated in the unvowed households. The two strands differ in authority structure and canonical status, not in the core formation logic named in Section 3 above.
- **Gender is explicitly found not to be an independent strand axis.** The same vowed/unvowed division recurs within each gender rather than tracking it: Sisters of the Common Life at Deventer (Strand A, unvowed) versus Canonesses Regular at Diepenveen (Strand B, vowed) mirrors Brothers of the Common Life (Strand A) versus Canons Regular at Windesheim and Mount St. Agnes (Strand B) exactly. The 1408 Diepenveen record itself shows women moving from the Strand A house to the Strand B house, the same transition pattern documented on the men's side. No evidence in what is vendored suggests a distinctly gendered formation logic independent of this vow-status division.
- **Honest limit, stated plainly rather than smoothed over.** Every piece of direct textual evidence cited above for Strand B, including the Diepenveen investiture record, is Kempis's own account — a man writing from the canons' side. Per Step 0 §2 A1 and §3 B2, **no vendored text carries the Sisters' own voice directly.** The census's own record of a women's-authored source — the Sister-Book of Diepenveen, including Salome Sticken's own "Way of Life for Sisters" — exists only in Middle Dutch manuscript and in Van Engen's and Scheepsma's secondary treatments (census `documentedStories`, itself flagged "primary text not yet read"), not in this world's vendored corpus. This document therefore cannot and does not claim that the two-strand authority-structure finding above captures how women in either strand actually experienced or understood their own formation — only that it captures the institutional and authority-structure division as recorded, exclusively, from outside the women's own community. This limit is carried forward as binding on Doc_02 and Doc_05, not resolved here.

**Governing consequence for later steps.** Per the Framework, formal cross-strand gravity testing occurs at Step 4. A candidate gravity that holds across both strands despite their different authority structures — for example, the shared commitment to rapiarium-keeping and graded interior devotion named above — is a stronger candidate for this world's irreducible core than one that holds only within one strand.

## 5. Preliminary Forces Identification

Per the Forces Framework's Step 1 requirement, this is orientation for Step 2's scope, not the complete forces analysis compiled at Step 8. Layer 1 (historical-event level) only; Layers 2 and 3 belong to Doc_08.

**What generated this world:** Geert Groote's own conversion, following an illness and the influence of a Carthusian friend (c. 1372–74), combined with a concrete legal-institutional act — his 20 September 1374 deed of gift converting his own inherited house into a home for lay women living without vows, under city rather than ecclesiastical oversight. The generative force is not the conversion experience alone but its immediate translation into a durable institutional form other devout townspeople could and did copy. **Confidence: Documented** for the deed and its date; **Widely Accepted** for the conversion narrative, which comes down through Kempis's own devotional biography rather than an independent contemporary record.

**What larger world was it embedded in:** The late-medieval Latin church of the Low Countries, itself embedded in a Western Christendom whose highest authority was visibly divided by the Western Schism (1378–1417) for nearly this entire founding period (directly vendored, *Founders* introduction) — a larger world in genuine institutional crisis at exactly the moment this movement was forming its own alternative form of religious life.

**What was it responding to:** Institutional and clerical laxity, most concretely clerical concubinage, which Groote preached against directly and was punished for (Documented, both census and the vendored friend's letter to the Bishop of Utrecht); more broadly, an era's own sense — recorded in the *Founders* introduction's discussion of plague, schism, and clerical failure together — that existing structures were not adequately forming ordinary Christians in genuine interior devotion. The vendored friend's letter names heretics and usurers as beneficiaries of Groote's silencing, suggesting the movement itself was understood, by its own defenders, as part of an active contest against a wider circle of religious and moral disorder, not only against lax clergy specifically.

**What was it refusing:** Formal religious vows, as a matter of explicit founding principle for the Common Life households — Groote's own "Resolutions and Intentions... not confirmed by vows" states this refusal in his own first-person voice (directly vendored). Alongside this, per this world's own census framing (not independently verified against a vendored primary text here, and so carried at **Widely Accepted** rather than Documented), a refusal of certain scholastic excesses — a preference for plain, interior, practical devotion over academic theological disputation as the primary mode of lay formation. These are, on the evidence gathered so far, two distinct refusals rather than one: the refusal of vows is Documented in Groote's own words; the refusal of scholastic excess is a broader characterization this document does not overstate.

**Preliminary six-cell sketch (Layer 1 only — Historical Event; Layers 2 and 3 belong to Doc_08's full compilation, not this preliminary pass):**

| | External | Internal |
|---|---|---|
| **Initiating** | The Western Schism (1378–1417) and a wider sense of institutional and clerical crisis in the late-medieval Latin church — Cell 1A | Groote's own conversion and his 1374 deed of gift, translating a personal spiritual turn into a durable, copyable household form — Cell 1B |
| **Ongoing** | Recurring plague; Mendicant institutional opposition to an unrecognized new form of religious life, met by adopting the established Augustinian Rule at Windesheim (directly vendored) — Cell 2A | The recurring, simultaneous coexistence of the unvowed Common Life households and the professed Windesheim/Diepenveen houses, exchanging members across decades (Section 4); the rapiarium practice as this world's own characteristic transmission form, carrying its formation logic forward from house to house and from the unvowed into the professed wing — Cell 2B |
| **Ending/Transforming** | The onset of the Reformation (1517) and the century-long dissolution of most of this world's own houses that followed — Cell 3A | The Imitation of Christ's own continued circulation, decoupled from the institutional houses that produced it, into living traditions this document does not attempt to identify (Section 1, Living Tradition Status); the claimed, asymmetrically-contested both-directions influence on Luther's schooling and Jesuit formation (Section 1) — Cell 3B |

## 6. World Continuity & Distinction

**What this world inherits:** An existing Western monastic and canonical vocabulary (the Rule of St. Augustine, canons regular as an existing form of religious life predating Windesheim's own adoption of it) that the professed wing draws on rather than invents; a wider late-medieval tradition of lay piety and vernacular devotion that this document does not claim to trace to any single named antecedent, since no vendored source in this world's own corpus addresses that lineage directly.

**What this world transmits forward:**
- *The rapiarium practice and its most consequential product, the Imitation of Christ* — "the most copied Christian book after the Bible" (Step 0 §1; census), continuing in active circulation across multiple living traditions well beyond this world's own 1517 close, independent of the institutional houses that produced it (Section 1 above).
- *A claimed, asymmetrical influence on the Society of Jesus's own early spiritual formation* — the strongest documented anchor being Coleridge's 1872 account of the early Jesuit companions reading the Imitation and Ignatius giving away copies of it (Step 0 §3 B1). This document names the relationship; it does not and cannot confirm it from the Jesuit side, since that world is not yet built.
- *A claimed but scholarship-contested influence on Luther's own schooling and, through him, the Reformation* — carried here exactly as contested, per Step 0 §3 B1, not upgraded to Documented status by this document.
- *The Windesheim Congregation itself*, as an ongoing canons-regular federation whose own further history (into and past this world's 1517 close) this document does not trace, since it lies outside this world's own window and is not addressed by the vendored corpus in that later period.

**Living heir(s), if any:** See Section 1 above — Living Tradition Status is PENDING, and this document does not assert a specific institutional heir. What continues most clearly is a text (the Imitation) and a claimed formative influence on later, still-living traditions (Catholic religious orders including the Jesuits; strands of Protestant piety through the contested Luther connection), rather than a single continuous institution of the kind Article 28/29 discipline elsewhere checks a world against. This is named as an open question for the project lead, not resolved here.
