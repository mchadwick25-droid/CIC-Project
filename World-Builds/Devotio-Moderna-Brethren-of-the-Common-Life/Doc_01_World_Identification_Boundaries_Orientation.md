## 1. Formation World Identity

**World Name:** Devotio Moderna / Brethren of the Common Life.

**Short Description:** A lay-centered spiritual reform movement of the Low Countries, c. 1374–1517, organized around households of devout men and women who lived a common life of prayer, mutual correction, and manual work — chiefly manuscript copying — without taking religious vows, alongside a formally professed canons-and-canonesses-regular wing that grew out of the same founding circle.

**Core Identity:** This is a world of households, not an institution or a single rule of life: laypeople and secular clergy gathered under a house Rector or Mistress, supporting themselves by copying manuscripts and other ordinary work, bound by custom and mutual correction rather than by canon law or profession (Step 0 §1; census `longDescription`). Its formative population is deliberately unglamorous — Deventer and Zwolle townspeople, schoolboys boarded for a Latin education, copyists, a few former university men (Groote himself resigned two prebends) — not a scholarly elite and not a persecuted minority. Its formation logic is interior and methodical before it is doctrinal or institutional: the *rapiarium* (a personal notebook of gathered readings, returned to and reworked in prayer), graded interior "ascent" (Zerbolt of Zutphen's method text, vendored as *The Spiritual Ascent*), and communal correction are this world's characteristic formative practices, in a way shared by none of the strictly monastic or juridical worlds already in this project's portfolio. Running alongside these unvowed households, and drawing recruits from them, is a second wing: canons and canonesses regular under the Rule of St. Augustine at Windesheim and its affiliated houses (including the women's house at Diepenveen), formally professed and monastically enclosed — Section 4 below gives this its own Strand Determination treatment rather than assuming either that it is the same thing as the unvowed households or that it is a separate world.

**Distinctive Contribution:** Against this same Era 6 batch's other two candidates — Lollardy and the Hussite and Bohemian Brethren Movement — this world shares no figure or text (Step 0 §3 B3) and offers the batch's one interior-devotional register: Lollardy and the Hussite movement are, at root, protest movements against ecclesiastical authority, while this movement submitted to that authority under real pressure (Groote's own suspended preaching license) rather than breaking from it (Step 0 §2 A1, §3 B4) — a genuine distinction in kind, though not an absence of friction. Its sharper, more consequential distinctiveness is the relationship the census's own `relationsSummary` names directly, quoted exactly: **"Parent-influence on both Luther's schooling and Jesuit pedagogy - a rare both-directions ancestor."** This world's disciplined, interior devotional formation is claimed as a parent-influence on both Luther's schooling and Jesuit spiritual formation, in two different confessional directions from the same source — a claim carried at the strength the census's own wording gives it, not upgraded to "documented" (the Luther direction specifically is contested, below). The Jesuit direction has a real vendored anchor, outside this world's own corpus: Coleridge's 1872 *Life and Letters of St. Francis Xavier* records the early Jesuit companions' spiritual reading including "the Imitation of Christ," and a footnote there (citing Alcazar) records Ignatius giving away copies of it (Step 0 §3 B1, B3). The Society of Jesus already has an open build thread in this portfolio — `World-Builds/Society-of-Jesus/` holds a Step 0 (Revision 3, independently reviewed and Approved to proceed) and a Doc_01 draft, not yet independently reviewed — so this is not a one-directional relationship in what can be checked: the Jesuit Step 0 (§3 B2) and its own Doc_01 draft (§1, §6) already name this same relationship from that side, citing the same Coleridge/Alcazar anchor. The relationship is named on both sides; only Wittenberg's own corpus-map entry stays silent on it (below). **Cross-thread flag, not corrected here:** the Society of Jesus Doc_01 draft itself misdescribes this world as "a separate, already-built world" and characterizes its core identity as "withdrawal into disciplined domestic devotion" — both wrong (this world has not passed Doc_01 review, and its own Core Identity above is a household movement staying inside ordinary town life, not withdrawal). That is a defect in the Jesuit thread's own document; it is named here so it is not lost, and left for that thread to fix, per this build's own discipline against touching another thread's content. The Luther/Erasmus schooling direction is claimed by the census but **contested in the wider scholarship** — R. R. Post's 1968 study argues the Brethren mostly ran student hostels rather than schools and that the Reformation-link thesis (Hyma's) is weak (Step 0 §3 B1) — so "documented ancestor" is too strong for that direction specifically; "claimed by the census, contested in the literature" is the accurate framing, carried forward rather than upgraded here. **Lutheran Wittenberg is a Built & Live world in this portfolio** (`worlds/witt`); as of this drafting, neither Wittenberg's own corpus-map entry nor this world's names the other, and no shared vendored text links them — a real, disclosed gap this document does not attempt to close on its own (Step 0 §3 B3, §5). Beyond the Era 6 batch and these two influence relationships, this world's own recurring formation ecology — see Section 3 below — occupies medieval Low Countries lay-communal space that no already-built world in this portfolio reconstructs; the clearest already-built analogue worth naming for contrast rather than overlap is Desert Monasticism (`worlds/desert`, c. 320s onward, Egypt) — a communal ascetic-withdrawal world separated from this one by thirteen centuries, a different continent, and, above all, a different relationship to ordinary town life: desert monasticism is built on renunciatory withdrawal from it, where this world's most distinctive wing is lay households that stay inside it. A vow structure sharpens the same contrast for Strand B specifically (desert monasticism's Pachomian wing takes formal cenobitic vows from the outset), but withdrawal versus staying in town is the clearer and more general difference, since this world's own Strand A households refuse vows while Strand B's Windesheim canons take them, so "vows" alone does not cleanly separate this whole world from Desert Monasticism the way it separates this world's own two strands from each other (Section 3, Section 4 below). Also worth naming directly, since the census marks it as a direct parent rather than a mere batch-mate: **Rhineland Mysticism & the Friends of God** (Atlas V.2, c. 1300–1400, same era, a Pre-Survey Candidate like this world, not yet built) — its own census `legacy` field states plainly that "the Devotio Moderna in the Low Countries grew in its wake," and its `relationsSummary` names this world directly: "Dominican line; feeds Devotio Moderna and (via Theologia Deutsch) Luther" (Section 3, Section 6 below).

**Living Tradition Status:** Not confirmed. Per Constitution Article 29 and this project's established practice, Living Tradition Status confirmation is a project-lead act this document cannot perform on its own behalf. Noted for the record, not decided here: this world does not present the same kind of single, continuously governing institutional heir that (for example) the Roman episcopate or the Coptic Orthodox Church give other worlds in this portfolio — its own houses "did not survive the century they helped bring about; most dissolved during the Reformation" (census `legacy`; Step 0 §3 B2 names this same institutional-decline gap as undocumented in the vendored corpus). What does continue is textual and formational rather than institutional: *The Imitation of Christ* "never stopped circulating" (Step 0 §1; census `legacy`) and remains in active devotional use across multiple living Christian traditions, Catholic and Protestant alike, without any one of them being this movement's institutional continuation. Whether that textual/formational continuity is the kind of thing Article 29 confirmation is meant to govern, and if so which living tradition(s) it should be checked against, is a question for the project lead, not a finding this document makes. **Status: PENDING.**

## 2. Historical Boundaries

**Time Horizon: c. 1374–1517.**

- **Beginning point.** The census's own window gives "c. 1380" as a round-number label, and Step 0 §2 A3 already established that 1380 is not itself a founding date. The defensible beginning point is **20 September 1374** — the day Geert Groote executed the deed of gift turning the house his parents had left him on the Bagijnenstraat in Deventer into a dwelling for "poor persons who wish to serve God," under the oversight of the city magistrates rather than any religious order, with residents lay, taking no vows, wearing no habit, and free to leave (census `documentedStories`, "Geert Grote Gives His House Away"). The census's own `verification` field for this story reads "from reference summaries; primary text not yet read" — this specific deed text is drawn from Van Engen's *Basic Writings*, a source the vendored corpus does not currently hold (Step 0 §4 item 5) — so this date and the deed's substance are carried at **Widely Accepted** confidence (standard scholarship: Van Engen), on the strength of the census's own dated-document citation, pending independent acquisition and reading of the deed itself; it is not carried at Documented on an unread source. It is a real beginning point even so, on a narrower claim than "no household anywhere": it is the first house *in this movement's own line* and, per the census, "the model for dozens more" across the Low Countries — not the first appearance anywhere of unvowed common life supported by manual work. The site itself was not an ecological blank: it stood on the Bagijnenstraat, Deventer's beguines' street, and Kempis's own *Chronicle* records that Groote's "dwelling-house and homestead lie bequeathed for ever to the poor Sisters, or Beguines, whom he had gathered together in that same place" (*Chronicle*, Part II, Ch. III, directly vendored) — Low Countries beguines had already lived unvowed common lives, self-supported by their own work, for more than a century before 1374. What is genuinely new at this house is not the form's first appearance but its formalization into a copyable, magistrate-overseen legal template — the census's own rule summary records a ban on anyone "who wanted the house as a convent," which defines the house against existing forms (beguinages, convents) rather than in an ecological vacuum. **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the date and the deed's substance; **Documented**, directly vendored, that the site already held a beguine community Groote had gathered before the deed. The surrounding conversion narrative — Groote's illness, and the Carthusian influence at Monnikhuizen — is itself Kempis's own devotional biography (vendored, *Founders*, Chs. III–VI) rather than an independent contemporary record, so it is carried at **Widely Accepted** rather than Documented. Kempis's own passage on Groote's pre-conversion reputation (*Founders*, directly vendored, l. 3493ff.) reports, as hearsay rather than settled fact: "It is said that Gerard had been skilled in astrology and necromancy, and before his conversion had been in the habit of displaying some few of the tricks of the art of magic: but I have learned from two of his pupils that more than these cannot be charged to him" — Kempis reports this as something "said" and then plays it down on the authority of two of Groote's own pupils, so it is carried here as contemporary hearsay about Groote's pre-conversion reputation, at **Widely Accepted**, not as an established fact about a "career" in astrology and magic (matching the census's own milder "a taste for astrology").
- **Ending point.** 1517. Defensible on the same "real ecological event, not a round number" test the beginning point applies: the census itself states the window "ends in 1517, not the round number 1500, since its houses ran into the sixteenth century and dissolved under the Reformation" (census `statusDescription`). 1517 is the conventional onset of the Reformation, and it is inside, not before, the unraveling this world's own houses underwent — the census's own `legacy` field states plainly that "the houses themselves did not survive the century they helped bring about; most dissolved during the Reformation," a process that continued for decades after 1517 rather than concluding on that date. Ending the window there keeps the boundary anchored to the real hinge event (the onset of the Reformation that dissolved these houses) rather than reaching for either the too-early round number 1500 or a later date tied to any one house's own final closure, which the vendored corpus does not document house-by-house (Step 0 §3 B2). **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for 1517 as the Reformation's conventional onset; **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for treating it as this world's own defensible closing hinge given the century-long dissolution it opens.

**Geographic Centers:** The Low Countries, above all the towns of the IJssel valley — **Deventer** (Groote's own city, site of Master Geert's House and, later, one of the region's leading Latin schools), **Zwolle** (site of the Zwolle Latin school and, nearby, Mount St. Agnes, Kempis's own monastery), and **Windesheim** itself, described in Arthur's 1905 editorial introduction to *Founders* (not Kempis's own words) as "a desolate place between Zwolle and Deventer" chosen as the site of the movement's first canons-regular house. **Diepenveen**, near Deventer, is the corresponding center for the women's canonesses-regular branch: the vendored *Chronicle of Mount St. Agnes* records that in 1408, on the Feast of St. Agnes, forty-three women "of the Order of Canons Regular" were invested there under the Rule of St. Augustine, "many of these Sisters... gathered and brought from Deventer from the house of Master Gerard Groote" (Kempis, *Chronicle of Mount St. Agnes*, directly vendored). The movement's own reach extended beyond this core: the vendored *Founders* records communities spreading "to the more remote regions of Flanders, Frisia, Westphalia and Saxony" after Groote's death, and this document's own §1 above places Luther's Brethren-linked household at Magdeburg, in Saxony. Kempis's own "Saxony" is a broad regional term for the vendored spread, following Step 0's own usage, and is not read here as a claim about a specific political territory. **Confidence: Documented** for Deventer, Zwolle, Windesheim, and Diepenveen as centers; **Documented** for the Westphalia/Saxony spread, on direct vendored textual evidence.

**Cultural Environment:** Urban, vernacular-adjacent lay piety in the Latin-liturgical Low Countries — a milieu of merchant and artisan townspeople, schoolboys boarded for a Latin education, and secular clergy, rather than a court, a university theology faculty, or a monastery in the older sense. Manuscript copying was not incidental labor but this world's own characteristic form of self-support and, per its own devotional logic, a spiritual discipline in its own right (rapiarium-keeping and manuscript work share the same underlying practice of slow, repeated engagement with a written text). The movement operated in a bilingual devotional culture — Latin for its formal method texts and (per Step 0 §3 B1) its founder's own letters, Middle Dutch for much of the women's houses' own record-keeping, which the vendored corpus does not currently hold (Step 0 §3 B2, §4 item 5). **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**

**Historical Pressures:** the register of each item below is now stated plainly — Kempis's own account, versus J. P. Arthur's 1905 editorial introduction to the same volume. Both are vendored, but they are not the same witness, and the difference matters for confidence.
- **The Western Schism (1378–1417).** Arthur's 1905 editorial introduction to *Founders* narrates the schism directly and at length — the double papal election after Gregory XI's death, the Avignon/Roman split, Urban VI's Italian line, the continuing "strife" after "Alexander died in 1410" — placing this movement's entire founding and early-growth period inside a Western church whose own highest authority was genuinely, visibly divided. This is Arthur's editorial narrative, not Kempis's own text, but the Schism itself is a well-established historical fact independent of either witness, so it stands. It began in 1378, four years *after* this world's own 1374 beginning point (above) — it is a pressure this world's early growth operated under, not a condition present at its own founding (Section 5 below). **Confidence: Documented** for the Schism as a historical event; **Widely Accepted** for Arthur's specific editorial framing of it, since that framing is the 1905 editor's own, not Kempis's.
- **Institutional and clerical laxity.** Groote's own preaching campaign targeted clerical concubinage directly — the vendored *Founders* preserves a friend's letter pleading to the Bishop of Utrecht for the restoration of Groote's suspended preaching license, naming "Clerks who live in concubinage" as among those who benefited from his silencing (vendored, *Founders*, c. line 5140). The census corroborates: Groote was "forbidden to preach the year before [his death] for attacking the clergy's concubines." This is Kempis's and the census's own evidence, not Arthur's. **Confidence: Documented.**
- **Recurring plague.** Arthur's 1905 editorial introduction discusses plague mortality directly — "According to Sismondi between four and five millions died in France alone during the first plague" (quoted in full; Arthur is himself citing Sismondi, not reporting an independent count) — and Kempis's own *Chronicle* records that Groote himself "died at Deventer, in the time of the pestilence" (*Chronicle*, Part II, Ch. III, directly vendored). Plague recurrence is part of this world's own lived environment throughout its window, not only at its founding. **Confidence: Documented** for plague as a recurring pressure (Arthur's editorial framing, corroborated as a well-established historical fact); **Documented**, not merely Widely Accepted, that Groote's own death occurred during plague, on Kempis's own direct vendored testimony above — the vendored *Founders* account (Ch. XVI) gives the date and his age without stating the cause, but the *Chronicle* passage does state it, in Kempis's own words.
- **Mendicant friction and the pressure toward institutional legitimacy.** Arthur's 1905 editorial introduction records the reasoning behind founding the Windesheim canons-regular house as Arthur's own paraphrase of Groote's deathbed instructions: adopting an established Rule (Augustine's) so as "to overcome or avoid the opposition of the Mendicants, who would certainly do their utmost to crush an entirely new society." This is Arthur's editorial paraphrase, not a passage in Kempis's or Groote's own voice, and it is not independently traced here to Busch. An unrecognized new form of religious life was a real institutional vulnerability this movement had to manage, not a background detail — but the specific reasoning is carried at **Widely Accepted**, not Documented, with its source register named, pending a Busch cross-check at Doc_02.

**Historical Catalysts** (dated, so far as the evidence allows):
- **1372/1374** — Groote's own conversion, following an illness and the influence of a Carthusian friend (census `documentedStories`; corroborated in outline by Kempis's own *Founders*, Chs. III–VI, though the exact year is debated within a two-year span per the census's own caveat).
- **20 September 1374** — Groote's deed of gift for the house on the Bagijnenstraat, Deventer: the movement's first house in its own line, for women, without vows — six years *before*, not inside, the census's own rounded "c. 1380" window-start (census `documentedStories`; see Beginning point above).
- **1383** — Groote forbidden to preach, "for attacking the clergy's concubines" (census; corroborated by the vendored friend's letter to the Bishop of Utrecht, above).
- **20 August 1384** — Groote's death, "in the forty-fourth year of his age," during the reign of Urban VI and the episcopate of Florentius de Wevelichoven, Bishop of Utrecht (directly vendored, Kempis, *Founders*, Ch. XVI, "Of his happy death and burial" — the vendored text gives the date as the feast of St. Bernard following the Assumption and "the thirteen hundred and eighty-fourth year"; the census gives his age as forty-three at death). This is not a real discrepancy: "the forty-fourth year of his age" is an ordinal year of life, meaning he had completed forty-three years, which is exactly what the census gives. The *Chronicle* itself confirms both readings agree: "the forty-fourth year of his age was nearly done" (*Chronicle*, Part II, Ch. III, directly vendored). Kempis's own *Chronicle* also gives the cause of death directly, "in the time of the pestilence" (same passage; see Historical Pressures above).
- **1386** — Mount St. Agnes, near Zwolle, founded "a year and a half after the death of the aforesaid Master Gerard" (directly vendored, Kempis, *Chronicle of Mount St. Agnes*, Ch. II).
- **1387** — Windesheim's own church consecrated, and the first six Brothers professed and invested as Canons Regular there, "on the day before the Feast of St. Luke the Evangelist" (directly vendored, Kempis, *Chronicle of Mount St. Agnes*, Part II, Ch. VI). This is a firm, independently vendored date, not "the mid-1380s" as an unverified placeholder, and it gives Strand B's own coexistence argument (Section 4 below) a firm start date: Master Geert's House at Deventer (Strand A) was still operating, and still recruiting members later transferred into Diepenveen (Strand B), more than two decades after this date.
- **1408** — First investiture of the Sisters of the Order of Canons Regular at Diepenveen, near Deventer, under the Rule of St. Augustine — forty-three women, several transferred from Master Geert's House at Deventer (directly vendored, Kempis, *Chronicle of Mount St. Agnes*, Ch. XIV).
- **1411** — First investiture of the Sisters of the Order of Canons Regular at Bronope, near Kampen, under "indelible and perpetual vows to live the life of the cloister" — fourteen women, of whom ten became nuns and four Converts (directly vendored, Kempis, *Chronicle of Mount St. Agnes*, Part II, Ch. XVIII; see Section 4 below for why this record, not the Diepenveen one, is the correct source for the "indelible and perpetual vows" language).
- **Progressive spread** to Flanders, Frisia, Westphalia, and Saxony after Groote's death (directly vendored, *Founders*).
- **Late 15th century** — Erasmus's education at Deventer (c. 1478–1485) and Luther's schooling connection at Magdeburg (1497) both fall late in this window, not in "the early-to-mid 16th century": both men were schooled before 1500, a generation before this world's own 1517 close (census `legacy`; the Luther connection contested per Step 0 §3 B1, above). Wittenberg's own window opens in 1517, exactly where this world's closes — the two windows are adjacent, not overlapping.
- **1517** — Conventional Reformation onset; this world's own closing hinge (see Ending point above).

## 3. Distinct World Criteria

Applying the Framework's four questions directly:

- **What recurring formation ecology exists?** A household-centered ecology of common life without (for one wing) or with (for the other) formal vows, organized around manual work — chiefly manuscript copying — as shared support and spiritual discipline; the *rapiarium* practice (a personal notebook of gathered devotional readings, kept and reworked over years) as the movement's characteristic interior-formation tool; the practice itself is directly attested in the vendored corpus (Busch's *Chronicon Windeshemense*, l. ~12018, 12506, 12758–12760, 12996, its own index entry at l. 45533, "Rapiarium. Notizbuch" — Latin, not independently translated here beyond the term itself), while the specific claim that the Imitation of Christ grew out of this practice rests on the census alone (Step 0 §1; census `why`), not on a vendored source, and is carried at that lighter sourcing weight rather than as "directly attested"; a graded, methodical "ascent" toward interior devotion, set out as an explicit method in Zerbolt of Zutphen's vendored *Spiritual Ascent*; mutual correction and communal accountability within each house under a Rector or Mistress; and attached schools for boys, which is how this world's own formation reached "far more people than ever joined it" (census `longDescription`). This recurs across the whole documented span — Groote's own founding households, the Windesheim/Diepenveen professed houses, and the movement's spread into Westphalia and Saxony all show the same basic formative cluster, not a feature confined to one phase or one house.
- **What recurring gravities exist?** **Preliminary only — full gravity analysis is explicitly deferred to Doc_04, per Forces Framework V1.1 §4 (Step 4 is where confirmed gravities are tested, not Step 1).** Candidates worth naming for Doc_02's scoping, not confirmed here: interior, methodical devotion prioritized over scholastic or purely institutional religious formation; communal mutual correction as a substitute for (or supplement to) canonical discipline; manuscript labor as simultaneously economic self-support and spiritual practice; and — a candidate specifically flagged by the Strand Determination below — a shared commitment to disciplined interior formation that appears to hold across both this world's vowed and unvowed wings despite their differing authority structures. None of these is asserted as a confirmed gravity by this document.
- **What recurring patterns of worship, formation, interpretation, belonging, and authority exist?** *Worship*: canonical hours, documented directly in the vendored *Chronicle* for the professed houses ("the Canonical Hours were sung in our church after the monastic manner"); the unvowed households' own worship practice is less directly attested in what's vendored and is a matter for Doc_02/Doc_05, not asserted here. *Formation*: the rapiarium and graded-ascent practices above, recurring across both wings. *Interpretation*: devotional and practical rather than exegetical-scholarly — this movement's own characteristic mode is excerpting and returning to short passages for spiritual use (the rapiarium), not producing biblical commentary or systematic theology; it is a sharp contrast with a world like Alexandria's learned catechetical tradition (`worlds/alx`), and this document names that contrast explicitly rather than leaving interpretation unaddressed. *Belonging*: household membership, dissolvable and vow-free for the Common Life households proper, formally professed and enclosed for the Windesheim/Diepenveen wing (Section 4 below). *Authority*: a Rector or Mistress within each house; for the professed wing, a General Chapter across the Windesheim congregation, directly attested in the vendored *Chronicle* ("their General Chapter was held at Windesem"); for the unvowed wing, custom and mutual correction rather than a canonical superior, per Groote's own "Resolutions and Intentions... not confirmed by vows" (directly vendored, *Founders*).
- **What distinguishes this world from adjacent worlds?** From this same Era 6 batch's Lollardy and the Hussite and Bohemian Brethren Movement: no figure or text overlap, and a difference in kind in relationship to church authority — submission under real pressure rather than open breach (Step 0 §2 A1, §3 B4). From Desert Monasticism (`worlds/desert`, already built): different era (this world's 1374 start is roughly thirteen centuries after Desert Monasticism's c. 320s beginning), different region, and, above all, a different relationship to ordinary town life — desert monasticism is built on withdrawal from it; this world's Strand A households stay inside it. A vow structure sharpens the contrast for Strand B specifically (Section 4 below), but is not the cleanest general marker, since Strand A and Strand B inside this world already differ on vows from each other. From the two worlds this movement is itself claimed as an ancestor of: Lutheran Wittenberg (`worlds/witt`, Built & Live) and the Society of Jesus — which already has an open build thread in this portfolio (`World-Builds/Society-of-Jesus/`, Step 0 Approved to proceed, Doc_01 drafted), not "source-ready, not yet built" — see the Distinctive Contribution discussion in Section 1 above; this document does not re-argue that relationship here, only confirms it is now named from both sides, with only Wittenberg's own corpus-map entry silent on it (Step 0 §3 B3, §4 item 4). And from the one sibling the census itself names as a direct *parent*, not a fellow descendant: **Rhineland Mysticism & the Friends of God** (Atlas V.2, c. 1300–1400, Rhineland/Switzerland, a Pre-Survey Candidate, not yet built). Its own census `legacy` field states "the Devotio Moderna in the Low Countries grew in its wake," and its `relationsSummary` reads "Dominican line; feeds Devotio Moderna and (via Theologia Deutsch) Luther." This world's own vendored corpus corroborates the lineage directly rather than leaving it silent: Kempis's *Chronicle* (Part II, Ch. II) records the death of "John Ruesbroeck, first Prior of the Groenendaal" and, separately (Ch. III), that Groote himself "translated two books of John Ruesbroeck from the Teutonic into the Latin tongue" — "Ecce Sponsus" and "De gradibus amoris" — and that he later "had it likewise in mind to found... a monastery for Regulars who should take the habit which he had seen in Brabant in the house of John Ruesbroeck" (Ch. V, directly vendored; see Section 4 below). The distinction from V.2 is one of register and form, not of doctrine: Rhineland Mysticism is a Dominican preaching and visionary-theological current, transmitted chiefly through sermons taken down by nuns and convent sister-books; this world is a lay household movement transmitted chiefly through the rapiarium and manuscript copying, with Ruusbroeck's own Augustinian canons-regular community at Groenendaal as the direct institutional and devotional model for this world's own professed wing.

## 4. Strand Determination (Constitution Article 21)

Per Article 21, strand is "a finding, never a presupposed universal schema," accountable to evidence, and never assigned "to satisfy an architectural preference for plurality." This determination is made here, for the first time — Step 0 correctly left it to Step 1 (Step 0 §2 A5: "not applicable in the developmental-tradition sense... the real relationships... are documented-influence questions, not developmental-tradition ones," which is a different question from strand-hood).

**The question this section must not confuse itself about.** Windesheim's canons-regular wing grew out of the same founding circle as the unvowed Common Life households, and grew later — the first Common Life house, for women (1374, see Beginning point above; not "Groote's own household" in the sense of where he himself lived) predates Windesheim's own consecration and first professions (1387, per the firm vendored date below) by thirteen years. That sequence, on its own, is not evidence of a strand: it would be sequential-development reasoning of exactly the kind Article 21 warns against, treating "happened later" as though it meant "is therefore a separate stream." What this section asks instead is whether the evidence shows a genuinely **recurring, simultaneous** divergence in authority structure that persists across the remainder of this world's own window, not merely a one-time developmental step.

**Finding: two strands identified, on recurring-ecology evidence in authority structure specifically — not on habit, which the vendored evidence itself shows is not a clean marker — with gender carried as a provisional, Inferential-Thin finding rather than a settled one.**

- **Strand A — Common Life (unvowed).** Households of laypeople and secular clergy living communally, self-supporting through manuscript copying and other work, explicitly without vows, and without profession, a Rule, or General Chapter governance. Evidenced directly: Groote's own founding deed specifies residents "were to be lay, take no vows, wear no habit, and be free to leave" (census `documentedStories`, citing Van Engen's edition of the deed — carried at Widely Accepted, per the Beginning point above, since the deed itself is not independently read here); Groote's own personal rule of life, vendored in full, is headed "Resolutions and Intentions set forth by Master Gerard in the name of the Lord, **but not confirmed by vows**" (*Founders*, directly vendored, Kempis's own text) — a first-person statement of the same principle from the founder himself, not merely a later summary of it. This is this world's own strongest, most directly vendored Strand A evidence, and it holds.
- **Strand B — Windesheim Canons and Canonesses Regular.** Formally professed religious life under the Rule of St. Augustine, at Windesheim (men) and its affiliated houses including Diepenveen and Bronope (women), with perpetual vows and General Chapter governance. Evidenced directly, and re-verified here against the vendored file: the *Chronicle of Mount St. Agnes* records the 1408 Diepenveen investiture — "the Order of Canons Regular... the habit and the order that followeth the rule of the Blessed Augustine the Bishop" (Part II, Ch. XIV, Kempis's own text) — and, separately, the 1411 Bronope investiture, three years later and at a different house: "This investiture, with indelible and perpetual vows to live the life of the cloister, was conferred by the Reverend Fathers and the Priors of our Order" (Part II, Ch. XVIII, Kempis's own text). The "indelible and perpetual vows" language belongs to Bronope 1411, not Diepenveen 1408; the two events are three years and one house apart, and are cited here as what they each actually are rather than spliced into one quotation. The 1412 General Chapter formally incorporated both Diepenveen and Bronope as member houses (same chapter, directly vendored) — direct evidence of General Chapter governance as the strand's own authority marker. Habit is deliberately **not** used here as a strand marker (see below).
- **Habit does not cleanly separate the two strands, and is dropped as a dividing marker.** Arthur's 1905 editorial introduction to *Founders* — not Kempis's own text — states: "The habit adopted **by the Brothers** was of dark grey cloth, and **when they became** Canons Regular they wore a white rochet with a black hood." This wording is sequential, not a fixed pair of markers: it has the *unvowed* Brothers themselves wearing a grey habit, before any of them became Canons Regular. That directly contradicts using "wears no habit" as Strand A's own marker (as the Beginning point deed's own wording, "wearing no habit," might otherwise suggest for Master Geert's House specifically — a single house's own practice, not a movement-wide rule). The real, evidenced dividing line between the strands is **profession, vows, the Rule of St. Augustine, and General Chapter governance** — not habit, which this vendored passage itself shows crossing the strand boundary.
- **Unvowed houses converting into professed houses, and the professed wing as the founder's own design, not a later split.** Kempis's own *Chronicle* (Part II, Ch. V, directly vendored) records that after Groote's death, devout households at Deventer, Zwolle, Kampen and neighboring towns multiplied, and that some of these — living first "after the example of the ancient Fathers" in a "hidden life" — "in process of time, as their numbers and their goods increased, they took upon them the habit of holy religion... and converted their houses into Monasteries of the Order of Canons Regular." This is unvowed-to-professed conversion happening at the level of individual houses, which pulls toward a developmental reading at that local scale even while the two structures coexist at the movement-wide scale (below). The same chapter also records that Groote himself, before his death, "had it likewise in mind to found... a monastery for Regulars who should take the habit which he had seen in Brabant in the house of John Ruesbroeck, but this purpose he committed to the followers whom he had made firm in the faith of Christ, that they should fulfil it, for death was beforehand with him" — so the professed wing was the founder's own plan, fulfilled by his disciples after his death, not a later break from his own intentions. Arthur's editorial introduction independently corroborates this from the outside (Groote's own deathbed instructions, per Arthur, directing that a House be built under the Rule of the Canons Regular of St. Augustine) — the two witnesses agree on substance even though only the *Chronicle* passage is Kempis's own text.
- **These two strands are nonetheless evidenced as recurring and simultaneous at the movement-wide scale, not merely sequential.** Windesheim's own church was consecrated and its first six Brothers professed as Canons Regular in **1387** (*Chronicle*, Part II, Ch. VI, directly vendored, a firm vendored date, not an estimate). Master Geert's House at Deventer (Strand A) continued operating for at least two decades after that date, and was still the source of new members recruited into Diepenveen (Strand B) as late as 1408 — the vendored *Chronicle* records that "many of these Sisters were gathered and brought from Deventer from the house of Master Gerard Groote" at the very moment of Diepenveen's own founding investiture. This is evidence of two authority structures coexisting and exchanging members across decades at the movement-wide level, even while individual houses (above) did convert from one to the other over time. Both things are true at once: the strand finding rests on the movement-wide coexistence, not on denying that individual-house conversion occurred.
- **What is shared across both strands, and does the actual work of not simply dissolving Strand B into "the same thing, later."** Zerbolt of Zutphen — a Brother of the Common Life, not a professed canon — wrote the movement's own method text for graded interior devotion (*The Spiritual Ascent*, vendored). Busch's *Chronicon Windeshemense* (Latin, vendored) attests the *rapiarium* practice directly among the professed Windesheim canons themselves (l. ~12018, 12506, 12758–12760, 12996; its own index, l. 45533, "Rapiarium. Notizbuch") — real vendored evidence, not asserted on the census alone, that the professed houses shared this formation practice rather than replacing it. The rapiarium's most famous product, the Imitation of Christ, came from Kempis, himself a professed canon regular at Mount St. Agnes; the claim that the rapiarium genre itself *originated* specifically in the unvowed households, rather than simply being shared across both, is not sourced in what is vendored and is not asserted here as a finding — it is a plausible reading the census's own framing invites, not a claim this document makes on vendored evidence. The two strands differ in authority structure and canonical status, not in the core formation logic named in Section 3 above.
- **Gender: carried as a provisional, Inferential-Thin finding, not a settled one.** The same vowed/unvowed division recurs within each gender rather than tracking it: Sisters of the Common Life at Deventer (Strand A, unvowed) versus Canonesses Regular at Diepenveen and Bronope (Strand B, vowed) mirrors Brothers of the Common Life (Strand A) versus Canons Regular at Windesheim and Mount St. Agnes (Strand B). The 1408 Diepenveen and 1411 Bronope records show women moving from Strand A houses into Strand B houses, a transition pattern also evidenced on the men's side at individual houses (Ch. V, above). But this is not, on its own, evidence that no distinctly gendered formation logic exists — it is evidence only that the vow-status division recurs on both sides of a divide from which the women's own voice is entirely absent (below). No vendored text records how the Sisters' houses themselves understood, named, or organized this same division in their own terms; the finding that "gender is not an independent strand axis" is therefore restated here as a **provisional finding, carried at Inferential-Thin confidence**, open for revision once the Diepenveen sister-book (Salome Sticken's own "Way of Life for Sisters," currently census-summary-only) is acquired and read — not as a settled exclusion.
- **Honest limit, stated plainly rather than smoothed over.** Not every piece of textual evidence cited for Strand B above is Kempis's own account. The Diepenveen and Bronope investiture records, and the Ch. V conversion and Ruusbroeck-plan passages, are Kempis's own text. The habit description and the Rule's own Kettlewell summary, by contrast, are Arthur's 1905 editorial framing, not Kempis's — one step further from the canons' own voice than a claim resting on Kempis alone would be, and named as such rather than folded silently into "directly vendored." Setting that source-register question aside, a more basic limit remains, per Step 0 §2 A1 and §3 B2: **no vendored text carries the Sisters' own voice directly**, in either strand. The census's own record of a women's-authored source — the Sister-Book of Diepenveen, including Salome Sticken's own "Way of Life for Sisters" — exists only in Middle Dutch manuscript and in Van Engen's and Scheepsma's secondary treatments (census `documentedStories`, itself flagged "primary text not yet read"), not in this world's vendored corpus. This document therefore cannot and does not claim that the two-strand authority-structure finding above captures how women in either strand actually experienced or understood their own formation — only that it captures the institutional and authority-structure division as recorded, exclusively, from outside the women's own community, and largely (with the exceptions named above) from Kempis's own side of that division specifically. This limit is carried forward as binding on Doc_02 and Doc_05, not resolved here.

**Governing consequence for later steps.** Per the Framework, formal cross-strand gravity testing occurs at Step 4. A candidate gravity that holds across both strands despite their different authority structures — for example, the shared commitment to rapiarium-keeping and graded interior devotion named above, now with direct Busch-vendored evidence on the professed side — is a stronger candidate for this world's irreducible core than one that holds only within one strand.

## 5. Preliminary Forces Identification

Per the Forces Framework's Step 1 requirement, this is orientation for Step 2's scope, not the complete forces analysis compiled at Step 8. Layer 1 (historical-event level) only; Layers 2 and 3 belong to Doc_08.

**What generated this world:** Geert Groote's own conversion, following an illness and the influence of a Carthusian friend (c. 1372–74), combined with a concrete legal-institutional act — his 20 September 1374 deed of gift converting his own inherited house into a home for lay women living without vows, under city rather than ecclesiastical oversight. The generative force is not the conversion experience alone but its immediate translation into a durable institutional form other devout townspeople could and did copy. **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the deed and its date (per the Beginning point above: the census's own citation, pending independent reading of the deed itself); **Widely Accepted** for the conversion narrative, which comes down through Kempis's own devotional biography rather than an independent contemporary record.

**What larger world was it embedded in:** The late-medieval Latin church of the Low Countries, itself embedded in a Western Christendom whose highest authority was visibly divided by the Western Schism (1378–1417) for nearly this entire founding period (directly vendored, *Founders* introduction) — a larger world in genuine institutional crisis at exactly the moment this movement was forming its own alternative form of religious life.

**What was it responding to:** Institutional and clerical laxity, most concretely clerical concubinage, which Groote preached against directly and was punished for (Documented, both census and the vendored friend's letter to the Bishop of Utrecht). The vendored friend's letter to the Bishop of Utrecht names, alongside clerics living in concubinage, "heretics" and "usurers" among those who benefited from Groote's silencing (*Founders*, directly vendored, c. l. 5110, 5140, 5155–5158) — a Layer 1 event (the letter's own named list), stated here as that and no further; what it implies about how the movement or its defenders understood their own place in a wider contest is a Layer 2/3 reading and is deferred to Doc_08 rather than asserted here.

**What was it refusing:** Formal religious vows, as a matter of explicit founding principle for the Common Life households — Groote's own "Resolutions and Intentions... not confirmed by vows" states this refusal in his own first-person voice (directly vendored). Alongside this, per this world's own census framing (not independently verified against a vendored primary text here, and so carried at **Widely Accepted** rather than Documented), a refusal of certain scholastic excesses — a preference for plain, interior, practical devotion over academic theological disputation as the primary mode of lay formation. These are, on the evidence gathered so far, two distinct refusals rather than one: the refusal of vows is Documented in Groote's own words; the refusal of scholastic excess is a broader characterization this document does not overstate.

## 6. World Continuity & Distinction

**What this world inherits:** An existing Western monastic and canonical vocabulary (the Rule of St. Augustine, canons regular as an existing form of religious life predating Windesheim's own adoption of it) that the professed wing draws on rather than invents; and, named directly rather than left untraced, **Rhineland Mysticism & the Friends of God** (census Atlas V.2) as this world's own direct parent — the census's own `legacy` field states "the Devotio Moderna in the Low Countries grew in its wake." This world's own vendored corpus addresses that lineage directly: Kempis's *Chronicle* records John Ruusbroeck's death as first Prior of Groenendaal, that Groote himself "translated two books of John Ruesbroeck from the Teutonic into the Latin tongue," and that Groote modeled his own planned canons-regular foundation on "the habit which he had seen in Brabant in the house of John Ruesbroeck" (Part II, Ch. II–V, directly vendored; Section 3, Section 4 above). A wider late-medieval tradition of lay piety and vernacular devotion beyond this specific, named parent is not traced further here, since no vendored source in this world's own corpus addresses that broader lineage directly.

**What this world transmits forward:**
- *The rapiarium practice and its most consequential product, the Imitation of Christ* — "the most copied Christian book after the Bible" (Step 0 §1; census), continuing in active circulation across multiple living traditions well beyond this world's own 1517 close, independent of the institutional houses that produced it (Section 1 above).
- *A claimed influence on the Society of Jesus's own early spiritual formation, now named from both sides* — the strongest documented anchor being Coleridge's 1872 account of the early Jesuit companions reading the Imitation and Ignatius giving away copies of it (Step 0 §3 B1). The Jesuit world's own Step 0 (Approved to proceed) and Doc_01 draft already name this same relationship independently from that side (Section 1 above); this document does not re-argue or rely on that side's own text, only confirms the relationship is no longer one-directional.
- *A claimed but scholarship-contested influence on Luther's own schooling and, through him, the Reformation* — carried here exactly as contested, per Step 0 §3 B1, not upgraded to Documented status by this document.
- *The Windesheim Congregation itself*, as an ongoing canons-regular federation whose own further history (into and past this world's 1517 close) this document does not trace, since it lies outside this world's own window and is not addressed by the vendored corpus in that later period.

**Living heir(s), if any:** See Section 1 above — Living Tradition Status is PENDING, and this document does not assert a specific institutional heir. What continues most clearly is a text (the Imitation) and a claimed formative influence on later, still-living traditions (Catholic religious orders including the Jesuits; strands of Protestant piety through the contested Luther connection), rather than a single continuous institution of the kind Article 28/29 discipline elsewhere checks a world against. This is named as an open question for the project lead, not resolved here.
