## 1. Formation World Identity

**World Name:** The Tridentine Church.

**Short Description:** The Counter-Reformation Catholic Church as reformed, defined, and disciplined by the Council of Trent (1545–63) and its immediate institutional implementation across Rome and Latin Christendom — the conciliar decrees and canons, the Roman Catechism they produced, the reformed liturgical books, and the papal and episcopal machinery that carried the Council's program into parish life.

**Core Identity:** This is a whole-church world, not one order or movement within it. Its characteristic material is conciliar and catechetical: canons that settle contested doctrine by anathematizing its denial, decrees that reform clerical discipline and diocesan structure, and a catechism written expressly so that parish priests could teach what the Council had settled to ordinary believers. Where the Society of Jesus (a sibling Era VII candidate, built separately) is the world of one religious order's own founding and formation logic, this world is the institutional and doctrinal charter that order — among several — went on to implement. — **Confidence: Documented** that Trent produced binding conciliar canons, decrees, and a commissioned catechism (Waterworth *Canons and Decrees*; McHugh & Callan *Roman Catechism*, both vendored and role: tradition per the corpus-map); **Widely Accepted** for the periodization "Counter-Reformation Catholic Church" as the world's own organizing frame.

**Distinctive Contribution — versus the Society of Jesus specifically.** The corpus-map has already settled the native/implementing split between these two worlds, not as an open question this document reopens but as a finding it inherits and applies (Step 0 §4 item 4; §3 B3): Waterworth's *Canons and Decrees* is role **tradition** for this world — "this world's own defining conciliar voice" — and role **context** for the Society of Jesus, where the same text is held as the Jesuits' own implementation of a program that originated elsewhere, not the Society's own composed voice. That corpus-map split is a cataloguing fact about which world holds native voice on the shared text, and it stands. What it is not is a clean historical separation between the body that authored Trent and the order that executed it — the vendored Waterworth narrative itself shows Jesuit theologians inside the deliberation, not only after it: Diego Lainez, "of the Society of Jesus," argued a minority position on justification in the first period (c. line 4902); Alfonso Salmeron opened debate in the second and third periods (c. lines 8625–8633, 9168–9201, 10383); and Lainez, by then general of the Society, cast a vote and delivered an argument on the episcopal-jurisdiction question in the third period whose "reasoning... appear to have produced the greatest impression on the congregation, one entire sitting of which was occupied in hearing him" (c. lines 8672–8675, 9382–9385) — he and Castagna went on to bring the Italian prelates to their preferred form of the disputed canon (c. line 9490). This world's own defining voice therefore includes Jesuit argument and a Jesuit General's vote, not merely later Jesuit implementation of a settlement handed down from outside the order. The distinction that survives this is not "authorship versus execution" but *unit of world*: this world is the whole-church, conciliar and institutional ecology — the twenty-five sessions across three periods (1545–47, 1551–52, 1562–63) as a corporate body's act, whoever sat in it; the Society of Jesus is one order's own formation logic (the *Exercises*, the *Constitutions*, the colleges), which both helped shape Trent's debates from inside them and then carried the settlement out in schools, missions, and confessionals — alongside, notably, episcopal reformers such as Carlo Borromeo in Milan, whose "executor" framing during the third period as Pius IV's cardinal-nephew in Rome is Widely Accepted in the secondary literature, though not independently checked against the vendored text here. — **Confidence: Documented** for the corpus-map role-split as a settled cataloguing fact, and for the Jesuit presence and voting role inside the conciliar debates as the vendored Waterworth narrative records it; **Widely Accepted** for Borromeo's own Rome-based executor role and for reframing the world-boundary as a unit-of-formation-logic distinction rather than an authorship/execution split.

**Distinctive Contribution — versus prior Catholic-world entries in the portfolio.** The current confirmed-world census, so far as this document can verify it, contains no earlier built Catholic world with which this candidate could overlap: the portfolio's confirmed worlds through Chalcedon (451 CE) predate this world's own 1517–1650 window by over a millennium, and the only other Catholic-adjacent candidate in this same Era VII batch is the Society of Jesus, addressed above. A separate, narrower census entry exists for Borromean Milan (VI.12, one diocese's own implementation) — not yet researched or built — which this world's own Step 0 explicitly declines to claim (Step 0 §0, §4 item 5). This document does not reopen that boundary; see §3 and §6 below for what it inherits from Step 0 on this point. — **Confidence: Documented** for the absence of temporal overlap with confirmed prior worlds; **Widely Accepted** for the VI.22/VI.12 scale distinction as Step 0 and the dossier state it (whole-church versus one diocese).

**Living Tradition Status: PENDING project-lead confirmation — not self-confirmed here.** The Roman Catholic Church is, without serious scholarly dispute, a still-living, present-day tradition of well over a billion adherents worldwide, with the papacy, the Roman Curia, and the diocesan/parish structure this world reconstructs continuing in direct, traceable institutional descent to the present day. That continuity is, if anything, more immediate and more visible than any other confirmed or candidate world in this portfolio's Living Tradition determinations to date — which makes the Article 28/29 distinction between the *historical formation world* (the Counter-Reformation Church of 1517–1650, as this document and its successors reconstruct it) and the *present-day Roman Catholic Church's own self-understanding* (which belongs to that living community and is not this project's to define) a matter requiring particular care, not less care because the continuity is obvious. Per Constitution Article 29 and this project's established practice, Living Tradition Status confirmation is a project-lead act this document cannot perform on its own behalf. **Status: PENDING.** — **Confidence: Documented** that the Roman Catholic Church is a living, present-day tradition; the confirmation act itself is a governance step, not a historical claim, and is recorded here as outstanding rather than assumed.

## 2. Historical Boundaries

**Time Horizon:** Step 0's own settled window is **1517–1650**, matching the census entry (`catholic-church-ancien-regime`) and the dossier — looser on both ends than the Council of Trent's own 1545–63 span, and deliberately so, given the vendored material this world's corpus now holds (Step 0 §1). This document adopts that window rather than narrowing it to Trent's own dates, because the vendored corpus itself extends past them in both directions: Bellarmine's genuine devotional works (early 17th century, c. 1615–1620), Borromeo's Milan *Acta* (1560s–80s, printed 1599), Pius V's apostolic letters (pontificate 1566–72, printed 1640), and Pius V's bull *Regnans in Excelsis* (1570) all postdate Trent's 1563 close, while the window's own 1517 start (Luther's 95 Theses) and its coverage of Leo X's and Clement VII's pre-Paul-III response to Luther (Step 0 §1) predate the Council's 1545 opening.

- **Beginning point (1517), defended.** 1517 marks Luther's 95 Theses and the opening of the specific Lutheran and Reformed controversy that Trent's own canons answer clause by clause — a real ecological rupture in the same sense IJC's Doc_01 defends 312 CE. This is narrower than a claim that the Catholic Church faced no organized, doctrinally systematic challenge of any kind before 1517: it did. The Hussite movement ran an organized Utraquist church with its own doctrinal program from the 1420s, and the 1436 Basel Compactata conceded the lay chalice — the very issue Session XXI later rules on (see this same batch's own Hussite-and-Bohemian-Brethren Step 0, `World-Builds/Hussite-and-Bohemian-Brethren-Movement/Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §1, §3 B3). Lollardy, also in this same batch, ran organized dissenting household networks from the 1380s, with burning for heresy made law in 1401 (`World-Builds/Lollardy/Step0_Movement_Scope_Confirmation.md` §1). What 1517 opens is not organized dissent as such, which predates it by over a century in both cases, but the specific controversy — Lutheran and confessional-Reformed, doctrinally systematic in the particular form Trent's own canons anathematize — that reached a scale and a spread across Latin Christendom that the Hussite and Lollard precedents did not. Doc_02 can cross-reference both precedents directly. Step 0 itself narrows its own *described* scope to Paul III (1534 accession) through the early 17th century while naming the full 1517–1650 census window as broader still (Step 0 §1; §4 item 7); Step 0 discloses this narrower description as a mismatch for Doc_01 to resolve, not as its own characterization of the window as "incidental" — this document's own decision, not Step 0's, is to adopt the full 1517–1650 window as the world's defended boundary. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for 1517 as the conventional opening date of the specific Lutheran/Reformed controversy Trent answers, and for the Hussite and Lollard movements as earlier organized, doctrinally organized challenges of a different kind and scale; **Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for adopting 1517, rather than 1545, as this world's own defended beginning.
- **Ending point (1650), defended provisionally.** The window's 1650 close reaches past this document's richest sourcing cluster, which sits between 1545 and 1599 (Trent's own sessions; the Roman Catechism, 1566; Borromeo's Milan reforms, printed 1599; Pius V's bull, 1570), but the later third is not unevidenced: Bellarmine's four vendored devotional works date to the early 17th century, specifically c. 1615–1620 (*De Ascensione mentis in Deum*, 1615, and *De aeterna felicitate sanctorum*, 1616, per the vendored *Mind's Ascent to God* file's own translator's introduction, c. lines 211–214), squarely inside the 1600–1650 window. What Step 0 actually names as not yet engaged is narrower than "the later third": the period's *named institutional developments* — Propaganda Fide (1622), the developed Index, the Roman Inquisition, the era's new active orders — have no vendored source (Step 0 §1). This document restates the gap accordingly: the 1600–1650 portion of the window is **thinly** evidenced, by devotional material only, while its institutional history is not engaged by any vendored source. It does not narrow the window on that basis; it flags the imbalance for Doc_02, which is the document responsible for testing whether the corpus actually supports reconstruction across the full span or only its earlier two-thirds. — **Confidence: Documented** that the window's later third is thinly evidenced (devotional material only) and that its named institutional developments are currently unevidenced in the vendored corpus; **Inferential-Thin** for any claim about what a fuller 1600–1650 institutional corpus would show.

**Geographic Centers:**

- **Trent** — the Alpine prince-bishopric (in the Holy Roman Empire, not papal territory) chosen as a compromise site acceptable to the Empire, where the Council's twenty-five sessions across three widely separated periods (1545–47, 1551–52, 1562–63) actually convened, debated, and enacted canon and decree.
- **Rome** — the implementing center: the papacy (Paul III, who convened the Council, through Pius IV, who closed it, and Pius V, whose pontificate promulgated the Roman Catechism and the reformed liturgical books, and issued *Regnans in Excelsis*) and the Curia that carried Trent's decrees into binding, empire-wide instruments.
- **Milan** — named here because the vendored corpus gives it primary-voice weight (Borromeo's own *Acta Ecclesiae Mediolanensis*, 1599 Latin printing, role: tradition), evidencing one archdiocese's own concrete implementation of the Tridentine episcopal-reform program; not claimed as this world's own separate jurisdiction (see §6 on the Borromean Milan boundary).

— **Confidence: Documented** for Trent's own conciliar dates and sessions and for Rome's implementing role; **Widely Accepted** for Milan's status as a documented implementation case within this world rather than a separate world.

**Cultural Environment:** Latin Christendom's educated, clerical, and increasingly print-literate culture of the mid-16th through mid-17th century — conciliar Latin as the language of binding doctrine and law; vernacular catechesis, by contrast, as the medium Trent's own Roman Catechism was designed to enable at the parish level (McHugh & Callan, 1923 English translation of a text meant for priests to teach from, not for lay reading in the original). This world's formation logic runs through office and instrument — conciliar canon, papal bull, diocesan synodal act, catechism — in a register closer to IJC's own "office-holders, not congregants" framing than to a devotional or ascetic world's, though with a genuine catechetical and liturgical register (the Roman Catechism; the Missal; the Breviary) that IJC's own world largely lacks. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**

**Historical Pressures:**

- The Reformation's direct doctrinal and disciplinary challenge — Lutheran and Reformed teaching on justification, the sacraments, scriptural authority, and church governance, each of which Trent's own canons name and anathematize (see §5 below for the precise content, verified against the vendored canons rather than assumed from popular narrative).
- A genuine internal-reform pressure that predates and is not reducible to the Lutheran challenge: clerical discipline, episcopal residency, and the training of parish clergy were recognized problems inside the Catholic Church before 1517, and Trent's own disciplinary decrees (on ordination, residency, and the founding of seminaries) address them as such, not merely as a reactive answer to Protestant critique. Step 0 does not develop this point in depth, and this document does not overclaim a fully worked internal-reform genealogy here — it is named as a real pressure requiring Doc_02's own sourcing to substantiate further, not asserted as settled beyond what the canons themselves show. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted** for the existence of a pre-Reformation internal-reform current in Catholic scholarship generally; **Inferential-Thin** for this document's own specific characterization of it, pending Doc_02.
- The need for doctrinal clarity in the face of a fracturing Western Christendom — the Council's own stated purpose, per its Session III preface, of "extirpating heresies" and "reforming manners" (verified verbatim against the vendored Waterworth text, lines 12320–12322).

**Historical Catalysts:**

- The three Trent sessions: 1545–47 (Paul III), 1551–52 (Julius III), 1562–63 (Pius IV) — twenty-five sessions in total.
- The Roman Catechism, 1566 (commissioned by Trent, promulgated under Pius V) — the text through which Trent's settlement reached parish clergy directly.
- Carlo Borromeo's Milan reforms — his own synodal acts and pastoral instructions (*Acta Ecclesiae Mediolanensis*, printed 1599), evidencing concrete diocesan-level implementation of Tridentine episcopal reform.
- The bull *Regnans in Excelsis* (Pius V, 1570) — excommunicating and purporting to depose Elizabeth I of England, verified directly against the vendored Barlow-transmitted parallel Latin/English text, opening "Regnans in excelsis, cui data est omnis in coelo et in terra potestas..."/"He that reigneth on high, to whom is given all power in Heaven and in Earth..." — normalized from the vendored file's own garbled OCR, which renders the two openings as ", Egnuans in Excelfis , cui dataeft Omnisin Calo ce? in Terra Po-teftas" (c. lines 2311–2316) and ": E that reigneth on 7 high » to whom is given all Power in Heaven & in Earth" (c. lines 2271–2276), drop-caps lost to the scan — a genuine primary papal act even though transmitted here inside a hostile 1681 Anglican polemical frame (Barlow's own commentary, role: context, distinguished from the bull text itself, role: tradition).

— **Confidence: Documented** for all four catalysts as dated events attested in the vendored primary sources.

## 3. Distinct World Criteria

Applying the Framework's four questions directly:

**What recurring formation ecology exists?** An ecology of conciliar definition and catechetical transmission: doctrine settled by anathema-bearing canon, discipline settled by decree, and both then carried to parish level through a commissioned catechism and reformed liturgical books — a formation logic that runs through *authoritative instrument* (conciliar text, papal bull, diocesan act, catechism) in a way distinct from a single founder's own devotional corpus or a single order's own formation rule. — **Confidence: Widely Accepted.**

**What recurring gravities exist (preliminary, deferred to Doc_04)?** Named here as candidates for later testing, not confirmed: doctrinal boundary-fixing through anathema (defining orthodoxy by naming and rejecting its denial, clause by clause); sacramental realism defended against reinterpretation (the Eucharistic canons, §5 below); hierarchical/conciliar authority as the proper locus of doctrinal settlement, defended against both individual private judgment and unilateral civil authority over the church. This document does not perform Doc_04's work; these are Step 1's own preliminary sketch only.

**What recurring patterns of worship, formation, interpretation, belonging, and authority exist?** Worship: the Mass, defended in its traditional Latin, ceremonial form against reformist critique (Session XXII canons, §5 below) and structured going forward by the Roman Missal (vendored, role: tradition, England 1843 translation, whole volume). Formation/catechesis: the Roman Catechism, written expressly for parish priests to teach from — this world's own doctrinal formation text, not a later commentary on it (corpus-map). Authority: episcopal reform and residency requirements, evidenced concretely in Borromeo's own Milan synodal acts. Interpretation: scriptural authority read alongside apostolic tradition and the Church's own magisterial teaching office, defended against a sola-scriptura hermeneutic — Session IV's own decree on the canonical Scriptures states that saving truth and moral discipline are "contained in the written books, and the unwritten traditions" which the Synod "receives and venerates with an equal affection of piety and reverence" alongside Scripture (verified verbatim, c. lines 12414–12424). Belonging: defined institutionally, through valid sacramental participation and communion with the hierarchical Church, rather than through a gathered-congregation or covenanted-community logic. — **Confidence: Documented** for the Mass/Missal, Catechism, and Borromeo's synodal acts as vendored primary evidence; **Widely Accepted** for the broader characterization of belonging and interpretation, pending Doc_02's fuller sourcing.

- **From the Society of Jesus, named directly:** the native/implementing split already settled in the corpus-map (§1 above) as a cataloguing fact, restated honestly (§1 above) as a unit-of-world distinction rather than a clean authorship/execution separation — Jesuit theologians and the Jesuit General took part in Trent's own debates and voting, not only in its later implementation. ## 4. Strand Determination (Constitution Article 21)

Per Article 21, strand is "a finding, never a presupposed universal schema," accountable to evidence, and never assigned to satisfy an architectural preference for plurality. This determination is made here for the first time; Step 0 did not perform it.

**The reasoning this section must not misuse, in either direction.** The sequence conciliar-decrees → Roman-Catechism → Borromeo's-episcopal-implementation is a temporal and causal sequence — the Catechism was commissioned by and derives its authority from the Council's own decrees; Borromeo's Milan reforms implement, rather than independently originate, the disciplinary program Trent legislated. Per the discipline IJC's own Doc_01 applied to its three sequential phases (§4 there), sequential, causally-linked development of a single program is *not*, by itself, evidence of strand plurality. But the same sequence is equally not, by itself, evidence of strand *unity* — arguing "the Catechism and Borromeo's Acta both derive from and answer to the Council's decrees, therefore all three share one ground" uses the identical causal-derivation move to reach the opposite conclusion, and is exactly as much a misuse of Article 21. Derivation shows a historical chain, not a shared or an unshared ground of authority. The ground itself has to be tested directly, on evidence, independent of sequence.

**The question Strand Determination must actually ask.** Setting the developmental sequence aside entirely: does any body of material in this world's corpus rest its own authority on a footing genuinely independent of, or in tension with, magisterial-hierarchical authority exercised through conciliar and episcopal office — not merely a distinct genre, audience, or moment within that same authority?

**Test 1 — the episcopal/papal ground-of-authority divergence, engaged directly.** Waterworth's narrative of the third period documents a long, organized dispute the Council itself never resolved: whether episcopal residence is "of divine right" (c. lines 7584–8177), and whether episcopal jurisdiction comes immediately from God or is mediated through the Pope (c. lines 9376–9490, 9684, 9847). Fifty-three of one hundred eighty-one Fathers backed the Archbishop of Granada's divine-right clause; Diego Lainez, general of the Jesuits, argued the papal-derivation position, and his reasoning "appear[s] to have produced the greatest impression on the congregation" (c. lines 9382–9385); Castagna and Lainez together brought the Italian prelates to a compromise form (c. line 9490), while the Legates were ultimately instructed "to abstain from defining it to be of divine right, in opposition to the opinions of so many of the prelates" (c. lines 9847–9849). *Regnans in Excelsis* later states the papal-primacy side in its strongest form, opening with plenitude of power committed "to one alone upon Earth, namely, to Peter... and to Peter's Successor" (Barlow, c. lines 2278–2290). This is a genuine, organized, unresolved divergence over where episcopal authority's *own* ground lies — divine right held immediately, versus authority derived through Petrine primacy. It is a real candidate under Article 21's actual bar. It does not, on the evidence, clear that bar: both positions were argued, voted, and left formally undefined *inside* the same Council, by Fathers who remained in the same communion and produced a single, if silent, canon rather than two independent bodies of authoritative material. This is a live, organized tension in how one authority structure allocates power internally, not two structures each generating their own formation material on an independent footing. It is named here as a tested and rejected candidate, not an untested one — and it remains a live question for Doc_04's own gravity testing, since an unresolved internal tension of this weight and duration is itself a candidate gravity, whether or not it rises to a strand.

**Test 2 — Bellarmine's devotional-ascent works, against the same bar.** The vendored corpus also holds four genuine Bellarmine works — *The Mind's Ascent to God by a Ladder of Created Things* (1615), *The Eternal Happiness of the Saints* (1616), *The Soul's Ascension to God, by the Steps of Creation*, and *The Joys of the Blessed* — which the translator's own introduction to the first describes as "the fruit of one of the great man's annual retreats," showing "the inner life of a public character... liberated in solitude and prayer" (c. lines 202–209). These works were not commissioned by the Council, are not addressed to parish clergy or diocesan enforcement, and do not exist to teach or enforce a settled decision; their own claim to being read rests on one Cardinal's personal contemplative practice and reasoned ascent from created things to God, not on conciliar promulgation. That is a materially different footing from the canons, the Catechism, Borromeo's *Acta*, or the liturgical books below — all four of which trace their own authority to make a formation claim directly to conciliar legislation or papal promulgation. Bellarmine's own case is the strongest candidate in this corpus for a genuinely distinct ground: an individual, ascetical-contemplative authority running alongside, rather than derived from, the corporate magisterial one. Against full strand status: it is four works by one figure, a minority register set against a large corporate mass, and Bellarmine remained a Cardinal in good standing whose devotional works were never in tension with the magisterial line — the difference is of *kind* of authority claimed, not of loyalty or content.

**Test 3 — the liturgical books (Missal and Breviary), against the same bar.** The Roman Missal (England, 1843, whole volume) and the Roman Breviary (Bute, 1908, one of four seasonal volumes) are both texts of the worship register that Doc_01's earlier drafting omitted from this analysis. Neither clears the bar for a distinct ground: both were promulgated by papal authority following directly from Trent's own liturgical reform mandate (the same footing as the Catechism), structuring corporate public worship rather than claiming any authority of their own apart from that promulgation. They belong with the canons, Catechism, and Borromeo's *Acta* on the magisterial-corporate side of the analysis, not as a fourth candidate strand.

**Working determination: strand-singular for the corporate-magisterial material, with Bellarmine's devotional-contemplative voice named as a real, small, and provisionally unresolved second candidate for Doc_04 — not settled here.** The canons and decrees, the Roman Catechism, Borromeo's synodal *Acta*, and the Missal and Breviary all rest on one tested and confirmed ground: magisterial definition exercised through conciliar and papal office, none of them claiming an authority independent of or in tension with that office (Test 3 above; the episcopal/papal divide of Test 1 tested as an internal tension within this same ground, not a second one). Bellarmine's four devotional works test differently: they rest on an individual contemplative footing this document cannot, on the evidence available at Step 1, fold into the same ground by derivation or genre-difference alone. This document does not resolve Bellarmine's own status as a strand: the corpus is thin (four works, one figure), and Doc_04's fuller gravity testing is the right place to determine whether a genuinely distinct organizing conviction — interior ascent versus corporate discipline and catechesis — does the ecological work a strand-plurality finding requires, or whether it is better described as one figure's own devotional register within a world that remains, in its main body, strand-singular. — **Confidence: Dominant Modern Reconstruction** for strand-singularity across the canons, Catechism, Borromeo's *Acta*, and the liturgical books; **Inferential-Thin** for whether Bellarmine's devotional works constitute a second strand or a minority register within the singular strand — named here as a live Doc_04 test, carrying no confidence tag of its own because it is an open question this document poses rather than a claim it makes.

## 5. Preliminary Forces Identification (Forces Framework Step 1)

This is orientation for Doc_02's scope, not the complete forces analysis compiled at Doc_08.

**What generated this world:** the Reformation's doctrinal and institutional challenge (Luther's 95 Theses, 1517, and the subsequent development of Lutheran and Reformed teaching and church polity), together with a genuine internal-reform pressure inside the Catholic Church that predates Luther — recognized problems of clerical discipline, episcopal absenteeism, and inadequate priestly formation that Trent's own disciplinary decrees address directly and that this document does not claim originated with, or are reducible to, the Protestant challenge (§2 above).

**What larger world was it embedded in:** Renaissance and early modern Latin Christendom — a Europe of contending confessional states, an Empire whose choice of Trent as council-site (rather than a papal city) itself reflects the political constraints on the Council's convening, and a print culture that made both Luther's challenge and Trent's own canons and catechism reproducible and transmissible at unprecedented speed and scale.

**What was it responding to:** Protestant doctrine and practice, named where the vendored canons themselves name it — not a generalized "Protestantism" but specific, citable positions. Session VI's canons on justification and Session XXI's and XXII's canons on the Eucharist and the Mass are addressed to named theological claims, verified directly against the vendored Waterworth text (see below).

**What was it refusing — precisely, verified against the canons rather than assumed from popular narrative:**

- **Justification by faith alone.** Session VI, Canon IX (verified verbatim, lines 13681–13686): "If anyone saith that by faith alone the impious is justified; in such wise as to mean that nothing else is required to co-operate in order to the obtaining the grace of Justification, and that it is not in any way necessary that he be prepared and disposed by the movement of his own will; let him be anathema." The canon's own precision matters: it anathematizes faith alone as *sufficient without any cooperation of the will*, not the broader claim that faith is necessary or central to justification.
- **Communion in one kind only, against the reformist demand for the chalice.** Session XXI's own decree is headed, in the vendored text, "ON COMMUNION UNDER BOTH SPECIES, AND ON THE COMMUNION OF INFANTS" (lines 18004–18005). Its **Canon I** (verified verbatim, lines 18007–18010) anathematizes the reformist claim directly: "If anyone saith that, by the precept of God, or by necessity of salvation, all and each of the faithful of Christ ought to receive both species of the most holy sacrament of the Eucharist; let him be anathema." The Church's own claimed authority to withhold the chalice as a matter of ecclesiastical discipline, rather than divine mandate, is stated in **Chapter II**, "The power of the Church as regards the dispensation of the Sacrament of the Eucharist" (verified verbatim, lines 17930–17938): "It furthermore declares that this power has ever been in the Church, that, in the dispensation of the sacraments, their substance being untouched, it may ordain, or change, what things soever it may judge most expedient for the profit of those who receive... Wherefore, holy Mother Church... has approved of this custom of communicating under one species, and decreed that it was to be held as a law."
- **Mass in the vernacular only, and other liturgical reforms.** Session XXII, Canon IX (verified verbatim, lines 18702–18708): "If anyone saith that the rite of the Roman Church, according to which a part of the canon and the words of consecration are pronounced in a low tone, is to be condemned; or that the mass ought to be celebrated in the vulgar tongue only; or that water ought not to be mixed with the wine that is to be offered in the chalice... let him be anathema." The precise target is "in the vulgar tongue *only*" — the canon defends the Church's authority to retain Latin, not a blanket claim that vernacular liturgical elements were forbidden everywhere. Trent's own decrees elsewhere permit vernacular catechetical instruction alongside the Latin Mass: Session XXII ch. VIII, "On not celebrating the Mass everywhere in the vulgar tongue," directs that "the mysteries of the Mass" be "explained to the people" (c. lines 18611–18617), and Session XXIV's reform decree ch. VII directs that a catechism be "faithfully translated into the vulgar tongue" and "expounded to the people by all parish priests," "even in the vernacular tongue" where sacraments are administered (c. lines 21069–21077).
- **Married clergy, specifically clerics in sacred orders or professed Religious under vow.** Session XXIV, Canon IX (verified verbatim, lines 20252–20261): "If anyone saith that clerics constituted in sacred orders, or Regulars, who have solemnly professed chastity, are able to contract marriage, and that being contracted it is valid, notwithstanding the ecclesiastical law or vow... let him be anathema." This is precisely scoped to clergy and religious under a prior vow of chastity, not a general condemnation of clerical marriage as such independent of that vow.

Each of these four refusals is verified here by direct grep and line-level reading of `council-of-trent_canons-and-decrees_waterworth1848.txt`, not asserted from general knowledge of the Counter-Reformation. — **Confidence: Documented** for all four, as the canons' own verbatim text.

## 6. World Continuity & Distinction

**What this world inherits:** medieval Catholic sacramental theology and ecclesiology, defended and clarified against reformist reinterpretation rather than reformed away — the Eucharistic realism, the seven sacraments, the authority of apostolic tradition alongside Scripture, and the hierarchical, Petrine-succession-grounded structure of church governance all continue from the medieval Church this world's own canons take as their starting, defended position, not as raw material to be redesigned. — **Confidence: Documented.**

**What this world transmits forward:** the entire early modern and modern Roman Catholic Church — its doctrinal settlement (the Tridentine profession of faith), its liturgical form (the Tridentine Mass, codified via the Roman Missal, in continuous use until the mid-20th century and beyond in some communities), its catechetical model (the Roman Catechism's own descendants, down to the modern *Catechism of the Catholic Church*), and its disciplinary structure (seminary formation, episcopal residency, parish-based pastoral care) — executed concretely, in this world's own vendored evidence, through orders such as the Society of Jesus and reforming bishops such as Carlo Borromeo, whose own Milan *Acta* stands as this world's most detailed primary case of implementation in practice. — **Confidence: Documented** for the broad transmission claim; **Widely Accepted** for the specific continuity of Tridentine liturgical form well beyond this world's own 1650 close.

**Living heir(s):** the Roman Catholic Church itself, in unbroken institutional continuity from this world to the present day — the most direct living-tradition continuity available to this portfolio (§1 above). Per Article 28, this document maintains, and every downstream document must maintain, a clear distinction between the historical formation world reconstructed here (the Counter-Reformation Church of 1517–1650, as its own conciliar, catechetical, and disciplinary texts present it) and the present-day Church's own self-understanding, which is not this project's to define and which has itself changed substantially since this world's own close (most consequentially at the Second Vatican Council, 1962–65 — named here only to mark that a genuine further discontinuity exists between this world's close and the present day, not to characterize it, which is outside this document's scope). — **Confidence: Documented** for the institutional continuity itself; the Article 28 differentiation duty is a governance requirement, not a historical claim, and is stated here as binding on all subsequent documents.
