## 1. Primary Sources

Ten corpus-map rows across eight vendored files (`cic/corpus-map/the-reformed-cities-zurich-and-geneva.yaml`), organized here by the two confirmed strands (Doc_01 §5) plus the document that bridges them.

**Geneva strand (Calvin):**
- *Institutes of the Christian Religion* (1559 final Latin edition, Beveridge 1845 English translation), across three vendored volumes matching the four Books: Book I (God the Creator), Books II–III (Christ the Redeemer; the mode of obtaining grace — faith, justification, predestination), Book IV (the church, sacraments, civil government). Calvin's own complete systematic theology — the largest single corpus this world holds by a wide margin (§6 below manages this directly).
- *The Catechism of the Church of Geneva* (Waterman 1815) — Calvin's own catechetical voice, distinct in register and audience from the Institutes.
- *Mutual Consent in Regard to the Sacraments* (the Consensus Tigurinus, 1549/1554, Beveridge 1844) — newly vendored this pass (§9 item 1 below); Calvin's own prefatory letter and appended exposition sit alongside the Zurich pastors' own reply and the 26 "Heads of Agreement" themselves, making this the one text in this world's corpus that is simultaneously a Geneva-strand and Zurich-strand primary source.

**Zurich strand (Zwingli, Bullinger):**
- *Selected Works of Huldreich Zwingli* (Jackson 1901): the Sixty-Seven Articles (1523, Zwingli's own founding doctrinal statement, individually corpus-mapped) plus a second row collecting the letter to Erasmus, the petition to the Bishop of Constance on clerical marriage, and the Acts of the First and Second Zurich Disputations — contemporary institutional record of the actual 1523 debates that opened Zurich's reform, not narrative reconstruction after the fact.
- *The Latin Works and the Correspondence of Huldreich Zwingli, Vol. I* (Jackson 1912) — a second, independent volume of early treatises and letters, not individually itemized within its own row (Doc_01 §8 item 6, still open).
- *The Second Helvetic Confession* (1566, Bullinger) — Bullinger's own mature confessional statement, the movement's doctrinal-floor text per Doc_01/Step0.

**Shared confessional wing (Palatinate, German Reformed):**
- *The Heidelberg Catechism* (1563, Ursinus/Olevianus) — "a distinct pastoral/catechetical voice alongside Calvin's Geneva Catechism, from the Reformed tradition's German wing" (Doc_01 §2, quoted verbatim rather than paraphrased into a stronger claim than that document itself makes). Not itself Zurich- or Geneva-authored. Whether its own treatment of predestination is as fully developed as Calvin's own doctrine, or notably more restrained despite its authors' shared Reformed convictions, is not independently checked against the vendored text this pass — flagged at Contested/not-yet-verified confidence (§8 below) rather than assumed either way.

**What this list does not contain, disclosed here rather than discovered later:** no narrative or hagiographic source (no martyr acts, no saint's life, no chronicle written after the fact to commemorate a founding figure); no vendored institutional-discipline record (the Ecclesiastical Ordinances, the Genevan Psalter, the Geneva consistory registers are all absent — §5, §6, §9 below); nothing in Beza's or Marie Dentière's own voice. This is a corpus of confession, catechism, systematic theology, and contemporary disputation record — doctrinally rich and institutionally thin, a real ecological shape this document manages directly rather than presenting as balanced.

## 2. Author Gravity Assessment

Per the Framework's own five dimensions (Visibility, Representativeness, Influence, Limitations, Transmission History — the fifth per the Expanded Author Gravity Assessment, used as standard practice in this project's prior worlds rather than treated as optional).

**John Calvin.**
- *Visibility:* Massively over-visible relative to every other figure in this world. Three full Institutes volumes plus the Geneva Catechism give Calvin roughly four times the vendored word count of Zwingli's own two volumes combined, and there is no vendored institutional or narrative counterweight (no consistory registers, no Beza, no lay voice) to balance it. Any claim about "the Reformed cities" built by word-count proportion alone would be a claim almost entirely about Geneva.
- *Representativeness:* Represents Geneva's own mature, systematized theology from the 1559 Institutes' own final form backward — not Calvin's own earlier, less settled 1536 first edition, and not the Consistory's own day-to-day practice, which this corpus does not independently evidence (§5 below).
- *Influence:* Foundational for the entire subsequent Reformed tradition (Doc_01 §7's own transmission findings — the Scottish Kirk, the Huguenots, the Dutch Reformed, English Puritanism all trace to this corpus's own doctrinal content, mediated further through Beza, not himself vendored).
- *Limitations:* A systematic theologian's own polished, revised-across-decades final statement is not a transcript of Geneva's own lived religious practice; Calvin's own conduct as a civic and disciplinary actor (the Consistory's actual case-by-case work, the Servetus affair) is documented far less in this vendored corpus than his own doctrinal writing.
- *Transmission History:* The Beveridge 1845 translation is the standard cited English edition (per the Source Readiness Dossier), itself translating the 1559 Latin *editio ultima* — the culmination of a text Calvin revised across five editions between 1536 and 1559; no vendored edition preserves the earlier editions' own distinct emphases.

**Huldrych Zwingli.**
- *Visibility:* Under-visible relative to Calvin in raw volume, though not thin in kind — the Sixty-Seven Articles, the Zurich Disputation Acts, and two further volumes of early treatises and correspondence together give a genuine, if smaller, first-person record of Zurich's own 1519–1525 reform.
- *Representativeness:* Represents Zurich's own earliest, most combative reforming moment — public disputation as the actual mechanism of civic reform — rather than the mature, institutionally settled Zurich of Bullinger's own four-decade pastorate; this world's own corpus is asymmetric in time as well as volume, weighted toward Zwingli's own six years (1519–1525) over Bullinger's own forty-four (1531–1575).
- *Influence:* The direct forerunner of the Consensus Tigurinus's own Zurich-side theology, and, distinctly, the point of origin for the Swiss Brethren Anabaptist tradition's own break from his circle — influence in two directions his own vendored corpus does not itself narrate. **Discharging Doc_01 §8 item 4's own binding instruction directly here:** the first Swiss Brethren believer's baptisms (21 January 1525, at Felix Manz's house) were performed by Conrad Grebel, baptizing Georg Blaurock, who then baptized the others present, including Manz. Grebel and Manz were both formerly members of Zwingli's own reform circle; Blaurock, a former priest from Chur who joined Zurich's radical circles in 1524–25, is not established on the standard accounts as having belonged to that circle specifically, and this document does not extend the claim to him. Both the Source Readiness Dossier §5 and this world's own Step 0 §3 B3 state "no figure overlap" with the Anabaptist movements — not accurate at this level of detail, per Doc_01 §7's own correction, restated here rather than left as a bare cross-reference.
- *Limitations:* Died in 1531, five years before Calvin's Geneva ministry began in earnest (Doc_01 §1); nothing in his own corpus can speak to the Consensus Tigurinus he did not live to see, a limitation this document must not blur (§6 below).
- *Transmission History:* Jackson's own 1901 and 1912 English translations are early-20th-century American scholarly editions; the Sixty-Seven Articles' own presence in the 1901 volume was independently verified this pass (Doc_01, per the Source Readiness Dossier's own prior flag).

**Heinrich Bullinger.**
- *Visibility:* The thinnest of this world's three central figures — one confession (the Second Helvetic, 1566) and a co-authorship in the Consensus Tigurinus, against Calvin's four vendored works and Zwingli's three.
- *Representativeness:* Represents Zurich's own mature, settled confessional voice after four decades of stability under his own pastorate — the opposite temporal register from Zwingli's own corpus, and the two together (not either alone) are what this world's own "Zurich strand" actually rests on.
- *Influence:* The documented mediating figure of the entire Zurich/Geneva bridge (Doc_01 §5) — his own historical role is disproportionately large relative to his own vendored word count, a mismatch this document names directly rather than letting volume stand in for significance.
- *Limitations:* A single mature confessional statement cannot itself evidence the decades of pastoral, catechetical, and disciplinary work that actually constituted his own ministry; this world's corpus knows Bullinger almost entirely through one finished document, not through his own working correspondence (he corresponded constantly across Reformed Europe; none of that correspondence is vendored here).
- *Transmission History:* Extracted from Schaff's *Creeds of Christendom* Vol. III (1919 printing), itself a 19th-century American Reformed compilation — a secondary reference volume supplying primary confessional text, not a dedicated Bullinger edition.

**The Consensus Tigurinus, considered as its own authorial case.** Not a single-author work and not treated as one: its own title page names it as between "the Ministers of the Church of Zurich" and "John Calvin, Minister of the Church of Geneva" (Doc_01 §5). Author Gravity here attaches to the negotiation itself rather than to either party alone — Visibility and Influence are high (it is the one document literally co-authored across the strand boundary), Representativeness is unusually direct (a text produced *to* represent both parties' agreement, not merely by one party describing the other), and its own Limitations are correspondingly narrow: it speaks only to the Supper, not to the full range of either party's own theology.

## 4. Formation Narrative Sources

Genuinely thin, disclosed rather than papered over. The Acts of the First and Second Zurich Disputations (within the *Selected Works* volume) are the closest thing this corpus holds to a formation narrative — a contemporary institutional record of the actual event that opened Zurich's reform, not a later commemorative narrative and not hagiography. No martyrdom account, no saint's life, no later chronicle exists in this vendored corpus, and none should be expected: this world's own formation logic runs through confession and disputation, not through narrative commemoration of holy lives, a genuine ecological difference from several of this project's already-built patristic worlds (Desert Monasticism, Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity) rather than a gap to be filled artificially. Doc_09's own Story Inventory should expect a correspondingly thin Tier 1/2 narrative base and should not manufacture narrative weight this ecology does not actually carry.

## 5. Material Culture and Daily Life Sources

The thinnest ecology lens this world's corpus currently offers, and the one place this document must be most honest about the gap between what justified this world's own selection and what is actually vendored. The census's own selection rationale for this world (`"why"` field) names Geneva's consistory registers as "one of the best ordinary-life archives in Christian history" — but, per Doc_01 §8 item 7, the only identified English edition (Kingdon/Lambert/Watt, trans. McDonald, Eerdmans 2000, vol. 1 only) is confirmed copyrighted and not vendored. No material-culture, archaeological, or documentary-daily-life source is vendored for this world at all. This world's own build cannot currently reconstruct ordinary believers' lived experience of church discipline directly from primary text — only from the doctrinal and confessional record's own account of what discipline was *for*. Per the Framework's own instruction, this thinness is "explicitly acknowledged rather than treated as absence of such conditions" — the sparseness is an evidentiary gap in this build's own corpus, not a claim that Reformed Geneva lacked a rich ordinary-life record (the opposite is true, and is exactly why the census selected this world).

## 6. Source Asymmetries and Missing Voices

**The Calvin/Zwingli asymmetry (Doc_01 §8 item 1, binding on this document).** Managed directly rather than averaged away: this document's own Author Gravity Assessment (§2 above) treats Calvin's greater volume as a source-visibility fact, not as evidence that Geneva's own theology is more central to this world than Zurich's. The Strand Determination (Doc_01 §5) rests on qualitative differences in practice, authority structure, and formation logic, not on word count, and this document does not let the corpus's own size imbalance quietly re-argue a question Doc_01 already settled on other grounds.

**Missing voices, under Article 20's Affirmative Duty regarding the marginalized within the community.** Two named, real absences, per Doc_01 §8 item 9:
- **Theodore Beza** — Calvin's own successor at Geneva, co-author of the Genevan Psalter, and (per Doc_01 §7) the direct theological figure standing between this world's own Geneva strand and the Synod of Dort. Entirely unvendored. This is not a marginalized voice in Article 20's own sense (a suppressed or structurally silenced figure) but a straightforward acquisition gap in an otherwise-prominent figure — named here for completeness and carried to the Acquisition Manifest, not claimed as an Affirmative Duty case.
- **Marie Dentière** — a former prioress of an Augustinian house at Tournai, turned Genevan reformer, who published (anonymously, in 1539; under her own name, in a signed 1561 preface) arguing that women could speak about scripture. This *is* an Article 20 case in the sense the Constitution actually tests for: a documented woman's voice, named by the census itself, whose own writing this world's corpus does not currently hold at all. Per Article 20's own primary duty, this absence is named plainly rather than smoothed over: this document cannot currently reconstruct Dentière's own argument in her own words, only report that she existed, wrote, and was prosecuted through her printer for it. Per the Framework's own secondary-duty test (bounded reconstruction only where independently supported by comparative evidence, material evidence, or the world's own sources read against the grain, never from silence alone), no bounded reconstruction is attempted here — the absence itself, not a reconstruction of what she might have said, is what this document records, and what a future Representative's own account of this world must disclose rather than paper over.
- **Ordinary believers, more broadly.** With no consistory registers and no material-culture source vendored (§5 above), this world's own corpus currently has no direct evidentiary access to lay religious experience at all — not even the mediated, institutional-filter access Donatism's own Author Gravity Assessment found in its own hostile narrative sources. This is the single largest missing-voices gap this document can name, larger than any single missing named figure, and it is a corpus-acquisition problem (§9 below, the consistory registers and any legitimately usable substitute), not a reconstruction problem this document could respectably solve by inference.

**Evidential visibility versus ecological visibility (Framework's own distinction).** What survives — confession, catechism, systematic theology, disputation record — reflects what the Reformed tradition itself chose to preserve and publish (doctrinally authoritative statements), not necessarily what was formatively largest in an ordinary believer's own week (a Sunday service, a consistory summons, a psalm sung in the vernacular). This document names that gap explicitly rather than letting the surviving record's own confessional self-selection stand in silently for the whole of this world's own formation ecology.
