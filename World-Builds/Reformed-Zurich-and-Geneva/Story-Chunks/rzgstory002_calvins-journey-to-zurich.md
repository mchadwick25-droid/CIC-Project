## Story Text

By 1549, a quiet worry had been spreading among people who respected both Zurich's and Geneva's own churches: did Calvin's own teaching on the Supper actually agree with what Zurich taught, or did it differ in some way no one could quite name? Calvin heard the worry directly — from, in his own words, "the constant entreaties of worthy individuals" who revered both churches and did not want an appearance of disagreement to slow their own progress in the faith.

Calvin decided a letter would not settle it. He went himself. He traveled to Zurich, and asked his own colleague William Farel — whom he called, in the same letter, "my venerable colleague... indefatigable soldier of Christ as he is" — to come with him. Farel needed no persuading; Calvin's own account states plainly that Farel had "suggested and advised the visit" himself, and did not decline to accompany him.

What the two of them worked out with Zurich's own pastors in that meeting became the document later published as the Consensus Tigurinus. Calvin's own letter afterward is careful about what the document actually is: not a new invention, but "a faithful record of the conference" — a considered attempt, in his own words, to give what had already been agreed "a tabular view," so that anyone could see for themselves that the two churches' teaching stood together. He is equally careful to note that the agreement was not his and Farel's own private business: it bound, he wrote, every colleague serving Christ under Geneva's own jurisdiction or in the Canton of Neuchâtel as well.

## Tier Justification

This is Tier 1 material: direct, first-person textual attestation from a named author (Calvin) about his own actions, addressed to a named, identifiable audience (the pastors and doctors of the Church of Zurich), datable to 1549 with the document's own 1554 publication date separately attested, and a historically credible narrative claim about Calvin's own conduct rather than a report mediated through anyone else's telling.

The one caveat Tier 1's own definition names for authorial perspective applies narrowly here: this is Calvin's own account of his own initiative, and the story presents it as such — his own characterization of Farel's enthusiasm ("indefatigable soldier of Christ") and his own framing of the conference as "friendly" and consensual are his own words about his own colleague and his own conduct, not an independently corroborated outside report of the meeting's own tone. The bare facts (that Calvin traveled to Zurich with Farel, met with the Zurich pastors, and that the meeting produced the Consensus text) rest on Calvin's own direct first-person statement and are not themselves in question.
