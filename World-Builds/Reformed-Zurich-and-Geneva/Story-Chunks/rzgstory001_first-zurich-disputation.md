## Story Text

The city council of Zurich did not simply permit the argument that would launch the Reformation there — it summoned it. Its own mandate, sent to every priest, preacher, and clergyman with a living in its territories, named the trouble plainly: dissension had grown between those who believed they preached the gospel faithfully and those who called them "sources of evil, deceivers and sometimes heretics." Rather than let the argument run on unresolved, the council called everyone to Zurich to settle it "with the aid of God's Scriptures."

On the appointed day, six hundred people — priests and laymen from across the canton, along with delegates from the bishop of Constance — filled the Town Hall. Zwingli had prepared sixty-seven articles as his own program for the debate; the bishop of Constance sent his Vicar General, John Faber, as the ablest man on the other side. Before the day even arrived, word had already reached the enemies of the new preaching, who mocked the whole gathering in advance: they called it a "tinker's day," and said nothing but tinkers would attend.

That mockery is the reason this world's own record of the day survives in the detail it does. A Zurich schoolmaster named Erhart Hegenwald sat through the entire disputation, and afterward wrote an account specifically to answer the sneer. "I was there myself and sat with them," he wrote, "heard and understood and remembered all that was said there, and after that I wrote it down in my home, questioned and examined others who had been present at the meeting as to the cases in which I thought I might not have understood correctly." He sent his finished account to a churchman he respected, so that "every one might see and know whether such action taken and speeches made were by tinkers and pan-menders" — or not.

The council did not merely watch the debate; it judged it, as it had promised to. When the day's arguments were finished, the council found for Zwingli's side, and did not stop at declaring a winner: it ordered that his teaching continue, and required every priest in the canton to preach the same way. What had begun as a summoned argument, held before an audience some had already dismissed in advance, ended as an official act of civic reform.

## Tier Justification

This is Tier 1 material: direct textual attestation within the world's own horizon, a named author (Hegenwald) with an identifiable social location (a Zurich schoolmaster, explicitly stating his own eyewitness method), datable with reasonable confidence, and a historically credible narrative claim with the caveats Tier 1's own definition names.

**A disclosed date inconsistency, not silently resolved.** This vendored volume's own materials give three different dates for the same event: the document's own title heading states the disputation was held "ON THE 29TH DAY OF JANUARY... 1523" (lines 1513–1516); the editor Jackson's own prose introduction states "the time set, which was the 23d of January, 1523" (line 1545) and again "on that eventful day" without repeating a date (line 1547); and the same introduction's own quoted German title of a contemporary pamphlet about the same event reads "uff den 29 tag Jenner" — "on the 29th day of January" (line 1578). Two of the three sources agree on 29 January, matching standard modern historiography's own dating of the First Zurich Disputation; the "23d" appears once, in Jackson's own editorial prose, not in either primary title. This story follows the two-source majority and the standard date (29 January 1523), naming the "23d" as this volume's own internal inconsistency rather than silently adopting either figure without comment or presenting the date as more settled than this specific vendored text, on its own, actually shows it to be.

**A second caveat, per this world's own established discipline for editorial apparatus.** Jackson's own introduction (lines 1529–1598) is his own 1901 editorial framing, not Hegenwald's primary text — it is used above only for the "six hundred persons," Faber's identity, and the council's ratification, each independently corroborated by Hegenwald's own primary preface and mandate (which separately confirm the six hundred figure, line 1632, and the council's own convening role, lines 1665–1678). The story's own most load-bearing detail — the "tinker's day" mockery and Hegenwald's own eyewitness method — is drawn entirely from Hegenwald's own primary preface, not from Jackson's editorial framing.
