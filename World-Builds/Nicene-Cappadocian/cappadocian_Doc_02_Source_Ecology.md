## 1. Primary Voices (with five-dimension Author Gravity Assessment)

### 1.1 Basil of Caesarea (c. 329/30 – 379; death redated to September 378 by a significant scholarly minority — Contested, carried)
The corpus: c. 360s–370s letters (well over three hundred, including the canonical letters to Amphilochius that became Eastern canon law; the correspondence with Eustathius' circle, with governors and prefects, with Athanasius and the West); **On the Holy Spirit** (c. 375, occasioned by controversy over his own doxology — the occasion is the treatise's own account); **Against Eunomius** (books 1–3; later books spurious — Widely Accepted); the **Hexaemeron** homilies (preached to a working congregation, morning and evening — internal evidence); the moral homilies (on famine and drought; on the rich fool's barns; against usury; against envy; against anger; against drunkenness; on the Forty Martyrs and other local martyrs); the ascetic corpus (**the Asketikon** — Longer and Shorter Rules in their developing recensions; the Moralia; the authenticity and layering of the wider ascetic corpus is a specialist field — Gribomont's textual work is the modern foundation); the **Address to Young Men** on the use of Greek literature. (Documented as corpus, with named authenticity questions carried.)
- **Visibility:** very high — the world's institutional center writes the world's largest surviving record.
- **Representativeness:** a curial-class, Athens-educated metropolitan and ascetic founder — not representative of the plateau farmer, the village presbyter, the ordinary brother of his own communities, or the radical ascetics he disciplined.
- **Influence:** immense in his lifetime (metropolitan of Caesarea; famine patron; ecumenical letter-writer) and definitive afterward (his Rules become Eastern monasticism's charter; his canons become its law; "the Great" within a generation).
- **Limitations:** much of the corpus is occasional and polemical; his accounts of Eustathius (after their rupture) and of Eunomius are adversarial evidence; his ascetic legislation shows us the legislator's intent, not the communities' lived compliance.
- **Transmission history:** preserved massively because Byzantium canonized him (liturgy, canon law, monastic rule all under his name — including a eucharistic anaphora whose attribution to Basil in its transmitted form is later and Contested); this prestige attracted spuria (later Rules recensions, pseudonymous works), so attribution discipline is required at every use. His letter collection was curated — by whom and with what omissions is not recoverable (Inferential/Thin), and the curation itself is a named force (Doc_08, Cells 2B/3B).
- **Builder rule adopted:** no world-level claim may rest on Basil alone without an explicit Author Gravity flag; and no claim about the ascetic movement's lived reality may rest on the Rules alone (legislation ≠ life).

### 1.2 Gregory of Nazianzus (c. 329/30 – c. 390)
The corpus: 44–45 orations (including the five **Theological Orations** delivered at Constantinople c. 380; the funeral orations on his father, his brother Caesarius, his sister Gorgonia, and on Basil — Oration 43, the era's greatest encomium; **On the Love of the Poor**; the invectives against Julian; the apology for his flight from ordination — Oration 2, the era's classic statement of the stillness-versus-office tension); c. 245 letters (including the earliest self-consciously curated Greek Christian letter collection — his own editing is Documented as his own claim); a large body of poems including **De vita sua**, a verse autobiography nearly unique in antiquity; and his **will**, which survives (Documented as a document; its details cited at work level only).
- **Visibility:** very high, and uniquely *interior* — no other voice in this world tells us so much about how it felt.
- **Representativeness:** even less socially representative than Basil — a rhetor-contemplative of independent means who held office briefly and unhappily; his interiority is the interiority of one exceptional, self-dramatizing man.
- **Influence:** the Theological Orations become the East's touchstone of Trinitarian teaching ("the Theologian" as his fixed title); his self-account fixes how posterity sees the whole circle — including Basil, whom we largely see through Gregory's grieving, wounded, admiring retrospect.
- **Limitations:** the sharpest self-fashioning problem in the world's record. His accounts of the Sasima affair, of his flight(s), of Constantinople 379–381, and of Basil himself are retrospective literary constructions of a man justifying his own life (the scholarly caution here — McGuckin, and analogously McLynn's treatment of self-reporting bishops — is carried at full strength). Oration 43's Basil is an icon painted by a bereaved friend with a case to make.
- **Transmission history:** superbly preserved (Byzantine school-text status; more manuscripts than nearly any Greek father); his self-curation means we inherit the archive he wanted us to have — a transmission force operating *inside* the primary evidence itself.

### 1.3 Gregory of Nyssa (c. 335 – after 394)
The corpus: **Against Eunomius** (continuing his dead brother's fight — the continuation itself is formation evidence); **On the Holy Spirit** and the sermons on the Deity of the Son and Spirit (including the famous complaint that Constantinople's money-changers and bath-attendants argued about the Unbegotten — Documented at work level); the **Catechetical Oration** (the world's most systematic catechesis); **On the Making of Man** (completing Basil's Hexaemeron — again, family continuation); the **Life of Macrina** and **On the Soul and Resurrection** (see §3); **On Virginity**; the **Life of Gregory Thaumaturgus** (see §3); homilies on the Song of Songs, Ecclesiastes (including the fourth homily's frontal attack on slaveholding — "who can buy the image of God?" — Documented and, in the ancient record, nearly unparalleled), the Beatitudes, the Lord's Prayer; **On the Love of the Poor / On the treatment of lepers**; consolation pieces including on infants' early deaths; letters (including the acid letter on pilgrimage to Jerusalem — Documented at work level).
- **Visibility:** high, weighted toward the world's last fifteen years.
- **Representativeness:** the least publicly effective of the three in his own day (Basil's letters complain of his political naivety — Documented at work level), the most speculative; his mystical-theological syntheses (perpetual progress, the darkness of knowing) are his own developments, not the world's common property — a specific Author Gravity risk this build flags every time it is tempted to make "epektasis" the whole world's word.
- **Influence:** modest in-period beyond his circle; enormous later (Byzantine mystical theology; twentieth-century revival).
- **Limitations:** as literary a constructor as his friend Gregory — the Life of Macrina and On the Soul and Resurrection present his sister through deliberate Socratic-Platonic framing (Macrina as Diotima/Socrates at the deathbed), so the world's principal window on a woman's formation is a male-authored philosophical artwork (Contested how much is Macrina, how much Gregory — carried at full strength; see §6).
- **Transmission history:** preserved through Byzantine dogmatic prestige (named in the imperial communion law of 381 — Documented at work level) despite later unease about his Origenist edges; the unease shaped what was copied and glossed (Widely Accepted).

### 1.4 Macrina the Younger (c. 327 – 379) — a primary *voice* with no primary *text*
Everything we have of Macrina — her leadership of the Annisa community, her formation of her brothers, her deathbed teaching, her title "the Teacher" (hē didaskalos) — comes through Gregory of Nyssa's two literary works and scattered mentions (Basil's letters barely name her — itself a datum requiring explanation: candidate explanations include genre convention, fraternal rivalry, and the ascetic modesty topos; none provable — Inferential/Thin). Author Gravity entry is therefore **for Gregory-writing-Macrina**: visibility high, representativeness nil (an aristocratic virgin-philosopher is not the ordinary sister), influence real (the Vita became a model of female sanctity), limitation total (her own words are unrecoverable as hers), transmission entirely male-mediated. **Builder rule adopted:** Macrina is cited as "Macrina as Gregory presents her" in every use; her historical leadership of a documented community is Widely Accepted; her ipsissima verba are not recoverable.

### 1.5 The opponents (as recoverable)
- **Eunomius of Cyzicus:** his first **Apology** survives intact (unusual for a condemned author — Documented); his **Apology for the Apology** survives only as quoted in Gregory of Nyssa's refutation (adversarial transmission — every use flagged); his confession of 383 survives. We can, exceptionally, check the Cappadocians' polemic against their opponent's own words for one round of the fight — a rare control on adversarial distortion, and this build treats it as such.
- **Eustathius of Sebaste:** no works survive. Known through Basil's letters (friendship, collaboration, rupture — the account of a wounded former disciple), the Gangra canons (censuring "those around Eustathius" — the council's hostile summary of the radicals' practices is itself against-the-grain evidence of what they actually did), Epiphanius, and later historians. The world's ascetic origins are thus transmitted by the movement's reformer and its censors. (Adversarial transmission; flagged at every use.)
- **The Pneumatomachians ("Spirit-fighters" — their enemies' name; they called themselves otherwise):** no corpus; positions reconstructed from Basil's On the Holy Spirit, the Theological Orations, and conciliar documents. Their self-understanding is largely unrecoverable (named as structural absence, §6).
- **The Homoian establishment** (the court church of Constantius and Valens): its Cappadocian face survives almost solely in its opponents' accounts and in imperial acts. (Adversarial transmission.)

### 1.6 Other primary voices (work-level)
**Amphilochius of Iconium** (cousin of Nazianzen; addressee of On the Holy Spirit and the canonical letters; some genuine works survive — the circle's junior member and first receiver); **Evagrius Ponticus** (Cappadocian-Pontic formation, ordained by the circle, departs for Constantinople and the desert at the world's edge — his later corpus belongs to another world and is *not* used as evidence for this one; the boundary is stated because the temptation is real); **Gregory Thaumaturgus** (third-century: the Canonical Epistle is Documented; the Address of Thanksgiving to Origen is traditionally his, with attribution questioned in recent scholarship — Contested; the creed attributed to him is transmitted by Nyssen — Tier 3 register); **Firmilian of Caesarea** (third-century; one letter, preserved in Latin in Cyprian's corpus — pre-boundary inheritance evidence); **the elder Gregory of Nazianzus** (bishop-father, formerly of the Hypsistarian sect — Documented via his son's funeral oration; a window on the region's pre-Nicene religious mixture); **Julian the emperor** (educated in part at the Cappadocian estate of Macellum — Documented via his own Letter to the Athenians; his school edict of 362 and his letters are the old religion's own voice pressing on this world); **Libanius** (correspondence with Basil transmitted, authenticity of parts Contested — carried; the pagan rhetor as external witness to the paideia network); **the church historians** (Socrates, Sozomen, Theodoret, Philostorgius — all fifth-century, all outside the horizon: used only as secondary narrative sources with their own Author Gravity entries in §3, never as the world's own voice; Philostorgius matters as the rare *Eunomian* historian — the losers' one surviving narrative frame).

## 2. Institutional, Legal, and Liturgical Evidence

- **Councils and canons:** Nicaea (325 — subscription lists include the region's bishops); **Gangra** (c. 340s; date Contested within about a decade) — twenty canons censuring radical ascetic practice (condemning those who dissolve marriages, encourage slaves to defy masters, fast on Sundays, despise the married priest, hold private assemblies): read against the grain, the era's best evidence for what the radicals actually did; Constantinople 360 (the Homoian settlement the world lived under); **Constantinople 381** and its creed; the imperial communion law of 381 naming, among the East's touchstone bishops, Helladius of Caesarea and Gregory of Nyssa (Documented at work level — the world's own men made the empire's standard). **Basil's canonical letters** to Amphilochius: penances graded for murder, abortion, bigamy and remarriage, magic-use, grave-robbery, apostasy under pressure — the single best window this world offers on ordinary village sin and the church's discipline of it (Documented; used heavily in this build precisely because it is *not* elite self-description).
- **Imperial law and administration:** the reign-by-reign religious policy (Constantius' councils; Julian's school edict of 362 — Documented; Valens' pressure on Nicene bishops including the deposition and exile of Gregory of Nyssa c. 376–378 — Documented via Basil's letters; Theodosius' settlement 380–381); the division of Cappadocia into two provinces (372) with its ecclesiastical fallout (the Anthimus of Tyana conflict; Basil's creation of suffragan sees including Sasima) — Documented via the letters and orations; tax and liturgy (public-burden) appeals in Basil's correspondence with governors (Documented) — the church visible as fiscal patron.
- **Liturgical evidence:** thin and mostly indirect, and this thinness is named as structural: worship is reconstructed from preachers describing it. What survives in-period: Basil's description of antiphonal night psalmody and congregational practice defended as the custom of the churches (in a letter answering Neocaesarean criticism — Documented at work level); the doxology controversy itself ("glory to the Father *with* the Son *with* the Holy Spirit" alongside "*through* the Son *in* the Holy Spirit") as evidence of live liturgical variety and its doctrinal weight; On the Holy Spirit's argument from **unwritten customs** (facing east, standing on Sunday, the threefold immersion, the words of invocation) — a catalogue of practices offered precisely because everyone knew them (Documented as his catalogue); baptismal exhortations (Basil's protreptic to delay-prone catechumens; Gregory Nazianzen's oration on baptism) attesting Epiphany/Easter baptism culture and the delay problem; festal homilies attesting the panegyris pattern (see §4). **The "Liturgy of St Basil":** the transmitted Byzantine anaphora under his name is later in its surviving form; a Basilian core is argued in liturgical scholarship but is Dominant Modern Reconstruction at best — this build does not treat the transmitted text as the world's own worship.

## 3. Formation Narrative Sources (named assessment)

- **Life of Macrina** (Gregory of Nyssa, c. 380–383): eyewitness family memoir *and* deliberate philosophical hagiography (the Christian Socrates dying at Annisa). Tier 1 as Gregory's own account of events he witnessed (the deathbed, the funeral, the community's grief); Tier 3 register for its idealizing frame; the formation ideal it communicates (the household become a school of the resurrection; slaves freed to be sisters — its own claim about Macrina's community, work-level) is genuine formation-ecology evidence. Author Gravity: maximal — see §1.4.
- **On the Soul and Resurrection** (Nyssen): a philosophical dialogue staged at the same deathbed — genre: Christian Phaedo. Used as evidence of the world's consolation-culture and of how this circle *wanted* grief and hope to be held; not used as a transcript.
- **Oration 43** (Nazianzen's funeral encomium on Basil, c. 381/2): the world's central narrative monument — Athens friendship, famine leadership, the Basileias, the confrontation with the prefect Modestus and the emperor Valens. Genre: epitaphios/encomium with an explicit case to make (and a friendship to repair posthumously). Every celebrated scene it carries is Tier 1 *as Gregory's public telling within living memory of eyewitnesses* — a real constraint on invention — and simultaneously genre-shaped; the famous Modestus dialogue ("perhaps you have never met a bishop") is carried as the world's own treasured account, not as court stenography. (This split is enforced at Doc_09.)
- **De vita sua and the autobiographical poems** (Nazianzen): unique first-person formation narrative — ordinations resisted, friendships broken, the Constantinople mission and its wounds. Documented as self-account; the self-justifying retrospect is the named caution.
- **Life of Gregory Thaumaturgus** (Nyssen): hagiography at a century's distance — miracle cycle, the creed-vision, the mission narrative ("seventeen Christians when he came, seventeen pagans when he died" — the world's own founding legend, work-level). Tier 3 throughout: the formation ideal (the apostolic founder whose authority still warrants regional custom — Basil appeals to the Thaumaturgan inheritance explicitly) is the evidence; the biographical details are genre.
- **The funeral orations on Caesarius, Gorgonia, and the elder Gregory** (Nazianzen): the era's domestic sanctity portraits — a Christian physician at court; a married laywoman as saint (Gorgonia — one of the very few extended portraits of a married woman's piety in patristic literature, through her brother's pen); a bishop-father of heterodox past. Tier 3 idealization on Tier 1 family fact; used for the household-formation ecology with the male-mediation caveat standing.
- **Martyr homilies** (Basil on the Forty of Sebaste, on Gordius, on Julitta, on Mamas; Nyssen on the Forty and on Theodore the Recruit): preached at the shrines on the feasts, within a century of the deaths — the community showing what it believed a faithful death was. The Forty's cult: Documented as cult and text; the passion details traditional (Tier 2 as collected tradition); the family's own relic-connection to the Forty (deposited at the Annisa estate shrine; Nyssen's account of his mother's role — work-level) is Documented as the family's claim.

## 4. Material Culture and Daily Life Sources

- **What this build can honestly use:** the martyrium/shrine pattern of rural Anatolia (feast-day panegyreis combining liturgy, market, and crowd — attested in the homilies themselves, e.g. Basil preaching to festival crowds; Nazianzen on the Mamas festival at work level); the family estate-shrine at Annisa (Vita Macrinae's own description — text-attested archaeology, not excavated certainty); Caesarea's suburban **Basileias** complex (hostel, poorhouse, leprosarium, staff — Documented as an existing institution via Basil's own letters to officials about it and Nazianzen's Oration 43; called "the New City" in the latter; its physical remains are **not** securely identified — stated plainly); the imperial road-and-frontier context (Documented as regional history).
- **What this build refuses to use:** the celebrated rock-cut churches and painted programs of Cappadocia (Göreme etc.) — Byzantine, centuries later, and no evidence for this world; any detailed reconstruction of fourth-century Caesarea's urban fabric (evidence thin).
- **Social-historical background:** great-estate agriculture with vulnerable smallholders; recurrent subsistence crisis (the famine of c. 368/9 — Documented via Basil's famine homilies and later reference); Cappadocia as a byword slave-exporting region (Widely Accepted); literacy a small minority; the honor-economy of the Greek city and the curial class's flight from its burdens (tax appeals in the letters); the Armenian frontier's military traffic. Where material evidence is absent, the ecology's material conditions are acknowledged as real but thinly recoverable (Inferential/Thin), per the Framework.

## 5. Secondary Scholarship Assessment (the interpretive layer named)

The reconstruction is read through, and must not silently absorb: **Philip Rousseau** (Basil of Caesarea) — the sober, de-idealizing Basil; **John McGuckin** (St Gregory of Nazianzus) — the rhetorical self-fashioning caution; **Anna Silvas** (The Asketikon of St Basil the Great; Macrina the Younger) — asketikon textual history and the maximal-Macrina reading; **Susanna Elm** (Virgins of God; Sons of Hellenism) — the ascetic movement's female and radical breadth before its Basilian ordering, and the Nazianzen-Julian paideia contest; **Raymond Van Dam** (the Cappadocia trilogy: Kingdom of Snow; Families and Friends; Becoming Christian) — region, family, and friendship as the world's real machinery; **Andrea Sterk** (Renouncing the World Yet Leading the Church) — the monk-bishop model as this world's export; **Susan Holman** (The Hungry Are Dying) — the poverty homilies and the Basileias in civic context; **Jean Gribomont** (the Asketikon's history; Basil's debt to Eustathius); **Daniel Caner** (Wandering, Begging Monks) — the radical-ascetic and Messalian-adjacent background; **Lewis Ayres** (Nicaea and Its Legacy) and **John Behr** (The Nicene Faith) — the revisionist account of pro-Nicene theology that dismantles the older "Cappadocian settlement" textbook narrative; **Khaled Anatolios** (Retrieving Nicaea); **Brian Daley** (Gregory of Nazianzus); **Morwenna Ludlow** and **Sarah Coakley** (Nyssen's modern reception — a named risk: reading twentieth-century Nyssen back into the fourth century); **Peter Brown** (Power and Persuasion; Through the Eye of a Needle) — bishops, paideia, and wealth.
**Named debates carried at full strength:** (1) whether "Cappadocian theology" is one coherent settlement at all, and the older *ousia/hypostasis*-as-agreed-formula textbook story versus the Ayres/Behr line (Contested — this build must not let the Representative recite a nineteenth-century dogmatics summary); (2) Macrina's recoverability (maximal Silvas-line vs. literary-construction readings); (3) Basil's death date (379 traditional; 378 redating); (4) Basil's debt to Eustathius and how original the Basilian koinobion is (Gribomont's "reformed Eustathianism" carried); (5) the Philocalia's attribution (traditional Basil–Nazianzen compilation; now questioned); (6) how far Nazianzen's accounts of Sasima, Basil, and Constantinople can be trusted as history rather than apologia (McGuckin/McLynn-register caution); (7) the historicity texture of Oration 43's Valens/Modestus scenes; (8) the coherence of "Messalianism" as a movement (Caner); (9) how much Origenism the circle transmitted and how deliberately (the anthology question); (10) whether Basil deliberately withheld calling the Spirit "God" and "homoousios" (the *oikonomia*/reserve debate — in-period evidence exists on both sides: Basil's own caution, and Gregory Nazianzen's defense of it against ascetic critics impatient with it — Documented at work level; the dispute about his motives is Contested).

## 6. Source Asymmetry, Missing Voices, and the Article 20 Duty

**Overrepresented:** one extended family and one friendship network; metropolitan-level bishops; Athens-trained rhetorical prose; the winning side of every controversy the world fought; the ascetic movement's *reformers* (over its rank and file and its radicals); Caesarea and the Iris valley over the plateau villages.
**Underrepresented or structurally absent — named, with cause:**
1. **Women** — the world's most celebrated woman teacher survives only in her brother's literary frame (§1.4); Nonna, Gorgonia, Emmelia, Theosebia (honored in a letter of Nyssen's — work-level) are all men's portraits; the sisterhoods wrote nothing that survives. Cause: literary production and transmission gate-kept by male clergy; female ascetic literacy real (the Vita presents Macrina as Scripture-formed) but unpublished. Participant-facing absence (to be named in encounter), not merely noted here.
2. **The rural poor and the plateau countryside** — visible as famine victims, festival crowds, penitents in the canons, and objects of the poverty homilies; almost never speakers. Cause: genre and language — no channel transmitted peasant speech; partial against-the-grain recovery via the canonical letters (what sins village discipline actually processed) and the homilies' asides about their hearers.
3. **The enslaved** — pervasive (a slave-exporting region; households and even communities with slaves; Nyssen's homily attacking slaveholding as such attests the practice among his hearers; the Asketikon legislates for slaves who flee to the brotherhoods — counsel of amelioration within the institution, stated without laundering). Cause: total exclusion from literary production. The world contains, in Nyssen's homily, antiquity's sharpest surviving protest against slavery itself — and no voice of the enslaved.
4. **The ordinary brothers and sisters of the communities** — the Rules address them; the questions embedded in the Shorter Rules are the nearest trace of their actual voices (questions asked at community meetings, mediated by the answerer — against-the-grain value, used in this build). Cause: the legislator's genre absorbed the community's speech.
5. **The defeated parties** — Eunomius partially self-voiced (the exception, §1.5); the Pneumatomachians, the Homoian church of the region, the censured radicals of Gangra, the later Messalian-adjacent enthusiasts: all transmitted by enemies. Cause: doctrinal victory-selection plus (past the horizon) imperial book-burning (398 — outside the window, shaping what reached us).
6. **The non-Greek-speaking faithful** — attested as a regional reality (Widely Accepted); their church life unrecorded. Cause: Greek-only literary transmission.
7. **Children and catechumens** — process visible from the teachers' side (delay scolded, exhortations preached); experience unrecorded.
**Bounded reconstruction rule adopted for this build:** reconstruction of any of these voices is admitted only on (a) comparative evidence from similar communities, (b) material evidence, or (c) against-the-grain textual traces (a canon penancing an attested practice; a rule answering a community's embedded question; a censure list cataloguing the radicals' actual behavior; a homilist's aside about his crowd) — never from silence; always at Inferential/Thin; naming the absence takes precedence over filling it.

## 7. Forces Lens on the Source Ecology (Forces Framework Step 2)

- Sources speaking directly to **external forces:** imperial religious policy through its Cappadocian effects (exiles, depositions, the 372 provincial division, the 381 communion law); Julian's own letters and edict (the old religion striking at the school); the famine homilies (subsistence crisis as formation event); the correspondence with governors and prefects (the church negotiating the state); the frontier correspondence (Gothic-martyr relic letters at work level; Armenian church interventions).
- **Silences that are themselves evidence:** the near-total loss of the losing sides (Eustathius, the Pneumatomachians, the Homoian province, the radicals) shows which voices successor transmission found dispensable or dangerous; the thinness of direct liturgical text shows a world that transmitted worship by practice and custom — Basil's own argument from unwritten tradition is the world *naming this about itself* (a rare case of a world describing its own transmission pattern); Macrina's textlessness shows the gender gate. All carried into Doc_08.
- **Survivorship pattern:** transmission ran through (i) the family-and-friends network's own curation (Gregory Nazianzen editing his letters; Nyssen completing and defending his brother's works — the world's archive is partly a family memorial), (ii) Byzantine dogmatic-liturgical canonization (the Three Hierarchs' prestige), (iii) monastic institutional interest (the Asketikon as law), and (iv) polemical preservation (opponents inside refutations). What survives is what those four channels valued.

## 8. Provisional Story Tiers (begun here; finalized in Doc_09)

Tier 2: the Forty Martyrs of Sebaste (homilies within a century; tradition authentic, details traditional; the family relic connection). Tier 3: the Thaumaturgus cycle (creed-vision, mission legend); Naucratius' death in the wilderness accident (Vita — family memory, genre-shaped); Macrina's refusal of remarriage after her betrothed's death (Vita). Tier 4 (to be built with full element-sourcing): a day in a brotherhood of the Iris valley; a panegyris at a rural martyr-shrine; a famine month at the Basileias. **No Tier 5 anywhere.**

## 9. Confidence Map (world-level, provisional)

- **Documented:** the existence, works, and broad careers of the major voices; the major public events (Nicaea, Gangra's canons, Julian's edict, the 372 division, Nyssa's exile, Constantinople 381, the communion law); the institutional forms (episcopate, chorepiscopoi, canonical discipline, martyr festivals, the Basileias' existence, the brotherhoods' existence).
- **Widely Accepted:** the formation-ecology pattern of Doc_01 §1–2 as a coherent world; the transmission asymmetries; the ascetic movement's Eustathian roots; the region's social-economic profile.
- **Dominant Modern Reconstruction:** the ending boundary; the Basilian-core reading of the transmitted anaphora; the Sterk-line monk-bishop synthesis as the world's distinctive resolution; Holman-line civic reading of the poverty preaching.
- **Contested (carried):** the "Cappadocian settlement" historiography; Macrina's recoverability; Basil's death date; the Eustathius debt; the Philocalia attribution; Nazianzen's self-accounts as history; Oration 43's scene-level historicity; Messalianism's coherence; the reserve/oikonomia motives; Gangra's date; the Address to Origen's attribution.
- **Inferential/Thin:** everything reconstructed for the structurally absent voices; village and non-Greek church life; the communities' lived compliance with the Rules; period liturgical detail beyond the attested customs; the interior experience of any non-author; the Basileias' physical form.
