## 1. Candidate identification

Atlas V.6, "The Hussite and Bohemian Brethren Movement," 1415–1517, Bohemia. Jan Hus preached reform in Czech for over a decade at Prague before being burned as a heretic at the Council of Constance in 1415. The shock turned a university movement into a national one, organized around a single practical demand — that ordinary lay people, not only priests, should receive the wine as well as the bread at communion (utraquism). Hussite armies repelled crusade after crusade through the 1420s–30s, and in 1436 Bohemia won the Compactata, formal recognition of Utraquist practice no other medieval dissenting movement secured. A stricter, pacifist branch, the Unity of the Brethren, printed what is often called the first hymnbook in a European vernacular by 1501. The Unity was later renewed, after this world's own window closes, as the Moravian Church at Herrnhut in Saxony — real forward legacy, but outside 1415–1517's own scope.

## 2. Section A — Movement-Scope Eligibility

### A1 — The floor: clears, per the census's own already-recorded floorNote, with a correction to what "vendored and read" actually means

Quoted directly, not paraphrased: floorNote "No question." Hus's dispute with Rome is ecclesiological — can the church exist, and exercise real authority, without a corrupt or heretical pope — not creedal. **Correction (Revision 2):** Revision 1 described the dispute as partly "eucharistic-practical (utraquism)." This is wrong. Per Workman's own note in the vendored *Letters* (c. p. 177), the lay chalice was introduced by Jakoubek in the summer of 1414, and "Hitherto, Hus had taken little interest in the matter — in fact, in his *De Coena Domini*, written at a later date, he still practically concedes the Roman position"; he committed to it only late, from prison. A direct search of *De Ecclesia* for utraquism/"both kinds"/"sub utraque" found nothing on the topic. Utraquism became the movement's own defining practical demand (§1), but it was not Hus's own personal concern and is not part of what his own dispute with Rome was actually about.

**Correction (Revision 2), overstated reading claim:** Revision 1 said both works were "vendored and read this pass." Per each file's own provenance header, only "the opening pages and a mid-document sample" were checked — sampling, not a reading, across roughly 1.4 million combined characters. This is disclosed honestly elsewhere in this document (§4 item 1) but the A1 heading itself overstated it.

**Positive evidence, not merely absence of denial:** *De Ecclesia* itself engages Marian/incarnational language directly, if at second hand — in its own discussion of the Eucharist (c. p. 10), it quotes without dissent Paschasius's account of the body "which was born of the Virgin Mary, suffered on the cross and rose again." This is not Hus's own creedal affirmation in his own voice, and this document does not claim more than that — but it is a real, citable data point rather than the pure absence-of-complication reasoning Revision 1 relied on. A real Step 0/Doc_01 pass should still check the full corpus against all five of Article 4's commitments directly, including the movement's other strands (Taborites, Pikarts, the early Unity), which this pass did not touch at all.

### B3 — Uniqueness

**Versus the six already-built patristic worlds:** no overlap, over a millennium's separation. **Versus this batch's two other Era 6 candidates:** no figure or text overlap with Devotio Moderna.

**Correction (Revision 2): the Lollardy relationship is direct textual borrowing, not merely an indirect influence line.** Schaff's own translator introduction in the vendored *De Ecclesia* states it plainly: "Huss appropriated paragraph after paragraph from his predecessor and transferred them often with little verbal change to his own pages," drawing on Wyclif's own *De Ecclesia* and *De potestate Papae* — "Never did a man owe more to mortal teacher than Huss did to John Wyclif." The vendored *Letters* add a direct exchange: Letter VI, to the English Wycliffite Richard Wyche, read aloud in the Bethlehem Chapel. This is real text-sharing, not just a historical echo — and it means *De Ecclesia*'s doctrinal core is substantially Wyclif's own, drawn from a corpus Lollardy's own vendored Wyclif works (seven volumes) already represent. Separating this candidate from Lollardy is still the right call given how sharply their outcomes diverged — Lollardy stayed an underground, persecuted lay network with no institutional standing; Hussitism won the 1436 Compactata and became a functioning national church — but the earlier "indirect, not text-sharing" framing was wrong. Flagged for whichever Doc_01 runs for either world to engage the actual textual relationship directly.

### B4 — Audiences

The institutional and political register this Era 6 batch does not otherwise have. Lollardy survived as an underground network; Devotio Moderna is an interior devotional current working entirely within existing church structures. This candidate is neither — a movement that fought and won: real military victories against repeated crusades, a real legal settlement, a functioning state-recognized church. A distinct appeal for a participant drawn to the question of what a dissenting movement looks like when it actually succeeds institutionally, rather than surviving underground or staying devotional.

### B5 — Scale

Bohemia only — a single, compact region, narrower even than Lollardy's England-only claim. The Unity of the Brethren's later diaspora (Herrnhut, Saxony, the Moravian Church) is real forward legacy but falls outside this window's own 1415–1517 close, per §1 above.
