## 1. Candidate identification

Atlas V.6, "The Hussite and Bohemian Brethren Movement," c. 1402–1517, Bohemia. Jan Hus began preaching reform in Czech at Prague's Bethlehem Chapel in 1402, continuing for over a decade before being burned as a heretic at the Council of Constance in 1415. The shock turned a university movement into a national one, organized around a single practical demand — that ordinary lay people, not only priests, should receive the wine as well as the bread at communion (utraquism). Hussite armies repelled crusade after crusade through the 1420s–30s, and in 1436 Bohemia won the Compactata, formal recognition of Utraquist practice no other medieval dissenting movement secured. A stricter, pacifist branch, the Unity of the Brethren, founded in 1457, printed what is often called the first hymnbook in a European vernacular by 1501. The Unity's own later generations — including its 1632/33 constitution, the *Ratio Disciplinae* — fall outside this window and belong to the separate `czech-churches-last-century` entry (1517–c. 1627); its eventual renewal, after both windows close, as the Moravian Church at Herrnhut in Saxony is real forward legacy but outside either entry's own scope.

## 2. Section A — Movement-Scope Eligibility

**Framing note**, as for every candidate in this project's Step 0 documents: this document's "clears" are a considered signal, not a self-administered disposition.

### A1 — The floor: clears, per the census's own already-recorded floorNote

Quoted directly, not paraphrased: floorNote "No question." Hus's dispute with Rome is ecclesiological — can the church exist, and exercise real authority, without a corrupt or heretical pope — not creedal, and not eucharistic-practical (utraquism). Per Workman's own note in the vendored *Letters* (c. p. 177), the lay chalice was introduced by Jakoubek in the summer of 1414, and "Hitherto, Hus had taken little interest in the matter — in fact, in his *De Coena Domini*, written at a later date, he still practically concedes the Roman position"; he committed to it only late, from prison. A direct search of *De Ecclesia* for utraquism/"both kinds"/"sub utraque" found nothing on the topic. Utraquism became the movement's own defining practical demand (§1), but it was not Hus's own personal concern and is not part of what his own dispute with Rome was actually about.

Per each file's own provenance header, this pass's OCR-quality spot-check covered only the opening pages, a mid-document sample, and (for *De Ecclesia*) the opening of Chapter I — the headers describe how much was checked for scan quality, not how much was read. Beyond that spot-check, this pass ran targeted full-text searches (for utraquism/"both kinds"/"sub utraque" in *De Ecclesia*) and checked specific passages in context — p. 177 of the *Letters*, p. xxvii of Schaff's introduction, and pp. 18 and 84 of *De Ecclesia* — across roughly 1.4 million combined characters. This is a targeted search-and-verify pass, not an exhaustive reading (disclosed further at §4 item 1).

**Positive evidence, not merely absence of denial:** *De Ecclesia* engages the creedal question of Christ's identity directly, in Hus's own words, not merely at second hand. Discussing Peter's confession at Caesarea Philippi, Hus writes (p. 84) that Peter "confessed Christ to be very God and very man." Discussing Christ as head of the church, Hus writes (p. 18) that "Christ is the individual, the true God and man, imparting spiritual life and motion to the church." Both passages sit in Hus's own body text, not in a translator's footnote or an editorial gloss. This is not a full treatment of Article 4's five commitments, and this document does not claim more than that — but it is a real, citable data point in Hus's own voice, not mere absence-of-complication reasoning. A real Step 0/Doc_01 pass should still check the full corpus against all five of Article 4's commitments directly, including the movement's other strands (Taborites, Pikarts, the early Unity), which this pass did not touch at all.

## 3. Section B — Seed Screening and Tiering

### B1 — Sourcing: two tradition-role works in Hus's own voice, now squarely inside this world's own window, plus six context-role works; real gaps remain on the Unity's own voice and post-1436 institutional history

Per the corpus-map, directly host-verified: *The Letters of John Hus* (Workman & Pope, 1904, spanning June 1408 to his final letters of June–July 1415) and *De Ecclesia. The Church* (Schaff, 1915, dated 1413), his central ecclesiological treatise, the work whose condemnation at Constance led directly to his execution. Under this world's own c. 1402–1517 window (§0/§1 above), both works fall squarely inside the window — Hus's preaching career began in 1402 — rather than predating it; his own writings across his whole career at Bethlehem Chapel, not only his final years, are this world's own-voice core.

Six further works are vendored, all filed `role: context`, not `tradition`: Piccolomini's *Historia Bohemica* (1592 Latin printing, a contemporary hostile source usable as primary evidence directly under Mark's Latin-source ruling, though filed context here — a question for Doc_02); three Lützow histories (*The Life & Times of Master John Hus*, 1909; *The Hussite Wars*, 1914; *Bohemia: An Historical Sketch*, 1920); and Gillett's *The Life and Times of John Huss*, Vol. II (1871) — all secondary. The Unity of the Brethren's own *Ratio Disciplinae* (1632/33, ed. Seifferth 1866) is also vendored and filed `role: context`: per the project lead's 2026-09-25 ruling, it falls outside this world's own c. 1402–1517 window and belongs instead to whichever Doc_02 eventually builds the separate `czech-churches-last-century` entry.

Real gaps remain. In tradition-role, own-voice terms, nothing vendored speaks directly for the Utraquists' own later practice, the Taborites, the 1420 Four Articles, or the 1436 Compactata itself — §2 A3 and §3 B4 rely on these as historical fact, narrated by the context-role secondary works (Lützow, Gillett, Piccolomini) rather than voiced by a tradition-role primary source. No Unity of the Brethren tradition-role material from inside the window (a confession, a catechism, or the 1501 hymnbook itself — see §1's own hedge on the "first vernacular hymnbook" claim) was found this pass; a broad search returned nothing, though a targeted search by the hymnbook's own Czech title, or a check of later Comenius-era editions that might quote it, could still turn something up.

The census's own `sourcing` field reads "Rich: confessions, hymnody, discipline records" and names primary material this document did not engage: Peter of Mladoňovice's eyewitness *Relatio*, the Four Articles/Taborite texts (in Fudge 2002), Unity confessions/discipline/hymnody, and Spinka's 1972 Letters edition. Most of this is modern and in-copyright, not vendorable — the honest framing is that this richer material *exists but isn't public-domain or vendored*, not that it may not exist at all.

A short 1662 English printing of Hus's own appeal against his excommunication was checked and closed — its OCR is unusable garbage throughout, not genuine legible text, the same failure mode this project has precedent for rejecting elsewhere.

### B2 — Ecology

Two works in Hus's own voice, tradition role, spanning epistolary and systematic-doctrinal registers — real range in genre, genuinely single-figure in named tradition-voice terms, the same shape this candidate's own dossier already names (closer to the Anabaptists' Menno Simons pillar than to a thin candidate, but without Menno's own second and third named voices, Dirk Philips and the collective Schleitheim Confession). The movement's later, stricter Unity of the Brethren branch — its own distinctive pacifism, its 1501 hymnbook — currently has zero tradition-role primary-source representation inside this world's own window; its own later institutional voice, the *Ratio Disciplinae*, is vendored but filed context and sits outside the window regardless, per §0/§1 above.

### B3 — Uniqueness

**Versus the six already-built patristic worlds:** no overlap, over a millennium's separation. **Versus this batch's two other Era 6 candidates:** no figure or text overlap with Devotio Moderna.

The Lollardy relationship is direct textual borrowing. Schaff's own translator introduction in the vendored *De Ecclesia* states it plainly: "Huss appropriated paragraph after paragraph from his predecessor and transferred them often with little verbal change to his own pages," drawing on Wyclif's own *De Ecclesia* and *De potestate Papae* — "Never did a man owe more to mortal teacher than Huss did to John Wyclif." The vendored *Letters* add a direct exchange: Letter VI, to the English Wycliffite Richard Wyche, read aloud in the Bethlehem Chapel. This is real text-sharing, not just a historical echo — and it means *De Ecclesia*'s doctrinal core is substantially Wyclif's own. Lollardy's own vendored Wyclif corpus now represents that source directly: eight Wyclif-authored works filed `role: tradition`, plus the Wycliffite Bible, including Wyclif's own *Tractatus de Ecclesia* (ed. Loserth, 1886, `wyclif_de-ecclesia-lat_loserth1886.txt`) — the very treatise Schaff names as Hus's main source, now sitting in Lollardy's own corpus alongside Hus's derivative version in this one. Separating this candidate from Lollardy is still the right call given how sharply their outcomes diverged — Lollardy stayed an underground, persecuted lay network with no institutional standing; Hussitism won the 1436 Compactata and became a functioning national church. Flagged for whichever Doc_01 runs for either world to engage the actual textual relationship directly.

### B4 — Audiences

The institutional and political register this Era 6 batch does not otherwise have. Lollardy survived as an underground network; Devotio Moderna is an interior devotional current working entirely within existing church structures. This candidate is neither — a movement that fought and won: real military victories against repeated crusades, a real legal settlement, a functioning state-recognized church. A distinct appeal for a participant drawn to the question of what a dissenting movement looks like when it actually succeeds institutionally, rather than surviving underground or staying devotional.

### B5 — Scale

Bohemia only — a single, compact region, narrower even than Lollardy's England-only claim. The Unity of the Brethren's later diaspora (Herrnhut, Saxony, the Moravian Church) is real forward legacy but falls outside this window's own c. 1402–1517 close, per §1 above; so does the intervening generation covered by the separate `czech-churches-last-century` entry (1517–c. 1627).
