## Quick Meaning

Sin in this world is not primarily a list of wrong acts requiring forgiveness — it is the soul's fundamental mis-orientation, the condition of having turned away from God, who is the soul's created telos, toward lesser goods that cannot fulfill the capacity the soul was made with; individual wrong acts are expressions of this condition, not its primary meaning.

---

## World Meaning

The Greek word is hamartia, and it means missing the mark. Not breaking a law. Not incurring a debt. Not violating a contract. Missing the mark — failing to arrive at what the soul was aimed at, directed at, created for. The metaphor is from archery, and it is exact: the arrow that misses the mark is not morally guilty of anything. It has simply failed to arrive at what it was sent toward.

The image carries the whole Alexandrian account if it is taken seriously. The soul is an arrow. The image of God in the soul (alexlex009) is what constitutes the soul as an arrow pointed toward God — made for, capable of, aimed at genuine relationship with and participation in the divine life. Sin is the condition of this arrow no longer flying toward its mark: the soul turned away from God, pointed toward lesser goods, no longer moving in the direction it was created to move. The image remains — the arrow is still an arrow — but its direction has been distorted.

This is not first an act. It is a condition. Individual sinful acts — lying, violence, greed, contempt, lust, pride — are expressions of the underlying condition of mis-orientation. The soul that is pointed away from God produces acts that express where it is pointed. These acts matter; they deepen the condition, habituate the soul further in its wrong orientation, and make the re-orientation of formation more difficult. But addressing individual wrong acts without addressing the underlying condition is like correcting the trajectory of an arrow while it is in flight without correcting the direction the bow is pointing. The correction will not hold.

The soul turned away from God is not turned away from nothing. The soul has the capacity for God — the image of the Logos that constitutes the soul's deepest nature (alexlex009) — and this capacity directs the soul's desire toward something. When the soul turns away from God, it does not stop desiring. It desires the wrong things: not the genuine good that satisfies the image's capacity, but goods that are real but lesser — pleasure, power, security, reputation, the love of those who will die, the approval of those who are themselves distorted. These goods are not nothing. They are genuine goods. But they cannot satisfy what the soul was made to desire, and the soul that pursues them as if they were the ultimate good never arrives at what it is most deeply seeking.

This is the condition formation addresses. Not by cataloguing and correcting individual wrong acts — though that is part of it — but by re-orienting the soul toward its created telos: toward God, toward the genuine good the soul was made for, toward the participation in divine life that the image's capacity was created to enable. Formation is not moral instruction. It is the re-direction of desire — which is why the formation practices of this ecology (fasting, prayer, community belonging, Scripture encounter) are practices of desire-reorientation, not merely practices of behavioral regulation.

The Image of God entry (alexlex009) establishes that the image is damaged but not destroyed by sin. This is the critical conviction that makes formation possible rather than futile. A soul whose image of God had been entirely destroyed by sin could not be formed toward God — it would need to be re-created from nothing. But the image is wounded, not absent. The capacity for God that sin has misdirected is the same capacity that formation can, with grace, re-orient. The freedom (alexlex013) that turned away from God is the same freedom that, under formation, can turn back. The mis-direction is not metaphysical damage that changes what the soul fundamentally is; it is directional damage that changes where the soul is aimed.

*Author Gravity note: Origen's account of sin connects to his speculative framework about the soul's pre-cosmic fall — a primordial turning away from contemplation of God that preceded embodied existence. The broadly shared Alexandrian account — sin as the soul's mis-orientation away from God in its present embodied condition, the image's distortion — is attested in Clement and Athanasius independently of Origen's pre-existence cosmology. This entry stays with what is broadly attested.*

## Ecological Function

Sin establishes the problem that the entire salvation arc addresses. Without a precise account of what sin is, none of the subsequent entries — Death, Resurrection, Restoration, Transformation — can be understood correctly.

**What the Sin entry anchors:**

- **The Death entry's account**: Death is the consequence of sin's specific condition. Because sin is the soul's turning away from God who is the source of life, death is the soul's progressive separation from that source. The Death entry picks up precisely what the Sin entry establishes.

- **Formation's purpose**: The formation ecology exists to address sin's condition — not by cataloguing sins and assigning forgiveness, but by re-orienting the soul toward God through the practices, community, and Scripture encounter that formation provides. Every formation practice is, at some level, a practice of desire re-orientation — of the soul being turned back toward the mark it was created to hit.

- **Repentance's formation function**: Repentance (metanoia — a change of mind and direction) makes sense in this account because sin is directional. The soul that repents turns — literally changes direction — and the whole formation ecology is what sustains that turning and progressively deepens it. Repentance is not a moment of self-accusation; it is the first move of a reorientation that the entire formation arc continues.

- **The connection to freedom**: The soul's genuine self-determination (alexlex013) is what makes sin possible — the soul genuinely turned away from God, not because it was forced to but because it was free to. This is why formation works with the soul's freedom rather than over it. Sin was the soul's exercise of freedom in the wrong direction; formation is the soul's exercise of freedom, with grace, in the right direction.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing**: The forensic Protestant frame — deeply embedded in modern English Christian culture and migrated even into secular moral discourse — understands sin primarily as wrongdoing. Sin is breaking a rule, violating a law, incurring a debt or guilt before a divine judge who requires payment. In this frame, God's response to sin is primarily judicial: the debt is forgiven, the guilt is absolved, the payment is made by Christ on behalf of the sinner. The sinner is acquitted, the account is settled, and the relationship is restored to legal standing.

This frame is not arbitrary — it has scriptural grounding and has served real theological purposes. But it frames the fundamental human problem as legal and God's response as judicial, which means that healing, transformation, formation, and participation are secondary (at best) to legal standing. The well-formed person and the newly forgiven person have the same standing before God in this frame; formation produces moral improvement, not a different ontological condition.

**World Hearing**: The fundamental human problem in this world is not legal guilt but directional mis-orientation — not the soul standing under condemnation but the soul turned away from God and thereby from its own deepest life and telos. God's response to this problem is not primarily judicial. It is medical, or more precisely, it is pedagogical and transformative: the soul that has turned away from its source of life is being turned back, re-oriented, healed, formed, and drawn progressively into the participation it was always created for.

The forensic frame fails not because it is wrong about everything but because it mislocates the primary problem. The soul that has been forgiven but not re-oriented is still aimed away from God; the forgiveness changes its legal standing without changing its direction. The Alexandrian account holds that what God is doing in salvation is changing the soul's direction — not just issuing an acquittal — and that changing the soul's direction is exactly what the formation ecology exists to accomplish and what the whole arc of sin, death, resurrection, restoration, and transformation is about.

## Key Sources

- **Clement of Alexandria**, *Paedagogus*, Book I; *Stromata*, Books II, IV — Sin as the soul's turning away from wisdom and toward lesser goods; formation as the re-orientation of desire toward the genuine good.
- **Athanasius**, *On the Incarnation*, chs. 3–6 — The human person's turning away from contemplation of God; the progressive dimming of the image; the soul drifting toward non-being by turning from Being.
- **Origen**, *On First Principles*, Book III, ch. 1 — Sin in relation to the soul's freedom; the soul's genuine self-determination as the condition that makes sin possible and formation necessary. [Author Gravity note: Origen's pre-existence cosmology is his specific contribution; the broadly shared account of sin as the soul's mis-orientation is attested independently in Clement and Athanasius.]
- **Romans 3:23** (scriptural) — "All have sinned and fall short of the glory of God." The formulation of sin as failing to arrive at the telos (glory/doxa) the soul was created for.
- **Genesis 3** (scriptural) — The narrative of the soul's turn away from God; the fundamental scriptural account of the sin-condition, read in this ecology as the paradigmatic mis-orientation rather than primarily as a legal violation.
