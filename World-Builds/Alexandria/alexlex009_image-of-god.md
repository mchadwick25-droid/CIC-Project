## Quick Meaning

The Image of God is the indelible mark in every human being that makes formation possible — not a quality that can be earned or lost, but the ontological ground of the soul's capacity for God, damaged by sin but never destroyed, which the entire formation ecology exists to restore and fulfill.

---

## World Meaning

Everything that follows in human anthropology — the soul's structure, the nous's capacity, the soul's freedom, the possibility of transformation — rests on one foundational conviction: human beings are created in the image of God.

This is not a poetic way of saying that human beings are impressive among the animals, or that they have certain rational capacities that other creatures lack, or that they are uniquely dignified and therefore deserve moral consideration. The Image of God in this world names something more precise and more astonishing: the human person is constituted by a real likeness to God, given in creation, that makes genuine relationship with God possible — not relationship in the sense of communication between two parties at a distance, but the kind of relationship in which the creature can genuinely receive and participate in the life of the Creator.

The image is specifically the image of the Logos (alexlex001). Human beings are not created in the image of God-in-general, as if God were an undifferentiated divine substance. They are created in the image of the eternal Word — the one through whom all things were made, who is himself the Image of the Father. This means that the capacity for reason, for love, for wisdom, for contemplative perception of divine reality, for genuine relationship with God — these are not accidental human features, not incidental gifts added to what human beings are. They are what the image of the Logos looks like in a creature. To be human is to be constituted by a likeness to the Logos, which is to say, to be constituted by a capacity for the very reality that the Logos is.

The Genesis text (1:26–27) is read here with theological precision: "Let us make humankind in our image, according to our likeness." Two words, and they are not synonyms. The image (eikon) is what human beings are by creation — the indelible ontological mark. The likeness (homoiosis) is what human beings are becoming through formation — the goal of restoration. The image is given; the likeness is the image fulfilled. This distinction is load-bearing for the entire formation ecology, and it must not be collapsed. The Image of God entry establishes the foundation; the Likeness of God entry (alexlex012) establishes the goal.

The fall means the image is damaged, distorted, obscured — but not destroyed. This is the critical conviction that makes formation possible rather than creation from scratch. A soul whose image of God had been entirely destroyed could not be formed toward God; it would need to be created again from nothing, given new capacities it had never had. But the image is wounded, not absent. The soul that has turned away from God retains the very capacity for God that it has misdirected. The freedom with which the soul turned away from God is the same freedom with which — under formation, through grace — it can turn back. The rational capacity with which the soul has pursued lesser goods is the same rational capacity that can be illumined to perceive the highest good. Formation is not the production of something new from nothing. It is the restoration and fulfillment of what has been present and damaged.

This is why Clement can speak of formation as restoring the soul to its archetype — the image being progressively clarified and fulfilled until it reflects what it was always created to reflect. And it is why Athanasius can frame the incarnation as God entering what God created: the Logos who is the image in whose likeness humanity was made enters human nature, and from within human nature begins the work of restoration that human nature was made for but could not accomplish on its own.

*Author Gravity note: Clement and Origen both develop Image theology, with Origen's account being the more systematic and speculative. The broadly shared conviction — the image as the ontological ground of formation — is Widely Accepted. Origen's specific account of the image's pre-cosmic condition, the soul's fall from original contemplation, and the image's eventual full restoration belongs to his speculative framework, which connects to the contested Apokatastasis and Fall/Descent entries. This entry stays with what is broadly attested: the image is given in creation, damaged by sin, and what formation restores.*

## Ecological Function

The Image of God is the foundational anthropological claim that every other entry in this cluster presupposes. Without it, the anthropological stack has no ground.

**What the Image of God anchors:**

- **The possibility of formation**: Formation is only intelligible because the image is damaged but present. Catechesis forms the person who already has the capacity for formation; illumination reaches the nous that is already, by nature, oriented toward divine reality; wisdom becomes visible in a person whose image was never fully extinguished. The whole formation ecology is the image being progressively restored and fulfilled.

- **The soul as formation subject**: The soul (alexlex010) is the subject of formation because the soul is the image-bearer. What the soul is, in this world, is a being constituted by the image of the Logos — oriented toward God by nature, damaged by sin, being restored through formation.

- **The nous as the image's highest expression**: The nous (alexlex011) is the highest human faculty because it is the faculty most directly in the image of the Logos — the capacity for direct perception of divine reality that reflects the Logos's own nature. The nous is where the image is most legible, most damaged by sin, and most directly restored by formation.

- **The likeness as the image's goal**: If the image is the given, the likeness (alexlex012) is what the image is moving toward. The whole formation sequence — catechesis, illumination, gnosis, wisdom, participation — is the image being progressively conformed to the likeness of the God in whose image it was made.

- **The connection to Logos-Centered Unity**: The image grounds the Logos-Centered Unity Supporting Gravity's anthropological dimension. The reason the Logos is the integrating center of the ecology is not merely theological — it is anthropological: human beings are made in the image of the Logos, which means every genuine human capacity for truth, love, wisdom, and communion with God is a reflection of the Logos. The ecology coherces around the Logos because human nature is itself Logos-shaped.

## Distortion Risk

**Modern Hearing**: "Image of God" in modern usage typically functions as a moral and political claim: human beings have intrinsic dignity, value, and rights because they are made in God's image. This is not wrong, but it is downstream of what the Alexandrian account means. On the modern reading, the image is primarily a ground of moral status — what gives human beings the dignity that demands ethical treatment. It says something about how human beings should be treated, not primarily about what human beings are constituted by or what they are capable of.

**World Hearing**: The image of God is not primarily a basis for human dignity (though dignity follows from it). It is the ontological ground of the soul's capacity for God — the reason why formation is possible, why the soul can be illumined, why gnosis is achievable, why participation in divine life is real rather than fictional. The image says something about what human beings are in their deepest structure: they are constituted by a likeness to the Logos that makes them capable of genuine relationship with and participation in the divine life. The moral and political implications are real, but they follow from an anthropological conviction that is prior: the soul is made for God, and the image is what being-made-for-God looks like in a creature.

## Key Sources

- **Genesis 1:26–27** (scriptural) — "Let us make humankind in our image, according to our likeness." The foundational scriptural locus for the entire cluster.
- **Clement of Alexandria**, *Stromata*, Book II, chs. 19, 22; Book V, ch. 14 — The image as the soul's constitution in the likeness of the Logos; formation as the progressive restoration of the image.
- **Origen**, *On First Principles*, Book I, ch. 1; *Commentary on Genesis* (fragments) — The image as the ground of the soul's rational nature and capacity for God. [Author Gravity note: Origen's speculative account of the image's pre-cosmic condition is his distinctive contribution; the broadly shared conviction about the image as formation's ground is separately attested in Clement.]
- **Athanasius**, *On the Incarnation*, chs. 3–5 — The image given in creation; the Logos entering human nature to restore what was being lost; the incarnation as the restoration of the image from within.
- **2 Corinthians 3:18** (scriptural) — "We are being transformed into the same image from one degree of glory to another." The progressive restoration of the image through formation.
