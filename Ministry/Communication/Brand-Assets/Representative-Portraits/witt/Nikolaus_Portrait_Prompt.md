## Part One — Research Brief

### Who is being pictured

Nikolaus is not one documented historical person. Per `witt_Representative_Identity_Decision.md`
(decided by Mark, 2026-09-19: "lets do nikolaus, it is unique and broader"), he is a sexton and
schoolmaster (Küster/Schulmeister) — a lay office, not an ordained one, standard across Lutheran
territorial churches for the whole 1517-1580 window, voicing this world's whole documented life as
"we," not a single life's years. The same category as five of the seven existing Representatives
(Theon, Marius, Yausep, Papnoute, Fidelis): appearance is silent and emblematic in the world's own
record, and must be grounded the same disciplined way — (1) this world's own documents first, (2)
time-bound and geography-bound external sources second, (3) only then, defensible diversity the
sources themselves actually support.

### Step 1 — What witt's own record establishes

- **The office itself, directly in the already-approved identity record.** `witt.voice.craft.md`'s
  own `identity` field states the office in full: "a sexton and schoolmaster, keeper of the parish,
  ringer of its bell, teacher of the town's children in their letters and catechism, and assistant
  at worship where the German hymns are sung." Two concrete, repeated activities are named
  directly, not merely implied: ringing the parish bell, and teaching children's letters and
  catechism. This is the strongest possible grounding for a held object — it comes from this
  world's own already-built, already-approved record, not from external inference.
- **Explicitly lay, not clerical.** The same record states plainly: "This office - lay, standard
  across the whole territorial-church window." No stole, chasuble, clerical gown, or any vestment
  implying ordained office belongs on this figure — a real accuracy line the other six portraits
  didn't have to hold as carefully, since none of them pictures a specifically *lay* office standing
  this close to (but distinct from) the pulpit.
- **No physical description of any kind.** No figure file, no gravity or force record, and no
  Representative-construction document anywhere in `records/witt/` or
  `World-Builds/Lutheran-Wittenberg/` describes hair, skin, build, or dress. Consistent with the
  whole portfolio's finding for every composite Representative to date.
- **Time and place, unlike any of the other six.** Witt's own 1517-1580 window sits eleven to
  fourteen centuries later than any other world in this portfolio (the other six all fall between
  70 and 451 CE). This is not a minor variation — it moves the whole visual register from
  late-antique Mediterranean draped dress to Northern-Renaissance German burgher dress, a genuinely
  different clothing and portraiture tradition, not a recolored version of the same one.

### Step 2 — Time-bound, geography-bound external sources

**Region and complexion.** Wittenberg sits in Electoral Saxony, central Germany — Northern
European, not Mediterranean. This is itself a first for the portfolio: all six existing
Representatives are grounded Mediterranean, North African, or Levantine/Parthian. A fair-to-medium
Northern-European complexion is the straightforwardly correct regional reading here, not a
diversity move in either direction — it is simply what this world's own geography is.

**The single best-matched external source available anywhere in this portfolio.** Lucas Cranach the
Elder worked as court painter in Wittenberg itself, through this exact span, and painted this exact
social world directly — reformers, professors, and ordinary Wittenberg townspeople, not a distant or
generic visual analogy the way the Fayum portraits or Segal's Edessa mosaics had to serve for
earlier worlds. Cranach's own portraits (Luther, Melanchthon, and Wittenberg's own burghers) are the
grounding source for hair, dress, and bearing here — better time-and-place matched than any external
source used for any of the other six.

**Hair and beard.** Cranach's Wittenberg portraits of this period consistently show men with
chin-to-shoulder-length hair (not cropped short, unlike the Roman-era convention researched for the
Mediterranean worlds) and a trimmed, moderate beard — a genuinely different convention from
antiquity's clean-shaven-or-philosopher's-beard choice, and not to be confused with it. A modest,
neatly trimmed brown beard and hair worn to about the jawline or collar is the well-attested,
period-correct reading for a respectable Wittenberg townsman of this office.

**Dress.** Because the office is explicitly lay, Nikolaus should read as a modest, literate
townsman — not a pastor, not a professor in an academic gown, not a wealthy patrician. Cranach's own
burgher portraits of this period and place show plain dark wool doublets and coats, a simple white
linen shirt visible at the collar (a plain band collar — the elaborate ruff is a later, more
southern/courtly convention, not yet standard in 1520s-1540s Saxony, where this Representative's own
sure-handed years run thickest), and often a flat, dark wool cap (a barett). No fur trim (too
wealthy for this office's own modest social standing), no elaborate embroidery, no gold thread, no
jewelry (the locked style rule's own standing prohibition, doubly appropriate to a lay office with
no ornament of rank to display).

### Step 3 — Defensible diversity, not a default

No further diversity move is invented beyond what Steps 1-2 already ground: a fair-to-medium
Northern-European, central-German townsman is the sources' own supported reading for this
specific world's own specific place, not a default carried over from the portfolio's other six
Mediterranean-grounded worlds and not an unsupported departure from what Cranach's own contemporary
portraiture actually shows.

## Part Two — The Held Object: Comparative Reasoning

Per the isolated-portrait two-state rule, the object is held at the chest, fully visible in both
hands — not resting on a table, not a hinted-at edge detail. Checked against silhouette-collision
with all seven existing images: Chloe's cup, Theon's opened scroll, Yausep's closed codex, Marius's
scroll-case, Papnoute's palm-frond rope-work, Albina's tablet-and-stylus, and Fidelis's own bare
hands holding nothing.

### Candidate (a): The household catechism, as a book — REJECTED, collides with three existing objects

G4 (the household catechism) is this world's own confirmed Primary gravity, and Nikolaus's own
teaching duty ("catechism to the young") sits directly on it. But a book, codex, or bound
catechism collides directly with the object system's own silhouette-recognition purpose: three of
the seven existing Representatives already hold a book-family object (Theon's scroll, Yausep's
codex, and — at one remove — Marius's scroll-case). A fourth would blur the very distinction this
system exists to preserve, exactly the reasoning that ruled out Fidelis's own first-choice codex
against Yausep. Rejected on the same structural ground, not on any weakness in the catechism's own
grounding.

### Candidate (b): A German hymnal — REJECTED, same collision, and thinner grounding

Nikolaus's own identity record names "assistant at worship where the German hymns are sung" (G11).
A hymnal is well-grounded in principle, but it is still a bound, written-text object — the same
collision as candidate (a) — and hymn-singing is explicitly the *congregation's* own act in this
world's record, not a leader's solo performance from a held text the way a lector's scroll or a
teacher's catechism might be. Rejected on both grounds.

### Candidate (c): A large iron church key — considered, set aside in favor of (d)

"Keeper of the parish" in Nikolaus's own identity record echoes the literal root of both "sexton"
(Latin *sacrista*, one who cares for sacred things) and "Küster" (Latin *custos*, keeper/guardian) —
and a large iron key, carried by the parish's own custodian, is a genuinely well-attested visual
convention for exactly this office across Northern European church life of this era. It is
collision-free (no existing Representative holds anything key-shaped) and would read cleanly at
icon scale. Set aside in favor of (d) below for one reason: it represents only the custodial half of
a two-part composite office ("sexton-schoolmaster"), leaving the schoolmaster half — "teacher of the
town's children in their letters and catechism" — entirely unrepresented. Not rejected as
ungrounded; simply the weaker of two well-grounded candidates once a single object that speaks to
*both* halves of the office is available.

### Candidate (d): A hand bell — RECOMMENDED

Nikolaus's own already-approved identity record names him directly as "ringer of its bell." A hand
bell is collision-free against all seven existing objects (nothing else in the portfolio has a
rounded, downward-opening bell silhouette), non-anachronistic and well-documented for this office
and era (a parish sexton's hand bell, used both to call the town's children to the schoolroom and to
summon the congregation to worship, was ordinary equipment for exactly this lay office across
16th-century German territorial churches), and — unlike candidate (c) — speaks to *both* halves of
the composite office at once: the same bell calls the school to lessons and the parish to worship,
the two acts Nikolaus's own identity record names side by side. Flagged honestly, in the same spirit
as Fidelis's own disclosed inference: the bell-ringing *duty* is stated directly in this world's own
approved build record, but no vendored primary-source text was separately re-checked by this
document for a specific bell description — this is a well-attested period convention for the office,
not a directly quoted textual detail the way Yausep's Diatessaron or Chloe's cup are.

### Disposition

**Recommended: a plain bronze or iron hand bell, held upright at the chest in both hands** — one
hand around the bell's own body, the other resting near the handle, as though caught in the
motion of ringing it. This is this office's own best-attested, most repeated, most collision-free
image: distinctly Northern-European in silhouette (a rounded bell shape, unlike any of the six
Mediterranean-world objects already in place), and the one object that speaks to sexton and
schoolmaster together rather than only one half of the title.

## Part Three — The Gemini Generation Prompt

A fine-art oil portrait, painterly and richly textured, of a German townsman of the Lutheran Reformation era, painted in the same style as a Cranach-period Northern Renaissance character study: visible canvas weave and grain throughout, fine cracquelure across the paint surface, warm layered brushwork rather than any photographic or digital smoothness. He is a man in early middle age, of fair-to-medium Northern-European, central-German complexion, with warm brown eyes, chin-length light brown hair worn loose to the jaw or collar, and a modest, neatly trimmed brown beard — the plain, respectable look of a 16th-century Wittenberg schoolteacher, not a courtier or a churchman of high rank. His expression is warm and open, as though caught mid-conversation with someone he trusts, gently engaged rather than stern, solemn, or mid-speech with an open mouth. His head, shoulders, and body face directly forward, squared straight on to the viewer at eye level — not turned three-quarters or angled to either side — and he looks directly out of the canvas at the viewer, making direct eye contact, so the whole composition sits symmetrically on a single central point of perspective. He wears the plain dress of a respectable but modest Wittenberg townsman: a dark wool doublet or coat in a muted charcoal or deep brown, with a simple white linen shirt visible at a plain flat collar (no ruff), and a small, dark, soft wool cap (a flat German barett) worn on his head. His clothing is entirely unornamented — no fur trim, no embroidery, no gold thread, and no jewelry of any kind: no rings, no chain, no pin. He holds a plain bronze hand bell upright at his chest, fully visible in both hands: one hand cupped around the bell's own rounded body, the other resting near the handle at the top, as though caught in the motion of ringing it to call the town's children to their lessons or the parish to worship. The background is a flat, plain, softly warm neutral tone with no scenery, no architecture, and no other figures, in keeping with an isolated character-study portrait. Compose the image as a wide horizontal landscape-oriented bust-up portrait, framed from roughly waist height up and centered directly on the figure, so the bell is clearly and fully visible in both hands at the chest rather than only hinted at along the frame's edge — do not crop tightly to the face or shoulders alone.
