1Scope and content

From the census entry · Steps 1 and 5

After the apostles died, Christianity had no buildings of its own, no clergy in any later sense, and no agreed list of Scriptures — it had houses. Believers gathered in a member's dining room or the back of a workshop, ate a meal together, read aloud whatever apostolic letters had reached them, and stayed connected to distant congregations by hand-carried correspondence and travelling teachers. Across these decades the shape of the church slowly emerged in practice rather than by decree. Who should preside, how to receive people through baptism, what to do when a member handed in his neighbours under interrogation, what to make of the fact that faithfulness kept costing people their lives. Ignatius of Antioch wrote to churches along his route while under armed guard on the way to his own execution in Rome; Justin Martyr, teaching in a rented room above a bathhouse in the same city, wrote down what a Sunday gathering actually looked like. And both were killed for it.

The tradition's defining character, as its own evidence presents it: argued, not assumed. Who leads, whether the body's suffering was real, whether mercy avails after failure, where the safe edge of public identity lies — these were live, community-defining, unresolved questions, held as the substance of belonging rather than problems awaiting resolution. The Representative speaks as a voice of this tradition; it does not resolve what the tradition itself did not resolve.

3Voices in the record

From the census entry

  • Ignatius of Antioch — bishop who wrote seven letters to congregations while being marched to his execution in Rome, c. 107-117
  • Clement of Rome — wrote from the Roman church to the Corinthians around 96 to settle a dispute over deposed leaders, the earliest Christian letter outside the New Testament
  • Polycarp of Smyrna — bishop who had known the apostolic generation, put to death at Smyrna around 155, his death recorded by his own congregation
  • Justin Martyr — a philosopher who kept teaching Christianity as a school in Rome and left the earliest detailed description of Christian Sunday worship
  • Most of these communities' hosts and householders are unnamed in the record — the gatherings met in the homes of people history did not bother to write down

4What organizes this tradition

Step 4 · Gravity Discovery — six-test discipline

A "gravity" is a force the evidence shows actually organizing the community's life. Each candidate was tested six ways and classed by how much of the record carries it. One candidate did not clear the bar, and is kept on record as a tested non-finding.draft

  1. Primary

    The translocal correspondence network.

    Three independent voices attesting the same behavior.

  2. Primary

    Eucharistic practice as the site of variation and convergence.

    Three genuinely distinct surviving orders — Didache, Justin, Ignatius — held apart rather than harmonized.

  3. Supporting

    Authority consolidation — bishop versus presbyter-college.

    Deliberately reclassified down from Primary because its specific resolution is Contested.

  4. Supporting

    State pressure and legal precarity.

    Scattered and uneven — not empire-wide policy.

  5. Supporting

    Martyrdom as meaning-response.

    Resting on exactly two data points — Ignatius and Polycarp — and explicitly not extended further.

  6. Tensional

    Boundary-drawing against rival movements — Marcionite, Valentinian, proto-docetic.

    The thinnest gravity evidentially, classified accordingly.

  7. Declined

    The household (oikos) as a technical meeting-unit.

    Did not clear the evidence bar; recorded as a tested non-finding — the method declining a claim modern historiography might have assumed.

Two internal strands, held apart

Strand A (Antioch/Asia Minor, Ignatian) argues toward a single episcopal office, treats the doctrinal and bodily boundary as the sharp front, and carries the martyrdom material. Strand B (Rome: 1 Clement, Hermas) argues toward a plural presbyteral college and treats post-baptismal mercy as the sharp front. The shared logic — argue rather than inherit — runs through both; the reconstruction refuses to collapse them.

5Confidence and contested ground

Constitution, Article 17 · applied at Steps 6 and 9

Every term and story in this record carries one of five labels, strongest to weakest. They are the Constitution's own words and they are never adjusted for who is asking.

Documented Widely Accepted Dominant Modern Reconstruction Contested Inferential / Thin

Where this record is contested, by its own account

  • The authenticity and date of the Ignatian letters — carried as contested, without dismissing the corpus or resting the record on it.
  • Whether leadership was consolidating toward a single bishop or a college of presbyters — reclassified down to Supporting for exactly this reason.
  • How typical any "network-wide" practice was — the evidence is occasional letters answering local trouble, and the Representative says so.

Standing on the Nicene Creed — the census's floor note

These communities lived two centuries before the Nicene Creed was written. What they confessed is part of the raw material the creed was later drawn from, not a departure from it.

6Declared limits

World Profile, Section 8 · Step 9

What this record says it cannot tell you. These are published with the same care as its strengths; a limit named is a claim you will not be handed by accident.draft

  1. The interior life of enslaved and non-literate members — the vast majority — is held as named absence, not filled with an invented voice.
  2. No confident claims about "typical" network-wide practice: the evidence is occasional letters answering local trouble, and the Representative says so.
  3. The period is archaeologically invisible; material-culture questions are answered from texts or not at all.
  4. The tradition's most vivid material concentrates in one contested-authenticity source (Ignatius) and one strand (A) — a consolidated disclosure the Representative itself carries.

9Legacy, and where to stand today

From the census entry

Almost everything on this map descends from these gatherings. The basic shape of Christian worship — assembling on the first day of the week, reading Scripture aloud, a shared table — was set here, along with the offices of bishop, presbyter and deacon, and the habit of reading apostolic letters in public until they became Scripture by use rather than by ruling. Their networks of hospitality between cities became the nervous system of the ancient church, and their correspondence — Clement, Ignatius, Polycarp — has been read ever since. The Didache, by contrast, was lost for centuries and recovered from a single surviving manuscript only in the 1870s.

Experience it today

  • The Dura-Europos house-church, Syria — an ordinary home converted for worship between about 233 and 256, the earliest identified Christian church building, preserved because a Roman defensive rampart buried it
  • Yale University Art Gallery, New Haven — the Dura-Europos baptistery wall paintings, probably the oldest surviving Christian paintings anywhere, are on display

Where a reviewer might press first

Published on purpose. These are the four questions the record's own authors would most want a scholar of the period to test.draft

  1. Is the Ignatian authenticity dispute weighted fairly — carried as contested without either dismissing the corpus or resting the tradition on it?
  2. Does the two-strand structure respect the evidence, or over-schematize it?
  3. Are the confidence labels calibrated the way the field would set them — especially the deliberate downgrades (authority consolidation to Supporting; boundary-drawing to Tensional)?
  4. Does the eucharistic diversity-first treatment (three orders, unharmonized) hold up?

And the live test: talk to the Representative. Ask it what it cannot know. See whether it answers like a witness or like a chatbot.
