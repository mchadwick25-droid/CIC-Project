The key inner journey

From the map to a chair: a visitor finds Theon

Eight beats, each drawn as a frame from homepage.html. Three annotations under every frame: what the visitor sees, what a screen reader announces, and which inherited rule is in force. The visitor here is someone who has never heard of the project and never asked for Alexandria — they find it by walking down the page. Nothing in these frames is invented: the text is the census's own entry for the Alexandrian Catechetical tradition and its Representative, Theon.

What the visitor sees

The rule in force

Hover = short, click = full (Full UX Design §2.4) — no third verb. The glimpse is role="tooltip", attached by aria-describedby only while shown; it never traps focus and Escape clears it. Trace-on-hover is the Atlas's existing behavior, kept; the 55% step-back replaces the Atlas's 18%, because on a page that is also the homepage the rest of the map must stay readable while one stop is examined.

About this tradition

Alexandria was the ancient world's great city of libraries and lecture halls, and Christianity there took the shape the city gave everything else — a school. Teachers received enquirers, catechumens preparing for baptism, and educated pagans curious about this new philosophy, and walked them through Scripture as a text with a surface and a depth…

Major voices

Pantaenus — the earliest teacher named in the tradition… · Clement of Alexandria — wrote for educated Greeks… · Origen — the tradition's towering figure…

What the visitor sees

A vellum panel slides in from the right — 440px, its own scroll, a gold-leaf rule on its edge. The map stays live behind it; the visitor can keep hovering other stops with the panel open. Top to bottom: portrait and name, title, tradition, dates and place, status; the teaser on a warm cream ground; then the two doors, before the long reading — because a visitor who already knows they want to sit down should never have to scroll past two thousand words to find the chair. Then the full entry: about, voices, legacy, where it stands on the Creed, lineage, visit today, sources.

What a screen reader announces

Escape closes the panel and focus returns to Theon's stop on the map. The URL is now #alexandria-catechetical — a place on the map that can be sent to someone.

The rule in force

Level-3 surfaces are panels alongside, never centered modals (§2.4, §9.1 DECIDED). On desktop the panel is non-modal (aria-modal="false"), because the map — the primary surface — must stay reachable; on phone it becomes a bottom sheet and is modal. The two hand-offs and their friction gradient are Mark's launch ruling of 2026-08-28, unchanged: the interview is the frictionless door; the Table seats and never auto-starts.

Beat 5 · scrolling the entryThe lineage, in words

Where it stands on the Creed

Nothing in this tradition's confession diverges from the doctrine the Nicene Creed later stated. Some of Origen's more speculative proposals — about the pre-existence of souls and the final restoration of all things — were disputed in his own lifetime and formally condemned by a church council three centuries after his death.

What the visitor sees

One madder button and one quiet underlined link. The button says what it does with a name in it. The line beneath says plainly what each door does and what it doesn't — nothing starts on its own. Both open the app in a new tab, so the map is still there when the visitor comes back.

What the site does not promise here

No seated Living Table scene, no Representative Modes, no multi-voice Table — none of that is live in the app yet (D0 note §2.D). The copy describes exactly the two behaviors the app has today: the interview lands in the room; the Table field seats one Representative and waits. The homepage only ever suggests one seat, the same rule the Atlas already keeps.

The rule in force

Madder is the system's action accent; gold-leaf is the witnesses' pigment — the button is madder, the panel's rule is gold (§2.1). "Come and join us at the Table" is used verbatim, once, as the doors' label. Participation verbs only: meet, ask, join.

What the visitor sees

Below 640px there is no canvas. The same DOM flows as the constitution's era-accordion: era heads on their grounds, stops as full-width tappable rows in chronological order, Eras III–X as one-line bands with their counts. There is nothing to pinch, nothing to pan, no horizontal scroll anywhere. First tap on a row opens the popover in place with a "Full entry →" action; the second tap, or that action, opens the bottom sheet with the whole entry and the same two doors.

The rule in force

Full UX Design §5.2 and §7.2 — "the map → era-accordion below 640px: a different form, not a shrink" — and §2.4's phone grammar: tap = the Level-2 popover with one "Full entry →" action; Level-3 = bottom sheet; tap-elsewhere dismisses; no long-press, no new verb. Every row is a 44px-plus target. This is the point at which atlas-v3's six rounds of pinch-zoom trouble simply do not exist, because the direction's phone form has no map to zoom.

What the visitor sees

Three things that make a map habitable rather than merely impressive. The year badge in the title plate always names the era under the viewport's center — "where am I" is never a guess. "Read as a list" strips the spatial layer on any screen and is remembered for that browser. And every entry's URL is a fragment — #alexandria-catechetical — so a stop can be bookmarked or sent to a friend and re-opens exactly where it was found.

What a screen reader announces

Polite announcement, on scroll: Era II · The Imperial Church Era · 312–451 CE. · Toggle button, pressed: Show the map.

The rule in force

WCAG 2.2 — 2.4.8 Location (AAA, adopted as binding for the map region in this direction's README §6), 2.4.11 Focus Not Obscured (the sticky plates never cover a focused stop; every stop carries a scroll margin), and the Atlas's own shareable-view discipline (deep links, replaceState, never history spam).

Where this journey could fail — named, for D2 to attack

  1. The visitor who wants to be told what to do. There is no "Start here" button above the map. The thesis plate's orientation line and the seven houses are the answer, and "Prefer a list?" is the fallback — but a visitor who reads nothing and wants a single button has to find a stop. This is the direction's largest bet and it should be tested with real first-time visitors, not argued.
  2. Search. With seven stops, the homepage carries no search; the full map does. The map study's own lesson — search-first beats browse-first when someone has a specific answer in mind — applies the moment the homepage carries more than roughly a dozen stops. A build rule, not a maybe: the next wave of traditions brings search onto the homepage plate.
  3. Hover on trackpad-less laptops and pen devices. Focus covers keyboard users and tap covers touch, but a pointer with no hover state (some pen and hybrid devices) gets neither the glimpse nor the trace until it taps. Acceptable, because tap still reaches the full entry; not ideal.
  4. Reading order on the wide stops. The origin band and the Church-and-Empire band span half the canvas; a low-vision visitor at 300% zoom sees them as ordinary cards (the canvas collapses to the list form under 900px CSS width), which is correct but loses the "everything hangs from here" picture. The words carry it; the picture does not.
  5. Two plates, one page. About and Get Involved are plates at the map's edge, not pages. A visitor arriving from a search result for "who runs this" lands on a map, not a paragraph. The skip link "Skip past the map" and the plate anchors mitigate; they do not remove the cost.
