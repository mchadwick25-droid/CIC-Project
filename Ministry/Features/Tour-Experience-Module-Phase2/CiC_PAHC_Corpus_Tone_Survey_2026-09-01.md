# pahc Corpus Tone Survey — 2026-09-01

**Status:** a real, saved record of a survey that RULING 6 (`CiC_Tour_PAHC_Worship_Service_V3_2026-09-01.md`) already cited as existing "(below)" — it did not, anywhere in the repository, until this file. Written up after an independent adversarial review flagged the broken cross-reference: every downstream claim resting on "the survey found X" was, until now, unverifiable by anyone who wasn't in the original conversation. This file is that record, compiled from the original work rather than reconstructed from memory.

**What prompted it.** Mark's question, after reviewing a first curated "encouraging" reading built from 1 Clement: *"so the whole trustworthness of this project is being transparent witness, not rucruitment or midigate to make it seem better. so this is a real pattern of the teaching of this movement it must then be accuratly presented."* The question behind that: is harsh warning/condemnation language in pahc's own pinned sources a rare exception one curated reading happened to cut around, or a real, recurring feature — and if the latter, is presenting only the warm vein, asset after asset, itself a form of the "recruitment, not witness" problem the project exists to avoid?

**Method.** Eight works read closely — two (1 Clement, Diognetus) read directly, chapter by chapter, in the main conversation; six (Ignatius's seven letters, Polycarp, Barnabas, Papias, Justin's First and Second Apology, the Shepherd of Hermas) surveyed by four parallel research passes, each reading the primary source XML directly (`cic/texts/anf01_apostolic-fathers-justin-irenaeus.xml` for all but Hermas, which is in `cic/texts/anf02_hermas-tatian-athenagoras-theophilus-clement-alexandria.xml`), classifying each chapter's dominant tone (warm/encouraging, harsh/warning/condemning, or neutral/expository), and pulling verbatim quotes for both registers. Scope explicitly excluded, and still excluded: Justin's *Dialogue with Trypho* (~75,000 words, apologetic dialogue with a Jewish interlocutor, different genre) and Irenaeus's *Against Heresies* (five books, systematic anti-Gnostic polemic, later in the window) — noted as a real scoping choice, not an oversight, at the time.

**A finding from the independent review that used this survey, worth stating up front:** Justin's *First Apology* — the tour's own spine source — was included in this survey's scope but its own anti-Jewish material (see below) was not surfaced clearly enough in the original synthesis to inform RULING 6's "internal vs. external harshness" distinction. That gap is corrected here.

## 1 Clement (read directly, full chapter-by-chapter pass)

Occasion: Rome's letter to Corinth, addressing Corinth's removal of several presbyters — a real institutional dispute. Roughly 40%+ of the letter by chapter count (the dispute apparatus proper, chapters in the forties through fifties, plus the closing prayer) is directly about that conflict or the repentance/reconciliation it calls for.

**Harsh material found and excluded from tour use:**
- Ch. XXXV's back half: a vice-list followed by a full Psalm 50 quotation addressed "to the sinner," closing "lest He tear you in pieces, like a lion, and there be none to deliver."
- Chs. XXIII–XXVII: resurrection hope via the phoenix legend — excluded per Mark's specific instruction to skip the phoenix chapters.
- Chs. XLIV, XLVI, XLVII, LI onward, and the closing prayer (Ch. LIX in this abridged ANF edition): direct dispute/reconciliation content, told elsewhere in pahc's own story records.
- Ch. XLVIII: opens directly on the dispute ("let us... put an end to this state of things").

**Warm material, used in RULING 5's curated reading:** Chs. XXXIII, XXXIV, XXXV (opening only), XXXVI, XLIX, L — good works, reward, blessings through Christ, the praise of love.

**Warm material, used in RULING 4's in-scene reading:** the salutation and Chapter XX ("The peace and harmony of the universe").

## Epistle to Diognetus (read directly, full chapter-by-chapter pass)

Occasion: an apologetic letter to a genuinely curious outside inquirer, not an internal dispute. Chapters II–IV (idol polemic, dismissive of Jewish practice as "folly" and "ridiculous") are harsh in a contemptuous register; Chapters V–VI ("the manners of the Christians," the soul-in-the-body analogy) are warm, universal, and among the most anthologized passages in all of early Christian literature for exactly that reason — not occasion-bound. Chapters XI–XII are widely regarded by textual critics (per this edition's own introductory note) as a later addition by a different hand. Not used in the tour to date; surfaced during the search for a "not as specific" alternative reading, ultimately not built.

## Ignatius's seven authentic letters (Shorter/Middle recension; Longer recension noted separately)

Overall: roughly 15–20% of chapters carry genuine condemnation/exclusion/violent-judgment language as dominant or co-dominant tone; the majority (60%+) are warm or neutral. Distribution is very uneven by letter.

- **Ephesians** (21 ch.): mostly warm; harsh material at V ("the wrath of God abideth on him"), VII ("ravening dogs"), XVI ("everlasting fire").
- **Magnesians** (15 ch.): almost entirely warm/neutral in the authentic text. The Longer recension inserts an extended OT-punishment catalogue here that is not in the Shorter text.
- **Trallians** (13 ch.): one of the two proportionally harshest — condemnation concentrated in Chs. VI, VII, XI (heresy, exclusion from the bishop). Explicitly says, of the very people it warns ("Not that I know there is anything of this kind among you," Ch. VIII) — the community is not currently guilty of what's warned against. No eternal-fire language, no shunning instruction, and (in the authentic text only) no anti-Jewish material. **Used complete and unedited in RULING 6.**
- **Romans** (10 ch.): intensely violent in imagery (Ignatius's own longing for martyrdom, "let me be ground by the teeth of the wild beasts") but entirely self-directed — not condemnation of anyone else. A separate register from the other six letters; not comparable to Trallians' harshness in kind.
- **Philadelphians** (11 ch.): moderate — schism/exclusion language in Chs. II–III, warm elsewhere. Longer recension adds "condemned to hell" language not in the authentic text.
- **Smyrnaeans** (13 ch.): the other proportionally harshest — sustained anti-docetic polemic Chs. IV–VII, including an actual shunning instruction ("keep aloof from such persons... in private or in public," Ch. VII) that Trallians has no equivalent of.
- **Polycarp** (8 ch.): warmest and gentlest of the seven — private pastoral counsel, essentially no condemnation language.

**Longer (4th-century) recension, checked separately:** consistently amplifies harshness beyond the authentic text — inserted OT punishment catalogues, explicit hell language, and (in Trallians XI specifically) anti-Jewish material ("those fighters against God, those murderers of the Lord," applied to Jews, not to the named heretics) with no counterpart in the Shorter/authentic letter. None of this is Ignatius's own words; none of it was used in the tour.

**A correction to the original synthesis, made during independent review:** Ignatius's *Magnesians* (Chs. VIII–X) and *Philadelphians* (Ch. VI), in the authentic Shorter text, do carry anti-Jewish material ("It is absurd to profess Christ Jesus, and to Judaize... Christianity did not embrace Judaism, but Judaism Christianity," Magn. X; those who preach the Jewish law called "monuments and sepulchres of the dead," Philad. VI) that the original four-way survey pass did not surface prominently. Trallians itself, in its authentic text, does not contain this — the "internal, not outside-group-directed" characterization holds for the specific letter used — but "Ignatius's authentic letters contain no anti-Jewish material" would be false, and RULING 6's phrasing came close to implying exactly that by contrast with Barnabas.

## Polycarp, Epistle to the Philippians

Predominantly warm and pastoral — praise, patience, restoration even for an erring presbyter (Valens). One sharply polemical chapter (VII, anti-docetic: opponents called "of the devil," "the first-born of Satan"), plus scattered brief judgment notes in otherwise-warm chapters. Not used in the tour to date.

## Epistle of Barnabas

The outlier. Roughly a third of its chapters are dominantly harsh, and — distinctively — a meaningful share of that is specifically anti-Jewish supersessionist argument, not merely "the ceremonial law is fulfilled": a chapter titled "Christians, and not Jews, the heirs of the covenant" (XIII); Israel called "the wretched [Jews], wandering in error" (XVI, "Jews" a translator-supplied word, bracket in source); an oracle read as Israel's own guilt for the crucifixion, "Let us bind the just one, because he is displeasing to us" (VI). **This is what RULING 6 excluded and explicitly declined to represent.** Separately and independently, `records/pahc/source/pahc.source.barnabas.md` already scopes Barnabas to chapters 18–20 only for this world's evidence base and states "the epistle as a whole is NOT a voice of this world" — a world-record-level exclusion basis that was available and not used in RULING 6's own reasoning, which instead reached for a "different kind of harshness" argument.

## Fragments of Papias

Overwhelmingly neutral historical/expository testimony (Gospel origins, millennial speculation, genealogical trivia) — one harsh passage (Judas's grotesque death, Fragment III), narrative rather than exhortation. Confirms harsh material is not universal across the corpus — genre matters.

## Justin Martyr, First and Second Apology

**First Apology** (68 ch.): the largest single bucket is neutral apologetic/prophecy-fulfillment argument. Eternal-fire/judgment language appears explicitly in roughly a quarter of the chapters, recurring steadily rather than clustering (VIII, XII, XV–XXI, XXVII–XXVIII, XL, XLIV–XLV, LII, LVII, LX–LXI, LXVIII) — a real, structurally recurring feature, not an aside. Warm material is also real and vivid: the moral-transformation catalog (XIV), Christ's teaching on mercy (XV–XVI), and the eucharist/care-for-the-poor material (LXV–LXVII, already used in this tour's spine).

**This survey's own gap, corrected here:** the First Apology also contains supersessionist material not flagged prominently in the original pass — Jerusalem's destruction read as deserved punishment for the crucifixion (Ch. XLVII: "the land of the Jews... was to be laid waste... as was predicted"); Jews described as having "not only" failed to recognize Christ "but even treated Him shamefully" (Ch. XLIX); Christians from the Gentiles called "more true" than "those from among the Jews" (Ch. LIII); "the senseless Jews" blamed, via demonic instigation, for the crucifixion (Ch. LXIII). **This is the tour's own spine source** — the same 68-chapter document RULING 4's in-scene reading, RULING 6's "internal vs. external harshness" contrast, and the tour's Register-Banner-Text all draw from or stand next to. That the tour's warm centerpiece (Chs. 65–67) is drawn from a document whose other chapters run this way was not disclosed anywhere in the tour document as of RULING 8, and is flagged as an open item there.

**Second Apology** (15 ch.): proportionally the most explicit and defensive statement of the eternal-fire doctrine in the whole survey — an entire chapter (IX, "Eternal punishment not a mere threat") exists solely to rebut the charge, already circulating in Justin's own day, that Christians use damnation as a scare tactic.

## The Shepherd of Hermas

If anything the most saturated text surveyed — two of its three books (Visions, Similitudes) are organized around literal taxonomies of the saved and the condemned (a tower built from accepted/rejected stones; sorted willow-branches). Vision 4: "better were it for them not to have been born." Similitude 9 alone is the single longest chapter in the work and runs almost entirely on a repeat "if you don't repent, X; but if you do, Y" structure. Countervailing and genuinely structural, not spin: nearly every harsh passage is explicitly and immediately paired with an offer of mercy and repentance — the text's own central figure is literally "the angel of repentance." Book Second (Commandments) is markedly gentler than Visions or Similitudes. Not used in the tour to date.

## Overall finding (restated, now with a citable record behind it)

Harsh warning/condemnation language is a real, recurring, and in several works (1 Clement, Barnabas, Hermas, Justin's Second Apology) structurally load-bearing feature of pahc's own pinned corpus — not a rare exception confined to one passage. It clusters around identifiable concerns: institutional unity and schism, doctrinal correctness (chiefly anti-docetic polemic), the limits of post-baptismal repentance, and — in Barnabas, in the Longer (inauthentic) Ignatian material, and in Justin's own First Apology — supersessionist argument against Jews specifically, which is a different and more serious register than the internal community-discipline harshness found in the authentic Ignatian letters.

That last point is the one this survey's original synthesis underweighted, and the one an independent review surfaced: the "Trallians is internal boundary-policing, Barnabas is the outward-facing kind, and they shouldn't be treated as interchangeable" distinction is real and defensible for the specific texts it compares — but it is not true that pahc's corpus as a whole, or even this tour's own spine source, is free of the outward-facing kind. Justin's First Apology has it. The Longer (excluded) Ignatian material has it. Presenting Trallians as "the" complete hard reading without disclosing that risks leaving a participant with the impression that internal boundary-policing is the hardest register this tradition's own sources reach — which this survey's own findings, read in full, do not support.
