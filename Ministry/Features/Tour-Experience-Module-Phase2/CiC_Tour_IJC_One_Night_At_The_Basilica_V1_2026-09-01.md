## Ijc / Marius — a siege, close to real time

**Anchors:** `ijc.story.vigil-in-basilica` (Tier 1, Class A — two independent near-contemporary sources: Ambrose's own letters/sermon from inside the crisis, and Augustine, an eyewitness in the same city, writing later) and `ijc.story.emperor-penance` (Tier 1 for its contemporary core; the famous dramatized scene is explicitly a later telling, held apart — see below).

## The correction this storyboard required, against my own first draft

My earlier sketch of this tour described "an emperor's mother wanting their church for a different creed" as if this were persecution by an outside power. **The record itself corrects that plainly**, in its own `modern_contrast`: *"for this stretch of the reign, the Homoian confession WAS the empire's own established church, and this is an intra-Christian contest over which confession the state itself backed, not church against a secular or pagan state."* This isn't a minor nuance — it's the actual shape of the story. Ambrose's side was not the empire's favored one that night. The tour has to hold that, not smooth it into a familiar shape it doesn't fit.

**A second correction, same principle:** the famous image most people already carry of this world — Theodosius stripped of his purple robes, weeping publicly before the congregation — is **not** part of the contemporary record. It's a telling by two church historians (Sozomen, Theodoret) writing a generation later, "written to edify." What Ambrose's own letter shows is quieter: a private demand for repentance, and the plain fact that the emperor complied. The tour stages what's actually documented and names the famous version as what it is, rather than dramatizing it as if witnessed.

## Beats

**Brief (4 beats)**

1. **Threshold — Facilitator.** Milan, likely 386 (the now-dominant scholarly dating; the record's own cited primary edition dates the same events to 385 — held open, not hidden). Not persecution by outsiders: an argument, inside the church, over which confession the empire itself would back.

2. **Inside — Marius, narrating; Ambrose's own words.** The imperial court wants one of Milan's basilicas for its own worship. Ambrose won't give it up. His own report to his sister: *"I cannot surrender the basilica, but I may not fight."* Soldiers stand outside. Nobody inside knows, night to night, whether the orders are to surround or to storm.
   *Visual: Sant'Ambrogio today — honestly captioned, since the standing building is an 11th-century rebuild; only fragments (some porphyry columns) may be original fabric. If a reconstructed interior is wanted instead, same Williamsburg-model grounding as the pahc room, built from real basilica architecture of the period, honestly labeled.*

3. **The singing — Augustine's own eyewitness words, verbatim.** *"The pious people kept guard in the church, prepared to die with their bishop... my mother... lived in prayer... At this time it was instituted that... hymns and psalms should be sung, lest the people should pine away in the tediousness of sorrow; which custom, retained from then till now, is imitated by... almost all... congregations throughout the rest of the world."* This is Augustine himself, in the building, describing his own mother among the people keeping watch. In the middle of it, Ambrose preaches the line this world kept: *the emperor is within the Church, not above it.*
   *Audio option: real recordings of the Ambrosian chant rite, still sung in Milan today — captioned honestly as a living continuation of the practice this crisis is credited with starting, never claimed as a recording of this specific night, since nothing from it survives.*

4. **Close — Facilitator.** The court doesn't take the building. No battle, no arrests recorded. Door to Marius for what came after.

**Extended cut adds**

- **The emperor's penance (390), staged as the record actually holds it — two tiers, kept apart.** After a riot kills one of his generals, Theodosius orders reprisal; soldiers kill a crowd at Thessalonica — "it is said," Theodoret's own hedge, "that seven thousand died," a figure not independently confirmed. Ambrose writes privately, in his own hand: *"I do have reason to fear. I dare not offer the sacrifice if you intend to be present. Is that which is not allowed after shedding the blood of one innocent person, allowed after shedding the blood of many?"* Theodosius submits to the demand. **That's where the contemporary record stops.** The tour then says so directly: *"The version most people already know — the emperor laying aside his crown, weeping his sin publicly before the church — comes from historians writing a generation later. It's been retold that way ever since. What Ambrose's own letter shows is quieter and harder: a bishop with no soldiers closed the altar to the man who commanded all of them, and the man submitted."*
- **Callinicum (388), the harder edge, same leverage turned the other way.** The same authority that closed the altar to an emperor also shields a mob that burned a synagogue — Ambrose stops the emperor's order to have it rebuilt and its arsonists punished. Named plainly, not softened: this world's own record calls this hypocrisy in its own central figure, not a story to be quietly dropped.
- **A closing note that doesn't resolve:** Chalcedon (451) — the same kind of authority, decades on, ending its own council on an argument nobody wins: Leo's Tome accepted with the acclamation *"Peter has spoken thus through Leo,"* then, weeks later, the same council grants Constantinople a rank Leo's own legates protest in the room and Leo formally rejects afterward. The tour doesn't manufacture a resolution this world's own record doesn't have.
