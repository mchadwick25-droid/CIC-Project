text: Common life of the place, not the teaching of any one voice. Sickness in the desert was nursed by
  neighbors. A man too ill to work was fed from other men's stores; the settlements kept their old alive
  for years past their strength, carrying water and bread to cells whose owners could no longer walk.
  Fevers came up from the valley; eyes failed in the glare and the smoke; the ordinary diseases of the
  country did not respect the boundary of the sand. When a man died, his neighbors washed and wrapped
  the body, said the psalms, and buried him in the sand or the rock near his cell, often before the day
  was out, as the climate demanded. Graves were plain and mostly unmarked. The practical shape of dying
  here was a village's shape, transplanted - neighbors' hands, quick burial, and the survivors dividing
  what little the cell held.
