THE SHAME OF EUROPE 


“ T WILL only say that these last weeks have been for me, as for thousands 
of Englishmen, days of trouble and rebuke and blasphemy.” So spoke 
Bishop Gore on October 15 at the Pannychidi in St. Sophia for the martyred 
Metropolitan, Chrysostom. 
Assuredly the time will come when, not only in London, but in Paris and 
Rome, men will hang their heads at the name of Smyrna. 
Think of the black shame, with which Europe has stained its soul! Four 
short years ago the unhappy Christians of the East went wild with relief 
at the news of the Turks’ surrender. What a welcome Constantinople— 


always Christian in the great mass of its people—gave the Union Jack and 


the Tricouleur as the ships of freedom steamed in sight of St. Sophia. Two 
millions or more of their blood and faith had been martyred by the red death 
or the white during the war. They had lived those latter days under the 
threat of a like extermination. Now the shadow had passed. The centuries 
of their helotage were over for ever. Their nations had risked everything to 
help the cause of liberty. Greek soldiers had won victories in Macedonia 
for Franchet d’Esperey. The Armenians of the mountains had drawn 
the Turks’ teeth. Both knew that their sin against their ancient tyrants 
was past forgiveness. If the Allies had lost, their punishment would have 
been swift, terrible, final. But the Allies had won, and they were safe. 
Small wonder that they crowded to cheer the victors. | 

In the four full years that have passed since then the Turk has been 
given licence and power to wring a vengeance from them such as beggars 
Timur or Genghis Khan. Asia Minor, unhappy homeland of civilization 
and Christianity, has been harvested of its gleanings, and is empty and 
Turkish at last. While the Allies’ ships lay off its quay, and Europe*wWatched 
curiously, if uneasily, Smyrna has been given over as a promised prize to 
the army which France and Italy boast to be their creation. 

Can anything in history equal the horror of the sack of the great Christian 
city? of the pitiful stream of myriads upon myriads of refugees who, pouring 
into it from up-country, sought saféty and found death? of the onrush of 
the Turkish hordes? of the four days’ pillage and massacre, and cruel, 
deliberate, torturing outrage ? of the firing of the Christian quarters, planned 
with cool calculation rather to blot out every remembrance that Smyrna, 
Giaour Ismir, had never been Turkish than to destroy the evidence of 
Kemal’s monstrous crime? of the huge mass of packed humanity rocking in

The stupendous infamy is past description, but the hideous manner of

he was paraded through the streets, mishandled unspeakably, and finally 
crucified outside his own cathedral. In strict accord with that exemplar, 
pillage and massacre reigned in Smyrna until it was judged time to burn 
out everything Christian left in it. | 

Now—as the Metropolitan Germanos cried aloud in St. Sophia, Bays- 
water—-nothing remains in Smyrna, for three thousand years the home of 
civilization and for two thousand years the home of Christianity, except 
ruins, dead bodies—and Turks. The Eye of Asia has become the Black 
Shame of Europe. 

How did it come about that France joined with Italy not only in equipping 
Kemal for his exploit, but in acclaiming it as her own triumph? Some tell 
us that she wills to wear the Kaiser’s mantle as hegoumen of the Moslem 


world. Others, that M. Poincaré was piqued because Great Britain vetoed a - 


French frontier on the left bank of the Rhine. Others, that M. Franktin- 
Bouillon had bartered her support for lucrative concessions. Others, again, 
that she suspected Great Britain to be determined to keep Constantinople 
and the Straits for her selfish self. 

The fact would appear to be that the Quai d’Orsay has played diplomati: 
poker with Downing Street—and has won. For that we do not care a 
burnt-out match: The thing which strikes us in the face as a shameful blow 
is that imperialistic rivalries—our own, if you will, as much as French— 
have steeped Europe in apathetic indifference to the infamy of the colossal 
crime which has been perpetrated in open day. How can France, the real 
France which we have loved so greatly, close her eyes to the shame which 
has fallen on Europe? The explanation, it would seem, must be searched 
for in something deeper than mere diplomatic rivalries. One half of France,

These~documents are patently of the first historical importance, but 
instead of attempting to forecast their probably profound and far-reaching 
effects, it will be well to consider their history and present significance. 

There exists, as far as we are aware, no record of an opinion given upon 
the validity of Anglican Ordinations by an Orthodox theologian before the 
_middle of the nineteenth century. Looking back from 1922 that seems a 
strange thing. Certainly in -the eighteenth and in the first half of the 
nineteenth c@nturies there was little intercourse between the two Com- 
munions, bit®there was a great deal in the seventeenth century. Under 
James I. and Charles I. many Greek visitors and students ' resorted to London 
and Oxford, among the latter being Kritopoulos Metrophanes, a distinguished 
theologian, whose ‘‘ Confession,” reckoned as one of the five Symbolic Books 
throughout the Orthodox world, was avowedly written with an eye to the 
West. Cyril Lucar, himself Gecumenical Patriarch, had very friendly 
relations with the Anglican authorities, as witness the presence of his gift, 
Codex Alexandrinus, to-day in the British Museum. During the Common- 
wealth English friendship with the Orthodox was, of course, impossible. At 
the Kestoration, however, its threads were picked up, and until German 
Latitudinarianism was brought in by the Hanoverians, it was developed 
continuously. Thus-schemes were set on foot for the education of Greek 
ordinands at Oxford, and we have extant a most informing and important 
correspondence not only with the four Greek Patriarchs, but also with the 
Tsar Peter I, concerning a proposal of Intercommunion from the Non-jurors.? 

How is it that im all this no question as to the validity of Anglican Ordina- 
tions appears to have been raised? The sufficient explanation seems to be 
that, knowing the Anglican Church to have kept her continuity at the Refor- 
mation, the Eastern Orthodox regarded her Ordinations as‘possessing exactly 
the same essentials for acceptance as were possessed by Roman Ordinations. 

“If any from among the Latins return to the Orthodox and Catholic 
Church,” decreed the Synod held at Constantinople in 1484, reaffirming the 
uniform Orthodox practice since the great Schism, “they are to be anointed 
with Holy Oil, but are not to be rebaptized,” the use of Chrism enjoined 
being a quasi-sacramental for the reconciliation of heretics, and not the

That it did not do so was due in the first instance to a practical problem 
in U.S.A. and the British Colonies. As far back as the eighties, Greeks, 
Russians, Serbs, Rumans, and Syrians, members of the large and scattered 
bodies of immigrants to these countries, finding themselves isolated from all 
Orthodox ministrations, and finding also the great affinity which the Anglican 
Churches have with the Eastern Orthodox, began to resort to Anglican 
priests for the Sacraments. Theirs was a pressing emergency, and sooner 
than leave them without Baptism or Christian Marriage, to say nothing of 
Absolution and Communion, more than one Orthodox hierarch,! considering 
the Economy of the case, authorized and even enjoined their doing so. 

From the Anglican side, the position obviously required regularizing, and 
to that end in 1902 certain American priests submitted a questionnaire to the 
Ecumenical Patriarchate. The validity of Anglican Ordinations was clearly 
cardinal to the reply. If the Patriarchate could not accept them as valid, it 
could not exercise Economy to sanction Anglican ministrations in emergency 
to the Orthodox. 

That questionnaire was referred to the Halki professoriate, which made 
its report to the Patriarchate in 1904 in the form of a treatise, “‘ The Validity 
of Anglican Ordinations,” by Professor Androutsos,* then at Halki and now 
at Athens. After reviewing the whole matter from a slightly different angle, 
Androutsos came altogether to the same conclusion as Sokolov and Bulgakov. 
The evidence, he concluded, pointed very strongly to a favourable judgment. 
Given an explicit Anglican answer to four questions bearing on the function 
and authority of the priesthood, and involving the Infallibility of the Church,
