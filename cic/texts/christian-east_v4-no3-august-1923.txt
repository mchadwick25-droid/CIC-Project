! WITH BISHOP GORE IN THE NEAR EAST. 


By THE Rev. HAROLD BUXTON. 


[SHE Editor has asked me to write a brief record of the Bishop's 

recent journey in the Near East. Bishop Gore left England on 

Apni 3rd and went directly to Prague, where he stayed for about 

three weeks, and had as his companion Rev. J. B. Seaton. I had the 

privilege of joining Bishop Gore before he left Prague and of travelling 

with him to each of the Balkan capitals until we reached Athens, after 
which I was occupied for some time in relief and refugee questions. 


PRAGUE, CZECHO-SLOVAKIA. 


Doctor Gore wished to make himself acquainted with the condition 
of things in the Czecho-Slovak Church, and he had also been invited to 
deliver three lectures at the Clementinum on ‘‘ Belief in God and Belief 
in Chnst.”” He stayed until Apnl 22nd. 


BUDAPEST, HUNGARY. 


We arrived in Budapest on April 23rd, and were hospitably received 
at the Legation. 


BUCHAREST, RUMANIA. 

We arrived at Bucharest on April 27th. The journey through 

_ Transylvania and the Carpathian Alps is very beautiful, but Bucharest 
: is an unattractive town on a vast flat plain. We were received by the 
British Chaplain, the Rev. C. Hinscliff, and by the Chief Secretary of 
the Legation. Apparently there had been some reports in the news 
papers that the Bishop was coming on a special mission “‘ to unite the 
Orthodox and Anglican Churches’’! It was necessary to repeat 
emphatically that the Bishop had come with no official mission. His 
' purpose was, as Chairman of the Archbishops’ Committee for dealing 

with the Eastern Churches, to visit the Orthodox Churches in the 
Balkans and to become better acquainted with them and their leaders. 
On April 28th the Bishop paid a ceremonial visit to the Metropolitan 
Myron Christea. On Sunday, Apml 29th, by express invitation, the 
Bishop, in his robes, drove to the Cathedral of St. Constantin, where 
he was received with much honour and made to sit on the throne of 
the Metropolitan, just outside the altar. Towards the end of the 
Liturgy (rendered in Rouman of the 17th century) the Metropolitan 
“addressed the congregation, telling them that Doctor Gore’s visit ;

The Rumanian Church is in process of reorganisation, which has been 
made necessary by the great increase of territory assigned to Rumania 
in IgIg and its consequent fusion with the Transylvanian Church. 
For instance, a complication has ansen in Bessarabia. The former 
Russian Bishop will not consent to remain now that his diocese has 
become Rumanian. 

We were taken to see many interesting sights in and around Bucharest 
—sculptures of the time of Trajan in the Carol Park, the Church of 
Bukar (15th century), the founder of Bucharest, the Church of Catrocen 
at the King’s summer residence ; also the copies of the Visigoth treasures 
in the University Museum. The originals (gold vases, plates, etc.), 
together with other treasures of the Rumanian State and Church, were 
sent to Moscow for safety in 1916. Later, of course, they were seized 
by the Soviet Government. | | 

During our week in Bucharest the Bishop had conversations with 
M. Duca, the Foreign Minister, M. Banu, Minister of Cults, Madame 
Romalo, the Rev. Mr. Adeney (Mission to the Jews) and the leaders of 
the Student Movement, the Y.M.C.A. and Y.W.C.A. 

All the town clergy have to take a University Degree, those who are 
only trained at a Seminary can be country clergy only. Almost every 
diocese has its Seminary ; there are also two Theological Faculties, one 
at Bucharest with 500 students and the other in Czernowitz with 200 
students. The normal course is eight years at the Seminary (i.e., from 
the age of 13 or 14 to 21 or 22) and four years at the Theological Faculty. 

One or two spoke hopefully of the signs of spiritual revival. The 
Orthodox Priest at Orsova, a lovely place close to the Iron Gates on 
the Danube, said that the people come to church well on the great 
festivals, but not very much otherwise. He appeared to be a very 
good man and his church was clean. According to what appears to be 
the habit in Transylvania, he was dressed like a Protestant pastor with 
hair cut short, etc. There are one or two parishes in Bucharest where, 
we were told, there is a spiritual revival goingon. |

SOFIA, BULGARIA. 


We were in Sofia from May 12th to May 18th. The future ornentation 
of the Bulgarian Church is uncertain. There is some talk of a revival of 


‘the Exarchate, which, if brought about, would confirm and_ prolong 


the schism with Constantinople. On the other hand, there has been a 
good deal of talk recently about a reconciliation with the Patriarchate, 
and tentative negotiations have been carried on, with the Russian Bishop 
Alexander as intermediary. The Patriarchate requires that the Bul- 
garians should acknowledge the existence of the schism as a fact and 
(without any expression of penitence) ask the Patriarchate to annul it ; 
but, apparently, these negotiations have not carried the matter any 
further towards a solution. | 

Incidentally, I was informed that the Bulgarian Church has never 
been declared schismatic by the other autocephalous Churches, and 
that there are no insuperable difficulties, therefore, in the way of a 
reconciliation between Sofia and Belgrade, for instance. 

We were met on arrival by Mr. Kissimoff, of the Foreign Office, the 
Metropolitan Stephan and others. We wrote our names in the King’s 
bool: and visited the Legation. We also saw Sir Elliott Colvin, who 
represents the Reparation Commission. On Sunday, May 13th, Bishop 
Gore was present at the Liturgy in the old Cathedral of Sofia. The new 
Cathedral, St. Alexander Nevsky, is not yet consecrated. We were 
told it would be consecrated next October, but others doubt this. It 
is very magnificent. After the Liturgy on Sunday morning, at which 
two other Bishops concelebrated with the Metropolitan, Dr. Gore was 
asked to distribute the antidoron to the clergy within the altar, who 
were not taking part in the service or communicating. He was then 
robed in the Metropolitan’s cope with a train borne behind him; he 
was given the Metropolitan’s staff and taken to a throne outside the 
sanctuary, where he briefly addressed the congregation, gave a blessing 
to the people, and distributed the antidoron to all of them. On another 
day we were taken to see the ancient churches in the city, three of them 
almost subterranean, dating from Turkish times, when this manner of 
building churches was required as a humiliation. All of them were 
well kept—one with a miraculous spring, much sought after. One of

The return journey via Samokoff was through beautiful country, 
very fertile, and everywhere thoroughly cultivated. The peasant 
farmers all have their own land and are rich and prosperous. The 
villages swarm with children. /We witnessed a very pretty village féte,

(Since the revolution early in June the new Government, I am informed,

here, recently closed owing to a strike of the professors at Government 
interference with orthography, has now been re-opened, It is said that 
there are about one hundred Russian students at the university. 
Bulgaria has been exceedingly generous in receiving Russian refugees

Russian Church, where we heard beautiful music.

CONSTANTINOPLE. 


The Bishop reached Constantinople in time for Whit Sunday, May 2oth. 
On this day he preached at the Crimean Memorial Church at the Sung 
Eucharist, in the Embassy Chapel, and at the Parade Service at British 
G.H.Q. by invitation of General Harrington. Both the Anglican 
chaplains, Canon Whitehouse and the Rev. R. Borough, met the Bishop 
on his arrival and helped in arranging his programme. On May 22nd 
he visited the Holy Synod, which was in session at the Phanar. The 
Patriarch made an address to Dr. Gore and he replied in French. On 
another day the Bishop visited the Congress of the Orthodox Churches, 
an unofficial assembly which was then in session. The President 
announced that they had accepted unanimously the Gregorian Calendar 
and would begin its use in October next. Christmas would therefore be 
observed on the same day with the West, but they were dissatisfied 
with the Western rules for the fixing of Easter. It seems that most of 
them would like a fixed Easter. Other matters inchided in the agenda 
ot the Congress were the second marriage of priests and the marriage 
of bishops. 

Dr. Gore visited the Armenian locum tenens of the Patriarch, Mgr. 
Arslanian. The plight of the Armenians generally is as wretched as 
ever. Six or seven hundred thousand of them—no less—are scattered 
throughout the whole of the Near East, exiled from Asia Minor and 
without a corner of territory to call their own; their condition is 
heartbreaking. 

The Bishop was taken by the acting High Commissioner, Mr. 
Henderson, up the Bosphorus, and visited Robert College. He was 
sorry not to have time to visit the Constantinople College. | 

The refugee problem in Constantinople is still acute in the extreme. 
The Turkish refugees from Eastern Thrace, who came out during the 
Greek occupation, have been sent across into Asia, and their numbers in 
Constantinople are almost negligible. The main problem is that of the 
Greek refugees. There are between fifteen and twenty thousand of 
these in the city, where they remain, either because they are too sick 
or feeble to travel further, or because travelling facilities are denied 
them, or because the Greek Government cannot accommodate any more. 
Greece is already overflowing with refugees, but it is to be hoped that 
those now left in Constantinople will shortly be transported somewhere 
else. In the camps they are an easy prey to typhus, smallpox and other 
miserable fevers, and there is not the remotest possibility of their being 
able to settle down in this locality with security of life. | | 


_ Inall the confusion and uncertainty which surrounds the Phanar there 
is one factor which, though often overlooked, is of no‘small importance, 
1.e., the influence of the Russian Church in exile. The Russian bishops 
and clergy have now a distinct position in the Near East, and constitute 


an important source of strength to Near Eastern Christendom at this 
moment.

ATHENS, GREECE. 
We reached the Pirzus on May 28th, having spent Trinity Sunday 
on the steamer in the A.gean. We were met by a Greek priest from the 
Metropolitan and Professor Alivasatos; also by Canon Wigram, the 
Chaplain, and a representative from the Legation. The Bishop paid 
an afternoon visit to the Metropolitan Chrysostom, who returned his 
call next day. He also had an interview with the King. 

On Wednesday, May 30th, Dr. Gore visited the Holy Synod and had 
a long conference with the members about the history and doctrine of 
the Church of England. Insumming up the Bishop said that he thought 
the first step which ought to be taken was to get, in Greek, some adequate 
account of the situation in England before the Reformation, the 
Reformation in England and the Anglican Prayer Book and Formularies, 
and the subsequent history of the Anglican Church. He thought that 
the general mind of the Anglican Church would be found expressed in 
the Terms of Inter-Communion.”’ 

The above is a somewhat cold and bald statement of the incidents of 
the Bishop’ s journey. I trust that one day I may read the Bishop’s 
own impressions of this tour in the pages of THE CHRISTIAN EAST. 

May I add two words :— 

(1) The journey was not an easy one for Bishop Gore. It taxed his 
strength and was carried out at no small sacrifice of time and effort. 


But it was made worth while from our point of view, by the appreciation _ 


that his visits aroused in many centres, His personality, his scholarship, 
and, above all, his modesty, made a very deep impression. Many 
comments were made, privately, to me, about his visits and his speeches. 
‘‘ The greatest man we have met,” said a Serbian student ; and a Greek 


Professor: ‘‘ His coming among us will not be forgotten; it will leave 


lasting impressions and will have big results in the future.” 

(2) And lastly, about Eastern Christendom in general, I would remind 
the English reader that the situation in the Near East is still best described 
as an inferno. Persecution, famine, insecurity of life; wars, assassina- 
tions, epidemics, diseases ; these things form almost the normal environ- 
ment in which our Eastern brethren live. These things must be told, 
and retold, to those who speak of spiritual or moral inertia in the 
Orthodox Churches. The Church seems to sleep? Get direct to the 
soul of Orthodoxy—to the holy lives, the splendid examples of faith, 
to the countless martyrdoms for Christ. They shame us into reverence 
and awe and thanksgiving. In the soul of Orthodoxy there isan 
undying flame of devotion to the Lord of All. ‘‘ Statesmen and friends 
have betrayed us, have forgotten. But Thou dost not forget. In our 
despair, we look for Thy return.”

**To His Grace the Archbishop of Canterbury, First Hierarch of all England, our 
most beloved and dear brother in Our Lord Jesus, Mgr. Randall. Greeting 
fraternally your beloved to us, Grace, we have the pleasure to address you the 
following :— 

‘“* Yesterday we dispatched to Your Grace the following telegram: ‘We have 
pleasure inform Your Grace that Holy Synod of our Patriarchate after studying in 
several meetings question Anglicén Orders from Orthodox point view resolved their 
validity.’ To-day, explaining this telegram, we inform Your Grace that the Hcly 
Synod, having as a motive the resolution passed some time ago by the Church of 
Constantinople, which is the Church having the First Throne between the Orthodox 
Churches, after examining this question under our presidency from all its aspects, 
resolved that the consecrations of bishops and ordinations of priests and deacons 
of the Anglican Episcopal Church are considered by the Orthodox Church as having 
the same validity which the Orders of the Roman Church have, because there exist 
all the elements which are considered necessary from an Orthodox point of view 
for the recognitionof the grace of the Holy Orders from Apostolic Succession. 

‘‘ We have a great pleasure in communicating to Your Grace, as the First Hierarch 
of all the Anglican Churches, this resolution of our Church, which constitutes a progress 
in the pleasing-to-God work of the union of al] Churches, and we pray God to grant 
to your Grace many years full of health and salvation, 

‘ (Signed) Damianos,

But this ‘‘ renewal on ancient principles ’’ 1s not only the motto of 
those theologians who desire a reform in the disciplinary system ef the 
Orthodox Church and in the spiritual life of her members, but also of 
those theologians who long for the reunion of the Orthodox Church 
with other Churches, and in particular with the venerable Anglican 
Church. In order that you may understand all this I crave your 
indulgence for some few moments while I talk on the following two 
points: (1) What is the meaning, from the Orthodox point of view, of 
the reunion of our Churches, and (2) What is a possible basis of such 
a reunion? 

(1). There are two views among us as to the meaning of the reunion 
of the Churches. The first of these may be termed the broad view. 
According to this view, it being granted that all communions of faith 
on the earth owe their origin to the same common founder, Jesus Christ, 
and are called Christian, it 1s not difficult to restore external inter- 
communion and cordial relations between them. And, indeed, it is 
possible that each communion should retain unchanged its own internal 
independence and life and yet that there should exist between them all 
an external bond of union modelled somewhat on the League of Nations : 
and that thus there should be formed a Society of Churches. In this 
Society would be debated questions of interest for the whole of 
Christianity as a Religion, such, ¢.g., as the problems which concern 
society at the present moment. And in this Society, by degrees, points 
of controversy between the different Churches would cease to be, and 
the points of agreement would increase in number: and the way would 
eradually be prepared for real, that is, dogmatic reunion. By such 
ideas exactly was the Patriarchal and Synodal Encyclical to all the 
Churches of Christ inspired—the one, I mean, which was sent out by 

the (Ecumenical Patriarchate three years ago—and also the programme, 
which was developed at very much greater length later by myself as 
Patriarchal Representative at the preliminary Conference on Faith and 
Order at Geneva. 

But it is evident that in this broad sense we do not mean an internal 
reunion. This internal union has as its necessary presupposition the 
full and complete unity of faith, that 1s, union in the narrower meaning 
of the word. In order to understand this union we must never forget 
that there is one Holy Catholic and Apostolic Church which, with your. 
permission, I shall call the original Church. All who accept the dogmas 
of this Church without addition or subtraction are internally united 
with this original Church and form a ‘‘ dogmatic unity.’ If-they are 
also externally united, that is, if they are in canonical communion, thev 
form an organic union. Such an organic union is formed by the various 
parts of the orthodox communion. But one must not imagine that the 
unity about which we have spoken excludes diversity of ‘‘ rites.’ The 
Christian religion, in those elements which admit of change, conforms 
itself to the various nations and to the exigencies of time. Such 
precisely was the case during the first ten centuries of Christianity, 
when canonical intercommunion existed without necessarily exacting

IS Holiness, The Catholicos-Patriarch of Georgia Ambrosios, 
Archbishop of Tiflis (whose civil name was Vissarion Khelaya), 
was born in 1863 in the village of Illori, with its monastery 

famous in Western Georgia. 

Having finished his studies at the Theological Seminary in Tiflis, 
he became a-priest in the Sukhum Diocese, where he remained until 
the death of his wife. 

Though he was burdened with the’care of three young children, he 
took the vows of a monk and entered the Theological College at the 
university of Kieff, where he graduated with distinction after four 
years of study. He was then appointed Prior of the Monastery of 
St. John the Baptist with the title of Archimandrite (fifty miles from 
Titlis). Not long afterwards he also became a member of the Georgian- 
Imerethian Synodal Office.

madman. Up to the present the cause of the assassination remains 
a mystery, but the Synodal Officials gave to this act a religico-political 
signification, and attributed it to the Georgian Autocephalists. 
A systematic persecution of the latter began; one of its first victims 
was'Ambrosios, who was considered by the Russian Bureaucracy as

ENGLISHMEN AND ORTHODOX 
INTERCOMMUNION. 


By THE Rev. W. A. WicrRaAm, D.D. 


OLK at home are often really interested in, and keen upon, the 
idea of intercommunion with the Orthodox Church, and even 
_ those who are not much interested in religious problems as a rule, 
may be struck with at least the picturesqueness of the idea. Nevertheless, 
it is an admitted fact that the ordinary lay Englishman living in Greece, 
in Constantinople, or in any other ‘‘ Orthodox ”’ land, is usually utterly 
indifferent to it at the best, often definitely hostile. 

This does not mean that those who take this line are by any means 
indifferent to religion. They are often men who are the most valued 
supporters of the local Church of England, and men on whom the 
chaplain can most firmly rely for help in any of the various duties of 
his office. It is therefore worth dealing with the question, why should 
they be so entirely uninterested in what the chaplain has often much

HE policy of the Department of Missions, through its Foreign-Born Americans 
Division, has had as its objective direct ministry of existing parishes to the 
foreign-born, rather than its establishment of racial missions. In the 

extension of this policy a growing number of instances of proffered hospitality and 
personal ministrations has been recorded. These instances, however, have been of 
but a temporary nature and irregular in their nature. Speedy attainment of our 
aim would, of course, be possible if unity or organic ynion within the Christian 
Church, or between sections of the Church, were imminent. Your Field Director's 
keen interest in the problem of Christian unity and conséquent study has convinced 
him that in most cases the world is not ready for a formal declaration .of union, 
except at the cost of misunderstandings which would nullify the effect of such 
declaration. The only other alternative seems to be in the establishment of more 
definite contact with the Mother Churches of Europe, and, where possible, obtaining 
such authorisation to acts now considered irregular as would warrant a broader 
extension of the policy undertaken by the Division. The friendly co-operation of 
local hierarchies and administrative organisations has been generously offered to 
the limit of the power resident in a body deriving its authority and jurisdiction from 
a parent organisation. If the work of the division was to be placed upon a more 
solid foundation, it seemed necessary that the Episcopal Church, in its administrative

Churches and the Churches of the Near East, and carry to them an expression of 
its desire to minister to the scattered members of their flocks until such time as the 
Mother Church could make proper provision for their spiritual needs. 

While the disinterested nature of the activities of our Church was understood 
by many leaders of foreign Churches, others had never known the Church in its 
administrative capacity. First-hand knowledge of most of the religious leaders in 
Europe and the Near East has been limited to experiences with groups of American 
Christians who have not always respected the sanctity of the ecclesiastical heritage 
of the native Churches. It has been quite natural that they should confuse us and 
our policy with the organisations and the policies which have, at times, brought to 
them much annoyance and sorrow. It was because this element of uncertainty 
retarded the work among foreign-born Americans that your Field Director was 
requested by the officers of the Department of Missions to undertake a mission to 
Europe and the Near East. |

view and with a desire to obtain fuller knowledge of the method of administration

We must clearly determine in our own minds whether we are moved with a sense 
of responsibility for the Near-Eastern Churches. If we can divorce the idea of 
missions from that of ecclesiastical propaganda, we would pronounce this the greatest 
missionary opportunity open to our Church. A sister Church, enjoying the oppor- 
tunity for independent action as a unit for the first time in centuries, longs for the 
immediate recovery of the intellectual and moral leadership which had given her 
the courage to prefer bondage to apostasy. In this great work of recovery she has 
need of encouragement and co-operation in attaining her ideals of educational or 
administrative efficiency. If we have been permitted to develop other and. more 
modern methods while the genius of the East was being stifled, is it amiss to hope 
that we are now prepared to share it ?

propagandists of their faith, and through their activities large educational institutions

ot regret that we are not known to the people. The heads of the Churches recognise 
the unique position of our Church, and also the great value of our co-operation. 
There is a real cry from Macedonia for our help.

T he Separation of the Monophysites. W.A.Wigram,D.D. Faith Press. 1923. 12s, 


There are books in plenty which give an outline of the Christological controversies 
of the 5th to the 7th centuries—from the point of view of the historian of dogmatic 
development. The rare merit of the work of Dr. Wigram in this book is that, though, 
at least at the outset, he may have been hardly conscious that he was taking it, his 
point of view all along is that of the historian of nationality. It is true that of 
necessity he deals with the dogmatic controversy, for the dogmatic controversies of 
the age formed the battle-ground on which the non-Hellenic nationalities both chose 
to fight and alone could have fought against Byzantisation. Once the CEcumenical 
Empire of New Rome had set itself to unify the varying cultures of its ancient 
peoples by Byzantinising their native Christianities, it became plain that, if the Syrian, 
the Egyptian—and also the Armenian—were not to become a species.of the Graco- 
Roman genus, they must break away from the Greco-Roman Church. A series of 
articles illustrating this theme appeared in the Curist1an East a year or so ago, 
their occasion being a request as to Intercommunion made to the Archbishop of 
Canterbury by the present Mar Ignatios—himself the lineal successor of the Monophy- 
‘site Patriarchs of Antioch whose break with New Rome Dr. Wigram’s pages describe. 
When we estimate the resistance of Egyptian and Syrian nationalism to the Byzan- 
tinisation of the Egyptian and Syrian Churches as a prime factor in the ‘‘ Monophy- 
site ’’’ schism, we neither minimise the fact that there were Monophysite heresies, 
nor doubt but that those heresies must, in any case, have produced passing schisms. 
Nor does Dr. Wigram make any such mistake. On the contrary, he brings out the 
theological issues with rare skill.. In doing so, however, he makes plain the fact that, 
in addition to the Christological controversy, there was acute national conflict. 
The Egyptian and Syrian had no will for heresy but they had will for schism. In the 
event they quarrelled—probably most of them without any theological reason—with 
the Chalcedonian formula “in two natures ’’—but whichever side the “ State” 
Church of the Empire had taken, they would have been in opposition. Not only 
does Dr. Wigram draw out all this with a clarity which leaves him no predecessor, 
but—and this is an additional warrant for classing his book among those absolutely 
necessary for the apparatus criticus of the student of Eastern Christianity—he gives 
us the picture not from the Graco-Roman but from the Syrian side. In other words, 
he has done just what Diehl and the Byzantinists have done for Constantinople 
during the past forty years. They found the current presentation of the Byzantine 
Empire and Church to be that traditional distortion—familiar to us all in Gibbon’s 
Dechine and Fall—of the true picture, which was inevitable when the canvas was 
painted by Latin and Papalist partisanship. So here Dr. Wigtam has studied the 
Syriac writers as his prime sources and has thus given us a corrected story of the 
schism. His book should, therefore, be a new point of departure in the study of the 
ecclesiastical history of the period. In all th Dr. V¥igram has very amply fulfilled 
his avowed intention of producing a book which would justify the Lambeth D.D. 
conferred on him by the present Archbishop of Canterbury. No University examiners 
could fail to appreciate his research, both for itself and for the linguistic and other 
equipment which it predicated, as a valuable contribution to knowledge. But his 
book has.a third and very remarkable merit, It possesses that fugitive and extra- 
ordinary quality which belongs alone to the writing of the experienced man of affairs 


,,who is endued with intuitive appreciation of human psychology and has the faculty
