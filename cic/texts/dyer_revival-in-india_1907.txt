PREFACE 


“ BLESS God that He has let me live to see this 

day. It is worth all the rest of my life to have 
lived to see what we are witnessing now.” These 
words from a missionary in the Telegu country voice 
the utterance of a thousand Christian hearts as they 
praise and give glory to God for the wondrous things 
He is doing in India. 

“It is impossible to describe,” and “I never saw 
anything like it.’ Expressions such as these have 
been met with in almost every account, written by 
eye-witnesses, from which the following pages have 
been compiled. Other features of similarity in the 
reports compel the attention and deepen the con- 
viction that this work is truly a Pentecostal outpour- 
ing of the Holy Spirit. 

There has been conviction of sin; both poignant 
and deep. It is the work of the Holy Spirit to 
convict of sin (John xvi. 8,9). God’s remedy for sin, 
the crucified Redeemer, the efficacy of the blood 
shed upon Calvary, has been revealed to dark hearts 
innumerable, by the power of the same Spirit, through 
the written Word of God, as well as in dreams, in 
visions, and in trances, 


4 Revival in India 


This revelation of God in Christ has come to the 
ignorant tribes in the Hills of Assam, to the despised 
widows in the Mukti Homes, to Christian workers 
who had been living backslidden lives, to nominal 
Christians, and to thousands of boys and girls, young 
men and young women, in schools, orphanages, and 
Homes throughout the land, far apart from each other, 
and speaking different languages, 

To all of these has come the joy of the redeemed 
and the power of pouring out the soul in prayer for 
others still in bondage, and in ardent intercession for 
a lost world; while the part of the missionary has 
been to “stand aside and see the glory of God.” 

To any inclined to doubt the reality of those ex- 
periences I would simply ask that they read the book 
to the end before passing judgment upon it. I would 
also ask them to read in conjunction with it the first 
ten chapters of the Acts of the Apostles, and the 
first three chapters of the Book of Revelation. Then 
prayerfully enquire whether the spirit and tone of our 
average Churches at home do not accord far less with 
the Scripture ideal than the scenes depicted in these 
pages. 

“To the law and to the testimony: if they speak 
not according to this word, it is because there is no 
light in them ” (Isa. viii. 20). 

“The testimony of Jesus is the spirit of prophecy ” 
(Rev. xix. 10). |

INTRODUCTION 


HAVE read with great interest this concise account of 

the recent Revivals in India, and have been asked to 

comment on various wonderful manifestations that have 
accompanied the movement. 

Taking into consideration the remarkable fact that it 
is the story of a direct action of the Spirit of God upon 
a heathen people of the Far East, deeply imbued with 
demon worship, incantations, and prodigies of all sorts, 
the first point that strikes me is the decided sobriety of 
the narration: as well marked, indeed, as that which 
describes the early years of our Lord in the Gospels, 
when compared with the weird, sensational, and unnatural 
stories that abound in the apocryphal narratives of His 
childhood. 

In reading this book two things at least must be borne 
in mind. The first is, that since the days of Pentecost 
there is no record of the sudden and direct work of the 
Spirit of God upon the souls of men that has not been 
accompanied by events more or less abnormal. It is 
indeed,. on consideration, only natural that it should be 
so. We cannot expect an abnormal inrush of Divine 
light and power, so profoundly affecting the emotions and 
changing the lives of men, without remarkable results. As 
well expect a hurricane, an earthquake, or a flood, to 
leave nothing abnormal in its course, as to expect a true 


10 Revival in India 


Revival that is not accompanied by | events quite out of our 
ordinary experience. 

The second point is that there is no nation on earth 
more hide-bound in its ideas of what is fit and not fit, 
what is proper and not proper, what is legitimate and 
what not, than the English; and none more slow to com- 
prehend the difference between the East and the West. 
This is remarkably shown in the misunderstanding of 
what is of the East in the Bible—and how much there is! 
As Dr. Bullinger and others have so well shown, the Bible 
is constantly wrongly interpreted, through the utter want of 
knowledge and sympathy with the Eastern style of language 
and thought that pervades it. Everything is taken au 
pied du lettre! A “day” must be twenty-four hours; “ for 
ever” (even if applied to a house) must mean eternally ; 
and so on. 

Having then grasped these two points, let us consider 
the abnormal (to us) in this book. Throughout the book 
we come across dreams, visions, waking visions, and a 
good deal of noise and irregular procedure. An attempt 
was made to introduce greater outward decorum, with 
apparently no good result, and so it was abandoned. 

In Chapter V. we get visional illusions of different 
sorts, such as seeing a girl on fire—which, after all, is 
not very different from seeing a light shining on a girl’s 
face. 

In Chapter VI. we get wailing, laughter, trembling, 
visions of the Saviour, and healing of disease. 

In Chapter VII., possession with a dumb demon and 
feelings like electric shocks. 

In Chapter VIII. we get intoxication with joy. 

In Chapter IX., possession of demons, beating of

breasts, and rolling on ground. We also get sounds 
of rushing wind, feelings of inward burning, and visions 
of fire. 

And really this is about all. Some, of course, will say it 
is enough, and others that it is too much. 

It must be remembered, however, that in Belfast and in 
South Wales many of us can remember manifestations at 
least as remarkable; which in cases specially examined 
were demonstrated to be genuine and free from fraud 
or exaggeration. 

It may be worth our while briefly to consider these 
phenomena. They may be classified as follows :— 


Dreams and visions when asleep (see Acts ii. 17). 
Emotional cries 

Emotional sion | (see many passages in the Gospels). 
Emotional feelings 

Possession of demons (see Mark xvi. 17, etc.). 

Healing of diseases (see Mark xvi. 18, etc.). 

Ocular phenomena (see Acts ii. 3). 

Auditory phenomena (see Acts ii. 2). 


I have set down opposite each one a passage of Scripture 
where similar abnormalities occur. 

Let us remember in considering these, that in India at 
least they are commonplaces; that in English Revivals 
they also occur; and that, above all, ¢ey are not the 
abnormalities and the marvels of the movement at all. 
These consist in changed hearts and lives, in men and 
women transformed from darkness to light in a moment ; 
and the very practical demonstration of this by the pay- 
ment of old debts, by loving their neighbours, by an entire 


12 Revival in India 


alteration of life. Without these real wonders the cries 
and visions and dreams would attract little notice. 

What, however, we are particularly prone to as Anglo- 
Saxons in the West is, to attempt to discredit the real 
marvels on account of their (to our ideas) unnatural and 
indecorous accompaniments. Now to understand these 
manifestations in any way we must know a little, a very 
little, about the mind of man and its wonders. 

We must understand that the eye of consciousness can 
see only a portion of our mental thoughts, emotions, and 
actions, and that a vast district of instincts, emotions, and 
thoughts, remain hidden from our ken in unconsciousness. 
It is indeed here in the unconscious mind that we find 
the seat of the character, of the personality, of the ego; 
and here also is the seat of the new life, the sphere of 
the new birth, the dwelling-place of the Holy Spirit. 

The Bible constantly shows that in a withdrawal from 
externals and active consciousness lies the best realisation 
of the hidden life—in being still, in waiting on God, in 
silence, in the night watches, etc. 

In religious services for the deepening of the spiritual 
life it is to be noted what stress is laid on all cessation of 
effort, on casting out of self, “in lying passive or yielding 
up our power,” etc. The larger part of our spiritual life 
lies ever behind the veil of consciousness, and from thence 
come those inward suggestions and thoughts by which we 
are so often guided. A well-known Christian teacher, the 
Rev. Dr. Andrew Murray, writes : “‘ Deeper down than where 
the soul with its consciousness can enter, there is spirit- 
matter linking man with God; and deeper down than the 
(conscious) mind and feelings or will—in the unseen 
depths of the hidden life—there dwells the Spirit of God.”

Once this is understood a flood of light is thrown on 
the value of revelation during sleep, and the remarkable 
words of Job as to the voice of the Almighty during 
sleep (Job xxxiit 14-18) are seen to have a deep 
psychological meaning. 

The presence of the Spirit is not the subject of direct 
consciousness. Visions, meditations, prayers, and dreams, 
have been undoubtedly occasions of spiritual revelations. 
1 Cor. ii. 3-5; 2 Cor. iv. 7,16; 2 Cor. xii. 2, may be 
adduced: as instances of the working of the Spirit apart 
from the action of reason or consciousness. 

A Christian worker of great experience told me that 
he believed that in one third of the people in England 
dreams were connected with their conversion. 

In view of these facts, what room is left for amazement 
or cavil at the manifestations recorded here in connection 
with a people so prone to subjective phenomena ? 

For we must remember that ocular and auditory 
phenomena may be subjective or objective; it really 
matters little. The point in all these wonders is the 
permanent effect for good that results in the lives of the 
subjects of them—then the record seems clear. As to 
the emotional cries and actions, little need be said, when 
we remember how extravagant these may become even in 
England in times of deep sorrow or sudden joy. The 
conduct of thousands of otherwise sober people was just 
as amazing during the Boer war (Mafeking night, etc.). 

The Apostle writes of being “ beside himself” with joy, 
and of “being filled with the Spirit” as a substitute for 
drunkenness, 

It will be observed that the seeing and feeling of fire 
is probably directly traceable to the prominence given in

all these Revivals in the present day to the teaching of 
Matt. iii. 1r—the Holy Ghost coming as fire, and to the 
hymns addressed to the “Spirit of Burning”; so that 
undoubtedly the ideas of all the manifestations and sounds 
and feelings were all there in the atmosphere and minds 
of the people. Under these circumstances it is quite 
natural that an impressionable people should see, hear, and 
feel, in accordance with their thoughts. Why, even we 
may shiver with cold in a warm room if we hear a graphic 
account of suffering in the Arctic regions. Hence I am 
inclined to believe these records are true and unexaggerated ; 
the real marvel being, I again repeat, not the manifestations 
at all, but that hidden work of the Spirit which accom- 
panied them in such power as to transform the men and 
women who experienced them into new creatures in 
Christ Jesus. 

I should like, in conclusion, to say one word about the 
‘‘ possession with demons.” 

I think those who know the East cannot doubt that 
Satan’s power there is beyond all dispute, and any who 
have read the painfully realistic pages of Zhings as They 
Are must know how devil-possessed many in India are. 
Lunacy is a general word that covers any departure from 
sanity, but I think that at times it covers even more. My 
experience even in England goes to show, and I think the 
experience of all skilled men directly connected with 
mental diseases proves conclusively, that here and there 
one comes across a case that is evidently “ possessed” by 
some evil spirit. 

There is seen a marked and special malignity against 
God in one naturally of a gentle and reverent character, a 
use of the most abominable words which could never

have been even heard, and a delight m ev) which is 
entirely contrary to the life and ways, that compels one 
to believe in actual “‘ possession” in certain cases. 

I, therefore, am one who believes that such cases occur, 
and still more that the demons may and can and have 
been “cast out” and their victims restored to sanity. So 
that even in this I see nothing incredible. 

It may be that all my readers cannot go so far. I 
merely give my testimony as an old student of nervous 
and mental phenomena. 

On the whole, therefore, I think this Introduction is 
really not needed, as I do not regard the story as anything 
but a sober narrative of facts. 

Many, however, may be staggered by what they read, 
and I trust will find some help in the suggestions I have 
made.

‘* In the beginning—God.” ‘‘ That God in all things may be 
glorified through Jesus Christ.” —GEN. i. 1: 1 PET. iv. 11. 


T is always a fascinating study to search out the 
beginning of great movements, to note how the Lord 
has prepared the way, by preparing hearts. and lives in 
which His purposes of mercy for communities, for countries, 
and for the world, could be ripened. It has been recently 
remarked that this old world is not the same as it was four 
or five years ago, by reason of the manifestations of God’s 
Holy Spirit in answer to prayer for world-wide Revival. 
But we must go further back than that to realise fully 
what God has done in answer to prayer; to reach the 
hidden springs that have welled up from burdened souls 
agonising for the Kingdom of God to come in power. 
The outpouring in Australia which commenced the days 
of grace of this present era, was traced to a band of four 
who “agreed to pray” thirteen years before the blessing 
- came in its fulness. One of the four, the sainted John 
MacNeil, was translated to heaven before the answer was 


18 Revival in India 


given; but the band constantly augmented its numbers 
and continued to pray, till the wave of blessing flooded 
the land. 

The early source of the Welsh Revival can no doubt 
be indicated in like manner. In all places where God’s 
Spirit has been poured out it may be counted as a fact that 
He has had watchers and messengers preparing the way, . 
as John the Baptist prepared the way of the incarnate Son 
of God. In the light of eternity will be seen a wondrous 
plan which our God has condescended to work out through 
human instruments, and for which we shall never cease to 
adore Him. Meanwhile He lets us have glimpses, as it 
were, of His mighty workings, and we exclaim with the 
Patriarch Job, ‘‘ Lo, these are parts of His ways.” 

In the past have lived many who prayed, who claimed 
India for Christ by faith, who fell asleep, not having seen 
the end for which they prayed. How their souls would 
have danced with gladness at the sight of what God has 
been doing in India since the spring of 1905 ! 

In this connection we recall the prayers of Henry Martyn 
and Samuel Hebich; of George Bowen, whose Christlike 
walk with God compelled the respect of European and 
Indian alike ; of the Newtons and others of North India, 
through whom the Evangelical Alliance Week of Prayer 
was instituted ; of J. F. Ullman, whose service of praise is 
voiced in all the Hindustani-speaking churches; of godly 
officials here and there whose memories shine like stars 
amid the surrounding darkness; and a roll of worthies 
whose names are written on high, and who will in no wise 
lose their reward. 

Rev. Thomas Hunter was the founder of the Panjab 
Mission of the Church of Scotland. He landed in Bombay in

1855, and took charge of the General Assembly’s Institution 
there for about ten months, during which time seven young 
men were baptised. Taking with him Mohamet Ismael, 
one of these converts, he went to Sialkot, and had hardly 
commenced the work there when the Mutiny broke out, 
and Mr. Hunter, his wife, and child were murdered. The 
following plea for intercession was written a few months 
before his death ; no words could better express the desire 
which is now, after fifty years, rising from missionaries all 
over India : 

‘‘ Allow me to beg from the Church of Scotland united 
supplications for India, and to demand of the Lord's 
people at home a prominent place in their prayers for ‘free 
course’ to the message of salvation. Were it possible for 
me to address the members of our beloved Church, and 
to plead especially in behalf of this infant mission in the 
Panjab, I would simply say: ‘Brethren! pray for us.’ 
Occupy you this glorious position. Let us go down to the 
noxious plain to fight: stand you on the brightened hill to 
pray. Take you the high situation of Moses supported by 
Aaron and Hur. Lift up holy hands which may bring 
down on us the blessing,—and oh ! let not the hands droop, 
nor the heart faint, nor the eye fall; for it is then, and 
then only, that Amalek can prevail. We know not who ie 
the greater missionary: he who, on the deadly plains of 
India, bears the brunt of the battle ; or that dear sister or 
brother, who, in the secret chamber of prayer, steadily and 
statedly invokes the God of Battles. This is our great 
strength ; and did we think that Scotland restrained or for- 
got prayer for her missionaries, ours would indeed he cad 
dispiriting work: yet, brethren, we are persuaded better 
things of you. We know that some beloved, far-off friends,

do, at set times, by secret and united prayer, strengthen 
our hands. Their intercessions ascend to the throne of 
the Eternal for the cause dear to them and tous. Oh 
that the number of these would rapidly increase!” 

In the later decades of the past century, one man 
stands out before us more nearly answering in his life 
and work than any to the mission of John the Baptist. 
A man who laboured and prayed with burdened soul ; 
a man who by voice and pen testified against the sins 
of the Government and the ruling classes, as well as against 
the idolatry and impurity of the people of the land. A 
rugged man, not given to prophesying smooth things, 
but whose prayers counted with his God and moved many 
to enter needy spheres of service. 

Wallace J. Gladwin was a natural pioneer. As the 
realisation of some one of India’s many needs fastened 
upon his mind and became a burden to his soul, he 
would commence to pray, and to write both publicly 
and privately about that one object, till the burden was 
in some measure removed. Crushed and humiliated in 
spirit by the increase of drinking dens in the European 
quarter of Bombay, wherein many soldiers and sailors 
were drawn to ruin, Wallace Gladwin began to pray that 
God would incline some heart to open Gospel work for 
this needy class. He spent one whole night on his face 
before God. Then through his pen the need was im- 
pressed on the heart of a motherly Christian woman, one 
born in the country of mixed British and Indian descent. 
She bravely threw herself into the breach, and thus was 
originated: the work at the Temperance Hall, Colaba, 
a centre for more than twenty-five years of Evangelistic 
and Temperance effort. Here hundreds of wayfarers have 


Pioneers; their Labours and Prayers 21 


signed the pledge, and scores of souls have been bom 
from above. 

In this enterprise, a favourite theme with Mr. Gladwin, 
that of self-supporting mission work, found an apt 
illustration. ‘To maintain her independence, Mrs. Briggs 
kept a home-like boarding-house and hotel, where mis- 
slonaries and temperance workers from many lands have 
enjoyed a temporary sojourn, and where many young 
men and women engaged in business life in Bombay 
have found a safe home and a sphere of service. 

Again, the hosts of women and girls drawn to death 
by the demon of impurity burdened the heart of W. J. 
Gladwin. To his pathetic pleadings for rescue workers 
to attack this evil in India, an English gentlewoman of 
means responded. Miss Helen Richardson, a deaconess 
of the Church of England, with some hospital training, 
came to India in 1886, and for fifteen years carried on a 
many-sided work, largely at her own expense. Other 
ladies joined her, and beside the open refuge for sinful 
women wishful to reform, a work of aid was carried on 
for the poorer classes of Indian and Eurasian Christians, 
by means of a work-room for the women and a labour 
bureau for men. Miss Richardson’s influence was marked 
among the church-going European community, from the 
Bishop downward. In later years her home became a 
spiritual centre, linked with Mrs. Baxter’s circle in England. 
Monthly gatherings for prayer were held there. India was 
on Miss Richardson’s heart as it was on that of Wallace 
Gladwin, and we count her as one whose prayers are being 
answered in the present outpouring of the Spirit of God. 

While rejoicing in Miss Richardson’s work as an 
answer to prayer, the crying sin of impurity in India

remained a burden on the heart of W. J. Gladwin. 
He published a little paper called the Purity Trumpet, 
devoted to a propaganda against the Government com- 
plicity with this evil; to arouse Christians to see its 
extent, and to draw attention to the connection between 
the Hindu religion and this iniquity. He pleaded for 
pity and help for the hosts of temple-women, and for the 
rescue of little children trained to this evil calling from 
their very babyhood. Those who have read Miss 
Carmichael’s books, Zhings as They Are and Overweights 
of Joy, will realise what this means, and will rejoice that 
the Lord has in more recent years raised up another 
voice to plead the cause of these innocents. 

Other instances could be related where W. J. Gladwin’s 
persistent prayers have developed workers for India, of 
the same cumulative character as that of Mrs. Briggs 
and Miss Richardson. What is here recorded is written 
from intimate acquaintance and fellowship in service, 
which also largely owed its origin, as far as India was 
concerned, to Wallace Gladwin’s prayers and letters. © 

When he passed to his eternal reward in 1897, C. B. 
Ward, a missionary colleague, another pioneer, whose own 
labours in the Nizam’s Dominions have been remarkahly 
blessed, wrote thus of him : 

‘‘Twenty years ago, soon after my arrival to my first 
field of labour in India, I received from Brother Gladwin 
a letter of warm Christian welcome to India. From that 
day we have been friends. He has been much more to 
me than an elder brother. Seasons of prayer, prayer for 
special subjects, and seasons for fasting, have been arranged 
by post. Thus we have prayed for the sick, sought special 
guidance, and helped each other in the work the Lord gave

us todo. Together we have prayed and planned for the 
distribution of holiness literature in India, and God owned 
and blessed our efforts. Sometimes the burden was on 
him, sometimes on me, and sometimes on both. But all 
the time we were true yokefellows in Christ’s service. 

‘‘T have valued his spiritual judgment and the worth of 
his sympathy and prayers more than I can tell. Till the 
very end he had his hand and heart in every enterprise of 
mine. I can trace large sums of money that have come to 
me for the Lord’s work, in the last twenty years, to his 
prayers and influence. 

“J well remember my stay with him during the 
Decennial Conference in 1892. Long before the dawn 
his earnest soul aroused me from slumber as he spent his 
strength in tears and prayers for India. On my return 
from America in July 1895 I saw him for about an hour. 
My soul was refreshed. He was full of the joy of God’s 
salvation. His manner of life few will follow, but it was 
vastly nearer that of the lowly Nazarene than most of the 
critics he hasever had. Thousands in India have received 
of his noble spiritual alms, and will one day call him 
blessed. He came to India a reputed invalid, and has, 
in feebleness and yet in power, outworked nearly two 
generations of missionaries. Few men in India have 
handled so much money for the Lord and have made 
such use of it as he has done. 

‘His character and work have been described as 
‘unique.’ But I sincerely doubt whether, if the facts were 
_known as God knows them, ten men in India can be 
found who have in this generation exerted so wide and

HIS is an independent Faith Mission carried on in 
connection with the Brethren in England. Each 
worker goes out depending entirely on the Lord for 
supplies. The work is reported very simply in Echoes of 
Service, in conjunction with other work under the care of 
Brethren in all parts of the world. Free-will offerings are 
sent to a central office, and thus the work appears to be 
administered very much on the same lines as the China 
Inland Mission. 

The work in this part of India dates back from 1836, 
and children of the pioneer workers are now among the 
elders. Some from the third generation are actively 
engaged in the Lord’s service in this field. The language 
is partly Canarese, and partly Telegu; and a good deal 
of itinerating work is done by boat when the Godavery is 
in flood. |

HE Lushais have been the most turbulent of all the 
hill tribes of Eastern Bengal, notorious for raids

The monthly Prayer Circular which is edited by Mr. 
Ward is being greatly used in welding together in fellowship 
missionaries of all sections of the Church in India. Itisa 
thought-arresting fact that the Revival has not passed by 
any single branch of the Christian Church working in 
India: Episcopalians, Presbyterians, Congregationalists, 
Baptists, Methodists, Brethren, and Quakers, wherever the 
Revival has had right of way, have been equally blessed, 
and the unity evoked by the Spirit of God has blessedly 
overflowed the usual boundaries and destroyed much of the 
aloofness even of the most exclusive. 

One cannot but realise that God is answering the prayer 
first breathed at Keswick, in 1898, for 200,000 Indian 
evangelists to go up and down the land proclaiming the Gospel 
of Christ. Through this Revival they are undoubtedly 
being called out and equipped for the work. The latest 
news from Mukti is more prayer for Pentecostal power to 
reach the heathen. In December, 1906, Miss Abrams 
writes: “Last Friday a little girl eleven or twelve years 
old invited a dozen or fifteen other girls to spend the night 
in prayer with her in a vacant room in their compound. 
They prayed so as to be heard all over the place; they

A mighty volume of prayer is constantly ascending from 
Mukti. Thousands in India and elsewhere are daily 
remembered there by name, and requests for prayer are 
systematically attended to. A great deal of prayer is 
offered for Europeans in India, including British soldiers. 
Remarkable instances of blessing poured out on these 
have recently occurred. Ramabai’s latest request for 
prayer is that there may be a continuous Holy Ghost 
Revival at Mukti until the Lord come. “Fire shall be 
kept burning upon the altar continually; it shall not go 
out” (Leviticus vi. 12).
