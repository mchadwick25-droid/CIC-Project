IKOLAI NIKANOROVITCH  (7.¢., son of  Nikanor) 
GLUBOKOVSKY, born on the 6th December, 1863, in the 
village Kitchmensky Gorodov, Nikolsky district of the 

Vologodsky county, is a son of aclergyman. When he was two years 
old he lost his parents, who left, besides Nikolai, four other sons and 
two daughters, none of whom were earning any money. One of his 
brothers, Alexander, a teacher in an ecclesiastical college, a man. of 
poor health, was recently shot by the Bolshevists. Nikolai Glubo- 
kovsky received his elementary education at the local church school, 
and continued his studies at the Nikolsky Ecclesiastical College, then 
at the Vologodsky Ecclesiastical Seminary, and completed his edu- 
cation at the Moscow Ecclesiastical Academy (Troitsky-Sergievsky 
Lavra) which was called by an eminent Englishman the “ Russian 
Oxford.”” At the said Academy he received his theological degrees 
of bachelor (1889), master (1891) and doctor (1897). In 1891 he was 
elected Professor of Holy Scripture (New Testament) in the Ecclesi- 
astical Academy of- Petrograd, which post he kept till 1918, when the 
Academy was destroyed by the Bolshevists. In the autumn of the 
same year he lectured at the University of Upsala by invitation of 
Olaus-Petri Stiftelsen, on the Reunion of Churches. In 191g he 
became Professor of the University and Theological Institute of 
Petrograd ; at the end of August, 1921, he succeeded in leaving 
Russia, and lived with his invalid wife in Finland, Germany, and 
Czechia by the help of kind friends; then he became Professor of 
Theology in the University of Belgrade; at present he is attached 
in the same capacity to the Ecclesiastical Academy at Sofia. From 
his numerous works we shall mention the following :—(1) His. 
magisterial dissertation on Blessed. Theodoret, Bishop of Kyrus 
(3 volumes, with an additional essay); (2) The Last Supper of 
Christ ; (3) Divorce on account of adultery and its consequences, 
according to Christ’s Teaching ; (4) Greek Evangelistarion in manu- 
script ; (5) Four Treatises on Greek biblical language ; (6) The Good

§ HE Gcumenical Patriarchate is the presumptive centre of Orthodoxy. The 
particular Orthodox Churches can take concerted action of an cecumenical 
character without its initiation, but there is no modern precedent for their 

doing so, When there is a change to be made or a situation to be resolved, it is 
the business of the Ecumenical Patriarchate to set going the machinery of conference 
and decision. Indeed, the principal reason why, in pre-war days, the Orthodox 
Church was charged, rightly or wrongly, with torpidity, was that under the Sultans 
the Ecumenical Patriarchate was, to say the least, discouraged from more than a 
minimum of communication with the other autocephalous churches. It was like a 
potent genie of the Arabsan Nights shut up in a bottle, and many forces conspired 
to keep it there. The resignation of the Patriarch Gerasimos at the Armistice left 
the Patriarchal throne in widowhood for four years, during which little could be 
done. The election of Meletios IV., however, in December, 1921, has been followed 
by a period of great activity. | . 

It is possible, indeed, that the resignation of that able and far-seeing prelate may 
be announced before this article is in print. If so, the Patriarchate will have seen 
the end of its “ Indian summer,” and will risk being reduced to silent impotence, or 
worse, under Turkish controk«.«lf, on the other hand, it be decided that the Patriarch 
and his Synod find their Avignon outside Ottoman territory, much may be expected 
from its freedom of action. But development is not measured by time, and in any 
case, for good or evil, the Patriarchate of Meletios IV. will be noteworthy, either as 

» the beginning of a new era in the history of the Orthodox Church, or as an indication 
of what, but for the betrayal of Lausanne, might have been. Thus in the past two 
years the Phanar has done much to indicate its historic claim to be the active centre 
of Orthodoxy, as Lambeth is the active centre of Anglicanism, Among other 
things, it has been the theatre of the Pan-Orthodox Conference, the proceedings 
and decisions Of which have been published recently in the volume of 225: pages 
issued by the Patriarchal Press under the title at the head of this article.

have been held in Constantinople, is noteworthy in itself. Unless we are mistaken, 
the last time that anything which could be termed an cecumenical action took place 
in the Orthodox Church was when the four Patriarchs’ letter on Wéstern Baptism 
was issued in 1756, and no meeting of representatives of the autocephalous Churches 
has been held since the Synod of Jerusalem in 1683. Moreover—we are writing 


_away from books of reference—again unless we are mistaken, the last meeting of 


such representatives held in Constantinople under the personal presidency of the 
Patriarch took place after the conquest of Mahommed II., about 1486. But, as a 
cursory perusal of the translation appended below will be sufficient to prove to 
anyone acquainted with Orthodoxy, the decisions taken by the Conference, if 
accepted and effective, will have drastic and far-reaching consequences. An estimate 
of the authority of the Conference and a summary of its work will, therefore, be of 
interest to readers of THE CHRISTIAN East. 

The Conference came into being in response to the Encyclical addressed to the 
chief pastors of the autocephalous Churches by Meletios IV. in February last, its 
first objects being specified as the need’ of adapting the Orthodox Calendar to that 
generally in use throughout Europe, and of relieving the faithful from observing 
the great number of obligatory feasts which fall in the working week. The facts 
that the Greek, Jugo-Slav, and Rouman States have adopted the Gregorian Calendar 
for civil purposes, and that the Orthodox in America and other countries are unable 
to attend weekday Liturgies without material loss, were adduced as making -the 
need for change irresistible. In answer to this Encyclical the Church of Cyprus 
nominated Basil, Metropolitan of Nicewa; the Church of Greece, Iaines, Metropolitan 
of Dyrrachium; the Church of Jugo-Slavia, Gabriel, Metropolitan of Montenegro, 
and Professor Milankovic ; the Church of Roumania, the Archimandnite Scriban and 
Professor Drangich, as their representatives. The Russian Bishop Alexander of 
the Aleutian Islands being present in Constantinople attended without special 
authonsation, and the (Ecumenical Patriarch with Kallinikos, Metropolitan of 
Cyzicus and Professor Antoniades of Halki, represented the great Church of Con- 
stantinople. 

The Conference thus consisted of delegates of five out of the nine autocephalous 
Churches. The Patriarchates of Alexandria, Antioch, Jerusalem and Russia sent 
none. Further, the Conference was at pains to declare, though such a declaration 
was unnecessary, that to obtain validity its decisions must be accepted, not only 
by those particular autocephalous Churches which it represented, but also by those 
which did not respond to the (Ecumenical Patnarch’s Encyclical of invitation. 
Lastly, the Conference itself, as will be remembered, was brought to an abrupt ending 
through the Turkish plot to kidnap Meletios IV., its penultimate session being 
actually interrupted by the mob led by the Gaiatian Damianiades. ‘That it should 
meet again at Constantinople under the Presidency of Meletios IV. is impossible 
under present circumstances. Indeed, while its resumption at all seems highly un- 
likely for the near future, there appears to be no less improbability of the general 
acceptance of its decisions without amendment. This will be apparent not only 
from the number of autocephalous Churches not represented at it, but also from 
the complexity of the questions dealt with by it. Many of these were not specified 
in the Encyclical, and the decisions taken upon them may be expected to arouse the 
strongest opposition, -both because of the effect that they would have upon con- 
ventional Orthodox life, and also for fear that, if adopted, they would be the 
harbinger of greater and inevitable change.

should be noted that the Orthodox Church stands committed by many authoritative 


documents to the rejection of the Gregorian Calendar, It is true that the Conference 
has not, as the Times and other journals have stated with pardonable error, advised

alike a mistake and an intrusion. We may be permitted none the less to express 
the earnest hope that their inevitable discussion will in no way impair that supra- 

national solidanty. among our fnends which we.admire so much, and at the same 
time our deep sense of the fact that the vacillation of Western Europe has, for the

It is readily recognised that this opinion seems unnatural and inconsistent in one

Church cannot enter satisfactorily into an arrangement which affects all of her people,

The common foe of the two Churches would see that a false interpretation of the act 
of the leaders would be circulated generally. It is manifest also, as shown in the 
report of the delegation to the Lambeth Conference, that the East does not as yet

fellowship with our separated brethren. She would also resent any effort on the part 
of the few to use the prospect of unity as a means of forcing doctrinal pronouncements 
from a reluctant body. 

On the other hand, there is no universal knowledge of the Eastern Church among 
us, In some cases, we Over estimate her rigorous attitude in matters of form, order 
and discipline, and in others we under estimate the importance she attaches to 
certain matters of faith and doctrine. Then, too; we cannot regard lightly our 
responsibility toward our separated brethren of our own race and language, who, 
while they show a tendency to turn to the East as a centre of unity, are not as yet 
prepared to follow our leadership in that direction. The great sympathy approaching 
enthusiasm, in which the statements of Greek theologians were received at the 
Geneva Conference, of 1920, the cordiality with, Bishop Nicolai and the Patriarch

A‘ one of the stopping-places during my wanderings in exile, I had an unexpected 
chance to acquaint myself with an article by Mgr. Batiffol, ‘‘ Catholiques 
Anglicains et Catholiques Romains ”’ in the Revue des Jeunes (Paris, 10-4-23, 
pp. 5-41). Tread the article with that attention which a well-known name always 
commands. This short essay has no subject of its own, as it only contains subtle 
a polemics against the pamphlet of the learned Anglican Bishop, Dr. Charles Gore, 
ait Catholictsm and Roman-Catholicism.+ Generally speaking, we see here the same 
old well-known picture, but in this particular case it has a certain importance as it is 
signed by an authoritative name and is illumined by the light of associations of unity 
(p. 5). For us, the Orthodox, there is also a special deep interest owing to the 
question : ‘‘ In separating itselt from the See of Rome in 1054, has the Eastern Church 
abandoned something in regard to the authority of S. Peter considered as perpetuating 
itself in the Roman Church, which had been in her creed for some time previous ? ”’ 
Bishop Gore's answer is: ‘‘No! Greek Catholicism has not recognised such a 
doctrine ’’ (p. 21); and Pére Batiffol declares: ‘‘ Yes! Greek Catholicism has 
denied what it professed at the time of the Council of Chalcedon” (p. 31). The 
second proposition is that we should come back ‘‘ to ourselves,’’ having submitted to 
the Roman supremacy which we had, in former times, definitely accepted and then 
unlawiully repudiated. The remedy recommended is simple and radical, but it 
sounds more like a death-knell than a guarantee of a free, long and happy life. The 
Apostle says: ‘‘ If I build again the things which I destroyed, I make myself a 
® transgressor.’’ (Gal. ii. 18). Evidently before taking such a step one must weigh all 
the possibilities and realities, in order to avoid becoming a criminal. 


I. Pére Batiffol directs our attention to the relations between the Churches during 
the period of the (Ecumenical Councils, and to all appearances entirely falls in with 
the Orthodox point of view. The Orthodox Church at no time, and to mo body, 
proposed any other ground for normal reunion, but the mutual acceptahce of the 
order of things and administration during the epoch of the Seven Ecumenical 
Councils with its unchanged dogmatic teaching. Notwithstanding such apparent 
concord, the Roman and the Orthodox are, in reality, entirely at variance. Pére 
Batiffol’s deduction is that Rome has remained faithful to the position of things, as 
they obtained in the time of the (Ecumenical Councils, and that the Eastern Church 
has deviated from it. That is the only point which, in this construction, so far 
requires an unbiassed verification.

II. Outside the peculiar ultra-montane adaptations, all such facts are incontestable,

Therefore it is necessary precisely to determine the specific gravity of all these facts. 
And the first essential consideration towards such a determination, is that Rome

papal decisions, and the Pope expressed them always in the most final language. It 
seemed to be the case that ‘‘ Roma locuta, causa fintta est.’’ Still, the whole affair 
was submitted for re-examination at the Council of Chalcedon, which, alone, 
re-instated Theodoret.

THE VATICAN AND THE RUSSIAN CHURCH. 
(CONTRIBUTED By A CORRESPONDENT.) 


¥ UMOURS have been spread in England to the effect that an attempt had been 
made.on the part of the Vatican to secure possession of the Troitzky 
Monastery, the great centre of Russian Church life and work, in pursuance 
of the Bolsheviks’ offer of Orthodox buildings to other denominations, with the plain 
purpose of further weakening the Church. As such action seemed incredible, we 
have secured the following information from the highest source at the Vatican. 

‘* As soon as the news reached Rome of the Soviet’s intended inventories of Church 
property, the Holy See, by personal order of the Pope, telegraphed on May 14th, 
1922, to Moscow offering to purchase all the sacred vessels in Russia. His Holiness 
sought thus to prevent their profanation and to preserve to the Russians their 
traditional treasures of faith, piety and religious art. The Holy Father was prepared 
to mortgage as much as was necessary in order to safeguard these Russian treasures 
and eventually restore them to a Russia once more officially Christian. 

**On May 17th, 1922, Tchitcheriu replied that the proposal of the Holy See was 
being seriously considered. As the days went on with no further result, the Holy 
Father, always watching attentively for some means to help the suffering Christians 
of Russia, telegraphed again on June 6th, this time directly to Lenin. The telegram 
renewed urgently the offer of purchase at their price in gold of ali the sacred vessels 


in Russia. Only in July was a reply received which was tantamount to a complete 


refusal: the Russian Government could not sell its national treasures to a foreign 
Sovereign. The Russian people would not tolerate it. 

‘‘ Neither in the proposals of the Holy See nor in the Soviet replies was any distinc- 
tion made between Catholic property and Orthodox property. Just as at the Genoa 
Conference where the Holy See demanded the restitution of all the religious property 
(hence especially benefiting the Orthodox), complete freedom of conscience, and 
liberty for public worship, there was no specsficatson of a particular denomination, 

‘Concerning the rumour about the Troitzky Monastery, there was never even 
an idea of negotiation for its purchase by the Holy See. From the very first it was 
clear that it was, like other institutions of its kind, impossible to be saved. If 
purchased by the Holy See with a view to restoration to its owners, it co ld be 
reconfiscated after payment.”’ 

Further, the Pope himself has given our informant the assurance that there is 
not a vestige of truth in this rumour.

THE AMERICAN LETTER. 


HE chief matter of importance since the last letter has been the consecration 
in Chicago, Illinois, on June 21st, of the Right Reverend Philaretos Joannides 
. as Greek Orthodox Bishop of Chicago, and the consecration in Boston, on 
June 28th, of the Archimandrite Joachim Alexopoulos as Bishop of Boston, Massa- 
chusetts. The two consecrating Bishops were Archbishop Alexander and Germanos, 
the Metropolitan of Thyatira. The proposal for the consecrating of new Bishops for 
the Greek Church in America had aroused considerable opposition on the part of both 
Royalists and the secular Press—in the first instance, because of instinctive hostility 
to any measure which had the stamp of the approval of His Holiness of Constantinople, 
and the non-Royalist hierarchy jn America, and in the second, because the hard- 
headed Greek business men cannot see the necessity of additional bishoprics in 
America. The measure authorising the creating of three new bishoprics in this 
country emanated from Constantinople, and was carried through with commendable 
celerity. Both the new Bishops, Philaretos and Joachim, are men of notable attain- 
ments, experiénce, and capacity. The new Bishop of Chicago is well-known to the 
Episcopal Church in America, as he was a student at Nashotah Seminary as well as 
at the Western Theological Seminary, Chicago. The former institution voted him a 
Doctorate, honorts causa, to be conferred at the opening of the Michaelmas term. 
The project to establish these two Bishoprics displays great statesmanship on 


part of the ecclesiastical authorities. Second only to the Greek communities in 
’ New York are those of Chicago and Boston. Inasmuch as for many years past 


practically all the Orthodox communities in America have, in practice, become con- 
gregational in polity, the measures taken toward diocesan organisation were regarded 
by some laymen as in the nature of an imposition and restraint upon their liberties. 
Soth of the new Bishops are men who are thoroughly in touch with American con- 
ditions, alive to the peculiar problems of Orthodoxy in the United States, and more 
than willing to profit by the experience of the Episcopal Church in this country. 
It is to be hoped that a new era is about to dawn for the Greek Orthodox communities 
here, as the choice of two new Bishops gives every promise of statesmanlike and 
efhcient administration. | 

The Russians have long maintained their own seminary in the East. The Greeks 
followed their example only a couple of years ago in creating Saint Athanasius’ 
Seminary for the training of clergy for the Greek Church. The present Bishop of 
Chicago is the late Dean of the Seminary, and is at present engaged in bringing his 
administration to a close before taking up his work in Chicago. The task of education 
is a serious one for the Greek Orthodox in America. While they have availed them- 
selves of the hospitality of our seminaries, and we have been as glad to offer the 
privilege as we are satisfied in the results of our efforts, it was long apparent both to 
the Slavs and Greeks that some more definite programme for maintaining the supply 
of clergy was needed than was obtained either by the ordination of a few Orthodox 
students in Episcopal seminaries or by the influx of exiled clergy from overseas. 
It has become more clear for each that the problem of Orthodoxy in America demands 
the raising of a new generation of American-trained clergy who have profited by the 
Western type of education available here, and are familiar with the whole outlook 
of American life. There are many hampering difficulties: the older clergy, born 
and educated in the old countries, do not see any great need for the type of inde- 
pendence from the old world which the erecting of seminaries in America would 
postulate; the vast bulk of the lay people preserve, with a kind of sentimental 
attachment, their relationship to the Church and their inherited views on the Church
