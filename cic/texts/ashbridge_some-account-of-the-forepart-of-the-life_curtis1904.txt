INTRODUCTION 

COMETHING  of  a  subtle  personal  nature 
^exhales  from  the  brown  leaves  and  faded 
ink  of  the  century-old  note  book  in  which  a 
Friend  had  copied  this  account  of  Elizabeth 
Ashbridge's  life.  Comparing  it  with  the  various 
editions  which  were  printed,  it  is  evident  that 
this  copy  was  taken  from  her  own  manuscript 
account,  and  the  story  of  a  beautiful  and 
spiritual  woman  is  revealed  in  that  clear  running 
script  more  intimately  than  in  the  cold  mo- 
notony of  type. 

It  was  at  once  a  disappointment  and  a 
treasure  trove  to  find  that  this  Autobiography 
had  been  previously  printed,  for  in  the  various 
editions,  of  which  the  earliest  is  dated  1774 
and  the  latest  1846,  lie  the  few  further  facts 
to  be  found  about  the  writer's  life. 

Hers  was  a  strange  career  indeed,  but  the 
account  she  has  left  is  really  the  history  of  the 
development  of  a  mystical  soul  ;  and  though 
Elizabeth  Ashbridge  went  through  thrilling 
adventures  enough,  she  mentions  them  but  as 
incidental  to  her  spiritual  development.

Every  where  in  the  manuscript  Elizabeth 
Ashbridge  unconsciously  reveals  the  charm 
that  must  have  been  hers,  a  charm  both  physical 
and  mental.  Fond  of  dancing  and  singing, 
evidently  attractive  of  love  and  admiration, 
from  her  earliest  infancy  the  spiritual  world 
was  very  near  to  her. 

One  cannot  help  wondering  what  manner 
of  man  that  stocking  weaver  was,  who  first 
charmed  her  away  from  her  mother's  side. 
With  a  child  such  as  this  Elizabeth  of  four- 
teen, of  whom  the  Irish  priest  said  later,  "  that 
she  was  the  most  innocent  creature  that  ever 
made  confession  to  him,"  it  cannot  have  been 
a  mere  vulgar  intrigue.  That  weaver  was 
something  strange  and  out  of  the  common  ; 
not  a  word  of  reproach  does  she  breathe  against 
him,  and  he  was  so  enshrined  in  her  memory, 
that,  writing  some  twenty  years  after,  she  calls 
him  by  no  other  name  than  "  the  darling  of 
my  soul." 

The  story  of  her  journey  to  America  under 
the  terrible  indenture  system,  reads  almost  as 
a  parallel  with  R.  L.  Stevenson's  "Kidnapped," 
and  is  a  vivid  testimony  to  its  attendant  evils. 
It  was  only  by  a  miracle  that  this  fair  creature 
remained  unsmirched  by  the  ordeal,  and  she

moves  among  those  sordid  characters  as  some 
being  of  another  world,  brighter  by  the  contrast. 

With  her  independent  mind,  and  soul  readily 
open  to  spiritual  impressions,  it  is  not  surprising 
that  she  came  by  hard  and  devious  ways  to  the 
form  of  religion  suited  to  her  needs.  During 
the  time  that  she  was  examining  the  Romish 
faith  she  must  have  been  a  tantalizing  enquirer 
to  that  Jacobite  priest  ;  so  much  of  intellect 
and  so  much  of  mysticism  combined  in  her 
character  to  make  her  a  desirable  convert.  In- 
deed that  witty  cynic,  who  was  about  the 
same  date  enquiring  into  the  beliefs  of  the 
Quakers,  would  himself  have  delighted  in  her 
reply  to  the  priest's  demand  that  she  must 
swear  to  believe  that  the  Pretender  was  the 
true  King,  and  son  of  King  James  ;  "  I  did 
not  believe  it  to  be  essential  to  salvation.  .  .  . 
And  to  take  an  oath  to  any  such  thing  would 
be  very  unsafe." 

It  is  not  easy  to  understand  how  such  a 
character  should  have  believed  herself  to  have 
actually  heard  unearthly  voices.  It  may  be  that 
these  manifestations  came  to  her  at  a  time  when 
she  was  physically  prostrated  by  her  troubles, 
in  much  the  same  way  that  many  religious 
people  have,  by  fasting  and  self  imposed  hard- 


4  QUAKER  GREY 

ships,  so  reduced  their  bodies,  that  their  souls 
seem  to  have  been  able  to  project  themselves 
into  the  atmosphere  of  the  spirit  world. 

Elizabeth  Ashbridge  was  one  of  those  rare 
souls  to  whom  the  direct  Divine  guidance  is 
a  very  real  thing.  Christian  believers  may  be 
classed  under  three  main  groups  ;  those  who 
believe  that  God  speaks  to  man  through  an 
inspired  Church  ;  those  who  hold  the  Bible 
to  be  the  one  great  guide  ;  those  who  seek 
for  guidance  in  their  own  souls,  in  the  direct 
inspiration  of  God  within  the  individual.  To 
the  first  and  last  sections  belong  Catholics  and 
Quakers,  and,  strange  as  it  may  seem,  the 
difference  between  these  two  bodies  is  really 
only  the  fundamental  one  of  yielding  to  the 
inspiration  by  God  of  men  collectively,  as  a 
Church,  or  of  submission  to  the  same  inspira- 
tion within  the  heart  of  each  individual. 

From  either  standpoint  follow  logically  the 
results  we  know,  ritualism  and  complete  sim- 
plicity of  worship.  With  the  exception  of  the 
worship  of  the  Virgin,  prayers  to  the  saints 
and  for  the  dead,  Quakerism  holds  spiritually 
all  the  truths  which  the  Catholic  Church 
embodies  in  ceremony.  An  interesting  com- 
parison may  be  drawn   between  Catholicism, 


INTRODUCTION  5 

built  up  on  spiritual  truths  crystallised  into 
forms,  appealing  to  an  enormous  number  of 
the  childlike  souls  of  the  world,  and  through 
its  inner  meanings  to  a  few  of  the  most  ad- 
vanced, and  Quakerism,  appealing  to  those 
spiritually  minded  few  alone.  One,  an  aggres- 
sive religion,  the  other  a  religion  of  waiting 
on  the  Lord.  Catholicism  sets  great  store  by 
the  outward  observance  of  the  Sacraments, 
appealing  to  the  weakest  of  mankind,  who  un- 
doubtedly gain  great  refreshment  thereby  ; 
Quakerism  sets  the  task  to  the  strong  of 
making  each  act  of  life  a  continual  sacrament. 
The  Catholic  at  various  times  submits  to 
fasts,  retirements,  penances ;  the  Quaker  denied 
himself,  and  in  many  cases  still  denies  himself, 
those  of  the  pleasant  things  of  life,  and  most 
of  its  luxuries,  which  he  thinks  might  hinder 
the  advancement  of  his  soul.  The  Quaker  ideal 
is  the  spiritual  ideal  of  the  cloistered  saint, 
carried  out  by  men  and  women  who  take  their 
full  part  in  the  development  of  the  world.  And 
the  spirit  which  finally  led  Elizabeth  Ash- 
bridge  into  the  Society  of  Friends,  would 
probably  have  left  her  in  the  Roman  church 
but  for  that  fatal  intolerance  of  crystallized 
religion    which    told    her    that    her    mother,

It  was  undoubtedly  this  sense  of  the

large  tolerance  of  the  necessity  that  various

It  has  been  well  said  that  Quakers  regard  the 
human  soul  "  as  an  organ  upon  which  God 
performs  divine  harmonies,"  and  if  Elizabeth 
Ashbridge's  heart  was  tuned  to  the  slightest 
impressions  from  the  other  world,  it  was  only 
through  much  suffering  that  that  harmony  was 
reached.  She  underwent  great  hardships  at  the 
hands  of  her  second  husband,  whose  name  was 
Sullivan,  on  the  subject  of  her  changed  views  of 
religion.  But  her  brave  fortitude  so  upheld  her 
and  at  last  so  won  upon  him,  that  even  that 
dissipated  man  was  finally  strong  enough  to

die  for  a  principle  which  he  had  learned  from 
his  wife. 

Very  few  further  details  can  be  found  of 
her  life  apart  from  her  own  account.  But 
one  or  two  facts  may  be  surmised.  The  first 
time  her  autobiography  was  published,  it  was 
printed  at  Nantwich,  in  Cheshire,  near  the 
place  of  her  birth,  and  the  inference  is  that 
it  was  given  to  the  world  under  the  auspices 
of  her  own  family  with  whom  she  had  been 
long  reconciled.  Appended  to  it  is  a  rather 
un-Quakerly  Lament  on  her  death,  which 
bears  internal  evidence  of  having  been  written 
by  her  Mother,  and  which  contains  expres- 
sions which  may  be  fairly  translated  as  refer- 
ring to  her  great  personal  charms.  This  touch- 
ing document  is  appended  in  full  ;  also  it  has 
seemed  well  to  print  the  testimonies  which 
the  Irish  Friends  have  placed  on  record  regard- 
ing this  strange,  strong  character,  at  the  time 
of  her  death.

TV/TY  life  being  attended  with  many  un- 
common occurrences,  some  of  which  I, 
through  disobedience  brought  on  myself;  and 
others,  I  believe,  being  sent  for  my  good,  I 
therefore  thought  proper  to  make  some  remarks 
on  the  dealings  of  Divine  Goodness  to  me,  and 
I  have  often  had  cause  with  David  to  say,  It 
was  good  for  me  that  I  have  been  afflicted.  Most 
earnestly  do  I  desire,  that  whosoever  reads  the 
following  lines,  may  take  warning  and  shun 
the  evils  that  I,  through  the  deceitfulness  of 
Satan,  have  been  drawn  into. 

To  begin  with  my  beginning,  I  was  born  in 
Middlewich,  in  Cheshire,  in  the  year  1 7 13,  of 
honest  Parents,  my  Father  a  Doctor  of  Physic 
or  Surgeon  ;  his  name  was  Thomas  Sampson, 
my  Mother's  name  was  Mary.  My  Father  was 
a  man  of  good  character,  but  not  so  strictly 
religious  as  my  Mother,  who  was  a  pattern  of

virtue  to  mc.  I  was  the  only  child  of  my 
Father,  but  not  of  my  Mother,  she  being  a 
widow  when  he  married  her,  having  had  two 
children  by  her  former  husband,  a  son  and  a 
daughter. 

Soon  after  my  birth  my  Father  took  to  the 
sea,  and  followed  his  profession  on  shipboard 
for  many  long  voyages,  till  I  was  of  the  age 
of  twelve  years,  when  he  retired.  For  this 
reason  my  education  lay  mostly  on  my  Mother, 
in  which  she  discharged  her  duty,  endeavour- 
ing to  instil  into  me  in  my  tender  age  the 
principles  of  virtue,  for  which  I  since  had 
cause  to  be  thankful  to  the  Lord,  that  he 
had  blessed  me  with  such  a  parent,  whose 
good  advice  and  counsel  to  me  has  been  As 
bread  cast  upon  the  waters.  May  all  Parents 
have  the  same  testimony  in  their  children's 
breasts.  In  a  word  she  was  good  to  all  about  her, 
and  beloved  by  most  that  knew  her,  though  not 
of  the  same  religious  persuasion  I  am  now  of. 

But  alas  for  me  !  As  soon  as  the  time  came 
that  my  Mother  might  reasonably  have  ex- 
pected the  benefit  of  her  labours  and  to  have 
had  comfort  in  me,  I  left  her,  of  which  I  shall 
speak  in  its  place. 

In  my  very  infancy  I  had  an  awful  regard

I  also  had  a  great  love  for  the  poor,  re-

As  I  grew  up  I  took  notice  that  there  were

services  I  often  went  alone,  and  wept  with 
desire  that  I  might  be  directed  to  the  right. 
Thus  my  young  years  were  attended  with 
these  and  such  like  tender  desires,  and  though 
I  was  sometimes  guilty  of  faults  incident  to

From  my  infancy  till  fourteen  years  of  age,

time  my  sorrows  began  and  have  continued  for
