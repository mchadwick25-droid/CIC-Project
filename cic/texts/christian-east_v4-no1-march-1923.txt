By THE LorD BIsHOp OF GIBRALTAR. 


HAVE already in various ways said so much about my recent 

4 experiences with the Orthodox Churches that I don’t want to 
fill the pages of the Christian East with a mere repetition of the bare 
facts of my journey ; but bearing in mind the special character of 

this paper, certain events seem worth recording in its pages, owing 

either to their unusualness, or to their revealing beyond peradventure 

the attitude of certain branches of the Orthodox East towards us. 

Of such events there stand out two, which seem to me to have this 
special value, one of which is Bulgaria, the other belon: ving to 
Rumania. 

I was dining with the Holy Synod of Bulgaria and discussing 
various matters with the Metropolitans of Ochrida and Sophia, 
when the former asked me how I was conducting Divine Service 

— on Sunday, seeing that we English have no church in Sophia. | 
explained that the kindness and keeness of gMr. and Mrs. Erskine, 
my hosts at the British Legation, were cupping me with a comely 
and beautiful temporary chapel, when, to my great surprise and 
pleasure, he offered me the use of one of their places of worship, 
adding that the private chapel of the Holy Synod itself was at our 
disposal. Of course F gratefully accepted, and when on inspection 
this beautiful little chapel proved all too small for anything but 
our early celebration, I was at once offered the parish church of 
St. Nicholas, Sophia, for our later service. 

Accordingly, on Sunday, October 2yth, I celebrated at eight | In 
the Holy Synod’s chapel, our service being attended by dignitanes 
of the Holy Synod, while there were added to our little congregation 
‘two or three of the leading laymen of the Bulgarian Church. Then 
later on we had at St. Nicholas, not only our small English congrega- 
tion of about forty, but a large and very attentive congregation of 
Bulgarians. We sang our English mattins, and then | celebrated 
at an altar, not as in the small chapel, behind the tconostasis, but 
after our English mode, openly in the chancel. A large number of 
priests and deacons were gathered round this improvised altar, 
all of whom were seeing the Blessed Sacrament celebrated after 
ourrite for the first time. I do not want to minister to more attci-

The other incident that I would likg to cite took place at Bucharest. 
There are in the Rumanian capital large and excellent schools which 
Mr. Adeney is head of for the L.J.S. I was talking to the Archi- 
mandrite Scriben the day after I had taken a confirmation in German 
for half-a-dozen converts and I was going to confirm a sick Jewess 
in her own home a few days later. It occurred to me that here was 
a good chance of clearing up an issue that had rather perplexed me. 
As every reader of the Christian East will know, the last thing the 
diocese of Gibraltar wishes-to do is to make Rumanians members 
of the Church of England. Yet here are we in Bucharest carrying 
on a mission which the Church of Rumania ought to carry on, 
baptising converts and confirming them, after which they quite 
naturally become communicants of the English Church. So I 
asked the Archmandrite, ‘““ Do you object to our doing this work ; 
and ought we in your opinion to withdraw from it and leave you to 
do it?) And do you view with any mislike my confirming these 
Kkumanian Jews and their recelving the Blessed Sacrament at our 
altars and from our priests ?’ 

To both these questions he returned a very decided and cordial 
negative. “ This work among our Jewish population is work that 
ought to be done, but we cannot take it up, and so we desire that 
you shall go on with it. And as for your baptising and confirming 
these converts, we don’t mind at all—far from it, we are glad that 
you should.” I was glad to get this assurance. It did not surprise 
me. In principle the Rumanian Church had already almost taken 
up this very position when we consecrated our Church in Bucharest 
a fortnight earlier, on which occasion the Metropolitan sent a repre- 
sentative who expressed in a striking address how warmly the exist- 
ence and work of our church in Rumania was welcomed by him. 
But the work among the Jews is not on the same footing, and I am 
very glad to be assured that there is no sort of dislike to our taking 
this part. 

It is by such steps as these that the great ohieat we all have in 
view, Re-union, will be achieved. There is a very long way for us 
to go before we reach that goal, but the atmosphere of friendship 
and co-operation which is beginning to exist will make the fulfil- 
ment of this great purpose secure. We have got a great deal to 


learn about one another and from one another. But if the process 


seems likely to take some time and even more trouble, it is well worth’ 


it, nor shall we have to wait till the last step is taken before we begin 
to reap our harvest. 


On the contrary, if we can go on living in this

crossing a desert who can find nothing to help him—neither fellow- 
ship nor water till he reaches the river that ends the desert. Rather 
we shall be like a company of brethren journeying to a home of 
great beauty through a rich and fertile land. Every step will have 
its pleasure and profit and when at last we reach our goal, the beauti- 
ful home that we seek, we shall be the more ready for it, the more 
meet to enter, because of all that we have learnt of and from one 
another on the way. 


THE VERY REV. ARCHPRIEST E. SMIRNOFF. 


HE Russian Church and the Russian Community in London have 
suffered a severe loss, through the death, on 4th January, of the 

Very Rev. E. Smirnoff, for many years Russian Priest in London, 

The Very Rev. Eugene Smirnoff, son of the Very Rev. Constantine 
Smirnoff, Vicar of one of the churches in Revel, was born in 1845, and 
received his primary education in German, inyone of the Russian Baltic 
schools of that town. On leaving school entered the Ecclesiastical 
Seminary of St. Petersburg, where he had already acquired a consider- 
able reputation through his writings. Having completed his studies 
here, he proceeded to the St. Petersburg Ecclesiastical Academy. In 
1870, he took his B.D. degree, and was appointed Church Reader to the 
Russian Church of the Holy Trinity in New York, and at the same time 
became instructor to the convert Orthodox Priest, the Rev. Bjering, a 
Dane by birth, who at first was not acquainted with the ritual of the 
Russian Orthodox Church. In 1874, he returned to St. Petersburg} 
where he married Zenaida, the eldest daughter of the Very Rev. Nicholas 
Naoumoff, Dean of the Cathedral of the Holy Trinity. A fortnight 
_ after his marriage he was ordained Priest, and was appointed Chaplain 
to Prince Nicholas Orloff in Brussels. On January ist, 1876, the

As a theologian the name of the Very Rev. Eugene Smirnoff is well-

of Anglicanism were a constant source of wonder and surprise to his 
many English friends and admirers. 

He wrote numerous articles and reports on Mormonism, American 
sects, English Sectarian Movements, and last but not least on the Old 
Catholics. He frequently contributed interesting articles and reviews 
to the lay press. His treatises on Old Catholicism finally established 
the view of the Russian Orthodox Church on this question. 

For over twenty years he was in constant correspondence with the — 
late C. P. Pobiedonostseff, the Procurator of the Holy Synod, and a 
personal friend of the. Emperor Alexander III.

The late Father Smirnoff left several important works in MSS. : 
he was a great stylist and his Russian was comparable with that of the 
the best Russian Classical authors. Every letter he wrote was an 
example of finished Russian prose. 

The Rev. Father Smirnoff was endowed with a singularly beautiful 
voice, and possessed a very clear articulation. These, combined with 
his deep sense of reverence, and with the dignity of his presence gave 
a wonderful and impressive beauty to his rendering of the Liturgical

Ti£ Russian Colony in London has lost a true incarnation of the 

Good Shepherd”’ sacrificing his life for the sheep. Those who 
knew Father Smirnoff will feel how truly this touching parable 
applies to one who, for more than forty years, was the loving spiritual 
Father of his flock, the best friend of the destitute and friendless ; who 
always had-the right words of sympathy and consolation for anyone 
in- distress or moral affliction. 

The Rev. E. Smirnoff was our Lord’s ‘‘ Good Shepherd,” not the 
‘hireling . .. whose own the sheep are not,’’ who “ seeth the wolf 
coming and leaveth the sheep, and fleeth ; and the wolf catcheth them, 
and scattereth the sheep. The hireling fleeth, because he is an hireling, 
and careth not for the sheep.” 


For forty years the Russian flock in London had the blessing of such 


a shepherd as is described in the beautiful verses of the tenth chapter 
of St. John, who taught “ as one having authority and not as the scribes,” 
a type of a true minister of God, such as our poet Pushkiev immortalised 
in his picture of Philaret, Metropolitan of Moscow.

That “ Seraph’s Lyre,” attributed by Pushkiev to the Metro- 
politan, was not a poetic hyperbole, for he really possessed it in the 
highest degree as one of these few chosen messengers of God’s boundless

The Rev. E. Smirnoff undoubtedly belonged to these rare chosen 
ones; he was a light, not only of love, but of profound erudition : 
having, besides, a complete knowledge of the principal dogmas of the 
leading churches of Christendom, and having studied the manifold 
intricacics of ancient and modern philosophy, Father Smirnoff was 
able to grasp any questions of a theological or philosophical nature. 
There are few men of his erudition; he would discuss almost any 
question of an abstract nature in his clear concise manner with unques- 
tionable authority.

The following sworn testimony of the Rev. Charles Dobson, the heroic 
Smyrna Chaplain, will be of value and interest. 


] WAS in Smyrna when the Kemalist troops entered the city. For 

some days, previous to the entry, there was increasing appre- 
hension among the residents. I called on the Metropolitan Chrys- 
sostomos and found that he, and, those immediately about him, 


were fearful of excesses when the Turks should arrive. The Metro- 


politan gave me a message signed by himself, and other dignitaries, 
including the Armenian Archbishop, praying me to get it sent with 
haste to the Archbishop of Canterbury. It was an appeal to him 
to use his influence with the British Cabinet in order to effect treating 
with Kemal outside the city, or in the event of an entry to insure 
protection of at least the lives of his people. I regret having left 
this message in my bureau. It was too compromising to be caught 
with in the last tragic days. The last clause was an appeal in the 
name of Christ for haste in averting the approaching calamity. 
In common with all British people, whom I consulted, I did not 
think that the Turks would behave in such a way as to justify the 
fears of the Metropolitan. However, I took the message to Sir 
Osmond de Beauvoir Brock, British Naval Commander-in-Chief. 
The Admiral discussed the matter sympathetically, but pointed 
out, that there was already considerable force on the spot, and, 
that he did not anticipate any but an orderly entry, if the Turks 
found it expedient to enter at all. He advised me to use my own 
discretion in cabling the Archbishop of Canterbury. But to any 
cable sent, to add that the British Admiral had seen the message, 
and, in the event of disorder, was prepared to give all the protection 
in his power to all sections of the Community. It must be borne 
in mind that there was, at that time, in official circles, no appre- 
hension of horrors of such a magnitude as soon transpired, and, 
that the British Navy nobly redeemed the Admiral’s promise of 
protection, when during the fire and killing, the naval pinnaces 
and destroyers stood all night into the quay of the burning city

The entry of the Kemalist cavalry came sooner than most of us 
expected. I personally became aware of their presence, while 
returning from the Consulate through a back street. There was 
suddenly a lot of screaming, and, a woman threw herself on her 
knees shrieking for protection. Next moment about a squadron 
of mounted rifles swept round the corner at an easy gallop ; some 
held sabres; most carried rifles at the ready across the crupper. 
Tnev pulled their horses aside to avoid riding down the woman at 
my feet. Up a side street I caught a glimpse of horsemen passing 
along the quay. Thev were walking and their rifles were slung. 
Further in the town I heard the trampling of hoofs, some shouting, 
and, two or three shots. When I reached home J found the masses 


of people in the square full of terror. Already some Turkish civi-. 


lians were beginning to loot and maltreat the Greek refugees. On 
the whole the entry seemed to me (speaking with a-close experience 
of actual war) to have been accomplished with very little bloodshed 
about our part (the Point). I bad a feeling of relief that some 
proper authority had come to take charge of the city. I found a 
Greek Naval rating hiding in the garden; he would not consider 
the proposition of surrendering himself to a Turkisn Officer (I am 
astonished now that it was ever possible to suggest surrender to 
the Turk)! So, I rigged him out with clothes, and, perforce turned 
him loose to take his chance. He proudly refused money. Italian 
mounted reser ists were co-operating with Turkish patrols in 
policing the citv. I brought an injured man into my church ; 
he died during the night. Next day, Sunday, I went to the Orthodox

THE ASSYRIAN CHRISTIANS. 


AM sending you the most recent news which I have been able to glean 
about the condition of affairs among the Assyrians. It appears that 
the people are feeling very acutely the long delay as to their future 

place of settlement. Until the Great Powers have concluded their 
deliberations at Lausanne, and the sphere of British influence is finally 
determined, there can be no relief from the present anxiety. It 1s not 
likely that any man will settle down contentedly in a new district to 
wake up one morning to find that he is after all a Turkish subject again — 
the very thing he and his forefathers have been trying to avoid for so 
many years. 

The Assyrians are a scattered nation. Many have gone to America ; 
several thousands are in Constantinople and the Greek Islands; three 
hundred are stranded in Marseilles waiting to go to South America, 
without much hope of being allowed to sail. Of the women, hundreds 
of them are still captive in Moslem harems, and no effort has been made 
to restore them to their own families! The saddest at heart are the men 
of N.W. Persia. They long for the Plain of Urmi, with its running 
streams, melon fields, and fruitful vineyards, perhaps, never to be seen 
again. There they invested all their savings, and the Moslems of Urmi 
city have ploughed up both gardens and vineyards in order to obliterate 
every trace of Christian ownership. A report has reached us that the 
Persian Government of Teheran would welcome the return of these 
industrious subjects; but it awaits confirmation. Mar Shimun and 
Surma Khanim, and some other members of the Patriarch’s family 
have been comfortably housed, for the past eighteen months, in the 
English Mission House at Bibaydi, near Amadia, kindly lent to them by 
the Archbishop until other provision can be made for them. Mar 
Shimun has made two extended tours of the Ashiret country in order 
to consecrate Churches recently restored after destruction by the 
Turks. Most gratifying accounts reach us of the reverent and dignified 
way in which the young Patriarch exercises the episcopal office. During 
his visitation of his diocese last October, he was hurriedly summoned 
to return by the Kaimakam of Amadia, as the Zebari Kurds were 
threatening Amadia and the adjacent Christian villages. But before 
Mar Shimun and his followers could return, the Assyrians of Baz, who 
had been visiting their ruined village in company with the Bishop of 
Jilu, turned up at the right moment and saved the situation, driving 
the Kurds in all directions. It is wonderful what spirit is left in these 
mountaineers and they are doing their best to restore their old home- 
stcads, where they long to settle down again, if only some kindly govern- 
ment will cast its mantle of protection over them and secure their 
future prosperity. They have at their own cost rebuilt many of their 
churches ; their devotion to their religion we cannot but admire. _ The
