SOME EXPERIENCES WITH THE 
ORIENTAL CHURCH 


By THE Rev. CANON GARLAND, D.D. 
HILE I was with the Australian Imperial Forces in Egypt, 


Palestine, and Syria, never-to-be-forgotten relations with _ 


Oriental Christians, and particularly with those of the Greek Orthodox 
Church. 

A few days after Jerusalem was entered by General Allenby, I had 
the wonderful privilege’of being the first priest of the Church of England, 
since the war began, to celebrate the Holy Eucharist in the Chapel 
of St. Abraham, adjoining the Holy Sepulchre, at an altar erected by 
the Greek Orthodox Patriarch of Jerusalem for the use of priests of 
the Church of England, in a chapel belonging to the Greek Orthodox 
Church, and set apart for the exclusive use of the Church of England ; 
only a wall divides the chapel from Calvary. The Patriarch himself 
had been taken away as a prisoner by the Turks, under circumstances 
of great cruelty and hardship, from Jerusalem to Damascus. There 
were none but priests left at that time, under the Archimandrite 
Porphirios, who very readtly gave permission to me to celebrate. I was 
accompanied by the senior chaplain of the Australian Imperial Forces, 


the Rev. Maitland Woods, and some other soldiers. The welcome we. 
received from the priests, monks, and nuns of the Greek Orthodox _ 


Church can never fade from memory ; their delight at, as they expressed 
it, “‘ seeing the English Church again,” and their manifestations of 
joy, are beyond words. Offerings of flowers and a linen embroidered 
altar-cloth, given out of their poverty approaching starvation ; prostra- 
‘tions in order to kiss the hem of our vestments; kissing of our hands ; 
all testified to the great depth of their welcome to us. Quite a number 
of times afterwards I had the same privilege of celebrating at that altar, 
and always was welcomed with eager manifestations. 
On a later occasion, when in Jerusalem, I was at the great thanksgiving 
service in the Greek Orthodox Cathedral of the Holy Sepulchre to cele- 


__ brate the driving of the Turks out of Palestine, the Archbishop of Sinai. 
_ presiding, the Patriarch being still a prisoner. I was present in vest~ - 
ments, and at first was asked to take my place among the Greek Orthodox 


priests, who stood in two rows facing inwards outside the iconastasis ; 


but when the Archbishop’came in and took his place on the throne, he 


~ had me brought to stand at his right hand. There were other Church 
of England priests present, in cassocks only, and I understood that it was 
because I had been the first priest to celebrate, and on this occasion 
attended in vestments, that this compliment was paid to me.

HE earnest desire for union with the Orthodox Church expressed 

by the Anglican Church during the visit paid to America and 
England in the year 1918 by an ecclesiastical mission under the Right 
Reverend Meletios, the Metropolitan of Athens, is neither strange nor 
new. The Anglican Church has often expressed this desire for union 
on previous occasions. For example, in the time of Theophilus, 
Metropolitan of Athens ; of Lycurgus, Archbishop of Syra ; of Germanos, 
Metropolitan of Athens; and of the Archbishop Dionysius, fervent 
desires for union were manifested. 

These facts do not surprise us at all. They are prompted by a 
sense of the nearness of the two Churches to each other in their 
principles. History shows that the attitude of the Auglican Church 
towards the Orthodox in the matter of proselytizing has been irreproach- 
able. Such instances as have occurred of a different attitude have 
been too isolated and infrequent to constitute a proof to the contrary. 
The Anglican Church, acknowledging the authority of the Orthodox 
Church, and regarding it with reverence, has never Uesired to behave 
towards it in a way calculated to promote misunderstanding. On the 
contrary, the Anglican Church has always been eager not only to 
promote mutual understanding, but also to give any possible help and 
support during the exceedingly difficult experiences which the Orthodox 
Church has endured. Anyone who has even an elementary idea of the 
Anglican Church will have found out that while this Church avoids the 
extremes of Protestantism, it in no way adopts the Papal exaggerations. 
Thus it stands between these two extremes, naturally approaching the 


Orthodox Church, which also occupies a middle position, and with - 
Which it desires to be united. No doubt there exist between these. 


two Churches many and, in some respects, serious differences. To 
these differences it is our duty to give special attention. For if these 


‘tendencies of the Anglican Church are properly regarded and directed 


they will contribute to their mutual understanding, and in all probability 
to their final union. 


It is no part of my purpose to write an historical dissertation on the 


mutual relations between these two Churches in the past. I think the 
facts are known. My purpose is to describe the recent events in New

Now the purpose of the Church is to promote men’s relation to.

The authority of the Church to legislate is founded on the direct

The decisions of the Church are not all of equal authority, nor do 
they possess the same binding power. Laws of the Church are of two 
separate kinds. | 

Canons~ concerning the faith, that is to say, dogmas, are for the

nature, and are as such unalterable for all time. 

Canons concerning discipline are of a different authority. They are 
by no means-unalterable, but may vary according to the changing 
requirements of the Church.

to these; membership without such assent is a contradiction. But laws 

relating to external discipline do not possess this supreme authority.

the alteration is not in conflict with its principles.

The Canons of the Eastern Church are classified in four main

As to the Church’s constitution, Bishop Milasch expounds the 
Orthodox conception to be that the Church is the Body of which - 
Christ is the Head. It consists of all the -baptized who strive: in 
common union for the realization of the purpose for which the Church 
was founded. 

According to the fandimenid principles of the Orthodox East, 
members of the Church exist in two divisions: Those who are divinely 
authorized to discharge ecclesiastical ministrations, such as teaching 

~and dispensing spiritual gifts; and those who, like the former, are 
duly incorporated through baptism, yet have no commission to 
discharge such ecclesiastical ministrations. 

Power is exercised in the Church through its’ specially constituted 
organs, and the power of the hierarchy rests on Divine right. But the 
Church does not consist only of its ministers. The laity, all faithful 
members of the Church, which-is the Body of Christ, possess in union 
with the*hierarchy a priesthood. Thus in the Liturgy the laity in 
union with the priest invoke the Spirit to sanctify the gifts. | 

Ecclesiastical authority resides in the collective Episcopate for the 
worldwide Church, and in the one bishop for the local Church. 

They are the only inheritors of this authority, as being the followers 

of the Apostles. Laity have, according to the fundamental constitu- 
tion of the East, no place in the Councils of the Church. Only 
bishops possess the power to decide (jus decisivum). Christ gave 
authority in the Church to the Apostles collectively, and the bishops

At Constantinople there is a permanent Holy Synod under the 
Patriarch, consisting. of twelve bishops, over whom the Patriarch 
presides. All metropolitans are eligible to this Synod, the election 
being for a limited period, during which residence at Squstanungple: 1S

Beside this Holy Synod, there is a mixed Synod concerned with 
civil affairs, also consisting of twelve members, of whom four are 
bishops (who are also members of the Holy Synod), together with 
eight laymen. This latter appears to have existed since 1860. 

As to the formularies of faith, Bishop Milasch lays stress on the 
unique authority of the Nicene-Constantinopolitan Creed. He also 
shows that value is attached in the Eastern Church to the Athanasian 
Creed. 

After an interval of many centuries come various confessions of 
faith. There is the confession produced at the Synod of Jerusalem i in 
1672. What is the precise dogmatic value of this document in the 
Orthodox view is not exactly clear. Apparently it would be set in an 
inferior position to the ancient Creed. 

Then there are certain important Catechisms which have consider- 
able authority.. There is the Catechism of Bernardaki, which was

The existence of various more or less official documents in the 
Eastern Church possessing different degrees of authority is somewhat )

of documents whose authority is only partial and local.

The question of the future of this much-tried little nation is still somewhat in- 
determinate, as it is part of the whole Mesopotamian problem. The wish of the 
people was to return to their own monntains, and to live there under the British 
mandate. Unfortunately, the British mandatory area cannot be extended to cover
