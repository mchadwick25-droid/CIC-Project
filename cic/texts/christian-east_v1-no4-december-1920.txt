A few notes on Nicawa and its Church may be helpful towards 
estimating the measure of his achievement and understanding the 


Metropolitan's letter. Nicaea stands—or must we now say stood?— 


about fifty-six miles as a bird flies from Constantinople, at the east end 
of beautiful Lake Ascania, ina fertile, though neglected and unhealthy, 
plain, ringed royind by great hills, with the glorious 
Bithynian Olympus away to the south. 

~ It has had a long history, which begins in the dim days naa Greek 
colonists first brought\Hellenism to Bithynia and it was known as 
Angora. Antigonus refounded it in 310 B.c. and Lysimachus gave it 
his wife’s name, Nicza, in 300 B.c. As was the way with the city 
builders of that epoch, Nicaza was laid out four square and its main 


Street still runs in a straight line from the gate—it has four, one in 


the centre of each of its sides—on the lake to the Lefke Gate in the 
eastern wall. It was a city of capital importance in the days of the 
Bithynian Kings, and it remained so when their kingdom was merged in 
‘the Empire of Rome. Trajan and Hadrian rebuilt its walls and baths 
and amphitheatre. Pliny wrote of it. It had thus a long record as a 
seat of government, of trade and of culture when Constantine chose: 
it as th place of meeting for the Council which in 325 was to lay the 
foundation of the Ecumenical system and to define the CEcumenical ~ 
Faith of Christendom. 

In the eight centuries which followed it retained much of its civil 
and economic importance, but above all came to be a symbolic city, 
the City of the Creed. As such it was particularly cherished by the 
Emperors, who prided themselves upon it as one of their chief posses- 
sions. Justinian especially beautified it with churches, baths, and so 
forth. This characteristic, and perhaps the neighbourhood of 
Olympus, which, until the rise of Mount Athos, was a Greek Thebaid in 


petto, made it a monastic city. In the thirteenth century, for example, 


it contained no less than forty-seven monasteries. The Seventh and 


last Gicumenical Council was held in it. The Seljuk Turks captured 


it in the eleventh century, and made it the capital of their short-lived - 
sultanate until the first Crusaders retook it, When the Pope’s 
Crusaders sacked Constantinople in 1204, and established Latinism by 
the sword in St. Sophia, Theodore Lascaris fled to Nicza as to the © 
cradle of Orthodoxy, and thus Eastern Christendom regathered its 


strength, until in 1261 the Emperor won back the Queen City. The 


Greek race and the Orthodox world can never forget that Niczea was for 
fifty-seven years the refuge of the Imperial and Patriarchal thrones. 
In 1330 Orchan, the second Osmanli’ Sultan, took it and made it .

No society of antiquaries safeguarded the ancient monuments of 
Nicaea. No masons cleared the old away to make room for the new. 


Its ruin was the ruin ofa city that had been slowly crumbling away for 


centuries, because its people were dwindling to nothing. and because its 
tyrants rejoiced to see it disappear. | 

That, I think, was what gave the place an almost eerie fascination. 
Its decay was the decay of an Indian jungle city. 

I shall never forget how, on my first visit in May, 1904, on coming 
in through the Lefke Gate, we saw a Greek boy and girl with ruddy 
gold hair, such as is not unusual in Bithynia, sitting by the waterfall of 
Trajan’s aqueduct, under a pomegranate with its dark leaves and 
flaring red flowers. The picture was one of the few I have ever wished 
to paint. Unhappy children to be Christian and dowried with such 
beauty in the land where the Turk ruled. What was the fate of such 
children in August last ? 

As has been said, there were many things to be seen in Nicza, but the 
most interesting were twochurches. Each of them was not only wortha 
visit for its history and itself, but was a parable of political significance. 

The one was St. Sophia—nearly every Greek city had its St. Sophia 
—and, as we should say, was the principal parish-church of the city. 
Murafieff maintained that it dated from Constantine’s time. A shrewd 
surmise would make us guess that the Isapostolic Emperor founded a 
St. Sophia as a symbol of the fulfilment of the Divine plan for the 
Earth, in the City of the Council as well as in New Rome. At any 
rate it was not later than the seventh century. 

The Seventh Council, which at the bidding of Irene settled the 
Iconoclastic controversy, met within its walls. The Turk seized it in 
1330, turned it into a mosque, and, as he has made a half desert of the 
most fertile country in the world, let it fall into rains’ When I visited 
it first, sixteen years ago, the roof was gone, and the walls were falling in. 

The domes of the sanctuary were still standing, and frescoes and 


_ the remains of. mosaic were still to be seen. A stark-naked, grey- 


bearded lunatic, one of the favoured of Allah, had made his lair on the 
spot where the Holy Table used to stand. Photos of twenty years 
before showed much more of the building. In 1912 one of the little 
domes was down, and I could mark the further ruin of the walls. 

The Niczan St. Sophia was emblematic of the Turk’s inability to 
safeguard anything that he has usurped. 

The other church was that of the Koimesis, which Djemal has

account of the different peoples of Armenia and Kurdistan may 
not be out of place. —— 

The race question is acute in Armenia as in many other parts of 
Turkey, but it has been rendered infinitely more severe than it need 
have been by the policy of the Government at Constantinople. Here 
are two races living side by side, the Armenians, a settled, the Kurds, a 
nomadic people. The economic tie between the Armenians, as cul- 
tivators and as traders and the Kurds as stock-raisers might have 
brought about a natural reconciliation of the two peoples, but a trade 
boycott of the Christians and secret religions propaganda among the 
Kurds were used to stir up fanaticism and to drive in a wedge between 
them. Furthermore, the distribution of arms among the Kurds pre- 


_ pared the way for the forcible transplantation of the Armenians | to 


Mesopotamia in 1915. 


In regard to the Turks we quote the following passage from 
Profesor Harvey Porter, of the American College, Beirut. , The 
Turks, he writes, ‘are not all Ottomans by descent ; sicdbable the 
larger number are descendants of the Seljuks and other Turkish clans 
who ‘immigrated into Asia Minor both before and after the Otto- 
mans. ..... The Ottomans were few when they invaded the empire 
(Othman or Osman I. reigned from a.p. 1288), but they increased rapidly 
by absorbing other clans and by taking women of their captives into 
their harems. ... This increase was at the expense of the purity of 
the race, so that the Ottomans of to-day are of decidedly mixed blood.” 

Again, “The language reflects the mixed character of the race. 
The grammatical structure is Turkish, but the vocabulary is chiefly 
Arabic and Persian, with a mixture of other dialects.” 

The Armenians originally occupied the north-eastern portion of 
Asia Minor. The traveller in Armenia to-day may readily detect both 
an Aryan and a Semitic type of physiognomy among the Armenian 
peasantry and would beled to deduce-a mixture of these elements.. It 
appears to be established that the original indigenous population of the 
plateau (proto-Armenian), which contained both Assyrian and Jewish 
elements, was first conquered by and then fuged with the Aryan peoples 
(Scythians and Cimmerians), who invaded the Caucasus and Armenia 


in the seventh century B.c. These elements, together with the bracing 


climate of a highland home, have contributed to produce a remarkably 


national consciousness for 2,500 years, although for the greater part

The language of these Aryan invaders _— completely displaced 
the language of the original population. From the seventh century 
B.C. onwards the language of the Armenians is predominantly Indo- 
European, although it has borrowed from the Persian. | 

The Armenian alphabet was constructed by Mesrop by order of 
the Patriarch Sahak about a.p. 404. Mesrop followed the Greek 
rather than the Syrian forms. Armenian is therefore written from left 
to right, not, like Oriental languages, from right to left. It-has thirty-six 
letters, including a separate symbol for each of the vowels. Writing of 


the characteristics of the Armenians, Lord Bryce has said: ‘‘ Among 


the peoples of Western Asia the Armenians are unquestionably the 
strongest ; and what I have seen of them both in their own country . 
and in America, where many of them have sought refuge and secured 
prosperity, leads me to believe them to be, in point of industry, intellect, 
and energy, the equals of any of the European races.’ 

The Kurds, to quote again from Professor Harvey Porter, “are 


_ regarded as one of the Scythian stock and identified with the Carduchi 


mentioned by Xenophon, who dwelt in the northern portion of the 


‘Assyrian Empire and were given to raiding and plundering much as the 


mountain Kurds of the present. ... They number more than a 
million and a half and are all Moslems, largely nomadic still, but many 
are pastoral and dwell in villages.” Their most flourishing period was 
in the twelfth and thirteenth centuries, when Saladin and his successors, 
of the Ayyubite line, made them famous. They came under the sway 


of the Ottomans when Selim I. defeated-the Persians in A.D. 1514. 


lidris, the Kurdish historian, installed the tribal system which has 
continued down to the present day. 

The language of,the Kurds is Aryan, with a sonsidenale mixture of 
Semitic and Turanian elements. Kurds have sought instruction both 
in Armenian and in American missionary schools and have shown a 
fair amount of intellectual ability. 

In religion, the Kurds mostly adhere to the Shiah branch of the 
Mohammedan faith, but a large proportion (perhaps one-third) of them 
are Kizilbashis (‘‘ redheads’’), heretical Moslems with pro-Christian | 


~ leanings, who are hated by the Sunni Turks almost as if they were 


Christians. Toa large extent it is true that the Kurds are the victims 
of a cruel fate which has debased the fundamental gold of human 
nature. But, in reality, there are few, if any, signs of the Kurds-showing 
any constructive ability. Internal strife among the various clans 
absorbs as much energy as the war upon their neighbours. The — 


absence of a written language precludes the spread of ideas among 
. them.

The Nestorians, Syro-Chaldzana,’ or Aenea are a Christian 
people,-largely Semitic by physical descent, who inhabit the eastern 
portion of Kurdistan and the western shores of Lake Urumia. Their 
Patriarch, Mar Shimun, is not only religious leader, but acts as 
national chieftain of the Nestorian people. They are subdivided into 
smaller'tribes, such as the Jelus and others. They have manifested a 


great desire for education, to meet which the Archbishop of 


Canterbury has maintained a mission among them at Kotchanis and at 


Urumia for the past forty years. They have a capacity for culture 
which indicates that they would play a leading part in the reconstruc- 


tion of the land if not too much reduced by war. They have had 
an eminent-representative recently in London, the Lady Surma, who is 
sister of the late Patriarch and aunt of the present Patriarch. 

The Yezidis are hardly to be distinguished from the Kurds. Their 
headquarters are at Jebel Sinjar, east of Mosul, although they may be 
met with almost anywhere throughout the region. They are often 
ceiled Devil-worshippers for want of a better description. The fact 
is that no one has yet given an adequate study to their obscure beliefs 
and rites. 

The Circassians, or Cherkesses, had their original home in the 
Caucasus, whence they emigrated into the Turkish Empire rather than 
submit to the Russians. The defence of their independence under their 
chief Shamy] brought them prominently to the notice of Europeans. 
Being Moslems, they were readily received by the Porte, and settled in 
Asia Minor, where they are widely dispersed. 

The Lazis are mostly confined to their own country of Lazistan, 


between Trebizond and Batum. They are akin to the Georgians of 
Transcaucasia, but are Moslems and not Christians, and are of 


altogether lower grade than their kinsfolk of the Caucasus.

who so ‘recently made the* most sdlemn assurances of friendship 
and of help; it is. for these reasons that we are ‘bound in 
honour to defend their cause. Moreover, they are by nature

By Rev J. A. DOUGLAS 
HE Editor suggests that I should give an account of a recent 
controversy between Dr. Adrian Fortescue and myself in the 
Tablet on the attitude of the Eastern Orthodox Church towards Anglican 
Orders. The value that he sees in such an account is that it may serve 
to make that attitude more easy of comprehension to the ordinary 
reader, and may help to correct the impression made upon susceptible 
Anglican readers by Dr. Fortescue’s books and similar propagandist 
writings. 
The occasion of our controversy was an unsigned article, entitled 
‘“ Communicatio in Concione,’’.in the issue of the Tablet for March 1, 


- 1920, which professed to establish an equal justification on Anglican 


principles for the preaching of the Congregationalist Dr. Jowett in 
Durham Cathedral, and for the preening of the Serbian Bishop 
Velimirovic in St. Paul’s. 

Certain sentences in this article when taken as a whole appeared to 


me to present a distorted view of the Eastern Orthodox Church, but 
_ when examined clause by clause, and taken in one of their possible 


meanings, to be individually defensible. In one, however, the writer 
had been led by his zeal into a serious overstatement. He had writter 
of Bishop Velimirovic: ‘‘ His Church formally rejects Anglican Orders, 


and shows this in the most efficient way by:reordaining without condi- 
tion any Anglican clergyman who may wish to become an Orthodox 


priest.’’ That sentence contained a non seguitur which is amazing to 
anyone possessed of even a slight knowledge of Orthodox theology : 
for the reordination of a heterodox. priest by ‘the Orthodox is not 
necessarily an evidence of the rejection of his Orders. 

The reference to the “ reordaining Anglican clergymen” and the 
‘“‘ without condition” seemed also likely to give a casual reader the very 
false impression that a rule had been formulated, that there were 
plentiful precedents of its being observed; and that the invalidity of our 
Orders was held to be so well established that a conditional ReOrGIOALON 
was out of the question. : 

The facts, however, are that no action in the matter has been 
concerted by the (Ecumenical Patriarchate with the other autocephalous

The (Ecumenical Patriarchate cannot overlook the difficulties 
which the unity of Churches can meet if this subject is to be discussed 
immediately. And just because they are conscious of the necessity o 
surmounting these difficulties, they have deemed it necessary to prepare

that this is the first step to be taken toward the realization of the 
unity for which our Lord prayed in John xvii. 21, ‘that they all may 
be one,” the prayer which all Churches have received as a heritage, 
and make common use of. | 7 
This is not the occasion to expose the reasons for the divisions in 
the Church. Nevertheless, you will allow me to emphasize the fact 
that the chasm started to widen at the very moment when human 
doctrines tried to replace the Divine. The conditions which the 
Greek Patriarchate have suggested for the success of the pegeue of 
Churches are the following : 
First, and indispensable, that proselytism between different con- 


fessions should cease to exist. This does not mean that the Greek 


Patriarchate is opposed to individual liberty or would restrict the 
right of every man to think according to his own wishes. They mean 
that the system which is purposely and premeditatedly organized to 
attract or to bait followers should’cease. We must not ignore what a 
lack of confidence, what uneasiness, and what a coolness, which 
approaches hate, this proselytism provokes among the Churches. On 
- account of this very often the internal harmony of families is broken, 
-as well as the internal peace of Churches. The different trials and 
afflictions which the Churches witness from other sources are sufficient ; 
they must not be overcharged by those which arise from proselytism. 

Secondly, that Churches should show in deeds a vivid interest in 
each other in. favour of their stability and of their welfare. Far from 
considering” herself a stranger to another, a Church should consider 
herself as related to another and belonging to Christ, and so follow 
‘ with great eagerness and careful study what is taking place in other 
Churches, be it irksome and sorrowful, or pleasant, easy, and 
_ accordingly to give a helping hand. : |
