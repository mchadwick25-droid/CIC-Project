T would be difficult to conceive a mgre psychological moment than 
the present in which to open negotiations for union with the 
Eastern-Orthodox, Everything is propitious. Canterbury has never 
had a quarrel with Constantinople. Before 1g14. the relations of the 
two Churches were most cordial. In the past five years they have 
become very close. The War Alliance with Great Britain has created 
a favourable disposition towards the English Church among the mass 
of Greeks, Russians, Serbs, and Roumans. Personal contact has 
produced friéndships and even spiritual intimacies between the rulers 
as well as between the rank and file of the two Churches. The hearts 
of the Orthodox nations have been won by the practical sympathy and 
support of the Anglican clergy and laity, and especially by the generous 
and fearless courage with which, in the simple name of humanity, the 
Archbishop of Canterbury, as spokesman of all the Anglican Churches, 
has again and again stepped forward splendidly and effectively to stand 
between the Christian remnant of Asia Minor and Kemalist cruelty, 
or to stem the brutal Bolshevik persecution of the Russian clergy. 
Moreover, apart from the mutual attraction of the two Churches, the 
quasi-political attack upon Eastern-Orthodoxy, now said te be developed | 
all along the line by Roman propaganda, gives them a strong impulse of 
common interest towards each other. The present aims of that propa- 
| ganda are not directed towards individual proselytism. In the first in- 
| stance they are the destruction of the solidarity of the Eastern-Orthodox 
nations, and in the second the persuasion or compulsion of local con- 
gregations, and even of autocephalous Churches, to become Uniate—a 
conversion which involyes no immediate change in religious atmo- 
sphere, but only the acceptance of papal overlordship. Great and 
devoted as is the stubborn fidelity of the Eastern-Orthodox to their 
Church, it would be idle to contend that the chaos and disintegration 
which follow from the break-up of the Tsardom, the bitter Bolshevik. 
_ determination to use every weapon at hand for the destruction of 
the Russian Church, the merciless Kemalist resolve to extirpate the 
Christians of Asia Minor, the suicidal internecine quarrel among the 
Greeks and many other phenomena, do not furnish this Uniate policy 


with a favourable field and with abundant, if strange and contrasted, 
instruments. |

Before the War Mr. W. J. Birkbeck, who seemed endued with a 
sixth sense to understand the Slav, never tired of warning us that an 
Anglican union with the Russian Church was not within the widest 
horizon of practicality. Bishop John Wordsworth and Bishop Collins, 
rare statesmen and scholars of the greatest authority in the matter, 
‘told us the same in regard to the Greek. The reason of their caution 
was obvious to anyone with first-hand knowledge of the East. The 
Eastern-Orthodox minimum implies the acceptance of the infallibility 
of the decrees of the Seven (Ecumenical Councils, the Septenary 
Sacramental system and many other things the categoric affirmation 
of which would necessitate the disruption of the Anglican Church. 
None the less, while these experts held that negotiation for union 
would then be a grave blunder of precipitancy, they were eager to 
prepare the terrain for a distant future and to cultivate the warmest 
comity and amity with the Eastern-Orthodox. 

Have the swift developments of the last eight years lessened the 
fundamental difficulty? We have never concealed the fact that we 
think they have not. It is possible, indeed, that in order to bring about 
Union, individual Eastern-Orthodox may will to be accommodating in 
scrutinizing its terms. But we doubt whether one of their leading 
bishops or theologians would at long last be found to accept the 
maximum to which the whole Anglican Church could be brought to 
agree, and we are certain that any such acceptance would at once be 
repudiated. In this opinion we are strengthened by such utterances 
as the plain speaking of the 1920 Constantinople Delegation,’ by 
Professor Comnenos’ brochure on our Orders,? by the Metropolitan of 
Nubia’s emphatic utterances at the Geneva World Conference,’ and by 
Professor Gloubokovsky’s article on Mr. Douglas’s book published in 
our last number. | | 

Above everything a sure indication will be found in the silence 
with which all the Greek ecclesiastical papers published a trans- 
lation of the Terms for Inter-communion prepared for the E.C.C., and 
published with its sanction asa ballon d'essai last year. That document 
was drafted by skilful“and friendly hands, and contained very few, i! 
any, statements which the Eastern-Orthodox would challenge, If 
there were not comments, it was because the elucidatory questions, 
which ‘if laid 'on the table at a formal- discussion it must provoke, 
would certainly expose fundamental differences. Diplomatic methods 
are rarely helpful to theological reconciliation. 

Accepting it, therefore, as a regrettable fact that things are ds they 
are, those who have been most engaged in our movertrent have shown.

themselves wise in still deprecating premature direct negotiations, 
and in confining their efforts to deepening the mutual friendship. The 
E.C.U. Declaration is meant to be no more than a modest step in that 
direction. 

The circumstances of its origin are these: Overseas there are large 
numbers of Eastern-Orthodox who are isolated from their own clergy 
and so are deprived of all spiritual ministry, even of Baptism or of Nuptial 
Benediction. Many of these either resort or desire to resort to Anglican 
clergy. In the exercise of Economy there seems to be only one great 
difficulty to their receiving the most formal authorization to do so. 
The sustained Roman attack upon the validity of Anglican Orders has 
naturally caused the Eastern-Orthodox to be cautious in such a matter. 
Thirty years ago their view of our Orders was prejudiced by such 
arguments as the Nag’s Head Fable. Since then patient investigation 
has done its work. The hostility of Rhossis and Maltzev has changed, 
first into the hesitation of Bulgakov and Androutsos, and then into the 
favourable judgments voiced by Komnenos, Dyobouniotes, and others. 
It would seem that all that is needed to determine the Orthodox World 
to accept our orders as valid is clear evidence that the Anglican doctrine 
of the Sacred Ministry is substantially the same as its own, and more 
than one Eastern-Orthodox theologian has formulated questions ad 
koc which would satisfy them on the point. 

The Declaration was prepared with a view to showing that a large 
proportion of the clergy of the Anglican Church, if not a majority, 
believe that without rejecting her formularies and in consonance with 
her living tradition aiey themselves can reply satisfactorily to those 
questions. 

Asa preliminary to its drafting the matter was discussed at length in 
a book, Anglican Relations with the Eastern-Orthodox, published last 
year by the Rev. J. A. Douglas. In that book it was made quite clear 
that, granted their power, the last thing which the author and his 
friends were willing to do would be to attempt to commit the Church 
of England to their own interpretation of her formularies. However 


much they might wish to do that, they would be altogether unwilling 


to force their fellow Anglicans who disagreed with them into schism. 
Indeed, it has been their constant anxiety that the great friendship 


between the Anglican and Eastern Churches should not provide muni- 


tions for Anglican domestic controversies. 

That is why they have not deprecated as prejudicial to their own 
movement the effect of some of the utterances made by groups or 
by official persons who are working in the cause of Home Reunion. 
On the contrary they have been inspired by the great Lambeth 
Encyclical to take it as the duty of those who have special affinities 
with the Protestant Churches to emphasize them in every way possible

A DECLARATION OF FAITH. 


To His Houiness CECUMENICAL PATRIARCH AND THE HOLY 
SYNOD OF THE GREAT CHURCH OF CONSTANTINOP L 


We , the undersigned, being priests of the English Church, 

earnestly desiring to promote an understanding with our 
brethren of the Eastern-Orthodox Churches, and rejoicing that in 
God's providence there are manifold evidences of the growth of 
sympathy and mutual love on both sides, now in all humility write to 
your Holiness and to the Holy Synod, in order to set forth plainly that 
which we hold to be the genuine teaching of the English Church on 
certain matters of faith, touching which we gather from the utterances 
of Eastern-Orthodox hierarchs and theologians that they would welcome 
some explicit statement from us. 

1. We affirm that the one saving faith, in which it is the will ot 
God that all Christians should be held together in the unity of the 
Spirit and the bond of peace, is the faith of the undivided Church of 
Christ, which faith is set forth in the Holy Scriptures, has been handed 
on by the Holy Fathers in their writings and by the tradition of the 
Church, and in opposition to the negations and corruptions of heretics 
has been reaffirmed and safeguarded by CEc: imenical Councus through 
the operation of the Holy Ghost. 

2. We afhrm that, notwithstanding individual departures therefrom, 
this faith has not been forsaken by the English Church. 

3. We affirm that each particular Church its rightly independent 
(autokephalos) and self-administered (autodiotketos) in matters whieh are 
not essential to the faith or practice of the Universal Church. 

4. We hold that an (Ecumenical Council is the supreme tribunal 
of the One Holy Catholic and Apostolic Church, and that the dogmatic 
decrees of the Councils which have been accepted as (Ecumenical by 
the who'e Church are incontrovertible and binding on all Christians. 

5. As touching any matters in which the Church of England may 
seem in the opinion of Eastern theologians to have departed from the 
dogmatic decrees of the GEcumenical Councils, we declare that we 
know of no such departure, and that we accept the faith as expounded - 
by those Councils. ; 

6. Though the number of the Sacraments has not been formally

fixed either by tradition from the Apostles or by any decision of an 
GEcumenical Council, we agree that in Baptism, the Eucharist, 
Confirmation, Penance, Orders, Matrimony, and Unction some special 
and appropriate grace is given by God to man as ordained either by 
Christ or by His Apostles, acting on the authority which He gave 
them ; and we adhere to the custom of calling these seven rites specific- 
ally sacraments. 

7. We affirm the essential necessity of the Sacrament of Order 
according to the institution of Christ, the practice of the Apostles, and 
the constant tradition of the Church. This is secured in the Churches 
of the Anglican Communion by the transmission of the several orders 
of the hierarchy by the imposition of the hands of the bishops, to 
whom it has been transmitted in like manner by an uninterrupted 
succession from the days of the Apostles. 

WE, the undersigned, therefore, hold that our Lord, through the 


_ ministry of the successors of the Apostles, has conferred on us and on 


all the members of the clergy of the Anglican Communion the 
Sacrament of Order, with the purpose that we, who are priests, 
should (.:) preach and teach the word of God; (6) offer the unbloody 
sacrifice of the Eucharist for both the living and the departed ; (c) 
sacramentally absolve sinners who repent and confess their sins; and 
(2) otherwise minister to the flock of Christ according to the ancient 
faith and practice of the Universal Church. 

8. We affirm that, by consecration in the Eucharist, the bread and 
the wine, being blessed by the life-giving power of the Holy Spirit, are 
changed and become the true body and the true blood of Christ, and 
as such are given to and received by the faithful. We hold, thercfore, 
that Christ thus present is to be adored. As to the actual manner of 
the change, and of the mode of the presence, no doctrine on this point 
is laid down by any CGicumenical Council, and, therefore, while believ- 
ing the fact, we do not venture to define the mode. There is here a 
divine mystery which passes human understanding. 

g. We agree with the Holy Orthodox Eastern Church that honour 


‘should be given to the holy and ever-virgin Mother of God and the 


Saints departed ; that there is a legitimate usé of sacred images; and 
that, alike in our public and in our private prayers, we should ask for 


the benefit of the intercession of the Saints. 


10. We account the Thirty-nine Articles of Religion as a document 
of secondary importance concerned with local controversies of the 
sixteenth century, and to be interpreted in accordance with the faith 
of that Universal Church of which the aie Church is but a part.

The history of the Orthodox Church of Georgia reveals a typical 
process of the birth and development of a local Orthodox Church: 
baptized by missionaries of a neighbouring Church, the newly con- 
verted flock passes the first period of its Church life in a close canonical 
dependence on the Mother-Church; as the local ecclesiastical life 
develops and the national hierarchy strengthens, the new diocese 
receives a larger autonomy; the ties which attach it to the distant 
Mother-Church weaken, becoming more and more nominal; in the 
last stage, the dependence on the Mother-Church expresses itself in 
forms of subordination more symbolical than practical (for instance, 
by the ratification by the Mother-Church of the local Primate before 
his elevation to this dignity; by the sending to the Metropolis of a 
certain amount of the Church receipts; by the mention of the name 
of the head of the Mother-Church at service; by the obligatory recep- 
tion of the holy oil from the Mother-Church, etc.). Lastly comes 
the formal granting of complete independence from the Mother- 
Church. As said above, this takes place by decision of the Mother-

certain autonomy in its interior affairs, but its head remained a Greek, 
receiving consecration from the Patriarch of Antioch. 

In 553, in the reign of the Georgian King Pharsinan, the Catholicos 
of Mtskheta and all Georgia was elected by and from among the 
Georgian clergy, continuing to receive consecration from Antioch. 
In the VIIIth century, after the death of the Catholicos Bartholomew, 
his successor, Catholicos John, went to Antioch, where the Council of 
the Antiochian Church, presided over by the Patriarch Theophilactus, 
granted autocephaly to the Georgian Church. In the Xth century 
the Catholicos of the Georgian Church was elevated to the dignity 
of Patriarch and Catholicos of all united Georgia. 

The decision concerning the granting of autocephaly to the Georgian | 
Church was later confirmed: (1) In the XIth century by the Patriarch 
of Antioch, Theodosius (in connection with the question of the prepara- 
tion of the holy oil); (2) by the Council of the Antiochian Church 
(also in the XIth century) presided over by the Patriarch Peter; 
(3) and in the same century by his successor, Theodosius. 

The great canonist of the Orthodox Church, Theodore Balsamon, 
Patriarch of Antioch, in his analy sis of Chapter 2 of the Resolutions 
of the (Ecumenical Council, mentions the Georgian Church among 
the autocephalous Churches of the East, and writes that the Arch- 
bishop of Iveria was recognized as independent by the Antiochian 
Council. The Byzantine canonist—Mathias Vlastav, author of the 
“Elementary Syntagma ’’—also states that the Georgian Church is 
not dependent on any of the Eastern Patriarchs. 

Being autocephalous, the Georgian Church took part in the common 
life of all the Churches. 

_ She was invited to take part in: ~ 

1. The Council of Florence (1439-1442), from which her repre- 
sentatives, the Metropolitans John-Gregory and Dositheos, withdrew, 
considering as unacceptable the plans of the Emperor John VI. 

2. The Council of Basle. 

3. The Council of Moscow (1666), which was convoked for the trial 
of the Patriarch Nikon of Russia; at which the Georgian Church was

But the process of the canonical development of the Georgian 
Church did not stop with the acquisition of complete canonical inde- 
pendence. The expansion of the Kingdom of Georgia, its gradual 
decentralization, and the development of the political autonomy of 
the Western Georgian provinces, involved the necessity of granting to 
the senior among the bishops of these provinces a canonical autonomy. 

This perfectly canonical process ended in 1390 by the granting 


of autocephaly to the Archbishop of Bitjvinta (or Abkhasia), with 


the title of Catholicos of Abkhasia and Litjvinta, and from 1444, of 
Catholicos of Abkhasia, Imeretia, and Western Georgia. His spiritual 
jurisdiction extended over all the autonomous principalities of Western 
Georgia (Imeretia, Guria, Mingrelia, Svanetia, and Abkhasia). 

In 1657 the seat of the Catholicos of Western Georgia was trans- 
ferred to Gelati, near Kutais. 

Such a canonical division of Georgia into two Catholicates was 
fully acknowledged by the Orthodox Patriarchs of the East. We 
have testimony to this effect from the Patriarch Macarius of Antioch, 
and the Patriarchs Dositheos and Chrysanthos of Jerusalem, who 
addressed themselves, according to a special formula, to each of these 
two Catholicoi. 

The existence of the autocephalous Catholicate of Western 
Georgia continued from 1390 to the end of the XVIIIth century.

Exhausted by a thousand years’ strife for the defence of Chris- 
tianity against Islam in the person of its diverse representatives 
(Arabs in the VIIth, VIIIth, and IXth centuries; Turks-Seldjuks 
in the XIth century; Mongolo-Tartars, under the leadership of 
Tchingis-Khan, in the XIIIth century; Tartars, led by-Famerlan, 
in the XIVth century; Persians and Turks in the XVIth, XVIIth, 
and XVIIIth centuries), Georgia turned for help to her northern 
neighbour and co-religionist, Russia. By a treaty, signed in 1783 
by Catherine II., Empress of Russia, and the Georgian King Heraclius 
[]., Georgia put herself under the protectorate of Russia, who, on 
her side, assumed the obligation of the defence.of Georgia from foreign 
aggression, Conserving to her a.complete autonomy in internal affairs, 
and..guaranteeing the succession of the Georgian Royal dynasty of 
the Bagratids. 

Although canonical law, as we have said above, cannot be the 
object of constitutional or international legislation, we will here note 


that, according to the treaty of 1783, the Orthodox Georgian Church 
was left unchanged.

Twelve years after the conclusion of this treaty, when the armies 
of the Persian Shah Aga-Mahomet-Khan, who was irritated by the 
fact that Georgia had put herself under Russian protection, ap- 
proached the Georgian frontiers, Russia, failing completely in her 
obligation, gave no help whatsoever to Georgia, which fell a victim 
to an invasion the like of which had not been sufferéd by her since 
the days of Tamerlan. | 

The rest of the story is known: King Heraclius II. died without 
having seen the liberation of his country, which was reduced to desola- 
tion and burning ruins. His son, King George XIJJ., reigned three 
years and a half. 

After the invasion of Georgia, which took place under the eyes 
of an indifferent Russia, in answer to the request of the Georgian 
Government to consider the question of a more effective defence otf 
Georgia in the future, the Russian Emperors Paul I. and Alexander I., 
after the death of King George XIII., simply annexed the kingdom 
of Georgia to Russia, proclaiming her a department of their empire, 
thus cancelling the treaty of 1783, concerning the protectorate of 
Russia over Georgia, and the guarantee of the sovereignty and auto- 
nomy of the latter. 

At the same time the very ancient Georgian Church was deprived 
simply by decree of a laic power of the possibility of exercising her 
autocephaly. 

‘‘T wish,” wrote Emperor Paul I. in 1801, ‘‘that Georgia be a depart- 
ment, and therefore place her immediately in relations with the Senate, 
and 1 all that concerns her ecclesiastical affairs, with the Synod.” 

In 1811, by decree of Emperor Alexander I., the Catholicos- 
Patriarch Antonius II. was ‘‘ convoked’’ to Russia, where he died. 
After his death the Georgian Church was not allowed to proceed to 
the election of a Catholicos-Patriarch, but a Governor-General of the 
Russian Synod was sent out from St. Petersburg withthe title of Exarch 
of Georgia, to rule the most ancient autocephalous Church of Georgia. 

It is not our purpose to criticize the motives of Emperor Paul I., 
and, beyond saying that he evidently thought that he was doing his 
best for his empire, we shall only remark that his order concerning 
the Georgian Church, being the decree of a Jaic power, was devoid 
of all canonical (ecclesiastical) importance. Therefore the ancient auto- 
cephalous Orthodox Church of Georgia (as we have already said) was 
not deprived of her autocephaly, but only of the physical possibility 
of exercising it, and remained, as she always had been, autocephalous. 

In fact, the Russian bishops who, in execution of an irregular 
and anti-canonical decree of the laic power, interfered in the govern- 
ment of the autocephalous Orthodox Georgian Church committed 
a grave offence against the laws of the Church.

The Exarchate of the Synod of the Russian Church in Georgia 
was, during the whole time of its existence (over a hundred years), 
the instrument of the Russian Government for the carrying out of 
a definite State policy in the Caucasus, but we will refrain from any 
judgment of the work of the Exarchate in this connection, firstly, 
because it was precisely for the carrying out of this policy that it 
was created by the Russian Government, and secondly, because the 
very existence of a Synodal Exarchate over the Georgian Church 
being illegal and anti-canonical, there is no occasion to approach its 
deeds from the point of view of legality or of justice. 

A few examples, however, will give some idea of the Exarchal 
régime to which the Georgian Church was obliged to submit: some 
time after the enforcement of the Exarchal tutelage over the Church 
of Georgia. Her property, valued at over a hundred million golden 
roubles, was confiscated; the Georgian language was systematically 
suppressed and replaced by Slavonic, even in those parishes where 
the congregation understood not a word of it; the beautiful ancient 
Georgian Church music—being considered to resemble the “‘ bleating 
of a goat ’!)—was replaced by Slavonic chants, incomprehensible to- 
the congregation; the Georgian bishops were exiled, etc. 

The rule of the Russian Synod over the Georgian Church was also 
exceedingly unprofitable for the latter—for instance, with the per- 
mission of the Exarch Eusebius, a very rare XIth-century text of. 
the Gospel was taken from the monastery of Gelati, stripped of its 


precious stones, and returned with false ones; from the Cathedral! 


of Sion very great quantities of precious stones were taken away; 
the same was done with the jewels belonging to the Patriarchal 
Cathedral of Mtskheta, and the monasteries of Alaverdi and Bodbi. 
The old historical sacred books and manuscripts, with artistic minia- 
tures and illuminations, were carried off; the famous image of the 
Virgin (valued at fifty thousand golden roubles) was taken away 
from the Castle Church of Metekhi in Tiflis by order of the Exarch 
Palladius. Mr. Sabingus took the best of the Georgian holy images, 
under pretext of restoring them, and‘despoiled them of their jewels. 
There was also systematic repression of the Georgian bishops and 
high clergy, for a peaceful and purely academic defence of the auto- 
cephalv of the Georgian Church. The exile of Bishops Kyrion and 
Leonidas and of the Archimandrite Ambrose are but typical incidents 
of this lamentable period in the history of the Georgian Church.

It is beyond possibility even to enumerate all the afflictions, insults, and 
humiliations inflicted upon the representatives of the Orthodox Russian hier-

esplonage and surveillance, inclusive of actual domiciliary arrest. Constraint 
is imposed on all his actions, and not in private alone, but even when he officiates 
in places of public worship, whither Bolshevist spies frequently penetrate with

happened once that for such base purposes the atheistic Hebrew magnate 
Zinoviev, together with his secretary, Antzelovitch, an unbaptized Jew, made 
his appearance in the Smolny cathedral while the Metropolitan was officiating. 
Every Russian hierarch lives under a continual risk of imprisonment, where, 
without formal accusation or warning, he is menaced with a summary execution. 
Being human he can but surmise om his fate and reverently beseech the Lord to

annoyances which are frequently purposely prolonged, as in the case of Bishop

bishop on his throne is treated like a criminal on the block, and after death may

Tie condition of the Orthodox in America is at present very difficult, as 


recent happenings have brought to lght the pressing necessity for some. 


sort of organization more than the loose federation of various Orthodox 
national units affords. As would be expected, the Russians have suffered very 
grievously, both from the conditions in the mother-country, which deprive 
the Russian Orthodox here of the former Government subsidy, and bring many 
poverty-stricken émigrés to this country, and from the inroads of Bolshevism, 
which is conducting a vigorous and intelligent campaign to win Russians away 
from the Church. Many Russians were not well grounded in their faith, and 
fall easy. victims to the lures of religious Bolshevism. In one instance known 
to the writer, a Russian priest said that of the Russian community of approxi- 
mately 1,000, scarcely a tenth had remained loyal to the Church. The Russian 
seminary has been labouring under many difficulties, partly financial, and 
partly due to the lack of cohesion and system in the polity of Orthodoxy in 
America. Of the different national groups, the Russians are probably the 
poorest, and the Greeks the richest. The Serbians are making steady and solid 
progress, and were much heartened by the visit, some months ago, of Bishop 
Nicolai. Of the minor groups, the problem of organization is exceedingly 
complicated, due to the ties with the old countries, and the devious policies of 
various national and racial rivalries.

JERUSALEM LETTER 


HE Easter ceremonies in the Church of the Holy Sepulchre attracted 

the usual number of visitors, and, although there were necessarily no 

Russian pilgrims, the number of Coptic pilgrims from Egypt was greater 
than usual. Particularly impressive and interesting were the traditional 
services of the Washing of the Feet on Maundy Thursday, conducted by the 
Orthodox and Armenian Patriarchs and the Jacobite Bishop. The Orthodox 
service is performed in the open, in the courtyard in front of the Holy Sepulchre ; 
and at this service the preaching of a sermon in English by one of the Archi- 
mandrites of the Patriarchate from the open-air pulpit in the wall of the Convent 
of Abraham is already becoming traditional. The Armenian ceremony of the 
Washing of the Feet, held in the magnificent Cathedral of St. James, is probably 
the most beautiful of all the Yerusalem Church ceremonies. Not only are the 
vestments worn on this occasion among the most remarkable vestments of” 
any Christian Church, but the service is rendered yet more touching by the 
singing of traditional Armenian Church melodies by a choir of Armenian girls 
sitting on the floor in the middle of the nave of the Cathedral. On this occasion 
‘an Armenian Patriarch of Jerusalem presided over the ceremony for the first 
time for eleven years; and at its conclusion the Anglican Bishop mounted the 
altar platform and read the Gospel in English, and pronounced the Benediction. 

On the evening of Maundy Thursday an English service was held in the 
open air below the Russian Church of Gethsemane. 

The Good Friday services in the Holy Sepulchre were above all remarkable 
for the ceremonies and processions of the Jacobites and Copts, the Coptic 
Metropolitan of Jerusalem having left the Egyptian Monastery where he usually 
resides to pay one of his infrequent visits to Jerusalem. At the Orthodox 
service in Golgotha, with its poignant ceremonial, the Archimandrite Kyriakos, 
who had preached at the Washing of the Feet, again preached in English. 

The Holy Fire on the Saturday before Easter, in which only the Orthodox 
and Armenian Churches participate, again attracted a vast and excited throng 
to the Rotunda of the Holy Sepulchre; but, thanks to the precautionary measures 
taken by the Governorate, the concomitants of the service were somewhat more 
seemly than on previous occasions. The curious Abyssinian procession around 
the Dome on the roof of St. Helena’s Chapel on the night of the Saturday was 
the climax of an orgy of Eastern ecclesiastical ceremonial. 

Among the Orthodox prelates present in Jerusalem during Holy Week were 
the Archbishop of Rhodes, Apostolos, who, it will be remembered, was expelled 
from Rhodes by the Italian authorities on account of his refusal to meet the 
Italian Crown Prince on the occasion of his visit to the Dodecanese, and Bishop 
Nicolai Velimirovic of Ochrida. Bishop Nicolai has been spending several 
weeks in the Holy Land, and on the Sunday after Easter preached an impressive 
English sermon in St. George’s Cathedral. 

An energetic effort is being made to collect money on behalf of Miss 
Warburton’s British High School for Girls in Jerusalem. This school, which 
fills most admirably an essential place in the educational system of Palestine, 
1S in urgent need of funds; and it would be a serious misfortune for the country 
if lack of money were to compel it to be closed. 

The series of Lenten lectures in St. George’s Cathedral Chapter Library | 
was brought to a close in the first week of May. These lectures dealt with a 
variety of Jewish, Christian, and Moslem topics, and included lectures on the 
relations between the Orthodox and Anglican Churches by Canon Danby, and 
on the liturgy of St. Chrysostom by the Archbishop of the Jordan. These 
lectures, which attract audiences drawn from all communities, are yet another 
instance of the manner in which St. George’s fulfils its peculiarly valuable 
function as a neutral meeting-ground for all in Jerusalem.
