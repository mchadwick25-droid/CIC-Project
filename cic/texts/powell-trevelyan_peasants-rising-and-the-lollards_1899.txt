PREFACE 


IN  the  Preface  to  the  recently  published  volume '  England  in 
the  Age  of  Wycliffe,'  the  author  expressed  his  intention  of 
following  up  that  work  by  the  publication  of  such  new 
materials  as  had  been  used  in  its  construction.  It  was  by 
Mr.  Edgar  Powell  that  he  was  first  introduced  to  these 
materials,  and  by  his  assistance  he  now  fulfils  the  promise 
of  publication  then  made.  It  is  hoped  that  these  docu- 
ments will  bear  out  that  part  of  '  England  in  the  Age  of 
Wycliffe  '  which  was  based  upon  their  authority.  But  they 
also  contain  much  interesting  detail  concerning  incidents 
which  were  only  casually  mentioned  in  that  work.  Students 
engaged  on  the  period,  either  from  love  of  reading  or  from 
hope  of  writing,  will  find  here  both  curious  and  important 
matter.  The  editors  have  here  and  there  inserted  references 
to  direct  search  to  other  sources  of  information  on  the  sub- 
jects in  question,  and  have  given,  on  page  18,  a  short  list 
showing  where  documents  relating  to  other  trials  of  the 
rioters  of  1381  have  been  printed,  either  wholly  or  in  part. 

It  has  been  thought  best,  in  order  to  avoid  much  repeti- 
tion and  mere  legal  phraseology,  to  give  the  documents  here 
published  in  an  abbreviated  form  except  in  cases  where  they 
are  described  as  transcripts  in  full. 


INTRODUCTION 


THE  first  section  of  these  documents  (pp.  1-23)  will  be  found 
to  contain  new  matter  relating,  first,  to  the  Peasants'  Kising 
of  1381  (pp.  1-18),  and,  secondly,  to  other  riots  arising 
from  similar  discontents  that  disturbed  the  later  years  of 
Eichard  II  (pp.  19-23). 

The  contemporary  evidence  about  the  actual  events  of  the 
Peasants'  Eising  of  1381,  as  distinguished  from  its  social 
causes,  falls  mainly  under  two  heads,  the  chronicles  and  the 
official  records  of  the  trials  of  individual  rioters.  It  ia 
difficult  to  say  which  of  these  two  sources  is  now  the  more 
important.  If  either  of  them  were  taken  away,  the  history  of 
the  rising  would  be  meagre  and  its  real  nature  misunder- 
stood. Only  if  we  put  the  two  together,  piece  by  piece,  and 
critically  compare  the  one  with  the  other  in  every  point, 
do  we  gradually  obtain  a  true  and  fully  coloured  picture 
of  these  'old,  unhappy,  far-off  things.'  On  the  one  hand 
the  chronicles  have  the  advantages  that  usually  belong  to 
historical  documents  written  on  purpose  to  inform  future 
generations  of  the  events  that  have  occurred.  It  is  in  the 
chronicles  that  we  find  the  clearest  general  account  of  the 
nature  and  characteristics  of  the  rising  as  it  appeared  to 
contemporaries.  It  is  from  the  chronicles  that  we  gather 
the  great  strategic  and  moral  importance  of  certain  inci- 
dents of  the  rising,  such  as  the  series  of  events  that  took  place 
in  London  between  June  12  and  15. 

It  is,  in  fact,  the  chronicles  that  give  us  the  main  out- 
lines of  the  story  of  the  rebellion,  and  so  enable  us  to

interpret  the  large  amount  of  detailed  information  contained 
in  the  trials  of  the  rioters. 

This  second  class  of  documents,  on  the  other  hand,  has 
many  great  advantages.  These  trials,  in  the  first  place, 
supply  us  with  dates  both  accurate  and  numerous,  while 
the  chronicles  are  often  wrong  as  to  the  days  on  which 
particular  riots  took  place.  In  the  story  of  the  brief  but 
fateful  occupation  of  London  by  the  rebels,  the  order  of  events 
and  the  days  on  which  they  occurred  is  of  the  first  import- 
ance. It  is,  therefore,  an  extremely  fortunate  circumstance 
that  while  the  chronicles  contradict  each  other  as  to  the  days 
on  which  the  proceedings  in  London  took  place,  a  final  court 
of  appeal  is  ready  to  hand  in  the  trials  of  the  rioters  who  took 
part  in  the  occupation  of  the  capital.  Still  more  important, 
perhaps,  are  the  dates  of  rioting  in  the  several  country 
districts,  which  the  trials  of  the  offenders  alone  supply  to  us. 
It  is  by  these  dates  that  it  is  possible  to  estimate  the  pace  at 
which  the  rebellion  spread  through  England,  and  the  length 
of  time  during  which  it  continued  to  break  out  afresh  in  the 
more  distant  provinces  after  it  had  been  crushed  at  the 
centre. 

But  it  is  not  as  regards  dates  alone  that  the  trials  are 
more  full  and  accurate  than  the  chronicles.  As  regards 
area  and  locality  they  give  far  more  detail. 

All  accounts  of  riots  in  small  country  places  must  be 
drawn  from  these  trials ;  the  chronicles  confine  them- 
selves chiefly  to  the  events  in  London  or  to  the  attacks 
on  well-known  abbeys  and  castles,  while  the  trials,  on  the 
other  hand,  supply  us  with  detailed  and  personal  accounts 
of  the  events  of  that  stormy  summer  in  a  number  of 
remote  villages  and  country  towns.  These  are  severally 
interesting  to  the  local  antiquarian,  and  collectively  they 
enable  us  to  form  a  judgment  of  the  nature  of  the  rising  in 
the  country  districts,  independent  of  the  generalisations  of 
the  chronicles,  which  indeed  they  in  some  points  correct. 
The  trials  have  also  considerably  enlarged  the  known  area  of 
the  rebellion.  The  chronicles  leave  it  uncertain  how  far  the 
rising  spread  beyond  the  eastern  and  home  counties,  but  the 
trials  give  us  details  of  riots  in  York,  Doncaster,  Bridge-

water,  and  villages  in  Leicestershire,  Wiltshire,  and  the 
Wirral  of  Cheshire.  Lastly,  whereas  the  chronicles,  except 
in  a  very  few  places,  speak  only  of  the  actions  of  the  mob  aa 
a  whole,  the  trials  more  frequently  give  us  the  action  of 
individual  rioters,  and  often  suggest  the  personal  motives,  as 
of  revenge  for  private  wrongs,  that  actuated  a  certain  propor- 
tion of  the  ringleaders  of  a  movement  which  nevertheless 
was,  upon  the  whole,  a  national  uprising  to  obtain  common 
ends. 

The  documents  on  pp.  1-18  below  are  the  latest  addition 
to  the  publications  of  this  important  class  of  documents. 
The  trials  here  printed  have  several  new  points  of  interest. 
In  the  first  place  another  county,  Cheshire  (p.  13),  has  been 
added  to  the  area  of  rebellion.  Curiously  enough  it  is  the 
Wirral,  the  most  remote  portion  of  the  county,  the  district 
seemingly  furthest  removed  from  the  contagion  of  the  eastern 
counties,  that  we  find  convulsed.  It  was  towards  the 
end  of  July,  after  the  news  of  the  suppression  of  the  rioters 
in  London  had  reached  the  district,  and  after  letters  from 
the  king  denouncing  the  rebellion  had  been  read  in  the  full 
shire  court  of  Cheshire,  that  the  serfs  of  the  Abbot  of 
Chester  rose  in  the  Wirral  (p.  15).  The  abbeys  were 
frequent  victims  of  mob  violence  during  this  summer,  as 
the  tenure  of  the  serfs  on  the  estates  of  corporate  bodies 
was  generally  more  antiquated  than  the  tenure  of  serfs  on 
the  estates  of  individual  landlords.  On  p.  17  we  find  another 
abbey  in  Middlesex  which  can  be  added  to  the  long  list  of 
religious  houses  attacked  during  the  rebellion.  The  new 
series  of  Kent  indictments  (pp.  3-12),  together  with  those 
printed  by  the  Kent  Archaeological  Society,  form  a  rich  source 
of  information  about  the  rising  in  Kent. 

On  pp.  19-23  will  be  found  official  records  of  charges 
against  rioters,  very  similar  to  those  of  the  year  1381. 
These  records  show  that  in  localities  the  events  of  the  great 
rising  repeated  themselves  in  the  later  years  of  Eichard  II. 
Of  these  later  social  disturbances  the  chronicles  tell  us 
nothing,  and  the  trials  are  the  sole  evidence  that  they 
occurred.  The  riots  of  the  year  1392  in  the  south  Yorkshire 
towns  are  made  specially  interesting  by  the  rebel  song,  which

is  recorded  at  length  in  the  accusation  (p.  19).  It  ranks  in 
the  nature  of  its  importance  with  those  rebel  songs  of  1381,  so 
redolent  of  the  mystery  and  earnestness  of  '  Piers  Plowman,' 
which  we  find  recorded  in  the  chronicles1 ;  for,  like  them, 
this  song  of  the  Yorkshire  rioters  of  1392  opens  a  narrow 
window  through  which  we  gain  a  glance  into  the  hearts  and 
minds  of  those  who  rose  in  revolt,  believing  that  '  the  King's 
son  of  heaven  should  pay  for  all.'  The  Yorkshire  song  is 
not  indeed  so  religious  in  sentiment  as  the  song  of  the  south 
country  rebels,  where  John  the  Miller,  who  '  yground  small, 
small,  small,'  bade  '  John  Trueman  and  all  his  fellows '  '  do 
well  and  better  and  flee  sin,  and  seek  peace  and  hold  therein,' 
as  a  preliminary  to  murder  and  rebellion ;  but  it  proves  that 
many  of  the  rioters  of  1392  were  not  merely  bent  on  plunder 
and  arson,  but  were  inspired  by  an  ideal  of  independent 
manhood  which  could  not  tamely  endure  slavery  and  wrong. 
Every  one  interested  in  the  ideals  of  those  troublous  times 
should  study  it  as  it  stands  on  p.  19  below. 

But  the  story  of  the  resistance  offered  in  1398  by  the 
peasantry  of  Wellington  to  the  manorial  dues  of  the  Bishop 
of  Bath  and  Wells  is  no  less  interesting  (p.  21),  for  here  the 
question  at  issue  is  clearer  than  the  grievances  of  the  York- 
shire rioters.  This  case  is  one  of  the  best  of  several  similar 
cases  that  show  how,  in  the  twenty  years  following  the 
rising,  feudal  dues  were  still  often  refused,  and  the  refusal 
backed  by  force.  '  This  attitude  of  resistance  was  an  impor- 
tant factor  in  the  economic  causes  which  drove  the  landlord 
to  manumit  his  serfs ;  if  they  worked  unwillingly  and 
rebelliously  at  the  forced  labour,  the  forced  labour  must  soon 
be  changed  for  paid  service.'2

Section  II  (pp.  27-38)  contains  documents  relating  to 
the  trial  of  John  of  Northampton,  Mayor  of  London  from 
1381  to  1383  :  he  was  the  partisan  of  John  of  Gaunt  against 
Richard  II ;  he  was  the  champion  of  the  clothing  trades 
and  of  the  majority  of  Londoners,  in  their  struggle  against 
the  economic  privileges  of  the  victualling  trades  and  the 
grocers ;  he  once  headed  a  mob  hostile  to  Church  juris- 
diction in  a  demonstration  against  the  Bishops.  Although 
some  account  of  these  events  has  been  given  in  '  England 
in  the  Age  of  Wycliffe,' l  a  deep  study  of  economic  questions 
and  political  parties  in  London  might  bring  to  light  some 
further  clue  to  the  rise  and  fall  of  John  of  Northampton. 
But  the  document  printed  on  pp.  27-38  below  will  probably 
remain  the  principal  authority  on  the  subject.  The  student 
will  bear  in  mind  that  the  words  are  the  words  of  North- 
ampton's enemies,  and  that  the  case  is  only  the  case  against 
him.  Among  the  many  eccentric  actions  by  which  he  curried 
favour  with  the  populace  of  London,  one  is  specially  interest- 
ing. He  appears  (p.  30)  to  have  assisted  through  the  action 
of  his  subordinates  in  the  acquittal  of  the  rebel  aldermen 
who  had  let  the  peasants  into  London  in  June  1381. 
Sybyle  and  Horn  certainly  deserved  to  be  hanged  for 
the  part  they  had  played,2  but  that  part  had  been  pleasing 
to  the  apprentices  and  to  the  lower  orders  of  the  capital, 
and  a  popular  mayor  was  not  likely  to  show  them  great 
severity.

E.  II. 

THIS  Table  is  made  out  from  the  names  of  County  Members 
given  in  the  Eeturn  of  Members,  Blue  Book  for  1878,  vol.  i. 
Only  the  County  Members'  names  are  there  recorded  with 
sufficient  completeness  to  afford  a  basis  for  calculation.  The 
names  of  the  Members  are  represented  by  letters  in  this  table, 
to  facilitate  a  bird's  eye  view  of  the  change  of  personnel. 
Every  member  of  the  Good  Parliament  (the  first  in  the  Table) 
is  called  a.  Every  member  of  the  next  Parliament  is  called  b, 
except  those  eight  members  who  were  previously  elected  for 
the  Good  Parliament,  who  continue  to  be  called  a.  In  the 
third  Parliament  in  the  Table  (Oct.  '77)  all  the  members  are  c, 
who  are  not  a  or  b  by  virtue  of  having  sat  in  one  of  the  two 
previous  assemblies.  And  so  all  the  way  through.  When 
there  are  two  members  of  the  same  county,  both  known  by 
the  same  letter,  they  are  distinguished,  whenever  either  reap- 
pears, by  small  numbers  added  above  :  e.g. — a1  or  62  can  be 
distinguished  from  a2  or  61,  each  being  a  different  member 
for  the  same  county. 

Peter  de  la  Mare,  the  Speaker  for  the  Good  Parliament, 
is  a2  for  Hereford.  It  will  be  observed  that  even  so  distin- 
guished a  Parliamentarian  as  De  la  Mare  was  by  no  means 
elected  every  time,  even  to  those  Parliaments  which  were  not 
packed  by  John  of  Gaunt.
