THE WORK OF LAUSANNE 


A S we write the diplomats at Lausanne are busy with a thrilling game. 
i It 1s a game to their taste—‘‘Give and take’’; ‘take and give."’ 


Great issues are involved—political, social, economical. The result in the 


play of forces will be tixed largely by the skill of the individual negotiators. 

Who will be master of the Straits? Wall Soviet Russia be able to shut 
tight the Bosphorus with a Kemalist lock and to turn the Black Sea into a 
Bolshevik lake? Will the Kemalists be placed in Constantinople as the 
spear-head of the Communist army which is to invade Capitahst Europe ’ 
Will France be able to exact those lucrative Anatolian concessions for which 
M. Franklin-Bouillon bargained at Angora? Will the Hearst Press secure 
the substitution of Turkish ownership for British trusteeship at. Mosul, and 
the exploitation of the great oil wells by its subventing trusts? Will Signor 
Mussolini secure ample and substantial satisfaction for [Italian dignity and 
Italian appetite? Will the Vatican wrest the effective control of the Holy 
Places from its immemorial holders’ Will the Bulgars get a foothold on 
the -Lgean : 

These vast interests depend upon the skill-and the personality of the 


individuals who sit at the Council tables of the Conference, or meet to chat 


and fraternize in the hotels and cou/oivs at Lausanne. Over-subtlety here, a 
morning’s irritability there, a little frankness that 1s indiscreet, a little honesty 
that is unwise, a bit of trickery that is over-grasping, and the consequences 
will be writ large in the Peace Treaty which is to fix the map of the Near 


' East and to settle the destiny of the world. If Angora makes Ismet talk too 


loudly, if M.Chicherin becomes over-rude and domineering, 1f Lord Curzon 


fails for a moment in his marvellous dignity and patience, if M. Venizelos 


forgets his wisdom, if M. Stambulisky raises his voice too much, the Con- 
ference mav fail and a new World War begin. 

It would be idie to declare that the various objectives for which the 
different nations are contending at Lausanne are trivial. Patently they are 
of tremendous importance, and each nation engaged in the diplomatic 
Struggle has, from its own necessity or from its own principles, good 
justification for struggling to reach its own end. None the less, against 
them one and all, against the British Empire no-tess if. no--more-than 
any other of the European States, the pitiable indictment stands that none 
of them puts the first thing first. The peace and prosperity of the world 
depend, no doubt, on the hoped-for provisional solution of Near Eastern

problems—military, political, economic, social, territorial. Every wise 
student of affairs agrees with that proposition. But the humanitarian 
problem transcends all such problems. No one, however, at Lausanne— 
unless, indeed, it be Lord Curzon—can be suspected of demanding for his 
nation as its first interest that the lives and elementary rights of the ancient 
Christian people of the Ottoman dominions—and especially of the great 
Christian majority in Constantinople—should receive effective guarantees. 
It would almost seem that much though abstract sentiment may make such 
guarantees desirable, the Governments of Europe are, each and all of them, 
‘satisfied that to obtain them is not their own interest, and therefore not their 
particular duty. There is no need to worry about the Christian minorities 
of Turkey, say France and Italy. That question will settle itself. 

This comfortable confidence is not shared by the British people, which, 
in spite of the suppression of facts by the bulk of the London Press, is moved 
instinctively with an uneasy apprehension, well warranted no less by the 
little which it is atlowed to know of current events than by the past. 

What has happened in the Near East since our last issue? There has, 
indeed, been no great achievement of the Kemalists to parallel the sack of 
Christian Smyrna, which, as the new Under-Secretary for Foreign Affairs, 
Mr. R. McNeill, told the House of Commons on the 27th ult., was incon- 
testably fired by Turkish soldiers. All the same, Mustapha Kemal has 
pressed on with drastic energy to complete the Turkification of Asia Minor. 
The half-million or so Christians who remained in the villages and towns of 
that ruined and desolate country have been given, either by direct order or 
indirect hint, a’short, sharp month in which to get out of their native land. 
That 1s to say, if the old men, women, and children have strength to reach 
the sea-ports and ships are available to take them away, they have leave to 
make good their flight before the sword falls on them. The able-bodied 
men have been drafted off to the “white death’’ and slavery of labour 
battalions, past experience of which shows that few will survive the hard- 
ships of the winter. Among the exiles are those women and children who 
survived the holocaust of Smyrna, and whom individual Turks have not 
thought it worth while to retain for their personal service and pleasure. 
Very pitiful are the tales of the gaunt and emaciated survivors of the parties 
which, starting, say, 10,000 in number, dwindled, under hardship and pillage, 
to 2,000 before they reached the ships at Samsun or Trebizond. | 

On the other hand, nearly the whole Christian population of Thrace has 
wisely preferred the indescribable suffering, physical and mental, involved in 
abandoning its homes, and has taken refuge on the other side of the Maritza. 
The hunger, sickness, and other privations which they are there enduring 
have been made known to us by the appeal of the relief agents, but at least 
they are safe from the rapine and murder which are taking place in Asia 
Minor. 

Meanwhile, events have moved quickly in Constantinople. Under pretext 
of passing through to Thrace, Refet Pasha came to the great Christian city 
soon after the Mudania Armistice. Once there, he has stopped there, and 
bit by bit, thanks to dissensions among the High Commissioners of the

Entente, has taken control.into his hands. The censorship, the post, the 
telegraph, all means of communication except on the part of the nationals 
of European States, above all, the police are in his hands. The Turkish 
rabble of Stambul has been armed and is being reinforced by a daily 
infiltration of butchers from the heroic army which sacked Smyrna. The 
scene is thoroughly well staged, and Europe may wake any morning to hear of 
a Greek or Armenian “ conspiracy ”’ or of a provoked and punitive massacre of 
the defenceless mass of Christians who have dared to remain in the city. If 
the signal for raising the curtain on that drama is delayed, and if Refet is 
contenting himself with arresting, trying, and hanging individuals, it is only 
because Allied troops are still inconveniently present in Constantinople, and 
because a massacre there would have signal reactions at Lausanne. Given 


time anda treaty which closes the Dardanelles to European warships, abolishes _ 


the capitulations, and leaves the Turk master to exercise his will on his 
Christian “ nationals ”»—or given a complete breakdown of the Conference— 
and the Kemalist policy stands to be executed as fully as it was in Smyrna. 

Delay is irritating, but patience is clearly the Turk’s wisdom of the day in 
Constantinople. Is not yavash—slowly—his natural principle ? Nothing can 
be done until the British are out of Constantinople. 

Waiting through these dragging days must be hard for the Turk. That he 
has possessed his patience until now is due to the fact that a Turkish mob or 
army never gets to the work of plunder and massacre without permission from 
above. Even the banditti waited four days for Nur-ed-Din at smyrna.. Accord- 
ingly, in the absence of a signal from Angora, nothing except the sharpening 
of knives and the weighting of clubs has happened as yet at Constantinople. 
Nothing indeed may happen, even when Kemal enters Stambul, if Europe 
assembled at Lausanne only makes him understand that a repetition of his 
exploit at Smyrna will be followed by swift and certain retribution. 

That the danger of a catastrophe has grown not less but greater, and is 
as substantial as any such danger can ever be, is manifest and admitted. 

The “treason” trials and hangings of Moslems which have begun an 
Thrace serve to illustrate Turkish desires in regard to the Greek and 
Armenian. That there is no reticence is evident from the visit paid the 
Phanar early in November by a Stambul mob, which, aware that the time 
for more was not ripe, withdrew after riddling the doors of the Patriarchate 
with revolver shots, but promised to return and deal with the Patriarch 
Smyrniate fashion. 

What is preparing, and what is expected to happen, may be gathered 
by the appeal to the Christian Churches of the Jugo-Slav Patriarch Dmitri, 
who, like all his countrymen, has an inherited aptitude to understand the 
Turk. If further evidence and further warning be needed, they are given in 
‘the straight speech of the Note, addressed on December 4 to the Lausanne 
Conference, by the Vatican, which, tardy though it be in date, will be 
welcomed by the civilized world. It rums:

“To reassuretfiese populations"! How does it come that any Christians 
remain in-Cotistantinople under the imminent and increasingly plain threat 
of extermination ? | 

Partly, no doubt, because flight by land is impossible, and because no 
steps are available to transport three-quarters of a million human beings in 
a few weeks. As it is, 3,000 fugitives have sailed from Constantinople on 
an average every day for the past two months. 

The absence of panic, however, among the Greeks is largely due to the 
sublime and wonderful courage of a single man, the Gecumenical Patriarch 
Meletios IV. 

When the Mudania surrender was signed, insistent advice—strong 
pressure, we might say—was put on the Patriarch to induce him to withdraw 
to Mount Athos, In view of the importance of his life to the whole Orthodox 

hurch, and to the fact, as the Bishop of Gibraltar has put it, that except 
the Sultan no man in Constantinople would be in such danger as he, many 
of his friends in this country urged him to do so. 

‘| will only go,”’ he is reported to have answered, ‘‘ when the last of my 
people is gone, and I will only go if by my departure I do not jeopardize the 
rights of the Patriarchate. Nor, if I stay, will I accept an amnesty for my- 
self which is not accompanied by a full amnesty for every member of my 
flock.” 

And so, well knowing the personal risk he runs and has always run in 
Constantinople—our readers will remember how, when he reached the 
Phanar in February last for his enthronization, he knelt in silent prayer 
at the closed door where Gregory V. was hanged—he decided to stay, and 
forbade his people to give way to panic. That is why, while the majority 
of the Armenians of Constantinople are now in safety, only a small minority 
of the Greeks have left the city. : 

It is a big risk which Meletios IV. determined to run. Who shall say 
that he is not justified ? 

At any rate, Lord Curzon is said to have won M. Poincaré to the view 
that however Kemal may rage or M. Franklin-Bouillon complain, the Allies 
Shall insistat Lausanne that the Greeks remain in Constantinople under 
other than paper guarantees, or, that if that be not carried, they shall be 
evacuated in an orderly and peaceful fashion before the Allies leave the city 
Even so, the courage of a single man may yet save the Mother City of Eastern 
Christianity from being altogether Turkified.

The wonderful Orthodox communities of Pontus, Galatia, and Cappa-

Those Seven Apostolic Churches of Smyrna, Ephesus, Philadelphia,

This staff which you have brought me comes at a seasonable time.

Tell the Christians of the West that we Christians of the East, ‘‘ who still 
live and remain,” are sore pressed and broken, but that we bear no grudge

pray: May our Lord, the Faithful Witness, shower His blessings on the 
English Church for its sympathy with us in our martyrdom. © 


Let Chenistian England lay those words to heart. 

‘If the name of Meletios IV. is to be added tothe long roll of the martyrs 
of the great Church of Constantinople, he will go down with the Flag of the 
Cross flying. 

If, in spite of the desperate odds, his constancy wins safety for his people 
and preserves the life of the Patriarchate, his will be an honoured name 
among those who have dared everything to confess the Faith of Christ. 

We are not sanguine as to the issue at Lausanne, and we have grave

THE METROPOLITAN EVLOGIOS 151 


THE METROPOLITAN EVLOGIOS 


Our frontispiece this number is a portrait of the Right Rev. the Metro- 
politan Evlogios, Archbishop of Volhynia, and was taken recently in Paris, 
where he resides. Born in the Toula Government on April 10/23, 1863, 
Basil Georgievsky showed singular piety and aptitude in early boyhood, and, 
having completed his studies in the famous Academy of Moscow, was 
awarded the degree of B.D. in 1892. Appointed Head Master of the 
Efremovsky Ecclesiastical School in 1893, he moved on to the Ecclesiastical 
Seminary at Toula in 1894, and there took the veil in 1895, adopting the 
name Evlogios, being ordained priest on February 12/25, and. becoming In- 
spector of the Vladimir Ecclesiastical Seminary. Being made an Archi- 
mandrite in 1897, he served as Rector of the Ecclesiastical Seminary at 
Holm until 1902, when he was consecrated Bishop of the See of Lublin 
on December 5/18. In these various offices he had shown conspicuous 
administrative gifts, and no one was surprised when, in 1905, he was trans- 
lated to the See of Holm, or that he was made a member of the second State 
Duma in 1905 and of the thirdin 1g07._ In 1912 he was raised tothe dignity 
of Archbishop, and in 1914 transferred to the important See of Volhynia, in 
which diocese his untiring efforts to protect the Church and his people made 
him particularly hated by the Bolsheviks, in whose hands he was actually 
a prisoner in 1919, his life being saved and his liberation effected only by the 
energetic action of the Archbishop of Canterbury. In 1920 he was appointed 
Head of all the Russian Churches (congregations) in Western Europe, and 
on January 17/30, 1922, was raised by decree of His Holiness the Patriarch 
Tikhon to the rank of Metropolitan of all the Russian Orthodox Churches in 
Europe, thus becoming one of the seven Metropolitans of the Russian Church. 
He has visited London twice this year, first in April, when he came 
especially to make acquaintance with the Archbishop of Canterbury and to 
thank His Grace for his intervention on his behalf, and again in October in 
order to be present at the anniversary of the A. and E. A. On both occasions 
he pontificated at St. Philip's, Buckingham Palace Road, on the former. the 
Bishop of Willesden, and on the latter Bishop Gore, who also preached the 
sermon, assisting. He also supported the Bishop of London, making a 
charming and significant speech, at the general meeting of the Association, 
of which he is a Patron, in the City Guildhall on October 25, He is 
expected to visit London again early next year. Among the distinctions 
which he holds are: The Order of St. Anna (first degree, 1903); the Order 
of St. Vladimir (third degree, -1goy, second degree, 1911); rank of Arch- 
bishop, 1912; diamond cross on the head-dress (cowl), 1915; rank of Metro- 
politan, white head-dress and cross on the mitre, January, 1922.

by the Rev. W. P. Ashe in the Jazaretto where, with his own family and his

“The magnitude of the disaster and the horror of the tragedy which 
centres round Smyrna is inexpressible. The short anguish of women and 
girls who first suffered_dreadful dishonour, followed almost invariably by 
death, and of those thousands and tens of thousands of helpless people who 
were Slaughtered in that vast shambles into which Smyrna was turned, 
before being burnt by the Turks, is hardly comparable in horror to the fate 
of the vast multitude who survive around Smyrna, Constantinople, and 
Chanak. Helpless, hapless, hopeless, a mass of human misery unimaginable! 
A multitude of starving, sick, and homeless people. One of my friends here 
spent an hour and a half searching among some three hundred corpses for 
the bodies of his wife and three children ; happily he did not find them, for, 
after terrible adventures, they had succeeded in reaching the harbour and 
getting on board a British ship, where he found them. Another friend, a 
gentleman who was in charge of the British and Foreign Bible Society’s 
Depot in Smyrna, was last seen standing on the quay distraught with an 
agony of apprehension, having become separated from and having lest his 
wife and children, and not knowing but that they had fallen into the hands 
of the ruthless murderers. Such details are those of which the vast horror 
consists. We Smyrna refugees have been snatched out of a veritable hell.” 


As Lord Hugh Cecil remarks, with a few splendid exceptions such as the 


Church Times and the Daily Telegraph, the London Press has been too busy 


with domestic politics, sport, and Old Bailey cases, to give much attention 
to the Smyrna holocaust. Indeed, our Turkophil and ultra-Francophil 
journals, which, in October, declared roundly that obviously no massacre 
had taken place at Smyrna, and that if the Armenians did not fire their own 
dwellings the Greeks fired them, have studiously avoided the subject. All 
the same, the British public, though less well informed in the matter, has 
come to realize that a stupendous crime has been perpetrated in the sight of 
European warships. | 


The Editorial in the October Curistian East gave a summary of what 
took place during the sack and burning of the great Christian City, and 
among other details gave the account of the martyrdom of the Metropolitan 
Chrysostom to which Lord Hugh Cecil refers. That account, which reached

OPEN LETTER OF THE PATRIARCH OF 
JUGO SLAVIA. 


E print this translation, made from Glazmk by the Rev. R. M. 
French, of a letter issued by the Patriarch of Jugo Slavia: 


Appeal of His Holiness, the Patriarch Dimitri, to the Presiding Bishops cf 
the Orthodox Churches, and also of the Anglican, Old Catholic, and 
Episcopalian Churches in Great Britain and America, in reference to 
the terrible persecution and annthilation of the Christians in Asia Minor, 
and for their protection. 


YOUR GRACE,—Gladly would we communicate to Your Grace some 
joyful news when one Church corresponds so rarely with another, but 
our heart is filled with agony and sorrow on account of the hitherto 
unparalleled crimes of the Moslems against the Christians in Asia Minor 
and Anatolia. : 

_We take for granted that Your Grace has already received informa- 
tion concerning the bloody massacre of Greeks and Armenians, a 
massacre without pity and to extermination; conférning the burning 
and destruction of Christian towns and villages; concerning the hun- 
dreds of thousands of men, women, and children who have been 
murdered, and the many thousands of terrified fugitives, starving and 
homeless creatures, both laity and clergy, bishops, priests, and deacons ; 
concerning the desecration of ancient holy things venerated by all of us 
who have been baptized into the name of the Lord Jesus Christ. 

But as we hold that you are already acquainted with all these things, 
we do not think of explaining to Your Grace all the details of this new 
tragedy of the Orthodox Church—details which have recently come to 
our knowledge. 

We only hasten to express our profound sorrow in regard to our 


tortured Christian brethren, both Greeks and Armenians, and our 


compassion for those who are now far from homes that have been 
burned and are in destitution and misery, feeding themselves with the 
bread of fear and tears. 

The Serbian Church, which lived for 500 years under the Turkish 
yoke, understands right well the nature of the fanatical violence directed 
against Christians, and also the affliction and despair of those who live 
and die under that violence. But the Serbian Church is not in a 
position to avert the crimes of the enemies of Christianity. All that we 
can we do. We pray our Most High Lord and Saviour Jesus Christ 
that of His mercy He will helpand save. We pray both for the martyrs 
of our holy faith and for their persecutors ; we pray that the former may 
be saved in the Kingdom of Heaven, and that the latter may not utterly 
lose their souls and perish for ever on account of the extremity of their 
iniquities.

HIS, perhaps the most notable of all the Anniversaries of the 

Association, was held on Wednesday, October 25. The Holy

tinguished platform, including the Bishops of Bangor and Southwell, 
and Bishop King. The Bishop of London, in welcoming the Eastern 
Prelates, recalled the pleasure of his visits to Greece and Russia, during 
the latter of which he was presented with an eikon of our Lord, in 
front of which he celebrated every day.

sun. The village has its own well, its own school-house—even its own 
mayor. His worship, who was elected by his fellows, is understood to 
rule them, for their good, with a rod of iron. The picturesque truth 
that he was a bandit by profession when at home is, after all, only 
evidence of initiative and of experience in the management of men ! 

One other grim fact we must mention as evidence of what went on 
when Kemal’s men had their will of the inhabitants of Smyrna in the 
terrible days of ravage. There is hardly a young woman in the village 
who is not now expecting to be a mother, as a result of personal mal- 
treatment at the hands of the Turkish soldiers. 

We have tried to put on paper what is now passing before our eyes 
in Athens, and our story has no doubt the defects, and we hope the 
advantages also, that come of special nearness to the facts. Now for

the question of the future: What can be done with the great mass of 
refugees ? Absorption in the population of the land may do much, but 
with the quantity we have to deal with that will not solve the whole 
question. To continue the metaphor, you speedily reach the saturation 
point. Hence the Government order, that every village in the land 
must receive a certain proportion of new inhabitants, is no more than a 
palliative, though it has, we believe, béen loyally accepted and acted on 
by a very hospitable peasantry. Some sort of general scheme of coloni- 
zation and settlement on the land seems inevitable, and the question is 
how this can best be done. The great scheme now ready for execution 
—viz., the draining and cultivation of the Struma Valley, as designed 
by Sir John Jackson and Co.—would certainly offer a means of disposal 
of an even greater number of workers on the land, supposing funds for 
its execution available, and such complications as “ malignant malaria” 
duly dealt with. A terrible difficulty in this scheme lies in the fact that 
so great a proportion of the refugees are not only townsfolk, but women 
and children. All men of military age were detained by Kemal as 
prisoners, to labour as prisoners-of-war do in Turkey. 

When one remembers the fate of the men of Kut, of whom 70 per 
cent. perished in Turkish hands, and one recails*the boast made bya 
Turkish officer to the writer, that in making a new railway “he had 
buried an Armenian under every sleeper of the line for a hundred 
miles,’ one wonders what proportion of those whom Kemal has detained 
will ever see their families again. 

Meantime, something must be undertaken soon if it is to be effective. 
The season of the year is a factor in the problem of settling folk on the 
land, and the writer has some,gx perience of how very long it takes, even 
under a British military administration, to move far smaller numbers 
across a roadless land to the homes marked out for them. Those 
responsible for the solution of the pressing problem might do well to 
remember the word of a British administrator in Mesopotamia, when 
dealing with a like problem: “I will try to manage Joshua’s job, and 
put these people in a promised land; remember, though, I have not got 


Joshua’s powers. I cannot stop the sun.”

HE resignation of the See of Gloucester after its tenure for seventeen 

years by the Right Kev. Edgar Charles Sumner Gibson is necessarily 
a noteworthy event for our movement. A fine scholar, a wise and withal 
conservative theologian of the first rank, and a prudent statesman, Dr. 
Gibson was profoundly interested in Eastern Christianity before his 
accession to the episcopate, and, so far as his diocesan office has allowed, has 
taken an active part in the development of relations between the Anglican and 


_ Orthodox Churches. Thus, during the 1920 Lambeth Conference he pre- 
sided over the Committee of Bishops which received and conferred with the 


Constantinople Delegation; while last year he became a patron of the 
Association. The increased leisure which he will now have will, we may 
be sure, be used by him to the benefit of the work of the Archbishop’s 
Eastern Churches Committee, so that the loss of the Gloucester diocese 
will be the gain of our movement.

The above had hardly been written when we read the announcement of 


Dr. Arthur Cayley Headlam’s nomination by the Crown as Bishop Gibson’s | 


successor at Gloucester. That is a nomination which will create profound 
satisfaction throughout every section of the Church of England. If it were 
only as an academic of the highest rank and a theologian of the greatest 
authority, Dr. Headlam would possess those qualifications for which Church- 
men look instinctively, and which they prize especially in their Bishops. 
But great though is his reputation as teacher and author, Dr. Headlam'’s is 
not an academic mind pur et simple. Few men have shown finer con- 
structive and administrative ability than he showed in those fourteen years 
when, as Principal of King’s College and member of the Senate, he exercised 
a shaping influence on the University of London, or in those later four years 
during which he has remodelled the Divinity Faculty at Oxford. Moreover, 
with his singular power of concentration, he has thought deeply on such 
varied problems as Church finance and reunion. To us, however, his 
nomination is a matter of peculiarly happy augury, because, since some 
thirty years ago he acted as the vigorous secretary of the Eastern Church 
Association, he has never ceased to take an active interest in and to keep in 
close touch with the Eastern Churches. Apart from his writings on the 
subject and his frequent visits to the Near Kast, our movement is greatly 
indebted to him, not only for what he has done for the E.C.A., the 
A. and E.A., and the Archbishop’s Eastern Churches Committee, but also 
for his unceasing interest and support of everything which could further 
Anglican and Eastern-Orthodox friendship. Thus during the war he 
extended his help very effectively to the Serbian Students Aid Committee ; 
he has taken a prominent part in the various conferences which have been 
held with Orthodox hierarchs and theologians; has entertained Orthodox 
visitors to Oxford; and only last year drafted with Canon Brightman those 
“Suggested Terms of Intercommunion” for the E.C.C., which most of us 
are agreed will serve admirably as the basis of negotiation on the Anglican 
side in the mear future. That all that he has written of a constructive 
character—e.g., in his remarkable and inspiring Bampton Lectures on 
Reunion—does not meet with universal agreement is inevitable in a Church 
such as the Anglican. But there will be no dissentient to the proposition 
that no choice of an English Bishop has ever seemed wiser, or to the 
anticipation that his episcopate will be rich and fruitful in service to the 
English Church and the whole of Christendom. 


We congratulate our movement upon the appointment of Sir Samuel 
Hoare, M.P., to be Minister for Air in the Bonar Law Government. Sir 
Samuel is, of course, a well-known Eastern expert and a member of the 
Archbishop’s Committee. That the new House of Commons is rich in 
sympathizers with Eastern Christianity is a thing for which to be thankful 
in these times.
