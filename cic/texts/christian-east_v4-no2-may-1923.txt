SMITING THE SHEPHERDS. 


O scatter the sheep, smite the shepherd. That is a common-sense 
maxim which has always appealed, though most often without 
success, to the persecutors of the Church. Its validity is now 

being put to a drastic and bitter test by the enemies of Eastern 
Christianity. | 
THE SOVIET. WAR ON RELIGION. 
There has been no doubt these four years past about the Bolshe- 
viks’ prime objective,-no doubt these twelve months past about their 


method of reaching it. As to neither, is it possible to-say that any of - 


the famous Seven Persecutions undergone by the Church under Heathen- 
dom was as direct or as thorough. From the day that Soviet Rule 
established itself at Moscow, its mind has been plain. There is a 
Communism which at least believes itself to be the embodiment of 
Christianity. Call it as wrong-headed as you please. You are able to 


respect its professors. There is also a Communism which, though 


impatient of all forms of organised religion, is prepared and is sometimes 
eager. to be tolerant with the toleration of indifference—towards 
religion itself. But the thing which calls itself Communism, and which, 
with a sudden spring, seized Russia by the throat in the hour of her 
exhaustion, and has since kept its stranglehold by means of Tartar and 


Chinese mercenaries, hates Religion with hatred inexpressible. Those 


who, in the old pre-war days, knew the Marble Arch and the strange 
beings which congregated at its platforms, will. remember a Russian 
Communist who used to speak there at times, his mouth literally frothing 
as he denounced Religion of every kind, and declared that no death 
could be too cruel for its teachers. The listeners always laughed 
amusedly. The man was taken asa freak. In fact, he was a represen 
tative of that type of revolutionary which we now know as a Bolshevik 


_ —of the utterly disgruntled, of the renegade Jew eager for his revenge, 


of the perverted idealist, of the man with a taste for blood and looking 
for a revolution in which to gratify it. Once the Bolshevik made himself 
at home in Moscow, he was open enough in speech as to what he meant 
to do. His policy, as his principles, were those of our friend of Hyde 
Park. To smash the existing social and economic fabric of sqciety

THE ORTHODOX CHURCH. 


It is true that, as witness the recent heaviness of its hand upon the Jews 
the Latins, the Baptists, etc.,! Bolshevism is frenzied with indiscriminate 
hatred of every form of Religion. Buddha and Mahommed have been the 
objects of its blasphemy only less than Christ. But, from the first, it 
has always envisaged the Orthodox Church as the one serious Russian 
force with which it had to reckon. Except for those of the Latin rite, 
who are Poles or Ruthenians and not Russians, the Pope’s subjects in 
Russia are a negligible quantity. Of the so-called -Raskolniks or 
Schismatics, a large section is in no sense Protestant, but holds itself 
and desires to be truly Orthodox with the greatest intensity. To the 
vast preponderance of the Russian nation the Orthodox Church 1s not 
an institution. It is simply a characteristic of the Divine Order, a 
necessary condition of human life as the air is a necessary condition of 
animal life, so obvious as to be accepted axiomatically. - Essential to 
the success of Bolshevism though the destruction of Christianity is, a 
frontal attack on the Orthodox Church was too perilous an adventure 
for the Soviet in its early days. That is the explanation of paradoxical 
scenes such as that in which, at Easter, 1919, the Red Guards, 
blaspheming one minute, fell the next on their knees outside the 
Yspensky Sobor to ask the Patriarch Tikhon’s blessing. Some Russians, 
in contrast with Jews and nondescripts, may have donned the Soviet’s 
badge, may even shout its catchwords and imagine themselves to mean 
them. But their apostasy is on the surface; in a crisis they are always 
liable to come to their senses, to reassert their old selves and to turn 


against their employers. It would be dangerous to use them as weapons — 


of offence against the Orthodox Church. The only thing to do was to 
try the subtler method of disintegrating it. Now, whatever Absolutism 
it may tolerate in the political sphere, the Orthodox world is essentially 
democratic in the ecclesiastical, and, in 1917, the Russian Church had 
reasserted the democratic principle in her constitution, so far as it had 
been obscured under the Tsars. From the Parish Council and the 
Diocesan Synod, up to the National Synod, she is equipped with a 
perfect systemr of assemblies, elected vn a democratic basis. 

It seemed easy for the strong, astute and a at Central Soviet 
to throw that machinery out of gear without showing its hand. All 
that had to be done was to make clever demands upon the rulers of the 
Church which, if refused, would lay them open to the charge of counter- 
revolutionary activity, and which, whether accepted or rejected, might 
split the clergy and the laity into warring factions. Well-timed material 
support of a complaisant bishop, or of an eccentric minority, would 
place either in control of the machine and would produce strife and 
confusion. Divide e concide. Once its solidarity was broken, the 
Russian Church would be at the Saori of its eneiny.. The artichoke

‘THE PATRIARCH TIKHON.?! 


That proposition, which seemed to present no difficulty to the 
malevolence which devised it, has been foiled hitherto by the magnificent 
courage and simple straightforwardness of a single man. Elsewhere in 
this number we give an appreciation of the career and character of the 
Patriarch Tikhon. It will be enough to say here that, while he abstained 
from political action, he refused altogether to become the Soviet’s tool. 
Whenever he could comply with their demands, he did so. When he 
could not, he rejected them with firm finality. An Orthodox Patriarch 
is not a Pope, but nothing can be done canonically, t.e., constitutionally, 
in his Church without him, and the one thing which the Soviet did not 
dare to risk was the open violation of the constitution of the Russian 
-Church. There are few more dramatic episodes in history than the 
long struggle in which, since 1917, by moral dignity and the sanctity of 
his office, a single old man has proved a match for the whole furious 
force of triumphant Bolshevism. If he had wavered for a moment, 
the Russian Church, and with it Religion in Russia, humanly speaking, 
would have been lost. What to de with the Patriarch Tikhon became 
the pressing problem of the Soviet. 


THE PATRIARCH’S ARREST. 


The famine in 1921-22 seemed to give promise of its solution. The 
Soviet promulgated a law confiscating the “‘ treasures’ of the Church 
for relief purposes and demanded that the Patriarch should order their 
handing over to it. If he bade_his people strip the shrines of the dead 
and hand over the Communion Vessels at the bidding of the atheist 
Bolshevist, he would bitterly wound the piety of his people. If he 
refused, there is a crude tradition everywhere which is prone to cry out 
upon sacerdotal avarice. The ground was well chosen. Tikhon 
answered by offering to sanction the disposal of everything of real 
intrinsic value provided that there should be security that the proceeds 
were spent on relief and not embezzled or squandered on armaments 
and the like. For propaganda, however, the issue was sufficiently 
confused. The Soviet Press, which is, of course, the only press allowed 
in Russia, shrieked that the Patriarch preferred to see the nation starve 
than part with his wealth. No one really believed the lie; but in Western 
Europe and in Russia itself the end was served. When, in May, 1922, 
the Soviet risked arresting the Patriarch, there was indignation and 
alarm, but no outbreak of vocal protest or resistance. Indeed, what 
had happened was hardly understood, the arrest being explained as no 
more than a “placing under restraint’ and only by degrees pene 
developed into isolated and rigorous incarceration.

JUDICIAL MURDER. 


The proposed State trial of the Patriarch is the darling project of the 
‘extreme’ men who are eager to break with the Western Proletariat, 
to provoke risings in Russia and to plunge into stark unbridled Terrorism. 
Whatever stomach the more moderate men, such as Troitsky, ever had 
for it, is gone. Lenin—Ilyitch—that strange mixture of fanaticism and. 
worldly wisdom, the dictator who held them altogether, the one Soviet 
leader whose name—for he, at least, is bone ot the people’s bone—has 
magic in Russia, is dying. When the house is insecure from within and 
may break at any moment with a crash, why set the ground trembling ? 
Moreover, they hope to transmute their rule into a bourgeois-communist 
republic. Renegade Jews that they are for the most part, their 
persistent bitter bloody harrying of their own kith and kin is a sop to 
Russian anti-Semitism and an indulgence of the unrestrainable rancour 
proper to apostates. When they condemned Archbishop Ciepliak and 
shot his coadjutor Budkiewicz, they had nothing to fear in Russia 
where, as a Pole, the Latin is a foreigner. In that atrocity they were 
feeling the pulse of Western Europe. If, in the House of Commons, the 
indignation with which British Labour received its news left the one 
communist of their brand pale and shaking, would the Patriarch Tikhon’s 
murder leave them the shadow of a chance of obtaining that British 
recognition which is vital to them ? 


AN ECCLESIASTICAL TRIAL. 


If, one day, the Soviet fixes a date for a State trial and the next 
postpones it, the plan of an Ecclesiastical trial, though easy of execution, 
does not command greater confidence for its result. A national Synod 
has, indeed, been convened and by the simple method of summoning 
only those who. are pledged in. writing to the Living Church, its 
unanimity appears secured. But even so a paper majority is not 
always reliable. Conscience ts a force. No Synod can try its Patriarch 
in enforced absence. When Tikhon stands before ‘‘ the Living Church ” 
who can guarantee its action? Moreover, Orthodox Canon Law 
demands that a Patriarch must be tried by thirty of his fellow-bishops — 
and that the indictment be clear. When can the former be found ?. 
and how can the latter be framed? Also, there are other Orthodox 


Churches. That brave man, Meletios of Constantinople, may take the .

world is searching for a general appeasement, but has found 
none attainable through the sphe re of politics and succeeds in 
maintaining a kind of unreal equipoise only by force or threats.

of the kind. A genuine movement towards religious union requires 
those real mutual attractions, on which it is necessary to base the

the Orthodox find increased immeasurably so as to have become well-

ULTITUDES of Russians, Serbians and Czecho-Slavs have settled both in 
the Prairie Provinces, and across the Rockies, in British Columbia. Toa 
very great extent they are without the ministrations of their own Orthodox Churches, 
and, to quote the words of a Russian churchman, heard by your correspondent in

munists have got into our “‘ skupshtina,’’ but we have also a splendid party of 
Bogomolytzi (God's Partisans). These are chiefly from the peasants. The move- 
ment is spreading widely over all Serbian terntory. They would remind you of the 
English Puritans in their great love for the word of Scnpture, but they are passionately 
devoted to the Orthodox Church, and will hear nothing of separation from her. 
They chose Bishop Nicolai of Ochnda as their Vodya (guide) in 1921, but he declined 
tu act. Thev have over fifty thousand registered members. _ 

Besides these there are the Adventists, who are schismatic, and are very active. 
Thev are the Seventh-Day Adventist type, small in number, and complain bitterly 
that they are not persecuted, The State has officially recognised them as a religious 
body, and they do not like it. 

In regard to the relation of the Serbian Church and State, we are still engaged 
in difficulties. For example, some of the five united Churches wish to preserve 
a measure of autonomy and differencetfrom the rest. Again, Our priests are very 
disappointed that, in the new relations between Church and State, the State is not 
to pay their salaries. But the best of them see that it would be better that it should 
not do so. 

The Encyclical of the Patnarch Meletios upon Anglican Orders has given many 
of us profound satisfaction. The question has already been discussed at the plenum 
of our Bishops’ Council. The decision is likely to be made this summer. . . . 

Finally, it may be noted that there is a great activity among the Serbian ‘‘ younger 
generation ’’ of theological students and teachers, who were almost all éducated at 
Oxford. They are stimulating the religion of the country and the result is most 
marked. They edit the best Church monthly magazine, Christyanskt Zhivot 
(The Chnstian Life), and have printed more books in one year than al! the rest 
together. For this we thank our Anglican teachers.
