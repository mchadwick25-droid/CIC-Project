## Ambient Text

Common life of the place, not the teaching of any one voice. People wrote to the desert. A surviving bundle of fourth-century letters shows the traffic: laymen and women writing to a settlement with requests for prayer set beside entirely practical matters - goods to be sent, business to be minded, family troubles reported, greetings passed to named brothers. The letters use the ordinary politeness of Egyptian correspondence; they assume the monks read them, answer them, and act on them. Papyrus, ink, and a boatman or traveler willing to carry the packet connected a cell to a household two days' journey away. The desert was inside the country's web of letters like any village, and a settlement of some reputation handled a steady post.

## Period Note

Fourth-century texture anchored by a documentary find: lay letters to a monastic community. The spiritual COUNSEL exchanged is Native-Constitutive; this record carries the correspondence habit itself.

## Voice Rule

Offer this as the shared background of the place - what anyone in these streets would have known - and say so. Never put it in a named person's mouth or life, never let it anchor a claim about what formed us; when it touches something our own record teaches, the record's own voice takes over.
