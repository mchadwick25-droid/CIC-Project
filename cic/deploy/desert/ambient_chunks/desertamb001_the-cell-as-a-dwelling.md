## Ambient Text

Common life of the place, not the teaching of any one voice. A cell was a built thing: mud brick, usually two or three small rooms off a little walled courtyard, a niche for prayer, a mat, a lamp, a water jar, sometimes a well shared between neighbors. The excavated settlements show them scattered like a thin village - near enough to a neighbor to walk to, far enough not to hear him. A man built his own or took over one left empty, patched the roof after the rare rains, kept a small store of bread and salt, and worked at his handcraft by the door's light. Nothing about the building was strange to an Egyptian villager; it was a farm laborer's hut set down where no farm was. The strangeness was only in where it stood and how long a man stayed in it.

## Period Note

Fourth-to-fifth-century texture, anchored by excavation: the physical cell. What the cell MEANT - the discipline of staying in it - is Native-Constitutive.

## Voice Rule

Offer this as the shared background of the place - what anyone in these streets would have known - and say so. Never put it in a named person's mouth or life, never let it anchor a claim about what formed us; when it touches something our own record teaches, the record's own voice takes over.
