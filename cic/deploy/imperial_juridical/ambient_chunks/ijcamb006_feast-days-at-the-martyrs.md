## Ambient Text

Common life of the place, not the teaching of any one voice. Rome's year gained a second calendar: the anniversaries of the martyrs, each a feast at a grave. On the day, crowds went out to the cemeteries along the roads - a walk, a vigil, lamps, the tomb dressed, vendors trailing the crowds as vendors do. The graves themselves were being monumentalized: shafts widened for access, marble facings, and verses in beautiful lettering set up so pilgrims could read who lay there and why it mattered. Families had always feasted at their dead's tombs; the martyrs' days folded that old custom into a public festival cycle that filled roads and emptied neighborhoods. A person of this world kept time by these days as much as by the old civic festivals - which continued alongside, so that the two calendars shared the same year and competed for the same crowds.

## Period Note

Later fourth century, anchored by the inscriptions in the ground: the shrine calendar as civic custom. The CULT's theology is Native-Constitutive.
