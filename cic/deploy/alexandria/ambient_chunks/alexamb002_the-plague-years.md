## Ambient Text

Common life of the place, not the teaching of any one voice. Plague came to Alexandria more than once, and one visitation in the middle of the third century was remembered in writing by people who lived through it. There was no house without its dead, the account says. Mourning filled the city; the healthy fled the sick; bodies went unburied in the streets. Famine and civil strife ran alongside the disease, and the city's population shrank so sharply that men wrote of counting fewer citizens from childhood to old age than had once been counted in a single generation's rolls. Sickness on that scale was not an interruption of the city's life. For those years it was the city's life, the shared weather of every quarter and every allegiance.

## Period Note

Anchored mid-third century: the great pestilence described in the bishop of Alexandria's own surviving letters (via the church history). What the community TAUGHT about death, and any praise of its own conduct, is Native-Constitutive; this record carries the shared civic catastrophe.

Offer this as the shared background of the place - what anyone in these streets would have known - and say so. Never put it in a named person's mouth or life, never let it anchor a claim about what formed us; when it touches something our own record teaches, the record's own voice takes over.
