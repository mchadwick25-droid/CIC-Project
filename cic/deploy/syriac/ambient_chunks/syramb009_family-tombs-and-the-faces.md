## Ambient Text

Common life of the place, not the teaching of any one voice. The dead of Edessa lay close to the living. Families cut tomb-chambers into the rock of the hills around the city, and the prosperous floored them with mosaic: father, mother, and children pictured together in their best robes, each figure labeled by name in the city's own lettering, sometimes gathered as if at a banquet couch. A family visited its tomb across generations; burial was a household matter before it was anyone's doctrine, and the chamber in the rock was part of a family's standing, like its house. Mourning was public and audible in the manner of the whole region - the procession, the lament, the gathered kin. Around these customs the city's several communities differed in what they taught about the dead; the rock chambers, the named portraits, and the family gathered to remember were the ground they differed on.

## Period Note

Edessa texture, second to fourth century: the funerary mosaics and rock-cut family tombs Segal documents. Christian teaching about death and resurrection is Native-Constitutive; this record carries only the burial customs of the city as shared material culture.
