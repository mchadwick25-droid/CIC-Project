## Story Text

The Chronicle of Edessa reports that in the month of Tishrin (November) of the year 513 by the reckoning of the Greeks (201 CE), under Abgar VIII "the Great," the Daysan river rose in a great flood. King Abgar watched from a tower as the water broke against the western wall of the city. The wall gave way, the flood swept through, and the royal palace was destroyed. More than two thousand people are said to have died. Amid the devastation, the chronicle also records one more detail, set down in the same breath as the destruction of the palace and the deaths. It says that the flood "destroyed the temple of the church of the Christians." Afterward, Abgar issued decrees for rebuilding and flood mitigation. The account names no bishop, no Christian by name, and no aftermath specific to the Christian community beyond this single line. The report is filed under four named non-Christian scribes and archivists of the royal court.

## Tier Justification

The Chronicle itself was compiled c. 540s CE, roughly 340 years after this event, from a mix of civic and (per J.B. Segal) plausibly genuine earlier archival material — collected tradition with an identifiable, if anonymous, compilation history (a single surviving manuscript, Vatican Syriac 163), placing it squarely at Tier 2 rather than Tier 1: it is not a named eyewitness account, but a later collection drawing, by the comparative sobriety Segal notes in its style, on real archival material rather than invented legend. The single specific detail relevant to this world — the destroyed church — carries a genuine, named scholarly dispute worth carrying at full strength rather than resolving: Walter Bauer (1934/1971) argued this detail is a likely later interpolation, noting that the parallel account in Dionysius of Tell Mahre's chronicle of the same flood omits any mention of a church, and that the Chronicle of Edessa elsewhere dates the church's actual founding to 313 CE — an apparent internal inconsistency. L.W. Barnard (1968) explicitly rebuts Bauer, citing Paul Kahle's judgment that the detail is "a certain fact." Neither position is treated here as settled.
