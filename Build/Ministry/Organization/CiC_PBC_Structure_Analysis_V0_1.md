# Church in Conversation — Public Benefit Corporation Structure Analysis

**V0.1 · 2026-07-19 · Funding Strategy & Execution thread**

**What this is:** a real viability check on converting from the nonprofit path (Articles drafted
2026-07-18) to a Colorado Public Benefit Corporation (PBC), triggered by Mark's own reasoning:
institutional financial relationships will be the primary funding engine either way (grants in
this space are thin), and he'd rather build toward a real, ownable asset — one he named
concretely: **$2–4M realized in a sale within 3–5 years, if it's also doing real good.** Two
independent research passes verified the legal mechanics and the capital landscape; this memo
puts real numbers behind the decision the way yesterday's funding memo did.

**Read this first, in one paragraph:** a Colorado PBC is legally straightforward, costs the same
$50 to file as the nonprofit path, and — genuinely reassuring — Colorado's statute explicitly
lists "religious" purpose as a qualifying public benefit, so your mission fits the form cleanly,
not as a stretch. **The honest surprise from this research: switching to PBC does NOT make
outside capital more available to you right now.** Every patient/mission-aligned capital source
researched — PRIs, faith-based impact funds, revenue-based financing, even Praxis's own
business track — is gated behind real revenue traction you don't have yet, exactly the same way
grants are gated behind a track record you don't have yet as a nonprofit. **The gate is your
stage, not your entity type.** What a PBC genuinely gives you — and a nonprofit structurally
cannot — is the real, legal option to realize $2–4M for yourself in a future sale. That's the
actual trade, named plainly: **give up grant eligibility and donor deductibility (both thin
right now anyway) in exchange for keeping open the one door you've now told me you actually
want.** Given that, and given the numbers below, **this is viable — not a naive plan.**

## Part A — What a Colorado PBC actually is (verified)

*(Full sourcing in the companion research artifact, "Colorado Public Benefit Corporation:
Verified Legal & Tax Research" — cited in full here.)*

- **The statute:** C.R.S. Title 7, Article 101, Part 5 (§§7-101-501–509), enacted 2013/effective
  2014, amended 2017 (naming "PBC" made optional) and 2022 (lowered conversion vote threshold to
  a simple majority). Colorado is a **light-touch, Delaware-style** jurisdiction — no mandated
  "benefit director" role, no required third-party audit.
- **Formation:** the standard Colorado "Articles of Incorporation for a Profit Corporation" form,
  with a PBC checkbox/supplement. **Filing fee: $50** — identical to the nonprofit path. The
  Articles must state the entity is a PBC and name one or more specific public benefits within
  the business-purpose statement.
- **The purpose statement is custom, within a broad statutory frame — and "religious" is
  explicitly one of the enumerated qualifying categories** (alongside artistic, charitable,
  cultural, economic, educational, environmental, literary, medical, scientific, technological).
  Your actual mission language (Article III/IV of the nonprofit draft) translates directly into
  this form — it is not a stretch to fit a faith mission into a PBC's public-benefit purpose.
- **Governance:** the board must "balance the pecuniary interests of the shareholders, the best
  interests of those materially affected by the corporation's conduct, and the specific public
  benefit identified" (§7-101-506). No special director/officer role required — this duty sits on
  the whole board (which, for now, is just you).
- **Annual benefit report required** (confirmed against the statute directly — one secondary
  source incorrectly said "biennial"): a narrative of how you promoted the stated benefit, plus
  an assessment against a **self-selected third-party standard** (you pick it; no audit or
  certification required). Goes to shareholders and your public website.
- **Benefit enforcement — the honest limit on accountability.** Standing to sue over the public
  benefit is legally restricted to shareholders holding **≥2% of outstanding shares**. **As sole
  100%-owner, there is, as a structural matter, no one who can bring this kind of action against
  you.** This is worth sitting with plainly: your nonprofit bylaws draft built in real outside
  accountability (5 seats, 3 clean independents) specifically because you named a board as an
  asset, not a threat. A PBC does not give you that by default — you would have to choose to
  build a real advisory board or governance structure voluntarily. Nothing forces it, which
  means it's easy to skip.
- **Tax:** a PBC is a normal C-corporation for tax purposes — **not exempt.** Combined effective
  corporate rate on net profit is roughly **24.5%** (21% federal + Colorado's 4.4%, with the
  state tax deductible against federal). **As a solo owner with no outside institutional
  investors, you can likely elect S-corp status**, which avoids the entity-level tax entirely
  (profit passes through and is taxed once, at your personal rate) — available only while
  ownership stays simple (≤100 shareholders, individuals only, one stock class); this option
  disappears the moment you bring in a VC fund or non-individual investor.
- **One real, valuable wrinkle worth a CPA's attention — Qualified Small Business Stock (QSBS,
  IRC §1202):** if your PBC stays a **C-corp** (not S-corp-elected) with gross assets under $50M
  when stock is issued, and you **hold the stock 5+ years**, gain on an eventual sale can be
  **excluded from federal tax entirely**, up to the greater of $10M or 10× your basis. At your
  stated $2–4M target, this could mean **the entire sale is federally tax-free** — but only if you
  hold 5 years, and only if you don't elect S-corp status. **This creates a real, concrete tension
  with your stated 3–5 year timeline:** sell at year 3 and QSBS doesn't apply (normal capital
  gains tax); stretch to year 5 and it likely does. This is genuinely worth a real conversation
  with a CPA before deciding on S-corp election or exact exit timing — not something to decide
  from this memo alone, but too significant not to flag now, while the timeline is still yours to
  shape.
- **PBC (legal status) vs. "B Corp" (private certification) — not the same thing.** B Corp
  certification (B Lab, ~$500/yr under $2M revenue) is a market-credibility layer you could add
  *later*, once you have customers/investors who'd notice the badge. At your stage, the PBC legal
  form alone likely delivers the mission-lock value you actually want.
- **Real precedent, directly on point:** **Hallow** — the closest comparable in this exact space —
  explicitly rejected nonprofit status (fundraising drag, a limited donor pool, couldn't compete
  on compensation) and rejected a plain C-corp (feared profit pressure eroding the mission),
  landing on PBC specifically to combine real fundraising capacity with a legally binding mission.
  They've since raised a $50M Series C under this exact structure. **Allbirds** adopted PBC status
  at the very earliest, pre-scale stage — proof this isn't something you wait to "earn." The
  transferable lesson from **Kickstarter's** conversion: the charter's real value is as a
  **self-binding commitment device that constrains future leadership** (successors, a future
  acquirer) — not primarily an external enforcement mechanism today, since (per the point above)
  a small company has essentially no outside enforcement anyway.

## Part B — Does a PBC actually get you access to capital a nonprofit can't? (The honest, humbling answer: not yet)

*(Full sourcing in the companion capital-research artifact.)*

This is the finding that most changes the shape of this decision, and it cuts against the
intuitive case for switching:

- **Program-Related Investments (PRIs):** closed to you right now, in either structure. Fewer
  than 2% of foundations even make PRIs; for-profits receive only 9% of PRI dollars; the closest
  real for-profit precedent found (Knight Foundation → Indiegraf, $5M) only happened after years
  of prior relationship and demonstrated traction. No solo-founder pre-seed PRI precedent exists.
- **Faith-based impact funds and mission-aligned angel networks:** nearly every one researched
  (Ambassadors Impact Network, Beyond Angels, Sovereign's Capital) explicitly requires
  **post-revenue traction** — not available at your current stage regardless of entity type.
- **Traditional VC math confirms what I told you last turn, now with real numbers:** ~65% of VC
  deals return less than 1×; a fund needs a portfolio company to exit in the *hundreds of
  millions* to matter to its returns. A $2–4M exit isn't below that bar — it's **two orders of
  magnitude below it**, an internal write-off to a VC, not a good outcome. Traditional VC is
  confirmed to be the wrong tool for your actual target.
- **A genuinely exit-agnostic, patient-capital tier does exist** (Calm Company Fund, TinySeed) —
  but it wants **~$50K MRR (~$600K ARR) first.** This is a tool for *later*, once you're already
  near your target scale, not a bootstrapping tool.
- **Praxis's business track** (previously flagged as a possible for-profit path): real, but not a
  grant-equivalent — it's an uncapped SAFE up to $50K bundled with a **non-refundable $8–10K
  program fee** (you pay to get in), and PBC eligibility isn't even confirmed on Praxis's own site
  (only "C corporation" stated — worth a direct inquiry). Next window: 2028 cohort, opening
  April 2027 — same timing as the nonprofit track.
- **Revenue-based financing** (Founderpath, Lighter Capital): real and active, but gated behind
  ~$120–200K ARR at the cheapest providers, $350K–1M+ for competitive terms — not a year-one or
  year-two tool. Several major RBF providers (Clearco, Pipe, Corl) have had shutdowns or layoffs
  since 2021, a caution flag on the sector's own stability.

**The honest synthesis: the gate blocking outside capital is your stage — no revenue, no track
record, solo founder — not your entity type.** A nonprofit at this same stage would be shut out of
grants for the same underlying reason. **Switching to PBC does not make year-one or year-two
fundraising meaningfully easier.** The realistic plan under either structure, for now, is founder
self-funding (Equivice, already decided) plus institutional/customer revenue — which is exactly
where the value of the PBC choice actually lives: not in unlocking capital sooner, but in what it
lets you keep for yourself once real revenue and an eventual sale materialize.

## Part C — The financial model: does the PBC's core engine actually work?

**Short answer: identically to the nonprofit hybrid, because earned revenue doesn't care about
tax status.** Yesterday's break-even memo modeled Scenario 3 (capped free tier + Supporter
subscriptions + institutional licenses) and found it clears break-even at Phase-A scale
(~1,500 users) via **4–9 institutional licenses** plus modest (~2%) individual conversion — all
using the same ~$1/user/month converged cost basis. **None of that math changes under a PBC.**
The same churches and seminaries can pay the same license fees to a for-profit as to a nonprofit
— **Logos proves this at real scale, as a straight for-profit, in this exact market.**

**What changes, quantified:**

| | Nonprofit | PBC |
|---|---|---|
| Institutional licenses ($1,200–5,000/yr) | Works | **Works identically** |
| Individual Supporter subscriptions (~$8–12/mo) | Works | **Works identically** |
| Individual gift deductibility | Yes | **Lost** — but per this session's own research, most small donors don't itemize anyway; impact on broad giving is real but smaller than it sounds |
| Grants (FLI, NEH, Lilly, Luce, etc.) | Eligible (though currently thin/closed per this session's own verification) | **Foreclosed entirely, including future reopenings** |
| Corporate income tax on net profit | None (mission-related revenue) | ~24.5% combined (or $0 if S-corp elected while solo) |
| Ability to raise outside capital later, once real traction exists | Structurally closed forever | **Open**, even though not usable *now* per Part B |
| Ability to realize personal sale proceeds, ever | **Structurally impossible** (Article VIII: assets lock to charity) | **Real, and the actual point of this whole analysis** |

**The genuine cost, named plainly:** you lose the deductible-gift and grant channels, both of
which are thin right now but not zero forever (NEH may reopen in 2027; Lilly runs waves).
**The genuine gain:** the one thing you told me actually matters to you — the real chance at
$2–4M for yourself — which no amount of clever nonprofit structuring can ever deliver.

## Part D — Stress-testing the $2–4M / 3–5 year target, now as a PBC

Reusing the value-analysis math (2026-07-18): small recurring-revenue businesses sell at roughly
2–6× ARR (2–4× realistic for a content-subscription business without breakout viral growth).

| Sale target | Needed ARR |
|---|---|
| $2M | ~$500K–$1M |
| $4M | ~$1M–$2M |

That's ~2–3× above the "calling-sized" $150–400K/yr milestone already modeled as realistic at
Stage A→B — the growth case, not the conservative planning case. **Two things sharpen this
specifically under the PBC lens:**

1. **The most realistic buyer at this size is a strategic acquirer** — a company like Logos,
   RightNow Media/Bluefish, or a publisher wanting your methodology, content catalog, and
   institutional relationships — not a financial buyer applying a pure ARR multiple. Strategic
   buyers pay for fit and credibility. **This converges directly with the same institutional-
   partnership-first strategy already underway** (the anchor seminary, Wabash, the review
   pipeline) — the exact relationships that make the business sustainable now are the same
   relationships that make it acquirable later. The two goals aren't in tension.
2. **The QSBS timing tension from Part A is the sharpest lever you actually control.** Selling at
   year 3 forfeits the federal tax exclusion; holding to year 5 likely captures it, potentially
   making the entire $2–4M federally tax-free. If the exit timeline has any flexibility, **leaning
   toward year 5 over year 3 is worth real money** — this is a case where patience has a specific,
   quantifiable payoff, not just a vague virtue.

**Honest verdict on the target itself:** ambitious, not fantastical — genuinely achievable if the
institutional-licensing engine executes well over the period, meaningfully harder if it doesn't.
No different, in difficulty, from what the nonprofit path would have needed to hit the
"calling-sized" income milestone and beyond — you're not choosing an easier number, you're
choosing what happens to the value once you're there.

## Part E — What this changes right now

1. **Pause filing the nonprofit Articles of Incorporation (V0.2, drafted yesterday).** Don't file
   them as-is if this is the real direction.
2. **The reserved name carries over cleanly — nothing lost.** "Church in Conversation" is held by
   the Colorado Secretary of State regardless of which entity type ultimately files against it.
3. **Reframe the pastor meeting before it happens.** A church-designated fund is a nonprofit-only,
   tax-deductibility mechanism — it doesn't fit a PBC and would need to be dropped or replaced.
   The conversation is still worth having, just reframed: ask whether his church would be an
   **early paying institutional partner or pilot customer** (a real ask a PBC can make cleanly),
   or simply keep it relational/prayer-support for now until the structure question is fully
   settled. Worth deciding which before you sit down with him.
4. **The Organizational Covenant and the Constitution's Five Convictions are unaffected and
   should stay exactly as they are.** This is worth being explicit about, because it directly
   answers "still with integrity": **the Constitution that governs Representative behavior — no
   persuasion, no engagement-maximization, Article 31's external scholarly review — is a separate
   governance layer from the corporate entity.** It does not live in the Articles of
   Incorporation and does not need to change if the entity does. The same integrity commitments
   translate cleanly into a PBC's public-benefit purpose statement and can be reinforced further
   with a founder's covenant or future shareholder agreement provisions. **Switching to PBC costs
   you nothing on the integrity front — that was never actually tied to nonprofit status.**
5. **Decide, deliberately, on the accountability question Part A raised.** A PBC gives you zero
   external accountability by default. If your earlier instinct — a board as an asset, not a
   threat — still matters to you, that has to be built in on purpose (a real advisory board with
   people who can genuinely push back), not assumed from the corporate form the way the nonprofit
   bylaws draft already did.
6. **New Articles of Incorporation would need drafting for the PBC** (same mission-lock language,
   translated into the PBC's public-benefit-purpose and governance provisions) — a same-scope
   piece of work as yesterday's nonprofit draft, ready whenever you confirm direction.

## Direct viability verdict

**Yes — this is viable, on the same real numbers as the nonprofit path, not a weaker set of
assumptions.** The institutional-licensing engine doesn't care about your tax status. Colorado's
PBC statute explicitly accommodates a religious mission. The one place a PBC is genuinely
different — and the reason to actually choose it — is that it opens a door nonprofit status
permanently closes: **a real, legal path to $2–4M for yourself in 3–5 years**, which per your own
answer is something you'd be at peace with, as long as it's also doing real good.

**What it costs you is smaller than it first sounds** (thin grants and deductibility you weren't
counting on heavily anyway) **and what it doesn't give you is worth knowing plainly**: no capital
sooner than a nonprofit would get, and no outside accountability unless you build it yourself.
**What it gives you that nothing else can** is the actual thing you named wanting.
