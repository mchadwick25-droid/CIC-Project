In 1525, in a printing house at Cologne, on the Rhine, an English New Testament was already on the press. A German churchman, an enemy of Luther, heard the printers boasting of it over their wine and went to the city council, which stopped the work. As he told it years later, two Englishmen fled up the river to Worms, carrying the printed sheets.

One of them was William Tyndale, a priest, a clergyman ordained to lead worship. He was putting the New Testament, the second part of the Bible, the gospels and letters, into English straight from the Greek it was first written in, not the church's Latin. At home an English Bible needed a bishop's leave, the permission of one of the church's chief officers. So he printed it abroad and had it smuggled in, and the king ordered the copies burned. Tyndale would offer that king his life for the Bible's words alone, and ordinary readers would suffer for owning his book. In 1536, betrayed and condemned, he was put to death.

He was born near the Welsh border and schooled at Oxford and Cambridge. As tutor at a manor in Gloucestershire, in the west of England, he argued with visiting senior churchmen, showing them the plain places of the Bible "in the book", until local priests accused him. In 1530 he wrote down why he began, thinking of lay people, everyone who was not clergy, the church's ordained men: "This alone moved me to translate the New Testament. Experience had taught me this. Lay people could not be made firm in any truth unless the Scripture lay plainly before their eyes, in their mother tongue, so that they might see the course, order and meaning of the text." By Scripture he meant the Bible.

He sought service with the bishop of London, the church's chief officer over the city and its region, but the bishop's house "was full". Finding "no place to do it in all England", he left for Germany in 1524 and never came back.

At Worms in 1526 he printed it again as a small book, the text alone, with a short address to the reader and no name. That August a friend of Luther's wrote in his diary that he had heard six thousand copies were printed there, by an Englishman so skilled in seven languages you would think each his native tongue.

English merchants carried the books in among other goods. That October the bishop of London ordered every copy handed in within thirty days, on pain of being shut out of the church and its worship, calling it the work of "Luther's sect", the followers of Martin Luther, the German reformer. King Henry VIII wrote that these "untrue translations" were to be burned, and their keepers and readers punished. The archbishop of Canterbury, the senior bishop of the English church, bought up copies at great cost, and an agent of the king's cardinal, Wolsey, a senior churchman named by the pope who was also the king's chief minister, seized English books hidden in sacks under flax.

Tyndale also wrote on the king's own matters. His book of 1528, The Obedience of a Christian Man, taught that God makes the king judge over all, with no judge over him but God. In Foxe's story, Anne Boleyn, later the king's queen, gave the king a copy, and he called it a book "for me, and all kings to read". In 1530 Tyndale printed the first five books of the Bible, put into English from the Hebrew, and soon after a book on the bishops' dealings with kings. That book, he later told the king's envoy, angered the king most, though he had only warned him against his clergy's scheming and against Wolsey. His nineteenth-century editor adds that it opposed the king's plan to end his marriage. And Tyndale called the clergy who fought his translation hypocrites who kept the people from the Bible.

In April 1531, in a field outside Antwerp, the great trading city of the Netherlands region, that envoy met a man who said his name was Tyndale. He spoke of exile, hunger and cold, and would not come home on any promise. The king, through his minister, had already called Tyndale's answer to the king's chancellor, Thomas More, full of "seditious, slanderous lies", lies stirring people against the king. But the minister added that the king would show mercy if Tyndale submitted. In May the envoy showed him that line. Tyndale asked for the bare text, the Bible's words alone, without notes or introductions, and the envoy reported his answer: "Suppose the king, of his grace, grants his people only the bare text of the Scripture, in the translation of anyone he pleases. Then I will at once make a faithful promise never to write again. … I offer my body to suffer whatever pain or torture, yes, whatever death the king wills, if only this is granted."

No reply from the king is held. By the next spring the king's envoy to the emperor, whose officers held Brussels and Antwerp, had orders to seize him. Still Tyndale kept revising, and in 1534 he wrote that he had compared his work with the Greek again, asking readers to refuse it wherever the Bible disallowed it.

At Antwerp he lodged with an English merchant. John Foxe, who collected the stories of Protestants put to death, tells from that merchant how an Englishman, Henry Phillips, well dressed like a gentleman, won Tyndale's trust and borrowed forty shillings. Then, in a narrow passage, he made Tyndale walk ahead and pointed down over his head for the waiting officers. Tyndale was held more than a year in the castle of Vilvorde, near Brussels, while his host wrote that the king had no truer subject. Condemned under the emperor's law, he was tied to a post, the stake, strangled, and his body burned. Foxe says he cried out, "Lord, open the king of England's eyes."

Foxe kept the readers' stories too. A monk, a man living under vows in a monastery, was whipped, gagged and put in the stocks after he was given the Testament. John Maundrel, a Wiltshire farmer who could not read, carried one everywhere, had others read it to him and knew most of it by heart. In 1556, when the old church was restored under Queen Mary, he was burned at Salisbury for his words against the Mass and purgatory. Printed abroad in thousands and carried in faster than bishops could buy and burn it, the book had reached readers whom no one had allowed to have it.

Within a year of Tyndale's death, the archbishop sent the king's minister an English Bible he liked better than any before, and the king allowed it to be bought and read. It bore the name Thomas Matthew, but his editors find that most of it was Tyndale's work.

In 1533 he had written to a young friend held in the Tower of London. He called God to witness that he had never changed one syllable of God's word against his conscience, and never would, for all the pleasure, honor or riches on earth.
