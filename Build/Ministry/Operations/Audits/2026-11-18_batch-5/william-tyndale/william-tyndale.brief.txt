In 1525, at Cologne on the Rhine, an English New Testament was already on the press when a German churchman, an enemy of Luther, heard the printers boasting of it over their wine. He went to the city council, and the work was stopped. As he told it years later, two Englishmen fled up the river to Worms with the printed sheets.

One was William Tyndale, a priest ordained to lead worship, and a scholar. He was putting the New Testament, the gospels and letters that make up the second part of the Bible, into English straight from the Greek they were first written in, not the church's Latin. At home, an English Bible needed the leave of a bishop, one of the church's chief officers, so he printed abroad and had the books smuggled in. The king ordered them burned. Tyndale would offer that king his life for the Bible's words alone, and in 1536, betrayed and condemned, he was put to death.

He was born near the Welsh border and schooled at Oxford and Cambridge. As a tutor at a manor in Gloucestershire, in the west of England, he argued with visiting churchmen until local priests accused him. In 1530 he wrote down why he began, thinking of lay people, everyone outside the clergy, the church's ordained men: "This alone moved me to translate the New Testament. Experience had taught me this. Lay people could not be made firm in any truth unless the Scripture lay plainly before their eyes, in their mother tongue, so that they might see the course, order and meaning of the text." By Scripture he meant the Bible.

He sought work with the bishop of London, the church's chief officer over the city, but the bishop's house "was full". Finding "no place to do it in all England", he left for Germany in 1524 and never came back.

At Worms in 1526 he printed it again as a small book, with no name on it. That August a friend of Luther's noted in his diary that he had heard six thousand copies were printed there. English merchants carried them in among other goods. In October the bishop of London ordered every copy handed in, on pain of being shut out of the church and its worship, and called it the work of "Luther's sect", the followers of Martin Luther, the German reformer. King Henry VIII wrote that these "untrue translations" must be burned, and their readers punished.

Tyndale also wrote on the king's own matters. His Obedience of a Christian Man, in 1528, taught that God makes the king judge over all, with no judge over him but God. In the story told by John Foxe, who later collected the stories of Protestants put to death, the king called it a book "for me, and all kings to read". In 1530 Tyndale put the first five books of the Bible into English from the Hebrew, and then wrote on the bishops' dealings with kings. That book, he later told the king's envoy, angered the king most. He was fierce, too, against the clergy who fought his translation, calling them hypocrites who kept the people from the Bible.

In 1531 that envoy met him in a field outside Antwerp, the great trading city of the Netherlands region. Tyndale spoke of exile, hunger and cold, and would not come home on any promise. The king had called his newest book full of "seditious, slanderous lies", lies stirring people against the king, yet had said he would show mercy if Tyndale submitted. Tyndale asked only for the bare text, the Bible's words alone, without notes or introductions, and the envoy reported his answer: "Suppose the king, of his grace, grants his people only the bare text of the Scripture, in the translation of anyone he pleases. Then I will at once make a faithful promise never to write again. … I offer my body to suffer whatever pain or torture, yes, whatever death the king wills, if only this is granted."

No reply from the king is held, and within a year his envoys were ordered to seize him. At Antwerp, Foxe tells, drawing on Tyndale's host there, an English merchant, an Englishman named Henry Phillips, well dressed like a gentleman, won his trust. In a narrow passage he made Tyndale walk ahead, and pointed down over his head for the waiting officers. Tyndale was held more than a year in the castle of Vilvorde, near Brussels. Condemned under the law of the emperor, whose officers held Brussels and Antwerp, he was tied to a post, the stake, strangled, and his body burned. Foxe says he cried out, "Lord, open the king of England's eyes."

Foxe kept the readers' stories too. A monk, a man living under vows in a monastery, was whipped, gagged and put in the stocks after he was given the Testament. John Maundrel, a Wiltshire farmer who could not read, had others read it to him until he knew most of it by heart. In 1556, when the old church was restored under Queen Mary, he was burned at Salisbury for his words against the Mass and purgatory. Printed in thousands and carried in faster than bishops could buy and burn it, the book had reached readers no one had allowed to have it.

Within a year of Tyndale's death, the archbishop of Canterbury, the senior bishop of the English church, praised a new English Bible above any before, and the king allowed it to be bought and read. It bore the name Thomas Matthew, but his editors find that most of it was Tyndale's work.

Three years before he died, he had written to a young friend held in the Tower of London. He called God to witness that he had never changed one syllable of God's word against his conscience, and never would, for all the pleasure, honor or riches on earth.
