In 1525, at Cologne on the Rhine, an English New Testament, the second part of the Bible, was on the press. A German churchman, an enemy of Luther, told years later how he heard the printers boasting of it over their wine and had the city council stop the press. Two Englishmen, he wrote, fled up the river with the printed sheets.

One was William Tyndale, a priest ordained to lead worship, and a scholar. He was putting the New Testament, its gospels and letters, into English straight from the Greek they were first written in, not the church's Latin. At home that needed leave from a bishop, a senior church officer, so he printed abroad and had the books smuggled in, where the king ordered them burned. He would offer that king his life for the Bible's words alone, and in 1536 he was put to death.

Only this, he wrote, moved him to translate: people outside the church's ordained ranks could hold no truth unless the Bible lay plain before their eyes in their own tongue. In 1530 Tyndale printed the first five books of the Bible, put into English from the Hebrew they were written in. And he called the churchmen who fought his translation hypocrites. Tyndale later told the king's envoy that, of his books, one on the bishops' dealings with kings had angered the king most.

In 1531 that envoy met him outside Antwerp and asked him to come home. Tyndale asked only for the bare text of Scripture, the Bible's words without his notes. The envoy reported his answer: "Suppose the king, of his grace, grants his people only the bare text of the Scripture … in the translation of anyone he pleases. Then I will at once make a faithful promise never to write again. … I offer my body to suffer whatever pain or torture, yes, whatever death the king wills, if only this is granted."

No answer from the king is known, and within a year the king's envoys were ordered to seize him. John Foxe later collected the stories of Protestants put to death. As he tells it, in 1535 an Englishman who had won Tyndale's trust pointed him out to waiting officers in Antwerp. After more than a year in the castle of Vilvoorde, near Brussels, he was tied to a post and strangled, and his body was burned. Foxe says he cried out, "Lord, open the king of England's eyes."

Foxe kept the readers' stories too. John Maundrel, a Wiltshire farmer who could not read, had others read the Testament to him until he knew most of it by heart. Some readers were later burned as heretics, for teaching the church condemned as false. Maundrel himself was burned at Salisbury in 1556, when the old church was restored under Queen Mary. Smuggled in faster than bishops could burn it, the book had reached people no one had allowed to have it.

Within a year of Tyndale's death, the archbishop of Canterbury, England's senior bishop, praised a new English Bible, and the king allowed it to be bought and read. It bore another man's name, but editors find that most of it was Tyndale's.

In 1533 he had written to a friend in prison. He called God to witness that he had never changed one syllable of God's word against his conscience, and never would, for all the pleasure, honor or riches on earth.
