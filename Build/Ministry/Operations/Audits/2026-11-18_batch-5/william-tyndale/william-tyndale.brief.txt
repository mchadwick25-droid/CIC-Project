In 1525, at Cologne on the Rhine, an English New Testament was on the press. A German churchman, an enemy of Luther, told years later how he heard the printers boasting of it over their wine and had the city council stop the press. Two Englishmen, he wrote, fled up the river with the printed sheets.

One was William Tyndale, a priest ordained to lead worship, and a scholar. He was putting the New Testament, the Bible's second part, its gospels and letters, into English straight from the Greek they were first written in, not the church's Latin. At home that needed leave from a bishop, a senior church officer, so he printed abroad and had the books smuggled in, where the king ordered them burned. He would offer that king his life for the Bible's words alone, and in 1536 he was put to death.

He was born near the Welsh border and schooled at Oxford and Cambridge. People outside the church's ordained ranks, he wrote, could hold no truth unless the Bible lay plain before their eyes in their own tongue. Finding "no place to do it in all England", he left for Germany in 1524 and never came back.

At Worms in 1526 he printed the Testament in thousands, and English merchants carried the books home among other goods. The bishop of London, chief officer of the church there, gave keepers thirty days to hand in every copy or be shut out of the church. He called it the work of "Luther's sect", the German reformer's followers. King Henry VIII wrote that these "untrue translations" must be burned.

Tyndale then wrote on the king's own matters, and one book taught that the king answers to God alone. The king praised it, in the story told by John Foxe, who later collected the stories of Protestants put to death. Another, on the bishops' dealings with kings, angered him most, Tyndale told the king's envoy. And he called the churchmen who fought his translation hypocrites.

In 1531 that envoy met him outside Antwerp and later showed him the king's word: Tyndale's newest book was full of lies, yet the king would show mercy if he submitted. Tyndale asked only for the bare text of Scripture, the Bible's words without his notes. The envoy reported his answer: "Suppose the king, of his grace, grants his people only the bare text of the Scripture, in the translation of anyone he pleases. Then I will at once make a faithful promise never to write again. … I offer my body to suffer whatever pain or torture, yes, whatever death the king wills, if only this is granted."

No reply is held, and within a year the king's envoys were ordered to seize him. In 1535, Foxe tells, an Englishman who had won his trust pointed him out to waiting officers in Antwerp. After more than a year in the castle of Vilvorde, near Brussels, he was condemned under the law of the emperor, whose officers ruled there. In Foxe's account he was tied to a post and strangled, and his body was burned. Foxe says he cried out, "Lord, open the king of England's eyes."

Foxe kept the readers' stories too, of people whipped, jailed and questioned for the book. John Maundrel, a Wiltshire farmer who could not read, had others read the Testament to him until he knew most of it by heart. Smuggled in faster than bishops could buy and burn it, the book had reached people no one had allowed to have it.

Within a year of Tyndale's death, the archbishop of Canterbury, England's senior bishop, praised a new English Bible, and the king allowed it to be bought and read. It bore another man's name, but editors find that most of it was Tyndale's.

In 1533 he had written to a friend in prison. He called God to witness that he had never changed one syllable of God's word against his conscience, and never would, for all the pleasure, honor or riches on earth.
