# Outline check: William Tyndale

## My own heart (written before reading Outline.md)

From the census entry (every field but `longDescription`), the source sheet, and what Tyndale is known for.

**Essence.** Tyndale, an English priest and scholar, translated the New Testament into English from the Greek, at a time when English Scripture needed a bishop's leave that none had given. Finding "no place to do it in all England", he printed it abroad (Cologne interrupted, 1525; Worms, 1526), and it was smuggled into England, where the bishops had copies handed in and burned. Hunted by the king's agents, he was betrayed in Antwerp in 1535 and strangled and burned at Vilvorde in 1536. He is on the tree as the forerunner of the English Reformation: within a year of his death the king allowed an English Bible built largely on his work (editors' view).

**Also told.** Why he translated: lay people could not hold any truth unless the text lay plain before them in their mother tongue (his own words, 1530). The ploughboy saying (Foxe alone). Readers who kept his Testament at the risk of their lives (Bainham, Maundrel, the Marian searcher). The king's turn against him: the Obedience praised by the king; the Practice of Prelates angering him; the Answer to More called "seditious, slanderous lies"; Vaughan's meeting and Tyndale's offer of his life for a bare text in anyone's translation. Revising "compared unto the Greek" (1534); the Five Books of Moses from the Hebrew (1530). His death in Foxe's voice ("Lord, open the king of England's eyes"). Contested: whether he met Luther; who sent Phillips.

## BLOCKING

**B1. The king's "answer" and "The king refused" put events in the wrong order and claim more than the record holds (Also told; beat 6).** Cromwell's letter is the source of "seditious, slanderous lies" and the king being "joyous to have his realme destytute of such a person" (DT 1950–1990). It answers Vaughan's April letter. It came before the offer, not after it. Vaughan's dispatch of 20 May 1531 says he was acting "upon the receipt of" those instructions. He showed Tyndale Cromwell's softer clause, and Q2 was Tyndale's reply to that clause (DT 2059–2101). No held source gives a reply from the king to the offer itself. The close of the talks and the king "resum[ing] his previous purpose" of arresting him are Walter's narration (DT 2174–2177). The record shows only that by 14 March 1532 the king had ordered Elyot to stay at Brussels "for the apprehension of Tyndale" (DT 2189–2200, Documented).
*Correction.* Beat 6 should read, in this order: April 1531, the field outside Antwerp, "My name is Tyndale"; he would not come home on any promise. The king, through his minister, called Tyndale's newest book, his answer to Thomas More, full of "seditious, slanderous lies", and said he was glad to have him out of the realm; but the minister added a line saying the king would show mercy if Tyndale submitted. In May the envoy showed him that line, and Tyndale answered with Q2. No reply from the king to the offer is held. By the next spring the king's envoy at the emperor's court had orders to seize him (M12).
In Also told, replace "The king refused (M11: Documented)" with "No answer to it is held; within a year the king's envoys were ordered to seize him (M12: Documented)." Add to Do not say: "Not 'the king refused the offer' or 'the king answered that his book was full of lies': the king's words came before the offer, and no reply to it is held."

**B2. Readers are said to have been burned for reading the Testament, and Maundrel "burned with the book" (Also told; Connection).** Also told says readers "were whipped, jailed and burned for it". The Connection says "a farm worker who could not read burned with the book he had others read to him". Foxe gives Maundrel's charges as his words against the Mass and purgatory, at Keevil church, in 1556 (F8 8670–8705). The Testament was not among them, and nothing says a book burned with him. Bayfield was whipped, gagged and stocked after he was given the Testament, the Mammon and the Obedience (F4 52209–52217). Bainham was examined for keeping it (F4 53741–53767). Neither passage makes the Testament the charge he died for.
*Correction.* Also told: "Ordinary people read his New Testament. Readers were whipped, jailed and examined for it, and some were later burned as heretics." Connection: "a farm worker who could not read, who had others read it to him and knew most of it by heart, and who was burned at Salisbury twenty years later under Queen Mary". Add to Do not say: "Not that Maundrel, or any reader, was burned for reading or owning the Testament, and not that a Testament burned with him. Foxe gives Maundrel's charges as his words against the Mass and purgatory (F8 8670–8705)."

## MINOR

1. **Beat 2 reaches ahead.** The Munmouth clause (letters burned after the 1526 sermon; the Tower in 1528) sits in a beat about 1520–1524. Move it to beat 4 or drop it. If it is kept, gloss "the Tower": "the Tower of London, a royal fortress used as a prison".
2. **Phillips was not "young"** (beat 7; Names). Foxe has "a comely fellow, like as he had been a gentleman" and "a tall comely person" (DT 2864–2865, 2965). Say "an Englishman, Henry Phillips, well dressed like a gentleman". Add "young" to Do not say.
3. **Poyntz's words** (beat 7). He did not write "a great loss". He wrote "a great hinderance to the gospel" (DT 3009–3010). Say "a great hindrance to the gospel".
4. **Glosses that say more than their source.** (a) Bishop's chancellor: DT 582–583 says he "acted as governor of the diocese", and it was the Gloucestershire bishop's chancellor, not London's. Gloss it "the bishop's chief officer, who ran the diocese for him". (b) The emperor: DT 2911–2923 names neither Charles V nor his rule of the Low Countries. Cite DT 1499 ("the emperor Charles V") and DT 1364–1366 (his aunt, the regent of the Netherlands), or gloss it "the emperor, whose officers held Brussels and Antwerp". (c) Excommunication: the gloss brings in "sacraments", a church word it does not explain, and "a public disgrace", which has no source. Say "shut out of the church and its worship".
5. **Missing glosses on passing words.** "Doctors" in beat 2: "learned churchmen, doctors of divinity", not physicians. "The Low Countries": "the Netherlands region". "Queen Mary": "when the old church was restored under Queen Mary" (F8 8646–8647).
6. **More's "vats full" (beat 4) is from 1532,** in a beat headed 1526–1530. Give the year, or move it to beat 6, where More is named. The beat also has three chancellors (the bishop's, Wolsey, More). Keep Wolsey as "the cardinal" and More as "the king's chancellor, Thomas More", so they do not blur.
7. **The Frankfurt seizure (beat 4).** Rincke was a councillor of Cologne working for the cardinal, not "an agent in Frankfurt". The books were "books printed in the English language" by Roye and "Hutchyns" (DT 1374–1404), not certainly Testaments. Say "English books".
8. **"Hypocrites" and "fiercely against the bishops"** (Also told; beat 5) are cited to M1, M7, M10, M15 and S2, which do not hold them. The word is in DT 25582–25591 ("our malicious and wily hypocrites"). It is aimed at the clergy who opposed the translation, not only at the bishops. Cite those lines, and DT 25724–25730 ("these enemies of all truth"), and say "the clergy who fought the translation".
9. **Length.** The seven beats as written, plus a third for what came of him, will not fit 500 words. Mark the first cuts: the chancellor's "dog", the Munmouth clause, More's "vats", the Greek closer to English than Latin, the searching priest, and the Index. Or set the length at about 3:20 to 3:40, by what the beats need. Do not fill to it.
10. **Do not say on Jonah.** Walter says Tyndale's Prologue to Jonah came "accompanied perhaps by a translation of that prophet" (DT 2179–2181). Say "and probably Jonah".
11. **Q1 rendering.** The last sentence, "So they might see the course, order and meaning of the text", is a fragment, and on its own it reads as a result, not a purpose. Join it to the sentence before: "...in their mother tongue, so that they might see the course, order and meaning of the text."
12. **Maundrel's trade.** Foxe calls him a "husbandman, ... brought up in husbandry", the son of a farmer, with a wife and children (F8 8614–8620). Say "a Wiltshire farmer", not "farm worker".
