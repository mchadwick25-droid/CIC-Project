# Outline: William Tyndale

Every line cites `Source_Sheet.md` (S = setting, M = moment, I = impact, L = link, D = disagreement, N = not held).

- **The essence:** William Tyndale, an English priest and scholar, put the New Testament into English from the Greek (M7: Widely Accepted). Barred from doing it at home, he had it printed abroad, at Worms in 1526, and smuggled into England (M3, M6: Widely Accepted, a diary's hearsay and the editor). There the bishop of London ordered every copy handed in, and the king ordered them burned (M8: Documented). Hunted by the king's agents, he was betrayed at Antwerp in 1535, and in 1536 he was strangled at the stake and his body burned (M12, M16, M17: Documented to Widely Accepted; the manner is Foxe's account, D3).
- **Also told:** Why he did it, in his own words: the lay people could hold no truth unless Scripture lay before their eyes in their own tongue (Q1, M1: Documented). The exile's work: he kept revising against the Greek, put the Five Books of Moses into English from the Hebrew in 1530, and wrote fiercely against the bishops (M7, M10, M15: Documented). In 1531 the king's envoy asked him to come home; he offered to stop writing and give himself up, to any death, if the king would only let the bare text of Scripture out among the people, in anyone's translation (Q2, M11: Documented as the envoy's report); the king refused (M11: Documented). What came of it: lay people read his Testament and were whipped, jailed and burned for it; a farmhand who could not read carried one and had others read it to him (I1: Documented as Foxe's accounts). Within a year of his death the king licensed an English Bible "to be bought and read" (I2: Documented, Cranmer's letters); that most of it was Tyndale's work, printed under another name, is the editors' finding (I2: Widely Accepted).
- **Length:** about 3 minutes (about 450 words). Reason: one clear line (why, exile, print, burning, offer, betrayal, death), a vivid record on both sides (his own prefaces and letters, the envoy's letters, the bishops' orders, his host's letter), the record's force on him and on his readers, and the readers' later life. The record is full; nothing is padded. The essence fills the first 150 words.
- **Setting:** "in the 1520s" and "1536" (S4, M6, M18); "born near the Welsh border, schooled at Oxford and Cambridge" (S1, Foxe); "a priest" (S1); "a manor in Gloucestershire, in the west of England" (S2); "London", "Worms, on the Rhine", "Antwerp, the great trading city of the Low Countries", "the castle of Vilvorde, near Brussels" (S4); tradition: "the English Reformation", which he did not live to see (S5). England's law since 1409: no English Scripture without a bishop's leave (S3; one clause at most, it is the Wyclif story's). Weave in a clause at a time.
- **Shape:** Missionary or translator. The bridge he built is a language: the New Testament in English. The impact is in what the readers did with it. Trap: telling it only from the sender's side. Give the readers' record (I1) its room, and say whose record it is (Foxe's). The death is the end of the life, not the whole story.
- **Sibling:** `martin-luther` (batch 5). Luther's job: his stand before the emperor at Worms in 1521, and the German Bible and hymns. This story's job: Tyndale's English Testament, printed at Worms in 1526, the exile, and his death. Do not retell Luther's stand; at most one clause may say Worms was the city where Luther had faced the emperor five years before (L2). His enemies called the Testament Luther's (M5, M8); say so in their voice. His use of Luther's prefaces is carried on the census Wittenberg link (I6); at most one clause, credited to "his nineteenth-century editor". Link `john-wyclif`: its live story already tells Tyndale taking up the work from the Greek and quotes his 1526 address ("no one's English to follow"); do not use those words, and do not retell Arundel's ban beyond one clause (S3, L1).
- **Openings:**
  (a) **Chosen.** Cologne, 1525. In a printing house on the Rhine, three thousand copies of an English New Testament had reached the sheets marked K. A German churchman, an enemy of Luther, heard printers boast of it over their wine, and went to the city council; the press was stopped. Two Englishmen fled up the river to Worms, carrying the printed sheets (M5; Cochlaeus, hostile, writing years later: credit him in a clause). About three sentences, then the essence.
  (b) Not chosen: Foxe's saying that he would make "a boy that driveth the plough" know more Scripture than a learned priest (M2). It rests on Foxe alone, decades later; it may stand in beat 2 in Foxe's voice, as a paraphrase.

**Story beats** (beat 1 is the first minute)
1. **Cologne and the essence.** Opening (a). Then the essence: an English priest and scholar, translating from the Greek, printing abroad because he could not at home; burned books; his own death. Point ahead in one sentence to the offer he made the king and to the readers. Widely Accepted (Cochlaeus's account) and Documented.
2. **The scholar and his reason, about 1520–1524.** Born near the Welsh border, schooled at Oxford and Cambridge, a priest (S1, Foxe). As tutor in a Gloucestershire manor he argued with visiting abbots and doctors, showing them "in the book" the plain places of Scripture (S2, Foxe). Local priests accused him; the bishop's chancellor, he wrote later, "rated me as though I had been a dog" (S2, his own words: optional short phrase, not one of the three quotes; paraphrase preferred). Foxe says he told a learned man he would make a ploughboy know more Scripture than he did (M2, Foxe's report). Q1: his reason, in his own words of 1530. English Scripture needed a bishop's leave (S3, one clause). Documented (his words); Widely Accepted (Foxe's).
3. **No room in England, 1523–1524.** He sought the bishop of London's service, bringing a speech he had put "out of Greek into English" as proof of his skill; the bishop's house "was full" (M3). He lived half a year with a London cloth merchant who said he studied day and night (M3, the merchant's own petition, 1528); in 1528 that merchant was sent to the Tower for helping him, and burned the letters Tyndale had sent him "for fear" (DT 818–838, 885–890; Documented, optional clause). He found, he wrote, "no place to do it in all England" (M3), and in 1524 left for Germany; he never came back (M3, editor's date). Greek, he later argued, sits closer to English than Latin does (M7c, paraphrase). Documented.
4. **Worms, 1526.** At Worms he printed it again in a small book: the text alone, with a short address to the reader and no name (M6, editor). A friend of Luther's noted in his diary that August that six thousand copies had been printed at Worms, by an Englishman so skilled in seven languages you would think each his native tongue (M6, hearsay: credit the diary, and keep "he heard"). Widely Accepted; the number is hearsay (D2).
5. **Smuggled and burned, 1526–1530.** English merchants carried the books in among other goods (M5, M9). In October 1526 the bishop of London ordered every copy handed in within thirty days, on pain of excommunication, calling it the work of "Luther's sect" (M8). King Henry VIII wrote that these "untrue translations" were to be burned, with punishment for keepers and readers (M8). The archbishop of Canterbury bought up copies at great cost (editor, from his letter); an agent in Frankfurt seized books packed in sacks under flax, bound for Scotland and England (M9). The king's chancellor complained they came "by whole vats full", and were scattered by night (M12, More's own words; optional). Documented.
6. **The exile's work and the king's offer, 1530–1534.** He put the Five Books of Moses into English from the Hebrew, printed at Marburg in 1530 (M10), and wrote hard against the bishops: in his words they were hypocrites who kept the people from Scripture (M1, S2; one clause, so his own force is not softened). In April 1531, outside the gates of Antwerp, in a field, he met the king's envoy: "My name is Tyndale" (M11, paraphrase or a few words). He spoke of exile, hunger and cold, and would not come home on a promise (M11). In May he made the offer: Q2. The king's answer, through his minister: the king was glad to have his realm "destitute of such a person" (M11; paraphrase). He kept revising: in 1534 he wrote that he had compared his work with the Greek again and weeded out faults, and asked readers to refuse it wherever Scripture disallowed it (M7a, M15). Documented.
7. **Betrayed, 1535; died, 1536.** At Antwerp he lodged with an English merchant. A young Englishman, Henry Phillips, won his trust and borrowed forty shillings. In a narrow passage he made Tyndale walk ahead, and pointed down over his head for the waiting officers (M16, Foxe, from the merchant). He was held more than a year in the castle of Vilvorde, near Brussels (M16, M17). His host wrote that the king had no truer subject and that his death would be a great loss (M17, the merchant's own letter, August 1535). He refused a lawyer and answered for himself (M17, Foxe). Condemned under the emperor's law, he was tied to the stake, strangled, and his body burned (M18, D3, Foxe). Foxe says he cried out: Q3. Widely Accepted; the last words are Foxe's report.

**What came of him** (about a third of the story)
1. **Heaviest: the readers.** A monk given his Testament was whipped, gagged and put in the stocks (I1c). A man examined in 1531 said the people should have the English Testament "as it is" (I1d, paraphrase). John Maundrel, a Wiltshire farm worker who could not read, carried the Testament everywhere, had others read it to him, and knew most of it by heart; he was burned at Salisbury in 1556 (I1e). Under Queen Mary a searching priest picked one up and called it "plain heresy" (I1f, optional). All Documented as Foxe's accounts; credit Foxe once.
2. **The king's turn.** Within a year of his death the archbishop of Canterbury sent the king's minister an English Bible, saying he liked it better than any translation made before, and that the bishops would not make a better one until "a day after doomsday" (I2, paraphrase or a few words). The king allowed it to be bought and read in England (I2, Documented). It bore the name "Thomas Matthew"; editors find most of it was Tyndale's translation (I2, Widely Accepted; say whose finding it is).
3. **Defended:** Scripture in the people's own tongue, the text alone if need be, in anyone's translation (Q2, M11); and his own work open to correction by Scripture (M15).
4. **Condemned by name:** Rome's Index of 1559 listed him (I3, optional, one clause).
End on 1 or 2, then close on the person (see Ending).

**Quotes** (he wrote in English; each rendering is from Walter's 1848 modernised text of his English)
- **Q1.** DT 25719–25724 (Preface to the Five Books of Moses, 1530): "Which thing only moved me to translate the new Testament. Because I had perceived by experience, how that it was impossible to establish the lay-people in any truth, except the scripture were plainly laid before their eyes in their mother-tongue, that they might see the process, order, and meaning of the text". Rendered: "This alone moved me to translate the New Testament. Experience had taught me this. Lay people could not be made firm in any truth unless the Scripture lay plainly before their eyes, in their mother tongue. So they might see the course, order and meaning of the text." Job: why he did it, in his own words, in beat 2. ("Process" rendered "course": its modern sense misleads.)
- **Q2.** DT 2090–2101 (Stephen Vaughan to Henry VIII, May 1531, reporting Tyndale's words), two spans with a cut: "if it would stand with the king's most gracious pleasure to grant only a bare text of the scripture to be put forth among his people, … be it of the translation of what person soever shall please his majesty, I shall immediately make faithful promise never to write more, … offering my body to suffer what pain or torture, yea, what death his grace will, so that this be obtained." Rendered: "Suppose the king, of his grace, grants his people only the bare text of the Scripture, in the translation of anyone he pleases. Then I will at once make a faithful promise never to write again. … I offer my body to suffer whatever pain or torture, yes, whatever death the king wills, if only this is granted." The first cut leaves out "as among the emperor's subjects here and other Christian princes'" (the narrator may say it); the second leaves out his promise to leave at once and kneel before the king. Plain sense of "bare text": the words of Scripture alone, without his notes or prefaces. Job: what he would die for, in beat 6. Credit the envoy.
- **Q3.** DT 3331–3333 (Foxe, quoted by Walter): "Lord, open the king of England's eyes". Rendered: "Lord, open the king of England's eyes." Job: his death, in beat 7, in Foxe's voice.

**Connection to carry into the story:** What passed from Tyndale was the English New Testament itself, in his words, read by lay people. Until then English Scripture in England was copied by hand, his editor says, and needed a bishop's leave (S3, L1). His was printed abroad in thousands and carried in by merchants faster than bishops could buy and burn it (M8, M9, M12). Those who read it paid for it: a monk whipped and gagged, readers examined, a farm worker who could not read burned with the book he had others read to him (I1). Within a year of his death the king allowed an English Bible to be bought and read, and editors find it was mostly Tyndale's work under another name (I2). The listener must understand two things: that the book reached ordinary readers in spite of the burnings, because it was printed and smuggled, not because anyone allowed it; and that the Bible the king licensed carried Tyndale's words without his name, which is the editors' finding, not the archbishop's claim. Tell it through what the readers and the archbishop did, and do not join the 1537 licence to his dying prayer as cause.

**Ending:** (a) His letter to his young friend in the Tower, 1533: he called God to witness that he had never changed a syllable of God's word against his conscience, and would not for all the pleasure, honour or riches on earth (M13; paraphrase, his own words). (b) His words a few lines on: his soul was not faint, though his body was weary (M13; paraphrase). Prefer (a).

**Who tells it**
- Tyndale's own prologues, prefaces and letters (1525–1534): his own words at the time. Q1 is from 1530, looking back seven years.
- Stephen Vaughan, the king's envoy, writing to Henry VIII in 1531: Tyndale's words as Vaughan reported them; he wrote to please a king (D7). Say "the envoy reported".
- The bishop of London's order (1526), the king's words (1526), the archbishop's 1537 letters: their own words.
- Thomas Poyntz, his host, in a letter of August 1535: his own words.
- Cochlaeus: hostile, an enemy of Luther, writing in 1549 about 1525. Say "a German churchman, an enemy of Luther, who told the story years later".
- Spalatin's diary, August 1526: hearsay from a friend; the number is not a count.
- John Foxe, the martyrologist, first printing in 1563, about 27 years after the death: the betrayal (from Poyntz), the death and last words, the ploughboy saying, and the readers. Name him once, as the one who tells the arrest and the death; the readers' stories are his too.
- Henry Walter, editor (1848): dates, the Worms edition, the 1536 and 1537 Bibles as Tyndale's. Credit as "his editors" or "historians". His admiring, providential language is not the record's.

**Do not say**
- Not "he studied with Luther at Wittenberg" or "he met Luther". Say his enemies called the work Luther's (D1).
- Not "the first English Bible". Wyclif's circle made an earlier one, copied by hand (L1). If "first" is used, only "the first New Testament printed in English, historians say" (DT 1001–1003, editor).
- Not "six thousand copies" or "three thousand copies" as fact. Credit each to its witness, or say "thousands" (D2).
- Not "burned alive" or "burned at the stake" alone. Say: tied to the stake, strangled, and his body burned, as Foxe tells it (D3).
- Not "Henry VIII had him arrested" or "Thomas More had him betrayed". Who sent Phillips is unknown; his host thought it was arranged from England without the king's knowledge (D4).
- Not "his last words were" as plain fact. Say "Foxe says he cried out" (D5).
- Not "the ploughboy" as his documented words. Say Foxe reports it.
- Not "within a year, the king answered his prayer" or any cause linking Q3 to 1537. Say "within a year" and let the facts stand.
- Not that Cranmer praised Tyndale's translation. Cranmer praised a Bible printed under the name "Thomas Matthew"; editors find most of it was Tyndale's.
- Not "the King James Bible is mostly Tyndale's" or any percentage: not held.
- Not "he translated the whole Bible". He translated the New Testament, the Five Books of Moses and Jonah; editors add the historical books to Chronicles (I2).
- Not "monk" or "friar". He was a priest (S1).
- Not "the bishops burned him". He was condemned under the emperor's law at Vilvorde, after Louvain theologians wrote against him (M17, M18).
- Not that Wolsey's bonfire of February 1526 is documented here: it rests on the editor (M8). Use the bishop of London's order of October 1526.
- Not "Vilvoorde, Belgium". Say "the castle of Vilvorde, near Brussels".
- Keep "the bare text", with its plain sense, and "in anyone's translation" (Q2): that he would accept a translation not his own is the point.
- Keep "of his grace" or the equivalent politeness in Q2; do not make the offer a demand.
- Do not soften his own polemic: he called the bishops hypocrites and worse (S2, M1; Preface to Moses). One clause is enough; do not leave it out.

**Leave out:** the Packington tale (Foxe, a "current tale", M9: card); the shipwreck and Coverdale at Hamburg (Foxe from memory, redated, D6); the Antwerp days of alms and Sunday readings (Foxe, M14: card); the keeper and his daughter converted (Foxe, wonder-adjacent: card); the conjuror at the Antwerp supper (Crespin, a wonder); Anne Boleyn's vellum copy and her letter for a merchant (editor, side story); Joye's unauthorised edition (side quarrel); the *Practice of Prelates* and the king's divorce (outside the job; text not held, N3); the doctrine of the Obedience on kings (outside the job); his debate with More beyond one clause (texts not held, N3); Frith's death (his own story, not on the tree); the Prologue to Romans and Luther (census edge; one optional clause); birth year 1484 (editor's probability); Little Sodbury and Walsh by name (say "a Gloucestershire manor"); Foxe's and Penn's "apostle of England" (card).
