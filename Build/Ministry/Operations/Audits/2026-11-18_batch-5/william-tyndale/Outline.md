# Outline: William Tyndale

Every line cites `Source_Sheet.md` (S = setting, M = moment, I = impact, L = link, D = disagreement, N = not held).

- **The essence:** William Tyndale, an English priest and scholar, put the New Testament, the gospels and letters first written in Greek, into English straight from the Greek (M7: Widely Accepted). The church's Bible was then in Latin, and English Scripture needed a bishop's leave (S3). Barred from the work at home, he had it printed abroad, at Worms on the Rhine in 1526, and smuggled into England (M3, M6: Widely Accepted, a diary's hearsay and the editor). There the bishop of London, the church's chief officer for the city, ordered every copy handed in, and the king ordered them burned (M8: Documented). Hunted by the king's agents, he was betrayed at Antwerp in 1535, and in 1536 he was strangled while tied to a post, and his body burned (M12, M16, M17: Documented to Widely Accepted; the manner is Foxe's account, D3).
- **Also told:** Why he did it, in his own words: lay people, everyone who was not clergy, could hold no truth unless the Bible lay before their eyes in their own tongue (Q1, M1: Documented). The exile's work: he kept correcting his English against the Greek, put the first five books of the Bible into English from the Hebrew in 1530, and wrote fiercely against the clergy who fought the translation (M7, M10, M15: Documented; DT 25582–25591, 25724–25730). His books on the king's matters turned the king against him. One taught that a Christian owes obedience to the king, who answers only to God; the king, in Foxe's story, praised it (M10a(a, b): Documented; the praise Widely Accepted). Another, on the bishops' dealings with kings, angered him most, Tyndale told the king's envoy (M10a(c): Documented as the envoy's report). Only his editor says it opposed the king's plan to end his marriage (M10a(d): editor's view). In 1531 the envoy asked him to come home. He offered to stop writing and give himself up to any death, if the king would only let the bare text of the Bible, without his notes, go out among the people, in anyone's translation (Q2, M11: Documented as the envoy's report). No answer to it is held; within a year the king's envoys were ordered to seize him (M12: Documented). What came of it: Ordinary people read his New Testament. Readers were whipped, jailed and examined for it, and some were later burned as heretics, people the church condemned for false teaching. A Wiltshire farmer who could not read carried one and had others read it to him (I1: Documented as the accounts of John Foxe, who collected the stories of Protestants put to death). Within a year of his death the king licensed an English Bible "to be bought and read" (I2: Documented, letters of the archbishop of Canterbury, the senior bishop of the English church). That most of it was Tyndale's work, printed under another name, is the editors' finding (I2: Widely Accepted).
- **Length:** about 3 minutes 20 (about 500 words), by Mark's ruling of 2026-11-18 that the king's turn against him be told. Reason: one clear line (why, exile, print, burning, the king's matters, offer, betrayal, death), a vivid record on both sides (his own prefaces and letters, the envoy's letters, the bishops' orders, his host's letter), the record's force on him and on his readers, and the readers' later life. The record is full; nothing is padded. The essence fills the first 150 words. First cuts, if the beats run long: the chancellor's "dog", the Munmouth clause, More's "vats", the Greek closer to English than Latin, the searching priest, and the Index.
- **Setting:** "in the 1520s" and "1536" (S4, M6, M18); "born near the Welsh border, schooled at Oxford and Cambridge" (S1, Foxe); "a priest" (S1); "a manor in Gloucestershire, in the west of England" (S2); "London", "Worms, on the Rhine", "Antwerp, the great trading city of the Low Countries", "the castle of Vilvoorde, near Brussels" (S4); tradition: "the English Reformation", which he did not live to see (S5). England's law since 1409: no English Scripture without a bishop's leave (S3; one clause at most, it is the Wyclif story's). Weave in a clause at a time.

- **Openings:**
  (a) **Chosen.** Cologne, 1525. In a printing house on the Rhine, three thousand copies of an English New Testament had reached the sheets marked K. A German churchman, an enemy of Luther, heard printers boast of it over their wine, and went to the city council; the press was stopped. Two Englishmen fled up the river to Worms, carrying the printed sheets (M5; Cochlaeus, hostile, writing years later: credit him in a clause). About three sentences, then the essence.
  (b) Not chosen: Foxe's saying that he would make "a boy that driveth the plough" know more Scripture than a learned priest (M2). It rests on Foxe alone, decades later; it may stand in beat 2 in Foxe's voice, as a paraphrase.

**Story beats** (beat 1 is the first minute)
1. **Cologne and the essence.** Opening (a). Then the essence: an English priest and scholar, translating from the Greek, printing abroad because he could not at home; burned books; his own death. Point ahead in one sentence to the offer he made the king and to the readers. Widely Accepted (Cochlaeus's account) and Documented.
2. **The scholar, his reason, and no room in England, about 1520–1524.** Born near the Welsh border, schooled at Oxford and Cambridge, a priest (S1, Foxe). As tutor in a Gloucestershire manor he argued with visiting abbots and doctors, showing them "in the book" the plain places of Scripture (S2, Foxe). Local priests accused him; the bishop's chancellor, he wrote later, "rated me as though I had been a dog" (S2, his own words: optional short phrase, not one of the three quotes; paraphrase preferred). Foxe says he told a learned man he would make a ploughboy know more Scripture than he did (M2, Foxe's report). Q1: his reason, in his own words of 1530. English Scripture needed a bishop's leave (S3, one clause). Documented (his words); Widely Accepted (Foxe's). He sought the bishop of London's service, bringing a speech he had put "out of Greek into English" as proof of his skill; the bishop's house "was full" (M3). He lived half a year with a London cloth merchant who said he studied day and night (M3, the merchant's own petition, 1528). He found, he wrote, "no place to do it in all England" (M3), and in 1524 left for Germany; he never came back (M3, editor's date). Greek, he later argued, sits closer to English than Latin does (M7c, paraphrase). Documented.
3. **Worms, 1526.** At Worms he printed it again in a small book: the text alone, with a short address to the reader and no name (M6, editor). A friend of Luther's noted in his diary that August that six thousand copies had been printed at Worms, by an Englishman so skilled in seven languages you would think each his native tongue (M6, hearsay: credit the diary, and keep "he heard"). Widely Accepted; the number is hearsay (D2).
4. **Smuggled and burned, 1526–1530.** English merchants carried the books in among other goods (M5, M9). In October 1526 the bishop of London ordered every copy handed in within thirty days, on pain of excommunication, calling it the work of "Luther's sect" (M8). Once the Testament was preached against, the London cloth merchant who had kept him burned the letters Tyndale had sent him, "for fear", and in 1528 he was sent to the Tower of London, a royal fortress used as a prison, for having helped him (DT 818–838, 881–890; Documented, optional clause). King Henry VIII wrote that these "untrue translations" were to be burned, with punishment for keepers and readers (M8). The archbishop of Canterbury bought up copies at great cost (editor, from his letter); at Frankfurt a councillor of Cologne, working for the cardinal, Wolsey, the king's chief minister, seized English books packed in sacks under flax, bound for Scotland and England (M9; DT 1374–1404). Later, in 1532, the king's chancellor complained they came "by whole vats full", and were scattered by night (M12, More's own words; optional). Documented.
5. **The king's matters, 1528–1530.** In 1528 (the editor's date) he printed *The Obedience of a Christian Man*, a book on obedience. It taught that God makes the king judge over all, with no judge over him but God; whoever resists the king resists God (M10a(a), his own words, paraphrase; Documented). Foxe, through a later historian, tells that Anne Boleyn, later the king's queen, got a seized copy back and asked the king to read it; he was delighted, and said it was a book "for me, and all kings to read" (M10a(b), Widely Accepted as Foxe's story; credit it, a few words at most). In 1530, at Marburg, he printed the first five books of the Bible from the Hebrew (M10), and, the editor says, soon after *The Practice of Prelates*, a book on the bishops' dealings with kings (M10a(c)). That book is not held. In his own words to the king's envoy, it angered the king most, though he had only warned him against his clergy's scheming and against his cardinal, Wolsey, whose wickedness, Tyndale said, the king had since found out (M10a(c, e), Documented as the envoy's report; the judgment on Wolsey is Tyndale's own). His nineteenth-century editor adds that it objected to the king's plan to end his marriage to Catherine of Aragon (M10a(d)): say it is the editor's view, in one clause, or leave it out. It was banned by a proclamation in London at the end of 1531 (M10a(c), Documented). He also wrote hard against the clergy who fought the translation: in his words they were hypocrites who kept the people from the Bible (DT 25582–25591, 25724–25730; one clause, so his own force is not softened).
6. **The king's offer, 1531, and the revised Testament, 1534.** April 1531, the field outside Antwerp, "My name is Tyndale" (M11, paraphrase or a few words); he spoke of exile, hunger and cold, and would not come home on any promise (M11). The king, through his minister, called Tyndale's newest book, his answer to the king's chancellor, Thomas More, full of "seditious, slanderous lies", and said he was glad to have him out of the realm; but the minister added a line saying the king would show mercy if Tyndale submitted (M10a(f), M11; DT 1950–1990, 2072–2084; paraphrase; the book is named by the editor). In May the envoy showed him that line, and Tyndale answered with Q2 (DT 2059–2101). No reply from the king to the offer is held. By the next spring the king's envoy at the emperor's court had orders to seize him (M12). He kept revising: in 1534 he wrote that he had compared his work with the Greek again and weeded out faults, and asked readers to refuse it wherever Scripture disallowed it (M7a, M15). Documented.
7. **Betrayed, 1535; died, 1536.** At Antwerp he lodged with an English merchant. An Englishman, Henry Phillips, well dressed like a gentleman, won his trust and borrowed forty shillings. In a narrow passage he made Tyndale walk ahead, and pointed down over his head for the waiting officers (M16, Foxe, from the merchant). He was held more than a year in the castle of Vilvoorde, near Brussels (M16, M17). His host wrote that the king had no truer subject and that his death would be a great hindrance to the gospel (M17, the merchant's own letter, August 1535). He refused a lawyer and answered for himself (M17, Foxe). Condemned under the emperor's law, he was tied to the stake, strangled, and his body burned (M18, D3, Foxe). Foxe says he cried out: Q3. Widely Accepted; the last words are Foxe's report.

**What came of him** (about a third of the story)
1. **Heaviest: the readers.** A monk given his Testament was whipped, gagged and put in the stocks (I1c). A man examined in 1531 said the people should have the English Testament "as it is" (I1d, paraphrase). John Maundrel, a Wiltshire farmer who could not read, carried the Testament everywhere, had others read it to him, and knew most of it by heart; he was burned at Salisbury in 1556 (I1e). When the old church was restored under Queen Mary, a searching priest picked one up and called it "plain heresy" (I1f, optional). All Documented as Foxe's accounts; credit Foxe once.
2. **The king's turn.** Within a year of his death the archbishop of Canterbury sent the king's minister an English Bible, saying he liked it better than any translation made before, and that the bishops would not make a better one until "a day after doomsday" (I2, paraphrase or a few words). The king allowed it to be bought and read in England (I2, Documented). It bore the name "Thomas Matthew"; editors find most of it was Tyndale's translation (I2, Widely Accepted; say whose finding it is).
3. **Defended:** Scripture in the people's own tongue, the text alone if need be, in anyone's translation (Q2, M11); and his own work open to correction by Scripture (M15).
4. **Condemned by name:** Rome's Index of 1559 listed him (I3, optional, one clause).
End on 1 or 2, then close on the person (see Ending).

**Quotes** (he wrote in English; each rendering is from Walter's 1848 modernised text of his English)
- **Q1.** DT 25719–25724 (Preface to the Five Books of Moses, 1530): "Which thing only moved me to translate the new Testament. Because I had perceived by experience, how that it was impossible to establish the lay-people in any truth, except the scripture were plainly laid before their eyes in their mother-tongue, that they might see the process, order, and meaning of the text". Rendered: "This alone moved me to translate the New Testament. Experience had taught me this. Lay people could not be made firm in any truth unless the Scripture lay plainly before their eyes, in their mother tongue, so that they might see the course, order and meaning of the text." Job: why he did it, in his own words, in beat 2. ("Process" rendered "course": its modern sense misleads.)
- **Q2.** DT 2090–2101 (Stephen Vaughan to Henry VIII, May 1531, reporting Tyndale's words), two spans with a cut: "if it would stand with the king's most gracious pleasure to grant only a bare text of the scripture to be put forth among his people, … be it of the translation of what person soever shall please his majesty, I shall immediately make faithful promise never to write more, … offering my body to suffer what pain or torture, yea, what death his grace will, so that this be obtained." Rendered: "Suppose the king, of his grace, grants his people only the bare text of the Scripture, in the translation of anyone he pleases. Then I will at once make a faithful promise never to write again. … I offer my body to suffer whatever pain or torture, yes, whatever death the king wills, if only this is granted." The first cut leaves out "as among the emperor's subjects here and other Christian princes'" (the narrator may say it); the second leaves out his promise to leave at once and kneel before the king. Plain sense of "bare text": the words of Scripture alone, without his notes or prefaces. Job: what he would die for, in beat 6. Credit the envoy.
- **Q3.** DT 3331–3333 (Foxe, quoted by Walter): "Lord, open the king of England's eyes". Rendered: "Lord, open the king of England's eyes." Job: his death, in beat 7, in Foxe's voice.

**Connection to carry into the story:** What passed from Tyndale was the English New Testament itself, in his words, read by lay people. Until then English Scripture in England was copied by hand, his editor says, and needed a bishop's leave (S3, L1). His was printed abroad in thousands and carried in by merchants faster than bishops could buy and burn it (M8, M9, M12). Those who read it paid for it: a monk whipped and gagged, readers examined, a Wiltshire farmer who could not read, who had others read it to him and knew most of it by heart, and who was burned at Salisbury in 1556, under Queen Mary (I1). Within a year of his death the king allowed an English Bible to be bought and read, and editors find it was mostly Tyndale's work under another name (I2). The listener must understand two things: that the book reached ordinary readers in spite of the burnings, because it was printed and smuggled, not because anyone allowed it; and that the Bible the king licensed carried Tyndale's words without his name, which is the editors' finding, not the archbishop's claim. Tell it through what the readers and the archbishop did, and do not join the 1537 licence to his dying prayer as cause.

**Ending:** (a) His letter to his young friend in the Tower, 1533: he called God to witness that he had never changed a syllable of God's word against his conscience, and would not for all the pleasure, honour or riches on earth (M13; paraphrase, his own words). (b) His words a few lines on: his soul was not faint, though his body was weary (M13; paraphrase). Prefer (a).
