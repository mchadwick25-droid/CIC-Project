# Source sheet: William Tyndale

Researcher's sheet for batch 5 (2026-11-18). The job (Mark's): the English New Testament from the Greek, printed in exile, and his death for it. The writer uses the outline, the shapes and the standard only; this sheet is the evidence behind the outline.

**Files and short names used below** (all in `cic/texts/` unless stated):
- **DT** = `tyndale_doctrinal-treatises_walter1848.txt` (Parker Society, ed. Henry Walter, 1848). Walter's Biographical Notice, 317 to about 3410, is the **editor's** narrative; inside it he prints contemporary documents (Munmouth's petition, Cochlaeus, Spalatin's diary, Vaughan's and Cromwell's letters, Tyndale's letters to Frith, Poyntz's letter) and long passages of **Foxe's** life of Tyndale (from the 1597 edition compared with Day's 1573 folio, DT 411–412). Tyndale's own works follow: the 1525 Cologne Prologue (only in Walter's notes to the Pathway, 3629–3645, 3702–3705), the Pathway (from 3601), Wicked Mammon, Obedience (from 10735), Epistle to the Reader 1526 (25472–25566), Preface to the Five Books of Moses 1530 (25569–25874), the Prologues, including the 1534 preface that opens the Prologue upon Matthew (30035–30068). Walter's spelling is modernised; his footnotes are his.
- **F3, F4, F7, F8** = `foxe_acts-and-monuments-v3/v4/v7/v8_cattley-townsend1837.txt` (John Foxe, *Acts and Monuments*, Cattley ed.). **Foxe's own life of Tyndale (Cattley vol. V, pp. 114–134) is not held** (N1); it is held only as Walter quotes it.
- **CR** = `crespin_histoire-des-martyrs-t1-fra_benoit-lelievre1885.txt` (Jean Crespin's French martyrology, 1619 text; Benoit's notes are the editor's).
- **RE** = `reusch_indices-librorum-prohibitorum-sechzehnten-jahrhunderts-deu-lat_1886.txt` (Reusch prints the English prohibition lists and the Roman Index in their own words).
- **CM** = `cranmer_miscellaneous-writings-and-letters_cox1846.txt` (Cranmer's letters; Cox's notes are the editor's).
- **BR** = `bradford-john_writings-v1_townsend1848.txt`.
- **CEN** = `cic-website/data/world-census.json` (not in `cic/texts/`).

**Conventions.** Line numbers are the file's own. Quotations keep the file's spelling (Walter's modernised English; Foxe's OCR), with runs of spaces collapsed; obvious OCR slips are shown in [brackets]. Nothing else changed.

**Languages.** Tyndale wrote in English; every quotation of him is in his own language (modernised by Walter). Cochlaeus (Latin) and Spalatin's diary (Latin) are held **only in Walter's English** (DT 1066–1146, 1169–1175). Crespin is in French. The index lists are in Latin and English. **Tyndale's 1526 New Testament itself is not held** (N2).

**Witness kinds.** OWN = Tyndale's own words at the time (prologues, prefaces, letters, the Obedience; also his words as reported verbatim by Vaughan in 1531). CONTEMP = others in his lifetime: Munmouth (1528, a prisoner answering charges), Cochlaeus (hostile, an opponent of Luther, writing in 1549 about 1525), Spalatin's diary (August 1526, a friend of Luther recording hearsay), Henry VIII, Tonstal, Warham, Hackett, Rincke, Vaughan, Cromwell, Elyot, More, Frith, Joye, Poyntz (1535). LATER = Foxe (martyrologist, first English edition 1563, about 27 years after the death; sympathetic; drew on Poyntz for the arrest, DT 2859–2861), Hall's chronicle (as quoted by Walter), Crespin (French martyrology; this text 1619), Cranmer 1537, the Marian searchers and martyrs in Foxe (1550s), the 1559 Roman Index. EDITOR = Walter (1848, a warm admirer: his providential and partisan language is his, not the record's), Cattley and Townsend, Cox, Benoit, Anderson (cited by Walter, not held).

## 1. Setting

S1. **Who he was.** Foxe (via Walter): "he was born about the borders of Wales, and brought up from a child in the university of Oxford, where he by long continuance grew and increased as well in the knowledge of tongues and other liberal arts, as specially in the knowledge of the scriptures" (DT 383–388); he read divinity privately to students "lying there in Magdalen hall" (DT 389–391); then went to Cambridge (DT 397–399). LATER (Foxe). Widely Accepted. A priest: Munmouth calls him "a priest called sir William Tyndal, otherwise called Hotchens" (DT 828–829); Walter notes that More called him "sir William", "the usual way of designating a priest" (DT 438–440). CONTEMP. Documented. Birth year: Walter says only "the probability is ... in the year 1484" (DT 377–378), from Anderson. EDITOR. Inferential-Thin; leave out. Plain: "an English priest and scholar, schooled at Oxford and Cambridge".
S2. **Gloucestershire, about 1520–1523.** Tutor in the house of Sir John Walsh (Foxe, DT 400–403); Walter names the place Little Sodbury and dates it no earlier than 1520 (DT 463–469, 939–945). Foxe: at Walsh's table "abbots, deans, archdeacons, with other divers doctors" disputed with him, and "he would shew them in the book, and lay plainly before them the open and manifest places of the scriptures" (DT 471–488). LATER (Foxe). Widely Accepted. Tyndale's own account (1530): the priests met "at the ale-house, which is their preaching-place", said "my sayings are heresy", and "accused me secretly to the chancellor"; "he threatened me grievously, and reviled me, and rated me as though I had been a dog" (DT 25750–25776). OWN. Documented.
S3. **The law against English Scripture.** Archbishop Arundel's constitution (1409) forbade anyone to translate Scripture into English or read such a translation "upon pain of greater excommunication, until the said translation be allowed by the ordinary of the place" (Walter's note quoting Foxe, DT 10830–10841). Walter: "no English translation had been so allowed" (DT 10841–10842, EDITOR). Documented (the constitution); the "never allowed" is Walter's. **Told on the Wyclif story already** (CEN `john-wyclif`): one clause at most here.
S4. **Places.** London (1523–24); Hamburg (1524–25); Cologne (1525); Worms, on the Rhine (1525–26); Marburg in Hesse (1528–30); Antwerp, the trading city in the Netherlands, his main home later (Foxe, DT 932–935); Vilvorde castle, "between Brussels and Mechlin" (DT 2978, 2987). Census region: "England, then Cologne, Worms and Antwerp" (CEN). Plain: "abroad, in the trading cities of Germany and the Low Countries".
S5. **Tradition.** Census: told within **The English Reformation & Elizabethan Settlement** (`toldWithin`), as its forerunner (CEN `relationsSummary`, `why`). Plain spoken forms: "the English Reformation", "the Church of England's reform". Census dates c. 1525–1536.

## 2. Recorded moments

M1. **Why he translated** (his own account, 1530). "Which thing only moved me to translate the new Testament. Because I had perceived by experience, how that it was impossible to establish the lay-people in any truth, except the scripture were plainly laid before their eyes in their mother-tongue, that they might see the process, order, and meaning of the text" (DT 25719–25724). OWN. Documented. The same preface: the clergy "say ... that it is not lawful for the lay-people to have it in their mother-tongue; some, that it would make them all heretics" (DT 25588–25591). **Q1.**
M2. **The ploughboy saying.** Foxe: to a learned divine who said "We were better to be without God's laws than the pope's", Tyndale replied "I defy the pope and all his laws", and "if God spared him life, ere many years he would cause a boy that driveth the plough to know more of the scripture than he did" (DT 625–635). LATER (Foxe), single witness, about 40 years on. Widely Accepted as Foxe's report; the words rest on him alone. A doctor also warned him "if you shall be perceived to be of that opinion, it will cost you your life" (DT 618–622, Foxe).
M3. **London, 1523–24: no room.** He sought the bishop of London's service, bringing "an Oration of Isocrates, which I had translated out of Greek into English" (DT 25786–25788); the bishop said "his house was full" (DT 25820); "I ... understood at the last not only that there was no room in my lord of London's palace to translate the new Testament, but also that there was no place to do it in all England" (DT 25832–25838). OWN (1530). Documented. A cloth merchant, Humphrey Munmouth, kept him half a year: "He studied most part of the day and of the night at his book; and he would eat but sodden meat ... nor drink but small single beer"; Munmouth paid him ten pounds when "he made his exchange to Hamborough" (DT 862–874). CONTEMP (Munmouth's petition from the Tower, 1528). Documented. Walter dates the departure 1524 (DT 903–909, EDITOR; Widely Accepted).
M4. **Did he go to Luther?** The 1528 charges against Munmouth: that Tyndale and Roye "went into Almayne to Luther, there to study and learn his sect" (DT 791–794, Strype's copy via Walter). Foxe: in Saxony "he had conference with Luther" (DT 929–935). Cochlaeus: "Two English apostates, who had been some while at Wittenberg" translated "Luther's New Testament" (DT 1066–1071). Walter: Foxe repeated More, and Tyndale answered More of the "confederacy": "This is not truth" (DT 950–966; Tyndale's *Answer to More* is not held, N3). Crespin: he came to the truth "par la lecture des liures de Martin Luther" (CR 49130–49132). **Contested** (D1).
M5. **Cologne, 1525: the press stopped.** Cochlaeus, an opponent of Luther, wrote (published 1549, DT 1177–1178) that at Cologne printers "boast over their cups"; one, "after they were treated with wine", told him that "three thousand copies of the Lutheran New Testament were in the press, and were already advanced as far as the letter K", paid for by English merchants who would "clandestinely disperse it through all England"; Cochlaeus told a city councillor, Herman Rincke, who had the house searched and got "a prohibition against the printer's proceeding"; "the two English apostates fled, carrying off in haste the quarto sheets already printed, and sailed up the Rhine to Worms" (DT 1096–1134); Rincke and Cochlaeus then wrote to the king, the cardinal and the bishop of Rochester (DT 1134–1146). CONTEMP but hostile and written years later, in Walter's English. Widely Accepted as Cochlaeus's account; Walter rejects its claim that the translation was from Luther's (DT 1148–1157). A surviving fragment of the Cologne quarto (the Prologue and part of Matthew) is described by Anderson via Walter (DT 3495–3563, EDITOR). Its first words: "I have here translated, brethren and sisters, most dear and tenderly beloved in Christ, the New Testament, for your spiritual edifying, consolation, and solace" (DT 3629–3631, OWN, the 1525 Prologue in Walter's note). Documented.
M6. **Worms, 1526: the Testament printed.** Spalatin's diary (August 1526, DT 1234), reporting Busche: "six thousand copies of the new Testament in the English tongue had been printed at Worms; and that this translation had been made by an Englishman, sojourning there with two other natives of Britain, who was so skilled in seven languages, Hebrew, Greek, Latin, Italian, Spanish, English, and Dutch, that whichever he might be speaking, you would think it to be his native tongue" (DT 1169–1175). CONTEMP hearsay, in Walter's English. Documented as Spalatin's entry. Walter: at Worms he printed it "anew in a much smaller form, containing nothing but the inspired text", with a short address to the reader and no name (DT 1199–1205). EDITOR. Widely Accepted. That address: DT 25480–25563 (OWN, 1526). Its lines 25533–25536 ("I had no man to counterfeit") **are already quoted on the Wyclif story; do not use.**
M7. **From the Greek.** (a) His own: in his revised Testament he wrote, "I have looked over again, now at the last, with all diligence, and compared it unto the Greek, and have weeded out of it many faults, which lack of help at the beginning, and oversight, did sow therein" (DT 30040–30044; Walter collated it with the Antwerp Testament of 1534, DT 30009–30012). OWN. Documented; the 1534 date is Widely Accepted (editor's collation). (b) The 1535 edition's title: "The newe Testament, dylygently corrected and compared with the Greeke, by Willyam Tyndale" (DT 3245–3247, title quoted by Walter). Documented. (c) Obedience (1528): "For the Greek tongue agreeth more with the English than with the Latin. And the properties of the Hebrew tongue agreeth a thousand times more with the English than with the Latin" (DT 11830–11853). OWN. Documented. (d) A rival, George Joye, 1535: "for all his high learning in Hebrew, Greek, and Latin" (DT 2712–2714, via Walter). CONTEMP. (e) Cochlaeus says the reverse (M5). **"From the Greek": Widely Accepted** (his own revising words, his contemporaries; one hostile witness against).
M8. **Burned in England, 1526.** Henry VIII, in his reply to Luther (1526): Luther "fell in device with one or two lewd persons, born in this our realm, for the translating of the new Testament into English", and the king had "determined the said untrue translations to be burned, with farther sharp correction and punishment against the keepers and readers of the same" (DT 1221–1252, quoted by Walter). CONTEMP, hostile. Documented. Bishop Tonstal of London, 23 October 1526: "maintainers of Luther's sect ... craftily have translated the New Testament into our English tongue ... of which translation there are many books imprinted, some with glosses, and some without"; all copies to be handed in within thirty days "under pain of excommunication" (F4 51038–51091; also DT 1254–1272). Documented. Archbishop Warham's mandate of 3 November 1526 with its list, "The New Testament of Tindall" (RE 321–332). Documented. Walter: on 11 February 1526 Wolsey and thirty-six bishops and abbots watched "great baskets full of books" burned at St Paul's, and "Tyndale tells us" copies of his version were among them (DT 1213–1219). EDITOR (Tyndale's words for this are not held). Widely Accepted.
M9. **Bought up, smuggled, reprinted.** Hackett, the king's agent, found Antwerp burgesses would not treat the English Testaments as heretical; Warham bought up copies at a cost of £66 9s 4d (DT 1294–1310, Walter from letters). CONTEMP via editor. Widely Accepted. Rincke, 1528: had he not bribed Frankfurt's magistrates, the books "would have been pressed together in paper packages, and inclosed in ten sacks craftily covered over with flax" and sent to Scotland and England (DT 1396–1401). CONTEMP, hostile. Documented. Hackett, 1527: Scottish merchants took copies "a part to Edinburgh, and most part to the town of St Andrew's" (DT 1444–1459). CONTEMP. Documented. Foxe's tale of the bishop buying the books through a merchant, Packington ("the bishop of London had the books, Packington had the thanks, and Tyndale had the money"; then "they came thick and threefold over into England") (F4 51380–51410; also DT 1508–1558, with Walter's caution that it is "the current tale", DT 1511–1512). LATER. Widely Accepted as a tale; card only.
M10. **The Five Books of Moses from the Hebrew, 1530.** Genesis printed "at Marlborow in the land of Hesse by me Hans Luft ... MDXXX., the xvii. dayes of Januarii" (DT 1665–1676, colophon quoted by Walter; also 25603–25605). Documented. Foxe: sailing to Hamburg to print Deuteronomy "he suffered shipwreck, by which he lost all his books, writings, and copies, and so was compelled to begin all anew"; Coverdale helped him at Hamburg (DT 1590–1604). LATER (Foxe, from Coverdale's memory, Walter says; Walter moves Foxe's 1529 to 1530, DT 1606–1612). Widely Accepted; date Contested.
M11. **The king's envoy, Antwerp, April and May 1531.** Stephen Vaughan to Henry VIII: Tyndale sent a messenger, led him "without the gates of Antwerp, into a field"; "'Do you not know me?' said this Tyndale. 'I do not well remember you,' said I to him. 'My name,' said he, 'is Tyndale'" (DT 1786–1801). Tyndale spoke of "mine exile out of my natural country, and bitter absence from my friends ... my hunger, my thirst, my cold, the great danger wherewith I am everywhere compassed" (DT 1823–1825), and asked whether a Christian prince could deny "the people to have the same in a tongue that they understand" (DT 1841–1843). He "neither would nor durst come into England", fearing "promises made with heretics ought not to be kept" (DT 1859–1864). CONTEMP (Vaughan's report, "word for word, as near as I could", DT 1880–1881). Documented. Cromwell, for the king, ordered Vaughan to stop: Tyndale's book was "fyllyd w[ith] scedycyous, slanderous lyes" and the king was "veray joyous to have his realme destytute of such a p[er]son" (DT 1950–1990). CONTEMP. Documented. Vaughan, May 1531, after showing Tyndale a softer clause: "water stood in his eyes", and he answered: "if it would stand with the king's most gracious pleasure to grant only a bare text of the scripture to be put forth among his people, like as is put forth among the subjects of the emperor in these parts, and of other Christian princes, be it of the translation of what person soever shall please his majesty, I shall immediately make faithful promise never to write more, nor abide two days in these parts after the same; but immediately repair into his realm, and there most humbly submit myself at the feet of his royal majesty, offering my body to suffer what pain or torture, yea, what death his grace will, so that this be obtained" (DT 2087–2101). CONTEMP (Vaughan reporting Tyndale's words). Documented as Vaughan's report; Walter thinks Vaughan may have added the praise of the king that follows (DT 2136–2140, EDITOR). **Q2.** John Frith repeated the same offer in print from the Tower in 1533: "Grant that the word of God ... may go abroad in our English tongue, as other nations have it in their tongues; and my brother William Tyndale and I have done, and will promise you to write no more" (DT 2462–2470, via Walter from Day's Frith). CONTEMP. Documented.
M12. **Hunted.** Thomas Elyot, envoy, 14 March 1532: the king wills him to stay at Brussels "for the apprehension of Tyndale ... like as he is in wit moveable, semblably so is his person uncertain to come by" (DT 2189–2200). CONTEMP. Documented. More, 1532, on the books from abroad: "yet cease they not, with money sent from hence, to print them there, and send them hither by whole vats full at once; and in some places, looking for no lucre, cast them abroad by night" (DT 2244–2249). CONTEMP, hostile (More's own words). Documented.
M13. **To Frith in the Tower, 1533.** "I call God to record against the day we shall appear before our Lord Jesus, to give a reckoning of our doings, that I never altered one syllable of God's word against my conscience, nor would this day, if all that is in the earth, whether it be pleasure, honour, or riches, might be given me" (DT 2378–2383). OWN; Frith quoted it in print (DT 2446–2451). Documented. And: "my soul is not faint, though my body be weary. But God hath made me evil-favoured in this world, and without grace in the sight of men, speechless and rude, dull and slow-witted" (DT 2390–2393). OWN. Documented. Frith was burned soon after (Walter, DT 2629–2632, EDITOR; Widely Accepted).
M14. **Antwerp life.** Foxe: two "days of pastime", Monday and Saturday, spent seeking out and relieving the poor and English refugees; on Sundays he read Scripture to merchants in a merchant's chamber (DT 2641–2678). LATER (Foxe). Widely Accepted as Foxe's report. Card only.
M15. **The 1534 revision and its protest.** "I beseech all men to read it for that purpose I wrote it, even to bring them to the knowledge of the scripture; and as far as the scripture approveth it, so far to allow it; and if in any place the word of God disallow it, there to refuse it" (DT 2802–2811, 1534 preface). OWN. Documented. "Caring for the wealth of the realm I was born in, for the king and all that are thereof, as a tender-hearted mother would do for her only son" (DT 2797–2800). OWN. Documented. Walter: four new Antwerp editions in 1534 (DT 2680–2682, EDITOR from Anderson); a printer had George Joye alter the text without him (DT 2684–2694, EDITOR). Widely Accepted.
M16. **Betrayal, Antwerp, 1535.** Foxe, from the merchant Thomas Poyntz who lodged him (DT 2859–2861): Henry Phillips, "a comely fellow, like as he had been a gentleman", befriended him and lodged in the same house (DT 2863–2878); he borrowed forty shillings from him (DT 2948–2951); in "a long narrow entry, so that two could not go in a front", Phillips put Tyndale in front, "a man of no great stature", and "pointed with his finger over master Tyndale's head down to him, that the officers ... might see that it was he whom they should take"; the officers "pitied to see his simplicity when they took him"; he was taken "to the castle of Vilford, eighteen English miles from Antwerp; and there he remained until he was put to death" (DT 2958–2980). LATER (Foxe, from Poyntz, an eyewitness to the house). Widely Accepted. Who sent Phillips is not settled: Poyntz wrote it "was done by procurement out of England, and, as I suppose, unknown to the king's grace" (DT 2996–2998); Walter, from Anderson, clears the king and More (DT 3063–3078, EDITOR); Crespin: "comme aucuns pensent", Phillips was "incité par les Euesques" (CR 49257–49261). Contested (D4).
M17. **In prison.** Poyntz to his brother, 25 August 1535: Tyndale "is in prison, and like to suffer death, except it be through his gracious help"; "the king has never a truer-hearted subject"; "The death of this man would be a great hinderance to the gospel"; two Englishmen at Louvain were translating his writings "out of English into Latin ... so that the clergy here may understand it and condemn him" (DT 2992–3027). CONTEMP. Documented. Poyntz, carrying letters from England for his release, was himself arrested, examined "upon not so few as an hundred articles", and escaped by night (DT 3143–3205, Foxe). LATER (Foxe). Widely Accepted. Foxe: he refused an advocate, "saying, that he would answer for himself; and so he did" (DT 3303–3310). Widely Accepted. Foxe also says he converted his keeper and the keeper's daughter (DT 3210–3214): a wonder-adjacent claim; card only. Crespin: held "vn an en prison" (CR 49265–49266).
M18. **Death, 1536.** Foxe: "he was condemned by virtue of the emperor's decree, made in the assembly at Augsburgh; and upon the same brought forth to the place of execution; was there tied to the stake; and then strangled first by the hangman, and afterward with fire consumed, in the morning [of October 6th], at the town of Vilvorden, in the year 1536; crying thus at the stake with a fervent zeal and a loud voice, 'Lord, open the king of England's eyes'" (DT 3320–3333; the date is from Foxe's calendar, Walter's note 3365). LATER (Foxe). Widely Accepted; the last words rest on Foxe alone. **Q3.** Crespin: sentence of death given at Brussels, and taken to Vilvorde "pour y estre bruslé: où il mourut constamment" (CR 49268–49273). LATER. Foxe and Crespin: it was said the emperor's prosecutor called him "a learned, a good, and a godly man" (DT 3311–3313; CR 49274–49277 "On dit"). Hearsay; credit it as "it was said".

## 3. Impact, heaviest first

I1. **Read by lay people, at the risk of their lives.** (a) Thomas Bilney, going to his death in 1531, gave an anchoress in Norwich "a New Testament of Tyndale's translation, and the Obedience of a Christian Man" (F4 49206–49211). (b) "John Tewkesbury was converted by the reading of Tyndale's Testament, and the 'Wicked Mammon'" (F4 52805–52806). (c) Richard Bayfield, a monk, was given "Tyndale's Testament in English", was "sore whipped, with a gag in his mouth, and then stocked", fled abroad and sold Tyndale's works (F4 52211–52227); he was burned in 1531, condemned partly for spreading Tyndale's *Answer to More* (DT 2146–2152, Walter). (d) James Bainham, examined December 1531, had "the New Testament ... translated into the English tongue by Tyndale within this month", thought "he offended not God in using and keeping the same", and "as concerning the New Testament in English, he thought it utterly good, and that the people should have it as it is" (F4 53741–53767). (e) John Maundrel, a Wiltshire farm worker burned at Salisbury in 1556: "after that the Scripture was translated into English by the faithful apostle of England, William Tyndale, became a diligent hearer ... never being without the New Testament about him, although he could not read himself. But when he came into any company that could read, his book was always ready, having a very good memory, so that he could recite by heart most places of the New Testament" (F8 8616–8634). (f) Lancashire, Mary's reign: a priest searching a house took up the book, "'Forsooth,' saith he, 'a Testament of Tyndale's translation, plain heresy, and none worse than it'" (F8 45697–45701). (g) John Bradford, burned 1555, wrote meditations in his own copy of "the New Testament of Tyndale, Jugge 1548", later rebound in his memory (BR 13305–13330, editor's description of the autograph). All LATER (Foxe's accounts; Bradford's book via its editor). Documented as Foxe's accounts. **Heaviest: this is what later witnesses carried and used.** Maundrel is the clearest single scene.
I2. **The king's turn, 1530–1537.** Foxe (following Hall): in 1530 the king prohibited the translations of Tyndale and Joye but commanded the bishops to have "a new translation to be made, so that the people might not be ignorant in the law of God"; the bishops did nothing, "which caused the people much to study Tyndale's translation" (F4 53455–53485). LATER. Widely Accepted. Cranmer to Cromwell, 4 August 1537, sending "a bible in English, both of a new translation and of a new print": "I like it better than any other translation heretofore made"; he asks a licence "that the same may be sold and read of every person ... until such time that we the bishops shall set forth a better translation, which I think will not be till a day after doomsday" (CM 32932–32960). CONTEMP. Documented. Its title names "Thomas Matthew" (CM 32971–32977, editor's note). **That this Bible was largely Tyndale's work** (his Pentateuch, his New Testament, and his unpublished translation of the historical books to 2 Chronicles, completed from Coverdale) rests on editors: Walter (DT 3284–3297, 3346–3352) and the Foxe editor's note "This was William Tyndale's translation, published at Hamburgh under the name of 'Thomas Matthewes'" (F8 38993–38994). EDITOR. Widely Accepted. Cranmer to Cromwell, 13 August 1537: the king has allowed it "to be bought and read within this realm" (DT 3380–3384; CM 33055–33060). Documented. Walter: before the end of 1536 the king's printer issued Tyndale's New Testament "with the long-proscribed name of William Tyndale openly set forth on its title-page" (DT 3338–3344, EDITOR; also CEN `legacy`). Widely Accepted.
I3. **Condemned by name abroad.** The Roman Index of Paul IV (1559) lists "Tindalus" among its authors (RE 11168–11189, 11961). Documented. An English provincial decree (1530–38, Reusch's dating) names "translatio Novi Testamenti in lingua Anglicana a Willelmo Hychyns, alias Tyndaly corrupta" (RE 809–831). Documented.
I4. **Named an apostle.** Foxe: "who, for his notable pains and travail, may be worthily called an apostle of England" (DT 3407–3409, Foxe quoted by Walter); elsewhere "the true apostle of these our later days" (F3 56208). LATER. Documented as Foxe's words. William Penn (1682): "that worthy apostle of the English reformation" (`penn_no-cross-no-crown_harvey-darton1842.txt` 5319). LATER. Card only.
I5. **What he defended.** That the people should have Scripture "in a tongue that they understand" (M11), and the text alone if need be, in anyone's translation (Q2); that readers should test his own work by Scripture and refuse it where Scripture disallows it (M15).
I6. **Debt to Luther.** Walter: the Prologue to Romans is "for the greater part ... a paraphrase, and sometimes a literal translation, of Luther's preface to the Romans" (DT 30815–30817, also 204–206). EDITOR. Widely Accepted. Carried on the census Wittenberg link (CEN edge), not this story's job (see outline).

## 4. Links

L1. **John Wyclif** (CEN `john-wyclif`): his circle's English Bible, copied by hand from the Latin; his live story already tells Tyndale taking up the work from the Greek in 1526 and quotes the 1526 Epistle. Walter: Wyclif's version "had never been printed" and was translated "from the Latin Vulgate" (DT 673–683, EDITOR). One reader in 1528 had "the gospel of Matthew and Mark in English, and certain of Paul's epistles after the old translation" (DT 1046–1051, Walter from Anderson): Tyndale's and the old Wycliffite text side by side. Link to `lollardy`.
L2. **Martin Luther** (sibling, batch 5): Worms, where Tyndale printed in 1526, is where Luther stood before the emperor in 1521 (Luther outline). His enemies called the Testament Luther's (M5, M8). Contested whether they met (D1).
L3. **The English Reformation** (told within): he stands before it as forerunner (CEN `why`); the Bible licensed in 1537 (I2).

## 5. Where sources disagree or come late

D1. **Wittenberg and Luther.** The 1528 charges, Cochlaeus and Foxe say he went to Luther; Walter says Tyndale denied a "confederacy" (Answer to More, not held) and nothing of his own shows he met Luther. Contested. Do not say he studied at Wittenberg or met Luther.
D2. **How many copies.** Cochlaeus: 3,000 at Cologne, interrupted (DT 1095, 1107–1108). Spalatin's informant: 6,000 printed at Worms (DT 1169–1170). Hearsay both. Say "thousands" or credit each.
D3. **How he died.** Foxe: strangled, then burned (DT 3328–3330). Crespin: taken to Vilvorde "to be burned" (CR 49270–49271). The date, 6 October, is from Foxe's calendar (DT 3365). Say "strangled at the stake and his body burned", credited to Foxe; the day as "early October", or "6 October, by Foxe's calendar".
D4. **Who betrayed him, for whom.** Phillips acted (Foxe from Poyntz). Who paid is unknown: Poyntz blamed "procurement out of England, ... unknown to the king's grace"; Crespin, some said the bishops; Walter clears king and More. Contested. Do not name a sponsor.
D5. **The ploughboy and the last words** rest on Foxe alone, decades later (M2, M18). Tell each in Foxe's voice.
D6. **Shipwreck and Hamburg** (M10): Foxe from Coverdale's memory; Walter redates. Leave out.
D7. **Vaughan's report** (M11): Vaughan wrote to please a king; Walter suspects added flattery, not the offer itself, which Frith repeated (M11). Credit Vaughan.
