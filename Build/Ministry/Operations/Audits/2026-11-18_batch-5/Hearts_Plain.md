# The hearts in plain words (for Mark, after his note "there are a lot of old church words, they need an explanation", 2026-11-18)

Each researcher added a "Words to explain" list to its outline, and rewrote the essence and also told in plain words.

## Nestorius
Nestorius was bishop of Constantinople, the empire's capital, from 428. He taught that Christ is God and man, and that the two stay distinct in one Christ who is worshipped as one. So he would not simply call Mary "Mother of God", the one who gave birth to God, and offered "Mother of Christ" instead. Cyril of Alexandria and a council of bishops at Ephesus in 431 judged that he split Christ in two and removed him from office. He denied it and died in exile.
Also told: his teaching in his own image; how he lost his post (Socrates's account of his hunts for "heretics", the quarrel over Mary's name, the council); his late defense under a borrowed name, willing to be cut off under the church's curse; and how the Church of the East, the church of Persia, kept his book in Syriac and named him in its prayers, while saying its faith was not his invention.
Words: see → post as bishop; council: bishops meeting to settle teaching; deposed → removed from office; heretics: people judged to teach falsely; Mother of God: the one who gave birth to God; natures: what Christ is as God, and as man; anathema: cut off under the church's curse; Syriac: the Church of the East's Aramaic church language; catholicos → head of the Church of the East; Doctors → great teachers; Leo → bishop of Rome, not "pope".

## Athanasius of Alexandria
For 45 years Athanasius was bishop, the church's leader, in Alexandria. He held to the creed of Nicaea, the 325 statement of faith that the Son is "of one being" with the Father, fully God, against Arius's party, who taught that the Son had a beginning. Five times he had to leave his city. He wrote the Life of Antony, an Egyptian hermit's story, for monks abroad. In 367 his yearly Easter letter listed the books of Scripture, with 27 for the New Testament.
Also told: a council of bishops removed him and soldiers came for him, with force on both sides; he would treat as brothers those who doubted only the one word; the Life went into Latin and Syriac; later church law and a 1921 Church of England proposal kept his list.
Added (Mark, 2026-11-18): his teaching, in the essence. He held to "of one being" because God's Son became human so that people might be made divine, joined to God and freed from death, which could happen, he wrote, only if the Son were truly God (Documented, On the Incarnation 54 and Against the Arians II.69 to 70; the early line "was made man that we might be made God" dated about 318 by the editor). The story ties the teaching to the word, as his own lines do, not to the exiles. Optional clause: the emperor Constantius's hostile letter says Athanasius made Frumentius bishop of Aksum (Documented; Socrates agrees). Length about 3:15.
Words: bishop (here, over all Egypt's churches), deacon, priest, creed, Nicaea, "of one being", Arius and the Arians, council of bishops, removed from office, exile, communion, Melitians, vigil, bishop's chair, psalm, monk and hermit, woman vowed to God, Easter letter, Scripture, canon (a list of books; in 692, "rulings"), apocryphal, heretics, the Fathers, Syriac, Church of England and Orthodox.

## Barsauma of Nisibis
Barsauma, bishop of Nisibis on the border between Rome and Persia, led a synod, a council of bishops, in 484 against the catholicos, the head of his church. He later took it back in writing. Yet his side's aims became law: clergy free to marry (486, 497), and the Greek Bible teacher Theodore of Mopsuestia as the sure guide (605). The Armenian and Syriac Orthodox churches, his opponents, named him as the one who set the Persian church apart.
Also told: his frontier letters (drought, raids, a Roman general); his call to excommunicate, cut off from the church, his own rebels; the catholicos Babowai's death, his part disputed; the 486 synod: Christ fully God and fully human, wandering holy men sent back to monasteries, clergy free to marry; Narsai; the quarrel of 491.
Added (Mark, 2026-11-18): his marriage, all Contested and from late witnesses, after the catholicos's death and beside Beth Lapat's marriage rule. Mari (his own church, 1100s): he married the nun Mammaea, at the Persian king's order. Bar Hebraeus (a rival church, 1200s, hostile): he kept a "concubine" in his cell and called her his wife. Michael the Syrian's chronicle charges that he made the marriage rule so he would not be blamed for her, credited to it alone. No held source dates the marriage. Length now about 3:00.
Words: synod; catholicos or patriarch, head of the church; see, the catholicos's office; clergy; deacon, a minister below a priest; canons, church rules; two natures; ascetics, wandering holy men in black; marzban, the Persian governor; Mar, "my lord"; the Fathers; ordained; Syriac Orthodox; the East, the Persian lands.

## Babai the Great
For close to twenty years the Persian king would not let the church have a catholicos, a head of the whole church (Documented; the length Contested). Babai, abbot, the head of the monastery of Izla, and not a bishop, toured the monasteries for the senior bishops, driving out teachers of condemned beliefs (Documented, Thomas of Marga). His Book of the Union holds that Christ is one person, fully God and fully human; the church honored his books (Documented). Historians judge that it settled the church's teaching (Dominant Modern Reconstruction).
Also told: monks with wives were stripped of the robe and driven out, and their huts burned; a gentle monk, Jacob, expelled; followers of Henana refused communion, the shared bread and wine, until they cursed him; the bishops before the king in 612; the monk George crucified; Babai refusing to head the church in 628.
Words: catholicos or patriarch, head of the church; metropolitan, senior bishop; abbot; cell, hut; the habit, the monk's robe; anathema, a solemn curse cutting someone off; communion; heretic; Messalians, condemned monks who said prayer alone was enough; natures; union, how God and man are joined; synod; Magian, the Persian state religion.

## Timothy I of Baghdad
For more than forty years from 780, Timothy was head of the Church of the East, the church of the Christians of Mesopotamia and Persia. He led it from Baghdad, under five caliphs, the Muslim rulers of the empire (Documented). A later list of his writings names a debate with the caliph al-Mahdi, and a later historian says the next caliph sent for him most days to talk about religion (Documented as their accounts; the debate's text is not held). He sent monks out as bishops to peoples south of the Caspian Sea, and let them make more bishops where they were, without the usual three bishops present or a journey to him (Documented, Thomas of Marga). His letters name a church reaching toward the Turks and China.
Also told: he won office by a trick, as his church's later histories tell it. His rivals refused to worship or work with him for about two years, and Christians fought, killed and jailed one another (Mari's account; the two years his own words). He made a rule for choosing the head of the church, so an election could not split it again (his own letter). He went before the caliph six times for leave to rebuild churches that had been pulled down (his own letter). A chief bishop he had removed accused him before the caliph, and failed.
Words: Church of the East; patriarch or catholicos; caliph (in his own letter, "the king"); bishop; metropolitan, chief bishop of a province; ordain, make a bishop, which then needed three bishops; synod; out of communion, refused to worship or work with him; removed from office; Book of the Gospels; "stone" for stele; khagan, the ruler of the Turks; Beth Sinaye, which scholars take to be China.

## Cyril of Alexandria
Cyril was bishop of Alexandria, head of the church in Egypt's great city, from 412 to 444. He taught that Christ is one: God's own Son, whom the Bible calls the Word, was born of Mary as a man, so she is "Mother of God", the one who gave birth to God. He led the attack that had a council of bishops at Ephesus remove Nestorius, bishop of Constantinople, from office in 431. His twelve statements of faith and his phrase "one incarnate nature of God the Word" (one Christ, God become man, never divided) became the test of faith for the churches that refused the council of 451. That council said Christ is "in two natures", fully God and fully human, and it too took his letters as a guide.
Also told: his teaching in his own words and his image of Moses's bush; his force: the Novatian churches (a strict Christian group outside the bishop's church) shut, the Jews driven out, Hypatia killed by a mob led by one of his church's readers; costly gifts to the emperor's court, from his deputy's own letter; later bishops who held to his words.
Words: bishop; patriarch; archdeacon; reader; prefect, the emperor's governor; Novatians; the Word, God's Son; incarnate, become human; natures; removed from office; anathema; martyr; diptychs, names read at the altar; "his post as bishop" for see.

## Martin Luther (job re-set by Mark, 2026-11-18)
Luther, a friar (a member of a religious order under vows), taught that forgiveness is God's free gift, received by trusting his promise, not bought (Documented: his 1517 theses, his 1518 answer to a cardinal, his 1520 treatise). So he protested against the sale of indulgences, bought pardons said to cancel punishment for sin (Documented). At Worms in 1521 he would not take back his books (Documented; "Here I stand" Contested). Outlawed and hidden, he put the New Testament into German (Documented).
Also told: the break of 1520, when he burned the popes' law book (Documented, his own words) and the pope's decree with it (Widely Accepted, the editors only). The whole Bible in German by 1534 (Widely Accepted). The hymns his churches kept (Documented). 1525: his call to both sides, then his tract against the peasants (Documented); his friends' offence rests on an editor quoting a letter of the time. His marriage to Katharina von Bora (Documented); the catechism in one clause. His late writings against the Jews go on the card.
About 3:45 (about 560 words; Mark's exception, up to 4). Beats: grace and the protest, 1517 to 1518; the break of 1520; Worms; the hidden year and the New Testament; the Peasants' War; the marriage; hymns, with the catechism. Not held: his own later account of finding the teaching (the "tower"), any count of the peasant dead; the 1525 tract only in English.

## William Tyndale
Tyndale, a priest, put the New Testament (the gospels and letters, first written in Greek) into English from the Greek, when the church's Bible was Latin and an English one needed a bishop's leave. He printed it at Worms in 1526 and smuggled it home. There London's bishop had copies handed in and the king had them burned. Betrayed in 1535, he was strangled at a post and burned in 1536.
Also told: lay people, everyone who was not clergy, needed the Bible in their own tongue; he offered his life if the king would allow the bare text, the words without notes, in anyone's translation; readers were burned as heretics.
Added (Mark, 2026-11-18): why the king turned against him, a new beat, "the king's matters, 1528 to 1530". His book on obedience said God makes the king judge over all, answerable to God alone (Documented, his own words; the 1528 date the editor's); the king praised it as a book "for me, and all kings to read" (Foxe's story through a later historian, Widely Accepted). His Practice of Prelates (not held) angered the king most, Tyndale told the envoy, though he had only warned him against his clergy and Wolsey (Documented, the envoy's report); it was banned in London in 1531 (Documented). That the divorce caused it is only the 1848 editor's view, one clause at most. The king called his answer to More "seditious, slanderous lies" (Documented). Length about 3:20 (500 words).
Words: priest; clergy and lay people; "the Bible" for Scripture; New Testament; from the Greek, not from the church's Latin; first five books of the Bible, from the Hebrew; bishop; archbishop of Canterbury; heresy, teaching condemned as false and punishable by burning; excommunication; Luther's sect, the bishop's hostile word; prologue; Foxe, collector of the stories of Protestants put to death; the emperor; stake; bare text.

## John Calvin
Calvin was a French scholar in exile. He wrote the Institutes, a book of instruction in the Christian faith, to defend the Protestants being burned in France. Passing through Geneva in 1536, he was held there. He gave the city a written plan for its church: pastors to preach, teachers, deacons for the poor and sick, and elders, laymen who watched over people's lives. Elders and pastors met weekly as the consistory, a church court. Churches in France and Scotland built on that plan, and his name became the label for the family.
Also told: the Institutes; the catechism, a question-and-answer book, and the church discipline, correcting members, with the fight until 1555 over barring people from communion, the shared bread and wine; the force, Servetus among it, who denied that God is three persons in one; the French national assembly's statement of belief and church rulebook, and Scotland following the church of Geneva.
Added (Mark, 2026-11-18): the book came to teach predestination, God choosing beforehand who will be saved, which brought bitter quarrels (Documented), shown in his own words. Bolsec, in 1551, called that God a tyrant, and was jailed and banished for life; Bern's pastors said he was "not so bad a man" (Documented, but Geneva's register and sentence, one side of the record). Castellio is left out: his held lines are about the Song of Songs, not predestination. The Latin of the 1559 Institutes is not held; the quote is rendered from Beveridge's English. Length about 3:15 to 3:30.
Words: Institutes; Protestant and Reformed; Mass; pastor; teacher (not "doctor"); elder; deacon; consistory; Ordinances; catechism; discipline; communion; excommunication; synod; confession; Kirk; Trinity; blasphemy; academy.

## John Wesley
Wesley, a priest, an ordained minister of England's state church, felt in May 1738 that he trusted Christ alone and was forgiven. From 1739 he preached outdoors to thousands. He gathered converts into "societies", voluntary prayer groups inside the church, and weekly "classes" of about twelve under a leader. These became the Methodist churches.
Also told, in plain senses: the bishop who told him to leave; preachers who were not ministers; God's coming judgment; his preachers' yearly meeting.
Words: priest and ordained; Church of England; Fellow; parish; bishop and diocese; Methodist, a nickname at first; society and class, both unlike today's sense; lay preacher; Moravians; Conference; superintendent; sacrament; "wrath to come"; salvation and assurance; revival.

## Augustine of Hippo (revised, Mark, 2026-11-18)
Augustine taught rhetoric, the art of persuasive public speaking, and, in his own words, sold the skill of talking to win. In a garden in Milan, by his own account, a child's voice sent him to the Bible, and he turned to Christ. As bishop, the church's leader, of Hippo in North Africa he wrote the Confessions and the City of God. He taught that grace, God's free and unearned gift, comes first: no one even begins to believe unless God gives it (Documented). The Western churches carried it on, from the bishops at Orange in 529 to Luther, Calvin and Jansen (each witness Documented; that Orange's rulings came from his writings is the editors' finding, Widely Accepted).
Also told: the garden, "Take it and read", Paul's letters, and telling his mother Monica (Documented, his own account). The forced ordination at Hippo. The two books. Force against the Donatists, a rival African church: in a letter of about 408 he says he first held that no one should be forced into unity, then backed the emperors' laws (Documented, his own words). Grace against Pelagius. Length about 3:30 (about 520 words).
Words: rhetoric; bishop; priest, a minister under the bishop; Manichaeans; baptized; grace; free will; Confessions, praise and admitting sin; the apostle, Paul; pagans; Donatists; Pelagians; council; heretics; Catholic; Jansenists; Goths and Vandals.

## Patrick
Patrick, a Briton from a Roman family, was taken to Ireland as a slave at about sixteen and escaped after six years (Documented). Years later he went back of his own will as a bishop, a senior church leader who could appoint ministers (Documented). Nearly all we know comes from his two Latin writings: the Confession, his life-story and thanks to God, and a letter against Coroticus's soldiers (Widely Accepted).
Also told: he says he baptized "many thousands" and saw chiefs' children become monks and women vowed to stay unmarried (his claim); he was chained by kings, and senior churchmen in Britain opposed him (Documented); soldiers killed new Christians still in their white baptism clothes (Documented); Armagh, an Irish church, copied his writings (Documented); later legend credited as late.
Words: bishop; deacon, an assistant minister, who could marry then; priest; baptism, the washing with water that made one a Christian; monks; virgins of Christ, women vowed to stay unmarried; elders, senior churchmen; heathen, worshippers of other gods; decurio, a Roman town-council member; Picts; Franks; Armagh; slave in Christ.
