# Outline check: Nestorius

## My own heart (written before reading Outline.md)

From the census entry (`why`, `legacy`, `floorNote`, `relationsSummary`, `voices`) and the source sheet.

**Essence.** Nestorius, a famous preacher from Antioch, was made bishop of Constantinople in 428. Within three years his refusal to call Mary "Mother of God" (Theotokos), and his teaching that Christ's divine and human natures stay distinct, set him against Cyril of Alexandria; the council at Ephesus in 431 deposed him, and he died in exile in Egypt, about 451. He is on the tree because the church that came to bear his name, the Church of the East, kept his own defense when the empire burned his books, and honoured him as one of its three teachers, while saying its faith was not his invention.

**Also told.**
- A zealous, harsh start: the first sermon ("Give me the earth purged of heretics"), the Arian chapel the Arians themselves set on fire, the pressure on other groups (Socrates, contemporary, hostile on this point).
- The quarrel over Mary's title told two ways: Socrates (he backed his priest Anastasius and rejected the word outright) against his own account (he found two factions and offered "Mother of Christ" as a word for agreement).
- What he taught, in his own words: two natures, one Christ, one worship ("I separate the natures, but I unite the worship"); Cyril's charge that he split Christ in two; his denial; Socrates' verdict that he did not make Christ a mere man but was "scared" of the word. The question is still argued (Contested).
- Deposition at Ephesus (told by the sibling), return to his monastery near Antioch, the edict of 435 ordering his writings burned, banishment to the Egyptian desert; raiders, forced marches, his letters of complaint (kept by a hostile later historian).
- The late book written in exile under another man's name (the Bazaar of Heracleides), so that readers would not be put off by his own; "let Nestorius be anathema" so long as God is rightly praised; praise for Leo of Rome; the closing farewell to the desert.
- What came of him: the Syriac translation (date uncertain) and one damaged copy at Kotchanes, harmed in 1843, noticed by Western scholars in the 1890s and printed in 1910; his name in the Church of the East's liturgy and rites, one of the "three Doctors"; that church's own insistence that it was "unjustly styled Nestorian"; in the empire, condemned and cursed.
- Death: when and how unknown; about 451 is a modern reckoning.

Shape I would expect: Teacher or thinker (or Martyr, as the Church of the East remembers him); the story's point lies in the book that survived and the church that kept it.
