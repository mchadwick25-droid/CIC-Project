A book about Christ came down to us under the name of Heracleides, a man who lived near Damascus. Its translator, who put it into Syriac, the Aramaic language of a church in Persia, explains why: "The writer chose to give the book this man's name. He feared that his own name, which many hated, would stop them from reading it and turning to the truth." The writer was Nestorius.

From 428 Nestorius was bishop, the leader of a city's churches, in Constantinople, the capital of the Roman empire. He taught that Christ is both God and man, and that the two stay distinct, joined in one Christ whom Christians worship as one. So he would not simply call Mary "Mother of God", the one who gave birth to God, and offered "Mother of Christ" instead. In 431 Cyril, bishop of Alexandria, and a council at Ephesus, a meeting of bishops called to settle teaching, judged that he split Christ in two and removed him from office. He denied it, and he died in exile, forced to live far away under guard. Historians still argue over what he really taught.

Socrates, a historian of the city writing in his lifetime, says the court wanted no one from among its quarreling clergy, the city's priests and deacons, so it sent for a stranger from Antioch, a native of Germanicia prized for his voice. In his first sermon, Socrates says, Nestorius asked the emperor for "the earth purged of heretics", people the church judged to teach falsely, and promised heaven in return. Within days he set out to pull down a chapel of the Arians, Christians who followed Arius, who taught that the Son of God was made and not eternal. They set fire to it themselves, and he went on to take churches from rival Christian groups.

Many already called Mary "Theotokos", the Greek for "Mother of God". Socrates says a priest Nestorius had brought from Antioch preached against the name, and that Nestorius backed him and rejected it. In his last book Nestorius tells it differently. He found two parties at his door, one saying "Mother of God", the other "Mother of man", though both believed the same, so he offered "Mother of Christ", a title taken from the Gospel's own words that both could share.

In a sermon kept by those who wrote against him, he said that a created being cannot give birth to her own Creator; Mary gave birth to a man, the instrument of God. And he said: "God is not separated from the one who is seen. So I do not separate the honour of the one who is not separated. I separate the natures, but I unite the worship." By the natures he meant what Christ is as God and what he is as man. The Son of God and the man born of Mary stay two, yet Christians bow in worship to one Christ. As God, he later wrote, Christ cannot suffer or die; as man, he does. His opponents heard two Christs. Socrates, who blames his temper, read his sermons and says he did not deny that Christ was God, but feared the word "as though it were some terrible phantom".

At Ephesus a council led by Cyril judged his sermons blasphemous, insulting to God, and removed him from office, while his friends met apart and removed Cyril. He was sent back to his monastery near Antioch, a house where monks, men vowed to prayer, live together.

In 435 the emperor's law ordered his writings burned, and the next year he was banished to the Oasis in southern Egypt. When desert raiders sacked it and took him captive, then let him go, he went to Panopolis and showed himself in public, writing to the governor so that no one could say he had fled. Soldiers then dragged him toward Elephantine and back, old and sick, with "a mangled hand and side". We have his letters because Evagrius kept them, a historian a century and a half later who saw his sufferings as God's punishment.

Near the end of his life, perhaps in 451, he wrote his defense in Greek, under Heracleides's name. Its translator says he set the book as a judge between himself and Cyril, each side speaking in its own defense. Nestorius says he did not write it for his own sake. He praises Leo, bishop of Rome, as a preacher who taught the truth without fear, though he says Leo agreed to his condemnation without hearing him. And he offers his own name to the curse: "So let Nestorius be anathema. Only let them say of God what I beg them to say." To be anathema is to be cut off from the church under its solemn curse.

His Greek is lost. The book survives because the Church of the East, the church of Persia beyond the Roman empire, put it into Syriac, perhaps in the sixth century. One damaged copy lasted, in the library of its patriarch, the head bishop over that whole church, in Kurdistan. Western scholars noticed it in the 1890s, and it was printed in 1910.

That church named him in its prayers. Narsai, a teacher at Nisibis writing within about two generations, says the priest prays "imitating Mar Nestorius", Mar being a Syriac title, "my lord", given to saints and bishops. Its rites, the church's set services, call him "the persecuted martyr", one who suffered for the faith, though no source says he was put to death.

Yet the church would not be called his. Around 680 the head of the Church of the East wrote that its faith was "not the invention" of Nestorius and an earlier teacher of Antioch, whose names "scandalize the simple". In 1298 the chief bishop of Nisibis wrote that they were wrongly called "Nestorians". Nestorius had never been their patriarch, and they did not speak his language. They had held the faith in two natures already, and they refused to cut him off: "Nestorius, then, followed them, and not they him."

When and how Nestorius died is not known; historians reckon about 451. In his last book he wrote that he had borne the pain of his whole life as if it were a single day's. He bids the desert rejoice for him, and calls the land of his exile his mother, which will guard his body after death until the resurrection, when the dead rise at the end of time.
