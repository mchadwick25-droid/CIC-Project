A book about Christ came down to us under the name of Heracleides, a man who lived near Damascus. Its translator into Syriac, the Aramaic language of a church in Persia, explains why: "The writer chose to give the book this man's name. He feared that his own name, which many hated, would stop them from reading it and turning to the truth." The writer was Nestorius.

From 428 he was bishop, the leader of the churches, in Constantinople, the empire's capital. He taught that Christ is God and man, the two kept distinct yet worshiped as one Christ. So he would not simply call Mary "Mother of God", the one who gave birth to God. In 431 a council at Ephesus, a meeting of bishops called to settle teaching, judged that he split Christ in two and removed him from office. He denied it, and died in exile, forced to live far away under guard. Historians still argue over what he really taught.

Socrates, a historian of the city in his lifetime, tells of the first sermon this newcomer from Antioch preached. In it he asked the emperor for "the earth purged of heretics", people the church judged to teach falsely. He set out to pull down a chapel of the Arians. They followed Arius in teaching that the Son of God was made, not eternal. They set fire to it themselves. Later he had the emperor take churches from a rival Christian group.

Socrates also says he backed a priest who preached against calling Mary "Mother of God", and rejected the name himself. Nestorius says he found two parties, for "Mother of God" and "Mother of man", and offered both a name drawn from the Gospel's own words, "Mother of Christ".

In a sermon kept by his opponents, he said: "God is not separated from the one who is seen. So I do not separate the honor of the one who is not separated. I separate the natures, but I unite the worship." The natures are what Christ is as God and as man, two, yet Christians bow in worship to one Christ. Socrates, who blames his temper, says he did not deny that Christ was God, but feared the name "as though it were some terrible phantom".

The council, led by his enemy Cyril of Alexandria, judged his sermons blasphemous, insulting to God. In 435 the emperor's law ordered his writings burned. Banished the next year to southern Egypt, he was dragged to and fro by soldiers, old and sick, with "a mangled hand and side", his own letters say.

Near the end, perhaps in 451, he wrote his defense in Greek under the borrowed name. In it he praised Leo, bishop of Rome, as a preacher of the truth, though he says Leo agreed to his condemnation unheard. And he offered his own name to be cut off under the church's solemn curse: "So let Nestorius be anathema. Only let them say of God what I beg them to say."

His Greek is lost. The book survives because the Church of the East, the church of Persia beyond the Roman empire, put it into Syriac, perhaps a century later. Its rites, its set services, call him "the persecuted martyr", one who suffered for the faith. Yet that church would not be called his. In 1298 its chief bishop of Nisibis wrote that Nestorius had never been its patriarch, its head. They had held the faith in two natures already, he wrote, and they refused to cut him off: "Nestorius, then, followed them, and not they him."

When and how he died is not known. In his last book he bids the desert rejoice for him. He calls the land of his exile his mother, which will guard his body until the resurrection, when the dead rise at the end of time.
