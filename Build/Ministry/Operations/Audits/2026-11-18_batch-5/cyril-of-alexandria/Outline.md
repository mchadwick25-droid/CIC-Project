# Outline: Cyril of Alexandria

Sheet = `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder; tags (S1, E4, I1, D1 …) point to its entries, which give file and lines. His own words survive well (letters in Greek, treatises in English); the story of his city comes mainly through one hostile contemporary, Socrates; his afterlife comes through the churches that kept him as their measure.

- **The essence:** Cyril was bishop of Alexandria, the head of the church in Egypt's great city, from 412 to 444. He taught that Christ is one: God's own Son, whom the Bible calls the Word, was himself born of Mary as a man, so she may rightly be called Mother of God, the one who gave birth to God (E4: Documented, his own words). He led the attack on Nestorius, bishop of Constantinople, which ended with a council of bishops at Ephesus in 431 removing Nestorius from office (E6: Documented). His twelve short statements of faith, each cursing those who denied it, and his phrase "one incarnate nature of God the Word", meaning one Christ, God become man and never divided, became the test of true faith for the churches of Egypt, Syria and Armenia that later refused the Council of Chalcedon (I1, I2: Documented for Egypt and Syria; Armenia from the census Chalcedon text, I4). Chalcedon, the council of 451 that said Christ is known "in two natures", fully God and fully human, also took his letters as a guide (I4: Documented).
- **Also told:** (1) **What he taught**, in his own words and image: the Word did not come down onto a man already born, but took a human body and soul as his own from the womb (E4, Q1); so there is one Christ, not two sons, a divine one and a human one (E4); and the fire in Moses's bush that did not burn it up, his picture of God made man without the man being destroyed (E9, Q2). (2) **How he used his power:** he took the bishop's chair amid a riot, with the army backing his rival (S1); shut the churches of the Novatians, a strict Christian group outside his church, and seized their goods (E1); drove the Jews from Alexandria after some of them killed Christians in a night ambush, and let the crowd plunder them (E2); and a mob from his church, led by one of its readers, killed the philosopher Hypatia, a shame Socrates, a historian of the time, lays on Cyril and his whole church, while a Coptic chronicler more than two centuries on praised it (E3, D1: the murder Documented; his responsibility Contested). (3) **How he won:** the council at Ephesus approved his letter (E6); his own deputy's letter lists costly gifts to the emperor's court, which Nestorius called "the gold of iniquity" (E7, D3: gifts Documented, bribery Contested); after making peace with the bishop of Antioch in 433, he explained his "one nature" to allies who feared he had given way (E8: Documented). (4) **How his words became the measure:** the churches that refused Chalcedon heard its "two natures" as splitting Christ, and held to Cyril's words instead: Dioscorus, his successor; Timothy, who read Cyril's name aloud at the altar; the emperor's peace formula of 482; Severus of Antioch, who called his every word "a law of the church"; the Coptic patriarch Theodosius; and a Syriac patriarch seven centuries on (I1–I3: Documented).
- **Length:** about 3 minutes (about 450 words). Three parts each need room: the teaching, the force in Alexandria and at court that must not be dropped, and the long afterlife in the churches that kept him. The Ephesus and Chalcedon councils are a sentence or two each, since their siblings tell them; the quarrel from Nestorius's side is his sibling's.
- **Setting:** Century: the fifth; bishop from October 412 (S1); died 444 (S4). Place: Alexandria, the great port city of Egypt, where the bishop was a power in the streets as well as the church (S1); Ephesus, on the coast of what is now Turkey (E6). Tradition: told within **the Church of Alexandria** (census short name), the church of Athanasius before him and of the Copts after him (S3); carried on by the churches that refused Chalcedon: the Coptic church of Egypt, the Syriac Orthodox, the Armenians (I1–I2). Spoken words: "Alexandria", "Egypt", "the Coptic church", "the Syriac church".

**Words to explain** (each glossed where it is first heard; a plain word may replace it)

- **bishop** — leader of the church in a city and its region. In Alexandria then, also a power in city affairs (Socrates, SOC 19112–19116).
- **see / bishop's chair** — use "his post as bishop" or "the bishop's chair"; do not say "see".
- **patriarch** — title later given to the bishops of the greatest cities; the Coptic texts call Cyril "patriarch" (HP 32736; SYN17 55215). Use "bishop" for Cyril in his lifetime; "patriarch" only for Theodosius and Michael, glossed "head of the Coptic church" / "head of the Syriac church".
- **archdeacon** — the bishop's chief assistant and deputy.
- **reader** — a junior church officer who read Scripture aloud in services.
- **prefect** — the emperor's governor of the city; or say "the emperor's governor".
- **Novatians** — a strict Christian group with its own churches, outside the bishop's (Socrates passim; census `novatianism`).
- **the Word** — God's own Son, as John's Gospel names him: "the Word was made flesh" (ACTS 15788–15790).
- **incarnate / made flesh** — "become a human being, with body and soul" (ACTS 15737–15739).
- **Mother of God (Theotokos)** — "the one who gave birth to God". For Cyril it means the child she bore was God, not that God began in her (ACTS 15795–15798). Avoid the Greek word, or gloss it.
- **natures, two natures** — what Christ is as God and what he is as man. Chalcedon: both remain, in one Christ.
- **one incarnate nature of God the Word** — Cyril's phrase: one Christ, the Word himself, now with flesh, "no commingling, no confusion" (CIO 19833–19848). It does not mean his humanity vanished.
- **two sons** — Cyril's charge: a divine Son and a separate human son, wrongly split apart (ACTS 15784–15785).
- **council / synod** — a meeting of bishops to decide church questions; use "council" for both.
- **depose / deposed** — removed from office as bishop.
- **anathema, Twelve Chapters / Twelve Anathematisms** — say "twelve statements of faith, each ending with a curse on anyone who denied it"; anathema = cut off from the church, under its curse.
- **martyr** — someone killed for refusing to deny Christ; Socrates says Ammonius was not (SOC 19514–19518).
- **Lent** — the weeks of fasting before Easter.
- **communion / union of the churches** — being one church, sharing worship; say "one church again".
- **list of names at the altar (diptychs)** — names of the church's dead fathers read aloud in worship (ZAC 5744).
- **book of saints (Synaxarium)** — the Coptic church's calendar of saints' days, read in church.
- **Coptic** — of Egypt's own church and its Egyptian Christians.
- **Syriac church / Syriac Orthodox** — the church of Syria that also refused Chalcedon, using the Syriac language.
- **Chalcedon / the emperor's peace formula (Henotikon)** — gloss Chalcedon as "the council of 451"; the formula as "a letter of the emperor Zeno meant to reunite the churches" (EVA 5875–5885). Never say "Henotikon".
- **chamberlains** — the emperor's palace officials.

- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker. The question (how God and man are one in Christ, and what to call Mary), what he taught, and who learned it from him. Trap: a list of books; carry it through his letter's one sentence and his image of the bush. Not a founder-or-leader story of offices, though his power as bishop is told through acts.
- **Sibling:** `nestorius` (this batch) tells the same quarrel from Nestorius's side, his book, and how the Church of the East kept him; here Nestorius acts only as the opponent Cyril wrote against and helped depose. None of its quotes is reused ("I separate the natures, but I unite the worship", the borrowed-name preface, "So let Nestorius be anathema"; nor its witness phrases "the impious Cyril", "as though it were some terrible phantom"). `council-of-ephesus-431` tells the council: here one sentence (E6), with none of its quotes ("which bears the name of Mary", "with many tears", "He was the whole tribunal… Who was judge? Cyril.", "a union of two natures", "pious Mar Nestorius"), and no procedure, sessions or arrests. `council-of-chalcedon-451` tells Chalcedon: here one or two sentences (I4), with none of its quotes (the Definition's "without confusion, without change…", "Leo and Cyril taught the same thing" in any form, Dioscorus's "I accept 'from two'", "tore into ten thousand pieces"). Also on the tree: `dioscorus-of-alexandria`, `timothy-aelurus`, `severus-of-antioch`, `theodosius-i-of-alexandria`, `theodoret-of-cyrrhus`; name them as links only and use none of their stories' quotes.

**Openings**

1. **(Chosen.)** The bush that did not burn (E9, Q2). Moses saw a bush in the desert on fire, yet not burned up. Cyril, bishop of Alexandria, read that fire as a picture of Christ: God came to live a human life, and the human life was not consumed (Q2, with its plain sense). It leads straight into the essence: one Christ, God and man, which he defended against Nestorius and which became his churches' measure.
2. (Alternative.) Severus of Antioch, about a century later, telling another bishop to settle a question from Cyril's letter, "every word of whom… is a law of the church" (I1, Q3). Use only if Q3 is then not used again in What came.

**Story beats** (the opening, the essence and beat 1 fall in the first minute, about 150 words)

1. **Bishop of Alexandria, 412** (first minute, briefly). When his uncle Theophilus, the bishop, died, a riot broke out over who would follow him; the army's commander backed the archdeacon Timothy, but on the third day Cyril took the see. Socrates of Constantinople, a historian writing in his lifetime, says he came "with greater power than Theophilus had ever exercised", and that from then on the bishop of Alexandria handled the city's worldly affairs too (S1). At once he shut the churches of the Novatians, a strict Christian body outside his communion, and seized their vessels (E1). Documented as Socrates's report. "Archdeacon" = the bishop's chief assistant.
2. **The city's quarrels, 414–415.** Socrates tells it, and blames Cyril (credit him; D2). Cyril fell out with Orestes, the emperor's prefect. After some Jews raised a false cry of fire at night and killed the Christians who ran out, Cyril led a crowd to the synagogues, took them, and drove the Jews from the city, letting the crowd plunder their goods (E2). Monks who came to fight for Cyril stoned Orestes; one, Ammonius, was tortured to death, and Cyril hailed him as a martyr, until, Socrates says, he let it be forgotten (E2). Then in Lent 415 a Christian mob led by a church reader dragged the philosopher Hypatia from her carriage, killed her in a church and burned her body. Socrates says it brought shame "not only upon Cyril, but also upon the whole Alexandrian church" (E3); he does not say Cyril ordered it. A Coptic chronicler more than two centuries later praised the deed and says the people hailed Cyril as "the new Theophilus" (D1). Whether Cyril was to blame is still argued (D1). Documented as each witness's report; responsibility Contested.
3. **The letter to Nestorius.** In Constantinople the new bishop, Nestorius, would not simply call Mary "Theotokos", the one who gave birth to God (sibling). Cyril wrote to him: the Word was not changed into flesh, but made a body and a soul his own; there is one Christ, not two sons (E4). He put it in one sentence: Q1 (E4, from the Greek). So Mary may be called Mother of God: not because God began in her, but because the body born of her was the Word's own (E4). Documented in his own words.
4. **Twelve Chapters and the council** (the council in one sentence). With a synod at Alexandria he sent Nestorius twelve condemnations, the first against anyone who will not confess that "the Holy Virgin is the Mother of God" (E5); Nestorius was to agree to all twelve. At Ephesus in 431, with Cyril presiding, the bishops approved his letter and deposed Nestorius, while John of Antioch's party deposed Cyril in turn (E6, sibling). Documented.
5. **Winning the court.** The fight went on in the palace. A letter from Cyril's own archdeacon, Epiphanius, lists gifts sent to the emperor's sister, Pulcheria, and her chamberlains and ladies, complains of "their greed", and says the church of Alexandria was stripped bare and owed 1,500 pounds of gold more (E7). Nestorius later wrote that Cyril won with "the gold of iniquity" (E7). A historian of the councils calls such presents the custom of the East, not all bribes (D3). Gifts Documented; bribery Contested; credit each view.
6. **One incarnate nature** (after 433). Cyril made peace with John of Antioch in 433 (one clause; sibling), and some on his own side feared he had given up too much. Hefele, a historian of the councils, names the letters; in them Cyril quotes the fear of one bishop, that "one nature" would mean the manhood was lost, and answers that he still confessed "one incarnate nature of God the Word": after the union there is one Christ, the Word with a body and soul of his own, not two sons, and not a mixture (E8). Documented in his own words. Plain sense, in his own terms: one Son, the Word, "not without flesh nor without body", and "no commingling, no confusion" (E8).
7. **His death** (end, not told early). He died in 444 (S4). The Coptic book of saints keeps his day, and says he fell slightly ill and died in peace after thirty-two years as patriarch (E11).

**What came of them** (about a third of the story; heaviest first)

1. **The measure for those who refused Chalcedon** (what they defended: one Christ, "one incarnate nature of God the Word", and the Twelve Chapters). Chalcedon in 451 received Cyril's letters, yet defined Christ "in two natures" (I4; Chalcedon sibling, one sentence). The churches of Egypt and Syria, and later Armenia, refused it, holding that his "one nature" was the faith. Severus of Antioch, bishop and exile, wrote of Cyril: Q3 (I1). The Coptic book of saints has Dioscorus answer Chalcedon in Cyril's words: Christ is one, as soul and body are one, or fire and iron (I2). Documented.
2. **Kept by name in Egypt.** Timothy Aelurus read Cyril's name with Dioscorus's in the list of names at the altar (I2). When the emperor Justinian pressed the patriarch Theodosius to accept Chalcedon, the Coptic *History of the Patriarchs* has him answer that he follows his fathers, "Athanasius and Cyril and Dioscorus and Timothy" (I2). The Coptic book of saints calls him "pillar of the faith" (I2). Documented as the Coptic texts.
3. **Even the empire's peace used him.** In 482 the emperor Zeno's formula of union, meant to heal the split without Chalcedon, accepted "the twelve chapters of Cyril" (I3). Optional, one clause.
4. **Seven centuries on.** Around 1170, when a Greek emperor proposed union, the Syriac patriarch Michael answered that he would unite with anyone who confessed, "with Athanasius and Cyril, one nature of the Word incarnate" (I1, rendered from Chabot's French). Documented. Both sides still claim him (I4; census).

- **Q2.** Cyril, *That Christ is One*, written between 431 and 441 (CIO 19876–19882; preface 4328–4333). Printed English (Pusey): "for He came in likeness of fire on the bush in the wilderness, and the fire kept playing on the shrub yet was it not consumed." **Rendering (from Pusey's English; the Greek is not held):** "He came like fire upon the bush in the desert. The fire kept playing over the bush, and yet the bush was not burned up." The narrator gives its plain sense, from the passage: some said that if Christ is one nature, his human nature must be "lost and consumed" in the Godhead; Cyril answers that God made himself bearable to human measure, as the fire did not burn up the bush. Job: the opening; his teaching in his own image. Say it is Cyril's reading of the story of Moses.
- **Q1.** Cyril, second letter to Nestorius, the letter Ephesus approved (EP3 954–956, Greek; ACTS 15753–15756): "οὐ γὰρ πρῶτον ἄνθρωπος ἐγεννήθη κοινὸς ἐκ τῆς ἁγίας παρθένου, εἶθ᾽ οὕτω καταπεφοίτηκεν ἐπ᾽ αὐτὸν ὁ Λόγος· ἀλλ᾽ ἐξ αὐτῆς μήτρας ἑνωθεὶς, ὑπομεῖναι λέγεται γέννησιν σαρκικὴν". **Rendering (from the Greek):** "An ordinary man was not born first of the holy Virgin, with the Word coming down on him afterwards. United from the womb itself, he is said to have gone through birth in the flesh." Job: his teaching against Nestorius in one sentence; why the one born of Mary is God, and so why she is Mother of God.
- **Q3.** Severus of Antioch, *Select Letters* VI.1 (SEV1 2105–2108). Printed English (Brooks, from the Syriac): "the epistle of the holy Cyril (every utterance of whom one would say is a law of the church) to Nestorius". **Rendering (from Brooks's English; the Syriac is not held):** "the letter of the holy Cyril to Nestorius. Every word he spoke, one could say, is a law of the church." Job: how his words became the measure, in the voice of the man who led the churches that refused Chalcedon. Say who Severus was: bishop of Antioch, later in hiding in Egypt.

**Connection to carry into the story:** What passed from Cyril was a test of faith in words: his letters to Nestorius, his Twelve Chapters, and the phrase "one incarnate nature of God the Word" (E4, E5, E8). The listener must be told two things to understand it. First, both sides took his letters as a guide: Chalcedon in 451 received them, yet confessed Christ "in two natures" (I4). Second, the churches that refused Chalcedon heard "two natures" as Nestorius's division come back, and held to Cyril's "one nature" as the faith itself (I1, I2; Chalcedon sibling). So the split after 451 was a quarrel over who read Cyril rightly. Carry it through what people did with his words: Dioscorus answered the council in them; Timothy read his name at the altar; Severus judged cases by his letters "as a law of the church"; Theodosius named him among the fathers he would not betray; a Syriac patriarch seven centuries later made him the condition of union (I1–I2). No "this matters because" sentence.

**Ending:** (1) **(Chosen.)** His death as the Coptic book of saints tells it (E11): he fell slightly ill and died in peace, after thirty-two years as patriarch, and his discourses and letters were still, it says, in the hands of the faithful. Paraphrase, in the book's voice. (2) Alternative: the bush seen again (opening image), only if What came has just ended on his "one nature" and the fire image is not repeated as a quote.

**Who tells it**

- Cyril himself: the second and third letters to Nestorius in Greek (EP3, ACTS); *That Christ is One* and the letters to Succensus in Pusey's English and Hefele's quotations (CIO, HEF). Credit "he wrote".
- Socrates of Constantinople, in Cyril's lifetime (writing about 439): the election, the Novatians, the Jews, Ammonius, Hypatia, Ephesus (S1, E1–E3, E6). He blames Cyril; an editor thinks his sympathy for the Novatians, whom Cyril had stripped, sharpened his censure (D2). Say once that Socrates is hostile, where the Alexandrian events are told.
- John of Nikiu, a Coptic bishop writing in the late seventh century, from Cyril's side: praises the attack on Hypatia (D1). Credit his voice.
- Epiphanius, Cyril's own archdeacon, in a letter written after the council and kept in Latin: the gifts (E7).
- Nestorius, his opponent, in his book in exile: "the gold of iniquity" (E7).
- Severus of Antioch (early sixth century), Zachariah's chronicle, Evagrius (late sixth century), the Coptic *History of the Patriarchs* (compiled about the tenth century) and *Synaxarium* (medieval), Michael the Syrian (twelfth century): the afterlife (I1–I3, E11).
- Modern editors and historians (Pusey, Hefele, Zenos, Lietzmann): dates, the gifts' meaning, the formula's source. Their claims stay theirs.
- The doubt over Hypatia is not made a scene: told once, plainly, with each witness credited and the open question stated (D1).

- WRONG "John of Nikiu says Cyril ordered it". RIGHT if told: "the chronicle's chapter heading says her body was burned on Cyril's command; the chapter itself does not" (D1). Best left to the card.
- WRONG "Cyril drove out the Jews unprovoked" or "the Jews were expelled for rioting" alone. RIGHT both halves, in Socrates's order: some Jews killed Christians in a night ambush; Cyril then took the synagogues and drove the Jews out, letting the crowd plunder them (E2).
