In the story of Moses, a bush in the desert burns but is not burned up. Cyril of Alexandria read it as a picture of Christ: "He came like fire upon the bush in the desert. The fire kept playing over the bush, and yet the bush was not burned up." God made himself bearable to human measure, Cyril meant, and the human life he took was not lost in him.

Cyril was bishop, the leader of the church, in Alexandria, the great port city of Egypt, from 412 to 444. He taught that Christ is one. God's own Son, whom John's Gospel calls the Word, was himself born of Mary as a man, so she may rightly be called Mother of God, the one who gave birth to God. Cyril led the attack on Nestorius, bishop of Constantinople, until a council, a meeting of bishops, removed Nestorius from office at Ephesus in 431. Cyril's words then became the test of true faith for the churches of Egypt and Syria, and later Armenia.

He came to power amid a riot. When his uncle Theophilus, the bishop before him, died in 412, the army's commander backed the archdeacon Timothy, the bishop's chief assistant, but on the third day Cyril took the bishop's chair. Socrates of Constantinople, a historian writing in Cyril's lifetime and hostile to him, says he came "with greater power than Theophilus had ever exercised", and that the bishop now handled the city's worldly affairs too. At once Cyril shut the churches of the Novatians, a strict Christian group outside his church, and seized the cups and plates used in their worship.

Socrates tells the city's quarrels, and he blames Cyril. Cyril fell out with the emperor's governor and threatened the Jewish leaders with the harshest measures. Some Jews then raised a false cry of fire at night and killed the Christians who ran out. Cyril led a crowd to the synagogues, took them, and drove the Jews from the city, letting the crowd plunder their goods. Monks who came to fight for him mobbed the governor in the street, and one hit him with a stone. The governor had the monk tortured until he died. Cyril hailed him as a martyr, someone killed for refusing to deny Christ, but Socrates says sober Christians would not accept it, and Cyril let it be forgotten.

Then, in the weeks of fasting before Easter in 415, a Christian mob led by a church reader, a junior officer who read Scripture aloud in services, dragged the philosopher Hypatia from her carriage. They killed her in a church and burned her body. Socrates says the shame fell on Cyril and the whole church of Alexandria, though he does not say Cyril ordered it. A Coptic chronicler, writing for Egypt's own church more than two centuries later, praised the deed. Whether Cyril was to blame is still argued.

In Constantinople, the new bishop, Nestorius, would not simply call Mary the one who gave birth to God. Cyril wrote to him that the Word was not changed into flesh, but made a human body and soul his own. There is one Christ, he insisted, not two sons, a divine Son and a separate human one. He put it in one sentence about Mary, the holy Virgin: "An ordinary man was not born first of the holy Virgin, with the Word coming down on him afterwards. Rather, the Word, united from the womb itself, is said to have gone through birth in the flesh." So Mary is Mother of God, not because God began in her, but because the body born of her was the Word's own.

With a council at Alexandria, Cyril sent Nestorius twelve statements of faith, each ending with a curse on anyone who denied it. At Ephesus, with Cyril presiding, the bishops approved his letter, while the bishop of Antioch's party removed Cyril in turn.

The fight moved to the palace. A letter from Cyril's own archdeacon lists gifts sent to the emperor's sister and to palace officials, men and women. It complains of "their greed", and says the church of Alexandria was stripped bare and still owed 1,500 pounds of gold. Nestorius later wrote of officials bought with "the gold of iniquity", and laid it on Cyril. A historian of the councils calls such presents the custom of the East, not all of them bribes.

In 433 Cyril made peace with the bishop of Antioch, and some on his own side feared he had given up too much. In this quarrel, a nature meant what Christ is, as God and as man, and one bishop feared that "one nature" meant the manhood was lost. Cyril answered that he still confessed "one incarnate nature of God the Word". By incarnate he meant become a human being, with body and soul: one Christ, the Word himself with a body of his own, with "no commingling, no confusion".

In 451, a council at Chalcedon received his letters, yet said Christ is known in two natures. The churches of Egypt and Syria, and later Armenia, refused it. They heard "two natures" as Nestorius's split come back, and held to Cyril's "one nature" as the faith itself. Severus, bishop of Antioch, who later hid in Egypt, settled a question of church rule from "the letter of the holy Cyril to Nestorius. Every word he spoke, one could say, is a law of the church." The Coptic book of saints, the calendar of saints' days read in Egypt's churches, has his successor Dioscorus answer Chalcedon in Cyril's words: Christ is one, as soul and body are one, or fire and iron.

Even the bishop the emperor set up in Alexandria on Chalcedon's side read Cyril's name in worship, among the church's dead fathers, hoping to win the people. A chronicle says they still would not follow him. Later the patriarch Theodosius, head of the Coptic church, refused the emperor and named his fathers as "Athanasius and Cyril and Dioscorus and Timothy". Around 1170, when a Greek emperor proposed union, a patriarch of the Syriac church, the church of Syria that also refused Chalcedon, answered that he would unite with anyone who confessed, "with Athanasius and Cyril, one nature of the Word incarnate". Both sides still claim him.

Cyril died in 444. The Coptic book of saints, which keeps his day, says he fell slightly ill and died in peace after thirty-two years as bishop, on the patriarch's throne, as it puts it, using the title the Coptic church gives its head. His discourses and letters, it says, were still in the hands of the faithful.
