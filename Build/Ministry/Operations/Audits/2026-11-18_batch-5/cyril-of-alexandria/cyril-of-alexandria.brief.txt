In the story of Moses, a bush in the desert burns but is not burned up. Cyril of Alexandria read it as a picture of Christ: "He came like fire upon the bush in the desert. The fire kept playing over the bush, and yet the bush was not burned up." God took a human life, he meant, and that life was not lost.

Cyril was bishop, the leader of the church, in Alexandria, Egypt's great port city, from 412 to 444. He taught that Christ is one: God's own Son, whom John's Gospel calls the Word, was himself born of Mary. He led the attack on Nestorius, bishop of Constantinople, which had him removed from office in 431. His words became the test of faith for the churches of Egypt, Syria and Armenia that later broke with the council of 451, a meeting of bishops.

Socrates, a historian writing in Cyril's lifetime and hostile to him, tells how Cyril took the bishop's chair amid a riot, though the army had backed a rival. He came with more power than his uncle, the bishop before him, had ever used. At once he shut the churches of the Novatians, a strict Christian group outside his own, and seized their goods.

Socrates blames him for what followed. Cyril threatened the Jewish leaders with the harshest measures, and some Jews then killed Christians in a night ambush. Cyril took the synagogues and drove the Jews from the city, letting a crowd plunder them. A monk who had come to fight for Cyril struck the emperor's governor with a stone and died under torture. Cyril hailed him as one who died for Christ, until he let it be forgotten. In 415 a Christian mob led by a church reader, who read Scripture aloud in services, killed the philosopher Hypatia. Socrates says the shame fell on Cyril and his whole church; a chronicler of Egypt's own church, more than two centuries later, praised the deed. Whether Cyril was to blame is still argued.

Nestorius would not simply call Mary the one who gave birth to God. Cyril answered that there is one Christ, not two sons, a divine one and a separate human one. He wrote of Mary, the holy Virgin: "An ordinary man was not born first of the holy Virgin, with the Word coming down on him afterwards. Rather, the Word, united from the womb itself, is said to have gone through birth in the flesh." So she is Mother of God, because the one born of her was the Word himself.

He sent Nestorius twelve statements of faith, each ending with a curse on anyone who denied it. In 431 a council at Ephesus approved the letter he had written to Nestorius about Mary, and removed Nestorius from office. Afterward Cyril's own archdeacon, his chief assistant, listed gifts to the emperor's court and a debt of 1,500 pounds of gold. Nestorius called it "the gold of iniquity"; a historian of the councils calls such gifts custom.

In 433 he made peace with the bishop of Antioch. Afterward one bishop on his side feared that his "one nature" meant what Christ is as man was lost in what he is as God. Cyril held to "one incarnate nature of the Word": the Word himself become a human being, body and soul, with "no commingling, no confusion".

In 451 the council of Chalcedon took his letters as a guide, yet said Christ is known in two natures. The churches of Egypt and Syria heard that as Nestorius's split returning, and held to Cyril's "one nature" instead. A chronicle tells how even the bishop the emperor set up in Alexandria on Chalcedon's side put Cyril's name among the church's dead fathers read aloud in worship. He hoped to win the people, but they still would not follow him. Severus, bishop of Antioch, who later hid in Egypt, settled a question from "the letter of the holy Cyril to Nestorius. Every word he spoke, one could say, is a law of the church."

Cyril died in 444. The Coptic church, Egypt's own, keeps his day in its calendar of saints. It says he fell slightly ill and died in peace after thirty-two years as bishop, his letters still in the hands of the faithful.
