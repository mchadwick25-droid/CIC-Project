## The three lines, from one hearing

1. **Who:** Cyril, bishop of Alexandria in Egypt from 412 to 444, who taught that Christ is one: the Word, God's Son, himself born of Mary.
2. **What happened:** he took office amid a riot and used force in his city (the Novatians shut, the Jews driven out, a mob from his church killing Hypatia, his blame argued). He led the attack on Nestorius, and a council at Ephesus in 431 approved his letter and removed Nestorius. Gifts to the court followed. After 433 he defended his "one nature" phrase.
3. **Why the connection matters:** Chalcedon in 451 took his letters as a guide but said "two natures". The churches of Egypt and Syria heard that as Nestorius's split, held to Cyril's "one nature", and treated his words as church law (Severus).

None is blank or wrong.

## Findings

### BLOCKING

**B1. "Approved his letter" reads as the letter of curses.** Paragraph 6 says: "He sent Nestorius twelve statements of faith, each ending with a curse on anyone who denied it. In 431 a council … at Ephesus approved his letter and removed Nestorius from office."
- The only letter the listener has just heard of is the one carrying the twelve statements. The brief never calls the paragraph 5 text a letter.
- So the sentence implies that Ephesus approved the curses. Whether it did is Contested (D4, ACTS 15867–15880), and the Do not say list bars "Ephesus adopted the Twelve Chapters" as fact.
- Ephesus approved the second letter, the one quoted in paragraph 5 (E6, ACTS 15816–15829).
- **Change:** name that letter. For example: "In 431 a council, a meeting of bishops, at Ephesus approved the letter he had written to Nestorius about Mary, and removed Nestorius from office." Or call the paragraph 5 quote a letter when it is introduced ("He wrote to Nestorius of Mary, the holy Virgin:"), and then say "approved that letter" here.
- **Do not say line to add:** WRONG "approved his letter" told straight after the twelve statements, so that the letter heard is the one ending in curses. RIGHT name the letter Ephesus approved: the earlier letter to Nestorius about Mary (Q1's letter, E6). Whether Ephesus approved the twelve is disputed (D4).

**B2. The afterlife, heaviest in What came, rests on one act.** The heart's Also told has "later bishops who held to his words", in the plural. The outline ranks "the measure for those who refused Chalcedon" heaviest, and its Connection says to carry it "through what people did with his words".
- The brief gives the churches' refusal and one act, Severus's.
- What came is meant to be about a third of the story. Here it is about 100 words of 645, counting the essence clause, paragraph 8 and the ending's calendar.
- Without at least one more act, the listener hears that his words were "the test of faith" but meets only one bishop who used them.
- **Change:** add one sentence, about 25 to 35 words, from What came 1 or 2. Two candidates:
  - The outline's sharpest: even the bishop the emperor set up in Alexandria on Chalcedon's side read Cyril's name among the church's dead fathers in worship, hoping to win the people, and they still would not follow him (ZAC 5726–5746; given by role, never as Timothy Aelurus).
  - Dioscorus, his successor, answering Chalcedon in Cyril's words: Christ is one, as soul and body are one, or fire and iron (SYN1 14328–14378; credit the Coptic book of saints).
- If the second is used, the fire-and-iron image sits close to the bush. Keep it in the book's voice, not as a repeated quote.
- The outline left no room for this error, so no Do not say line is needed.

### MINOR

**M1. The essence could carry two short clauses from the approved heart.**
- After "He led the attack on Nestorius, bishop of Constantinople", add "which had him removed from office in 431" (about 7 words).
- After "the churches of Egypt, Syria and Armenia", add "that later broke with the council of 451" (about 8 words).
- The essence may reach ahead, so paragraph 6 can then tell Ephesus without repeating the outcome. Optional: the listener is not lost without them.

**M2. "Some allies feared" (paragraph 7) is one bishop's fear.**
- Hefele names several letters defending the peace of 433 (HEF 7870–7890).
- The fear that "one nature" meant the manhood was lost is Succensus's, quoted by Cyril (CIO 19825–19831; outline beat 6: "the fear of one bishop").
- **Change:** "some allies questioned the peace, and one bishop feared that his 'one nature' meant …". Or simply "one ally, a bishop, feared …".

**M3. "Two centuries later" drops the outline's qualifier.** John of Nikiu wrote in the late seventh century, over 250 years after 415 (D1; outline beat 2 and Also told (2): "more than two centuries"). **Change:** "a chronicler of Egypt's own church, more than two centuries later, praised the deed."

**M4. Sentence past 30 words.** "After he made peace with the bishop of Antioch in 433, some allies feared that his 'one nature' meant what Christ is as man was lost in what he is as God." (32 words). **Split:** "In 433 he made peace with the bishop of Antioch. Afterward one bishop on his side feared that his 'one nature' meant what Christ is as man was lost in what he is as God." This also fixes M2. The 28- and 29-word sentences in paragraphs 3 and 4 are within the line.

**M5. The monk's side is not said.** "When a monk who struck the emperor's governor with a stone …" does not say that the monks had come to fight for Cyril (SOC 19484–19519; beat 2). Without that, the listener cannot tell why Cyril hailed him. **Change:** "When a monk who had come to fight for Cyril struck the emperor's governor with a stone and died under torture, …". This adds about 6 words, so split the sentence if it then runs past 30.
