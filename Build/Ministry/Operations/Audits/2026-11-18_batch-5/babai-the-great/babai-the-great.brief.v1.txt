Thomas of Marga tells of an oath. The Persian king Khosrau swore by the Sun, his god, that while he lived, the Christians of his land would have no new head of their church. He kept his word for close to twenty years. In that time the bishops, the leaders over each city and its region, turned to a man who was not even a bishop.

He was Babai, a monk vowed to prayer, poverty and an unmarried life, and abbot, or head, of the Great Monastery on Mount Izla, near Nisibis. For the Church of the East, the church of the Persian empire, he drove out of its monasteries those the bishops judged heretics, people teaching what the church had condemned. He also wrote the Book of the Union, on how God and man are joined in the one Christ.

Thomas, who tells most of his story two centuries later, says some monks at Izla were found living with wives. Babai and the senior monks stripped them of the monk's robe, drove them out with their families and burned their huts. Babai then drove out a gentle monk named Jacob, unjustly, Thomas says, and condemned him in writing. The monks rebuked their abbot, and many left to found monasteries across the East, among them Jacob's, where Thomas would later write.

After the last head of the church died, around 609, three senior bishops asked Babai to visit the monasteries for them. At Izla, by his own account, followers of Henana of Adiabene, whose books the bishops had condemned, were refused communion, the shared bread and wine of worship. They were let back only after cutting Henana off from the church with a solemn curse.

In 612, Babai says, the bishops set their faith before the king. In Christ, they wrote, are two natures, what makes him God and what makes him human, each real and complete, in one person. But while they honored Nestorius, their teacher, a bishop of Constantinople condemned in 431, the king answered, they would have no head. Babai's Book of the Union says: "Here too, in the same way, we say that Christ was crucified, that Christ died and rose again. But we do not say that God was crucified and died." The man Jesus suffered and God did not, yet both are one Christ. That answer set his church apart from its neighbors. Thomas counted eighty-four of Babai's works still honored in the church, and historians judge that his books helped make it the church's settled teaching.

In 615 the king had George, a monk of Izla, crucified on a charge of leaving the Persian state religion, and Babai wrote the account of his life.

In 628, after the king was murdered, the bishops all begged Babai, Thomas says, to become head of the church. He refused, choosing to end his days in his hut. Thomas tells that an angel on a white horse, with a fiery sword, then stood before it. It had stood beside him, it said, all the years he took the place of the head of the church, and now must go to the new one. Babai answered: "If I had known you were with me, I would have taken on this great work with all care. But now go in peace, and pray for me." Babai, by then an old man, died about that time.
