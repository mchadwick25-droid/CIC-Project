Thomas of Marga tells that the Persian king Khosrau swore by the Sun, his god, that while he lived, the Christians of his land would have no new head of their church. He kept his word for close to twenty years. In those years the bishops, the church's leaders over each city and its region, turned to a man who was not even a bishop.

Babai was a monk, vowed to prayer, poverty and an unmarried life, and abbot, or head, of the Great Monastery on Mount Izla near Nisibis, in what is now southeastern Turkey. For the Church of the East, the church of the Persian empire, he drove out of its monasteries those the bishops judged heretics, people teaching what the church had condemned. He also wrote the Book of the Union, on how God and man are joined in the one Christ.

Thomas, who tells most of his story, calls him learned and wise but harsh in his rule. When monks at Izla were found living with wives, Thomas says, Babai and the senior monks stripped them of the monk's robe, drove them out with their wives and children, and burned their huts. Babai then drove out a gentle monk named Jacob and laid a long written condemnation on him. The monks rebuked their abbot, and many left to found new monasteries across the East. Thomas himself wrote two centuries later at Jacob's own monastery, and he says Jacob was driven out unjustly.

Around 609 the last head of the church died, and fear of the government kept three senior bishops, each over the bishops of a province, from traveling. They asked Babai to visit the monasteries in their place. At Izla, by his own account, followers of Henana of Adiabene, whose books the bishops had condemned, were refused communion, the shared bread and wine of worship. They received it only after laying on Henana a solemn curse that cut him off from the church.

In 612 the bishops came before the king. Babai says they set down their faith: in Christ, two natures, what makes him God and what makes him human, each real and complete, in one person. A later chronicle says the old abbot helped them with his pen. But while they honored the name of Nestorius, the king answered, they would have no head. Nestorius was a bishop of Constantinople, condemned in 431, whom this church honored as a teacher.

Babai's Book of the Union says: "Here too, in the same way, we say that Christ was crucified, that Christ died and rose again. But we do not say that God was crucified and died." The man Jesus suffered, while God, who cannot suffer, did not, and yet both are one Christ. That answer set his church apart from its neighbors. Two centuries on, Thomas counted eighty-four of Babai's works still honored by the church, and historians judge that his books, so widely kept, helped make it the church's settled teaching.

In 615, a chronicle written a generation later says, George, a monk of Izla, was accused of leaving the Persian state religion, and the king had him crucified. Babai wrote the account of his life.

In 628 the king was murdered, and the bishops all begged Babai, Thomas says, to become head of the church. He refused, preferring to end his days in his hut. Thomas tells that an angel on a white horse, with a fiery sword, then stood before it. It had stood beside him, it said, all the years he took the place of the head of the church, and now it must go to the new one. Babai answered: "If I had known you were with me, I would have taken on this great work with all care. But now go in peace, and pray for me."

He was an old man, and he died about that time.
