Thomas of Marga tells how the Persian king Khosrau swore by the Sun, his god, that while he lived, the Christians of his land would have no new head of their church. He kept his word for close to twenty years. In that time the bishops, the leaders over each city and its region, turned to a man who was not even a bishop.

He was Babai, a monk vowed to prayer, poverty and an unmarried life, and abbot, or head, of the Great Monastery on Mount Izla, near Nisibis. For the Church of the East, the church of the Persian empire, he drove out of its monasteries those the bishops judged heretics, people teaching what the church had condemned. He also wrote the Book of the Union, on how God and man are joined in the one Christ. His church kept and honored his books, and historians judge that they helped settle its teaching on Christ.

Thomas, who tells most of his story two centuries later, says some monks at Izla were found living with wives. Babai and the senior monks stripped them of the monk's robe, drove them out with their families and burned their huts. Babai then drove out a gentle monk named Jacob, unjustly, Thomas says, and condemned him in writing. The monks rebuked their abbot, and many left, and founded monasteries across the East, among them Jacob's, where Thomas would later write.

In 612, Babai says, the bishops set their faith before the king. In Christ, they wrote, are two natures, what makes him God and what makes him human, each real and complete, in one person. But while they honored Nestorius, their teacher, a bishop of Constantinople condemned in 431, the king answered, they would have no head. Babai's Book of the Union says: "Here too, in the same way, we say that Christ was crucified, that Christ died and rose again. But we do not say that God was crucified and died." The man Jesus suffered and God did not, yet both are one Christ. That teaching set his church apart from its neighbors. Two centuries on, Thomas counted eighty-four of his works still honored in the church.

In 628, after the king was murdered, the bishops all begged Babai, Thomas says, to become head of the church. He refused, choosing to end his days in his hut. Thomas tells that an angel on a white horse, with a fiery sword, then stood before it. It had stood beside him, it said, all the years he took the place of the head of the church, and now must go to the new one. Babai answered: "If I had known you were with me, I would have taken on this great work with all care. But now go in peace, and pray for me." Babai, by then an old man, died about that time.
