Thomas of Marga tells that the Persian king Khosrau swore by the Sun, his god, that as long as he lived, the Christians of his land would have no new head of their church. He kept his word until his own death, close to twenty years later. In those years the church's bishops, its leaders over each city and its region, turned to a man who was not even a bishop.

He was Babai, a monk, vowed to prayer, poverty and an unmarried life, and abbot, or head, of the Great Monastery on Mount Izla near Nisibis, in what is now southeastern Turkey. For the Church of the East, the church of the Persian empire, he went round its monasteries, testing what the monks believed and driving out those the bishops judged heretics, people teaching what the church had condemned. He also wrote the Book of the Union, on how God and man are joined in the one Christ, and his church kept and honored his books.

Babai had learned from the monastery's founder and became its third head. Thomas, who admired him, calls him learned and wise, but quick of speech and harsh in his rule. Some monks were found living with wives in the huts around the monastery, against their vows. By Thomas's account, Babai and the senior monks stripped them of the monk's robe and the monk's shaved head, drove them out with their wives and children, and burned their huts.

Then Babai drove out a gentle monk named Jacob, accused of knowing and keeping silent, and afterwards laid a long written condemnation on him. The other monks told their abbot that he ruled God's house the way men rule, and many left. Thomas, who tells most of Babai's story, wrote about two centuries later at Beth Abe, the monastery Jacob went on to found, and he says Jacob was driven out unjustly. Those who left, he says, founded new monasteries across the East.

Around 609 the last head of the church died, and the king allowed no successor. Three senior bishops, each over the bishops of a province, feared the Messalians, wandering monks the church condemned, who said prayer alone was enough. Since fear of the government kept them at home, they asked Babai, a monk and not a bishop, to visit the monasteries in their place. A later historian says he and an archdeacon, the chief church official in the capital, ran the church's affairs. At Izla, by Babai's own account, followers of Henana of Adiabene, a teacher at Nisibis whose books the bishops had condemned, were refused communion, the shared bread and wine of Christian worship, until they laid on their master a solemn curse that cut him off from the church.

In 612 the bishops met before the king. First, he said, let it be examined which faith is true, and then they would get a head. Babai says they wrote their faith down: in Christ there are two natures, what makes him God and what makes him human, each real and complete and keeping what is its own, in one person. But as long as they honored the name of Nestorius, the king answered, they would have no head. Nestorius was a bishop of Constantinople, condemned in 431, whom this church honored as a teacher. Babai was too old and weak to go, a later chronicle says, so he helped them with his pen.

His Book of the Union puts the teaching in his own words: "Here too, in the same way, we say that Christ was crucified, that Christ died and rose again. But we do not say that God was crucified and died." The man Jesus suffered, while God, who cannot suffer, did not, and yet both are one Christ. Babai called those who taught otherwise people who make God suffer, and the teaching set his church apart from its neighbors. Two centuries on, Thomas counted eighty-four of his works, still kept and honored by the church. Historians judge that his books, copied so widely, helped this teaching become his church's settled answer.

In 615 a physician at court accused George, a monk of Izla, of leaving the Persian state religion he was born to. A chronicle written a generation later says the king held him a year in prison and then had him crucified, and Babai wrote the account of his life.

In 628 the king was murdered, and the bishops met to choose a new head of the church. All of them, Thomas says, begged Babai to take it, but he refused, preferring to end his days in his hut. A bishop of his own day, who later led the church himself, called him a great and most illustrious leader.

The new head of the church and some bishops took him home. Thomas tells that an angel on a white horse, with a fiery sword, then stood before his hut. It had stood beside him, it said, all the years he took the place of the head of the church, and now it must go to the new one. Babai answered: "If I had known you were with me, I would have taken on this great work with all care. But now go in peace, and pray for me."
