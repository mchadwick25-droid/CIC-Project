The Church of the East gathered its synods, its councils of bishops, into one book. Its compiler, about three centuries later, left one out on purpose. Barsauma had held it at Beth Lapat, and he himself had said it was held "out of ill will and human passion."

Barsauma was bishop of Nisibis, a city on the border between the Roman and Persian empires, in the late 400s. In 484 he led that synod against the catholicos, the head of his church at the Persian capital, and later took it back in writing. Yet his church kept what his side stood for: married clergy, from bishops to assistant ministers, and the Greek Bible teacher Theodore of Mopsuestia as its sure guide. Writers of the churches that broke with it remembered him as the man who set the Persian church apart. The Persian church had run its own affairs since 424; what his synods set apart was its teaching and its rules.

In April 484 came the break. By his own account, "Satan stirred up a quarrel" between him and the head of the church, Babowai. At Beth Lapat, Barsauma and the bishops on his side drew up a written case against Babowai and made rules on clergy marriage. That year the Persian king put Babowai to death. A later historian of Barsauma's own church says Barsauma passed Babowai's letter to the king and then regretted it, while writers of a rival church say he had him killed. His own letters speak only of the quarrel.

That historian, writing in the 1100s, says Barsauma then married a nun, a woman vowed to a single life in religion, on the king's order. A chronicler of the rival Syriac church, in the 1200s, says instead that he kept a woman and called her his lawful wife. A hostile chronicle of the 1100s, built on old men's memories, says he made the marriage rule so he would not be blamed for her.

In 485, with Acacius as the new head of the church, Barsauma took back everything done at Beth Lapat, its rules included. His six letters, the nearest witness to his life, show a frontier pressed by drought, raiding tribes and a Roman army. He asked Acacius to cut the rebels of Nisibis off from the church's worship and fellowship, "like the doctor who amputates," and to threaten to report them to the king.

A synod in 486, which he stayed away from but accepted in advance, confessed Christ fully God and fully human, the two never mixed.

In 497 a synod let every cleric, from the head of the church down, marry one wife openly. It accepted Beth Lapat "but only on this point."

In 605 a synod ruled that every teacher in the church must study Theodore's writings. It quoted Beth Lapat's ruling on Theodore. It called the synod left out of the book "the divine assembly," and its leader, with the title Mar, "my lord," "the blessed Mar Barsauma."
