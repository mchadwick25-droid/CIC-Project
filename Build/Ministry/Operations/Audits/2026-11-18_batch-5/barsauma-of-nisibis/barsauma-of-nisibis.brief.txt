The Church of the East gathered its synods, its councils of bishops, into one book. Its compiler, three centuries later, left one out on purpose: the synod Barsauma held at Beth Lapat, because Barsauma himself had said it was held "out of ill will and human passion."

Barsauma was bishop of Nisibis, a city on the border between the Roman and Persian empires, in the late 400s. In 484 he led that synod against the catholicos, the head of his church at the Persian capital, and later took it back in writing. Yet his church kept what his side stood for: clergy, its bishops, priests and assistant ministers, free to marry, and the Greek Bible teacher Theodore of Mopsuestia as its sure guide. Writers of the churches that broke with it remembered him as the man who set the Persian church apart.

A Persian, he had studied at Edessa, in the Roman empire. At Nisibis he welcomed the teacher Narsai and asked him to found a school, which counts both men as founders.

In April 484, by his own account, "Satan stirred up a quarrel" between him and the head of the church, Babowai. At Beth Lapat, Barsauma and the bishops on his side drew up a written case against Babowai, of "murmurs, blames, charges and testimonies," and made rules on clergy marriage. That year the Persian king put Babowai to death. A later historian of Barsauma's own church says Barsauma passed Babowai's letter to the king and then regretted it, while writers of a rival church say he had him killed. His own letters speak only of the quarrel.

That historian, Mari, writing in the 1100s, says Barsauma then married a nun, a woman vowed to a single life in religion, and that the king ordered him to take a wife. A chronicler of the rival Syriac church, in the 1200s, says instead that he kept a woman in his cell, his small room, and calls her a "concubine," a woman kept without marriage.

In 485, once a new head of the church, Acacius, was in office, Barsauma took back everything done at Beth Lapat, its rules included. His six letters, the nearest witness to his life, show a bishop on a hard frontier. Drought, raiding tribes and a Roman army pressed on Nisibis, and when he brought the Roman general to feast with the Persian governor, horsemen of a tribe subject to Persia raided Roman villages, and the Romans blamed him.

In another letter he wrote: "Now Nisibis is shaken, troubled and tossed like a stormy sea. If you, Father, do not quickly send a letter of excommunication to calm the city, I will not stay on as bishop of Nisibis. And Nisibis itself will not stay under the great empire of the Persians." He asked Acacius to cut his rebels off from the church's worship and fellowship, "like the doctor who amputates," and to threaten to report them to the king.

A synod in 486, which he accepted in advance, ruled that no bishop could stop his clergy marrying, and it expected new assistant ministers to have a lawful wife. Yet by 491 he and Acacius were excommunicating each other again, and he died in a year no one knows.

In 497 a synod let every cleric, from the head of the church down, marry one wife openly. It accepted Beth Lapat "but only on this point." About 506, Armenian bishops refused the rules Acacius and Barsauma had set up. Writers of the rival Syriac church, drawing on old men's memories a century and a half later, said he told the Persian king his Christians would never be loyal until their faith differed from the Romans'. With the king's soldiers, they said, he killed thousands who refused.

In 605 a synod ruled that every teacher in the church must study Theodore's writings. It quoted the synod left out of the book, calling it "the divine assembly," and its leader, with the title Mar, "my lord," "the blessed Mar Barsauma."
