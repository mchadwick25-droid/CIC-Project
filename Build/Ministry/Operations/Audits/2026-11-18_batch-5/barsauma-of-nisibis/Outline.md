# Outline: Barsauma of Nisibis

Sheet items (S, E, I, D, Q) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each.

- **The essence:** Barsauma was bishop of Nisibis, a city on the border between the Roman and Persian empires, in the late 400s. In 484 he led a synod, a council of bishops, at Beth Lapat against the catholicos, the head of his own church, and later took it back in writing (E5, E8, E10; Documented, his own letters). Yet what his side stood for became the Church of the East's settled law: clergy, the church's ordained ministers, free to marry, allowed in 486 and, in 497, accepted from Beth Lapat "only on this point"; and Theodore of Mopsuestia, a Greek Bible teacher, as its sure guide, confirmed in 605 (E11, I2, I3; Documented, the church's own synods). Writers of the churches that broke with it remembered him as the man who set the Persian church apart (I4, I5; Documented as their hostile claims).
- **Also told:** the frontier bishop, from his own letters: drought, raiding tribes, a Roman general brought to Nisibis, and a city he feared would slip from Persia (E9, E10; Documented). That frontier bishop asked the catholicos to excommunicate his own rebels, cutting them off from the church, and to threaten to report them to the king (E10; Documented). The rebel synod's written charges against the catholicos Babowai, and Babowai's death, in which Barsauma's part is disputed (E5, E7, D2; Contested). The synod of 486, which he did not attend but accepted in advance: Christ confessed as both fully God and fully human, "two natures" never mixed; it ordered wandering men in monks' black, whom it called frauds teaching error, out of the towns and villages, back to the monasteries and the wild places, under the bishops; clergy free to marry (E11; Documented). His welcome of the teacher Narsai, in one clause (E4). The quarrel renewed in 491, when he and the catholicos excommunicated each other (E12; Documented).
  The sources will not hold "Barsauma made the Church of the East independent": that was ruled in 424 (S4). They hold that his synods fixed its teaching and its church rules.
- **Length:** about 3 minutes (about 450 words). Why: the record is unusually rich for the 400s, with six of his own letters, the acts of three synods that touch his work, and witnesses from both sides. The heart has three parts (the rebel synod and its retraction, what the church kept from it, and how others remembered him), and all three must land early. The frontier letters carry the craft. The hostile tradition must be told openly, with its lateness. Mark (2026-11-18) added his own marriage, told in two credited sentences beside the marriage rules; that adds about 15 seconds. Not more: the school is Narsai's story, and the embassy legends and the wonders are left out. Three minutes is a pressure, not a cap; never fill to it.
- **Setting:**
  - Time: "the late 400s"; Beth Lapat, April 484; reconciliation, August 485; the synod at Seleucia, February 486 (S2).
  - Place: Nisibis, "a city on the border between the Roman and Persian empires", Persian since 363 (S1). Today Nusaybin in southeastern Turkey (census `modernCountries`; the census tradition entry names "Nusaybin, Turkey").
  - Tradition: "the Church of the East, the church of the Persian empire" (S3). Its head, the catholicos, sat at Seleucia-Ctesiphon, the Persian capital on the Tigris (S3). It had governed itself since 424 (S4).
  - Plain words: see "Words to explain", below.
- **Words to explain** (the writer works the gloss into the sentence where the word is first heard; or uses the plain word instead):
  - **synod**: "a council of bishops". Here a meeting of the Persian church's bishops, not a Roman empire-wide council (SO 20191–20192 "réunion d'évêques").
  - **catholicos**: "the head of the Church of the East, at the Persian capital" (S3; SO 35473). Use once, then "the head of the church".
  - **patriarch** (497 ruling, 605 quote): the same office as catholicos; the sources use both for Acacius (SO 35209–35213). Say "from the head of the church down".
  - **see** ("disciple of the see"): say "the catholicos's office" instead (SO 35381 "siège").
  - **clergy**: "the church's ordained ministers: bishops, priests and deacons".
  - **deacon**: "a minister ranked below the priest". Plain word: "assistant ministers".
  - **canons**: say "church rules" (SO 20257–20259).
  - **excommunicate / excommunication**: "cut off from the church's worship and fellowship". Here it also barred a cleric from serving (SO 19801–19802).
  - **two natures**: "Christ fully God and fully human, the two never mixed" (SO 19739–19746).
  - **ascetics**: men who live by fasting and self-denial. Here, wandering men in monks' black whom the synod called frauds spreading false teaching (SO 19699–19703, 19752–19754).
  - **marzban**: say "the Persian governor" (SO-ed 20234 "marzban ou gouverneur").
  - **nun**: "a woman vowed to a single life in religion". Mari's "monialis" (MARI 14386).
  - **cell**: a monk's or nun's small room (BH 3759, 4548).
  - **Akak**: the Armenian form of Acacius, the catholicos (TM 36711, 36823; ARB 3884).
  - **concubine**: Bar Hebraeus's word; gloss "a woman kept without marriage". Say it is his word (BH 3758–3762).
  - **Mar** (in "the blessed Mar Barsauma"): a Syriac title of respect, "my lord"; or drop it.
  - **the Fathers** (Q3): "the church's founding teachers and early councils"; Simeon means the bishops of Nicaea and later councils (SIM 75099–75104).
  - **Apostles** (Q3): "Jesus's first followers".
  - **ordained / ordination**: "made a deacon or priest".
  - **Syriac Orthodox**: "the rival Syriac church, which rejected the two-natures teaching" (TM 36838–36842 for the Armenian rejection; census `simeon-of-beth-arsham` "Syriac Orthodox side").
  - **the East** (Q2): his church's lands in Persia, not "the East" in today's sense.
  - **Nestorian**: do not use (see Do not say).
- **Shape:** Founder or leader. Trap: titles and offices in place of acts. Keep to what he did: the synod he led, the letters he wrote, the rules that outlasted him.
- **Sibling:** `narsai` (on the tree) holds the school of Nisibis; here it is one clause: Barsauma asked Narsai to stay and found a school, and the school counts both as founders (E4). Reuse none of its quotes; its "Edessa grew dark; Nisibis grew bright" line is not to be used. `babai-the-great` (batch 5) is a different man from the catholicos Babowai of this story, and from the catholicos Babai of 497 (D6). No shared event.
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** The book that left one synod out. The Church of the East gathered its synods into one book. Its compiler wrote that he had left one out on purpose: the synod Barsauma held at Beth Lapat, because Barsauma himself had said it was held "out of ill will and human passion" (I1; SO 20183, 20257–20259). It leads straight into the essence: a synod struck from the book, whose rules the church kept anyway. The narrator may say the compiler worked about three centuries later.
  2. Not used as the opening: the stormy sea (Q1). It goes in beat 4, where it falls in time.

**Story beats** (the opening and beats 1 to 2 are the first minute, about 150 words; beats 3 to 6 deepen it)

1. **Who he was.** A Persian who studied at the school of the Persians in Edessa, in the Roman empire, among students loyal to Ibas, bishop of Edessa, who, Simeon says, handed on the teaching of the Greek teacher Theodore of Mopsuestia (E1; SIM 75044–75046; Documented as Simeon of Beth Arsham's account, hostile). Driven out of Edessa with the other Persians after Ibas died, by Simeon's account, he became bishop of Nisibis (E2). When he became bishop is disputed, 435 or after 457 (D1; Contested): do not give a year. A later historian of his own church says the catholicos picked him to brief the Persian king on Roman affairs (E3; Contested, Mari, 1100s). He welcomed the teacher Narsai and asked him to found a school at Nisibis; the school counts both as its founders (E4; Documented as the school's account). One clause.
2. **The rebel synod, April 484.** With bishops at odds with their catholicos, Babowai, he held a synod at Beth Lapat, a city near Susa (E5, E6; TM-ed 36721; LAB 8326 "la métropole du Huzistan"). Why: by his own later account, "Satan stirred up a quarrel" between him and Babowai, and bishops whom Babowai meant to judge urged him on (E5; Documented, his words). What it did: it drew up a written case against the catholicos, "murmurs, blames, charges and testimonies" (E5; his words). It also made rules: on clergy marriage (I2), and that no one should doubt Theodore of Mopsuestia, "the first among the teachers" (E6; known only as quoted by a synod of 605). That same year Babowai was put to death by the king. Barsauma's part is disputed: a later historian of his own church says he passed Babowai's letter to the king and then regretted it; writers of a rival church say he had him killed; his own letters speak only of the quarrel (E7, D2; Contested). Tell all three in a sentence or two, each credited. **His own marriage** (Mark, 2026-11-18; E13, D4; Contested), told here, after Babowai's death, where a historian of his own church places it, and beside Beth Lapat's marriage rule. In one or two sentences, both versions, each credited with its date: Mari, a historian of his own church in the 1100s, says he married a nun named Mammaea, and that the Persian king ordered him to take a wife, as was the Persians' custom, and he did not dare refuse (MARI 14287–14290); Bar Hebraeus, a chronicler of the rival Syriac Orthodox church in the 1200s, says he kept a woman in his cell, a "concubine", and called her his lawful wife (BH 3758–3763). Their opponents used it against him: a hostile chronicle of the 1100s, built on old men's memories, says he made the marriage rule so he would not be blamed for the woman he kept (MS 33595–33597). Credit that charge to it; the narrator does not carry it.
3. **The climb-down, 485.** The king Peroz died; a new catholicos was elected, Acacius, who had studied with him at Edessa, as Mari and Bar Hebraeus both say (S5, E8). At Beth Edrai, a village in the north (LAB 8440, 8472), in August 485, Barsauma took back everything done at Beth Lapat and made peace with Acacius (E8; Documented). In his first letter he wrote that he had wholly destroyed and annulled the book that synod made (E5; SO 35255–35258); in his second he promised to be a disciple of the see "for ever" (E9; SO 35381; his words).
4. **The frontier bishop, 485–486** (the craft scene). He would not come to the great synod: two years of drought; tribes from the south pillaging villages and even Roman land; a Roman army at the border (SO 35278–35284). To keep the peace he persuaded the Roman general to come to Nisibis to the Persian governor. While they ate, drank and celebrated, four hundred horsemen of a tribe subject to Persia raided Roman villages, and the Roman officers blamed him (E9; Documented, his letter). He reports that the governor told the king the bishop could not be spared from the border talks (E9). In another letter, Nisibis is "tossed like a stormy sea" (Q1). He asks Acacius to strike his rebels with excommunication, "like the doctor who amputates", and to threaten to report them to the king (E10; Documented). And he writes that the East suffers whenever Nisibis does not obey the church's head (Q2).
5. **The synod of 486, Seleucia-Ctesiphon.** Barsauma stayed away but accepted its rulings in advance: "do not wait for us" (E11). It ruled that Christ is confessed in two natures, divine and human, without mixture, "perfect God and perfect man"; it ordered wandering men in monks' black, whom it called frauds teaching error, out of the towns and villages, back to the monasteries and the wild places, under the bishops; and it ruled that no bishop may stop his clergy from marrying, deacons included, and that priests may marry, and marry again if widowed. The reason it gave: scandal among the clergy had made them a mockery to outsiders (E11; Documented). Labourt, a French historian, reads the marriage rule as renewed from Beth Lapat (E11; modern view).
6. **The end.** By 491 he and Acacius were excommunicating each other again (E12; Documented). He died after that; the year is not known, and by 496 another bishop held Nisibis (E14; Documented as editors' reading). A hostile chronicler of the 1200s gives two reports: that nuns killed him with the keys of their cells, or that his tomb was shown at Nisibis (E14; late, hostile). Optional; if used, keep it in Bar Hebraeus's voice and short.

**What came of them** (about a third of the story; heaviest first)

1. **Married clergy, from the head of the church down (497).** DEFENDED. A synod in 497 allowed every cleric, "from the patriarch to the lowest", to marry openly one wife. It accepted the Beth Lapat synod "but only on this point" (I2; Documented). The 486 canon calls the rule it replaced "the old custom" (SO 19873). Chabot's note explains it as the rule, still kept by the Greek church in his day, that a deacon or priest may not marry after ordination (SO-ed 19909–19916, 19906; editor's note, Widely Accepted). Tell it after beat 6.
2. **Theodore as the church's teacher (605).** DEFENDED. More than a century later, a synod of the Church of the East quoted the annulled synod of Beth Lapat, "the divine assembly" led by "the blessed Mar Barsauma", and ruled that every teacher in its churches must study Theodore's writings (I3; Documented). This is the close.
3. **Seen from outside: a church apart.** About 506, Persian opponents of these synods, Simeon of Beth Arsham among them, told the Armenian bishops at Dvin that the true faith had held in Persia 'until the 27th year of Peroz', when 'Akak and Barsauma' and their companions held synods (TM 36702–36716). The Armenians' letter answers that they, the Greeks, the Iberians and the Albanians did not accept the canons that Akak and Barsauma had set up (TM 36823–36842). (I4; Documented as their statements.) Simeon of Beth Arsham, a bishop of the rival Syriac Orthodox side, wrote that in these synods they cut themselves off from the faith of the Fathers (Q3). Told inside beat 2 or here, once.
4. **The hostile legend.** Writers of the rival Syriac Orthodox church, drawing on a letter written from old men's memories about a century and a half later, said Barsauma told the Persian king that his Christians would never be loyal until their faith differed from the Romans'; that the king gave him soldiers; and that he killed thousands who refused (I5; Documented as hostile claims; the killings Contested). Tell it openly, credited, with its lateness, in about two sentences. The narrator does not judge it.
- **Who keeps this today:** leave out. No held source names Barsauma's keepers today.

**Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from Barsauma was a set of church rules, made in a synod he led and then renounced, that his church kept as its own. The synod of 497 accepted Beth Lapat "only on this point", married clergy, and allowed it to everyone up to the head of the church (I2). The synod of 605 quoted Beth Lapat as "the divine assembly" of "the blessed Mar Barsauma" and made Theodore's writings required study for every teacher (I3). The listener must understand three things the record holds: the Persian church already governed itself (since 424), so what these synods set apart was its teaching and its discipline, not its government; Barsauma took back the whole synod, its canons included: 'so that no one after us may use these canons' (SO 35460). Yet the church later accepted its marriage rule (497) and quoted its ruling on Theodore (605); and neighbours who rejected those rules, Armenian and Syriac Orthodox, named him as a cause of the split (I4, I5). Carry it through the 497 and 605 acts, told as acts, not with a "this matters because" sentence.

**Ending:** one of these, from the record.
1. **Chosen.** The opening image seen again: the synod the compiler left out of the book was still quoted inside it. In 605 the bishops called it "the divine assembly" and its leader "the blessed Mar Barsauma" (I3; SO 31682–31686).
2. His own promise in letter 3, "as long as I live", set against 491 (E12). Only if told in time order in beat 3 or 4; do not go back to it at the end.

**Who tells it**

- Barsauma himself: six letters to Acacius and to bishops, kept in his church's book of synods (OWN, 485–491). The heart of the story. Say once that these letters are the nearest witness.
- The church's own synods of 486, 497 and 605: its official acts (Documented). The compiler of the book, about three centuries on, explains why Beth Lapat is missing.
- Mari ibn Sulayman, a historian of his own church in the 1100s: friendly, late, inexact in dates. Credit the "brief the king" claim and the "passed the letter, then regretted it" claim to "a later historian of his own church".
- Simeon of Beth Arsham, the Persian delegates at Dvin, and the Armenian bishops: hostile, within a generation. Credit the "27th year of Peroz" claim to the Persian delegates, Simeon among them, not to the Armenian bishops; credit the refusal of the canons to the Armenian bishops (TM 36702–36716, 36823–36842). Credit by name or role.
- Bar Hebraeus (1200s) and Michael the Syrian (1100s), of the rival Syriac Orthodox church: hostile, late, built on a letter written from old men's memories some 150 years after (I5). Credit as "writers of the rival Syriac Orthodox church". Say they drew on oral memories a century and a half later.
- The doubt is part of the story only at Babowai's death (beat 2) and the hostile legend (What came 4).

**Do not say**

- Not "Barsauma made the Church of the East independent" or "broke it from Rome". It governed itself from 424 (S4). Say his synods fixed its teaching and rules.
- Not "Barsauma founded the Nestorian Church". "Nestorian" is its opponents' name (census tradition entry). Connolly's "began ... the definite formation of what is now known as the Nestorian Church" is an editor's view: leave it out.
- Not "Barsauma founded the school of Nisibis" alone. He asked Narsai to found it; the school counts both as founders (E4).
- Not "Barsauma had Babowai killed" or "betrayed him to the king" as fact. Credit each version to its writer (D2).
- Not "Beth Lapat deposed the catholicos". His own words: a written case of "murmurs, blames, charges and testimonies" against him (E5).
- Not 'he took back only the attack on the catholicos' or 'he kept its rules': he renounced its canons too (SO 35455–35461).
- Not "Barsauma's synod of 486" or "Barsauma attended the synod of 486". He stayed away and accepted it in advance (E11).
- The 486 rule barred bishops from stopping clergy marrying, made a lawful marriage a condition for ordaining new deacons, and sent those who chose not to marry to the monasteries. The 497 rule allowed everyone, up to the head of the church, to marry. Not 'forced every priest to marry'. Not 'it only allowed marriage' either: for new deacons it expected it (SO 19878–19900, 19937–19939).
- Not "the Beth Lapat synod made Theodore the church's teacher" as if its full acts survive. Its ruling is known only as quoted in 605 (E6, I3).
- Not "he killed 7,700 people" or "thousands" as fact. Say "writers of the rival church, a century and a half and more later, said he killed thousands". Keep "said" (I5, D3).
- Not "he was governor of Nisibis" or "commander of the frontier troops". Chabot hedges the first ("semble-t-il"); the second is Labourt's (E3).
- Not "in 435" or "in 457" as the year he became bishop (D1).
- Not "the Henoticon" or "Zeno's decree" as what his letter meant by "the confusion Satan stirred in the land of the Romans". That is Chabot's guess (S6).
- Do not drop the force: he asked Acacius to excommunicate his rebels "like the doctor who amputates" and to threaten to report them to the king (E10). Keep it.
- Not "the Roman general dined with Barsauma". He brought the general to Nisibis, to the Persian governor; the raid came while they feasted (E9).
- Not "Babai" for the catholicos of 484. Use "Babowai" (Mark, 2026-11-18), never "Babai the Great".
- Not "the East" without its plain sense in Q2: his church's lands in Persia.
- Do not date the stormy-sea letter (Q1, Q2). Chabot thinks it may belong to another time than the other letters (SO-ed 35625–35628). Say "in another letter".
- Not "Acacius was Babowai's relative" unless credited; that is Mari's and Chabot's note. It is not needed.

- On his marriage, do not choose a side. Not "he married a nun" or "he kept a concubine" as fact: "Mari says", "Bar Hebraeus says". Not "he changed the rule to suit himself", "conveniently" or "secretly", unless credited to the hostile chronicle that says so. Not "the king forced him", "he married for love" or "a chaste marriage" as fact; Mari's chaste-marriage line is only "it is said" (MARI 14210). Not "Mamoe" as settled: use "Mammaea", Mari's form in Gismondi's Latin. Not "the nuns killed him because of it": no source links the two.
- Do not date the marriage beyond "after Babowai's death", Mari's placing.
- Each word under "Words to explain" used without its plain sense the first time it is heard.

**Leave out**

- Mari's further detail on the marriage: Peroz's law stripping unmarried women of their goods, the nun lending her estate to the church, the white dress, the claim it opened the way to second marriages for priests (MARI 14199–14213, 14293–14299): too much at this length, and much of it "it is said".
- Labourt's view that he "certainly" married after Beth Lapat: a modern reading; the beat follows Mari's placing instead.
- The quarrel with Narsai over a woman: Narsai's story, and contested.
- The embassy to Zeno, his profession of faith, and the physician Gabriel ('Amr, 1300s): late legend.
- The monk Theodulus calling him "the light of the East" (Mari): legend.
- Acacius's proof of his chastity (Mari): side story, and not fit for the register.
- Details of the hostile legend beyond two sentences: the burning of Mar Mattai, the ninety priests, Armenia, the nuns' keys at Karmah (MS). Do not pile them up.
- The 100 darics he sent Acacius (E-letter 6): true, his own words, but a side detail at this length.
- The fragments on simony and on metropolitans named by the king's letter (E6): real but known only from a writer of the 1000s; too fine for this length.
- The nickname "swimming among the nests" (Simeon): side detail.
- That he was still at Edessa in 449: rests on editors citing a text not held.
