# Outline: Augustine of Hippo

Sheet references (S, E, I, L, D, G) point to `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder, which gives each file and line.

- **The essence:** A teacher of rhetoric who sold, in his own words, the art of talking to win, Augustine turned to faith in Milan and became bishop of Hippo, a port town in Roman North Africa (E3, E5–E11). There he wrote the Confessions and the City of God, and taught that God's grace comes first: no one turns to God, or even begins to believe, unless God gives it (E12–E16). Confidence: Documented (his own words; Possidius, his friend). That the Western church carried this teaching on, at a council at Orange in 529 and in Luther, Calvin and the Jansenists, is Documented for each witness; that Orange's chapters came from his writings is its editors' finding, Widely Accepted (I1, I3, I4, D5).

- **Also told:**
  - **The rhetorician's turn.** He taught rhetoric at Carthage, Rome and Milan; a speech praising the emperor, full of lies, made him envy a cheerful beggar; he gave up the post, for his choice to serve God and for pain in his chest (E3–E5, E7). Documented.
  - **The bishop.** At Hippo the crowd seized him and brought him to be made a priest while he wept; the old bishop, Greek-born and less at home in Latin, let him preach, against African custom; he was made bishop beside him, and later said it should not have been done (E9–E11). Documented as Possidius's account, from what Augustine told him.
  - **What the Confessions are.** Thirteen books addressed to God, praising him for Augustine's bad and good deeds; ten tell his own life, three turn to Scripture; he says they still moved him when he read them (E13). Documented.
  - **What the City of God says.** After the Goths sacked Rome, pagans blamed the Christian religion; he wrote twenty-two books over several years, answering them and telling of two cities made by two loves (E14, E15). Documented.
  - **What the grace teaching held.** Early in his time as bishop he changed his own mind: he had argued for the human will, "but God's grace won" (E12). Against Pelagius he taught that free will and good teaching are not enough; God's Spirit must give the love of the good (E16). African councils, two bishops of Rome and the emperor's laws condemned the Pelagians, in Possidius's account (E17). Documented.
  - **What came of it.** Orange (529) set out chapters on grace drawn largely from his writings (editor); Luther sent an opponent to his *On the Spirit and the Letter*; Calvin said he could build a whole volume from him; Jansen's book *Augustinus* was condemned at Rome (I1, I3, I4). The teaching both shaped and divided the Western church.

- **Length:** about 3 minutes, about 450 words. Reason: a major figure on the tree (CEN `major`), a very rich record, and a heart with three works and a long afterlife that needs room to be understood. Kept to 3 minutes by giving the Manichaean years, the garden and the Donatists one clause each, since other stories on the tree tell them.

- **Setting:**
  - Time: the late three-hundreds and early four-hundreds; he died in 430, at seventy-six (S2, S5, E22). Plain: "in the late Roman Empire".
  - Places: born at Thagaste in Roman Africa (S1); taught at Carthage, Rome and Milan, "where the emperor then held court" (S1); bishop at Hippo, "a port town in Roman North Africa, in what is now Algeria" (S3).
  - Tradition: "the Latin-speaking churches of North Africa" (S4). Do not say the census name aloud.

- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker. Trap to avoid: a list of books. Each work comes through a moment or his own words: the speech for the emperor, the two loves, "but God's grace won".

- **Sibling:**
  - `antony-the-great` (on the tree): its job is Antony; it tells the *Life of Antony* reaching Augustine in Milan, the officials at Trier, the garden, and quotes *Confessions* VIII.12.29. Here the garden is one clause, with no quote and no retelling.
  - `conference-of-carthage-411` (on the tree): its job is the hearing with the Donatists; Augustine is one of the Catholic speakers. Here at most a clause on his long quarrel with the Donatists.
  - Also on the tree, not to be retold: `ambrose-of-milan` (the "led to Ambrose" line), `possidius-of-calama` (the table prayer, the fever, "while we stood by and watched and prayed"), `pelagius` ("Give what Thou commandest"), `prosper-of-aquitaine` (the chronicle entry on his death), `port-royal-jansenism` (the five propositions), `manichaeism`.

- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** Milan, the emperor's city. A young professor of rhetoric is getting ready to give a speech in praise of the emperor; he knows it is full of lies, and that his listeners know it too (Q1). In the street he passes a beggar who has eaten well, joking and happy, and he envies him (E5). Then straight to the essence: the man who sold words gave them up, and became the bishop whose books the Western church argued over for a thousand years.
  2. In reserve: at Hippo, a visitor stands in the crowd in church; the people seize him and bring him to their bishop to be made a priest, while he weeps (E9). Usable if the writer opens on the turn from teacher to bishop; the rhetoric then comes in the next sentences, still inside the first minute.

- **Story beats** (beats 1–2 with the opening and the essence make the first minute, about 150 words):
  1. **The seller of words** (about 45 words). At nineteen a book of Cicero turned him toward the search for wisdom (E1); for nine years he followed the Manichaeans (E2: one clause). He taught rhetoric at Carthage, Rome and Milan, sent to Milan by the prefect of Rome after a trial speech (S1, E4). In his own words he sold the art of talking to win (E3). Documented as his own account.
  2. **Giving it up** (about 45 words). The day of the emperor's speech (Q1, E5). He heard Ambrose preach (E6). After a struggle he tells in the Confessions, he turned to faith in Milan in 386 (CEN `why`; the year is the editors', the garden is the Antony story's). He told Milan to find its students another teacher, because he had chosen to serve God and because of pain in his chest; Ambrose baptized him at Easter (E6, E7). Documented.
  3. **Seized at Hippo** (about 60 words). Home in Africa, he lived almost three years in common with friends who had given themselves to God (E8). Visiting Hippo, he was seized by the crowd and brought to Bishop Valerius to be made a priest, weeping. Some took the tears for wounded pride; he told Possidius he wept at the dangers of ruling a church (E9). Valerius, Greek by birth and less at home in Latin, let him preach, against African custom, and some bishops objected (E10). Then he was made bishop beside the living Valerius, and later said it should not have been done (E11). Documented as Possidius's account. "Coadjutor" is not needed: say "made bishop alongside the old bishop".
  4. **Grace wins, and the Confessions** (about 70 words). Early in his time as bishop, answering questions from Simplicianus, Ambrose's successor at Milan, he changed his mind (Q3, E12). He came to hold that even the beginning of faith is God's gift (E12, NP5 22523). A few years after becoming bishop he wrote the Confessions: thirteen books addressed to God, praising him for Augustine's bad and good deeds; ten tell his own life, three turn to Scripture. He wrote that they moved him as he wrote them, and still did when he read them (E13). Documented as his own words.
  5. **The City of God** (about 55 words). In 410 the Goths under their king Alaric sacked Rome. Pagans blamed the Christian religion, and Augustine, "burning with zeal for the house of God", began the City of God (E14). It took him years, through twenty-two books: the first ten answer the pagans, the last twelve tell of two cities, God's and the world's (E14, Q2). Documented as his own account; the year is the editor's (D4).
  6. **Against Pelagius** (about 60 words). In the same years he wrote against Pelagius and his followers, who held that God had given human nature the power to do good, and that grace meant outside help such as teaching (CEN `pelagius`). Augustine answered that free will and good teaching are not enough: God's Spirit must give a person delight in God and love for him (E16). Possidius says African councils persuaded two bishops of Rome to condemn them, and the emperor Honorius made them heretics by law (E17). Documented; the condemnations are the winning side's account (D6).
  7. **The siege** (about 40 words). The Vandals crossed from Spain and laid waste the African provinces, sparing, Possidius says, no one, man or woman, young or old, not even priests. They besieged Hippo for about fourteen months (E19; narration credited to Possidius, not a quote). In the third month he fell ill. He had the penitential psalms copied and hung on the wall, read them from his bed and wept; for his last ten days he asked to be left alone (E20). Documented as Possidius's eyewitness account. Death and ending: see Ending.

- **What came of him** (ranked heaviest first; about a third of the story):
  1. **The grace teaching became the West's.** A council at Orange in Gaul, in 529, set out chapters on grace and free will, most of them drawn from his writings, as its modern editor shows; Pope Boniface II confirmed it (I1, D5). Prosper of Aquitaine had defended him in Gaul, and says he died "defending the grace of Christ" (I2; link to the Prosper story, no quote). DEFENDED: the grace of Christ.
  2. **The reformers read him.** Martin Luther, in 1521, told an opponent that no teacher but Augustine, in *On the Spirit and the Letter*, would teach him rightly about letter and spirit (I3a). John Calvin wrote that Augustine had changed his mind on grace, and that he could build a whole volume out of Augustine's words alone (I3d).
  3. **And divided the church that kept him.** In the 1600s Cornelius Jansen, a Catholic bishop of Ypres, spent twenty years on a book he called *Augustinus*, on Augustine's teaching on grace; Rome condemned it, and in 1653 condemned five propositions said to be in it (I4; link to Port-Royal).
  4. **His books.** He ordered the church's library kept for future generations (E21). Two centuries on, Isidore of Seville wrote that he wrote so much no one could read it all, let alone copy it (I5). One clause at most, or card.
  Optional line on who keeps him today: none held in a source; leave it out.

- **Quotes (3):**
  - **Q1.** Job: the opening; the rhetorician's trade in his own voice. Source: *Confessions* VI.6.9. Latin AUGL 9309–9310: "cum pararem recitare imperatori laudes, quibus plura mentirer, et mentienti faueretur ab scientibus". Printed English AUG 9231–9240. **Rendering (from the Latin):** "I was getting ready to recite praises of the emperor. In them I would tell many lies, and people who knew it would applaud me as I lied."
  - **Q2.** Job: what the City of God says, in one image. Source: *City of God* XIV.28. Latin CDL 3380–3381, 3392: "Fecerunt itaque ciuitates duas amores duo, terrenam scilicet amor sui usque ad contemtum Dei, caelestem uero amor Dei usque ad contemtum sui." Printed English CDE 27268–27272. **Rendering (from the Latin):** "So two loves have made two cities. Love of self, even to contempt of God, made the earthly city. Love of God, even to contempt of self, made the heavenly city."
  - **Q3.** Job: the turn in his own thinking on grace, in his own words, looking back near the end of his life. Source: *Retractations* II.1, on his answer to Simplicianus. Latin RET 9059–9064: "in cuius quaestionis solutione laboratum est quidem pro libero arbitrio uoluntatis humanae, sed uicit dei gratia, nec nisi ad illud potuit perueniri, ut liquidissima ueritate dixisse intellegatur apostolus: quis enim te discernit? quid autem habes, quod non accepisti?" Printed English (his own reuse of it) NP5 20972. **Rendering (from the Latin):** "In answering this question, I worked hard for the free choice of the human will. But God's grace won. I could only come to see that the apostle spoke the plainest truth: 'Who sets you apart? What do you have that you did not receive?'" (The writer should say in the sentence before that the question was from Paul's letter to the Romans, and that he was answering a bishop of Milan.)

- **Connection to carry into the story:** What passed on is a teaching: that God's grace comes before anything a person does. Free will and good teaching are not enough; God must give the love of the good, and even the beginning of faith is his gift (E12, E16, Q3). It passed through his books, which he ordered kept (E21), through defenders such as Prosper (I2), into the chapters of a council at Orange in 529 (I1), and a thousand years later into Luther, Calvin and Jansen, who each read and claimed him (I3, I4). What the listener must be told: (1) what Pelagius held, in a clause, so the dispute is clear; (2) that Orange's chapters were drawn from his writings, by the modern editor's finding; (3) that later Western churches on opposite sides each claimed him, the reformers against Rome and Jansen inside it, and that Rome condemned what was said to be Jansen's reading. Show it through these acts; no "this matters because" sentence.

- **Ending:** close on the person, after the connection.
  1. **The wall and the library.** He made no will, Possidius says, because as a poor man of God he had nothing to leave. He told them again and again to keep the church's library and all its books for future generations (E21). Possidius closed by recalling a poet's epitaph: whoever reads the words aloud gives the writer his voice (E21; paraphrase, credited to Possidius; not a fourth quote). This returns to the opening's man of words.
  2. In reserve: the penitential psalms on the wall, read from his bed, weeping, until he died in the third month of the siege; Prosper gives the day, 28 August (E20, E22). Do not use "while we stood by and watched and prayed" (on the tree).

- **Who tells it:**
  - **Augustine himself**, in the Confessions, written as a bishop about ten years after the events in Milan and addressed to God (E13), and in the *Retractations*, written near the end of his life looking back over his books (E12, E14). Credit Q1 and the beggar to the Confessions, Q3 to his look back.
  - **Possidius of Calama**, his friend and fellow bishop for almost forty years, who was in Hippo during the siege (E19). Credit him beside the forced ordination, Valerius, the Pelagians' condemnation, the psalms on the wall, the will and the library. Say once that he was a friend writing to honor him (D6).
  - **Prosper of Aquitaine**, his defender, for the date and "defending the grace of Christ" (E22).
  - **Modern editors**: Friedrich Maassen for where Orange's chapters came from (I1); Neale (1858) for Jansen. Credit "its modern editor" where the claim is the editor's.

- **Do not say:**
  - Do not say he was born to Christian parents. Possidius says so, but Augustine says his father was only lately a catechumen (D1). Leave the parents out.
  - Do not retell the garden scene, the voice, or Antony's Gospel reading, and do not quote *Confessions* VIII.12; say only that he turned to faith in Milan in 386, after a struggle he tells in the Confessions (sibling).
  - Do not use "Give what you command", "led to Ambrose that Ambrose might lead him to God", or "while we stood by and watched and prayed": all are already told on the tree.
  - Do not say "the Confessions is his autobiography" without its form: say a book addressed to God, ten books on his life and three on Scripture (E13).
  - Do not give a year for his priesthood or for becoming bishop (D2). Say "a few years later".
  - Do not say he fought Pelagius "for twenty years" or "for ten years" as fact. If a span is given, it is Possidius's "almost ten years" (D3); otherwise "for the rest of his life", as Prosper shows.
  - Do not say the Pelagians were condemned by "the church" in general. Say African councils, two bishops of Rome (Innocent and Zosimus), and the emperor Honorius's laws, in Possidius's account (E17). Keep the emperor's law: it is the record's force.
  - Do not say Orange adopted all his teaching, or that it "made Augustinianism official". Say its chapters on grace and free will were drawn largely from his writings, as its modern editor shows (I1, D5).
  - Do not say Luther's line was about grace. He sent his opponent to *On the Spirit and the Letter* on the meaning of "letter" and "spirit" in Scripture (I3a).
  - Do not say Calvin "could fill a book with Augustine" as a boast of agreement on everything; he said it of the teaching on God's choice and grace (I3d, CAL 25389–25391).
  - Do not say Jansen read Augustine's works twenty times as fact; it "is said" (I4). Do not say the five propositions were Augustine's; they were said to be in Jansen's book.
  - Do not call him "the Doctor of Grace" as an ancient title; it is Neale's phrase for how he was regarded (I4).
  - Do not say "Rome fell" or "the end of the empire". Say the Goths under their king Alaric sacked Rome (E14), in 410 (editor's year).
  - Do not say Valerius "could not speak Latin". Say he was Greek by birth and less at home in Latin (E10).
  - Do not say he died when Hippo fell. He died in the third month of a siege that lasted about fourteen months; the city was burned later (E19, E21).
  - Do not explain the tears at his ordination one way only: some took them for pride; he said he foresaw the dangers (E9).
  - Do not add feeling or motive the record does not give: he "envied" the beggar is in his own account (E5: "he was joyous, I anxious"); his reason for leaving the post is both his choice and his chest (E7).
