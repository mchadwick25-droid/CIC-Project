# Outline: Augustine of Hippo

Sheet references (S, E, I, L, D, G) point to `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder, which gives each file and line.

- **The essence:** Augustine taught rhetoric, the art of persuasive public speaking, and in his own words sold the skill of talking to win (E3). In a garden in Milan, by his own account, a child's voice sent him to the Bible, and he turned to Christ (E6a). He became bishop, the leader of the church, in Hippo, a port town in Roman North Africa (E9–E11). There he wrote the Confessions, his life told as a prayer to God, and the City of God. He taught that grace, God's free and unearned gift, comes first: no one turns to God, or even begins to believe, unless God gives it (E12–E16). Confidence: Documented (his own words; Possidius, his friend). The churches of western Europe carried this teaching on: a meeting of bishops at Orange in 529, then Luther, Calvin and the Jansenists. Each witness is Documented; that Orange's rulings came from his writings is its editors' finding, Widely Accepted (I1, I3, I4, D5).

- **Also told:**
  - **The speaker's turn.** He taught public speaking at Carthage, Rome and Milan. A speech praising the emperor, full of lies, made him envy a cheerful beggar (E3–E5). Documented.
  - **The garden, as the Confessions tell it.** Weeping under a fig tree, he heard a child next door chanting "Take it and read." He opened Paul's letters at a line telling him to put on Christ. He and his friend went in and told his mother, Monica, who rejoiced (E6a, E6b). He gave up his post, because he had chosen to serve God and because of pain in his chest (E7). Documented as his own account, written about ten years later.
  - **The bishop.** At Hippo the crowd seized him and brought him to be made a priest, a minister under the bishop, while he wept. The old bishop, Greek-born and less at home in Latin, let him preach, which African priests did not usually do. He was then made bishop alongside the old man, and later said it should not have been done (E9–E11). Documented as Possidius's account, from what Augustine told him.
  - **What the Confessions are.** "Confess" here means both praising God and owning up to sin. The book is thirteen parts addressed to God, praising him for Augustine's bad and good deeds. Ten tell his own life; three turn to the Bible. He says they still moved him when he read them (E13). Documented.
  - **Force against the Donatists.** The Donatists were a rival African church that called itself the only pure one. At first, he wrote, he held that no one should be forced into the church; argument alone should win. His fellow bishops changed his mind by pointing to his own town, brought back by fear of the emperors' laws. He defended that force from the Bible's "compel them to come in" (E17a). Documented, in his own letter of about 408 (editor's date).
  - **What the City of God says.** After the Goths, a Germanic people, sacked Rome, worshippers of the old Roman gods blamed the Christians. He wrote twenty-two books over several years to answer them, telling of two cities made by two loves: love of self and love of God (E14, E15). Documented.
  - **What the grace teaching held.** Early as bishop he changed his own mind: he had argued for the human will's own power to choose, "but God's grace won" (E12). Pelagius, a monk teaching at Rome, held that God had already given human nature the power to do good. Against him Augustine taught that free choice and good teaching are not enough; God's Spirit must give the love of the good (E16). In Possidius's account, meetings of African bishops, two bishops of Rome and the emperor's laws condemned the Pelagians as false teachers (E17). Documented.
  - **What came of it.** At Orange in Gaul, now France, bishops in 529 set out rulings on grace drawn largely from his writings (editor). Luther sent an opponent to his book *On the Spirit and the Letter*. Calvin said he could build a whole volume from Augustine's words. Jansen, a Catholic bishop, wrote a book called *Augustinus*, and Rome condemned it (I1, I3, I4). The teaching both shaped and divided the churches of the West.

- **Length:** about 3 minutes 30, about 520 words (Mark, 2026-11-18: "the 3 minutes is a pressure not a cap"). Reason: a major figure with a very rich record; the heart has the rhetoric, the garden, the two books, the force against the Donatists and the grace teaching with its long afterlife. The garden and the Donatist beat add about 70 words; nothing is filled to reach the length. The Manichaean years and the Carthage hearing stay one clause each, since other stories on the tree tell them.

- **Setting:**
  - Time: the late three-hundreds and early four-hundreds; he died in 430, at seventy-six (S2, S5, E22). Plain: "in the late Roman Empire".
  - Places: born at Thagaste in Roman Africa (S1); taught at Carthage, Rome and Milan, "where the emperor then held court" (S1); bishop at Hippo, "a port town in Roman North Africa, in what is now Algeria" (S3).
  - Tradition: "the Latin-speaking churches of North Africa" (S4). Do not say the census name aloud.

- **Words to explain** (each glossed in the sentence where it is first heard; or use the plain word):
  - **rhetoric**: the art of persuasive public speaking, a school subject he taught for pay. Plain word: "public speaking" (E3, AUGL 5745–5746; POS 1609–1611).
  - **bishop**: here the leader of the church in one town, chosen with its people's loud consent, not a distant official (E11, POS 2133–2137).
  - **priest** (Possidius's "presbyter"): a minister serving under the town's bishop. In Africa then, priests did not usually preach before their bishop (E10, POS 1883–1885).
  - **Manichaeans**: followers of Mani, a Persian teacher who taught a war between light and darkness (CEN `manichaeism`).
  - **baptized**: washed in water to join the church; adults then were usually baptized at Easter (E6, POS 1671–1673).
  - **grace**: God's free gift of help, given though no one has earned it (E12, E16).
  - **free will / free choice of the will**: a person's own power to choose good or evil (Q3, E16).
  - **Confessions**: in Latin "confession" is both praise of God and admitting sin; his own description says the books "praise" God (E13, RET 9401–9403).
  - **Scripture**: the Bible.
  - **the apostle** (Q3): Paul, whose letters are in the Bible (RET 9063–9064, 1 Cor 4:7).
  - **pagans**: people who worshipped the old Roman gods; his own words: "worshippers of false gods" (E14, CDE 769–770).
  - **City of God**: his name for all who love God above themselves, as against the "earthly city" (Q2, CDE 27268–27272).
  - **Pelagius, Pelagians**: a British-born monk teaching at Rome, and his followers, who held that God had given human nature the power to do good (CEN `pelagius`, `pelagianism`).
  - **council**: a meeting of bishops that made rulings for the churches. Plain: "a meeting of bishops".
  - **bishops of Rome**: use this, not "popes" (Possidius's words, E17); gloss once: "the leaders of the church at Rome".
  - **condemned as heretics**: officially judged false teachers and cut off from the church; here the emperor's law also punished them (E17, POS 3010–3021).
  - **penitential psalms**: songs from the Bible's Psalms asking God's forgiveness for sin (E20; editor lists them, POS 5963).
  - **Gaul**: roughly modern France.
  - **Orange's "chapters"**: short rulings, statements of what the church teaches (I1). Plain: "rulings".
  - ***On the Spirit and the Letter***: "letter" is the written law that commands; "spirit" is God's Spirit giving love to keep it (E16; NP5 1055, editor's summary).
  - **Catholic** (Jansen): a member of the church in union with the bishop of Rome. Here distinct from Luther's and Calvin's churches.
  - **Jansenists**: Catholics, mainly in France, who followed Jansen's reading of Augustine on grace (I4, CEN `port-royal-jansenism`).
  - **five propositions**: five short statements; plain word "five statements" (I4).
  - **Goths, Vandals**: Germanic peoples who invaded the Roman Empire (E14, E19).
  - **Donatists**: a rival church in Roman Africa, for a century the larger one. It held that bishops who had handed over the Scriptures in persecution had lost the true church, and it baptized converts again (CEN `donatism`). Plain gloss: "a rival African church that called itself the only pure one".
  - **unity of Christ / Catholic unity** (Q2): the one church in communion across the empire, as against the Donatist church (E17a).
  - **heretics** (Q2): people judged to teach falsely; here Augustine's word for the Donatists.
  - **imperial edicts**: the emperors' laws (E17a).
  - **the volume of the apostles**: the book of Paul's letters (E6a, AUG 13126). Plain: "Paul's letters".
  - Not to be used at all: "coadjutor", "catechumen", "panegyric", "semi-Pelagian", "Doctor of Grace".

- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker. Trap to avoid: a list of books. Each work comes through a moment or his own words: the speech for the emperor, the two loves, "but God's grace won".

- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** Milan, the emperor's city. A young professor of rhetoric is getting ready to give a speech in praise of the emperor. He tells in the Confessions that it was full of lies, and that those who knew it would cheer him as he lied (E5; narration, not a quote). In the street he passes a beggar who has eaten well, joking and happy, and he envies him. Then straight to the essence.
  2. In reserve: at Hippo, a visitor stands in the crowd in church; the people seize him and bring him to their bishop to be made a priest, while he weeps (E9). Usable if the writer opens on the turn from teacher to bishop; the rhetoric then comes in the next sentences, still inside the first minute.

- **Story beats** (beat 1 with the opening and the essence makes the first minute, about 150 words):
  1. **The seller of words** (about 45 words). At nineteen a book of Cicero turned him toward the search for wisdom (E1); for nine years he followed the Manichaeans (E2: one clause). He taught rhetoric at Carthage, Rome and Milan, sent to Milan by the prefect of Rome after a trial speech (S1, E4). In his own words he sold the skill of talking to win (E3). He heard Ambrose, bishop of Milan, preach (E6). Documented as his own account.
  2. **The garden** (about 90 words). In Milan, in 386 (census year), torn and weeping, he left his friend Alypius and threw himself down under a fig tree. "How long? Tomorrow, and tomorrow? Why not now?" he cried (E6a; narration of his words may stand without quote marks). Then he heard a child's voice from the house next door (Q1). He opened Paul's letters and read the first lines he saw: not drunken revels or quarrels, but put on the Lord Jesus Christ (E6a; plain sense, not a quote). He and Alypius went in and told his mother, Monica, who had followed him to Milan; she rejoiced (E6b). He told Milan to find its students another teacher, because he had chosen to serve God and because of pain in his chest; Ambrose baptized him at Easter (E6, E7). Documented as his own account. Keep it to his own moment: no Antony, no Trier.
  3. **Seized at Hippo** (about 60 words). Home in Africa, he lived almost three years in common with friends who had given themselves to God (E8). Visiting Hippo, he was seized by the crowd and brought to Bishop Valerius to be made a priest, weeping. Some took the tears for wounded pride; he told Possidius he wept at the dangers of ruling a church (E9). Valerius, Greek by birth and less at home in Latin, let him preach, against African custom (E10). Then he was made bishop beside the living Valerius, and later said it should not have been done (E11). Documented as Possidius's account.
  4. **Grace wins, and the Confessions** (about 70 words). Early in his time as bishop, answering questions from Simplicianus, Ambrose's successor at Milan, he changed his mind (Q3, E12). He came to hold that even the beginning of faith is God's gift (E12). A few years after becoming bishop he wrote the Confessions: thirteen books addressed to God, praising him for Augustine's bad and good deeds; ten tell his own life, including the garden, three turn to the Bible. He wrote that they moved him as he wrote them, and still did when he read them (E13). Documented as his own words.
  5. **Force against the Donatists** (about 60 words). Hippo had a rival Donatist church, and he argued with it for years (E18; Carthage 411 one clause at most, sibling). In a letter of about 408 to a Donatist bishop, Vincentius, he explained why he now backed the emperors' laws against them (Q2, E17a). His fellow bishops had pointed to his own town, brought back to the church by fear of those laws. He cited Jesus's parable: "compel them to come in" (E17a; may be narrated). Documented as his own words. Tell the force plainly, credited to him.
  6. **The City of God, and Pelagius** (about 80 words). In 410 the Goths under their king Alaric sacked Rome. Worshippers of the old gods blamed the Christian religion, and Augustine began the City of God; it took him years, through twenty-two books. Two loves, he wrote, made two cities: love of self, even to contempt of God, the earthly; love of God, even to contempt of self, the heavenly (E14, E15; narration credited to him, not a quote). In the same years he wrote against Pelagius and his followers, who held that God had given human nature the power to do good (CEN `pelagius`). Augustine answered that free will and teaching are not enough: God's Spirit must give delight in God and love for him (E16). Possidius says African councils persuaded two bishops of Rome to condemn them, and the emperor Honorius made them heretics by law (E17). Documented; the condemnations are the winning side's account (D6).
  7. **The siege** (about 40 words). The Vandals crossed from Spain and laid waste the African provinces, sparing, Possidius says, no one, man or woman, young or old, not even priests. They besieged Hippo for about fourteen months (E19; narration credited to Possidius, not a quote). In the third month he fell ill. He had the penitential psalms copied and hung on the wall, read them from his bed and wept; for his last ten days he asked to be left alone (E20). Documented as Possidius's eyewitness account. Death and ending: see Ending.

- **What came of him** (ranked heaviest first; about a third of the story):
  1. **The grace teaching became the West's.** A council at Orange in Gaul, in 529, set out chapters on grace and free will, most of them drawn from his writings, as its modern editor shows; Pope Boniface II confirmed it (I1, D5). Prosper of Aquitaine had defended him in Gaul, and says he died "defending the grace of Christ" (I2; link to the Prosper story, no quote). DEFENDED: the grace of Christ.
  2. **The reformers read him.** Martin Luther, in 1521, told an opponent that no teacher but Augustine, in *On the Spirit and the Letter*, would teach him rightly about letter and spirit (I3a). John Calvin wrote that Augustine had changed his mind on grace, and that he could build a whole volume out of Augustine's words alone (I3d).
  3. **And divided the church that kept him.** In the 1600s Cornelius Jansen, a Catholic bishop of Ypres, spent twenty years on a book he called *Augustinus*, on Augustine's teaching on grace; Rome condemned it, and in 1653 condemned five propositions said to be in it (I4; link to Port-Royal).
  4. **His books.** He ordered the church's library kept for future generations (E21). Two centuries on, Isidore of Seville wrote that he wrote so much no one could read it all, let alone copy it (I5). One clause at most, or card.
  Optional line on who keeps him today: none held in a source; leave it out.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** What passed on is a teaching: that God's grace comes before anything a person does. Free will and good teaching are not enough; God must give the love of the good, and even the beginning of faith is his gift (E12, E16, Q3). It passed through his books, which he ordered kept (E21), through defenders such as Prosper (I2), into the chapters of a council at Orange in 529 (I1), and a thousand years later into Luther, Calvin and Jansen, who each read and claimed him (I3, I4). What the listener must be told: (1) what Pelagius held, in a clause, so the dispute is clear; (2) that Orange's chapters were drawn from his writings, by the modern editor's finding; (3) that later Western churches on opposite sides each claimed him, the reformers against Rome and Jansen inside it, and that Rome condemned what was said to be Jansen's reading. Show it through these acts; no "this matters because" sentence.

- **Ending:** close on the person, after the connection.

He told them again and again to keep the church's library and all its books for future generations (E21). Possidius closed by recalling a poet's epitaph: whoever reads the words aloud gives the writer his voice (E21; paraphrase, credited to Possidius; not a fourth quote). This returns to the opening's man of words.
  2. In reserve: the penitential psalms on the wall, read from his bed, weeping, until he died in the third month of the siege; Prosper gives the day, 28 August (E20, E22). Do not use "while we stood by and watched and prayed" (on the tree).
