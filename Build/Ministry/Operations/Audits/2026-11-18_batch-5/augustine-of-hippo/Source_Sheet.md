## 1. Setting

S1. **Birthplace and schooling.** "Augustine was born in the African province, in the city of Tagaste of honorable Christian parents of curial rank ... educated chiefly in secular literature ... For he first taught grammar in his own town and then rhetoric at Carthage, the capitol of Africa. Subsequently [he taught] across the sea in the city of Rome and at Milan where the Emperor Valentinian the Younger had then established his court." (POS ch. I, 1606–1614; Latin 1565–1576). CONTEMP. Documented. "Christian parents" disagrees with the *Confessions* (D1).
S2. **Birth date and age.** Weiskotten: born "November 13, 354" (POS 134, EDITOR), from Augustine's own *De beata vita* (POS 152–153). Possidius: "he lived seventy-six years, almost forty of which he spent as a priest or bishop" (POS ch. XXXI, 4919–4921). CONTEMP. Documented for the age; 354 is Widely Accepted (editor's reckoning from Augustine's own day of birth plus Possidius's age).
S3. **Place words.** Census: "Hippo Regius · Milan"; modern Algeria and Italy (CEN 79940–80002, `placeLabel`, `modernCountries`). Plain spoken forms: "Hippo, a port town in Roman North Africa, in what is now Algeria"; "Milan, where the emperor then held court" (POS 1613–1614).
S4. **Tradition.** Census `toldWithin`: `latin-pastoral-congregational-christianity`, informal name **"The Latin Pastoral Congregations"** (CEN 32960 entry; `informalName`). Spoken: "the Latin-speaking churches of North Africa". Do not say the census proper name aloud.
S5. **Century.** The record dates by rulers: Valentinian the Younger's court at Milan (POS 1613–1614); the Goths under Alaric at Rome (RET 12158–12159); Honorius (POS 3018); the Vandals (POS 3946, Latin 3946ff). Prosper dates his death to 28 August (V. kal. Sept.) in the year Mommsen prints as 430 (PRC 54206–54207). Plain: "in the late three-hundreds and early four-hundreds".

## 2. Life events (in time order)

E1. **The book that turned him toward wisdom, at nineteen.** "In the ordinary course of study, I lighted upon a certain book of Cicero ... This book of his contains an exhortation to philosophy, and is called *Hortensius*. This book, in truth, changed my affections, and turned my prayers to Thyself, O Lord" (Conf. III.4.7, AUG 5639–5644; Latin AUGL 4767–4775). "in that my nineteenth year" (AUG 5655–5656). OWN. Documented.
E2. **Nine years among the Manichaeans.** "During this space of nine years, then, from my nineteenth to my eight and twentieth year, we went on seduced and seducing, deceived and deceiving" (Conf. IV.1.1, AUG 6334–6337). Possidius: "while still a youth at Carthage, he had been carried away by the error of the Manichaeans" (POS 1661–1663). OWN and CONTEMP. Documented. The tree's Manichaeism story already tells this (CEN, `manichaeism`); one clause here.
E3. **Selling words.** "In those years I taught the art of rhetoric, and, overcome by cupidity, put to sale a loquacity by which to overcome." (Conf. IV.2.2, AUG 6379–6381). Latin: "Docebam in illis annis artem rhetoricam et uictoriosam loquacitatem uictus cupiditate uendebam." (AUGL 5745–5746). Plain sense: he sold, for money, the art of talking to win. OWN. Documented.
E4. **Milan, through the city prefect.** "When, therefore, they of Milan had sent to Rome to the prefect of the city, to provide them with a teacher of rhetoric for their city ... that Symmachus, the then prefect, having proved me by proposing a subject, would send me. And to Milan I came, unto Ambrose the bishop" (Conf. V.13.23, AUG 8514–8521). OWN. Documented. (The next line, "led to him ... that by him I might knowingly be led to Thee", is already told on the tree in the Ambrose story; do not reuse.)
E5. **The panegyric and the beggar (the opening).** "How wretched was I at that time, and how didst Thou deal with me, to make me sensible of my wretchedness on that day wherein I was preparing to recite a panegyric on the Emperor, wherein I was to deliver many a lie, and lying was to be applauded by those who knew I lied ... For, while walking along one of the streets of Milan, I observed a poor mendicant,—then, I imagine, with a full belly,—joking and joyous" (Conf. VI.6.9, AUG 9229–9241). Latin: "quam ergo miser eram et quomodo egisti, ut sentirem miseriam meam die illo, quo, cum pararem recitare imperatori laudes, quibus plura mentirer, et mentienti faueretur ab scientibus easque curas anhelaret cor meum ... transiens per quendam uicum Mediolanensem animaduerti pauperem mendicum iam, credo, saturum iocantem atque laetantem." (AUGL 9307–9314). "And in truth he was joyous, I anxious; he free from care, I full of alarms." (AUG 9254–9255). OWN. Documented. Editor's note: the emperor was "probably Valentinian the younger" (AUG 9232–9234, EDITOR; Benedictine suggestion).
E6. **Ambrose and baptism.** Possidius: "As he stood among the people in the church he used to listen in eager suspense to the frequent sermons of this preacher of the Word of God ... with the coming of the holy days of Easter he received the water of baptism. And thus it happened that by divine grace he received through the great and illustrious prelate Ambrose the salutary doctrine of the Catholic Church and the divine Sacraments." (POS ch. I, 1657–1674). CONTEMP. Documented. The garden in Milan (386) is told in full on the tree in the Antony story; here one clause only, without its quote.
E6a. **The garden in Milan (his own account; added 2026-11-18 on Mark's ruling).** In tears he left his friend Alypius and "flung myself down, how, I know not, under a certain fig-tree, giving free course to my tears" (Conf. VIII.12.28, AUG 13095–13096; Latin "ego sub quadam fici arbore straui me nescio quomodo et dimisi habenas lacrimis", AUGL 13452–13453). He cried, "How long, how long? Tomorrow, and tomorrow? Why not now?" (AUG 13102–13104; Latin "quamdiu, quamdiu 'cras et cras'? quare non modo?", AUGL 13458–13459). Then: "lo, I heard the voice as of a boy or girl, I know not which, coming from a neighbouring house, chanting, and oft repeating, 'Take up and read; take up and read.' Immediately my countenance was changed, and I began most earnestly to consider whether it was usual for children in any kind of game to sing such words; nor could I remember ever to have heard the like." (Conf. VIII.12.29, AUG 13107–13113). Latin: "et ecce audio uocem de [OCR diuina; English "neighbouring", i.e. uicina] domo cum cantu dicentis et crebro repetentis quasi pueri an puellae, nescio: 'tolle lege, tolle lege'. statimque mutato uultu intentissimus cogitare coepi, utrumnam solerent pueri in aliquo genere ludendi cantitare tale aliquid, nec occurrebat omnino audisse me uspiam" (AUGL 13463–13467). He took it as a command to open the book, went back to where he had left "the volume of the apostles", and read the first passage his eyes fell on: "Not in rioting and drunkenness, not in chambering and wantonness, not in strife and envying; but put ye on the Lord Jesus Christ, and make not provision for the flesh, to fulfil the lusts thereof." (AUG 13114–13115, 13125–13131; Romans 13:13–14). OWN, written about ten years later as a bishop. Documented as his own account. Between the voice and the reading he recalls Antony (AUG 13116–13125): that passage and its quote belong to the Antony story on the tree and are not used here. The year 386 is the census's (CEN `why`, 79994), Widely Accepted; the Confessions give no year here.
E6b. **His mother.** "Thence we go in to my mother. We make it known to her,—she rejoiceth. We relate how it came to pass,—she leapeth for joy, and triumpheth, and blesseth Thee" (Conf. VIII.12.30, AUG 13146–13148; Latin "inde ad matrem ingredimur, indicamus: gaudet, narramus quemadmodum gestum sit: exultat et triumphat et benedicebat tibi", AUGL 13522–13524). She had followed him to Milan (Book VI ch. 1 title, AUG 8630–8633). Her name is in his own text only once: "Monnicae, famulae tuae" (Conf. IX.13.37, AUGL 15251; English "Monica, Thy handmaid", AUG 14539). OWN. Documented. Possidius: "his mother alone surviving and clinging to him, exulting more in his determination to serve God than in the hope of offspring after the flesh" (POS 1730–1733). CONTEMP. Documented.
E7. **Leaving the trade.** "The vintage vacation being ended, I gave the citizens of Milan notice that they might provide their scholars with another seller of words; because both of my election to serve Thee, and my inability, by reason of the difficulty of breathing and the pain in my chest, to continue the Professorship." (Conf. IX.5.13, AUG 13651–13656). Latin: "Renuntiaui peractis uindemialibus, ut scholasticis suis Mediolanenses uenditorem uerborum alium prouiderent, quod et tibi ego seruire delegissem et illi professioni prae difficultate spirandi ac dolore pectoris non sufficerem" (AUGL 14130–14133). Possidius: "He also gave notice to his pupils in rhetoric to secure another teacher, as he had resolved to serve God." (POS ch. II, 1734–1736; Latin 1696–1698: "Renunciavit etiam scholasticis quos rhetor docebat, ut sibi magistrum alium providerent"). OWN and CONTEMP. Documented. Both reasons stand: his choice to serve God, and his chest.
E8. **Home to Africa.** "he determined, with others of his neighbors and friends who served God with him, to return to Africa to his own home and lands to which he came and in which he was settled for almost three years. He now gave up these possessions and began to live with those who had also consecrated themselves to God, in fastings and prayers and good works" (POS ch. III, 1739–1746). CONTEMP. Documented.
E9. **Seized for the priesthood at Hippo.** "The Catholics, already acquainted with the life and teaching of the holy Augustine, laid hands on him — for he was standing there among the people secure and unaware of what was about to happen ... So they laid hands on him and, as is the custom in such cases, brought him to the bishop to be ordained ... and they demanded it with great zeal and clamor, while he wept freely." (POS ch. IV, 1808–1817; Latin 1770–1778). Some thought the tears were wounded pride; "But the man of God, as he told us" wept because he foresaw the dangers of ruling a church (POS 1818–1827; Latin 1778–1781, 1795–1798). CONTEMP, from what Augustine told him. Documented. Weiskotten dates it 391 (POS 5227, EDITOR).
E10. **A priest who preached, against African custom.** Bishop Valerius, "a Greek by birth and less versed in the Latin language and literature, saw that he himself was less useful for this end. Therefore he gave his presbyter the right of preaching the Gospel in his presence in the church and very frequently of holding public discussions — contrary to the practice and custom of the African churches. On this account some bishops found fault with him." (POS ch. V, 1879–1886). He also founded "a monastery within the church", where "no one should possess anything of his own" (POS 1867–1872). CONTEMP. Documented. This is where the rhetorician's skill turns to preaching.
E11. **Made bishop beside a living bishop.** Valerius, afraid another church would take him, had him made coadjutor bishop; "under compulsion and constraint he yielded ... Later he both said and wrote that this should not have been done in his case, namely to ordain him while his bishop was still living, because of the prohibition of the Ecumenical Council of which he learned after his ordination" (POS ch. VIII, 2078–2149). CONTEMP. Documented. Year: Weiskotten's note says Megalius consecrated him "in 391" and that Valerius died in 396 (POS 5309–5311, EDITOR): 391 there contradicts his own date for the priesthood (5227). Census and the tree's tradition story give bishop "from 395" (CEN, `latin-pastoral-congregational-christianity`). Dominant Modern Reconstruction: about 395–396. Spoken: "a few years later".
E12. **Grace: his own turn, at the start of his time as bishop.** On the question from Romans 9 (Jacob and Esau) answered for Simplicianus, bishop of Milan: "in cuius quaestionis solutione laboratum est quidem pro libero arbitrio uoluntatis humanae, sed uicit dei gratia, nec nisi ad illud potuit perueniri, ut liquidissima ueritate dixisse intellegatur apostolus: quis enim te discernit? quid autem habes, quod non accepisti? si autem accepisti, quid gloriaris, quasi non acceperis?" (*Retractations* II.1, RET 9059–9066). He quotes himself in *On the Predestination of the Saints* 8: "In the solution of this question I laboured indeed on behalf of the free choice of the human will, but God's grace overcame, and I could only reach that point where the apostle is perceived to have said with the most evident truth, 'For who maketh thee to differ? and what hast thou that thou hast not received?'" (NP5 20972). He dates the book: "in the very beginning of my episcopate to Simplicianus ... Bishop of the Church of Milan, and successor to St. Ambrose" (NP5 20955). And in *On the Gift of Perseverance* 52: those books "were both composed and published before the Pelagians had begun to appear ... in that disputation which I wrote to Simplicianus ... in the beginning of my episcopate, when, moreover, I both perceived and asserted that the beginning of faith is God's gift." (NP5 22523). OWN, written about thirty years after the event (the two treatises are of 428–429, NP5 headings, EDITOR). Documented as his own account of his change of mind. Calvin later notes the same change (I3d).
E13. **The Confessions.** His own description: "Confessionum mearum libri tredecim et de malis et de bonis meis deum laudant iustum et bonum atque in eum excitant humanum [OCR Lumanum] intellectum et affectum; interim, quod ad me adtinet, hoc in me egerunt, cum scriberentur, et agunt, cum leguntur ... a primo usque ad decimum de me scripti sunt, in tribus ceteris de scripturis sanctis" (*Retractations* II.6, RET 9401–9403, 9435–9439). Plain sense: thirteen books that praise the just and good God for his bad and good deeds, and stir the human mind and heart toward God; they did this to him when written and still do when read; books one to ten are about himself, the last three about Scripture. OWN. Documented. The book is addressed to God throughout ("Thou movest us to delight in praising Thee; for Thou hast formed us for Thyself, and our hearts are restless till they find rest in Thee", Conf. I.1.1, AUG 3889–3890). Date: WORKS.yaml gives "397-400 CE" (`cic/corpus-map/WORKS.yaml` 119, EDITOR); the *Retractations* list it among his books as bishop. Dominant Modern Reconstruction for the dates; spoken: "a few years after he became bishop". Possidius chose not to retell it: "It is not my purpose to touch on all those things which the blessed Augustine noted about himself in his books of the Confessions" (POS 1590–1592).
E14. **The City of God: why it was written.** "Interea Roma Gotthorum inruptione agentium sub rege Allaricho atque impetu magnae cladis euersa est. cuius euersionem deorum falsorum multorumque cultores, quos usitato nomine paganos uocamus, in Christianam religionem referre conantes solito acerbius et amarius deum uerum blasphemare coeperunt, unde ego exardescens zelo domus dei aduersus eorum blasphemias uel errores libros de ciuitate dei scribere institui, quod opus per aliquot annos me tenuit" (*Retractations* II.43, RET 12158–12159, 12208–12213). Dods's English: "Rome having been stormed and sacked by the Goths under Alaric their king, the worshippers of false gods, or pagans, as we commonly call them, made an attempt to attribute this calamity to the Christian religion, and began to blaspheme the true God with even more than their wonted bitterness and acerbity. It was this which kindled my zeal for the house of God ... This work was in my hands for several years ... at last completed in twenty-two books." (CDE 766–779). The year 410 is Dods's note (CDE 768, EDITOR); Widely Accepted. Plan: the first ten books answer the pagans; the last twelve tell "the origin of these two cities—the city of God, and the city of the world", their history and their ends (CDE 780–806). OWN. Documented.
E15. **The two cities.** "Accordingly, two cities have been formed by two loves: the earthly by the love of self, even to the contempt of God; the heavenly by the love of God, even to the contempt of self. The former, in a word, glories in itself, the latter in the Lord." (*City of God* XIV.28, CDE 27268–27274). Latin: "Fecerunt itaque ciuitates duas amores duo, terrenam scilicet amor sui usque ad contemtum Dei, caelestem uero amor Dei usque ad contemtum sui. Denique illa in se ipsa, haec in Domino gloriatur." (CDL 3380–3381, 3392–3393; apparatus between left out). OWN. Documented.
E16. **Against Pelagius: what he taught.** His positive teaching, in *On the Spirit and the Letter* 5: "We, however, on our side affirm that the human will is so divinely aided in the pursuit of righteousness, that (in addition to man's being created with a free-will, and in addition to the teaching by which he is instructed how he ought to live) he receives the Holy Ghost, by whom there is formed in his mind a delight in, and a love of, that supreme and unchangeable good which is God ... A man's free-will, indeed, avails for nothing except to sin, if he knows not the way of truth ... Now, in order that such a course may engage our affections, God's 'love is shed abroad in our hearts,' not through the free-will which arises from ourselves, but 'through the Holy Ghost, which is given to us.'" (NP5 5105–5110; chapter title "True Grace is the Gift of the Holy Ghost, Which Kindles in the Soul the Joy and Love of Goodness", 5101). OWN. Documented. Date 412: Luther's editor (LUE 18095, EDITOR). Plain sense for the story: free will and good teaching are not enough; God must give the love that makes a person want the good. What Pelagius held is told on the tree (CEN `pelagius`, `pelagianism`): God gave human nature the power, and grace meant outside help such as teaching and Christ's example.
E17. **Against Pelagius: the struggle, as his friend tells it.** "Against the Pelagians also, new heretics of our time and skilful debaters ... against these he labored for almost ten years, writing and publishing many books and very frequently arguing in the church with people of that error." African councils persuaded Innocent and Zosimus, bishops of Rome, who "censured them and cut them off from the membership of the Church"; the Emperor Honorius "decreed that they should be condemned by his laws and should be regarded as heretics." (POS ch. XVIII, 3002–3021). CONTEMP. Documented as Possidius's account, a partisan friend (see D3 on "ten years").
E17a. **Forcing the Donatists back: his own case (added 2026-11-18 on Mark's ruling).** Who the Donatists were: a rival church in Roman Africa, the larger of the two for a century, which held that bishops who had handed over the Scriptures in persecution, and all in communion with them, had lost the church; it baptized again those who came over (CEN `donatism`). Augustine, Letter 93, to Vincentius, a bishop of a Donatist splinter group ("ad Vincentium Rogatistam", AUG 2898, EDITOR), dated "a.d. 408" by the editor (AUG 37689–37692): "For originally my opinion was, that no one should be coerced into the unity of Christ, that we must act only by words, fight only by arguments, and prevail by force of reason, lest we should have those whom we knew as avowed heretics feigning themselves to be Catholics. But this opinion of mine was overcome not by the words of those who controverted it, but by the conclusive instances to which they could point. For, in the first place, there was set over against my opinion my own town, which, although it was once wholly on the side of Donatus, was brought over to the Catholic unity by fear of the imperial edicts" (Ep. 93.5.17, AUG 38247–38256). Latin: "His ergo exemplis a collegis meis mihi propositis cessi, nam mea primitus sententia non erat nisi neminem ad unitatem Christi esse cogendum, uerbo esse agendum, disputatione pugnandum, ratione uincendum, ne fictos catholicos haberemus, quos apertos haereticos noueramus. sed haec opinio mea non contradicentium uerbis sed demonstrantium superabatur exemplis, nam primo mihi opponebatur ciuitas mea, quae cum [OCR eum] tota esset in parte Donati, ad unitatem catholicam legum imperialium timore conuersa est" (`augustine_epistulae-1-123-lat_goldbacher-csel34.txt` **EPL** 31521–31545). His Bible text for it: "You are of opinion that no one should be compelled to follow righteousness; and yet you read that the householder said to his servants, 'Whomsoever ye shall find, compel them to come in.'" (Ep. 93.2.5, AUG 37845–37847; Luke 14:23). OWN. Documented. "My own town" is Thagaste (S1), by the plain sense; the letter does not name it here. The laws that followed the 411 hearing took the Donatist churches by name (CEN `donatism`; the hearing is the sibling's). The force is the record's and must be told plainly.
E18. **Rival preaching and the other quarrels.** Possidius: he rejoiced to see "the Manichaeans, Donatists, Pelagians and pagans for the most part diminishing" (POS 3074–3075). CONTEMP. The Donatist conference of 411 is the sibling's (CEN `conference-of-carthage-411`); one clause at most.
E19. **The Vandals.** "a great host of savage foes" crossed from Spain to Africa (POS ch. XXVIII, 3946ff; Latin 3947–3956: "Vandalorum et Alanorum commixtam secum habens Gothorum gentem"), "raging with cruelty and barbarity ... sparing neither sex nor age, nor even the priests or ministers of God" (POS 3987–3992). The enemy "came to besiege the city of the Hippo-Regians ... For almost fourteen months they shut up and besieged the city; and they even cut off its sea-coast by blockade." (POS 4078–4084). CONTEMP, eyewitness ("We ourselves ... took refuge in this city", 4084–4085). Documented. The table prayer and the fever are told on the tree in the Possidius story, with its quote: do not reuse (CEN `possidius-of-calama`).
E20. **The penitential psalms on the wall.** "For he commanded that the shortest penitential Psalms of David should be copied for him, and during the days of his sickness as he lay in bed he would look at these sheets as they hung upon the wall and read them; and he wept freely and constantly. And that his attention might not be interrupted by anyone, about ten days before he departed from the body he asked of us who were present that no one should come in to him, except only at the hours in which the physicians came" (POS ch. XXXI, 4925–4932). "Up to the very moment of his last illness he preached the Word of God in the church incessantly" (POS 4936–4937). CONTEMP, eyewitness. Documented. Possidius's reason: even baptized Christians and priests "ought not depart from this life without fitting and appropriate repentance" (POS 4922–4924).
E21. **No will; the library kept.** "He made no will, because as a poor man of God he had nothing from which to make it. He repeatedly ordered that the library of the church and all the books should be carefully preserved for future generations." (POS ch. XXXI, 4983–4986; Latin 4944–4946). He left "monasteries of men and women ... together with the library and books containing treatises of his own and of other holy men. By the help of God, one may find therein how great he was in the Church and therein the faithful may always find him living." (POS 4995–5000). Then the poet's epitaph: "Wouldst know that poets live again, O traveller, after death? These words thou readest, lo, I speak! Thy voice is but my breath." (POS 5004–5006; Latin "Vivere post obitum vates vis nosse viator? / Quod legis ecce loquor, vox tua nempe mea est.", 4961–4962). CONTEMP. Documented. Possidius himself reports that "after Augustine's death the city of Hippo, abandoned by its inhabitants, was burned by the enemy" (POS 4071–4073).
E22. **Death date.** Prosper: "Aurelius Augustinus episcopus per omnia excellentissimus moritur V. kl. Sept., libris Iuliani inter impetus obsidentium Vandalorum in ipso dierum [OCR dierimi] suorum fine respondens et gloriose in defensione Christianae [OCR Cliristianae] gratiae perseverans." (PRC 54206–54211). LATER (written within a generation; Prosper was writing to Augustine in 429). Documented. The tree's Prosper story already paraphrases this entry: link only.

## 3. Impact as the record gives it (heaviest first)

I1. **His grace teaching became the Western church's ruling at Orange (529).** Maassen, the editor: "e canonibus viginti quinque Arausicanis canones IX. et XI–XXV. ad verbum cum nonnullis 'sententiis' S. Prosperi consentire, reliquos vero, canones scilicet I–VIII et X, cum locis ex variis S. Augustini scriptis quam maxime cohaerere" (MGHC 4124–4127); Prosper's *Sentences* are "ex Operibus S. Augustini delibatarum" (MGHC 4135). Plain: of the twenty-five canons, eight and one more closely follow passages of Augustine, and the rest repeat word for word sentences Prosper drew from his works. EDITOR (Maassen, 1893). The council's own heading: "constitutio episcoporum in civitate Arausica de gratia et libero arbitrio" (MGHC 4113–4114); its chapters were "ab apostolica . . sede transmissa" (MGHC 4120). "S. Caesarius, qui concilio Arausicano praesedit" (MGHC 4152–4153); confirmed by Pope Boniface II (MGHC 4158–4159). Warfield: Orange met "on the 3d of July, 529", affirmed "an anxiously guarded Augustinianism, a somewhat weakened Augustinianism, but yet a distinctive Augustinianism; and, so far as a formal condemnation could reach, semi-Pelagianism was suppressed by them in the whole Western Church. But councils and popes can only decree; and Cassian and Vincent and Faustus ... retained an influence" (NP5 957–958, EDITOR, 1887). Widely Accepted that Orange drew on him (two editors agree; the canons themselves are not quoted here). Spoken safely: "a council at Orange in Gaul, in 529, set out chapters on grace and free will, most of them drawn from his writings, as its modern editor shows". The tree's Caesarius story tells Orange (CEN `caesarius-of-arles`): link.
I2. **Prosper defended him in Gaul.** Prosper calls him "per omnia excellentissimus", still "gloriose in defensione Christianae gratiae perseverans" at his death (PRC 54206–54211). Augustine's last two treatises on grace answer the party at Marseilles that Prosper had reported in his letter (NP5 20824, 21275, editor's notes; headings dated 428–429, EDITOR). The tree's Prosper and Cassian stories tell the Gallic quarrel (CEN `prosper-of-aquitaine`, `john-cassian`). LATER/CONTEMP. Documented. Link only.
I3. **The reformers read him.** (a) Luther, *Answer to the Superchristian, Superspiritual, and Superlearned Book of Goat Emser*, "in print by the end of March" 1521 by the editor (LUE 14576–14580): "I will give you better instruction in this matter—I say this without boasting—than any you have received heretofore from any teacher except St. Augustine, if perchance you have read his De Spiritu et Litera. None of the others will teach you aright." (LUE 18056–18063). LATER (~1,090 years). Documented as Luther's words in English translation. Context: the meaning of "letter" and "spirit" in Scripture, not grace as such; the book he names is the one in E16. (b) Luther, *Bondage of the Will*: Augustine, "in his book against Julian, calls Free-will 'under bondage rather than free'" (LUB 4685–4687; OCR badly damaged; card only). (c) Luther was an Augustinian friar (CEN `martin-luther`), an order named for him; the name alone carries no teaching (card only). (d) Calvin, *Institutes* III.22.8: "Augustine also was for some time of this opinion; but after he had made greater progress in the knowledge of Scripture, he not only retracted it as evidently false, but powerfully confuted it" (CAL 25351–25354); "Were we disposed to frame an entire volume out of Augustine, it were easy to show the reader that I have no occasion to use any other words than his" (CAL 25389–25391). LATER (~1,100 years). Documented. Schaff: these anti-Pelagian works "exerted a powerful influence upon the Reformers of the sixteenth century and upon the Jansenists in the seventeenth" (NP5 695, EDITOR, card only).
I4. **Jansen's *Augustinus* and its condemnation.** Neale names him "Cornelius Jansen, Bishop of Ypres" (NEA 562, OCR "lius Jansen"). Neale: Jansen's "Augustinus; in which he endeavoured to restore the theology of the seventeenth century to the doctrine of the saint who has always been regarded as the Doctor of Grace,—Augustine" (NEA 653–656); "In this he attempted to develope the teaching of S. Augustine on grace. He devoted to it the patient labour of twenty years, and is said to have read the entire works of that father as many times." (NEA 794–797). The book was condemned by Urban VIII's bull of 6 March 1642 (NEA 888–890); "on the 31st of May, 1653, appeared the famous dogmatic constitution of Innocent X., Cum occasione [OCR "occasioned"], in which the Five Propositions were condemned as temerarious, impious, blasphemous, and heretical" (NEA 1098–1104; OCR margin notes and faults "tious wcrc", "bias- phcmous" corrected). LATER (Neale, 1858, sympathetic to the Jansenists). Documented for the events; "read his works twenty times" is a report ("is said to"); "Doctor of Grace" is Neale's phrase for the tradition, not a held ancient title. The tree's Port-Royal story tells the condemnation (CEN `port-royal-jansenism`): one sentence and a link.
I5. **His books themselves, kept and read.** Possidius added a list of his works so readers could copy them "from the library of the church of Hippo" (POS 3087–3094); "so many things were dictated and published by him ... that scarcely any student would be able to read and know them all" (POS 3082–3087). CONTEMP. Isidore of Seville, about 170 years later: "Horum tamen omnium studia Augustinus ingenio vel scientia sui vicit. Nam tanta scripsit ut diebus ac noctibus non solum scribere libros eius quisquam, sed nec legere quidem occurrat." (*Etymologies* VI.7.3, ISE 13167–13171). Plain: Augustine outdid them all in mind and learning; he wrote so much that no one, day and night, could copy his books, or even read them. LATER. Documented. Isidore's library verses: "Mentitur, qui te totum legisse fatetur. / An quis cuncta tua lector habere potest? / Namque voluminibus mille, Augustine, refulges" (ISV 123392–123396; OCR "Meatitur", "toturn", "cuneta", "mile"). LATER. Documented as the verses printed under Isidore's name in Migne; authorship not checked here (card only). Facundus of Hermiane, an African bishop writing to Justinian (about 120 years later): "sapientissimus atque clarissimus Ecclesiarum Christi magister Augustinus" (FAC 43120–43121, *Pro defensione trium capitulorum* I). LATER. Documented (card only).
I6. **Readers on the tree.** Fulgentius of Ruspe turned to the monks after reading Augustine on Psalm 36; Eugippius gathered 338 chapters from him (CEN `fulgentius-of-ruspe`, `eugippius`). Links only.
I7. **DEFENDED.** The grace of Christ (Prosper's words, PRC 54210–54211; E12, E16, E17). Also Nicene faith (census `floorNote`, "He wrote On the Trinity"; not told here) and the church's unity against the Donatists (sibling).

## 5. Where sources disagree, or are later than the life

D1. **His parents' faith.** Possidius: "honorable Christian parents" (POS 1607). Augustine: "my father was only a catechumen as yet, and that but recently" (Conf. II.3.6, AUG 5089–5091), and calls him a "heathen father" in the editor's summary (AUG 1123, EDITOR). Contested between witnesses; leave the parents out.
D2. **Year he became bishop.** Weiskotten's note says 391 (POS 5309), against his own 391 for the priesthood (POS 5227) and the tree's "from 395". Dominant Modern Reconstruction: 395/396. No year in the story.
D3. **How long he fought the Pelagians.** Possidius: "almost ten years" (POS 3005). The tree's Pelagianism story: "the last twenty years of his life" (CEN `pelagianism`). Prosper: still answering Julian at his death (PRC 54207–54209). The two counts may measure different things; do not give a number. Say "for the rest of his life" (Prosper) or "for years".
D4. **Date of the Confessions and of the City of God.** No held ancient source dates them by year. The Confessions: "397-400" (WORKS.yaml, EDITOR). City of God: begun after the sack of Rome (RET), the year 410 Dods's note. Say "a few years after he became bishop" and "after the Goths sacked Rome in 410".
D5. **Orange.** That its canons came from Augustine is the editors' finding (Maassen, Warfield), not stated by the council. The council says its chapters came from the apostolic see (MGHC 4120). Warfield calls the result "a somewhat weakened Augustinianism" (NP5 957). Tell it with the editor credited, and do not say Orange adopted all his teaching.
D6. **Partisan witnesses.** Possidius is his friend and a fellow bishop on the winning side of every quarrel he tells; Prosper is his defender. Their praise and the defeat of his opponents are theirs. Neale writes in sympathy with the Jansenists.

## 6. Gaps

G1. *On the Spirit and the Letter* and the anti-Pelagian treatises are held in English (NP5); their Latin was not found in the Library under those titles. Renderings below are from the Latin only where Latin is held (Confessions, Retractations, City of God XIV).
G2. The canons of Orange are held in Latin (MGHC 4142 onward) but were not read canon by canon; the story relies on Maassen's and Warfield's statements of their source.
G3. *Bondage of the Will* is held only in badly damaged OCR (LUB); its Augustine line is card only.
G4. The *De beata vita*, where Augustine gives his birthday, is not held as such; the date rests on Weiskotten's note (POS 134, 152–153).
