Milan, where the emperor then held court. A young teacher is preparing a speech in praise of the emperor, in which, he says, he would tell many lies. In the street he passes a beggar, well fed and joking, and sighs: the beggar already has the happiness he is straining after.

The teacher was Augustine. He taught rhetoric, the art of persuasive public speaking, and in his own words he sold the skill of talking to win. In a garden in Milan, he says, a child's voice sent him to the Bible, and he turned to Christ. He became bishop, the leader of the church, in Hippo, a port town in Roman North Africa. And he taught that grace, God's free and unearned gift, comes first: no one turns to God, or even begins to believe, unless God gives it.

Born in Roman Africa in the late Roman Empire, he taught at Carthage and Rome, then Milan, where he heard its bishop, Ambrose, preach. In 386, torn and weeping, he threw himself down under a fig tree. His Confessions, thirteen books addressed to God in praise and in owning up to sin, go on: "And look, I hear a voice from the house next door, singing and repeating it again and again, like a boy's or a girl's voice, I do not know which: 'Take it and read, take it and read.'" He opened Paul's letters at the first lines he saw: not drunken revels or quarrels, but put on the Lord Jesus Christ. He told his mother, Monica, who rejoiced, and at Easter Ambrose baptized him, washing him in water to join the church.

Back in Africa, on a visit to Hippo, the crowd in church seized him and brought him to their bishop to be made a priest, a minister under the bishop, while he wept. A few years later he was bishop himself.

Early on, answering a bishop of Milan's questions on Paul's letter to the Romans, he changed his mind about free will, a person's own power to choose good or evil. Near the end of his life, calling Paul the apostle, he looked back: "In answering this question, I worked hard for the free choice of the human will. But God's grace won. I could only come to see that the apostle spoke the plainest truth: 'Who sets you apart? What do you have that you did not receive?'"

Beside his church stood the Donatists, a rival African church, for a century the larger one, which held that bishops who had handed over the Bible in persecution had lost the church. At first, Augustine wrote in about 408, he held that no one should be forced into the church; argument alone should win. But his fellow bishops pointed to his own town, brought back by fear of the emperors' laws, and he came to back those laws to force the Donatists back, citing Jesus's words: compel them to come in.

In 410 the Goths, a people from beyond the empire's frontier, sacked Rome. Answering those who blamed the Christians, Augustine wrote the City of God: two loves, he said, made two cities, love of self the earthly city, and love of God the heavenly city, God's own. Against Pelagius, a monk at Rome who held that God had already given human nature the power to do good, he answered that free will and good teaching are not enough: God's Spirit must give the love of the good. His friend Possidius, who wrote his life to honor him and stood on the winning side, says meetings of African bishops moved two bishops of Rome, the leaders of the church there, to condemn Pelagius's followers, and the emperor's laws punished them as heretics, false teachers cut off from the church.

He died in 430, at seventy-six, while the Vandals, another people from beyond the frontier, besieged Hippo. He made no will, Possidius says, since as a poor man of God he had nothing to leave, but he told them to keep the church's library.

Those books carried his teaching on. In 529 a meeting of bishops at Orange, in what is now France, set out rulings on grace drawn largely from his writings, as their modern editor shows. A thousand years later Martin Luther sent an opponent to his book On the Spirit and the Letter, on the law that commands and the Spirit that gives the love to keep it. John Calvin said he could build a whole volume on God's choice and grace from Augustine's words alone. Both stood against Rome, but Cornelius Jansen, a Catholic bishop, in the church united with Rome, also wrote a book on him, called Augustinus, and Rome condemned it.

Possidius closed his account with a poet's thought: whoever reads the words aloud gives the writer his voice.
