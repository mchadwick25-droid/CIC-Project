Milan, where the emperor then held court. A young teacher is getting ready to give a speech praising the emperor, a speech in which, he says, he would tell many lies, and those who knew it would cheer him. In the street he passes a beggar who has eaten well, joking and happy, and he sighs, because the beggar already has the happiness he is straining after.

The teacher was Augustine. He taught rhetoric, the art of persuasive public speaking, and in his own words he sold the skill of talking to win. In a garden in Milan, by his own account, a child's voice sent him to the Bible, and he turned to Christ. He became bishop, the leader of the church, in Hippo, a port town in Roman North Africa, in what is now Algeria. There he wrote the Confessions, a book that both praises God and owns up to sin, and the City of God, about the people who love God above themselves. And he taught that grace, God's free gift of help that no one has earned, comes first: no one turns to God, or even begins to believe, unless God gives it. That teaching both shaped and divided the churches of the West.

He was born at Thagaste in Roman Africa, in the late Roman Empire. At nineteen a book by Cicero turned him toward the search for wisdom, and for nine years he followed the Manichaeans, who held to a Persian teacher's war between light and darkness. He taught at Carthage and Rome, until the city's governor sent him to Milan after a trial speech. There he heard Ambrose, the bishop of Milan, preach.

In 386, torn and weeping, he left his friend and threw himself down under a fig tree. How long, he cried: tomorrow, and tomorrow? Why not now? The Confessions go on: "And look, I hear a voice from the house next door, singing and repeating it again and again, like a boy's or a girl's voice, I do not know which: 'Take it and read, take it and read.'" He took it as a command. He opened Paul's letters and read the first lines he saw: not drunken revels or quarrels, but put on the Lord Jesus Christ. He and his friend went in and told his mother, Monica, who had followed him to Milan, and she rejoiced. He gave up his post, because he had chosen to serve God and because of pain in his chest, and at Easter Ambrose baptized him, washing him in water to join the church.

Home in Africa, he lived almost three years with friends who had given themselves to God. Then, on a visit to Hippo, the crowd in church seized him and brought him to their bishop, Valerius, to be made a priest, a minister serving under the bishop, while he wept. His friend Possidius, a fellow bishop who later wrote his life to honor him, says some took the tears for wounded pride, but Augustine told him he wept at the dangers of ruling a church. Valerius, Greek by birth and less at home in Latin, let him preach, which African priests did not usually do. A few years later Augustine was made bishop beside the living Valerius, and he later said it should not have been done.

Early in those years a bishop of Milan sent him questions on Paul's letter to the Romans. He had argued for free will, a person's own power to choose good or evil. Near the end of his life, looking back on that answer to Paul, whom he calls the apostle, he wrote: "In answering this question, I worked hard for the free choice of the human will. But God's grace won. I could only come to see that the apostle spoke the plainest truth: 'Who sets you apart? What do you have that you did not receive?'" Even the beginning of faith, he now held, is God's gift. Soon after, he wrote the Confessions: thirteen books addressed to God, praising him for Augustine's bad deeds and good. Ten tell his life and three turn to the Bible, and he said they moved him as he wrote, and still did whenever they were read.

In many African towns a Donatist church stood beside his own. The Donatists were a rival African church, for a century the larger one, which held that bishops who had handed over the Scriptures, the Bible's books, in persecution had lost the church. Augustine called his own church Catholic, the one church joined across the whole empire, and called that oneness the unity of Christ; the Donatists he called heretics, people teaching falsely. In a letter of about 408 he wrote: "At first my view was simply this: no one should be forced into the unity of Christ. We should act by words, fight by debate, and win by reason. Otherwise we would have pretend Catholics where we had known open heretics." His fellow bishops changed his mind by pointing to his own town, brought back to the church by fear of the emperors' laws. He now backed those laws to force the Donatists back, and defended them from Jesus's words in a parable: compel them to come in.

In 410 the Goths, a people from beyond the empire's frontier, sacked Rome under their king. Worshippers of the old Roman gods blamed the Christians, and Augustine began the City of God, twenty-two books written over many years. Two loves, he wrote, made two cities: love of self, even to contempt of God, made the earthly city, and love of God, even to contempt of self, the heavenly. In the same years he wrote against Pelagius, a British-born monk teaching at Rome, and his followers, who held that God had already given human nature the power to do good. Free will and good teaching are not enough, Augustine answered: God's Spirit must give delight in God and love for him. Possidius, writing for the winning side, says meetings of African bishops persuaded two bishops of Rome, the leaders of the church there, to condemn the Pelagians, and the emperor's laws then made them heretics, officially false teachers, cut off from the church and punished.

At the end the Vandals, another people from beyond the frontier, crossed from Spain and laid waste the African provinces, sparing no one, Possidius says, not even priests, and they besieged Hippo for about fourteen months. In his last illness, during the siege, Augustine had the penitential psalms, the Bible's songs asking God's forgiveness, copied and hung on the wall, and he read them from his bed and wept. He made no will, because as a poor man of God he had nothing to leave, but again and again he told them to keep the church's library for future generations. He died in 430, at seventy-six, in the third month of the siege.

His books carried the teaching on. In 529, at Orange in Gaul, now France, a meeting of bishops set out rulings on grace and free will drawn largely from his writings, as its modern editor shows. A thousand years later, in 1521, Martin Luther told an opponent that only Augustine's book On the Spirit and the Letter would teach him rightly what those words mean in the Bible: the letter is the law that commands, and the spirit is God's Spirit, giving the love to keep it. John Calvin said he could build a whole volume on God's choice and grace from Augustine's words alone. In the 1600s Cornelius Jansen, a Catholic bishop of the church in union with Rome, spent twenty years on a book he called Augustinus, and Rome condemned it.

Possidius ended his account with a poet's thought: whoever reads the words aloud gives the writer back his voice.
