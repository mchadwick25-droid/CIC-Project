Milan, where the emperor then held court. A young teacher is preparing a speech in praise of the emperor, in which, he says, he would tell many lies. In the street he passes a beggar, well fed and joking, and sighs: the beggar already has the happiness he is straining after.

The teacher was Augustine. He taught rhetoric, the art of persuasive public speaking. In a garden in Milan, he says, a child's voice sent him to the Bible, and he turned to Christ. He became bishop, the leader of the church, in Hippo, a port town in Roman North Africa. There he wrote the Confessions, his life told as a prayer to God, and the City of God, on those who love God above themselves. He taught that grace, God's free and unearned gift, comes first: no one even begins to believe unless God gives it. The Western churches carried that teaching on, and it both shaped and divided them.

Born in Roman Africa, he taught at Carthage and Rome, then Milan; in his own words, he sold the skill of talking to win. At Milan he heard its bishop, Ambrose, preach. There, in 386, came the moment he tells in his Confessions, written to God in praise and in owning up to sin. Torn and weeping, he threw himself down under a fig tree. Then, he writes: "And look, I hear a voice from the house next door, singing and repeating it again and again, like a boy's or a girl's voice, I do not know which: 'Take it and read, take it and read.'" He opened Paul's letters at the first lines he saw: not drunken revels or quarrels, but put on the Lord Jesus Christ. He told his mother, Monica, who rejoiced, and at Easter Ambrose baptized him, washing him in water to join the church.

Back in Africa, he visited Hippo. His friend Possidius later wrote his life to honor him. He says the crowd in church seized him and brought him, weeping, to be made a priest, a minister under its bishop. A few years later he was bishop himself.

Early in those years, a bishop of Milan sent him questions on Paul's letter to the Romans. Answering them, he changed his mind about free will, a person's own power to choose good or evil. Near the end of his life, calling Paul the apostle, he looked back: "In answering this question, I worked hard for the free choice of the human will. But God's grace won. I could only come to see that the apostle spoke the plainest truth: 'Who sets you apart? What do you have that you did not receive?'"

Beside his church stood the Donatists, a rival African church, for a century the larger one. It held that bishops who had handed over the Bible in persecution had lost the church. At first, Augustine wrote in about 408, he held that no one should be forced into the church; argument alone should win. But his fellow bishops pointed to his own town, brought back by fear of the emperors' laws. He came to back those laws to force the Donatists back, citing Jesus's parable: compel them to come in.

When the Goths, a people from beyond the empire's frontier, sacked Rome in 410, worshippers of the old gods blamed the Christians. Augustine answered with the City of God: two loves, he said, made two cities, love of self the earthly city, and love of God the heavenly city. Pelagius, a monk at Rome, held that God had already given human nature the power to do good. Augustine answered that free will and good teaching are not enough: God's Spirit must give the love of the good. Possidius, who was on the winning side, says meetings of African bishops moved two bishops of Rome, the leaders of the church there, to condemn Pelagius's followers. The emperor's laws then punished them as heretics, false teachers cut off from the church.

He died in 430, at seventy-six, while the Vandals, another people from beyond the frontier, besieged Hippo. Possidius says he told them to keep the church's library.

Those books carried his teaching on. In 529 a meeting of bishops at Orange, in what is now France, set out rulings on grace drawn largely from his writings, as their modern editor shows. A thousand years later Martin Luther sent an opponent to his book On the Spirit and the Letter. Its letter is the law that commands; its spirit is God's Spirit, giving the love to keep it. John Calvin said he could build a whole volume on God's choice and grace from Augustine's words alone. Both stood against Rome. Yet inside the church united with Rome, the Catholic bishop Cornelius Jansen wrote a book called Augustinus, and Rome condemned it.

Near the end of his account, Possidius recalled a poet's lines: whoever reads the words aloud gives the writer his voice.
