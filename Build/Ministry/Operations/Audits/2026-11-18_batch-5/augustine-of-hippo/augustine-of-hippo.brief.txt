Milan, where the emperor then held court. A young teacher is getting ready to give a speech praising the emperor, in which, he says, he would tell many lies. In the street he passes a beggar, well fed and joking, and sighs, because the beggar already has the happiness he is straining after.

The teacher was Augustine. He taught rhetoric, the art of persuasive public speaking, and in his own words he sold the skill of talking to win. In a garden in Milan, he says, a child's voice sent him to the Bible, and he turned to Christ. He became bishop, the leader of the church, in Hippo, a port town in Roman North Africa. There he wrote the Confessions, a book that praises God and owns up to sin, and the City of God, on those who love God above themselves. And he taught that grace, God's free and unearned gift, comes first: no one turns to God, or even begins to believe, unless God gives it. That teaching shaped, and divided, the churches of the West.

Born in Roman Africa in the late Roman Empire, he taught at Carthage and Rome, until the governor of Rome sent him to Milan. There he heard its bishop, Ambrose, preach.

In 386, torn and weeping, he threw himself down under a fig tree, and the Confessions go on: "And look, I hear a voice from the house next door, singing and repeating it again and again, like a boy's or a girl's voice, I do not know which: 'Take it and read, take it and read.'" He opened Paul's letters at the first lines he saw: not drunken revels or quarrels, but put on the Lord Jesus Christ. He and a friend went in and told his mother, Monica, and she rejoiced. He gave up teaching, to serve God and because of pain in his chest, and Ambrose baptized him, washing him in water to join the church.

Back in Africa, on a visit to Hippo, the crowd in church seized him and brought him to the bishop, Valerius, to be made a priest, a minister under the bishop, while he wept. His friend Possidius, who later wrote his life to honor him, says some took the tears for pride, but Augustine said he wept at the dangers of ruling a church. A few years later he was made bishop while Valerius still lived, and he later said it should not have been done.

Early on, a bishop of Milan sent him questions on Paul's letter to the Romans. He had argued for free will, a person's own power to choose good or evil. Near the end of his life, calling Paul the apostle, he looked back: "In answering this question, I worked hard for the free choice of the human will. But God's grace won. I could only come to see that the apostle spoke the plainest truth: 'Who sets you apart? What do you have that you did not receive?'" Soon after, he wrote the Confessions: thirteen books addressed to God, ten on his own life and three on the Bible.

Beside his church stood the Donatists, a rival African church, for a century the larger one, which held that bishops who had handed over the Bible in persecution had lost the church. Augustine called his own church Catholic, one church joined across the empire, a oneness he named the unity of Christ, and he called the Donatists heretics, false teachers. In a letter of about 408 he wrote: "At first my view was simply this: no one should be forced into the unity of Christ. We should act by words, fight by debate, and win by reason. Otherwise we would have pretend Catholics where we had known open heretics." His fellow bishops changed his mind by pointing to his own town, brought back by fear of the emperors' laws. So he backed those laws to force the Donatists back, citing Jesus's words: compel them to come in.

In 410 the Goths, a people from beyond the empire's frontier, sacked Rome. Worshippers of the old Roman gods blamed the Christians, and Augustine began the City of God. Two loves, he wrote, made two cities: love of self, even to contempt of God, made the earthly city, and love of God, even to contempt of self, the heavenly. He also wrote against Pelagius, a monk teaching at Rome, and his followers, who held that God had already given human nature the power to do good. Augustine answered that God's Spirit must give the love of the good. Possidius, writing for the winning side, says meetings of African bishops persuaded two bishops of Rome, the leaders of the church there, to condemn them, and the emperor's laws made them heretics, cut off from the church and punished.

At the end the Vandals, another people from beyond the frontier, besieged Hippo. Ill in bed, Augustine had the penitential psalms, the Bible's songs asking God's forgiveness, hung on the wall, and he read them and wept. He made no will, Possidius says, since as a poor man of God he had nothing to leave, but he told them to keep the church's library. He died in 430, at seventy-six.

His books carried his teaching on. In 529 a meeting of bishops at Orange, in what is now France, set out rulings on grace drawn largely from his writings, as their modern editor shows. Martin Luther sent an opponent to his book On the Spirit and the Letter, to learn what letter and spirit mean in the Bible: the law that commands, and God's Spirit giving the love to keep it. John Calvin said he could build a whole volume on grace from Augustine's words alone. In the 1600s Cornelius Jansen, a Catholic bishop, of the church in union with Rome, wrote a book called Augustinus, and Rome condemned it.

Possidius closed his account with a poet's thought: whoever reads the words aloud gives the writer his voice.
