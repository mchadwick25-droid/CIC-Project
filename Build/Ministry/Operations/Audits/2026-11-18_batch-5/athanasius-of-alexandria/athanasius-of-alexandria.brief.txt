One night in 356, soldiers surrounded a church in Alexandria, in Egypt, as its people prayed. Their bishop, the leader of the city's church, later wrote: "I thought it wrong to leave the people in such an uproar, rather than face the danger for them first." He sat in the bishop's chair, the seat he led them from, had a psalm, a song from the Bible, read, and sent the people home before monks and ministers of the church dragged him out past the soldiers.

This was Athanasius, and it was one of five times he had to leave his city in forty-five years as its bishop. He held to the creed of Nicaea, the statement of faith that bishops agreed in 325: that God's Son is "of one being" with the Father, fully God as the Father is. The Son became human, he taught, so that people might be made divine, joined to God and freed from death, and only a Son who was truly God could do that. Against him stood the party of Arius, a priest, or church elder, who said the Son had a beginning. Athanasius also wrote a book that carried the desert monks' way of life abroad, and he listed the books the church reads as God's word, its Scripture.

At Nicaea, a town in today's Turkey, he was a young deacon, an officer under the bishop, says Socrates, a later historian. In 328 he became bishop. When Arius was allowed home, Athanasius refused him communion, the shared bread and wine of one church, and the emperor Constantine threatened to remove him.

Most of our record of these fights comes from Athanasius, or from writers who used him. But in 335 a monk of the Melitians, a breakaway church in Egypt, wrote that Athanasius's followers came with soldiers and beat their monks bloody. That year a council of bishops at Tyre removed him from office, and Constantine sent him far away to Trier, in Gaul, in today's Germany.

He came home in 337. Two years later a rival bishop arrived with soldiers, Socrates says, and Athanasius went to Rome, returning only in 346. Then Constantine's son, now emperor, sent another rival, and after that night in 356 Athanasius hid for six years. His church's chronicle records that searchers tortured a woman vowed to God to find him.

Yet those who accepted all of Nicaea but its one word he would meet "as brothers with brothers", he wrote; those who rejected it were enemies of Christ. In those years, by his editor's dating, he wrote the Life of Antony, a hermit who prayed alone in the desert, for monks abroad who had asked how Antony lived. The emperor Julian drove him out in 362, and in 365 he hid four months near the city until a crowd led him back.

Each year the bishop of Alexandria wrote to the churches of Egypt and Libya, which he oversaw, to set the date of Easter. In his Easter letter of 367, Athanasius named the books passed down as Scripture: twenty-two of the Old Testament and twenty-seven of the New. Other books were for new believers, and others, he said, heretics, teachers he held false, had made up and dated falsely. "Let no one add to them," he wrote of the true books, "or take anything away from them."

A churchman of Antioch put the Life into Latin, Jerome says, and it reached Syriac, the language of the churches of Syria and Mesopotamia. In 692 a council made his rulings church law, and Greek copies of that law carry his list. In 1921 a Church of England proposal for sharing communion with the Orthodox churches of the East still took the books as Athanasius had defined them.

He died in his own city in 373, five days after he ordained an old priest to follow him. Gregory of Nazianzus, preaching a few years later, called his teaching the rule of right belief, and said that many tears were shed for him.
