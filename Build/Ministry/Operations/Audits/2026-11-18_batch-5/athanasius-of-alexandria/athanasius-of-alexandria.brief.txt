One night in 356, soldiers surrounded a church in Alexandria, in Egypt, as its people prayed. Their bishop, the leader of the city's church, later wrote: "I thought it wrong to leave the people in such an uproar, rather than face the danger for them first." He sat in the bishop's chair, the seat he led them from, had a psalm, a song from the Bible, read, and sent the people home. Only then did the monks, men who lived for prayer, and the church's ministers drag him out past the soldiers.

This was Athanasius, and it was one of five times he had to leave his city in forty-five years as its bishop. He held to the creed of Nicaea, the statement of faith that bishops agreed in 325: that God's Son is "of one being" with the Father, fully God as the Father is. God's Son became human, he taught, so that people might be made divine, joined to God and freed from death, and only a Son who was truly God could do that. Against him stood the party of Arius, a priest, or church elder, who said the Son had a beginning. Athanasius also wrote the life of Antony, a hermit who prayed alone in the desert, and he listed the books the church reads as God's word, its Scripture.

At Nicaea, a town in today's Turkey, he was a young deacon, an officer under the bishop, and he opposed Arius's party, says Socrates, a historian writing seventy years after his death. In 328 Athanasius became bishop. When Arius was allowed home, Socrates says, Athanasius refused him communion, the shared bread and wine of one church, and the emperor Constantine threatened to remove him.

Most of what we know of these fights comes from Athanasius, or from writers who used him. But in 335 a monk of the Melitians, a breakaway church in Egypt, wrote that Athanasius's followers came with soldiers and beat their monks bloody. That year a council of bishops at Tyre removed him from office. Constantine sent him far away to Trier, in Gaul, in today's Germany. Socrates gives two possible reasons: a charge that he threatened Egypt's grain ships, or the emperor's wish for unity.

He came home in 337, after Constantine died. In 339 a rival bishop arrived with soldiers, Socrates says, and Athanasius went to Rome. When he returned in 346, his people came a hundred miles out to meet him. Then Constantine's son, now emperor, sent another rival, and after that night in 356 Athanasius hid for six years. A chronicle of his church records that searchers tortured a woman vowed to God to find him.

Still he wrote that those who accepted all of Nicaea but its one word should be met "as brothers with brothers". Those who rejected the council he called enemies of Christ. In those years, by his editor's dating, he wrote the Life of Antony, for monks abroad who had asked how Antony lived. The emperor Julian drove him out in 362, and in 365 he hid for four months near the city, until a crowd led him back.

Each year the bishop of Alexandria wrote to the churches of Egypt and Libya, which he oversaw, to set the date of Easter. In his Easter letter of 367, Athanasius named the books passed down as Scripture, twenty-two of the Old Testament and twenty-seven of the New. Other books were for new believers to read. Others still, he said, were made up by heretics, teachers he held false, and given false dates. "Let no one add to them," he wrote of the true books, "or take anything away from them."

A churchman of Antioch put the Life into Latin, Jerome says, and it reached Syriac, the language of the churches of Syria and Mesopotamia. In 692 a council made his rulings church law, and Greek copies of that law carry his list. In 1921 a Church of England proposal for sharing communion with the Orthodox churches of the East still took the books as Athanasius had defined them.

He died in his own city in 373, five days after he ordained an old priest to follow him. Gregory of Nazianzus, preaching a few years later, called his teaching the rule of right belief. The honors at his death, Gregory said, were greater even than at his homecomings, and many tears were shed for him.
