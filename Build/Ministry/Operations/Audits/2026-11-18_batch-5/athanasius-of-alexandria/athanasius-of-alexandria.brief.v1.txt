One night in 356, soldiers surrounded a church in Alexandria, in Egypt, as its people prayed. Their bishop, the leader of the city's church, later wrote: "I thought it wrong to leave the people in such an uproar, rather than face the danger for them first." He sat in the bishop's chair, the seat he led them from, had a psalm read, one of the Bible's songs, and sent the people home. Then monks, men who had left ordinary life for prayer, dragged him out past the soldiers.

This was Athanasius, and in forty-five years as bishop he had to leave his city five times. He held to the creed of Nicaea, the statement of faith bishops agreed in 325: that God's Son is "of one being" with the Father, fully God. The Son became human, he taught, so that people might be made divine, joined to God and freed from death. Only a Son who was truly God could do that. Against him stood the party of Arius, a priest, or church elder, who said the Son had a beginning. He also wrote the life of a hermit, a monk living alone, and listed the books of Scripture, those the church reads as God's word.

At Nicaea, a town in today's Turkey, he was a young deacon, an officer under the bishop, says Socrates, a later historian. He became bishop in 328. When Arius was allowed home, Socrates says, he refused him communion, the shared bread and wine of one church, and the emperor Constantine threatened to remove him.

Most of our record comes from Athanasius or writers who used him, but a monk of the Melitians, a breakaway church in Egypt, gave the other side. In 335 he wrote that Athanasius's followers came with soldiers and beat their monks bloody. That year a council of bishops at Tyre, a meeting that judged church matters, removed him from office. Constantine sent him far away to Trier, now in Germany, in the region then called Gaul.

He came home in 337, and when a rival bishop came in 339 he fled to Rome, returning in 346. After that night in 356, under Constantine's son, now emperor, he hid for six years. A chronicle of his church records that searchers tortured a woman vowed to God to find him. In those years, by his editor's dating, he wrote the Life of Antony, for monks abroad who had asked how Antony lived. The emperor Julian drove him out in 362, and in 365 he hid four months near the city before he was led home.

Each year the bishop of Alexandria wrote to the churches of Egypt and Libya to set the date of Easter. In his Easter letter of 367, Athanasius named the books passed down as Scripture: twenty-two of the Old Testament and twenty-seven of the New. Others were for new believers, and others, he said, heretics, teachers he held false, had made up and passed off as old. Of the true books he wrote: "Let no one add to them, or take anything away from them."

A churchman of Antioch put the Life into Latin, Jerome says. In 692 a council made his rulings church law, and Greek copies of that law carry his list. In 1921 a Church of England proposal for sharing communion with the Orthodox churches of the East took the books as Athanasius had defined them.

He died in his own city in 373. Gregory of Nazianzus, preaching a few years later, said that many tears were shed for him.
