One May evening in 1738, John Wesley went very unwillingly to a society meeting in Aldersgate Street, in London: a small group that met weekly, inside the church, to pray. Someone was reading Martin Luther's preface to Paul's letter to the Romans. At about a quarter to nine, Wesley felt his heart warmed.

He wrote, "I felt that I did trust in Christ, in Christ alone, for salvation. And I was given an assurance that he had taken away my sins, even mine, and saved me from the law of sin and death." He meant a felt certainty that God had forgiven him, and freed him from sin's hold and from death, its end. That night, his brother wrote, he declared, "I believe."

Wesley was a priest of the Church of England, England's official state church: a minister formally set apart, or ordained, to lead its worship. From the next year he preached in the open air, to crowds he counted in thousands. And he gathered those who answered into societies, split into weekly classes of about twelve under a leader who asked how each member was living. Those groups held together the people mocked as Methodists, a nickname from his Oxford days, and became the frame of the Methodist churches.

In 1739 his friend George Whitefield, a fellow priest, called him to Bristol, a port city in the west of England, where Whitefield had begun preaching outdoors. Wesley could barely accept it, for he had thought saving souls, bringing people to faith and forgiveness, almost a sin outside a church. Yet on 2 April, in a brickyard by the city, he preached to about three thousand people.

By his own account, the bishop of Bristol, the senior churchman over that region, told him he had no business preaching in his area and advised him to go. Wesley stayed. In London, people came asking how to flee from the wrath to come, that is, how to escape God's judgment. That wish became the one condition for joining his society.

The class came by accident. In 1742 the Bristol society agreed to pay off a building debt at a penny a week each, and divided into classes of about twelve, each with one person to collect it. When the collectors told him some members were not living as they should, he saw in it the very thing they had long wanted. From then on each leader saw every member weekly, and advised, rebuked or comforted them. Some changed, and some were put out.

The work drew violence. At Wednesbury, in 1743, a mob surrounded the house where he was writing, crying, "Bring out the minister." That night a second mob dragged him through Walsall, many crying out to knock his brains out. He came back with a little skin off one hand. That winter, one of his preachers reported, mobs there beat Methodists and plundered their houses.

His preachers were ordinary men who had not been ordained, allowed at first only in cases of necessity. One was John Nelson, a stonemason, who had heard him preach outdoors in London and felt this man could tell the secrets of his heart. In 1784 Wesley ordained two preachers for America himself, though only bishops normally did so.

That year the Americans formed their own Episcopal church, meaning one led by bishops. An American preacher later wrote that they kept his Rules with very little alteration, and their rule book still set out classes under a leader. Richard Allen, who later founded the African Methodist Episcopal Church, chose Methodism for its good discipline. Yet he wrote that some preachers became tyrants to Black members, turning them out without a trial.

In 1789 Wesley still said that the Methodists were never meant to leave the Church of England. In 1791 his preachers counted more than 130,000 members in Europe and America.

The day before he died, too weak to make himself understood, he gathered his strength and said twice, "The best of all is, God is with us." A woman who kept his house wrote it down. He died the next morning, 2 March 1791, nearly eighty-eight, with friends kneeling around the bed.
