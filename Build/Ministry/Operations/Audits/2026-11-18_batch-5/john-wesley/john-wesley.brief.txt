One May evening in 1738, John Wesley went very unwillingly to a society meeting in Aldersgate Street, in London, one of the small groups that met each week to pray. Someone was reading Martin Luther's preface to Paul's letter to the Romans. At about a quarter to nine, as the reader described the change God works in the heart through faith in Christ, Wesley felt his heart warmed.

He wrote, "I felt that I did trust in Christ, in Christ alone, for salvation. And I was given an assurance that he had taken away my sins, even mine, and saved me from the law of sin and death." He meant a felt certainty that God had forgiven him, and had freed him from sin's hold and from death, its end. He told the room at once, and that night, his brother wrote, he declared, "I believe."

Wesley was a priest, a formally ordained minister, of the Church of England, England's official state church. What began that evening reached far beyond one room. From the next year he preached in the open air, to crowds he counted in thousands. And he gathered those who answered into societies, and split each one into weekly classes of about twelve under a leader, who asked how each member was living. Those groups held the Methodists together, and became the frame of the Methodist churches.

It had begun with failure. At Oxford, where he was a Fellow, one of a college's teaching staff, a student mocked him and a few friends as Methodists, perhaps, he says, for their regular method of study and behavior. In 1736 he sailed to Georgia, in America, and in a storm on the way he watched Moravians, members of a small German Protestant brotherhood, sing on while the English screamed. Georgia went badly, and on the voyage home he asked who would convert him. Early in 1738 a Moravian convinced him that he lacked the faith that saves.

A year after Aldersgate, George Whitefield, a fellow priest and friend, called him to Bristol, a port city in the west of England, where Whitefield had begun preaching outdoors. Wesley could barely accept it. Most of what we know of these years comes from the journal he kept, and published, for more than fifty years, and there he wrote, "I would have thought the saving of souls almost a sin, if it was not done in a church." By saving souls he meant bringing people to faith and forgiveness. Yet on 2 April 1739, at four in the afternoon, he preached in a brickyard by the city to about three thousand people. In London, a Yorkshire stonemason named John Nelson heard him in Moorfields, open ground where Whitefield also preached, and felt that this man could tell the secrets of his heart. Nelson later became one of his first preachers.

The church did not welcome it. That August, by Wesley's own account, the bishop of Bristol, the senior churchman over that region, told him he had no business preaching in his area and advised him to go. Wesley answered that he would stay where he could do most good. Late that year, eight or ten people in London asked him to pray with them and advise them how to flee from the wrath to come, that is, how to escape God's judgment. He set them a Thursday evening, and more came each week. That was the first society. Its one condition was that wish, and its Rules asked members to do no harm, to do good, and to keep to the church's worship and its sacrament, the shared bread and wine that only an ordained minister could give.

The class came by accident. In February 1742 the Bristol society needed to pay off the debt on its meeting room, so each member who could gave a penny a week, and they divided into classes of about twelve, each with one person to collect it. Soon the collectors told him that some members were not living as they should, and he saw that this was the very thing they had long wanted. From then on each leader saw every member weekly, asked how their souls prospered, and advised, rebuked or comforted them, while collecting for the poor. Some members changed, and some were put out.

The work drew violence. At Wednesbury, in Staffordshire, in October 1743, a mob surrounded the house where he was writing, crying, "Bring out the minister." He agreed to go with them to a magistrate, walking ahead through the dark and heavy rain. On the way back a second mob, from Walsall, seized him and dragged him through that town, many crying out to knock his brains out. He came back with one flap of his waistcoat gone and a little skin off one hand. That winter, one of his preachers reported, mobs there beat Methodists and plundered their houses.

In 1744 he and a few clergy allowed lay preachers, men who were not ordained, only in cases of necessity. After American independence, a later editor of his journal explains, his preachers in America had no one to give them the sacrament. So in 1784 Wesley ordained two of them himself, though only bishops normally ordained, and set apart a fellow priest as their superintendent, his word for their leader.

That year the American preachers formed an Episcopal church, meaning one led by bishops. They took his own line on why God had raised up Methodist preachers, changing one word: not to reform the nation, but the continent, and to spread scriptural holiness, a holy life as the Bible teaches it, over these lands. An American Methodist preacher wrote in 1810 that they kept his Rules with very little alteration, and their book of 1798 still printed classes of about twelve under a leader. Richard Allen, who later founded the African Methodist Episcopal Church, wrote that he chose Methodism for its plain doctrine and good discipline. He also wrote that some preachers became tyrants to Black members, turning them out of society without a trial.

In 1789 Wesley still said the Methodists were never meant to be separate from the Church of England. In 1791 his preachers counted more than 130,000 members in Europe and America.

The day before he died, too weak to make himself understood, he gathered his strength and said twice, "The best of all is, God is with us." A woman who kept his house was at the bedside and wrote it down. He died the next morning, 2 March 1791, nearly eighty-eight, at his house in City Road in London, with friends kneeling around the bed.
