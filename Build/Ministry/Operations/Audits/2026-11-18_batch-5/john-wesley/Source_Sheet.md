# Source sheet: John Wesley

Researcher's sheet for the batch-5 Church Family Tree story, 2026-11-18. The job (Mark's): the warmed heart, preaching in the open air, and the societies and classes that became Methodism. The writer uses the outline and the kit only; this sheet is the evidence behind the outline.

**Conventions.** Line numbers are the file's own lines. Quotes are printed as in the file; the OCR's double spaces are reduced to single and words split across lines are joined. In PA, CM and DIS (long s, two-column bleed) I give the readable words in order and mark OCR faults in [brackets]; where PA is garbled, DIS or LEE prints the same text cleanly and is given beside it. Curnock's notes and Diary entries are marked as such; they are not Wesley's Journal text. Square brackets inside J-text quotes are Curnock's restorations from the manuscript, kept as printed.

**Witness kinds.** OWN = Wesley's own words (Journal, Diary, tracts, letters). CONTEMP = another person in his lifetime who was there (Charles Wesley, John Nelson, the mob's words as Wesley reports them, Elizabeth Ritchie at his death). LATER = written after his death with a rough gap (Lee 1810, about 20 years; Jackson's and Curnock's notes, 70 to 120 years). EDITOR = an editor's note or identification, not the source's own words.

**The main limit.** Most of the life rests on Wesley's own Journal, which he edited and published in extracts during his life (Curnock's notes compare it with his private Diary). For the events of this story there are independent checks at three points: Charles Wesley's journal on the night of 24 May 1738 (M4), John Nelson's account of hearing him preach in the open air (M7), and the Conference Minutes and American records for what the societies became (I1–I5).

## 1. Setting

S1. **Century and place.** Eighteenth century, England; also Georgia in America (1735–1737). The Journal runs from the voyage to Georgia to 1790 (J8 header; CEN "why"). His preaching base in these years: London and Bristol (J2 10522–10528, 10850–10851; J2 32254–32262). Documented.

S2. **Who he was when it began.** A priest of the Church of England and a Fellow of Lincoln College, Oxford. CM title page: "John Wesley, M.A., Fellow of Lincoln College in Oxford" (CM 1). To the bishop of Bristol, 1739: "being ordained a priest, by the commission I then received I am a priest of the Church Universal. And being ordained as Fellow of a College, I was not limited to any particular cure, but have an indeterminate commission to preach the word of God in any part of the Church of England" (J2 16439–16445; Wesley's own record, see M10). OWN. Documented. Plain sense: as a college fellow he had no parish of his own.

S3. **The name "Methodist".** "this is not a Name which they take to themselves; but One fixt upon them, by Way of Reproach, without their Approbation or Consent. It was first given to three or four young Men at Oxford, by a Student of Christ-Church" (CM 229–234; OCR "fit" read as fixt, "Approbation-or" as Approbation or). He says he was "one of the first to whom that Name was given" (CM 186). OWN. Documented. The Large Minutes as printed after his death date the start: "In 1729 the late Mr. Wesley and his brother, upon reading the Bible, saw they could not be saved without holiness" (LM 380–385; "1/29" OCR for 1729). LATER (posthumous wording). Widely Accepted.

S4. **Tradition.** Census: John Wesley is told within **"The Methodist Revival"** (`toldWithin: the-methodist-revival`, CEN). Plain spoken forms: "the Methodists", "the Methodist societies". The church he served all his life: "the Church of England" (J7 29881–29884).

S5. **Age and death.** "On the second day of March, this year (1791,) Mr. John Wesley died in London, at his own house in City Road ... He was not quite eighty-eight years old when he died" (LEE 7561–7567). LATER (about 20 years), Widely Accepted. Elizabeth Ritchie's account of the deathbed (J8 8376 ff.) gives the scene (I7). Do not give a birth date beyond "nearly eighty-eight".

## 2. Recorded moments, in time order

M1. **Georgia and the storm (January 1736).** On the voyage, a storm broke over the German Moravians at their service: "the sea broke over, split the mainsail in pieces, covered the ship, and poured in between the decks ... A terrible screaming began among the English. The Germans [looked up, and without intermission] calmly sang on. I asked one of them afterwards, 'Was you not afraid?' He answered, 'I thank God, no.' I asked, 'But were not your women and children afraid?' He replied mildly, 'No; our women and children are not afraid to die.'" (J1 6957–6986). OWN. Documented.

M2. **Georgia ends badly (December 1737).** The magistrates forbade him to leave the colony until he answered "the allegations laid against me"; he refused bond or bail, and "as soon as evening prayers were over, about eight o'clock, the tide then serving, I shook off the dust of my feet, and left Georgia, after having preached the gospel there ... not as I ought, but as I was able, one year and nearly nine months" (J1 22528–22558). OWN. Documented as his account; the allegations themselves are not set out in the held lines. On the voyage home: "I went to America, to convert the Indians; but oh, who shall convert me?" (J1 23660–23661). OWN. Documented.

M3. **Peter Böhler (March 1738).** "with him Peter Bohler, by whom (in the hand of the great God) I was, on Sunday the 5th, clearly convinced of unbelief, of the want of that faith whereby alone we are saved." He asked whether to stop preaching: "He said, 'Preach faith till you have it; and then, because you have it, you will preach faith.'" (J1 25024–25036). OWN. Documented. Böhler later brought three witnesses who "testified, of their own personal experience, that a true living faith in Christ is inseparable from a sense of pardon for all past and freedom from all present sins" (J1 26904–26907). OWN. Documented.

M4. **Aldersgate Street, 24 May 1738.** The Journal's numbered account (§13–14): "In the evening I went very unwillingly to a society in Aldersgate Street, where one was reading Luther's preface to the Epistle to the Romans. About a quarter before nine, while he was describing the change which God works in the heart through faith in Christ, I felt my heart strangely warmed. I felt I did trust in Christ, Christ alone for salvation; and an assurance was given me that He had taken away my sins, even mine, and saved me from the law of sin and death." (J1 26957–26959, 27070–27074). §15: "I began to pray with all my might for those who had in a more especial manner despitefully used me and persecuted me. I then testified openly to all there what I now first felt in my heart." (J1 27076–27078). OWN. Documented.
- Second witness, the same night, Charles Wesley's journal (Wed. 24 May, CW 7675): "Towards ten, my brother was brought in triumph by a troop of our friends, and declared, 'I believe.' We sang the hymn with great joy, and parted with prayer." (CW 7721–7724). CONTEMP. Documented.
- Earlier that day he opened his Testament on "Thou art not far from the kingdom of God" and heard the anthem "Out of the deep have I called unto Thee, O Lord" at St Paul's (J1 26929–26937). OWN. Side detail.
- **Site.** Curnock: "Two rival sites claim the honour of being the place of John Wesley's conversion" (J1 26952–26955 note); "On the whole the balance of evidence is in favour of the Nettleton Court site" (J1 27030–27033). EDITOR; Contested. Say only "a society meeting in Aldersgate Street, London".
- **Reader.** "It has been suggested that William Holland was the 'one' who 'was reading Luther's Preface'" (J1 27035–27037 note). EDITOR; Inferential-Thin. Do not name the reader.

M5. **The invitation to the fields, Bristol, 31 March 1739.** "In the evening I reached Bristol, and met Mr. Whitefield there. I could scarce reconcile myself at first to this strange way of preaching in the fields, of which he set me an example on Sunday; having been all my life (till very lately) so tenacious of every point relating to decency and order, that I should have thought the saving of souls almost a sin if it had not been done in a church." (J2 10522–10529). OWN. Documented. Whitefield's own words that night, printed by Curnock from Whitefield's Journal: "I was much refreshed with the sight of my honoured friend, Mr. John Wesley, whom God's providence has sent to Bristol" (J2 10558–10560, in Curnock's Diary note). CONTEMP (via editor). Documented.

M6. **The first open-air sermon, 2 April 1739.** "At four in the afternoon I submitted to be more vile, and proclaimed in the highways the glad tidings of salvation, speaking from a little eminence in a ground adjoining to the city, to about three thousand people. The scripture on which I spoke was this ... 'The Spirit of the Lord is upon Me, because He hath anointed Me to preach the gospel to the poor.'" (J2 10850–10852, 10967–10971). OWN. Documented.
- "more vile": Curnock's note gives the source as 2 Sam. vi. 22 (David dancing before the ark), "current in Methodist circles" (J2 10853–10856). EDITOR. Plain sense: he accepted the shame of it.
- The place: Curnock quotes a Moravian letter of 9 April 1739, "a brickyard ... adjoining the city ... to three or four thousand people" (J2 10871–10876 note). CONTEMP via EDITOR. Use Wesley's own "about three thousand".
- Diary entries show the open-air preaching went on in the weeks after with counts in the thousands, e.g. for Sunday 10 June: "7 Bowling Green, 3,000 ... 5 Rose Green, 3,000" (J2 13686–13689). OWN (Diary). Documented.

M7. **A hearer: John Nelson, London, 1739.** A Yorkshire stonemason ("brought up a mason", LEP 1277–1280) working in London: "I was like a wandering bird, cast out of the nest, till Mr. John Wesley came to preach his first sermon in Moorfields. O, that was a blessed morning to my soul! As soon as he got upon the stand, he stroked back his hair, and turned his face towards where I stood, and I thought fixed his eyes upon me ... When he had done, I said, 'This man can tell the secrets of my heart'" (LEP 1631–1642). His friends: "We wish you had never heard Mr. Wesley, for we are afraid it will be the ruin of you" (LEP 1659–1661). CONTEMP (his own journal, written later in life; reprinted 1865). Documented as Nelson's account. Moorfields is an open public ground in London (Nelson says Whitefield also preached there, LEP 1600). Jackson's introduction names Nelson among the first men who, like Thomas Maxfield, began to preach without ordination, "known as 'lay preachers'" (LEP 335–352), and says that the vicar of Birstal reported him for the army (LEP 982–991). EDITOR (Jackson); Widely Accepted.

M8. **"All the world as my parish" (letter, printed under 11 June 1739).** To a friend who said he had no right to preach in other men's parishes: "Man forbids me to do this in another's parish: that is, in effect, to do it at all; seeing I have now no parish of my own, nor probably ever shall. Whom, then, shall I hear, God or man?" (J2 13790–13792). "I look upon all the world as my parish; thus far I mean, that, in whatever part of it I am, I judge it meet, right, and my bounden duty to declare unto all that are willing to hear the glad tidings of salvation." (J2 13807–13810). OWN. Documented. The friend: Curnock identifies him as James Hervey, who had been his pupil (J2 13740–13742 note). EDITOR; Widely Accepted. The phrase is used in this entry's census teaser and "why", but in no live story (checked, §7).

M9. **The first society (late 1739).** Wesley's own Rules: "In the latter end of the year 1739, eight or ten persons came to me in London, who appeared to be deeply convinced of sin, and earnestly groaning for redemption. They desired, as did two or three more the next day, that I would spend some time with them in prayer, and advise them how to flee from the wrath to come ... I appointed a day when they might all come together, which from thence forward they did every week, namely, on Thursday in the evening." (LEE 656–667, Lee quoting Wesley; the same text in DIS 6926–6940 in the third person: "came to Mr. Wesley"). OWN as quoted LATER. Documented. Wesley's own 1748 telling: "If you will all of you come together every Thursday, in the Evening, I will gladly spend some Time with you in Prayer, and give you the best Advice I can. Thus arose, without any previous Design on either Side, what was afterwards called A Society: A very Innocent Name, and very common in London" (PA 194–199, cleaned; OCR "ej. ther" read as either, "Landon" as London). OWN. Documented.
- **What a society was.** "a company of men having the form and seeking the power of godliness, united in order to pray together, to receive the word of exhortation, and to watch over one another in love, that they may help each other to work out their salvation" (DIS 6942–6946; LEE 671–675 has "persons"). OWN as printed. Documented.
- **The one condition.** "There is One only Condition previously required, in those who desire Admission into this Society, A Desire to flee from the Wrath to come, and to be saved from their Sins. But wherever this Desire is fixt in the Soul, it will be shewn by its Fruits." (PA 209–213, cleaned); the same in DIS 6984–6988. OWN. Documented. The fruits the Rules then list: doing no harm, doing good, and attending "all the Ordinances of God" (public worship, the Lord's Supper, prayer, Scripture, fasting) (PA 213–247). OWN. Documented.

M10. **The bishop of Bristol, August 1739.** Bishop Butler, in Wesley's own record of their talk: "Sir, the pretending to extraordinary revelations and gifts of the Holy Ghost is a horrid thing—a very horrid thing!" ... "You have no business here; you are not commissioned to preach in this diocese. Therefore I advise you to go hence." Wesley: "My lord, my business on earth is to do what good I can. Wherever, therefore, I think I can do most good there must I stay, so long as I think so. At present I think I can do most good here; therefore, here I stay." (J2 16385–16387, 16425–16435). OWN, but preserved through Henry Moore's *Life*, who says "The original is now before me in his own hand", as quoted in Curnock's note (J2 16298–16306, note); Curnock dates it to the second interview, 18 August (J2 16458–16460). Documented as Wesley's record, kept by a LATER editor. Plain sense: Butler was the bishop of Bristol; Wesley had no licence for his diocese.

M11. **Classes begin, Bristol, 15 February 1742.** "Many met together to consult on a proper method for discharging the public debt; and it was at length agreed, (1) that every member of the society who was able should contribute a penny a week; (2) that the whole society should be divided into little companies or classes—about twelve in each class; and (3) that one person in each class should receive the contribution of the rest, and bring it in to the stewards, weekly." (J2 31833–31840). OWN. Documented. "The public debt": Curnock's note, "for building the Room in the Horsefair" (J2 31855–31857, note). EDITOR.
- **How collecting money turned into pastoral care** (Wesley's 1748 telling): "one stood up and said, 'Let every Member of the Society give a Penny a Week till all are paid.' Another answered, 'But many of them are poor, and cannot afford to do it.' Then said he, 'Put Eleven of the Poorest with me, and if they can give any Thing, well. I will call on them weekly, and if they can give Nothing, I will give for them as well as for myself.'" ... "In a while some of these inform'd me, They found, such and such an one did not live as he ought. It struck me immediately, 'This is the Thing: The very Thing we have wanted so long.' I called together all the Leaders of the Classes ... and desired, That each would make a particular Enquiry, into the Behaviour of those whom he saw weekly. They did so. Many disorderly Walkers were detected. Some turned from the Evil of their Ways. Some were put away from us." (PA 503–533, cleaned; OCR "ee up" read as stood up, "Claes" as Classes, "tem" as them, "nve" as give). OWN. Documented.
- **The man who proposed it.** Curnock: "Captain Foy proposed the penny-a-week subscription", citing Wesley's "Thoughts upon Methodism" (J2 31864–31870 note). That work is not held. EDITOR; do not name him.
- **The leader's work.** "I. To see each Person in his Class, once a Week at the least: In order, To enquire how their Souls prosper? To advise, reprove, comfort or exhort, as Occasion may require; To receive what they are willing to give, toward the Relief of the Poor. II. To meet the Minister and the Stewards of the Society, in order, To inform the Minister of any that are Sick, or of any that are disorderly and will not be reproved; To pay to the Stewards what they have receiv'd of their several Classes" (PA 549–567, cleaned). OWN. Documented. Soon they met together rather than at home, because "Many Persons lived with Masters, Mistresses or [others] who would not suffer them to be thus visited" (PA 580–583, cleaned; the word after Mistresses is lost in the OCR). OWN.
- **The word.** Curnock: "'Class' has no suggestion of teaching in it. It was the Latin classis" (J2 31881–31884 note). EDITOR. Do not describe a class as a Bible class or a lesson.
- **London, 25 March 1742.** "I appointed several earnest and sensible men to meet me, to whom I showed the great difficulty I had long found of knowing the people who desired to be under my care. After much discourse, they all agreed there could be no better way ... than to divide them into classes, like those at Bristol ... This was the origin of our classes at London, for which I can never sufficiently praise God" (J2 32254–32262). OWN. Documented.

M12. **The mob at Wednesbury, 20 October 1743.** "Before five the mob surrounded the house again in greater numbers than ever. The cry of one and all was, 'Bring out the minister; we will have the minister.'" (J3 5739–5742). Asked before a justice what the Methodists had done: "'Why, an't please you, they sing psalms all day; nay, and make folks rise at five in the morning. And what would your Worship advise us to do?' 'To go home,' said Mr. Lane, 'and be quiet.'" (J3 5784–5787). The Walsall mob "dragged me along"; "Many cried out, 'No, no! knock his brains out; down with him; kill him at once.'" (J3 5803–5837). He came back to Wednesbury "a little before ten ... having lost only one flap of my waistcoat and a little skin from one of my hands" (J3 5858–5860). A woman of Darlaston who had led one mob fought for him and "had probably been killed" (J3 5866–5875). OWN. Documented as his account. Charles Wesley, told the story days later, adds a rioter's words: "all the fault I find with him is, that he preaches better than our parsons" (J3 5808–5820, Curnock's note citing Charles's Journal, 25 Oct. 1743). CONTEMP via EDITOR. In January and February 1744 James Jones sent Wesley an account, which he printed, of mobs at Darlaston and Wednesbury who beat Methodists, assaulted a woman and plundered houses (J3 6697–6830, running heads "Rioting in Wednesbury", Feb. 1744). Reported to Wesley; Documented as Jones's report.

M13. **The first Conference and lay preachers, June 1744.** "The following persons being met at the Foundery, John Wesley, Charles Wesley, John Hodges, Rector of Wenvo, Henry Piers, Vicar of Bexley, Samuel Taylor, Vicar of Quinton, and John Meriton ... the design of our meeting was proposed, namely, to consider, 1. What to teach; 2. How to teach; and, 3. What to do; i.e., How to regulate our doctrine, discipline, and practice." (MIN 450–460). "Q. 1. Are Lay Assistants allowable? A. Only in cases of necessity." (MIN 2011–2012). OWN (the conference's minutes). Documented. Plain sense: lay assistants were preachers who were not ordained.

M14. **Why the preachers were sent.** "Q. 1. In what view may the Methodist Preachers be considered? A. As messengers sent by the Lord, out of the common way, to provoke the regular Clergy to jealousy, and to supply their lack of service towards those who are perishing for want of knowledge; and, above all, to reform the nation, by spreading scriptural holiness over the land." (LM 363–371, text as printed 1797). An earlier form in MIN's comparison of the Minutes: "To reform the nation, and, in particular, the Church; to spread scriptural holiness over the land" (MIN 64830–64838). OWN (conference text). Documented.

M15. **Ordaining for America, September 1784.** Journal: "Being now clear in my own mind, I took a step which I had long weighed in my mind, and appointed Mr. Whatcoat and Mr. Vasey to go and serve the desolate sheep in America." (J7 1110–1113). His Diary for the same days: "Prayed, ordained R[ichar]d Whatcoat and T[homas] Vasey" (J7 1144) and "Prayed, ordained Dr. Coke" (J7 1151), with Curnock adding from Wesley's certificate "as a Superintendent, by the imposition of my hands" (J7 1151–1152). OWN. Documented. Note: the published Journal says "appointed"; the private Diary says "ordained". Curnock's summary of his reasoning: he was "too loyal a churchman to advise his people in the American Societies to countenance any violation of ecclesiastical law and order", and acted on "a theory which he had held for many years—namely, that there was no distinction between presbyters (or elders) and bishops" (J7 250–257). EDITOR. The bishop's role in ordaining is the point of conflict; the story should say only that he ordained men himself, which a priest of his church did not normally do, and credit that framing to the editor if used.

M16. **His own summary, Dublin, Easter Day 1789.** "I met the society, and explained to them at large the original design of the Methodists, viz. not to be a distinct party, but to stir up all parties, Christians or heathens, to worship God in spirit and in truth; but the Church of England in particular, to which they belonged from the beginning. With this view, I have uniformly gone on for fifty years, never varying from the doctrine of the Church at all, nor from her discipline, of choice, but of necessity. So, in a course of years, necessity was laid upon me (as I have proved elsewhere), (1) to preach in the open air; (2) to pray extempore; (3) to form societies; (4) to accept of the assistance of lay preachers" (J7 29875–29890; "(i)" OCR for (1)). Two days later: "the design of Methodism, namely, not to separate from the Church, but to unite together all the children of God that were scattered abroad" (J7 29940–29943). OWN. Documented.

M17. **Death, 2 March 1791.** Elizabeth Ritchie, who kept his house in his last months and was at the bedside (J8 8397–8420, Curnock's introduction; EDITOR for the setting): "with all the remaining strength he had, cried out, 'The best of all is, God is with us'; and then ... lifting up his dying arm in token of [victory, and raising his feeble voice with a] holy triumph not to be expressed, again repeated the heart-reviving words, 'The best of all is, God is with us!'" (J8 9031–9037; bracketed words are Curnock's from other copies). Through most of the following night "he could only get out: 'I'll praise—I'll praise!'" (J8 9067–9071); "the last word he was heard to articulate was, 'Farewell!'" (J8 9095). CONTEMP (an eyewitness, printed by Curnock from the Ritchie manuscript). Documented as her account. Date and place: LEE 7561–7563 (S5).

## 3. Impact, heaviest first (what later witnesses carried on and used)

I1. **His Rules and the class went to America unchanged.** Jesse Lee, an American Methodist preacher, on the societies in 1770: "The Rules by which the societies were then governed, were the same that Mr. Wesley had previously drawn up in England, and have with very little alteration been continued among us ever since" (LEE 1366–1371). The American church's Discipline of 1798 prints them as "The Nature, Design, and General Rules of the United Societies", with "This was the rise of the United Society, first in Europe and then in America" and "each society is divided into smaller companies, called classes, according to their respective places of abode. There are but twelve persons in every class; one of whom is stiled The Leader" (DIS 6920–6957, cleaned; OCR "jinurica" read as America). LATER (Lee about 40 years after 1770; DIS within his decade of death). Documented. DEFENDED: the Rules and the class meeting.

I2. **The conference he left kept his discipline.** At the first Conference after his death (Manchester, July 1791) the preachers wrote that they would show their veneration for him "by endeavouring, with great humility and diffidence, to follow and imitate him in doctrine, discipline, and life" (MIN 33726, 33770–33777). They read out his letter of 1785 asking them to "let all things go on ... exactly in the same manner as when I was with you" (MIN 33730–33745). CONTEMP. Documented.

I3. **The American church was formed in 1784 on his plan and words.** The Christmas Conference of 1784 at Baltimore (LEE 4164), as Lee prints its minutes: "We will form ourselves into an Episcopal church under the direction of superintendants, elders, deacons, and helpers" and, asked God's design in raising up the Methodist preachers, "To reform the continent, and to spread scriptural holiness over these lands" (LEE 4277–4287). LATER (Lee printing the 1784 minutes). Documented. Compare M14: the American preachers changed only "the nation" to "the continent". The same minutes bound them to obey "Mr. Wesley's commands" in church government, which Lee says "was afterwards the cause of some uneasiness" (LEE 4266–4269). Documented.

I4. **Numbers.** The Conference of July 1791 counted members of the societies: "Total in Europe" 72,476 and "Total in Europe and America" 136,622 (MIN 35404–35412; the America total prints as "61,146", an OCR fault for 64,146, since 57,621 for the United States plus 6,525 for the West Indies make 64,146, and 64,146 + 72,476 = 136,622). A note says only half the West Indies return was set down (MIN 35418–35420). CONTEMP. Documented. Say "more than 130,000 members in Europe and America, counted a few months after his death".

I5. **Lay preachers who had been hearers.** John Nelson heard him in Moorfields in 1739 (M7) and became one of his first lay preachers (Jackson, LEP 348, 982–991, EDITOR). The volume's own content note calls it "The autobiographies of Wesley's first lay preachers" (LEP header). Widely Accepted.

I6. **Black Methodists chose his discipline, and suffered its abuse.** Richard Allen, founder of the African Methodist Episcopal Church (CEN note at line 973), on why he wanted his Philadelphia congregation to stay Methodist: "I was confident that there was no religious sect or denomination would suit the capacity of the colored people as well as the Methodist; for the plain and simple gospel suits best for any people; for the unlearned can understand, and the learned are sure to understand; and the reason that the Methodist is so successful in the awakening and conversion of the colored people, the plain doctrine and having a good discipline. But in many cases the preachers would act to please their own fancy, without discipline, till some of them became such tyrants, and more especially to the colored people. They would turn them out of society, giving them no trial, for the smallest offence" (ALLEN 734–750). LATER (Allen wrote in old age; 1880 reprint of 1833). Documented as Allen's view. This is the record's force: the same class discipline that held people together could be used to put them out (compare PA 533, "Some were put away from us").

I7. **Who keeps it today (optional, one clause).** The tree's own tradition entries: "A movement that began as a society inside the Church of England spent the nineteenth century becoming a worldwide family of churches" (CEN, `nineteenth-century-methodism` longDescription); this entry's census legacy: "The Methodist churches grew from the societies he gathered" (CEN, `john-wesley` legacy). Use only as "the Methodist churches", no number or present-day claim.

D1. **"Strangely warmed" is already used on the tree** (`the-methodist-revival` longDescription, CEN). By the kit's rule it cannot be quoted again without Mark's leave. The outline uses the next sentence of the same entry (M4) and asks Mark (outline, Quotes).
