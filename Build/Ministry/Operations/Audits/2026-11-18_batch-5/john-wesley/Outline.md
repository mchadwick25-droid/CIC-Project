# Outline: John Wesley

Sheet references (S, M, I, K, D, Q) point to `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder. File short names: J1–J8 = Wesley's Journal, Curnock edition, vols. I–VIII; CW = Charles Wesley's Journal; PA = Wesley, *Plain Account of the People called Methodists* (1749); MIN = Conference Minutes; DIS = American *Discipline* (1798); LEE = Jesse Lee, *Short History* (1810); LEP = John Nelson's Journal in *Lives of Early Methodist Preachers*; ALLEN = Richard Allen's own *Life*.

- **The essence:** John Wesley was a priest (an ordained minister) of the Church of England, England's state church. One May evening in 1738 he felt, for the first time, that he trusted Christ alone and that God had forgiven him (his own Journal, "what I now first felt", J1 27078, M4; Documented). From 1739 he preached outdoors, in fields and open ground, to crowds he counted in thousands, though he had thought preaching belonged only inside a church (his own Journal, M5–M6; Documented). And he gathered those who answered into "societies", small voluntary prayer groups within the church, and split each society into weekly "classes" of about twelve under a leader. Those groups held the "Methodists", a nickname from his Oxford days, together and became the frame of the Methodist churches (his own Plain Account and Journal, M9, M11; the American record, I1; Documented).

- **Also told:**
  - **What changed that evening, at a meeting in Aldersgate Street, London:** what he felt that evening: that he did trust in Christ alone, and an assurance that his sins were taken away (J1 27071–27074). He told the room at once, and that night his brother heard him say "I believe" (M4; Documented, two witnesses).
  - **What preaching outdoors cost him:** he had thought saving souls almost a sin outside a church building (Q2). The bishop of Bristol, the senior churchman over that region, told him he had no business preaching there; Wesley stayed (M10; Wesley's own record, kept by a later biographer; Documented as his record). A mob dragged him through a town, some crying for his death (M12; his own account; Documented).
  - **How a society and a class worked:** the one condition for joining was a wish to escape God's coming judgment and be freed from sin. A class leader saw each member weekly, asked how they were living, advised, rebuked or comforted, and collected money for the poor. Members who would not change were put out (M9, M11; PA; Documented). The class began in 1742 as a way to pay off a building debt, a penny a week each (M11; Documented).
  - **Who preached:** ordinary men who were not ordained as ministers, allowed at first "only in cases of necessity" (M13; Documented). John Nelson, a stonemason who heard him preach outdoors in London, became one of the first (M7, I5; Nelson's own account; Widely Accepted for his becoming a preacher).
  - **What it became:** after American independence, in 1784, Wesley ordained ministers for America himself, though only bishops normally did so (Curnock's note, J7 250–257, editor). American Methodists then formed their own church that year and kept his written Rules for the societies "with very little alteration" (M15, I1, I3; Documented). At the yearly meeting of his preachers held a few months after his death, the societies counted more than 130,000 members in Europe and America (I4; Documented). In 1789 he still said the Methodists were never meant to be a separate group from the Church of England (M16; Documented as his claim).

- **Length:** 3 minutes (about 450 words, Neo). He is a major figure on the tree, his own record is rich and close, and the job has three linked parts (the heart, the fields, the societies) plus a connection that needs room: the class and its Rules crossing to America. The force in the record (the bishop, the mob, members put out, Allen's complaint) must stay. This is the room the story needs, not filler.

- **Setting:** facts for the writer, not a sentence to paste.
  - Time: the eighteenth century; Aldersgate on 24 May 1738 (M4); the fields from April 1739 (M6); death on 2 March 1791 (S5).
  - Place: London and Bristol; Georgia in America before (M1, M2). Spoken: "in London", "in Bristol, a port city in the west of England".
  - Tradition: "the Methodist revival" (census `toldWithin`), inside "the Church of England" (S4, M16).
  - Who he was: a priest of the Church of England and a Fellow of an Oxford college, with no parish of his own (S2, M8). The name "Methodist" began at Oxford as a mocking nickname (S3).

- **Words to explain** (give the plain word, or work the gloss into the sentence where the word is first heard):
  - **priest / ordained:** a minister of the Church of England, formally set apart by a bishop to lead worship. "Ordained" = formally made a minister.
  - **Church of England:** England's official state church, Protestant, led by bishops. Methodists began inside it (J7 29881–29884).
  - **Fellow of an Oxford college:** a member of a college's teaching staff; he had no parish (S2, J2 16443–16445).
  - **parish:** the local area served by one church and its minister. He had none of his own (J2 13790–13792).
  - **bishop / diocese:** a bishop is the senior churchman over a region; that region is his diocese. Better: "the bishop of Bristol told him he had no business preaching in his area" (J2 16427–16429).
  - **Methodist:** at first a mocking nickname for a few Oxford students, he says perhaps for their regular method of study and behaviour; not yet a church (CM 229–240).
  - **society:** a voluntary group that met weekly to pray and be advised, inside the Church of England, not a separate church. Wesley calls it "a very innocent name, and very common in London" for any group of people joined together (PA 197–199). Differs from today's sense of a club or a church.
  - **class:** a group of about twelve members meeting weekly with a leader to talk about how they were living; not a lesson. Curnock: the word "has no suggestion of teaching in it" (J2 31881–31884).
  - **lay preacher / lay assistant:** a preacher who was not an ordained minister (MIN 2011–2012).
  - **Moravians:** members of a small German Protestant brotherhood; Wesley calls them simply "the Germans" (J1 6960).
  - **the Conference:** Wesley's yearly meeting with his preachers to settle what to teach and how to run the societies (MIN 450–460).
  - **superintendent:** the leader Wesley set over the American preachers; his own word for the office (J7 1151–1152).
  - **Episcopal church:** a church led by bishops; the Americans' own word in 1784 (LEE 4277–4279).
  - **sacrament / the Lord's Supper:** the shared bread and wine of Christian worship, also called Communion (PA 244–252; "the Supper of the Lord" is among the "Ordinances of God" listed, OCR "per of the Los p").
  - **"flee from the wrath to come":** escape God's judgment to come. Give the plain sense beside the phrase (LEE 661–662).
  - **"law of sin and death" (Q1):** sin's hold over him, and death as its end (Paul, Romans 8:2).
  - **"saving souls" (Q2):** bringing people to faith and forgiveness.
  - **"decency and order" (Q2):** the church's proper rules for where and how worship is done.
  - **"to be more vile":** to accept being looked down on; a Bible phrase (J2 10853–10856, Curnock's note).
  - **salvation / assurance (Q1):** being forgiven and made right with God; "assurance" = a felt certainty of it.
  - **revival:** a spread of new religious fervour among many people at once.

- **Shape:** Founder or leader. Trap: titles and offices in place of acts. Tell what he did on given days (the meeting, the brickyard, the penny a week, the mob, the ordinations).

- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen. Aldersgate, the evening of 24 May 1738.** He went "very unwillingly" to a society meeting in Aldersgate Street in London, where someone was reading Luther's preface to Paul's letter to the Romans. About a quarter to nine, as the reader described the change God works in the heart through faith in Christ, Wesley felt his heart warmed (narrator's words, see the Quotes note), and then Q1. Leads straight into the essence: what that evening started. (J1 26957–26959, 27070–27074.)
  2. **Bristol, 2 April 1739.** At four in the afternoon, on a rise of ground in a brickyard by the city, an Oxford priest preached to about three thousand people in the open air, on "The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he hath anointed me to preach the gospel to the poor." Two days before he had thought this almost a sin. Use only if the writer reaches the essence within about 40 words. (J2 10850–10852, 10967–10971.)

- **Story beats** (beats 1–2 are the hook and essence, the first minute, about 150 words; from beat 3 the life runs once, in order):
  1. **The hook: Aldersgate.** As Opening 1. He prayed for those who had wronged him and told the room what he now felt. Towards ten his friends brought him to his brother Charles, and he declared, "I believe." (M4. J1 27070–27078; CW 7721–7724. Documented; two witnesses.)
  2. **The essence.** What came of that evening: preaching in the open air, and societies and classes that held the people together and became the Methodist churches. (Essence above. Documented.)
  3. **Before: the nickname, the storm, the failure.** At Oxford a student mocked three or four young men, he among them, as "Methodists", he says perhaps for their regular method of study and behaviour (he also gives a second possible origin). On the voyage to Georgia in 1736, in a storm, German Moravians sang on while the English screamed; one told him their women and children were not afraid to die. Georgia went badly: he left by night in December 1737, after the magistrates forbade him to go, under allegations he says he was never allowed to answer, and wrote on the way home, "I went to America to convert the Indians; but oh, who shall convert me?" At Oxford in March 1738 a Moravian, Peter Böhler, convinced him that he lacked the faith that saves, and told him to preach faith until he had it. One beat only; do not dwell. (S3, M1–M3. CM 229–234; J1 6957–6986, 22528–22558, 23660–23661, 25024–25036. Own words; Documented. "Moravians": a German Protestant brotherhood.)
  4. **The fields, Bristol, spring 1739.** George Whitefield, a fellow priest and friend, had begun preaching outdoors near Bristol and called Wesley to help (Curnock's note, J2 10699–10700). Wesley could barely accept it (Q2). On 2 April, at four in the afternoon, he preached in a brickyard by the city to about three thousand. He called it submitting "to be more vile", a Bible phrase for accepting shame (Curnock's note gives the verse). In London, John Nelson, a Yorkshire stonemason, heard his first sermon in Moorfields in London, where Whitefield also preached outdoors (LEP 1600), and said, "This man can tell the secrets of my heart." (M5–M7. J2 10522–10529, 10850–10856, 10967–10971; LEP 1631–1642. Own words; Nelson's own account; Documented.)
  5. **The cost, and the societies.** The church did not welcome it. In August 1739 the bishop of Bristol told him he had no business preaching in that diocese and advised him to go; Wesley answered that he would stay where he could do most good. Mark that this is Wesley's own record, kept by a later biographer. Late in 1739, eight or ten people came to him in London asking him to pray with them and advise them how "to flee from the wrath to come". He set a Thursday evening; more came each week. That was the society: people joined to pray together, hear teaching and "watch over one another in love". The one condition was that desire; the Rules then asked them to do no harm, do good, and keep to the church's worship and sacrament. (M10, M9. J2 16385–16435; LEE 656–667; PA 194–247; DIS 6920–6988. Documented.)
  6. **The class, 1742, and the mob, 1743.** In Bristol, in February 1742, the society met to find a way to pay off its debt (for its new meeting room, Curnock's note says, J2 31855–31857). They agreed that each member who could would give a penny a week, and divided into classes of about twelve, each with one person to collect it. Soon the collectors told him some members were not living as they should. He wrote, "This is the thing: the very thing we have wanted so long." From then the leader saw each member every week, asked how their souls prospered, advised, rebuked or comforted them, and collected for the poor. Some members changed; some were put out. He brought the classes to London that March. The work drew violence. At Wednesbury, in Staffordshire, in October 1743 a mob surrounded the house where he was writing, crying "Bring out the minister". They marched him by night in heavy rain to a magistrate's house; asked there what the matter was, one said the Methodists sang psalms all day and made people rise at five, and the magistrate told them to go home. On the way back a second mob, from Walsall, seized him and dragged him through that town, many crying "knock his brains out", until a few men, led by one who had just been heading the mob, took him through the crowd. He came back with a flap of his waistcoat gone and a little skin off one hand. That winter, a member reported, mobs beat Methodists in the district and plundered their houses. (M11, M12. J2 31833–31840, 32254–32262; PA 503–583; J3 5739–5875, 6697–6830. Own words; the 1744 riots as James Jones's report printed by Wesley; Documented.)
  7. **The preachers, America and the end.** In June 1744 he met his brother and four others, three of them parish clergy, at the Foundery, the London society's meeting place (LEP 302), to settle "what to teach; how to teach; and what to do"; they allowed preachers who were not ordained "only in cases of necessity". The Revolution had changed church affairs in the new United States ("passed through a very considerable change", as the American preachers' minutes of their Christmas conference of 1784 later put it, LEE 4164, 4271–4274); Curnock adds that the sacraments were no longer given there (J7 240–246, editor). In September 1784 he ordained two preachers for America and set apart a fellow priest as their superintendent, though he was not a bishop. In 1789 he told a Dublin society he had never left the Church's teaching, and left its rules only from necessity: to preach in the open air, to pray in his own words, to form societies and to take lay preachers. He died at his house in City Road, London, on 2 March 1791, nearly eighty-eight. (M13, M15, M16, M17. MIN 450–460, 2011–2012; J7 1110–1113, 1144, 1151–1152, 29875–29890; LEE 4271–4287, 7561–7567. Documented. "Revolution": the War of Independence; LEE 4271–4274 gives the change as the reason for the new plan.)

- **What came of them** (about a third of the story; ranked heaviest first):
  1. **The class and the Rules went to America. DEFENDED: the Rules and the class meeting.** Jesse Lee, an American Methodist preacher, wrote in 1810 that the Rules the societies kept from 1770 "were the same that Mr. Wesley had previously drawn up in England", continued "with very little alteration". The American *Discipline* of 1798 prints them, with "the rise of the United Society, first in Europe and then in America", and classes of twelve under a leader. (I1. LEE 1366–1371; DIS 6920–6957. Documented.)
  2. **The American church took his words.** In 1784 the American preachers formed "an Episcopal church" and answered the question of God's design in raising up the Methodist preachers with his own line, changing one word: not "to reform the nation" but "to reform the continent, and to spread scriptural holiness over these lands". (I3, M14. LEE 4277–4287; LM 363–371. Documented.)
  3. **The preachers he left kept his discipline.** At the first Conference after his death they promised "to follow and imitate him in doctrine, discipline, and life". They counted more than 130,000 members in Europe and America. (I2, I4. MIN 33770–33777, 35398–35412. Documented; the total is checked by sum, Sheet I4.)
  4. **The discipline drew people and could be used against them.** Richard Allen, who later founded the African Methodist Episcopal Church, chose Methodism because "the plain and simple gospel suits best for any people", and for its "plain doctrine and having a good discipline". He also wrote that some preachers became "tyrants" to Black members, turning them out of society without a trial. Told in Allen's voice. (I6. ALLEN 734–750. Documented as Allen's view.)
  Optional, one clause, never the closing line: "The Methodist churches grew from the societies he gathered" (this entry's census legacy, I7).

  1. **Q1, Aldersgate.** Source text (J1 27071–27074): "I felt I did trust in Christ, Christ alone for salvation ; and an assurance was given me that He had taken away my sins, even mine, and saved me from the law of sin and death."   2. **Q2, Bristol, 31 March 1739.** Source text (J2 10524–10529): "I could scarce reconcile myself at first to this strange way of preaching in the fields, of which he set me an example on Sunday ; having been all my life (till very lately) so tenacious of every point relating to decency and order, that I should have thought the saving of souls almost a sin if it had not been done in a church." - **Connection to carry into the story:** What passed on was a way of holding converts together, not only a message: the society with one condition for entry, and inside it the class of about twelve meeting weekly under a leader who asked each member how their soul prospered (PA 549–567; J2 31833–31840). It passed to America in his own written Rules, kept "with very little alteration" (LEE 1366–1371), printed in the American church's book (DIS 6920–6957), and in his own words about why the preachers were sent, changed only from "the nation" to "the continent" (LEE 4286–4287; LM 369–371). The listener needs to understand three things the writer must make plain: the class began by accident, as a way to collect a penny a week, and became the place where people were known and held to account; it was ordinary people, not clergy, who led classes and later preached; and this structure, more than any one sermon, is what later Methodists took with them and kept. Carry it through what the leaders and the American preachers did, not through a "this matters because" sentence. Allen's witness shows it reached Black Methodists too, and was sometimes turned against them.

- **Ending:**
  1. **Chosen.** The day before he died, too weak to make himself understood, he gathered his strength and said twice, "The best of all is, God is with us" (Q3). He died the next morning, 2 March 1791, at his house in City Road, with friends kneeling round the bed. Credit Elizabeth Ritchie. (J8 9031–9037, 9095–9100; LEE 7561–7563.)
