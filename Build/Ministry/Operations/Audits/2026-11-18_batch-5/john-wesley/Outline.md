# Outline: John Wesley

Sheet references (S, M, I, K, D, Q) point to `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder. File short names: J1–J8 = Wesley's Journal, Curnock edition, vols. I–VIII; CW = Charles Wesley's Journal; PA = Wesley, *Plain Account of the People called Methodists* (1749); MIN = Conference Minutes; DIS = American *Discipline* (1798); LEE = Jesse Lee, *Short History* (1810); LEP = John Nelson's Journal in *Lives of Early Methodist Preachers*; ALLEN = Richard Allen's own *Life*.

- **The essence:** An Oxford priest of the Church of England felt, one May evening in 1738, that he trusted Christ alone and was forgiven (his own Journal, M4; Documented). The next year he began preaching in the open air to crowds the parish churches did not reach (his own Journal, M5–M6; Documented). And he gathered those who answered into societies, and the societies into weekly classes of about twelve, which held the Methodists together and became the frame of the Methodist churches (his own Plain Account and Journal, M9, M11; the American record, I1; Documented).

- **Also told:**
  - **What changed at Aldersgate:** not new teaching but trust felt as his own; he told the room at once, and his brother heard him say "I believe" that night (M4; Documented, two witnesses).
  - **What the fields cost him:** he had thought saving souls almost a sin outside a church (Q2); a bishop told him he had no business preaching in his diocese, and he stayed (M10; Wesley's own record, kept by a later editor; Documented as his record); a mob dragged him through a town and called for his death (M12; his own account; Documented).
  - **How a society and a class worked:** one condition to join, a desire "to flee from the wrath to come"; a leader who saw each member weekly, asked how their souls prospered, advised, rebuked or comforted, and collected for the poor; members who would not change were put out (M9, M11; PA; Documented). The class began as a plan to pay off a building debt, a penny a week (M11; Documented).
  - **Who preached:** ordinary men, "lay assistants", allowed at first "only in cases of necessity" (M13; Documented); John Nelson, a stonemason who heard him in Moorfields, was one of the first (M7, I5; Nelson's own account; Widely Accepted for his becoming a preacher).
  - **What it became:** in 1784 he ordained men for America himself, and American Methodists formed their own church that year, keeping his Rules "with very little alteration" (M15, I1, I3; Documented). At his death the societies counted more than 130,000 members in Europe and America (I4; Documented). He said to the end that he had never meant to leave the Church of England (M16; Documented as his claim).

- **Length:** 3 minutes (about 450 words, Neo). He is a major figure on the tree, his own record is rich and close, and the job has three linked parts (the heart, the fields, the societies) plus a connection that needs room: the class and its Rules crossing to America. The force in the record (the bishop, the mob, members put out, Allen's complaint) must stay. This is the room the story needs, not filler.

- **Setting:** facts for the writer, not a sentence to paste.
  - Time: the eighteenth century; Aldersgate on 24 May 1738 (M4); the fields from April 1739 (M6); death on 2 March 1791 (S5).
  - Place: London and Bristol; Georgia in America before (M1, M2). Spoken: "in London", "in Bristol, a port city in the west of England".
  - Tradition: "the Methodist revival" (census `toldWithin`), inside "the Church of England" (S4, M16).
  - Who he was: a priest of the Church of England and a Fellow of an Oxford college, with no parish of his own (S2, M8). The name "Methodist" began at Oxford as a mocking nickname (S3).

- **Shape:** Founder or leader. Trap: titles and offices in place of acts. Tell what he did on given days (the meeting, the brickyard, the penny a week, the mob, the ordinations).

- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen. Aldersgate, the evening of 24 May 1738.** He went "very unwillingly" to a society meeting in Aldersgate Street in London, where someone was reading Luther's preface to Paul's letter to the Romans. About a quarter to nine, as the reader described the change God works in the heart through faith in Christ, Wesley felt his heart warmed (narrator's words, see the Quotes note), and then Q1. Leads straight into the essence: what that evening started. (J1 26957–26959, 27070–27074.)
  2. **Bristol, 2 April 1739.** At four in the afternoon, on a rise of ground in a brickyard by the city, an Oxford priest preached to about three thousand people in the open air, on "The Spirit of the Lord is upon me, because he hath anointed me to preach the gospel to the poor." Two days before he had thought this almost a sin. Use only if the writer reaches the essence within about 40 words. (J2 10850–10852, 10967–10971.)

- **Story beats** (beats 1–2 are the hook and essence, the first minute, about 150 words; from beat 3 the life runs once, in order):
  1. **The hook: Aldersgate.** As Opening 1. He prayed for those who had wronged him and told the room what he now felt. Towards ten his friends brought him to his brother Charles, and he declared, "I believe." (M4. J1 27070–27078; CW 7721–7724. Documented; two witnesses.)
  2. **The essence.** What came of that evening: preaching in the open air, and societies and classes that held the people together and became the Methodist churches. (Essence above. Documented.)
  3. **Before: the nickname, the storm, the failure.** At Oxford a few young men, he among them, were mocked as "Methodists" for their strict rule of life. On the voyage to Georgia in 1736, in a storm, German Moravians sang on while the English screamed; one told him their women and children were not afraid to die. Georgia went badly: he left by night in December 1737, after the magistrates forbade him to go, under allegations he says he was never allowed to answer, and wrote on the way home, "I went to America to convert the Indians; but oh, who shall convert me?" At Oxford in March 1738 a Moravian, Peter Böhler, convinced him that he lacked the faith that saves, and told him to preach faith until he had it. One beat only; do not dwell. (S3, M1–M3. CM 229–234; J1 6957–6986, 22528–22558, 23660–23661, 25024–25036. Own words; Documented. "Moravians": a German Protestant brotherhood.)
  4. **The fields, Bristol, spring 1739.** George Whitefield, a fellow priest and friend, had begun preaching outdoors near Bristol and called Wesley to help (Curnock's note, J2 10699–10700). Wesley could barely accept it (Q2). On 2 April, at four in the afternoon, he preached in a brickyard by the city to about three thousand. He called it submitting "to be more vile", a Bible phrase for accepting shame (Curnock's note gives the verse). In London, John Nelson, a Yorkshire stonemason, heard his first sermon in Moorfields in London, where Whitefield also preached outdoors (LEP 1600), and said, "This man can tell the secrets of my heart." (M5–M7. J2 10522–10529, 10850–10856, 10967–10971; LEP 1631–1642. Own words; Nelson's own account; Documented.)
  5. **The societies, and the cost.** Late in 1739, eight or ten people came to him in London asking him to pray with them and advise them how "to flee from the wrath to come". He set a Thursday evening; more came each week. That was the society: people joined to pray together, hear teaching and "watch over one another in love". The one condition was that desire; the Rules then asked them to do no harm, do good, and keep to the church's worship and sacrament. The church did not welcome it. In August 1739 the bishop of Bristol told him he had no business preaching in that diocese and advised him to go; Wesley answered that he would stay where he could do most good. Mark that this is Wesley's own record, kept by a later biographer. (M9, M10. LEE 656–667; PA 194–247; DIS 6920–6988; J2 16385–16435. Documented.)
  6. **The class, 1742, and the mob, 1743.** In Bristol, in February 1742, the society met to find a way to pay off its debt (for its new meeting room, Curnock's note says, J2 31855–31857). They agreed that each member who could would give a penny a week, and divided into classes of about twelve, each with one person to collect it. Soon the collectors told him some members were not living as they should. He wrote, "This is the thing: the very thing we have wanted so long." From then the leader saw each member every week, asked how their souls prospered, advised, rebuked or comforted them, and collected for the poor. Some members changed; some were put out. He brought the classes to London that March. The work drew violence. At Wednesbury, near Birmingham, in October 1743 a mob dragged him through the streets of the next town crying "knock his brains out"; asked what the Methodists had done, one said they sang psalms all day and made people rise at five. (M11, M12. J2 31833–31840, 32254–32262; PA 503–583; J3 5739–5875. Own words; Documented.)
  7. **The preachers, America and the end.** In June 1744 he met his brother and four others, three of them parish clergy, at the Foundery, the London society's meeting place (LEP 302), to settle "what to teach; how to teach; and what to do"; they allowed preachers who were not ordained "only in cases of necessity". In 1784, after the Revolution, the American preachers' own minutes say church affairs in the United States had "passed through a very considerable change" (LEE 4271–4274); Curnock adds that the sacraments were no longer given there (J7 240–246, editor). That September he ordained two preachers for America and set apart a fellow priest as their superintendent, though he was not a bishop. In 1789 he told a Dublin society he had never left the Church's teaching, and left its rules only from necessity: to preach in the open air, to pray in his own words, to form societies and to take lay preachers. He died at his house in City Road, London, on 2 March 1791, nearly eighty-eight. (M13, M15, M16, M17. MIN 450–460, 2011–2012; J7 1110–1113, 1144, 1151–1152, 29875–29890; LEE 4271–4287, 7561–7567. Documented. "Revolution": the War of Independence; LEE 4271–4274 gives the change as the reason for the new plan.)

- **What came of them** (about a third of the story; ranked heaviest first):
  1. **The class and the Rules went to America. DEFENDED: the Rules and the class meeting.** Jesse Lee, an American Methodist preacher, wrote in 1810 that the Rules the societies kept from 1770 "were the same that Mr. Wesley had previously drawn up in England", continued "with very little alteration". The American *Discipline* of 1798 prints them, with "the rise of the United Society, first in Europe and then in America", and classes of twelve under a leader. (I1. LEE 1366–1371; DIS 6920–6957. Documented.)
  2. **The American church took his words.** In 1784 the American preachers formed "an Episcopal church" and answered the question of God's design in raising up the Methodist preachers with his own line, changing one word: not "to reform the nation" but "to reform the continent, and to spread scriptural holiness over these lands". (I3, M14. LEE 4277–4287; LM 363–371. Documented.)
  3. **The preachers he left kept his discipline.** At the first Conference after his death they promised "to follow and imitate him in doctrine, discipline, and life". They counted more than 130,000 members in Europe and America. (I2, I4. MIN 33770–33777, 35398–35412. Documented; the total is checked by sum, Sheet I4.)
  4. **The discipline drew people and could be used against them.** Richard Allen, who later founded the African Methodist Episcopal Church, chose Methodism because "the plain and simple gospel suits best for any people", and for its "plain doctrine and having a good discipline". He also wrote that some preachers became "tyrants" to Black members, turning them out of society without a trial. Told in Allen's voice. (I6. ALLEN 734–750. Documented as Allen's view.)
  Optional, one clause, never the closing line: "The Methodist churches grew from the societies he gathered" (this entry's census legacy, I7).

- **Quotes, 2 or 3 at most:**
  1. **Q1, Aldersgate.** Source text (J1 27071–27074): "I felt I did trust in Christ, Christ alone for salvation ; and an assurance was given me that He had taken away my sins, even mine, and saved me from the law of sin and death." Rendering, mine, from the English: "I felt that I did trust in Christ, in Christ alone, for salvation. And I was given an assurance that he had taken away my sins, even mine, and saved me from the law of sin and death." Plain sense for the narrator to give beside it: from sin's hold, and from death as its end (Paul's phrase, Romans 8). Job: the heart of the evening in his own words; what "warmed" meant to him.
     **Note for Mark.** The famous words just before, "I felt my heart strangely warmed", are already quoted in the Methodist Revival's live story, so by the kit's rule they are not used here. The narrator may say in plain words that he felt his heart warmed, without quotation marks and without "strangely". If Mark wants the famous phrase itself, he says so and Q1 is cut to fit.
  2. **Q2, Bristol, 31 March 1739.** Source text (J2 10524–10529): "I could scarce reconcile myself at first to this strange way of preaching in the fields, of which he set me an example on Sunday ; having been all my life (till very lately) so tenacious of every point relating to decency and order, that I should have thought the saving of souls almost a sin if it had not been done in a church." Rendering, mine, from the English: "At first I could hardly bring myself to accept this strange way of preaching in the fields, which he showed me on Sunday. All my life, until very lately, I had held so tightly to every rule of decency and order. I would have thought the saving of souls almost a sin, if it was not done in a church." Say first that "he" is Whitefield. Job: the stakes of the fields, in his own words.
  3. **Q3, his deathbed.** Source text (J8 9033): "The best of all is, God is with us". Rendering: "The best of all is, God is with us." Told in Elizabeth Ritchie's voice: she was there and wrote it down. Job: the ending.
  Not chosen, all unused on the tree (checked): "I look upon all the world as my parish" (on this entry's card already; J2 13807–13810); Nelson's "This man can tell the secrets of my heart" (LEP 1640–1641) and the Wednesbury "they sing psalms all day" (J3 5784–5785) are given above as narrated speech; if the writer wants either as a quote, it replaces Q3, not adds to it.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** What passed on was a way of holding converts together, not only a message: the society with one condition for entry, and inside it the class of about twelve meeting weekly under a leader who asked each member how their soul prospered (PA 549–567; J2 31833–31840). It passed to America in his own written Rules, kept "with very little alteration" (LEE 1366–1371), printed in the American church's book (DIS 6920–6957), and in his own words about why the preachers were sent, changed only from "the nation" to "the continent" (LEE 4286–4287; LM 369–371). The listener needs to understand three things the writer must make plain: the class began by accident, as a way to collect a penny a week, and became the place where people were known and held to account; it was ordinary people, not clergy, who led classes and later preached; and this structure, more than any one sermon, is what later Methodists took with them and kept. Carry it through what the leaders and the American preachers did, not through a "this matters because" sentence. Allen's witness shows it reached Black Methodists too, and was sometimes turned against them.

- **Ending:**
  1. **Chosen.** The day before he died, too weak to make himself understood, he gathered his strength and said twice, "The best of all is, God is with us" (Q3). He died the next morning, 2 March 1791, at his house in City Road, with friends kneeling round the bed. Credit Elizabeth Ritchie. (J8 9031–9037, 9095–9100; LEE 7561–7563.)
  2. The opening image seen again: the society meeting in a London room where he had gone unwillingly, beside the societies now counted in the tens of thousands. Use only if it does not turn into a summing up.

- **Do not say:**
  - Do not quote "strangely warmed" (used in the Methodist Revival story; D1). Say he felt his heart warmed, in the narrator's words, unless Mark rules otherwise.
  - Do not name the person reading Luther's preface, and do not name the building. Say "a society meeting in Aldersgate Street" (D2).
  - Say he went "very unwillingly", not that he went eagerly or by chance.
  - Do not say Wesley invented open-air preaching or began it. Whitefield preached outdoors first and called him to Bristol (M5).
  - Say "about three thousand" at Bristol, Wesley's own count (D3).
  - Do not name who proposed the penny a week; Wesley's held text leaves him unnamed (D4).
  - Say classes of "about twelve", not "twelve" or "a dozen exactly" (D5).
  - Do not call a class a Bible class, lesson or study group. It was a weekly meeting under a leader who asked about each member's life (M11; Curnock's note).
  - Do not say the class was designed for pastoral care from the start. It began as a way to pay a debt, and Wesley saw its other use afterwards (M11).
  - Do not drop "some were put out". Members who would not change were removed (PA 533).
  - Do not say he founded a new church or left the Church of England. He said he never left its teaching; American Methodists formed their own church in 1784; the separation in England after his death is the movement's story (M16, I3).
  - Do not say "he was made a bishop" or "he consecrated bishops". Say he ordained two preachers and set apart a fellow priest as superintendent (M15).
  - Say the bishop "advised him to go", in Wesley's own record; not that he banned or expelled him (M10).
  - Do not overstate his injuries at Wednesbury: he lost a flap of his waistcoat and some skin from one hand (M12). Do not say he was beaten unconscious.
  - Do not say why he left Georgia beyond his own words: he left by night after the magistrates forbade it, under allegations he says he was not allowed to answer. Do not add a romance (M2).
  - Say "more than 130,000 members in Europe and America", counted at the Conference after his death, not "at his death" or "millions" (I4).
  - The "best of all" words were said the day before he died, not as his last words. His last heard word was "Farewell" (M17).
  - "Reform the continent" is the American conference's wording of 1784, not Wesley's own (I3).
  - Do not say "nearly eighty-eight" as an exact age; that is Lee's phrase ("not quite eighty-eight").
