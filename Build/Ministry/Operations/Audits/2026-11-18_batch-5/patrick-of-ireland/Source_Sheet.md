# Source sheet: Patrick

Researcher's sheet for batch 5 (2026-11-18). The job: the captive who came back as a bishop, told in his own two short writings, the Confession and the Letter to the soldiers of Coroticus. Later legend stays out or is credited plainly as late. The writer uses the outline, the shapes and the standard only; this sheet is the evidence behind the outline.

**Files and short names used below** (all in `cic/texts/` unless marked):
- **PE** = `patrick_writings-and-life-with-muirchu-eng_white1920.txt`: Patrick's Confession and Letter in the English of Newport J. D. White (SPCK, 1920), with White's Introduction and Muirchu's Life. White's own Introduction (PE 232–1602), his introductions to each text (PE 1609–1663, 2780–2833) and his notes are a 1920 editor's words, marked **PE-ed** below. Muirchu's Life (from PE 3589) is marked **MU**.

- **ST** = `stokes_tripartite-life-of-patrick-v2-lat-muirchu-tirechan-confession_1887.txt`: Whitley Stokes, Rolls Series 89 (1887). Patrick's Latin Confession (from ST 5944) and Letter (from ST 7239), readable where PL is garbled. Used to check every Latin quote PL cannot carry.

- **NEN** = `nennius_history-of-the-britons-giles.txt`: the *History of the Britons*, a Welsh compilation of c. 829 (file header, NEN 2–3), Giles's English.
- **LIS** = `stokes_lives-of-saints-from-the-book-of-lismore-iri-eng_1890.txt`: Book of Lismore, 15th century.

**Conventions.** Line numbers are the file's own lines. Quotes are exact as printed; where a word breaks across lines I join it, and I leave out page numbers, running heads ("CONFESSION", "ST. PATRICK", "Digitized by Google"), and Bible-reference markers. OCR faults are left as printed, with my reading in [brackets]. White prints Bible words in italics; the italics are lost in the OCR and do not matter here.

**Latin.** Patrick's Latin is held (PL, ST). Every rendering in the outline is made from the Latin and checked against White's English. Where PL is garbled, ST is used.

**Witness kinds and gaps.**
- OWN = **PE / PL / ST** (the Confession and the Letter). The only writing by Patrick, and almost the only witness to his life. The Confession calls itself written "in Ireland" and "my confession before I die" (PE 2766–2771; PL 1416–1421). Neither text gives a year. White dates the Confession "towards the close of his career, probably not earlier than a.d. 454" (PE-ed 645–646); Bury, cited by White, "conjectures" the raid of Coroticus was 459 (PE-ed 2810–2811). Both are editors' reckonings. Which of the two texts was written first is not known.
- CONTEMP, but silent on Patrick = **PROS**. Prosper's Chronicle, written in Patrick's century, records Palladius sent to the Irish in 431 and never names Patrick (PROS 54222–54223; see E7).
- LATER, uncertain = **BAN** hymn. The manuscript is c. 680–691. The hymn may be older; White reports Bury's view that it "is undoubtedly contemporary" (PE-ed 1281–1282), but that rests on an annal date for its supposed author (PE-ed 1277–1280). Contested.
- LATER (~200–240 years) = **Tirechan** (written "not earlier than a.d. 664, possibly about a.d. 670", PE-ed 1389–1390; text not held except in ST, OCR) and **MU** (first book before 699, PE-ed 3593–3595). Both wrote for the church of Armagh (PE-ed 1402–1408; corpus-map note, `cic/corpus-map/insular-irish-monastic-christianity.yaml` 116, 169).
- LATER, gap uncertain = **AU**. Annals compiled and copied over centuries; their dates for Patrick disagree (D2).
- LATE-LEGEND = **NEN** (c. 829, about 370 years on) and **LIS** (15th century). Used only to show where legend departs from his own words.

## 1. Setting

S1. **His family and home, Britain.** "My father was Calpurnius, a deacon, one of the sons of Potitus, a presbyter, who belonged to the village of Banavem Tabemids [Taberniae]. Now he had a small farm hard by, where I was taken captive." (PE 1682–1685). Latin: "Patrem habui Calpornum diaconum filium quendam Potiti presbyteri qui fuit uico Banauem Taberniae. Uillulam enim prope habuit ubi ego capturam dedi." (PL 197–200, footnote markers removed). And: "I was freeborn according to the flesh ; I am born of a father who was a decurion" (PE 2989–2990). OWN. Documented. "Presbyter" = priest. A *decurio* was a member of a Roman town council (White: "The Roman municipal organization is implied in the casual statement that Patrick's father was a decurio", PE-ed 403–406). Where Bannavem Taberniae was is not known from any held text; do not place it.

S2. **Britain as Roman.** The Letter writes to soldiers who are, by right, "my fellow-citizens" and "fellow-citizens of the holy Romans" (PE 2857–2858; ST 7256–7258, "ciuibus meis atque ciuibus sanctorum Romanorum"). White: "in these writings Britain is a recognized part of the Roman Empire" (PE-ed 400–401), and the legions left "in a.d. 410" (PE-ed 407–408). OWN for the words; the period gloss is PE-ed, Widely Accepted. Plain words: "Roman Britain", "a Roman family in Britain".

S3. **Century.** Fifth century. White gives his life as "between a.d. 389 and a.d. 461, the dates of St. Patrick's birth and death according to the most recent calculations" (PE-ed 251–253), and accepts 389 for the birth "without discussion" from Bury (PE-ed 501–502). These are editors' reckonings; Patrick gives no year. The census gives "c. 432-461" and notes "His dates are not his own" (CEN, `sourcing`). The annals put his coming in 432 (AU 362–363). Plain word: "the fifth century". Exact years: Contested (D2).

S4. **Ireland outside the empire, heathen and Christian.** Patrick: "in Ireland they who never had the knowledge of God, but until now only worshipped idols and abominations" (PE 2412–2414). But Christians were already there: Prosper, under 431, "Ad Scottos in Christum credentes ordinatus a papa Caelestino Palladius primus episcopus mittitur" (PROS 54222–54223): Palladius, ordained by Pope Celestine, was sent as first bishop "to the Irish who believe in Christ". Bede repeats it (BEDE 1125, 1134). White: "Christianity had a footing in Ireland before the fifth century" (PE-ed 1127–1128), and Patrick's own c. 51 implies other preachers before him (PE-ed 1149–1153). CONTEMP (Prosper). Documented. "Scotti" in these texts means the Irish (S6).

S5. **Tradition, spoken name.** Census entry for the tradition: "Insular / Irish Monastic Christianity", informal "The Irish Monastic World" (CEN). Its own words: "Ireland was never Roman, and the church that took root there in the fifth century was built to match." Patrick is a bishop, not a monk-founder; the plainest spoken form is **"the first church in Ireland"** or **"the young church in Ireland"**. Do not say "Insular" or "the Celtic Church" aloud.

S6. **Words that puzzle.** "Scots" / "Scottic" in White's English means **Irish** (Patrick's "Scottorum", e.g. ST 6847; White: "the Scots, that is, the Irish", CEN `legacy`). "Picts": a people of northern Britain. "Virgins of Christ": women vowed to live unmarried for God (Conf 42, PE 2423–2426). "Confession" in the title means an open, thankful acknowledgement of God, not an admission of sins (PE-ed 1617–1625; Patrick's own words, "this is my confession before I die", PE 2771). "Newly baptized ... anointed": oil put on the forehead at baptism (Letter 3, S9 below).

## 2. Events (his own words unless marked)

E1. **Taken captive, about sixteen.** "I was then about sixteen years of age. I knew not the true God ; and I went into captivity to Ireland with many thousands of persons, according to our deserts, because we departed away from God, and kept not his commandments, and were not obedient to our priests" (PE 1687–1692). Latin: "Annorum eram tunc fere sedecim. Deum enim uerum ignorabam, et Hiberione in captiuitate adductus sum cum tot milia hominum secundum merita nostra" (PL 202–205). OWN. Documented. The "many thousands" is his figure. The Letter adds that the raiders "made havoc of the menservants and maidservants of my father's house" (PE 2987–2989). He reads the capture as punishment deserved; that is his view.

E2. **Slavery and prayer.** "Now, after I came to Ireland, tending flocks was my daily occupation ; and constantly I used to pray in the day time. ... so that in one day [I would say] as many as a hundred prayers, and at night nearly as many, so that I used to stay even in the woods and on the mountain [to this end]. And before daybreak I used to be roused to prayer, in snow, in frost, in rain ; and I felt no hurt" (PE 1924–1950). Latin: "cotidie itaque pecora pascebam ... ut in die una usque ad centum orationes et in nocte prope similiter, ut etiam in siluis et in monte manebam. Et ante lucem excitabar ad orationem per niuem per gelu per pluuiam" (PL 502–508). OWN. Documented. "Pecora" is flocks or herds, not named further. Six years with one master (E3). He names no place; the mountain as Slemish is tradition only (E-legend, L3).

E3. **Escape after six years.** A voice in his sleep: "“Thou fastest to good purpose, thou who art soon to go to thy fatherland.” And again, after a very short time, I heard the answer [of God] saying to me, “Lo, thy ship is ready.” And it was not near at hand, but was distant perhaps two hundred miles. ... And thereupon I shortly took to flight, and left the man with whom I had been for six years" (PE 1954–1962). Latin "ducenta milia passus" (PL 515–516), Roman miles; "annis sex" (PL 518). The ship's master at first refused him, "On no account seek to go with us", then the sailors called him back (PE 1966–1975); "they were heathen" (PE 1995). OWN. Documented as his account; the voice is his own experience, in his voice. After landing, a 28-day journey "through a desert" and a herd of swine found after he told them to pray (PE 1999–2019): his own wonder, Leave out.

E4. **A second captivity.** "And, again, after many years, I went into captivity once more ... On the sixtieth night after, the Lord delivered me out of their hands." (PE 2059–2064; PL 618, "Et iterum post annos multos adhuc capturam dedi"). OWN. When and where is unclear; Leave out.

E5. **Home in Britain; the call.** "And again, after a few years, I was in Britain with my kindred, who received me as a son, and in good faith besought me that at all events now, after the great tribulations which I had undergone, I would not depart from them anywhither." (PE 2073–2077). The dream: "a man whose name was Victorious [Victoricus] coming as it were from Ireland with countless letters. And he gave me one of them, and I read the beginning of the letter, which was entitled, “The Voice of the Irish”; ... I heard the voice of them who lived beside the Wood of Foclut which is nigh unto the western sea. And thus they cried, as with one mouth, “We beseech thee, holy youth, to come and walk among us once more.” And I was exceedingly broken in heart, and could read no further. And so I awoke." (PE 2079–2104). Latin: "uirum uenientem quasi de Hiberione, cui nomen Uictoricus, cum epistolis innumerabilibus ... Uox Hiberionacum ... iuxta siluam Focluti quae est prope mare occidentale, et sic exclamauerunt quasi ex uno ore, Rogamus te, sancte puer, ut uenias et adhuc ambulas inter nos." (PL 662–670; ST 6480–6485 "ambules"). OWN. Documented. "Victorious" in PE is OCR for Victoricus (PL 663 "Uictoricus"; ST 6507 note "Victoricius, C."). White on *adhuc*: "It is of secondary importance whether we translate adhuc “hither” or “once more”" (PE-ed 542–543). Where the Wood of Foclut was: White places it in Co. Mayo, by tradition (PE-ed 545–546); editor's; not needed.

E6. **Opposed by his elders; gifts refused.** "And many gifts were proffered me with weeping and tears. And I displeased them, and also, against my wish, not a few of my elders ; but, God being my guide, in no way did I consent or yield to them ... so that I came to the heathen Irish to preach the Gospel, and to endure insults from unbelievers ... and that I should give up my free condition for the profit of others." (PE 2314–2323; PL 914–922; ST 6742, "ut darem ingenuitatom [ingenuitatem]" as printed). And: "many were forbidding this embassage. Moreover they used to talk amongst themselves behind my back and say, “Why does this fellow thrust himself into danger amongst hostile people who know not God?” They did not say this out of malice; but it did not seem meet in their eyes, on account of my illiteracy" (PE 2514–2519). OWN. Documented. Keep his own fairness: "not out of malice".

E7. **Made a bishop: what he says and does not.** "I confess that I am a bishop, appointed by God, in Ireland. Most surely I deem that from God I have received what I am." (PE 2842–2844; PL 1426–1428, "Hiberione, a Deo constitutus, episcopum me esse fateor"). His dearest friend once told him, "“Lo, thou art to be raised to the rank of bishop”" (PE 2226–2227). OWN. Documented. **He never says who made him bishop, where, or when.** Who sent him is told only late: MU says Germanus of Auxerre consecrated him at once on Palladius's death (PE-ed 997–999, summarizing MU); NEN says Pope Celestine sent him (NEN 1224–1227). White: "all the really ancient evidence is against the notion that Celestine was consulted about Patrick's consecration" (PE-ed 995–996), and Patrick's "own silence on the subject is the most illuminating fact of all" (PE-ed 1004–1005). Contested; Leave out of the story except as "he never says".

E8. **The work: baptism, clergy, monks and virgins.**
- "I may leave a legacy to my brethren and sons whom I baptized in the Lord, many thousands of persons." (PE 1914–1915; PL 423–425 "tot milia hominum").
- "many peoples through me should be regenerated to God and afterwards confirmed, and that clergy should everywhere be ordained for them" (PE 2329–2331).
- "Sons and daughters of Scottic chieftains are seen to become monks and virgins of Christ." (PE 2416–2417; ST 6847–6848, "Filii Scottorum et filiae regulorum monachi et uirgines Christi esse uidentur").
- One noble Irish woman he baptized chose to become "a virgin of Christ" six days later; such women act "not with the consent of their fathers ; but they endure persecution and lying reproaches from their kindred" (PE 2419–2428). "But the women who are kept in slavery suffer especially ; they constantly endure even unto terrors and threats." (PE 2433–2434).
- He went "even to outlying regions beyond which no man dwelt, and where never had any one come to baptize, or ordain clergy, or confirm the people" (PE 2584–2586).
OWN. Documented as his claim. The numbers ("many thousands") are his. Contested in scope: he is the only witness, and others were there before him (S4).

E9. **The cost: gifts, kings, chains.**
- He gave back the ornaments devout women laid on the altar, "And they were scandalized at my doing so", so that the heathen would have no ground "to defame or disparage" (PE 2544–2571). "if I demanded from one of them even the price of my shoe ; tell it against me" (PE 2579–2580).
- "On occasion, I used to give presents to the kings, besides the hire that I gave to their sons who accompany me ; and nevertheless they seized me with my companions. And on that day they most eagerly desired to kill me ... and me myself they bound with irons. And on the fourteenth day the Lord delivered me from their power" (PE 2590–2597; ST 7041–7048, "me ferro uinxerunt. Et quarto decimo die absoluit me").
- "I reckon that I distributed to them not less than the price of fifteen men" (PE 2603–2605; ST 7051–7055), paid to "those who were judges throughout all the districts which I more frequently visited".
- "daily I expect either slaughter, or to be defrauded, or reduced to slavery" (PE 2639–2640).
OWN. Documented. "The price of fifteen men" is his measure; do not convert it to money.

E10. **The attack from his own side.** "And when I was assailed by not a few of my elders, who came and [urged] my sins against my laborious episcopate" (PE 2129–2131; PL 718–720 "temptatus sum ab aliquantis senioribus meis ... contra laboriosum episcopatum meum"). "After the lapse of thirty years they found, as an occasion against me, a matter which I had confessed before I was a deacon. Because of anxiety, with sorrowful mind, I disclosed to my dearest friend things that I had done in my youth one day, nay, in one hour ... I cannot tell, God knoweth, if I was then fifteen years old" (PE 2157–2163; PL 727–732). The same friend "had fought for me in my absence" and had told him he would be made bishop, yet later put him "to shame publicly before every one" (PE 2219–2232). OWN. Documented. **What the sin was, he never says.** Where the elders met: "nor was I in Britain" is said of an earlier contention (PE 2223–2224); White "supposes" the attack was in Britain (PE-ed 850–851), editor's. White's guess that the friend was Germanus is "tempting" to him only (PE-ed 844–853); Leave out.

E11. **Why he wrote the Confession.** "I wish my brethren and kinsfolk to know what manner of man I am, and that they may be able to understand the desire of my soul." (PE 1778–1781). He hesitated to write "because I have not studied as have others" and "my speech and language is translated into a tongue not my own" (PE 1796–1804). OWN. Documented. White: "As addressed to men, the Confession is an Apologia pro vita sua" (PE-ed 1628–1629), written for his kin in Britain "who for the most part opposed and depreciated him, and his “sons” in Ireland" (PE-ed 1633–1635). Editor's reading; Widely Accepted.

E12. **The raid of Coroticus's soldiers.** Letter 2: "With mine own hand have I written and composed these words to be given and delivered and sent to the soldiers of Coroticus — I do not say to my fellow-citizens or to the fellow-citizens of the holy Romans, but to those who are fellow-citizens of demons because of their evil deeds. ... allies of the Scots and apostate Picts" (PE 2855–2861; PL 1437–1442).
Letter 3: "On the day following that on which the newly baptized, in white array, were anointed — it was still fragrant on their foreheads while they were cruelly butchered and slaughtered with the sword by the aforesaid persons — I sent a letter with a holy presbyter whom I had taught from his infancy, clergy accompanying him, with a request that they would grant us some of the booty and of the baptized captives whom they had taken. They jeered at them." (PE 2866–2890). Latin: "Postera die qua crismati neophyti in ueste candida, flagrabat in fronte ipsorum, dum crudeliter trucidati atque mactati [sunt] gladio" (ST 7266–7268, "erismati ncophiti" as printed); "cachinnos fecerunt de illis" (ST 7296). OWN. Documented.
Letter 7: Christians are not to eat or drink with them, "nor ought one to accept their almsgivings, until [doing] sore penance with shedding of tears, they make amends to God, and liberate the servants of God and the baptized handmaidens of Christ" (PE 2919–2957).
Letter 14: "This is the custom of the Roman Gauls : — They send holy and fit men to the Franks and other heathen with many thousands of solidi to redeem baptized captives. Thou rather slayest and sellest them to a foreign nation which knows not God. Thou handest over the members of Christ as it were to a brothel." (PE 3070–3076; ST 7444–7449). "Solidi": gold coins.
Letter 15: the captives are "carried off to distant lands ... There freemen are put up for sale, Christians are reduced to slavery, and, worst of all, to most degraded, most vile and apostate Picts." (PE 3088–3092).
Letter 16: "Perchance they do not believe that we receive one baptism, and that we have one God and Father? It is in their eyes a disgraceful thing that we were born in Ireland." (PE 3100–3120; ST 7509, "indignum est illis quod de Hibernia nati sumus").
Letter 21: he asks that the letter "be read in the presence of all the people, yea, in the presence of Coroticus himself ; if so be that God may inspire them to amend their lives ... and may liberate the baptized women captives whom they had taken" (PE 3182–3191; ST 7579–7583).
OWN. Documented. **The outcome is not known**: no held text says whether any captive was freed. Who Coroticus was: White, following Zimmer and Bury, identifies him with "Ceretic who ruled under the Romans in Strathclyde about A.D. 420-450, or later", with his capital at "Alcluith (the Rock of Clyde, Dumbarton)" (PE-ed 2803–2812). Stokes: "Hence it seems that Coroticus was a Briton" (ST 7462–7463, note). Dominant Modern Reconstruction (editors'). Plain form a narrator may use: "a British warlord named Coroticus" (the Letter's "fellow-citizens" supports British; the king's name supports a ruler).

E13. **Born free, now a slave in Christ.** Letter 10: "Was it without God, or according to the flesh, that I came to Ireland ? Who compelled me ? I am bound in the Spirit not to see any one of my kinsfolk. Is it from me that springs that godly compassion which I exercise towards that nation who once took me captive ... ? I was freeborn according to the flesh ; I am born of a father who was a decurion ; but I sold my noble rank — I blush not to state it, nor am I sorry — for the profit of others ; in short, I am a slave in Christ to a foreign nation for the unspeakable glory of the eternal life which is in Christ Jesus our Lord." (PE 2983–3012). Latin: "Ingenuus fui secundum carnem, decorione patre nascor: uendidi enim nobilitatem meam (non erubesco neque me penitet) pro utilitate aliorum: denique seruus sum in Christo [traditus] genti extere ob gloriam ineffabilem perennis uite, que est in Christo Iesu Domino nostro." (ST 7370–7376; "[traditus]" is Stokes's bracket; PL 1543–1570 garbled). OWN. Documented.

E14. **Bound to stay, and his wish to die there.** He longed to go to Britain, "and not that only, but to go as far as Gaul in order to visit the brethren and to behold the face of the saints of my Lord ... yet I am bound in the Spirit, who witnesseth to me that if I should do this, he would note me as guilty" (PE 2439–2446). "I pray him to grant to me that I may shed my blood with those strangers and captives for his name's sake, even though I should lack burial itself" (PE 2705–2709; ST 7176–7179, "cum illis proselitis et captiuis pro nomine suo effundam sanguinem meum"). OWN. Documented.

E15. **His last word on why he went back.** Conf 61: "I testify in truth and in exultation of heart before God and his holy angels, that I never had any cause except the Gospel and his promises for ever returning to that nation from whence previously I scarcely escaped." (PE 2757–2762; ST 7198–7203, "numquam habui ullam occasionem preter euangelium et promissa illius, ut umquam redirem ad gentem illam, unde autem prius uix euaseram"). Conf 62: "this writing which Patrick the sinner, unlearned verily, composed in Ireland ... And this is my confession before I die." (PE 2765–2771; PL 1416–1421). OWN. Documented.

E16. **His opening words.** Conf 1: "I, Patrick the sinner, am the most illiterate and the least of all the faithful, and contemptible in the eyes of very many." (PE 1678–1680). Latin: "Ego Patricius peccator, rusticissimus et minimus omnium fidelium et contemptibilis sum apud plurimos." (PL 195–196; ST 5944–5945). OWN. Documented. *Rusticissimus*: most rustic, most unlearned; White glosses the related "rusticity" (PE 1855).

## 3. What came of him (ranked heaviest first)

I1. **The two writings were kept and copied.** The Book of Armagh, "written sometime between a.d. 807 and a.d. 846, by Ferdomnach, the official scribe of Armagh" (PE-ed 1532–1536), holds "An imperfect copy of St. Patrick's Confession" (PE-ed 1549–1550); "The earliest title of the two tracts — Confession and Letter — is that found in the Book of Armagh, Libri Sancti Patricii" (PE-ed 1657–1659). Other MSS are cited by White (PE-ed 1617–1619). White: "Any satisfactory answer to the question, Who was St. Patrick, and what did he do? must begin with the two short Latin writings" (PE-ed 234–238). The census: "They are the only first-person texts that survive from fifth-century Ireland" (CEN `sourcing`); the tradition entry on the tree: "They are the only voices from the beginning that we can be sure are genuine" (CEN). Documented (the copying); "only first-person texts" Widely Accepted (census and file note, PE header 18–19).

I2. **The Irish church took him as its founder and teacher.** The Bangor manuscript (c. 680–691) heads a hymn "HYMNUS SANCTI PATRICII MAGISTRI SCOTORUM" (BAN 5011), the hymn of Saint Patrick, teacher of the Irish, and opens "Audite omnes amantes Deum sancta merita Viri in Christo beati, Patricii episcopi" (BAN 5020–5023): "Hear, all who love God, the holy merits of a man blessed in Christ, Patrick the bishop". In it, "Dominus illum elegit Ut doceret barbaras Nationes" (BAN 5053–5055): the Lord chose him to teach the barbarian nations. LATER (manuscript ~220 years on; hymn's own date Contested, see Witness kinds). Documented as the hymn's words. The annals call him "Patrick, the arch-apostle (or archbishop and apostle) of the Scoti" (AU 2638–2639), a later entry. White reports one of the "Four Honours due to Patrick in all the monasteries and churches of Ireland (Book of Armagh)" is that his hymn be sung at his festival (PE-ed 1328–1331); editor's report of a text not held.

I3. **Armagh claimed his name and churches.** Tirechan's purpose was "to set forth ... a formal record of the sphere of authority of the See of Armagh, the paruchia Patricii as against the rival claims and encroachments of other religious centres in Ireland" (PE-ed 1402–1406). White: Muirchu and Tirechan "both belonged to the then Romanizing party in the Irish Church" (PE-ed 1001–1003). LATER. Documented as White's account. The tree tells this in the stories of Tirechan and Muirchu (links).

I4. **Legend grew around him.** Muirchu, about 240 years on, admits: "My skill is small; my authorities are uncertain [pty anonymous]; my memory is treacherous" (MU 3810–3811; "[pty" is OCR for White's "[or"). In his Life, Patrick prays against Coroticus, "O God, if it be possible, banish this faithless man both from this world and the world to come", and Coroticus "took on the spot the form of a little fox" (MU 5208–5210, 5228). The *History of the Britons* (c. 829) makes him a swineherd "for seven years" (NEN 1210–1211), sent by "pope Celestine" (NEN 1225), and says "He attained the period of one hundred and twenty years" (NEN 1300). The Book of Lismore, 15th century, calls him "chief apostle of the west of the world, father of baptism and belief of the men of Ireland" (LIS 16281–16282). LATE-LEGEND. Documented as their words. White sets the man of the letters against "the somewhat vindictive saint who was Muirchu's hero" (PE-ed 461–463).

I5. **His wider reach, as an editor sees it.** White quotes Bury: Patrick "organized the Christianity which already existed ; he converted kingdoms which were still pagan, especially in the west ; and he brought Ireland into connexion with the Church of the Empire" (PE-ed 1195–1201). Editor's judgement, Dominant Modern Reconstruction. Not for the story's voice; it may sit on the card.

## 4. Links

- **Muirchu** (on the tree): wrote his Life, about 700; tells the fox story.
- **Tirechan** (on the tree): wrote down his churches for Armagh, about 670.
- **Nennius** (on the tree): the *History of the Britons* tells the Celestine and seven-years legends.
- **Germanus of Auxerre** (on the tree): named as his consecrator only by Muirchu (PE-ed 997–999). Not in Patrick's writings. Leave out.
- **Palladius**: not on the tree. Sent to "the Irish who believe in Christ" in 431 (PROS).
- **Brigid of Kildare** (on the tree): no held tie to Patrick in his writings.
- **Coroticus**: acts in the Letter; not on the tree.

## 5. Where sources disagree or come late

D1. **Six years or seven.** His own: "the man with whom I had been for six years" (PE 1961–1962). NEN: "a swineherd for seven years" (NEN 1210–1211). Tirechan, by White's note, also counts seven ("Tirechdn says that Patrick served Miliuc for seven years", PE-ed 5871–5872). Say six, from his own words.

D2. **When he came and when he died.** AU: arrived 432 (AU 362–363). Death: "Repose of Old Patrick, as some books state" under 457 (AU 1473–1475); "Here some record the repose of Patrick" under 461 (AU 1649); "The Scoti say that Patrick, the Archbishop, died this year" under 491 (AU 2632–2633); and under 492, "rested on the 16th of the Kalends of April, in the 120th year of his age" (AU 2638–2640). White uses 461 (PE-ed 251–253). Contested. Say "the fifth century"; give no death year as settled.

D3. **Who sent him.** His own: "appointed by God" (PE 2843) and silence. MU: Germanus (PE-ed 997–999). NEN: Celestine (NEN 1224–1227). White: the early evidence is against Celestine (PE-ed 995–996). Contested.

D4. **The first Christians in Ireland.** Patrick writes as if Ireland had worshipped "only ... idols" (PE 2413) but also of regions "where never had any one come to baptize" (PE 2585), implying others elsewhere. Prosper names a bishop sent in 431 to Irish believers (PROS 54222–54223). Documented: he was not the first Christian or the first bishop in Ireland.

D5. **What he was accused of.** He never names the sin of his youth (PE 2157–2163). Any guess is invention.

D6. **The place of his slavery.** He names none. White: tradition "constant and unanimous" for Slemish in Co. Antrim (PE-ed 528–531); Bury argued for Mayo (PE-ed 582–587). Tradition and editors only. Leave out.

D7. **The fate of Coroticus.** Patrick's letter hopes he will repent and free the captives (PE 3186–3191). MU has him banished by prayer and turned into a fox (MU 5208–5228). No held witness says what happened.

## 6. Wanted but not held

- Tirechan's *Collections* in English: held only in Latin OCR inside ST (from ST 2251); used here only through White's report.
- The hymn *Audite omnes* in English translation; BAN holds the Latin only (White quotes two stanzas in English, PE-ed 1348–1376).
- Bury, *Life of St. Patrick* (1905), and Gwynn's *Book of Armagh* (1913), on which White leans: not held.
- No independent fifth-century witness names Patrick.
