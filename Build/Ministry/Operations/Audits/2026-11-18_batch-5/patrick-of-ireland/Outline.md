# Outline: Patrick

Sheet items (S, E, I, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. PE is White's English (1920), PL White's Latin (1918), ST Stokes's Latin (1887).

- **The essence:** Patrick, a Briton from a Roman family, was carried off to Ireland as a slave at about sixteen. He escaped after six years, and years later went back of his own will as a bishop, to the people who had enslaved him (E1, E3, E5, E7, E13; Documented, his own words). Almost all we know of him comes from his own two short Latin writings, the Confession and a letter against the soldiers of a warlord named Coroticus, the only words that survive from the first church in Ireland (I1; Documented that they are his and were kept; "only words" Widely Accepted, the census and White).
- **Also told:**
  - What he did there, in his own account: he says he baptized "many thousands", had clergy ordained everywhere, and saw sons and daughters of Irish chiefs become monks and women vowed to God (E8; Documented as his claim; he is the only witness, and Christians were in Ireland before him, S4, D4).
  - What it cost: kings seized him, robbed him and held him in chains for fourteen days. He paid kings, their sons and judges to move safely, and expected death or slavery every day (E9; Documented). Elders in Britain tried to stop him going (E6), and later brought up against him a sin of his youth that his dearest friend had made known (E10; Documented; the sin is never named).
  - The Letter: soldiers of Coroticus killed newly baptized converts the day after their baptism and sold others as slaves. Patrick told Christians to shun them until they freed the captives (E12; Documented). What happened next is not known (D7).
  - What came of him: his writings were kept and copied at Armagh (I1). The Irish church later sang of him as "teacher of the Irish" (I2). Legend grew around him centuries later, and it is credited plainly as late (I4).
- **Length:** about 2¾ minutes (about 410 words). Why: his own two writings are rich in scenes (the capture, the escape, the dream, the raid), and the heart has three parts that must land in the first minute: captive, return as bishop, and his own voice as our source. The rest needs room for the cost and the opposition, the raid, and a third for what came of him. His own words give almost no dates, places or names, so nothing is spent on chronology. All the later legend is left out but one line. Up to about 3 minutes for a person (about 460 words); craft comes before time.
- **Setting:**
  - Time: the fifth century (S3). He gives no year; the annals put his coming in 432, and they disagree on his death (D2). Plain word: "the fifth century".
  - Place: Roman Britain, where his father was a deacon and a member of the town council (S1, S2). The farm was near a village he names, Bannavem Taberniae, whose place no one knows. Then Ireland, which was never part of the empire (S4, S5). Plain words: "Roman Britain", "Ireland, beyond the empire's edge".
  - Tradition: the first church in Ireland (S5). Plain words: "the young church in Ireland". Do not say "Insular" or "Celtic Church".
- **Shape:** Missionary or translator. The trap is telling it only from the sender's side. Here every word is the sender's, so say whose record it is, once, and let the Irish converts appear through what they did: the chiefs' children who took vows, and the women who took them against their fathers' will. The bridge he built is a church: baptism, clergy and vowed lives, among a people outside the empire.
- **Sibling:** none. The tree's stories of Muirchu, Tirechan and Nennius tell the later Patrick of legend; this story keeps to his own writings and names those writers only as late.
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** His own first line (Quote 1; E16). In about three sentences: a bishop in Ireland, near the end of his life, begins his account of himself by calling himself a sinner, unlearned, the least of believers, and looked down on by many. Then: he had first come to Ireland as a slave. This leads straight into the essence and into beat 1 in time order.
  2. Not chosen: the raid (Letter 3, E12): converts in white, the oil still fragrant on their foreheads, cut down the day after their baptism. It is the strongest image, but it comes late in his life, and opening on it would tell the raid twice. Keep it for beat 6.

**Story beats** (the opening, the essence and beats 1 and 2 are the first minute, about 150 words; beats 3 to 6 deepen it)

1. **Taken.** He grew up in Roman Britain. His father was a deacon and a town councillor, and his grandfather a priest (S1; E13). At about sixteen he was carried off to Ireland "with many thousands" from his father's farm; those who took him also laid waste the servants of his father's house (E13). He says that he did not then know the true God, and he reads the capture as deserved (E1; Documented as his own view).
2. **Six years a slave, and the escape.** He tended flocks for one master. Faith grew in him, and he prayed up to a hundred times a day and as often at night, in the woods and on the mountain, through snow, frost and rain (E2). After six years a voice in his sleep told him his ship was ready, about two hundred miles away. He ran from his master, found the ship, and the heathen sailors, after first refusing, took him (E3; Documented; the voice is his own experience, told in his voice).
3. **The call back, and the bishop.** Home in Britain, his family begged him never to leave again. In a dream a man came from Ireland with many letters, and one was headed "The Voice of the Irish". As he read it he heard them cry out (Quote 2; E5). His elders were against it. Patrick says they asked why this fellow should throw himself into danger among hostile people who did not know God, and he adds that they meant no malice; they judged him too unlearned (E6). People offered him gifts with tears to stay, but he went (E6). He writes only that he is "a bishop, appointed by God, in Ireland"; he never says who made him bishop, or when (E7; Documented, and the silence is his).
4. **The work, and its cost.** He says he baptized many thousands and had clergy ordained everywhere, even in far places where no one had come to baptize before (E8). He writes that sons and daughters of Irish chiefs became monks, and that young women vowed themselves to God against their fathers' will; the slave women, he writes, suffered most, under terror and threats (E8). He would take nothing: he gave back the ornaments women laid on the altar, and asks if he ever took even the price of a shoe (E9). He paid kings and the sons who escorted him, and gave judges the value of fifteen people. Even so, kings seized him and his companions, robbed them, wanted to kill him, and held him in irons for fourteen days (E9; Documented). He says that every day he expected to be killed or made a slave again (E9).
5. **Attacked from home.** Some of his elders came and set his sins against his work as a bishop. Their weapon was something he had done in a single hour at about fifteen, before he believed. He had confessed it, before he became a deacon, to his dearest friend. That friend had once told him he would be made a bishop, and later shamed him in public (E10; Documented; he never says what the sin was). He wrote the Confession so that his own people would know "what manner of man I am" (E11).
6. **The raid, and the Letter.** Soldiers of a warlord named Coroticus, allies, he writes, of the Irish and of the Picts, fell on his converts the day after their baptism. They were still in their white baptismal clothes, with the anointing oil fragrant on their foreheads, when they were cut down by the sword. Others were carried off and sold (E12). Patrick sent a priest he had taught from childhood to ask for the captives back, and the soldiers jeered. So he wrote with his own hand, to soldiers he would not call his fellow citizens, but "fellow-citizens of demons" (E12). Christians were not to eat or drink with these men, or take their gifts, until they did penance and freed the baptized captives. In Gaul, he wrote, Christians sent money to buy captives back from the heathen Franks, but these men killed Christians and sold them. To them, he says, it was a disgrace that his people were born in Ireland. He asked that the letter be read aloud before Coroticus himself (E12). Here he sets his own life beside theirs (Quote 3; E13). Whether any captive came home, no record says (D7).

**What came of him** (about a third of the story; heaviest first. Items 1 and 2 close the story; item 3 is one line)

1. **His two writings were kept.** They were copied. One copy is in the Book of Armagh, written by the scribe of Armagh between 807 and 846, where the two are titled "the Books of Saint Patrick" (I1; Documented). They are the reason we can hear him at all. Prosper, a chronicler of his own century, records a bishop ordained by the pope and sent to "the Irish who believe in Christ" in 431, and never mentions Patrick (S4, D4; Documented).
2. **The Irish church made him its founder and teacher.** More than two centuries on, the monks of Bangor, in the north of Ireland, copied a hymn headed "the hymn of Saint Patrick, teacher of the Irish". It praises Patrick the bishop, whom the Lord chose to teach the barbarian nations (I2; Documented as the hymn's words; the hymn's own date is Contested). Armagh, the church that kept his writings, claimed many churches as his (I3; Documented as White's account of Tirechan).
3. **Legend, credited as late.** One line only. His first biographer, Muirchu, writing about 240 years later, admits his sources are uncertain. In Muirchu's telling, Patrick prays that Coroticus be banished from this world and the next, and the warlord turns into a little fox. Patrick's own letter only hopes the men will repent and free the women (I4, D7; Documented as Muirchu's story). Use only if words allow. The pope, the seven years, the 120 years and the rest stay out.

**Quotes** (3)

1. Latin (PL 195–196; ST 5944–5945): "Ego Patricius peccator, rusticissimus et minimus omnium fidelium et contemptibilis sum apud plurimos." (White's English, PE 1678–1680: "I, Patrick the sinner, am the most illiterate and the least of all the faithful, and contemptible in the eyes of very many.") Rendering (from the Latin): "I am Patrick, a sinner. I am utterly unlearned, and the least of all believers. Very many people look down on me." ("Illiterate" is not used: its modern sense, unable to read, misleads; he wrote these lines himself.) Job: the opening. A bishop's own first words about himself, which turn at once to the boy who came as a slave.
2. Latin (PL 669–670; ST 6484–6485): "Rogamus te, sancte puer, ut uenias et adhuc ambulas inter nos." (White's English, PE 2100–2101: "We beseech thee, holy youth, to come and walk among us once more.") Rendering (from the Latin): "We beg you, holy boy, come and walk among us once more." (White notes *adhuc* may mean "once more" or "hither", PE-ed 542–543; "once more" is White's choice.) Job: the call back, in beat 3. The people of the land where he was a slave ask him to return.
3. Latin (ST 7370–7374, Stokes; PL garbled here): "Ingenuus fui secundum carnem, decorione patre nascor: uendidi enim nobilitatem meam (non erubesco neque me penitet) pro utilitate aliorum: denique seruus sum in Christo genti extere" (the sentence goes on, "for the unspeakable glory of eternal life in Christ Jesus our Lord"; the quote stops before it). (White's English, PE 2989–3011.) Rendering (from the Latin): "By birth I was a free man, and my father was a town councillor. I sold my noble rank for the good of others, and I am not ashamed or sorry. In short, I am a slave in Christ to a foreign people." Job: in the Letter, beat 6. The freeborn captive who chose to serve the people who once enslaved him: the essence, in his own words.

**Names**

- Patrick: acts. Spelling "Patrick" (census).
- Coroticus: acts (his soldiers' raid; the Letter is to them). Not on the tree.
- Muirchu: link (wrote his Life; on the tree as "Muirchu maccu Machtheni", spoken "Muirchu"). Only if What came 3 is used.
- Optional, by role unless needed: his father (Calpurnius, "a deacon"), the man in the dream (Victoricus). Anyone else by role: "a chronicler of his own century" (Prosper), "the scribe of Armagh", "the monks of Bangor".

**Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from Patrick was a church among a people outside the empire. He baptized converts and had clergy ordained for them, and the children of Irish chiefs took vows as monks and as women given to God (E8). The listener must understand two things. First, he was not the first Christian there; a bishop had been sent to Irish believers just before him (S4). What he did was to go, a former slave, as a bishop to the people who had enslaved him, and to live and stay among them (E13, E14). Second, the Patrick the Irish church later sang of, "teacher of the Irish", grew from that work, and his memory was carried by Armagh and its writers (I2, I3). But the sure Patrick is the one in his two letters, which Armagh copied and kept (I1). Tell it through these acts: the copying, the hymn, Armagh's claim. No "this matters because" sentence.

**Ending:** close on his own words at the end of the Confession, with the opening image seen again. He wished to see Britain and the brothers in Gaul, but believed he was bound to stay. He prayed that he might shed his blood with "those strangers and captives", even if he lay unburied (E14). He testifies that he had no reason to go back to the people he had barely escaped except the gospel (E15). He signs it as written in Ireland by "Patrick the sinner, unlearned", and calls it "my confession before I die" (E15; PE 2765–2771). If a narrator's line on his death is wanted: when he died is not known; the annals give several years (D2).

**Links**

- Muirchu: wrote his Life, about 700 (I4).
- Tirechan: wrote down his churches for Armagh, about 670 (I3).
- Nennius: the *History of the Britons*, with the later legends of the pope and the seven years (I4, D1, D3).
- Coroticus: the Letter is written against his soldiers (E12).
- Germanus of Auxerre: named as the bishop who made him bishop only by Muirchu (D3). Not told.

**Who tells it**

- **Patrick himself** wrote the Confession and the Letter in Latin, late in life; neither gives a year. The whole life rests on him. Say so once, in or right after the essence, inside a sentence about the writings: "almost everything we know of him comes from two short letters in his own hand" (I1). The beats then are his account; credit with "he says" or "he writes" where he praises or judges: the numbers he baptized, his opponents, his own innocence.
- **Prosper**, a chronicler of his own century, names the bishop sent in 431 and not Patrick (S4). Only in What came 1.
- **The monks of Bangor** copied the hymn about two centuries later (I2).
- **Muirchu**, about 240 years later, wrote the first Life, for Armagh, and admits his sources are uncertain (I4). Credited as late wherever used.
- **The annals**, compiled over centuries, disagree on his death by more than thirty years (D2).

**Do not say**

- Not "Patrick brought Christianity to Ireland", "the first bishop of Ireland" or "converted Ireland". A bishop was sent to Irish believers in 431 (S4, D4). Say he baptized many and had clergy ordained, as he says.
- Not "Pope Celestine sent him", "Germanus made him bishop" or "he studied in Gaul". He never says who made him bishop (E7, D3). He only wished to visit "the brothers" in Gaul (E14).
- Not "seven years" or "swineherd". He says six years, "tending flocks" (E2, E3, D1).
- Not "on Slemish" or any named mountain. He names no place of his slavery (D6).
- Not "Irish pirates kidnapped him" as his words. Say "he was carried off to Ireland", "with many thousands" (E1).
- Not "illiterate". Say "unlearned"; he wrote these texts himself.
- Not "the Irish called him back" as fact. It was a dream: "in a dream he heard them" (E5).
- "Many thousands" baptized is his own figure: "he says he baptized many thousands" (E8).
- Not "his friend betrayed him" as the narrator's verdict, and never what the sin was. Say "the friend he had told shamed him in public"; the sin is unnamed (E10, D5).
- Keep the elders fair, as he does: they "did not say this out of malice" (E6).
- "The price of fifteen men" is his measure: say "the value of fifteen people", not a sum of money (E9).
- Keep the force of the Letter: killed "with the sword", "sold", "as it were to a brothel", the slave women who "suffer especially" (E8, E12). Do not soften to "attacked" or "taken".
- Not "he excommunicated Coroticus". Say what he wrote: no one was to eat or drink with them or take their gifts until they did penance and freed the captives (E12).
- Not "Coroticus, king of Strathclyde" as fact. Say "a warlord named Coroticus" or "a British warlord"; Strathclyde is White's identification (E12).
- Not "the captives were freed", "Coroticus repented" or "Coroticus was punished". No record says (D7). The fox is Muirchu's, about 240 years later.
- Not "the Confession is a confession of his sins". It is his thanks to God and his defence of his life (S6, E11).
- Not "Scots" aloud for the Irish. Say "the Irish". "Picts" may stand, as "Picts from northern Britain".
- Not "he died in 461", "on 17 March" or "aged 120". The dates are not his and the annals disagree (D2).
- Not "the Letter came after the Confession", or the reverse. Which came first is not known. Tell the attack (beat 5) and the raid (beat 6) without "later" or "then" between them; use "once" or "at another time".
- Not "he wrote St Patrick's Breastplate" or any other text. Only the two writings are his (Library sweep).
- "Apostate Picts" is Patrick's phrase. White thinks it means they had once been Christians (PE-ed 432–438); that is his reading. Say "Picts" or "Picts he called apostates".
- Keep his anger, which is the Letter's force: "fellow-citizens of demons", "ravening wolves" (PE 2859, 2902). Do not make him only mild.
- Not "Ireland was wholly heathen" as fact. That is his phrasing in one place (D4).
- Not "the monks and nuns of Ireland began with him". He says chiefs' children became monks and virgins of Christ (E8); do not make him the founder of Irish monasticism.

**Leave out**

- The 28-day journey "through a desert", the herd of swine and the honey; Satan's attack and the cry to "Helias"; the second captivity of sixty days (E3, E4). His own wonders and side stories; they would crowd the escape.
- Where the Wood of Foclut was, and Slemish or Croagh Patrick (E5, D6): editors' and tradition's debates.
- The name Victoricus, unless the writer needs it for the dream (E5).
- All later legend except the one line in What came 3: the paschal fire at Tara, the shamrock, the snakes, the forty days on the mountain, the pope, the seven years, the 365 books and churches, the 120 years, the "chief apostle of the west of the world" (I4; D1–D3). The tree's stories of Muirchu, Tirechan and Nennius carry them.
- The Lorica, the Sayings and the canons ascribed to him (Library sweep): tradition, not his.
- White's guesses: that the false friend was Germanus, that Patrick studied at Lérins, Bury's dates (E10, E7, S3).
- Bury's summary of his achievement (I5): an editor's judgement, for the card.
