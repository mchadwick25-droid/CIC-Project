Near the end of his life, a bishop in Ireland, a senior church leader who could appoint other ministers, began his account of himself: "I am Patrick, a sinner. I am utterly unlearned, and the least of all believers. Very many people look down on me."

He had first come to Ireland as a slave and escaped after six years. Years later he went back of his own will, as a bishop, to the people who had enslaved him. Almost all we know of him comes from two short Latin writings of his own. One is a letter against a warlord's soldiers, and the other is the Confession, his account of his life and thanks to God.

He grew up in fifth-century Roman Britain, then a province of the empire, where his father was a member of the town council. At about sixteen he was carried off to Ireland with many thousands of others. There he tended flocks and prayed, he says, up to a hundred times a day, until a voice in his sleep told him a ship was ready.

Back in Britain, he dreamed of a letter headed The Voice of the Irish, and heard them cry: "We beg you, holy boy, come and walk among us once more." Senior churchmen judged him too unlearned, meaning no malice, he says, but he went.

He says he baptized many thousands, washing them in water to make them Christians. Children of Irish chiefs, he writes, became monks, men who left ordinary life for God, and women vowed to stay unmarried for God, some against their fathers' will. The slave women among them suffered most. Kings robbed him and held him in chains for fourteen days, and every day he expected to be killed or made a slave again.

At one time, churchmen held against him a sin of his youth, once told to his dearest friend, who later shamed him in public.

At another time, soldiers of a warlord named Coroticus cut down new converts with the sword the day after their baptism, still in white, the blessing oil on their foreheads. Others they sold, he writes, as if into a brothel. Patrick called the killers fellow-citizens of demons, and told Christians not to eat with them or take their gifts until they showed sorrow and freed the captives. He wrote: "By birth I was a free man, and my father was a town councillor. I sold my noble rank for the good of others, and I am not ashamed or sorry. In short, I am a slave in Christ to a foreign people." He had given his life to serve the Irish, for Christ's sake. No record says whether any captive came home.

His writings were kept, and Armagh, a church in the north of Ireland that claimed him as founder, copied the Confession. A chronicler of his own century records a bishop sent to Irish believers in 431, and never names Patrick. More than two centuries on, a hymn copied by monks called him the teacher of the Irish.

He had longed to see Britain again, but believed he was bound to stay, praying to shed his blood with his new Christians and captives. Only the gospel, the Christian message, had brought him back, he writes. He signed his Confession as written in Ireland, by Patrick the sinner, unlearned.
