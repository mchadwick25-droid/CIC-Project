Near the end of his life, a bishop in Ireland, a senior church leader who could appoint other ministers, began his own story like this: "I am Patrick, a sinner. I am utterly unlearned, and the least of all believers. Very many people look down on me."

He had first come to Ireland as a slave. Carried off at about sixteen, he escaped after six years, yet later he went back of his own will, as a bishop, to the people who had enslaved him. Almost everything we know of him comes from two short Latin writings in his own hand: the Confession, his account of his life and thanks to God, and a letter against the soldiers of a warlord named Coroticus.

He grew up in the fifth century in Roman Britain, then a province of the empire, where his father sat on the town council. Taken to Ireland with many thousands of others, he tended flocks and prayed, he says, a hundred times a day, through snow and rain. After six years a voice in his sleep told him a ship was ready, and he ran to find it.

Home again, he dreamed of a letter headed The Voice of the Irish, and heard them cry out: "We beg you, holy boy, come and walk among us once more." Senior churchmen judged him too unlearned, without malice, he adds, but he went.

He says he baptized many thousands, washing them in water to make them Christians. Sons and daughters of Irish chiefs took vows, as monks who left ordinary life for God, and as women vowed to stay unmarried, some against their fathers' will. Kings robbed him and chained him for fourteen days, and he expected death or slavery every day.

At one time, churchmen set against him a sin from his youth that he had confided to his dearest friend, the friend who later shamed him in public. He never names it.

At another time, soldiers of Coroticus cut down his converts with the sword the day after their baptism, while they were still in white, the blessing oil on their foreheads, and sold others as slaves. Patrick called them fellow-citizens of demons, and told Christians to shun them until they showed sorrow and freed the captives. He set his life beside theirs: "By birth I was a free man, and my father was a town councillor. I sold my noble rank for the good of others, and I am not ashamed or sorry. In short, I am a slave in Christ to a foreign people." He had given his life to serve the Irish, for Christ's sake. Whether any captive came home, no record says.

His writings were kept, and the scribe of Armagh, a church in the north of Ireland that claimed him as founder, copied the Confession. A chronicler of his own century records a bishop sent to Irish believers in 431, and never mentions Patrick. Yet more than two centuries on, the monks of Bangor copied a hymn calling him the teacher of the Irish.

He had longed to see Britain again, but believed he was bound to stay, and prayed to shed his blood among his captives and converts. Only the gospel, the Christian message, had brought him back, he writes. He signed it Patrick the sinner, unlearned, written in Ireland.
