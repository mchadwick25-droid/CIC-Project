In August 1536 a young French scholar came to Geneva meaning to stay one night, because war had closed his road to Strasbourg. The city's preacher, William Farel, begged him to stay, and when begging failed, he did something more frightening. The scholar told it himself, twenty years later: "When he saw that asking got him nowhere, he went as far as a curse. He called on God to curse my quiet life if I held back from helping in so great a need. Struck by that terror, I gave up the journey I had begun."

The scholar was John Calvin. Thrown out two years later and called back in 1541, he gave Geneva a written plan for running its church, with four kinds of office and a weekly church court. Churches in France and Scotland built theirs the same way, and his name became the label for a family of churches that had begun at Zurich before him.

Born at Noyon, in northern France, in 1509, he says he longed for quiet and left France to hide. But believers burned in France were being slandered as rebels, so he addressed a short handbook to the French king to clear their name: the Institutes, a book of instruction in the Christian faith.

In 1538 Geneva's assembly of citizens gave him and Farel three days to leave, after they defied a ban on their preaching. When the city begged him back, he wrote that he would rather die a hundred times, yet remembering he was not his own, he returned.

He gave the city written rules for its church. Pastors preached, baptized and gave communion, the shared bread and wine of worship, while teachers taught and deacons cared for the poor and sick. Twelve elders, laymen chosen from the city's councils, watched over people's lives and warned those who went wrong. Each Thursday they met with the pastors as the consistory, a church court that questioned people on their faith.

But the councils kept the last word on punishment. They jailed a man who refused to answer to Calvin, and in 1547 they beheaded another for speaking against God, for acts against the city's government, and for threatening the pastors.

By then the Institutes, now much larger, taught that God chooses beforehand who will be saved. "Some are ordained beforehand to eternal life," Calvin wrote, "others to eternal damnation," meaning life with God for ever, or being lost for ever. In 1551, as Geneva's pastors recorded it, a doctor of medicine, Jérôme Bolsec, rose at their weekly Bible meeting and said that such teaching made God a tyrant, an idol like Jupiter, the chief god of the old Romans. He was jailed, and though Bern's pastors replied that some said he was not so bad a man, the city sent him out for good, to the sound of a trumpet.

Two years later the council burned Michael Servetus alive for books against the teaching that God is three persons in one. Calvin had written that if Servetus came, he would not leave alive, and during the trial he hoped for a death sentence, though a less cruel one.

Elsewhere, others built offices of the same kind with no friendly city behind them. In 1555, believers meeting in a Paris house chose a pastor and set up a consistory of elders and deacons. By 1559, when going to Geneva could mean death under the king's law, the French churches met secretly and adopted a rulebook with pastors, elders and deacons in each church. In 1562 Scotland's church, where John Knox had come home from Geneva, ruled that it would baptize, marry and bury the way Geneva's church did. Decades later an English critic of Geneva, Richard Hooker, called Calvin's books almost the standard everyone measured by.

In April 1564, gravely ill, Calvin spoke to the city's pastors, and one of them wrote his words down. "I have lived here amid extraordinary battles. In mockery, I was greeted in the evening outside my door with fifty or sixty gunshots. Think how that could shake a poor, timid scholar, as I am and always have been, I confess." They took his hand one by one, in tears. He died that May.
