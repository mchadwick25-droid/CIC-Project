In August 1536 a young French scholar, already the author of a book that defended his persecuted fellow believers, stopped at Geneva for one night, on his way to Strasbourg. The city's preacher, William Farel, begged him to stay, then did something more frightening. The scholar told it himself, twenty years later: "When he saw that asking got him nowhere, he went as far as a curse. He called on God to curse my quiet life if I held back from helping in so great a need. Struck by that terror, I gave up the journey I had begun."

The scholar was John Calvin. His book was the Institutes, a book of instruction in the Christian faith. He stayed, and gave Geneva a written plan for its church, with four kinds of office. Churches in France and Scotland built theirs the same way, and his name became the label for a family of churches begun at Zurich before him.

Born in France in 1509, he says he left it to hide in some quiet corner. But believers burned there were being slandered as rebels, so he wrote a short handbook to the French king to clear their name.

In 1538 Geneva's citizens voted him and Farel out for defying a ban on their preaching. He went back in 1541, but would take up his post only once the city swore to keep a catechism, a book of questions and answers on the faith. It also swore to keep a church discipline, a system for correcting members' lives.

His rules set pastors to preach, baptize and give communion, the shared bread and wine of worship. Teachers taught sound teaching, and deacons cared for the poor and sick. Twelve elders, laymen from the city's councils, watched over people's lives and met weekly with the pastors as the consistory, a church court that questioned and warned. But the councils kept the power to punish. Who could bar people from communion stayed in dispute between them for years. In 1547 the city beheaded a man for speaking against God, for acts against the city's government, and for threatening the pastors.

By then the much larger Institutes taught that God chooses beforehand who will be saved. In Calvin's words, "Some are ordained beforehand to eternal life, others to eternal damnation," to life with God, or to being lost forever. In 1551, as Geneva's pastors recorded it, a doctor of medicine, Jérôme Bolsec, rose at their weekly Bible meeting. He said this made God a tyrant, an idol like Jupiter, the old Romans' chief god. He was jailed, and when Geneva asked other Swiss churches, Bern's pastors wrote that some said he was not so bad a man, and hoped for agreement. Geneva still sent him away for good.

In 1553 the city council burned Michael Servetus alive for books against infant baptism and against the teaching that God is three persons in one. It had first asked the advice of four Swiss churches. Calvin had written that if Servetus came, he would not leave alive, and during the trial he hoped for a death sentence, though a less cruel one.

Others built the same offices with no friendly city behind them. In 1555, the year Geneva's councils confirmed the consistory's rules, believers meeting in a Paris house set up a consistory of elders and deacons. In 1559, when going to Geneva could mean death, French churches met secretly to adopt a statement of belief and a rulebook with pastors, elders and deacons. In 1562 Scotland's church, where John Knox had come home from Geneva, ruled to give baptism and communion, marry and bury as Geneva's church did.

Gravely ill in 1564, Calvin spoke to the city's pastors, and one of them wrote it down: "I have lived here amid extraordinary battles. In mockery, I was greeted in the evening outside my door with fifty or sixty gunshots. Think how that could shake a poor, timid scholar, as I am and always have been, I confess." He died that May.
