In August 1536 a young French scholar, already the author of a book defending his persecuted fellow believers, came to Geneva meaning to stay one night, because war had closed his direct road to Strasbourg. Someone recognized him, and the city's preacher, William Farel, begged him to stay. When begging failed, Farel did something more frightening. The scholar told it himself, twenty years later: "When he saw that asking got him nowhere, he went as far as a curse. He called on God to curse my quiet life if I held back from helping in so great a need. Struck by that terror, I gave up the journey I had begun."

The scholar was John Calvin. He stayed, was thrown out two years later, and came back in 1541 to give Geneva a written plan for running its church, with four kinds of office and a weekly church court. Churches in France and Scotland built theirs on that plan, and his name became the label for a whole family of churches that had begun at Zurich before him.

Calvin was born at Noyon, in northern France, in 1509. His father meant him for theology, then switched him to law, which he saw making men rich. After what Calvin called a sudden conversion, he says, he longed for quiet and left France to hide. At Basel he found that believers burned in France were being slandered abroad as rebels, so he addressed a short handbook to the French king to clear their name. It was the Institutes, a book of instruction in the Christian faith, and it opened with the idea that true wisdom is knowing God and knowing ourselves.

In Geneva the councils banned him and Farel from preaching, because they would not follow ceremonies the city of Bern required. They preached anyway, and in 1538 the assembly of all the city's citizens gave them three days to leave. At Strasbourg, friends warned Calvin that God would find him as he found Jonah, the prophet who ran from God's call, and he took charge of a congregation of French refugees. When Geneva begged him back, he wrote that he would rather die a hundred times than take up that cross, the suffering he had carried there. Yet remembering he was not his own, he offered his heart to the Lord as a sacrifice, and returned.

He would stay only if the city swore to keep a catechism, a book of questions and answers on the faith, and a church discipline, a system for correcting members' lives. His rules set out four offices. Pastors preached, baptized and gave communion, the shared bread and wine of worship. Teachers taught true teaching, while deacons kept the money for the poor and cared for the sick. Twelve elders, laymen chosen from the city's councils, one in every quarter of town, watched over people's lives and warned in friendship those who went wrong. Each Thursday they met with the pastors as the consistory, a weekly church court.

But the councils amended his text and kept the last word on punishment, and the right to bar people from communion, the church's heaviest penalty, stayed in dispute until 1555. The consistory questioned people on their faith; a man who refused to answer to Calvin was jailed by the council. In 1547 the city beheaded a man for speaking against God, for acts against its government, and for threatening the pastors.

By then the Institutes, now much larger, taught predestination: God choosing beforehand who will be saved. In Calvin's words, "Not all are created on equal terms. Some are ordained beforehand to eternal life, others to eternal damnation," meaning life with God for ever, or being lost for ever. In 1551, as Geneva's pastors recorded it, Jérôme Bolsec, a doctor of medicine, stood up at their weekly Bible meeting. He said the only choice is the one seen when people believe or refuse, and that teaching a decision made before time turned God into a tyrant, an idol like Jupiter, the chief god of the old Romans. Calvin answered him on the spot. Bolsec was jailed, and Bern's pastors, asked for advice, replied that some said he was not so bad a man, and hoped for agreement. The city sent him out for good, to the sound of a trumpet.

In 1553 the city council burned Michael Servetus alive, for books against the teaching that God is three persons in one, after advice from four Swiss churches. Calvin had written that if Servetus came, he would not leave alive, and during the trial he hoped for a death sentence, though a less cruel one.

Far from Geneva, others built offices of the same kind with no friendly city behind them. In 1555 a gentleman in Paris, unable to travel to Geneva, asked the believers meeting in his house to choose a pastor, so that his newborn child need not be baptized in the Catholic church. They set up a consistory of elders and deacons. Two years later the king's law made death the penalty for, among others, those who went to Geneva. Yet in 1559 the French churches met secretly and adopted a statement of belief and a rulebook, with pastors, elders and deacons in each church. That month Calvin named pastors Geneva was sending. In 1562 Scotland's church, where John Knox had come home from Geneva, ruled that it would baptize, give communion, marry and bury the way the church of Geneva did. Some thirty years after Calvin's death, Richard Hooker, an English critic of Geneva's discipline, still wrote that his books had become almost the standard everyone measured by. In 1672, bishops of the Orthodox church of the East met at Jerusalem against the Calvinists, and called him the chief heretic.

On 28 April 1564, gravely ill, Calvin spoke to the city's pastors, and one of them wrote his words down from memory. When he first came, he said, there was preaching and nothing more, and everything was in uproar. "I have lived here amid extraordinary battles. In mockery, I was greeted in the evening outside my door with fifty or sixty gunshots. Think how that could shake a poor, timid scholar, as I am and always have been, I confess." He asked them to change nothing, and they took his hand one by one, in tears. He died on 27 May 1564.
