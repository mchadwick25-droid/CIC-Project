# Outline: John Calvin

All references are to `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder (S = setting, M = life events, I = impact, L = links, D = disagreements). Each item there gives the file and its lines.

- **The essence:** John Calvin was a French scholar in exile. He wrote the Institutes, a book of instruction in the Christian faith, first to defend the Protestants being burned in France. It came to teach that God chooses beforehand who will be saved, a teaching that brought bitter quarrels. Passing through Geneva for one night in 1536, he was held there. He gave the city a written plan for running its church: pastors to preach, teachers, deacons to care for the poor and sick, and elders, laymen who watched over people's lives. Elders and pastors met each week as the consistory, a church court. Protestant churches in France and Scotland built their own churches on that plan, and his name became the label for the whole family.
  - Confidence: the book's purpose, the 1536 stop and the curse are Documented as his own account, written 21 years later (M3, M5, D1). The teaching on God's choice is Documented in his own words (M13a); the quarrel with Bolsec is Documented from the pastors' register and the city's sentence, the record of one side (M13b). The plan and its offices are Documented from the 1541 draft and the council registers (M10, M11). The French and Scottish churches taking it up is Documented (I1, I2). The name as a label is Documented through a 1672 council of the Greek church and a 1662 Dutch teaching book (I5).

- **Also told:**
  - **The Institutes.** First a short handbook, printed in 1536 and addressed to the French king, to show that the people burned in France were not rebels (M3). It grew into a large work over his life (I4). One idea from it, in his words: true wisdom is "the knowledge of God and of ourselves" (M4). Documented.
  - **The teaching, and the quarrel (1551).** The Institutes taught predestination: that God decided beforehand, for each person, eternal life or eternal loss (Q2; M13a). In 1551 Jérôme Bolsec, a doctor of medicine living in Geneva, stood up at the pastors' weekly Bible meeting. He said such a God was made a tyrant, an idol like the pagans' Jupiter. Calvin answered him on the spot. Bolsec was jailed, and the city asked other Swiss churches for advice. Bern's pastors answered that some said he was not so bad a man, and hoped for agreement. Geneva banished him for life, to the sound of a trumpet (M13b). Documented, from the pastors' register and the sentence: Geneva's side of the record.
  - **The plan, and the fight over it.** Expelled in 1538, recalled in 1541, he would only stay if the city swore to keep a catechism, a question-and-answer book of the faith, and a church discipline, a system for correcting members (M7, M10). The city's councils amended his Church Ordinances, kept the last word on punishment, and until 1555 fought the consistory over its right to bar people from communion, the shared bread and wine (M11, M13). Documented.
  - **The force.** The consistory questioned people on their faith and sent a defiant man to the city council, which jailed him. A man who threatened the pastors was beheaded in 1547. Michael Servetus, who denied the standard teaching that God is three persons in one, was burned alive in 1553 by the city council, with Calvin wanting his death (M13, M14). Documented.
  - **What it gave others.** In Paris, in 1555, a congregation meeting in a private house set up its own consistory of elders and deacons. In 1559 the French churches met secretly in a national assembly, and adopted a statement of belief and a rulebook for their churches, with pastors, elders and deacons. At that time, going to Geneva could mean death in France. In 1562 the Church of Scotland ruled that it would conduct baptism, communion, marriages and burials the way the church of Geneva did (I1, I2). Documented.
  - Where the sources will not hold it: that Calvin wrote the French statement of belief is contested (D4); that the Paris church copied Geneva is the editors' reading; the church history of 1580 names the church of the apostles' time as its model (D5). Tell what each church did.

- **Words to explain** (gloss each where it is first heard, worked into the sentence; or use the plain word):
  - **Institutes**: the book's title; "a book of instruction in the Christian faith". In 1536 he called it rudiments for training in godliness (INST1 4744–4749), and later a short handbook (K 28968–28970).
  - **Protestants / the Reformed**: Protestants, "Christians who broke with the Pope's church". The Reformed are "the branch of Protestants that began in the Swiss cities" (census relationsSummary). Calvin's own word is "my brothers" (K 28964–28966).
  - **Pope's church / Rome / Roman rites / the Mass**: "the Catholic church"; the Mass is "the Catholic service of bread and wine". In 1550s Paris, "Roman rites" means Catholic baptism (BEZA1 6551–6552).
  - **minister / pastor**: use "pastor", glossed once as "who preached, baptised and gave communion" (CO10 1493–1499). The source uses both words for the same office.
  - **teacher** (French "docteur"): "who taught sound doctrine"; never "doctor" (CO10 1864–1866).
  - **elder**: "laymen chosen from the city councils to watch over people's lives" (CO10 1947–1963). In Geneva they were council members, not elected by the congregation. For Paris the source does not say who chose them.
  - **deacon**: "who kept the money for the poor and cared for the sick" (CO10 2030–2042). In Geneva the deacons ran the city hospital; that is different from deacons in many churches today.
  - **consistory**: "a weekly church court of the pastors and elders" (CO10 2540–2544). It warned and questioned people; the city council held the power to punish (K 34888–34893; M13).
  - **Church Ordinances**: "the city's written rules for its church" (K 32444).
  - **catechism**: "a book of questions and answers on the faith, for teaching" (CAT 229–232).
  - **discipline / church discipline**: "a system for correcting members' lives" (CO10 1947–1951; INST3 11139–11140).
  - **communion / the Lord's Supper**: "the shared bread and wine of worship". To be barred from it was the church's heaviest penalty (K 34888–34893).
  - **excommunication**: avoid the word; say "barring people from communion".
  - **sacraments**: avoid; name them, "baptism and communion".
  - **synod**: "a national assembly of pastors and elders" (K 36509–36522).
  - **confession / Confession of Faith**: "a statement of belief". Not a confession of sins.
  - **Discipline (the French book)**: "a rulebook for the churches" (K 36945–36951).
  - **overseers**: the 1559 statement's word for elders (SCHAFF 1971–1976); say "elders".
  - **Kirk**: the Scots word for "church"; say "the church of Geneva" (PET 1208–1210).
  - **the Trinity**: "the teaching that God is three persons in one". Servetus wrote against it (tree link; Kidd's note names his book on the "errors" of the Trinity, K 34765, EDITOR).
  - **blasphemy**: "speaking against God" (K 35265–35277).
  - **heresiarch / heretic**: "chief heretic, a leader of false teaching" (DOS 579–583).
  - **lecturer at St Peter's**: "who gave Bible lectures in the city's main church, St Peter's" (K 29999–30002).

  - **decree**: "decision"; in Q2, God's decision made before time.
  - **eternal life / eternal damnation**: "life with God for ever" / "being lost for ever". Q2's words; the narrator gives the plain sense once.
  - **election / the elect / reprobation**: avoid; say "God's choice", "those God chose", "those God passed over" (K 35300–35308).
  - **free will**: "the power to choose for oneself"; one clause at most (K 35288).

- **Openings** (chosen: 1):
  1. **One night in Geneva** (M5, Q1). In August 1536 a young French scholar, already the author of a book that defended his persecuted fellow believers, came to Geneva. War had closed his direct road to Strasbourg, and he meant to stay one night. Someone recognised him, and Geneva's preacher, William Farel, begged him to stay. When begging failed, Farel did something more frightening. Calvin told it himself, twenty years later: Q1. Leads straight into the essence: he stayed, and what he built there was copied far beyond it.
  2. **"That Frenchman"** (M6). Weeks later, the city's council register did not yet record his name. Farel asked the council to keep and pay the lecturer that "ille Gallus", that Frenchman, had begun at St Peter's. Use only as a line inside beat 3, or as an alternative hook.

- **Story beats:**
  1. **Hook and essence (first ~150 words).** Opening 1 with Q1 (M5). Then the essence in plain words: the man was John Calvin; he stayed, was thrown out two years later, came back in 1541, and gave Geneva a written order for its church, with four kinds of office and a consistory of ministers and elders (M7, M10, M11). The book he had written was the Institutes (M3). Churches in France and Scotland took that order for their own, and his name became the name of the whole family, used by friends and enemies (I1, I2, I5). Documented; the curse is his own account.
  2. **The exile who wanted to hide (1509–1536).** Born at Noyon in 1509. His father meant him for theology, then switched him to law, because he saw law making men rich (S1, S2). He tells of a "sudden conversion" (M1). He says he always loved shade and quiet, and left France to hide in some obscure corner (M2). At Basel, he says, believers burned in France were being slandered abroad as rebels. He published a short handbook to clear their name, addressed to the French king (M3). Its opening idea: true wisdom is knowing God and knowing ourselves (M4). Documented as his account.
  3. **First years, and expulsion (1536–1538).** He began as a lecturer on the Bible at St Peter's; the council register first calls him only "that Frenchman" (M6). The councils banned him and Farel from preaching, because they would not follow the church ceremonies the city of Bern required. They preached anyway, and in April 1538 the General Council gave them three days to leave. Calvin answered that if they had served men they would be badly rewarded, but they served a great master who would reward them (M7). Documented, council register.
  4. **Strasbourg, and the recall (1538–1541).** At Strasbourg he became pastor of a congregation of French refugees. Friends pressed him into the post by warning that God would find him as he found Jonah, the prophet who ran from God (M8). When Geneva's councils begged him back, he wrote to Farel that he would rather die a hundred times than go back to that cross (M9). He went in 1541. To Farel he wrote that he was not his own, and offered his heart to God as a sacrifice (M9). Documented, his letters.
  5. **The order of 1541.** He would accept the post only if the city swore to keep a catechism and a discipline (M10). Within twenty days he and the other ministers, with six councillors, drafted the Church Ordinances, which set out four kinds of office (M11). Pastors preached, gave the sacraments and corrected with the elders; teachers taught sound doctrine; deacons kept the goods of the poor and cared for the sick; elders, twelve men chosen from the city's councils, set in every quarter of the town, watched over everyone's life and warned in friendship those who went wrong (M11). Elders and ministers met every Thursday morning as the consistory (M11). He wanted church discipline kept apart from the city's courts. But the councils amended the text, would not let the ministers see the changes, and kept the last word on punishment (M11). He also wrote the catechism in haste, its pages carried to the printer as he wrote (M12). Documented.
  6. **The force (1542–1547).** The consistory questioned people on their faith; a woman who still went to the Catholic service was put outside the faith (M13). In 1546 a man who refused to answer "to Mr Calvin" was sent by the council to prison (M13). In 1547 the city beheaded a man for speaking against God and threatening the pastors (M13). Documented. Tell it plainly.
  7. **The teaching on God's choice, and the quarrel (1551).** The Institutes, now much larger, taught predestination, God choosing beforehand who will be saved (M13a). Q2. Calvin wrote that only this showed salvation coming from God's free mercy (M13a). In 1551 Jérôme Bolsec, a doctor of medicine, stood up at the pastors' weekly meeting on the Bible. He said the only choice was the one seen when people believe or refuse, and that those who taught an eternal decree made God a tyrant, an idol like Jupiter (M13b). Calvin answered him on the spot. An officer had Bolsec taken to prison. Geneva asked other Swiss churches; Bern's pastors replied that some said he was not so bad a man, and wished for agreement (M13b). In December the city banished him for life, to the sound of a trumpet (M13b). Documented, from the pastors' register and the sentence; say once that this is Geneva's record.
  8. **Servetus, and the right to bar from communion (1553–1555).** In 1553 the city council burned Michael Servetus alive, for what it called great errors and blasphemies, after asking the advice of four Swiss churches. Calvin had written years before that if Servetus came, he would not let him leave alive. During the trial he hoped for a death sentence, though a less cruel one (M14). For twelve years the councils had denied the consistory the right to bar people from communion; Calvin had said such a decree must be sealed by his death or his exile. The right was granted in 1555 (M13). Documented. Tell it plainly.
  9. **The school (1559).** In 1559 Geneva opened an academy, with teachers of Hebrew, Greek, philosophy and theology. Calvin spoke in French and called everyone to prayer, and Theodore Beza, one of the city's ministers, became its first rector (M15). Documented. Keep to one or two sentences. Do not say "to train ministers for France"; that is the editor's (M15).

- **What came of them** (about a third; heaviest first):
  1. **France: the order went underground.** In Paris, in 1555, a gentleman who had moved to the city to be less hunted for his faith asked the believers who met in his house to choose a minister, so his newborn child need not be baptised with Roman rites. The father said he could not travel to Geneva for it. They set up a consistory of elders and deacons (I1). In 1557 the king's edict made the death penalty mandatory for, among others, those who went to Geneva (I1). In May 1559, deputies of the French churches met secretly in Paris and adopted a confession and a discipline: no church over another, and pastors, overseers and deacons in each (I1). Calvin's letter that month names ministers Geneva was sending (I1). Documented. DEFENDED: a church ruled by its own order of ministers and elders, and its own discipline, apart from the civil courts (M11, M13).
  2. **Scotland.** John Knox went to Frankfurt at Calvin's command, came back to Geneva, then went home to Scotland (I2). In 1562 the Scottish church ordered its sacraments, marriages and burials "according to the Kirk of Geneva" (I2). Its book of 1611 still printed his catechism (I2). Documented.
  3. **A critic's witness.** Some thirty years after his death, an English churchman, Richard Hooker, wrote against those who wanted Geneva's discipline in England. He still called Calvin the wisest man the French church ever had. He wrote that Calvin's books had become "almost the very canon" for judging doctrine and discipline, and that the French churches and the church of Scotland were cast in his pattern (I3). Hooker's judgment; credit it.
  4. **The name.** In 1672 a synod of the Greek church met at Jerusalem, under its patriarch, "against the Calvinists", and called him "the heresiarch", the arch-heretic (I5). In lessons on the catechism printed in 1662, a Dutch professor at Utrecht taught that his church agreed with Calvin, but Calvin was not its head (I5). Documented. One or two sentences.

- **Quotes** (3; rendered by the researcher from the original language):

  - **Q3** (farewell to the ministers, 28 April 1564, as the minister Pinant wrote it down; French, K 35741–35745). - **Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from Calvin to other churches was a written order for running a congregation. It had ministers and teachers, deacons for the poor, and elders: lay men chosen to watch over the members' lives and correct them, sitting with the ministers each week as a consistory with its own discipline (M11, CO10 1947–1963). The listener must understand three things, through what people did. First, what an elder and a consistory were: in Geneva, twelve laymen set in every quarter of the town to watch and warn, meeting with the ministers (M11, M13). Second, that the French built the same offices with no friendly city behind them: in Paris in 1555 a congregation meeting in a private house set up "a consistory of some elders and deacons", and in 1559 the French churches met in secret to adopt a discipline, while going to Geneva could cost a man his life (I1). Third, that Scotland ordered its worship "according to the Kirk of Geneva" (PET 1208–1210). Do not say the Paris church named Geneva as its model; its own account names the apostles' church (D5). Do not say Calvin wrote the French Confession (D4).

  - Wrong: "Bolsec was banished for disagreeing" / "for heresy" / "Calvin banished Bolsec". Right: the city banished him for "a false opinion against the holy Scriptures", raised too boldly in the pastors' meeting (M13b). The city council sentenced him, not Calvin.
  - Wrong: Bolsec's view told as "free will" alone. Right: that the only choice is the one seen when people believe or refuse, and that an eternal decree made God a tyrant (M13b).
  - Wrong: "the Swiss churches backed Calvin". Right: Bern's pastors answered that some said Bolsec was not so bad a man, and wished for agreement (M13b). Say no more of the other churches; their replies are summarised only by the editor.
  - Wrong: "Castellio was expelled for attacking predestination". Right: leave Castellio out; his held quarrel is about the Song of Songs and the Creed (M13b).
  - Wrong: "the Institutes always taught predestination". Right: its chapter on predestination was added in 1539 (M13a, editor's note); say "the Institutes, now much larger".
  - Wrong: "the elders spied on people" / "the consistory punished people". Right: the elders watched and warned; the consistory questioned and admonished; the council imprisoned and executed (M11, M13).
  - Wrong: Hooker's praise told as the narrator's. Right: credit it to Hooker, a critic of Geneva's discipline (I3).
  - Wrong: "He died young / worn out at 54" or any cause of death. Right: he died on 27 May 1564; give no cause (M17).
