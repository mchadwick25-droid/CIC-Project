# Outline: John Calvin

All references are to `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder (S = setting, M = life events, I = impact, L = links, D = disagreements). Each item there gives the file and its lines.

- **The essence:** John Calvin was a French exile and scholar who wrote the Institutes, a book defending France's persecuted Protestants. Passing through Geneva for one night in 1536, he was held there, and gave the city a written church order: pastors, teachers, elders and deacons, with a consistory to watch over the members' lives. Reformed churches in France and Scotland built their own churches on that order, and his name became the label for the whole family.
  - Confidence: the book's purpose, the 1536 stop and the curse are Documented as his own account, written 21 years later (M3, M5, D1). The order is Documented from the 1541 draft and the council registers (M10, M11). The French and Scottish churches taking it up is Documented (I1, I2). The name as a label is Documented through a 1672 synod and a 1662 Dutch catechism (I5).

- **Also told:**
  - **The Institutes.** First a short handbook, printed in 1536 and addressed to the French king, to show that the people burned in France were not rebels (M3). It grew into a large work over his life (I4). One idea from it, in his words: true wisdom is "the knowledge of God and of ourselves" (M4). Documented.
  - **The order, and the fight over it.** Expelled in 1538, recalled in 1541, he would only stay if the city swore to keep a catechism and a discipline (M7, M10). The city's councils amended his Ordinances, kept the last word on punishment, and fought his consistory over the right to bar people from the Lord's Supper until 1555 (M11, M13). Documented.
  - **The force.** The consistory questioned people on their faith and sent a defiant man to the council, which jailed him. A man who threatened the ministers was beheaded in 1547. Michael Servetus was burned alive in 1553 by the city council, with Calvin wanting his death (M13, M14). Documented.
  - **What it gave others.** In Paris, in 1555, a secret congregation set up a consistory of elders and deacons. In 1559 the French churches met in a secret synod and adopted a confession and a discipline, with pastors, overseers and deacons, while going to Geneva was punishable by death in France. In 1562 Scotland's church ordered its sacraments, marriages and burials "according to the Kirk of Geneva" (I1, I2). Documented.
  - Where the sources will not hold it: that Calvin wrote the French Confession is contested (D4); that the Paris church copied Geneva is the editors' reading; the Histoire names the early church as its model (D5). Tell what each church did.

- **Setting:**
  - Time: "in 1536"; he lived 1509 to 1564 (S1, M17).
  - Place: born at Noyon, in northern France (S1). Geneva: "a small city on its lake, at the edge of the Swiss lands, that had just broken with Rome", and split into factions (S4). Zurich's pastors placed it on the borders of France, Italy and Germany (M9). Strasbourg, where he was pastor of a congregation of French refugees (M8).
  - Tradition: "the Reformed churches"; the tree's tradition is "the Reformed cities of Zurich and Geneva" (S5). That family began at Zurich in 1519, before him (census relationsSummary).
  - Weave in a clause at a time.

- **Shape:** Founder or leader. What he built (a written order for a church), how it ran (the consistory, the elders, the fight with the councils, the force), and who took it up (France, Scotland). Trap: titles and offices in place of acts. Show the order through what it did: the elders' rounds, the consistory's questions, the jailing, the French congregation choosing a minister.

- **Sibling:** none.

- **Openings** (chosen: 1):
  1. **One night in Geneva** (M5, Q1). In August 1536 a young French scholar, already the author of a book that defended his persecuted fellow believers, came to Geneva. War had closed his direct road to Strasbourg, and he meant to stay one night. Someone recognised him, and Geneva's preacher, William Farel, begged him to stay. When begging failed, Farel did something more frightening. Calvin told it himself, twenty years later: Q1. Leads straight into the essence: he stayed, and what he built there was copied far beyond it.
  2. **"That Frenchman"** (M6). Weeks later, the city's council register did not yet record his name. Farel asked the council to keep and pay the lecturer that "ille Gallus", that Frenchman, had begun at St Peter's. Use only as a line inside beat 3, or as an alternative hook.

- **Story beats:**
  1. **Hook and essence (first ~150 words).** Opening 1 with Q1 (M5). Then the essence in plain words: the man was John Calvin; he stayed, was thrown out two years later, came back in 1541, and gave Geneva a written order for its church, with four kinds of office and a consistory of ministers and elders (M7, M10, M11). The book he had written was the Institutes (M3). Churches in France and Scotland took that order for their own, and his name became the name of the whole family, used by friends and enemies (I1, I2, I5). Documented; the curse is his own account.
  2. **The exile who wanted to hide (1509–1536).** Born at Noyon in 1509. His father meant him for theology, then switched him to law, because he saw law making men rich (S1, S2). He tells of a "sudden conversion" (M1). He says he always loved shade and quiet, and left France to hide in some obscure corner (M2). At Basel, he says, believers burned in France were being slandered abroad as rebels. He published a short handbook to clear their name, addressed to the French king (M3). Its opening idea: true wisdom is knowing God and knowing ourselves (M4). Documented as his account.
  3. **First years, and expulsion (1536–1538).** He began as a lecturer on the Bible at St Peter's; the council register first calls him only "that Frenchman" (M6). The councils banned him and Farel from preaching, because they would not follow the church ceremonies the city of Bern required. They preached anyway, and in April 1538 the General Council gave them three days to leave. Calvin answered that if they had served men they would be badly rewarded, but they served a great master who would reward them (M7). Documented, council register.
  4. **Strasbourg, and the recall (1538–1541).** At Strasbourg he became pastor of a congregation of French refugees. Friends pressed him into the post by warning that God would find him as he found Jonah, the prophet who ran from God (M8). When Geneva's councils begged him back, he wrote to Farel that he would rather die a hundred times than go back to that cross (M9). He went in 1541. To Farel he wrote that he was not his own, and offered his heart to God as a sacrifice (M9). Documented, his letters.
  5. **The order of 1541.** He would accept the post only if the city swore to keep a catechism and a discipline (M10). Within twenty days he and the other ministers, with six councillors, drafted the Ecclesiastical Ordinances (M11). Q2. Pastors preached, gave the sacraments and corrected with the elders; teachers taught sound doctrine; deacons kept the goods of the poor and cared for the sick; elders, twelve men chosen from the city's councils, set in every quarter of the town, watched over everyone's life and warned in friendship those who went wrong (M11). Elders and ministers met every Thursday morning as the consistory (M11). He wanted church discipline kept apart from the city's courts. But the councils amended the text, would not let the ministers see the changes, and kept the last word on punishment (M11). He also wrote the catechism in haste, its pages carried to the printer as he wrote (M12). Documented.
  6. **The force.** The consistory questioned people on their faith; a woman who still went to Mass was put outside the faith (M13). In 1546 a man who refused to answer "to Mr Calvin" was sent by the council to prison (M13). In 1547 the city beheaded a man for blasphemy and for threatening the ministers (M13). For twelve years the councils denied the consistory the right to bar people from communion; Calvin said the decree must be sealed by his death or his exile; the right was granted in 1555 (M13). In 1553 the city council burned Michael Servetus alive, for what it called great errors and blasphemies, after asking the advice of four Swiss churches. Calvin had written years before that if Servetus came, he would not let him leave alive. During the trial he hoped for a death sentence, though a less cruel one (M14). Documented. Tell it plainly.
  7. **The school (1559).** In 1559 Geneva opened an academy, with teachers of Hebrew, Greek, philosophy and theology. Calvin spoke in French and called everyone to prayer, and Theodore Beza, one of the city's ministers, became its first rector (M15). Documented. Keep to one or two sentences. Do not say "to train ministers for France"; that is the editor's (M15).

- **What came of them** (about a third; heaviest first):
  1. **France: the order went underground.** In Paris, in 1555, a gentleman who had moved to the city to be less hunted for his faith asked the believers who met in his house to choose a minister, so his newborn child need not be baptised with Roman rites. The father said he could not travel to Geneva for it. They set up a consistory of elders and deacons (I1). In 1557 the king's edict made the death penalty mandatory for, among others, those who went to Geneva (I1). In May 1559, deputies of the French churches met secretly in Paris and adopted a confession and a discipline: no church over another, and pastors, overseers and deacons in each (I1). Calvin's letter that month names ministers Geneva was sending (I1). Documented. DEFENDED: a church ruled by its own order of ministers and elders, and its own discipline, apart from the civil courts (M11, M13).
  2. **Scotland.** John Knox went to Frankfurt at Calvin's command, came back to Geneva, then went home to Scotland (I2). In 1562 the Scottish church ordered its sacraments, marriages and burials "according to the Kirk of Geneva" (I2). Its book of 1611 still printed his catechism (I2). Documented.
  3. **A critic's witness.** Some thirty years after his death, an English churchman, Richard Hooker, wrote against those who wanted Geneva's discipline in England. He still called Calvin the wisest man the French church ever had. He wrote that Calvin's books had become "almost the very canon" for judging doctrine and discipline, and that the French churches and the church of Scotland were cast in his pattern (I3). Hooker's judgment; credit it.
  4. **The name.** In 1672 a synod of the Greek church met at Jerusalem, under its patriarch, "against the Calvinists", and called him "the heresiarch", the arch-heretic (I5). In lessons on the catechism printed in 1662, a Dutch professor at Utrecht taught that his church agreed with Calvin, but Calvin was not its head (I5). Documented. One or two sentences.

- **Quotes** (3; rendered by the researcher from the original language):
  - **Q1** (preface to his Commentary on the Psalms, 1557; Latin, K 29989–29995). Latin: "ubi se vidit rogando nihil proficere, usque ad exsecrationem descendit, ut Deus otio meo malediceret, si me a ferendis subsidiis in tanta necessitate subducerem. Quo terrore perculsus, susceptum iter ita omisi ut mihi et verecundiae et timiditatis meae conscius obeundo certo muneri fidem meam non obstringerem." **Rendering:** "When he saw that asking got him nowhere, he went as far as a curse. He called on God to curse my quiet life if I held back from helping in so great a need. Struck by that terror, I gave up the journey I had begun. Yet, knowing my own shyness and timidity, I would not pledge myself to any fixed office." Job: the opening; the moment the scholar was held. The writer may stop after "the journey I had begun", but not before.
  - **Q2** (draft Ecclesiastical Ordinances, Sept.–Oct. 1541; French, K 32448–32452; archive minute CO10 1368–1370, 1421–1422). French: "Il y a quatre ordres d'offices, que Nostre Seigneur a institué pour le gouvernement de son Eglise. Premièrement les pasteurs, puis les docteurs, après les anciens, quartement les diacres." **Rendering:** "There are four orders of office that our Lord set up for the government of his Church. First the pastors, then the teachers, after them the elders, and fourth the deacons." Job: the order itself, in the document's words; the narrator then says what each did. "Docteurs" is rendered "teachers" because "doctors" misleads.
  - **Q3** (farewell to the ministers, 28 April 1564, as the minister Pinant wrote it down; French, K 35741–35745). French: "J'ay vescu icy en combats merveilleux : j'ay esté salué par mocquerie le soir devant ma porte de 50 ou 60 coups d'arquebute. Que pensez-vous que cela pouvoit estonner un pauvre escholier timide comme je suis, et comme je l'ay tousjours esté, je le confesse ?" **Rendering:** "I have lived here amid extraordinary battles. In mockery, I was greeted in the evening outside my door with fifty or sixty gunshots. Think how that could shake a poor, timid scholar, as I am and always have been, I confess." Job: the ending; the timid scholar of the opening, at the end. Introduce it as his words to the ministers, written down by one of them.

  - Only Q1, Q2 and Q3 are quoted in the story. Short phrases in quotation marks elsewhere in this outline ("according to the Kirk of Geneva", "against the Calvinists", "almost the very canon", "that Frenchman", "sudden conversion") are evidence for the writer: tell them in the narrator's words, without quotation marks.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from Calvin to other churches was a written order for running a congregation. It had ministers and teachers, deacons for the poor, and elders: lay men chosen to watch over the members' lives and correct them, sitting with the ministers each week as a consistory with its own discipline (Q2; M11, CO10 1947–1963). The listener must understand three things, through what people did. First, what an elder and a consistory were: in Geneva, twelve laymen set in every quarter of the town to watch and warn, meeting with the ministers (M11, M13). Second, that the French built the same offices with no friendly city behind them: in Paris in 1555 a congregation meeting in a private house set up "a consistory of some elders and deacons", and in 1559 the French churches met in secret to adopt a discipline, while going to Geneva could cost a man his life (I1). Third, that Scotland ordered its worship "according to the Kirk of Geneva" (PET 1208–1210). Do not say the Paris church named Geneva as its model; its own account names the apostles' church (D5). Do not say Calvin wrote the French Confession (D4).

- **Ending** (chosen: 1):
  1. **The farewell and death.** On 28 April 1564, gravely ill, he spoke to the city's ministers. One of them wrote down his words from memory. He told them that when he first came there was preaching and nothing more, no reform, everything in uproar. Then Q3. He asked them to change nothing. They took his hand one by one, in tears (M16). He died on 27 May 1564 (M17). Documented as the minister's record.
  2. Shorter: his last letter to Farel, now old, on 2 May: it was enough that he lived and died for Christ (M17). Then his death. Paraphrase only.
