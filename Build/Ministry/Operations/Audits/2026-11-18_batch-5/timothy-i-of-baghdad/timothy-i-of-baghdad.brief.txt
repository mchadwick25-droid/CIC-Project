Early in 781, Christians in China's capital set up a great stone. On it, in Syriac, the language of their church, they named their patriarch, the head of the whole church, far away in Mesopotamia. They called him "Father of Fathers", the stone's title for that head. But he was dead, and a new patriarch had been installed months before; the news, it seems, had not yet reached them.

The new patriarch was Timothy. For more than forty years he led the Church of the East, the church of the Christians of Mesopotamia and Persia. He led it from Baghdad, under five caliphs, the Muslim rulers of the empire. Historians of his church say he argued for his faith at the caliphs' court. And he sent monks out as bishops, the church's leaders for a region, and let them make more bishops where they were. His letters show a church that reached toward the Turks and China.

Timothy came from Hazza in Adiabene, and was already a bishop when the patriarch before him died. Mari, a historian of his church writing more than three centuries later, says Timothy showed the electors sacks he said held money for them, and won. He was installed in May 780 at Seleucia, the old royal city on the Tigris. When the electors asked for their money, he answered, "The priesthood is by no means sold for money," and showed them the sacks held only stones. Other later histories tell the trick differently.

Two rivals, the chief bishops of the province of Elam and of Merv, refused to worship or work with him. The man from Merv accused him before the caliph al-Mahdi, without success. Then the chief bishop of Elam and thirteen bishops declared Timothy cursed and cut off from the church, and he cut them off in turn. The split lasted about two years, by Timothy's own count, and in it Christians fought, killed and jailed one another.

A list of his writings, made five centuries later, names a debate with al-Mahdi. A later historian says the next caliph, al-Hadi, sent for him on most days to put hard questions to him about religion.

About 790, at a meeting of bishops, he made a church rule that no candidate for patriarch could promise gifts, so that an election could not split the church again. In his letter from that meeting, he told the chief bishop of Elam why he had not come to visit: he had been rebuilding destroyed churches. "Almighty God allowed it, and the king, may God keep him alive, agreed that the churches be rebuilt. Even so, we had to go before him six times on this matter." The king he means is the caliph.

Thomas of Marga, a monk who wrote about twenty years after Timothy's death and had read his letters, tells of the bishops Timothy sent. Two brothers from one monastery, Kardagh and Yahballaha, went as chief bishops to Gilan and Dailom, south of the Caspian Sea. When many peoples there came to the faith, Yahballaha asked leave to make bishops. But to ordain a bishop, to make one, took three bishops, and the brothers were two, far from Baghdad, where a new bishop normally went to have his making confirmed.

Timothy's answer speaks of the throne, the bishop's chair in the church, and of the Book of the Gospels, the four Gospels of the life of Jesus, read in worship. "Make bishops of whomever you and the pious Kardagh choose. In place of the third bishop, let the Book of the Gospels be laid on the throne at the right hand. So, by the hand of God, ordain the first bishop." They made seven bishops.

A historian of a rival church, centuries later, passes on a report that the bishops of Fars, in southern Persia, had refused the patriarch until Timothy won them over. He let their chief bishop complete his own bishops' making, a custom that, this historian says, still held in his day. From Timothy's letters, Thomas learned of a chief bishop of Beth Sinaye, which scholars take to be China. And Timothy himself counts Indians and Chinese, Tibetans and Turks among the peoples in his care.

He died in the early 820s, under the fifth caliph; one chronicle gives 9 January 823. Mari says he was ninety-five. He was buried at Baghdad, in the monastery he had restored and made his home.
