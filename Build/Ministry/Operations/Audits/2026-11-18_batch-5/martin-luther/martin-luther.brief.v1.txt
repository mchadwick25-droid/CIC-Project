On 31 October 1517, a friar, a man living under religious vows, wrote to the archbishop of Mainz, a great prince of the church. Pardons, letters said to cancel punishment for sin, were being sold in the archbishop's name to build St Peter's, the pope's new church in Rome. People believed, he warned, that a pardon made them sure of heaven.

The friar was Martin Luther, who taught at the small university in Wittenberg, in eastern Germany. He taught that forgiveness is grace, God's free gift, received by faith, by trusting what God promises, so it could not be bought. Condemned by the pope, he stood before the emperor, ruler of the Holy Roman Empire, a union of German and central European lands. He would not take back his books unless Scripture, the Bible, or clear reason proved him wrong. Outlawed and hidden, he put the New Testament, the gospels and letters of the early church, into German, and the Lutheran churches, grown from his teaching, carry his name.

With his letter he sent theses, short statements for a university debate, and one put his teaching in a line: "Every true Christian, living or dead, has a share in all the good things of Christ and the church. God gives it to him, even without letters of pardon."

In December 1520 he burned the popes' book of church law, and editors say the pope's decree against him burned with it. In January the pope cut him off from the church.

In April 1521, under the emperor's promise of safe passage there and back, he came to Worms. His friends printed his answer: "Unless I am convinced by the witness of the Scriptures or by clear reason, I cannot and will not take back anything." It ends, "God help me. Amen." The famous "Here I stand, I cannot do otherwise" appears only in prints from one Wittenberg press.

In May the emperor, Charles V, made him an outlaw: no one was to house or feed him, and whoever found him was to seize him. Hidden on his friends' advice, he made that German New Testament, printed in 1522. He wanted the people to sing in their own language, not only to hear trained singers. By one editor's count, four of the eight hymns in Wittenberg's first hymnbook, in 1524, were his.

In 1525 peasants across the German lands rose against their lords. Luther first warned both sides. The rulers, and above all the bishops, did nothing but rob their people, he wrote, while to the peasants he said that whoever takes the sword will perish by the sword. Then armed bands began robbing and burning, and he urged rulers, and anyone who could, to strike them down: "So let everyone who can strike, kill and stab, in secret or in the open. Remember that nothing can be more poisonous, harmful or devilish than a rebel."

One friend wrote to him that people who favored him found it strange that he let the tyrants kill without mercy. That June he married Katharina von Bora, a former nun, who had lived under vows in a convent. In July he held to his call: rulers should strike quickly, guilty or innocent. But once they had won, he said, they should show mercy even to the guilty, and he condemned the "furious, raving, senseless tyrants" who could not get their fill of blood. A later, hostile chronicle of the Anabaptists, a church of believers baptized as adults, said he had leaned on worldly power.

His short catechism, a book of questions and answers on the faith, taught fathers to teach their households. His churches kept his songs, and two centuries later the hymnbook of Halle, a Lutheran town in Germany, still printed them.

In 1539 he wrote that in putting the Bible into German he had hoped for less writing and more reading of Scripture, so that each might "drink for himself from the fresh spring".
