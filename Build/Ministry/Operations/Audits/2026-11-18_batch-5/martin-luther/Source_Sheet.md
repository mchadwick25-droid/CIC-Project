## 1. Setting

S1. **Who he was, in his own time's words.** The Theses are headed as a disputation at Wittenberg "praesidente R. P. Martino Luther, Artium et S. Theologiae Magistro, eiusdemque ibidem lectore ordinario" (KD 2439–2442). He signs the letter to the archbishop "Brother Martin Luther" (W1 1098). The Edict names him "Augustinianae familiae quidam Martinus Lutherus" (KD 6032). OWN and CONTEMP. Documented. Plain: "a friar of the Augustinian order who taught theology at Wittenberg". Morley (editor) says he was born at Eisleben in 1483 and his father was "a poor miner" (TT 97); Luther calls Eisleben "my native town" (CUR 16342–16343). Miner's son: EDITOR only, card.
S2. **Place.** Wittenberg, a university town in Saxony (KD 2440; CEN "Wittenberg, Saxony"). His prince was the Elector of Saxony, in whose lands Tetzel was not allowed to preach (KD 2411–2412, Kidd's headnote). Worms, on the Rhine, where the emperor held his assembly of princes, the Diet (KD 5640ff.). Plain: "Wittenberg, a small university town in Saxony, in eastern Germany"; "Worms, where the emperor met the princes of the empire".
S3. **Years.** Letter and Theses: "From Wittenberg on the Vigil of All Saints, MDXVII" (W1 1089), i.e. 31 October 1517 (W1 headings 991–993; KD 2421–2422). Worms: answer 18 April 1521 (KD 5791–5792; RTA 36257 running head). New Testament out September 1522; whole Bible 1534 (W6 17307, 17332, Jacobs). First Wittenberg hymnbook 1524 (W6 13417–13419). German Mass 1526 (W6 contents). Documented for 1517, 1521, 1523, 1524, 1526 (dates in the documents); Widely Accepted for 1522 and 1534 (Jacobs, editor).
S4. **Tradition.** Census: told within **Lutheran Wittenberg and Its Congregations**, informal "Lutheran Wittenberg" (CEN, `toldWithin`; that entry's name and informalName). Plain spoken forms: "the Lutheran churches", "the churches that grew from Wittenberg". Do not say "Protestant" as if it were a church name; the census lane is "Protestant & Evangelical".
S5. **What an indulgence was** (for the writer's gloss). Kidd prints the indulgence preacher's own model sermon: the dead cry to their children to free them from the flames "parva eleemosyna" (by a small alms), and buyers are told that with "his literis confessionalibus" they can have full remission of penalties once in life and at death (KD 2282–2327). Luther's letter: people believe "if they have purchased letters of indulgence they are sure of their salvation" and that "so soon as they cast their contributions into the money-box, souls fly out of purgatory" (W1 1018–1021). Jacobs (editor): the 1515 indulgence was to pay for the new St Peter's church in Rome, under Albrecht of Brandenburg as commissioner (W1 706–716). Plain gloss: "a letter of pardon, bought from the church's preachers, said to lift the penalties of sin, even for the dead in purgatory". Documented (the preachers' own texts and Luther's letter); the money's split with the Fuggers is Jacobs's (W1 715–721), card only.

## 2. Recorded moments

M1. **The letter to the archbishop, 31 October 1517.** OWN (English, W1 991–1094; Latin cited by Kidd from de Wette, KD 2421–2422, not printed in KD). "Papal indulgences for the building of St. Peter's are circulating under your most distinguished name … I grieve over the wholly false impressions which the people have conceived from them; to wit,—the unhappy souls believe that if they have purchased letters of indulgence they are sure of their salvation; again, that so soon as they cast their contributions into the money-box, souls fly out of purgatory" (W1 1014–1021). He asks the archbishop to withdraw the *Instruction* and change the preaching, "lest, perchance, one may some time arise, who will publish writings in which he will confute both them and that treatise" (W1 1070–1077). Postscript: he may see "these my Disputations" (W1 1092–1095). Documented.
M2. **The Theses.** OWN, Latin (KD 2436ff.). Th. 1: "Dominus et magister noster Iesus Christus, dicendo: Poenitentiam agite, &c., omnem vitam fidelium poenitentiam esse voluit" (KD 2446–2448). Th. 27: "Hominem praedicant, qui statim, ut iactus nummus in cistam tinnierit, evolare dicunt animam" (KD 2537–2538). Th. 43: "Docendi sunt Christiani, quod dans pauperi aut mutuans egenti melius facit quam si venias redimeret" (KD 2597–2598). Th. 50: if the pope knew the preachers' exactions "mallet basilicam S. Petri in cineres ire, quam aedificari cute, carne et ossibus ovium suarum" (KD 2627–2629). Th. 86: "cur papa, cuius opes hodie sunt opulentissimis Crassis crassiores, non de suis pecuniis magis quam pauperum fidelium, struit unam tantummodo basilicam S. Petri?" (KD 2761–2763). Documented. Jacobs (editor): the word "faith" does not occur in them, penance is not disputed, and indulgences are admitted within limits (W1 499–505); Luther was then "a devoted son of the Church" (W1 509–510). EDITOR, Widely Accepted.
M3. **He expected the pope to agree.** OWN-LATE, Latin, 1545 preface: "In iis certus mihi videbar me habiturum patronum Papam, cuius fiducia tam fortiter nitebar, qui in suis decretis clarissime damnat quaestorum (ita vocat indulgentiarios praedicatores) immodestiam" (KD 2426–2430). English of the same preface: "I was so full and drunken, yea, so besotted in papal doctrine" (W1 357–360); "At the beginning I was quite alone and without any helpers … it was without any intention, purpose, or will of mine that I fell, quite unexpectedly, into this wrangling and contention" (W1 386–390). Documented as his own later account (28 years on).
M4. **The Theses spread.** EDITOR: "Within two months, before the end of 1517, three editions of the Latin text had been printed, one at Wittenberg, one at Nürnberg, and one as far away as Basel" (W1 488–491). CONTEMP: the Basel printer Froben to Luther, 14 Feb. 1519: "Sexcentos in Galliam misimus et in Hispaniam, venduntur Parisiis, leguntur etiam a Sorbonicis" (KD 4108–4110); "libellos tuos in Brabantiam et Angliam misimus" (KD 4121–4122); "Exemplaria nostra nos usque ad decem vendidimus omnia, haud feliciorem venditionem in aliquo libro sumus unquam experti" (KD 4134–4136). Documented (Froben's letter is of 1519 and concerns his collected writings, not only the Theses).
M5. **The bull, 1520.** CONTEMP, Latin: *Exsurge Domine*, issued 15 June 1520 (KD 5359, Kidd). It condemns his writings and orders them "publice … comburant" (KD 5609–5615), and gives him sixty days to recant (KD 5619–5628). Documented. Kidd (editor): his books were burnt at Louvain and Cologne, and he "retaliated by burning the Papal Bull and the Decretals at Wittenberg, 10 Dec. 1520" (KD 5382–5386). EDITOR, Widely Accepted; not in any held document of the day.
M6. **Worms, 17 April 1521: the first hearing.** CONTEMP, Latin *Acta* (RTA 36071–36096): the herald brought him by a back way for fear of the crowd; "Non tamen latuit multos, qui ab ingressu vix vi prohibebantur; plaerique casulas conscenderunt studio visendi" (RTA 36077–36079: many could hardly be kept from the door by force; many climbed onto the little houses to see). The emperor's spokesman, Johann von Eck, official of the archbishop of Trier, asked him, in Latin then German, to own the books published under his name and say whether he would take any of them back (RTA 36086–36092). His lawyer called out, "intitulentur libri" — let the titles be read (RTA 36094–36095). He had asked for time to think (KD 5800–5815, the official's words next day: "deliberationem petisti"). Documented.
M7. **18 April: the answer.** CONTEMP, Latin, the *Acta*, in Luther's first person (RTA 36580–36595; same text, slightly different, KD 5794–5953). He sorted his books into three kinds and said that if he took back the second kind he would only add strength to tyranny, "tyrannidi robur adiecero" (KD 5858–5911, phrase at 5890). Pressed for a plain answer "neque cornutum neque dentatum" (KD 5944–5945): "Nisi convictus fuero testimoniis scripturarum aut ratione evidenti (nam neque papae neque conciliis solis credo, cum constet eos errasse saepius et sibi ipsis contradixisse), victus sum scripturis a me adductis et capta conscientia in verbis dei, revocare neque possum neque volo quicquam, cum contra conscientiam agere neque tutum neque integrum sit. Gott helf mir. Amen." (RTA 36588–36595). Documented. He spoke in Latin and German: "Respondit D. Martinus et ipse latine et germanice" (KD 5826).
M8. **The emperor's answer, 19 April.** CONTEMP, Charles V's own declaration in French (RTA 39098–39128; Kidd's English KD 5955–5990). "car yl est certain que ung seul frère erre en son opinion, laquelle est contre toute la crestiennité, tant du temps passé mille ans et plus que du présent" (RTA 39105–39108). He resolved to stake "mes royaulmes et segnouries, mes amis, mon corps, mon sang, ma vie et mon âme" (RTA 39108–39110), repents having waited so long, will send him home under his safe-conduct without preaching, and will proceed "comme contre notoire hérétique" (RTA 39116–39125). Documented.
M9. **The Edict, May 1521.** CONTEMP, Latin (KD 6025–6122; Kidd dates it 26 May; the German text is RTA No. 92). It declares him "manifestum pertinacemque haereticum" (KD 6049–6050); after twenty days "nemo vestrum hospitio, tecto lectove recipiat ac foveat, nemo cibo potuque alat" (KD 6058–6062); whoever finds him is to seize him "vinctum" and hand him over (KD 6068–6072); his helpers may be seized and their goods taken (KD 6077–6081); his books are to be burnt, "igni comburendas" (KD 6100–6107). Documented.
M10. **Hidden.** OWN, letter to the painter Lucas Cranach, 28 April 1521: "I shall submit to being hidden away, and as yet do not know where. I would have preferred being put to death by the tyrants … but was obliged to follow the advice of friends" (CUR 6021–6028). His summary of Worms in the same letter: "they only cried, 'Are the books yours?' 'Yes.' 'Will you retract them or not?' 'No.' 'Then get away.'" (CUR 6034–6039). Documented as his own words (English only). EDITORS: "spirited off out of harm's way by 'the Saxon fox'" (KD 5688–5689, Kidd); "he entered the Wartburg on May 4, 1521", "the secure hiding-place where the imperial proscription could not affect him" (W2 14413–14416, editor); rumour that he had been captured and perhaps killed (WB 2600–2602, Buchheim). Widely Accepted for the Wartburg and the date; that his prince arranged it is the editors'.
M11. **The New Testament.** OWN, letter to Spalatin, 30 March 1522 (English only): "I have not only translated the Gospel of St. John in my Patmos, but the whole of the New Testament, and Philip and I are now busy correcting it … we need your help, to find out proper words, therefore be ready to supply us with the common terms for some things we require, but not those used at Court, for this book is to be written in the simplest language that all may understand it; and so that I may begin at once, send the names of the precious stones mentioned in Rev. chap. xxi." (CUR 7737–7746; OCR "^irnplp<^f" = simplest, "tHatTmay" = that I may). "Patmos", the island of John's exile, is his name for his hiding place (CUR 6481, 6504). Documented. EDITOR (Jacobs): begun at the Wartburg in 1521, "published in September, 1522" (W6 17305–17307). Widely Accepted.
M12. **The whole Bible.** EDITOR (Jacobs): Pentateuch 1523, the prophets complete 1532, "Luther's complete Bible appeared in 1534"; he kept revising with Wittenberg colleagues, whom he called "his Sanhedrim", to the last edition he supervised in 1545 (W6 17321–17351). OWN-LATE (TT): "as we have prepared the same here at Wittemberg, and, God be praised, have translated it out of Hebrew into the High German tongue" (TT 507–509). OWN 1539: "it was our intention and our hope, when we began to put the Bible into German, that there would be less writing, and more studying and reading of the Scriptures … In this way every one may drink for himself from the fresh spring" (W1 280–286). Widely Accepted (dates); Documented (his words).
M13. **"Gospel" means good news.** OWN, Preface to the New Testament (1522 text as kept in 1545, English only): "'Gospel' is a Greek word, and means in Greek, a good message, good tidings, good news, a good report, which one sings and tells with rejoicing" (W6 21041–21043). Documented.
M14. **German songs for the people, 1523.** OWN, Latin, *Formula Missae* (KD 8089ff.): "Cantica velim etiam nobis esse vernacula quam plurima, quae populus sub Missa cantaret, vel iuxta Gradualia, item iuxta Sanctus et Agnus Dei. Quis enim dubitat eas olim fuisse voces totius populi quae nunc solus chorus cantat … ? Sed poetae … nobis desunt, aut nondum cogniti sunt qui pias et spirituales cantilenas … nobis concinant … Haec dico ut si qui sunt poetae Germanici exstimulentur et nobis poemata pietatis componant." (KD 8307–8314). Also: "vernacula Concio" (sermon in German) in the meantime, and "si futurum est ut vernacula Missa habeatur (quod Christus faveat)" (KD 8148–8150). Documented.
M15. **The first hymnbook, 1524.** OWN, preface to the Wittenberg *Spiritual Hymn Booklet* 1524 (English only, W6 13417–13466): "together with some others, I, too, have collected some spiritual songs as a fair beginning" (13434–13436); the songs are set in four parts "for no other reason than that I greatly desire the youth … to have something whereby they may be weaned away and freed from the love ballads and worldly (carnal) songs" (13452–13457); "I would gladly see all arts, especially music, in the service of Him who has given and created them" (13461–13463). Documented. EDITOR (Bacon): the 1524 Wittenberg book held eight hymns, four by Luther, three of them psalms (12, 14, 130) and the fourth *Nun freut euch* (HY 685–697). Widely Accepted. *Aus tiefer Not* (Ps. 130) German text HY 1696ff.
M16. **The German Mass, 1526.** OWN (English only): the German Mass is "introduced for the sake of the simple laymen" (W6 8695–8696); for the young, "we must read, sing, preach, write and compose, and if it would help the matter along, I would have all the bells pealing, and all the organs playing, and let everything chime in that has a clapper" (W6 8643–8647); he keeps the Latin service too, "for the youth is my chiefest concern" (W6 8669–8671). LATER, Johann Walter, court composer and his friend, writing "some forty years" after: Luther called him and Conrad Rupff to Wittenberg to settle the chant tones; Luther "himself made the notes over the Epistles, and the Gospels, and the Words of Institution … and sung them over to me"; Walter stayed three weeks "until the first German Mass was sung in the parish church" (HY 752–770). Bacon's note: first printed in the *Syntagma Musicum* of Michael Praetorius, taken from Rambach (1813) (HY 873–881); Bacon's English. Documented as Walter's account.
M17. **A Mighty Fortress.** OWN, German text (HY 3708–3744): "Ein' feste Burg ist unser Gott, / Ein' gute Wehr und Waffen" (3708–3709); last stanza "Das Wort sie sollen lassen stahn … Das Reich muss uns doch bleiben" (3738–3746). EDITOR (Bacon): written in 1529, not on the way to Worms; the Worms story comes from a likeness to his saying about devils on the roofs (HY 856–863). Kidd prints it, in English, under "The beginnings of German Hymnody", in his 1523–4 section (KD 8352–8400), without a date of its own. The date: Widely Accepted as Bacon's (1529); see D4.

## 3. Impact, heaviest first (what later witnesses carried on and used)

I1. **The hymns were sung and kept.** CONTEMP, Cyriacus Spangenberg, preface to the *Cithara Lutheri*, 1545, in Luther's lifetime (English via Bacon): "among all Mastersingers from the days of the Apostles until now, Luther is and always will be the best and most accomplished; in whose hymns and songs one does not find a vain or needless word" (HY 556–562). LATER, the Halle hymnbook, 16th edition, 1730: its index still lists "Aus tiefer noth schrey ich zu", "Nun freut euch, lieben Christen", "Vom himmel hoch da komm" (FH 1405, 1420, 1550), and its preface says that at the Reformation "in dem seligen Luthero und andern frommen Männern" the gift of building up God's congregation "mit kräfftigen Gesängen" came forth (FH 668–671). LATER, London 1754: the English Moravian hymnal prints Luther's Ten Commandments hymn in English, "These are the holy ten Commands" (MOR 31911–31916; translator noted MOR 459). Documented (each witness's text).
I2. **Worship in the people's own tongue, carried north.** LATER (in his lifetime), the Danish-Norwegian church order of 1537: King Christian III says the draft was sent "ad Reverendum patrem Doctorem Martinum Lutherum", through whom God "restituit sacri Evangelii Christi synceritatem", and that he approved it with the Wittenberg theologians (DK 111–120, OCR "Lutherũ", "appꝛobauit"). It urges that the Lord's Prayer, the words of Christ's institution and the last collect with the blessing be said "Danice", to help "fidei rudium et indoctorum", and so as not to be ashamed "de lingua Danica in qua nati sumus" (DK 6540–6548, OCR read). Documented. (Already the census edge `lutheran-wittenberg-and-its-congregations--scandinavian-reformation-kingdoms`.)
I3. **Defended: conscience bound by Scripture and clear reason** (M7). Carried on as the *Acta* printed in May 1521 (RTA 35675–35700) and in his collected works (KD 5788–5790, from "Lutheri Op. Lat. vi"). He held the same line in his letter to Count Albrecht of Mansfeld, 3 May 1521: "I said the Christian must judge for himself, even as he must live and die for himself, and that the Pope was not umpire in spiritual things—God's Word being the property of all believers" (CUR 6127–6134). Documented.
I4. **The movement grew while he hid.** CONTEMP: the Venetian envoy Contarini, 25–26 April 1521: "His books are sold publicly in Worms, although the Pope and the Emperor, who is on the spot, have prohibited them" (KD 6133–6151, English). The Archbishop of Mainz to Leo X, July 1521: "augentur copiae Lutheranorum, iamque rarissimi inveniuntur laici qui candide et simpliciter faveant ecclesiasticis; sed et bona pars sacerdotum facit cum Luthero" (KD 6153–6160). Hostile CONTEMP, Aleander to the vice-chancellor, February 1521: "tutta la Germania è in volta, et delle X. parti di essa le nove crida Luther et la decima, se non se cura dei ditti di Luther, saltem crida la morte alla Corte di Roma" (BAL 6674–6677; Kidd's English KD 5783–5786). Documented.
I5. **The German Bible's later life.** Only EDITORS in the Library: Jacobs on the revisions to 1545 (W6 17337–17351); Bacon: "Luther's Bible and Luther's Hymns gave life not only to the churches of the Reformation, but to German nationality and the German language" (HY 546–551). Coleridge, quoted by Bacon (HY 572–577). Card only; no held later church witness quotes or orders Luther's Bible (N1).
I6. **The catechisms** (outside the job). The Formula of Concord (1577) acknowledges "the Small and Large Catechisms of Dr. Luther … as the Bible of the laity" (BC 25515–25521). Documented; card.

## 5. Where sources disagree or come late

D1. **"Here I stand. I cannot do otherwise."** Kidd prints "Hie stehe ich. Ich kan nicht anders. Gott helff mir. Amen." at the end of the answer (KD 5951–5952), from the Latin works. Wrede (RTA, editor) prints only "Gott helf mir. Amen" (RTA 36594–36595) and explains: the words "Ich kann nicht änderst, hie stehe ich" are found "nur in zwei gleichzeitigen Drucken", one Latin, one German, both from the same Wittenberg printer, so "nicht zwei unabhängige Quellen, sondern nur eine"; the German translation, in manuscript and print, "hat aber stets nur: Gott helf mir. Amen"; the longer ending was added in Wittenberg (RTA 36651–36670). He reports that church historians (Köstlin, Kolde) keep the longer form and secular historians (Bezold) accept only the short cry (RTA 36643–36648). **Contested.** The doubt is a small scene in itself (see outline).
D2. **The church door.** Kidd (editor): "On 31 October 1517 Luther posted up ninety-five theses … on the door of the Castle Church" (KD 2413–2416). Jacobs (editor) says they were "nailed up" at midday (W1 467–474). TT's editor says the same (TT 101–103). No held witness from 1517, and the earliest account (Melanchthon, after Luther's death) is **not held**. The census Wittenberg story already tells it as a later tradition historians dispute (CEN `lutheran-wittenberg-and-its-congregations`). **Contested**; leave out.
D3. **The Table Talk account of Worms.** TT 3494–3600 (Luther at table, late, Bell's 1646 English) has "although in Worms there were as many devils as there are tiles on the houses, yet, God willing, I will go thither" (TT 3507–3509) and says his books were burned at Worms before the hearing. Wrede: Luther's table account "is to be used only with the greatest caution" ("Nur mit größter Vorsicht", RTA 35654–35661), though some facts in it are right. **Inferential-Thin** for the details; leave out.
D4. **Date of *Ein feste Burg*.** Bacon (editor): 1529 (HY 861; heading "_Melody,_ 1529", HY 3665). Kidd sets it, undated, in his 1523–4 section (KD 8352). The popular story that it was written or sung on the road to Worms: Bacon calls it an impression from the "devils … tiles" saying (HY 856–860); Heine repeats the Worms story (HY 595–600). **Contested** as to the exact year; not 1521.
D5. **Who wrote the tunes.** Bacon: some tunes come from older hymns, some "are conjectured to have been originally secular airs", many plausibly Luther's own (HY 714–735). Walter: Luther "himself made the notes" for the sung readings of the German Mass (HY 752–770). Sleidan (via Bacon) says he made the tune for *Ein feste Burg* (HY 798–801). **Contested** in general; say "wrote hymns" and, for the chant, credit Walter.
D6. **The crowd at Worms and the safe-conduct.** TT says the safe-conduct was "soon broken" (TT 3499–3501); Charles V's declaration says he will be sent home under it (RTA 39121–39122), and Luther's own letter of May 1521 says he left "with a safe-conduct of twenty-one days" (CUR 6148–6149). Leave the TT claim out.

## 6. Not held, or not in the record

N1. **Luther's German Bible** (1522 New Testament or 1534 Bible) is not held. Nor is the *Sendbrief vom Dolmetschen* (1530, on translating), cited only in editors' notes (W3 6460; Weimar 30/3 20605).
N2. Melanchthon's life of Luther (1546), the earliest account of the theses posted on the door: not held (searched for "Historia de vita et actis" and "valvis templi"; one unrelated hit).
N3. The German originals of the letters to Cranach and Spalatin, the 1524 hymnbook preface, the German Mass and the Bible prefaces: not held. Renderings of them are made from English.
N4. Luther's treatises against the Jews (1543): not held. His letter of 1 February 1546 to his wife says "I must endeavour to banish the Jews" from Eisleben (CUR 26641–26648). Outside this story's job; flagged for Mark.
N5. Melanchthon's own words on the Bible revision: not held. Luther's 1522 letter is the only held first-hand witness to the translation work.
