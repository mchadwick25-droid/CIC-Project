# Outline: Martin Luther

Every line cites `Source_Sheet.md` (S = setting, M = moment, I = impact, D = disagreement, N = not held; G, B, P, K, C = added for the re-set job, 2026-11-18).

- **The essence:** Martin Luther, a friar (a member of a religious order under vows) who taught the Bible at Wittenberg, taught that forgiveness is God's free gift, received by trusting God's promise, not earned or bought (G1–G3: Documented, his own words of 1517, 1518 and 1520). So in 1517 he protested against the sale of indulgences, letters of pardon said to cancel punishment for sin, even for the dead (M1, M2, S5: Documented). Condemned by the pope, he would not take back his books before the emperor at Worms in 1521 unless Scripture or clear reason proved him wrong (M5, M7: Documented; "Here I stand" is Contested, D1). Outlawed and hidden, he put the New Testament into German (M9–M11: Documented).
- **Also told:** In 1520 he broke with Rome: he burned the pope's book of church law, and, editors say, the pope's decree against him (B1, B2: Documented for the law book, Widely Accepted for the decree). The whole Bible followed in German by 1534, with colleagues (M12: Widely Accepted, editor's dates). He wrote hymns so that congregations, not only a choir, could sing in their own language, and his churches kept them for centuries (M14, M15, I1: Documented). In 1525 the peasants rose. He first blamed the rulers and warned both sides; then, when the rebels turned to violence, he urged the princes to strike them down. Many of his own friends were offended, and he later condemned the rulers' revenge (P2–P4: Documented, his own writings; the reaction from an editor quoting letters of the time). The same summer he married Katharina von Bora, a former nun (K1: Documented). His short catechism, a book of questions and answers, carried the faith into homes, fathers teaching their households (C1: Documented).
- **Length:** about 3¾ minutes (about 560 words), within Mark's time exception of up to 4 for Luther. Reason: seven beats across nine years (1517–1526), with the force on both sides (the pope's decree, the emperor's ban, and his own call against the peasants) told plainly. Not filled to 4. The essence fills the first 150 words.
- **Setting:** "In 1517" (S3); "Wittenberg, a small university town in Saxony, in eastern Germany" (S2); "a friar of the Augustinian order who taught the Bible and theology there" (S1); "Worms, on the Rhine, where the emperor met the princes of the empire" (S2); "the German lands, where peasants rose against their lords in 1525" (P1); tradition: "the Lutheran churches", "the churches that grew from Wittenberg" (S4). Weave in a clause at a time.
- **Words to explain** (plain word to use instead, or a gloss under about 12 words, worked in where the word is first heard):
  - **friar:** "a member of a religious order, living under vows". Luther was an Augustinian friar (S1, KD 6032).
  - **indulgence / pardon:** "a letter of pardon, bought from church preachers, said to cancel punishment for sin" (S5; KD 2290–2292; W1 1018–1021). Not today's sense of "indulgence" (a treat).
  - **purgatory:** "a place of cleansing after death, as the church then taught" (W1 1020–1021). Say once; it is the preachers' and Luther's word, not the narrator's belief.
  - **theses:** "short statements for a university debate" (S1, KD 2436–2442; W1 448–456, Jacobs). Not a long essay, today's sense.
  - **repentance (Thesis 1):** "turning away from sin and back to God". Thesis 1's Latin "poenitentia" also means penance; keep "repent" (KD 2446–2448).
  - **archbishop of Mainz:** "the chief bishop over many dioceses, a great prince of the church". Luther's letter addresses him as "Primate" (W1 996–997); he held two archbishoprics (W1 711–714, Jacobs). Diocese, if used: "a bishop's district".
  - **St Peter's:** "the pope's great new church in Rome", then being built (W1 706–716).
  - **the pope's bull:** use "a decree from the pope", or "a papal decree, named for its seal". Not "bull" alone.
  - **excommunication:** "being cut off from the church and its sacraments". The 1520 decree threatened it; an editor calls it a warning, not yet the sentence (M5; W3 260–264).
  - **papal law / decretals:** "the popes' book of church law" (W3 5285–5286; CUR 5307).
  - **grace:** "God's free gift, not earned". Luther's own sense in G1–G3.
  - **faith:** "trust in what God promises", his own definition (KD 3315–3316, G2).
  - **justified / righteous:** use "made right with God" (G2, G3).
  - **cardinal:** "one of the pope's senior advisers", for Cajetan (G2).
  - **Diet / imperial assembly:** use "a meeting of the emperor with the princes of the empire" (S2).
  - **the empire / the emperor:** "the Holy Roman Empire, a union of German and central European lands". Not Rome's ancient empire.
  - **safe-conduct:** "the emperor's promise of safe passage there and back" (D6; RTA 39121–39122).
  - **heretic:** "one who holds a belief the church has condemned" (KD 6049–6050).
  - **Edict / outlaw:** "an imperial law"; "outlaw: no one might shelter him; anyone could seize him" (KD 6058–6072).
  - **Patmos:** "the island where John wrote Revelation in exile", Luther's name for his hiding place (CUR 7737).
  - **Mass:** "the main church service, with Communion". Then it was in Latin (M14).
  - **choir:** "the trained singers, clergy and boys, who sang the service". Then the people mostly listened (KD 8309–8310).
  - **councils:** "meetings of bishops that decided church teaching" (Q1).
  - **Christendom:** "all the Christian peoples together" (Q2).
  - **Gospel:** "good news", Luther's own gloss (W6 21041–21043). For the read passages, say "readings from the Gospels and the apostles' letters", not "Gospels and Epistles".
  - **hymnbook in four parts:** "set for four singing voices" (W6 13452).
  - **Communion / the Lord's Supper / words of institution:** "the shared bread and wine"; "Christ's words at the Last Supper" (DK 6540–6543).
  - **laymen:** "ordinary church members, not clergy" (W6 8696).
  - **parish church:** "the town's own church", here Wittenberg's (HY 767–768).
  - **church order:** "a written rulebook for how churches worship and are run" (DK 111–120).
  - **Lutheran:** "the churches that grew from Luther's teaching" (S4).
  - **nun / nunnery, monastery:** "a woman living under vows in a convent"; "a house where monks or friars lived" (CUR 9791–9797).
  - **take the veil:** use "become a nun" (CUR 9795).
  - **catechism:** "a short book of questions and answers on the faith" (SC 51).
  - **Twelve Articles:** "the peasants' twelve written demands" (P1).
  - **princes and lords:** "the rulers of the German lands and the landowners" (P2).
  - **Moravians / Halle:** "a Protestant church of German origin"; "Halle, a Lutheran town in Germany" (I1).
- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker. The question he was answering: how a person is made right with God, and so what a bought pardon can do (G1, G2). The impact is in the readers, singers and households. Trap: a list of books. Show the 1520 writings through one idea only, the free lord and dutiful servant (G3).

- **Openings:**
  (a) **Chosen.** The letter, 31 October 1517. A friar at Wittenberg writes to one of the most powerful churchmen in Germany, the archbishop of Mainz. Pardons for building St Peter's, the pope's new church in Rome, are being sold in the archbishop's name. People believe that once they have bought the letters they are sure of heaven, and that souls fly out of purgatory as soon as coins drop into the box (M1). Paraphrase; about three sentences, then the essence.
  (b) Not chosen for the opening: Worms, 17 April 1521, the crowd so thick that he was led in by a back way, and people climbing onto the roofs to see him (M6). Use it in beat 3.

**Story beats** (beat 1 is the first minute)
1. **The teaching and the protest, 1517–1518.** Opening (a), then the essence. He sent the archbishop theses, short statements for a university debate (M1). Thesis 37 gives the teaching in his words: Q1. Thesis 43: giving to the poor is better than buying pardons (M2). They still allowed pardons within limits and did not deny the pope's power to forgive (M2, Jacobs); he later wrote that he had expected the pope to back him (M3, his later account). Printers spread them fast (M4, Jacobs; Froben's letter of 1519). In 1518, questioned by a cardinal, he answered that no one is right with God except one who trusts God, and that faith is believing what God promises (G2, Documented, paraphrase).
2. **The break, 1520.** He set out his teaching in books that year; show them through one idea only: a Christian is a free lord of all, subject to no one, and a dutiful servant of all, subject to everyone (G3, his own words in translation; paraphrase). In June the pope's decree condemned his writings, ordered them burned, and gave him sixty days to take them back, or be cut off from the church (M5). His books were burned at Louvain and Cologne (M5, Kidd). In July he wrote that if he could get a fire he would burn the popes' whole book of church law (B1). In December he did, outside Wittenberg's gate, with the pope's decree, before students (B2: his own words for the law book; editors for the decree and the scene).
3. **Worms, April 1521.** He came under the emperor's promise of safe passage (D6). Opening (b) here (M6). Asked whether the books were his and whether he would take any back, he asked for time. Next day he said he could not withdraw books even his enemies called useful, nor ones that would strengthen tyranny; he admitted he had been harsher than fitting against some opponents (M7). Then Q2. Then the doubt as a short scene: the words "Here I stand, I cannot do otherwise" appear only in prints from one Wittenberg press; other reports end "God help me. Amen." (D1, Contested). The next day the emperor, Charles V, declared that a single friar stood against a thousand years of Christendom, and that he would stake his lands, his friends, his body and his life on the cause (M8, his own declaration; paraphrase). In May an imperial law made Luther an outlaw: no one was to house or feed him; whoever found him was to seize him; his books were to be burned (M9).
4. **The hidden year and the New Testament, 1521–1522.** He wrote to a friend that he had to let himself be hidden away, though he would rather have died, on the advice of friends (M10). Editors say he was taken to the Wartburg in May 1521 (M10). He called it his Patmos, after the island where John wrote Revelation in exile (M11). There he put the whole New Testament into German. In March 1522 he wrote that he and his colleague Philip were correcting it, that it must be in the simplest language all could understand, and asked for the names of the precious stones in Revelation (M11). It was printed in September 1522; with colleagues he finished the whole Bible by 1534 (M12, Jacobs). "Gospel", he told his readers, means good news, which one sings and tells with joy (M13).
5. **The Peasants' War, 1525.** In spring the peasants of the south set out twelve written demands, offering to drop any that Scripture showed was wrong (P1). Luther first wrote to both sides. To the princes and lords: they alone were to thank for the rebellion, for they did nothing but flay and rob their subjects. To the peasants: whoever takes the sword will perish by the sword (P2). Then armed bands began robbing and burning, and he wrote again, urging rulers to strike the rebels down: Q3 (P3). By the time it spread the war was nearly over, and an editor says the victorious rulers were ruthless, and his tract seemed to defend them (P4, Jacobs). Many of his own friends were offended; one wrote to him that people who favoured him found it strange that he let the tyrants kill without mercy (P4, a letter of the time quoted by the editor). That July he held to his view, but said the victors should show mercy, even to the guilty, and condemned the "furious, raving, senseless tyrants" who after the battle could not get their fill of blood (P4, his Open Letter). An Anabaptist chronicle later said he had leaned on worldly power (P4, a hostile witness). Tell it plainly and credited; say no more about what it did to his churches than this.
6. **The marriage, June 1525.** He married Katharina von Bora, a nun who had fled her convent two years before with others (K1, Currie; Luther's own letter). He wrote to the man who had carried her off that she was returning to a cloister, not to become a nun again but as Dr Luther's wife, in the old empty friary at Wittenberg where he had lived alone (K1). To another he wrote that the change seemed so strange he could hardly believe it himself (K1).
7. **Hymns and the catechism carry it into homes.** He wanted the people to sing in their own language, not only hear a choir (M14, paraphrase of the 1523 order). The first Wittenberg hymnbook came in 1524, with four of its eight hymns his by one editor's count; he set them for four voices to give the young something better than love songs (M15). In 1526 came a German Mass, the main service in German, "for the sake of the simple laymen" (M16); a court composer, his friend, remembered him writing the notes for the sung readings (M16). In 1529 came *Ein feste Burg*, "A mighty fortress is our God" (M17, Bacon's date). In one clause: his short catechism taught fathers how to present the faith to their households (C1).

**What came of him** (about a quarter of the story, as the beats now carry more)
1. **Heaviest: the hymns were kept and sung.** In his lifetime an admirer called him the best Christian song-maker since the apostles (I1, Spangenberg, 1545). Two centuries later the Halle hymnbook still printed his hymns and named "the blessed Luther" among those who built up the church with strong songs; in London in 1754 the Moravians sang his Ten Commandments hymn in English (I1).
2. **Defended:** that a person is made right with God by trust in God's promise, not by bought pardons (G1, G2, Q1); and that conscience is bound by Scripture and clear reason, not by pope or councils alone (Q2).
3. **Worship in the people's own tongue, carried north.** A Danish church order he approved urged that the Lord's Prayer and Christ's words at the Supper be said in Danish, so as not to be ashamed of "the Danish tongue in which we were born" (I2, 1537).
End on 1 or 2, then close on the person (see Ending).

**Quotes**
- **Q1.** KD 2576–2578 (Latin, Thesis 37, 1517): "Quilibet verus Christianus, sive vivus sive mortuus, habet participationem omnium bonorum Christi et ecclesiae, etiam sine literis veniarum, a Deo sibi datam." Rendered from the Latin: "Every true Christian, living or dead, has a share in all the good things of Christ and the church. God gives it to him, even without letters of pardon." Job: the teaching and why pardons were wrong, in one line of his own, in beat 1.
- **Q2.** RTA 36588–36595 (Latin, the *Acta* printed by his side in May 1521): "Nisi convictus fuero testimoniis scripturarum aut ratione evidenti (nam neque papae neque conciliis solis credo, cum constet eos errasse saepius et sibi ipsis contradixisse), victus sum scripturis a me adductis et capta conscientia in verbis dei, revocare neque possum neque volo quicquam, cum contra conscientiam agere neque tutum neque integrum sit. Gott helf mir. Amen." Rendered from the Latin: "Unless I am convinced by the witness of the Scriptures or by clear reason, I cannot and will not take back anything. For I do not trust the pope or councils alone, since it is plain they have often erred and contradicted themselves. I am conquered by the Scriptures I have brought forward, and my conscience is captive to the words of God. For to act against conscience is neither safe nor right. God help me. Amen." Job: the stand, in beat 3. The writer may use the first, third and last sentences alone; keep "alone" in any use of the second.
- **Q3.** W4 11958–11962 (English, Jacobs's translation of *Against the Robbing and Murdering Hordes of Peasants*, May 1525; the German is not held): "Therefore let everyone who can, smite, slay, and stab, secretly or openly, remembering that nothing can be more poisonous, hurtful, or devilish than a rebel." Rendered from the English: "So let everyone who can strike, kill and stab, in secret or in the open. Remember that nothing can be more poisonous, harmful or devilish than a rebel." Job: the force of his call against the peasants, in his own words, in beat 5. Do not soften or cut it.

**Connection to carry into the story:** What passed from Luther was a teaching and its means of carrying. The teaching: forgiveness is God's gift to those who trust his promise, so it cannot be bought (G1–G3, Q1). The means: Scripture and song in the people's own language, and a short catechism for the home (M11, M15, M16, C1). His churches kept the hymns: the Halle hymnbook still printed them two hundred years later, and English Moravians sang one in 1754 (I1). A church order he approved urged Danish at the altar (I2). The listener must understand that this changed who reads and sings in church, not only what was believed; and that 1525 showed the cost of his reliance on rulers, told through what he wrote and what his friends answered (P2–P4). Tell it through what people did, not a "this matters because" sentence.

**Ending:** (a) His 1539 preface: he would gladly have seen all his books forgotten; in putting the Bible into German he had hoped people would read Scripture more and his books less, so that each might "drink for himself from the fresh spring" (M12, W1 260, 280–286). (b) The last stanza of *Ein feste Burg*: "they shall let the Word stand" (M17; plain sense in the narrator's voice; do not tie the hymn's date to Worms, D4). Prefer (a).

**Who tells it**
- Luther's own theses, letters, treatises and prefaces (1517–1539): his words at the time. The 1545 preface and the Table Talk are his later memory; mark them so.
- The *Acta* of Worms: printed in May 1521 by his own side. Say: "the account his friends printed that May".
- Charles V's declaration and the Edict: the emperor's words and the empire's law. The bull: the pope's decree.
- For 1525: his own three writings (P2–P4); the friend's letter is known through an editor who quotes it (P4); the Anabaptist chronicle is hostile and later.
- For the marriage: his own letters; the escape from the convent comes from his letter and an editor (K1).
- Johann Walter: his friend, writing about forty years later. The Halle hymnbook (1730), the Moravian hymnal (1754), the Danish church order (1537): later churches' own books.
- Editors (Kidd, Wrede, Jacobs, Currie, Bacon): dates, the bull's burning, the door. The doubt over "Here I stand" is Wrede's finding; tell it as a scene (beat 3).

**Do not say**
- Any word in "Words to explain" used without its plain sense the first time it is heard (heart test, 2026-11-18).
- Not "he nailed his theses to the church door". Say he sent them to the archbishop (D2).
- Not "Here I stand, I can do no other" as his settled last words (D1).
- Not "faith alone" as the theses' words: the 1517 theses do not use the word "faith" (M2, Jacobs). The teaching is in Thesis 37's "given by God, even without letters of pardon" (Q1), in the 1518 answer (G2), and in the 1520 treatise (G3).
- Not "in his tower he discovered..." or any story of a sudden discovery: his own later account of it is not held (G4).
- Not "he burned the bull" in the narrator's voice as his own testimony: he wrote that he burned the papal law; that the decree burned with it, by the gate, before students, is the editors' (B2).
- Not "excommunicated at Worms" or "the bull excommunicated him": the 1520 decree threatened it; Worms was the emperor's ban (M5, M9; W3 260–264).
- Not that *Ein feste Burg* was written or sung on the road to Worms (D4). Not "the first German Bible" (N1). No span of weeks for the translation.
- Not "his prince kidnapped him" in the narrator's voice (M10). Not "castle".
- Not that the March 1522 letter was written from hiding (M11).
- Not "Tetzel said 'as soon as the coin rings...'" as fact (Thesis 27; Myconius).
- Not that Luther "betrayed" or "sided with" the princes, nor that he "caused" the killings, in the narrator's voice. Tell what he wrote, in order, and credit the reaction to the friend's letter and the editor (P2–P4).
- Not that he "never" called for mercy: in July he said the victors should show grace, even to the guilty (P4). Do not drop this, and do not let it soften Q3: both stand.
- Not a figure for the peasants killed: not held (P4).
- Not "his teaching caused the revolt": the held sources do not say so; the peasants cited the Gospel in their articles (P1), and Luther denied their cause was the Gospel (W4 11898–11900).
- Not that Katharina was "rescued in herring barrels" or any detail of the escape: not held. Say she had left the convent two years before with others, carried off by a man Luther then invited to his home-coming (K1).
- Not that his marriage "scandalized Europe": not held; he wrote that "the outcry" had surely reached his friend (K1).
- Not "the people could now read the Bible" or "every household had a Bible": not held.
- Keep "alone" in "the pope or councils alone" (Q2). Keep "friar", not "monk", for the emperor's word.
- Not "he wrote the tunes" (D5). Not "four of the eight hymns" without "by one editor's count".
