# Outline: Martin Luther

Every line cites `Source_Sheet.md` (S = setting, M = moment, I = impact, D = disagreement, N = not held).

- **The essence:** In 1517 Martin Luther, a friar who taught at Wittenberg, protested against the sale of indulgences (M1, M2: Documented). In 1521, before the emperor at Worms, he refused to take back his books unless Scripture or clear reason proved him wrong (M7: Documented; the words "Here I stand" are Contested, D1). Outlawed and hidden, he put the New Testament into German (M9, M10, M11: Documented).
- **Also told:** He wanted the people to sing in their own language, not only hear a choir (M14: Documented). He wrote hymns, helped make the first Wittenberg hymnbook in 1524 and a German service in 1526 (M15, M16: Documented, his prefaces; four of the book's eight hymns are his by Bacon's count, Widely Accepted). With colleagues he finished the whole Bible in German by 1534 (M12: Widely Accepted, editor's dates). What his churches kept: his hymns were still printed in Halle's hymnbook two centuries later and sung in English by the Moravians (I1: Documented). A church order he approved for Denmark had the Lord's Prayer and the words of the Supper said in Danish (I2: Documented). The Library holds no later church witness that quotes or orders his Bible; that it lasted rests on editors (I5, N1), so the story tells the making of the Bible, and the keeping of the hymns.
- **Length:** about 3 minutes (about 450 words). Reason: three linked events in five years (protest, stand, translation), the force against him (the bull, the emperor, the Edict), a contested ending at Worms that is itself a scene, and the hymns and their later life. The record is full; nothing is padded. The essence fills the first 150 words.
- **Setting:** "In 1517" (S3); "Wittenberg, a small university town in Saxony, in eastern Germany" (S2); "a friar of the Augustinian order who taught the Bible and theology there" (S1); "Worms, on the Rhine, where the emperor met the princes of the empire" (S2); tradition: "the Lutheran churches", "the churches that grew from Wittenberg" (S4). Weave in a clause at a time.
- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker. The question he was answering: what repentance is, and what a bought pardon can do (M2, Th. 1). The impact is in the readers and singers. Trap: a list of books. Show the idea through 1517, Worms and the hymnbook, not titles.
- **Sibling:** `william-tyndale` (batch 5). Tyndale printed his English New Testament at Worms in 1526 (his live story, CEN). This story's job: Luther's stand at Worms in 1521, and the German Bible and hymns. Tyndale's job: his own English Bible and printing. Do not mention Tyndale's printing or his use of Luther here; that is his story's. Also on the tree: `lutheran-wittenberg-and-its-congregations` tells the 1517 theses and the church door; do not retell the door (D2).
- **Openings:**
  (a) **Chosen.** The letter, 31 October 1517. A young friar at Wittenberg writes to the most powerful churchman in Germany, the archbishop of Mainz. Pardons for the building of St Peter's in Rome are being sold in the archbishop's name. People believe that once they have bought the letters they are sure of heaven, and that souls fly out of purgatory as soon as coins drop into the box (M1). Paraphrase; about three sentences, then the essence.
  (b) Not chosen for the opening: Worms, 17 April 1521. The crowd was so thick that he was led in by a back way, and many climbed onto the roofs to see him (M6). Use it in beat 4.

**Story beats** (beat 1 is the first minute)
1. **The letter and the essence.** Opening (a). The pardons, called indulgences, were letters bought from the church's preachers, said to lift the penalties for sin, even for the dead (S5). He sent the archbishop his theses for a debate (M1). Then the essence, pointing ahead in one or two sentences to Worms and the German Bible and hymns. Documented.
2. **What the theses said, and how they spread.** Thesis 1: when Christ said "repent", he meant the whole life of believers to be repentance. Thesis 43: giving to the poor is better than buying pardons. Thesis 86: why does the pope, richer than the richest, not build St Peter's with his own money rather than the poor's? (M2, all Documented, paraphrase.) They did not yet attack the pope, and he later wrote that he had expected the pope to take his side (M2 Jacobs; M3, his own 1545 words, told as his later account). Printers spread his writings fast: within two months the theses were printed in three cities (M4, Jacobs, Widely Accepted); by 1519 a Basel printer was sending six hundred copies of his books to France and Spain and had never sold a book better (M4, Froben's letter, Documented).
3. **Condemned.** In June 1520 the pope's bull condemned his writings, ordered them burned, and gave him sixty days to take them back (M5, Documented). His books were burned in Louvain and Cologne, and he burned the bull at Wittenberg (M5, Kidd, Widely Accepted: credit lightly, "in December 1520").
4. **Worms, April 1521.** He came under the emperor's promise of safe passage (D6). Opening (b) here: the crowd, the back way, people on the roofs (M6). His books were named. Asked whether they were his and whether he would take any back, he asked for time (M6). Next day he said he could not take back books that his own enemies called useful, nor ones that, withdrawn, would strengthen tyranny (M7). Pressed for a plain answer, he gave Q1 (M7). Then the doubt as a short scene: the famous words "Here I stand, I cannot do otherwise" appear only in prints from one Wittenberg press; other reports of the day end with "God help me. Amen." (D1, Contested). The next day the young emperor, Charles V, wrote out his answer: Q2 (M8). In May the Edict made him an outlaw: no one was to house or feed him; whoever found him was to seize him; his books were to be burned (M9, Documented).
5. **Hidden; the New Testament.** He wrote to a friend that he had to let himself be hidden away, though he would rather have died, on the advice of friends (M10, his letter). Editors say he was taken to the Wartburg, a secure hiding place, in May 1521 (M10, Widely Accepted). He called it his Patmos, after John's island of exile (M11). There he put the whole New Testament into German. In March 1522 he wrote that he and Philip were correcting it, that it must be in the simplest language everyone could understand, and asked for the names of the precious stones in Revelation (M11, his letter, Documented). It was printed in September 1522; with colleagues he finished the whole Bible in German by 1534 (M12, Jacobs, Widely Accepted). "Gospel", he told readers, means good news, which one sings and tells with joy (M13).
6. **Worship and song in German.** In 1523 his order for the Mass asked for songs in the people's own language, and called on German poets to write them (Q3; M14, Documented). In 1524 a first Wittenberg hymnbook came out, eight hymns, four of them his by Bacon's count (M15). He set the songs in four parts, he wrote, to give the young something better than love ballads, and wanted music to serve the God who made it (M15, his preface). In 1526 came the German Mass, "for the sake of the simple laymen" (M16). A court composer, his friend, remembered Luther writing the notes for the sung Gospels and Epistles and singing them to him, until the first German Mass was sung in the parish church (M16, Walter's account). In 1529 came *Ein feste Burg*, "A mighty fortress is our God" (M17, Bacon's date).

**What came of him** (about a third of the story)
1. **Heaviest: the hymns were kept and sung.** In his own lifetime an admirer called him the best of all the Christian song-makers since the apostles, without a needless word (I1, Spangenberg, 1545). Two centuries later the Halle hymnbook still printed *Aus tiefer Not*, *Nun freut euch* and *Vom Himmel hoch*, and its preface named "the blessed Luther" among those who built up the church with strong songs (I1). In London in 1754 the Moravians sang his Ten Commandments hymn in English (I1). Documented, each in its witness's voice.
2. **Worship in the people's own tongue, carried north.** The king of Denmark sent his new church order to Luther, and Luther and the Wittenberg teachers approved it (I2). It had the Lord's Prayer and Christ's words at the Supper spoken in Danish, to help the unlearned, so as not to be ashamed of "the Danish tongue in which we were born" (I2, paraphrase). Documented, 1537.
3. **Defended:** that conscience is bound by Scripture and clear reason, not by pope or councils alone (M7, Q1). He said the same in a letter weeks later: the Christian must judge for himself, as he must live and die for himself (I3).
4. **The movement grew while he hid.** At Worms his books were sold openly though pope and emperor had banned them (I4, the Venetian envoy). By July the archbishop of Mainz wrote to Rome that the Lutherans grew every day and hardly any lay people still favoured the clergy (I4). Optional, one sentence.
End on 1 or 3, then close on the person (see Ending).

**Quotes**
- **Q1.** RTA 36588–36595 (Latin, the *Acta* printed by his side in May 1521): "Nisi convictus fuero testimoniis scripturarum aut ratione evidenti (nam neque papae neque conciliis solis credo, cum constet eos errasse saepius et sibi ipsis contradixisse), victus sum scripturis a me adductis et capta conscientia in verbis dei, revocare neque possum neque volo quicquam, cum contra conscientiam agere neque tutum neque integrum sit. Gott helf mir. Amen." Rendered from the Latin: "Unless I am convinced by the witness of the Scriptures or by clear reason, I cannot and will not take back anything. For I do not trust the pope or councils alone, since it is plain they have often erred and contradicted themselves. I am conquered by the Scriptures I have brought forward, and my conscience is captive to the words of God. For to act against conscience is neither safe nor right. God help me. Amen." Job: the stand, in his own words, in beat 4. The writer may use the first, third and last sentences alone; if so, keep "alone" in any use of the second.
- **Q2.** RTA 39105–39108 (French, Charles V's own declaration, 19 April 1521): "car yl est certain que ung seul frère erre en son opinion, laquelle est contre toute la crestiennité, tant du temps passé mille ans et plus que du présent, selon laquelle oppinion toute ladite crestienité seroit et auroit tousiours esté en erreur". Rendered from the French: "For it is certain that a single friar is wrong in his opinion. It goes against all Christendom, for a thousand years and more, and now. By that opinion all Christendom would be in error, and always would have been." Job: what was at stake, in the opposing voice, in beat 4.
- **Q3.** KD 8307–8310 (Latin, *Formula Missae*, 1523), two spans with a cut between: "Cantica velim etiam nobis esse vernacula quam plurima, quae populus sub Missa cantaret, … Quis enim dubitat eas olim fuisse voces totius populi quae nunc solus chorus cantat". Rendered from the Latin: "I would also like us to have as many songs as possible in our own language, for the people to sing during the Mass. … For who doubts that these were once the voices of the whole people, which now the choir alone sings?" Job: why he wrote hymns, in beat 6. The cut leaves out where in the Mass they would be sung.

**Connection to carry into the story:** What passed from Luther was Scripture and song in the people's own language, carried into his churches' worship. Before him, the Mass and its sung parts were in Latin, and a choir sang what he believed had once been the whole people's song (Q3, M14). He made the German New Testament in the plainest words he could (M11), then German hymns and a German service for "the simple laymen" (M15, M16). His churches kept the hymns: the Halle hymnbook still printed them two hundred years later, and English Moravians sang one in 1754 (I1). A church order he approved had Danish spoken at the altar, "not ashamed" of the people's own tongue (I2). The listener must understand that this was a change in who sings and reads in church, not only in what was believed; and that the change was carried by printers, singers and church orders, not by Luther alone. Tell it through what they did.

**Ending:** (a) His 1539 preface: he would gladly have seen all his books forgotten; he had hoped, in putting the Bible into German, that people would read Scripture more and his books less, so that each might "drink for himself from the fresh spring" (M12, W1 260, 280–286, his own words). (b) The last stanza of *Ein feste Burg*: "Das Wort sie sollen lassen stahn" ("they shall let the Word stand"), the Word he would not take back at Worms (M17; plain sense in the narrator's voice; do not tie the hymn's date to Worms, D4). Prefer (a).

**Who tells it**
- Luther's own letters, theses and prefaces (1517–1539): his own words at the time. The 1545 preface and the Table Talk are his words looking back; mark them as his later memory.
- The *Acta* of Worms: printed in May 1521 by his own side (RTA 35675–35700). Say: "the account his friends printed that May".
- Charles V's declaration (19 April 1521): the emperor's own words. The Edict (May 1521): the empire's law.
- The bull (1520): the pope's decree.
- Johann Walter: Luther's friend, writing about forty years later.
- The Halle hymnbook (1730), the Moravian hymnal (1754), the Danish church order (1537): later churches' own books.
- Editors (Kidd 1911, Wrede 1896, Jacobs, Bacon): dates and the door; credit as "historians" where needed. The doubt over "Here I stand" is Wrede's finding; tell it as a scene (beat 4), not as a stock how-we-know sentence.

**Do not say**
- Not "he nailed his theses to the church door". The Library holds no witness from 1517; the door is told on the Wittenberg entry as a later tradition (D2). Say he sent them to the archbishop.
- Not "Here I stand, I can do no other" as his settled last words. Say the words appear only in prints from one Wittenberg press, and other reports end "God help me. Amen." (D1).
- Not that *Ein feste Burg* was written or sung on the road to Worms. Bacon dates it to 1529 (D4). Not "the battle hymn of the Reformation" as a source's phrase.
- Not "the first German Bible". Earlier German Bibles are not in the Library either way (N1); say he put the New Testament, then the whole Bible, into German.
- Not "he translated the New Testament in eleven weeks" or any span of weeks: not held. Say he did it in hiding, and it was printed in September 1522.
- Not "his prince kidnapped him" in the narrator's voice. Luther says he followed his friends' advice to be hidden (M10); editors say he was taken to the Wartburg. Not "castle": the held texts call it a hiding place.
- Not that the theses attacked the pope or taught faith alone. They did not use the word "faith" and allowed indulgences within limits (M2, Jacobs); he still expected the pope's support (M3).
- Not "Tetzel said 'as soon as the coin rings, the soul springs'" as fact. Thesis 27 says certain preachers say it; a hearer, Myconius, wrote later that Tetzel did (KD 2349ff.). If used, credit it.
- Not "excommunicated at Worms". The bull of 1520 threatened him; Worms was the emperor's ban, the Edict (M5, M9).
- Keep "alone" in "the pope or councils alone" (Q1). Keep "friar", not "monk", for Q2 ("frère").
- Not "he wrote the tunes". Say he wrote hymns; for the German Mass, credit the friend who saw him write the notes (D5, M16).
- Not "four of the eight hymns" without "by one editor's count" or similar (M15, Bacon).
- Not "the people could now read the Bible" or "every household had a Bible": not held. Say he wanted it in language all could understand (M11).
- Not that his churches "kept his Bible" as a sourced fact; the Library holds only editors for its later life (I5).
- Do not give the Edict's force as "banned" only. Say no one was to house or feed him, and whoever found him was to seize him (M9).
