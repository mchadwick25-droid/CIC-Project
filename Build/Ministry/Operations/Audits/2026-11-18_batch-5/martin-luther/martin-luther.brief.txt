On the last day of October 1517, a friar, a man living under religious vows, wrote to the archbishop of Mainz, a great prince of the church. Pardons, letters said to cancel punishment for sin, were being sold in the archbishop's name to build St Peter's, the pope's new church in Rome, and people believed that a pardon made them sure of heaven.

The friar was Martin Luther, who taught at the small university in Wittenberg, in eastern Germany. He taught that forgiveness is grace, God's free gift, received by faith, by trusting what God promises, so it could not be bought. Condemned by the pope, he would not take back his books before the emperor, ruler of the Holy Roman Empire, a union of German and central European lands, unless Scripture, the Bible, or clear reason proved him wrong. Outlawed and hidden, he put the New Testament, the gospels and letters of the early church, into German, and the Lutheran churches, grown from his teaching, carry his name.

With his letter he sent theses, short statements for a university debate, and one put his teaching in a line: "Every true Christian, living or dead, has a share in all the good things of Christ and the church. God gives it to him, even without letters of pardon."

In 1520 a decree from the pope threatened to cut him off from the church. That November he wrote that a Christian is a free lord of all, subject to no one, and a dutiful servant of all, subject to everyone. In December he burned the popes' book of church law, editors say with the decree, and in January he was cut off.

In April 1521, under the emperor's promise of safe passage, he came to Worms, on the Rhine. His friends printed his answer: "Unless I am convinced by the witness of the Scriptures or by clear reason, I cannot and will not take back anything." It ends, "God help me. Amen." The famous "Here I stand, I cannot do otherwise" appears only in prints from one Wittenberg press.

In May the emperor, Charles V, made him an outlaw by imperial law: no one was to house or feed him, and whoever found him was to seize him. He wanted the people to sing in their own language too, not only to hear trained singers, and by one editor's count four of the eight hymns in the first Wittenberg hymnbook, in 1524, were his.

In 1525 peasants across the German lands rose against their lords. Luther first warned both sides, telling the rulers, and above all the bishops, that they did nothing but rob their people, and the peasants that whoever takes the sword will perish by the sword. Then armed bands began robbing and burning, and he urged rulers, and anyone who could, to strike them down: "So let everyone who can strike, kill and stab, in secret or in the open. Remember that nothing can be more poisonous, harmful or devilish than a rebel."

An editor says the winning rulers were ruthless, and his tract seemed to defend them. Many of his friends were offended. That June he married Katharina von Bora, a former nun, who had lived under vows in a convent. In July he held to his call, but said the victors should show mercy even to the guilty, and he condemned the "furious, raving, senseless tyrants" who could not get their fill of blood. A later, hostile chronicle of the Anabaptists, a church of believers baptized as adults, said he had leaned on worldly power.

His short catechism, a book of questions and answers on the faith, taught fathers to teach their households, and by 1534, with colleagues, he had finished the whole Bible in German. His churches kept his songs, and two centuries later the hymnbook of Halle, a Lutheran town in Germany, still printed them.

In 1539 he wrote that in putting the Bible into German he had hoped for less writing and more reading of Scripture, so that each might "drink for himself from the fresh spring".
