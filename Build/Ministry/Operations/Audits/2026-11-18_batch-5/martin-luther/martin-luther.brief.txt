On the last day of October 1517, a friar, a man living under religious vows, wrote from Wittenberg to the archbishop of Mainz, a great prince of the church. In the archbishop's name, preachers were selling pardons, letters said to cancel punishment for sin, to help build St Peter's, the pope's great new church in Rome. People believed, he wrote, that a pardon made them sure of heaven, and that souls flew out of purgatory, a place of cleansing after death as the church then taught, as soon as their coins dropped into the box.

The friar was Martin Luther, and he taught theology at the small university in Wittenberg, in Saxony, in eastern Germany. He taught that forgiveness is grace, God's free gift, not earned or bought, and that it is received by faith, by trusting what God promises. So he protested against the pardons. When the pope condemned him, he stood before the emperor, the ruler of the Holy Roman Empire, a union of German and central European lands. He would not take back his books unless Scripture, the Bible, or clear reason proved him wrong. Outlawed and hidden, he put the New Testament, the gospels and letters of the early church, into German. The Lutheran churches, the churches that grew from his teaching, carry his name.

With his letter he sent theses, short statements for a university debate. One gave his teaching in a single line: "Every true Christian, living or dead, has a share in all the good things of Christ and the church. God gives it to him, even without letters of pardon." Another said that giving to the poor is better than buying pardons. The theses still allowed pardons within limits, and he later wrote that he had expected the pope to back him. Printers spread them fast, and in 1518, when a cardinal, one of the pope's senior advisers, questioned him, he answered that no one is made right with God except one who trusts God.

In June 1520 a decree from the pope condemned his writings, ordered them burned, and gave him sixty days to take them back or be cut off from the church. That November he wrote that a Christian is a free lord of all, subject to no one, and a dutiful servant of all, subject to everyone. In December he burned the popes' book of church law, as he wrote himself; editors add that the decree went into the fire with it, outside Wittenberg's gate, before students. In January a second decree cut him off from the church.

In April 1521 he came to Worms, on the Rhine, where the emperor met the princes of the empire, with the emperor's promise of safe passage there and back. The crowd was so thick that he was led in by a back way, and people climbed onto the roofs to see him. Asked whether he would take back his books, he asked for time. The next day he admitted he had been harsher than fitting against some opponents, but the account his friends printed that May gives his answer: "Unless I am convinced by the witness of the Scriptures or by clear reason, I cannot and will not take back anything." It ends, "God help me. Amen."

And there lies a doubt. The famous words "Here I stand, I cannot do otherwise" appear only in prints from one Wittenberg press, while other reports end with that short prayer.

The next day the emperor, Charles V, declared that a single friar was in error against all Christendom, all the Christian peoples together, of the last thousand years. In May an imperial law made Luther an outlaw: no one was to house or feed him, whoever found him was to seize him, and his books were to be burned.

He wrote to a friend that, on his friends' advice, he had to let himself be hidden away, though he would rather have died. Editors say he was taken to the Wartburg, which he called his Patmos, after the island where John wrote Revelation, the Bible's last book, in exile. There he put the whole New Testament into German. In March 1522 he wrote that he and his colleague Philip were correcting it, that it must be in the simplest language all could understand, and he asked for the names of the precious stones in Revelation. It was printed that September.

He wanted the people to sing in their own language, not only to listen to the choir, the trained singers who sang the service. The first Wittenberg hymnbook came in 1524, and by one editor's count four of its eight hymns were his. They were set for four singing voices, he wrote, to give the young something better than love songs.

In the spring of 1525 peasants across the German lands rose against their lords. In the south they set out twelve written demands, offering to drop any that Scripture showed was wrong. Luther first wrote to both sides. The rulers and the landowners, and above all the bishops and clergy, had only themselves to thank, he told them, for they did nothing but rob and skin their people. To the peasants he wrote that whoever takes the sword will perish by the sword.

Then armed bands began robbing and burning, and he wrote again, urging rulers, and anyone who could, to strike them down: "So let everyone who can strike, kill and stab, in secret or in the open. Remember that nothing can be more poisonous, harmful or devilish than a rebel." An editor says that by the time the tract spread the war was nearly over, that the winning rulers were ruthless, and that his tract seemed to defend them. Many of his friends were offended. One wrote to him that people who favored him found it strange that he let the tyrants kill without mercy.

That June, as the war was ending, he married Katharina von Bora, a nun, a woman who had lived under vows in a convent, until she left it two years before with others. He wrote to the man who had carried her off that she was returning to a convent, not to become a nun again but as Dr Luther's wife, in the empty friars' house at Wittenberg where he had lived alone. To another he wrote that the change seemed so strange he could hardly believe it himself.

In July Luther held to his word: rulers should strike the rebel bands quickly, guilty or innocent, but once they had won, they should show mercy even to the guilty. He condemned the "furious, raving, senseless tyrants" who after the battle could not get their fill of blood. A later chronicle of the Anabaptists, a church of believers baptized as adults and hostile to him, said he had leaned on worldly power.

In 1526 came a German Mass, the main church service with its shared bread and wine, until then held in Latin, now in German "for the sake of the simple laymen", ordinary church members, not clergy. His short catechism, a book of questions and answers on the faith, taught fathers how to teach their households. By 1534, with colleagues, he had finished the whole Bible in German.

His churches kept his songs. In his lifetime an admirer called him the best Christian song-maker since the apostles, Christ's first followers. Two centuries later the hymnbook of Halle, a Lutheran town in Germany, still printed his hymns and named "the blessed Luther" among those who built up the church with strong songs. In London in 1754 the Moravians, a church begun in Saxony by refugees from Bohemia, printed his hymn on the Ten Commandments in English.

In 1539 he wrote that he would gladly have seen all his books forgotten. In putting the Bible into German, he said, he had hoped for less writing and more reading of Scripture, so that each might "drink for himself from the fresh spring".
