# Second, independent renderings (Q1 to Q21)

## Q1
"begotten, not made."

(a) "Begotten" is a fixed creed term and should stay. It means "born of" or "fathered by," as opposed to created. No plain word carries the creed's sense exactly, so a reader may need it introduced nearby, as in "begotten (born of the Father), not made."
(b) The Greek behind it is γεννηθέντα, οὐ ποιηθέντα. Pusey's line is the standard rendering, and the nuance matches. Pusey prints it without the comma. Modern usage adds one.

## Q2
"He became human so that we might become God."

(a) "Made man": in 1892 "man" here means "a human being," not "a male." "Made God" is the strong patristic claim of deification. It is not "made gods" or "made godlike." Keep "God" and do not soften it.
(b) The endnote gives the Greek θεοποιηθῶμεν, "that we might be deified / made divine." The first verb is passive in English ("was made man"), and the Greek is probably ἐνηνθρώπησεν, which is active (it is not shown in the file): "he became human." "Became" is closer than "was made" for the first clause. For the second, "be made God" keeps the passive, which shows that God does the deifying. An alternative is "so that we might be made God," which keeps the passive exactly. Either works. "Made divine" is acceptable, but it is weaker than the Greek.

## Q3
"our blessed and highly honoured Father Athanasius."

(a) "All-celebrated" means "praised by all," "renowned," or "highly honoured." "Blessed" is an honorific for a holy person who has died. It does not mean "lucky."
(b) Pusey's Greek is probably πανευφήμου, "praised by everyone." "Highly honoured" or "praised by all" both fit. "Famous" would be too secular. "Father" is a title for an authoritative church teacher, not a family relation.

## Q4
"By this, conquer."

Or "Conquer by this." This is already plain. It could stay unchanged.
(a) "This" points to the sign of the cross. "Conquer" can also mean "win" or "be victorious."
(b) The Greek is τούτῳ νίκα, best known in Latin as "in hoc signo vinces." The Greek is a short command, "By this, win." The Latin adds "sign" and makes it a future promise ("you will conquer"). Eusebius's own Greek is a command, not a promise. Keep it a command.

## Q5
"your fellow servant"

(a) Constantine uses this for himself. He means a fellow servant of God, the same as the bishops he is writing to. He does not mean a household servant. It is a humble style. It does not literally give up his rank.
(b) The Greek is probably ὁ συνθεράπων, "fellow servant," a term of church humility. The plain reading holds. The hyphen is dated, and modern English writes "fellow servant."

## Q6
"of one being with the Father,"

Or keep the footnote's own gloss: "of the same essence."
(a) "Essence" here means "being," "substance," or "what God is." It does not mean "the core idea" or "perfume." "Consubstantial" in the creed line translates the same Greek word.
(b) The Greek is ὁμοούσιον, "of the same being (οὐσία)." The span is the footnote's own gloss, "of the same essence." That is accurate and plain enough. If it stays as printed, mark it unchanged. "Of one being" is the common modern liturgical form. Do not render it "similar." That would be ὁμοιούσιος, the rival term.

## Q7
"the first of the emperors to become zealous for the Church."

(a) "Zealous" means "eager," "devoted," or "keen in support." "Began to be zealous" means he was the first emperor who took up this devotion. It does not mean he only half-started.
(b) The Greek is Sozomen's. It is not shown in the file. Nothing here suggests a different nuance.

## Q8
"Look, I will take this on for you myself. I will run the hostel."

As one sentence: "Look, for your sake I am making myself the keeper of the guesthouse."
(a) "Hospitaller" means a person who runs a house of care for strangers, the poor, and the sick. Here it is a famine relief house where he nursed and buried people. It is not a hotel keeper. "Relief house," "guesthouse," and "hospice" are all near. "On your behalf" can mean "for your sake" or "in your place / as your agent." The context supports both: the rich do not trust anyone else to handle their money.
(b) The underlying Greek is probably ξενοδόχος, "one who receives strangers." It is not shown in the file. Clarke's word is a little archaic, but it is exact. A modern rendering should keep the sense of care for strangers and the sick, not only lodging.

## Q9
"It defiled my pure ears."

(a) "Tainted" can mean "defiled," "polluted," "stained," or "corrupted." The image is ritual or moral impurity, not physical damage.
(b) This translates Ephrem's Syriac. The Syriac is not shown. The Syriac verb is probably one of defilement (ṭammeʾ), so "defiled" is a little closer than "tainted." Either is acceptable.

## Q10
"We seemed to have one soul living in two bodies."

(a) "Inhabiting" means "living in" or "dwelling in." "Soul" is the inner life or self.
(b) Gregory's Greek echoes the classical saying about friendship (one soul in two bodies, said of Aristotle). The English keeps that. There is no different nuance.

## Q11
"Why should I fight over piglets and chickens, and not even my own,"

(a) "Sucking pigs" means "suckling pigs," or "piglets." "Fowls" means "chickens" or "poultry." "Those not my own" means the animals belong to other people.
(b) The Greek is not shown. Gregory is mocking a quarrel over the revenue and tolls of a mountain pass. Keep the scorn: these are trivial things.

## Q12
"as if I were fighting for souls and church law?"

Or "as though they were souls and canons?" Keep the fragment's place after Q11.
(a) "Canons" here means "church laws" or "rules of the Church." It does not mean weapons, and it does not mean the list of scripture books. "Souls" means people in his pastoral care.
(b) The Greek is probably κανόνων, "canons / rules." The English is exact. "Church rules" makes it plain for a modern reader.

## Q13
"keeping equally far from Sabellius, who merges the Persons into one, and from Arius, who splits them apart,"

Closer to the printed form: "staying equally far from the merging of Sabellius and from the splitting of Arius,"
(a) "Confusion" here is the technical sense: blending or fusing things together into one. It does not mean "bewilderment." "Division" means splitting into separate, unequal parts.
(b) The Greek is probably σύγχυσις (fusion) and διαίρεσις (division). These are technical terms. "Merging" and "splitting" carry them plainly. The fuller rendering adds a short gloss of each error, so it slightly expands the clause. The closer form adds nothing.

## Q14
"who taught me the Scriptures,"

As a fragment in place: "and my teacher in the Scriptures,"
(a) "Instructor" means "teacher." Jerome is the speaker, so he is claiming Gregory as his teacher.
(b) Jerome's Latin is "praeceptor meus, quo Scripturas explanante didici" ("my teacher, from whose explaining of the Scriptures I learned"). It is not shown in the file. The Latin stresses that Gregory explained the Scriptures to him. "My teacher in the Scriptures" covers it.

## Q15
"and the dispute with Eunomius was handed down to me."

Active, in the same place: "and I took over the dispute with Eunomius as my inheritance,"
(a) "Legacy" means something passed on after a death. Here Basil had died, and Gregory inherited Basil's unfinished argument. "Controversy" means "dispute" or "argument." It does not mean "scandal."
(b) "Eunomius' controversy" is ambiguous in English. It means the dispute *against* Eunomius, not a dispute that Eunomius owned. The Greek is not shown here, but the context makes this clear. A modern rendering must say "with Eunomius" or "against Eunomius."

## Q16
"is above every name."

Unchanged.
(a) "Name" here carries the biblical sense of a name as expressing what a thing is. "Above" means "beyond" or "higher than."
(b) This echoes Philippians 2:9 ("the name which is above every name"). Here Gregory applies it to the divine nature, which no name can capture. Keep the wording so the echo stays.

## Q17
"equal in rank to the Father and the Son."

(a) "Rank" means "honour," "dignity," or "standing." It does not mean military or social rank. Do not read it as order in sequence.
(b) The Greek is probably ὁμότιμον, "of equal honour." "Equal in honour" may be closer than "of the same rank." Both say equal standing with no difference.

## Q18
"Glory to God for everything."

Unchanged in substance. The printed form is already plain, and "for all things" can stay.
(a) None.
(b) The Greek is Δόξα τῷ θεῷ πάντων ἕνεκεν. It is not shown in the file. The English is exact. Note that in the printed sentence only "for all things" sits inside the quotation marks. The narrator supplies "gave glory to God."

## Q19
"with the same parts, unharmed and immortal, while sin is completely destroyed."

(a) "Parts" can mean "members," "limbs," or "body parts." The point is that the same body rises, not a new one. "Uninjured" means "unharmed" or "undamaged." "Utterly and entirely" is a doubled phrase for emphasis. "Completely and entirely" or "wholly" covers it. Dropping one word loses only the doubling, not the sense.
(b) This Methodius passage survives in Greek through Epiphanius. The Greek is not shown in the file. The underlying word for "parts" is probably μέλη, "members," the same body.

## Q20
"Be careful not to change your mind because of us."

(a) "Take heed" means "be careful" or "watch out." "Change your mind" here means giving up his resolve to die as a martyr. "On our account" means "because of us." Origen means his mother and brothers, the family left behind.
(b) The Greek is probably ἐπέχε, μὴ δι' ἡμᾶς ἄλλο τι φρονήσῃς, "watch that you do not think otherwise for our sake." It is not shown in the file. The English is faithful.

## Q21
"the hardworking Origen"

Or "the diligent Origen" / "the tireless scholar Origen."
(a) "Labour-loving" means "hardworking," "diligent," or "industrious." Used of a scholar, it praises tireless study.
(b) The endnote gives the Greek φιλοπόνου, literally "lover of toil." It was a common compliment for a learned writer. "Hardworking" or "diligent" both keep it. The note says the word appears elsewhere in Athanasius, so it is a set term of praise, not irony.
