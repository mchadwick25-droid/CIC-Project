# First renderings: Q1 to Q21

## Q1
Unchanged: "begotten, not made."

(a) "begotten" keeps its credal sense: the Son comes forth from the Father's own being, as a son from a father, and is not a created thing. "made" means created. Do not replace either with "born" or "produced".
(b) The Greek column in this file is OCR-garbled at this point ("ἘΝ Ξ ... δ᾽ N¥*"), so it can't be compared. The standard Nicene Greek is γεννηθέντα οὐ ποιηθέντα, and the printed English follows it exactly. The printed comma is missing ("Begotten not made"). Adding it changes no sense.

## Q2
"He became human so that we might be made God."

(a) "made man": the man here is ἄνθρωπος, meaning "human being", not "male". "Became human" is the exact sense. "Made God": see (b).
(b) The endnote gives the Greek as θεοποιηθῶμεν, "that we might be deified", "made divine". Athanasius means sharing in God by grace, not becoming God by essence. "Be made God" keeps the force of the Greek word. "Become divine" is an acceptable gentler alternative, but it softens the statement, and the strong wording is the point of the quotation.

## Q3
"our blessed Father Athanasius, praised by all."

(a) "all-celebrated" = praised by everyone, renowned everywhere. If word order must stay fixed, use "our blessed and universally praised Father Athanasius."
(b) The Greek isn't shown at this point in the file. The epithet probably stands for πανεύφημος, "praised by all", which fits this rendering. That reading is not verified against the file.

## Q4
Unchanged: "Conquer by this,"

(a) "Conquer" = win the victory, overcome. "Win by this" is an alternative, but "Conquer by this" is already plain modern English.
(b) The file shows no original. Eusebius' Greek is usually given as τούτῳ νίκα, "by this, conquer". The well-known Latin "in hoc signo vinces" ("in this sign you will conquer") is a later form and should not be put in its place. Not verified against the file.

## Q5
Unchanged: "your fellow servant,"

(a) "fellow-servant" = one who serves the same master alongside you. Here Constantine means a fellow servant of God, speaking to Alexander and Arius. It doesn't mean a household servant. Modern spelling drops the hyphen.
(b) None shown in the file.

## Q6
Unchanged: "of the same essence,"

(a) "essence" = what a thing is, its very being. In this creed it means the same thing as "substance" in the line above ("of the substance of the Father"). It must not be weakened to "of the same kind" or "similar".
(b) The phrase is the editor's gloss on the Greek ὁμοούσιον, which the endnote shows. The main text renders the same word as "consubstantial". Both are correct for ὁμοούσιος. "Of the same essence" is the plainer form of the two.

## Q7
"the first of the emperors who began to be zealous for the Church."
Plainer alternative: "the first emperor who began to show zeal for the Church."

(a) "zealous" = eagerly devoted, working hard for. It's still current English, so it is kept. "Began to be" is kept because Sozomen says he was the first to start this, not only the first to be it.
(b) None shown in the file. The printed English translates Sozomen's Greek.

## Q8
"Look, I appoint myself, on your behalf, to run the shelter."

(a) "hospitaller" = the person in charge of a house that takes in strangers, the poor and the sick. The context shows him setting up 300 beds and nursing famine victims. Possible senses: keeper of a guest-house, keeper of a hospital, keeper of a shelter for the needy. Avoid "hospice", whose modern sense (end-of-life care) misleads. "On your behalf" = acting for you, as your trusted agent with your money. It could also mean "for your sake"; context favours "acting for you".
(b) No Greek is shown. Palladius' word is likely ξενοδόχος, "one who receives strangers", which fits "run the shelter". Not verified against the file.

## Q9
"It made my pure ears unclean."
Alternative: "It soiled my pure ears."

(a) "tainted" = stained, polluted, made impure (a moral and ritual uncleanness, not damage to hearing). "Defiled" is the closest single word, but it is harder for a B2 reader.
(b) The printed English translates Ephrem's Syriac, which the file does not show. The likely Syriac verb carries the sense of defiling or polluting, which fits "unclean". Not verified.

## Q10
"We seemed to have one soul living in two bodies."

(a) "inhabiting" = living in, dwelling in. Swapping "inhabiting" for "living in" is the only change.
(b) The printed English translates Gregory's Greek, which the file doesn't show. The image echoes the classical saying that a friend is "one soul in two bodies".

## Q11
"Why should I fight over piglets and chickens, and ones that are not even mine,"

(a) "sucking pigs" = suckling pigs, young piglets still nursing. "fowls" = poultry, farm birds, mainly chickens. "for" = over, for the sake of. "even" is not in the printed text. It only carries the tone of "and those not my own". Drop it if strict wording is wanted: "and ones that are not mine,".
(b) None shown. Context: the dispute with Anthimus was over road tolls and church revenues (the seized mules). Gregory makes them sound petty on purpose.

## Q12
"as if they were souls and church canons?"
Alternative fitting the same slot: "as if I were fighting for souls and church law?"

(a) "canons" = church rules, the laws of councils. Here it may also point to canonical rights, a bishop's lawful jurisdiction. That was what the quarrel over the province was about. Senses: church rules / church law / canonical jurisdiction.
(b) None shown in the file.

## Q13
"equally far from Sabellius's merging and from Arius's dividing,"
Fuller alternative: "equally far from the way Sabellius merges God into one and the way Arius divides Him,"

(a) "confusion" is technical here: it means fusing or blending into one, so that Father, Son and Spirit are no longer distinct. It does not mean bewilderment, which is its usual modern sense and would mislead. "division" = splitting apart into unequal beings. The next sentence in the source ("fuse God together, or cut Him up into inequality") confirms both senses. The fuller alternative takes "God" from that sentence. It adds a word, but no new meaning.
(b) The printed English translates Gregory's Greek, not shown. The likely Greek terms are σύγχυσις (blending, mixing together) and διαίρεσις (division). Not verified against the file.

## Q14
"my teacher in the Scriptures,"

(a) "instructor" = teacher. It means someone who taught him personally, not only an author he read.
(b) This is Jerome (De viris illustribus), translated from Latin, which the file does not show. From memory, the Latin reads roughly "praeceptor meus, quo Scripturas explanante didici", "my teacher, from whose explaining of the Scriptures I learned". That says a little more than the printed English: Gregory explained the Scriptures and Jerome learned from that. This should be checked against a Latin text before it is relied on.

## Q15
"inherited the dispute with Eunomius."

(a) "received the legacy of" = took over, as an heir, something left behind by someone who died (here Basil). "Eunomius' controversy" = the controversy with Eunomius, the fight against his teaching. It doesn't mean a controversy that belonged to Eunomius. "dispute" and "controversy" are interchangeable, and "controversy" can be kept.
(b) Translated from Gregory's Greek, not shown in the file.

## Q16
Unchanged: "is above every name."

(a) "above" = beyond, higher than, surpassing. Every name falls short of it. This echoes Philippians 2:9.
(b) Not shown in the file. The Greek is probably ὑπὲρ πᾶν ὄνομα, as in Philippians.

## Q17
Unchanged: "of the same rank as the Father and the Son."

(a) "rank" keeps its technical sense: equal standing, honour and dignity within the Godhead, with no lower place. It doesn't mean a military or social rank. The rest of the sentence makes this clear: "no difference between them in anything... that devotion can ascribe to a Divine nature".
(b) The Greek isn't shown. The underlying word is likely ὁμότιμος, "of equal honour", which the printed "same rank" translates. "Equal in honour" would be the more literal option. Not verified against the file.

## Q18
Unchanged: "Glory to God for all things."

(a) "for all things" = for everything, in every circumstance. It has no narrower sense.
(b) In this printed source only "for all things" is inside quotation marks. "Glory to God" is the editor's paraphrase ("he gave glory to God"). The full saying comes from Palladius' Dialogue. Its usual Greek form is Δόξα τῷ Θεῷ πάντων ἕνεκεν, which the file does not show. The full rendering is still exact.

## Q19
"with the same parts, unharmed and immortal, while sin is completely and entirely destroyed."

(a) "uninjured" = unharmed, undamaged, whole. "the same parts" = the same members of the body. The point is bodily identity in the resurrection. "utterly and entirely" is a doubled intensifier, and both words are kept.
(b) Translated from Methodius' Greek, not shown in the file.

## Q20
"Be careful not to change your mind because of us."

(a) "Take heed" = be careful, make sure. "change your mind" = turn back from his decision to stand firm toward martyrdom. "on our account" = because of us, for our sake (his wife and children left behind).
(b) Translated from the Greek of Eusebius, not shown in the file. The endnote says this is the only surviving sentence of the letter.

## Q21
"the hard-working Origen"

(a) "labour-loving" = hard-working, painstaking, devoted to study and effort. A scholarly diligence is meant. The sense is "industrious" or "studious", not physical labour.
(b) The endnote gives the Greek as φιλοπόνου (φιλόπονος, "loving toil"). "Hard-working" matches it. "Diligent" or "studious" are acceptable alternatives that bring out the scholarly side.
