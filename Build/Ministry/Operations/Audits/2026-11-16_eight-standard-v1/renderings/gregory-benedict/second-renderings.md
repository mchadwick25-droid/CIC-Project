# Second, independent renderings: G1 to G12

## G1

"all in ruins, wrecked by the fire."

(a) "ruined and destroyed" is a pair; the Latin is a pair too, so both are kept. "the fire": Dalton's article points back to a fire told of earlier. The Latin has only "ab incendio" (by fire), with no article.
(b) Latin (HF X.31): "ab incendio dissolutam dirutamque nanctus sum", "I found it broken apart and torn down by fire." "dissoluta" means broken apart, coming to pieces; "diruta" means pulled down or wrecked. "all" is Dalton's word, not in the Latin, but it is in the printed span and is kept.

## G2

"Alas for our times, because the study of letters has died out among us,"

(a) "letters" means literary learning: reading, writing and composing with skill. It does not mean correspondence. "Woe to" is a cry of lament, rendered "Alas for". "our day" means our age, not one day.
(b) Latin (HF, preface): "Vae diebus nostris, quia periit studium litterarum a nobis". "diebus nostris" is plural, "our days", so "our times". "periit" is "has perished" or "has died out". "studium" is study or the pursuit of learning.

## G3

"so that no reader may doubt that I hold the catholic faith,"

(a) "whoso reads" means anyone who reads. "catholic" here, lower-case, means the orthodox faith of the creeds, set against the heretics Gregory names just before. It does not name a modern denomination.
(b) Latin (HF I, preface): "ut qui legerit me non dubitet esse catholicum", literally "so that whoever reads may not doubt that I am a catholic". Dalton's "hold the catholic faith" fits the sense. The "that" at the start means "so that" (purpose), and the rendering makes this explicit.

## G4

"This is what I want: that you believe this, and so do the other teachers of the Church."

(a) "doctors" means teachers, in the old sense; it does not mean physicians. "such be thy faith" means "believe in this way".
(b) Latin (HF V.44): "Sic, inquid, volo, ut tu vel reliqui doctores eclesiarum credatis", literally "Thus I want you and the other teachers of the churches to believe." "eclesiarum" is plural, "of the churches"; Dalton's singular "the Church" is kept because the plural changes nothing a reader needs. "credatis" is a verb, "believe", not a noun "faith". "Sic" (thus, in this way) refers to the doctrine just read out.

## G5

"what Hilary and Eusebius taught."

(a) "that which" is simply "what". Hilary is Hilary of Poitiers. Dalton marks Eusebius with a note; he is usually taken as Eusebius of Vercelli. This rendering adds neither name to the text.
(b) Latin (HF V.44): "quod Hilarius Eusebiusque docuerunt", "what Hilary and Eusebius taught". It matches exactly. The fragment stays one item in Gregory's list.

## G6

"So the king gave up his plan."

(a) "desisted from his purpose" means stopped pursuing his aim.
(b) Latin (HF V.44): "Et sic rex ab hac intentione quievit", literally "And so the king rested from this intention". The index of the edition itself glosses "quiescere" here as "abstinere", to hold back from. "this" (hac) points to his plan to impose his doctrine of the Trinity; "his" is a fair rendering.

## G7

"the books of that revered man Gregory, bishop of Tours."

(a) "reverend man" means a man worthy of reverence. It is not the modern clerical title "the Reverend".
(b) The Latin of Paul the Deacon is not among the files I was given. The usual Latin form "viri reverendi" would mean "of the reverend man"; I have not checked this against a held text.

## G8

"You do much evil every day."

(a) "wickedness" is evil deeds. "daily commit" means do every day.
(b) The Latin of the Dialogues is not among the files I was given, so I could not check it. Gardner prints a revision of an early English version (1608). From memory, the Latin reads "Multa mala facis, multa fecisti", "You do many evil things; you have done many". If so, "daily" belongs to the English translator, not the Latin. This should be checked against a Latin text before the rendering is relied on.

## G9

"a school for the service of the Lord. In setting it up, we hope to lay down nothing harsh and nothing heavy."

(a) "ordained" means laid down as a rule, not ordained as clergy. "burdensome" means heavy to bear; rendered "heavy".
(b) Latin (Rule, Prologue): "Constituenda est ergo nobis dominici schola servitii; in qua institutione nihil asperum, nihil grave nos constituturos speramus." "dominici ... servitii" is "of the Lord's service", so "the Lord" is closer than "God". "in qua institutione" is "in this founding" or "in this setting-up", so "in setting it up" keeps that sense. The Latin repeats "nihil": "nothing harsh, nothing heavy", kept. "schola" here means a place of learning and training. A full stop is used because the clause after "in which" is long. If the span must stay one sentence, read: "a school for the service of the Lord, and in setting it up we hope to lay down nothing harsh and nothing heavy."

## G10

"stability, a reformed way of life, and obedience,"

(a) "amendment of manners" is the hardest phrase here. "manners" today means politeness, which misleads. Here it means one's whole way of living and habits. "amendment" means change for the better.
(b) Latin (Rule, ch. 58): Hunter Blair's text prints "de stabilitate sua, et conversione morum suorum, et obedientia". Many manuscripts read "conversatio morum". Both mean turning one's way of life toward God, lived out as a monk. "stability" means staying for life in one community. The Latin has "suorum", "of his own", and "sua", "his", which Gasquet leaves out; the rendering also leaves them out, since the sentence already makes clear they are the monk's own.

## G11

"that the strong have something to reach for and the weak are not driven away."

(a) "have their scope" is uncertain in English; it means room or a goal for their effort. "be not scared" means not be frightened off.
(b) Latin (Rule, ch. 64): "ut sit quod fortes cupiant, et infirmi non refugiant", literally "so that there is something the strong desire and the weak do not run from". "cupiant" is "may long for", so "reach for". "refugiant" is "flee back from" or "shrink from", so "driven away" or "run away". The fragment still follows "let him so temper all things".

## G12

"outstanding for its wise moderation and fine and clear in its language,"

(a) "discretion" here means wise judgment and a sense of the right measure, the quality the Rule calls "the mother of virtues". It does not mean keeping secrets. "eloquent for the style" means well and clearly written.
(b) The Latin of the Dialogues is not among the files I was given. From memory, it reads "discretione praecipuam, sermone luculentam". "praecipuam" is outstanding, first-rate. "luculentam" is bright, clear and fine, rather than "eloquent" in the sense of persuasive speech. This should be checked against a Latin text.
