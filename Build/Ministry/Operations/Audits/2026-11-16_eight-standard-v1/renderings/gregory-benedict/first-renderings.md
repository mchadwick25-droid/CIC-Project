# First renderings, G1 to G12

## G1
unchanged: "all ruined and destroyed by the fire."

Notes:
(a) None. "Ruined" and "destroyed" are both plain modern words.
(b) Latin (MGH 1885, Hist. X.31): "ab incendio dissolutam dirutamque nanctus sum": I found it "broken apart and torn down by fire". Latin has no article, so "the fire" is Dalton's choice. It does not change the sense. The word "all" is also Dalton's. It is a fair reading of the doubled participles, so I kept it.

## G2
"Alas for our times, because the study of literature has died out among us,"

Notes:
(a) "Letters" (litterae) covers learning, literature and skill in writing. Today a reader may take "letters" to mean mail, so I put "literature". "Learning" would also be fair. "Woe to" is archaic, so I put "Alas for". "Pursuit" became "study", which is the direct sense of Latin "studium".
(b) Latin (Pref.): "Vae diebus nostris, quia periit studium litterarum a nobis": "woe to our days, because the study of letters has perished from us." "Days" means "times". "Quia" is "because", and "periit" is "has died out / perished". Every clause is covered.

## G3
"so that anyone who reads this may have no doubt that I hold the catholic faith,"

Notes:
(a) "Catholic" here means the universal, orthodox faith of the Nicene creed, as opposed to the Arian kings. It does not mean the later Roman Catholic Church as a separate body. Keep it lowercase.
(b) Latin (I, Pref.): "ut qui legerit me non dubitet esse catholicum": word for word, "that whoever reads may not doubt that I am catholic." Dalton's "hold the catholic faith" fairly expands "esse catholicum", and "prius fidem meam proferre" nearby supports it. I kept Dalton's wording.

## G4
"It is my will that this be your faith, and the faith of the other teachers of the Church."

Notes:
(a) "Doctors" means teachers. It does not mean physicians, and it does not mean the later title "Doctor of the Church". The ones meant here are the bishops. "Thy" became "your".
(b) Latin (V.44): "Sic volo, ut tu vel reliqui doctores ecclesiarum credatis": "I want you and the other teachers of the churches to believe this way." "Ecclesiarum" is plural ("of the churches"), and Dalton made it singular. I kept "the Church" because the sense is the same: the bishops of the churches. "Credatis" is a verb ("that you believe"), and Dalton turned it into the noun "faith". My rendering keeps his noun.

## G5
"what Hilary and Eusebius taught."

Notes:
(a) None. "That which" is simply "what".
(b) Latin (V.44): "quod Elarius Eusebiusque docuerunt": "what Hilary and Eusebius taught." These are Hilary of Poitiers and, most likely, Eusebius of Vercelli, both defenders of the Nicene faith. That identification is Dalton's note, not part of the quote.

## G6
"So the king gave up his plan,"

Notes:
(a) "Desisted" is formal and less common, so I put "gave up". "Purpose" means a plan or intention, not a reason for being.
(b) Latin (V.44): "Et sic rex ab hac intentione quievit": "and so the king rested from this intention", that is, he dropped it. MGH's glossary gives "quiescere = abstinere". "This" (hac) became "his" in Dalton. Both point to the same plan, which was his own decree on the Trinity.

## G7
"the books of that revered man, Gregory, bishop of Tours."

Notes:
(a) "Reverend" today sounds like a clergy title ("the Reverend Smith"). Here it means "worthy of respect", so I put "revered". "That" stands in for the force of "the reverend man", which points to one specific man. It adds no new content.
(b) Paul the Deacon's Latin is not among the files I was given, so I did not check it. The likely original, "reverendi viri", fits "revered man".

## G8
"Every day you do much evil."

Notes:
(a) "Wickedness" could stay. I put "evil" because it is a simpler word with the same weight. The inverted word order ("Much wickedness do you") is archaic, so I put it in normal order. "Daily" became "every day". "Commit" folds into "do", since "do evil" is a natural modern phrase.
(b) The Latin of the Dialogues is not among the held files, so I did not check it. From memory the Latin is "Multa mala facis, multa fecisti". If that is right, "daily" may be the 1608 translator's own addition. A second renderer should check this against a held Latin text before relying on "every day".

## G9
"a school for the service of God, in which we hope to set down nothing harsh or hard to bear."

Notes:
(a) "Ordained" here means "laid down as a rule". It does not mean made a priest. I changed it to the active verb "set down". "Burdensome" became "hard to bear".
(b) Latin (Prol.): "dominici schola servitii; in qua institutione nihil asperum, nihil grave nos constituturos speramus": "a school of the Lord's service, in which, in setting it up, we hope to establish nothing harsh, nothing heavy." The Latin is active ("we hope we shall establish"), so my active "we hope to set down" is closer than Gasquet's passive. "Dominici" means "the Lord's", and Gasquet's "God" keeps that sense. "Schola" can also mean a body of men serving together. "School" holds both meanings, so I kept it.

## G10
"stability, a change of his way of life, and obedience,"

Notes:
(a) "Manners" today means politeness, so it misleads. Here "mores" means habits or way of life. "Stability" is a technical monastic term: a vow to stay for life in this one community. I kept the word because it is the term itself. It may need a gloss elsewhere on the page.
(b) Latin (ch. 58): "promittat de stabilitate sua, et conversione morum suorum, et obedientia": "let him promise his stability, the conversion of his ways, and obedience." This edition reads "conversione". Many manuscripts read "conversatione", meaning faithfulness to the monastic way of life. Either way, "amendment" means turning or changing one's life, and "a change of his way of life" covers it. The "his" comes from "suorum".

## G11
"that the strong have something to strive for and the weak are not frightened away."

Notes:
(a) "Have their scope" is unclear today. It means room to do more. "Be not scared" means "not scared off", which is a sense of retreating, not just fear. I put "frightened away" to keep that sense.
(b) Latin (ch. 64): "sic omnia temperet, ut et sit quod fortes cupiant, et infirmi non refugiant": "so balance all things that there is something the strong long for, and the weak do not run from." "Something to strive for" follows the Latin "sit quod fortes cupiant" more closely than Gasquet's "scope". "Refugiant" means flee or shrink back, so "frightened away" fits.

## G12
"outstanding for its sound judgement and also eloquent in its style,"

Notes:
(a) "Discretion" today usually means keeping things private. Here it means "discretio": discernment, sound judgement, a sense of measure. The Rule itself calls this "the mother of virtues" (ch. 64). "Excellent for" became "outstanding for", and "for the style" became "in its style".
(b) The Latin of the Dialogues is not among the held files, so I did not check it. From memory it reads "discretione praecipuam, sermone luculentam". "Luculentus" means clear, bright, well expressed, so "eloquent" is fair. "Clear" would also be fair, if the second renderer prefers it.
