TEXT X
In 335 the churches of the Roman East were still split over Arius, ten years after the council of Nicaea condemned him. Athanasius was bishop of Alexandria in Egypt, in the church the tree calls Alexandrian Christianity, and that year, at a council in Tyre, his accusers held up a severed hand. They said he had murdered a bishop named Arsenius and used the hand for magic. Socrates of Constantinople, who tells it with plain sympathy, says Arsenius was led in alive with his hands under his cloak, and Athanasius showed both. Sozomen adds a rumor that Athanasius had hidden the man himself. Either way, the council deposed him.

He had been a young deacon at Nicaea, where Socrates says he spoke strongly against the party of Arius, and in 328 he became bishop. Arius's poem, which Athanasius quotes, said God was not always a Father and the Son was made out of nothing. Nicaea answered that the Son is "begotten, not made." If the Son were only a creature, Athanasius argued, his becoming man would have left us as we were, not joined to God. In his own words: "He became human so that we might be made God."

That stand cost him his city again and again. Socrates says Constantine the Great sent him to Trier on a charge of threatening the grain ships, though some said it was for the unity of the church, since Athanasius would hold no communion with Arius. The index kept with his Easter letters records four more flights. In 366 he came back to his church for good, and he died in 373.

Gregory of Nazianzus, who praised him in a sermon, credits him with more. When some spoke of three hypostases and others of three persons, Athanasius found they meant the same, let each keep its words, and so bound them together. He also set before the emperor a written confession that Father, Son and Spirit are one in Godhead, and Gregory says few had dared to write it so plainly.

His Life of Antony told of the hermit he says he had served, and Jerome says Marcella of Rome heard of Antony from Athanasius, then an exile in Rome, and took up that life herself. Sixty years after his death, Cyril of Alexandria wrote that he followed, especially, "our blessed Father Athanasius, praised by all."

In 335, at a council in Tyre, Athanasius's accusers held up a severed hand. They said he had murdered a bishop named Arsenius and used the hand for magic. Socrates of Constantinople, who tells it with plain sympathy, says Arsenius was found alive and led in with his hands under his cloak. Athanasius showed both. The church historian Sozomen adds a rumor that Athanasius had hidden the man himself. Either way, the council deposed him in his absence.

He had been a young deacon at Nicaea in 325, where Socrates says he spoke strongly against the party of Arius, and three years later, at about thirty, he became bishop of Alexandria. Athanasius quotes Arius's poem, which said God was not always a Father and that the Son was made out of nothing. Nicaea answered that the Son is "begotten not made, One in essence with the Father."

If the Son were only a creature, Athanasius argued, then becoming man would have left humanity just as it was, for a creature would not have joined creatures to God. In his own words: "For He was made man that we might be made God."

Constantine the Great sent him to Trier, Socrates says, on a charge of threatening the grain ships. Socrates gives another account too: some affirm the emperor did it for the unity of the church, since Athanasius refused any communion with Arius and his followers. Four more exiles followed. In one, the emperor's agents searched towns, monasteries and tombs for him, and no one gave him up. By the count of Archibald Robertson, who edited a volume of his writings, more than half of what survives was written in those years of hiding. He came home for good in 366, named Peter as his successor, and died in May of 373.

Years later, Gregory of Nazianzus began a sermon: "In praising Athanasius, I shall be praising virtue." Sixty years after his death, as Cyril of Alexandria made peace with the bishops of the East, he wrote that he followed, especially, "our blessed and all-celebrated Father Athanasius."
