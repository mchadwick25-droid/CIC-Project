## 1. The heart, before hearing the story

Around the year 200, Clement was a presbyter and teacher in the church at Alexandria. He argued that Greek philosophy was God's preparation for Christ: it led the Greeks to Christ as the Law led the Hebrews. So a Christian could keep learning and faith together. He is on the tree because that idea, in his books, set the course of the Alexandrian teaching tradition, and because Origen was his pupil.

What the sources hold for each part:

- **Presbyter at Alexandria, around 200.** Documented. Alexander calls him "the blessed presbyter" (M7). Jerome places him in the Alexandrian church (S2). His own count of years reaches 194 years after Jesus' birth (S3).
- **He taught at Alexandria.** Widely Accepted (Eusebius, Jerome; M4). That he headed a formal school is Contested (D1).
- **Philosophy as a guide to Christ.** Documented, in his own words (T1, T2, T3), against Christians who wanted "bare faith alone" (S5). Held only in Wilson's English (N2).
- **His books.** Documented (M5, M6, M6a). The card names three: the *Exhortation*, the *Instructor*, the *Stromata* (*Miscellanies*).
- **Origen his pupil.** Widely Accepted, on Eusebius and Jerome (M4, I1). Origen's own held words do not name him (D4).
- **What came of him.** Eusebius kept and trusted him (I2), and Jerome used his example to defend Christian learning (I4). Documented.

Nothing central is missing. The outline's thread is the heart itself: "Greek learning could be a guide that leads to Christ". The outline carries Origen in beat 3 and in "What came of them".

## 2. Hearing the story once

The story has 354 words. The first 150 end at "Perhaps, he suggested, God gave philosophy".

What the first 150 words carry:

- **Words 1–70: the search scene.** Around 200, Alexandria, "a Christian teacher named Clement" (words 1–16). His search through Greece, Italy and the East, and the quote "I found rest" (16–55). Eusebius names Pantaenus as that teacher (56–70).
- **Words 71–97: his notes.** He wrote things down against forgetting, and called them *Miscellanies*.
- **Words 98–110: his office.** One of the elders who led the church beside the bishop, and he taught there.
- **Words 111–150: the question.** Christians feared Greek philosophy like masks. Some wanted bare faith alone, which was like wanting grapes without the vine. Then the answer begins, and stops at word 150.

Where each part of the heart first arrives:

- Teacher at Alexandria, around 200: word 1 to 16. In full.
- Presbyter: word 99. In full.
- Philosophy as a guide to Christ: the problem arrives at 117. The answer starts at 145 and lands at 151–185 ("to lead them ... a guide to bring the Greek mind to Christ ... as the Law brought the Hebrews"). It falls just past the minute, so it lands **in part**: the listener hears the question in the first minute but not Clement's answer.
- Origen his pupil: word 285. **Not at all** in the first minute.
- Why he matters (his idea shaped how Christians used learning): not said in the first minute. Later it comes only by example: Origen's copy (296–310) and Jerome's defence (311–354).

Weight given later: the idea gets about 70 words (117–200), with its own quote. That is real weight, but it sits in the middle. Origen gets one sentence and a half near the end (about 25 words). That is close to passing him in a clause.

Where the time goes: about 55 words on the search scene, 15 on how-we-know (Eusebius naming Pantaenus), 27 on how the notes were kept, and 13 on his office. Almost half of the first minute is the opening scene and its witness, before anything about what he taught.

## 3. Verdict

**FIX.** The outline and sources hold the heart. The story puts the teacher in the first minute but not his answer, and leaves Origen to the last 70 words.

Missing from the first minute:

1. Clement's answer: philosophy as God's guide, leading the Greeks to Christ as the Law led the Hebrews. Outline thread and beat 4; T1; Q2 ("For it was a guide to bring the Greek mind to Christ, as the Law brought the Hebrews").
2. Origen among his pupils, given as Eusebius's account. Outline beat 3; M4; I1.
3. Why that matters, in one line. Outline "What came of them" 1 and 3: Origen followed his example, and Jerome later used him to defend Christian learning (I1, I4).

What can make room, with no new claims: shorten the search scene to the one quote "I found rest". Move "Eusebius names that teacher as Pantaenus" after the heart, or drop it to the card. The "patched-together notes" line can come after the idea. The search scene can stay as the opening image. It just needs to hand over to the idea within about 60 words.
