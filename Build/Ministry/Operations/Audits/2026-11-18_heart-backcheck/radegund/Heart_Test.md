## 1. The heart, before hearing the story

Radegund was a Thuringian princess, taken captive and married by the Frankish king Lothar. She left him to become a nun and founded a monastery for women at Poitiers, later called Holy Cross. She gave it everything she owned, a written Rule and a relic of the Cross. She is on the tree as a founder: her house drew some two hundred women, and her own letter defended its Rule and abbess after her death.

What the sources hold for each part:
- **Captive princess, married to Lothar.** Gregory, *History* III.4 and III.7 (S1, S2, L3). Documented. The year of capture (about 531) is Dalton's: Widely Accepted.
- **Left him; consecrated at Noyon.** Gregory has the brother's killing and her leaving the court (L7). Documented. The altar scene and her charge to Medard are Fortunatus's account (L8, Q1). Documented as his. Whether the king sent her is Inferential-Thin (D3).
- **Founded the house at Poitiers and gave it all she had.** Her own letter, kept by Gregory (L11, L14). Documented. The name Holy Cross rests on the editors (I3). Widely Accepted.
- **The Rule of Caesarius, with lifelong enclosure.** Her letter and the bishops' letter (L13). Documented. Whether she went to Arles for it is Contested (D2), and the outline rightly leaves it out.
- **The relic of the Cross.** Gregory and Baudonivia (L16, L17). Documented. Who refused it at Poitiers is Contested (D5).
- **Women drawn to her.** The bishops' letter and Gregory at her bier, about two hundred (I2, L25). Documented.
- **Her letter defended the house after her death.** Her letter, read aloud and copied in the revolt; "the custom of the lady Radegund" (I1). Documented. The sheet ranks it the heaviest impact.

Nothing central is missing from the sheet or the outline. The outline's thread names the heart plainly: "A captive queen turned her royal wealth into a house of women at Poitiers, gave it a written Rule and a relic of the Cross, and wrote the letter that defended it after she died."

## 2. Hearing the story once

What words 1–150 carry:
- **1–25:** the scene at Noyon. Nobles drag a bishop from his altar; a queen puts on a nun's dress.

Weight later: fair. The founding, endowment and Rule get about 45 words (160–200). The Cross gets about 70 (201–270), but most of that is Gregory's lamp, a how-we-know wonder. The first minute never says what she left the king to build.

## 3. Verdict

**FIX.**

Missing from the first minute: that she founded a monastery for women at Poitiers, gave it all she owned, a written Rule and a relic of the Cross, and wrote the letter that defended it after her death. These are what she did and why she is on the tree. As it stands, a listener at word 150 knows only a queen who fled to a convent.

What in the outline can carry it, with no new claims:
- The outline's own thread (quoted in section 1) can stand as one sentence near the top, after the setting (around word 34). It covers the founding, the Rule, the Cross and the letter.
- Room can come from the Noyon scene, which is told twice. The opening (1–25) and the retelling (99–159) can become one telling. The fierce altar scene and Q1 are design and can stay; only the repeat needs to go.
- Optional: the census card's name for the house, Holy Cross (I3, the editors' naming, Widely Accepted), can go with the founding.

A note outside the heart test: words 349–363 ("led by Clotild, who called herself a king's daughter and said they were insulted and humiliated there") go beyond the outline, which leaves the revolt in detail out and keeps only the letter's use in it (Leave out; I1). Gregory's text does support the words (DAL 19406–19416), so this is a fit-to-outline point, not an invention. It also spends about 15 words the first minute needs more.
