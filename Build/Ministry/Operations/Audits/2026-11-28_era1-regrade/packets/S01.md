## tree/methodius-of-olympus

Methodius of Olympus

Methodius of Olympus, a bishop who died a martyr at the end of the last persecution, Jerome says, wrote a book called the Banquet. In it a young woman and two friends climb a rough, steep path to a garden where ten guests will meet. A tall woman in a robe white as snow embraces them like a mother, and her name means Virtue. They feast under a wide, shady tree, and she asks each guest to praise the virgin life.

Methodius was bishop of Olympus, a city in Lycia, in the south of Asia Minor. In the Banquet, ten young women each praise chastity, the unmarried life given to God. In a dialogue, On the Resurrection, he argued against Origen, the great teacher of Alexandria and Caesarea, that the body is no chain on the soul and that the same body will rise. A later bishop copied his words into his own case against Origen.

The Banquet is a story within a story, in which one friend begs another to repeat the speeches. One guest says praising virginity does not abolish marriage, which is still God's work. "The moon may be greater than the stars, but its light does not put out the light of the other stars." Virtue, speaking last, says it is no chastity to keep the body pure but not the tongue, the eyes, the hands or the mind.

The dialogue, as a later reader summed it up centuries on, asks what the body is to the soul. Origen, Methodius wrote, held that the soul lived without a body before the fall, the first sin, and was given the body afterwards as a chain. At the resurrection, Origen held, the same flesh does not return, only each person's shape, stamped on a different, spiritual body. Methodius could not bear, he says, those who turn bones and flesh into symbols, so that the dead rise without flesh.

If the body were a chain to hold back evil, he answered, it had failed, since Cain committed murder in this body. A human being, he held, is soul and body joined in one. In his telling, a craftsman finds that an envious man has spoiled a beautiful statue he made, so he melts it down and casts it again. "For the statue cannot be lost while it is in the same craftsman's hands, even if it is melted down into its material again. It can be restored. But the blemishes and the damage can be lost, for they melt away. They cannot be restored." So, he wrote, God, seeing his finest work spoiled by envy, dissolves the body in death and remakes it in the resurrection.

Eusebius of Caesarea, in a book he wrote in Origen's defense, asked how Methodius dared to write against Origen now. Jerome, who reports the question, says Methodius was crowned with martyrdom at Chalcis in Greece, in that last persecution, though others, he adds, placed it under earlier emperors, Decius and Valerian.

Later in the fourth century, Epiphanius of Salamis wrote against Origen and chose to rest his case on On the Resurrection, setting out its words on the rising of the same body word for word.

More than a century after Methodius, the church historian Socrates, who defended Origen, put him first among four men who attacked Origen. "Worthless people, who cannot be noticed on their own, want to be seen by finding fault with their betters. Methodius was the first to fall into this." Socrates adds that Methodius, after railing against Origen in many places, took it all back, as it were, and praised him in a dialogue he called the Xenon. Socrates alone tells of this change of heart.

At the Banquet's close, Virtue tells the guests to rise and stand under the tree. One guest begins a hymn, and the others answer her, each keeping herself for Christ, the Bridegroom.

## tree/suetonius

Suetonius

In the first century, the emperor Claudius freed the people of old Troy from tax forever, because, he says, they founded the Roman race, and he let German envoys sit with the senators at the theater. The Roman writer Suetonius sets down these acts one after another in his life of Claudius, and between those two comes one sentence: "He drove out of Rome the Jewish people who kept rioting at the urging of Chrestus."

Suetonius was a Roman scholar who wrote the lives of Rome's rulers from Julius Caesar to Domitian. He has one more line like it, under Nero. Each is one item in a list of an emperor's acts, and only the second names the Christians. Later, Christians complained, without naming him, that outsiders got their name wrong, calling them "Chrestians" and Christ "Chrestus", the name in his first line. A Christian chronicle set that line down almost in his words and joined it to a story in the New Testament.

His father, in his own telling, was an army officer, and Suetonius remembers being a young man under the emperor Domitian. His lives end with that emperor, so he wrote after Domitian's death. He later ran the emperor Hadrian's letters.

The Acts of the Apostles, in the New Testament, also tells of Claudius ordering the Jewish people out of Rome. At Corinth, the apostle Paul met a Jewish man and his wife lately come from Italy because of that order. The name Chrestus is close in sound to Christus, the Latin form of Christ, and it was also a real name.

In his life of Nero, Suetonius lists measures by which, he says, abuses were severely punished and put down, and new rules made. Spending was limited, and taverns were barred from selling cooked food except beans and greens. Then comes the second line: "Punishments were laid on the Christians, a kind of people holding a new and harmful superstition." Superstition was his word for a religion he despised. The list runs straight on to the chariot drivers, who were stopped from roaming the streets and cheating people for fun.

He names no charge, number or kind of punishment, and he files these among Nero's acts that deserve no blame, some even praise, before he turns to Nero's crimes. In another chapter he says Nero himself set Rome on fire, and there he names no Christians. Another Roman, Tacitus, tells at length of Christians put to death under Nero.

About 200, at Carthage in North Africa, the Christian writer Tertullian had read Suetonius and names him on other matters. In his appeal to the pagans, without naming Suetonius, he says the name "Christian" comes from anointing; in plain words, Christ means "the anointed one", for the Jewish people anointed their priests and kings with holy oil. Then he turns to them: "You say it wrong, and call us 'Chrestians', for you are not even clearly sure of the name itself. Even so, it rings of sweetness or goodness." In Greek, chrestos means good or kind.

About 725, Bede, in his chronicle of the world, set down the Claudius line almost in Suetonius's words, without Chrestus. He put it in Claudius's ninth year and added that Luke also reports it, joining it to the Acts.

## tree/hippolytus-of-rome

Hippolytus of Rome

Twice the elders, the church's priests, called a teacher from Smyrna named Noetus before the church. He said that Christ was the Father himself, so that the Father was born, suffered and died, and in the end they put him out.

Hippolytus, who tells that story, taught in the church at Rome in the early 200s and wrote in Greek. Against Noetus's teaching he held that the Son was another beside the Father, yet one God with him. He would not give way to two bishops of Rome, Zephyrinus and Callistus, on this, or on how far the church could forgive. He set the quarrel down in a book against every teaching he held false. In 235 he was sent to Sardinia with Rome's bishop, and Rome kept his day among its martyrs.

In Hippolytus's telling, the only account of the quarrel that survives, Zephyrinus let Noetus's teaching spread at Rome, with Callistus as his adviser. Hippolytus and his side stood against them for the truth.

He called the Son the Word, a name from John's gospel for the Son as he was with the Father before he became man: "When I say 'another,' I do not mean two Gods. I mean it as light from light, or water from a spring, or a ray from the sun. For there is one power, which comes from the whole. The whole is the Father, and from him comes the power, the Word." He imagined someone asking whether he spoke of two Gods, and answered that he spoke of one God, in two persons, with the grace of the Holy Spirit as a third.

While Zephyrinus still lived, Hippolytus says, Callistus called him and his side worshippers of two gods, because they would not go along with Callistus.

When Zephyrinus died, Callistus took his place. Hippolytus charges that Callistus forgave every sin of the body to anyone who came over to him, and held that a bishop should not be removed even for the gravest sin. Some whom Hippolytus's own side had judged and put out of the church went over to Callistus.

His book, the Refutation of All Heresies, traced the teachings he held false back to Greek philosophers, mystery cults and astrologers. Its last part turned to the teachings of his own day, Callistus's among them. In its opening he wrote that the apostles had received the Holy Spirit and handed it on to those who believed rightly: "We are their successors. We share in the same grace, in the high priesthood and in the teaching, and we are counted guardians of the church. We do not let our eyes droop, and we do not keep the right word silent." The high priesthood meant the church's chief priestly office.

He drew up a table, on a cycle of sixteen years, for finding the day of Easter, a day over which Christians differed, some keeping the moon's fourteenth day. Jerome says it gave Eusebius the occasion for his own nineteen-year cycle.

In 235, Rome's list of bishops records, Pontianus, the bishop, and Hippolytus, whom it calls an elder, were deported to Sardinia. Rome's calendar of martyrs kept them both on 13 August. In the East, Eusebius wrote that Hippolytus had led another church, without saying which, and a later bishop there quoted him for the faith and called him bishop and martyr.

More than a century later, Damasus, bishop of Rome, set a verse on his tomb on the Tiburtine Road, telling what he had heard. Hippolytus, an elder, it was said, had always stayed in the split of Novatus while a tyrant's orders pressed. Yet that split came later: Novatian's party made it from the church at Rome in 251, sixteen years after the exile, refusing to take back those who had given way in persecution. As Hippolytus went to the kingdom of the faithful, the people asked where to go, and he told them all to follow the catholic faith, the faith of the whole church. So, the verse says, he earned his place as a martyr.

## tree/tacitus

Tacitus

When daylight failed in the emperor Nero's gardens in Rome, after the city's great fire, Christians were set alight to light the dark. Some, covered in the skins of animals, were torn apart by dogs, and many were nailed to crosses. The Roman who tells it, the historian Tacitus, thought them guilty and despised them.

Writing a generation or more after the great fire of Rome in 64, Tacitus gave the fullest account from outside the church of how Nero punished the Christians. In it he named Christus, that is, Christ, from whom their name came, put to death under Pontius Pilate. His account runs to a few sentences, about 140 words of Latin, and the details of these deaths rest on him alone. Later, one Christian, Tertullian, answered him by name on another matter, and another, Sulpicius Severus, retold his scene in nearly his own words and called the dead innocent.

In the Annals, his year-by-year history of Rome, Tacitus tells how Rome turned after the fire to prayers and offerings to the gods. But nothing, not human effort, not the emperor's gifts, not the offerings, ended the shameful talk that someone had ordered the fire.

So, to kill the rumor, Nero put forward others as the guilty: people hated for their shameful acts, whom the common crowd called Christians. He punished them, Tacitus writes, with the most refined cruelty. Then Tacitus stops to explain the name, naming no source for it: "Christus, from whom that name comes, had been put to death in the reign of Tiberius by the governor Pontius Pilate." Their religion was a deadly superstition, he says with contempt, and though it was held back for a moment, it broke out again, in Judaea, where the evil began, and in Rome itself.

First those who confessed were seized. Then, on their information, a huge number were convicted, and he names none of them. They were convicted, he says, less for setting the fire than for hatred of the human race. Mockery was added to their deaths, and Nero offered his own gardens for the show. He also put on chariot races, mixing with the crowd dressed as a charioteer, or standing in a chariot himself.

About 200, in Carthage, the Christian Tertullian answered Tacitus by name in his Apology, his defense of the Christians. His quarrel was with another of Tacitus's histories, which told where the Jewish people came from. That account of the Jewish people, Tertullian says, started the tale that the Christians, close to the Jewish people in religion, worshiped a donkey's head. Tertullian called Tacitus a liar, and a wordy one, and pointed to his own words against him: the same Tacitus tells that the Roman general who took Jerusalem went into the temple and found no image there. If the Jewish people worshiped an image, Tertullian says, the temple was the very place it would be shown.

About 400, Sulpicius Severus, a Christian writing a short history of the world, told the fire and the blame, and gave the same deaths in nearly Tacitus's own Latin words, though he names no source. Where Tacitus called the dead guilty, Sulpicius wrote: "So he turned the blame onto the Christians. And the cruellest tortures were used on the innocent."

Tacitus closed his own account this way: "So pity began to rise, even toward the guilty, who had earned the harshest punishment as a warning. It was as if they were being destroyed not for the public good, but for one man's cruelty."

## tree/josephus

Josephus

In 67, when the Roman general Vespasian took Jotapata, a walled town in Galilee, in the north of the country, Josephus hid in a cave with forty leading men. When the Romans found him on the third day and offered him his life, he prayed in secret, telling God he would surrender. "I give them my hands willingly, and I am content to live. And I declare openly that I do not go over to the Romans as a deserter from the Jewish people, but as a servant sent by you."

Josephus was a priest of the Temple in Jerusalem, from a priestly family, who commanded Jewish forces in Galilee when the Jewish people rose against Rome in 66. That year he gave himself up to Vespasian and foretold that the general would be emperor. When that came true, he lived on at Rome under the emperors' favor. In his people's history, finished there about 93, a few lines tell of John the Baptist, of James, the brother of Jesus, and of Jesus himself. Christians copied those lines for centuries as the word of a Jew outside their faith, and argued over the one on Jesus, which the Christian writers who quote it give in different words.

Back in the cave, after his prayer, the men with him held out a sword. He could die as general of the Jewish people, they said, or as a traitor to them. He argued that it was wrong to take one's own life, and they rushed at him and called him a coward. He proposed that they draw lots, each to be killed in turn by the next. He was left last with one other man, by chance or by God's providence, he writes, and persuaded him to live.

At Rome he wrote the books that tell nearly everything known of his life. He wrote a history of the war in Greek, because others, he says, had written falsely of it, out of flattery to Rome or hatred of the Jewish people. Then came the Jewish Antiquities, his people's history in twenty books, to set their story before Greek-speaking readers. In his Life, a book on his own life, he defended himself against a rival historian who, he says, had written falsely of him, that he and the Galileans had begun the revolt against Rome.

In the Antiquities, Josephus tells of John, called the Baptist, a good man, he writes, who urged the Jewish people to deal justly with one another and to honor God. The ruler Herod, he says, feared that John's hold on the crowds might lead to a rising, so he had him imprisoned and killed.

In the same book, between a riot under Pilate, the Roman governor of Judea, and another disaster, the banishing of the Jewish people from Rome, stands a passage of about a hundred words on Jesus. Its key word, the Christ, means the Messiah, the anointed one the Jewish people awaited. "About this time there lived Jesus, a wise man, if one ought to call him a man. For he did astonishing deeds, and taught people who gladly receive the truth. He won over many of the Jewish people, and many of the Greeks as well. This man was the Christ." The Greeks are those who were not Jewish. The passage goes on: Pilate, at the urging of the leading men, had him crucified; he appeared alive on the third day; and the Christians, named after him, have not died out.

Further on, the high priest, the chief priest of the Temple, called a council of judges. He brought before them "the brother of Jesus who was called Christ, James by name", with some others, charged them with breaking the law, and had them stoned. The fairest-minded citizens protested that he had no right to call the court without the Roman governor's consent.

Origen, a Christian teacher of the third century, knew the lines on John and the phrase on James, and wrote that Josephus did not accept Jesus as the Christ. Early in the fourth century, Eusebius of Caesarea copied all three passages into his history of the church. He set the lines on John and Jesus, a Jew's own word, against forged records of Pilate's dealings with Jesus. Those records, he says, were written against Jesus and sent out in his own day with the emperor's approval.

Late in that century, another Christian writer, Jerome, quoted the passage in Latin, and his Latin says only that Jesus was believed to be the Christ. The passage itself says he was the Christ, and Origen wrote that Josephus did not accept him as the Christ.

At the close of his Life, Josephus called it the account of his whole life, and left others to judge his character by it, as they pleased.

## tree/cyprian-of-carthage

Cyprian of Carthage

His deacon, Pontius, who writes as an admirer, says that soon after Cyprian's baptism the whole people of Carthage wanted him as their bishop. A crowd, he says, besieged the doors of his house, while he stepped back to give place to older men. He was still a new Christian when they made him bishop.

Jerome, writing long after, says he had once been a teacher of public speaking. In the middle of the third century Cyprian led the Latin-speaking church of Carthage, in Roman Africa, and the councils of Africa's bishops through persecution, plague and division. He taught that the church is one, and a mother: no one outside her has God as Father, and each bishop answers to Christ alone. He would not accept the ruling of Stephen, bishop of Rome, on baptism, and in 258 he was put to the sword. Later the Donatists and Augustine both claimed him.

In 250 the emperor Decius ordered Christians before the magistrates to sacrifice to the gods. At Carthage, Cyprian wrote later, the crowds kept demanding him with violent shouting. So he withdrew for a while, thinking less of his own safety than of his fellow Christians' peace, so that his presence would not stir the riot further, and from hiding he led his church by letter.

The clergy at Rome wrote to Carthage's clergy that he had withdrawn, and had done rightly. In the same letter they spoke of the gospel's good shepherd, who lays down his life for the sheep, and the hired man who runs from the wolf. They asked Carthage's clergy not to be hired men. Pontius later wrote that fear was not the cause: he did suffer afterwards, and would have run from that too, had fear made him run before.

At Rome a second bishop, Novatian, was set up against the one already chosen, and as that rival rose, Cyprian wrote a book on the church's unity. In it the church is a single mother, from whose womb Christians are born and by whose milk they are fed. Of anyone who leaves the church of Christ, he writes: "He is a stranger, he is unholy, he is an enemy. The one who does not have the Church as mother cannot have God as Father." By the church he meant the one body joined to its lawful bishops, while Novatian's side held a bishop and a church of its own.

Afterwards a dreadful plague broke out over the whole city. Some Christians were troubled that it struck them as hard as it struck the pagans, and Cyprian answered them in a sermon.

"This plague and pestilence, which seems so dreadful and deadly, searches out how just each person is. It tests the minds of the human race. Do the healthy serve the sick? Do relatives love their kin as they ought? Do masters pity their sick slaves? Do doctors not abandon the sick who plead with them?"

Then came a quarrel with Rome over baptism, the washing that makes a Christian, which is given once. The question was whether a washing given by a group outside the church counted when the one washed came over to it. Cyprian counted as heretics all who stood outside the one church. With councils of African bishops he held that there is one baptism because the church is one, and none outside it, so those who came over were to be baptized in the church.

Stephen, bishop of Rome, answered otherwise. His letter, as Cyprian copies it, said: let nothing new be done beyond what has been handed down. Those who came over were to be received with hands laid on them for repentance, without a new washing, since the heretics themselves do not wash again those who come over to them.

In the months of that quarrel, eighty-seven African bishops met in a council, and Cyprian spoke first. Communion meant belonging to the church and sharing its bread and cup. He said: "It remains for each of us to say what we think on this matter. We judge no one, and we cut no one off from communion if he thinks otherwise. For none of us sets himself up as a bishop of bishops. None of us drives his colleagues to obey by a tyrant's terror."

In 258, at his gardens, he learned that officers were coming to take him to Utica, another city. In his last letter he says he left the gardens for a while, because a bishop should confess his faith in the city where he leads the church, before all his people. He was waiting in hiding, he wrote, for the governor's return to Carthage.

In the end he was taken at his gardens, and next morning, 14 September 258, he stood before the governor. The emperors had ordered him to perform the rites, the sacrifices to Rome's gods, and he said he would not. The sentence, in Rome's own terms, named him an enemy of the Roman gods whose blood would make the law hold, and ordered him put to the sword. Cyprian said, "Thanks be to God." In the field of Sextus he covered his eyes with his own hand. When he could not tie the cloth, a presbyter and a subdeacon, both named Julian, tied it, and there he was put to death.

From the 310s a rival church in Africa, the Donatists, baptized again all who came over to it, and quoted his letters and council in its defense. Augustine of Hippo, a later African bishop, answered them about 140 years after Cyprian's death. Stop citing Cyprian's authority for repeating baptism, he told them, and hold with us to his example of keeping unity.

Augustine also preached on Cyprian's birthday, the martyr's feast on the day he died, kept, he said, through all Africa. Where Cyprian was put to death, he said, a table for God's altar now stood, called Cyprian's table because there he was offered up. Worshippers, he said, now ringed the place where persecutors had ringed him.
