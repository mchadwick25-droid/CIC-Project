On a March day in 250, in the stadium at Smyrna, in the province of Asia, a presbyter named Pionius stretched himself out on a stake and let the soldier drive in the nails. He had told the Roman governor that he feared the living God, and he would not sacrifice. Beside him they raised Metrodorus, a presbyter of the Marcionites. Pionius was on the right and Metrodorus on the left, the eyewitnesses wrote, but both faced the east, and the wood was heaped around them.

The two men were elders of rival churches. The Marcionites followed Marcion, whom the church of Rome cast out in the middle of the second century, for what Tertullian calls a restless curiosity that spread to others. For about three hundred years, from Rome to Persia and down to the villages of Syria, they gathered with their own presbyters, teachers and martyrs. They held that the Father whom Jesus made known was a good God, other than the God who made the world and gave the law. Their Scripture was Marcion's two books, a Gospel cut down from Luke and ten letters of Paul. Irenaeus, writing within a generation of Marcion, says these were trimmed of whatever made the world's maker Jesus' Father. Like every witness to them, he was their opponent, for no line of theirs survives in their own hand.

Late in the second century, a writer from Asia named Rhodon found Marcion's followers divided over how many first sources there were, the beginnings from which all things come. Apelles, one of their teachers and by then an old man, held there was only one. Rhodon, who argued with him, reports his faith: "He said there was no need at all to examine a person's teaching, and that each should keep to what he believed. For those who trusted in the one who was crucified would be saved, he said, if only they were found doing good works."

In Carthage, about 207, Tertullian wrote five books against Marcion. He reports that their churches baptized only virgins, widows, the unmarried and the divorced, which to him meant they condemned marriage. An accused Christian was told to sprinkle incense to the gods from a box, and doing it meant denying Christ and going free. Tertullian put the question to his Marcionite reader: "Why, in times of persecution, do you not save your life at once by denying, when the incense box is held out? 'Never,' you say, 'never!'" His good God punished no one, yet he would not deny him.

In Caesarea in Palestine, in the persecution that began under Diocletian, the historian Eusebius recorded an ascetic, a man who lived in strict self-denial, burned in Eusebius's own city. The judge begged him many times to spare himself, and he would not. With him, on the same pyre, died Asclepius, who Eusebius says was supposed to be a Marcionite bishop, with zeal, as he thought, for God.

After the persecutions, the emperor Constantine wrote to the Novatians, Valentinians, Marcionites and others by name. His edict, a law read out against them, said their teaching made the healthy soul sick. It forbade their meetings, even in private houses, and gave their houses of prayer to the catholic Church. Yet their meetings went on. At Jerusalem in the fourth century, Cyril explained the creed's words, one Holy Catholic Church, to those about to be baptized. He warned that heretics, groups whose teaching he rejected, also called their meetings churches, and he named the Marcionists first. So in a strange city, he told them, they should ask for the Catholic Church, the church spread over all the world.

In the fifth century, whole villages still followed Marcion in the district of Cyrrhus, in northern Syria, which held eight hundred parish churches. John Chrysostom had urged the bishop there to drive Marcion's followers out, offering the aid of the imperial edicts. Theodoret, bishop of Cyrrhus, wanted to bring them to the one Son, whom he held to be just as well as good, and the heretics he fought stoned him many times. After twenty-five years as bishop, he wrote to a consul: "I led eight villages of Marcion's followers, and the country around them, into the way of truth."
