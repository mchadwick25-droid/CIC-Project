On a March day in 250, in the stadium at Smyrna, a presbyter named Pionius stretched himself out on a stake and let the soldier drive in the nails. A presbyter was an elder of a congregation, and Pionius had refused to sacrifice to the gods, saying he feared the living God. Beside him they raised a presbyter of the Marcionites, Metrodorus. The eyewitnesses wrote that Pionius was on the right and Metrodorus on the left, both facing the east, and the wood was heaped around them.

The two men belonged to rival churches. The Marcionites were the churches that followed Marcion after the church of Rome cast him out, in the middle of the second century. Tertullian gives the reason as a restless curiosity that spread to others. For about three hundred years, from Rome to Persia and down to the villages of Syria, they gathered with their own presbyters, teachers and martyrs. Their Scripture was two books, a Gospel cut down from Luke's and ten letters of Paul. Irenaeus, a bishop who wrote against them within a generation, says Marcion trimmed both books of whatever made the world's maker Jesus' Father. Like every witness to them, he was their enemy, for no line of theirs survives in their own hand. They held one belief above all: Jesus came from a good Father, above the God who made the world and gave the law and the prophets, which Jesus set aside.

Late in the second century, a writer from Asia named Rhodon found Marcion's followers divided over how many first sources, or beginnings of all things, there are. Apelles, one of their teachers, held there was only one. Rhodon, who argued with him, set down his words: "He said there was no need at all to examine a person's teaching, and that each should keep to what he believed. For those who trusted in the one who was crucified would be saved, he said, if only they were found doing good works."

At Carthage, about 207, Tertullian reported that their churches baptized only virgins, widows, the unmarried and the divorced, a rule he said condemned marriage. In Tertullian's report, their good God punished no one, and only cast the sinner away out of sight. So Tertullian pressed them on persecution. An accused Christian was told to sprinkle incense from a box to the gods, and whoever did it denied Christ and went free. He put it to his Marcionite reader: "Why, in times of persecution, do you not save your life at once by denying, when the incense box is held out? 'Never,' you say, 'never!'"

In the persecution that began under the emperor Diocletian, the historian Eusebius records that Asclepius was burned in his own city, Caesarea in Palestine. He was supposed to be, Eusebius says, a bishop of the Marcionites, with zeal for God, as he thought.

When the persecutions ended, the emperor Constantine issued an edict, a law read out to the heretics by name, the Marcionites among them. Their teaching, it said, made the healthy soul sick. It forbade their meetings, even in private houses, and gave their houses of prayer to the catholic Church.

Yet their meetings went on. At Jerusalem in the fourth century, explaining the creed's "one Holy Catholic Church" to those about to be baptized, Cyril named the Marcionists first among the heretics whose meetings were also called churches. He told them to ask in a strange city for the Catholic Church, the church over all the world.

In the fifth century Theodoret was bishop of Cyrrhus, in northern Syria. There John Chrysostom had once urged the bishop to drive out Marcion's followers, offering the aid of the imperial edicts. He worked to bring them to the one Son, who was, he wrote, not only good but just. He was stoned many times by the heretics he fought. After twenty-five years as bishop, he wrote to a consul: "I led eight villages of Marcion's followers, and the country around them, into the way of truth."
