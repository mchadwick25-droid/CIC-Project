On a March day in 250, in the stadium at Smyrna, in the province of Asia, a presbyter named Pionius stretched himself out on a stake and let the soldier drive in the nails. A presbyter was an elder of a congregation, and Pionius had refused to sacrifice to the gods, saying that he feared the living God. Beside him they raised a presbyter of the Marcionites, Metrodorus. The eyewitnesses wrote that Pionius was on the right and Metrodorus on the left, both facing the east, and the wood was heaped around them both.

The two men belonged to rival churches. The Marcionites were the churches that followed Marcion after the church of Rome cast him out, in the middle of the second century, for what Tertullian calls a restless curiosity that spread to others. For about three hundred years, from Rome to Persia and down to the villages of Syria, they gathered with their own presbyters, teachers and martyrs. Their Scripture was two books, a Gospel cut down from Luke's and ten letters of Paul. Irenaeus, a bishop who wrote against them within a generation, is the first of the enemies who tell us of them, since no line of theirs survives in their own hand. He says Marcion trimmed both books of whatever made the world's maker Jesus' Father. They held one belief above all: Jesus came from a good Father, above the God who made the world and gave the law and the prophets, which Jesus set aside.

Late in the second century, a writer from Asia named Rhodon found Marcion's followers divided over how many first sources there are, the beginnings from which all things come. One of their teachers, Apelles, held that there was only one. Rhodon, who argued with him, set down his words: "He said there was no need at all to examine a person's teaching, and that each should keep to what he believed. For those who trusted in the one who was crucified would be saved, he said, if only they were found doing good works."

At Carthage, about 207, Tertullian reported that their churches baptized only virgins, widows, the unmarried and the divorced, a rule he said condemned marriage. Their good God, as Tertullian gives their teaching, punished no one, and a sinner was only cast away out of sight. So Tertullian pressed them on persecution, when an accused Christian was told to sprinkle incense from a box to the gods, and whoever did it denied Christ and went free. He put it to his Marcionite reader: "Why, in times of persecution, do you not save your life at once by denying, when the incense box is held out? 'Never,' you say, 'never!'" Their God would not punish them, and still they would not deny him.

In the persecution that began under the emperor Diocletian, the historian Eusebius records that in his own city, Caesarea in Palestine, a man named Asclepius was burned. He was supposed to be, Eusebius says, a bishop of the Marcionites, and he had zeal for God, as he thought.

When the persecutions ended, the emperor Constantine issued an edict, a law read out to the heretics by name, the Marcionites among them. Their teaching, it said, made the healthy soul sick. It forbade their meetings, even in private houses, and gave their houses of prayer to the catholic Church.

Yet their meetings went on. In the fourth century, at Jerusalem, Cyril explained the creed's words "one Holy Catholic Church" to those about to be baptized. The Marcionists, he said, came first among the heretics whose meetings were also called churches. So in a strange city, he told them, they should ask for the Catholic Church, the name for the church over all the world.

In the district of Cyrrhus, in northern Syria, John Chrysostom had once urged the bishop to drive out Marcion's followers, offering the aid of the imperial edicts. In the fifth century Theodoret was bishop there. He worked to bring them to the one Son, who was, he wrote, not only good but just, and he was stoned many times by the heretics he fought. After twenty-five years as bishop, he wrote to a consul: "I led eight villages of Marcion's followers, and the country around them, into the way of truth."
