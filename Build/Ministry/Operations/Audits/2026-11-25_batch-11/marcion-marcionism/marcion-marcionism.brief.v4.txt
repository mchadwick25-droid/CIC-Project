In 250, in the stadium at Smyrna, a presbyter, or church elder, named Pionius stretched himself on a stake and let the soldier drive in the nails. He had refused to sacrifice to the gods, saying he feared the living God. Beside him they raised Metrodorus, a presbyter of the rival Marcionites. Both faced east, and the wood was heaped around them.

The Marcionites were the churches that followed Marcion after Rome's church cast him out, in the middle of the second century. For about three hundred years, from Rome to Persia and down to the villages of Syria, they gathered with their own presbyters, teachers and martyrs. Their Scripture was two books, a Gospel cut down from Luke's and ten letters of Paul. They held one belief above all: Jesus came from a good Father, above the God who made the world, and Jesus abolished that God's law and prophets, the Jewish Scriptures. Tertullian, a writer at Carthage, in North Africa, says Marcion was cast out for his restless curiosity, which spread to others. Irenaeus, a bishop writing within a generation, says Marcion cut from both books every passage that called the maker of the world the Father of Jesus. Irenaeus, like every witness this story draws on, was their enemy.

Late in the second century, a writer from the Roman province of Asia named Rhodon found Marcion's followers divided over how many first sources, or beginnings of all things, there are. Apelles, one of their teachers, held there was only one, one God, where Marcion held two. Rhodon, who argued with him, set down his words: "He said there was no need at all to examine a person's teaching, and that each should keep to what he believed. For those who trusted in the one who was crucified would be saved, he said, if only they were found doing good works."

About 207, Tertullian reported that their churches baptized only virgins, widows, the unmarried and the divorced, a rule he said condemned marriage. Their good God, he said, punished no one, and only cast the sinner away out of sight. So he pressed them on persecution. An accused Christian was told to sprinkle incense from a box to the gods, and whoever did it denied Christ and went free. He put it to his Marcionite reader: "Why, in times of persecution, do you not save your life at once by denying, when the incense box is held out? 'Never,' you say, 'never!'"

In the persecution that began under the emperor Diocletian, Asclepius was burned at Caesarea in Palestine, the historian Eusebius's own city. He was said to be, Eusebius writes, a bishop of the Marcionites, eager for religion as he understood it, but without true knowledge.

When the persecutions ended, the emperor Constantine issued an edict, a law addressed by name to the Marcionites and other heretics, those who, their enemies said, chose error. Their teaching, it said, made the healthy soul sick. It forbade their meetings, even in private houses, and gave their houses of prayer to the catholic Church.

Yet their meetings went on. At Jerusalem in the fourth century, explaining the creed's "one Holy Catholic Church" to those about to be baptized, Cyril named the Marcionites first among the heretics whose meetings were also called churches. He told them to ask in an unfamiliar city for the Catholic Church, the church over all the world.

In the fifth century Theodoret was bishop of Cyrrhus, in northern Syria. There John Chrysostom had once charged an earlier bishop to drive Marcion's teaching out of the district, offering the aid of the imperial edicts. Theodoret worked to bring them to his own faith, that Christ was, as he wrote, not only good but just. He was stoned many times by the heretics he fought. After twenty-five years as bishop, he wrote: "I led eight villages of Marcion's followers, and the country around them, into the way of truth."
