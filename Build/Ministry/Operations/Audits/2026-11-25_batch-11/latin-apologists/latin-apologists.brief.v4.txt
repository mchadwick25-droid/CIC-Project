After his friend Octavius died, Minucius Felix, a pleader, or court lawyer, wrote down a day from their friendship in his dialogue the Octavius. On a court holiday at the grape harvest, the two of them and Caecilius walked the shore at Ostia, near Rome. Caecilius saw an image of the god Serapis and raised a hand to his mouth, kissing it toward the god. Octavius, a Christian, scolded Minucius for letting Caecilius bow to stones smeared with oil, and the three sat on the breakwater to argue it out.

Minucius was one of the Latin Apologists, Christians of Rome and Roman Africa who, from around 200 to the reign of the emperor Diocletian, defended their faith in Latin to the educated who despised it. An apology was a defense speech for the accused, and theirs answered the charges out of the pagans' own books.

The old religion is ancient and useful, Caecilius holds, and the Christians are "A people that lurks and shuns the light, silent in public, chattering in corners." By common report, he says, newcomers kill an infant hidden in flour, and their feasts end in incest when a dog tied to the lamp puts out the light.

Octavius calls them fables no one looked into. He and others like him, Octavius says, had once believed them too, and as lawyers had defended the worst criminals but tortured Christians who confessed, to make them deny it. Christians, he says, keep one marriage, eat sober meals, and love one another as brothers, because they do not know how to hate. No temple holds God, who is unseen and everywhere, and the offering he wants is a good mind. Beaten with the philosophers' own weapons, Caecilius yields.

At Carthage around 200, Tertullian met the same charges in his Apology to the governors who judged Christians. He said torture, used on others to make them confess, was turned around on Christians, to make them deny what they were. Christians prayed for the emperors, he wrote, and gave each month to bury the poor and keep orphans, a love that branded them in the eyes of people who hated one another. Jerome, a Christian scholar writing later, says the Roman clergy's envy and insults drove Tertullian to the New Prophecy. It held that the Holy Spirit now gave a stricter rule.

In Diocletian's reign Arnobius taught rhetoric, the art of public speaking, at Sicca in Africa. The story of how he became a Christian rests on Jerome alone, who wrote about eighty years later that dreams drove him toward belief, and the bishop would not take him in. "He could not win from the bishop the faith he had always attacked. So he laboured by lamplight over most brilliant books against his old religion. At last, as if with hostages for his devotion, he won the treaty." His seven books were his pledge, and he was let in. In them he owns his past: "Whenever I had seen a stone made slick and smeared with olive oil, I fawned on it as if a power were present in it. I spoke to it, and begged favours from a block that felt nothing."

Arnobius's pupil, Jerome says, was Lactantius, called to teach rhetoric at Nicomedia, a Greek city in Bithynia. He was teaching there when, Lactantius says, the emperor's officers burned the Christians' Scriptures and leveled their church, on a day picked to end Christianity. Next day an order laid every Christian, whatever his rank, open to torture. Tertullian's Apology, Lactantius granted, had pleaded the cause in full, but answering accusers was one thing and teaching the whole faith another, and he took on the second.

Later, asked why he quoted pagan books, Jerome named all four together. Their books, he said, were full of the pagans' own learning.

Of the writers known to him, Lactantius wrote, Minucius Felix had stood well among the pleaders, and his Octavius showed how fit a champion of truth he might have been, had he given himself wholly to it.
