Carthage, 197 CE. A convert trained in rhetoric sat down to answer the slanders against his new faith. He found that Latin had no words yet for most of what he needed to say. So he made them. Trinitas, persona, substantia, sacramentum: Western Christians still use these technical words for the Trinity and the sacraments. In large part they came off Tertullian's own pen. They appear in the Apology and in the string of treatises that followed it. Against Praxeas argues that Father and Son are truly distinct. The Prescription Against Heretics holds that Scripture belongs to the churches that received it. Five long books Against Marcion answer the one rival he judged most dangerous. In his later years he grew steadily stricter, and he sided with the New Prophecy out of Phrygia. That is one reason the Latin church quoted him endlessly and never made him a saint. Roughly a generation on, and in a very different key, came a Roman lawyer named Minucius Felix. He wrote a dialogue set on the beach at Ostia. In it a pagan friend states the case against Christianity. It is a religion of the ignorant, he says. Its god was a criminal. Rome's greatness is proof enough that Rome's gods work. Then he is answered and won over. What makes the Octavius unusual is that its author gives the other side the better speech. Caecilius' half is the fullest surviving statement of the educated pagan case against Christianity. A century later, in a different register again, came Arnobius, an African rhetorician at Sicca. He wrote seven furious books Against the Heathen. If Jerome is to be believed, he wrote them to persuade his own bishop that his conversion was real. Along the way he left the most detailed account of Roman religious practice that any Christian left. He had to describe what he was attacking. Between them sits Commodian, whom no one can date to within three centuries. He wrote eighty short poems in acrostic verse that does not scan by the old rules. They speak to ordinary people, half lesson in the faith and half warning. And at the end of the line stands Lactantius, whom Jerome makes Arnobius' pupil. He was a professor of Latin rhetoric, called to Diocletian's new capital at Nicomedia. He was still teaching there when the persecution broke out. He wrote the Divine Institutes in the middle of it. He was old by the time it was over, when Constantine brought him to Trier to teach his son. What these five share is not a place or a generation. Each had to make the argument in Latin, in words that had not been built to carry it. Tertullian came first and left the deepest mark; the others came after him.
