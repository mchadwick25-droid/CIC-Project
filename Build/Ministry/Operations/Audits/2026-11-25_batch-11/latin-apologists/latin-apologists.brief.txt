On a holiday from the courts, at the grape harvest, three friends walked the shore at Ostia, the seaside town near Rome, in the early morning. By the road to the sea stood an image of the god Serapis, and Caecilius lifted his hand to his mouth and kissed it toward the god. Octavius, a Christian, rounded on their friend Minucius Felix for letting a friend bow to anointed stones, and the three sat down on the breakwater to have it out.

Minucius, a pleader, a lawyer who argued cases in the courts, tells it in a dialogue, the Octavius. He was one of the Latin Apologists, Christians of Rome and Roman Africa who, from around 200 until the persecution under Diocletian, argued for their faith in Latin before the educated people who despised it. An apology was a defense speech made to judges for the accused. Theirs answered the charges against Christians out of the pagans' own books.

Minucius gives the pagan his full case. The old religion, Caecilius holds, is ancient and useful, and the Christians are "A people that lurks and shuns the light, silent in public, chattering in corners." By common report, he says, a newcomer is made to kill an infant hidden in meal, and their feasts end in incest once a dog tied to the lamp puts out the light.

Octavius calls these fables no one ever looked into. He and Minucius had believed them too, and as lawyers had defended the worst criminals while they tortured Christians to make them deny. Christians, he says, keep one marriage and eat sober meals, and they love one another as brothers because they do not know how to hate. No temple holds God, who is unseen and everywhere, and the offering he wants is a good mind. Octavius turns the philosophers' own weapons on him, and at last Caecilius says Octavius has conquered him, and he has conquered his error. The rest can wait, for the sun is setting, and they part glad. Minucius wrote it after Octavius died, at a date no one knows.

At Carthage, around 200, Tertullian wrote his Apology to the governors who judged Christians. He met the same charges, and said torture was used to force a denial, never a confession. Christians, he wrote, prayed for the emperors, and each month gave freely to bury the poor and keep orphans, a love that marked them out to people who hated one another. Later, driven, Jerome says, by the envy and insults of the Roman clergy, he joined the New Prophecy, whose followers held that the Holy Spirit now gave a stricter rule. Writing for it, he judged a second marriage unlawful.

In Diocletian's reign Arnobius taught rhetoric, the art of public speaking, at Sicca in Africa. His turning is known only from Jerome, who wrote about eighty years later that dreams drove him toward belief, and the bishop would not take him in. "He could not win from the bishop the faith he had always attacked. So he laboured by lamplight over most brilliant books against his old religion. At last, as if with hostages for his devotion, he won the treaty." His seven books were his pledge, and he was let in. In them he owns his past: "Whenever I had seen a stone made slick and smeared with olive oil, I fawned on it as if a power were present in it. I spoke to it, and begged favours from a block that felt nothing." Now he asked why Christian writings were burned and their meeting places torn down, where prayer was made for magistrates, armies, kings, friends and enemies.

Arnobius's pupil, Jerome says, was Lactantius, called to teach rhetoric at Nicomedia, a Greek city in Bithynia. He found few pupils and turned to writing, and was teaching there, he says, when the church was thrown down. He had read those who came before him, and looked back on them. Truth, Lactantius wrote, had lacked fit heralds. Tertullian's Apology had pleaded the cause in full, but answering accusers was one thing and teaching the whole faith another, and he took on the second.

Of the defenders he knew, he wrote, Minucius Felix had stood well among the pleaders, and his Octavius showed how fit a champion of truth he might have been, had he given himself wholly to it.
