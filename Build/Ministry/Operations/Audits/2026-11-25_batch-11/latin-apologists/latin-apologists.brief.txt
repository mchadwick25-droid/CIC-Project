On a court holiday at the grape harvest, three friends walked the shore at Ostia, near Rome, early one morning. By the road to the sea, Caecilius saw an image of the god Serapis and lifted his hand to his mouth to kiss it toward the god. Octavius, a Christian, rounded on their friend Minucius Felix for letting him bow to anointed stones, and the three sat on the breakwater to have it out.

Minucius, a pleader, or court lawyer, tells it in his dialogue the Octavius. He was one of the Latin Apologists, Christians of Rome and Roman Africa who, from around 200 to the persecution under Diocletian, defended their faith in Latin to the educated people who despised it. An apology was a defense speech for the accused, and theirs answered the charges out of the pagans' own books.

Minucius gives the pagan his full case. The old religion is ancient and useful, Caecilius holds, and the Christians are "A people that lurks and shuns the light, silent in public, chattering in corners." By common report, he says, newcomers must kill an infant hidden in meal, and their feasts end in incest when a dog tied to the lamp puts out the light.

Octavius calls them fables no one looked into. As lawyers, he and Minucius had believed them too, defending the worst criminals while they tortured Christians to make them deny. Christians, he says, keep one marriage, eat sober meals, and love one another as brothers, because they do not know how to hate. No temple holds God, who is unseen and everywhere, and the offering he wants is a good mind. Octavius fights with the philosophers' own weapons, and Caecilius yields: Octavius has conquered him, and he has conquered his error. The rest can wait, for the sun is setting, and they part glad. Minucius wrote it after Octavius died, at a date no one knows.

At Carthage around 200, Tertullian met the same charges in his Apology to the governors who judged Christians. He said torture was meant to force denial, not confession. Christians prayed for the emperors, he wrote, and gave each month to bury the poor and keep orphans, a love that marked them out to people who hated one another. Later, driven by the Roman clergy's envy and insults, Jerome says, he joined the New Prophecy, which held that the Holy Spirit now gave a stricter rule, and he judged a second marriage unlawful.

In Diocletian's reign Arnobius taught rhetoric, the art of public speaking, at Sicca in Africa. His turning rests on Jerome alone, who wrote about eighty years later that dreams drove him toward belief, and the bishop would not take him in. "He could not win from the bishop the faith he had always attacked. So he laboured by lamplight over most brilliant books against his old religion. At last, as if with hostages for his devotion, he won the treaty." His seven books were his pledge, and he was let in. In them he owns his past: "Whenever I had seen a stone made slick and smeared with olive oil, I fawned on it as if a power were present in it. I spoke to it, and begged favours from a block that felt nothing." He asked why Christian writings were burned and meeting places torn down, where prayer was made for magistrates, armies, kings, friends and enemies.

Arnobius's pupil, Jerome says, was Lactantius, called to teach rhetoric at Nicomedia, a Greek city in Bithynia. Finding few pupils, he turned to writing, and was teaching there, he says, when the church was thrown down. Looking back on those before him, Lactantius wrote that truth had lacked fit heralds. Tertullian's Apology had pleaded the cause in full, but answering accusers was one thing and teaching the whole faith another, and he took on the second.

Of the defenders he knew, he wrote, Minucius Felix had stood well among the pleaders, and his Octavius showed how fit a champion of truth he might have been, had he given himself wholly to it.
