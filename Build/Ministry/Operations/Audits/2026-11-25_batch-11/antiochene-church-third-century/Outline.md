# Outline: The Church of Antioch in the Third Century

Census id `antiochene-church-third-century`. Kind: tradition (`kinds/tradition.md`). Source lines are in `Source_Sheet.md` (codes such as C6, L5) and the files it names under `cic/texts/`. Job (slate, 2026-11-25): the church told through its fight with its own bishop, Paul of Samosata; ending with Lucian's circle of students, reached through what passed from the fight.

## The heart

- **The essence:** In the later third century the Christians of Antioch, in Roman Syria, were one church under a bishop, with presbyters and deacons, bound by letters to churches across the empire. When their own bishop, Paul of Samosata, was charged with denying that the Son of God had come down from heaven, the bishops of the East met again and again to put him out. (C7, T2 E-Eng 42173-42175; M5 E-Eng 42003-42006; M6 E-Eng 42298-42299; G3-G5.)
- **Who they were:** "the church of Antioch"; the bishops call it "the parish" and "the church of God" (E-Eng 42041, 42026). Spoken name: The Church of Antioch in the Third Century (census `name`).
- **When and where:** Antioch on the Orontes, Roman Syria, with "the neighboring cities and nations" around it (E-Eng 42025); from Paul's becoming bishop (S1, S2) to Lucian's death under Maximinus (L1-L3).
- **What they faced:** their own bishop's teaching and rule, which the other bishops could not dislodge for years (G5-G7); then a bishop who would not leave the church's house until an emperor ruled (A1); then persecution (L2, L3).
- **What they believed:** as the bishops give it, that the Son of God came down from heaven (T2), and that a bishop must be an example to the people (E-Eng 42228-42230). Paul's side survives only in his enemies' words (T1-T4).
- **How they practiced:** psalms sung to Christ, and Easter kept with singing in the church (C6); a bishop on a seat in the assembly, the people listening "reverently and orderly as in the house of God" (C4); meetings of bishops; letters of communion sent and received (M6); Scripture studied and expounded by learned presbyters (L1, L16); Greek learning held by a presbyter who ran a school (M2).
- **What held them together:** the communion of bishops by letter: who wrote to whom decided who belonged (M6), and in the end decided who held the church's house (A1). And the faith in Christ come down from heaven, which they sang (C6, T2).
- **What came of them:** Lucian's circle of pupils, who led the party that would oppose Nicaea's word (L5-L7, L14, L15); a church of Paul's followers still in being at Nicaea (Nic 5296-5306); the council's refusal of "homoousios", argued over in the 350s (Source sheet, Impact 2).

## Setting

- **Century and years:** Paul became bishop when Demetrianus died, about the time Dionysius became bishop of Rome (S1); Jerome's Chronicle puts it in Gallienus's seventh year (S2). Say "around 260". The last synod is given no year (D1). Lucian died under Maximinus (L1); say "in the persecution under the emperor Maximinus".
- **Place:** Antioch, in Roman Syria (census region "Antioch on the Orontes, and its Syrian hinterland"). Nicomedia, "where at that time the emperor happened to be staying" (L3). Tarsus, where Firmilian died on the road (G7).
- **Tradition:** the church of Antioch, with its bishop, presbyters and deacons and the churches round it (S3).

## Openings

1. **The Easter singing (recommended).** The bishops' letter says that Paul stopped the psalms sung to Christ and had women sing psalms to himself in church at Easter (Q1). Those who sang to him called him an angel come down from heaven (C7). Then the essence: this was the bishop of Antioch, as the bishops' letter tells it. (C6, C7; E-Gk 11896-11910.)
2. **The empty road at Tarsus.** The bishops sat at Antioch calling for Firmilian, who had come twice and had set out a third time; at Tarsus he died, and they were still waiting (G7). Weaker for the first minute, since Paul is not yet in the scene.

## Story beats

1. **Paul becomes bishop, around 260, and what he taught.** When Demetrianus died, Paul of Samosata became bishop (S1, S2; Documented; "around 260" Widely Accepted from S2). Eusebius: he held that Christ was "in his nature a common man" (T1). The bishops: he would not say the Son of God came down from heaven, and said "Jesus Christ is from below" (T2; Documented as the charge). Explain it: his teaching about Christ, in the bishops' terms, a Christ who did not come down from heaven but was a man from below (T1, T2). Say once, here, inside the sentence about the letter: almost all we know comes from the letter his judges sent out, which Eusebius of Caesarea copied into his history (E-Eng 41997-42001). Confidence: Documented as the charge; Paul's own teaching Contested.
2. **The bishops come, and come again.** Bishops hurried from every side as against a man ravaging Christ's flock (G3, Eusebius, in paraphrase); they met often, Paul's side hiding his teaching (G5). The aged bishop of Alexandria could not come, being old and weak, and would not address Paul, but wrote to the whole church (G1, G2; a clause). [cut: card] Firmilian of Caesarea in Cappadocia came twice and condemned his new teachings; Paul promised to change; Firmilian believed him and put off the matter (G6). He set out a third time, reached Tarsus, and died while the bishops waited (G7). Explain it: "synod" glossed here as a meeting of bishops. Reason for the meetings: his teaching (T1, T2). Confidence: Documented.
3. **What the bishops charged, and what the people did.** In the bishops' words: once poor, now rich from the people's lawsuits (C1) [cut: card]; called himself by an emperor's official's title, ducenarius, rather than bishop (C2) [cut: card]; built himself a high throne and a curtained private room, like a magistrate (C3); scolded those who would not cheer and wave handkerchiefs as in the theatre (C4); kept women in his house and let his clergy do the same, though the bishops grant he may have done nothing wanton (C8) [cut: card]. Everyone grieved in private, and no one dared accuse him (C9). Explain it: each charge in its own words; every one is the bishops' charge and is told as theirs. Confidence: Documented as the charge.
4. **Malchion and the last synod.** Malchion, a presbyter who ran Antioch's school of Greek learning, questioned Paul while shorthand writers took the debate down; Eusebius says he alone could catch out a man who hid his mind (M2; a judgment, credited to Eusebius). A last meeting of many bishops cut Paul off from the whole church (M1). They named Domnus, son of the earlier bishop, and asked every church to write to him and take letters of communion from him (M6). [cut: card] Reason given: he sets himself against God and refuses to obey (M6, the bishops, in paraphrase). Explain it: excommunication and letters of communion, in the letter's terms (M6). Confidence: Documented; the year is not told (D1).
5. **The house and the emperor.** Paul would not leave the church's house (A1; no reason given, none added). The emperor Aurelian, who defeated Zenobia, queen of the East, near Antioch (S6, Jerome's Chronicle; a clause, Cut first 5) [cut: Leave out], was petitioned. He ordered the house given to those to whom the bishops of Italy and Rome wrote (A1, Greek). Paul was driven out by the worldly power (A1, Eusebius, in paraphrase). Explain it: the ruling, plainly: the emperor did not judge the teaching; he gave the building to the side the bishops of Italy and Rome wrote to (A1, ἐπιστέλλοιεν); the listener links this to the letters of communion of beat 4. Why he so ruled: Eusebius says only that he judged most fittingly (A1, αἰσιώτατα, E-Gk 12118; Eusebius, in paraphrase); add no motive. Confidence: Documented.
6. **Lucian.** A presbyter of Antioch studied the Scriptures so closely that, long after, in Jerome's day, copies still bore his name (L1, Jerome). The bishop of Alexandria who later fought Arius charged that Lucian was Paul's successor and stayed out of communion with the bishops for many years (L4), and that Arius and his party sprang from 'the same stock' (Thdt 5772-5775; an enemy's charge, credited, told in its own content, in about six words). In the persecution under the emperor Maximinus he was taken to the emperor at Nicomedia, defended his faith, and was imprisoned and killed (L2, L3; Eusebius). John Chrysostom, in a sermon on Lucian two generations later, told how he answered under torture (Q3; credited). Explain it: why he died: for his faith, defended before the ruler (L3). Confidence: Documented; the answer is Chrysostom's account.

## What came of it

1. **Lucian's pupils led the party that would oppose Nicaea's word.** Arius calls Eusebius of Nicomedia "fellow-Lucianist" (L5); Philostorgius lists pupils who became bishops of Nicomedia, Chalcedon, Nicaea, Antioch and Tarsus (L6); three of them taught Aetius (L7). The bishops at Antioch in 341 claimed a creed of Lucian's, which Sozomen doubted (L14); Asian bishops under the emperor Valens still held to creeds "maintained by Lucian, the martyr" against "consubstantial" (L15). Defended: the Nicene side kept his feast (L11) and Constantine named a city after his mother where Lucian was buried, "in honour of the martyr Lucian" (L10).
2. **The council's refusal of "homoousios".** In the 350s, bishops wary of Nicaea's word said the council against Paul had refused it; Athanasius, Hilary and Basil answered, each with a different reason (Source sheet Impact 2; D3). For the card.
3. **Paul's followers kept a church.** Nicaea ordered "Paulianists" rebaptized and their blameless clergy ordained again (Nic 5296-5306). For the card.
4. Who keeps this today: no held source or tree entry says. Leave it out.

## Connection to carry into the story

What passed from this church to the Arian party was a teacher and his pupils. Lucian was a presbyter of the same church. An enemy of Arius called Lucian Paul's successor, who stood out of communion for years (L4); that is a charge, and the listener must hear it as one. Lucian's pupils then became bishops across the East (L6), and Arius and Eusebius of Nicomedia greeted each other as "fellow-Lucianist" (L5, Q2). The listener needs to understand that the link is the teacher, through the pupils' own word for themselves. Only Arius's enemy Alexander says the teaching ran on from Paul through Lucian to Arius. He calls Arius and his party 'offsets of the same stock' (Thdt 5772-5775). The story tells this as his charge, never in the narrator's voice. It comes through the sequence of what people did: Lucian studied and taught, died for his faith, and his pupils named themselves after him.

## Ending

1. **Recommended: the pupils.** A historian on Arius's side says some of his pupils became bishops of Nicomedia, Nicaea and Antioch itself (L6). Driven out of Alexandria by his bishop, one of them, Arius, wrote to another, Eusebius of Nicomedia, and ended with Q2. (L5, L6; Thdt 6005-6026 for the occasion; Epiph-Gk 45744-45746; Thdt 6063-6064; Philost 138.) It ends on the circle in its own words, as the slate asks.
2. **Alternative: Lucian's one answer.** End on Q3 and move the pupils before his death, as his pupils (L6). Weaker for the connection, since the pupils' own word (Q2) is lost or moved before the death it follows.

Considered and set aside: closing on the 350s quarrel over "homoousios". It falls after the pupils in time, so the story would have to end on later writers; and its three witnesses disagree on the reason (D3), which needs about 90 words to tell fairly. It goes to the card.
