The bishops who judged Paul of Samosata, bishop of Antioch, wrote that he had changed the singing in his own church. "He stopped the psalms to our Lord Jesus Christ, as if they were too new, the work of newer men. But on the great day of Easter he trains women to sing psalms to himself, in the middle of the church. Anyone who heard them would shudder." Those who sang to him, they added, called him an angel come down from heaven.

In the later third century the Christians of Antioch, in Roman Syria, were one church under a bishop, with presbyters, its priests, and deacons, bound by letters to churches across the empire. When their own bishop was charged with denying that the Son of God came down from heaven, the bishops of the East met again and again to put him out.

Paul became bishop around 260. Almost all we know of him comes from the letter his judges sent out, which Eusebius of Caesarea copied into his history of the church. They wrote that Paul called Jesus Christ a man from below, and Eusebius says he held that Christ was by nature an ordinary man.

Bishops hurried to Antioch from every side, Eusebius says, as against a man ravaging Christ's flock, and they met there often while Paul's side hid what he taught. Firmilian, bishop of Caesarea in Cappadocia, came twice and condemned his new teaching, but when Paul promised to change, Firmilian believed him and put the matter off. On his third journey he died at Tarsus, while the bishops waited for him.

The letter's other charges are the bishops' own. Paul, they wrote, built himself a high throne and a curtained private room, like a magistrate. He scolded those who would not cheer and wave handkerchiefs as in the theater, when the people should listen with reverence, as in the house of God. A bishop, they held, must be an example to the people. Everyone grieved in private, and no one dared accuse him.

At last Malchion, a presbyter who ran the city's school of Greek learning, questioned Paul while shorthand writers took the debate down, and Eusebius says he alone could catch out a man who hid his mind. A final meeting of many bishops cut Paul off from the whole church, because, they wrote, he set himself against God and refused to obey. No church was to send him letters of communion, the letters bishops exchanged to show they shared one church.

Paul would not leave the church's house. The emperor Aurelian was petitioned, and he ordered the house given to those to whom the bishops of Italy and Rome wrote. He left the teaching alone and let those bishops' letters decide, and Paul was driven out by the worldly power. Eusebius says only that the emperor judged most fittingly.

Lucian, a presbyter of the same church, studied the Scriptures so closely that long after, a Latin writer of the late fourth century says, copies still bore his name. The bishop of Alexandria who later fought Arius charged that Lucian was Paul's successor and stayed out of communion for many years, and that Arius and his party came from the same stock. In the persecution under the emperor Maximinus, Lucian defended his faith before the emperor at Nicomedia and was imprisoned and killed. Two generations later, John Chrysostom told in a sermon how he answered under torture. "To each question he answered only, I am a Christian. When the torturer asked, What is your homeland? I am a Christian, he said. What is your trade? I am a Christian. Who are your parents? To all of it he said, I am a Christian."

A historian on Arius's side says that some of Lucian's pupils became bishops. Driven out of Alexandria by his bishop, Arius wrote to one of them, Eusebius of Nicomedia, and ended the letter with their own name for the pupils of Lucian. "I pray you fare well in the Lord, remembering our troubles, fellow-Lucianist."
