In 268 or thereabouts, the bishops of Syria and Asia Minor met at Antioch to deal with their own metropolitan, for the third time in a few years. Paul of Samosata was bishop of the greatest city in the Roman East. The council that removed him says he liked to be called ducenarius, a salaried official's title, probably held under the Palmyrene rulers of the city. The charges mix doctrine with deportment. He had a raised throne built for himself in church, he stopped the old hymns and had new ones sung to himself, and women in his household gave scandal. The teaching at issue was that Christ was a man in whom the Word had come to dwell, not the Word made flesh. The reading was later called adoptionist. The council could not make its removal of him stick, because the civil power was Palmyrene and Paul had its favor. He went only when Aurelian retook the city in 272. The emperor settled who owned the church building by asking which claimant was in communion with the bishops of Italy and Rome. It was the first time a Roman emperor ruled on a Christian dispute. The letter announcing the deposition was drafted by Malchion, who ran a school of rhetoric in the city and had cross-examined Paul in front of shorthand writers. A generation later, in the same city, a presbyter named Lucian worked over the text of the Greek Bible. He taught a circle of students, Arius among them, who afterwards used his name as a badge.
