On 28 August 392, at Hippo in Roman Africa, a Manichaean priest named Fortunatus debated Augustine, then a priest of the city's church. Augustine had once been one of them, and Fortunatus called on him as a witness to how they lived: "You were at our prayer." Augustine answered that he had been only a Hearer, never one of the Elect, and that at their prayer he had seen nothing shameful, only that they prayed facing the sun.

They were the followers of Mani, a prophet of Babylonia. From the 200s they held that Light and Darkness were two powers without beginning, now mixed in the world, and that in the end the two would be parted again. A few, the Elect, lived in strict purity and poverty, while the many, the Hearers, lived in the world and fed and served them. Hunted by Persian kings and Roman emperors, they spread from Mesopotamia west to Roman Africa and east to the Turks and China.

Mani was born in 216 in Babylonia, in Mesopotamia, which Persia then ruled. He wrote a book for King Shapur of Persia, and Biruni, a Muslim scholar of about 1000, quotes how it begins. Mani says there that God's messengers brought wisdom and deeds to people from age to age: the Buddha to India, Zoroaster to Persia, Jesus to the West. Now, in this last age, the revelation had come down through Mani himself, God's messenger to Babylonia. He said he was the Paraclete, the Helper Jesus had promised, and he wrote his own scriptures.

His followers held that they must harm no animal, fire, water or plant. The Elect kept from marriage, owned only a day's food and a year's clothing, and went about preaching. The Hearers gave a tenth of what they had, fasted, married one wife and served the Elect. The Hearers' own confession says that the Light was in the food they ate, and that their sins sent it "to the Bad Place." Augustine says the Hearers carried food to the Elect, and mocks it: their stomachs, he says, were meant to make "angels and gods" out of it, who would then set the Hearers free.

Biruni says that King Bahram had Mani sought, and gives the king's reason: "This man has come forward calling people to destroy the world." A writer in Egypt about 300 heard instead that Mani died under Shapur, after offending him in some way. Mani died at the king's hands, but the accounts differ on how. Some say he was flayed and his skin hung at the city gate. A book by one of his own pupils, which Biruni reports, says that he failed to cure a relative of the king and was chained in prison until he died.

His followers kept the day he died as their great feast, the Bema. Augustine says that when he asked why, they told him the day to keep was the death of one who had truly suffered, since Christ, they held, was never born in real flesh and only seemed to suffer. After Mani's death, says the Fihrist, a Baghdad scholar's book of the 900s, the king killed his followers wherever he found them, and they fled east into the lands of the Turks.

In 372 the Roman emperors ordered that wherever their meetings were found, the teachers be heavily fined, the members shut out of society and the houses seized, for teaching what the law called a profane doctrine. Faustus of Milevis, a bishop of theirs, wrote a book of answers, and Augustine, a Hearer for nine years, kept his words, setting out each part of the book before he answered it. Faustus wrote: "If you press me whether I accept the gospel, I may answer: I have left all that was mine. Father, mother, wife, children, gold, silver, eating, drinking, comforts, pleasures. Take this as answer enough to your questions. And believe that you will be blessed, if you do not stumble because of me."

Christians brought Faustus before the governor's court, and once he was found to be a Manichaean, he was exiled to an island. Augustine calls this the lightest of penalties, given because those same Christians pleaded for him.

Far to the east, in 732, a Chinese emperor's order called Mani's teaching a perverse belief that falsely took the name of Buddhism and deceived the people, and forbade it. In 843 an edict closed their temples. A Chinese chronicle written four centuries later says that some seventy of their women religious at the capital all died.

Near the oasis of Dunhuang, a cave library kept a scroll written in Mani's script, in a Turkish tongue. It is the Hearers' confession, and one of its articles begins: "Every day, how many evil thoughts we think! How many wretched words we speak that should not be spoken! How many deeds we do that should not be done!" Each article ends the same way: my God, cleansing ourselves of sin, we pray, forgive my sin.
