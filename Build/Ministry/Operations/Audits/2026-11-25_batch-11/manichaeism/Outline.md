# Outline: Manichaeism

Census id `manichaeism` (census name Manichaeism; spoken name "the followers of Mani"). Kind: tradition (`kinds/tradition.md`). Source codes (A1, B4, C7, BIR, FIH ...) are rows and files in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives each file and line. Job (slate, 2026-11-25): the followers of Mani, a church with its own scriptures and its Elect and Hearers, followed from Mesopotamia across the Roman Empire and east along the Silk Road, hunted by Persian king and Roman emperor alike, and told as they held their faith.

## The heart

- **The essence:** From the 200s, the followers of Mani, a prophet of Babylonia, held that Light and Darkness were two powers without beginning, now mixed in the world, and that in the end the two would be parted again. The Elect lived lives of strict purity and poverty; the Hearers lived in the world and fed and served them. Hunted by Persian kings and Roman emperors, they spread from Mesopotamia west to Roman Africa and east to the Turks and China. (A1, B4, C1, C4-C6, B11, B13, B22.)
- **Who they were:** the followers of Mani, who called him the Paraclete, the Helper Jesus promised (B3); the Romans called them Manichaeans (A5). Two ranks: the Elect and the Hearers (C4, C5, B19).
- **When and where:** from Mani's birth in Babylonia in 216 (A1) and his preaching under the first Sasanian kings (A2, A4); in Egypt by about 300 (B12); in Roman Africa in the 370s-400s (B14-B21); among the Turks and in China from the 700s (B22-B26); scattered remnants about 1000 (B28, B29).
- **What they faced:** Mani's death at the hands of the Persian king, and the hunting of his followers after it (B9-B11); Roman laws against their meetings, teachers and houses (B13, B16, B21), and Faustus's exile (B20); a Chinese edict in 732 and the closing of their temples in 843 (B22, B26).
- **What they believed:** the line of messengers ending in Mani (B4; BIR 29054-29061, told in the narrator's words); two Roots, Light and Darkness, mixed and to be parted (C1, C2, C13); no harm to animals, fire, water or plants (C3); the Light bound up in the food they ate (KHU 17364-17371). In their own words where held: Q2 (an Elect), Q3 (the Hearers); Mani's claim told by the narrator (B4).
- **How they practiced:** prayer four or seven times a day facing the sun (C8, B19); the Elect's poverty, celibacy and preaching (C4, C7); the Hearers' tithe, fasts, one wife and service to the Elect (C5, C10); the Hearers' confession, a prayer for forgiveness (C9; KHU 15390, 17443-17449); the Bema, the feast of Mani's death (C11); books written by Mani himself (B5, B6).
- **What held them together:** the bond of Elect and Hearers, the Hearers serving and feeding the Elect (C5; Augustine, NPNF 6343-6346), with the Light, in their own confession, bound up in the food they ate (KHU 17364-17371); and Mani's own books, the last revelation in a line of messengers (B4-B6).
- **What came of them:** their books and prayers survived, east and west (E1); the faith lasted centuries among the Turks and in China (E2); Augustine kept their words in his replies (E3).

## Setting

- **Century and years:** Mani born in Babylonia in 216 (A1; Widely Accepted). By his own book as Biruni gives it, he was born in the fourth year of King Adharban, whom Biruni takes for Ardawan, the last of the Ashkanians, the house Ardashir overthrew; by Mani's thirteenth year Ardashir was King of Kings (A1, A2; BIR 29094-29100, 12373-12378; "Ardawan, the last" closes Biruni's table of the Ashkanian kings, 11939, 12068; Ardashir took the empire "out of the hands of the Ashkanians", 12293-12295; own words via a later witness, Documented as Biruni's account; Biruni's identification of Adharban is his own "I believe"). So the story does not say Persia ruled Babylonia at his birth, and it needs no name for Adharban's house: the next sentence's "King Shapur of Persia" carries Persia. ("Parthian" is not Biruni's word; it stays out.) His first revelation "at twelve" (B2; A2). Under King Shapur (A4, B4), then, by Biruni's and the Fihrist's account, King Bahram, under whom he died; Alexander, writing about 300, puts the death under Shapur (B10, ALX 24496-24498; the 270s is modern reckoning, so say "under King Bahram"). Egypt "by about 300" (B12; Alexander writes then). Roman laws: 372 and 399 (B13, B21). Hippo: August 392 (B17). China: 732, 768, 806, 843 (B22-B26). Biruni "about the year 1000" (BIR, title note; Widely Accepted).
- **Place:** Babylonia, in Mesopotamia, the land between the Tigris and Euphrates (A1, A3); Gundeshapur, a city (B10; BIR 29126 "the gate of Gundisapur"; FIH 5115 "der Stadt Dschundisäbür"); Hippo and Carthage in Roman Africa, today's Algeria and Tunisia (B15, B17); Samarkand in Central Asia (B27); the Chinese capital (B26); Dunhuang, an oasis town in China's far west (B30).
- **Tradition:** a church of its own, with Mani's books, the Elect and the Hearers (B5, C4, C5). It called its founder the Paraclete and claimed the gospel (B3, C7); its enemies called it a heresy (A5) and the Chinese edict a false Buddhism (B22). The story does not settle what it was.

## Shape

## Openings

1. **(Chosen.) Hippo, 28 August 392.** In the Baths of Sossius, before the people, a Manichaean priest named Fortunatus debated Augustine, who had once been one of them (B17). Fortunatus called on him as witness to their way of life (CFo Latin 7658-7663): "You were at our prayer" (B18). Augustine said he had been only a Hearer, not one of the Elect; at their prayer he had seen nothing shameful, only that they prayed facing the sun (B19). Narrated, no quotation marks except Fortunatus's three words if wanted. Then the essence.
2. **(Alternate.) The king's book.** Mani wrote a book for King Shapur, and it began with the line of messengers (B4). Use if the Hippo scene is judged too Augustine-led; the messengers then move from beat 2 to the opening, still in the narrator's words.

## Story beats

1. **Babylonia, the 200s: the baptizers and the call.** Mani was born in Babylonia in 216 (A1, own words via Biruni; Widely Accepted for the year). The Fihrist, written by a Baghdad scholar in the 900s from Manichaean tradition, says his father had joined the baptizers of the marsh country (B1). Credit the Fihrist here, once, as late. At twelve, by Mani's own account as it gives it, an angel he called at-Taum, the Companion, told him to leave them: he did not belong among them; his task was purity and the taming of desire. At twenty-four the angel told him to come out and preach (B2). Explain it: the baptizers (Words); the break, in the angel's words as the reason (B2). Confidence: Documented as the Fihrist's account; the angel stays in Mani's voice.
2. **The king and the books.** Told in the narrator's words, not quoted (Mark's Length ruling): He wrote a book for King Shapur of Persia. Biruni, a Muslim scholar of about 1000, quotes how it begins. Mani says there that God's messengers brought wisdom and deeds to people from age to age: the Buddha to India, Zoroaster to Persia, Jesus to the West. Now, in this last age, the revelation had come down through Mani himself, God's messenger to Babylonia (B4; BIR 29054-29061). This is Biruni's first credit; beat 4 then says only "Biruni says". He said he was the Paraclete, the Helper Jesus had promised (B3; BIR 29061-29064), and he wrote his own scriptures (B6; BIR 29112-29115: "his gospel, the Shaburkan, ... the Book of the Giants"; a later witness, about 700 years on, who says he had read some of Mani's books, BIR 29090; Documented). The book for Shapur, the Shaburkan, is the one the story describes; it names no other. Seven books, six in Syriac and one in Persian, the Fihrist says (B5). The sources differ on the king: the Fihrist says Shapur honored him and let his disciples go freely through the realm (B7); one of Biruni's informants told him that Shapur banished Mani under Zoroaster's law, which orders false prophets expelled (B8; BIR 29161-29167). Credit each. Explain it: the Paraclete. Confidence: Mani's claim Documented, as his own book gives it through Biruni; the king's response Contested.
3. **Elect and Hearers.** They held that Light and Darkness had no beginning (C1), and that they must harm no animal, fire, water or plant (C3). The Elect owned a day's food and a year's clothing, kept from marriage and went about preaching (C4); the Hearers gave a tenth of what they had, fasted, married one wife and served the Elect (C5). Whoever loved the faith but could not tame desire was to serve it and the Elect (C6). They prayed four or seven times a day, turned toward the sun (C8). In their own confession the Hearers held that the Light was in the food they ate, and that their sins sent it 'to the Bad Place' (KHU 17364-17371, 17443). Augustine says the Hearers carried food to the Elect, and mocks it: the Elect's stomachs, he says, were meant to make 'angels and gods' from it, by whom the Hearers would be set free (NPNF 6343-6346; Latin 5730-5733). Confidence: Documented, each as its witness gives it; the Elect's part is Augustine's report. Explain it: Elect, Hearers, the alms, from both sides.
4. **Mani's death and the hunt in Persia.** Biruni says King Bahram had him sought, and gives the king's reason: "This man has come forward calling people to destroy the world" (B9). ~~A writer in Egypt about 300 heard instead that Mani died under Shapur, after offending him in some way (ALX 24496-24498).~~ (Cut first 29: card, with its dispute.) Bahram had him killed; the accounts differ on how: flayed and his skin hung at the city gate, or, in a book by one of his own pupils that Biruni reports, chained in prison till he died, after he failed to cure a relative of the king (BIR 29132-29138). Credit each. His followers kept the day he died as their great feast, the Bema, with a platform of five steps covered in precious cloth, set before the worshippers; Augustine, who had kept it, says they kept Easter with little care but the Bema with great honor (C11). When Augustine asked why, they told him the day to keep was the death of one who had truly suffered, since Christ, they held, was never born in real flesh and only seemed to suffer (CEF Latin 12879-12883). After his death, says the Fihrist, a Baghdad scholar's book of the 900s, the king killed his followers wherever he found them, and they fled east across the river of Balkh into the lands of the Turks (B11). Explain it: the king's reason in his own words (B9); the Bema. Confidence: the death Documented, its manner Contested; the flight Documented as the Fihrist's account.
5. **West: the Roman Empire.** A Christian historian of the early 300s says Mani's teaching had come "from Persia to our part of the world" (A5); in Egypt, about 300, two teachers were spreading it (B12). In 372 the emperors ordered that wherever their meetings were found, the teachers be heavily fined, the members shut out of society and the houses seized, for teaching a profane doctrine (B13). Faustus of Milevis, a bishop of theirs whom Augustine, a Hearer for nine years, had met at Carthage (B14, B15), wrote a book answering their opponents' questions on the gospel and the Old Testament, and Augustine kept his words (Faustus's own words in his opponent's book, NPNF English printed 1887; Documented: CFa English 4314, I.2, he gives his people "brief and pointed replies to the captious objections of our adversaries"; the questions as he sets them, "Do I believe the gospel?" 4338, 4512, and "Do I believe the Old Testament?" 4471): **Q2** (C7; CFa English 4305-4312, 4314-4315). Christians brought Faustus and some others before the governor's court, and, found to be a Manichaean, he was exiled to an island. Augustine calls this the lightest of penalties, given because those same Christians pleaded for them. Not long after, a public decree freed them all together (B20; CFa Latin 16830-16840). In 399 a law for Africa ordered them sought out, brought into public and punished most severely, with those who hid them in their houses (B21). Explain it: each law's reason in its own words; the exile's cause (B20). Confidence: Documented.
6. **East: the Turks and China.** In 732 a Chinese emperor's order called Mani's teaching a perverse belief that falsely took the name of Buddhism and deceived the people, and forbade it, though the foreigners from the West, whose own religion it was, might still keep it themselves (B22; CHI 6880-6886; near-contemporary, the *Tong tien* of 766-801; Documented). "The foreigners from the West" glosses itself; "the Hu" stays out of the story. An Uyghur khagan, a ruler of a Turkish people north of China, brought four of its teachers home, and his people's stone inscription says their master initiated the Uyghurs into "the true religion" (B23). Chinese decrees of 768 and 771 let the Uyghurs found temples in Chinese cities (B24). In 843 an edict closed their temples across the empire; a Chinese chronicle written four centuries later says that some seventy of their women religious at the capital all died (B26; credit the late chronicle). About the year 1000 Biruni wrote that most of the eastern Turks, and many in China and Tibet, still held Mani's law (B29). Explain it: the 732 reason in its own words; the 843 edict told as the text gives it, with no reason added. Confidence: Documented as each text gives it.

## What came of it (heaviest first)

1. Their own books and prayers outlived them: Mani's book quoted by Biruni about 1000 (B4), the Hearers' confession copied in Turkish (B30, C9), a Chinese summary of the teaching (C13). These are what later witnesses used to tell what Mani taught.
2. In the East the faith lasted for centuries: Uyghur rulers' favor and temples in Chinese cities (B23-B25), Samarkand's Manichaeans shielded by a Turkish ruler's threat in the 900s (B27), and Biruni's report about 1000 (B29).
3. Augustine kept their words: the Hippo debate's transcript (B17-B19) and Faustus's book set out "as if stated by himself" before each answer (CFa English 4312). So the fullest Latin statement of their case survives in his reply. Defended: their claim to keep the gospel (C7), by an enemy's hand.
4. The Syriac church answered them in their own language (Ephrem; sibling). Card only.
5. Their name became a charge flung at other dissenters (on `cathars-albigensians`, `pelagianism`). Card only.

Who keeps it today: none in a held source; Leave out.

## Connection to carry into the story

Augustine of Hippo was a Hearer for nine years (B14). Through him the West kept the Manichaeans' own words: at Hippo in 392 he testified, when their priest asked, that he had seen nothing shameful at their prayer (B19), and in his reply to Faustus he set out Faustus's own book "as if stated by himself" before answering each part (CFa 4312), so an Elect's account of his life survives (Q2). The listener must understand that the same man who argued against them is the witness who kept their voice in the West. This comes through what he did in the opening and in beat 5 (Faustus's words "kept" by Augustine), never as a sentence saying it matters. In the East the books passed on by themselves, through Turkish and Chinese believers (B23-B25, B30).

## Ending

1. **(Chosen.) The confession at Dunhuang.** Among the Buddhist books of a cave library near the oasis of Dunhuang, in China's far west, part of which an explorer acquired in 1907, lay a scroll nearly fifteen feet long, written in Mani's script in a Turkish tongue (B30). It is the Hearers' confession, and one of its articles begins: **Q3**. Then the refrain in plain words: "My God, cleansing ourselves of sin, we pray: forgive my sin" (KHU 17443-17449). End there.
2. **(Alternate.) Ibn al-Nadim's Baghdad.** He had known about three hundred of them in Baghdad; in his own day barely five were left there (B28). An end, told as he gives it.
