At Hippo in Roman Africa, in August 392, Augustine met Fortunatus in public debate. Fortunatus was a priest of the followers of Mani, and Augustine had once been one of them, so Fortunatus asked him to tell the people whether the charges against their way of life were true. Augustine said he had been only a Hearer, one who served the Elect, and at their prayer had seen nothing shameful, only that they prayed facing the sun.

Mani was a prophet of Babylonia, and from the 200s his followers held that two powers without beginning, Light and Darkness, were mixed in the world and would one day be parted. The few, the Elect, gave up marriage, meat, wine and property, owned only a day's food and a year's clothing, and went about preaching. The many, the Hearers, lived in the world, married once, gave a tenth of what they had and served the Elect. Hunted by Persian kings and Roman emperors, they spread west to Roman Africa and east to China. The Romans called them Manichaeans.

Mani was born in 216 in Babylonia, between the Tigris and the Euphrates, then ruled by Persia. The Fihrist, a book written in Baghdad in the 900s, says his father had joined the baptizers of the marsh country, known as those who wash themselves. When Mani was twelve, by his own account as the Fihrist gives it, an angel he called at-Taum, the Companion, told him to leave them, because his task was purity and the taming of desire.

He wrote a book for King Shapur of Persia, and it opens: "Wisdom and deeds have always been brought to people, from time to time, by the messengers of God. So in one age the messenger called the Buddha brought them to India. In another, Zoroaster brought them to Persia, and in another, Jesus brought them to the West. Then this revelation came down, this prophecy in this last age, through me, Mani, the messenger of the God of truth to Babylonia." He called himself the Paraclete, the Helper Jesus had promised, and wrote his own scriptures.

His followers were to harm no animal, fire, water or plant, and they prayed four or seven times a day. The Hearers' own confession says the Light was in the food they ate, and that their sins sent it "to the Bad Place." Augustine says the Hearers brought food to the Elect, and he mocks it: the Elect's stomachs, he says, were to make "angels and gods" of it, by whom the Hearers would be set free.

Biruni, a Muslim scholar writing about the year 1000, says King Bahram had Mani sought as a man who called people to destroy the world, while a writer in Egypt about 300 heard that Mani died under Shapur. He died at the king's hands, and the accounts differ on how. Some say he was flayed and his skin hung at the city gate. A pupil's book that Biruni reports says he was jailed after failing to cure a relative of the king, and died in chains. His followers kept that day as their great feast, the Bema, because, they told Augustine, it was the death of one who had truly suffered, while Christ was never born in real flesh and only seemed to suffer. After Mani's death, the Fihrist says, the king killed his followers wherever he found them, and they fled east into the lands of the Turks.

In 372 the Roman emperors ordered that wherever the Manichaeans met, their teachers be heavily fined, their members shut out of society and the houses seized, for teaching a profane doctrine. Faustus of Milevis, a bishop of theirs whom Augustine had met at Carthage, wrote a book of answers for his people against their opponents. Augustine, a Hearer for nine years, kept each answer in Faustus's own words in his reply. One reads: "If you press me whether I accept the gospel, I may answer: I have left all that was mine. Father, mother, wife, children, gold, silver, eating, drinking, comforts, pleasures. Take this as answer enough to your questions. And believe that you will be blessed, if you do not stumble because of me." Christians brought Faustus and some others before the governor's court, and when he was found to be a Manichaean he was exiled to an island. Augustine calls it the lightest of penalties, given because those same Christians pleaded for them, and soon a public decree freed them all.

In 732 a Chinese emperor forbade Mani's teaching as a perverse belief that falsely took the name of Buddhism and deceived the people. Since it was the religion of the Hu, the foreigners from the West, they were not to be punished for keeping it themselves. A ruler of the Uyghurs, a Turkish people north of China, brought four of its teachers home. In 843 an edict closed their temples across the empire, and a chronicle written four centuries later says that some seventy of their women religious at the capital all died.

Near the oasis of Dunhuang, in China's far west, a cave library of Buddhist books held a scroll in Mani's script and a Turkish tongue. It is the Hearers' confession, and one of its articles begins: "Every day, how many evil thoughts we think! How many wretched words we speak that should not be spoken! How many deeds we do that should not be done!" Each article ends the same way, its last words in Persian: my God, cleansing ourselves of sin, we pray, forgive my sin.
