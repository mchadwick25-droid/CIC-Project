## 1. The test, for a tradition

- **Who these people were:** filled. "the followers of Mani, a prophet of Babylonia", with the Elect and the Hearers set out.
- **What happened to them:** filled. Hunted in Persia after Mani's death, banned by Roman law in 372, Faustus exiled, banned in China in 732, temples closed in 843.
- **What their faith was about:** filled. Light and Darkness mixed and to be parted, Mani as the last messenger, Light held in food, harm to no living thing.
- **Why the connection matters:** filled, but thin. The listener hears that Augustine had been a Hearer, debated Fortunatus, and kept Faustus's words to answer them. They can say "Augustine was once one of them and argued against them." Nothing says what that quarrel left the wider church. This is not a block. The reviser may want one clause where Augustine answers Faustus.

## 2. The opening (first three sentences)

Who, where and when are all there: Fortunatus and Augustine, Hippo in Roman Africa, 28 August 392. Why they were there is half there. We hear it was a debate, but not what it was about. "Hearer" and "Elect" arrive in sentence three before anyone explains them. The next paragraph explains them, so the listener is not lost. MINOR.

## 3. Sentences that fail on one hearing

1. "The Hearers' own confession says that the Light was in the food they ate, and that their sins sent it 'to the Bad Place.'" The listener has never heard of this confession and cannot tell what it is. It is explained only in the last paragraph. How a sin "sends" Light anywhere is lost too. Fix: "In their own confession, a prayer said aloud by the Hearers, ..." MINOR.
2. "Augustine says the Hearers carried food to the Elect, and mocks it: their stomachs, he says, were meant to make 'angels and gods' out of it, who would then set the Hearers free." Whose stomachs is unclear (the Elect's), and so is what "who" points to. The idea that the Elect's eating freed the Light is lost. Fix: name "the Elect's stomachs". MINOR.
3. "A writer in Egypt about 300 heard instead that Mani died under Shapur ..." followed by "Mani died at the king's hands, but the accounts differ on how." After Bahram and Shapur, "the king" could be either. The listener cannot tell which king the narrator means. Fix: "Either way, Mani died at a king's hands, ..." MINOR, close to the thread of the story.
4. "Augustine says that when he asked why, they told him the day to keep was the death of one who had truly suffered, since Christ, they held, was never born in real flesh and only seemed to suffer." (38 words.) "Why" has no clear object. The contrast it needs, that they kept this day and not Easter, is never said, so the listener misses the point. Fix: "when he asked why this, and not Easter, was their great day, ..." MINOR.
5. The Faustus quote, ending "... if you do not stumble because of me." A listener with no church background will not hear that "I have left all that was mine" is the gospel's own demand, so Faustus's answer ("I live it, so I accept it") is lost. "Stumble because of me" is a biblical idiom and stays opaque. Fix: a plain-sense clause before the quote, e.g. "Faustus answered that he kept the gospel by living it:". MINOR.
6. "A Chinese chronicle written four centuries later says that some seventy of their women religious at the capital all died." "All died" leaves open whether they were killed, and "women religious" is church jargon. Fix: say how they died if the source says so, and use "nuns" or "women of the Elect". MINOR.

## 4. Bare statements (Part 1, Explain it)

1. **BLOCKING.** "He said he was the Paraclete, the Helper Jesus had promised, and he wrote his own scriptures." "His own scriptures" is a named thing with nothing said of what they were or why they mattered to his followers. Fix: say in a clause what the scriptures were (as the outline holds them), or cut "and he wrote his own scriptures." (The craft check also flags this sentence.)

The craft check flags two others, which I do not count:
- "Faustus of Milevis ... wrote a book of answers ..." It is glossed as "a book of answers", and the quote shows it answering Christian questions. It would be clearer as "a book of answers to Christian questions", but this is not a block.
- "Christians brought Faustus before the governor's court ... exiled to an island." The 372 law, told just before, gives the reason (being a Manichaean). Not bare.

For the trust readers, not counted here: the 843 edict and the king's killing of Mani's followers are told with no reason. That is right only if the old sources give none.

## 5. Citations read aloud

Most of the named witnesses are ones Part 1 calls for: Biruni, the Egyptian writer and Mani's pupil, where the accounts of Mani's death differ; Augustine, for an enemy's mockery; Biruni and the Chinese chronicle, because they wrote long after. Two go beyond that:

1. "He wrote a book for King Shapur of Persia, and Biruni, a Muslim scholar of about 1000, quotes how it begins." The second half is about how we know it, not about Mani. In the narrator's voice: "He wrote a book for King Shapur of Persia, and a Muslim scholar seven centuries later kept its opening." MINOR.
2. "After Mani's death, says the Fihrist, a Baghdad scholar's book of the 900s, ..." The title "the Fihrist" gives the listener nothing they can use. In the narrator's voice: "After Mani's death, a Baghdad scholar writing in the 900s says, the king killed ..." MINOR.

## 6. Tells Part 1 names, and lines that sound machine-made

**Tells Part 1 names: 0.** No dash, stock word, rhetorical question, moral close or sentence that only says what is missing. "nothing shameful, only that they prayed facing the sun" looks like a "not X but Y" turn, but it reports what Augustine answered. It is not the narrator's own flourish, so I do not count it.

**Lines that sound machine-made:**
1. "Mani died at the king's hands, but the accounts differ on how." It is a tidy referee's line that weighs the accounts instead of telling one. With the four witnesses around it, it is the most assistant-like sentence in the piece.
2. "... after offending him in some way." "In some way" sounds like filler. If the Egyptian writer gives no more, the line reads better as "after he fell out with him."
3. The run of attributions in the third, fifth and sixth paragraphs ("Biruni ... quotes", "Mani says there", "Augustine says", "Biruni says", "A writer in Egypt ... heard", "which Biruni reports", "Augustine says", "says the Fihrist"). Each one is defensible. Together they tip these paragraphs into a report about the record, which Part 1's Craft rules out, and that rhythm is the main thing a skeptic hears as machine-assembled. Cutting the two citations in item 5 eases it.

## 8. Pull, drift, staying

- **Pull:** yes. An exact date, a debate, a former member called as a witness, and a quote in the second sentence.
- **First drift:** "He wrote a book for King Shapur of Persia, and Biruni, a Muslim scholar of about 1000, quotes how it begins." The story stops to source itself. Attention drifts properly in the paragraph on Mani's death (from "Biruni says that King Bahram had Mani sought" to "chained in prison until he died"): four witnesses and two kings in five sentences, so the listener follows the sources instead of Mani.
- **Would I listen to the end:** yes. The Faustus quote and the closing confession are the strongest moments, and the close ends on the community's own words, as the kind kit asks.
