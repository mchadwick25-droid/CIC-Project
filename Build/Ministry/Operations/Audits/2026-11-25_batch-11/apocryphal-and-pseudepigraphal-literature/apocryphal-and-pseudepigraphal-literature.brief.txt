About the year 200, on a visit to Rhossus, a town on the coast of Syria near Antioch, Bishop Serapion of Antioch found the church quarreling over a gospel that carried Peter's name. He had not read it, and he thought them all sound in faith. So he gave a short answer: "If this is the only thing that seems to be causing bad feeling among you, let it be read."

That gospel was one of many books like it. In the second to fourth centuries, Christians wrote and read gospels and tales of the apostles under the apostles' own names, books that never entered the New Testament. These are the Christian Apocrypha, the books that were never canon. Congregations read them aloud and argued over them, and bishops and teachers had to judge them against what had been handed down, received in the churches from the apostles.

After he left Rhossus, Serapion was told that the book's backers had minds caught up in some heresy, a teaching he held false. He wrote that he would come to them soon. Meanwhile he borrowed the book from the group that had used it from the first, and he read it through. Most of it, he found, agreed with the Saviour's true teaching, but some things had been added, and he set those out for them. His letter, which Eusebius of Caesarea kept in his Church History a century later, lays down his rule: "Brothers, we receive Peter and the other apostles as we receive Christ. But the writings falsely put under their names we refuse, as people with experience. We know that such things were not handed down to us."

In the same years, a book of Paul's travels and deeds told of a girl named Thecla. In its later copies she is a girl of Iconium who hears Paul preach, refuses the man she was promised to, and is condemned to the fire for it. The book says a storm of rain and hail put the fire out, and she was saved. Later a governor sent her to the beasts for shaming a powerful man who had seized her in the street. In the arena she threw herself into a pit of water and baptized herself in the name of Jesus Christ, on what she called her last day. Afterward, the book says, Paul sent her to teach the word of God.

Some Christians cited her example as leave for women to teach and baptize. Tertullian, writing in Carthage in Roman Africa, answered them that Paul did not even let a woman learn too boldly. Then he told them about the man who wrote the book, a presbyter, an elder of the church: "Let them know that the presbyter in Asia who put that writing together, as if adding to Paul's fame from his own store, was found out. He confessed that he had done it for love of Paul, and he left his office."

Other books told what the Gospels leave untold. One under James's name, kept in many later copies, tells of Mary's childhood. She is born to a childless couple, and from the age of three she lives in the temple, fed, it says, by an angel's hand, until she is betrothed to Joseph.

Early in the fourth century, Eusebius, writing his Church History, set these books in order. The Acts of Paul he counted among books that were known to most church writers, but were not genuine. He set the gospels of Peter and Thomas and the Acts of Andrew and John lower still. They carried the apostles' names, but they had not been handed down, and he judged that they should be cast aside entirely as absurd and godless.

The reading went on all the same. Late in the fourth century, a pilgrim named Egeria, from far-distant lands, came to Seleucia, on the south coast of today's Turkey. In one form of Thecla's book, this was where she taught many and fell asleep. On a low hill outside the city stood her church, with cells of men and women around it. There, Egeria writes, prayer was made at the memorial, and then the whole of the Acts of Thecla was read, the book whose writer had left his office, read through from beginning to end.
