About the year 200, Bishop Serapion of Antioch visited Rhossus, a town on the Syrian coast, and found its church quarreling over a gospel that carried Peter's name. He had not read it, and he thought them all sound in faith. So he gave a short answer: "If this is the only thing that seems to be causing bad feeling among you, let it be read."

In the second to fourth centuries, Christians wrote and read gospels and tales of the apostles under the apostles' own names, books that never entered the New Testament. Congregations read them aloud and argued over them. Bishops and teachers had to judge them against what had been handed down, the teaching received in the churches from the apostles.

After he left, Serapion was told that the book's backers had minds caught up in some heresy, a teaching he held false. So he borrowed the book from the group that had used it from the first, and read it through. Most of it agreed with the Saviour's true teaching, he found, but some things had been added. He set them out in a letter that Eusebius kept in his Church History a century later, and gave his rule: "Brothers, we receive Peter and the other apostles as we receive Christ. But the writings falsely put under their names we refuse, as people with experience. We know that such things were not handed down to us."

In the same years, the Acts of Paul, a book of his travels and deeds, told of Thecla. In its surviving copies, she is a girl of Iconium who listened at a window to Paul, refused the man she was promised to, and was condemned to the fire for it. Later, at Antioch, a governor sent her to the beasts for shaming a powerful man who had seized her in the street. In the arena she threw herself into a pit of water, where, the book says, fire killed the seals. So she baptized herself in the name of Jesus Christ, on what she called her last day, and afterward Paul sent her to teach the word of God.

Some Christians cited her example as leave for women to teach and baptize. Tertullian, writing in Carthage, in Roman Africa, answered that Paul did not even let a woman learn too boldly. Then he told of the book's writer, a presbyter, an elder of the church in Asia, the west of today's Turkey. "Let them know that the presbyter in Asia who put that writing together, as if adding to Paul's fame from his own store, was found out. He confessed that he had done it for love of Paul, and he left his office."

In Alexandria, Origen reported that some, drawing on the Gospel of Peter or a book of James, held Jesus's brothers to be Joseph's sons by an earlier wife, to keep Mary's virginity honored to the end. A book under James's name survives in many later copies, and it tells what the Gospels do not. Mary is born to a childless couple, and from the age of three she lives in the temple, fed, it says, by an angel's hand, until she is betrothed to Joseph, an old man with children.

Early in the fourth century, Eusebius of Caesarea, in Palestine, set the books in order in that same history, by whether they had been handed down. The Acts of Paul he counted among books known to most church writers, but not genuine. Lower still he set the gospels of Peter and Thomas and the Acts of Andrew and John, books that heretics put forward under the apostles' names. No church writer had thought them worth mention, and they were to be cast aside as absurd and godless.

Late in the fourth century, Egeria, a pilgrim from far-distant lands, came to Seleucia, on the south coast of today's Turkey, where, in one form of Thecla's book, she had taught many and fallen asleep. On a low hill outside the city stood her church, with the cells where men and women lived around it. At the memorial, Egeria writes, prayer was made, and then the whole of the Acts of Thecla was read, the book whose writer had confessed and left his office.
