From about the second century on, Christians wrote, read and copied a great deal that never went into the New Testament. It was never quite meant to. Some of it fills gaps the gospels leave. The Protevangelium of James supplies Mary's parents, her childhood in the Temple and the birth in a cave. Almost everything later Christians believed about her early life comes from it, not from scripture. Some of it carries the apostles into adventures: Andrew among cannibals, Philip at Hierapolis, Thomas in India. These tales read like Greek romances, and they often carry a strong push toward self-denial. And a third body is not Christian in origin at all. The Life of Adam and Eve and the Testament of Abraham are Jewish works. They survive because Christian scribes kept copying them after Judaism itself had let them go. Sometimes the scribes worked Christian passages in. The Testaments of the Twelve Patriarchs may belong here too. But scholars still argue whether it began as a Jewish book or a Christian one. And this writing kept coming. The Dormition stories of Mary's death gave the church the Assumption. The tour-of-hell apocalypses shaped medieval visions of the afterlife. Pseudo-Matthew and its later, shorter Latin version carried Mary's childhood into the Latin West. These run to the ninth century and beyond. What holds all of it together is not a community or a doctrine. It is a fact about how texts were handed down. This is the literature the church read for centuries and never made part of its Bible.
