A confessor named Natalius, one who had stood trial for the faith and not given way, let a rival group make him their bishop, for a monthly salary. They followed a leather-worker whom the bishop of Rome before Zephyrinus had put out of the church's fellowship for teaching that Christ was only a man. A writer of that day says Natalius was warned in visions, and then beaten all night by holy angels. In the morning he put on sackcloth and ashes and threw himself down before Bishop Zephyrinus, rolling at the feet of clergy and people alike and showing his welts. Even then he was scarcely taken back.

In the first half of the 200s, the Christians of Rome were one large, mixed church. It held slaves and women of rank, dozens of priests, and more than fifteen hundred widows and poor people whom it fed, under a bishop the people chose. They argued in public over taking back grave sinners, over whom a Christian woman of rank might marry, and over how to speak of Father and Son, until a stricter party broke away in 251. Their fullest witness for the early decades is Hippolytus, a learned teacher in the city who withstood two of its bishops and lost.

Under Zephyrinus, bishop from around 200, a teaching from Smyrna reached Rome, that Father and Son were one and the same. Zephyrinus told the people, Hippolytus reports, that he knew one God, Jesus Christ, and no other who was born and suffered. His right hand was Callistus, who had been a slave and had once been sent to the mines, and whom he set over the church's cemetery. Hippolytus and his side resisted them, and Callistus, he says, accused them of worshipping two gods.

When Zephyrinus died, around 217, Callistus became bishop and put out of the church Sabellius, who taught that Father and Son were the same. Hippolytus says Callistus did it because he feared Hippolytus, and to clear himself of the charge, among the churches, that his own views were strange. Callistus's own words come down in his enemy's report: "I will not profess belief in two Gods, Father and Son, but in one."

Callistus let people off for sins of the body, Hippolytus says, claiming that he forgave them all. People troubled in conscience came to him, and people other groups had refused, even some that Hippolytus's side had thrown out. As his enemy reports, Callistus pointed to Paul's word that no one should judge another's servant, and to the weeds left to grow with the wheat, so that sinners stayed in the church. His people called themselves the Catholic Church, the church of the whole. Hippolytus called them a school, as if they were a teacher's circle and no church, and named them Callistians.

Callistus also let a woman of rank, who would not overturn her own dignity by a lawful marriage, take a companion, slave or free, and count him her husband. Hippolytus charged that such women then took drugs to prevent or end a pregnancy, not wanting a child by a slave, and he called this teaching adultery and murder.

Later, a historian of the next century says, all the believers met to choose a bishop by vote and set Fabian on the bishop's seat.

In 250 persecution came under the emperor Decius, and Christians were forced to sacrifice to the gods. The same historian says it grew from Decius's hatred of the emperor before him, who was reported to be a Christian. Fabian died a martyr, Rome was left without a bishop, and many gave way in terror, from fear of men. Rome's elders and deacons wrote to the clergy at Carthage, in Africa, that they had called back some who were on their way up to sacrifice. Of the fallen, those who had given way and sacrificed, they wrote: "They are separated from us, but we have not abandoned them. We urged them, and urge them still, to repent, if by some means they can receive pardon from the One who is able to grant it. If we abandoned them, they would grow worse." They told Carthage, too, that its martyrs must be buried.

In 251 Cornelius was made bishop by almost all the clergy and the vote of the people then present. That year he counted his church in a letter: forty-six elders, seven deacons, seven subdeacons and forty-two attendants, and fifty-two exorcists, who drove out evil spirits, together with readers and doorkeepers. It fed more than fifteen hundred widows and people in distress.

But Novatian, an elder of Rome, held that the fallen had no more hope. With a stricter party and confessors at his side, he was made a second bishop of Rome, set up against the first. That year sixty bishops met at Rome, declared his party outsiders, and resolved to heal the fallen through repentance.

That year, too, the confessors who had sided with Novatian came to the elders and five bishops and asked that what they had done be forgotten. The people were told, so that they could see in the church the men they had mourned as wanderers. Cornelius wrote to the bishop of Carthage of their return: "A great crowd of the brothers and sisters came running together. All of them had one voice, giving thanks to God. They showed the joy of their hearts in tears. They embraced them as if they had been set free from prison that very day." The confessors said they held to one God, one Christ and one Holy Spirit, and to one bishop in the catholic church. The elder who had led them took his place again, and the rest were received with the people's loud approval.
