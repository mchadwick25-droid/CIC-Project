In the ANF and NPNF files, editors' endnotes stand inside the running text. Every span below leaves them out, marked "[note]" where one falls; the rest is verbatim. Greek and Latin are given as printed.

Witness kinds: **own words** (the people in the story), **contemporary**, **later (gap)**, **late legend**. Confidence: Documented / Widely Accepted / Dominant Modern Reconstruction / Contested / Inferential-Thin.

**The main witness, stated once.** For the years from Zephyrinus to Callistus's death, almost everything comes from Hippolytus's Refutation, Book IX. He was a writer of that time at Rome (E 35447-35449, "presided over another church, has left writings") who says he "resisted" both bishops, and he wrote after Callistus died (H 13816 "after the death of Zephyrinus"; the book's account runs to Callistus's school "which continues", 13957-13958). He is a contemporary and an enemy, and the losing side: the church that kept Callistus kept the see. Callistus left nothing in his own voice; his words and his reasons come only as Hippolytus reports them. From 250 the record changes: the Roman clergy, the Roman confessors and Bishop Cornelius speak in their own letters, kept in Cyprian's correspondence and in Eusebius.

Explain it, "Sabellius's teaching" and the Father-Son quarrel, from each side the texts hold:
- Hippolytus's side: Noetus taught "that the Son and Father are the same" (Z6); Sabellius held "the system of Cleomenes", Noetus's pupil (H 13609-13616). Hippolytus held Father and Son distinct and was called a worshipper of two gods for it (Z9, narrated).
- Zephyrinus's and Callistus's side, as Hippolytus reports it: one God, Jesus Christ; "The Father did not die, but the Son" (Z8); "I will not profess belief in two Gods, Father and Son, but in one" (C5). Callistus cut Sabellius off (C3). Hippolytus calls this a cover, and charges that Callistus still made the Father suffer with the Son (C4, C6).
- A later Roman voice, Dionysius of Rome in the 260s, condemns both Sabellius, who "says that the Son is the Father, and the Father the Son", and those who preach three Gods (R1-R2 below).

Explain it, "Callistus's rule on forgiveness": in Hippolytus's words only (C7-C11). Callistus's own reasons, as reported: Romans 14:4 and the tares (C10). Who came to him and why: the troubled in conscience and those Hippolytus's side had thrown out (C8). Hippolytus's verdict: a school, not the church (C14). No word of Callistus's own survives on it.

Explain it, "the marriage rule": a woman whose rank a lawful marriage would overturn could take a companion, slave or free, and count him a husband (C12). Why, in the record: "if they were not disposed to overturn their own dignity through a legal marriage" (C12). Hippolytus's charge of the consequence (C13). The Roman law behind "dignity" is given only by modern works; the story keeps to Hippolytus's words.

Explain it, "the fallen" (the lapsed): Christians who sacrificed or got a paper saying they had, under Decius's order (D3 "those who were ascending to do that to which they were constrained"; H 31633-31658, the clergy's later letter on certificates and sacrifices). Rome's clergy in 250: do not abandon them; urge repentance; give communion to the dying penitent; leave the full settling to a bishop (D3, D4, D7). The stricter side: "no longer for them a hope of salvation" (N4). The synod: heal them with repentance (N11).

Explain it, "the split of 251": a stricter party set up Novatian as a second bishop of Rome (N4); the sixty bishops declared his party outsiders (N11); Cornelius's principle, "one bishop in a catholic church" (N6). Why, in the record: on the fallen (N4, Eusebius) and, in Cornelius's charge, Novatian's long wish for the office (card quote). The Liberian list blames Novatus from Africa (N4b).
