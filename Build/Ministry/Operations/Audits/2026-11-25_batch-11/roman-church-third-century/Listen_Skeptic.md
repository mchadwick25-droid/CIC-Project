## 1. The test

- **Who these people were:** filled. "one large, mixed church. It held slaves and women of rank, and the poor it fed, under a bishop the people chose."
- **What happened to them:** filled. The quarrels under Zephyrinus and Callistus, Decius in 250, the fallen, the split of 251, the confessors' return.
- **What their faith was about:** partly unfilled. I can tell forgiveness and marriage back in plain words. I cannot tell the Father and Son quarrel. I hear that Sabellius "taught that Father and Son were one and the same" and was put out. Then Callistus, who put him out, says: "I will not profess belief in two Gods, Father and Son, but in one." So what did Callistus hold that Sabellius did not? And who believed in "two Gods"? Hippolytus's own view is never given. The quote sounds aimed at someone, but the story does not say at whom. I come away knowing there was a quarrel, not what it was about. BLOCKING (see 4b for the change).
- **Why the connection matters:** filled, thinly. Through what people did, I can say this church split in 251 when Novatian's stricter party set up a second bishop. Nothing links it back to the earlier church at Rome, but the kind kit asks only that the connection become clear through what people did, and the split does that.

## 2. The opening

First three sentences: Natalius, the paid bishop of a group that "taught that Christ was only a man", the sackcloth before "Bishop Zephyrinus".

- **Who:** there. Natalius, a confessor, with the word explained.
- **Where:** missing. Rome is not heard until sentence 5. "Bishop Zephyrinus" gives no city. The rule makes an opening that leaves the listener lost about where BLOCKING. **Change:** "threw himself down before Zephyrinus, bishop of Rome, rolling at the feet…" (+2 words).
- **When:** missing until sentence 5 ("the first half of the 200s"). That is acceptable inside the first minute. No change needed.
- **Why there:** "Then he put on sackcloth and ashes" gives no reason for the turn. A listener asks why he repented. This is not an act of conflict or punishment, so it is not BLOCKING. If the record's reason is a wonder, it can stay out, as it does now.

## 3. Sentences that fail on one hearing

a. Sentence 7: "They argued in public over whether the church could take back grave sinners, whom a Christian woman of rank might marry, and how to speak of Father and Son, until a stricter party broke away in 251." This is a garden path. Heard once, "grave sinners, whom a Christian woman of rank might marry" reads as a clause about the sinners. The second subject of the quarrel is lost. It does not lose the thread, because paragraph 5 makes the marriage quarrel clear, so it is MINOR. **Change:** "They argued in public over how to speak of Father and Son, whether the church could take back grave sinners, and whom a Christian woman of rank might marry, until a stricter party broke away in 251." That also puts the three subjects in the order the story then tells them.

b. Sentence 4: "Scarcely was he taken back into communion, the church's shared bread and cup." A listener in a second language may hear "scarcely" as "hardly at all", when it means "only with difficulty". The inverted order hides the point. MINOR. **Change:** "Even then, the church barely took him back into communion, its shared bread and cup."

## 4. Bare statements

a. "Callistus, who had been a slave and had once been sent to the mines." This is a punishment with no reason given. Part 1 says each punishment says why it happened, where the old sources give the reason. I have no source, so I cannot tell whether they do. As heard, it is bare. BLOCKING. **Change:** if the outline gives a reason, tell it as the source gives it, in its witness's voice. Otherwise cut "and had once been sent to the mines" (−7 words, which helps the 675 cap).

b. The Father and Son quarrel, as in 1. Sabellius's side is explained. Callistus's side comes only through a quote that names no target. Hippolytus's side is not given, and the story never says why the quarrel mattered to anyone in it. BLOCKING. **Change:** after the Callistus quote, add one clause, only as the outline holds it, saying whom "two Gods" was aimed at and what that side held about the Son. If the outline does not hold it, the trust read should decide whether the quote can stand alone. As heard, it raises a question the story never answers.

Noted, not counted: "a learned teacher who withstood two of Rome's bishops and lost." I am never told what he lost. The charges that follow suggest why he withstood the bishops, so this is understood well enough.

## 6. Tells

**Part 1 tells:** none. There is no narrator's "not X but Y" turn (the "not… two Gods… but in one" is Callistus's own quote), no dash, no rhetorical question, no stock word, no moral close and no missing-only sentence.

**Lines that sound machine-made (MINOR):**

a. "Scarcely was he taken back into communion, the church's shared bread and cup." The inverted archaic opening sounds like translation English. It is then followed by a gloss set off by commas.

b. The appositive-gloss device used as a rule: "a confessor, one who had stood trial…", "communion, the church's shared bread and cup", "known as the fallen", and most of all "…and fifty-two exorcists, readers and doorkeepers together, the exorcists being those who drove out evil spirits." That last gloss is bolted onto the tail of a number list. It sounds like a definition put in to satisfy a checker, not something a narrator would say. **Change:** drop that gloss, or trim the list to "forty-six elders and more than a hundred other clergy" (the trust read to confirm the sum). That saves about 15 words.

c. "That year" opens three sentences in a row across paragraphs 8–9: "That year he counted…", "That year sixty bishops…", "That year, too, the confessors…". It is a mechanical tic. **Change:** "Cornelius was made bishop in 251, and counted his church:" and keep the other two.

d. "It held slaves and women of rank, and the poor it fed, under a bishop the people chose." This is a neat three-part summary in an encyclopedia cadence, with an awkward "held… the poor it fed". **Change:** "Slaves and women of rank belonged to it, it fed its poor, and its people chose their bishop."

## 8. Attention

- **Opening:** it pulls me in. A confessor rolling at the feet of the people is vivid and strange. But Natalius never returns, and his link to the quarrels is left for the listener to infer.
- **First drift:** sentence 7, the garden-path list (3a).
- **Real drift:** the Fabian paragraph: "Some years after Callistus, as a historian of the next century tells it, all the brethren met…" Why: the story's question drops here. The quarrels of paragraphs 3–5 are left without any end: no word on what became of Callistus or Hippolytus. A one-sentence paragraph then jumps years ahead, to an election where nothing else happens. **For the reviser:** join it to the Decius paragraph so it reads as the lead-in to 250, not as a scene on its own. Add nothing the outline does not hold.
- **Listen to the end:** yes. The Roman clergy's letter and the closing scene of the crowd running together hold well. The ending is on the community itself.
