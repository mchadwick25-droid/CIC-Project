At Hippo, on the feast day of a former bishop, the people wanted their old feast in the church, with eating and drinking, which they called the Gladness. Their priest, Augustine, read them the Scriptures against drunken feasts and pleaded with them, and later wrote: "I did not move their tears with my own. But as these things were said, I confess, their weeping came first, and I could not hold back mine. When we had wept together, my sermon ended in the fullest hope that they would mend their ways." On the feast day itself, from the church of those he called heretics, came the sound of their feasting.

From the 250s to 430, the Latin-speaking Christians of Roman Africa lived as congregations around one bishop, and called themselves the Catholic Church. At Carthage, the great city of Roman Africa, in today's Tunisia, that bishop was Cyprian, and later, at Hippo, in today's Algeria, it was Augustine. The bishop taught his people, fed their poor, bought back their captives, judged their quarrels and decided with them whom to take back after sin. Persecution, plague and a rival church in the same towns pressed on them, and they kept asking what a bishop and his people owed each other in danger.

In 250 the emperor Decius began a persecution of the churches, and his edict called for sacrifice to the gods. At Carthage the crowd kept shouting for Cyprian, and he withdrew for the peace of the brethren, as he called his people. From hiding he told his clergy: "I could not write back alone. From my first days as bishop I resolved to do nothing on my own private judgment, without your counsel and the people's consent."

Many had given way and sacrificed, some running to the forum of their own accord, and now these fallen asked to come back. Cyprian held that no one should be barred from penance and the hope of peace. Penance was a time of public repentance, and peace was the church's word for taking someone back to share the bread and cup.

At Rome a stricter party held, in Cyprian's report, that sharing communion with the fallen stained everyone else, and they set up Novatian as a second bishop of Rome, against the one already chosen. When his envoys came to Carthage, Cyprian and the bishops with him at once refused them communion, because his ordination was unlawful, against the Catholic Church.

A few years later a plague struck, and the dead lay in the streets while people left their own sick. Pontius, Cyprian's deacon, who wrote his life as an admirer, says the bishop gathered the people and told them that caring only for their own was nothing great. Tasks, he says, were shared out by rank, and the poor, who had no money to give, gave their work, so that good was done for all.

Then a new emperor ordered the Roman rites kept again, the sacrifices to the Roman gods. In 258 Cyprian was arrested and held at an officer's house, and his whole people gathered in the street outside the door. He refused the rites, and his sentence named him an enemy of the Roman gods and their rites, and the leader of men gathered in a wicked conspiracy. He was led out to a field and beheaded.

Soon after the great persecution ended, early in the 300s, the people of Carthage chose a new bishop. A party said the bishop who consecrated him had handed over the Scriptures in the persecution, and they made a rival bishop, setting up altar against altar. This rival church, the Donatists, held that one who was himself stained could not wash away sins, so they baptized again those who came over to them. A later Catholic writer says both churches held the same sacraments, and the quarrel was over who could rightly give them.

Augustine, later bishop of Hippo, preached in church and in homes. Christians and men of any sect brought him their disputes, and he heard them, since Paul had told believers not to go to law before unbelievers. Against the rival church he had at first wanted argument only. Then, he wrote, his fellow bishops pointed to his own town, brought over by fear of the emperors' laws against the rival church, and he came to back those laws.

In his last years the Vandals, peoples from beyond the empire who had crossed from Spain, overran Africa. A fellow bishop wrote to ask him whether bishops and clergy should leave their churches. His answer turned on baptism and reconciliation, the taking back into communion after penance. "But if the clergy are there, everyone is helped, as far as the Lord gives them strength. Some are baptized, others are reconciled. No one is cheated of communion in the Lord's body. All are comforted, built up and urged to ask God, who has the power to turn away all that is feared."
