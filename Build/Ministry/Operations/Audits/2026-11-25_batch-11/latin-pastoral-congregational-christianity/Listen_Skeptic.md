## 1. The test, for a tradition

- **Who these people were:** filled. "the Latin-speaking Christians of Roman Africa lived as congregations around one bishop, at Carthage under Cyprian and later at Hippo under Augustine."
- **What happened to them:** mostly filled (Decius, the fallen, Novatian, plague, Cyprian's death, the rival church, imperial laws, the Vandals). Partly open: the question of the fallen is raised ("now these fallen Christians asked to come back") and never resolved. I cannot say whether they were taken back, or how. MINOR (the story's own question is not carried to where it resolves; Part 1, narrated story).
- **What their faith was about:** filled, thinly but enough: penance and taking back the fallen; a bishop who acts with "your counsel and the people's consent"; the quarrel over whether one "himself stained could not wash away sins."
- **Why the connection matters:** UNFILLED. BLOCKING. The rival church that broke with them is never given a name I could find on the tree; I hear "a party", "a rival bishop", "the rival side", "the rival church", "those he called heretics". I can say a split happened, but not with whom, and nothing tells me what came of these congregations or who carried any of it on. The story stops at the Vandal threat. Change: name the rival church by the name the tree uses, where it first breaks off ("so they made a rival bishop"), so the break links to its entry; and let what was handed down come through, if the outline holds it.

## 2. The opening

After sentences 1 to 3: who (the people of Hippo, their priest Augustine), where (Hippo, Roman Africa) and why there (a feast with drinking they wanted in the church) are given. **When** is missing until sentence 7 ("From the 250s to 430"), and even then I do not know when the Hippo scene sits in that span. Not lost about who or where: not blocking. Suggest a time clause in sentence 1 or 2 ("Late in the 300s, ...").

The opening scene is then left behind: the story goes back about 140 years to Decius and never returns to the Gladness feast, so it plays as a detached prologue.

## 3. Sentences that fail on one hearing

1. "At Carthage, in today's Tunisia, the crowd kept shouting for Cyprian, and he withdrew, for the brethren's peace, as he wrote." Meaning lost: shouting for him why? A listener with no church background can hear a cheering crowd. That it was a hostile crowd demanding his death is not said, so his withdrawal seems odd. MINOR.
2. "Cyprian held that no one should be barred from penance, a time of public repentance, or from the hope of peace, the church's word for taking someone back, reconciling them, to share the bread and cup." Three glosses stacked in one sentence ("a time of...", "the church's word for...", "reconciling them"), and I lost which word was being explained. This is where my attention first drifted. Change: split it and drop "reconciling them". MINOR.
3. "the bishop gathered the people and told them that caring only for their own was nothing great." Meaning lost: care for whom else? The point (caring for outsiders, even enemies, in the plague) is implied, not said. "The tasks were shared out by rank": what tasks? MINOR.
4. "A Catholic writer of the next generation said the two churches held the same sacraments, and he kept the charge of breaking the creed for heretics." Meaning lost entirely: that this writer counted the rival church as split off rather than heretical. Worse, it seems to contradict the opening, where Augustine called them "heretics". BLOCKING: it loses the thread of the quarrel the story turns on. Change: say it plainly, e.g. that he held the rival church kept the same sacraments and the same creed, so it had split off but had not fallen into heresy. Or cut it.
5. "Then, he wrote, his fellow bishops pointed to his own town, brought over to the Catholics by fear of the emperors' laws, and he came to back those laws." "His own town" reads as Hippo, the town I was just told he led. If another town is meant, I cannot tell. MINOR (see also bare statement 3).

## 4. Bare statements (Part 1, Explain it)

1. "On the feast day itself, from the church of those he called heretics, came the sound of their feasting." The label "heretics" is named with no plain word on who they were or what made them so; heard in the opening, I do not know a second church exists. BLOCKING. Change: give them a plain clause where first heard ("the rival church in the same town, whom he called heretics").
2. "A party said the bishop who had consecrated him had handed over the Scriptures during the persecution, so they made a rival bishop." The charge is named but not why it was so grave: handed over to whom, and why was it a betrayal? A listener with no church background cannot fill it. BLOCKING. Change: add the plain sense (gave up the sacred books to the persecuting officials, to be destroyed), as the outline holds it.
3. "fear of the emperors' laws ... and he came to back those laws." The laws are named but never said: what did they order or punish, and against whom? This is the story's act of force, and I cannot say what it was. BLOCKING. Change: say in plain words what the laws did to the rival church, as the source gives it.

The craft tool's three flags, judged by Part 1:
- s6 ("On the feast day itself..."): confirmed, bare statement 1 above.
- s20 ("Pontius, Cyprian's deacon..."): not a Part 1 bare statement (no named thing or act left without its sense); it is a failing sentence, item 3.3 above.
- s23 ("Cyprian was arrested, and the whole people of the brethren gathered..."): not bare. The arrest's reason comes in the next sentence (he refused the rites), and the gathering is an act told as given. "the whole people of the brethren" sounds like translation (see 6).

## 5. Citations read aloud

None counted. Noted: "he withdrew, for the brethren's peace, as he wrote". The motive is the bishop's own, for an act that could look like flight, so crediting it is defensible. "Pontius ... who wrote his life as an admirer, says" is praise kept in its witness's voice, which Part 1 calls for.

## 6. Tells

Part 1's named tells: none heard. There is no "not X but Y" in the narrator's voice (the "I did not ... But ..." is Augustine's quote), no dash for drama, no question it answers itself, no stock word, and no moral close. The ending is the subject's own words.

Lines that sound machine-made (not named tells, so not blocking):
- "Persecution, plague and a rival church in the same towns pressed on them, and each time they had to work out what a bishop and his people owed each other in danger." A triad and then a thesis, in the cadence of an essay's topic sentence. It is the essence, which must be said, but "each time they had to work out" is framing laid over the story, not drawn from it.
- "Their bishop taught them, fed their poor, bought back their captives, judged their quarrels and decided with them whom to take back after sin." A five-item catalog, the encyclopedia-list rhythm the tradition kit's Traps warn of.
- The appositive-gloss habit, used as a template: "penance, a time of public repentance", "peace, the church's word for...", "the Roman rites kept, the sacrifices of the Roman gods", "the Vandals, enemies who came by sea", "in today's Tunisia", "in today's Algeria". Each is fine alone. Together they sound like a pattern applied, not a voice.
- "On the feast day itself, from the church of those he called heretics, came the sound of their feasting." An inverted stinger built as a scene-closing button.

## 7. Words, runs, tone

Unexplained words: "Catholic" (first heard in "against the Catholic Church"; to a newcomer it means today's Roman Catholic Church), "heretics", "consecrated", "sacraments", "creed", "brethren", "Paul" (who he was is not given; a listener with no church background does not know him). "Communion" is helped only by the earlier "bread and cup". "Men of any sect" and "go to law before unbelievers" are dated but understood.

There are no runs of short one-act sentences. "A few years later plague struck." is a single short turn, which is fine. Nothing talks down.

## 8. Attention

- **The opening pulls in:** yes. The shared weeping is vivid and human, and the sound of the rival feast is a strong image, even if I do not know who is feasting.
- **First drift:** the penance sentence (3.2), where the stacked glosses bury the point.
- **Second drift:** the Catholic-writer sentence (3.4), where I lose who is on which side. From there to the laws (4.3) the quarrel turns abstract, with unnamed parties, an unexplained charge and unexplained laws.
- **Would I listen to the end:** yes. The Vandal letter restores grip, because it is concrete and in Augustine's own words.
