At Hippo, a town in Roman Africa, on the feast day of a former bishop, the people wanted their old feast in the church, with eating and drinking, which they called the Gladness. Their priest Augustine read them Scripture against drunken feasts and pleaded with them. In a letter he wrote: "I did not move their tears with my own. But as these things were said, I confess, their weeping came first, and I could not hold back mine. When we had wept together, my sermon ended in the fullest hope that they would mend their ways." On the feast day itself, from the church of those he called heretics, came the sound of their feasting.

From the 250s to 430, the Latin-speaking Christians of Roman Africa lived as congregations around one bishop, at Carthage under Cyprian and later at Hippo under Augustine. Their bishop taught them, fed their poor, bought back their captives, judged their quarrels and decided with them whom to take back after sin. Persecution, plague and a rival church in the same towns pressed on them, and each time they had to work out what a bishop and his people owed each other in danger.

In 250 the emperor Decius began a persecution of the churches, and his edict called for sacrifice to the gods. At Carthage, in today's Tunisia, the crowd kept shouting for Cyprian, and he withdrew, for the brethren's peace, as he wrote. From hiding he led his church by letter, and told his clergy: "I could not write back alone. From my first days as bishop I resolved to do nothing on my own private judgment, without your counsel and the people's consent."

Many had given way and sacrificed, and now these fallen Christians asked to come back. Cyprian held that no one should be barred from penance, a time of public repentance, or from the hope of peace, the church's word for taking someone back, reconciling them, to share the bread and cup. At Rome a stricter party held that sharing communion with the fallen stained the rest. They set up Novatian as a second bishop of Rome, against the one already chosen. When his envoys came to Carthage, Cyprian and the bishops with him kept them from communion at once, since Novatian had been made bishop against the Catholic Church.

A few years later plague struck. Pontius, Cyprian's deacon, who wrote his life as an admirer, says the bishop gathered the people and told them that caring only for their own was nothing great. The tasks were shared out by rank, and the poor, who had no money to give, gave their work.

Then a new emperor ordered the Roman rites kept, the sacrifices of the Roman gods. Cyprian was arrested, and the whole people of the brethren gathered in the street outside the door where he was held. He refused the rites, and the sentence called him an enemy of the Roman gods and their rites, and the leader of men gathered in a wicked conspiracy. He was led out and beheaded on 14 September 258.

Soon after the great persecution of the early 300s ended, the people of Carthage chose a new bishop. A party said the bishop who had consecrated him had handed over the Scriptures during the persecution, so they made a rival bishop and set up altar against altar. The rival side held that one who was himself stained could not wash away sins, and they baptized again those who came over to them. A Catholic writer of the next generation said the two churches held the same sacraments, and he kept the charge of breaking the creed for heretics.

Later, as bishop of Hippo, in today's Algeria, Augustine judged the disputes that Christians and men of any sect brought him, because Paul had told believers not to go to law before unbelievers. Against the rival church he had first wanted argument only. Then, he wrote, his fellow bishops pointed to his own town, brought over to the Catholics by fear of the emperors' laws, and he came to back those laws.

In his last years the Vandals, enemies who came by sea, overran Africa. A fellow bishop wrote to ask whether bishops and clergy should leave their churches. Augustine answered: "But if the clergy are there, everyone is helped, as far as the Lord gives them strength. Some are baptized, others are reconciled. No one is cheated of communion in the Lord's body. All are comforted, built up and urged to ask God, who has the power to turn away all that is feared."
