**Blocking: 2.** Verdict from this read: REVISE.

## 1. The test

- **Who they were:** filled. Christians of Alexandria who learned their faith from teachers, like a school ("the Christians of Alexandria learned their faith from teachers, as the city's pagans learned grammar and philosophy").
- **What happened to them:** filled. Teachers driven out and pupils killed; Origen's crowded classroom split in two; Origen leaves; his pupils become bishops; Dionysius wins over the villages in three days.
- **What their faith was about:** filled. Scripture read for a deeper sense ("has a body, a soul and a spirit"); the Word who "urges… trains… teaches"; faith whose handbook "is called love".
- **Why the connection matters:** filled, thinly. I can say: the school's pupils led the city's church and carried its way of reading to the villages, where it changed minds ("he was convinced, and would no longer hold or teach it"). Not a finding.

## 2. The opening (first three sentences)

Where (Alexandria, Egypt), when (around 200) and why there (persecution) are clear. Origen is clear.

**Missing — who (BLOCKING):** the people are never named as Christians. "Teachers of the faith", "pagans" and "the word of God" leave a listener with no church background guessing which faith; "Christians" first comes at about word 110. Change, for the trust readers to check against Eusebius: "…had driven every Christian teacher out of Alexandria, in Egypt."

## 3. Sentences that fail on one hearing (MINOR)

1. "Pagans came instead to hear the word of God from Origen, who in his eighteenth year took charge of the instruction." "Instead" of what is lost; "the instruction" does not say whose; "in his eighteenth year" can be heard as a year of a reign. Suggested shape (trust check needed): "With no one else left to teach, pagans came to hear the word of God from Origen, who took charge of the teaching at seventeen."
2. "He tells of one of the dead… in Origen's own phrase: 'Among the women, too, Herais… as he himself says somewhere…'" I heard the quote as Origen speaking, then "as he himself says" made me unsure who "he" is. Who speaks is lost.
3. "Faith is taught without letters." I heard "letters" as mail. Plain sense alongside would hold it: "without letters, that is, without schooling".
4. "Some teachers who relied on it, he wrote, led simpler believers to hope for small and mortal things in God's kingdom, and left them no lofty thought of the resurrection and of being made like Christ." Too many hard words in one breath ("mortal", "lofty", "resurrection"); I caught only "they hoped for small things".
5. "…called together the presbyters, the elders, and the teachers of the villages…" Heard as three groups, not a gloss. Change: "the presbyters, or elders, and the teachers of the villages".

## 4. Bare statements

None. The checker's s22 ("…his teaching had split whole churches") is not bare: the sentence before says what the teaching was, and the one after says why it mattered to Dionysius.

## Bar: event told twice (BLOCKING)

"When village churches rejected that reading, Dionysius, once a pupil there, sat with them for three days and argued it through." The three days are told here, then the story goes back to Clement and tells the three days again in full. On one hearing I thought the second telling was a new meeting until "three days" came again. Change: cut this sentence from the essence paragraph, or say the essence without the event. If the approved heart needs Dionysius in the first minute, the reviser takes that to the outline, not a new wording here.

## 8. Pull and attention

- **Opening pulls in:** yes. Teachers driven out, a teenager in charge, pupils killed, Herais. The Eusebius sentence in the middle cools it a little.
- **First drift:** "Before Origen, Clement of Alexandria taught there…" Why: the story jumps back in time from the vivid opening, and Clement's ideas come as abstract statements with no scene. Attention returns at "the crowds came from morning to evening and left him no time to breathe".
- **Second drift:** "When Origen left for Caesarea, about 231, Heraclas took over…" — a list of who followed whom; "Caesarea" is not placed.
- **Would listen to the end:** yes. Nepos's book and "like a weapon and a fortress no one could take" pull me back, and Coracion's change of mind is a real ending.
