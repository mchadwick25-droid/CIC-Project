Around 200, persecution under the emperor Severus had driven every teacher of the faith out of Alexandria, in Egypt. Pagans came instead to hear the word of God from Origen, who in his eighteenth year took charge of the instruction. Some of his pupils were taken and put to death. Eusebius of Caesarea, writing about a century later from letters and from people who had known Origen, gives nearly all we know of these classrooms. He tells of one of the dead, a catechumen, or learner not yet baptized, in Origen's own phrase: "Among the women, too, Herais was still a catechumen. She received baptism, as he himself says somewhere, the baptism by fire, and left this life."

From about 180 to the middle of the next century, the Christians of Alexandria learned their faith from teachers, as the city's pagans learned grammar and philosophy, in what Eusebius calls a school. Teachers took pagans and catechumens through Scripture, read for a deeper sense beneath the plain story. When village churches rejected that reading, Dionysius, once a pupil there, sat with them for three days and argued it through.

Before Origen, Clement of Alexandria taught there, Eusebius says, and Origen was his pupil. Clement wrote that the Word, his name for Jesus, first urges people on, then trains them, and last of all teaches them. He wrote, too: "Faith is taught without letters. Its handbook, unpolished and divine at once, is called love, a spiritual book."

In Origen's classroom the crowds came from morning to evening and left him no time to breathe, so he divided them. Heraclas, one of his first pupils, took the beginners, and Origen kept the advanced. Scripture, Origen wrote before he left the city, has a body, a soul and a spirit, as a person does. The plain story feeds the simple, its soul feeds those who have gone further, and its spirit the perfect.

When Origen left for Caesarea, about 231, Heraclas took over the instruction and then became the city's bishop. Dionysius, another of Origen's pupils, followed him in both.

Nepos, a bishop in Egypt, had written a book called Refutation of Allegorists, against those who read Scripture for hidden meanings. As his opponents describe it, he taught that Christ would reign on this earth for a thousand years of bodily plenty. In the district of Arsinoe, in the Egyptian countryside, his teaching had split whole churches. Dionysius loved Nepos for his faith and his work on Scripture, but he would answer the book. Some teachers who relied on it, he wrote, led simpler believers to hope for small and mortal things in God's kingdom, and left them no lofty thought of the resurrection and of being made like Christ.

In his own account, the only one left to us, Dionysius called together the presbyters, the elders, and the teachers of the villages, and asked for a public examination of the book. They brought it to him like a weapon and a fortress no one could take, and he sat with them for three days, from morning to evening. "If the argument won," he wrote, "we were not ashamed to change our minds and agree."

At the end, Coracion, who had led this teaching in the villages, said before all the brethren that he was convinced, and would no longer hold or teach it.
