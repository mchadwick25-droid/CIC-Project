# Outline: Alexandrian Christianity (`alexandria-catechetical`)

Kind: tradition. Sources: `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder (S-numbers below are its entries; file keys E-en, E-gr, Cl, Ph, Ath as defined there).

## The heart

- **The essence:** From about 180 to the middle of the next century, the Christians of Alexandria, in Egypt, learned their faith from teachers, as the city's pagans learned grammar and philosophy, in what Eusebius calls a school. Teachers took pagans and catechumens, learners not yet baptized, through Scripture read for a deeper sense beneath the plain story. (B1, C1, C3, C4, C7, C9, D5. The three days at Arsinoe are told once, in Beat 4. Widely Accepted; "school" as Eusebius's word, Contested as an institution.)
- **Who they were:** "Alexandrian Christianity" (census spoken name): the city's believers, their teachers, and the pupils who came to them, pagans, catechumens and the newly baptized, men and women (C1, C4, C5, C6). Documented / Widely Accepted.
- **When and where:** Alexandria, about 180 (Pantaenus, B1) to the middle of the third century (Dionysius, C11-C17); the Arsinoite villages of the Egyptian countryside (C15). Widely Accepted.
- **What they faced:** persecution that killed the learners themselves, about 202 (B7, C5); crowds too large for one teacher (C7); and dissent from inside, a bishop's book against the allegorists, whose teaching had held so long in the district of Arsinoe that whole churches had broken away and fallen away, in Dionysius's words "schisms and apostasies of entire churches" (C13, C15; E-en 41349-41351, E-gr 10720-10721; own words; Documented). Widely Accepted / Documented.
- **What they believed:** that the Word, Jesus, first urges people on, then trains them, then teaches them (D1, D2); that Scripture has a body, a soul and a spirit, a plain sense for the simple and deeper ones for those further on (D5); that faith is taught to those who cannot read too, and its handbook is love (D3); and that God's promises meant the resurrection and being made like Christ, not small and mortal things on this earth (C14). Their own words; Documented.
- **How they practiced:** pupils with a teacher, from morning to evening (C7); beginners with one teacher, the advanced with another (C7); geometry and philosophy for the gifted, the basics for the less learned, all toward reading Scripture (C8); learners went with the teacher to the martyrs (C2). Widely Accepted.
- **What held them together:** the bond of teacher and pupil around Scripture read in layers (C7, C8, D5); and, when the teaching of a book against that reading had led whole churches in the district of Arsinoe to break away and fall away (E-en 41349-41351, E-gr 10720-10721; own words; Documented), three days of public examination of it from Scripture, as Dionysius tells it (C14-C16). (The built world's gravities: the Word as the one center, Scripture as formation, learning as formation; D1, D2.)
- **What came of them:** pupils became the teachers and bishops of the city (C10-C12); the reading itself was carried on (F2); the school ran on in Eusebius's own day (C18).

## Setting

- **Time:** late second century to the mid-third: "the first year of Commodus" for Pantaenus (E-en 28467-28476, 28488), about 180; "the tenth year of the reign of Severus", about 202 (E-en 32523-32533); Origen leaves about 231 (E-en 36330-36332); Dionysius bishop from about 247/8 (E-en 37071-37080). Spoken: "about 180", "around 200", "in the middle of the next century".
- **Place:** Alexandria, in Egypt (E-en 32428-32431); the district of Arsinoe, villages in the Egyptian countryside (E-en 41343-41353). Spoken: "Alexandria, in Egypt"; "villages around Arsinoe, in the Egyptian countryside".
- **Tradition:** "Alexandrian Christianity" (census `informalName`).

## Words to explain

- **catechumen:** a learner not yet baptized (E-en 32914-32916). Glossed where Herais is first heard, before the quote.
- **the deeper sense (allegory):** reading beneath the plain words for a hidden meaning about Christ and the soul (Ph 76). Give the word "allegorists" only with Nepos's book title, glossed.
- **Refutation of Allegorists:** a book against those who read Scripture for hidden meanings (E-en 41293-41295).
- **the thousand-year kingdom:** that Christ would reign on this earth for a thousand years of bodily luxury, as Nepos's opponents describe it (E-en 41288-41291).
- **presbyters:** elders (E-en 41352).
- **the philosopher's garment:** the cloak philosophers wore (E-en 35277-35280).
- **the Word:** Clement's name for Jesus (Cl 19998-19999).
- "school": tell as Eusebius's word ("what Eusebius calls a school"), never "academy" or "university".

The story form (kind kit): open on the pupils going to their deaths around 200, then the essence, then the community forward through time, from Clement and Origen in those same years to Dionysius's three days. No beat goes back before the hook's years.

## Openings

1. **(Planned.) The pupils who died, soon after 202.** The threat of persecution under the emperor Severus had driven every teacher of the faith from Alexandria, and pagans came to hear the word of God from Origen, who in his eighteenth year took charge of the instruction. Some of his pupils were taken and put to death: one still a catechumen, one just baptized, and a woman, Herais, who had not yet been baptized. Quote 1. (E-en 32670-32682, 32732-32733, 32893-32925; E-gr 1607-1614. Widely Accepted.)
2. (Alternative, for the card or Leave out.) The boy Origen puzzling his father with questions about the true meaning of Scripture, and the father rebuking him to his face, then thanking God in private (E-en 32588-32599). Set aside: it opens on a founder, the kit's trap.

## Story beats

**Hook** (Opening 1). One witness: say once, inside a sentence about him, that nearly all we know of these classrooms comes from Eusebius of Caesarea, writing about a century later from letters and from people who had known Origen (E-en 32511-32517).

**Essence** (as in the heart).

1. **Clement, in the same years (Severus's reign).** Open on Origen, so the beat does not go back before the hook: Origen, still a boy, had been one of the pupils of Clement, who had charge of the instruction in those years, Eusebius says (E-en 33040-33044, 33079-33086; later; Widely Accepted). The time words stay "in those years"; no "before", no "about 180". Eusebius says a school of the faithful had stood in Alexandria "from ancient times", and that about 180 Pantaenus, trained among the Stoics, was its best-known teacher (E-en 28488-28539, 28561-28576; Widely Accepted; the institution, Contested). A bishop who had known them both later called Pantaenus and Clement "master" in a letter (E-en 34330-34356; Documented); do not say he studied in Alexandria. Clement, who taught there next (E-en 33040-33044; Widely Accepted), wrote that the Word, his name for Jesus, first urges people on, then trains them, and last of all teaches them (Cl 18726-18729, 19998-19999; Documented). Give the plain sense in Clement's own terms, as what it meant for those he taught: a sick body needs a doctor before it can learn anything, and a soul must first be healed of its passions, then trained, and only then taught, once it can take in the Word, so a learner was led step by step toward salvation (Cl 18716-18729; own words; Documented). Draw no link from this order to Origen's division of beginners and advanced; no source makes it. He wrote that faith was taught to those who could not read as well. Quote 2 (Cl 26367-26372). Explain: the "school" is Eusebius's word; tell it as his.
2. **Origen's classroom (about 202 to 231).** The bishop, Demetrius, put the instruction in Origen's hands alone, and he gave up teaching grammar because he thought it did not fit with teaching the faith (E-en 32796-32813; Widely Accepted). Men and women came to study with him (E-en 33201-33203). The crowds came one after another from morning to evening and scarcely left him time to breathe, or to study and interpret Scripture himself, so he divided them: Heraclas, one of his first pupils, took the beginners and Origen kept the advanced (E-en 34407-34418; "scarcely" 34412, his own study 34408-34409; later; Widely Accepted). The gifted learned geometry, arithmetic and the philosophers; the less learned the basics; he said both helped them understand Scripture (E-en 34825-34843; Widely Accepted). Explain the reading in his own terms: Scripture, like a person, has body, soul and spirit; the plain story feeds the simple, its soul those who have gone further, its spirit the perfect (Ph 76; Documented; written before he left Alexandria, HE 6.24.3, E-en 36068-36069, 36098). Narrator's words, no quote. Tell the reading and the division side by side; draw no link between them (no source makes it).
3. **The pupils go on (231 to about 248).** Heraclas had already studied five years with a philosophy teacher and wore the philosopher's cloak, Origen wrote (E-en 35270-35280; Documented). When Origen left for Caesarea about 231, Heraclas took over the instruction and then became bishop (E-en 36330-36339; Widely Accepted). Dionysius, another of Origen's pupils, followed him in both (E-en 36552-36559, 37071-37080; Widely Accepted). The reason Origen left is not this story's (Origen story); give only that he left.
4. **The villages of Arsinoe (Dionysius's time; Eusebius gives no year).** Nepos, a bishop in Egypt, now dead, had written a book called Refutation of Allegorists. As his opponents describe it, he taught that Christ would reign for a thousand years of bodily luxury on this earth (E-en 41287-41295, "bodily luxury" 41290, E-gr 10578; Documented that he wrote it; its content only through opponents). In the district of Arsinoe, his teaching had held for a long time, and whole churches had broken away and fallen away (E-en 41349-41351; E-gr 10720-10721; own words; Documented). Dionysius loved Nepos for his faith, his work on Scripture and the many psalms of his that the brethren still delighted in, but he would answer the book (E-en 41312-41320). He gives his reason: some teachers, relying on the book, led simpler believers to hope for small and mortal things in God's kingdom, and left them no lofty thought of the resurrection and of being made like Christ (E-en 41324-41340; Documented, own words). He called together the presbyters and teachers of the villages, with any others who wished to come, and asked for a public examination; they brought him the book like a weapon and a fortress no one could take; he sat with them for three days, from morning to evening (E-en 41352-41359; Documented, own words). Quote 3 (E-gr 10734-10735). Say once that this is his own account; the other side left none.

**Ending** (Option 1): Coracion, who had led this teaching in the villages, said before all the brethren that he was convinced and would no longer hold or teach it; some of the brethren said they were glad of the meeting and of the agreement all had shown (E-en 41374-41381; Documented).

## What came of it (heaviest first)

1. The reading was carried on. A fifth-century historian, Socrates, says Basil and Gregory of Nazianzus drew "the right interpretation of the sacred Scriptures" from Origen's works (Soc 13913-13915); Gregory sent a friend "the volume of the Philocalia of Origen" (GN 47450-47451), a book of his passages that opens with how to read Scripture (Ph 16-17, 76). Documented (that Gregory sent it); the date is the editor's, not used. Card.
2. Pupils became the city's teachers and then its bishops: Heraclas, then Dionysius (C10-C12). Widely Accepted. Told in Beat 3.
3. The school ran on: in Eusebius's own day Achillas "was placed over the school of the sacred faith" (E-en 43201-43204). Documented. Card.
- Marked as defended: the deeper reading of the promises, and the hope of the resurrection and of being made like Christ, against Nepos's thousand years (E-en 41322-41341); defended by a public examination from Scripture (E-en 41349-41372).
- Who keeps it today: leave out.

## Quotes

   Source: Clement, The Instructor 3.11, Cl 26367-26372: English printed in 1885 (Wilson, ANF 2); the Greek is not held. "rude" rendered "unpolished" (its modern sense misleads). 3. **Dionysius.** Rendering: "If the argument won, we were not ashamed to change our minds and agree."
   Source: Dionysius, On the Promises, in Eusebius, Church History 7.24.8.
