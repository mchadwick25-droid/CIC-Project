# Outline: Alexandrian Christianity (`alexandria-catechetical`)

Kind: tradition. Sources: `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder (S-numbers below are its entries; file keys E-en, E-gr, Cl, Ph, Ath as defined there).

## The heart

- **The essence:** From about 180 to the 260s, the Christians of Alexandria, in Egypt, learned their faith the way their city learned everything, from teachers, in what Eusebius calls a school. Teachers took pagans and catechumens, learners not yet baptized, through Scripture read for a deeper sense beneath the plain story, and when village churches rejected that reading, their bishop sat with them for three days and argued it through. (B1, C1, C7, D5, C15. Widely Accepted; "school" as Eusebius's word, Contested as an institution.)
- **Who they were:** "Alexandrian Christianity" (census spoken name): the city's believers, their teachers, and the pupils who came to them, pagans, catechumens and the newly baptized, men and women (C1, C4, C5, C6). Documented / Widely Accepted.
- **When and where:** Alexandria, about 180 (Pantaenus, B1) to the 260s (Dionysius as bishop, C12-C17); the Arsinoite villages of the Egyptian countryside (C15). Widely Accepted.
- **What they faced:** persecution that killed the learners themselves, about 202 (B7, C5); crowds too large for one teacher (C7); and dissent from inside, a bishop's book against the allegorists that split whole churches (C13, C15). Widely Accepted / Documented.
- **What they believed:** that the Word, Jesus, first urges people on, then trains them, then teaches them (D1, D2); that Scripture has a body, a soul and a spirit, a plain sense for the simple and deeper ones for those further on (D5); that faith is taught to those who cannot read too, and its handbook is love (D3). Their own words; Documented.
- **How they practiced:** pupils with a teacher, from morning to evening (C7); beginners with one teacher, the advanced with another (C7); geometry and philosophy for the gifted, the basics for the less learned, all toward reading Scripture (C8); learners went with the teacher to the martyrs (C2). Widely Accepted.
- **What held them together:** the bond of teacher and pupil around Scripture read in layers (C7, C8, D5); and a habit of settling a quarrel over reading by public argument rather than by decree (C14-C16). (The built world's gravities: Scripture as formation, learning as formation.)
- **What came of them:** pupils became the teachers and bishops of the city (C10-C12); the reading itself was carried on (F2); the school ran on in Eusebius's own day (C18).

## Setting

- **Time:** late second century to the mid-third: "the first year of Commodus" for Pantaenus (E-en 28467-28476, 28488), about 180; "the tenth year of the reign of Severus", about 202 (E-en 32523-32533); Origen leaves about 231 (E-en 36330-36332); Dionysius bishop from about 247/8 (E-en 37071-37080). Spoken: "about 180", "around 200", "in the middle of the next century".
- **Place:** Alexandria, in Egypt (E-en 32428-32431); the district of Arsinoe, villages in the Egyptian countryside (E-en 41343-41353). Spoken: "Alexandria, in Egypt"; "villages around Arsinoe, in the Egyptian countryside".
- **Tradition:** "Alexandrian Christianity" (census `informalName`).

## Words to explain

- **catechumen:** a learner not yet baptized (E-en 32914-32916). Glossed where Herais is first heard, before the quote.
- **Stoics:** a school of Greek philosophers (E-en 28575-28576).
- **the deeper sense (allegory):** reading beneath the plain words for a hidden meaning about Christ and the soul (Ph 76). Give the word "allegorists" only with Nepos's book title, glossed.
- **Refutation of Allegorists:** a book against those who read Scripture for hidden meanings (E-en 41293-41295).
- **the thousand-year kingdom:** that Christ would reign on this earth for a thousand years of bodily plenty, as Nepos's opponents describe it (E-en 41288-41291).
- **presbyters:** elders (E-en 41352).
- **the philosopher's garment:** the cloak philosophers wore (E-en 35277-35280).
- **the Word:** Clement's name for Jesus (Cl 19998-19999).
- "school": tell as Eusebius's word ("what Eusebius calls a school"), never "academy" or "university".

## Openings

1. **(Planned.) The pupils who died, about 202.** Persecution under the emperor Severus had driven every teacher of the faith from Alexandria, and pagans came to hear the word of God from Origen, then in his eighteenth year. Some of his pupils were taken and put to death: one still a catechumen, one just baptized, and a young woman, Herais, who had not yet been baptized. Quote 1. (E-en 32670-32682, 32732-32733, 32893-32925; E-gr 1607-1614. Widely Accepted.)
2. (Alternative, for the card or Leave out.) The boy Origen puzzling his father with questions about the true meaning of Scripture, and the father rebuking him to his face, then thanking God in private (E-en 32588-32599). Set aside: it opens on a founder, the kit's trap.

## Story beats

**Hook** (Opening 1). One witness: say once, inside a sentence about him, that nearly all of this comes from Eusebius of Caesarea, writing about a century later from Origen's letters and from people who had known him (E-en 32511-32517).

**Essence** (as in the heart).

1. **Before the persecution: a teacher, then Clement (about 180 to 200).** Eusebius says a school of the faithful had stood in Alexandria "from ancient times", and that about 180 Pantaenus, trained among the Stoics, was its best-known teacher (E-en 28488-28539, 28561-28576; Widely Accepted; the institution, Contested). A bishop who studied there called Pantaenus and Clement "master" (E-en 34353-34356; Documented). Clement, who taught there next (E-en 33040-33044; Widely Accepted), wrote that the Word, his name for Jesus, first urges people on, then trains them, and last of all teaches them (Cl 18726-18729, 19998-19999; Documented). He wrote for those who could not read as well. Quote 2 (Cl 26367-26372). Explain: the "school" is Eusebius's word; tell it as his.
2. **Origen's classroom (about 202 to 231).** The bishop, Demetrius, put the instruction in Origen's hands alone, and he gave up teaching grammar because he thought it did not fit with teaching the faith (E-en 32796-32813; Widely Accepted). Men and women came to study with him (E-en 33201-33203). The crowds came from morning to evening and left him no time to breathe, so he divided them: Heraclas, one of his first pupils, took the beginners and Origen kept the advanced (E-en 34407-34418; Widely Accepted). The gifted learned geometry, arithmetic and the philosophers; the less learned the basics; he said both helped them understand Scripture (E-en 34825-34843; Widely Accepted). Explain the reading in his own terms: Scripture, like a person, has body, soul and spirit; the plain story feeds the simple, its soul those who have gone further, its spirit the perfect (Ph 76; Documented). The classroom matched the reading: beginners and the advanced. Narrator's words, no quote.
3. **The pupils go on (231 to about 248).** Heraclas had already studied five years with a philosophy teacher and wore the philosopher's cloak, Origen wrote (E-en 35270-35280; Documented). When Origen left for Caesarea about 231, Heraclas took over the instruction and then became bishop (E-en 36330-36339; Widely Accepted). Dionysius, another of Origen's pupils, followed him in both (E-en 36552-36559, 37071-37080; Widely Accepted). The reason Origen left is not this story's (Origen story); give only that he left.
4. **The villages of Arsinoe (Dionysius as bishop, about 248 to 264).** Nepos, a bishop in Egypt, now dead, had written a book called Refutation of Allegorists. As his opponents describe it, he taught that Christ would reign for a thousand years of bodily plenty on this earth (E-en 41287-41295; Documented that he wrote it; its content only through opponents). In the district of Arsinoe, his teaching had split whole churches from the rest (E-en 41349-41351). Dionysius loved Nepos for his faith, his work on Scripture and the psalms the brethren still sang, but he would answer the book (E-en 41312-41320). He called together the presbyters and teachers of the villages, with any others who wished to come, and asked for a public examination; they brought him the book like a weapon and a fortress no one could take; he sat with them for three days, from morning to evening (E-en 41352-41359; Documented, own words). Quote 3 (E-gr 10734-10735). Say once that this is his own account; the other side left none.

**Ending** (Option 1): Coracion, the leader of Nepos's party, said before all the brethren that he was convinced and would no longer hold or teach it; some of the brethren said they were glad of the meeting and of the agreement all had shown (E-en 41374-41381; Documented).

## What came of it (heaviest first)

1. Pupils became the city's teachers and then its bishops: Heraclas, then Dionysius (C10-C12). Widely Accepted. Told in Beat 3.
2. The reading was carried on. A fifth-century historian, Socrates, says Basil and Gregory of Nazianzus drew "the right interpretation of the sacred Scriptures" from Origen's works (Soc 13913-13915); about 382 Gregory sent a friend "the volume of the Philocalia of Origen" (GN 47450-47451), a book of his passages that opens with how to read Scripture (Ph 16-17, 76). Documented. Card.
3. The school ran on: in Eusebius's own day Achillas "was placed over the school of the sacred faith" (E-en 43201-43204). Documented. Card.
- Marked as defended: the public examination of a disputed reading, against settling it by decree (C14, C16).
- Who keeps it today: leave out.

Faith belongs not to the wise by the world's measure, but to the wise by God's. It is taught without letters. Its handbook, unpolished and divine at once, is called love, a spiritual book."
   Source: Clement, The Instructor 3.11, Cl 26367-26372: English printed in 1885 (Wilson, ANF 2); the Greek is not held. "rude" rendered "unpolished" (its modern sense misleads). 3. **Dionysius.** Rendering: "If the argument won, we were not ashamed to change our minds and agree."

Not used elsewhere on the tree; the built world's line "the truth should be loved and honored most of all" is not used.

## Connection to carry into the story

- The teacher-pupil bond passed the instruction down a line of the city's own pupils: Origen to Heraclas, Heraclas to Dionysius, each taking the instruction and then the bishop's chair (E-en 36330-36339, 36552-36559, 37071-37080). The listener needs to see that the bishop who sat three days in the villages was himself a pupil of that classroom (E-en 36557-36559); say it in Beat 3, so Beat 4 shows the classroom's habit of question and answer carried into a quarrel (E-en 41322-41324, 41361-41369). Through action, not a "this matters because" line.
- The villages had "teachers" of their own (E-en 41352-41353): the same pattern of teaching reached the countryside. Let the word stand; do not explain it further.

## Do not say

- "the Catechetical School of Alexandria" as a fixed institution, "academy", "university", "the first Christian university" → "what Eusebius calls a school", "the instruction in the faith".
- "an unbroken line of heads from Mark" → leave out; Jerome's late claim (Jer 40209-40211).
- "Herais was burned alive" / "at the stake" → only Origen's phrase, "the baptism by fire" (E-gr 1614).
- "Origen's pupils were arrested because they were his pupils" → no charge is given; the persecution under Severus is the setting (E-en 32397-32433).
- "Origen escaped by a miracle" in the narrator's voice → if told, "Eusebius says God's hand guarded him" (E-en 32766-32770). Planned: leave out.
- "Clement headed the school" in the narrator's voice → "Clement taught there next, Eusebius says" (E-en 33040-33044).
- "Nepos was a heretic" / "Nepos's followers were heretics" → "Nepos, a bishop in Egypt"; the record calls the result "schisms and apostasies of entire churches" (E-en 41349-41351), Dionysius's words.
- "Nepos taught a crude, sensual paradise" → "as his opponents describe it, a thousand years of bodily plenty on this earth" (E-en 41288-41291).
- "Dionysius condemned Nepos" → he loved him and answered his book (E-en 41312-41320).
- "everyone agreed" / "the villages were all won back" → Coracion said he was convinced, and "some of the other brethren" were glad (E-en 41374-41380).
- "Dionysius alone changed no one's mind by force" → no claim about force either way; tell what he did.
- "Dionysius changed his mind" → the quote's "we" is the whole meeting; do not say who changed.
- "the classroom fed the desert monks" / "Antony learned from Origen" → leave out (see Leave out).
- "Origen was condemned for these ideas" → not this story.
- "philosophy" as a modern academic subject → "the Greek philosophers' teaching" where needed.

## Leave out

- The desert link (census, Contested): Athanasius's Antony grew up at home, "knowing nought else but them and his home", and "could not endure to learn letters" (Ath 31218-31223); the held old sources give no Alexandrian teacher in his life. The only support is modern. Not told; the census line stands unchanged.
- Origen's "daring deed" and the break with Demetrius: the Origen story and the built world tell them; Contested in part.
- Porphyry's charge against Origen's reading (E-en 34974-35124; E-gr 3461-3468): an enemy's view of the way of reading, strong, but written after the three days and so out of order for the story's close. Card, in its own content: that the readers "boast that the plain words of Moses are enigmas" and that Origen carried the figurative reading of the Greek mysteries over to the Jewish Scriptures.
- Pantaenus's journey to India (E-en 28578-28635): "it is reported"; a side story.
- Dionysius's vision, "Read everything" (E-en 39569-39577): a wonder in his own voice; card.
- Dionysius's suspicion of a deeper sense in Revelation (E-en 41418-41428): the Dionysius story's chapter.
- The plague, the Decian persecution, Potamiaena: other stories and the built world; barred quotes.
- Origen's account of testing hearers (Against Celsus 3.51): of Christians in general, written at Caesarea.
- Jerome's "from the time of Mark" (Jer 40209-40211) and Photius's succession list: late and derived.
- The History of the Patriarchs's legend of Dionysius and the widow's book (HP 11712-11892): late legend; card.
- Didymus as a late head of the school: his own story; card.
