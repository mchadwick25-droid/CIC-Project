## A. How we know: the main witness

**A1. Eusebius says where his account of Origen comes from.** E-en 32511-32517. Later (Eusebius writes about a century after 202; he names his sources). Widely Accepted.
> "Many things might be said in attempting to describe the life of the man while in school; but this subject alone would require a separate treatise. Nevertheless, for the present, abridging most things, we shall state a few facts concerning him as briefly as possible, gathering them from certain letters, and from the statement of persons still living who were acquainted with him."

Use: the story leans on Eusebius for almost every event before Dionysius. Say so once, early, inside a sentence about him.

## B. Setting

**B1. A school of the faithful at Alexandria, "from ancient times."** E-en 28488, 28537-28539, 28561-28576. Later (Eusebius, a century on; he hedges: "as we have been informed", "it is reported"). The teaching and Pantaenus: Widely Accepted. An organized school with an unbroken line of heads: Contested (Eusebius's frame; Clement's own surviving words never describe heading one). "About that time" follows HE 5.9, the first year of Commodus (E-en 28467-28476), so about 180: Widely Accepted.
> "1. About that time, Pantænus, a man highly distinguished for his learning, had charge of the school of the faithful in Alexandria. A school of sacred learning, which continues to our day, was established there in ancient times, and as we have been informed, was managed by men of great ability and zeal for divine things. Among these it is reported that Pantænus was at that time especially conspicuous, as he had been educated in the philosophical system of those called Stoics."

**B2. Pantaenus head of the school.** E-en 28637-28645. Later. Widely Accepted.
> "4. After many good deeds, Pantænus finally became the head of the school at Alexandria, and expounded the treasures of divine doctrine both orally and in writing."

**B3. A contemporary calls Pantaenus and Clement "master."** E-en 34330-34356 (Alexander of Jerusalem's letter to Origen, quoted by Eusebius). Contemporary, own words of a pupil. Documented.
> "Pantænus, the truly blessed man and master, and the holy Clement, my master and benefactor, and if there is any other like them, through whom I became acquainted with thee, the best in everything, my master and brother."

**B4. Clement in charge of the instruction; Origen his pupil.** E-en 33015-33044, 33079-33086. Later. Widely Accepted.
> "Clement having succeeded Pantænus, had charge at that time of the catechetical instruction in Alexandria, so that Origen also, while still a boy, was one of his pupils. In the first book of the work called Stromata, which Clement wrote, he gives a chronological table, bringing events down to the death of Commodus. So it is evident that that work was written during the reign of Severus, whose times we are now recording."

**B5. Jerome, two centuries on, takes the teachers back to Mark.** Jer 40207-40214 (On Illustrious Men 36, written about 392). Later, gap about 200 years after Pantaenus; Jerome's own editor (E-en endnote 1507, inside 28561-28571) says he draws on Eusebius's "from ancient times." Inferential-Thin for the Mark claim.
> "Pantaenus, a philosopher of the stoic school, according to some old Alexandrian custom, where, from the time of Mark the evangelist the ecclesiastics were always doctors, was of so great prudence and erudition both in scripture and secular literature that, on the request of the legates of that nation, he was sent to India by Demetrius bishop of Alexandria,"

**B6. Jerome on Clement.** Jer 40249-40252 (On Illustrious Men 38). Later. Widely Accepted (follows Eusebius).
> "Clemens, presbyter of the Alexandrian church, and a pupil of the Pantaenus mentioned above, led the theological school at Alexandria after the death of his master and was teacher of the Catechetes."

**B7. The persecution under Severus fills Alexandria with martyrs.** E-en 32397, 32428-32433, 32459-32460. Later. Widely Accepted. Date: "the tenth year of the reign of Severus" (E-en 32523-32533), about 202, Widely Accepted.
> "When Severus began to persecute the churches, glorious testimonies were given everywhere by the athletes of religion. This was especially the case in Alexandria, to which city, as to a most prominent theater, athletes of God were brought from Egypt and all Thebais according to their merit, and won crowns from God through their great patience under many tortures and every mode of death. Among these was Leonides, who was called the father of Origen, and who was beheaded while his son was still young."

## C. Events

**C1. The teachers have fled; pagans come to an eighteen-year-old.** E-en 32670-32682, 32727-32733. Later; Eusebius cites Origen's own writing ("as he tells us himself"). Widely Accepted. Greek E-gr 1215-1219 (μηδενός τε ἐπὶ τῆς Ἀλεξανδρείας τῷ κατηχεῖν ἀνακειμένου, πάντων δ' ἀπεληλαμένων ὑπὸ τῆς ἀπειλῆς τοῦ διωγμοῦ, προσῄεσαν αὐτῷ τινες ἀπὸ τῶν ἐθνῶν ἀκουσόμενοι τὸν λόγον τοῦ θεοῦ).
> "1. But while he was lecturing in the school, as he tells us himself, and there was no one at Alexandria to give instruction in the faith, as all were driven away by the threat of persecution, some of the heathen came to him to hear the word of God. 2. The first of them, he says, was Plutarch, who after living well, was honored with divine martyrdom. The second was Heraclas, a brother of Plutarch; who after he too had given with him abundant evidence of a philosophic and ascetic life, was esteemed worthy to succeed Demetrius in the bishopric of Alexandria. 3. He was in his eighteenth year when he took charge of the catechetical school."

Note: "lecturing in the school" here is his grammar school (see C3); the Greek σχολάζοντι δὲ τῇ διατριβῇ.

**C2. Origen beside the martyrs; soldiers round his house.** E-en 32758-32764, 32773-32784. Later. Widely Accepted (Eusebius has the escapes as God's doing: "through the helping hand of God, he escaped absolutely and marvelously", 32766-32770; that stays in Eusebius's voice).
> "4. For not only was he with them while in bonds, and until their final condemnation, but when the holy martyrs were led to death, he was very bold and went with them into danger. So that as he acted bravely, and with great boldness saluted the martyrs with a kiss, oftentimes the heathen multitude round about them became infuriated, and were on the point of rushing upon him."
> "So great was the enmity of the unbelievers toward him, on account of the multitude that were instructed by him in the sacred faith, that they placed bands of soldiers around the house where he abode. 6. Thus day by day the persecution burned against him, so that the whole city could no longer contain him; but he removed from house to house and was driven in every direction because of the multitude who attended upon the divine instruction which he gave."

**C3. The bishop gives him the instruction; he gives up his grammar school.** E-en 32796-32813. Later. Widely Accepted.
> "8. But when he saw yet more coming to him for instruction, and the catechetical school had been entrusted to him alone by Demetrius, who presided over the church, he considered the teaching of grammatical science inconsistent with training in divine subjects, and forthwith he gave up his grammatical school as unprofitable and a hindrance to sacred learning."

**C4. Learned pagans come; some of his pupils die.** E-en 32877-32884. Later. Widely Accepted.
> "13. By giving such evidences of a philosophic life to those who saw him, he aroused many of his pupils to similar zeal; so that prominent men even of the unbelieving heathen and men that followed learning and philosophy were led to his instruction. Some of them having received from him into the depth of their souls faith in the Divine Word, became prominent in the persecution then prevailing; and some of them were seized and suffered martyrdom."

**C5. The pupils who died: Plutarch, Serenus, Heraclides, Hero, a second Serenus, Herais.** E-en 32893-32925. Later; the Herais line cites Origen's own words ("as Origen himself somewhere says"); the second Serenus is "it is reported." Widely Accepted. Greek E-gr 1485-1491, 1607-1614.
> "1. The first of these was Plutarch, who was mentioned just above. As he was led to death, the man of whom we are speaking being with him at the end of his life, came near being slain by his fellow-citizens, as if he were the cause of his death. But the providence of God preserved him at this time also. 2. After Plutarch, the second martyr among the pupils of Origen was Serenus, who gave through fire a proof of the faith which he had received. 3. The third martyr from the same school was Heraclides, and after him the fourth was Hero. The former of these was as yet a catechumen, and the latter had but recently been baptized. Both of them were beheaded. After them, the fifth from the same school proclaimed as an athlete of piety was another Serenus, who, it is reported, was beheaded, after a long endurance of tortures. And of women, Herais died while yet a catechumen, receiving baptism by fire, as Origen himself somewhere says."

Greek of the Herais line, E-gr 1613-1614 (OCR as printed; "Hoais" is Ἡραΐς, "χατηχουμένη" is κατηχουμένη):
> "xci γυναικῶν δὲ “Hoais ἔτι χατηχουμένη τὸ βάπτισμα, ὥς / πού φησιν αὐτός. τὸ διὰ πυρὸς λαβοῦσα, τὸν βίον ἐξελήλυϑεν."

Whether Herais died by burning: the phrase "the baptism through fire" points that way, and Serenus before her died "through fire"; Eusebius does not state her manner of death separately. Inferential-Thin. Tell only Origen's phrase.

**C6. Men and women together at his lessons.** E-en 33140-33144, 33196-33203. Later. Widely Accepted (the act itself, the "daring deed," is the Origen story's and the built world's; Contested; leave out here).
> "for, although young, he met for the study of divine things with women as well as men,"

**C7. The crowds; Origen divides his students with Heraclas.** E-en 34407-34418. Later. Widely Accepted.
> "1. But when he saw that he had not time for the deeper study of divine things, and for the investigation and interpretation of the Sacred Scriptures, and also for the instruction of those who came to him,—for coming, one after another, from morning till evening to be taught by him, they scarcely gave him time to breathe,—he divided the multitude. And from those whom he knew well, he selected Heraclas, who was a zealous student of divine things, and in other respects a very learned man, not ignorant of philosophy, and made him his associate in the work of instruction. He entrusted to him the elementary training of beginners, but reserved for himself the teaching of those who were farther advanced."

**C8. What he taught: geometry and philosophy for the gifted, the basics for the less learned, all toward Scripture.** E-en 34818-34843. Later. Widely Accepted.
> "2. Many others also, drawn by the fame of Origen's learning, which resounded everywhere, came to him to make trial of his skill in sacred literature. And a great many heretics, and not a few of the most distinguished philosophers, studied under him diligently, receiving instruction from him not only in divine things, but also in secular philosophy. 3. For when he perceived that any persons had superior intelligence he instructed them also in philosophic branches—in geometry, arithmetic, and other preparatory studies—and then advanced to the systems of the philosophers and explained their writings. … 4. And he instructed many of the less learned in the common school branches, saying that these would be no small help to them in the study and understanding of the Divine Scriptures."

**C9. Origen's own account of who came to him, and of Heraclas.** E-en 35259-35280 (a letter of Origen quoted by Eusebius). Own words. Documented.
> "12. “When I devoted myself to the word, and the fame of my proficiency went abroad, and when heretics and persons conversant with Grecian learning, and particularly with philosophy, came to me, it seemed necessary that I should examine the doctrines of the heretics, and what the philosophers say concerning the truth. 13. And in this we have followed Pantænus, who benefited many before our time by his thorough preparation in such things, and also Heraclas, who is now a member of the presbytery of Alexandria. I found him with the teacher of philosophic learning, with whom he had already continued five years before I began to hear lectures on those subjects. 14. And though he had formerly worn the common dress, he laid it aside and assumed and still wears the philosopher's garment; and he continues the earnest investigation of Greek works.”"

**C10. Origen leaves; Heraclas takes the school, then the bishop's chair.** E-en 36330-36339 (HE 6.26). Later. Widely Accepted. Date: "the tenth year" of Alexander Severus, about 231, Widely Accepted.

Not long afterward Demetrius, bishop of the church of Alexandria, died, having held the office for forty-three full years, and Heraclas succeeded him."

**C11. Dionysius, Origen's pupil, takes the school.** E-en 36552-36559 (HE 6.29.4). Later. Widely Accepted.
> "And in Alexandria Heraclas, having received the episcopal office after Demetrius, was succeeded in the charge of the catechetical school by Dionysius, who had also been one of Origen's pupils."

**C12. Dionysius becomes bishop.** E-en 37071-37080 (HE 6.35). Later. Widely Accepted. Date "the third year" of Philip, about 247/8.
> "In the third year of this emperor, Heraclas died, having held his office for sixteen years, and Dionysius received the episcopate of the churches of Alexandria."

**C13. Nepos and his book.** E-en 41287-41295 (HE 7.24.1-2). Later (Eusebius summarizes; the winning side's description). Documented that the book existed and its title; its content only as its opponents give it. Greek E-gr 10575-10584 (Ἔλεγχον ἀλληγοριστῶν).

## D. Belief and practice: the way of reading, in their own words

**D1. Clement: the Word first urges, then trains, then teaches.** Cl 18716-18729 (The Instructor 1.1). Own words. Documented.
> "As, then, for those of us who are diseased in body a physician is required, so also those who are diseased in soul require a pædagogue to cure our maladies; and then a teacher, to train and guide the soul to all requisite knowledge when it is made able to admit the revelation of the Word. Eagerly desiring, then, to perfect us by a gradation conducive to salvation, suited for efficacious discipline, a beautiful arrangement is observed by the all-benignant Word, who first exhorts, then trains, and finally teaches."

**D2. Clement: the Word is Jesus.** Cl 19998-20000 (The Instructor 1.7). Own words. Documented.
> "But our Instructor is the holy God Jesus, the Word, who is the guide of all humanity. The loving God Himself is our Instructor."

**D3. Clement: those who cannot read, and the market and the shop.** Cl 26363-26372, 26382-26383 (The Instructor 3.11, under the heading "Religion in Ordinary Life", 26361). Own words. Documented.
> "But it is said we do not all philosophize. Do we not all, then, follow after life? What sayest thou? How hast thou believed? How, pray, dost thou love God and thy neighbour, if thou dost not philosophize? And how dost thou love thyself, if thou dost not love life? It is said, I have not learned letters; but if thou hast not learned to read, thou canst not excuse thyself in the case of hearing, for it is not taught. And faith is the possession not of the wise according to the world, but of those according to God; and it is taught without letters; and its handbook, at once rude and divine, is called love—a spiritual book."
> "And in this way those who frequent the market-place and the shop philosophize."

**D4. Clement: women too.** Cl 38643-38645 (Stromata 4.8). Own words. Documented. For the card; the story uses D3.
> "Women are therefore to philosophize equally with men, though the males are preferable at everything, unless they have become effeminate."

**D5. Origen: Scripture has flesh, soul and spirit, for the simple, the advancing and the perfect.** Ph line 76 (Philocalia 1.11 = On First Principles 4.2.4). Own words (English of the Greek Philocalia, 1911). Documented.
> "A man ought then in three ways to record in his own soul the purposes of the Holy Scriptures; that the simple may be edified by, as it were, the flesh of Scripture (for thus we designate the primary sense), the more advanced by its soul, and the perfect by the spiritual law, which has a shadow of the good things to come. … As man consists of body, soul, and spirit, so too does Scripture which has been granted by God for the salvation of men."

Note: the live Origen story tells this teaching in paraphrase. This story gives it as the reading its classrooms were built on (C7, C8), in the narrator's words, with no quote.

**D6. The young Origen asks for more than the plain sense.** E-en 32588-32599 (HE 6.2.9-10). Later. Widely Accepted. Possible second opening; set aside below.
> "9. … And he was not satisfied with learning what was simple and obvious in the sacred words, but sought for something more, and even at that age busied himself with deeper speculations. So that he puzzled his father with inquiries for the true meaning of the inspired Scriptures. 10. And his father rebuked him seemingly to his face, telling him not to search beyond his age, or further than the manifest meaning. But by himself he rejoiced greatly and thanked God, the author of all good, that he had deemed him worthy to be the father of such a child."

**D7. Testing hearers before admitting them.** `cic/texts/anf04_tertullian4-minucius-felix-commodian-origen1-2.xml` 44839-44854 (Origen, Against Celsus 3.51). Own words, but written at Caesarea of Christians in general, not of Alexandria. Documented for the practice in general; not tied to Alexandria. Leave out.
> "The Christians, however, having previously, so far as possible, tested the souls of those who wish to become their hearers, and having previously instructed them in private, when they appear (before entering the community) to have sufficiently evinced their desire towards a virtuous life, introduce them then, and not before, privately forming one class of those who are beginners, and are receiving admission, but who have not yet obtained the mark of complete purification; …"

**D8. Dionysius reads the heretics' books, at a vision's command.** E-en 39558-39577 (HE 7.7.1-3). Own words; a wonder (the vision) that stays in his voice. Documented that he says so. Card or Leave out.

## E. Explain it: each thing or act the story could name, from each side the held texts give

- **Catechumen.** Their side: someone receiving instruction who is not yet baptized; Eusebius sets "as yet a catechumen" against "had but recently been baptized" (E-en 32914-32916). The Greek κατηχούμενος, "being instructed" (E-gr 1609, 1613). No hostile text names it. Plain word: "a learner not yet baptized."
- **The school / the catechetical instruction.** Eusebius: "the school of the faithful," "a school of sacred learning … established there in ancient times" (B1), with a head who was put in charge by the bishop (C3). Clement's surviving works (D1, D3) describe the Word's order of teaching, not an institution. Jerome takes it back to Mark (B5), later and derived. Plain word: "the instruction in the faith that Eusebius calls a school"; never "an academy" or "a university".
- **Stoics.** A school of Greek philosophy; the only held line is Eusebius's "the philosophical system of those called Stoics" (B1). Gloss: "a school of Greek philosophers."
- **Reading for the deeper sense (allegory).** Origen's side: Scripture, like a person, has a body (the plain story), a soul and a spirit; the simple feed on the first, the advancing on the second, the perfect on the third (D5). The enemy's side: Porphyry, a pagan philosopher who met Origen when young, charged that such readers "boast that the plain words of Moses are enigmas," and that Origen took the figurative reading of the Greek mysteries and "applied it to the Jewish Scriptures" (E-en 34974-34982, 35122-35124; Greek E-gr 3461-3468). An insider's side: Nepos titled his book "Refutation of Allegorists" and read the promises literally (C13); his own text is lost. Plain gloss: "reading beneath the plain words for a hidden sense about Christ and the soul."
- **The thousand-year kingdom Nepos taught.** Only his opponents give it: "a certain millennium of bodily luxury upon this earth" (Eusebius, C13); Dionysius: it led "simpler brethren" to "hope for small and mortal things in the kingdom of God" (C14). Nepos's own words are not held. Plain gloss: "that Christ would reign on this earth for a thousand years of plenty." Tell it as what Nepos taught "as his opponents describe it."
- **Refutation of Allegorists** (the book). Its title and aim from Eusebius (C13); held like "a weapon and fortress impregnable" by the villages (C15). Gloss: "a book against those who read Scripture for hidden meanings."
- **Presbyters and teachers of the villages.** Dionysius's own phrase (C15). Gloss presbyters: "elders."
- **The three days.** Why: the doctrine had split whole churches (C15); Nepos was dead, so his book had to be answered in person (C14, "as if he were present"). Told as Dionysius tells it.
- **The pupils' deaths.** Why they died: the persecution under Severus (B7); Eusebius gives no charge for each. Tell the act with the persecution as its setting, no reason added.
- **Origen giving up his grammar school.** Why: he thought it "inconsistent with training in divine subjects" (C3).
- **Origen dividing his students.** Why: no time "to breathe" between the crowds and his own study (C7).
- **The philosopher's garment.** Origen's words (C9): Heraclas left "the common dress" for it. Gloss: "the cloak philosophers wore."
- **Desert link (Contested on the census).** Tested: Athanasius's Life of Antony does not carry it. Antony grew up at home with Christian parents, "knowing nought else but them and his home," and "could not endure to learn letters" (Ath 31215-31223); later he met philosophers "although he had not learned letters" (Ath 32982-32984). The held old sources show no Alexandrian teacher in Antony's formation. The only support named on the census edge is a modern reading (Rubenson). Leave out of the story.

## F. What came of it (heaviest first: what later witnesses carried on and used)

1. **Pupils became the city's teachers and bishops.** Heraclas, Origen's first pupils' brother, takes the school and then the see (C1, C10); Dionysius, Origen's pupil, takes the school and then the see (C11, C12). Widely Accepted. Carried on by Eusebius.
2. **The reading itself carried on.** Socrates (fifth century, later, gap about two centuries): Basil and Gregory of Nazianzus "procured Origen's works, and drew from them the right interpretation of the sacred Scriptures" (Soc 13913-13915). Gregory of Nazianzus, about 382, own words: "we send you the volume of the Philocalia of Origen, containing a selection of passages useful to students of literature" (GN 47450-47451); the date and the joint editing by Basil and Gregory come from the editor's heading (GN 47442-47443, modern) and the editors' prolegomena (modern). Documented that Gregory sent it. The Philocalia opens with the chapters on reading Scripture (Ph lines 16-17, table of contents; Ph 76).
3. **The school outlives its founders.** In Eusebius's own day, Achillas "was placed over the school of the sacred faith" (C18); "which continues to our day" (B1). Documented (contemporary). Later still, Didymus is remembered as teaching there (live Didymus story; Socrates 4.25, Soc 13797-13808); for the card.
4. **Later lists of heads.** Photius, ninth century (later, gap about 600 years), repeats the succession as hearsay ("It is said that Clement was the pupil of Pantaenus and his successor as head of his school") and calls Pierius "the head of the catechetical school at Alexandria" (`cic/texts/photius_library-v1_freese1920.txt` 11474-11478, 11506-11507). Inferential-Thin. Leave out.
5. **The Coptic church's late memory (argued against Origen, honored Dionysius).** History of the Patriarchs, compiled much later (late legend; the census edge gives the 900s): "And Heraclas gave the direction of the studies at Alexandria to Dionysius" (HP 11706-11710); a legend of Dionysius the pagan philosopher converted by reading Paul's letters bought from an old widow (HP 11712-11720, 11884-11892); and it calls Origen's books "full of lies" (HP 10942-10943). Late legend. Card only.

## G. Links to other people and traditions

- Origen (person, live): taught; his pupils are this story's learners. Shared event: his eighteenth year (C1). This story tells the pupils, not him.
- Clement of Alexandria (person, live): taught; his teaching order and his word to those who cannot read (D1, D3).
- Dionysius of Alexandria (person, live): pupil, head of the instruction, bishop; the three days (C15-C17). His Revelation argument belongs to his own story.
- Eusebius of Caesarea (person on the tree): the record's main writer (A1).
- Gregory of Nazianzus (person on the tree): sent the Philocalia (F2). Card.
- Antony the Great / Desert Monasticism: census gave-rise-to, Contested; the old sources tested do not carry it (E, last item).
- Antiochene Church (batch sibling): Dionysius's letter on Paul of Samosata; not used here.

## H. Where sources disagree or come late

- The "school" as an institution with a line of heads: Eusebius (a century on) and Jerome (two centuries on, from Eusebius) say so; Clement and Origen's surviving words describe teachers and pupils, never an office of head. Tell it as Eusebius's word.
- Jerome's "from the time of Mark" (B5) is later and, by his own editor's note, drawn from Eusebius. Leave out.
- Nepos's teaching survives only in his opponents' words (C13, C14). Say so.
- Dionysius's account of the three days is his own, of his own success (C15-C17). Say so once.
- Porphyry (enemy) versus Eusebius (defender) on whether Origen was a convert from paganism: Eusebius says Porphyry lies there (E-en 35189-35198). Card, if Porphyry is used at all.
