Around 124 a Christian named Quadratus handed the emperor Hadrian a written defense of his religion. For the next eighty years, others kept doing the same. Aristides did it at Athens. Justin did it twice, and was put to death for it about 165. He was a philosopher who kept wearing the philosopher's cloak after his conversion, and he ran a school at Rome. He also attacked Marcion and the Valentinians, whom he called Christians in name only. Athenagoras addressed Marcus Aurelius, and so did Melito of Sardis. Theophilus wrote three books to a pagan friend named Autolycus. An unknown writer produced the letter to Diognetus, still the most quoted thing any of them wrote. The charges were always roughly the same, and so were the answers. Christians were called atheists because they had no images. They were called cannibals because of what they said about eating a body. And they were called disloyal because they would not sacrifice. What made the effort interesting was the second move. Several of them, Justin above all, did more than deny the charges. They argued that the best of Greek philosophy had been reaching toward what the church now held. Christians were therefore the true heirs of reason, not its enemies.
