## Story or summary

I heard it as a **summary with scenes in it**, not a story. Each paragraph opens on a reign or a city ("Long before, in the reign of Hadrian", "Under the next emperor", "Under Marcus Aurelius", "In the Roman province of Asia", "At Antioch", "At Carthage"). That is a survey's march. Each person comes in, does one thing and leaves. Quadratus only "addressed a defense". Theophilus and Tertullian get two or three sentences each. The danger is in the record: being hunted by name, informers, Justin's death. But it never builds or resolves. It just sits under each new name. Athenagoras gives the piece a spine: he opens it and comes back with the gladiator answer, the strongest scene. The Diognetus close ends on the community's own words. The middle is where the roll-call risk the slate named comes true.

## 1. The test (tradition)

- **Who they were:** filled. Greek-writing Christians who wrote defenses to emperors and pagans.
- **What happened to them:** filled. Called godless, cannibals and incestuous; hunted "for their name alone"; robbed by informers; Justin put to death.
- **What their faith was about:** filled. "the one God who made all things"; worshipped God alone; prayed for the emperors; asked to be judged by how they lived.
- **Why the connection matters:** thin. All I can say is "Tertullian took up the same task in Latin". I cannot say what passed to him, or why refuting Valentinus's followers belongs to a defense made to emperors (see B1).

## 2. The opening

Sentences 1–3 give who (Athenagoras, Christians), why (hunted for the name, false accusers) and, by sentence 3, when ("In the second century"). "Athenian" gives where he came from. The emperors' names mean nothing to a newcomer until "second century" arrives, but it arrives in time. Nobody is lost.

## 3. Sentences that fail on one hearing (MINOR)

1. "Long before, in the reign of Hadrian, certain wicked men were trying to trouble the Christians." Long before what? After the essence the listener cannot place Hadrian, and "certain wicked men" names no one and no act.
2. "…and we gladly accept the honour of such a death." No death has been mentioned. The paragraph is about robbery, so the listener loses whose death and why.
3. "Yet one stood against Christians, who said the same." "One" has to be held back to "law" across a short sentence. On one hearing it slips.
4. "He granted that they were atheists of such gods…" It is understood, but only just: "atheists of such gods" is a translator's phrase.

## 4. Bare statements (BLOCKING)

- **B1.** "He wrote against the followers of a teacher, Valentinus. They stacked heaven on heaven for their gods, he scoffed, and he named Justin first among the earlier writers who had refuted them." A quarrel is named, but the listener never learns what Valentinus taught beyond a scoff, or why it mattered. Right after "took up the same task", it also sounds as if refuting Valentinians *was* the task of defending Christians to emperors. **Change:** give, in one plain line from the outline, what the Valentinians taught and why the apologists opposed them, and make clear that this was a second task beside the defense. Or cut both sentences and end the paragraph at "in Latin."
- **B2.** "…thought Christian books were new and their teaching nonsense. Theophilus wrote to him, counting back the years to show that the books of Moses… were older than the Greeks' own." The answer is told, but its point is not: a newcomer does not know why being older would answer the charge. **Change:** add the reason from the outline's Theophilus line (why age counted for truth to his friend). If the source gives none, tell the act alone without "to show" building a case the listener cannot follow.

## 5. Citation read aloud (MINOR)

- "…and Eusebius, a church historian, still had the book in the early 300s." This is a bookkeeping note, not part of the story. In the narrator's voice: "So Quadratus, a Christian, addressed a defense to the emperor, and his book was still read more than a century later."

## 6. Tells

- **B3 (BLOCKING).** "Only the passages Eusebius copied from his petition survive." This sentence only says what is missing. It is a standalone how-we-know sentence, which Part 1 bars. **Change:** cut it.

No "not X but Y", no dashes, no stock words. "How then could we commit murder?" is Athenagoras's own question inside a quote, so it is not a tell.

### Lines that sound machine-made (skeptic)

1. **The appositive-gloss tic.** I count nine noun-comma-gloss pairs: "an Athenian Christian, Athenagoras", "an apology, the kind of speech made in court", "Quadratus, a Christian", "Eusebius, a church historian", "new decrees, new written orders", "Informers, people who laid charges…", "the gladiator shows, fights to the death…", "the books of Moses, the first in the Bible", "a teacher, Valentinus". Each one is fine alone. Together they are the cadence of a model glossing on cue. Work some glosses into what happens instead.
2. **The reign/city paragraph openers** listed above. This is template structure.
3. "Such a defense was called an apology, the kind of speech made in court." A textbook definition sentence, dropped in after the summary.
4. "In the second century, from Athens and Rome to Sardis and Antioch, Christians who wrote in Greek put written defenses…" The sweeping "from X and Y to Z and W" survey opener is a generated-summary habit.
5. "certain wicked men were trying to trouble" / "the godly were driven about". These are translation English pasted into the narrator's voice. They read as lifted, not told.

## 7. Words, runs, tone

- **Unexplained:** "Carthage" has no place, where Antioch gets "in Syria". "The godly" is archaic for "Christians".
- **Short-sentence runs:** (a) "No law stood against them. Yet one stood against Christians, who said the same. As for eating human flesh, no one claimed to have seen it." (b) The end of the Justin paragraph: "Everywhere, he wrote, they took care to pay their taxes… They worshipped God alone, and gladly served the emperors in all else." Also, the one-sentence Justin-death paragraph lands as a bare notice between two scenes.
- **Talking down:** none.

## 8. Attention

The opening pulls me in. Emperors hailed as philosophers by the people they were hunting is a good hook. Attention first drifts at "Long before, in the reign of Hadrian…": a jump back, an emperor I cannot place, faceless villains, then a note about who kept the book. It drifts again at the Melito gap sentence, and in the Tertullian paragraph, which changes subject. I would listen to the end, carried by the gladiator answer and the Diognetus close.
