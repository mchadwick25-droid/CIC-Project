## 1. The test, for a tradition

- **Who these people were:** filled. Greek-speaking Christian writers of the second century who sent written defenses to emperors and pagans ("They are known as the apologists, from apology, a defense like a speech in court.").
- **What happened to them:** filled. They were charged with three crimes, robbed under new decrees in Asia, and Justin "was condemned and put to death for refusing to sacrifice to the gods."
- **What their faith was about:** filled, but thin. I learn "the one God who made all things", prayer for the emperors, and no watching of killings. Almost all of it comes as an answer to a charge. I never hear what they believed about Christ, who is named only once ("as Christ had taught them"). Not blocking, because I can still say what they held. It is a note for the reviser.
- **Why the connection matters:** filled. "At Carthage, Tertullian took up the same task in Latin… and named Justin first." I can say that later writers built on them. The Marcion clause does not help me here (see 4).

## 2. The opening

After three sentences I know who (Athenagoras, one of a circle of Christians writing in Greek), where (Athens, then "from Athens and Rome to Sardis and Antioch"), when ("in the second century", under Marcus Aurelius and Commodus) and why (Christians "were hunted for their name"). Nothing is missing. One small point: I do not know at first whether "plea" means a speech or a written text. Paragraph 2 makes it clear.

## 3. Sentences that fail on one hearing (MINOR)

1. "Atheists of such gods, yes, he granted, but not of the most true God." This is a fragment with no verb of its own. On one hearing I lost who was granting what to whom. (It is also the tell in 6.)
2. "Eusebius, writing in the early 300s, still had the book." I could not tell whether this means the book is lost today. I also do not know who Eusebius is, except that he came much later.
3. "On godlessness, the poets and philosophers, he said, had searched for God and spoken freely of one God, yet a law lay against Christians alone." I lost "a law lay against". I worked out only later that the philosophers said the same thing and were not punished.
4. "…and we gladly accept the honour of such a death." Up to this point the brief has told me only that Christians in Asia were robbed. "Such a death" arrives with no death told before it, so I lose what Melito is accepting.
5. "Athenagoras named the three charges." I had already heard the three charges in paragraph 2, and Athenagoras opened the story. For a moment I thought this was a second, separate writing. It is not the same event told twice, but it sounds like it. Suggested change: "In the same plea, Athenagoras took the three charges one by one."

## 4. Bare statements (BLOCKING)

1. **"He also named Marcion, still alive and teaching another god above the Creator."** I cannot tell whether Justin named Marcion as a friend or as an enemy, or why it mattered to him. The quarrel is named but not explained. **Change:** give what Justin said against him and why, as the outline's source gives it, for example "He also warned the emperors against Marcion, still alive and teaching another god above the Creator, who had [what the source says]". If the outline gives no reason, cut the sentence.
2. **"…been told that Christian books were new."** This charge is named without saying why being new counted against a book. Theophilus's answer ("older than the Greeks' own") only works if I know that. **Change:** add, from the outline's source, why newness was held against a teaching. If the source does not say, keep the charge and the answer exactly as the source gives them, and cut "to show".
3. **"Writing against teachers whose ideas he rejected, he said he drew on earlier writers…"** Here "teachers whose ideas he rejected" stands alone, which is the "his ideas" fault. Who were these teachers, and what did they teach? **Change:** name the teaching in plain words, as the outline gives it, or cut the clause and keep "he said he drew on earlier writers… and named Justin first" with its subject made plain.

## 5. Citations read aloud (MINOR)

1. "His petition survives only because Eusebius copied it." This is a how-we-know sentence. It also breaks the line between "something new was happening" and what that new thing was. **Narrator's voice:** cut it, and let "The godly in Asia, he wrote…" follow straight on.
2. "Eusebius, writing in the early 300s, still had the book." The first naming ("because, Eusebius says") is called for, because the witness wrote long after. This second sentence only reports a book's survival. **Narrator's voice:** cut it, or fold it into the first: "because, as the historian Eusebius said in the early 300s, certain wicked men were troubling the Christians."

## 6. Tells (BLOCKING)

1. **"Atheists of such gods, yes, he granted, but not of the most true God."** This is the "not X but Y" turn that Part 1 bans. It may follow Justin's own contrast, but here it is told in the narrator's voice. **Change:** "He granted that they did not believe in such gods. The God they worshipped, he said, was the most true God." Or quote Justin word for word, if the outline gives this line as a quote.

There is no dash for drama, no stock word, and no moral close. The closing quote from Diognetus is the subject's own words.

## 7. Words, runs, tone (MINOR)

- **Unexplained words:**
  - "bishop" (Melito, Theophilus): I have no church background and do not know what a bishop did.
  - "pagan" / "pagans": I am not told this means people who worshipped the old gods.
  - "root and branch": an idiom, though the context saves it.
  - "Carthage": no region is given, while Sardis and Antioch are placed.
- **Many names:** I count about fifteen, among them four emperors (Hadrian, Antoninus Pius, Marcus Aurelius, Commodus) with no years. Commodus is heard once and never acts. The emperors were where I struggled most to keep track.
- **Pattern:** "At Rome…", "At Antioch…", "At Carthage…". The last two paragraphs before the close are each one person doing one act. They begin to sound like a list of names, which is the roll-call risk. This is not a run of short sentences, but the reviser should break it.
- **Talking down:** none.

## 8. Attention

- **Pull:** yes. Emperors greeted "as philosophers", and Christians "hunted for their name", made me want to know why.
- **First drift:** the Quadratus paragraph (s8–9). After a strong second paragraph I get a new name, the reason given second-hand ("Eusebius says"), and then a sentence about who still owned a book. Nothing happens in it. Attention came back with Justin and stayed through Melito and the gladiator passage, which is the strongest part. It slipped again at Theophilus and Tertullian (the roll-call pattern above).
- **Would I listen to the end:** yes.
