# Outline: The Second-Century Greek Apologists (`greek-apologists-second-century`)

Kind: tradition. Every line points to `Source_Sheet.md` and, through it, to `cic/texts/` (short names as in the sheet). Census spoken name: "The Second-Century Greek Apologists"; short form "the apologists".

## The heart

- **The essence:** In the second century, from Athens and Rome to Sardis and Antioch, Christians who wrote in Greek put written defences of their faith into the hands of emperors, and of pagans who asked them about it. Accused of being godless, of eating human flesh and of incest, they answered that they worshipped the one God who made all things and prayed for the emperors, and they asked to be judged by how they lived. (Documented: athen-grc 72–74, 179–182, 196–198, 1601–1602; 1apol-grc 186–188, 506–516; anf02 8241–8246, 10239–10244; npnf201 20994–20995, 25471–25476.)
- **Who they were:** "the apologists", writers of an *apologia*, a speech for the defence (athen-grc 652; npnf201 20994–20995). A number of writers, not one church: a philosopher-teacher at Rome, two bishops, an Athenian, an unknown letter-writer. Documented.
- **When and where:** from the reign of Hadrian to the years after Marcus Aurelius; Athens, Rome, Sardis in the province of Asia, Antioch (npnf201 20992–20995, 25471–25476; anf02 11076). Documented.
- **What they faced:** a name that was itself the charge; rumours of cannibal feasts and incest; the charge of "atheism", meaning refusal of the cities' gods and sacrifices; informers enriched by new decrees; philosophers' scorn; death (athen-grc 104–111; anf02 11973–11979; npnf201 25818–25829; anf01 16849–16859; npnf201 23749–23764). Documented.
- **What they believed:** one God, maker of all, with his Son or Word and his Spirit or Wisdom; that the poets and philosophers had groped toward this God; that every life is judged (1apol-grc 186–192, 517–519; athen-grc 404–411; anf02 11728–11735, 8990–8996). Documented, in their own words.
- **How they practiced:** they gave up the gladiator shows, would not expose infants, paid taxes and prayed for the emperors, took in strangers as brothers and gave to those who had nothing (athen-grc 1543–1550, 1555, 1601–1602; 1apol-grc 506–516; anf09 18960–18964). Documented.
- **What held them together:** the claim that Christians could be judged by their lives and their reasons, and would be found loyal to the emperor while worshipping God alone (athen-grc 196–198; 1apol-grc 506–516). Documented.
- **What came of them:** their books were kept and quoted by Eusebius; the Latin defenders, Tertullian first, took up the same task and named them; the argument from Moses's age passed to Alexandria (Source Sheet §4). Documented.

## Setting

- Century: the second century. Spoken: "under Hadrian", "at Rome, under Antoninus Pius", "under Marcus Aurelius". No year numbers for Quadratus or Justin (Source Sheet §6).
- Places: Athens; Rome; Sardis, "in the Roman province of Asia, in today's western Turkey"; Antioch, "in Syria". (npnf201 20992–20995, 25471–25476; 1apol-grc 68–75; npnf201 25228–25231.)
- Tradition: Greek-speaking Christians writing outward, to rulers and pagan readers.

## Words to explain

- **apology / defence**: "a written defence, the kind of speech made in court". Prefer "defence"; use "apology" once, glossed.
- **atheists**: "people who refused the gods of the cities and would not sacrifice to them" (anf02 11973–11979).
- **"Thyestean feasts" and "Oedipean unions"**: say in plain words, "eating human flesh at their feasts, and incest" (athen-grc 179–180, δεῖπνα, "dinners"; anf02 10239–10244 gives the charge with no setting). Do not use the Greek names.
- **informers**: "people who laid charges against others and took their goods" (npnf201 25823–25826).
- **decree**: "a new written order".
- **gladiator shows**: "fights to the death between armed men, or with wild beasts, staged for crowds" (athen-grc 1543–1546).
- **province of Asia**: "the Roman province of Asia, in today's western Turkey".

## Shape

The tradition kit has no shapes, only its form: a story. The story follows the Christians these writers spoke for, through what happened to them, reign by reign: troubled under Hadrian; called atheists under Antoninus Pius; Justin put to death, Asia plundered under new decrees, and the rumours of their feasts answered under Marcus Aurelius. Each writer's defence enters where those events call for it, as his answer to them; no beat opens on a writer's name and a reign alone (the first-time listener's roll-call finding, `../Grades/listener.md`). The opening moment is Athenagoras's plea, near the end of the line; the story then goes back to Hadrian and comes forward until it reaches Athenagoras again, so his answer to the charges is the turn the earlier beats build to. Theophilus and the writer to Diognetus carry no reign in the held record, so they come last with no order claim (Do not say).

## Openings

1. **(Recommended) Athenagoras's heading.** An Athenian Christian, Athenagoras, addressed his plea to the emperors Marcus Aurelius and his son Commodus ("ruling together": card, Cut first 19), hailing them, above all, as philosophers (Armenia and Sarmatia: card, Cut first 12). Only Christians, he told them, are hunted, for a name, and slaughtered on the word of false accusers (each people's own gods, the Egyptians' cats and crocodiles: card, Cut first 15). (athen-grc 72–74, 88–95, 104–111.) Then the essence.
2. **Theophilus and his friend.** At Antioch a bishop, Theophilus, had argued with a pagan friend who mocked him for bearing the name Christian and asked to be shown his God. They parted, Theophilus says, with much friendliness, each to his own house, and then he wrote three books to win him. (anf02 7757–7762, 7780–7782, 8241–8246.)

## Story beats

Each beat gives the event first, then the defence it called out. The planned wording is given for the writer as a guide to sense and length, not as a draft; the count of 675 rests on it. Every clause points to the sheet line that holds it.

0. **Opening, under Marcus Aurelius and Commodus: Athenagoras's heading** (Openings 1, as approved). "An Athenian Christian, Athenagoras, addressed his plea to the emperors Marcus Aurelius and his son Commodus, and hailed them, above all, as philosophers. Only Christians, he told them, were hunted for their name alone, and slaughtered on the word of false accusers." (athen-grc 72–74, 104–111.) Then the essence, whole, as the heart holds it, with the apology gloss (athen-grc 652; npnf201 20994–20995). Documented.
1. **Under Hadrian: the Christians troubled, and Quadratus answers.** Event first: in the reign of Hadrian, certain wicked men were trying to trouble the Christians; so Quadratus, a Christian, addressed a defence to the emperor; Eusebius, a church historian, still had the book in the early 300s (npnf201 20992–20995, 21048–21052). The bridge back from the opening is "Long before," (Hadrian before Marcus Aurelius is the reigns' own order). "So" carries Eusebius's "because" (21048), and nothing more. Aristides: card (Cut first 7). Documented (Eusebius's reason).
2. **Under Antoninus Pius: the charge of atheism, and Justin answers.** Event first: under the next emperor, Antoninus Pius, Christians were called atheists, because they refused the cities' gods and would not sacrifice to them (1apol-grc 186–188; anf02 11973–11979). Justin, a Christian teacher of philosophy at Rome, wrote to the emperor (1apol-grc 68–75). He granted that they were atheists of such gods, and said they worshipped the most true God (1apol-grc 186–188). Everywhere, he wrote, they took care to pay their taxes, as Christ had taught them; they worshipped God alone, and gladly served the emperors in all else (1apol-grc 506–516). Explain "atheists". Documented.
3. **Under Marcus Aurelius: Justin's death.** One clause-sentence, the event that ends his part: under Marcus Aurelius, Justin was condemned and put to death for refusing to sacrifice to the gods and obey the emperor's command (anf01 29396–29398; npnf201 23749–23764). His trial stays justin-martyr's; Crescens stays on the card (Cut first 3). Documented; under Marcus Aurelius Widely Accepted (Eusebius places it there).
4. **Under Marcus Aurelius, in Asia: new decrees and informers, and Melito answers.** Melito, bishop of Sardis, in the Roman province of Asia, in today's western Turkey, wrote to Marcus Aurelius (name the emperor here: "him" would read as Justin) (npnf201 25471–25476). Christians there, he wrote, were hounded by new decrees, new written orders (npnf201 25820–25821, "the race of the pious ... being driven about"). In the passages Eusebius copied, Melito wrote that informers, people who laid charges against others and took their goods, used the decrees to rob the innocent night and day (npnf201 25818–25826; "And a little further on he says", 25826). Quote 1. But if the decree was not the emperor's, he begged not to be left to the plundering of the crowd (npnf201 25834–25838). No order claim against Justin's death (Do not say). Documented.
5. **The rumours answered: Athenagoras again.** The story is back at the opening's plea, and this is its answer. If the charges were true, Athenagoras said, let the emperors destroy the Christians root and branch; if not, let them look into Christian lives, teaching and loyalty (athen-grc 179–182, 196–198). The poets and philosophers, in three short sentences, so the listener keeps the thread (`../Grades/listener.md`, thread-losing): "Poets and philosophers, he said, wrote freely about God, and almost all spoke of one God. No law stood against them. Yet one stood against Christians, who said the same." (athen-grc 311–318: "almost all" is ὡς ἐπὶ τὸ πλεῖστον, "with impunity" ἐπ᾿ ἀδείας, "against us a law lies" ἐφ᾿ ἡμῖν δὲ κεῖσθαι νόμον; anf02 11728–11735.) As for eating human flesh, no one claimed to have seen it (athen-grc 1534–1546). Then he turned to the emperors' own games, with the gladiator gloss, and Quote 2 (athen-grc 1543–1550). Do not retell the heading. Documented.
6. **At Antioch: a friendship, the rumours believed, and Theophilus answers.** Theophilus and a pagan friend talked about God and parted friends (anf02 8241–8246: "we went with much mutual friendliness each to his own house"). But the friend believed the rumours (anf02 10239–10243: "moved by senseless men … to give credit to the prevalent rumor"), and thought Christian books were new and their teaching nonsense (anf02 10155–10157). Theophilus wrote to him (anf02 10157–10160), counting back the years to show that Moses's books, the first in the Bible, were older than the Greeks' own (anf02 10880–10885). No time word placing him after Athenagoras (Do not say). Bishop: Widely Accepted (npnf201 25228–25231). Documented.
7. **At Carthage: Tertullian.** Tertullian took up the same task in Latin. He wrote against the followers of a teacher, Valentinus. They stacked heaven on heaven for their gods, he scoffed (anf03 43105, "we are quite aware why we call them Valentinians"; 43233–43237, "storeys upon storeys … as a dwelling for each several god of theirs"; 43250, "the god even of the Valentinians"). He named Justin first among those who had refuted them (anf03 43155–43163: "the opinions and their refutations … for instance Justin"). This answers the listener's "Writing against teachers he held false" (`../Grades/listener.md`), names the teachers from the held text, and keeps Tertullian's use of Justin against heresy, not for his defence (Do not say). Tertullian is their enemy: "he scoffed" says so. Documented.
8. **The close: the writer to Diognetus.** One defence, a letter to a man named Diognetus, has no known writer or date. Quote 3 ends the story on the community itself (diog-grc 311–317). Documented (text); date Contested.

## What came of it

1. Eusebius kept their books and quoted the lost ones: Quadratus's passage, Melito's petition (npnf201 21049–21068, 25818–25859). Documented. **Defended:** the one God and Christians' innocence. Told inside beats 1 and 4 ("still had the book"; "In the passages Eusebius copied"); no closing sentence of its own (Leave out, Cut first 9).
2. Tertullian at Carthage took up the same task in Latin. He said he drew on earlier Christian writers, and named Justin first (anf03 43155–43163). His opponents are named, the followers of Valentinus, with Tertullian's own scorn for their gods as the gloss (anf03 43105, 43233–43237; Cut first 8 reversed, see below). Latin Apologists clause. Documented. Narrate it; do not quote Tertullian's phrase for Justin (barred: live on justin-martyr).
3. At Alexandria, Clement praised Tatian, a hearer of Justin's, for his careful account of Moses's age, the argument Theophilus also made (anf02 29724–29727, 10155–10160; Irenaeus, anf01 33664). Documented.
4. Aristides's book was lost, carried unrecognised inside a later Christian romance, Barlaam and Josaphat, and found in Syriac at Sinai in 1889 (anf09 18398–18403, 18416–18421, 18451–18462). Modern event. Documented.
Keepers today: none sourced; left out.

## Connection to carry into the story

What passed on, in the held record: their books, which Eusebius kept and quoted, still holding Quadratus's defence and copying passages of Melito's petition, without which both would be names only (npnf201 21049–21068, 25818–25859); and Justin's writing, which Tertullian, taking up the same task in Latin, says he drew on: "He said he drew on earlier Christian writers, and named Justin first" (anf03 43155–43163; his opponents, the followers of Valentinus, are named with Tertullian's gloss, beat 7). (Clement's use of the proof from Moses's age, crediting Tatian, anf02 29724–29727, goes to the card under Cut first 6.) The writer must make clear that Eusebius preserved the actual texts and that Tertullian used Justin's work, not merely that both came later.
