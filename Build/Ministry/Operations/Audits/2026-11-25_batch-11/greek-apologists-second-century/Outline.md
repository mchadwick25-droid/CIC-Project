# Outline: The Second-Century Greek Apologists (`greek-apologists-second-century`)

Kind: tradition. Every line points to `Source_Sheet.md` and, through it, to `cic/texts/` (short names as in the sheet). Census spoken name: "The Second-Century Greek Apologists"; short form "the apologists".

## The heart

- **The essence:** In the second century, from Athens and Rome to Sardis and Antioch, Christians who wrote in Greek put written defences of their faith into the hands of emperors, and of pagans who asked them about it. Accused of being godless, of eating human flesh and of incest, they answered that they worshipped the one God who made all things and prayed for the emperors, and they asked to be judged by how they lived. (Documented: athen-grc 72–74, 179–182, 196–198, 1601–1602; 1apol-grc 186–188, 506–516; anf02 8241–8246, 10239–10244; npnf201 20994–20995, 25471–25476.)
- **Who they were:** "the apologists", writers of an *apologia*, a speech for the defence (athen-grc 652; npnf201 20994–20995). A number of writers, not one church: a philosopher-teacher at Rome, two bishops, an Athenian, an unknown letter-writer. Documented.
- **When and where:** from the reign of Hadrian to the years after Marcus Aurelius; Athens, Rome, Sardis in the province of Asia, Antioch (npnf201 20992–20995, 25471–25476; anf02 11076). Documented.
- **What they faced:** a name that was itself the charge; rumours of cannibal feasts and incest; the charge of "atheism", meaning refusal of the cities' gods and sacrifices; informers enriched by new decrees; philosophers' scorn; death (athen-grc 104–111; anf02 11973–11979; npnf201 25818–25829; anf01 16849–16859; npnf201 23749–23764). Documented.
- **What they believed:** one God, maker of all, with his Son or Word and his Spirit or Wisdom; that the poets and philosophers had groped toward this God; that every life is judged (1apol-grc 186–192, 517–519; athen-grc 404–411; anf02 11728–11735, 8990–8996). Documented, in their own words.
- **How they practiced:** they gave up the gladiator shows, would not expose infants, paid taxes and prayed for the emperors, took in strangers as brothers and gave to those who had nothing (athen-grc 1543–1550, 1555, 1601–1602; 1apol-grc 506–516; anf09 18960–18964). Documented.
- **What held them together:** the claim that Christians could be judged by their lives and their reasons, and would be found loyal to the emperor while worshipping God alone (athen-grc 196–198; 1apol-grc 506–516). Documented.
- **What came of them:** their books were kept and quoted by Eusebius; the Latin defenders, Tertullian first, took up the same task and named them; the argument from Moses's age passed to Alexandria (Source Sheet §4). Documented.

## Setting

- Century: the second century. Spoken: "in the reign of Hadrian", "at Rome, under Antoninus Pius", "under Marcus Aurelius". No year numbers for Quadratus or Justin (Source Sheet §6).
- Places: Athens; Rome; Sardis, "in the Roman province of Asia, in today's western Turkey"; Antioch, "in Syria". (npnf201 20992–20995, 25471–25476; 1apol-grc 68–75; npnf201 25228–25231.)
- Tradition: Greek-speaking Christians writing outward, to rulers and pagan readers.

## Words to explain

- **apology / defence**: "a written defence, the kind of speech made in court". Prefer "defence"; use "apology" once, glossed.
- **atheists**: "people who refused the gods of the cities and would not sacrifice to them" (anf02 11973–11979).
- **"Thyestean feasts" and "Oedipean unions"**: say in plain words, "eating human flesh at their feasts, and incest" (athen-grc 179–180, δεῖπνα, "dinners"; anf02 10239–10244 gives the charge with no setting). Do not use the Greek names.
- **informers**: "people who laid charges against others and took their goods" (npnf201 25823–25826).
- **decree**: "a new written order".
- **gladiator shows**: "fights to the death between armed men, or with wild beasts, staged for crowds" (athen-grc 1543–1546).
- **province of Asia**: "the Roman province of Asia, in today's western Turkey".

## Openings

1. **(Recommended) Athenagoras's heading.** An Athenian Christian, Athenagoras, addressed his plea to the emperors Marcus Aurelius and his son Commodus ("ruling together": card, Cut first 19), hailing them, above all, as philosophers (Armenia and Sarmatia: card, Cut first 12). Only Christians, he told them, are hunted, for a name, and slaughtered on the word of false accusers (each people's own gods, the Egyptians' cats and crocodiles: card, Cut first 15). (athen-grc 72–74, 88–95, 104–111.) Then the essence.
2. **Theophilus and his friend.** At Antioch a bishop, Theophilus, had argued with a pagan friend who mocked him for bearing the name Christian and asked to be shown his God. They parted, Theophilus says, with much friendliness, each to his own house, and then he wrote three books to win him. (anf02 7757–7762, 7780–7782, 8241–8246.)

## Story beats

1. **Under Hadrian: Quadratus and Aristides.** Quadratus addressed a defence to the emperor because, says Eusebius, who still had the book in the early 300s, certain wicked men were trying to trouble the Christians ("had attempted to trouble", npnf201 21048–21052). Eusebius quotes its one surviving passage: people Jesus healed or raised lived on, some "even to our day" (narrated, not quoted). Aristides, an Athenian, also wrote to the emperor, Hadrian as Eusebius tells it (the Syriac version's longer heading reads "Titus Hadrianus Antoninus", which its editor took for Antoninus Pius: card). He told the emperor that Christians take a stranger under their roof and rejoice over him as a brother, and that those who have give to those who have not. (npnf201 20992–20995, 21048–21068, 21073–21075; anf09 19005–19008, 18960–18964.) Reason for the act: Christians being troubled (Eusebius's reason). Documented; which emperor Contested.
2. **At Rome, under Antoninus Pius: Justin.** Justin, a Christian philosopher teaching at Rome, wrote to the emperor and his sons. Christians were called atheists, he said, because they refused such gods. He granted that they were atheists of such gods, and said they worshipped the most true God (1apol-grc 186–192). Everywhere, he said, they took care to pay their taxes, as Christ had taught them (1apol-grc 506–508; ANF anf01 14580–14581); they worshipped God alone and in other things gladly served the emperors. ("You can kill us, but you cannot harm us": card, Cut first 17.) He also named Marcion, still teaching another god above the Creator (the Greek has "still teaching", not "alive": 1apol-grc 729–731), whose followers, also called Christians, were not persecuted for their teaching (1apol-grc 735–743; anf01 14949–14961). That is Justin's own reason for naming him to the emperor: men the demons put forward were not persecuted but honoured (1apol-grc 706–709), and their followers bore the name and went free. The contrast with Christians hunted for the name is the opening's; do not tell it again. A philosopher named Crescens, Justin writes, was telling crowds that Christians were godless and impious, and Justin expected to be killed (card, Cut first 3); he was put to death under Marcus Aurelius for refusing to sacrifice to the gods and obey the emperor's command ("at Rome", "condemned with others": card, Cut first 11) (Acts of Justin, anf01 29397–29398; the trial itself is justin-martyr's and is not retold). (1apol-grc 68–71, 94–96, 186–188, 506–516; anf01 14943–14945, 16849–16859; npnf201 23746–23747, 23749–23764.) Explain "atheists". Documented; his death under Marcus Aurelius Widely Accepted (Eusebius places it there).
3. **In Asia, under Marcus Aurelius: Melito of Sardis.** Melito, bishop of Sardis, wrote that in Asia the godly were driven about by new decrees, and informers used them to rob the innocent night and day. Quote 1. But if the decree was not the emperor's, he begged not to be left to the plundering of the crowd, and reminded him that his father had written to the cities forbidding new measures against Christians. (npnf201 25818–25829, 25834–25838, 25890–25897.) Explain informers, decrees. The whole beat rests on Eusebius's quotation of Melito: say so once, as "only the passages Eusebius copied from his petition survive" (he copies extracts: "a little further on he says", npnf201 25818–25838). Documented.
4. **Athenagoras the Athenian, to Marcus Aurelius and Commodus: Athenagoras answers.** He named the three charges: if they were true, he said, destroy us root and branch, with our wives and children; if not, look into our lives, our teaching and our loyalty. As for godlessness, the poets and philosophers had searched for God and guessed at him, and spoke and wrote freely of one God, yet a law lay against Christians alone, who could show their reasons (athen-grc 230, 311–324). As for eating human flesh, no one claimed to have seen it (their slaves: card, Cut first 14). Then he turned to the emperors' own games. Quote 2. (His closing prayer that the throne pass from father to son: card, Cut first 13.) (athen-grc 179–182, 196–198, 311–318, 1534–1550, 1601–1602; anf02 11553–11558, 11728–11735.) Do not retell the opening heading. Documented.
5. **At Antioch: Theophilus to Autolycus.** His friend had heard the rumours, and thought Christian books were new and their teaching nonsense: his own view, in one breath, not something he was told (anf02 10155–10157, "still of opinion that the word of truth is an idle tale, and suppose that our writings are recent"; Otto Latin 20360–20362, "nugas esse arbitraris doctrinam veritatis, recentes admodum esse scripturas nostras existimans") (the rumours listed, which repeat the essence: Leave out, Cut first 10). Theophilus answered that Christians may not even watch gladiators, and counted back the years to show Moses's books older than the Greeks'. (anf02 10155–10160, 10239–10246, 10610–10614, 10880–10885; npnf201 25228–25231.) Documented.
6. **What came of them** (see below), told through what later writers did.

## What came of it

1. Eusebius kept their books and quoted the lost ones: Quadratus's passage, Melito's petition (npnf201 21049–21068, 25818–25859). Documented. **Defended:** the one God and Christians' innocence. Told inside beats 1 and 3 ("still had the book"; "only the passages Eusebius copied from his petition survive"); no closing sentence of its own (Leave out, Cut first 9).
2. Tertullian at Carthage took up the same task in Latin. He said he drew on earlier Christian writers, and named Justin first (anf03 43155–43163). His opponents, the Valentinians, are neither named nor described (no gloss for them is held in this sheet; Leave out, Cut first 8). Latin Apologists clause. Documented. Narrate it; do not quote Tertullian's phrase for Justin (barred: live on justin-martyr).
3. At Alexandria, Clement praised Tatian, a hearer of Justin's, for his careful account of Moses's age, the argument Theophilus also made (anf02 29724–29727, 10155–10160; Irenaeus, anf01 33664). Documented.
4. Aristides's book was lost, carried unrecognised inside a later Christian romance, Barlaam and Josaphat, and found in Syriac at Sinai in 1889 (anf09 18398–18403, 18416–18421, 18451–18462). Modern event. Documented.
Keepers today: none sourced; left out.

## Quotes (three)

   Source: diog-grc 311–317 (Lake 1917, clean Greek, hand-keyed); English cross-check anf01 2735–2738. Rendered from the Greek. Job: the close, the community in its own words.

## Who tells it

- Quadratus and Melito: old, own words, but only as quoted by Eusebius, writing in the early 300s, 150 to 190 years later. The story says so once, inside the Quadratus or Melito sentence.
- Eusebius (old, later): credited beside Quadratus's reason, the books he still had, and Melito's petition.
- Jerome (old, later, dependent on Eusebius): card only.
- Acts of Justin (old; a court record later shaped for reading in church): credited only for the reason Justin was condemned, refusing to sacrifice to the gods and to yield to the command of the emperor (anf01 29396–29398).
