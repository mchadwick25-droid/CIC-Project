# Listen (shape): first-time listener

One hearing of `greek-apologists-second-century.brief.txt` (675 words), as a first-time visitor with no church background and English as a second language. Read: the Reviewer prompt, Kit.md Part 1 and the bar, `kinds/tradition.md`, and the brief. Not read: the outline, source sheet, any other read, any `.v*` or approved file.

## Story or summary

**Heard as a summary with two good scenes in it, more than as a story.** The opening is a real moment (Athenagoras writing to two emperors), and the middle has life: Melito's protest about the informers and Athenagoras on the gladiator shows. But the shape is a tour of writers, one per paragraph, each doing nearly the same act, writing a defense to someone: Quadratus, Justin, Melito, Athenagoras, Theophilus, Tertullian, the Diognetus writer. About fourteen names in all, with the emperors, Eusebius and Valentinus. The story's question (will the emperors listen, and what happens to Christians if they do not?) is raised in the opening, answered once in a single line about Justin's death, and then dropped. From Theophilus on, nothing is at stake. No ordinary believer acts; only the writers do (the kind kit's trap: "only the leaders and none of the ordinary believers"). What I carried away was "many Christians wrote defenses", not something that happened to a community.

## 1. The test (tradition)

- **Who these people were:** filled. "Christians who wrote in Greek put written defenses of their faith into the hands of emperors, and of pagans."
- **What happened to them:** filled. Accused of being godless, cannibalism and incest; "hunted for their name alone"; informers robbing them under new decrees; Justin put to death.
- **What their faith was about:** filled. "The one God who made all things"; praying for the emperors; paying taxes; giving up the gladiator shows.
- **Why the connection matters:** **cannot fill. BLOCKING.** The only link outward is "At Carthage, Tertullian took up the same task in Latin" and his naming Justin against the Valentinians. I could not say why these writers matter to the rest of the church: what they handed on, or who carried it. The Tertullian paragraph turns to a different quarrel (against a teacher's followers) instead of showing what the apologists left.

## 2. The opening

After "...hailed them, above all, as philosophers. Only Christians, he told them, were hunted for their name alone, and slaughtered on the word of false accusers. In the second century, from Athens and Rome to Sardis and Antioch, ...": who, when and why are there. Missing: where Athenagoras is writing from (only "an Athenian"), and why he calls the emperors philosophers, which hangs unexplained. Not lost.

## 3. Sentences that fail on one hearing (MINOR)

- "Long before, in the reign of Hadrian, certain wicked men were trying to trouble the Christians." Lost: long before what? Hadrian means nothing to me as a date, and "wicked men" says nothing about who or how. I hear a jump back in time right after the essence.
- "He granted that they were atheists of such gods, and said they worshipped the most true God." "Atheists of such gods" is odd English on one hearing; I had to guess he meant "yes, we reject those gods".
- "No law stood against them. Yet one stood against Christians, who said the same." "One" just after "one God" in the sentence before: I heard "one God" again, not "one law". Meaning lost for a moment. Change: "Yet a law stood against Christians, who said the same."
- "...and we gladly accept the honour of such a death." Which death? The paragraph is about informers robbing people, not killing them; I could not tell what death Melito meant.
- Also (not counted, noted): Athenagoras comes back in para 7 after five paragraphs away. I recognized him, but for a moment wondered if this was the same plea told again.

## 4. Bare statements (Part 1, Explain it): BLOCKING

- "So Quadratus, a Christian, addressed a defense to the emperor..." The defense is named but not what it said. I learn nothing about it except that a book existed. Explain what it argued, as the old source gives it, or cut Quadratus.
- "He wrote against the followers of a teacher, Valentinus. They stacked heaven on heaven for their gods, he scoffed..." The quarrel is named; what Valentinus taught, and why it mattered to the people in it, is not said beyond a scoff. "He named Justin first among the earlier writers who had refuted them": Justin's refuting them was never told. Explain it, or leave the paragraph to what the Latin heirs took up.

## 5. Citations read aloud (MINOR)

- "...and Eusebius, a church historian, still had the book in the early 300s." A how-we-know clause about a source, not part of the story. In the narrator's voice: "So Quadratus, a Christian, addressed a defense to the emperor." (with its content, per 4).

## 6. Tells Part 1 names: BLOCKING

- "Only the passages Eusebius copied from his petition survive." A sentence that only says what is missing, and a stock how-we-know sentence. Cut it; if one-witness matters, fold it inside a sentence about Melito.
- Noted, not counted: "One defense, a letter to a man named Diognetus, has no known writer or date." Leans the same way, but it introduces the letter and the quote follows, so it does work.

## 7. Words, runs, tone

- Unexplained words: "the godly" (old-fashioned; I would say "Christians"); "driven about" (I guessed "persecuted"); "Carthage" (no clause says where it is; every other city gets one).
- Run of short one-act sentences: para 7, "No law stood against them. Yet one stood against Christians, who said the same. As for eating human flesh, no one claimed to have seen it." Three short turns in a row; they feel like a list of points.
- Talking down: none.
- Good: "apology", "informers", "decrees" and the gladiator shows are all explained inside the sentence.

## 8. Pull and drift

- **Opening pulls in:** yes. "Hunted for their name alone, and slaughtered on the word of false accusers" is strong.
- **Attention first drifts:** para 3, "Long before, in the reign of Hadrian, certain wicked men were trying to trouble the Christians." Why: a jump backward in time, a new name who does nothing but write a book I hear nothing of, then a second name (Eusebius) only to say the book survived. It drifts again at Tertullian, where the defenders-and-emperors thread gives way to a quarrel about heavens.
- **Would I listen to the end:** yes. Melito and the gladiator passage bring me back, and the Diognetus ending ("Every foreign land is a homeland to them, and every homeland is foreign") is a good last line.
