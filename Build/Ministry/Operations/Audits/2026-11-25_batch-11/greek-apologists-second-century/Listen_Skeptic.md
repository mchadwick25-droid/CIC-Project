## 1. The test (tradition)

- **Who these people were:** filled. Greek-writing Christians of the second century who wrote defenses to emperors and pagans, called the apologists.
- **What happened to them:** filled. They were accused, robbed by informers under new decrees (Melito), and Justin was put to death.
- **What their faith was about:** filled. One God who made all things, refusal to sacrifice, prayer for and service to the emperors, no killing even as spectators, books older than the Greeks', citizens who live as strangers.
- **Why the connection matters:** partly unfilled. **BLOCKING.** I can say that Tertullian carried the work into Latin and named Justin first. I cannot say why Justin naming Marcion matters, or what the "teachers whose ideas he rejected" have to do with the apologists. Both links are dropped in without a reason (see 4).

## 2. The opening

After sentence 3 I know who (Athenagoras, one of a circle of Greek-writing Christians), where (Athens, and the empire from Rome to Antioch) and why (Christians hunted for their name). "When" waits for "second century" in sentence 3. The listener is not lost. Nothing blocking.

## 3. Sentences that fail on one hearing

1. **The plea told twice. BLOCKING** (the bar: an event told twice is a failure to understand on one hearing). The opening gives Athenagoras's plea to Marcus Aurelius and Commodus. Five paragraphs later, "Athenagoras named the three charges" returns to the same plea, and the three charges were already listed in paragraph 2. On one hearing I could not tell whether this was a second document or the first one again. Change: tell the plea once. Either open on another moment and give Athenagoras his place in order, or keep the opening and fold its two sentences into the later paragraph, so that the opening is only the image and the content is told once.
2. "Athenagoras named the three charges." Then only two are answered: godlessness and eating human flesh. Incest is never answered. Meaning lost: I wait for a third answer that does not come. MINOR. Change: "named the charges" and answer the two told, or give his answer to the third.
3. "Informers, people who laid charges against others and took their goods, used them to rob the innocent night and day." Meaning lost: "them" lands after "goods", so I heard the informers using the goods. MINOR. Change: "Informers, people who laid charges against others to take their goods, used these decrees to rob the innocent night and day."
4. "...and we gladly accept the honour of such a death." Meaning lost: no death has been mentioned in Melito's paragraph, only robbery. MINOR. Change: say before the quote that the decrees ended in death as well as robbery, if the outline holds it; otherwise cut the quote's last clause only if the outline's quote allows it.
5. "On godlessness, the poets and philosophers, he said, had searched for God and spoken freely of one God, yet a law lay against Christians alone." Meaning lost: "a law lay against" is old phrasing, and I do not know which law or what it did. MINOR. Change: "yet the law punished only the Christians for saying the same."

**Lines that sound machine-made, and why:**
1. **The place-by-place roll.** "In the reign of Hadrian, Quadratus..." / "At Rome, under Antoninus Pius, Justin..." / "Melito, bishop of Sardis..." / "At Antioch, in Syria, the bishop Theophilus..." / "At Carthage, Tertullian..." Each paragraph opens with the same place-and-reign frame and a new name. It reads like a template filled five times, and it is the roll call the slate rules out. Quadratus and Theophilus each get one act and leave. Fourteen names are heard (Part 1 asks past six whether each is needed); Commodus, Hadrian, Antoninus Pius and Diognetus only mark time or address.
2. **Stacked dictionary glosses.** "apology, a defense like a speech in court"; "new decrees, new written orders"; "Informers, people who laid charges..."; "gladiator shows, fights to the death between armed men, or with wild beasts, staged for crowds"; "the books of Moses, the first in the Bible". Each gloss is right, but the same appositive pattern five times sounds like inserted definitions, not a narrator. Part 1 asks that a word be worked into what happens.
3. "Melito ... wrote to Marcus Aurelius that something new was happening." A placeholder phrase. Say what was new in the same breath.
