An Athenian Christian, Athenagoras, addressed his plea to the emperors Marcus Aurelius and his son Commodus, and hailed them, above all, as philosophers. Only Christians, he told them, were hunted for the name Christian alone, and slaughtered on the word of false accusers.

He was one of a number of Christians who wrote in Greek in the second century, from Athens and Rome to Sardis and Antioch. They put written defenses of their faith into the hands of emperors, and of pagans who asked about it. They are known as the apologists, from apology, a defense like a speech in court. Accused of being godless, of eating human flesh at their feasts and of incest, they answered that they worshipped the one God who made all things and prayed for the emperors. They asked to be judged by how they lived.

In the reign of Hadrian, Quadratus addressed a defense to the emperor. Eusebius, a church historian who still had the book in the early 300s, says Quadratus wrote because certain wicked men were trying to trouble the Christians.

Under Antoninus Pius, Justin, a Christian teacher of philosophy at Rome, wrote to the emperor and his sons. Christians were called atheists, he said, because they refused the cities' gods and would not sacrifice to them. He granted that they were atheists of such gods, and said they worshipped the most true God. Everywhere, he wrote, they took care to pay their taxes, as Christ had taught them, and though they worshipped God alone, they gladly served the emperors in all else. Under Marcus Aurelius, Justin was condemned and put to death for refusing to sacrifice to the gods and obey the emperor's command.

Melito, bishop of Sardis, in the Roman province of Asia, in today's western Turkey, wrote to Marcus Aurelius. In the passages of his petition that Eusebius copied, he wrote that the godly in Asia were driven about by new decrees, new written orders. Informers, people who laid charges against others and took their goods, used the decrees to rob the innocent night and day. "If this is done at your command, well and good. For a just ruler will never take unjust measures, and we gladly accept the honour of such a death." But if the decree was not the emperor's, he begged not to be left to the plundering of the crowd.

If the charges were true, Athenagoras said, let the emperors destroy the Christians root and branch, with their wives and children. If not, let them look into Christian lives, teaching and loyalty. On godlessness, the poets and philosophers, he said, had searched for God and spoken freely of one God, yet a law stood against Christians alone. On eating human flesh, no one claimed to have seen it. Then he turned to the emperors' own games, gladiator shows, fights to the death between armed men, or with wild beasts, staged for crowds. "But we hold that to watch a man being killed is close to killing him, so we have given up such shows. We do not even watch, so that we bring no guilt or stain on ourselves. How then could we commit murder?"

Theophilus, the bishop of Antioch in Syria, wrote to a pagan friend who had heard the rumors, and thought Christian books were new and their teaching nonsense. He counted back the years to show that the books of Moses, the first in the Bible, were older than the Greeks' own.

At Carthage, Tertullian took up the same task in Latin. Writing against teachers he held false, he said he followed earlier Christian writers, and named Justin first.

One defense, a letter to a man named Diognetus, has come down with no writer's name and no date. It says of the Christians: "They live in their own homelands, but as resident foreigners. They share in everything as citizens, and endure everything as strangers. Every foreign land is a homeland to them, and every homeland is foreign."
