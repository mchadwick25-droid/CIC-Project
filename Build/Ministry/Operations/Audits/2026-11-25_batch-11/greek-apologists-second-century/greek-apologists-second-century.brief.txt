An Athenian Christian, Athenagoras, addressed his plea to two emperors ruling together, Marcus Aurelius and his son Commodus, and he hailed them, above all, as philosophers. Only Christians, he told them, were hunted, for a name, and slaughtered on the word of false accusers.

Athenagoras was one of a circle of Christians who wrote in Greek in the second century, from Athens and Rome to Sardis and Antioch. They are called the apologists, after the apology, a written defense of the kind made in court. A teacher at Rome, two bishops, Athenagoras himself and a letter writer whose name is lost put these defenses into the hands of emperors, and of pagans who asked about their faith. They were accused of being godless, of eating human flesh at secret meals, and of incest. They answered that they worshipped the one God who made all things and prayed for the emperors, and they asked to be judged by how they lived.

In the reign of Hadrian, a Christian named Quadratus gave the emperor a defense because, Eusebius says, certain wicked men were troubling the Christians. Eusebius, who wrote the church's history in the early 300s, long afterward, still had the book.

At Rome, under Antoninus Pius, Justin, a Christian philosopher who taught there, wrote to the emperor and his sons. Christians were called atheists, he said, because they refused the gods of the cities and would not sacrifice to them. They were atheists of such gods, he granted, but not of the most true God. Everywhere, he wrote, they took care to pay their taxes, as Christ had taught them, and while they worshipped God alone, in other things they gladly served the emperors. He also named Marcion, a teacher still alive in his day, who set another god above the Creator. Under Marcus Aurelius, Justin was condemned for refusing to sacrifice to the gods, and put to death.

Under Marcus Aurelius too, Melito, bishop of Sardis, in the Roman province of Asia, in today's western Turkey, wrote that something new was happening. His petition survives only because Eusebius copied it into his history. In Asia, Melito wrote, the godly were being driven about by new decrees, new written orders from above. Informers, people who laid charges against others and took their goods, were using them to rob the innocent night and day. He told the emperor: "If this is done at your command, well and good. For a just ruler will never take unjust measures, and we gladly accept the honour of such a death." But if the decree was not the emperor's, he begged not to be left to the plundering of the crowd.

Athenagoras faced the three charges head on. If they were true, he said, the emperors should destroy the Christians root and branch, with their wives and children. If not, they should look into how Christians lived, what they taught and how loyal they were. As for godlessness, the poets and philosophers had searched for God and guessed at him, and they spoke and wrote freely of one God, yet a law lay against Christians alone, who could show their reasons. As for eating human flesh, no one claimed to have seen it. Then he turned to the emperors' own games, the gladiator shows, fights to the death between armed men, or with wild beasts, staged for crowds. "But we hold that to watch a man being killed is close to killing him, so we have given up such shows. We do not even watch, so that we bring no guilt or stain on ourselves. How then could we commit murder?"

At Antioch, in Syria, a bishop named Theophilus also wrote, in three books to a pagan friend. The friend had mocked him for bearing the name Christian, had heard the rumors, and had heard that Christian books were new. Theophilus counted back the years to show that the books of Moses, the first books of the Bible, were older than the writings of the Greeks.

At Carthage, Tertullian took up the same task in Latin. Writing against teachers whose ideas he rejected, he said he was not inventing his own arguments. Earlier writers had already answered those teachers, he said, and he named Justin first among them.

One of these defenses has come down with no writer's name and no date. It is a letter to a man named Diognetus, and it says of the Christians: "They live in their own homelands, but as resident foreigners. They share in everything as citizens, and endure everything as strangers. Every foreign land is a homeland to them, and every homeland is foreign."
