An Athenian Christian, Athenagoras, addressed his plea to the emperors Marcus Aurelius and his son Commodus, and hailed them, above all, as philosophers. Only Christians, he told them, were hunted for their name alone, and slaughtered on the word of false accusers.

In the second century, from Athens and Rome to Sardis and Antioch, Christians who wrote in Greek put written defenses of their faith into the hands of emperors, and of pagans who asked them about it. Accused of being godless, of eating human flesh and of incest, they answered that they worshipped the one God who made all things and prayed for the emperors, and they asked to be judged by how they lived. Such a defense was called an apology, the kind of speech made in court.

Long before, in the reign of Hadrian, certain wicked men were trying to trouble the Christians. So Quadratus, a Christian, addressed a defense to the emperor. Christ's works were real, he wrote: some Christ healed lived to his day. Eusebius, a church historian, still had the book in the early 300s.

Under the next emperor, Antoninus Pius, Christians were called atheists, because they refused the gods of the cities and would not sacrifice to them. Justin, a Christian teacher of philosophy at Rome, wrote to the emperor. He granted that they were atheists of such gods, and said they worshipped the most true God. Everywhere, he wrote, they took care to pay their taxes, as Christ had taught them.

Under Marcus Aurelius, Justin was condemned and put to death for refusing to sacrifice to the gods and obey the emperor's command.

In the Roman province of Asia, in today's western Turkey, Christians were hounded by new decrees, new written orders, as Melito, bishop of Sardis, wrote to Marcus Aurelius. Eusebius copied passages from his petition. Informers, people who laid charges against others and took their goods, used the decrees, he wrote, to rob the innocent night and day. "If this is done at your command, well and good. For a just ruler will never take unjust measures, and we gladly accept the honour of such a death." But if the decree was not the emperor's, he begged not to be left to the plundering of the crowd.

If the charges were true, Athenagoras said, let the emperors destroy the Christians root and branch. If not, let them look into Christian lives, teaching and loyalty. Poets and philosophers, he said, wrote freely about God, and almost all spoke of one God. No law stood against them. Yet a law stood against Christians, who said the same. As for eating human flesh, no one claimed to have seen it. Then he turned to the emperors' own games, the gladiator shows, fights to the death between armed men, or with wild beasts, staged for crowds. "But we hold that to watch a man being killed is close to killing him, so we have given up such shows. We do not even watch, so that we bring no guilt or stain on ourselves. How then could we commit murder?"

At Antioch, in Syria, a bishop, Theophilus, talked about God with a pagan friend, and they parted friends. But the friend believed the rumors, and thought Christian books were new and their teaching nonsense. Theophilus wrote to him, counting back the years to show that the books of Moses, the first in the Bible, were older than the Greeks' own, and so their teaching true.

At Carthage, Tertullian took up the same task in Latin. He also wrote against the followers of a teacher, Valentinus. They stacked heaven on heaven for their many gods, he scoffed, and he drew on earlier writers against them, Justin first.

One defense, a letter to a man named Diognetus, has no known writer or date. Of the Christians it says: "They live in their own homelands, but as resident foreigners. They share in everything as citizens, and endure everything as strangers. Every foreign land is a homeland to them, and every homeland is foreign."
