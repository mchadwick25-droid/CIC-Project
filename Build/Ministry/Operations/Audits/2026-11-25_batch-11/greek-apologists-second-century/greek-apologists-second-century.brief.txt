Athenagoras, an Athenian Christian, addressed his plea to Marcus Aurelius and his son Commodus, who ruled together, and hailed them as conquerors of Armenia and Sarmatia and, above all, as philosophers. In your empire, he told them, each people keeps its own gods, and the Egyptians worship even cats and crocodiles. Only Christians are hunted, for their name, and slaughtered on the word of false accusers.

He was one of the apologists, Greek-speaking Christians of the second century who put written defenses of their faith into the hands of emperors, and of pagans who asked about it. An apology was a defense of this kind, like a speech made in court. Accused of being godless, of eating human flesh at secret meals and of incest, they answered that they worshipped the one God who made all things and prayed for the emperors, and they asked to be judged by how they lived.

In the reign of Hadrian, a Christian named Quadratus gave the emperor a defense because wicked men were troubling the Christians, says Eusebius, a church historian who still had the book in the early 300s.

At Rome, under Antoninus Pius, Justin, a Christian philosopher who taught in the city, wrote to the emperor and his sons. Christians were called atheists, he said, because they refused the gods of the cities and would not sacrifice to them. They were atheists toward such gods, he granted, though never toward the most true God. They paid their taxes before anyone else and worshipped God alone, and in other things they gladly served the emperors. The emperors could kill them, he said, but could not harm them. He also named Marcion, still alive and teaching that another god stood above the Creator. Under Marcus Aurelius, Justin was condemned at Rome with others for refusing to sacrifice to the gods, and put to death.

In the same reign Melito, bishop of Sardis, in the Roman province of Asia, in today's western Turkey, wrote that something new was happening there. His petition survives only because Eusebius copied it into his history. Godly people were being driven about by new decrees, new written orders, and informers, who laid charges against others and took their goods, were using them to rob the innocent night and day. "If this is done at your command, well and good. For a just ruler will never take unjust measures, and we gladly accept the honour of such a death." But if the decree was not the emperor's, Melito begged him not to leave them to the plundering of the crowd.

Athenagoras named the three charges. If they were true, he said, destroy us root and branch, with our wives and children; if not, look into our lives, our teaching and our loyalty. Poets and philosophers had searched for God and guessed at him, and spoken freely of one God, he said, yet a law lay against Christians alone, who could give their reasons. No one claimed to have seen them eat human flesh, and not one of their slaves had told such a lie. Then he turned to the emperors' own gladiator shows, fights to the death staged for crowds, between armed men or with wild beasts. "But we hold that to watch a man being killed is close to killing him, so we have given up such shows. We do not even watch, so that we bring no guilt or stain on ourselves. How then could we commit murder?" He ended by praying that their throne would pass from father to son.

At Antioch, in Syria, a bishop named Theophilus wrote three books to a pagan friend who had heard the rumors. Christians, the friend had heard, shared their wives, lay with their own sisters and ate human flesh, and their books were new. Theophilus counted back the years to show that the books of Moses were older than those of the Greeks.

Eusebius kept their books, and from the ones later lost he copied passages into his history, Quadratus's defense and Melito's petition among them. At Carthage, Tertullian, writing in Latin against a rival Christian group, the Valentinians, said that earlier writers had already refuted them, and he named Justin first.

One more defense survives, a letter to a man named Diognetus, though no one knows who wrote it, or when. Of the Christians it says: "They live in their own homelands, but as resident foreigners. They share in everything as citizens, and endure everything as strangers. Every foreign land is a homeland to them, and every homeland is foreign." Those who hate them, it adds, cannot give a reason for their hatred.
