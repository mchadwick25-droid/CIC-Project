## Planned telling (counted, 675 words)

An Athenian Christian, Athenagoras, addressed his plea to the emperors Marcus Aurelius and his son Commodus, and hailed them, above all, as philosophers. Only Christians, he told them, were hunted for their name alone, and slaughtered on the word of false accusers.

He was one of a number of Christians who wrote in Greek in the second century, from Athens and Rome to Sardis and Antioch. They put written defenses of their faith into the hands of emperors, and of pagans who asked about it. They are known as the apologists, from apology, a defense like a speech in court. Accused of being godless, of eating human flesh at their feasts and of incest, they answered that they worshipped the one God who made all things and prayed for the emperors. They asked to be judged by how they lived.

Long before, in the reign of Hadrian, certain wicked men were trying to trouble the Christians. So Quadratus, a Christian, addressed a defense to the emperor. Eusebius, a church historian, still had the book in the early 300s.

Under the next emperor, Antoninus Pius, Christians were called atheists, because they refused the cities' gods and would not sacrifice to them. Justin, a Christian teacher of philosophy at Rome, wrote to the emperor. He granted that they were atheists of such gods, and said they worshipped the most true God. Everywhere, he wrote, they took care to pay their taxes, as Christ had taught them. They worshipped God alone, and gladly served the emperors in all else. Under Marcus Aurelius, Justin was condemned and put to death for refusing to sacrifice to the gods and obey the emperor's command.

Melito, bishop of Sardis, in the Roman province of Asia, in today's western Turkey, wrote to Marcus Aurelius. Only the passages Eusebius copied from his petition survive. The godly there, they say, were driven about by new decrees, new written orders. Informers, people who laid charges against others and took their goods, used the decrees to rob the innocent night and day. "If this is done at your command, well and good. For a just ruler will never take unjust measures, and we gladly accept the honour of such a death." But if the decree was not the emperor's, he begged not to be left to the plundering of the crowd.

If the charges were true, Athenagoras said, let the emperors destroy the Christians root and branch. If not, let them look into Christian lives, teaching and loyalty. Poets and philosophers, he said, wrote freely about God, and almost all spoke of one God. No law stood against them. Yet one stood against Christians, who said the same. As for eating human flesh, no one claimed to have seen it. Then he turned to the emperors' own games, gladiator shows, fights to the death between armed men, or with wild beasts, staged for crowds. "But we hold that to watch a man being killed is close to killing him, so we have given up such shows. We do not even watch, so that we bring no guilt or stain on ourselves. How then could we commit murder?"

At Antioch in Syria, a bishop, Theophilus, and a pagan friend talked about God and parted friends. But the friend believed the rumors, and thought Christian books were new and their teaching nonsense. Theophilus wrote to him, counting back the years to show that Moses's books, the first in the Bible, were older than the Greeks' own.

At Carthage, Tertullian took up the same task in Latin. He wrote against the followers of a teacher, Valentinus, who, he scoffed, stacked heaven on heaven for their gods. He named Justin first among those who had refuted them.

One defense, a letter to a man named Diognetus, has no known writer or date. It says of the Christians: "They live in their own homelands, but as resident foreigners. They share in everything as citizens, and endure everything as strangers. Every foreign land is a homeland to them, and every homeland is foreign."
