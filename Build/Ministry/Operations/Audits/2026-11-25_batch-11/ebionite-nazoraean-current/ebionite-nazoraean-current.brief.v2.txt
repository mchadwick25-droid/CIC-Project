In the middle of the second century, a Jew named Trypho put a question to the Christian teacher Justin. Suppose a man believes that Jesus is the Christ, the Messiah God had promised, and obeys him, but still wants to keep the Law of Moses: will he be saved? In my view, yes, Justin answered, if he does not press the Law on non-Jewish believers. Trypho asked whether some Christians said no. "There are some, Trypho, I answered, who do not dare to share even company or a home with such people. I do not agree with them."

Such people lived in Palestine and Syria from Justin's day to about 400. They were Jews who believed that Jesus was the Christ and went on keeping the Law God gave through Moses: circumcision, the Sabbath, the food laws. Their own books are lost, so we meet them only through church writers who argued against them, and those writers do not agree on what they believed about Jesus.

About 180, Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon in Gaul, counted them among the heresies, the teachings he held false, and called them Ebionites. They were circumcised, he writes, and lived so much as Jews that they revered Jerusalem "as if it were the house of God." They rejected Paul as "an apostate from the Law," one who had abandoned it. On Jesus, Irenaeus writes, they held that he was the son of Joseph.

In his last years Origen, the Alexandrian teacher, explained the name. Ebion, he says, is Hebrew for "poor," and Jews who received Jesus as the Christ were called Ebionites. One of them, Symmachus, put the Hebrew Scriptures into Greek, and Origen set his version beside the others in a great book of columns, where the Greek translations stood side by side. Symmachus's own writings on the Scriptures came to Origen from a woman, Juliana. Origen knew two kinds of Ebionites: some confessed with the church that Jesus was born of a virgin, while others denied it and said he was born like other men.

About 375, Epiphanius, a bishop on Cyprus, wrote of them under two names, Nazoraeans and Ebionites. The Nazoraeans, named, he says, after Nazareth, were Jews in everything. They read the Law and the prophets in Hebrew, and they believed in one God and in his child Jesus Christ. What they held about Christ himself, he says, he cannot tell: whether he was a mere man, or born of Mary by the Holy Spirit. In the synagogues, he says, the Jews cursed them three times a day, praying that God would curse the Nazoraeans, because they were Jews who preached that Jesus is the Christ.

In 392 the Latin scholar Jerome wrote that the Nazoraeans of Beroea, in Syria, had let him copy the gospel they used, written in Hebrew letters. He calls it the Gospel according to the Hebrews, and says he put it into Greek and Latin. In it, after the resurrection, the Lord appears to James, his brother, who had sworn not to eat bread from the hour he drank the Lord's cup until he saw him risen. "He took bread and blessed it and broke it, and gave it to James the Just. He said to him, 'My brother, eat your bread, for the Son of Man has risen from among those who sleep.'" Risen, that is, from the dead.

In 404 Jerome wrote to Augustine that in all the synagogues of the East, to this day, the Jewish teachers, the Pharisees as he called them, still condemned a sect commonly called the Nazoraeans. He set down what they believed about Jesus as the church's own faith, and in the same breath gave his verdict. "They believe in Christ, the Son of God, born of the Virgin Mary. They say he is the one who suffered under Pontius Pilate and rose again. We too believe in him. But while they want to be both Jews and Christians, they are neither Jews nor Christians."
