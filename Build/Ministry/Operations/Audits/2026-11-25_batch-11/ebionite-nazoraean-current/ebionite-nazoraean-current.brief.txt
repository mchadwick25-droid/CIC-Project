In the middle of the second century, at Ephesus, as Eusebius later recorded, a Jew named Trypho questioned the Christian teacher Justin. Suppose a man believes that Jesus is the Christ, the Messiah God had promised, and obeys him, but still wants to keep the Law of Moses: will he be saved? In my view he will, Justin answered, if he does not press the Law on non-Jewish believers. Trypho asked whether some said no. "There are some, Trypho, I answered, who do not dare to share even company or a home with such people. I do not agree with them."

Such people were Jews who believed that Jesus was the Christ and went on keeping that Law: circumcision, the Sabbath, the food laws. Their own books are lost, so we meet them only through other church writers, nearly all of whom argued against them, and those writers do not agree on what they believed about Jesus. Church writers wrote of them from Justin's day to about 400, and the later ones place them in Syria and beyond the Jordan.

By about 180 they were called Ebionites, and Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon in Gaul, counted them among the heresies, the teachings he held false. They were circumcised, he writes, and lived so much as Jews that they revered Jerusalem "as if it were the house of God". They rejected Paul as "an apostate from the Law", one who had abandoned it. They held, he writes, that Jesus was the son of Joseph.

One of them, Symmachus, put the Hebrew Scriptures into Greek, and the writers who called them heretics used his version. In his last years Origen, the teacher from Alexandria, said that their name came from Ebion, Hebrew for "poor". In another book he scorned them as poor in understanding, named from the poverty of their minds. He knew two kinds of them: some confessed with the church that Jesus was born of a virgin, and some denied it and said he was born like other men.

The Nazoraeans named themselves after Nazareth, says Epiphanius, a bishop on Cyprus, who about 375 wrote of them and the Ebionites as two heresies, side by side. They were Jews in everything: they read the Law and the prophets in Hebrew, and believed in one God and in his child Jesus Christ. What they held about Christ himself, he says, he cannot tell, whether a mere man, or born of Mary by the Holy Spirit. In the synagogues, he says, the Jews prayed three times a day that God would curse the Nazoraeans, because they were Jews who preached that Jesus is the Christ.

In Beroea, a city of Syria, the Nazoraeans used a gospel written in Hebrew letters, and they let Jerome copy it, as he wrote in 392. Elsewhere he calls it the Gospel according to the Hebrews, and says he put it into Greek and Latin. Its words survive only in their opponents' quotations. In it, after the resurrection, the Lord appears to James, his brother. James had sworn not to eat bread from the hour he drank the Lord's cup until he saw him risen. Jerome quotes what follows, where the dead are those who sleep: "He took bread and blessed it and broke it, and gave it to James the Just. He said to him, 'My brother, eat your bread, for the Son of Man has risen from among those who sleep.'"

In 404 the Nazoraeans were still a sect among the Jews in all the synagogues of the East, Jerome wrote to Augustine, and the Pharisees, the Jewish teachers, still condemned them. What they believed about Jesus he set down as the church's own faith, and in the same breath gave his verdict. "They believe in Christ, the Son of God, born of the Virgin Mary. They say he is the one who suffered under Pontius Pilate and rose again. We too believe in him. But while they want to be both Jews and Christians, they are neither Jews nor Christians."
