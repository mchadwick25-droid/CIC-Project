## Story or summary?

I heard it as a story, but only just, and its middle sounds like a summary. The frame is a story. Trypho asks at Ephesus whether a Jew who follows Jesus and keeps the Law can be saved. That question carries through to the end, where Jerome says such people "are neither Jews nor Christians." That ending answers the opening question, so the story resolves. There are real scenes too: the curse spoken three times a day, the gospel copied at Beroea, James breaking his fast, and Jerome's letter.

Paragraphs 3 to 5 sound like a summary because they move witness by witness: Irenaeus says, Origen says, Epiphanius says. The community does almost nothing in these paragraphs. Symmachus translating is the only act. The other sentences there just list what each writer said they believed. The slate makes the doubt among the witnesses the story, so this shape is allowed. But in paragraphs 3 and 4 the doubt is reported, not shown happening, and that is where I stopped feeling carried along.

## 1. The test, for a tradition

- **Who they were:** filled. Jews who believed Jesus was the Christ and kept the Law (circumcision, Sabbath, food laws), called Ebionites and Nazoraeans, in Syria and beyond the Jordan.
- **What happened to them:** filled, thinly. Other church writers condemned them, the synagogues cursed them, and in 404 they were still "a sect among the Jews in all the synagogues of the East." I was not told how they ended, and I did not expect to be.
- **What their faith was about:** filled. Jesus as the promised Messiah, together with keeping the Law. Rejecting Paul. Disagreement over whether Jesus was born of a virgin.
- **Why the connection matters:** filled, through what they did. They left a Greek translation of the Scriptures that their opponents read, and a Hebrew gospel that Jerome copied. They defended keeping the Law, and the wider church came to reject that path ("neither Jews nor Christians").

## 2. The opening (first three sentences)

- **Who:** Trypho and Justin, and an imagined believer who keeps the Law.
- **Where:** Ephesus.
- **When:** the middle of the second century.
- **Why there:** a debate between a Jew and a Christian.
- **What is missing:** that the imagined man stands for a real community, which is not named yet. Paragraph 2 resolves this at once ("Such people were Jews who..."). I was never lost about who or where. Not blocking.

## 3. Sentences that fail on one hearing

1. **"There are some, Trypho, I answered, who do not dare to share even company or a home with such people."** Inside the quote, "I answered" switches to Justin's own voice, and "such people" has no clear referent yet. I recovered by "I do not agree with them," so this is MINOR. Possible change: "'There are some, Trypho,' Justin answered, 'who do not dare to share even company or a home with such people. I do not agree with them.'" This only applies if the source's wording allows the attribution to move outside the quote.
2. **"...said that their very name came from the poverty of their Law, for Ebion is Hebrew for 'poor'."** I understood that "Ebion" means poor. I did not understand what a "poor Law" is or why Origen called it that. The meaning is lost, but the thread of the story holds. See item 4.

The craft check also flagged s27 (52 words). That is a tool artifact: it joined the closing quote of paragraph 6 to the first sentence of paragraph 7. The real sentence ("In 404 the Nazoraeans were still a sect...") is about 34 words, and I understood it on one hearing.

## 4. Bare statements: BLOCKING

1. **Origen's "poverty of their Law" (para 4).** This is an opponent's charge, but its content is not said in plain words. I could not tell what was poor about their Law or why it mattered to Origen. The next sentence ("poor in understanding, named from the poverty of their minds") repeats the insult without explaining it.
   **Exact change:** say in plain words what Origen meant by the poverty of their Law, as the old source gives it. If the source does not say, cut "from the poverty of their Law, for" and keep "their very name, Ebion, is Hebrew for 'poor'", leaving the mockery to the next sentence. The outline should then gain a Do not say line: *Do not say "the poverty of their Law" without saying what Origen meant by it.*
