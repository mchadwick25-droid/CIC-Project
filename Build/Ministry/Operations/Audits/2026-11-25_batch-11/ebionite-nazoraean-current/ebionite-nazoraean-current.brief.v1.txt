In the middle of the second century, a Jew named Trypho put a question to the Christian teacher Justin. Suppose a man believes that Jesus is the Christ, the Messiah God had promised, and obeys him, but still wants to keep the Law God gave through Moses. Will he be saved? In my view he will, Justin answered, so long as he does not press the Law on non-Jewish believers. Trypho asked whether some Christians said otherwise. "There are some, Trypho, I answered, who do not dare to share even company or a home with such people. I do not agree with them." On those terms, he would take them in "as kin and brothers".

Such people, Jews who believed in Jesus and went on keeping the Law, with circumcision, the Sabbath and the food laws, lived in Palestine and Syria from Justin's day until about 400. Their own books are lost, and we meet them only through church writers who argued against them, writers who do not agree on what they believed about Jesus.

About 180, Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon in Gaul, counted them among the heresies, the teachings he held false, and called them Ebionites. They were circumcised, he writes, and kept the customs the Law commands, and they lived so much as Jews that they revered Jerusalem "as if it were the house of God". They rejected the apostle Paul as "an apostate from the Law", one who had abandoned it. Their own charge, as Epiphanius later reports it, was that Paul wrote against circumcision, the Sabbath and the Law. On Jesus, Irenaeus writes, they held that he was the son of Joseph.

In his last years Origen, the teacher from Alexandria, wrote that ebion is Hebrew for poor, and that Jews who received Jesus as the Christ were called Ebionites. One of them, Symmachus, put the Hebrew Scriptures into Greek, and Origen set his version beside the other Greek translations in a great book of columns. Symmachus's own writings on the Scriptures came to Origen from a woman named Juliana. Origen knew two kinds of Ebionites: some confessed with the church that Jesus was born of a virgin, while others denied it and said he was born like other men.

About 375, Epiphanius, a bishop on Cyprus, wrote of them under two names, Nazoraeans and Ebionites. The Nazoraeans, named after Nazareth, he says, were Jews in everything, who read the Law and the prophets in Hebrew and believed in one God and in his child Jesus Christ. Jews would not agree with them because they believed in Christ, he says, and Christians would not because they were still bound by the Law. What they held about Christ himself he says he cannot tell: whether he was a mere man, or born of Mary by the Holy Spirit. In the synagogues, he says, the Jews prayed three times a day that God would curse the Nazoraeans, because they were Jews who preached that Jesus is the Christ.

In 392 Jerome, the Latin scholar, wrote that the Nazoraeans of Beroea, in Syria, had let him copy the Hebrew gospel they used. He calls it the Gospel according to the Hebrews, and says he put it into Greek and Latin. In it, after the resurrection, the Lord appears to his brother James, who had sworn not to eat bread from the hour he drank the Lord's cup until he saw him risen from the dead. The Lord called for a table and bread. "He took bread and blessed it and broke it, and gave it to James the Just. He said to him, 'My brother, eat your bread, for the Son of Man has risen from among those who sleep.'"

In 404 Jerome wrote to Augustine, a bishop in Africa. To this day, he said, in all the synagogues of the East there was a sect commonly called Nazoraeans, which the Pharisees, as he called the Jewish teachers, still condemned. In one breath he set down their faith and gave his verdict on them: "They believe in Christ, the Son of God, born of the Virgin Mary. They say he is the one who suffered under Pontius Pilate and rose again. We too believe in him. But while they want to be both Jews and Christians, they are neither Jews nor Christians."
