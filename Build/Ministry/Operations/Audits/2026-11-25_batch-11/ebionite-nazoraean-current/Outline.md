# Outline: The Ebionites and Nazoraeans

Kind: tradition (`kinds/tradition.md`). Census id `ebionite-nazoraean-current`; census name "Ebionite / Nazoraean Current", shortName **Ebionites**, informalName "The Ebionite and Nazoraean Current". Spoken: "the Ebionites and Nazoraeans". Job (slate): Jewish believers in Jesus who went on keeping the Law, met only through the writers who opposed them; the doubt is the story, told as a scene of its own in the old witnesses' words. All references are rows of `Source_Sheet.md` and, through them, lines of `cic/texts/`.

## The heart

- **The essence:** From the middle of the second century to about 400, in Palestine and Syria, there were Jews who believed that Jesus was the Christ and went on keeping the Law of Moses: circumcision, the Sabbath, the food laws. Their own books are lost. We meet them only through church writers who argued against them, and those writers do not agree on what they believed about Jesus. (B1, B2, B8–B10, B12; E1–E10; A2, I4; census `region` "Palestine, Syria". The census `dates` "1st-4th c." reach back to the first century only through Epiphanius's Pella beginning, so the story's span is the witnesses' own. Documented for the practice as every witness reports it; Contested for their belief about Jesus.)
- **Who they were:** Jews who believed in Jesus as the Christ, called Ebionites (from the Hebrew for "poor", Origen) and Nazoraeans (from Nazareth, Epiphanius). (C2, C6; Documented as each witness reports. Whether the two names mean one people or two: Contested, J.)
- **When and where:** from Justin's day, the middle of the second century, to Jerome's letter of 404 and Augustine's "still continues"; Palestine and Syria: Beroea in Syria, Pella beyond the Jordan, Kokaba (A2, A9, A11, A12, I4; Documented as reported; dates Widely Accepted.)
- **What they faced:** some Christians would not share company or a home with them (B1b); the synagogues, Epiphanius says, cursed them three times a day (D3); the church writers named them heretics (D5–D7) and Jerome said they were "neither Jews nor Christians" (E10). (Documented as each reports.)
- **What they believed:** that Jesus is the Christ; that the Law still bound them; that Paul was "an apostate from the Law" (B5, B12). About Jesus's birth and nature the witnesses part (E): this is the scene. Their own words survive only in their gospel as Jerome quotes it (F6). (Documented as reported; Contested on Christ.)
- **How they practiced:** circumcision, the Law's customs, Jerusalem revered "as if it were the house of God" (B2); the Sabbath and also the Lord's day (B10); Scripture read in Hebrew (B11); their own gospel in Hebrew letters (F7); elders and synagogue rulers (B13, Epiphanius only). (Documented as reported.)
- **What held them together:** the Law kept with faith in Jesus as the Christ: "Jews in everything", Epiphanius says, but for believing in Christ (B11–B12). (Documented as reported.)
- **What came of them:** their books passed into their opponents' hands: Symmachus's Greek Scriptures into Origen's columns and Jerome's reading (G1–G4); their gospel copied, translated and quoted by Jerome (F5–F8). By about 400 Augustine says they were "very small" in number, but still there (I4). (Documented.)

## Shape

The kind kit's form: a story in time order, following the witnesses as they meet the community from about 155 to about 404. The doubt is its own scene (Beat 6), set at Jerome's letter of 404, where the witnesses' answers on Jesus are laid side by side. None of those answers is told earlier, so no event is told twice; the scene compares what the witnesses wrote rather than going back to tell events. If Mark prefers, the alternative is to give each witness's answer in its own beat and keep only Jerome's letter as the scene.

## Openings

1. **(Planned.) "Will he be saved?"** In the middle of the second century, a Jew named Trypho put a question to the Christian teacher Justin. Suppose a man believes Jesus is the Christ and obeys him, but still wants to keep the Law of Moses: will he be saved? In my view, yes, Justin answered, if he does not press the Law on non-Jewish believers. And are there some who say no? (Quote 1.) (A1, B1, B1b; Documented.)
2. (Alternative, for the card.) The curse in the synagogues, three times a day (D3). Set aside: it opens on an enemy's report written two centuries after Justin, and the curse belongs in its time (Beat 4).

## Story beats

**Hook** (Opening 1), then **the essence** in the narrator's words, with the one-witness line inside it: their own books are lost, and we meet them only through church writers who argued against them (sheet head note; F, G2). Justin gives them no name; the essence brings them in as "such people", Jews who believed Jesus was the Christ and kept the Law. Justin would take them in "as kin and brothers", if they kept the Law themselves and did not press it on others (B1c; narrator's words).

1. **Irenaeus names them, about 180.** Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon in Gaul, counts them among the heresies, teachings he held false, and calls them Ebionites (D5). They were circumcised, he writes, kept the customs the Law commands, and lived so much as Jews that they revered Jerusalem "as if it were the house of God". They read only Matthew's Gospel. They rejected Paul as "an apostate from the Law", one who had abandoned it. Reason, from Paul's own side: he had written that circumcision would profit a non-Jewish believer nothing (B6). Their charge against Paul in its own content goes to the card (B5b). (B2, B3, B5; Documented as Irenaeus's report.) Irenaeus's words on Jesus are kept for Beat 6.
2. **Origen explains the name; Symmachus and Juliana.** Origen, the great teacher of Alexandria, writing in his last years, says that ebion is Hebrew for "poor", and that Jews who received Jesus as the Christ were called Ebionites; they had not deserted the law of their fathers. They blamed other Christians, he says, for eating food the Law calls unclean. One of them, Symmachus, put the Hebrew Scriptures into Greek, and Origen set his version beside the others in his great book of columns. Origen had Symmachus's own commentaries from a woman, Juliana, who had the books from Symmachus himself. Eusebius, writing later, kept Origen's note. (A4, B8, B9, C2, G1, G2; Documented.) Origen's two kinds are kept for Beat 6.
3. **(Cut first 1.) Eusebius: the Sabbath and Sunday.** Under Constantine, Eusebius of Caesarea writes that they kept the Sabbath and the rest of the Jewish way just as Jews did, and also, like other Christians, kept the Lord's day in memory of the Saviour's resurrection. (A5, B10; Documented as his report; his editor notes he may mean one kind only: say "some of them" if the beat stays.)
4. **Epiphanius: two names, the curse, Pella.** About 375 Epiphanius, a bishop on Cyprus, wrote of them under two names, Nazoraeans and Ebionites. The Nazoraeans, named, he says, after Nazareth, were Jews in everything: they read the Law and the prophets in Hebrew and believed in one God and in his child Jesus Christ. Jews would not agree with them because they believed in Christ; Christians, because they were still bound by the Law. He places them at Beroea in Syria, near Pella beyond the Jordan, and at Kokaba. Their beginning, he says, was at Pella, where all the disciples went when they left Jerusalem before its siege, as Christ had told them. In the synagogues, he says, the Jews cursed them three times a day, morning, noon and evening: "May God curse the Nazoraeans", because they were Jews who preached that Jesus is the Christ. Reason for the curse: Epiphanius's own (D3). Everything in this beat in his voice ("he says"); the descent is his alone (slate). (A6, A9, B11, B12, C6, D3, H2; Documented as Epiphanius's report; the descent Contested.)
5. **Jerome copies their gospel, 392.** Jerome, the Latin scholar, says the Nazoraeans of Beroea in Syria let him copy the Hebrew gospel they used. He calls it the Gospel according to the Hebrews, says that most take it for Matthew's, and says he put it into Greek and Latin. In it, after the resurrection, the Lord appears to James, his brother. James had sworn not to eat bread from the hour he drank the Lord's cup until he saw him risen. The Lord called for a table and bread (Quote 3). This is the one place their own book speaks. (A7, F5–F8; Documented as the gospel's words in Jerome's quotation.)
6. **The doubt, set at Jerome's letter of 404.** What did they believe about Jesus himself? Here the witnesses part. Irenaeus said they held he was the son of Joseph. Origen knew two kinds: some confessed with the church that Jesus was born of a virgin, and some denied it. Eusebius said even those who confessed it would not confess that he was God before he was born. Epiphanius said of the Nazoraeans that he could not say whether they held Christ a mere man or born of Mary by the Holy Spirit. Then in 404, writing to Augustine, Jerome said that to this day, in all the synagogues of the East, there was a sect the Pharisees still condemned, commonly called Nazoraeans (Quote 2). Each witness credited by name; no verdict from the narrator. (A8, A12, D4, E2, E4, E6, E8, E10; Documented as each reports; the community's belief Contested.)

## What came of it

1. **Symmachus's Greek Scriptures** set in Origen's columns and read by Jerome (G1, G4): told in Beat 2. Defended by them: the Hebrew Scriptures (B11).
2. **Their gospel** listed by Eusebius among the disputed books, "with which those of the Hebrews that have accepted Christ are especially delighted" (F1); copied, translated and twice quoted by Jerome (F5–F9). Told in Beat 5; Eusebius's line and the baptism scene, card.
3. **"Ebion" became a byword** for those who held Christ only a man: Damasus's creed anathematizes Photinus "renewing the heresy of Ebion"; Hilary calls Photinus "the Ebion of our day" (I3). Card.
4. **Still there, very few:** Augustine, about 400 (I4). Ending 1.
5. Jerome's later claim that John wrote his gospel against the Ebionites (I5). Card; late.

Defended (as the witnesses report): circumcision, the Sabbath and the Law (B2, B12); Jerusalem (B2); against Paul (B5, B5b); against sacrifices, in their gospel (F4) and the Ascents of James (B16): card.

Who keeps them today: no held source; leave out (census: "Its carriers ended").

## Connection to carry into the story

Everything about them passed through their opponents' hands, and some of their own work did too. Symmachus's books went to Juliana and from her to Origen, who set his translation in his columns (G1–G2); the Nazoraeans of Beroea let Jerome copy their gospel, and he translated and quoted it (F5–F7). The listener must understand that the writers who called them heretics were also the ones who kept and used their books, so that the only words of their own that survive are James's bread and the risen Lord's, in Jerome's Latin. Show it through Juliana's books and Jerome's copying; never in a "this matters because" line. No line of descent is claimed: Epiphanius's Pella beginning stays in his voice.

## Ending

1. **(Planned.) Augustine, about 400.** In Africa, in those same years, Augustine wrote that they had insisted that non-Jewish believers become Jews. Their number, he said, was now very small, but the sect still continued. (AugF 6787; I4; Documented as his report.)
2. **Jerome's verdict as the last words** (Quote 2), ending the doubt scene and the story together; Augustine to the card. (E10.)
