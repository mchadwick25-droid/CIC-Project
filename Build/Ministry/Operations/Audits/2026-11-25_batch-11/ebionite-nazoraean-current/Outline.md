# Outline: The Ebionites and Nazoraeans

Kind: tradition (`kinds/tradition.md`). Census id `ebionite-nazoraean-current`; census name "Ebionite / Nazoraean Current", shortName **Ebionites**, informalName "The Ebionite and Nazoraean Current". Spoken: "the Ebionites and Nazoraeans". Job (slate): Jewish believers in Jesus who went on keeping the Law, met only through the writers who opposed them; the doubt is the story, told as a scene of its own in the old witnesses' words. All references are rows of `Source_Sheet.md` and, through them, lines of `cic/texts/`.

## The heart

- **The essence:** From the middle of the second century to about 400, church writers wrote of Jews who believed that Jesus was the Christ and went on keeping the Law of Moses: circumcision, the Sabbath, the food laws, and the later ones place them in Syria and beyond the Jordan. Their own books are lost, so we meet them only through other church writers, nearly all of whom argued against them, and those writers do not agree on what they believed about Jesus. (B1, B1b–B1c, B2, B8–B10, B12; E1–E10; A2, A9, A11, A13, A14, I4. Places: Epiphanius first, about 375, then Jerome (Pan 25026–25028; PL23 48161–48162); Justin, Irenaeus and Origen give none. No witness gives an end: Jerome in 404 says "usque hodie", to this day (CSEL34 23235). "Nearly all": Justin would take them in (JusG 2166–2173). The census `region` "Palestine, Syria" is not spoken. The census `dates` "1st-4th c." reach back to the first century only through Epiphanius's Pella beginning, so the story's span is the witnesses' own. Documented for the practice as every witness reports it; Contested for their belief about Jesus.)
- **Who they were:** Jews who believed in Jesus as the Christ, called Ebionites (from the Hebrew for "poor", Origen) and Nazoraeans (from Nazareth, Epiphanius). (C2, C6; Documented as each witness reports. Whether the two names mean one people or two: Contested, J.)
- **When and where:** the witnesses write from Justin's day, the middle of the second century, to Jerome's letter of 404 ("to this day") and Augustine's "still continues"; none gives an end. Places come only from Epiphanius on: Beroea in Syria, Pella beyond the Jordan, Kokaba (A2, A9, A11, A12, A13, A14, I4; Documented as reported; dates Widely Accepted.)
- **What they faced:** some Christians would not share company or a home with them (B1b); the synagogues, Epiphanius says, cursed them three times a day (D3); the church writers named them heretics (D5–D7) and Jerome said they were "neither Jews nor Christians" (E10). (Documented as each reports.)
- **What they believed:** that Jesus is the Christ; that the Law still bound them; that Paul was "an apostate from the Law" (B5, B12). About Jesus's birth and nature the witnesses part (E): this is the scene. Words from the gospels they used survive only as their opponents quote them (Jerome, F6, F9; Epiphanius, F3–F4). (Documented as reported; Contested on Christ.)
- **How they practiced:** circumcision, the Law's customs, Jerusalem revered "as if it were the house of God" (B2); the Sabbath and also the Lord's day (B10); Scripture read in Hebrew (B11); the gospel they used, in Hebrew letters (F7, F8); elders and synagogue rulers (B13, Epiphanius only). (Documented as reported.)
- **What held them together:** the Law kept with faith in Jesus as the Christ: "Jews in everything", Epiphanius says, but for believing in Christ (B11–B12). (Documented as reported.)
- **What came of them:** their books passed into their opponents' hands: Symmachus's Greek Scriptures into Origen's columns and Jerome's reading (G1–G4); their gospel copied, translated and quoted by Jerome (F5–F8). By about 400 Augustine says they were "very small" in number, but still there (I4). (Documented.)

## Shape

The kind kit's form: a story in time order that follows the people themselves, from about 155 to 404: where they were, what they kept, what they did, and what was done to them and by whom. Each beat opens on the community, as the grammatical subject, at the place the record gives; the witness comes in inside the scene as the one who saw or reported it ("Irenaeus reports", "he says"), never as the beat's subject.

**What the sources allow, said plainly.** The held texts give one dated scene in which the community itself acts: the Nazoraeans of Beroea, in Syria, letting Jerome copy the gospel they used (F7, before 392). They also give one named member at work (Symmachus putting the Hebrew Scriptures into Greek, G1–G3), and the Nazoraeans naming themselves after Nazareth (C6). Everything else is custom as an outsider reports it (circumcision, Jerusalem, the Law read in Hebrew), or what others did to them: some Christians refused them a home (B1b), Irenaeus named them a heresy (D5), the synagogues cursed them (D3), the Pharisees condemned them, and Jerome judged them (D4, E10). No speech of theirs is held except the words of their gospel as Jerome quotes it (F6) and their charge against Paul as Epiphanius reports it (B5b, card). So the strongest shape is a journey through what was done to them and what they kept, with Beroea as its one scene of their own, placed where it falls in time. No scene, speech or setting is added to fill the gaps.

**Places.** Only Ephesus (the talk, Eusebius), "Syria and beyond the Jordan" (the later writers), Beroea in Syria (Jerome), and "the synagogues of the East" (Jerome). Irenaeus and Origen give no place for them (A13): those beats carry the witness's own city as a clause about the witness only, never as where the people lived. Pella and Kokaba stay out (Cut first 3, 4b, 8, as ruled).

The thread of what was done to them runs through every beat: shut out of a home (hook), named a heresy (Beat 1), scorned in their name (Beat 2), cursed three times a day (Beat 4), condemned and judged "neither" (Beat 6). Each witness's answer on Jesus stays in its own beat. The doubt comes to its turn at Jerome's letter of 404 (Beat 6), where the fullest report of their faith and the hardest verdict come in one breath. No earlier answer is told again.

## Openings

1. **(Planned.) "Will he be saved?", at Ephesus.** In the middle of the second century, at Ephesus, as Eusebius later recorded, a Jew named Trypho put a question to the Christian teacher Justin. Suppose a man believes Jesus is the Christ and obeys him, but still wants to keep the Law of Moses: will he be saved? In my view, yes, Justin answered, if he does not press the Law on non-Jewish believers. And are there some who say no? (Quote 1.) The place is the opening's plain where, in Eusebius's voice only: Justin's own Dialogue names no city for the talk, and Trypho says only that he fled the war and lives in Greece, "chiefly at Corinth" (A1: HE 24310–24312, "which he held in the city of Ephesus with Trypho"; ANF1 17344–17351). Eusebius writes about 170 years later: Documented as his report. The people's own place follows in the essence ("Syria and beyond the Jordan"). (A1, B1, B1b; Documented.)
2. (Alternative, for the card.) The curse in the synagogues, three times a day (D3). Set aside: it opens on an enemy's report written two centuries after Justin, and the curse belongs in its time (Beat 4).

## Story beats

Each beat opens on the community and what they did or what was done to them; the witness is credited inside the scene. Where a beat has no place for them, none is given.

**Hook** (Opening 1): what was done to them: "some" (Justin says no more of who; never "some Christians" aloud) would not share company or a home with such people, and Justin would not join them (Quote 1; B1b). Then **the essence** in the narrator's words, with the one-witness line inside it: their own books are lost, and we meet them only through other church writers, nearly all of whom argued against them (sheet head note; F, G2; Justin the exception, B1b–B1c). The essence carries their place: the later writers place them in Syria and beyond the Jordan (A9, A11, A14). Justin gives them no name; the essence brings them in as "such people", Jews who believed Jesus was the Christ and kept the Law. (Cut first 13.) Justin would take them in "as kin and brothers", if they kept the Law themselves and did not press it on others (B1c; narrator's words).

1. **Called Ebionites, about 180 (no place given).** By about 180 they were called Ebionites (C1, "Those who are called Ebionites", ANF1 33534–33535; never "first"). Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon in Gaul, counted them among the heresies, the teachings he held false (D5): what was done to them, and by whom. Then the people as subject, each line his report: they were circumcised, he writes, (Cut first 14:) kept the customs the Law commands, and lived so much as Jews that they revered Jerusalem "as if it were the house of God". (Cut first 4c: They read only Matthew's Gospel.) They rejected Paul as "an apostate from the Law", one who had abandoned it. (Cut first 15.) Their own charge, as Epiphanius reports it, was that he wrote against circumcision, the Sabbath and the Law (Pan 26744–26745). The rest of that charge goes to the card (B5b). On Jesus, he writes, they held that he was the son of Joseph (E2; ANF1 43299–43300). Lyon and Gaul are Irenaeus's, a clause about the witness; no line says where they lived (A13). (B2, B3, B5, C1, D5; Documented as Irenaeus's report.)
2. **One of their own at work, and their name turned to scorn (Origen's last years; no place given).** The beat opens on a member acting: one of them, Symmachus, put the Hebrew Scriptures into Greek (G2, HE 34737–34738, "Symmachus was an Ebionite"; G3, Jerome, "an adherent of the same sect"; G1, his Greek version). (Card: Origen set his version beside the others in his book of columns, G1; the brief cut the clause because no held line says why, `Fresh_Read.md`.) (Cut first 4: the whole Juliana line, card.) (Cut first 4d.) Then Origen, the teacher from Alexandria, as witness: in his last years he said that their very name came from the poverty of their Law. Ebion, he says, is Hebrew for "poor", and Jews who received Jesus as the Christ were called Ebionites. The plain sense follows at once, in Origen's voice: he took the name to fit a Law he held poor; it was scorn, and in another book he calls them poor in understanding (ANF4 39116–39121; ANF4 34023–34025; see Words to explain). (Cut first 4e: they had not deserted the law of their fathers.) (Cut first 2: their charge that other Christians ate food the Law calls unclean, B9; card.) Origen knew two kinds of them: some confessed with the church that Jesus was born of a virgin, and some denied it and said he was born like other men (E4; ANF4 53648–53653). Alexandria is Origen's, a clause about the witness only. (A4, B8, C2, C3, E4, G1–G3; Documented as reported.)
3. **(Cut first 1.) The Sabbath and Sunday, under Constantine.** They kept the Sabbath and the rest of the Jewish way just as Jews did, and also, like other Christians, kept the Lord's day in memory of the Saviour's resurrection: so Eusebius of Caesarea writes. (A5, B10; Documented as his report; his editor notes he may mean one kind only: say "some of them" if the beat stays.)
4. **Nazoraeans, cursed in the synagogues, about 375.** About 375 Epiphanius, a bishop on Cyprus, wrote of them as two heresies, side by side: the Nazoraeans and the Ebionites (E11; Pan 12578). Then the people as subject, every line in his voice ("he says"): the Nazoraeans named themselves, he says, after Nazareth (C6, Pan 24398–24399, "οὗτοι γὰρ ἑαυτοῖς ὀνομα ἐπέϑεντο ... Ναζωραίων"; their own act of naming, as he reports it). They were Jews in everything: they read the Law and the prophets in Hebrew and believed in one God and in his child Jesus Christ (B11). (Cut first 16.) What they held about Christ himself, he says, he cannot tell: whether a mere man, or born of Mary by the Holy Spirit (E8; Pan 25023–25026). (Cut first 3 and 4b: his places, card. Cut first 8: the Pella beginning, left out by the length ruling.) Last, what was done to them and by whom: in the synagogues, he says, the Jews cursed them three times a day, (Cut first 5:) morning, noon and evening, praying that God would curse the Nazoraeans, because they were Jews who preached that Jesus is the Christ. Reason for the curse: Epiphanius's own (D3). Cyprus is his see, not their home. (A6, B11, B12, C6, D3, E8, E11; Documented as Epiphanius's report.)
5. **At Beroea, they let Jerome copy their gospel (before 392).** The one scene in the held texts where the community itself acts. In Beroea, a city of Syria, the Nazoraeans used a gospel written in Hebrew letters, and they let Jerome, the Latin scholar, copy it; he wrote of it in 392 (F7; PL23 48155–48163, "Mihi quoque a Nazarwis, qui in Berea urbe Syrie hoc volumine utuntur, describendi facultas fuit"; "describendi" is "of copying", not "described"; there he calls it Matthew's Hebrew gospel, which stays out under Cut first 5). Nothing more of the visit is held: no year, no host, no words of theirs to him; tell only the leave to copy. Elsewhere he calls it the Gospel according to the Hebrews (F8; PL23 44505–44509, Against the Pelagians, undated in held texts, the one place that ties that name to the Nazoraeans, "quo utuntur usque hodie Nazareni"), and he says he put it into Greek and Latin (F5; PL23 48081–48083). (Cut first 5: "says that most take it for Matthew's", card.) In it, after the resurrection, the Lord appears to James, his brother. James had sworn not to eat bread from the hour he drank the Lord's cup until he saw him risen. (Cut first 11.) The Lord called for a table and bread. (Quote 3.) (A7, A11, F5–F8; Documented as Jerome's report, and as the gospel's words in Jerome's quotation.)
6. **Still in the synagogues of the East, 404: the doubt.** In 404, writing to Augustine (Cut first 12: no gloss on him), Jerome said that to this day, among the Jews in all the synagogues of the East, there was a sect commonly called the Nazoraeans, which the Pharisees still condemned (A12, D4; CSEL34 23235–23237): their place and what was done to them, in his voice. What they believed about Jesus he set down as the church's own faith, and in the same breath gave his verdict on them (Quote 2), the story's last words (Ending 2). The listener has already heard each earlier witness's answer in its own time; the scene does not repeat them, and the narrator gives no verdict. (A8, A12, D4, E10; Documented as Jerome's report; the community's belief Contested.)

## What came of it

1. **Symmachus's Greek Scriptures** set in Origen's columns and read by Jerome (G1, G4): told in Beat 2. Defended by them: the Hebrew Scriptures (B11).
2. **Their gospel** listed by Eusebius among the disputed books, "with which those of the Hebrews that have accepted Christ are especially delighted" (F1); copied, translated and twice quoted by Jerome (F5–F9). Told in Beat 5; Eusebius's line and the baptism scene, card.
3. **"Ebion" became a byword** for those who held Christ only a man: Damasus's creed anathematizes Photinus "renewing the heresy of Ebion"; Hilary calls Photinus "the Ebion of our day" (I3). Card.
4. **Still there, very few:** Augustine, about 400 (I4). Ending 1.
5. Jerome's later claim that John wrote his gospel against the Ebionites (I5). Card; late.

Defended (as the witnesses report): circumcision, the Sabbath and the Law (B2, B12); Jerusalem (B2); against Paul (B5, B5b); against sacrifices, in their gospel (F4) and the Ascents of James (B16): card.

Who keeps them today: no held source; leave out (census: "Its carriers ended").

## Connection to carry into the story

Everything about them passed through their opponents' hands, and some of their own work did too. Symmachus's Greek version stood in Origen's columns (G1), and Origen had Symmachus's own writings on the Scriptures from Juliana, who had them from Symmachus himself (G2; card, Cut first 4). The Nazoraeans of Beroea let Jerome copy the gospel they used, and he translated and quoted it (F5–F8). The listener must understand that the writers who called them heretics were also the ones who kept and used their books, so that what survives of the gospels they used survives in their opponents' quotations. Show it through Symmachus's Greek Scriptures (Beat 2) and, above all, the Beroea scene, where they let Jerome copy their gospel (Beat 5); Origen's columns go to the card (no held line says why); never in a "this matters because" line. No line of descent is claimed: Epiphanius's Pella beginning stays in his voice.

## Ending

1. **(Planned.) Augustine, about 400.** In Africa, in those same years, Augustine wrote that they had insisted that non-Jewish believers become Jews. | "They denied that Jesus was divine" / "They believed he was only a man" in the narrator's voice | Each witness by name; Jerome says they believed as the church does (E). |
| "They rejected Paul because he wrote that circumcision profits nothing" | No witness gives Galatians 5.2 as their reason; Irenaeus: 'an apostate from the law'; Epiphanius: their charge that he wrote against circumcision, Sabbath and Law (B5, B5b). |

## Leave out

- Ebion as a person (C5): the witnesses differ and the doubt over him is modern.
- Their charge that Paul was a Greek who was circumcised to marry a priest's daughter (B5b): strong, and given in its own content, but no room to tell it with its source and their side; card, in full.
- Epiphanius on Ebionite rites, marriage, Christ and the devil, rejecting the prophets after Joshua, the "Circuits of Peter", the "Ascents of James", and "I came to abolish the sacrifices" (B13–B16, E9, F3–F4, J): one late hostile witness; card.
- The tale of Count Joseph of Tiberias (Panarion 30.4–12): not about their life.
- Hippolytus's "they too can become Christs" (E5): card.
- The baptism scene from the Gospel according to the Hebrews (F9): card.
- Eusebius's Pella account (H1) as a story beat: the census has no line from it; Epiphanius's version in his voice only.
- The Clementine romances, Aristo of Pella, the Testaments (sheet sweep): ties modern.
- Justin's chapter 48 ("man of men"): the two prints read "your" and "our race", and its tie to them is an editor's.
- Jerome's later claim on John's Gospel (I5): card.
- Augustine, On Heresies 9–10 (E12): card; it is late and leans on Epiphanius.
