## Story or summary

**Summary, with two scenes in it.** The Justin and Trypho opening is a true scene, and the James meal from the Hebrew gospel is another. Everything between is a procession of opponents, one per paragraph, in time order: Irenaeus says, Origen says, Epiphanius says (four times), Jerome writes, Jerome writes again. The community itself almost never acts. Symmachus translates in one clause; the Beroea Nazoraeans "let Jerome copy" a book. The rest is what was written about them. The slate asks that the doubt be "a scene of its own, in their own words". Here the doubt is spread across four reported paragraphs instead: Origen's two kinds, Epiphanius's "he cannot tell", Jerome's creed. None is staged as a moment. The kind kit's traps ("an encyclopedia entry", "a list of beliefs with no people in it") are close.

## 1. The test

- **Who they were:** filled. Jews who believed Jesus was the Christ and kept the Law. Under two names. Whether Ebionites and Nazoraeans are one people or two is left for the listener to guess until Epiphanius's "two heresies, side by side". That fits a story whose doubt is the point, so not counted.
- **What happened to them:** filled. Called heretics by the church, cursed in the synagogues, still present in 404.
- **What their faith was about:** filled. Law kept, Jesus the Christ, a split over his birth.
- **Why the connection matters: cannot fill. BLOCKING.** The listener hears that opponents read Symmachus's Greek and that Jerome copied and translated their gospel. Nothing says what either gave the church, or why it mattered to them or to anyone. The James scene is the longest passage in the brief. Its link to this community (why their gospel puts James first) is never drawn. Exact change: carry, through what people did, the connection the outline names (what passed from them to the church, and what the church defended against them), in the Symmachus and Beroea paragraphs. If the outline does not hold it, the James scene shrinks to what the outline says it shows about them.

## 2. The opening

The first three sentences give **who** (Trypho, Justin, and the community as Trypho's hypothetical man), **where** (Ephesus) and **when** (the middle of the second century). Missing: **why there** (nothing says this was a debate between a Jew and a Christian teacher, though "questioned" implies it), and **who Eusebius is**: an unexplained name in the first sentence, before the hook has landed. The listener is not lost about who or where. Not blocking.

## 3. Sentences that fail on one hearing (MINOR)

1. "There are some, Trypho, I answered, who do not dare to share even company or a home with such people." The voice jumps from narration ("Justin answered") into Justin's "I" with no signal. Lost: who "I" is, for a second.
2. "In his last years Origen, the teacher from Alexandria, said that their very name came from the poverty of their Law, for Ebion is Hebrew for 'poor'." Lost: what "the poverty of their Law" means, and whether it is praise or scorn. It only becomes scorn in the next sentence.
3. "What they believed about Jesus he set down as the church's own faith, and in the same breath gave his verdict." Lost: did Jerome think their belief was orthodox, or is the narrator saying it was? This is the sentence that leads to the ending, and it is the hardest to hold.
4. "In the synagogues, he says, the Jews cursed them three times a day, praying God to curse the Nazoraeans, because..." Understood, but "cursed... praying God to curse" says the same act twice and the clause order blurs it.

## 4. Bare statements (BLOCKING)

1. "One of them, Symmachus, put the Hebrew Scriptures into Greek, and their opponents read his version." What the book was: said. Why it mattered to them, or why opponents read it: not said. Exact change: give the reason as the outline holds it, or cut the sentence.
2. "In Beroea, a city of Syria, the Nazoraeans used a gospel written in Hebrew letters..." What this gospel was to them (their own gospel? their only one?) and why it mattered to them is not said. The James scene then has no point for the listener. Exact change: one clause from the outline on what this gospel was to the Nazoraeans; if it holds none, keep the scene only as far as the outline ties it to them.

Checked and not counted: "counted them among the heresies" (glossed, and the reasons follow); "rejected Paul" (the quote gives the reason); "the Pharisees... still condemned them" (an act given without reason, told as given).

## 6. Tells

**Named by Part 1, BLOCKING (1):** "never a report about the record". Paragraphs 3 to 7 are each one writer's report, joined by "he writes", "he says", "wrote", "calls". See "Story or summary". Exact change: tell what the old sources agree on in the narrator's own voice. Keep the credit only where the sources differ, or for an enemy's charge. Make the disagreement over Jesus's birth one scene in the witnesses' own words, as the slate asks.

No "not X but Y" turn in the narrator's voice ("neither Jews nor Christians" is Jerome's). No dash, no stock word, no rhetorical question (s2 is Trypho's own question from the record). No moral close: it ends on Jerome's quoted verdict, which fits a people met only through opponents.

**Lines that sound machine-made (5):**
1. "Church writers wrote of them from Justin's day to about 400, and the later ones place them in Syria and beyond the Jordan." A coverage note, the voice of a summary generator: "wrote of them" with a span of dates.
2. "Their own books are lost, so we meet them only through other church writers, nearly all of whom argued against them..." Close to the stock how-we-know sentence. It is needed once, but here it stands as its own sentence.
3. "They were circumcised, he writes... They rejected Paul... They held, he writes, that Jesus was the son of Joseph." Three "They..." sentences in parallel, with the attribution repeated: a list of beliefs.
4. "and in the same breath gave his verdict". A stock narrator flourish set up for the closing quote.
5. "cursed them three times a day, praying God to curse". A doubled verb that reads like stitched paraphrase.
