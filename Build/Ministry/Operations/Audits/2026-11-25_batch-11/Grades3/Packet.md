## alexandria-catechetical

Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition

Around 200, the threat of persecution under the emperor Severus had driven every teacher of the Christian faith out of Alexandria, in Egypt. With no one else left to teach, pagans came to Origen, then in his eighteenth year, to hear the word of God. Nearly all we know of these classrooms comes from Eusebius of Caesarea, writing about a century later from letters and from people who had known Origen, and he tells of the pupils who were put to death. One was a catechumen, or learner not yet baptized, and Eusebius borrows Origen's phrase: "Among the women, too, Herais was still a catechumen. She received baptism, as he himself says somewhere, the baptism by fire, and left this life." Origen counted her death as her baptism.

From about 180 to the middle of the next century, the Christians of Alexandria learned their faith from teachers, as the city's pagans learned grammar and philosophy, in what Eusebius calls a school. Teachers took pagans and catechumens through Scripture, read for a deeper sense beneath the plain story.

Origen, still a boy, had been one of the pupils of Clement of Alexandria, who taught there in those years. Clement wrote that the Word, his name for Jesus, first urges people on, then trains them, and last of all teaches them. A sick body needs a doctor before it can learn anything, he explained. In the same way, a soul must first be healed of its passions, then trained, and only then taught, once it can take in the Word. So a learner was led step by step toward salvation. He wrote, too: "Faith is taught without letters," that is, to those who cannot read. "Its handbook, unpolished and divine at once, is called love, a spiritual book."

In Origen's classroom the crowds came one after another from morning to evening and scarcely left him time to breathe, or to study Scripture himself, so he divided them. Heraclas, one of his first pupils, took the beginners, and Origen kept the advanced. Scripture, Origen wrote before he left the city, has a body, a soul and a spirit, as a person does. The plain story feeds the simple, its soul feeds those who have gone further, and its spirit those made perfect.

When Origen left for Caesarea, about 231, Heraclas took over the instruction and then became the city's bishop. Dionysius, another of Origen's pupils, followed him in both.

Nepos, a bishop in Egypt, had written a book called Refutation of Allegorists, against those who read Scripture for hidden meanings. As his opponents describe it, he taught that Christ would reign on this earth for a thousand years of bodily luxury. In the district of Arsinoe, in the Egyptian countryside, his teaching had held for a long time, and whole churches had broken away and fallen away. Dionysius loved Nepos for his faith and his work on Scripture, but he would answer the book. Some teachers who relied on it, he wrote, led simpler believers to hope for small and mortal things in God's kingdom, and left them no lofty thought of the resurrection and of being made like Christ.

In his own account, the only one left to us, Dionysius called together the presbyters, or elders, and the teachers of the villages, and asked for a public examination. They brought him the book like a weapon and a fortress no one could take, and he sat with them for three days, from morning to evening. "If the argument won," he wrote, "we were not ashamed to change our minds and agree."

At the end, Coracion, the leader of this teaching, said before all the believers who were there that he was convinced, and would no longer hold or teach it.

## antiochene-church-third-century

The Church of Antioch in the Third Century

Paul of Samosata became bishop of Antioch around 260. The bishops who judged him wrote that he had changed the psalms, the sacred songs, sung in his own church. "He stopped the psalms to our Lord Jesus Christ, as if they were too new, the work of newer men. But on the great day of Easter he trains women to sing psalms to himself, in the middle of the church. Anyone who heard them would shudder."

In the later third century the Christians of Antioch, in Roman Syria, were one church under a bishop, with presbyters, its priests, and deacons, its helpers, bound by letters to churches across the empire. When their own bishop was charged with refusing to confess that the Son of God came down from heaven, the bishops of the East met again and again over him, until at last they put him out.

Almost all we know of him comes from the letter his judges sent out, which Eusebius of Caesarea copied into his history of the church. They wrote that Paul said Jesus Christ was from below, and Eusebius says he held that Christ was by nature an ordinary man.

Bishops hurried to Antioch from every side, Eusebius says, as against a man ravaging Christ's flock, and they met there often while Paul's side hid what he taught. Firmilian, bishop of Caesarea in Cappadocia, came twice and condemned his new teaching, but when Paul promised to change, Firmilian believed him and put the matter off. On his third journey he died at Tarsus, while the bishops waited for him.

The bishops also wrote that Paul built himself a high throne and a curtained private room, like a magistrate. He scolded those who would not cheer and wave handkerchiefs as in the theater, though they listened with reverence, as in the house of God. A bishop, they held, must be an example to the people.

At last Malchion, a presbyter who ran the city's school of Greek learning, questioned Paul while shorthand writers took the debate down. A final meeting of many bishops cut Paul off from the whole church, because, they wrote, he set himself against God and refused to obey. No church was to send him letters of communion, the letters bishops exchanged to show they shared one church.

Paul refused to give up the church's house, so the case went to the emperor Aurelian. He gave the house to whichever side the bishops of Italy and Rome wrote to. Paul's judges had asked them to write to a new bishop, not Paul, so he was driven out of the church by the worldly power.

In the persecution under the emperor Maximinus, Lucian, a presbyter of the same church, defended his faith before the emperor at Nicomedia and was imprisoned and killed. Two generations later, John Chrysostom told in a sermon how he answered under torture. "To each question he answered only, I am a Christian. When the torturer asked, What is your homeland? I am a Christian, he said. What is your trade? I am a Christian. Who are your parents? To all of it he said, I am a Christian."

Later, in Alexandria, a presbyter named Arius said that the Son of God had a beginning, and God had none. His bishop, Arius wrote, preached that the Son always was, and drove him out of the city because he would not agree. That bishop set Lucian in a line from Paul to Arius: Lucian, he wrote, kept out of communion with the bishops for many years, and Arius and his party were shoots of the same root.

A church historian of the next century, on Arius's side, says that some of Lucian's pupils became bishops. One was Eusebius, bishop of Nicomedia, not Eusebius of Caesarea. Arius had written to him about being driven out, and ended that letter with the name Lucian's pupils gave themselves. "I pray you fare well in the Lord, remembering our troubles, fellow-Lucianist."

## roman-church-third-century

The Roman Church in the Third Century

Natalius was a confessor, one who had stood trial for the faith and not given way. Yet, early in the 200s, he let a group teaching that Christ was only a man make him their paid bishop. A writer of his day says he ignored warning visions, so holy angels whipped him all night. Then he put on sackcloth and ashes and threw himself down before Zephyrinus, bishop of Rome, rolling at the feet of clergy and people. He was barely taken back into communion, the church's shared bread and cup.

In the first half of the 200s, the Christians of Rome were a large, mixed church. It held slaves and women of rank, and fed more than fifteen hundred widows and poor, under a bishop the people chose. They argued in public over how to speak of Father and Son, whether the church could take back grave sinners, and whom a woman of rank might marry, until a stricter party broke away in 251. Almost all that is told of its first decades comes from Hippolytus, a writer of that time who withstood two of Rome's bishops and lost.

Zephyrinus's right hand was Callistus, who had been a slave. When Zephyrinus died, Callistus became bishop and put Sabellius, who taught that Father and Son were one and the same, out of the church. Hippolytus says Callistus did it because he feared Hippolytus, and to clear himself of the charge that he shared Sabellius's view. In his own words, as his enemy reports them: "I will not profess belief in two Gods, Father and Son, but in one." Hippolytus spoke of one God but two persons, and Callistus, he says, called that two Gods.

Hippolytus says Callistus let people off for sins of the body, saying he forgave them all. People troubled in conscience came to his church, along with people other groups had refused, and some whom Hippolytus's side had thrown out. Callistus pointed, his enemy says, to the weeds in Christ's parable, left to grow with the wheat.

He also let a woman of rank, who would lose that rank by a lawful marriage, take a companion, slave or free, and count him her husband. Hippolytus charged that such women took drugs to avoid or end a pregnancy, not wanting a child by a slave, and he called the rule a teaching of adultery and murder.

Some years after Callistus, as a later historian tells it, all the brothers and sisters met to choose a bishop by vote, and set Fabian on the bishop's seat. In 250 the emperor Decius forced Christians to sacrifice to the gods. The same historian traces it to Decius's hatred of Philip, the emperor before him, who was reported to be a Christian. Fabian died a martyr, leaving Rome without a bishop. Some gave way from terror or fear of men, and became known as the fallen. Rome's clergy wrote of them to Carthage's clergy: "They are separated from us, but we have not abandoned them. We urged them, and urge them still, to repent, if by some means they can receive pardon from the One who is able to grant it. If we abandoned them, they would grow worse."

Cornelius, made bishop in 251, counted his church: forty-six elders, seven deacons, and more than a hundred lesser clergy. Novatian, an elder, held that the fallen had no more hope, and with a stricter party and confessors at his side he was made a second bishop, set up against the first. That year sixty bishops met at Rome, declared Novatian's party outsiders, and resolved to heal the fallen through repentance.

That year, the confessors who had sided with Novatian came back, saying they had been deceived. Cornelius wrote how the people met them: "A great crowd of the brothers and sisters came running together. All of them had one voice, giving thanks to God. They showed the joy of their hearts in tears. They embraced them as if they had been set free from prison that very day."

## greek-apologists-second-century

The Second-Century Greek Apologists

An Athenian Christian, Athenagoras, addressed his plea to the emperors Marcus Aurelius and his son Commodus, and hailed them, above all, as philosophers. Only Christians, he told them, were hunted for their name alone, and slaughtered on the word of false accusers.

In the second century, from Athens and Rome to Sardis and Antioch, Christians who wrote in Greek put written defenses of their faith into the hands of emperors, and of pagans who asked them about it. Accused of being godless, of eating human flesh and of incest, they answered that they worshipped the one God who made all things and prayed for the emperors, and they asked to be judged by how they lived. Such a defense was called an apology, the kind of speech made in court.

Long before, under Hadrian, certain wicked men were trying to trouble the Christians. So Quadratus addressed a defense to the emperor, arguing that Christ's works were real: some people Christ healed lived to Quadratus's day. Eusebius, a church historian, still had the book in the early 300s.

Under the next emperor, Antoninus Pius, Christians were called atheists, because they refused the gods of the cities and would not sacrifice to them. Justin, a Christian teacher of philosophy at Rome, wrote to the emperor. He granted that they were atheists of such gods, and said they worshipped the most true God. Everywhere, he wrote, they took care to pay their taxes, as Christ had taught them. They worshipped God alone, and gladly served the emperors in all else.

Under Marcus Aurelius, Justin was condemned and put to death for refusing to sacrifice to the gods and obey the emperor's command.

In the Roman province of Asia, in today's western Turkey, Christians were hounded by new decrees, new written orders, as Melito, bishop of Sardis, wrote to Marcus Aurelius. In the passages Eusebius copied, Melito wrote that informers, people who laid charges against others and took their goods, used the decrees to rob the innocent night and day. "If this is done at your command, well and good. For a just ruler will never take unjust measures, and we gladly accept the honour of such a death." But if the decree was not the emperor's, he begged not to be left to the plundering of the crowd.

If the charges were true, Athenagoras said, let the emperors destroy the Christians root and branch. If not, let them look into Christian lives, teaching and loyalty. Poets and philosophers, he said, wrote freely about God, and almost all spoke of one God. No law stood against them. Yet a law stood against Christians, who said the same. As for eating human flesh, he turned to the emperors' own games, the gladiator shows, fights to the death between armed men, or with wild beasts, staged for crowds. "But we hold that to watch a man being killed is close to killing him, so we have given up such shows. We do not even watch, so that we bring no guilt or stain on ourselves. How then could we commit murder?"

At Antioch, in Syria, a bishop, Theophilus, talked about God with a pagan friend, and they parted friends. But the friend believed the rumors, and thought Christian books were new and their teaching nonsense. Theophilus wrote to him, counting back the years to show that the books of Moses, the first in the Bible, were older than the Greeks' own, and so Christian teaching true.

At Carthage, Tertullian took up the same task in Latin. He also wrote against the followers of a teacher, Valentinus. They stacked heaven on heaven for their many gods, he scoffed, and he drew on earlier writers against them, Justin first.

One defense, a letter to a man named Diognetus, has no known writer or date. Of the Christians it says: "They live in their own homelands, but as resident foreigners. They share in everything as citizens, and endure everything as strangers. Every foreign land is a homeland to them, and every homeland is foreign."

## latin-apologists

The Latin Apologists

After his friend Octavius died, Minucius Felix, a pleader, or court lawyer, wrote down a day from their friendship in his dialogue the Octavius. On a court holiday at the grape harvest, the two of them and Caecilius walked the shore at Ostia, near Rome. Caecilius saw an image of the god Serapis and raised a hand to his mouth, kissing it toward the god. Octavius, a Christian, scolded Minucius for letting Caecilius bow to stones smeared with oil, and the three sat on the breakwater to argue it out.

Minucius was one of the Latin Apologists, Christians of Rome and Roman Africa. From around 200 to the emperor Diocletian's reign, they defended their faith in Latin to the educated who despised it. An apology was a defense speech for the accused, and theirs answered the charges out of the pagans' own books.

The old religion is ancient and useful, Caecilius holds, and the Christians are "A people that lurks and shuns the light, silent in public, chattering in corners." By common report, he says, newcomers kill an infant hidden in flour, and their feasts end in incest when a dog tied to the lamp puts out the light.

Octavius calls them fables no one looked into. He and others like him, Octavius says, once believed them too. As lawyers they had defended the worst criminals. But sometimes they tortured Christians who confessed, to make them deny it. Christians, he says, keep one marriage, eat sober meals, and love one another as brothers, because they do not know how to hate. No temple holds God, who is unseen and everywhere, and the offering he wants is a good mind. Beaten with the philosophers' own weapons, Caecilius yields.

At Carthage around 200, Tertullian met the same charges in his Apology to the governors who judged Christians. He said torture, used on others to make them confess, was turned around on Christians, to make them deny what they were. Christians prayed for the emperors, he wrote, and gave each month to bury the poor and keep orphans, a love that branded them in the eyes of people who hated one another. Jerome, a Christian scholar writing later, says the Roman clergy's envy and insults drove Tertullian to the New Prophecy. It held that the Holy Spirit now gave a stricter rule.

In Diocletian's reign Arnobius taught rhetoric, the art of public speaking, at Sicca in Africa. The story of how he became a Christian rests on Jerome alone, who wrote about eighty years later that dreams drove him toward belief, and the bishop would not take him in. "He could not win from the bishop the faith he had always attacked. So he laboured by lamplight over most brilliant books against his old religion. At last, as if with hostages for his devotion, he won the treaty." His seven books were his pledge, and he was let in. In them he owns his past: "Whenever I had seen a stone made slick and smeared with olive oil, I fawned on it as if a power were present in it. I spoke to it, and begged favours from a block that felt nothing."

Arnobius's pupil, Jerome says, was Lactantius, called to teach rhetoric at Nicomedia, a Greek city in Bithynia. He was teaching there when, Lactantius says, the emperor's officers burned the Christians' Scriptures and leveled their church, on a day picked to end Christianity. Next day an order laid every Christian, whatever his rank, open to torture. Tertullian's Apology, Lactantius granted, had pleaded the cause in full, but answering accusers was one thing and teaching the whole faith another, and he took on the second.

Later, asked why he quoted pagan books, Jerome named all four together. Their books, he said, were full of the pagans' own learning.

Of the writers known to him, Lactantius wrote, Minucius Felix had stood well among the pleaders, and his Octavius showed how fit a champion of truth he might have been, had he given himself wholly to it.

## post-apostolic-house-church

Post-Apostolic Household-Church Christianity

Early in the second century, Pliny governed a Roman province. He wrote to the emperor that Christians had spread through its cities, villages and countryside. He questioned people who said they had stopped being Christians. An informer had named them. Pliny was unsure whether to punish the name Christian itself, or crimes that went with it. Part of their guilt, or their error, they told him, was this: "On a fixed day they used to meet before dawn. They sang a hymn to Christ as to a god, taking turns among themselves." To find the truth, Pliny, a hostile witness, had two slave women tortured. They were called ministrae, servers.

In the hundred years after the apostles, Christians in Antioch, in the cities of Asia Minor and in Rome met together, and their letters greet one another household by household. They taught newcomers a way of life before baptism, gave thanks over bread and a cup on the Lord's day, and were led by elders, bishops and deacons. Letters and messengers carried between their churches held them together.

A short manual, whose writer, date and place no one knows, opens on the Two Ways. "There are two ways, one of life and one of death, and there is a great difference between the two ways. The way of life is this. First, you shall love God, who made you. Second, your neighbour as yourself." Newcomers learned this way of life before being baptized in the name of the Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Only the baptized could share the thanksgiving, the eucharist in Greek.

Near the end of the first century, the church at Corinth, in Greece, removed some of its elders, the senior men who led the church together. At Corinth's request, the church of Rome wrote. Its letter, the only side that survives, blames envy, and one or two people who stirred Corinth against men who had served blamelessly. God sent Christ, it argues, and Christ sent the apostles. They appointed bishops and deacons, knowing there would be strife over the office, and ordered that approved men succeed them. Men appointed with the whole church's consent, who served blamelessly, could not justly be removed.

Ignatius, bishop of Antioch, was brought from Syria as a prisoner, he wrote, for the name of Christ. On the road he wrote to the churches of Asia, a Roman province in today's western Turkey. Each church, he urged, should act as one around its bishop, with its elders and deacons. He greeted households, and asked Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna, to have churches send messengers or letters to Antioch.

Justin, a Christian, wrote to the emperor Antoninus Pius what Christians did each week: "On the day named after the Sun, all who live in the towns or in the country gather in one place. The memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets are read, for as long as time allows." These memoirs are called Gospels. Bread, wine and water were brought to the one who presides. He gave thanks as he was able, and the people said Amen. Deacons gave to each and carried a share to those absent. They met on the Sun's day because on it God made the world and Christ rose from the dead.

Later, Dionysius, bishop of Corinth, wrote, in words the church historian Eusebius keeps, that his church still drew counsel from Rome's letter in the quarrel, written through Clement.

Near the end of the century, the churches of Asia kept Easter on the Passover date, the moon's fourteenth day; others kept it only on Sunday. Rome's bishop tried to cut them off from fellowship. Irenaeus of Lyon reminded him of the elders who had led Rome's own church before Soter, an earlier bishop. These same men, Irenaeus wrote elsewhere, stood in Rome's line of bishops, which carried the apostles' teaching to his day. Those elders did not keep Asia's day, yet lived at peace with visitors who did, and sent them a share of the thanksgiving.

## syriac-edessa-nisibis

Syriac Christianity (Edessa/Nisibis)

In November 201, after a great rain in the night, the river rose against Edessa and broke the city's western wall. The flood killed more than two thousand people, and destroyed the church of the Christians. The Chronicle of Edessa, put together some 340 years later, says the city's scribes recorded the flood at the time.

Those Christians spoke Syriac, and with the churches of Nisibis they made up Syriac Christianity, in two frontier cities between the Roman and Persian empires, from the flood to the year 410. In the 300s they sang their teaching in hymns and counted among them men and women vowed to God for life. Edessa's later founding story pictures its people hearing the Diatessaron read: the one story Tatian wove from the four Gospels.

Across the border in Persia, a teacher named Aphrahat wrote letters. Some men and women took a vow, before baptism, to live unmarried and holy for God. They were called the sons and daughters of the covenant. One of his letters asks them to keep watch at night, to sing psalms and pray, to give their goods to the poor, and not to live with the other sex, so lust will not draw them back.

If a vowed man asks a daughter of the covenant to live with him and serve him, Aphrahat gives her the words to answer. "I am promised in marriage to a royal husband, and I serve him. If I leave his service and serve you, my bridegroom will be angry with me. He will write me a letter of divorce and send me away from his house." The husband she means is Christ.

In Nisibis, Ephrem taught in hymns, each with a refrain, a line that came back after every verse. In his hymn for a siege by a Persian army, the river threatens the city, and Ephrem sets that flood beside Noah's. After Noah's flood, God set his bow, the rainbow, over the earth as his promise. So the refrain runs: "Stretch out your bow against the flood, for see, it has lifted up its waves against our walls!"

In Persia, the Christians had grown in number, and this angered the Magi, the priests who guarded the Persian religion. Sozomen, a Greek church historian of the next century, says the king believed a charge that their bishop was a friend of the Roman emperor. The king taxed the Christians heavily, hoping that want would make them give up their faith. Then he ordered their priests killed and their churches pulled down. Aphrahat, writing in 344, said that in his own days many declared their faith and were killed.

In 363, to save its starving army inside Persia, Rome handed Nisibis to Persia, and Ephrem moved west to Edessa. In prose, Ephrem answered three rival teachers by name. Marcion taught a Stranger God, apart from the Maker of the world; Mani taught two first Roots, Light and Darkness; and Bardaisan said the world was made from five first beings. Of their followers Ephrem wrote: "Their works are like our works, just as their fast is like our fast, but their faith is not like our faith." He warned that whoever prayed with Mani's followers "prays with Satan."

Ephrem died at Edessa in 373, and the Syrians went on singing his hymns.

A Roman envoy, a bishop, came to a later Persian king. Through his work, the king ordered the churches his fathers had torn down to be rebuilt. He let "the priests, the leaders and all the holy covenant" go about freely and without fear. In 410, at the king's order, forty bishops of the Persian church met in a synod, a meeting of bishops to set rules for the church. Among the forty was the bishop of Nisibis, Ephrem's old city, now inside Persia. The day they met, the bishops said, was for them like seeing the dead rise.

## manichaeism

Manichaeism

On 28 August 392, at Hippo in Roman Africa, a Manichaean priest named Fortunatus debated Augustine, then a priest of the city's church. Augustine had once been one of them, and Fortunatus called on him as witness to how they lived: "You were at our prayer." Augustine answered that he had been only a Hearer, never one of the Elect. At their prayer he had seen nothing shameful, only that they prayed facing the sun, which went against the faith he later learned.

They were the followers of Mani, a prophet of Babylonia. From the 200s they held that Light and Darkness were two powers without beginning, now mixed in the world, and that in the end the two would be parted again. The Elect lived in strict purity and poverty, while the Hearers lived in the world and fed and served them. Hunted by Persian kings and Roman emperors, they spread from Mesopotamia west to Roman Africa and east to the Turks and China.

Mani was born in 216 in Babylonia, in Mesopotamia. He wrote a book for King Shapur of Persia, and Biruni, a Muslim scholar of about 1000, quotes how it begins. Mani says God's messengers brought wisdom and deeds to people from age to age: the Buddha to India, Zoroaster to Persia, Jesus to the West. Now, in this last age, the revelation had come down through Mani himself, God's messenger to Babylonia. He said he was the Paraclete, the Helper Jesus had promised, and he wrote his own scriptures.

His followers held that they must harm no animal, fire, water or plant. The Elect kept from marriage, owned only a day's food and a year's clothing, and went about preaching. The Hearers gave a tenth of what they had, fasted and married one wife. The Hearers' own confession, a prayer for forgiveness, says the Light was in the food they ate, and that their sins sent it "to the Bad Place." Augustine mocks this. Eating the Hearers' food, he says, the Elect freed bits of God inside it. Their stomachs made it into "angels and gods", who would free the Hearers.

Biruni says King Bahram had Mani hunted down, and gives the king's reason: "This man has come forward calling people to destroy the world." Bahram had him killed, but the accounts differ on how. Some say he was flayed and his skin hung at the gate of the city of Gundeshapur. A book by one of his pupils, which Biruni reports, says he failed to cure a relative of the king and was chained in prison until he died.

His followers kept the day he died as their great feast, the Bema. Augustine says he asked why they honored it far above Easter. They told him the day to keep was the death of one who had truly suffered, since Christ, they held, was never born in real flesh and only seemed to suffer. After Mani's death, a Baghdad scholar of the 900s says, the king killed his followers wherever he found them, and they fled east into the lands of the Turks.

In 372 the Roman emperors ordered that wherever their meetings were found, the teachers be heavily fined, the members shut out of society and the houses seized, for teaching what the law called a profane doctrine. Faustus of Milevis, a bishop of theirs, wrote a book answering their opponents' questions on the gospel, and Augustine, a Hearer for nine years, kept his words, quoting each part before answering. Faustus wrote: "If you press me whether I accept the gospel, I may answer: I have left all that was mine. Father, mother, wife, children, gold, silver, eating, drinking, comforts, pleasures. Take this as answer enough to your questions. And believe that you will be blessed, if you do not stumble because of me."

Christians brought Faustus before the governor's court, and, found to be a Manichaean, he was exiled to an island. Augustine calls this the lightest of penalties, since those same Christians pleaded for him.

In 732 a Chinese emperor's order called Mani's teaching a perverse belief that falsely took the name of Buddhism and deceived the people, and forbade it, though the foreigners from the West, whose own religion it was, might keep it themselves. A later emperor's letter says China let the faith grow because the Uyghurs, a Turkish people, revered it. In 843, with the Uyghurs defeated, an edict closed the faith's temples. A Chinese chronicle written four centuries later says some seventy of their women religious at the capital all died.

Near the oasis of Dunhuang, a cave library kept a scroll written in Mani's script, in a Turkish tongue. It is the Hearers' confession, and one of its articles begins: "Every day, how many evil thoughts we think! How many wretched words we speak that should not be spoken! How many deeds we do that should not be done!" Each article ends the same way: my God, cleansing ourselves of sin, we pray, forgive my sin.

## marcion-marcionism

Marcion / Marcionism

In 250, in the stadium at Smyrna, a presbyter, or church elder, named Pionius stretched himself on a stake and let the soldier drive in the nails. He had refused to sacrifice to the gods, saying he feared the living God. Beside him they raised Metrodorus, a presbyter of the rival Marcionites. Both faced east, and the wood was heaped around them.

The Marcionites were the churches that followed Marcion after Rome's church cast him out, in the middle of the second century. For about three hundred years, from Rome to Persia and down to the villages of Syria, they gathered with their own presbyters, teachers and martyrs. Their Scripture was two books, a Gospel cut down from Luke's and ten letters of Paul. They held one belief above all: Jesus came from a good Father, above the God who made the world, and Jesus abolished that God's law and prophets, the Jewish Scriptures. Everything this story tells of them comes from their enemies. Tertullian, a writer at Carthage, in North Africa, says Marcion was cast out for his restless curiosity, which spread to others. Irenaeus, a bishop writing within a generation, says Marcion cut from both books every passage that called the maker of the world the Father of Jesus.

Late in the second century, a writer from the Roman province of Asia named Rhodon found Marcion's followers divided over how many first sources, or beginnings of all things, there are. Apelles, one of their teachers, held there was only one, one God, where Marcion held two. Rhodon, who argued with him, set down his words: "He said there was no need at all to examine a person's teaching, and that each should keep to what he believed. For those who trusted in the one who was crucified would be saved, he said, if only they were found doing good works."

About 207, Tertullian reported that their churches baptized only virgins, widows, the unmarried and the divorced, a rule he said condemned marriage. Their good God, he said, punished no one, and only cast the sinner away out of sight. An accused Christian was told to sprinkle incense from a box to the gods, and whoever did it denied Christ and went free. So he asked his Marcionite reader, who feared no punishment: "Why, in times of persecution, do you not save your life at once by denying, when the incense box is held out? 'Never,' you say, 'never!'"

In the persecution that began under the emperor Diocletian, Asclepius was burned at Caesarea in Palestine, the historian Eusebius's own city. He was said to be, Eusebius writes, a bishop of the Marcionites, eager for religion as he understood it, but without true knowledge.

When the persecutions ended, the emperor Constantine issued an edict, a law addressed by name to the Marcionites and other heretics, those who, their enemies said, chose error. Their teaching, it said, made the healthy soul sick. It forbade their meetings, even in private houses, and gave their houses of prayer to the catholic Church.

Yet their meetings went on. At Jerusalem in the fourth century, explaining the creed's "one Holy Catholic Church" to those about to be baptized, Cyril named the Marcionites first among the heretics whose meetings were also called churches. He told them to ask in an unfamiliar city for the Catholic Church, the church over all the world.

In the fifth century Theodoret was bishop of Cyrrhus, in northern Syria. There John Chrysostom had once charged an earlier bishop to drive Marcion's teaching out of the district, offering the aid of the imperial edicts. Theodoret worked to bring them to his own faith, that Christ was, as he wrote, not only good but just. He was stoned many times by the heretics he fought. After twenty-five years as bishop, he wrote: "I led eight villages of Marcion's followers, and the country around them, into the way of truth."
