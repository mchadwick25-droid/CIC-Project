# Packet B11c, three

## antiochene-church-third-century

The Church of Antioch in the Third Century

Paul of Samosata became bishop of Antioch around 260. The bishops who judged him wrote that he had changed the psalms, the sacred songs, sung in his own church. "He stopped the psalms to our Lord Jesus Christ, as if they were too new, the work of newer men. But on the great day of Easter he trains women to sing psalms to himself, in the middle of the church. Anyone who heard them would shudder."

In the later third century the Christians of Antioch, in Roman Syria, were one church under a bishop, with presbyters, its priests, and deacons, its helpers, bound by letters to churches across the empire. When their own bishop was charged with refusing to confess that the Son of God came down from heaven, the bishops of the East met again and again over him, until at last they put him out.

Almost all we know of him comes from the letter his judges sent out, which Eusebius of Caesarea copied into his history of the church. They wrote that Paul said Jesus Christ was from below, and Eusebius says he held that Christ was by nature an ordinary man.

Bishops hurried to Antioch from every side, Eusebius says, as against a man ravaging Christ's flock, and they met there often while Paul's side hid what he taught. Firmilian, bishop of Caesarea in Cappadocia, came twice and condemned his new teaching, but when Paul promised to change, Firmilian believed him and put the matter off. On his third journey he died at Tarsus, while the bishops waited for him.

The bishops also wrote that Paul built himself a high throne and a curtained private room, like a magistrate. He scolded those who would not cheer and wave handkerchiefs as in the theater, though they listened with reverence, as in the house of God. A bishop, they held, must be an example to the people.

At last Malchion, a presbyter who ran the city's school of Greek learning, questioned Paul while shorthand writers took the debate down. A final meeting of many bishops cut Paul off from the whole church, because, they wrote, he set himself against God and refused to obey. No church was to send him letters of communion, the letters bishops exchanged to show they shared one church.

Paul refused to give up the church's house, so the case went to the emperor Aurelian. He gave the house to whichever side the bishops of Italy and Rome wrote to. Paul's judges had asked them to write to a new bishop, not Paul, so he was driven out of the church by the worldly power.

In the persecution under the emperor Maximinus, Lucian, a presbyter of the same church, defended his faith before the emperor at Nicomedia and was imprisoned and killed. Two generations later, John Chrysostom told in a sermon how he answered under torture. "To each question he answered only, I am a Christian. When the torturer asked, What is your homeland? I am a Christian, he said. What is your trade? I am a Christian. Who are your parents? To all of it he said, I am a Christian."

Later, in Alexandria, a presbyter named Arius said that the Son of God had a beginning, and God had none. His bishop, Arius wrote, preached that the Son always was, and drove him out of the city because he would not agree. That bishop set Lucian in a line from Paul to Arius: Lucian, he wrote, kept out of communion with the bishops for many years, and Arius and his party were shoots of the same root.

A church historian of the next century, on Arius's side, says that some of Lucian's pupils became bishops. One was Eusebius, bishop of Nicomedia, not Eusebius of Caesarea. Arius had written to him about being driven out, and ended that letter with the name Lucian's pupils gave themselves. "I pray you fare well in the Lord, remembering our troubles, fellow-Lucianist."

## roman-church-third-century

The Roman Church in the Third Century

Natalius was a confessor, one who had stood trial for the faith and not given way. Yet, early in the 200s, he let a group teaching that Christ was only a man make him their paid bishop. A writer of his day says he ignored warning visions, so holy angels whipped him all night. Then he put on sackcloth and ashes and threw himself down before Zephyrinus, bishop of Rome, rolling at the feet of clergy and people. He was barely taken back into communion, the church's shared bread and cup.

In the first half of the 200s, the Christians of Rome were a large, mixed church. It held slaves and women of rank, and fed more than fifteen hundred widows and poor, under a bishop the people chose. They argued in public over how to speak of Father and Son, whether the church could take back grave sinners, and whom a woman of rank might marry, until a stricter party broke away in 251. Almost all that is told of its first decades comes from Hippolytus, a writer of that time who withstood two of Rome's bishops and lost.

Zephyrinus's right hand was Callistus, who had been a slave. When Zephyrinus died, Callistus became bishop and put Sabellius, who taught that Father and Son were one and the same, out of the church. Hippolytus says Callistus did it because he feared Hippolytus, and to clear himself of the charge that he shared Sabellius's view. In his own words, as his enemy reports them: "I will not profess belief in two Gods, Father and Son, but in one." Hippolytus spoke of one God but two persons, and Callistus, he says, called that two Gods.

Hippolytus says Callistus let people off for sins of the body, saying he forgave them all. People troubled in conscience came to his church, along with people other groups had refused, and some whom Hippolytus's side had thrown out. Callistus pointed, his enemy says, to the weeds in Christ's parable, left to grow with the wheat.

He also let a woman of rank, who would lose that rank by a lawful marriage, take a companion, slave or free, and count him her husband. Hippolytus charged that such women took drugs to avoid or end a pregnancy, not wanting a child by a slave, and he called the rule a teaching of adultery and murder.

Some years after Callistus, as a later historian tells it, all the brothers and sisters met to choose a bishop by vote, and set Fabian on the bishop's seat. In 250 the emperor Decius forced Christians to sacrifice to the gods. The same historian traces it to Decius's hatred of Philip, the emperor before him, who was reported to be a Christian. Fabian died a martyr, leaving Rome without a bishop. Some gave way from terror or fear of men, and became known as the fallen. Rome's clergy wrote of them to Carthage's clergy: "They are separated from us, but we have not abandoned them. We urged them, and urge them still, to repent, if by some means they can receive pardon from the One who is able to grant it. If we abandoned them, they would grow worse."

Cornelius, made bishop in 251, counted his church: forty-six elders, seven deacons, and more than a hundred lesser clergy. Novatian, an elder, held that the fallen had no more hope, and with a stricter party and confessors at his side he was made a second bishop, set up against the first. That year sixty bishops met at Rome, declared Novatian's party outsiders, and resolved to heal the fallen through repentance.

That year, the confessors who had sided with Novatian came back, saying they had been deceived. Cornelius wrote how the people met them: "A great crowd of the brothers and sisters came running together. All of them had one voice, giving thanks to God. They showed the joy of their hearts in tears. They embraced them as if they had been set free from prison that very day."

## syriac-edessa-nisibis

Syriac Christianity (Edessa/Nisibis)

In November 201, after a great rain in the night, the river rose against Edessa and broke the city's western wall. The flood killed more than two thousand people, and destroyed the church of the Christians. The Chronicle of Edessa, put together some 340 years later, says the city's scribes recorded the flood at the time.

Those Christians spoke Syriac, and with the churches of Nisibis they made up Syriac Christianity, in two frontier cities between the Roman and Persian empires, from the flood to the year 410. In the 300s they sang their teaching in hymns and counted among them men and women vowed to God for life. Edessa's later founding story pictures its people hearing the Diatessaron read: the one story Tatian wove from the four Gospels.

Across the border in Persia, a teacher named Aphrahat wrote letters. Some men and women took a vow, before baptism, to live unmarried and holy for God. They were called the sons and daughters of the covenant. One of his letters asks them to keep watch at night, to sing psalms and pray, to give their goods to the poor, and not to live with the other sex, so lust will not draw them back.

If a vowed man asks a daughter of the covenant to live with him and serve him, Aphrahat gives her the words to answer. "I am promised in marriage to a royal husband, and I serve him. If I leave his service and serve you, my bridegroom will be angry with me. He will write me a letter of divorce and send me away from his house." The husband she means is Christ.

In Nisibis, Ephrem taught in hymns, each with a refrain, a line that came back after every verse. In his hymn for a siege by a Persian army, the river threatens the city, and Ephrem sets that flood beside Noah's. After Noah's flood, God set his bow, the rainbow, over the earth as his promise. So the refrain runs: "Stretch out your bow against the flood, for see, it has lifted up its waves against our walls!"

In Persia, the Christians had grown in number, and this angered the Magi, the priests who guarded the Persian religion. Sozomen, a Greek church historian of the next century, says the king believed a charge that their bishop was a friend of the Roman emperor. The king taxed the Christians heavily, hoping that want would make them give up their faith. Then he ordered their priests killed and their churches pulled down. Aphrahat, writing in 344, said that in his own days many declared their faith and were killed.

In 363, to save its starving army inside Persia, Rome handed Nisibis to Persia, and Ephrem moved west to Edessa. In prose, Ephrem answered three rival teachers by name. Marcion taught a Stranger God, apart from the Maker of the world; Mani taught two first Roots, Light and Darkness; and Bardaisan said the world was made from five first beings. Of their followers Ephrem wrote: "Their works are like our works, just as their fast is like our fast, but their faith is not like our faith." He warned that whoever prayed with Mani's followers "prays with Satan."

Ephrem died at Edessa in 373, and the Syrians went on singing his hymns.

A Roman envoy, a bishop, came to a later Persian king. Through his work, the king ordered the churches his fathers had torn down to be rebuilt. He let "the priests, the leaders and all the holy covenant" go about freely and without fear. In 410, at the king's order, forty bishops of the Persian church met in a synod, a meeting of bishops to set rules for the church. Among the forty was the bishop of Nisibis, Ephrem's old city, now inside Persia. The day they met, the bishops said, was for them like seeing the dead rise.
