## montanism-the-new-prophecy

Montanism (the New Prophecy)

In a village called Ardabau, in the uplands of what is now western Turkey, a recent Christian convert named Montanus fell into what an enemy calls a frenzy and began to say strange things. Some who heard him called him possessed and told him to be silent. Others, who thought themselves filled with the Holy Spirit, urged him on, and he could no longer be kept quiet.

Montanus and his followers were Christians of Phrygia, in the second century, and two women, Priscilla and Maximilla, prophesied as he did. Enemies called them Montanists or Phrygians, but they spoke of the New Prophecy, their name for the prophets and their words. They held that the Paraclete, the Holy Spirit Jesus had promised as Helper, spoke through their prophets now, in ecstasy, carrying them out of their own senses, and called the church to a stricter life.

That enemy, a church elder whom Eusebius does not name, argued with them while the movement was new; Eusebius later copied his account, and like most of the record it comes from their opponents.

The elder says Montanus longed to lead. Apollonius, another opponent writing forty years after Montanus began, says Montanus gave the name Jerusalem to two small Phrygian towns, Pepuza and Tymion, to gather people there from everywhere.

The faithful of the Roman province of Asia met often, in many places, to examine the new sayings. Finding them novel and profane, they drove the prophets' followers out, barred from communion: shut out of the church's fellowship and its shared meal. The prophets' side called their opponents killers of the prophets, for refusing prophets who, they said, were the ones the Lord had promised to send. When bishops came to refute the spirit in Maximilla, her followers stopped them. She said: "I am driven away from the sheep like a wolf. I am not a wolf. I am word and spirit and power." The elder dared that spirit to prove its power by forcing the bishops who had been there to confess it.

Maximilla also said, in words a later opponent kept, that after her there would be no prophet any more, but the end of the age. The end she foretold did not come, and around 200, at Carthage in North Africa, the writer Tertullian took up their cause in Latin.

The quarrel, he says, was this: "The new prophecies are rejected, not because Montanus and Priscilla and Maximilla preach another God. Nor is it because they divide Jesus Christ, or overturn any rule of faith or hope. It is because they plainly teach fasting more often than marrying." The rule of faith was the short creed he set out: one God the Creator, and his Son Jesus Christ, born of the Virgin, crucified, risen and coming to judge. The main church blamed them for fasts of their own, Stations kept on into the evening, and dry food, with no meat, juice or wine. He defended their rule of one marriage only: no second marriage, even after a husband or wife had died. When Christians were hunted for their faith, he urged them to face death rather than flee. He held that such rules were the Paraclete's teaching.

Epiphanius, that later opponent, writing about 375, says that in his day Pepuza lay razed and deserted, yet the believers still went there for their rites, saying that the Jerusalem from above, the heavenly city, comes down there. He gives the vision of a prophetess, Quintilla or Priscilla, he could not say which: "Christ came to me in the form of a woman, clothed in a shining robe. He put wisdom into me. He showed me that this place is holy, and that Jerusalem comes down here from heaven."

Under the emperor Justinian, in the sixth century, the empire ordered groups such as the Montanists to give up their beliefs for the established faith, says Procopius, a writer hostile to Justinian. In Phrygia, the Montanists shut themselves inside their churches, set them alight and died in the flames.
