In a village called Ardabau, where Mysia meets Phrygia, a recent convert named Montanus fell into what one witness calls a frenzy, and began to say strange things. Some who heard him thought he was possessed and told him to be silent. Others, believing they were filled with the Holy Spirit and a gift of prophecy, urged him on, and from then on he could not be kept quiet.

That witness was an enemy, a presbyter, or church elder, whose name is lost, and his account survives because the historian Eusebius later copied it. Nearly everything known of Montanus and his followers comes through the people who fought them.

They were Christians of Phrygia, in the uplands of what is now western Turkey, in the second century. Their enemies called them Montanists, or simply Phrygians, but they called their movement the New Prophecy, their own name for the prophets and what they said. Two women, Priscilla and Maximilla, began to prophesy as Montanus did, and their followers said the Paraclete was speaking through them. The Paraclete was the Helper Jesus had promised, the Holy Spirit, who would lead his followers into all truth. The New Prophecy held that he was speaking now, in ecstasy, carrying the prophets out of their own senses, and calling the church to a stricter life.

The anonymous writer had his own explanation: Montanus, he says, longed to lead, and prophesied against the church's settled custom. Apollonius, another opponent writing forty years later, says Montanus gave the name Jerusalem to Pepuza and Tymion, two small towns in Phrygia, to gather people there from everywhere, and that he set up collectors of money and paid his preachers.

The faithful of Asia gathered again and again, in many places, to examine the new sayings. They judged them novel and profane, and the spirit behind them hostile to the whole church, so they drove the prophets' followers out, barred from communion, shut out of the church's fellowship and its shared meal. The prophets' side answered that the bishops were killing the prophets the Lord had promised to send. When bishops came to refute the spirit in Maximilla, her followers stopped them. Her own words survive inside the enemy's book: "I am driven away from the sheep like a wolf. I am not a wolf. I am word and spirit and power." The spirit in her claimed power, and her opponent dared it to prove that power on the bishops.

Maximilla also said that after her there would be no prophet any more, but the end. Epiphanius, who wrote against the movement about 375, kept those words. The end did not come.

At Carthage, in North Africa, around 200, a Christian writer named Tertullian took up their cause in Latin, as one of them. He said what the quarrel was about: "The new prophecies are rejected, not because Montanus and Priscilla and Maximilla preach another God. Nor is it because they divide Jesus Christ, or overturn any rule of faith or hope. It is because they plainly teach fasting more often than marrying." The rule of faith was the short creed Tertullian set out: one God the Creator, and his Son Jesus Christ, born of the Virgin, crucified, risen and coming to judge. On that, in his telling, both sides agreed. The main church blamed them, he says, for fasts of their own: Stations, fast days kept on into the evening, and dry food, with no meat, no juice and no wine. He defended their rule of one marriage only, and he urged facing martyrdom rather than flight. For Tertullian, these rules were the Paraclete's, spoken through the prophets.

In the same book that kept Maximilla's words, Epiphanius wrote that Pepuza lay razed and deserted. Yet the believers still went there to hold their rites, saying that the Jerusalem from above came down there. He tells the vision behind that hope, seen by a prophetess, Quintilla or Priscilla, though he could not say which: "Christ came to me in the form of a woman, clothed in a shining robe. He put wisdom into me. He showed me that this place is holy, and that Jerusalem comes down here from heaven." Among the groups of his own day, he says, women served as prophets, bishops and presbyters.

Under the emperor Justinian, in the sixth century, the empire ordered such groups to give up their beliefs for the established faith. So says Procopius, a writer of that time who was hostile to the emperor, and he tells how agents went out across the empire to force everyone they met to give up the faith of their fathers. In Phrygia, where the prophets had first spoken, the Montanists shut themselves inside their churches, set them alight and died in the flames.
