In a village called Ardabau, where Mysia meets Phrygia, a recent convert named Montanus fell into what an enemy calls a frenzy and began to say strange things. Some who heard him said he was possessed and told him to be silent. Others, who thought themselves filled with the Holy Spirit, urged him on, and from then on he could not be kept quiet.

That enemy, a church elder whose name is lost, wrote within living memory, and his account survives because the historian Eusebius copied it. Like most of what we know of the movement, it comes from the other side.

Montanus and his followers were Christians of Phrygia, in the uplands of what is now western Turkey, in the second century. Two women, Priscilla and Maximilla, began to prophesy as he did. Their enemies called them Montanists or Phrygians, but they spoke of the New Prophecy, their own name for the prophets and their words. They held that the Paraclete was speaking through them now. He was the Helper Jesus had promised, the Holy Spirit, who would lead his followers into all truth, and he spoke in ecstasy, carrying the prophets out of their own senses, to call the church to a stricter life.

The elder says Montanus longed to lead and prophesied against the church's settled custom. Apollonius, another opponent writing forty years later, says Montanus named two small Phrygian towns, Pepuza and Tymion, Jerusalem, to gather people there from everywhere, and that he set up collectors of money and paid his preachers.

The faithful of Asia met often, in many places, to examine the new sayings. They judged them novel and profane, and the spirit behind them hostile to the whole church, so they drove the prophets' followers out, barred from communion: shut out of the church's fellowship and its shared meal. The prophets' side called the bishops killers of the prophets the Lord had promised to send. When bishops came to refute the spirit in Maximilla, her followers stopped them. Her words survive in the enemy's book: "I am driven away from the sheep like a wolf. I am not a wolf. I am word and spirit and power." The spirit in her claimed power, and the elder dared it to prove that power on the bishops.

Maximilla also said that after her there would be no prophet any more, but the end. Epiphanius, who wrote against the movement about 375, kept those words. The end did not come.

Around 200, at Carthage in North Africa, the Christian writer Tertullian took up their cause in Latin. In his telling, this was the quarrel: "The new prophecies are rejected, not because Montanus and Priscilla and Maximilla preach another God. Nor is it because they divide Jesus Christ, or overturn any rule of faith or hope. It is because they plainly teach fasting more often than marrying." The rule of faith was the short creed he set out: one God the Creator, and his Son Jesus Christ, born of the Virgin, crucified, risen and coming to judge. On that, he says, both sides agreed. The main church blamed them for fasts of their own: Stations, fast days kept on into the evening, and dry food, with no meat, juice or wine. He defended their rule of one marriage only and urged facing martyrdom rather than flight, rules he took for the Paraclete's own.

Epiphanius writes that in his day Pepuza lay razed and deserted, yet the believers still went there to hold their rites, saying that the Jerusalem from above came down there. He tells the vision behind that hope, seen by a prophetess, Quintilla or Priscilla, though he could not say which: "Christ came to me in the form of a woman, clothed in a shining robe. He put wisdom into me. He showed me that this place is holy, and that Jerusalem comes down here from heaven." In the groups of his own day, he says, women served as prophets, bishops and presbyters.

In the sixth century the emperor Justinian ordered such groups to give up their beliefs for the established faith. Procopius, a writer of that time who was hostile to the emperor, says agents went across the empire forcing everyone they met to give up the faith of their fathers. In Phrygia, where the prophets had first spoken, the Montanists shut themselves inside their churches, set them alight and died in the flames.
