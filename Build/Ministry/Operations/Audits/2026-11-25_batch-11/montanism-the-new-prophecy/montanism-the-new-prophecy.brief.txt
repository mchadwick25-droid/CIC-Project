In a village called Ardabau, where Mysia meets Phrygia, a recent convert named Montanus fell into what an enemy calls a frenzy and began to say strange things. Some who heard him called him possessed and told him to be silent. Others, who thought themselves filled with the Holy Spirit, urged him on, and he could no longer be kept quiet.

That enemy, a church elder whose name is lost, wrote within living memory, and the historian Eusebius later copied his account, which like most of the record comes from the movement's opponents.

Montanus and his followers were Christians of Phrygia, in the uplands of what is now western Turkey, in the second century, and two women, Priscilla and Maximilla, prophesied as he did. Enemies called them Montanists or Phrygians, but they spoke of the New Prophecy, their name for the prophets and their words. They held that the Paraclete, the Helper Jesus had promised, the Holy Spirit, spoke through their prophets now, in ecstasy, carrying them out of their own senses, and called the church to a stricter life.

The elder says Montanus longed to lead and prophesied against the church's settled custom. Apollonius, another opponent writing forty years later, says Montanus named two small Phrygian towns, Pepuza and Tymion, Jerusalem, to gather people there from everywhere, and set up collectors of money and paid his preachers.

The faithful of Asia met often, in many places, to examine the new sayings. Finding them novel and profane, and the spirit behind them hostile to the whole church, they drove the prophets' followers out, barred from communion: shut out of the church's fellowship and its shared meal. The prophets' side called the bishops killers of the prophets the Lord had promised. When bishops came to refute the spirit in Maximilla, her followers stopped them. Her words survive in the enemy's book: "I am driven away from the sheep like a wolf. I am not a wolf. I am word and spirit and power." The elder dared that spirit to prove its power on the bishops.

Maximilla also said that after her there would be no prophet any more, but the end. Epiphanius, who wrote against the movement about 375, kept those words. The end did not come.

Around 200, at Carthage in North Africa, the writer Tertullian took up their cause in Latin. The quarrel, he says, was this: "The new prophecies are rejected, not because Montanus and Priscilla and Maximilla preach another God. Nor is it because they divide Jesus Christ, or overturn any rule of faith or hope. It is because they plainly teach fasting more often than marrying." The rule of faith was the short creed he set out: one God the Creator, and his Son Jesus Christ, born of the Virgin, crucified, risen and coming to judge. The main church blamed them for fasts of their own: Stations, fast days kept on into the evening, and dry food, with no meat, juice or wine. He defended their rule of one marriage only and urged facing martyrdom rather than flight, taking both for the Paraclete's teaching.

Epiphanius writes that in his day Pepuza lay razed and deserted, yet the believers still went there for their rites, saying the Jerusalem from above came down there. He gives the vision of a prophetess, Quintilla or Priscilla, he could not say which: "Christ came to me in the form of a woman, clothed in a shining robe. He put wisdom into me. He showed me that this place is holy, and that Jerusalem comes down here from heaven."

Under Justinian, in the sixth century, the empire ordered such groups to give up their beliefs for the established faith, says Procopius, a writer hostile to the emperor. Agents went across the empire forcing everyone they met to give up the faith of their fathers. In Phrygia, where the prophets had first spoken, the Montanists shut themselves inside their churches, set them alight and died in the flames.
