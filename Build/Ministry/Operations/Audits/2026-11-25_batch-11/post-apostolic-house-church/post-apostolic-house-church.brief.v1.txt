Under the emperor Trajan, Pliny, the Roman governor of Bithynia on the Black Sea, questioned people who said they had once been Christians and had given it up. The whole of their guilt, or their error, they told him, was this: "On a fixed day they used to meet before dawn. They sang a hymn to Christ as to a god, taking turns among themselves." They bound themselves by oath against theft, robbery and adultery, and came together again later for everyday, innocent food.

They had stopped even that after his order against clubs, given at the emperor's command. To find the truth, Pliny had two slave women tortured, who were called ministrae, servers. He found nothing, he wrote, but a superstition, depraved and without measure, his word of contempt for a foreign cult. He was a hostile witness, and he had it all from people who had left and from two women under torture.

These were the house churches of the hundred years after the apostles. Christians in Antioch, in the cities of Asia Minor and in Rome met in small gatherings, each calling itself the church of God in its city, and their letters greet one another household by household. They taught newcomers a way of life before baptism, gave thanks over bread and a cup on the Lord's day, and were led by elders, bishops and deacons. Letters and messengers carried between their churches held them together.

A short manual from one community, whose writer, date and place no one knows, begins with the words a newcomer heard first: "There are two ways, one of life and one of death, and there is a great difference between the two ways. The way of life is this. First, you shall love God, who made you. Second, your neighbour as yourself. And whatever you would not want to happen to you, do not do to another."

This way of life meant blessing those who curse you, giving to anyone who asks and sharing everything with your brother. Only once it was taught came baptism, after a fast of a day or two, in the name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, in running water if there was any. Only the baptized shared the thanksgiving, as they called the bread and cup. This one manual's prayers give thanks over the cup first, then over the bread: as this broken bread was scattered on the hills and gathered into one, so gather your church. On the Lord's day they confessed their sins, and no one at odds with a neighbor came until they were reconciled.

Near the end of the first century, the church of Rome answered a letter from the church at Corinth, in Greece. Some at Corinth had removed their elders, the senior men who led a church together. Rome's letter, the only side that survives, blames one or two people and says the men had served without blame. God sent Christ, it argues, and Christ the apostles, who appointed bishops and deacons and, foreseeing strife over the office, ordered approved men to succeed them. Such men could not justly be removed. The letter speaks of the same men as holding the bishop's office and as elders. Three messengers carried it, to return quickly with news of peace.

Ignatius, bishop of Antioch, was brought from Syria as a prisoner, he wrote, for the name and the hope they all shared. On the road he wrote ahead to the churches of Asia, a Roman province in today's western Turkey. Each church, he urged, should act as one, its bishop presiding in the place of God, its elders in the place of the apostles' council, with its deacons. A proper thanksgiving was the bishop's, or one he entrusted. He greeted households by name, and asked Polycarp of Smyrna to have the churches send messengers or letters to Antioch.

Polycarp wrote to Philippi, in northern Greece, in his own name and that of the elders with him. Elders were to visit the sick and not neglect the widow, the orphan or the poor. He grieved for Valens, once an elder there, who had failed to grasp the place given him, and for his wife, warned against the love of money, and asked the Philippians to call the two back. As they had asked, he sent them the letters of Ignatius his church held.

At Rome, under the emperor Antoninus Pius, Justin wrote to the emperor what Christians there did each week. The memoirs of the apostles, he explained, were what they called Gospels. "On the day named after the Sun, all who live in the towns or in the country gather in one place. The memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets are read, for as long as time allows. Then, when the reader has stopped, the one who presides speaks. He counsels them, and calls them to imitate these good things. Then we all stand up together and pray."

Then bread, wine and water were brought, the one who presides gave thanks as he was able, and the people said Amen. Deacons gave each a share and carried it to those absent. Those who could gave what they chose, and the one who presides cared for orphans and widows, the sick, prisoners and strangers. They met on the Sun's day, Justin wrote, because on it God made the world and Christ rose from the dead.

When Soter was bishop of Rome, Dionysius, bishop of Corinth, thanked the Romans for their custom, kept from the beginning, of sending gifts to churches in every city. That Lord's day, he wrote, his church had read Rome's new letter aloud, as it still read the earlier one, written to them through Clement. His words survive only as Eusebius, the church historian, quotes them about 150 years later.

Near the end of the century, Rome's bishop tried to cut off the churches of Asia, which kept Easter on a different day. Irenaeus, in a letter Eusebius also quotes, reminded him of the elders who had presided over that same church before Soter. Elsewhere Irenaeus counts these men in the bishop's office, one after another, in a line from the apostles. They did not keep Asia's custom themselves, yet they lived at peace with those who came to Rome from churches that did. And to those from other churches who kept it, they sent the thanksgiving.
