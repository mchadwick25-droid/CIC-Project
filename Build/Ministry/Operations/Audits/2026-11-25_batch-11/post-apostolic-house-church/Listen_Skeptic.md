## 1. The test (tradition)

- **Who these people were:** filled. "Christians in Antioch, in the cities of Asia Minor and in Rome met in small gatherings."
- **What happened to them:** partly unfilled. BLOCKING. Pressure appears once (Pliny) and is dropped. Ignatius is "brought from Syria as a prisoner", but the story never says where to or what came of it. The last paragraph raises a risk ("Rome's bishop tried to cut off the churches of Asia") and never says whether he did. Part 1: carry the story's question "through to where it resolves". Change: say in one clause how the Easter quarrel ended, as the outline holds it, and where Ignatius was being taken and why, as the outline holds it; or cut the quarrel.
- **What their faith was about:** thinly filled. Practice is told; belief comes only through the hymn "to Christ as to a god", the Two Ways commands and "Christ rose from the dead". Passes, just.
- **Why the connection matters:** unfilled. BLOCKING. Nothing says what these households handed on or who carried it on. The census's direct line (the Roman Church in the Third Century) never surfaces. Change: one sentence, from the outline's "What came of them", on what passed to the Roman church after about 200, told through what people did.

## 2. The opening

After three sentences: who (people who had been Christians, and Pliny) and where (Bithynia on the Black Sea) are clear. Missing:
- **When:** "Under the emperor Trajan" is a when only for a listener who knows Trajan. No century until paragraph 2. MINOR.
- **Why there:** BLOCKING (it is also the first bare statement, below). Why was a governor questioning these people at all? "This... was the whole of their guilt" names a guilt with no charge. The listener does not know being a Christian was itself the charge. Change: say, as the outline holds it, why these people were before the governor.

## 3. Sentences that fail on one hearing

1. "This, they told him, was the whole of their guilt, or their error:" Meaning lost: "or their error" sounds like a correction by the narrator, not their words. MINOR.
2. "To find the truth, Pliny, a hostile witness, had two slave women tortured, who were called, he wrote, ministrae, servers." Four interruptions; "who were called" lands after "tortured" and seems to name the torture. Meaning lost: that the women held an office in the church. MINOR.
3. "It also has them appoint their own bishops and deacons." "It" and "them" both unclear (the manual? the newcomers?). Meaning lost: the point that the community chose its own leaders. MINOR.
4. "God sent Christ, it argues, and Christ the apostles, who appointed bishops and deacons, knowing there would be strife over the office, and ordered that approved men succeed them." BLOCKING: the argument at the centre of the Corinth quarrel is lost. On one hearing "Christ the apostles" is not heard as "Christ sent the apostles", and who "knew" and who "ordered" is unclear. Change: two sentences, subject named in each.
5. "Later, Dionysius... his church had read Rome's new letter aloud, and that it kept reading the old one, written through Clement." BLOCKING: Clement was never named, so the listener cannot tie "the old one" to the Corinth letter told two paragraphs back; the thread of letters holding churches together (the essence) is lost here. Change: "the old one" becomes "the letter Rome had sent in the quarrel over the elders", with Clement named there or dropped.
6. "Irenaeus, in a letter Eusebius also quotes, reminded him of the elders who had presided over that same church before Soter, an earlier bishop, men he elsewhere lists among its bishops from the apostles on." BLOCKING: 35 words, three references ("him", "that same church", "he"), two citations. The point, that Rome's own earlier leaders had kept peace with Asia, is lost. Change: "Irenaeus reminded him that Rome's own earlier bishops had kept a different day..." and drop both citation clauses.
7. "They kept a different day, yet lived at peace with visitors who kept Asia's, and sent them the thanksgiving." "Asia's" is an ellipsis the ear misses; "sent them the thanksgiving" is not heard as sending the eucharist bread. MINOR (it is the closing line, so worth fixing).

## 4. Bare statements (Explain it)

1. Pliny's questioning: an interrogation with no reason given (see 2). BLOCKING.
2. "Rome's new letter" (Dionysius paragraph): a letter named with nothing on what it was or why it mattered. BLOCKING. Explain from the outline or cut.
3. "over the date of Easter": the quarrel is named but not what it was. What two days? Why would a date split churches? BLOCKING. One clause from the outline on what Asia kept and what Rome kept.
4. "for the name and the hope they all shared": the reason for Ignatius's arrest is given in words the listener cannot unpack. "The name" means nothing to a non-specialist. BLOCKING. Say plainly what the old source says the charge was.

(Ignatius's request that churches "send messengers or letters to Antioch" has no reason either. It is close to an ordinary act, so not counted, but the reviser should say why if the outline gives it.)

## 5. Citations read aloud not called for (MINOR)

1. "Eusebius, the church historian, keeps his words." A stock how-we-know sentence, which Part 1 bars outright (counted again as a tell). Cut it.
2. "in a letter Eusebius also quotes". Cut. Narrator: "Irenaeus wrote to remind him..."
3. "men he elsewhere lists among its bishops from the apostles on". Cut. Narrator: "Rome's earlier bishops".
4. "Pliny, a hostile witness,". The label is commentary. Pliny's hostility shows from his own torture of the women. Cut the appositive.

## 6. Tells Part 1 names (BLOCKING)

1. **A report about the record.** Part 1: "never quotations linked by commentary, never a report about the record." Six of eight paragraphs are built as "[writer] wrote / argues / explains / keeps / quotes". Pliny questioned, the manual opens, Rome's letter blames and argues, Ignatius wrote, Justin wrote, Dionysius wrote, Eusebius keeps, Irenaeus reminded. The households are almost always heard through a document about them, rarely in a scene. Change: in at least the Corinth, Ignatius and Easter paragraphs, the narrator tells what people did, with the writer as a person acting, not a source being summarised.
2. **The stock how-we-know sentence:** "Eusebius, the church historian, keeps his words." Cut.
3. **A sentence that only says what is missing** (as a clause): "One community's manual, whose writer, date and place no one knows, opens on the Two Ways." It reads as a disclaimer leading into the content. Change: "One community's manual opens on the Two Ways." The doubt belongs on the card.

No stock words, dashes for drama, "not X but Y" turns, rhetorical questions or moral close.

## 6b. Lines that sound machine-made, and why

1. "Letters and messengers carried between their churches held them together." The kind kit's heading "What held them together" spoken back. It is the template, not the narrator.
2. Paragraph 2 as a whole: "They taught..., gave thanks..., and were led by elders, bishops and deacons." This is the slate's job list (Two Ways, Sunday bread and cup, bishops and elders, letters) read out in order, with no person in it. Kind kit trap: "a list of beliefs with no people in it."
3. "It also has them appoint their own bishops and deacons." "Also" tacks on a checklist item the heart required.
4. "Its letter, the only side that survives," A hedge placed for the rule, not for the listener.
5. "Pliny, a hostile witness," Sounds like a rule about naming an enemy witness being followed by rote.
6. "Then the one who presides spoke" Present tense from the translation, stitched into past narration ("presides... spoke", "he explains... spoke"). It reads as quoted text pasted in, not retold.
7. "Men appointed with the whole church's consent, who served blamelessly, could not justly be removed." Legal paraphrase register, flat and generic.
8. The length is exactly 675 words, the cap to the word. Taken with the last two paragraphs that read as footnotes, it suggests the text was filled to the limit rather than stopped where the heart was told.

## 8. Pull, drift, finish

- **Attention first drifts:** "God sent Christ, it argues, and Christ the apostles..." (paragraph 4). Why: it is an argument summarised, with no person in it, in a sentence the ear cannot parse, right after a paragraph that was also summary.
