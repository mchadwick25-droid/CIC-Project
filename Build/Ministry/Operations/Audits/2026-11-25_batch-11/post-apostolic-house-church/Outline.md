# Outline: The Post-Apostolic House Churches (`post-apostolic-house-church`)

Kind: tradition. Every line points to `Source_Sheet.md` (item numbers) and, through it, to `cic/texts/` (short names as in the sheet). Spoken name (batch header, from the census): "The Post-Apostolic House Churches"; census name "Post-Apostolic Household-Church Christianity"; short forms "the house churches", "these households of believers". The heart also serves the rebuilt census entry texts (`tile`, `intro`, `summary`, `recordHolds`).

## The heart

- **The essence:** In the hundred years after the apostles, Christians in Antioch, in the cities of Asia Minor and in Rome met together, and their letters greet one another household by household. They taught newcomers a way of life before baptism, gave thanks over bread and a cup on the Lord's day, and were led by elders, bishops and deacons. Letters and messengers carried between their churches held them together. (Documented: anf01 8503–8508, 8868–8869; anf07 12700–12708, 12910–12920, 13087–13095, 13112–13116; anf01 519–521, 2395–2398, 3608–3618.)
- **Who they were:** "the house churches": local assemblies, each calling itself the church of God in its city ("The Church of God which sojourns at Rome", anf01 519–521), greeting one another's households (anf01 8503–8508, 8868–8869). Ordinary members of every age and rank, men and women, slaves and free (mirbt 2799–2801; anf07 12831–12835). Documented.
- **When and where:** from near the end of the first century to near the end of the second; Antioch in Syria, Smyrna and the province of Asia, the province Pliny governed (unnamed in the held old texts), Corinth, Philippi, Rome (Source Sheet §1). Documented; Widely Accepted for the first date.
- **What they faced:** the apostles gone, and the question of who should lead, which the apostles had foreseen would bring strife (anf01 1916–1922); a church that removed its elders (anf01 2053–2055, 1941–1943); members who fell (Valens, anf01 3531–3534); the state: a governor's trials and torture (mirbt 2795–2797), a bishop brought from Syria as a prisoner, he wrote, for the name and the hope they all shared (anf01 4703). Where it ends: near the end of the second century, Rome's bishop tried to cut off the churches of Asia over Easter, and Irenaeus pointed him to the elders who had presided at Rome before Soter and kept peace with churches of another custom (npnf201 31527–31553). Irenaeus also counts those same men in the bishop's office in a line from the apostles (anf01 39823–39851). Documented.
- **What they believed:** one God, maker of all, blessed "through His Son Jesus Christ, and through the Holy Ghost" (anf01 16497–16502); baptism into the name of Father, Son and Holy Spirit (anf07 12914); a hymn to Christ "as to a god" (mirbt 2790); Sunday kept because God made the world and Christ rose on it (1apol-grc 1779–1785); what they argued over was who should lead and whose thanksgiving was proper (anf01 1916–1922, 8278–8284). Documented, in their own words.
- **How they practiced:** the Two Ways taught first, fasting, baptism in running water (anf07 12700–12708, 12910–12920); fasting Wednesday and Friday, the Lord's Prayer three times a day (anf07 12937–12945); confession and reconciliation before the Lord's-day thanksgiving (anf07 13087–13095); readings, a talk, prayer standing, bread, wine and water, deacons carrying it to the absent, a collection for orphans, widows, the sick, prisoners and strangers (1apol-grc 1759–1779); an oath against theft, robbery and adultery and a common meal (mirbt 2791–2795). Documented.
- **What held them together:** the Lord's-day thanksgiving, carried even to those absent and sent to those from other churches who kept another custom (1apol-grc 1772–1773; npnf201 31549–31553); and the letters and messengers that passed between churches (anf01 2395–2398, 8854–8863, 3608–3618; npnf201 25110–25117). In the built world's terms, its gravities of liturgical practice and the translocal network, with the authority question (Contested) running through both. Documented.
- **What came of them:** their letters went on being read in the churches (npnf201 25114–25117, 16683–16684); Rome's church went on, its leaders counted in a line from the apostles (anf01 39823–39851) and its customs of gifts and the shared thanksgiving recalled (npnf201 25099–25107, 31549–31553). Documented.

## Words to explain

- **the Two Ways**: the manual's own first words, "a way of life and a way of death" (anf07 12700–12704); taught before baptism (anf07 12914).
- **living water**: "running water", as the manual's editor reads it; the manual allows other water if there is none (anf07 12916–12920).
- **thanksgiving / eucharist**: Justin's own gloss, "this food is called among us eucharist", the Greek for thanksgiving (1apol-grc 1730). Use "the thanksgiving"; "eucharist" once, glossed, or not at all.
- **elders (presbyters)**: "the senior men who led a church together" (anf01 3403–3405; anf02 1025–1026). Use "elders".
- **ministrae**: "two slave women who, he wrote, were called ministrae, servers" (mirbt 2796). Do not say "deaconesses" (the 1890 translator's word).
- **clubs (hetaeriae)**: "private clubs and associations, which the emperor had banned" (mirbt 2795; npnf201 19501–19502).
- **superstition**: Pliny's word of contempt for a foreign cult (mirbt 2797).
- **memoirs of the apostles**: Justin's own gloss, "which are called Gospels" (1apol-grc 1743–1744).
- **the Sun's day**: Sunday, and Justin's reason for it (1apol-grc 1779–1785).
- **the mines**: "Christians sent to labour in the mines"; no reason is given (npnf201 25102). Card (Cut first 6).
- **province of Asia**: as in Setting.
- **the name**: Ignatius's own "bound for the name [of Christ]" (anf01 4793–4794; the bracket is the 1885 translator's; the longer recension reads "bound for His name", anf01 4805). Say "the name of Christ". Widely Accepted.
- **the moon's fourteenth day / the Lord's day**: Asia kept Easter on "the fourteenth day of the moon"; the other churches ended the fast only on "the Lord's day", the day of the resurrection (npnf201 31136–31140, 31217–31222, 31262–31265). Documented (Eusebius, later, ~130 years).
- **cut off**: "cut off from the common unity … declared all the brethren there wholly excommunicate" (npnf201 31415–31419). Say "cut them off from fellowship". Documented.

## Openings

1. **(Recommended) Before dawn, under Trajan.** Early in the second century, Pliny governed a Roman province. He wrote to the emperor that Christians had spread through its cities, villages and countryside (mirbt 2768, 2800–2801; npnf201 19511–19512; Setting; grade fix passes). He questioned people who said they had stopped being Christians (mirbt 2786–2787, "fuisse quidem, sed desisse"). An informer had named them (mirbt 2786, "ab indice nominati", OCR "Ahi" for Alii; npnf201 19487–19489, "accused by an informer"). Pliny was unsure whether to punish the name Christian itself, or crimes that went with it (mirbt 2772–2775; npnf201 19463–19469; Source Sheet L7; grade-2 fix pass). Part of their guilt, or their error, they told him, was this. Quote 1. Then they bound themselves by oath against theft, robbery and adultery, and came together again later for everyday, innocent food. They gave that up after his edict against clubs, and he had two slave women, who were called ministrae, servers, tortured to find the truth: he found nothing, he wrote, but a depraved superstition without measure. (mirbt 2789–2797; npnf201 19489–19506.) Then the essence.
2. **The first words a newcomer heard.** Quote 3, then: in this manual, these were the first words taught to anyone who wished to be baptized; only after them came the water (anf07 12700–12708, 12914). Then the essence; Beat 1 then begins with the fasting and the water, and does not repeat the quote.

## Story beats

Spans in quotation marks inside the beats (1880s–1890 English) are wording for the writer to paraphrase, not to quote; each is marked (paraphrase). Only the three Quotes below are quoted.

1. **The way in: a manual whose writer, date and place no one knows.** (If Opening 1 is used.) Quote 3. The newcomer was taught this way of life: bless those who curse you, give to anyone who asks, share everything with your brother, and the master is not to command a slave in bitterness, since both hope in the same God. Then the one to be baptized fasted a day or two, and was baptized in the name of Father, Son and Holy Spirit, in running water if there was any. Only the baptized could share the thanksgiving. Its prayers gave thanks first over the cup, then over the bread: as this broken bread was scattered on the hills and gathered into one, so gather your church. On the Lord's day they confessed their sins, and no one at odds with a neighbour came until they were reconciled. "Appoint for yourselves bishops and deacons," it says (paraphrase). (anf07 12700–12738, 12827, 12831–12835, 12910–12920, 12960–12970, 13087–13095, 13112–13116.) Explain the Two Ways, running water, thanksgiving. Reason for the order: "Having first said all these things, baptize" (anf07 12914). Documented; the manual's date and place unknown (anf07 12668–12672).
2. **Near the end of the first century: Rome writes to Corinth.** Some at Corinth had removed elders from office; Rome's letter, the only side that survives, blames envy, and "one or two persons" (paraphrase) who stirred Corinth against men who had served blamelessly (grade fix pass, 2026-11-25: envy, anf01 605–608, Source Sheet C7; a few who kindled it, anf01 531–535; one or two persons, anf01 2053–2055; blamelessly, anf01 1941–1943). Corinth's own reason is not held. Its argument: God sent Christ, Christ the apostles, and the apostles appointed bishops and deacons, knowing there would be strife over the office, and ordered that approved men succeed them; men appointed with the whole church's consent, who had served blamelessly, could not justly be removed. The letter speaks of the same men as holding the bishop's office and as elders. It went by three messengers, to be sent back quickly with news of peace. (anf01 519–535, 1849–1860, 1916–1943, 2053–2055, 2395–2398; anf09 17638–17640.) Explain elders. Reason for the letter: Corinth had asked (anf01 525–528). The letter adds that the report of the quarrel had reached outsiders (anf01 2055–2059); it does not give that as its reason for writing. Documented; the letter names no author (npnf201 16679–16680).
3. **On the road from Antioch: Ignatius.** Ignatius, bishop of Antioch, a prisoner brought from Syria, he wrote, for the name of Christ and the hope they all shared, wrote to the churches of Asia, from Smyrna and Troas. Each church, he urged, should act as one, its bishop presiding in the place of God, its elders in the place of the apostles' council, with its deacons; a proper thanksgiving was the bishop's, or one he entrusted. He greeted households by name, the house of Tavias, the wife of Epitropus with all her house and children, and asked Polycarp to have churches send messengers or letters to Antioch. (anf01 4703, 4793–4794, 6221, 7914, 8467, 5851–5856, 8274–8289, 8503–8508, 8854–8869.) Documented; "bishop of Antioch", Widely Accepted (S5); his own "the bishop of Syria" (anf01 6962), Documented; how widely churches followed his pattern, Contested.
4. **From Smyrna to Philippi: Polycarp and the elders with him.** Polycarp's letter opens in his name "and the presbyters with him" (paraphrase). Elders were to visit the sick and not neglect the widow, the orphan or the poor. He grieved for Valens, once an elder at Philippi, and his wife, warned against love of money, and asked the Philippians to call them back. As they had asked, he sent them the letters of Ignatius his church held, and offered to send their own letter on to Syria. (anf01 3232–3235, 3403–3405, 3531–3562, 3608–3618.) Documented. (Do not retell Ignatius's journey.)
5. **At Rome: Hermas's two copies.** In a vision, Hermas writes, the Church as an old woman told him to make two copies of her words: one for a man named Clement, who would send it to cities abroad, one for a woman named Grapte, who would teach the widows and orphans, while he read it in Rome with the elders who preside over the church. Elsewhere in his book, bishops take God's servants into their houses. (anf02 1005–1026, 4946–4952.) Stays in Hermas's voice. Documented.
6. **At Rome, under Antoninus Pius: Justin's Sunday.** Justin wrote to the emperor what Christians did each week. Quote 2. Then bread, wine and water were brought; the one who presides gave thanks as he was able; the people said Amen; deacons gave to each and carried a share to those absent. Those who could gave what they chose, and the one who presides cared for orphans and widows, the sick, prisoners and strangers. They met on the Sun's day, he wrote, because on it God made the world and Christ rose from the dead. (1apol-grc 1743–1744, 1755–1785.) Explain memoirs, the Sun's day. Documented.
7. **Corinth, when Soter was bishop of Rome: Dionysius.** Dionysius, bishop of Corinth, praised the Romans for their custom "from the beginning" (paraphrase) of sending gifts to churches in every city and to Christians in the mines. And he wrote that on that Lord's day they had read Rome's letter, as they read the earlier one, "written to us through Clement" (paraphrase). These words survive only as Eusebius quotes them: say so once, here, inside the Dionysius sentence. That the letter "through Clement" is Rome's letter of Beat 2 rests on Eusebius (npnf201 25109–25110, "Clement's epistle to the Corinthians"; 16679–16680, written "in the name of the church of Rome to the church of Corinth, when a sedition had arisen") and Irenaeus (anf01 39832–39835): Documented that they say so. Rome's new letter, which the story cannot explain, is left out. (npnf201 25082–25117.) Explain the mines. Documented.

## What came of it

1. Their letters were kept and read aloud in the churches: Corinth read Rome's on the Lord's day (npnf201 25114–25117); Eusebius, in the early 300s, says Clement's letter was "publicly used in a great many churches both in former times and in our own" (npnf201 16683–16684); Polycarp gathered and sent on Ignatius's (anf01 3613–3616). Documented. **Defended:** the order received from the apostles (anf01 1849–1860, 1916–1935).
2. Rome's church went on, and is the census's direct line, continued as the Roman Church in the Third Century (Widely Accepted). Irenaeus counted its leaders one after another from the apostles, by whose succession, he wrote, the tradition from the apostles and the preaching of the truth "have come down to us" (anf01 39823–39853), and, writing to its bishop, recalled their custom of sending the thanksgiving to those from other churches who kept a different Easter (npnf201 31549–31553). Told in the Ending.
3. Two of their books became reading for newcomers: in 367 Athanasius set "the Teaching of the Apostles, and the Shepherd" among books "appointed by the Fathers to be read by those who newly join us" (npnf204 68817–68823). Card.
4. The manual was lost and found again: in 1873, in a copy made in 1056, published in 1883 (anf07 12524, 12585). Modern event. Card.
Keepers today: none sourced; left out.

## Connection to carry into the story

What passed on was the practice of being one church across many cities: a thanksgiving carried to the absent and sent to those from other churches who kept another custom (1apol-grc 1772–1773; npnf201 31549–31553), gifts sent to churches everywhere (npnf201 25099–25107), and letters carried, kept and read aloud on the Lord's day (npnf201 25114–25117; anf01 3608–3618). Rome is where this is seen continuing: Dionysius thanks Rome for a custom kept "from the beginning", and Irenaeus, writing to Rome's bishop near the end of the century, points him to what the elders before him did. The writer must make clear that this is the same Roman church going on (the census's continued-as line), and that the old texts name its earlier leaders both as elders who presided and as holders of the bishop's office; the story does not say when or how one bishop came to rule.

## Ending

1. **(Recommended) Irenaeus to Rome's bishop.** As told (Cut first 25, review fix pass): "Near the end of the century, the churches of Asia kept Easter on the Passover date, the moon's fourteenth day; others kept it only on Sunday. Rome's bishop tried to cut them off from fellowship. Irenaeus of Lyon reminded him of the elders who had led Rome's own church before Soter, an earlier bishop. These same men, Irenaeus wrote elsewhere, stood in Rome's line of bishops, which carried the apostles' teaching to his day. Those elders did not keep Asia's day, yet lived at peace with visitors who did, and sent them a share of the thanksgiving." (grade-2 fix pass: npnf201 31530–31534, 31549–31553; "a share", from J3, J4) Sources: what each side kept (npnf201 31136–31140, 31217–31222, 31262–31265); cut off (npnf201 31415–31419); the elders before Soter and the thanksgiving sent (npnf201 31527–31533, 31550–31553); the line of bishops, and the tradition and preaching that "have come down to us" by it (anf01 39823–39853). What these churches handed on, so far as a held source says: the apostles' teaching, by Irenaeus's own claim, and the shared thanksgiving to churches of another custom. The outcome of the break is not held: Eusebius records only that bishops rebuked Victor and that Irenaeus wrote for peace (npnf201 31431–31434, 31659–31666). Paraphrase, not a fourth quote; no credit here (Who tells it); closes on the elders sending the thanksgiving to those of another custom.
2. **The Lord's day at Corinth.** End on Beat 7 itself: the Corinthians reading Rome's letter on the Lord's day, and the older one with it (npnf201 25114–25117). Then the Irenaeus ending moves to the card, and Beat 7 closes the story.

- Dionysius and Irenaeus: old, own words, written inside the period, but held only as Eusebius quotes them about 150 years later. The story says so once, inside the Dionysius sentence (Beat 7); the Ending carries no credit of its own.
