Under the emperor Trajan, Pliny, the Roman governor of Bithynia on the Black Sea, questioned people who said they had once been Christians and had given it up. An informer had named them. Part of their guilt, or their error, they told him, was this: "On a fixed day they used to meet before dawn. They sang a hymn to Christ as to a god, taking turns among themselves." To find the truth, Pliny, a hostile witness, had two slave women tortured. They were called, he wrote, ministrae, servers.

In the hundred years after the apostles, Christians in Antioch, in the cities of Asia Minor and in Rome met together, and their letters greet one another household by household. They taught newcomers a way of life before baptism, gave thanks over bread and a cup on the Lord's day, and were led by elders, bishops and deacons. Letters and messengers carried between their churches held them together.

A short manual, whose writer, date and place no one knows, opens on the Two Ways. "There are two ways, one of life and one of death, and there is a great difference between the two ways. The way of life is this. First, you shall love God, who made you. Second, your neighbour as yourself." Newcomers learned this way of life before being baptized in the name of the Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Only the baptized could share the thanksgiving, the eucharist in Greek.

Near the end of the first century, the church at Corinth, in Greece, removed some of its elders, the senior men who led the church together. At Corinth's request, the church of Rome wrote. Its letter, the only side that survives, blames one or two people and says the men had served blamelessly. God sent Christ, it argues, and Christ sent the apostles. They appointed bishops and deacons, knowing there would be strife over the office, and ordered that approved men succeed them. Men appointed with the whole church's consent, who served blamelessly, could not justly be removed.

Ignatius, bishop of Antioch, was brought from Syria as a prisoner, he wrote, for the name of Christ and the hope they all shared. On the road he wrote to the churches of Asia, a Roman province in today's western Turkey. Each church, he urged, should act as one around its bishop, with its elders and deacons. He greeted households, and asked Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna, to have churches send messengers or letters to Antioch.

Justin wrote to the emperor Antoninus Pius what Christians did each week: "On the day named after the Sun, all who live in the towns or in the country gather in one place. The memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets are read, for as long as time allows." These memoirs are called Gospels. Then the one who presides spoke, and all stood to pray. Bread, wine and water were brought, he gave thanks as he was able, and the people said Amen. Deacons gave to each and carried a share to those absent. What was given went to orphans and widows, the sick, prisoners and strangers. They met on the Sun's day because on it God made the world and Christ rose from the dead.

Later, Dionysius, bishop of Corinth, wrote, in words the church historian Eusebius keeps, that his church still read aloud on the Lord's day the letter Rome had sent in the quarrel, written through Clement.

Near the end of the century, the churches of Asia kept Easter on the Passover date, the moon's fourteenth day; others kept it only on the Lord's day. Rome's bishop tried to cut them off from fellowship. Irenaeus reminded him of the elders who had led Rome's own church before Soter, an earlier bishop. Elsewhere he counts these men among its bishops, and says the apostles' teaching came down to his day through their line. They kept a different day, yet lived at peace with visitors who kept Asia's day, and sent them the thanksgiving.
