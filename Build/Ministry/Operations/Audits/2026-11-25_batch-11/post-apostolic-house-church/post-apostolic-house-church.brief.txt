Under the emperor Trajan, Pliny, the Roman governor of Bithynia, a province on the Black Sea, questioned people who said they had once been Christians and had given it up. This, they told him, was the whole of their guilt, or their error: "On a fixed day they used to meet before dawn. They sang a hymn to Christ as to a god, taking turns among themselves." To find the truth, Pliny, a hostile witness, had two slave women tortured. They were called ministrae, he wrote, which means servers.

In the hundred years after the apostles, Christians in Antioch, in the cities of Asia Minor and in Rome met in small gatherings, and their letters greet one another household by household. They taught newcomers a way of life before baptism, gave thanks over bread and a cup on the Lord's day, and were led by elders, bishops and deacons. Letters and messengers carried between their churches held them together.

One community kept a short manual, whose writer, date and place no one knows. It opens on what it calls the Two Ways: "There are two ways, one of life and one of death, and there is a great difference between the two ways. The way of life is this. First, you shall love God, who made you. Second, your neighbour as yourself." Only when newcomers had been taught all of this way of life were they baptized, in the name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit. Then they could share the thanksgiving, the bread and cup that Christians called by the Greek word for thanksgiving, the eucharist. The manual also tells its readers to choose bishops and deacons for themselves.

Near the end of the first century, some at Corinth, in Greece, removed their elders, the senior men who led a church together, from office. Corinth had asked Rome for help, and the quarrel had spread, so the church of Rome wrote to them. Its letter, the only side that survives, blames one or two people and says the men had served blamelessly. God sent Christ, it argues, and Christ sent the apostles, who appointed bishops and deacons. The apostles knew there would be strife over the office, so they ordered that approved men should succeed them. Men appointed with the whole church's consent, who had served blamelessly, could not justly be removed.

Ignatius, bishop of Antioch, was brought from Syria as a prisoner, he wrote, for the name and the hope they all shared. On the road he wrote ahead to the churches of the Roman province of Asia, in today's western Turkey. Each church, he urged, should act as one around its bishop, with its elders and deacons. He greeted households, and asked Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna, to have the churches send messengers or letters to Antioch.

Under the emperor Antoninus Pius, Justin wrote to the emperor what Christians at Rome did each week: "On the day named after the Sun, all who live in the towns or in the country gather in one place. The memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets are read, for as long as time allows." The memoirs, he explains, are called Gospels. Then the one who presides spoke, urging them to imitate these good things, and all stood to pray. Bread, wine and water were brought, the one who presides gave thanks as he was able, and the people said Amen. Deacons gave to each one present and carried a share to those who were absent. What was given went to orphans and widows, the sick, prisoners and strangers. They met on the Sun's day, he wrote, because on it God made the world and Christ rose from the dead.

Later, Dionysius, bishop of Corinth, wrote that on the Lord's day his church had read Rome's new letter aloud, and that it kept reading the old one, written through Clement. Eusebius, the church historian, keeps his words.

Near the end of the century, Rome's bishop tried to cut off the churches of Asia over the date of Easter. Irenaeus of Lyon, in a letter Eusebius also quotes, reminded him of the elders who had presided over that same church before Soter, an earlier bishop, men Irenaeus elsewhere counts as its bishops in a line from the apostles. They kept a different day, yet lived at peace with visitors who kept Asia's, and sent them the thanksgiving.
