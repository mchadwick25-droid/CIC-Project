## alexandria-catechetical

Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition

Around 200, the threat of persecution under the emperor Severus had driven every teacher of the Christian faith out of Alexandria, in Egypt. With no one else left to teach, pagans came to Origen, then in his eighteenth year, to hear the word of God. Nearly all we know of these classrooms comes from Eusebius of Caesarea, writing about a century later from letters and from people who had known Origen, and he tells of the pupils who were put to death. One was a catechumen, or learner not yet baptized, and Eusebius borrows Origen's own phrase for her death: "Among the women, too, Herais was still a catechumen. She received baptism, as he himself says somewhere, the baptism by fire, and left this life."

From about 180 to the middle of the next century, the Christians of Alexandria learned their faith from teachers, as the city's pagans learned grammar and philosophy, in what Eusebius calls a school. Teachers took pagans and catechumens through Scripture, read for a deeper sense beneath the plain story.

Origen, still a boy, had been one of the pupils of Clement of Alexandria, who taught there in those years. Clement wrote that the Word, his name for Jesus, first urges people on, then trains them, and last of all teaches them. A sick body needs a doctor before it can learn anything, he explained. In the same way, a soul must first be healed of its passions, then trained, and only then taught, once it can take in the Word. So a learner was led step by step toward salvation. He wrote, too: "Faith is taught without letters," that is, to those who cannot read. "Its handbook, unpolished and divine at once, is called love, a spiritual book."

In Origen's classroom the crowds came one after another from morning to evening and scarcely left him time to breathe, or to study Scripture himself, so he divided them. Heraclas, one of his first pupils, took the beginners, and Origen kept the advanced. Scripture, Origen wrote before he left the city, has a body, a soul and a spirit, as a person does. The plain story feeds the simple, its soul feeds those who have gone further, and its spirit those made perfect.

When Origen left for Caesarea, about 231, Heraclas took over the instruction and then became the city's bishop. Dionysius, another of Origen's pupils, followed him in both.

Nepos, a bishop in Egypt, had written a book called Refutation of Allegorists, against those who read Scripture for hidden meanings. As his opponents describe it, he taught that Christ would reign on this earth for a thousand years of bodily luxury. In the district of Arsinoe, in the Egyptian countryside, his teaching had held for a long time, and whole churches had broken away and fallen away. Dionysius loved Nepos for his faith and his work on Scripture, but he would answer the book. Some teachers who relied on it, he wrote, led simpler believers to hope for small and mortal things in God's kingdom, and left them no lofty thought of the resurrection and of being made like Christ.

In his own account, the only one left to us, Dionysius called together the presbyters, or elders, and the teachers of the villages, and asked for a public examination of the question. They brought him the book like a weapon and a fortress no one could take, and he sat with them for three days, from morning to evening. "If the argument won," he wrote, "we were not ashamed to change our minds and agree."

At the end, Coracion, the leader of this teaching, said before all the believers who were there that he was convinced, and would no longer hold or teach it.

## antiochene-church-third-century

The Church of Antioch in the Third Century

The bishops who judged Paul of Samosata, bishop of Antioch, wrote that he had changed the psalms, the sacred songs, sung in his own church. "He stopped the psalms to our Lord Jesus Christ, as if they were too new, the work of newer men. But on the great day of Easter he trains women to sing psalms to himself, in the middle of the church. Anyone who heard them would shudder."

In the later third century the Christians of Antioch, in Roman Syria, were one church under a bishop, with presbyters, its priests, and deacons, its helpers, bound by letters to churches across the empire. When their own bishop was charged with refusing to confess that the Son of God came down from heaven, the bishops of the East met again and again over him, until at last they put him out.

Paul became bishop around 260. Almost all we know of him comes from the letter his judges sent out, which Eusebius of Caesarea copied into his history of the church. They wrote that Paul said Jesus Christ was from below, and Eusebius says he held that Christ was by nature an ordinary man.

Bishops hurried to Antioch from every side, Eusebius says, as against a man ravaging Christ's flock, and they met there often while Paul's side hid what he taught. Firmilian, bishop of Caesarea in Cappadocia, came twice and condemned his new teaching, but when Paul promised to change, Firmilian believed him and put the matter off. On his third journey he died at Tarsus, while the bishops waited for him.

The bishops also wrote that Paul built himself a high throne and a curtained private room, like a magistrate. He scolded those who would not cheer and wave handkerchiefs as in the theater, though they listened with reverence, as in the house of God. A bishop, they held, must be an example to the people.

At last Malchion, a presbyter who ran the city's school of Greek learning, questioned Paul while shorthand writers took the debate down, and Eusebius says he alone could catch out a man who hid his mind. A final meeting of many bishops cut Paul off from the whole church, because, they wrote, he set himself against God and refused to obey. No church was to send him letters of communion, the letters bishops exchanged to show they shared one church.

Paul would not leave the church's house. The emperor Aurelian was petitioned, and he ordered that the house go to the side the bishops of Italy and Rome wrote to. So those bishops' letters decided it, and Paul was driven out of the church by the worldly power.

In the persecution under the emperor Maximinus, Lucian, a presbyter of the same church, defended his faith before the emperor at Nicomedia and was imprisoned and killed. Two generations later, John Chrysostom told in a sermon how he answered under torture. "To each question he answered only, I am a Christian. When the torturer asked, What is your homeland? I am a Christian, he said. What is your trade? I am a Christian. Who are your parents? To all of it he said, I am a Christian."

Later, in Alexandria, a presbyter named Arius said that the Son of God had a beginning, and God had none. His bishop, Arius wrote, preached that the Son always was, and drove him out of the city because he would not agree. That bishop set Lucian in a line from Paul to Arius: Lucian, he wrote, kept out of communion with the bishops for many years, and Arius and his party were shoots of the same root.

A church historian of the next century, on Arius's side, says that some of Lucian's pupils became bishops. Arius had sent that letter to one of them, a different Eusebius, bishop of Nicomedia, and ended the letter with their own name for the pupils of Lucian. "I pray you fare well in the Lord, remembering our troubles, fellow-Lucianist."

## roman-church-third-century

The Roman Church in the Third Century

Natalius was a confessor, one who had stood trial for the faith and not given way. Yet he let a group that taught that Christ was only a man make him their paid bishop. Then he put on sackcloth and ashes and threw himself down before Zephyrinus, bishop of Rome, rolling at the feet of clergy and people. He was barely taken back into communion, the church's shared bread and cup.

In the first half of the 200s, the Christians of Rome were a large, mixed church. It held slaves and women of rank, and fed more than fifteen hundred widows and poor, under a bishop the people chose. They argued in public over how to speak of Father and Son, whether the church could take back grave sinners, and whom a Christian woman of rank might marry, until a stricter party broke away in 251. Almost all that is told of its first decades comes from Hippolytus, a writer of that time who withstood two of Rome's bishops and lost.

Zephyrinus's right hand was Callistus, who had been a slave and, Hippolytus says, was sent to the mines for disturbing a synagogue. When Zephyrinus died, Callistus became bishop and put Sabellius, who taught that Father and Son were one and the same, out of the church's fellowship. Hippolytus says Callistus did it because he feared Hippolytus, and to clear himself of the charge that he shared Sabellius's view. In his own words, as his enemy reports them: "I will not profess belief in two Gods, Father and Son, but in one." Hippolytus spoke of one God but two persons, and Callistus, he says, called that two Gods.

Hippolytus says Callistus let people off for sins of the body, saying that he forgave them all. People troubled in conscience came to his church, along with people other groups had refused, and some whom Hippolytus's side had thrown out. Callistus pointed, his enemy says, to the weeds in Christ's parable, left to grow with the wheat.

He also let a woman of rank, who would lose that rank by a lawful marriage, take a companion, slave or free, and count him her husband. Hippolytus charged that such women then took drugs to avoid or end a pregnancy, not wanting a child by a slave, and he called the rule a teaching of adultery and murder.

Some years after Callistus's time, as a historian of the next century tells it, all the brothers and sisters met to choose a bishop by vote, and set Fabian on the bishop's seat. In 250 the emperor Decius forced Christians to sacrifice to the gods. The same historian traces it to Decius's hatred of Philip, the emperor before him, who was reported to be a Christian. Fabian died a martyr, and Rome went without a bishop. Some gave way from terror or the fear of men, and became known as the fallen. Rome's clergy wrote of them to Carthage's clergy: "They are separated from us, but we have not abandoned them. We urged them, and urge them still, to repent, if by some means they can receive pardon from the One who is able to grant it. If we abandoned them, they would grow worse."

Cornelius was made bishop in 251, and counted his church: forty-six elders, seven deacons, and more than a hundred lesser clergy. Novatian, an elder of Rome, held that the fallen had no more hope, and with a stricter party and confessors at his side he was made a second bishop, set up against the first. That year sixty bishops met at Rome, declared Novatian's party outsiders, and resolved to heal the fallen through repentance.

That year, too, the confessors who had sided with Novatian came back. Cornelius wrote of how the people met them: "A great crowd of the brothers and sisters came running together. All of them had one voice, giving thanks to God. They showed the joy of their hearts in tears. They embraced them as if they had been set free from prison that very day."

## apocryphal-and-pseudepigraphal-literature

Apocryphal and Pseudepigraphal Literature in Christian Transmission

About the year 200, on a visit to Rhossus, a town on the coast of Syria near Antioch, Bishop Serapion of Antioch found the church quarreling over a gospel that carried Peter's name. He had not read it, and he thought them all sound in faith. So he gave a short answer: "If this is the only thing that seems to be causing bad feeling among you, let it be read."

In the second to fourth centuries, Christians wrote and read gospels about Jesus and tales of his first followers, the apostles, under the apostles' own names. These books never entered the New Testament: they are the Christian Apocrypha. Congregations read them aloud and argued over them, and bishops and teachers had to judge them against what had been handed down, received in the churches from the apostles.

After he left Rhossus, Serapion was told that the book's backers had minds caught up in a teaching he held false. He borrowed the book from the group that had used it from the first, and he read it through. Most of it, he found, agreed with the Savior's true teaching, but some things had been added, and he set those out for them. What he wrote to them, which Eusebius of Caesarea kept in his Church History a century later, lays down his rule: "Brothers, we receive Peter and the other apostles as we receive Christ. But the writings falsely put under their names we refuse, as people with experience. We know that such things were not handed down to us."

In the same years, a book of Paul's travels and deeds told of a girl named Thecla. In its later copies she hears Paul at Iconium, refuses the man she was promised to, and is condemned to the fire for it. The book says a storm of rain and hail puts the fire out, and she is saved. Later a governor sends her to the beasts for shaming a powerful man who had seized her in the street. In the arena she throws herself into a pit of water and baptizes herself in the name of Jesus Christ, on what she calls her last day. Afterward Paul sends her to teach the word of God.

Some Christians cited her example as permission for women to teach and baptize. Tertullian, writing in Carthage in Roman Africa, answered them that Paul did not even let a woman learn too boldly. He also told of the book's writer, a presbyter, an elder of the church: "Let them know that the presbyter in Asia who put that writing together, as if adding to Paul's fame from his own store, was found out. He confessed that he had done it for love of Paul, and he left his office."

Other books told what the Gospels leave untold. One under James's name, kept in many later copies, tells of Mary's childhood. She is born to a childless couple, and from the age of three she lives in the temple, fed, it says, by an angel's hand, until she is betrothed to Joseph.

Early in the fourth century, Eusebius, writing his Church History, set such books in order. The Acts of Paul, Paul's travels and deeds, he counted among books known to most church writers but not genuine. He set lower still the gospel of Peter and other books like it. In his judgment they carried the apostles' names but had not been handed down, and they should be cast aside entirely as absurd and godless.

Late in the fourth century, a pilgrim named Egeria came to Seleucia, on the south coast of today's Turkey. In one form of Thecla's book, this was where she taught many and fell asleep in death. On a low hill outside the city stood her church, with the small houses of men and women around it. There, Egeria writes, prayer was made at the memorial, and then the whole of the Acts of Thecla was read.

## ebionite-nazoraean-current

Ebionite / Nazoraean Current

In the middle of the second century, a Jew named Trypho put a question to the Christian teacher Justin. Suppose a man believes that Jesus is the Christ, the Messiah God had promised, and obeys him, but still wants to keep the Law of Moses: will he be saved? In my view, yes, Justin answered, if he does not press the Law on non-Jewish believers. Trypho asked whether some said no. Justin wrote: "There are some, Trypho, I answered, who do not dare to share even company or a home with such people. I do not agree with them."

Church writers wrote of such people from Justin's day to about 400, and the later ones place them in Syria and beyond the Jordan. They were Jews who believed that Jesus was the Christ and went on keeping the Law God gave through Moses: circumcision, the Sabbath, the food laws. Their own books are lost, so we meet them only through other church writers, nearly all of whom argued against them, and those writers do not agree on what they believed about Jesus.

About 180, Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon in Gaul, counted them among the heresies, the teachings he held false, and called them Ebionites. They were circumcised and lived so much as Jews that they revered Jerusalem "as if it were the house of God." They rejected Paul as "an apostate from the Law," one who had abandoned it. On Jesus, Irenaeus writes, they held that he was the son of Joseph.

Origen, the teacher from Alexandria, said in his last years that the name came from the poverty of their Law. Ebion, he says, is Hebrew for "poor," and Jews who received Jesus as the Christ were called Ebionites. One of them, Symmachus, put the Hebrew Scriptures into Greek. Origen knew two kinds of Ebionites: some confessed with the church that Jesus was born of a virgin, while others denied it and said he was born like other men.

About 375, Epiphanius, a bishop on Cyprus, wrote of them as two heresies, side by side: the Nazoraeans and the Ebionites. The Nazoraeans, named, he says, after Nazareth, were Jews in everything. They read the Law and the prophets in Hebrew, and they believed in one God and in his child Jesus Christ. What they held about Christ himself, he says, he cannot tell: whether Christ was a mere man, or born of Mary by the Holy Spirit. In the synagogues, he says, the Jews cursed them three times a day, praying that God would curse the Nazoraeans, because they were Jews who preached that Jesus is the Christ.

In 392 the Latin scholar Jerome wrote that the Nazoraeans of Beroea, in Syria, had let him copy the gospel they used, written in Hebrew letters. Elsewhere he calls it the Gospel according to the Hebrews, and he says he put it into Greek and Latin. In it, after the resurrection, the Lord appears to James, his brother, who had sworn not to eat bread from the hour he drank the Lord's cup until he saw him risen. "He took bread and blessed it and broke it, and gave it to James the Just. He said to him, 'My brother, eat your bread, for the Son of Man has risen from among those who sleep.'" Risen, that is, from the dead.

In 404 Jerome wrote to Augustine that to this day, among the Jews in all the synagogues of the East, there was a sect commonly called the Nazoraeans. The Jewish teachers, the Pharisees as he called them, still condemned it. Of their faith in Jesus, and of them, he wrote: "They believe in Christ, the Son of God, born of the Virgin Mary. They say he is the one who suffered under Pontius Pilate and rose again. We too believe in him. But while they want to be both Jews and Christians, they are neither Jews nor Christians."

## greek-apologists-second-century

The Second-Century Greek Apologists

An Athenian Christian, Athenagoras, addressed his plea to the emperors Marcus Aurelius and his son Commodus, and hailed them, above all, as philosophers. Only Christians, he told them, were hunted for the name Christian alone, and slaughtered on the word of false accusers.

He was one of a number of Christians who wrote in Greek in the second century, from Athens and Rome to Sardis and Antioch. They put written defenses of their faith into the hands of emperors, and of pagans who asked about it. They are known as the apologists, from apology, a defense like a speech in court. Accused of being godless, of eating human flesh at their feasts and of incest, they answered that they worshipped the one God who made all things and prayed for the emperors. They asked to be judged by how they lived.

In the reign of Hadrian, Quadratus addressed a defense to the emperor. Eusebius, a church historian who still had the book in the early 300s, says Quadratus wrote because certain wicked men were trying to trouble the Christians.

Under Antoninus Pius, Justin, a Christian teacher of philosophy at Rome, wrote to the emperor and his sons. Christians were called atheists, he said, because they refused the cities' gods and would not sacrifice to them. He granted that they were atheists of such gods, and said they worshipped the most true God. Everywhere, he wrote, they took care to pay their taxes, as Christ had taught them, and though they worshipped God alone, they gladly served the emperors in all else. Under Marcus Aurelius, Justin was condemned and put to death for refusing to sacrifice to the gods and obey the emperor's command.

Melito, bishop of Sardis, in the Roman province of Asia, in today's western Turkey, wrote to Marcus Aurelius. In the passages of his petition that Eusebius copied, he wrote that the godly in Asia were driven about by new decrees, new written orders. Informers, people who laid charges against others and took their goods, used the decrees to rob the innocent night and day. "If this is done at your command, well and good. For a just ruler will never take unjust measures, and we gladly accept the honour of such a death." But if the decree was not the emperor's, he begged not to be left to the plundering of the crowd.

If the charges were true, Athenagoras said, let the emperors destroy the Christians root and branch, with their wives and children. If not, let them look into Christian lives, teaching and loyalty. On godlessness, the poets and philosophers, he said, had searched for God and spoken freely of one God, yet a law stood against Christians alone. On eating human flesh, no one claimed to have seen it. Then he turned to the emperors' own games, gladiator shows, fights to the death between armed men, or with wild beasts, staged for crowds. "But we hold that to watch a man being killed is close to killing him, so we have given up such shows. We do not even watch, so that we bring no guilt or stain on ourselves. How then could we commit murder?"

Theophilus, the bishop of Antioch in Syria, wrote to a pagan friend who had heard the rumors, and thought Christian books were new and their teaching nonsense. He counted back the years to show that the books of Moses, the first in the Bible, were older than the Greeks' own.

At Carthage, Tertullian took up the same task in Latin. Writing against teachers he held false, he said he followed earlier Christian writers, and named Justin first.

One defense, a letter to a man named Diognetus, has come down with no writer's name and no date. It says of the Christians: "They live in their own homelands, but as resident foreigners. They share in everything as citizens, and endure everything as strangers. Every foreign land is a homeland to them, and every homeland is foreign."

## latin-apologists

The Latin Apologists

On a court holiday at the grape harvest, three friends walked the shore at Ostia, near Rome, early one morning. On the way to the sea, Caecilius saw an image of the god Serapis and raised a hand to his mouth, kissing it toward the god. Octavius, a Christian, scolded their friend Minucius Felix for letting Caecilius bow to stones smeared with oil, and the three sat on the breakwater to argue it out.

Minucius, a pleader, or court lawyer, tells it in his dialogue the Octavius. He was one of the Latin Apologists, Christians of Rome and Roman Africa who, from around 200 to the reign of the emperor Diocletian, defended their faith in Latin to the educated who despised it. An apology was a defense speech for the accused, and theirs answered the charges out of the pagans' own books.

The old religion is ancient and useful, Caecilius holds, and the Christians are "A people that lurks and shuns the light, silent in public, chattering in corners." By common report, he says, newcomers kill an infant hidden in flour, and their feasts end in incest when a dog tied to the lamp puts out the light.

Octavius calls them fables no one looked into. He and others like him, Octavius says, had once believed them too, and as lawyers had defended the worst criminals but tortured Christians who confessed, to make them deny it. Christians, he says, keep one marriage, eat sober meals, and love one another as brothers, because they do not know how to hate. No temple holds God, who is unseen and everywhere, and the offering he wants is a good mind. Beaten with the philosophers' own weapons, Caecilius yields. Minucius wrote it after Octavius died.

At Carthage around 200, Tertullian met the same charges in his Apology to the governors who judged Christians. He said torture, used on others to make them confess, was turned around on Christians, to make them deny what they were. Christians prayed for the emperors, he wrote, and gave each month to bury the poor and keep orphans, a love that branded them in the eyes of people who hated one another. Jerome, a Christian scholar writing later, says the Roman clergy's envy and insults in time drove Tertullian to the New Prophecy. It held that the Holy Spirit now gave a stricter rule.

In Diocletian's reign Arnobius taught rhetoric, the art of public speaking, at Sicca in Africa. The story of how he became a Christian rests on Jerome alone, who wrote about eighty years later that dreams drove him toward belief, and the bishop would not take him in. "He could not win from the bishop the faith he had always attacked. So he laboured by lamplight over most brilliant books against his old religion. At last, as if with hostages for his devotion, he won the treaty." His seven books were his pledge, and he was let in. In them he owns his past: "Whenever I had seen a stone made slick and smeared with olive oil, I fawned on it as if a power were present in it. I spoke to it, and begged favours from a block that felt nothing."

Arnobius's pupil, Jerome says, was Lactantius, called to teach rhetoric at Nicomedia, a Greek city in Bithynia. He was teaching when the Christians' church there was torn down. Lactantius says the emperor's officers burned its Scriptures and leveled it, and next day an order laid every Christian, whatever his rank, open to torture. Tertullian's Apology, Lactantius granted, had pleaded the cause in full, but answering accusers was one thing and teaching the whole faith another, and he took on the second.

Later, asked why he quoted pagan books, Jerome named all four together. Their books, he said, were full of the pagans' own learning.

Of the writers known to him, Lactantius wrote, Minucius Felix had stood well among the pleaders, and his Octavius showed how fit a champion of truth he might have been, had he given himself wholly to it.

## marcion-marcionism

Marcion / Marcionism

In 250, in the stadium at Smyrna, a presbyter, or church elder, named Pionius stretched himself on a stake and let the soldier drive in the nails. He had refused to sacrifice to the gods, saying he feared the living God. Beside him they raised Metrodorus, a presbyter of the rival Marcionites. Both faced east, and the wood was heaped around them.

The Marcionites were the churches that followed Marcion after Rome's church cast him out, in the middle of the second century. For about three hundred years, from Rome to Persia and down to the villages of Syria, they gathered with their own presbyters, teachers and martyrs. Their Scripture was two books, a Gospel cut down from Luke's and ten letters of Paul. They held one belief above all: Jesus came from a good Father, above the God who made the world, and Jesus abolished that God's law and prophets, the Jewish Scriptures. Tertullian, a writer at Carthage, in North Africa, says Marcion was cast out for his restless curiosity, which spread to others. Irenaeus, a bishop writing within a generation, says Marcion cut from both books every passage that called the maker of the world the Father of Jesus. Irenaeus, like every witness this story draws on, was their enemy.

Late in the second century, a writer from the Roman province of Asia named Rhodon found Marcion's followers divided over how many first sources, or beginnings of all things, there are. Apelles, one of their teachers, held there was only one, one God, where Marcion held two. Rhodon, who argued with him, set down his words: "He said there was no need at all to examine a person's teaching, and that each should keep to what he believed. For those who trusted in the one who was crucified would be saved, he said, if only they were found doing good works."

About 207, Tertullian reported that their churches baptized only virgins, widows, the unmarried and the divorced, a rule he said condemned marriage. Their good God, he said, punished no one, and only cast the sinner away out of sight. So he pressed them on persecution. An accused Christian was told to sprinkle incense from a box to the gods, and whoever did it denied Christ and went free. He put it to his Marcionite reader: "Why, in times of persecution, do you not save your life at once by denying, when the incense box is held out? 'Never,' you say, 'never!'"

In the persecution that began under the emperor Diocletian, Asclepius was burned at Caesarea in Palestine, the historian Eusebius's own city. He was said to be, Eusebius writes, a bishop of the Marcionites, eager for religion as he understood it, but without true knowledge.

When the persecutions ended, the emperor Constantine issued an edict, a law addressed by name to the Marcionites and other heretics, those who, their enemies said, chose error. Their teaching, it said, made the healthy soul sick. It forbade their meetings, even in private houses, and gave their houses of prayer to the catholic Church.

Yet their meetings went on. At Jerusalem in the fourth century, explaining the creed's "one Holy Catholic Church" to those about to be baptized, Cyril named the Marcionites first among the heretics whose meetings were also called churches. He told them to ask in an unfamiliar city for the Catholic Church, the church over all the world.

In the fifth century Theodoret was bishop of Cyrrhus, in northern Syria. There John Chrysostom had once charged an earlier bishop to drive Marcion's teaching out of the district, offering the aid of the imperial edicts. Theodoret worked to bring them to his own faith, that Christ was, as he wrote, not only good but just. He was stoned many times by the heretics he fought. After twenty-five years as bishop, he wrote: "I led eight villages of Marcion's followers, and the country around them, into the way of truth."

## post-apostolic-house-church

Post-Apostolic Household-Church Christianity

Under the emperor Trajan, Pliny, a Roman governor ruling a province, questioned people who said they had once been Christians and had given it up. An informer had named them. Part of their guilt, or their error, they told him, was this: "On a fixed day they used to meet before dawn. They sang a hymn to Christ as to a god, taking turns among themselves." To find the truth, Pliny, a hostile witness, had two slave women tortured. They were called, he wrote, ministrae, servers.

In the hundred years after the apostles, Christians in Antioch, in the cities of Asia Minor and in Rome met together, and their letters greet one another household by household. They taught newcomers a way of life before baptism, gave thanks over bread and a cup on the Lord's day, and were led by elders, bishops and deacons. Letters and messengers carried between their churches held them together.

A short manual, whose writer, date and place no one knows, opens on the Two Ways. "There are two ways, one of life and one of death, and there is a great difference between the two ways. The way of life is this. First, you shall love God, who made you. Second, your neighbour as yourself." Newcomers learned this way of life before being baptized in the name of the Father, Son and Holy Spirit. Only the baptized could share the thanksgiving, the eucharist in Greek.

Near the end of the first century, the church at Corinth, in Greece, removed some of its elders, the senior men who led the church together. At Corinth's request, the church of Rome wrote. Its letter, the only side that survives, blames one or two people and says the men had served blamelessly. God sent Christ, it argues, and Christ sent the apostles. They appointed bishops and deacons, knowing there would be strife over the office, and ordered that approved men succeed them. Men appointed with the whole church's consent, who served blamelessly, could not justly be removed.

Ignatius, bishop of Antioch, was brought from Syria as a prisoner, he wrote, for the name of Christ and the hope they all shared. On the road he wrote to the churches of Asia, a Roman province in today's western Turkey. Each church, he urged, should act as one around its bishop, with its elders and deacons. He greeted households, and asked Polycarp, bishop of Smyrna, to have churches send messengers or letters to Antioch.

Justin, himself a Christian, wrote to the emperor Antoninus Pius what Christians did each week: "On the day named after the Sun, all who live in the towns or in the country gather in one place. The memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets are read, for as long as time allows." These memoirs are called Gospels. Then the one who presides spoke, and all stood to pray. Bread, wine and water were brought, he gave thanks as he was able, and the people said Amen. Deacons gave to each and carried a share to those absent. The well-off gave what they chose, for orphans, widows, the sick, prisoners and strangers. They met on the Sun's day because on it God made the world and Christ rose from the dead.

Later, Dionysius, bishop of Corinth, wrote, in words the church historian Eusebius keeps, that his church still drew counsel from the letter Rome had sent in the quarrel, written through Clement.

Near the end of the century, the churches of Asia kept Easter on the Passover date, the moon's fourteenth day; others kept it only on Sunday. Rome's bishop tried to cut them off from fellowship. Irenaeus of Lyon reminded him of the elders who had led Rome's own church before Soter, an earlier bishop. Elsewhere he counts these men among its bishops, and says the apostles' teaching came down to his day through their line. Those elders kept a different day, yet lived at peace with visitors who kept Asia's day, and sent them the thanksgiving.

## syriac-edessa-nisibis

Syriac Christianity (Edessa/Nisibis)

In November of the year 201, after a great rain in the night, the river rose against Edessa and broke through the city's western wall. The flood killed more than two thousand people, and it destroyed the church of the Christians. The Chronicle of Edessa, put together some 340 years later, says the city's scribes recorded the flood at the time.

Those Christians spoke Syriac, and with the churches of Nisibis they made up Syriac Christianity, in two frontier cities between the Roman and Persian empires, from the flood to the year 410. In the 300s they sang their teaching in hymns and counted among them men and women vowed to God for life. Edessa's later founding story pictures its people hearing the Diatessaron read: the one story Tatian wove from the four Gospels.

Across the border in Persia, a teacher named Aphrahat wrote letters for the church. Some men and women took a vow, before baptism, to live unmarried and holy for God. They were called the sons and daughters of the covenant. One of his letters asks them to keep watch at night, to sing psalms and pray, to give their goods to the poor, and not to live with the other sex, so that lust will not draw them back.

If a vowed man asks a daughter of the covenant to live with him and serve him, Aphrahat gives her the words to answer. "I am promised in marriage to a royal husband, and I serve him. If I leave his service and serve you, my bridegroom will be angry with me. He will write me a letter of divorce and send me away from his house." The husband she means is Christ.

In Nisibis, Ephrem taught in hymns, each with a refrain, a line that came back after every verse. In his hymn for a siege by a Persian army, the river threatens the city, and Ephrem sets that flood beside Noah's. After Noah's flood, God set his bow, the rainbow, over the earth as his promise. So the refrain runs: "Stretch out your bow against the flood, for see, it has lifted up its waves against our walls!"

In Persia, the Christians had grown in number, and this angered the Magi, the priests who guarded the Persian religion. Sozomen, a Greek church historian of the next century, says the king believed a charge that their bishop was a friend of the Roman emperor. The king taxed the Christians heavily, hoping that want would make them give up their faith. Then he ordered their priests killed and their churches pulled down. Aphrahat, writing in 344, said that in his own days many declared their faith and were killed.

In 363, to save its starving army inside Persia, Rome handed Nisibis to Persia, and Ephrem moved west to Edessa. In his prose writings Ephrem answered three rival teachers by name. Marcion taught a Stranger God, apart from the Maker of the world; Mani taught two first Roots, Light and Darkness; and Bardaisan said the world was made from five first beings. Of their followers Ephrem wrote: "Their works are like our works, just as their fast is like our fast, but their faith is not like our faith." He warned that whoever prayed with Mani's followers "prays with Satan."

Ephrem died at Edessa in 373, and the Syrians went on singing his hymns.

In Persia, a later king ordered the churches his fathers had torn down to be rebuilt. He let "the priests, the leaders and all the holy covenant" go about freely and without fear. In 410, at the king's order, forty bishops of the Persian church met in a synod, a meeting of bishops to set rules for the church. Among the forty was the bishop of Nisibis, Ephrem's old city, now inside Persia. The day they met, the bishops said, was for them like seeing the dead rise.

## latin-pastoral-congregational-christianity

Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity

In the 390s, at Hippo, in today's Algeria, on a feast day, the people wanted their old feast in the church, with eating and drinking, which they called the Gladness. Their priest Augustine read them Scripture against drunken feasts and pleaded with them. He wrote: "I did not move their tears with my own. But as these things were said, I confess, their weeping came first, and I could not hold back mine. When we had wept together, my sermon ended in the fullest hope that they would mend their ways." On the feast day itself, from the rival church, whose people he called heretics, came the sound of their feasting.

From the 250s to 430, the Latin-speaking Christians of Roman Africa lived as congregations around one bishop, at Carthage under Cyprian and later at Hippo under Augustine. Their bishop taught them, fed their poor, bought back their captives, judged their quarrels and decided with them whom to take back after sin. Persecution, plague and a rival church in the same towns pressed on them, and the record keeps asking what a bishop and his people owed each other in danger.

In 250 the emperor Decius began a persecution, and his order called for sacrifice to the gods. At Carthage, in today's Tunisia, the crowd kept shouting violently for Cyprian, and he withdrew, as he wrote, to keep his people in peace. From hiding he led his church by letter, and told his clergy: "I could not write back alone. From my first days as bishop I resolved to do nothing on my own private judgment, without your counsel and the people's consent."

Many had given way and sacrificed, and now these fallen Christians asked to come back. Cyprian held that no one should be barred from penance, a time of public repentance, or from the hope of peace. Peace was the church's word for taking someone back, reconciling them, to share the bread and cup. At Rome a stricter party held that sharing communion with the fallen stained the rest. They set up Novatian as a second bishop of Rome, against the one already chosen. When his envoys came to Carthage, Cyprian and the bishops with him kept them from communion at once, since Novatian had been made bishop against the Catholic Church.

A few years later plague struck. Pontius, Cyprian's deacon and admirer, says the bishop gathered the people and told them that caring only for their own was nothing great. The tasks were shared out, and the poor, who had no money to give, gave their work.

Then the emperors ordered all who did not keep the Roman religion to take part in the Roman rites. Cyprian was arrested, and all his people gathered in the street outside where he was held. He refused, and the sentence called him an enemy of the Roman gods and their rites, and the leader of men gathered in a wicked conspiracy. He was led out and beheaded on 14 September 258.

Soon after the great persecution of the early 300s, the people of Carthage chose a new bishop. A party said the bishop who had made him bishop had handed over the Scriptures to be burned, so they made a rival bishop and set up altar against altar. The rival side, the Donatists, held that one who was himself stained could not wash away sins, and they baptized again those who came over. A Catholic writer some sixty years later said the Donatists had split away, yet shared his church's true sacraments, such as baptism. So he did not call them heretics: to him, heretics taught falsely about God.

Later, as bishop of Hippo, Augustine judged the disputes that Christians brought him, because Paul had told believers not to go to law before unbelievers. Against the rival church he had first wanted argument only. Then, he wrote, his fellow bishops pointed to his own town. Fear of the emperors' laws of exile and fines had brought it over to the Catholics, and he came to back those laws.

In his last years the Vandals, enemies who came by sea, overran Africa. Of countless churches, only three still stood: Carthage, Hippo and Cirta. A fellow bishop wrote to ask whether bishops and clergy should leave their churches. Augustine answered: "But if the clergy are there, everyone is helped, as far as the Lord gives them strength. Some are baptized, others are reconciled. No one is cheated of communion in the Lord's body. All are comforted, built up and urged to ask God, who has the power to turn away all that is feared."
