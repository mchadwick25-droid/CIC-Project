# Source sheet: Syriac Christianity (census id `syriac-edessa-nisibis`)

Spoken name (census `informalName`): **Syriac Christianity**. Census dates 200–410 CE; places Edessa and Nisibis.

File paths are under `cic/texts/` unless given in full. Line numbers are the file's own lines. Witness kinds: **own words** (a member of the community writing at the time), **contemporary**, **later (gap)**, **late legend**. Confidence uses the project's five levels.

The built world (`cic-website/data/worlds/syriac-edessa-nisibis.json`, records in `records/syr/`) was read first. Its six gravities are: symbol-and-hymn teaching (raza/shrara, Primary), the covenant life (Primary), the one-story Gospel (Supporting), self-definition against named rivals (Supporting), endurance under persecution on the Persian side (Supporting), and unsettled authority (Tensional). Each claim below is traced to the old text itself, not to the records.

---

## 1. Setting

**S1. The flood at Edessa and the church it destroyed.** `chronicle-of-edessa_cowper.txt` 53. Later (compiled c. 540 from the city archives; event c. 201, gap about 340 years), but the entry itself says the city's own scribes wrote it at the time (line 59). Confidence: Documented (that the chronicle holds the entry); Widely Accepted (that it records a church building at Edessa about 201). In the chronicle, this entry (8) is its first mention of a church in the city; entries 1-7 (lines 39-51) name none there. That it is "the first sure sign of a church" anywhere is a modern weighing of all the evidence (Cowper's note 106, line 368, sets it beside Eusebius's Abgar story). Not for the story.
> "8. In the year 513, in the reign of Severus, and in the reign of Abgar the king, son of Maano the king, in the month Tishrin the latter (i. e., November), the fountain of water which proceeds from the great palace of Abgar the great king increased ... And Abgar the king stood on the great tower which was called that of the Persians, and saw the water by the light of torches, and he commanded, and they took away the gates and the eight cataracts (? flood-gates) of the western wall of the city where the river flowed out. But that very hour the waters broke down the western wall of the city, and entered the city, and overthrew the great and beautiful palace of our lord the king ... And they destroyed the temple of the church of the Christians. And there were killed by that occurrence more than two thousand men, upon many of whom as they slept in the night the waters came suddenly, and they were drowned, and the city was filled with the sound of lamentation."

Line 59: "Now Mar Yahab Bar Shemesh, and Kajuma Bar Magartat, the scribes of Edessa, recorded this event, and the command of Abgar the king: and Bar Din and Bulid, who are prefects over the archives of Edessa, received and deposited it within them as trusted of the city (i.e., archivists.)"

Date of the chronicle: its last dated entries fall in "the year 850" of the Greeks, under Justinian (lines 251, 253). Assemani's introduction (Cowper's version, line 32, modern editor, clarifies the text): "he brought down his history to the year 540". Year 513 of the Greeks (Seleucid era) is AD 201/202; November 513 is November 201. Confidence for "201": Widely Accepted.

**S2. Nisibis on the frontier.** Theodoret, *Church History* II.26, `npnf203_theodoret-jerome-gennadius-rufinus.xml` 11065-11067. Later (written c. 440s; gap about a century). Documented.
> "Nisibis, sometimes called Antiochia Mygdonia, lies on the confines of the realms of Persia and of Rome."

**S3. Nisibis handed to Persia (363).** Socrates, *Church History* III.22, `npnf202_socrates-sozomen-ecclesiastical-histories.xml` 11697. Later (c. 440; gap about 75 years). Documented.
> "and giving up also Nisibis, a city of Mesopotamia, he withdrew from their territories."
(The subject is the emperor Jovian making peace with Persia after Julian's death, lines 11680-11697.)
Why Rome gave it up, 11679-11684 (same witness kind and confidence): "Perceiving himself suddenly left in very difficult circumstances, in the midst of the Persian territory, where his army was in danger of perishing for want of necessaries, he agreed to terminate the war, even on terms by no means honorable to the glory of the Roman name, but rendered necessary by the exigencies of the crisis." Line 11699 gives Julian's death. In plain words: to save its starving army inside Persia. The year 363: Widely Accepted.

**S4. Ephrem moves to Edessa.** Barhadbeshabba, *Cause of the Foundation of the Schools*, `barhadbeshabba_cause-de-la-fondation-des-ecoles-po4-syr-fra_scher1908.txt` 20435-20438. Later (c. 600; gap over two centuries). Widely Accepted.
> "Le bienheureux Mar Ephrem, dont nous avons parlé plus haut, lorsque la ville de Nisibe fut livrée aux Perses, se retira à Édesse, où il passa le reste de sa vie‘; 1l y ouvrit une école et il eut de nombreux disciples *."

**S5. Ephrem's death at Edessa (373).** Chronicle of Edessa 30, `chronicle-of-edessa_cowper.txt` 103. Later (c. 540). Widely Accepted (684 of the Greeks = 373).
> "30. In the year 684, in the month Haziran (June), on the ninth in it, departed from the world Mar Ephraim "of his wisdoms" (= the wise)."
Palladius, *Lausiac History* 40, `palladius_lausiac-history_clarke1918.txt` 471 (later, c. 420): "Ephraim, the deacon of the Church of Edessa".

**S6. The language.** The tree's census entry calls these "Syriac-speaking Christians". Old witness: Sozomen III.16, `npnf202_socrates-sozomen-ecclesiastical-histories.xml` 32053-32054: Ephrem "became ... so proficient in the learning and language of the Syrians". Documented. (That Syriac is a form of Aramaic: the Tatian and Bardaisan stories on the tree already say so; it is a fact of the language, not a claim of this story.)

## 2. Events (in time order)

**E1. The one-story Gospel (Tatian's harmony).**
- Eusebius, *Church History* IV.29.6, `npnf201_eusebius-church-history-life-of-constantine.xml` 26424-26425, 26433, 26479 (text lines between McGiffert's notes). Later (c. 320s; gap about 150 years from Tatian). Documented.
> "But their original founder, Tatian, formed a certain combination and collection of the Gospels, I know not how, ... to which he gave the title Diatessaron, ... and which is still in the hands of some."
- The Doctrine of Addai, `addai_doctrine-of-addai.txt` 65. Late legend (Edessa's own founding story, written down c. 400 or later; set in the 30s). It shows how the later Edessan church pictured its worship. Contested as history; Documented as the legend's picture.
> "But a large multitude of people assembled day by day and came to the prayer of the service, and to the reading of the Old and New Testament, of the Diatessaron, 62 and they believed in the revival of the dead, and |35 they buried their dead in the hope of the resurrection. They also observed the festivals of the Church in their times, and every day they were constant in the vigils of the Church, and they likewise performed acts of charity to the sick and those who were whole, according to the instruction of Addai to them."
- The word in line 65 (Phillips's note 62, same file 219): "In the text of Cureton is Ditornon. The reading of the MS., he remarks, is not quite clear, and he is disposed to think that the word ought to be Diatessaron. The reading of the St. Petersburgh MS., as we see, confirms Dr. Cureton's supposition." So "Diatessaron" stands on the St Petersburg manuscript. Addai 65 is the only old line that has these churches reading it; no old line places it at Nisibis or across 200-410. The rest of note 62 ("seems to have been publicly read at Edessa up to the fourth century", 221) is the editor's and stays out.
- Phillips's note 62, same file 219 and 221 (modern editor, finds sources): the manuscript reading "Diatessaron"; later Syriac writers (Bar Salibi, Bar Hebraeus, via Assemani) say Ephrem wrote a commentary on it. The commentary itself is **not held** in the Library. Modern-only for this story; Leave out.
- Explain it: *Diatessaron* is Greek for "through four" (Eusebius gives the title, line 26433); in plain words, one continuous Gospel story woven out of the four. Tatian's own side: the tree's Tatian story (live) tells the weaving; Theodoret's later hostile side (he removed copies in the 400s) is also on that page. This story does not retell either.

**E2. The covenant: sons and daughters vowed to God.**
- Aphrahat, *Demonstration* VI "Of Monks", NPNF 13 (tr. Johnston, 1898), `npnf213_gregory-great-ephraim-syrus-aphrahat.xml`. Own words (written inside Persia, 337). Documented.
  - 25803-25808 (VI.1-2, how they resist the adversary): "If he wishes to come in against them by sleep, they are wakeful and vigilant and sing psalms and pray. If he allures them by possessions, they give them to the poor. ... If he inflames them with the desire of Eve, they dwell alone, and not with the daughters of Eve."
  - 25835-25846 (VI.4): "Therefore, my brethren, if any man who is a monk or a saint, who loves the solitary life, yet desires that a woman, bound by monastic vow like himself, should dwell with him, it would be better for him in that case to take (to wife) a woman openly and not be made wanton by lust. So also again the woman, if she be not separated from the solitary, it is better for her to marry openly. Woman then ought to dwell with woman, and man to dwell with man. ... to you, my beloved solitaries, who do not take wives, and to the virgins who do not marry, and to those who have loved holiness."
  - 25948-25961 (VI.7, the quote; see Q2): "O ye virgins who have betrothed yourselves to Christ, when one of the monks shall say to one of you, "I will live with thee and minister thou to me," thus shalt thou say unto him:—"To a royal husband am I betrothed, and Him do I serve; and if I leave His service and serve thee, my betrothed will be wroth with me, and will write me a letter of divorce, and will send me away from His house; ...""
  - 25962-25968 (VI.8): "Hearken then, my beloved, unto that which I write unto thee, namely, whatsoever things become solitaries, monks, virgins, saints. Before all things it beseems the man on whom the yoke is laid, that his faith should be firm; as I wrote to thee in the first epistle; that he should be zealous in fasting and prayer; that he should be fervent in the love of Christ; and should be humble and mild and wise."
  - 25929-25930 (VI.6): "The Only Begotten Who is from the bosom of His Father shall cause all the solitaries to rejoice."
- The same in Parisot's Latin (1894) of the Syriac, `aphrahat_demonstrationes-1-22-syr-lat_parisot1894.txt`. Parisot's Latin confirms the NPNF English; the Syriac in this file is unreadable OCR.
  - 18150-18151 (VI.8): "quae deccnt mo- nachos, foederis filios, caelibes ac san- ctos." ("monks, sons of the covenant, celibates and holy ones": the NPNF "monks" stands for the vowed members.)
  - 18126-18134 (VI.7): "sic ei responde : Viro Regi sum desponsata et illi servio."
  - 18075-18076 (VI.6): "Solitarios omnes Unigenitus a sinu Patris laetificabit."
  - Parisot's introduction 3291-3293 (modern editor, clarifies a word): the Syriac name "ad litteram sonat « filii foederis », eosque significat qui se Deo ad servandam continentiam pacto devoverunt" (it literally means "sons of the covenant", and names those who vowed themselves to God to keep celibacy).
- Aphrahat, *Demonstration* VII.20-21 and 25, tr. Hallock (1932), `aphrahat_demonstrations-2-7_hallock1932.txt` 57, 58, 62. Own words; a translation printed after 1931, so it is used for claims, never quoted. Confirmed by Parisot's Latin VII.21, line 21939-21942 ("Cum igitur universo foederi Dei praedicaverint ... ad aquas baptismi omnes ad praelium electos iubebunt accedere"). Documented.
  - 57: "it is fitting for the sounders of trumpets, the preachers of the Church, to warn all (who are in) the covenant of God~1~before baptism, and to those who choose for themselves virginity and holiness, young men and virgins".
  - 62: "in our age there are those who themselves choose to become solitaries, sons of a covenant, and religious."
- The covenant in 410: Synod of Isaac, Chabot, *Synodicon orientale*, `synodicon-orientale_recueil-de-synodes-nestoriens-syr-fra_chabot1902.txt` 16159-16166 (see E7). Contemporary (acts of 410). Documented. Chabot's note 16174-16180 (modern editor, clarifies a word): "bar qyama" and "bat qyama", "filius fœderis", "filia fœderis", name sometimes clergy, sometimes vowed religious or women bound by vows to the Church's service. The note is on the later synod acts; no line of Aphrahat says the vowed served in the church. On the 410 line itself the note reads *qyama* as "tout le clergé ¥ [y] compris les moines" (16179-16180), so the 410 covenant is not shown to be the same order Aphrahat wrote for: only the word is the same.
- Ephrem's own city voice (Nisibene Hymn I.9, `npnf213...xml` 13810-13812, contemporary): "Instead of the wedded wives that were in it, lo! my virgins that are unmarried."
- Later witness that the order went on: John of Ephesus, *Lives of the Eastern Saints*, `john-ephesus_lives-eastern-saints-po17_brooks1923.txt` 14152 (sixth century): "and they were now becoming readers and daughters of the covenant". Card only.
- Explain it: the covenant (Syriac *qyama*) was a vow, taken before baptism, to live unmarried and holy for God (Aphrahat VI, VII; Hallock 57; Parisot 3291-3293). Its members were called sons and daughters of the covenant, and "solitaries" or "single ones" (*ihidaye*). Aphrahat's own reason: those "betrothed to Christ" are kept from "the curse of the Law" and the pains of childbirth (VI.6, lines 25932-25940); his reason for men and women living apart is to avoid lust (VI.4). That the word for "single one" is also the Syriac title of Christ the Only-Begotten rests on the Syriac itself, which the Library holds only in unreadable OCR, and on modern works (Griffith, in the world records). Card only.
- Where the record is thin: no word written by a daughter of the covenant survives in the Library; every line about them is a man's (built world, `syr.limit.f5-women-own-words`). Aphrahat's VI.7 gives a woman words to say; he wrote them.

**E3. Aphrahat dates his own letters.** `npnf213...xml` 28554-28561 (Dem. XXII.25). Own words. Documented.
> "These twenty-two discourses have I written according to the twenty-two letters of the alphabet. The first ten I wrote in the six hundred and forty-eighth year of the kingdom of Alexander the son of Philip the Macedonian, as is written in the end of them. And these twelve last I wrote in the six hundred and fifty-fifth year of the kingdom of the Greeks and of the Romans, which is the kingdom of Alexander, and in the thirty-fifth year of the Persian King."
Gwynn's note in the same line gives "337 and 344". Widely Accepted. Aphrahat as the "Persian sage" and his rank: the twenty-third Demonstration and the Synodal letter (XIV) are not in the NPNF selection; XIV is in Parisot's Latin (from line 33057). Whether he was a bishop is unknown (world record `syr.contested.aphrahat-episcopacy`, Contested). Leave out.

**E4. Teaching in hymns; the siege of Nisibis.**
- Ephrem, *Nisibene Hymn* I, tr. Stopford (1898), `npnf213...xml` 13725-13734 (stanza 1 and refrain), 13746-13751, 13781-13782, 13839-13840. Own words (composed in the besieged city). Documented.
  - 13725-13732: "O God of mercies Who didst refresh Noah, he too refreshed Thy mercies. He offered sacrifice and stayed the flood; he presented gifts and received the promise. With prayer and incense he propitiated Thee: with an oath and with the bow Thou wast gracious to him; so that if the flood should essay to hurt the earth, the bow should stretch itself over against it, to banish it away and hearten the earth."
  - 13733-13734 (refrain, Q1): "R. Stretch forth Thy bow against the flood, for lo! it has lifted up its waves against our walls!"
  - 13746-13751: "Lo! all the billows trouble me; and Thou hast given more favour to the ark: for waves alone encompassed it, mounds and weapons and waves encircle me. ... the flood bore it, the river threatens me."
  - 13781-13782: "But my lands have thrice been filled and emptied again; and now against me the waves rebel".
  - 13839-13840: "O Thou who didst rise on the third day, give us not over to death in our third peril!"
  - The heading of the collection, 13710-13715: "1. The Siege of Nisibis (I.–III.). 2. The Persian Invasion (IV.–XII.). 3. The Bishops of Nisibis (XIII.–XVI.)."
- Gwynn's introduction (modern editor, 1898), same file 11229-11231: the first twenty-one Nisibene Hymns belong "to the epoch of the third siege (a.d. 350)". The year rests on the editor; Ephrem himself gives only "our third peril". Dominant Modern Reconstruction for 350; told as "the third siege" only.
- Theodoret, *Church History* II.26, `npnf203...xml` 11124-11185 (text, between the editor's notes). Later (c. 440s; about a century after). Documented as Theodoret's account; the vision and the gnats are wonders in his voice.
  - 11124-11127: "One however I will record because of the subject before us. The city which Jacobus ruled was now in possession of the Romans, and besieged by the Persian Army. The blockade was prolonged for seventy days."
  - 11142-11148: "The river Mygdonius flowing through the middle of the town, at last the Persians dammed its stream a considerable distance up, and increased the height of its bank on both sides so as to shut the waters in. When they saw that a great mass of water was collected and already beginning to overflow the dam, they suddenly launched it like an engine against the wall. The impact was tremendous; the bulwarks could not sustain it, but gave way and fell down."
  - 11155-11160: "But he found the wall built up on both sides, and all his labour vain. For that holy man, through prayer, filled with valour both the troops and the rest of the townsfolk, and both built the walls, withstood the engines, and beat off the advancing foe. And all this he did without approaching the walls, but by beseeching the Lord of all within the church."
  - 11170-11177: "Therefore the excellent Ephraim (he is the best writer among the Syrians) besought the divine Jacobus to mount the wall to see the barbarians and to let fly at them the darts of his curse. So the divine man consented and climbed up into a tower but when he saw the innumerable host he discharged no other curse than to that mosquitoes and gnats might be sent forth upon them ... On his prayer followed clouds of mosquitoes and gnats".
- Where the sources disagree: Jacob alive or dead at the siege.
  - Ephrem, *Nisibene Hymn* XIII, `npnf213...xml` 14695-14704 and 14774-14787 (own words). The city had three bishops through "three dark seasons of siege", and the first was already buried inside it: 14774-14778 "The first priest was her vinedresser; he made her branches to grow even unto heaven.—Lo! being dead and buried within her, he has become fruit in the midst of her bosom:—when therefore the pruners came, the fruit that was in her midst preserved her." 14784-14787 "Be ye wise like Nisibis, O ye daughters of Nisibis,—for that she laid the body within her, and it became a wall without her."
  - Chronicle of Edessa 17, `chronicle-of-edessa_cowper.txt` 77 (later, c. 540): "17. In the year 649, died Mar Jacob, bishop of Nisibis." (649 = 337/338.)
  - Theodoret (above) has Jacob alive and praying during the siege.
  - Confidence: Contested. Tell both, credited, or leave to the card.
- Explain it: the *madrasha* (teaching hymn) had stanzas and a refrain; Ephrem's Hymn I shows the form, stanza then "R." (refrain) (13725-13734), and later hymns print the refrain after every stanza (e.g. 15126-15195). No held old line says who sang which part: Sozomen says only that Harmonius gave his verses "to the choirs" (32088) and that "the Syrians sang the odes of Ephraim" (32101-32103). One voice singing and the people answering rests on modern description (Gwynn 11249-11254, barred). The bow is the rainbow God set after Noah's flood as a promise (stanza 1, 13728-13731). The Persian side's reason for the siege is not held in the Library in any Persian voice; Theodoret says only that "war being waged against the Romans by Sapor King of Persia" (11058-11059). Tell the act, no reason added.

**E5. Hymns and prose against the rival teachers.**
- Sozomen, *Church History* III.16, `npnf202...xml` 32079-32103. Later (c. 440s; gap about 70 years after Ephrem). Documented as Sozomen's account. Already told on the live Ephrem page; here a clause only.
  - 32085-32089: "and Harmonius, his son. It is related that this latter was deeply versed in Grecian erudition, and was the first to subdue his native tongue to meters and musical laws; these verses he delivered to the choirs, and even now the Syrians frequently sing, not the precise copies by Harmonius, but the same melodies."
  - 32094-32103: "When Ephraim perceived that the Syrians were charmed with the elegance of the diction and the rhythm of the melody, he became apprehensive, lest they should imbibe the same opinions; and therefore, although he was ignorant of Grecian learning, he applied himself to the understanding of the metres of Harmonius, and composed similar poems in accordance with the doctrines of the Church ... From that period the Syrians sang the odes of Ephraim according to the law of the ode established by Harmonius."
- Ephrem, *Prose Refutations*, tr. Mitchell (1912), `ephraim_prose-refutations_mitchell1912-1921.txt`. Own words. Documented.
  - 584: "I DESIRE to utter one more refutation against the three of them (i.e., Marcion, Mani, and Bardaisan), that is against Marcion in the first place who (says) that a heaven is found also beneath the Stranger."
  - 640: "And, therefore, on these grounds we have opposed Mani also with a true refutation. For he, too, calls God the Earth of Light, which (Earth) is not perfect".
  - 431: "For they have put together two Roots with preposterous reasoning".
  - 1609 (Against Marcion I, vol. II): "But if Marcion still persists in cavilling, let him be asked again as to whether he believes the word of the Stranger or not."
  - 626-628 (on Bardaisan): "Bardaisan's revelation was not accredited by Signs nor is it Scriptural. ... But it was revealed to this Bardaisan who was unable to prevent the dew which dropped upon his bed!"
  - 1205 (Fifth Discourse to Hypatius, conclusion, lines 1203-1205; the quote, Q3): "For their works are like our works as their fast is like our fast, but their faith is not like our Faith. And, therefore, rather than being known by the fruit of their works they are distinguished by the fruit of their words. ... so he who prays with the Manichaeans prays with Satan, and he who prays with the Marcionites (?) prays with Legion, and he who (prays) with the followers of Bardaisan (?) (prays) with Beelzebub".
- Explain it, each side:
  - Marcion, as Ephrem tells it: a "Stranger" God apart from the Maker of the world (584, 1609; 1605: "was it because it did not [become] Him to compel, not only because He is good but also because He is not our Creator ?"). Marcion's own side: no line of his survives (the tree's Marcion story says so).
  - Mani, as Ephrem tells it: two Roots, Light and Darkness; God as the "Earth of Light" bordered by Darkness (431, 640). Mani's own followers' side: the live Ephrem page cites a Manichaean confession-prayer naming the two Roots; it belongs to the Manichaeism story.
  - Bardaisan, as Ephrem tells it: the world made from five "Entities" set in Space (606-608; 917: "And those things which Bardaisan makes (i.e., considers to be) five Entities, Mani makes (to be) from a single Essence."); the soul made of seven parts (427-428); a "revelation" with no signs and not in Scripture (626-628); Sozomen's version is that his son Harmonius put Greek ideas on soul and body into songs (32090-32094). Bardaisan's own side: the *Book of the Laws of Countries* (held, `anf08_...edessa-syriac.xml`), told on his own live page.
  - Why Ephrem answered: Sozomen gives his fear that "they should imbibe the same opinions" (32096-32097); Ephrem's own reason in the prose is that their faith differs though their fasting looks the same (1205).
- Confidence: Documented (Ephrem named and answered all three; Sozomen reports the hymn contest).

**E6. Persecution on the Persian side.**
- Sozomen II.9, `npnf202...xml` 28873-28877, 28885-28901. Later (c. 440s; a century after; Sozomen draws on Syriac martyr acts, editor's note 28878-28880). Documented as Sozomen's account.
  - 28873-28877: "When, in course of time, the Christians increased in number, and began to form churches, and appointed priests and deacons, the Magi, who as a priestly tribe had from the beginning in successive generations acted as the guardians of the Persian religion, became deeply incensed against them."
  - 28889-28899: "and charged him with being a friend of the Cæsar of the Romans, and with communicating the affairs of the Persians to him. Sapor believed these accusations, and at first, ground the Christians with excessive taxes, although he knew that the generality of them had voluntarily embraced poverty. He entrusted the exaction to cruel men, hoping that, by the want of necessaries, and the atrocity of the exactors, they might be compelled to abjure their religion; for this was his aim. Afterwards, however, he commanded that the priests and conductors of the worship of God should be slain with the sword. The churches were demolished, their vessels were deposited in the treasury".
  - 28885-28888: the charge against the bishop Symeon was brought, Sozomen says, by "The Jews, who through envy are in some way naturally opposed to the Christian religion". A hostile charge in Sozomen's voice; it belongs to Simeon's story (the continued-as entry). Leave out here, named.
- The king's reason, in the Syriac martyr acts (later, fifth century?; German tr. Braun 1915), `persian-martyr-acts_ausgewahlte-akten-persischer-martyrer-bkv22-deu_braun1915.txt` 1571-1575: "„Simon will seine Jünger und sein Volk gegen meine Majestät zur Empörung bringen und zu Knechten des Kaisers (xaiGao) machen, der ihr Glaubensgenosse ist. Deshalb gehorcht er meinem Befehle nicht."" (Simon wants to stir his disciples and people to revolt against my majesty and make them servants of the emperor, who shares their faith.) This is the persecutor's reason, as the Christian acts report it. Contested in detail; Widely Accepted that Persia suspected its Christians of siding with Christian Rome.
- Aphrahat, *Demonstration* XXI "Of Persecution", `npnf213...xml`. Own words (written 344 inside Persia). Documented.
  - 27416-27419 (XXI.1): "I have heard a reproach, which has greatly vexed me. The unclean (the heathen) say, that this people, which is gathered together out of all nations, has no God. And thus say the impious:—"If they have a God, why does He not avenge His people?""
  - 27420-27431: a Jewish sage's taunt that no Christian prayer had made "your persecutors ... cease from you".
  - 27988-27991 (XXI.23): "And also in our days these things happened to us also on account of our sins; but also that what is written might be fulfilled, even as our Redeemer said:—These things are to be. ... The Apostle also said:—Also over us is set this cloud of confession; ... which (is) our honour, wherein many confess and are slain."
- Sozomen II.14, `npnf202...xml` 29190-29196: "the number of men and women whose names have been ascertained, and who were martyred at this period, have been computed to be sixteen thousand; while the multitude outside of these is beyond enumeration, and on this account to reckon off their names appeared difficult to the Persians and Syrians and to the inhabitants of Edessa, who have devoted much care to this matter." Later. Documented as Sozomen's count.
- Explain it: the Magi were the priestly guardians of the Persian religion (Sozomen's own gloss, 28875-28877). "Confess" in Aphrahat means to declare one's faith before the persecutor, not to admit guilt (sense from the passage itself, 27954 "names of martyrs, of confessors").

**E7. The synod of 410 (hand-off).** Synod of Isaac, Chabot (French, 1902; the Syriac in the same file has many misread letters, per its header), `synodicon-orientale_recueil-de-synodes-nestoriens-syr-fra_chabot1902.txt`. Contemporary (the synod's own acts, kept in a ninth-century collection). Documented.
- 16159-16166 (the king's grant): "il avait, en effet, ordonné dans tout son empire que les temples détruits par ses pères fussent magnifiquement reconstruits de son temps; que Îles autels qui avaient été démolis fussent soigneusement res- laurés: que ceux qui avaient 616 éprouvés pour Dieu, qui avaient souffert les prisons et les tortures, sortissent en liberté: que les prêtres, les chefs et toute la sainte alliance®) circulassent en toute liberté et sans crainte."
- 16226-16229 (Marutha): "Mar Marouta®), évèque, qui fut le médiateur de la paix et de la concorde entre l'Orient et l'Occident".
- 16422 and 16499-16500 (the joy): "Et le mardi premier du mois de $ebat 6) fut pour nous (le jour de) la résur-" / "rection et nous vimes la reviviscence des morts; ce fut pour nous le salut; la paix s'accrut sur nous". (A printed page of notes falls between the two lines.)
- 16521-16530 (one bishop to a city; the feasts): "nous tous, évêques rassemblés de divers hieux, au nombre de quarante ... premièrement, au sujet des évèques : qu'au plustôt 115 ne seraient plus deux ou trois dans une seule ville, mais seulement un pour chaque ville et sa ju- ridiction; ... secondement, qu'ensemble, uniformément, nous ferons la fète sainte ... le jour olorieux de la Nativité et de l'Épiphanie".
- 17724-17726 and 17746 (Nisibis as a metropolitan see): "Ensuite vient le siège de Nisibe; lévèque qui loccupe est métropolitain d'Arzôn, de Qardou, de Beit Zabdai, de Beit Rahimai, de Beit Môksayé et des évêques qui sy trouvent." ... "Second méiropo- litain : Osée, év. de Nisibe."
- 17861: "3. Moi, OséE, cv. de Nisibe, J'adhère à tout ce qui est écrit ci-dessus."
- Explain it: a synod is a meeting of bishops that sets rules for the church (plain gloss).
- What one bishop to a city settled (contemporary, the acts of 410; Documented). 16525-16526: "qu'au plustôt 115 ne seraient plus deux ou trois dans une seule ville, mais seulement un pour chaque ville et sa ju- ridiction" (that from now on there would no longer be two or three in one city, but only one for each city and its district). 17740-17742, on Beit Lapat: "il n°v a point d'évêéque dont nous puissions inscrire le nom parmi les métropolitains ses collègues: parce que dans toute ville où il ¥ à deux ou trois evéques, le synode ne les accepte pas." In plain words: some cities had two or three bishops at once; the rule ended that. The acts say the bishops accepted the rule from the letter sent from the West (16519-16523). No held line says how the rival bishops arose (Papa's dispute is later chronicles only; Leave out).
- The bishop of Nisibis at the synod (contemporary, the acts of 410; Documented). 17856 heads the list "Ann. 410. Signatures"; 17861: "3. Moi, OséE, cv. de Nisibe, J'adhère à tout ce qui est écrit ci-dessus." (Third to sign, after Isaac and Marutha.) 17746 names him among the bishops then in office: "Second méiropo- litain : Osée, év. de Nisibe." With S3 (Nisibis Persian since 363), this is the held line that Ephrem's old city's church was in the synod of 410. Story: his presence only; his rank and name, card.

## 3. Impact, ranked heaviest first (what later witnesses carried on and used)

1. **Ephrem's hymns sung on in Syriac churches.** Sozomen III.16, 32101-32103 ("From that period the Syrians sang the odes of Ephraim"); Theodoret IV.26 (cited on the live Ephrem page). Defended: the church's teaching against rivals. Documented.
2. **Nisibis and its church passed into the Persian church**, where in 410 it stood second among the metropolitan sees (Chabot 17724-17746, 17861). The census line "continued as" the early Persian Church of the East (Documented). Defended: the 410 synod sets one bishop to a city and a common feast calendar.
3. **The word covenant went on**: named in the 410 synod (16166, where Chabot's note, 16179-16180, reads it as the whole clergy) and still in the sixth century (John of Ephesus, 14152). Documented.
4. **Edessa kept the record of Persia's martyrs** (Sozomen II.14, 29194-29196). Documented as Sozomen's statement.
5. **Ephrem's school at Edessa and, later, the school at Nisibis** (Barhadbeshabba 20435-20438; the census edge to the Church of the East under Persia). Belongs to the later school story and the live Ephrem page. Card only.
6. **The one-story Gospel's later fate** (removed in the 400s by Theodoret and a bishop of Edessa): told on the live Tatian page. Card only.

## 5. Where sources disagree or come late

- Jacob of Nisibis at the siege: dead and buried (Ephrem, Hymn XIII; Chronicle of Edessa 17, year 649) or alive and praying (Theodoret II.26). Contested.
- The year of the siege in Ephrem's hymns: Ephrem says "third peril"; 350 is Gwynn's. Do not date.
- The Abgar-and-Addai founding story: late legend (Doctrine of Addai; Eusebius I.13 accepted the letters). Leave out of the audio.
- That Ephrem founded the women's choirs: later tradition (Jacob of Serug, c. 500, per the world record `syr.story.choirs-tradition`; **not held** in the Library); Gwynn's introduction (11249-11254) repeats it from "his biographer". Do not say.
- The modern claim that the flood entry's church line may be a later addition: modern view only (world record `syr.story.edessa-flood-201`). Leave out.
- Aphrahat's rank (bishop or not): Contested. Leave out.
- Papa bar Aggai's contested primacy (c. 315): later chronicles only (Synod of 424 recalls it, Chabot from 18551). Leave out.
