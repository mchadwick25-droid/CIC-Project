In November of the year 201, after a great rain in the night, the river rose against Edessa and broke through the city's western wall. The flood killed more than two thousand people, and it destroyed the church of the Christians. The Chronicle of Edessa, put together some 340 years later, says the city's scribes recorded the flood at the time, and its line is the first sure sign of a church there.

Those Christians spoke Syriac, and with the churches of Nisibis they made up what is called Syriac Christianity, in two frontier cities between the Roman and Persian empires. From the flood to the year 410, they sang their teaching in hymns, heard the Gospel as one story that Tatian had woven out of the four, and counted among them men and women vowed to God for life.

Across the border in Persia, a teacher named Aphrahat wrote letters for the church. Some men and women took a vow, before baptism, to live unmarried and holy for God while serving in the church. They were called the sons and daughters of the covenant. One of his letters asks them to keep watch at night, to sing psalms and pray, to give their goods to the poor, and not to live with the other sex, so that lust will not draw them back.

If a vowed man asks a daughter of the covenant to live with him and serve him, Aphrahat gives her the words to answer. "I am promised in marriage to a royal husband, and I serve him. If I leave his service and serve you, my bridegroom will be angry with me. He will write me a letter of divorce and send me away from his house." The husband she means is Christ.

In Nisibis, Ephrem taught in hymns: a single voice sang the verses, and the people sang a line back. In his hymn for a siege by a Persian army, the river threatens the city, and Ephrem sets that flood beside Noah's. After Noah's flood, God set his bow, the rainbow, over the earth as his promise. So the people sang: "Stretch out your bow against the flood, for see, it has lifted up its waves against our walls!"

In Persia, the Christians had grown in number, and this angered the Magi, the priests who guarded the Persian religion. Sozomen, a Greek church historian of the next century, says the king believed a charge that their bishop was a friend of Rome's Christian emperor. The king taxed the Christians heavily, hoping that want would make them give up their faith. Then he ordered their priests killed and their churches pulled down. Aphrahat, writing in 344, said that in his own days many declared their faith and were killed.

In 363 Rome handed Nisibis over to Persia, and Ephrem moved west to Edessa. Rival teachers preached in these cities, and in his prose writings Ephrem answered "the three of them" by name. Marcion taught a Stranger God, apart from the Maker of the world; Mani taught two first Roots, Light and Darkness; and Bardaisan said the world was made from five first beings. Of their followers Ephrem wrote: "Their works are like our works, just as their fast is like our fast, but their faith is not like our faith." He warned that whoever prayed with Mani's followers "prays with Satan."

Ephrem died at Edessa in 373, and the Syrians, Sozomen says, went on singing his hymns.

In Persia, a later king ordered the churches his fathers had torn down to be rebuilt. He let "the priests, the leaders and all the holy covenant" go about freely and without fear, the same vowed order Aphrahat had written for. In 410, at the king's order, forty bishops of the Persian church met in a synod, a meeting of bishops to set rules for the church, and agreed on one bishop to a city. The day they met, the bishops said, was for them like seeing the dead rise.
