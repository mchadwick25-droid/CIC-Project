# Outline: Syriac Christianity (census id `syriac-edessa-nisibis`)

Kind: tradition (`Brief-Kit/kinds/tradition.md`). Every line points to `Source_Sheet.md` (S = setting, E = event, Q = quote) and through it to file and line. Files are under `cic/texts/`.

## The heart

- **The essence:** Syriac-speaking Christians in Edessa and Nisibis, two frontier cities between the Roman and Persian empires, from the flood of 201 to the year 410. In the 300s they sang their teaching in hymns and counted among them men and women vowed to God for life (E2, E3, E4). Edessa's later founding story pictures its people hearing the four Gospels read as one story, woven by Tatian (E1: Addai 65, late legend, the only old witness that it was read there; Tatian's making, Eusebius 26424-26433). (Census entry.)
- **Who they were:** "Syriac Christianity", the Syriac-speaking churches of Edessa and Nisibis (census `informalName`; S6).
- **When and where:** from about 200 (the flood of 201, S1) to the synod of 410 (E7); Edessa under Rome, Nisibis a Roman frontier city until it was handed to Persia in 363 (S2, S3), and Christians of the same tongue inside Persia (Aphrahat, E3).
- **What they faced:** floods and sieges on the frontier (S1, E4); rival teachings, which Ephrem answered by name (E5); in Persia, a king who, Sozomen says, believed a charge that their bishop was a friend of the Roman emperor, and who taxed them and had their priests killed (E6).
- **What they believed:** a faith they held apart from rivals who fasted as they did (Q3, E5); God's promise read in Scripture's images, the bow over the flood (Q1, E4); the four Gospels read as one story, in the picture their later founding story gives (E1, late legend).
- **How they practiced:** hymns, each with a refrain, a line that came back after every verse (E4; the print's "R."); daily readings, vigils and care for the sick in the picture their founding legend gives (E1, legend); the covenant's fasting, prayer, night watches and gifts to the poor (E2).
- **What held them together:** the world's gravities, in plain words: teaching through symbol and hymn, and the covenant life (both Primary); with them, the one-story Gospel, the answer to named rivals, and on the Persian side, holding on under persecution.
- **What came of them:** their eastern half continued as the early Persian Church of the East, which in 410 named the covenant in its first synod and set Nisibis among its metropolitan sees (E7, Documented); Ephrem's hymns were sung on (Impact 1).

## Setting

- **Century and place:** about 200 to 410; Edessa (Roman) and Nisibis (Roman frontier fortress until 363, then Persian), on the frontier of Rome and Persia (S1, S2, S3). Plainest words: "Edessa", "Nisibis", "the Roman and Persian empires".
- **Tradition:** "Syriac-speaking Christians"; spoken name "Syriac Christianity" (census; S6).
- Weave in a clause at a time: the frontier in the essence; 363 in beat 5; Persia in beats 2 and 4.

## Words to explain

- **covenant:** the vow some men and women took, before baptism, as Aphrahat's preachers warned those who chose it (Hallock 57; Parisot 21939-21942), to live unmarried and holy for God; they were called sons and daughters of the covenant (E2; Parisot 3291-3293; Hallock 57, 62).
- **harmony / Diatessaron:** one Gospel story woven out of the four; Eusebius gives Tatian's Greek title, "through four" (E1). Use "one Gospel woven from the four"; the Greek name is optional.
- **teaching hymn with a refrain:** stanzas, each with a refrain, a line that came back after every verse (the print marks it "R.": Hymn I, 13725-13734; after every stanza in later hymns, e.g. 15126-15195). No held old line says who sang which part. Say it in plain words; the Syriac word *madrasha* is not needed.
- **the bow:** the rainbow God set over the earth after Noah's flood, as his promise (Hymn I.1, 13728-13731).
- **Magi:** the priests who guarded the Persian religion (Sozomen's own gloss, 28875-28877).
- **synod:** a meeting of bishops that set rules for the church (E7).
- **confess:** in Aphrahat, to declare one's faith before the persecutor (E6). Not needed if the story paraphrases.

## Shape

The kind has one form: a story. Open on the flood, follow the community through time, end on the community at 410: the king's grant first, then the synod, closing on the bishops' own words.

## Openings

1. **The flood (recommended).** In November of the year 201, after a great rain in the night, the river rose against Edessa. The king stood on the tower called the Persians' and watched the water by torchlight, and that hour it broke through the western wall. The flood wrecked his palace and killed more than two thousand people, many of them asleep, "and they destroyed the temple of the church of the Christians." (S1, 53. The chronicle line is short enough to stand in the narrator's telling as the record's own words; it is not one of the three quotes and may be said in plain words instead.) Then: the chronicle, put together some 340 years later, says the city's scribes recorded it at the time (S1, 59; 251-253).
2. **The besieged city sings.** Inside besieged Nisibis, the city's own hymn-writer has the city surrounded by mounds, weapons and waves, with the river threatening it (13746-13751), and gives it a refrain: Q1. (Stronger as sound, but it opens mid-story; the slate runs from the flood.)

## Story beats

1. **One Gospel story.** Told in the essence, inside its witness, never as the narrator's fact: Edessa's later founding story pictures its people hearing the four Gospels read as one story, woven by Tatian, a teacher born in the east, a pupil of Justin (link; `anf02...xml` 7460; `npnf201...xml` 26368). No old line places it at Nisibis or across the span. "Diatessaron" is the St Petersburg manuscript's reading, which confirms Cureton's guess at an unclear word (Phillips's note 62, `addai...` 219). The same founding story pictures crowds coming every day to the readings of the Old and New Testament "and of the Diatessaron", keeping the night vigils and caring for the sick (E1, `addai...` 65; late legend, told as their story of themselves). Explain it: the harmony in one plain line, above.
2. **The covenant.** Inside Persia, the teacher Aphrahat wrote letters for "the children of the Church of God", one for each of the twenty-two letters of the alphabet (E3, 28517-28519, 28554-28556; Documented). One is addressed to the vowed men and women: they keep watch at night, sing psalms and pray, give their goods to the poor, and do not live with the other sex (E2, 25803-25808, 25835-25846; Documented). Reason (Aphrahat's): so lust does not draw them back (25835-25843). He gives each daughter of the covenant her answer if a vowed man asks her to live with him and serve him: **Q2**. Explain it: the covenant (above). Aphrahat wrote her words; say so in the telling ("he gives her the words").
3. **The hymns and the siege.** In Nisibis, Ephrem, a native of the city or its countryside (Sozomen 32051), taught in hymns, each with a refrain, a line that came back after every verse (E4; 13733-13734; Documented). His title of deacon is held only for Edessa (Palladius, S5); give it in beat 5. When a Persian army besieged the city in what Ephrem counts its third peril (13839-13840), his hymn has the city surrounded by mounds, weapons and waves, with the river threatening it (13746-13751; own words, Documented), and sets that flood beside Noah's. So the refrain runs: **Q1** (13733-13734). Explain it: the bow (above). Where sources differ: Theodoret has the bishop, Jacob, praying inside the church and then cursing the army with gnats from a tower (11155-11177, wonder in his voice); Ephrem's own hymn says the city's first bishop already lay buried within it, his body a wall for the city (14774-14787), and the Chronicle of Edessa dates Jacob's death to 337 or 338 (77). Contested; credit each, or send to the card (Cut first 1).
4. **Persecution in Persia.** Across the border, Christians had grown in number, built churches and appointed priests and deacons, and the Magi were angered (E6, 28873-28877). Sozomen, a Greek church historian of the next century, says the king believed the charge that their bishop was a friend of the Roman emperor; he taxed the Christians heavily, hoping want would make them give up their faith, then ordered their priests killed and their churches pulled down (28889-28899; Documented as Sozomen's account). The persecutor's reason, as the martyr acts give it: the bishop would make his people "servants of the emperor, who shares their faith" (Braun 1571-1575). Aphrahat, writing in 344, wrote that in his own days many declared their faith and were killed (27988-27991; Documented; the year from 28558-28561, Widely Accepted). Explain it: the Magi (above); the reason in each side's words.
5. **The rivals answered, and Ephrem's last years.** In 363, to save its starving army inside Persia, Rome handed Nisibis to Persia, and Ephrem moved west to Edessa, where he spent the rest of his life (S3, 11679-11697: the new emperor, "in the midst of the Persian territory, where his army was in danger of perishing for want of necessaries", ended the war, "giving up also Nisibis"; S4, 20435-20438; Widely Accepted). Sozomen says Bardaisan's son set his teaching to tunes the Syrians loved, and Ephrem, fearing they would take in its ideas, wrote hymns of the church's teaching in the same meters (32085-32103; Documented as Sozomen's account; a clause, since the Ephrem page tells it). In his prose writings Ephrem answered rival teachers, "the three of them," by name (584; no held line puts Marcion or Mani preaching in these cities): Marcion, who taught a Stranger God, apart from the Maker of the world; Mani, who taught two first Roots, Light and Darkness; and Bardaisan, who said the world was made from five first beings set in empty space (584, 1605-1609, 431, 640, 606-608, 917; Documented as Ephrem's telling). Of their followers he wrote **Q3** (1205; the next sentence names Mani's followers, then, as the editor reads the damaged page, Marcion's and Bardaisan's), and he warned that whoever prayed with Mani's followers "prays with Satan" (1205). Keep that warning: it is the record's force. He died at Edessa, a deacon of its church, in June 373 (S5, 103, 471; Widely Accepted).
6. **The grant and the synod of 410 (hand-off, a clause or two).** Told in this order: the grant, then the synod, then the Ending. The grant's word "covenant" echoes beat 2; let the word stand alone, with no line that it is the same order (Chabot's note, 16179-16180, reads it here as the whole clergy). In the eleventh year of his reign, the Persian king ordered (16155) the churches his fathers had torn down rebuilt, the prisoners set free, and "the priests, the leaders and all the holy covenant" allowed to go about freely and without fear (E7, 16159-16166). Forty bishops met at the king's order in his capital (16412-16416) with an envoy from the Roman side, and agreed that a city would no longer have two or three bishops, only one (16525-16526; the synod would not accept a city's two or three bishops, 17741-17742; Documented). Among them was the bishop of Nisibis, Ephrem's old city, now inside Persia (17746, 17861; Documented): this is the connection line. His rank, second among their metropolitans, stays on the card (Cut first 6). From there their story is the Persian church's own.

## What came of it (heaviest first)

1. Ephrem's hymns sung on in the churches that spoke Syriac (Sozomen 32101-32103). **Defended:** the church's teaching, against the songs of Bardaisan's son.
2. Nisibis and its church passed into the Persian church, one of its metropolitan sees in 410, its bishop the second of its metropolitans, after Beit Lapat (Chabot 17716-17746). **Defended:** one bishop to a city; one shared calendar of feasts (16525-16530).
3. The word covenant went on in the Persian church's acts (16166; Chabot's note 16179-16180 reads it there as the whole clergy) and was still named in the sixth century (John of Ephesus 14152). Card for the later witness.
4. Edessa took care to record the names of Persia's martyrs (Sozomen 29190-29196). Card.
- Who keeps it today: leave out of the audio (no held old source; the census `legacy` text covers it on the card).

## Connection to carry into the story

**Continued as the early Persian Church of the East (Documented).** What passed: Nisibis, Ephrem's city, went over to Persia in 363 (Socrates 11697; beat 5). In 410 the king freed 'the priests, the leaders and all the holy covenant' (16159-16166), and forty bishops agreed that a city would no longer have two or three bishops, only one (16521-16526). The connection line: "Among them was the bishop of Nisibis, Ephrem's old city, now inside Persia" (the list of sees, 17746; the signatures, 17861: "Moi, Osée, év. de Nisibe, J'adhère à tout ce qui est écrit ci-dessus"). The grant's word "covenant" echoes Aphrahat's, but the listener is not told it is the same order (Chabot 16179-16180 reads it there as the whole clergy). The writer shows the hand-off through what the king ordered and who met, not through a 'this mattered because' line. Card: Nisibis listed after Seleucia-Ctesiphon and Beit Lapat, its bishop Hosea the second metropolitan and the third to sign (17716-17746, 17861).
