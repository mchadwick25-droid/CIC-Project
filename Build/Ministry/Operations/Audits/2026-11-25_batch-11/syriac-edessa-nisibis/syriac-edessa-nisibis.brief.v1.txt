In November of the year 201, after a great rain in the night, the river rose against Edessa. The king stood on the tower called the Persians' and watched the water by torchlight, and in that hour it broke through the western wall. The flood wrecked his palace, killed more than two thousand people, many of them asleep, and destroyed the church of the Christians. The city's chronicle, put together some 340 years later, says its scribes recorded the flood at the time, and their record is the first sure sign of a church there.

That church belonged to Syriac-speaking Christians in Edessa and Nisibis, two frontier cities between the Roman and Persian empires, and their story runs from the flood to the year 410. They sang their teaching in hymns, heard the Gospel as one woven story, and counted among them men and women vowed to God for life. Others who shared their language lived across the border, inside Persia.

That one story was a harmony that Tatian, a teacher born in the east and a pupil of Justin, had woven out of the four Gospels.

Inside Persia, a teacher named Aphrahat wrote letters to instruct the churches, and one of them is addressed to the sons and daughters of the covenant. These were men and women who vowed, before baptism, to live unmarried and holy for God while they served in the church. His letter has them keep watch at night, sing psalms and pray, give their goods to the poor, and live apart from the other sex, so that desire does not draw them back. He even gives each daughter of the covenant her answer, if a vowed man asks her to live with him and serve him. "I am promised in marriage to a royal husband, and I serve him. If I leave his service and serve you, my bridegroom will be angry with me. He will write me a letter of divorce and send me away from his house."

In Nisibis, a Roman fortress on the frontier, Ephrem, born in the city or its countryside, taught in hymns. A single voice sang the verses, and the people sang a line back after each one. When a Persian army besieged the city, in what Ephrem counts its third peril, his hymn pictures Nisibis ringed by mounds, weapons and waves, with the river rising against it. He set that flood beside Noah's, after which God put his bow, the rainbow, over the earth as his promise. Then he gave the people this line to sing back: "Stretch out your bow against the flood, for see, it has lifted up its waves against our walls!"

Across the border, Christians had grown in number, built churches and appointed priests and deacons, and this angered the Magi, the priests who guarded the Persian religion. Sozomen, a Greek church historian of the next century, says the Persian king believed a charge that their bishop was a friend of the Roman emperor, who was a Christian too. The king taxed the Christians heavily, hoping want would make them give up their faith, then ordered their priests killed and their churches pulled down. Aphrahat, writing in 344, said that in his own days many declared their faith and were killed.

In 363 Rome handed Nisibis to Persia, and Ephrem moved west to Edessa, where he spent the rest of his life. Rival teachers preached in these cities in the same language, and Ephrem answered the three of them by name in prose. In his telling, Marcion taught a Stranger God, apart from the Maker of the world. Mani taught two first Roots, Light and Darkness. Bardaisan said the world was made from five first beings set in empty space. Of their followers Ephrem wrote, "Their works are like our works, just as their fast is like our fast, but their faith is not like our faith." He warned that whoever prayed with Mani's followers "prays with Satan." Ephrem died at Edessa, a deacon of its church, in June 373, and the Syrians went on singing his hymns, Sozomen says.

In the eleventh year of his reign, a later Persian king ordered the churches his fathers had torn down rebuilt and the prisoners set free. He also allowed "the priests, the leaders and all the holy covenant" to go about freely and without fear. In 410, at his order, forty bishops of the Persian church met in his capital, with an envoy from the Roman side. In this synod, a meeting that set rules for the church, they agreed on one bishop to a city. The day they met, the bishops said, was for them like seeing the dead rise.
