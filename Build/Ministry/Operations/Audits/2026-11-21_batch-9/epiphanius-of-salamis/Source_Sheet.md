## 1. Setting

**S1. Birth and early training (Palestine, Egypt).** SS 39786-39798. Sozomen, later (a generation or more after the death: Socrates' history "stops with the year 439", SS 1265, Zenos's introduction; that Sozomen wrote about the same time is an inference from his use of Socrates, e.g. SS 43498 "Mainly after Soc. vi. 10, 12, 14", Hartranft's note). Documented (single witness for the birthplace).
> "and Epiphanius, afterwards bishop of Salamis in Cyprus, deserve to be particularly noticed. ... and Epiphanius fixed his abode near the village of Besauduc, which was his birthplace, in the government of Eleutheropolis. Having been instructed from his youth by the most celebrated ascetics, and having on this account passed the most of his time in Egypt, Epiphanius became most celebrated in Egypt and Palestine by his attainments in monastic philosophy, and was chosen by the inhabitants of Cyprus to act as bishop of the metropolis of their island."
- Note: Hartranft's note at SS 39771-39773: "This chapter is probably derived from local Palestinian biographies familiar to him as a native." (editor's view).

**S2. His monastery near Eleutheropolis.** J6 18736-18739. Jerome, Letter 82 to Theophilus, contemporary, ally. Documented.
> "For the monastery of the reverend pope Epiphanius—called the old monastery—where my brother was ordained presbyter is situated in the district of Eleutheropolis and not in that of Ælia."
- "pope" here is Jerome's honorific (father), not the bishop of Rome. "Founded" the monastery rests on the editor only (J6 40447-40448, Fremantle's introduction: "the monastery which he had founded at Ad, in the diocese of Eleutheropolis").
- Letter 51 (own words) speaks of "the church of the villa which adjoins our monastery" (J6 10688-10689).

**S3. Bishop of Salamis, a seaport; chosen by the Cypriots.** SS 39798-39806 (Sozomen). Documented.
> "Hence he is, I think, the most revered man under the whole heaven, so to speak; for he fulfilled his priesthood in the concourse of a large city and in a seaport; and when he threw himself into civil affairs, he conducted them with so much virtue that he became known in a little while to all citizens and every variety of foreigner"
- Salamis also called Constantia: Zenos's note, SS 17864-17865 ("His see—that of Constantia in Cyprus—was the old 'Salamis' of Acts xiii. 5.") (editor); Jerome, Letter 108, J6 20957-20958 ("Epiphanius, bishop of Salamis or, as it is now called, Constantia, in Cyprus").

**S4. Length of his episcopate.** PALL 8919-8921. Dialogue on Chrysostom (Palladius per Moore), near-contemporary, John's partisan. Documented (single witness).
> "He vituperated Epiphanius, the blessed Bishop of Constantia in Cyprus, who for thirty-six years ruled the Church there,"

**S5. Dates (editors only).** Richardson's note to Jerome, Lives 114, J3 41528: "Born about 310, bishop about 368–9, died 403." Fremantle's chronology, J6 3193: "402. Death of Epiphanius." J6 3042: "392. Epiphanius visits Jerusalem." But the introduction to Against John, J6 40439-40440, puts the visit "in 394". **The editors disagree with each other (402 or 403 for the death; 392 or 394 for Jerusalem). No held ancient source gives a year of birth or death.** Inferential-Thin as years; the story should give none.

**S6. Date of the Panarion (his own regnal dating).** HOLL1 13424-13426, own words (Panarion, Proem II 2.3). Documented as his own reckoning; the A.D. year is Holl's.
> "ἐξότε ἄνϑρωπος ἐπὶ γῆς πέπλασται ἄχρι τοῦ ἡμετέρου χρόνου τουτέστι βασιλείας Οὐαλεντινιανοῦ καὶ Οὐάλεντος ἑνδεχάτου ἔτους καὶ Γρατιανοῦ ἑβδόμου."
("from when man was formed on earth until our own time, that is, the eleventh year of the reign of Valentinian and Valens, and the seventh of Gratian.")
- Holl's note, HOLL1 13447: "gemeint ist das Jahr 375" (editor).

**S7. Tradition.** Census `palestinian-ascetic-monasticism-early`: spoken forms "Palestinian Monasticism", "The Monks of Gaza and the Judean Desert". Its `sourcing` names Jerome's Life of Hilarion and Palladius as the main witnesses. Plainest spoken word: "the monks of Palestine". Epiphanius ties to it: S1, S2, and Jerome's Life of Hilarion (L6 below).

## 2. Events, in order

**E1. In his youth, a sect tries to draw him in; he reads their books and gives about eighty names to the bishops.** Own words, Panarion 26.17, HOLL1 22680-22686 and 22740-22747. Documented as his own account (single witness).
> "ταύτῃ γὰρ τῇ αἱρέσει, ἐπιπόϑητοι, καὶ αὐτὸς περιέτυχον, ἐκ στόματος δὲ τῶν ταύτην ἐπιχειρούντων φύσει αὐτὰ αὐτοπροσώπως ἐνηχήϑην" γυναῖχες δὲ οὕτως ἠπατημέναι οὐ μόνον τὴν λαλιὰν ταύτην ἡμῖν προέτειναν καὶ τὰ τοιαῦτα ἡμῖν ἀπεκάλυψαν, ἀλλὰ καὶ κατὰ τὴν Αἰγυπτίαν € ἐχείνην τὴν τοῦ ἀρχιμαγείρου τὴν ὀλετήριον χαὶ μοχϑηρὰν zal αὗται ἀϑυρογλώττῳ τόλμῃ κατασπάσαι ἡμᾶς ἐν τῇ νέᾳ ἡμῶν ἡλικίᾳ ὀρεχϑεῖσαι ἐπεχείρουν."
(My working sense: "For I myself, beloved, met this heresy, and heard these things in person from the mouths of those who practise it. Women so deceived not only put this talk to us and revealed such things, but, like that Egyptian woman, the chief cook's deadly and wicked wife, they too with shameless boldness tried to drag us down in our youth, reaching out for us.")
> "ἀλλὰ ὁ ἐλεήμων ϑεὸς τῆς μοχϑηρίας αὐτῶν ἡμᾶς ἐρρύσατο, ὥστε μετὰ τὸ ἀναγνῶναι ἡμᾶς καὶ τὰς βίβλους αὐτῶν ... τὸ τηνικαῦτα ἐσπουδάσαμεν καὶ τοῖς ἐπισχόποις τοῖς ἐν τῷ τόπῳ ἐχείνῳ αὐτοὺς ὑποδεῖξαι καὶ τὰ ὀνόματα (Ta) ἐν τῇ ἐκκλησίᾳ κεχρυμμένα φωρᾶσαι, (ὡς) καὶ τοὺς αὐτοὺς ἀπελαϑῆναι τῆς πόλεως, ὀνόματα περί που ὀγδοήκοντα, καὶ τὴν πόλιν τῆς αὐτῶν ζιζανιώδους ὕλης καὶ ἀχκανϑώδους καϑαρϑῆναι."
(Working sense: "But merciful God rescued us from their wickedness, so that after we had read their books too ... we then took care to point them out to the bishops of that place, and to bring to light the names hidden in the church, so that they were driven out of the city, about eighty names, and the city was cleared of their weedy and thorny growth.")
- The heresy is the one Panarion 26 treats; Holl's running head says "(Gnostiker)" (HOLL1 22655: "Panarion haer. 26, 16, 1—17, 6 (Gnostiker)").
- **The "Egyptian" is Potiphar's wife (the chief cook's wife, Gen. 39; Holl's note HOLL1 22696 "vgl. Gen. 39"). The passage does not name the city or say it was in Egypt.** "τῷ τόπῳ ἐκείνῳ" = "that place". Locating the episode in Egypt would be an inference from S1. Inferential-Thin for place.
- "(ὡς)" and "(Ta)" are Holl's supplements.

**E2. Ties to the Palestinian monks: Hilarion.** J6 30110-30112. Jerome, Life of Hilarion, contemporary. Documented.
> "It is true that that holy man Epiphanius, bishop of Salamis in Cyprus, who had much intercourse with Hilarion, set forth his praises in a short but widely circulated letter."
- That letter is not held.

**E3. Bishop in Cyprus: generosity (Sozomen's report, with wonders).** SS 42328-42355. Sozomen, later. Documented as report; the wonders stay in Sozomen's voice.
> "He was celebrated, not only for the virtues he manifested and miraculous deeds during his life, but also for the honor that was rendered to him by God after his death; for it was said that demons were expelled, and diseases healed at his tomb. ... He was extremely liberal towards the needy, either to those who had suffered from shipwreck or any other calamity; and after expending the whole of his own patrimony in the relief of such cases, he applied the treasures of the church to the same purpose."
- Sozomen's Greek: SOZG 25459-25473 (legible in part).
- Monks came to Cyprus for him: Jerome, Letter 108 (Paula's journey), J6 21020-21027, contemporary: "where falling at the feet of the holy and venerable Epiphanius, she was by him detained ten days ... For she visited all the monasteries in the island, and left ... substantial relief for the brothers in them whom love of the holy man had brought thither from all parts of the world."

**E4. The Panarion (about 375): what it is, by its own preface.** Own words, HOLL1 12381-12399 (Proem I, a letter to the presbyters Acacius and Paul, HOLL1 12378-12379). Documented.
> "ἐπειδὴ γὰρ μέλλομεν ὑμῖν τά τε ὀνόματα τῶν αἱρέσεων δηλοῦν τάς TE παρ᾽ αὐτοῖς ἀϑεμίτους πράξεις ἀνακαλύπτειν, ὥσπερ ἰοὺς καὶ δηλητήρια ὄντα, σὺν αὐτοῖς δὲ ἅμα καὶ ἀντιδότους ἐφαρμόσαι, ἀλεξητήρια τῶν δεδηγμένων zal προχαταληπτικὰ τῶν μελλόντων εἰς τοῦτο | ἐμπίπτειν, τοῖς φιλοκάλοις διαγράφομεν αὐτὸ τοῦτο τὸ προοίμιον, Πανάριον εἴτ᾽ οὖν κιβώτιον ἰατρικὸν τῶν ϑηριοδήχτων ἕρ gumvevovres, ὅπερ ἐστὶ διὰ βιβλίων τριῶν ,συγγραφέν, {(περι)έχον αἱρέσεις ὀγδοήκοντα, αἵτινές εἰσι ϑηρίων εἴτ᾽ οὖν ἑρπετῶν αἰνίγματα."
(Working sense: "For since we are about to show you the names of the heresies and uncover their unholy deeds, which are like venoms and poisons, and with them to fit antidotes, cures for those who have been bitten and safeguards for those who may yet fall into this, we write out this very preface for those who love the good, explaining 'Panarion', that is, a medicine chest for those bitten by wild beasts, written in three books, holding eighty heresies, which are figures of wild beasts or snakes.")
- **Scan and text notes.** The OCR is fair. In 12387-12391 the words are legible apart from two trivial slips ("TE" for τε, "zal" for καί). In 12393 the participle ἑρμηνεύοντες is garbled ("ἕρ gumvevovres"). Holl's apparatus (HOLL1 12405-12413) shows "τῶν ϑηριοδήκτων" and "συγγραφέν, περιέχον" are his choices from the Anacephalaeosis (the manuscripts GRM read "καὶ ϑηριοδηχτιχὸν" and omit "συγγραφέν, περιέχον"). The title itself is confirmed by the work's own heading, HOLL1 13363-13364: "᾿Επιφανίου ἐπισκόπου κατὰ αἱρέσεων τὸ ἐπικληϑὲν πανάριον εἴτουν κιβώτιον." ("Of Epiphanius the bishop, against heresies, the work called Panarion or chest.") **No other held print of the Proem** (PG 41 and Oehler's first volume are not held).
- **The church after the eighty**, HOLL1 12395-12399: "»ula δὲ μετὰ τὰς ὀγδοή- χοντας ἡ τῆς ἀληϑείας βάσις ἅμα καὶ διδασκαλία καὶ σωτήριος πραγματεία καὶ Χριστοῦ »νύμφη ἁγία« ἐκκλησία" ("»ula" is OCR for μία.) (Working sense: "But one, after the eighty, is the foundation of truth, its teaching and saving work, Christ's holy bride, the church.") Holl's note HOLL1 12402: "vgl. Hohesl. 6,7" (the Song of Songs: queens, concubines and the one dove), editor.

**E5. How he knew what he wrote.** Own words, HOLL1 13426-13434 (Proem II 2.4). Documented.
> "τῶν δὲ ὑφ᾽ ἡμῶν μελλόντων εἰς γνῶσιν τῶν ἐντυγχανόντων ἥχειν (περὶ αἱρέσεών τε καὶ σχισμάτων τὰ μὲν ἐχ φιλομαϑίας ἴσμεν, τὰ δὲ ἐξ ἀκοῆς κατειλήφαμεν, τοῖς δέ τισιν ἰδίοις ὠσὶ καὶ ὀφϑαλμοῖς παρετύχομεν"
(Working sense: "Of the heresies and schisms we will bring to our readers' knowledge, some we know from study, some we learned by hearsay, and some we met with our own ears and eyes.")

**E6. He copies his opponents' own words to answer them.** Own words, HOLL1 34006-34011 (Panarion 33.2). Documented.
> "εἰς δὲ περισσότερον ἔλεγχον τοῦ ἀπατεῶνος καϑεξῆς ὑποτάξας παραϑήσομαι τὰ ὑπ᾿ αὐτοῦ φύσει Πτολεμαίου Φλώρᾳ τινὶ γυναικὶ γραφέντα ἐπαγωγά τε καὶ δηλητήρια ῥήματα, (ἵνα μή τις νομίσειεν ἡμᾶς ἐξ ἀκοῆς μόνον τὸν ἀπατεῶνα ἐλέγχειν, μὴ πρότερον ἐντυχόντας τῇ παρ᾽ αὐτοῦ (παρα)πεποιημένῃ διδασχαλίᾳ"
(Working sense: "For a fuller refutation of the deceiver I will set out below the alluring and poisonous words Ptolemy himself wrote to a woman named Flora, so that no one may think we refute the deceiver from hearsay alone, without first meeting his falsified teaching.")
- The letter follows whole, HOLL1 34016 ("Πτολεμαίου πρὸς Φλώραν.") to 34746 ("Πτολεμαίου πρὸς Φλώραν ἐπληρώϑη."). Documented that he copied it whole. Other stories on the tree already tell that he copied Aetius's book (`aetius-of-antioch`), Marcellus's letter (`marcellus-of-ancyra`), Irenaeus's and Methodius's words (`irenaeus-of-lyon`, `methodius-of-olympus`; their words are barred here).

**E7. He loved a man and still listed his followers' teaching: Apollinaris.** Own words, PG42 48853-48857 (Panarion 77.2.1). Documented; Greek legible in this line apart from OCR slips ("πρεσδύτης" for πρεσβύτης, "ἀγα- Ὦ κητὸς" for ἀγαπητός, "μαχαρίτῃ" for μακαρίτῃ, "Λαοδιχείας" for Λαοδικείας); the scan elsewhere is poor; no other held print of Panarion 77.
> "Ὁ πρεσδύτης καὶ σεμνοπρεπὴς, ὁ ἀεὶ ἡμῖν ἀγα- Ὦ κητὸς, χαὶ τῷ μαχαρίτῃ πάπᾳ ᾿Αθανασίῳ, καὶ πᾶσιν ὀρθοδόξοις, ᾿Απολλινάριος ὁ ἀπὸ Λαοδιχείας,"
(Working sense: "The old and venerable man, always dear to us and to the blessed father Athanasius and to all the orthodox, Apollinaris of Laodicea,")
- Lietzmann (editor), `cic/texts/apollinaris-laodicea_fragmente-apollinaris-von-laodicea-und-seine-schule-grc-deu_lietzmann1904.txt` 1340-1380, reconstructs a visit to Antioch about 374 where Epiphanius questioned Vitalis (editor's reconstruction; Dominant Modern Reconstruction at best; not for the story).
- His later letter pairs the two teachers: Letter 91, J6 19765-19766 (own words in Jerome's Latin, English only): "particularly oppose the disciples of Origen and of Apollinaris".

**E8. Jerusalem: his sermon against Origen, and the bishop's party mocks him.** Jerome, Against John of Jerusalem §11, J6 40982-41015. Contemporary eyewitness ("We were present"), Epiphanius's ally, hostile to the bishop. Documented as Jerome's account; the tone is a partisan's.
> "We were present (we know the whole case) when the bishop Epiphanius spoke against Origen in your church, and he was the ostensible, you the real object of attack. You and your crew grinned like dogs, drew in your nostrils, scratched your heads, nodded to one another, and talked of the 'silly old man.' Did you not, in front of the Lord's tomb, send your archdeacon to tell him to cease discussing such matters? ... When you were going from the Church of the Resurrection to the Church of the Holy Cross, and a crowd of all ages, and both sexes, was flowing to meet him, presenting to him their little ones, kissing his feet, plucking the fringes of his garments, and when he could not stir a step forward ..."
> J6 41000-41015: "You spoke, forsooth, with indignant rage against the Anthropomorphites, who, with rustic simplicity, think that God has actually the members of which we read in Scripture; and showed by your eyes, hands, and every gesture that you had the old man in view ... He rose ... and proceeded thus: 'All that has been said by one who is my brother in the episcopate, but my son in point of years, against the heresy of the Anthropomorphites, has been well and faithfully spoken, and my voice, too, condemns that heresy. But it is fair that, as we condemn this heresy so we should also condemn the perverse doctrines of Origen.' You cannot, I think, have forgotten what a burst of laughter, what shouts of applause ensued."
- The bishop of Jerusalem is John (J6 10631-10632). Year: editors disagree (S5).

**E9. Why he opposed Origen: his charges, in his own letter.** Letter 51 to John of Jerusalem, own words, **held only in English (Fremantle) from Jerome's Latin of the Greek**; Jerome says it was written in Greek and he put it into Latin (J6 10627-10628, editor's headnote; Jerome's own account J6 13202-13212). Documented as his charges; hostile to Origen.
> J6 10779-10782: "Withdraw, dearly beloved, from the heresy of Origen and from all heresies. For I see that all your indignation has been roused against me simply because I have told you that you ought not to eulogize one who is the spiritual father of Arius, and the root and parent of all heresies."
> J6 10813-10818: "Can any one, moreover, brook Origen's assertion that men's souls were once angels in heaven, and that having sinned in the upper world, they have been cast down into this, and have been confined in bodies as in barrows or tombs, to pay the penalty for their former sins"
> J6 10876-10879: "This wonderful doctor presumes to teach that the devil will once more be what he at one time was, that he will return to his former dignity and rise again to the kingdom of heaven."
> J6 10899-10903 (what he defends): "Who can patiently listen to the perilous arguments of Origen when he denies the resurrection of this flesh ... Or who can tolerate him when he gives us a paradise in the third heaven, and transfers that which the Scripture mentions from earth to the heavenly places, and when he explains allegorically all the trees which are mentioned in Genesis"
> J6 10914-10922 (the rivers of Eden): "'It was parted and became into four heads. The name of the first is Pison…and the name of the second is Gihon.' I myself have seen the waters of Gihon, have seen them with my bodily eyes. ... I have drunk also from the great river Euphrates, not spiritual but actual water, such as you can touch with your hand and imbibe with your mouth. But where there are rivers which admit of being seen and of being drunk, it follows that there also there will be fig-trees and other trees"
> J6 10965-10968: "Origen has not lived in my day, nor has he robbed me. I have not conceived a dislike to him nor quarrelled with him because of an inheritance or of any worldly matter"
> J6 10657-10659 (what he says he protects): "Feeling, therefore, that too great strictness or scrupulosity on my part might have the effect of alienating them from the Church with its ancient faith"
- These are a foe's summaries of Origen. Origen's defenders: Socrates (D3) and the bishop of Scythia (E13).

**E10. He seizes and ordains Jerome's brother.** Letter 51, own words (English only), J6 10676-10702. Documented as his own account; the force is in his own words.
> "I knew, moreover, that you could not find or lay hands on this servant of God who had several times fled from you simply because he was reluctant to undertake the onerous duties of the priesthood ... While, therefore, the Collect was being celebrated in the church of the villa which adjoins our monastery—he being quite ignorant and wholly unsuspicious of my purpose—I gave orders to a number of deacons to seize him and to stop his mouth, lest in his eagerness to free himself he might adjure me in the name of Christ. First of all, then, I ordained him deacon, setting before him the fear of God, and forcing him to minister; for he made a hard struggle against it, crying out that he was unworthy, and protesting that this heavy burden was beyond his strength. ... And when he had ministered in the offering of the holy sacrifices, once more with great difficulty I closed his mouth and ordained him presbyter."
- His defence, J6 10653-10654: "My action concerned a monastery whose inmates were foreigners in no way subject to your provincial jurisdiction." And J6 10713-10714: "I held the ordination in a monastery, and not within the limits of your jurisdiction."
- The man is Paulinian, Jerome's brother: Fremantle's note J6 10677-10678 (editor), confirmed by Jerome's own "my brother" (J6 18725, 18737).
- Jerome on the age: J6 18741-18742, "as he has now attained to thirty years" (Letter 82, a few years later).

**E11. He tears down a painted curtain at Anablatha.** Letter 51 §9, own words (English only), J6 11096-11120. Documented as his own account.
> "I came to a villa called Anablatha and, as I was passing, saw a lamp burning there. Asking what place it was, and learning it to be a church, I went in to pray, and found there a curtain hanging on the doors of the said church, dyed and embroidered. It bore an image either of Christ or of one of the saints; I do not rightly remember whose the image was. Seeing this, and being loth that an image of a man should be hung up in Christ's church contrary to the teaching of the Scriptures, I tore it asunder and advised the custodians of the place to use it as a winding sheet for some poor person. They, however, murmured, and said that if I made up my mind to tear it, it was only fair that I should give them another curtain in its place. ... I beg that you will order the presbyter of the place to take the curtain which I have sent ... and that you will afterwards give directions that curtains of the other sort—opposed as they are to our religion—shall not be hung up in any church of Christ."
- Fremantle's headnote calls it "an early instance of the iconoclastic spirit" (J6 10625, editor's view).
- The same letter warns against "Palladius of Galatia" (J6 11122-11126): sibling `palladius-of-helenopolis` tells that warning; it stays there.

**E12. Theophilus of Alexandria turns to him; the synod in Cyprus.**
- Socrates 6.10, SS 17868-17899. Later, and a defender of Origen (SS 18135-18181, his own chapter 6.13). Documented as Socrates' account; his view of motives is his.
> "For Theophilus accused Epiphanius of entertaining low thoughts of God, by supposing him to have a human form. Now although Theophilus was really unchanged in sentiment ... yet on account of his hatred of others, he openly denied his own convictions; and he now professed to be friendly with Epiphanius ... He then managed it so that Epiphanius by letter should convene a Synod of the bishops in Cyprus, in order to condemn the writings of Origen. Epiphanius being on account of his extraordinary piety a man of simple mind and manners was easily influenced by the letters of Theophilus: having therefore assembled a council of bishops in that island, he caused a prohibition to be therein made of the reading of Origen's works. ... not having this as his first object, but rather his purpose of revenge on Dioscorus and his brethren."
- Zenos's note, SS 17871-17876 (editor): "It seems strange that Epiphanius should be classed with the Anthropomorphitæ as Epiphanius himself repudiates their views according to the testimony of Jerome."
- Sozomen 8.14, SS 43502-43520 (later; admiring): "It also occurred to him that it would be advantageous to enlist Epiphanius, bishop of Salamis, in Cyprus, on his side, a man who was revered for his life, and was the most distinguished of his contemporaries; and he therefore formed a friendship with him, although he had formerly blamed him for asserting that God possessed a human form. ... Epiphanius had long regarded the writings of Origen with peculiar aversion, and was therefore easily led to attach credit to the epistle of Theophilus. He soon after assembled the bishops of Cyprus together, and prohibited the examination of the books of Origen."
- Theophilus's own request, Letter 90 (Jerome's Latin, English only), J6 19716-19728, contemporary: "has with the sword of the gospel cut down the Origenist serpents crawling out of their caves ... As your excellency has often fought in contests of the kind before me, it is your present duty ... to gather together to this end the bishops of your entire island. A synodical letter should be sent to myself and the bishop of Constantinople". Date 400 is the editor's (J6 19704).
- Epiphanius's joy, Letter 91 (own words, English only), J6 19779-19782: "what a great blessing the Lord has granted to my last days in approving the principles which I have always proclaimed by the testimony of so great a prelate."
- Socrates 6.12 on what was condemned, SS 18080-18082: "a copy of the synodical decree in which they did not excommunicate Origen himself but condemned his books."
- The "Long Brothers": Sozomen 8.15, SS 43604-43605 (Ammonius: "We are, O father, the Long Brothers"); Dioscorus and his brothers are the monks Theophilus drove out (SS 17899; Letter 90, J6 19729-19733 names "Ammonius, Eusebius, and Euthymius").

**E13. Constantinople: ordination outside his see, refusal to pray with John, the objection of a bishop from Scythia.**
- Socrates 6.12, SS 18078-18118 (later). Documented.
> "at the suggestion of Theophilus, the bishop Epiphanius again came from Cyprus to Constantinople ... On reaching St. John's church, which is seven miles distant from the city, he disembarked, and there celebrated a service; then after having ordained a deacon, he again entered the city. In complaisance to Theophilus he declined John's courtesy ... producing his copy of the synodical decree condemnatory of Origen's works, recited it before them; not being able to assign any reason for this judgment, than that it seemed fit to Theophilus and himself to reject them. Some indeed from a reverential respect for Epiphanius subscribed the decree; but many refused to do so among whom was Theotimus bishop of Scythia, who thus addressed Epiphanius:—'I neither choose, Epiphanius,' said he, 'to insult the memory of one who ended his life piously long ago; nor dare I be guilty of so impious an act, as that of condemning what our predecessors did not reject: and especially when I know of no evil doctrine contained in Origen's books.'"
- Zenos's note on the canons, SS 18085-18086 (editor): "It was contrary to the canons of the church for a bishop to ordain a presbyter or a deacon in another's diocese."
- Socrates 6.14, SS 18197-18200: "He, however, replied that he would neither stay nor pray with him, unless he would expel Dioscorus and his brethren from the city, and with his own hand subscribe the condemnation of Origen's books." John: "nothing ought to be done rashly before investigation by a general council" (SS 18201-18202).
- Sozomen 8.14, SS 43531-43538 and 43552-43556: "Epiphanius was the first to sail from Cyprus; he landed at Hebdomos ... In order to do him honor, John went out with all his clergy to meet him. Epiphanius, however, evinced clearly by his conduct that he believed the accusations against John ... But Epiphanius declared that he would neither reside with John nor pray with him publicly, unless he would denounce the works of Origen and expel Dioscorus and his companions."
- Palladius's Dialogue: Moore's summary note only, PALL 4729-4740 (editor's footnote, not the Dialogue's words).

**E14. Did he denounce John in the Church of the Apostles? The two historians differ.**
- Socrates 6.14, SS 18203-18207: "they contrived it so that as a meeting was in the church named The Apostles, Epiphanius came forth and before all the people condemned the books of Origen, excommunicated Dioscorus with his followers, and charged John with countenancing them." Then John's message, SS 18212-18218, and "Epiphanius becoming alarmed on hearing these admonitions, left the church" (SS 18220-18221).
- Sozomen 8.14, SS 43565-43572: "The following day, when Epiphanius was about entering the church, in order to carry his design into execution, he was stopped by Serapion, at the command of John ... By these arguments Epiphanius was induced to relinquish his attack."
- **Contested between the two held witnesses.** The story credits each or says only that he planned it and was warned off.

**E15. The Long Brothers: "have you read any of our works?"** Sozomen 8.15, SS 43603-43620. Later; Sozomen's note says "Independent chapter" (SS 43586). Documented as Sozomen's report (single witness).
> "Ammonius and his companions went to Epiphanius, at the permission of the empress. Epiphanius inquired who they were, and Ammonius replied, 'We are, O father, the Long Brothers; we come respectfully to know whether you have read any of our works or those of our disciples?' On Epiphanius replying that he had not seen them, he continued, 'How is it, then, that you consider us to be heretics, when you have no proof as to what sentiments we may hold?' Epiphanius said that he had formed his judgment by the reports he had heard on the subject; and Ammonius replied, 'We have pursued a very different line of conduct from yours. We have conversed with your disciples, and read your works frequently, and among others, that entitled "The Anchored." ...' Epiphanius was measurably convinced, and dismissed them."
- "The Anchored" is the Ancoratus, **which is held** (HOLL1, from about line 1200; e.g. "Ancoratus 1, 1—2, 5", HOLL1 1576). The card's claim that it is not held is out of date.
- The empress's son episode, SS 43582-43595 (side story; leave out).

**E16. He sails home and dies at sea; the parting words.**
- Sozomen 8.15, SS 43620-43631 (English) and SOZG 30100-30121 (Greek). Later; "It is reported" (Λέγεται). Documented that he died at sea; the words are hearsay.
> SS 43620-43626: "Soon after he embarked for Cyprus, either because he recognized the futility of his journey to Constantinople, or because, as there is reason to believe, God had revealed to him his approaching death; for he died while on his voyage back to Cyprus. It is reported that he said to the bishops who had accompanied him to the place of embarkation, 'I leave you the city, the palace, and the stage, for I shall shortly depart.'"
> SOZG 30107-30115: "Λέγεται γοῦν τοῖς αὐτῷ συνελθοῦσιν ἐπισκόποις ἐπὶ θάλασσαν εἰπεῖν, μέλλων ἐπιβαίνειν τοῦ σκάφους, Αφιημι ὑμῖν τὴν πόλιν καὶ τὰ βασίλεια, καὶ τὴν ὑπόκρισιν" ἐγὼ δὲ ἀπειμι᾿ σπεύ- 7 δω γὰρ, πάνυ σπεύδω."
- Scan note: the Greek lines are interleaved with stray accent rows in the OCR (e.g. SOZG 30110, 30112); the words of the saying are legible. The English (Hartranft) confirms "the city, the palace, and the stage" and compresses "σπεύδω γάρ, πάνυ σπεύδω" into "for I shall shortly depart". Moore's footnote (PALL 4740, editor) gives "I leave to you the city, the court, and the hypocrisy." No second Greek print held.
- Sozomen's hearsay exchange, SS 43626-43631 (and SOZG 30115-30121): "I have been informed by several persons that John predicted that Epiphanius would die at sea ... Epiphanius said to John, 'I hope you will not die a bishop,' and that John replied, 'I hope you will never return to your bishopric.'"
- Socrates 6.14, SS 18222-18230 (Greek SOCG 13072): "Some say that when he was about to depart, he said to John, 'I hope that you will not die a bishop': to which John replied, 'Expect not to arrive at your own country.' I cannot be sure that those who reported these things to me spoke the truth; but nevertheless the event was in the case of both as prophesied above. For Epiphanius did not reach Cyprus, having died on board the ship during his voyage; and John a short time afterwards was driven from his see".

## 3. Impact, heaviest first (what later witnesses carried on and used)

**I1. The Panarion became the list later writers followed.**
- Augustine, De haeresibus (editors' date, OCR "Seriptus circiter annum 428.", AUG 1098), AUG 2411-2426. Later, about 25 years after his death. Documented. OCR has slips ("dietus", "hz-reticis", "Grecos").
> "Usque ad istos ergo de heresibus opus suum perduxit supra dietus episcopus Cyprius, apud Grecos inter magnos habitus, et a multis in catholice fidei sanitatelaudatus. Cujus ego in commemorandis hz- reticis non modum, sed ordinem sum secutus. ... Proinde ille de octoginta haresibus, separatis viginti, quas ante Domini adventum exstitisse, sicutei visum est, computavit ; reliquas post Domini ascensum natas sexaginta, brevissimis libris quinque comprehendit ... quinquaginta septem ex Epiphanii ipsius opere in meum transtuli"
(Working sense: "So far, then, the bishop of Cyprus named above carried his work on heresies. Among the Greeks he is counted among the great, and many praise him for the soundness of his catholic faith. In listing heretics I have followed his order, not his measure. ... Of the eighty heresies, he set apart twenty which, as he judged, existed before the Lord's coming; the other sixty, born after the Lord's ascension, he covered in five very short books ... I carried fifty-seven from Epiphanius's own work into mine.")
- Augustine names his sources: the asker Quodvultdeus's letter, AUG 906-912: "Nam etsi Philastrius et Epiphanius venerandi episcopi tale aliquid scripsisse memorantur" (Augustine's correspondent; context).
- Photius, Library, codex 122 (ninth century; about 450 years later), PHOT 11652-11654 and 11678-11683. Later. Documented.
> "Read the Pauaria of the most holy bishop Epiphanlus, against eighty heresies, in three volumes containing seven books. It begins with barbarism and goes down to the Messalians. The author writes more fully and effectually against heretics than any of his predecessors, since he has not omitted any useful argument of theirs, and has added any others that he himself could find. His style is poor, like that of one who is unfamiliar with Attic elegance."
- Photius codex 123, PHOT 11690-11691: "Read the same author's Ancoratus, a sort of synopsis of the Pan an' a."
- John of Damascus's book On Heresies: the PG 94 editors call it "Damasceni epitome Panarii s. operis Epiphanii adversus 80 hereses cum continuatione ad suam usque ætatem" (`cic/texts/john-damascus_opera-omnia-t1-with-vita-pg94-grc-lat_migne1860.txt` 1152-1153). **Editor's claim only; the Greek OCR of the work itself is unreadable.** Not for the story.
- It keeps texts it attacks: E6 (Ptolemy to Flora, whole). Documented that they are copied there.
- Its counts differ: "three books" (Proem I, HOLL1 12394, a phrase Holl supplies from the summary), "three volumes containing seven books" (Photius), the summary in "six books" (Augustine, AUG 2424-2425). The card can carry this.

**I2. The campaign against Origen's books, and the fall of John Chrysostom.**
- The Cyprus decree and Theophilus's use of it: E12, E13.
- What followed: Socrates says John "a short time afterwards was driven from his see" (SS 18229); John's fall was the work of Theophilus's synod (Socrates 6.15 on; Sozomen 8.16-17). Documented that it followed; **no source makes Epiphanius its cause**.
- At the synod that deposed John, one charge was that John "had called St. Epiphanius a fool and a demon" (Photius's summary of the synod's acts, PHOT 3877; later). Documented as a charge only.
- Theophilus's changing view, PALL 8919-8925 (Dialogue): "He vituperated Epiphanius ... as a heretic, or a paltry schismatic, in the time of Damasus or of the blessed Sericius; but afterwards, in his letter to Pope Innocent, in which he reviled the blessed John, we find him calling Epiphanius a most holy saint." Theophilus's own later words, Letter 113 (Jerome's Latin, English only), J6 22470-22474: "he has taken the Origenists into his confidence ... and by committing this crime has saddened with no slight grief that man of God, Epiphanius of blessed memory, who has shone throughout all the world a bright star among bishops."
- Origen's books: "condemned his books", not Origen (SS 18080-18082). Later condemnations of Origen (to 553) are Zenos's note (SS 18125-18130), editor; not for the story.

**I3. Four centuries on, both sides of the quarrel over images claimed him.**
- The Second Council of Nicaea (787), Session 6, MEN 26229-26241 (the iconoclast definition of 754, read out) and MEN 26242-26281 (the council's reply). Later, about 380 years. Documented.
> MEN 26231-26239: "Wherefore he who was illustrious among the champions of the Church, the far-famed Epiphanius Bishop of Cyprus says, ... 'In this respect be mindful, my beloved children, not to introduce images into the churches nor into the cemeteries of the Saints; but labour by memory ever to have God dwelling in your hearts.'"
> MEN 26273-26281: "Just in the same way we find here a passage attributed to Saint Epiphanius which is none of his: for the same divine father, having made a treatise of eighty chapters, utterly triumphs over every heresy, whether Gentile, Jewish, or such as have sprung up since the establishment of Christianity, and he passes by none: had he therefore considered the making of images to have been against the mind of Christ, he no doubt would have placed that also in his catalogue of heresies."
> MEN 26327-26331: "For St. Epiphanius flourished in the time of Theodosius and Arcadius; between that period and the rise of the present heresy is about four hundred years; and no Christian ever admitted these books against images except the ... promoters of this late innovation."
- The Caroline Books, compiled by order of Charlemagne to answer the council (Mendham's title, MEN header line 1), in Mendham's footnote, MEN 26282-26316: "If this same Epiphanius had considered those who despise the worship of images as heretics, he would without doubt have included them in his general treatise on heretics ... But that this same Epiphanius not only despised the worship of images, but would not allow to images any place in churches at all, we have the following proof: for, in an epistle which was sent by him to John Bishop of Jerusalem, which has been translated by Jerome from Greek into Latin, he writes as follows" (then the curtain passage). Documented as the Caroline Books in Mendham's translation, in a note on the page.
- Whether the anti-image writings under his name are his: **Contested** (the council says forged; the iconoclasts cited them). The curtain passage itself was accepted by the Caroline Books as his.

**I4. Remembered as a holy man.**
- Tomb wonders "it was said" (Sozomen, E3).
- Jerome, Lives of Illustrious Men 114, J3 41528-41533, contemporary (Jerome writes that he is "still living"): "Epiphanius, bishop of Salamina in Cyprus, wrote books Against all heresies and many others which are eagerly read by the learned, on account of their subject matter, and also by the plain people, on account of their language. He is still living, and in his extreme old age composes various brief works."
- Photius's opposite judgment of the style: I1.
- Augustine: "apud Grecos inter magnos habitus" (I1).
- Late legend: the Ethiopic Synaxarium keeps the day of his death, 17 Genbot (`cic/texts/ethiopian-synaxarium_book-of-saints-ethiopian-church-v2-v4-microform_budge1928.txt` 29514-29521); the Acta Sanctorum keep 12 May (`cic/texts/acta-sanctorum_maii-tomus-3-lat-grc_palme1866.txt` 16081). Late legend. Not for the story; the card may note the feast.

## 5. Where sources disagree or come late

1. **Did he publicly denounce John?** Socrates yes; Sozomen says Serapion stopped him (E14). Contested.
2. **Theophilus's motive and Epiphanius's judgment.** Socrates: Theophilus pretended, for revenge on Dioscorus and his brothers, and Epiphanius was "a man of simple mind and manners" (E12). Sozomen: Epiphanius "had long regarded the writings of Origen with peculiar aversion". Each is credited.
3. **The charge that he thought God has a human form.** Theophilus made it (Socrates, Sozomen). Jerome shows him condemning that teaching in public (E8). Zenos (editor) sides with Jerome. Contested as a charge; the story credits it to Theophilus through the historians.
4. **Origen's teaching.** Epiphanius's account is a foe's (E9). Socrates defends Origen (SS 18135-18181); a bishop from Scythia read from Origen and found nothing wrong (E13). Rufinus mocked his claim to have read 6,000 books (J3 53897-53901). 
5. **Parting words and predictions.** Hearsay in both historians; Socrates doubts his informants (E16).
6. **Anti-image writings.** The 754 council quoted them as his; Nicaea II called them forged; the Caroline Books replied with the curtain letter (I3). Contested.
7. **Dates.** Editors disagree; no held ancient year (S5).
8. **Letter 51 and Theophilus's letters** survive for us only in Jerome's Latin, and are held only in English. Jerome was charged with mistranslating Letter 51 (J6 13192-13198, Letter 57).
9. **Theophilus's judgment of him changed** (I2).
