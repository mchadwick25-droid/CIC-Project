Epiphanius called his book the Panarion, which means a medicine chest. In its preface he says what it is for. "We are going to show you the names of the heresies. We will uncover their unholy deeds, which are like venom and poison. Alongside them we will fit antidotes: cures for those who have been bitten, and protection for those who may yet fall into it."

A heresy, in his book, is a teaching the church judged false. Epiphanius was a monk from Palestine who became bishop of Salamis, the chief city and port of Cyprus. He named eighty heresies as poisons and set a cure beside each. Later writers, from Augustine to a council of bishops at Nicaea four centuries on, took his book as their list. And in old age he carried the same fight to Jerusalem and then to Constantinople, against the followers of Origen, a Christian teacher who had died long before.

The historian Sozomen, writing later, says he was born near Eleutheropolis in Palestine and chosen bishop by the people of Cyprus. He wrote the Panarion about 375, by his own dating. Each chapter names a heresy, tells its teaching and answers it. He copied his opponents' own writings, so that no one could say he answered hearsay.

In the 390s he preached against Origen in the church at Jerusalem. The scholar Jerome, who was there and on his side, says the party of the bishop of Jerusalem mocked him as an old fool, while crowds pressed to kiss his feet. In a letter to that bishop Epiphanius says why. Origen, he writes, taught that souls were angels who fell into bodies, and read the garden of Eden as hidden meanings. Epiphanius held to the plain sense. Genesis names the Euphrates among the rivers of paradise, and he writes: "I have also drunk from the great river Euphrates. It was not spiritual water but real water, the kind you can touch with your hand and take into your mouth." If the rivers were real, so was Eden.

The same letter defends two acts of force. Jerome's brother had fled the priesthood several times. During a service near his monastery, he says, he had deacons seize the man and stop his mouth. Then he made him a deacon, and later a priest, as the man cried that he was unworthy. In a village church he found a curtain painted with an image of Christ or of one of the saints. He tore it apart and asked that such curtains be banned.

Then Theophilus, bishop of Alexandria, wrote to win him against Origen, though Theophilus had once accused Epiphanius of thinking God has a human form. Epiphanius called the bishops of Cyprus, and they forbade the reading of Origen's books. The historian Socrates says Theophilus wanted revenge on monks he had driven out of Egypt, the Long Brothers, and used a man of simple mind and manners. Sozomen says instead that Epiphanius had long loathed Origen's books.

At Theophilus's urging, he sailed to Constantinople. There he ordained a deacon in the territory of its bishop, John Chrysostom, against church rules. He would not pray with John unless John condemned Origen's books and sent the Long Brothers away. Sozomen says one of those monks asked whether he had read any of their books. He had not, and had judged them by report. Epiphanius sailed for Cyprus, and died at sea before he reached home.

Augustine counted twenty heresies from before Christ and sixty after, and followed Epiphanius's order. Four centuries on, both sides in the quarrel over holy images claimed him. Opponents of images quoted writings under his name. In 787 the council at Nicaea ruled that the passage was not his, and its bishops argued that his eighty chapters never listed the making of images. A reply written at Charlemagne's order answered with his own curtain letter.

Epiphanius set one thing more after the eighty heresies in his chest of cures: the church, Christ's holy bride, which he says was there from the beginning.
