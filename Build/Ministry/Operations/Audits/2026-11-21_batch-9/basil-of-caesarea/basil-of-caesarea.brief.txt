Years later, defending himself in a letter, Basil of Caesarea looked back on his youth. He had wasted nearly all of it, he wrote, in the vain labor of learning a wisdom God had made foolish. Then: "Then one day, like a man roused from deep sleep, I turned my eyes to the wonderful light of the truth of the gospel."

That waking shaped the church in Caesarea, an inland city in Asia Minor. Basil gave up teaching rhetoric for a life of prayer and shared work. He wrote rules that taught monks to live together and serve one another, and he built a great lodging for the poor and the sick a little way outside the city. As bishop from 370, he defended the Holy Spirit's place beside the Father and the Son. He died in 378 or 379, before a council confessed it.

He taught rhetoric, the art of public speaking, the historian Rufinus says. Who turned him away? In his own telling, he read in the gospel of selling goods and sharing with the poor, and went looking for monks. His brother, Gregory of Nyssa, gives the credit to their sister Macrina. Rufinus says his friend Gregory took him from the teacher's chair.

In Pontus, near the Black Sea, Basil founded a community and led it, Gregory of Nazianzus says. His rules were answers to his brothers' questions, and one asks whether to live alone or with others. Love does not seek its own good, Basil answers, and Christ washed his disciples' feet. Then he asks: "Whom, then, will you wash? Whom will you care for? Behind whom will you be last, if you live by yourself?" A man alone has no one to serve.

The bishop of Caesarea made him a priest, though they quarreled. When the emperor Valens, who favored the party that ranked the Son below the Father, moved against the churches that held them equal, Basil came back to serve the bishop. In a great famine he fed the hungry and tended them himself, Gregory says.

When the old bishop died, Gregory says, Basil was chosen against the bishops of the province. The emperor's highest official ordered him to accept the emperor's faith, on pain of losing his goods, exile, torture and death. What follows is Gregory's speech in Basil's memory, so it is praise. In his telling, Basil answered that a man who owns only rags and a few books cannot be robbed, and that death would only send him sooner to God. Basil made his brother bishop of Nyssa and his friend Gregory bishop of Sasima, a pain, Gregory said, that time had not healed.

Accused before the governor, Basil wrote back: whom does it harm to build a lodging for strangers, travelers and the sick, with doctors and transport? Gregory says it stood a little way from the city, filled by the rich at Basil's urging. Lepers, driven from their towns, were taken in, and Basil greeted them as brothers and tended them himself, Gregory says.

Then came the fight over the Spirit. Basil closed prayers with praise to the Father with the Son, together with the Holy Spirit, and at other times through the Son, in the Holy Spirit. Some attacked these as new words that contradicted each other. In a letter he answered from what the church already did: "As we were baptized, so we confess our faith. As we confess our faith, so too we give praise." Gregory admits that Basil held back the bare words that the Spirit is God, from caution.

Rufinus put the Rule into Latin. In the sixth century Benedict of Nursia ended his own Rule by pointing his monks to Basil's as an example of good living. In 787 a council told the houses where men and women lived as monks, side by side but apart, to keep it. And in 381 a council at Constantinople confessed the Spirit worshipped and glorified with the Father and the Son.

Gregory says the whole city poured around Basil as he died. At his funeral the crowds filled the market places, the porches and the houses, and in the crush, Gregory says, many died.
