# Outline: Evagrius Ponticus (`evagrius-ponticus`)

Every line points to `Source_Sheet.md` (S, E, I numbers) and through it to the file and lines. Files are under `cic/texts/`.

**The essence:** Evagrius Ponticus, a gifted deacon at Constantinople, fled the city over a love affair and became a monk in the Egyptian desert, where he named the eight tempting thoughts that trouble a monk and taught that prayer is the mind's own work. Long after his death, councils named him with Origen among the condemned, yet monks in the Latin West and the Syriac East went on using what he taught.
- Deacon and flight: Palladius, clarke1918 : 457 §2-7 (E1-E2); Socrates IV.23, npnf202 : 13529; Sozomen VI.30, npnf202 : 39669-39685. Documented as reported; the affair rests on Palladius and Sozomen, who differ.
- Eight thoughts, prayer: Praktikos 6 and 49, dysinger : 187-197, 373-375 (E5). Documented.
- Condemned long after: councils of 680, 692, 787, npnf214 : 25789-25791, 26636-26641, 39712-39715 (I3). Documented that they say so; whether the council of 553 itself named him is Contested (Hefele, hefele v4 : 14656-14660).
- Teaching used later: Gennadius, npnf203 : 42330-42347; Cassian, npnf211 : 20092-20097; Thomas of Marga, budge1893 : 22401-22404 (I1-I2). Documented.

**Also told:**
- What the book says: eight "generic" thoughts, named in order (gluttony, sexual immorality, love of money, sadness, anger, acedia, vainglory, pride); the thought comes unbidden, but whether it stays is up to us (Q2). Acedia, the "noonday demon", makes the day seem fifty hours long (dysinger : 215-217). Documented.
- Prayer: we are not told to work, keep vigil or fast all the time, but there is a law to pray without ceasing, "since the mind is naturally made for prayer" (dysinger : 373-375). Documented.
- A second book, not held, set verses of Scripture against each of the eight thoughts, as Christ answered his tempter with Scripture (Socrates, npnf202 : 13540-13543; Gennadius, npnf203 : 42330-42335). Documented.
- How the desert life went: two years at Nitria, fourteen at the Cells; a pound of bread (no period given), a pint of oil in three months; he earned by writing only the value of what he ate; he made a hundred prayers (no period given) (clarke1918 : 459 §10). Documented as Palladius's report.
- What changed: a city debater learned, Socrates says, "the philosophy of deeds" from the old desert monks (npnf202 : 13527-13529). Documented (Socrates).
- The hostile view: Jerome in 415 ranked him with heretics over his teaching on freedom from passion (Q3). Documented.
Where the sources will not hold a claim: no held text gives him the treatise *On Prayer* (the Philokalia prints it under Nilus); no held text says Cassian learned from him; the council of 553's surviving acts do not name him (Hefele). What they do hold is set out above.

**Setting:**
- Century: the late 300s; born about 345 (editors: Richardson, npnf203 : 42324 n.; census), died at fifty-four (Palladius, clarke1918 : 455 §1), in 399 or 400 by Clarke's reckoning (clarke1918 : 1177). Say "the late fourth century"; give no birth year in the story.
- Places, each as the story reaches it: Pontus, on the Black Sea, in what is now northern Turkey (Ibora, clarke1918 : 457 §2; Sozomen "near the Euxine", npnf202 : 39666-39667); Constantinople, "the great city", the eastern capital (457 §2); Jerusalem (457 §7-8); Nitria and then the Cells, in the Egyptian desert (459 §10; Sozomen VI.31, npnf202 : 39696-39698).
- Tradition: census "Desert Monasticism", informal "Desert Fathers and Mothers". Plainest spoken form: "the monks of the Egyptian desert".

**Words to explain:**
- country-bishop: "a bishop for the countryside" (Clarke's note, clarke1918 : 1155).
- the Cells: "a settlement of hermits' huts deeper in the desert, called the Cells" (clarke1918 : 459 §10, "the place they call Cellia").
- tempting thoughts (*logismoi*): "thoughts that tempt a monk"; use "tempting thoughts", not "sins", for Evagrius's own word (dysinger : 187). Gennadius and Cassian call them "principal sins/faults": use their words only when crediting them.
- passions: "the strong, unruly feelings the monks fought" (dysinger : 197; 327, ch. 35: "The passions of the body are cut back by self-control; those of the soul are cut back by spiritual love.").
- acedia: "a listless boredom the monks called the noonday demon" (dysinger : 215).
- freedom from passion (*apatheia*): "a calm the passions no longer stir" (dysinger : 161; Jerome's "impassivity", npnf206 : 27824-27825).
- Origen's followers / Origenists: "followers of Origen, a third-century teacher of Alexandria, who held, among other things, that souls existed before bodies" (Trullo canon 1, npnf214 : 26639-26640: "the circlings of certain bodies and souls"; Cyril of Scythopolis, 7762-7763: "de praeexistentia").
- Epiphany: "the feast of Epiphany" (clarke1918 : 459 §13); no date added.
- Not glossed: deacon, bishop, Scripture, gospel, monk.

**Shape:** Monk or ascetic. One wonder only, the vision, kept in Palladius's voice.

Job of this story: Evagrius's turn from city to desert, what he taught, and how it travelled despite his condemnation.

**Openings:**
1. (Preferred) The vision. In Constantinople, one night, a young deacon saw in a vision soldiers seize him and chain him among forty prisoners. A friend came, offered to free him, and made him swear on the gospel to leave the city. He came out of the trance and reasoned with himself: Q1. (clarke1918 : 457 §4-7.) Lead straight into the essence: he packed his things and sailed; the man who kept that oath was Evagrius Ponticus.
2. Jerome's charge, 415: a monk's book claims a calm in which the mind "becomes either a stone or a god" (Q3). Who wrote it, and how did a deacon of the capital come to write it? (npnf206 : 27824-27826.) Reaches ahead; use only if opening 1 is not used.

**Story beats:**
1. **The city.** Born in Pontus, son of a country-bishop. Basil of Caesarea made him a reader; after Basil's death, Gregory of Nazianzus made him deacon. At the great synod of 381 Gregory left him with the new bishop of the city, because he was skilled in arguing against every heresy; Evagrius "flourished in the great city", honoured across it. Reason for the act: his skill in argument (Palladius gives it). Source: clarke1918 : 457 §2; 1157-1159 (n. 259-260); Socrates npnf202 : 13529; Sozomen 39667-39669 (says archdeacon). Confidence: Documented for Gregory (three witnesses); Widely Accepted for Basil and the bishop (Palladius alone).
2. **The love and the oath.** A married woman of the highest rank loved him, and he her. Fearing God, his conscience and the gloating of the heretics, he prayed for something to stand in the way, yet could not break off. Then came the vision (if the opening used it, refer back in a clause only, never retell it). He sailed for Jerusalem. Palladius says Evagrius told him of this love himself, years later, when such thoughts no longer held him. Sozomen, a historian a generation on, tells it harder: her husband was plotting his death. Palladius says only that Evagrius imagined the husband had acted. Source: clarke1918 : 457 §3-7; npnf202 : 39670-39686. Confidence: Documented as each witness's report; the husband's plot Contested.
3. **Melania.** In Jerusalem Melania, a Roman lady, took him in. He wavered, went back to his old clothes and his old talk, and fell ill, first with fever, then with an illness that lasted six months. When the doctors gave up, Melania told him his illness was not without God and asked what his thoughts were. He confessed everything. She made him promise before the Lord to live as a monk, and said she would pray for him. Within a few days he was well; he took new clothes from her own hands and went to Nitria in Egypt. Source: clarke1918 : 457 §8-9; 1167, 1171 (Clarke's notes on the clothes). Confidence: Widely Accepted (Palladius alone).
4. **The desert.** Two years at Nitria, then fourteen at the Cells. He lived on a pound of bread and a pint of oil every three months, "though he was a man who had come from a luxurious and refined and voluptuous life." He wrote a fine hand, and in a year wrote only enough to pay for what he ate. He made a hundred prayers. Socrates says the old monks, the two Macarii, taught him the philosophy of deeds, where before he had known only that of words; Evagrius's own book tells of asking Macarius for water at midday and being told to make do with the shade. Desire still troubled him: he told Palladius he stood a winter night naked in a well. A circle formed round him, and Palladius was one of it. Source: clarke1918 : 459 §10, §14; 423 §5; npnf202 : 13527-13529, 13711-13712; dysinger : 554-555. Confidence: Documented (Palladius and Socrates, his own words).
5. **The book.** In his Praktikos, written for a fellow monk, he names eight tempting thoughts that hold every other: gluttony, lust, love of money, sadness, anger, acedia, vainglory, pride. Q2. Acedia, the noonday demon, makes the sun seem to stand still and the day last fifty hours. Of prayer he writes that no law makes a monk work, keep vigil or fast all the time, but a law tells him to pray without ceasing, "since the mind is naturally made for prayer." A second book, now lost, set verses of Scripture against each of the eight, as Christ answered his tempter (Gennadius). Source: dysinger : 187-197, 215-217, 373-375; npnf202 : 13540-13543; npnf203 : 42330-42335. Confidence: Documented.

**What came of them** (about a third of the story), heaviest first:
1. **The eight, carried west.** Gennadius of Marseilles, about eighty years later, says he was the first to teach the eight, "or among the first at least", and put that book into Latin himself, with his books of chapters. John Cassian, who wrote down talks with the Egyptian fathers, gave eight books of his Institutes to the same eight, with sadness and anger in the other order, written for a bishop in Gaul who wanted the ways of Egypt's monks in his province; he never names Evagrius. Source: npnf203 : 42330-42347, 43253-43256; npnf211 : 20092-20097, 30132-30137, 16433-16437 (Castor; Gaul is the editor's note 634). Documented (the link of influence is not told: DMR).
2. **Prayer, quoted in the East.** In 840 Thomas of Marga, a monk and bishop of the Church of the East in Mesopotamia, quotes "the holy Evagrius" on prayer in Syriac: the very words of Praktikos 49 above. (Optional clause: the Greek Philokalia of 1782 printed his chapters under the name of "Evagrius the Monk.") Source: budge1893 : 22386-22404; philokalia v1 : 4205, 58634-58641. Documented.
3. **The charge, told openly.** Jerome, writing against Pelagius in 415, ranked him with heretics: his book on freedom from passion promised a calm in which, Jerome said, Q3. Jerome adds that the book was "widely read, in the East in Greek and in the West in a Latin translation." Later councils, from 680 to 787, listed him with Origen and Didymus as condemned at Constantinople in 553; the council of 692 charged all three with bringing back Greek myths of bodies and souls going round in cycles. A nineteenth-century historian of the councils notes that the surviving acts of 553 do not name him at all. Source: npnf206 : 27824-27828; npnf214 : 25789-25791, 26636-26641, 39712-39715; hefele v4 : 14656-14660, 14713-14719. Documented (that they condemned him), Contested (553 itself).
- What he defended (marked): the faith of Nicaea in the capital's debates, "speaking with youthful zeal against every heresy" (clarke1918 : 457 §2).
- Who keeps him today: no held source says; leave out.

**Connection to carry into the story:** What passed on was a working map of temptation: eight named thoughts, each with its remedy from Scripture, and the claim that a thought's coming is not ours but its staying is. Gennadius in Gaul had the eight-part book in Greek, called him the first or among the first to teach the eight, and put it into Latin himself (npnf203 : 42330-42337). The same eight, nearly in his order, fill eight books of Cassian's Institutes for the monks of Gaul (npnf211 : 20092-20097). The writer must say that Cassian does not name him, so the listener hears a match of lists, not a stated debt. In the East, his line that the mind is made for prayer reached a Syriac monk-bishop four and a half centuries later, quoted by name (budge1893 : 22401-22404). The listener needs to hear that this happened after the councils had counted him among the condemned: the condemnation and the reading run side by side, and the story tells both without judging between them. Tell it through these acts of translating and quoting, not with a "this matters because" sentence.

**Ending:**
1. (Preferred) His death, told last once the connection lands. Shortly before he died he told his friends that for three years fleshly desire had not troubled him, "after so long a life and toil and labour and ceaseless prayer." He died at fifty-four, Palladius writes, after receiving communion in church at the feast of Epiphany. (clarke1918 : 455 §1, 459 §13. That Palladius was present is Clarke's reading, 1177; do not say "with Palladius beside him".) The image of the opening seen again: the desire he fled the city over, quiet at the last. Quote marks only if a fourth quote is allowed; otherwise paraphrase.
2. His own last lines in the Praktikos: he asks for the prayers of "the righteous Gregory who planted me" and of the holy fathers "who now water me" (dysinger : 580). Paraphrase; do not name which Gregory.

**Do not say:**
- "Evagrius Scholasticus" or the historian Evagrius. Say: Evagrius Ponticus, the monk.
- "the council of 553 condemned him" as fact. Say: later councils, from 680, said the council of 553 had condemned him; its surviving acts do not name him (Hefele).
- "he wrote a treatise On Prayer" or "his chapters On Prayer". Say: he wrote of prayer in his Praktikos.
- "Cassian learned the eight from him" / "Cassian copied him" / "his student Cassian". Say: the same eight appear in Cassian's Institutes; Cassian never names him.
- "the first to teach the eight" without the qualifier. Say: "the first, or among the first at least" (Gennadius).
- "the husband plotted to kill him" as fact. Say: Sozomen says so; Palladius says Evagrius imagined the husband had acted.
- "an affair", "they became lovers", "he slept with her". The record says she loved him in return and he could not break away (clarke1918 : 457 §3). Say: "he fell in love with a married woman of high rank, and she with him."
- "an angel freed him" in the narrator's voice. Say: Palladius says the friend was an angel in a friend's shape.
- "Melania healed him." Say: she promised to pray, and within a few days he was well.
- "a pound of bread a day", "a hundred prayers a day". Palladius gives no period (Budge's "daily allowance" is an editor's summary). Say: "a pound of bread", "a hundred prayers".
- "he copied books for a living" as Palladius's words. Palladius says he wrote only the value of what he ate and wrote a fine hand; that it was books for sale is Clarke's reading (clarke1918 : 1173).
- "Evagrius told Palladius of the vision." Palladius says Evagrius told him of the desire (457 §3); he does not say who told him the vision.
- "archdeacon" without credit. Say: deacon (Palladius, Socrates); Sozomen alone says archdeacon.
- "Gregory of Nyssa" ordained him. Say: Gregory of Nazianzus.
- Adding "of Nazianzus" to the Gregory of the Praktikos epilogue. Say: "the righteous Gregory who planted me".
- "he never returned to Constantinople." Not in the sources.
- "Gennadius, a contemporary". Say: writing about eighty years later.
- "he was condemned in his lifetime" or "he called himself a follower of Origen". Neither is held.
- "Jerome's council" or "the church condemned him in 415". Say: Jerome's letter, written against Pelagius.
- "sins" for Evagrius's own eight. Say: tempting thoughts (Gennadius's and Cassian's "sins" and "faults" only when credited).
- "he died in 399" as fact. Say: at fifty-four; "about 399" only if a year is needed, as the editors' reckoning.
- Any name for the woman, her husband or the governor. None is given.
