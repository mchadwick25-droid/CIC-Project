One night in Constantinople, the deacon Evagrius saw in a vision that soldiers had seized him and chained him among forty prisoners. A friend came and offered to free him, but first made him swear on the gospel to leave the city. Palladius says the friend was an angel in a friend's shape. He woke and reasoned with himself. "Even if the oath was sworn in a trance, I still swore it." He sailed.

That oath made Evagrius Ponticus a monk of the Egyptian desert, where he named eight tempting thoughts that trouble a monk and taught that prayer is the mind's own work. Long after his death, councils named him with Origen among the condemned. Yet monks in the Latin West and the Syriac East went on using what he taught.

He was born in Pontus, on the Black Sea, the son of a bishop for the countryside. Gregory of Nazianzus made him a deacon, and in 381 left him with the new bishop of the great city, since Evagrius argued so well against every heresy. Palladius, his disciple, is our main witness.

A married woman of the highest rank fell in love with him, and he with her. He prayed for something to stand in the way, yet could not break away. Palladius says Evagrius told him of this himself, years later. Sozomen, a church historian a generation on, tells it harder: the woman's husband was plotting his death. Palladius says only that Evagrius imagined the husband had acted. Then came the vision, and he sailed for Jerusalem.

There Melania, a Roman lady, took him in. He slipped back into his old ways and fell ill, and the doctors gave up. Melania asked what his thoughts were, and he confessed everything. She made him promise before the Lord to live as a monk, and said she would pray for him. Within days he was well, and he went to Egypt.

He spent two years at Nitria, then fourteen at the Cells, a settlement of hermits' huts deeper in the desert. Palladius reports he lived on a pound of bread and a pint of oil in three months. Socrates, another historian, says the debater learned the philosophy of deeds from the old monks.

Out of that life came his book, the Praktikos. It names eight tempting thoughts: gluttony, lust, love of money, sadness, anger, acedia, vainglory and pride. Acedia, a listless boredom the monks called the noonday demon, makes the day seem fifty hours long. "Whether these thoughts can trouble the soul is not up to us. But whether they linger, and whether they stir up passions, is up to us." On prayer he writes that no law makes a monk work or fast all the time, but a law tells him to pray without ceasing, since the mind is made for prayer.

Gennadius, a priest of Marseilles writing eighty years later, calls him the first to teach the eight, "or among the first at least", and put that book into Latin himself. John Cassian gave eight books of his Institutes to the same eight, for the monks of Gaul. Cassian never names Evagrius; we can say only that the lists match. In the East, in 840, the monk and bishop Thomas of Marga quoted "the holy Evagrius" in Syriac on that law to pray continually.

In 415 Jerome, writing against Pelagius, ranked Evagrius with heretics for a book that promised freedom from passion, a calm the passions no longer stir. "In that state the mind is stirred no more. To put it simply, it becomes either a stone or a god." Later councils, from 680 to 787, listed Evagrius with Origen and Didymus as condemned at Constantinople in 553. A nineteenth-century historian of the councils notes that the surviving acts of 553 do not name him at all.

Shortly before he died, Evagrius told his friends that for three years the desire he had fled the city over had not troubled him, after so long a life of ceaseless prayer. He died at fifty-four, Palladius writes, after receiving communion in church at the feast of Epiphany.
