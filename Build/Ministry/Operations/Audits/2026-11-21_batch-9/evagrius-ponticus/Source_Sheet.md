# Source sheet: Evagrius Ponticus (`evagrius-ponticus`)

Census: name "Evagrius Ponticus", dates "c. 345-399", toldWithin `desert-monasticism` (census name "Desert Monasticism", informal name "Desert Fathers and Mothers"). Not Evagrius Scholasticus, the sixth-century historian, and not Evagrius of Antioch, the presbyter in Basil's and Jerome's letters.

Paths are under `cic/texts/`. Witness kinds: **own** (his own words), **contemp.** (written in or near his lifetime by someone who knew him or his world), **later (+gap)**, **late legend**. Confidence: Documented (D), Widely Accepted (WA), Dominant Modern Reconstruction (DMR), Contested (C), Inferential-Thin (IT).

Main witnesses:
- **Palladius**, *Lausiac History* ch. 38, tr. W. K. Lowther Clarke (1918), `palladius_lausiac-history_clarke1918.txt`. His disciple; written c. 419-420 (Budge's and the corpus map's dating, editor). Tells much "as he himself told us". The Greek is not held. Clarke's text runs each chapter as one long line, so the line holds several numbered sections; the section number is given beside it.
- **Socrates of Constantinople**, *Church History* III.7 and IV.23, NPNF II.2 English (`npnf202_socrates-sozomen-ecclesiastical-histories.xml`), Greek in Hussey 1853 (`socrates_historia-ecclesiastica-tomus2-grc-lat_hussey1853.txt`, OCR fair, some letters broken). Writes a generation after his death; quotes his books.
- **Sozomen**, *Church History* VI.30, same NPNF file. Same generation as Socrates.
- **Evagrius**, *Praktikos*, tr. Luke Dysinger (Guide to Evagrius Ponticus, CC BY 4.0, NOT public domain: Dysinger and the Guide must be credited), `evagrius_praktikos_dysinger.txt`. The Greek of the Praktikos is not held, except the chapters 91-99 that Socrates quotes (Hussey).

## 2. Events, in order

**E1. Reader under Basil; deacon under Gregory; left at Constantinople.**
- palladius…clarke1918.txt : 457 (§2), contemp., D for the ordinations (Socrates and Sozomen agree on Gregory), WA for Basil and Nectarius (Palladius alone): "He was ordained reader by the holy Basil, the bishop of the church of Caesarea. After the death of the holy Basil, Gregory Nazianzea the bishop, that very wise and most impassive and highly cultured man, ordained him deacon. Then at the great synod of Constantinople he left him to the blessed Nectarius the bishop, since he was skilled in argument against all heresies."
- Clarke's note on which Gregory, same : 1157 (note 259), editor: "Not Gregory of Nyssa, as Palladius seemed to say, in contradiction of Soz. VI. 30, before the true text was established." Note 260 (line 1159) dates the synod "A.D. 381."
- Socrates IV.23, npnf202_… : 13529, later: "He was ordained deacon at Constantinople by Gregory of Nazianzus". Greek, Hussey tomus2 : 2998-2999: "ὃς ἐν τῇ Κωνσταντινουπόλει ὑπὸ Γρηγορίου τοῦ Ναζιίανζηνοῦ εἰς τὴν τοῦ διακόνου" (OCR).
- Sozomen VI.30, npnf202_… : 39667-39669, later: "He had philosophized and studied the Sacred Scriptures under Gregory, bishop of Nazianzen, and had filled the office of archdeacon when Gregory administered the church in Constantinople."
- Disagreement: Sozomen alone says "archdeacon". The 1782 Philokalia's life and Budge's note to Thomas of Marga follow the older text of Palladius and give the deacon's ordination to Gregory of Nyssa (philokalia_ton-ieron-neptikon-v1-grc_tzelatis1893.txt : 4133-4136; thomas-of-marga_…budge1893.txt : 2353-2354). The NPNF editors' notes to Socrates (npnf202_… : 13522-13524) and to Jerome (npnf206_… : 27811-27813) say the same of Palladius, and the Jerome note wrongly gives Gregory of Nyssa to Sozomen. These are editors' readings and are set aside.
- What he defended, Palladius §2 above: he argued "against every heresy". Palladius §11 (line 459) has three demons as an Arian, a Eunomian and an Apollinarian, a wonder (Leave out).

**E2. The love affair and the vision.** Palladius says he tells it as Evagrius told it.
- palladius…clarke1918.txt : 457 (§3), contemp., D as Palladius's report: "Now it happened that this man, who was held in high honour by the whole city, was congealed by an image of the desire of a woman, as he himself told us at a later time, when his soul was freed from such thoughts. The woman loved him in return; now she belonged to the highest rank. So Evagrius, fearing God and respecting his own conscience, and putting before his eyes the greatness of the shame and the malicious joy of the heresies, prayed to God in supplication that he would put some obstacle in the way. Now the woman was pressing and madly excited, while he, though desiring to withdraw, had no power to, being constrained by the chains of this servitude."
- Same (§4): "there appeared to him an angel vision in the shape of soldiers of the governor, and they seized him and took him apparently to the tribunal and threw him into the so-called custody … But he knew in his conscience that for the sake of the above fault he was suffering these things, and imagined that her husband had intervened."
- Same (§5): "the angel who brought the vision transformed himself to represent the coming of a genuine friend and said to him, tied up as he was among forty prisoners chained together: 'Why are you retained here, my lord deacon?'"
- Same (§6): "He said to him: 'If you will listen to your friend, it is not expedient for you to stay in this city.' … 'Let me bring the gospel, and swear to me by it that you will leave this city and care for your soul, and I will free you from this durance.'"
- Same (§7), **quote Q1 source**: "So he brought the gospel and he swore to him by the gospel: 'Except for one day, to give me time to put my clothes on board, I certainly will not remain.' So when the oath had been produced, he came back out of the trance which had come on him in the night; and he arose and argued with himself: 'Even if the oath was in a trance, nevertheless I did take it.' So having put all his belongings into the ship he went to Jerusalem."
- Clarke on the Greek of §3, line 1161 (note 261), editor: "The rendering given hardly makes sense." So "congealed by an image of the desire of a woman" is a doubtful English; the plain sense (he fell in love) is safe, the wording is not.
- Sozomen VI.30, npnf202_… : 39670-39686, later, C on the husband: "He was handsome in person, and careful in his mode of attire; and hence an acquaintanceship he had formed with a certain lady excited the jealousy of her husband, who plotted his death. While the plot was about being carried forward into deed, God sent him while sleeping, a fearful and saving vision in a dream. … a man who held in his hand the book of the Holy Gospels addressed him, and promised to deliver him from his bonds, and confirmed this with an oath, provided he would quit the city. Evagrius touched the book, and made oath that he would do so. Immediately his chains appeared to fall off, and he awoke. He was convinced by this divine dream, and fled the danger. He resolved upon devoting himself to a life of asceticism, and proceeded from Constantinople to Jerusalem."
- **Disagreement:** Sozomen says the husband "plotted his death"; Palladius says only that Evagrius "imagined that her husband had intervened". Socrates IV.23 (npnf202_… : 13529-13530) has no affair and a different route: "and afterwards went with him into Egypt". Card carries the route.

**E3. Jerusalem and Melania.**
- palladius…clarke1918.txt : 457 (§8), contemp., WA (Palladius alone for Melania's part): "And there he was received by the blessed Melania, the Roman lady. But once again the devil hardened his heart, as he did Pharaoh's, and since he was young and vigorous doubts beset him, and he hesitated, saying nothing to any one, and changing his clothes and his habit of speech back to his old ways, vain-glory stupefying him. But God Who wards off destruction from us all involved him in a bout of fever, and after that in a long illness lasting six months, drying up his flesh, the source of his trouble."
- Same (§9): "But when the physicians were at a loss and could find no way of cure, the blessed Melania said to him: 'Son, your long illness does not please me. Tell me therefore what are your thoughts. For this illness of yours is not without God.' Then he confessed to her the whole matter. But she said to him: 'Give me your word before the Lord that you will keep to the mark of the monastic life; and, sinner though I am, I will pray that you may be granted a furlough of life.' And he consented. So within a few days he got well, and he arose and received a change of clothes at the hands of the lady herself and went away and exiled himself in the mount of Nitria which is in Egypt."
- Clarke's notes, editor: line 1167 (note 264) on §8 "changing his clothes": "That is, from the clerical to the lay. But the text is difficult and probably corrupt."; line 1171 (note 266): "I. e. clerical or monastic clothes."
- Melania's house, Palladius ch. 46, line 501 (§5): "After they were recalled she founded a monastery in Jerusalem, and spent twenty-seven years there in charge of a convent of fifty virgins. With her lived also the most noble Rufinus, from Italy, of the city of Aquileia".
- Sozomen (39684, above) gives Jerusalem but not Melania.

**E4. The desert: Nitria, then the Cells.**
- palladius…clarke1918.txt : 459 (§10), contemp., D as Palladius's report: "Having lived there two years, in the third year he entered the desert. So he lived fourteen years in the place they call Cellia, and he used to eat a pound of bread, and in three months a pint of oil, though he was a man who had come from a luxurious and refined and voluptuous life. And he made 100 prayers; and he wrote during the year only the value of what he ate----for he wrote the Oxyrhyncus characters excellently. So in the course of fifteen years having purified his mind to the utmost he was counted worthy of the gift of knowledge and wisdom and the discerning of spirits."
- Clarke line 1173 (note 267), editor, on "Oxyrhyncus characters": "this must refer to some sort of handwriting reserved for MSS. de luxe."
- Same (§14, as numbered in the file), Evagrius's own telling: "The demon of fornication troubled him grievously, as indeed he told us himself. And all night long he stood naked in the well, though it was winter, so that his flesh was frozen."
- Same (§12): "And he said: 'From the time that I took to the desert, I have not touched lettuce nor any other green vegetable, nor any fruit, nor grapes, nor meat, nor a bath.'"
- His teachers. Socrates IV.23, npnf202_… : 13521-13532 (Greek, Hussey : 2996-2998), later, D: "Evagrius became a disciple of these men, acquired from them the philosophy of deeds, whereas he had previously known that which consisted in words only." ("these men" are the two Macarii, 13509-13520.) Greek, verbatim OCR: "Τούτων Εὐάγριος γενόμενος μαθητὴς vo / τὴν δὶ ἔργων φιλοσοφίαν ἐκτήσατο, πρότερον λόγῳ / μόνῳ φιλόσοφος ὦν" ("vo" is a stray line number).
- Gennadius ch. 11, npnf203_… : 42324-42325, later (~80 yrs): "Evagrius the monk, the intimate disciple of the above mentioned Macarius".
- His own words, Praktikos 94, evagrius_praktikos_dysinger.txt : 554-555, own, D: "I once visited the holy father Macarius right in the full heat of the day, and I was burning with thirst and asked for some water to drink He said, 'Make do with the shade; there are many people traveling or sailing at this very moment, and they lack even shade'". (Also in Socrates IV.23, 13583-13587.)
- A school of his own. Palladius, ch. 35 (John of Lycopolis), line 423 (§5): "'For I conjecture that you belong to the convent of Evagrius.' … And I confessed that I belonged to Evagrius' society." Palladius, ch. 26 (Pachon), line 361 (§1): "nor to my teacher Evagrius". (The John of Lycopolis journey is told in the live `palladius-of-helenopolis` story; not retold here.)
- Socrates IV.23, npnf202_… : 13711-13715, later, D: "composed by the monk Palladius, who was a disciple of Evagrius … Both Evagrius and Palladius flourished a short time after the death of Valens."
- The bishop's offer, Socrates IV.23, npnf202_… : 13688-13697, later, WA (Socrates alone): "But when long afterwards Evagrius, whom Theophilus, bishop of Alexandria, wished to make a bishop, having effected his escape without maiming himself in any way, afterwards happened to meet Ammonius … Ammonius replied, 'And do you think, Evagrius, that you will not be punished, who from self-love have cut out your own tongue, to avoid the exercise of that gift of utterance which has been committed to you?'"
- Sayings of the desert (Anan-isho, Syriac, tr. Budge), anan-isho_paradise-v2-sayings_budge1907.txt : 170, 172, late collection (7th c. compiler): §59 "Now Evagrius was sorry, but he was not offended … 'Verily, it is even as thou sayest, O my fathers; I have spoken once, but I will not do it a second time.'" (Leave out; card may carry.)

**E5. What he wrote.**
- The eight thoughts, Praktikos 6, evagrius_praktikos_dysinger.txt : 187-197, own, D; **quote Q2 source** at 197:
  "There are eight generic [tempting-] thoughts (logismoi), that contain within themselves every [tempting-]thought: / first is that of gluttony; / and with it, sexual immorality; / third, love of money; / fourth, sadness; / fifth, anger; / sixth acedia; / seventh, vainglory; / eighth, pride. / Whether these thoughts are able to disturb the soul or not is not up to us; but whether they linger or not, and whether they arouse passions or not; that is up to us."
- Acedia, Praktikos 12, lines 215-216: "The demon of acedia, which is also called the noonday demon, is the most burdensome of all the demons. It besets the monk at about the fourth hour (10 am) of the morning, encircling his soul until about the eighth hour (2 pm)." Line 217: "First it makes the sun seem to slow down or stop moving, so that the day appears to be fifty hours long."
- Prayer, Praktikos 49, lines 373-375, own, D: "We are not commanded to work the whole time or to keep vigil the whole time or to fast the whole time, but there is a law that we should / pray ceaselessly [1Th 5.17]. … but prayer makes the mind strong and pure for the struggle, since the mind is naturally made for prayer and it is natural to it to fight demons even without the body, on behalf of all the powers of the soul."
- Freedom from passion, Praktikos prologue 8, lines 157-161: "from these is born apatheia [dispassion], which brings into being love." Praktikos 81, line 497: "Charity is the offspring of apatheia; apatheia is the flower of the ascetical life (praktike)."
- His teacher Gregory, Praktikos epilogue, lines 577-580, own, D (the Gregory is not named further; that he is Gregory of Nazianzus is a reading, DMR): "at the prayers and intercession of the righteous Gregory who planted me, and at those of the holy fathers who now water me, and by the power of Jesus Christ our Lord who makes me grow". Also The Gnostic, quoted by Socrates IV.23, npnf202_… : 13633-13635: "We have learned from Gregory the Just, that there are four virtues".
- The Antirrhetikos (not held). Palladius line 459 (§10): "So he composed three holy books for monks, called Antirrhetica, in which he taught the arts to be used against demons." Socrates IV.23, npnf202_… : 13536-13542, later, D: "A third is designated Antirrheticus, and contains selections from the Holy Scriptures against tempting spirits, distributed into eight parts, according to the number of the arguments." (Greek, Hussey : 3044-3046: "ἐν ὀκτὼ διῃρημένον μέρεσι κατὰ τὸν ἀριθμὸν τῶν ὀκτὼ λογισμῶν", "according to the number of the eight thoughts": the NPNF "arguments" is a weak English for λογισμῶν.) Gennadius ch. 11, npnf203_… : 42329-42335: "Suggestions against the eight principal sins. He was first to mention or among the first at least to teach these setting against them eight books taken from the testimony of the Holy Scriptures only, after the example of our Lord, who always met his tempter with quotations from Scripture, so that every suggestion, whether of the devil or of depraved nature had a testimony against it."
- Other titles, Socrates IV.23, npnf202_… : 13533-13548: *The Monk, or, On Active Virtue*; *The Gnostic* "divided into fifty chapters"; *Six Hundred Prognostic Problems*; two verse works "To the Monks living in Communities" and "To the Virgin". "Whoever shall read these productions will be convinced of their excellence." (card, Remembered).
- God beyond definition, Socrates III.7, npnf202_… : 10602-10617, later quoting own: "'for,' says he, 'definition belongs only to things which are compound.' … 'Let then what is inexplicable be adored in silence.'" (The NPNF note at 10603-10604, "The only work of Evagrius preserved to our days is his Ecclesiastical History", confuses him with Evagrius Scholasticus: editor's error, set aside.)

**E6. Death.**
- palladius…clarke1918.txt : 459 (§13), contemp., D: "And later, in the sixteenth year of his life without cooked food, his flesh felt a need, owing to the weakness of the stomach, to partake of (something that had been) on the fire; he did not however take bread even now, but having fed on herbs or gruel or pulse for two years, in this regime he died, after communicating in church at Epiphany. Shortly before his death he told us: 'For three years I have not been troubled by fleshly desire----after so long a life and toil and labour and ceaseless prayer.'"
- **Disagreement:** Gennadius, npnf203_… : 42349-42350: "He lived to old age, mighty in signs and miracles." Palladius, who was there, says fifty-four.

## 3. Impact, heaviest first

**I1. The eight thoughts, carried west.** (Heaviest: three later witnesses carry the scheme itself.)
- Gennadius of Marseilles, *Lives of Illustrious Men* 11, npnf203_… : 42330-42347, later (~80 yrs; Richardson dates most of the work about 480, census card), D: "He was first to mention or among the first at least to teach these" (above). "This work I have, under instructions, translated into Latin translating with the same simplicity which I found in the Greek. He composed also a book of One hundred sentiments for those living simply as anchorites, arranged by chapters, and one of Fifty sentiments for the erudite and studious, which I first translated into Latin."
- John Cassian, *Institutes* V.1, npnf211_sulpitius-severus-vincent-lerins-cassian.xml : 20092-20097 (and *Conferences* V.2, 30132-30137), later (~25-30 yrs), D for the list: "to approach the struggle against the eight principal faults, i.e. first, Gluttony or the pleasures of the palate; secondly, Fornication; thirdly, Covetousness, which means Avarice, or, as it may more properly be called, the love of money, fourthly, Anger; fifthly, Dejection; sixthly, 'Accidie,'" (and from Conf. V.2) "seventhly cenodoxia, i.e., boasting or vain glory; and eighthly pride." The same eight as Praktikos 6, with sadness and anger in the other order. Written for Castor (preface, 16426-16434: "you are anxious that the institutions of the East and especially of Egypt should be established in your province, which is at present without monasteries"); the editor's note 634 there places his see "in Gallia Narbonensis". **Cassian does not name Evagrius** (searched npnf211 and the Latin Petschenig 1888: no Evagrius in Cassian's text). That Cassian learned the scheme from Evagrius is a modern view not held here: DMR, not to be told as fact. Gennadius ch. 62 on Cassian, npnf203_… : 43253-43254: "eight books On the origin, nature and remedies for the eight principal sins, a book on each sin."
- The Philokalia (1782) prints Cassian's "On the eight thoughts" next after Evagrius's chapters in its first volume: contents, philokalia…v1… : 58634-58650 ("Τοῦ Ἁγίου Κασσιανοῦ τοῦ Ῥωμαίου Λόγος, πρὸς Κάστορα, περὶ τῶν ὀκτὼ τῆς κακίας λογισμῶν"). Late, D.

**I2. His chapters on the ascetic life and prayer, read on in Greek, Latin and Syriac.**
- Rufinus's Latin. Jerome, Letter 133, npnf206_… : 27826-27828, hostile, D: "His work is widely read, in the East in Greek and in the West in a Latin translation made by his disciple Rufinus." Gennadius on Rufinus, npnf203_… : 42465-42475: "translating … the Sentences of Evagrius".
- Thomas of Marga, *Book of Governors* V.9, thomas-of-marga_book-of-governors-v2-english_budge1893.txt : 22338 (chapter), 22386-22404, later (840; Syriac, Budge's English), D: "On account of this the holy fathers the monks were more anxious about this than any other virtue, and they have taught us to do as they did … And again the holy Evagrius saith, 'We are not commanded to watch and fast at all times, but that we should pray continually is a law laid down for us; for those who would heal the part which is liable to affections and passions need the labour of the body.'" (= Praktikos 49.) Also 2330 (Book I): "the wise Mar Evagrius". Budge's note, 22317-22318 (editor): "The quotations from Evagrius made by Thomas of Marga probably form part of the Μοναχὸς ἢ περὶ πρακτικῆς".
- The Philokalia, vol. 1 (1782; Tzelatis ed. 1893), late, D: header, verbatim OCR, "ΕΥ̓ΑΓΡΙΟΥ͂ ΜΟΝΑΧΟΥ͂, ΓΠΟΤΥΠΩΣΙΣ ΜΟΝΑΧΙΚῊ" (4205); contents 58634-58641, normalised from the OCR (which prints χ for κ and δ for β): "Εὐαγρίου Μοναχοῦ ὑποτύπωσις Μοναχικὴ διδάσκουσα πῶς δεῖ ἀσκεῖν, καὶ ἡσυχάζειν. Τοῦ αὐτοῦ, περὶ διακρίσεως παθῶν, καὶ λογισμῶν, κεφάλαια εἴκοσι τρία." (OCR: "Εὐαγρίου Μοναχοῦ ὑποτύπωσις Νο- / ναχικὴ διδάσχουσα πῶς δεῖ ἀ-- / σχεῖν, χαὶ ἡσυχάζειν.") Its short life, 4125-4163 (OCR damaged; normalised): "Εὐάγριος ὁ σοφὸς καὶ ἐλλόγιμος … ἤκμασε μὲν περὶ τὸ τριακοσιοστὸν ὀγδοηκοστὸν ἔτος" (OCR: "ΆΨ. **I3. The charge: Origen's follower, and the later condemnations.** (Told openly; credited.)
- Jerome, Letter 133 to Ctesiphon (dated 415 by the NPNF editors, npnf206_… : 27657-27658), npnf206_… : 27810-27834, hostile, near-contemporary (~16 yrs after his death), D; **quote Q3 source** at 27824-27826: "Evagrius of Ibera in Pontus who sends letters to virgins and monks and among others to her whose name bears witness to the blackness of her perfidy, has published a book of maxims on apathy, or, as we should say, impassivity or imperturbability; a state in which the mind ceases to be agitated and—to speak simply—becomes either a stone or a God." And 27829-27835: "He has also written a book which professes to be about monks and includes in it many not monks at all whom he declares to have been Origenists, and who have certainly been condemned by the bishops. I mean Ammonius, Eusebius, Euthymius, Evagrius himself, Horus, Isidorus". The letter is against Pelagius (summary, 27645-27650). Jerome's Latin is not held. Jerome's "her whose name…" (Melania) is quoted in the live `melania-the-elder` story: not reused.
- Jerome, Dialogue against the Pelagians, prologue, npnf206_… : 42557: "Evagrius of Ibora" among heretics (with Origen's Stromata on the passions, 42543).
- Cyril of Scythopolis, Life of Sabas §40 (Surius's Latin), cyril-scythopolis_vita-sabae-in-surius…_1880.txt : 7756-7763, later (6th c.), D: "laborabat autem morbo gentilium et Manichaeorum, Didymique et Evagrii et Origenis, qui nugati sunt de praeexistentia." ## 5. Where sources disagree or come late

1. **Route from Constantinople:** Palladius and Sozomen, to Jerusalem; Socrates, with Gregory into Egypt (13529-13530).
2. **The husband:** Sozomen, he "plotted his death"; Palladius, Evagrius "imagined that her husband had intervened".
3. **Rank:** Palladius, deacon; Sozomen, archdeacon.
4. **Which Gregory ordained him:** Clarke's text of Palladius, Socrates and Sozomen give Gregory of Nazianzus; the older text of Palladius, followed by the 1782 Philokalia and by Budge, gives Gregory of Nyssa (see E1).
5. **Age at death:** Palladius, fifty-four; Gennadius, "lived to old age".
6. **The vision:** Palladius, an angel in soldiers' and then a friend's shape; Sozomen, a man holding the Gospels, in a dream.
7. **The condemnation of 553:** later councils and Evagrius Scholasticus say it named him; Hefele finds no name in its surviving acts (Contested).
8. **Who carried the eight:** Gennadius says "first … or among the first at least"; Cassian has the eight but never names him.
9. **Melania's part** rests on Palladius alone.
10. **Late witnesses:** Thomas of Marga (840), the Philokalia (1782).
