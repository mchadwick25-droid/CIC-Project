A leading pagan of Rome, Praetextatus, used to say to the bishop, Damasus: "Make me bishop of the city of Rome, and I will be a Christian at once." Jerome remembered the joke. The office had been won with blood.

Damasus was bishop of Rome from 366 to 384. He won the office in a fight that left many dead. Then he held his church to the creed agreed at Nicaea, that the Son is of one being with the Father. In 380 a law of the emperors named him as the standard of that faith. He is on the tree because his years show the church at its sharpest, when emperors took sides in its quarrels.

When the bishop Liberius died, two parties chose two bishops, the deacons Ursinus and Damasus. Fighting followed in the churches, and the accounts disagree. Ammianus, a former soldier and historian, most likely a pagan, blames both men's ambition. He counts 137 dead in one day in a church. An account written by Ursinus's own party blames Damasus. It says he paid charioteers and arena fighters, and with them and gravediggers stormed a church held by the other side. They used axes, swords and fire, and killed 160 men and women, with no dead on his own side. That is one side's charge. Church writers of his own time and the next blame Ursinus instead.

The empire's officers ended it, not any council. The city's governor, Praetextatus, calmed the tumult, and Ursinus was expelled.

From about 369, Damasus held the West to Nicaea. A council at Rome under him cut off the bishop of Milan, an Arian, who rejected the creed of Nicaea. His letters went east, offering communion, which meant to count as one church with him. At Alexandria, the bishop there later wrote, the deacon who carried them was led through the town bound and flogged.

In the 370s Apollinaris of Laodicea taught that the Word of God stood in place of a human reasoning mind in Christ. Damasus and the bishop of Alexandria condemned this in council at Rome.

After the Arian emperor Valens died, a general enforced an edict handing the churches to those in communion with Damasus.

An emperor ordered that any bishop condemned by Damasus with a council of five or seven bishops who held on to his church be brought to Rome by the governors. In February 380, the emperors issued the law: "It is our will that all the peoples under our merciful rule live in the religion that the divine apostle Peter handed down to the Romans. The faith he brought them, kept to this day, shows that he did. It is plain that Bishop Damasus follows it, and Peter, bishop of Alexandria, a man of an apostle's holiness."

The law did not make Damasus ruler of the church, but it named him, with the bishop of Alexandria, as the measure of the right faith. Everyone else, it said, would bear the shame of heretical teaching.

Two priests of a strict group, followers of Lucifer of Cagliari, petitioned the emperors. They say Damasus's clergy, with officials, broke up a night service held by their priest Macarius and dragged him over the stones. A judge, acting as if under an imperial order, pressed him to join Damasus's communion. He refused, was exiled, and, they say, died of his wound. The petition gives no date and names no law, and we have no answer from Damasus's side.

The book of the popes' lives was written about 150 years later. It says Damasus searched out the graves of the martyrs, in the burial galleries outside the city, and marked them with verses. At his urging, too, Jerome, his friend and secretary, corrected the old Latin Gospels against the Greek.

Damasus died in December 384, almost eighty years old, Jerome says. His epitaph, which its editors take to be his own work, praises the one who stilled the sea's bitter waves, and who gave a brother back to his sister Martha. Then it ends: "I believe that, after the ashes, he will make Damasus rise."
