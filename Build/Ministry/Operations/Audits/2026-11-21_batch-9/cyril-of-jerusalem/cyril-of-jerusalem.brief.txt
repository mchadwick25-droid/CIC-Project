"Do you see this place, Golgotha?" Cyril of Jerusalem asks his hearers, and they answer with a shout. He answers it: "You answer with a shout of praise, as if you agree."

Golgotha is the hill where Jesus was crucified, close by the great church that Constantine built at the tomb. Around 348, Cyril taught the people preparing for baptism through the forty days of fasting before Easter. He went through the creed, their short statement of faith, line by line. After Easter he explained the rites they had just been through. His lectures survive because some hearers took them down as he spoke, an old note in one copy says. As bishop he was driven from his city three times in the quarrels over Arius's teaching that the Son was less than the Father. Jerusalem's way of making new Christians became a pattern that a pilgrim from the West wrote home about.

He welcomed even a man who had come only to court a woman: Jesus, he said, was angling for him.

He gave the creed in a few lines, since not everyone could read the Scriptures, to be learned by heart and not kept on paper. He told the candidates to tell no one unbaptized what they heard. And he pointed to the places around them, Golgotha and the tomb, as his witnesses.

In Easter week he explained the rites of baptism, anointing and communion, in the small chapel over the tomb itself. "I knew well that seeing persuades far more than hearing. So I waited for this time." They had faced west, the side of darkness, stretched out a hand and renounced Satan, then turned east toward the light. They had stripped and stood naked, unashamed, and he taught them to make the left hand a throne for the right at communion. Some old copies name his successor John as author of these five lectures. His modern editors hold them Cyril's, since the last Lent lecture promises them.

About 350 he became bishop, and how is disputed. Socrates and Sozomen, church historians writing about ninety years later, say the party of Acacius, bishop of Caesarea, pushed out the old bishop and put Cyril in. Jerome, hostile to him, says worse: that Cyril gave up his ordination as a priest to be given the see by the followers of Arius. The bishops who met in 382 wrote that he was rightly ordained by the bishops of the province, and had fought a good fight against the Arians in several places.

Acacius was the senior bishop of the province, and Cyril claimed rank for Jerusalem as an apostle's see. In a famine, Sozomen says, Cyril sold the church's veil and sacred ornaments to feed the poor. Acacius, Sozomen says, used the sale as a pretext to depose him. Socrates does not know the charges. He says Cyril was deposed for staying away two whole years, though summoned again and again, owing to fear. Once deposed, about 357, he gave notice that he would appeal to a higher court, and the emperor allowed it.

Two more removals followed. In 360 a synod at Constantinople deposed him again. The emperor Julian recalled the exiles, but under a later emperor he was driven out a third time, probably about 367.

In 381 he sat among the leaders of the great council at Constantinople. Socrates says he had by then accepted the word for the Son being of one being with the Father, giving up his former view. His modern editors reply that the lectures taught this from the start, without the word. He died about 386.

The lectures were kept. A note in the copies lets them be lent to the baptized and to those preparing, never to outsiders. Jerome listed them within a few years of his death. The pattern held too. Jerome, writing against Cyril's successor John, says forty days of public teaching was their custom. Egeria described it for the women at home. Of Easter week in the tomb chapel she wrote: "The bishop talks through each point and sets it out. Meanwhile the voices of those applauding are so loud that they can be heard outside the church." She never names the bishop, and her editors date her visit to the end of the fourth century, so it may have been Cyril or John.

After the shout, Cyril says to his hearers: "See that you do not take it back when persecution comes."
