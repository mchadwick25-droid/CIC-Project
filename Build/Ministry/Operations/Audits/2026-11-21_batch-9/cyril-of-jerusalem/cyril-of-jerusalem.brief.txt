"Do you see this place, Golgotha?" Cyril of Jerusalem asks his hearers, and they answer with a shout. He takes it up in his next words: "You answer with a shout of praise, as if you agree."

Golgotha is the hill where Jesus was crucified, close by the great church that Constantine built at the tomb. The lecture is from Lent, around the year 348 by modern reckoning. Cyril taught the people preparing for baptism through the forty days of fasting before Easter, going through the creed, their short statement of faith, line by line. After Easter he explained the rites they had just been through. His lectures survive because some hearers took them down as he spoke, an old note in one copy says. As bishop he was driven from his city three times in the quarrels over Arius's teaching that the Son was less than the Father. Jerusalem's way of making new Christians became a pattern that a pilgrim from the West wrote home about.

He gave the creed in a few lines, since not everyone could read the Scriptures, to be learned by heart and not kept on paper. He told the candidates to tell no one unbaptized what they heard. And he pointed to the places around them, Golgotha and the tomb, as his witnesses.

In Easter week he explained the rites of baptism, anointing and communion, in the small chapel over the tomb itself. "I knew well that seeing persuades far more than hearing. So I waited for this time." They had faced west, the side of darkness, stretched out a hand and renounced Satan, then turned east toward the light. Some old copies name his successor John as author of these five lectures. His modern editors hold them Cyril's, since the last Lent lecture promises them.

About 350 he became bishop, and how is disputed. Socrates and Sozomen, church historians writing about ninety years later, say the party of Acacius, bishop of Caesarea, pushed out the old bishop and put Cyril in. Jerome, who was hostile to him, says worse: that Cyril gave up his ordination as a priest to win the see from the followers of Arius. The bishops who met in 382 wrote that he was rightly ordained by the bishops of the province.

Acacius was the senior bishop of the province, and Cyril claimed rank for Jerusalem as an apostle's see. In a famine, Sozomen says, Cyril sold the church's veil and sacred ornaments to feed the poor. Acacius used the sale as a pretext to depose him. Socrates says Cyril had stayed away two whole years, though summoned again and again, owing to fear. Once deposed, he gave notice that he would appeal to a higher court, and the emperor allowed it. This was about 357.

Two more removals followed: in 360 a synod at Constantinople deposed him again, and under Valens he was driven out a third time, probably about 367.

In 381 he sat among the leaders of the great council at Constantinople. Socrates says he had by then accepted the word for the Son being of one being with the Father, giving up his former view. His modern editors reply that the lectures taught this from the start, without the word. He died about 386.

The lectures were kept, and Theodoret of Cyrrhus later quoted the fourth as testimony in his argument about Christ. The pattern held too. Egeria, a pilgrim from the West, described it for the women at home. The Scriptures were taught from Genesis, the creed came after five weeks, and in Easter week the bishop taught in the tomb chapel with the doors shut. "The bishop talks through each point and sets it out. Meanwhile the voices of those applauding are so loud that they can be heard outside the church." She never names the bishop, and her editors date her visit to the end of the fourth century, so it may have been Cyril or John.

After the shout, Cyril says to his hearers: "See that you do not take it back when persecution comes."
