Hilary, a bishop in Gaul, wrote these words from exile, in a book for the bishops of Gaul and Britain. "I was baptized some time ago, and I had been a bishop for a while. I never heard the faith of Nicaea until I was going into exile. But the Gospels and the Apostles showed me what 'one in substance' and 'like in substance' mean."

Nicaea was the council of 325, whose creed said the Son is "of one substance" with the Father. Hilary was bishop of Poitiers, and in 356 the emperor Constantius sent him to Phrygia, in Asia Minor. There he learned how the Eastern bishops were quarreling over the Son. He wrote against the Arian party, who held that the Son was less than the Father, and he wrote to explain the Eastern creeds to the West. Then he came home and led the bishops of Gaul back to the faith of Nicaea.

Most of the outward story rests on his own letters and on Sulpicius Severus, who wrote about forty years later and was openly on Hilary's side. Sulpicius says that before the exile, a young man named Martin, fresh from the army, sought Hilary out and stayed with him. Hilary tried to make him a deacon. Martin refused, crying that he was unworthy. So Hilary made him an exorcist, one who prayed over people thought troubled by evil spirits. The office was so lowly that Martin could not refuse it without seeming proud. He would become bishop of Tours.

Hilary and the bishops of Gaul had refused communion, the sharing of the sacrament that shows churches belong together, to Saturninus, bishop of Arles, who, Sulpicius says, harassed Gaul for the Arian party. Hilary denounced the leaders of that side at a council in Béziers. Saturninus then deceived the emperor, Hilary says, and Hilary was sent away. He later wrote that a faction had exiled him on false reports, not for any crime.

In Phrygia he learned what the Eastern bishops believed, and wrote home about it from several cities. He wrote twelve books against the Arians, which we know as On the Trinity. Then the bishops of Gaul asked him to tell them what the Eastern bishops had confessed, and he answered with On the Councils. It also reached out to Eastern bishops who said the Son is "like in substance" to the Father, his being only like the Father's. Hilary wrote to them as brothers. To accept "like," he told them, they need not reject "one in substance," which says the Son shares the Father's very being.

In 359 the governors sent him to the Eastern council at Seleucia, where he set out a faith that matched Nicaea and was received. At Constantinople he asked three times, in writing, to debate before the emperor. The Arian party blocked it, and he was sent back to Gaul as a troublemaker in the East, his exile not lifted. Other accounts speak of leave to return.

That same year, at Ariminum in Italy, Western bishops had been terrified by threats into signing a creed that dropped the word "substance." Back in Gaul, many wanted nothing to do with those who had signed. Hilary chose to bring them back to repentance, and in frequent councils almost all the bishops confessed their error.

The bishops of Gaul wrote to the East. "From your letters, which you entrusted to our dear brother and fellow bishop Hilary, we have learned the devil's fraud. We have learned how the minds of heretics plotted against the Lord's church. So we were split between East and West, and misled by each other's differing opinions." Saturninus resisted, and was cast out of the church. Martin followed Hilary home and set up a monastery not far from Hilary's town.

Hilary died in his own country, at Poitiers, in the sixth year after his return, about 367. Nothing is recorded of how. Augustine, writing his own book on the Trinity, studied Hilary's way of stating the three persons. And Sulpicius, writing as a friend, said: "All agree on this: by the good work of Hilary alone, our provinces of Gaul were freed from the guilt of heresy."

Hilary himself had said where he found the meaning of the creed. He found it in the Gospels and the Apostles.
