Hilary, bishop of Poitiers in Gaul, wrote these words from exile, in a book for the bishops of Gaul and Britain. "I was baptized some time ago, and I had been a bishop for a while. I never heard the faith of Nicaea until I was going into exile. But the Gospels and the Apostles showed me what 'one in substance' and 'like in substance' mean."

Nicaea was the council of 325, whose creed said the Son is "of one substance" with the Father. In 356 the emperor Constantius sent him to Phrygia, in Asia Minor. There he learned the Eastern bishops' quarrel over the Son. He wrote against the Arian party, who held the Son was less than the Father, and explained the Eastern creeds to the West. Then he came home and led the bishops of Gaul back to the faith of Nicaea. Before the exile, Martin, later bishop of Tours, had sought him out.

Most of the outward story rests on his own letters and Sulpicius Severus, who wrote about forty years later and was openly on Hilary's side. Sulpicius says Martin, fresh from the army, stayed with him a while. Hilary tried to make him a deacon. Martin refused, crying he was unworthy. So Hilary made him an exorcist, who prayed over people thought troubled by evil spirits. The office was so lowly Martin could not refuse it without seeming proud.

Hilary and the bishops of Gaul had refused communion, the shared sacrament that shows churches belong together, to Saturninus, bishop of Arles. He had, Sulpicius says, harassed Gaul for the Arian party. At a council in Béziers he denounced that side's leaders. Saturninus then deceived the emperor, Hilary says, and a faction had him exiled on false reports.

In Phrygia he wrote twelve books against the Arians, now called On the Trinity. The bishops of Gaul asked him what the Eastern bishops had confessed, and he answered with On the Councils. In it he also wrote, as to brothers, to the Eastern bishops who said the Son is "like in substance," his being like the Father's. To accept "like," he told them, they need not reject "one in substance," which says the Son shares the Father's very being.

In 359 the governors sent him to the Eastern council at Seleucia, where he set out a faith matching Nicaea and was received. At Constantinople he asked three times, in writing, to debate before the emperor. The Arian party blocked it, and he was sent back to Gaul as a troublemaker in the East, his exile not lifted.

That same year, at Ariminum in Italy, Sulpicius says, Western bishops had been terrified by threats into signing a creed that dropped the word "substance." In Gaul many wanted nothing to do with the signers. Hilary chose to bring them back to repentance, and in frequent councils almost all the bishops confessed their error.

The bishops of Gaul wrote to the East that the letters Hilary brought had shown them the devil's fraud and the heretics' plots against the church, which had split East from West and misled each side about the other. They cut off Auxentius, bishop of Milan, and other Arian leaders from communion. Saturninus resisted, and was cast out of the church, Sulpicius says. Another exiled bishop broke even with those who took the signers back. Sulpicius will not say who was right. Martin followed Hilary home and set up a monastery not far from Hilary's town. Sulpicius wrote: "All agree on this: by the good work of Hilary alone, our provinces of Gaul were freed from the guilt of heresy."

In 364, at Milan, Hilary accused Auxentius, who then wrote a confession that sounded orthodox. The new emperor took communion with him. Hilary called it all a pretense and was ordered out of Milan. Only Hilary tells it.

Hilary died at Poitiers, in the sixth year after his return, about 367. Nothing is recorded of how.
