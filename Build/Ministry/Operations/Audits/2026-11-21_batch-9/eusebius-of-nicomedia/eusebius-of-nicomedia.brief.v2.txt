Arius, a priest driven out of Alexandria, wrote to Eusebius, bishop of Nicomedia, and greeted him as a fellow pupil of Lucian, a teacher at Antioch who died a martyr. He told him why he was being hounded: "We are persecuted because we said: the Son has a beginning, but God has no beginning." Eusebius answered by taking up his cause.

That choice set the course of his life. Nicomedia was the emperor's city, in north-west Asia Minor, and that gave its bishop weight. After a short exile he led the bishops who drove Arius's opponents out of their churches, and he died bishop of Constantinople. Nearly everything we know of him was written by his enemies.

First he wrote to a fellow bishop at Tyre, urging him to write for their side. His letter says the Son was not made from the Father's own being, and that everything came to be by God's will. He also wrote to Alexander of Alexandria, and to bishops everywhere, asking them to take Arius back. Alexander told the bishops to ignore his letters.

Then came Nicaea, in 325, and what Eusebius signed. Philostorgius, the one friendly historian, whose work survives only in a hostile summary, says he signed. Socrates, a historian of Constantinople a century later, says he was one of five who would not. His own account was written from exile, some three years later, with another bishop: "We signed the creed, but we did not sign the condemnation. We were not finding fault with the creed. We did not believe the accused man was what he was said to be." The condemnation was the council's curse on Arius.

Within three months of the council, Philostorgius says, Eusebius was exiled. Constantine charged him in a letter to the people of Nicomedia: "Who taught these things to so innocent a crowd? Eusebius, plainly: the partner in the tyrant's cruelty." The tyrant was Licinius, the rival emperor who had made war on Constantine. The letter's stated cause was that Eusebius had sheltered men from Alexandria whom Constantine had ordered seized. Socrates gives another reason: Eusebius would not join in condemning Arius.

After three years his plea won his recall, and he took his church back; the bishop of Antioch was removed. A historian of the next century says Eusebius's party bribed the woman who charged him, though Philostorgius tells her charge as true. Athanasius, now bishop of Alexandria, says Eusebius pressed him with threats to receive Arius. Socrates names Eusebius first among the men behind the charges that removed Athanasius by a council at Tyre. Then they told Constantine that Athanasius had threatened Alexandria's grain ships, a charge Socrates calls slander, and the emperor exiled him.

Dying near Nicomedia, Constantine called the bishops and asked for baptism. His biographer, another Eusebius, writes only that "the prelates" gave it. Jerome, writing about forty years later, names Eusebius of Nicomedia. He says that with it Constantine "turned aside" to Arius's teaching, and that seizures of churches and discord across the world followed.

Socrates says Constantine's son Constantius removed the bishop of Constantinople, and moved Eusebius to the emperor's new city. Eusebius had ninety bishops called to a meeting at Antioch, which Socrates says aimed to undo "of one being," Nicaea's word that the Son shares the Father's very being.

Philostorgius says Ulfila, sent by the Goths to Constantine's court, was made their bishop by a bishop named Eusebius. A modern historian takes this to be Eusebius of Nicomedia, and sets it under Constantius instead.

Eusebius died soon after the meeting at Antioch, before the bishop of Rome had ruled on Athanasius. At once the people of Constantinople brought back the bishop he had replaced. The men who had worked with him took up "all his authority." Athanasius had a name for them: "those around Eusebius." After his death, Socrates says, many lives were lost in the fight over the city's bishop.

Philostorgius called him "the Great." Socrates called him "that disturber of the public peace."
