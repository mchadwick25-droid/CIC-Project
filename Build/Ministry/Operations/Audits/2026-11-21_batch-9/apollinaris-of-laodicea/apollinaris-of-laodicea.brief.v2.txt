When the emperor Julian barred Christian children from the schools of the Greek poets, a scholar at Laodicea in Syria answered with poems of his own. The historian Sozomen says he wrote an epic of Hebrew history in twenty-four books, to stand in for Homer, with plays and odes beside it. His name was Apollinaris, and he was already known as a friend of Athanasius and the creed of Nicaea.

In the fourth century he stood with Athanasius for the creed of Nicaea, which said the Son is of one being with the Father. The Arians, who followed Arius, held that the Son was created and lower. Then he taught that in Christ the divine Word, the Son of God as John's Gospel names him, took the place of a human mind. The church condemned that teaching at Rome and at Constantinople in 381, and his followers formed churches of their own.

He taught rhetoric, as his father taught grammar. Bishop George put him out of the church. Sozomen says the cause was his friendship with Athanasius, whom George's party shunned. Socrates says it was friendship with a pagan teacher, or, as some told it, a quarrel over George's wavering creed. In 362 Apollinaris sent envoys to a meeting of bishops that Athanasius held at Alexandria, and its letter calls him bishop.

Under Julian, Socrates says, father and son rewrote Scripture: the father the books of Moses in epic verse, the son the Gospels as dialogues like Plato's. Sozomen gives all of it to Apollinaris alone.

Almost all we have of Apollinaris comes through opponents, or under other names. One of them, Gregory of Nyssa, quoted a book of his to refute it. There Apollinaris gave his reason: "If God was joined to a man, complete to complete, they would be two. One would be Son of God by nature, and one adopted." Epiphanius of Salamis, who had loved him, at first would not believe the reports.

Jerome heard him lecture at Antioch, and says he learned Scripture from him but never took his teaching. His followers formed churches in many cities under their own bishops, and sang his songs. Sozomen says: "Men sang his songs at drinking parties and at work. Women sang them at the loom."

Power came down on them. A council at Rome under Damasus condemned him and his pupil, in the 370s. After the council at Constantinople in 381, Gregory of Nazianzus answered that whatever Christ did not take on, he did not heal. Laws of the emperor Theodosius from 383 forbade his followers to meet or to ordain. In 388 their bishops lost the name of bishop and were barred from the cities. Jerome says he died under Theodosius; the year is not known.

His books lived on in disguise. Men who shared his views but stayed inside the main church put honored bishops' names, Athanasius among them, on his writings, and so, says the German scholar Lietzmann, saved them. One was a statement of faith to the emperor Jovian, carried as Athanasius's. It says Christ is not two natures but "one nature of God the Word made flesh", that is, God the Word and his flesh are one nature. In 429 Cyril of Alexandria cited it as Athanasius's. Lietzmann and Hefele give it to Apollinaris himself; another editor thought it might be Athanasius's. If they are right, the man condemned in 381 gave Cyril's side, unseen, what Hefele calls a watchword of orthodoxy.

In the 540s the emperor Justinian charged the one-nature party of his day with passing such writings off under false names. He said: "They cunningly push aside the name of Apollinaris, because it plainly proves ungodliness. They put the names of the holy Fathers on his writings and on their own inventions." The holy Fathers are the church's honored early bishops and teachers.

Sozomen thought that, but for people's love of old books, his writings would be prized like the ancients'. Socrates said the poems and dialogues from Julian's years were now as if never written.
