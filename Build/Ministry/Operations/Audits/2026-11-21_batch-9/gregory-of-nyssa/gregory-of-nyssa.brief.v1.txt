In 378 the emperor Valens, who had driven bishops loyal to Nicaea into exile, was killed fighting the Goths. The new emperor Gratian called the exiles home. One of them was Gregory of Nyssa, a bishop of Cappadocian Christianity, in Asia Minor. His brother Basil of Caesarea wrote that Gregory had been made bishop of Nyssa by force. When an imperial official, a friend of heretics in Basil's words, had him summoned on charges about church money and his ordination, Basil answered that any fault lay with those who ordained him.

After Basil died, Gregory wrote, he "inherited the controversy with Eunomius." Eunomius held, as Gregory reports him, that being unbegotten was the Father's very essence, so the Son was unlike Him in being. Gregory answered that the Deity "is above every name." He also wrote that the Holy Spirit is "of the same rank as the Father and the Son." In 381 an imperial law handed the churches to bishops who confessed one majesty in Father, Son and Spirit. It named Gregory as one whose communion marked them, though it gives no reason.

In the Great Catechism he wrote that we were made able to change, so we can keep moving toward the good without end. He looks there to a day when those now lying in sin are restored and all creation gives thanks. Christ, he writes, frees humanity from evil and heals even the one who brought evil in. The sources we have do not show what came of this teaching in his own day.

In 553 the emperor Justinian, writing to a council at Constantinople, named him among the holy Fathers whose writings on the true faith he followed. In 692 the council in Trullo set its seal on his canons, making them church law.
