Eunomius of Cyzicus wrote a defence of his faith for two sets of readers: those who would hear it in his own day, and those who would find it later. He begged both not to measure the truth by numbers.

His argument turned on one word. "Unbegotten" means having no source, never born and never made. Eunomius taught that it is not only a name for God. It is what God is. So the Son, who is begotten, born of a Father, cannot share the Father's being. That teaching drew answers from Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nyssa. His followers took his name. And under the emperor Theodosius he was banished.

Gregory of Nyssa, his enemy, says his father was a poor farmer who carved letters for schoolboys in winter. The son left the plough, learned shorthand and hoped to be an orator. He went to Alexandria to study under Aetius, says Philostorgius, the one friendly historian. Philostorgius's own history survives only in a hostile summary by Photius, a patriarch centuries later. Philostorgius praised him as a teacher with "a style well suited to learners." At Constantinople, Philostorgius says, Eunomius and Aetius led the party that opponents named Anomoean, from the Greek for "unlike." This was about 360.

The bishop of Constantinople made him bishop of Cyzicus. He came with an imperial edict that put out the city's bishop, whom its people wanted to keep. Why he left is told three ways. Philostorgius says he refused to sign what his patron asked. Socrates says the people tired of his showy talk and drove him out. Sozomen says the patron found no fault, but Eunomius would not go back to people who suspected him.

In his Apology he reasoned that nothing was before God. "It has been shown that he was not before himself, nor anything else before him, and that he is before all things. Then 'unbegotten' follows. Or rather, he himself is unbegotten being." To be unbegotten is what God is, and God has no parts, so he cannot share his being. The Son did not exist before he was begotten, and he was begotten before all things, by the Father's will. Eunomius called him the only-begotten God and the Father's image in what he does, not in what he is. He refused the Nicene creed's word that the Son is of one being with the Father, because that would divide God.

Basil quoted it piece by piece. At "or rather" he stopped: it does not agree, he said, with what came before. He answered: "I too would say that God's being is unbegotten. But I would not say that 'unbegotten' is his being." Basil did not deny that God is unbegotten; he denied that the word says what God is. For Basil the Son is like the Father in his very being, and equal in power.

Twice, Philostorgius says, Valens's officials exiled him. After Basil's death, Photius says, Eunomius showed his friends a reply to Basil he had worked on, and Gregory of Nyssa answered it. Then Theodosius gave the churches of Constantinople to the party of one being. In June 383 he summoned the head of every party to the palace, each with a written creed. Eunomius came for his own party. The emperor prayed alone, read them all, and kept only the creed of one being. About this time, Sozomen says, Theodosius banished him. Socrates gives the reason: he held meetings in private houses and read them his books. Not long after his banishment, Sozomen says, he died, and was buried at Dacora, his birthplace in Cappadocia.

Sozomen judged that the greater part of the church would have gone over to this teaching, had it not met Basil and Gregory, both from Cappadocia. A law of 398 ordered the books of Eunomian clergy burned, and made hiding one a crime punishable by death.

Later, Philostorgius says, a powerful court official had the body moved to Tyana and ordered his books destroyed by public edict. Yet the Apology he wrote for readers still to come survives.
