# Outline: Eunomius of Cyzicus (`eunomius-of-cyzicus`)

Line references are to `Source_Sheet.md` rows (S, E, C, A, B, X, T, D, I), each of which gives the file and its lines. Files are under `cic/texts/`.

- **The essence:** Eunomius of Cyzicus taught that "unbegotten" is not only a word for God but God's very being, so the Son, who is begotten, cannot share the Father's being. His Apology drew an answer from Basil of Caesarea, and his reply to Basil one from Gregory of Nyssa; his followers were called by his name; he was briefly bishop of Cyzicus, and under the emperor Theodosius he was banished and died soon after. (A3, A4, A7, A10, B2, T7, X2, C1, T4, D1)
- **Also told:**
  - The argument, in his own words: nothing was before God, so he is unbegotten, "or rather" unbegotten being. God has no parts and cannot share his being; as for the Son, he did not exist before he was begotten, and he was begotten before all things, by the Father's will (A7; Whiston 52, 58); he is like the Father in what he does, not in what he is. (A3-A10; Documented)
  - The answer: Basil agreed that God's being is unbegotten, but denied that "unbegotten" is his being. For Basil the Son is like the Father in his very being. (B1-B3; Documented)
  - The cost: three accounts of why he left Cyzicus; two exiles under Valens; the creeds of 383, when the emperor kept only the creed of one being; banishment for meeting in houses and reading his books; after his death, his body moved and his books ordered destroyed. (C4-C6, X5, T3, T4, D2; Documented or Contested, as marked)
  - Checked against the Library: all held. "Unlike the Father in substance" is held as his "difference of their Substances" (A8) and his clergy's charge (C3); he himself called the Son like the Father in operation (A9). - **Openings:**

- **Story beats:**
  1. **Pupil and secretary (350s).** Gregory of Nyssa, his enemy, says his father was a poor farmer who in winter carved letters of the alphabet for schoolboys, and that the son left the plough, learned shorthand and aimed to become an orator (E1). He came from Cappadocia to Antioch and then to Alexandria, to study under Aetius (S4, E2), says Philostorgius, his admirer, who praised him as a teacher with a clear way of explaining, Quote 3 (T6). Socrates calls him Aetius's secretary (E3). Philostorgius says Eunomius, then a deacon, and Aetius led the party that called the Son unlike the Father at a council in Constantinople in 360 (E4, E5; the year from the census `anomoean-eunomian-christianity` `relationsSummary`, "The break began at Constantinople in 360": Widely Accepted, the tree's tradition entry). Confidence: Documented; the family in Gregory's voice only; the year 360 Widely Accepted.
  2. **Bishop of Cyzicus (c. 360, or later).** The bishop of Constantinople made him bishop of Cyzicus. He came with an imperial edict, which put out the city's bishop, whom its people wanted to keep, and he took over the churches (C1; Socrates 12456-12458, Sozomen 37854-37861). That bishop's people built a church outside the walls (C1). His clergy charged that he made the Son unlike the Father (C3). Why he left is told three ways: he refused his patron's terms and went home, says Philostorgius (C4); the people tired of his showy talk and drove him out, says Socrates (C5); his patron found no fault, but he would not return to people who suspected him, says Sozomen (C6). Reason for the act: in Philostorgius, he would not sign what his patron asked; in Socrates, the people's anger at his talk; in Sozomen, his clergy's suspicion of his teaching. Confidence: the appointment Documented; the date and the departure Contested. The doubt is a scene: three short credited clauses.
  3. **The Apology (date not held).** He wrote it as a defence against charges and insults (A1). God is before all things, so he is unbegotten: Quote 1 (A3). Plain sense: to be unbegotten is what God is. God has no parts, so he cannot share his being; sharing would split him (A4, A5). So the Son is not of the Father's being: he did not exist before he was begotten, and he was begotten before all things, by the Father's will (A7; Whiston 52, 58). He calls the Son the only-begotten God, maker of all else, and the Father's image in what he does, not in what he is (A7, A9). He will not say the Son is of one being with the Father, because that would divide God (A10). He cites Christ's saying that the Father is greater (A6, paraphrase). Confidence: Documented, his own words.
  4. **Basil's answer (the 360s, by an editor's date).** Basil of Caesarea quoted the Apology passage by passage and answered it (B1). Of the "or rather", he says it does not agree with what came before (B1). Then Quote 2 (B2). Plain sense: "unbegotten" tells how the Father is, without a source; it does not say what God is. For Basil the Son is like the Father in his very being, and equal in power (B3). His brother Gregory says Basil wrote to cure Eunomius and bring him back to the church (B4). Confidence: Documented; the year 364 is an editor's (B5).
  5. **His own church, and exile (360s-370s).** He broke with the bishop of Constantinople, who would not take Aetius back (X1). His followers came to be called by his name; Sozomen says he only renewed Aetius's teaching, more boldly (X2). He lived at Chalcedon, Philostorgius says; no bishop of his party acted without his advice, and when Aetius died, Eunomius closed his eyes and buried him (X3, X4). Officials of the emperor Valens twice sent him into exile, Philostorgius says, the second time condemned unheard (X5). Confidence: Documented; the exiles rest on Philostorgius.
  6. **Theodosius (c. 379-c. 390).** After Basil's death, Photius says, Eunomius at last showed his friends a reply to Basil he had worked on; Gregory of Nyssa answered it (T7). The new emperor, Theodosius, gave the churches of Constantinople to the party of one being and drove out the party of Arius and Eunomius, Philostorgius says (T1). In June 383 the emperor called the head of every party to the palace, each with a written creed; Eunomius came for his own party. The emperor prayed alone, read them, and kept only the creed of one being. Socrates says he condemned the rest; Sozomen, that he destroyed them (T3). About this time, Sozomen says, Theodosius banished him. Socrates gives the reason: he held meetings in private houses and read his books to them; Sozomen tells the same meetings. Socrates adds that of all the parties, Theodosius punished only Eunomius (T4). Philostorgius says he was sent to a place on the Danube, then to Caesarea, where the people disliked him for writing against their bishop, Basil, and at last to his estate at Dacora (T5). Confidence: Documented; the reason for the banishment Widely Accepted; the route rests on Philostorgius.
- **What came of them** (ranked; about a third of the story):
  1. **The answers he drew, and what they worked out.** Basil's books against him, and Gregory of Nyssa's reply to his answer (B2, T7). Sozomen judged that the greater part of the church would have gone over to this teaching had it not met Basil and Gregory, both from Cappadocia; he does not say which Gregory (I2, paraphrase). Against him Basil set out that the name "unbegotten" says how the Father is, not what God is, and that the Son is like the Father in his very being (B2, B3; the tree's entry, I3, frames it as the distinction between God's unknowable being and his knowable acts: Dominant Modern Reconstruction; say it through Basil's words, not as a scholar's verdict).
  2. **What he defended: the Father alone as the source of all.** He wrote that the Father's sole rule over all, which Whiston's English calls God's monarchy, must be kept in all things (A11, paraphrase, not a quote).
  3. **A church named for him, and its suppression.** His followers were the Eunomians (X2). A council at Constantinople in 381 condemned them by name, and laws of 381 and 383 forbade their churches and meetings (I4; clause, no quote). A law of 398 ordered the books of Eunomian clergy sought out and burned before the judges, and made hiding one a crime punished by death (I5).
  4. **His words kept by his enemies.** His Apology survives; his later reply is known from the passages Gregory of Nyssa quotes to refute it (I8; the tree's entry and the editors). Optional, in a clause: the tree's entry says the sect left no continuing church (I9).
