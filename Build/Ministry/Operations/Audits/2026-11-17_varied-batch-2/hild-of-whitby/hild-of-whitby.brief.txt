A man at a feast used to leave the table whenever the harp came near him, because he could not sing. One night, Bede says, someone stood by him in his sleep and told him to sing the beginning of created things, and next morning he was brought to the abbess, Hild.

This was the seventh century, in Northumbria, the kingdom of the English north of the River Humber. Its people had received the faith within living memory, from two missions: one from Rome, and one from the Irish monks of Iona, whom Bede calls the Scots. Almost all we know of Hild comes from Bede, a monk in the same kingdom, who wrote about fifty years after she died.

The daughter of King Edwin's nephew, she took the faith with Edwin at the preaching of Paulinus. At thirty-three she chose the monastic life, intending to live in Gaul as an exile for the Lord's sake, until Bishop Aidan called her home.

She ruled a house at Hartlepool, then gained land at Whitby, where she built, or set in order, a monastery for men and women. No one there was rich and no one poor, and she made her people study the Scriptures so closely that many became fit to be priests.

In 664 the kings met in her monastery, because the Irish-trained clergy and those who followed Rome kept Easter on different dates. Hild held with the Scots, the side of her teacher Aidan, but King Oswy ruled for the Easter of Rome, and Bede says all present agreed.

When the steward brought the man to her, she had him repeat his verses before learned men. Because they judged his gift a grace from God, she made Caedmon a monk and had him taught sacred history, which he turned into English song.

Although a fever held her for six years, she kept teaching. She died at cockcrow in 680, after calling the women of her house together and urging them to keep the peace of the Gospel among themselves and with all others.

Bede says Caedmon's verses turned many minds to heaven, and that others after him attempted religious poems in English, but none could match him. "All who knew her used to call her Mother, for her outstanding devotion and grace." Eight centuries later, prayers at York still asked Saint Hild to pray for them. Because she had made her people study the Scriptures, the young English church took bishops from her house. Bede saw it himself: "Later we saw five bishops come from that same monastery. All of them were men of rare merit and holiness."
