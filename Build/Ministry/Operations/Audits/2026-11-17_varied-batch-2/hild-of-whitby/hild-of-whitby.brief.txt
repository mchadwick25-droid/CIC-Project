A man at a feast used to leave the table whenever the harp came near him, because he could not sing. One night, Bede says, someone stood by him in his sleep and told him to sing the beginning of created things. The next morning he was brought to the abbess, Hild of Whitby.

This was the seventh century, in Northumbria, the kingdom of the English north of the River Humber. Its people had heard the faith within living memory, from two missions: one from Rome, and one from the Irish monks of Iona, whom Bede calls the Scots. Almost all we know of Hild comes from Bede, a monk in the same kingdom, who wrote about fifty years after she died.

She was of noble birth, the daughter of King Edwin's nephew, and she took the faith with Edwin at the preaching of Paulinus. At thirty-three she chose the monastic life, meaning to cross to Gaul, where her sister was a nun, and live as an exile for the Lord's sake. She had waited a year among the East Angles when Bishop Aidan called her home.

Aidan and other learned men taught her while she ruled a house at Hartlepool. Later she gained land for ten households at Whitby, where she built, or set in order, a monastery for men and women. As in the first church of Jerusalem, no one there was rich and no one poor. She made her people read the Scriptures so closely that many became fit to be priests. "Her wisdom was so great that not only ordinary people in their needs, but even kings and princes, sometimes sought her advice and found it."

In 664 the kings met in her monastery, because the Irish-trained clergy and those who followed Rome kept Easter on different dates. Hild and her people held with Bishop Colman, on the side of Aidan who had taught her, while the priest Wilfrid argued for Rome. King Oswy ruled for Rome, and Bede says all present agreed, though he records no words of hers.

When the steward brought her the man who could not sing, she had him repeat his verses before learned men. They judged it a grace from God and set him a passage of Scripture, which he returned the next morning in verse. So she made Caedmon a monk and had him taught the whole of sacred history, which he turned into English song.

A fever held her for six years, yet she kept teaching. At cockcrow on 17 November 680, she called the women of the house together and urged them to keep the peace of the Gospel among themselves and with all others.

Bede says others after Caedmon tried religious poems in English, but none could match him. One of her students, Oftfor, went on to Archbishop Theodore in Kent and then to Rome. "All who knew her used to call her Mother, for her outstanding devotion and grace," and eight centuries later, prayers at York still asked Saint Hild to pray for them. Bede himself gave the measure of her house: "Later we saw five bishops come from that same monastery. All of them were men of rare merit and holiness."
