# Source sheet: Hild of Whitby

Researcher's sheet for the Who brief `hild-of-whitby` (era 4), 2026-11-17. The writer works from the outline; the checker uses this sheet.

**Conventions.** Line numbers are the file's own lines. HE, HEL, VW, ASC and YP are raw OCR. In quotes I joined words broken across lines ("re- / called" becomes "recalled") and left out page running heads, margin notes and footnote markers. Nothing else is changed: OCR faults stay in place, with the right reading after them in [square brackets marked "read"]. Chapter headings in HE (such as "[a.d. 680.]") are the editor's, not Bede's.

**Chapter numbers.** HE numbers its chapters one way (Giles). HEL numbers them two ways, e.g. "XXI [XXIII]" for Hild's life. This sheet cites by file and line, and gives the book and chapter as the file prints it in HE.

**Time gaps.** Bede finished his history in 731 ("in the 731st year of the incarnation of our Lord", HE 15190–15192; the last dated entry is for 731, HE 15126). Hild died in 680 (HE 10890–10896). So Bede wrote about fifty years after her death. He was a monk of Wearmouth and Jarrow in the same kingdom (HE 15420–15429). For the Northumbrian church he names no single informant: "I received not from any particular author, but by the faithful testimony of innumerable witnesses, who might know or remember the same; besides what I had of my own knowledge" (HE 216–224). Of the five bishops from her house he writes "we afterwards saw" (HE 10973). The rough gaps below are my own estimates, for the card only.

**Witness kinds used.** No writing by Hild herself survives in the Library, and none is known to me from it: there is no OWN witness. There is no CONTEMP witness either, written in her lifetime. LATER = written after her death, with a rough gap. LATE-LEGEND = no item here.
- Bede (HE, HEL): LATER, about 50 years, from the same kingdom, writing while men from her house were still bishops.
- Stephen, *Life of Wilfrid* (VW): LATER, about 30 years. Raine dates it after Wilfrid's "first anniversary or year day, which was in A.D. 710" (VW 1763–1764). Stephen came north in 669 (VW 1680–1684), so he lived in Northumbria in Hild's time. He wrote for Wilfrid's own monastery of Ripon (VW 1735–1738) and takes Wilfrid's side.
- The letter of Pope John, copied into VW chapter 54 (VW 9395–9426): LATER, written some years after her death. The file's lines give no year for it.
- *Anglo-Saxon Chronicle* (ASC, ASCOE): LATER, about 200 years. Gomme's introduction places its making in Alfred's time, "towards the end of the ninth century" (ASC 167–168, 211–240).
- York Prymer (YP): LATER, about 800 years (15th–16th-century books, YP title header).

**No other Hild material is held.** I searched `cic/texts/` and `cic/corpus-map/` for Hild, Hilda, Whitby, Streaneshalch and its spellings. Beyond the files above, the hits are later retellings of Bede (Aelred of Rievaulx, 12th century, `aelred-rievaulx_opera-pl195-lat_migne1855.txt` 62268–62278, on Elfleda and Whitby), modern histories (Hefele), or other people with similar names. None adds to Bede. Not held: Caedmon's Hymn in Old English (Bede gives only its Latin sense, and says so, HE 11183–11188); any Whitby text of the period; any letter or writing of Hild's.

## 1. Setting

S1. **The year and the century.** Bede dates her death "TN [read In] the year of the incarnation of our Lord 680" (HE 10890), "on the 17th of November, at the age of sixty-six years" (HE 10895–10896). The synod at her monastery "happened in the year of our Lord's incarnation 664" (HE 8236–8237). Her life therefore falls in the **seventh century**. LATER (~50). Documented. Her birth year (about 614) is my arithmetic from these two figures, not a statement in any source: Inferential-Thin as an exact year; say "born about 614" only if a date is needed, never "born in 614".

S2. **The place: Northumbria.** Bede defines the kingdom: "the nation of the Northumbrians, that is, the nation of the Angles that live on the north side of the river Humber" (HE 4268–4270). LATER. Documented. Her places:
- **Heruteu.** "the monastery, called Heruteu, or, " The island of the Hart,"" (HE 7768–7769). Jane's note gives it as Hartlepool (HE 15949, OCR "7/ifrtt/(fw = Hartlepool"). Identification: Widely Accepted (editor's note, 1903).
- **Streaneshalch.** "the monastery of Streaneshalch, which signifies the Bay of the Lighthouse" (HE 7958–7960); Bede's Latin "quod interpretatur sinus Fari" (HEL 21400). Jane's note: "Streaneshalch = Whitby" (HE 15953, OCR "'WWithy"). The English text also prints "Streaneshalch [Whitby]" (HE 10952). The Chronicle names her "abbess in Whitby" (ASC 2827–2828), but in the Old English it reads "on Streones heale" (ASCOE 2551, 2609); Gomme's index gives "Whitby [Streonesheal]" (ASC 21141). Whitby: Widely Accepted.
- **Hackness.** A monastery she built in her last year (HE 11065–11067). Jane: "Hackness. Near Whitby" (HE 16013). Bede: "These monasteries are about thirteen miles distant from each other" (HE 11108–11109).
- **The river Wear.** "the land of one family on the north side of the river Wear" (HE 10926–10927).
- **The East Angles.** Where she waited a year (HE 10909–10910, 10923–10924).

S3. **The tradition.** The census name is **Anglo-Saxon Christianity** (CEN 42026), dates 597–793 (CEN 42029). Its teaser: "England, 597 to 793 — converted within living memory, and already sending its own monks and nuns back across the Channel" (CEN 42102). Its summary: "Born of two missions, one from Rome and one from Ireland, which settled their quarrel over Easter at Whitby in 664" (CEN 42059). This name is for the card.

(a) **Plain spoken forms a narrator may say**, each tied to Hild's own record:
1. "the newly converted English of Northumbria". Support: Bede on the Northumbrians "from the time when they received the faith of Christ till this present" (HE 214–216); CEN 42102, "converted within living memory". Hild herself took the faith with King Edwin (HE 10900–10903), whose baptism Bede dates to 627 (HE 4996–4999).
2. "a church brought by two missions, one from Rome and one from the Irish". Support from Hild's own life: she took the faith "at the preaching of Paulinus, the first bishop of the Northumbrians" (HE 10902–10904); Paulinus was one of the helpers Pope Gregory sent to Augustine (HE 2778–2784). And Bishop Aidan "frequently visited and diligently instructed her" (HE 10943–10945); Aidan was sent by "the elders of the Scots" (HE 5729–5746) and was "a monk of the island called Hii" (HE 5794–5795). CEN 42060 gives the same two missions.
3. "the Scots, as Bede calls the Irish". Support: Bede, "the Scots, who, migrating from Ireland" (HE 381); Giles's bracket "Scots [Irish]" (HE 5372). Needed for the synod.

(b) **Do not say:** "the Celtic Church" or "Celtic Christianity" (no held source uses the phrase; it suggests a separate church). Do not say "Roman Catholic" in its modern sense: Bede's "Catholic Easter" (HE 8230, 8235) means the Easter of the whole church as Bede saw it. Do not say that Whitby "settled the English church" as a whole: Bede shows the Northumbrian king and his council (HE 7962–7963, 8214–8218). Do not say "Anglo-Saxon Christianity" aloud as if it were a church's name.

## 2. Life events

M1. **Birth and family.** "For she was nobly born, being the daughter of Hereric, nephew to King Edwin" (HE 10899–10901). Latin: "filia nepotis Eduini regis, uocabulo Hererici" (HEL 24831–24832), "the daughter of the king's nephew, named Hereric". So Hild was Edwin's great-niece, not his niece. Her mother was "Bregusuit" (HE 11021; Latin "Bregusuid", HEL 25000). Her father "lived in banishment, under Cerdic, king of the Britons, where he was also poisoned" (HE 11022–11024). LATER (~50). Documented as Bede's account. (See D6 for a late, different family line.)

M2. **She took the faith with Edwin.** "with which king she also embraced the faith and mysteries of Christ, at the preaching of Paulinus, the first bishop of the Northumbrians, of blessed memory, and preserved the same undefiled till she attained to the sight of him in heaven" (HE 10901–10906). Edwin "was baptized at York, on the holy day of Easter, being the 12th of April" in 627 (HE 4996–5002). LATER. Documented. Bede does not give her age, nor say she was baptized on the same day: say "with King Edwin", not "as a girl of thirteen" or "on that Easter Day".

M3. **Thirty-three years in the world.** Of her sixty-six years, "the first thirty-three of which she spent living most nobly in the secular habit; and more nobly dedicated the remaining half to our Lord in a monastic life" (HE 10896–10899). "Secular habit" means ordinary dress, life outside a monastery. LATER. Documented. Bede gives nothing of these thirty-three years: no marriage, no household, no deeds. Nothing may be added.

M4. **The plan to go abroad.** "Resolving to quit the secular habit, and to serve him alone, she withdrew into the province of the East Angles, for she was allied to the king; being desirous to pass over from thence into France, to forsake her native country and all she had, and so live a stranger for our Lord in the monastery of Cale" (HE 10908–10914). "Allied" is the translator's word; the Latin "erat namque propinqua regis illius" (HEL 24856) means "she was related to that king". "France" is Giles's word; Bede's Latin is "Galliam" (HEL 24858), Gaul. "a stranger for our Lord" renders "peregrinam pro Domino uitani [read uitam]" (HEL 24859): to live as an exile, away from home for good, for God's sake. Her reason: her sister "Hercsuid [read Heresuid], mother to Aldwulf, king of the East Angles, at that time living in the same monastery" (HE 10915–10921); "Being led by her example" (HE 10923). LATER. Documented.

M5. **A year of waiting, then Aidan calls her home.** "she continued a whole year in the aforesaid province, with the design of going abroad ; afterwards, Bishop Aidan being recalled home, he gave her the land of one family on the north side of the river Wear ; where for a year she also led a monastic life, with very few companions" (HE 10923–10929). **The English here is wrong.** Bede's Latin: "deinde ab Aidano episcopo patriam reuocata accepit locum unius familiae" (HEL 24866–24867), "then, called back home by Bishop Aidan, she received the land of one household". It was Hild who was called home, by Aidan. "The land of one family" is a unit of land: enough to keep one household. LATER. Documented (from the Latin).

M6. **Abbess of Heruteu (Hartlepool).** "After this she was made abbess in the monastery called Heruteu, which monastery had been founded, not long before, by the religious servant of Christ, Heiu, who is said to have been the first woman that in the province of the Northumbrians took upon her the habit and life of a nun, being consecrated by Bishop Aidan" (HE 10931–10937). Heiu "went away to the city of Calcacestir" (HE 10938; Jane: "Site doubtful", HE 16006). "Hilda, the servant of Christ, being set over that monastery, began immediately to reduce all things to a regular system, according as she had been instructed by learned men ; for Bishop Aidan, and other religious men that knew her and loved her, frequently visited and diligently instructed her, because of her innate wisdom and inclination to the service of God" (HE 10939–10946). "A regular system" means a life under a rule (Latin "regulari uita", HEL 24882). LATER. Documented. Aidan died in 651 (HE 15317–15318), so this began before 651: my inference, Inferential-Thin as a date.

M7. **The king's infant daughter is given to her, 655.** After his victory over Penda, King Oswy "gave his daughter Elfleda, who was scarce a year old, to be consecrated to Him in perpetual virginity" (HE 7755–7758), and twelve small estates for monasteries (HE 7758–7766). "The aforesaid daughter of King Oswy, thus dedicated to God, was put into the monastery, called Heruteu, or, " The island of the Hart," where, at that time, the Abbess Hilda presided" (HE 7767–7770). The chapter is dated "[A.D. 655.]" by the editor (HE 7703); Bede places the battle "in the thirteenth year of his reign, on the 15th of November" (HE 7786–7788), and his annals give "In the year 65 5, Penda was slain" (HE 15323). LATER. Documented. Bede does not say Oswy gave Hild land.

M8. **Whitby, about 657.** "and, two years after, having acquired a possession of ten families, at the place called Streaneshalch, she built a monastery there, in which the aforesaid king's daughter was first a learner, and afterwards a teacher of the monastic life" (HE 7770–7779). "Ten families" is land for ten households (Latin "possessione X familiarum", HEL 21156). Elsewhere Bede is less sure she built it: "it happened that she also undertook either to build or to arrange a monastery in the place called Streaneshalch [Whitby], which work she industriously performed" (HE 10950–10953; Latin "construendum siue ordinandum", HEL 24926). See D1. LATER. Documented that she ruled it; Contested whether she founded it or set an existing house in order.

M9. **How Whitby was run.** "for she put this monastery under the same regular discipline as she had done the former; and taught there the strict observance of justice, piety, chastity, and other virtues, and particularly of peace and charity ; so that, after the example of the primitive church, no person was there rich, and none poor, all being in common to all, and none having any property" (HE 10953–10964). "The primitive church" is the first church of Jerusalem in the Book of Acts (Plummer's margin cites "Act. iv. 32-34; cf. ii. 44-45", HEL 24919–24921). LATER. Documented.

M10. **A house of men and women.** Bede calls Whitby "the monastery, which we have so often mentioned, of men and women servants of God, at Streaneshalch" (HE 11517–11519). Caedmon was joined to "the rest of the brethren in her monastery" (HE 11216–11217). LATER. Documented.

M11. **Scripture and the altar.** "she obliged those who were under her direction to attend so much to reading of the Holy Scriptures, and to exercise themselves so much in works of justice, that many might be there found fit for ecclesiastical duties, and to serve at the altar" (HE 10967–10971). "To serve at the altar" means to be priests. LATER. Documented.

M12. **Her advice sought.** "Her prudence was so great, that not only indifferent persons, but even kings and princes, as occasion offered, asked and received her advice" (HE 10964–10967). Latin: "Tantae autem erat ipsa prudentiae, ut non solum mediocres quique in necessitatibus suis, sed etiam reges ac principes nonnumquam ab ea consilium quaererent, et inuenirent" (HEL 24936–24939). "Indifferent persons" means ordinary people ("mediocres"); the Latin adds "in their needs". "As occasion offered" renders "nonnumquam", sometimes. LATER. Documented as Bede's praise. No king is named as asking her.

M13. **The synod at her monastery, 664.** Bede sets up the quarrel. Under Aidan the difference "was patiently tolerated by all men" (HE 7903–7904). At the court "Easter was twice kept in one year ; and that when the king having ended the time of fasting, kept his Easter, the queen and her followers were still fasting, and celebrating Palm Sunday" (HE 7898–7902). Under Bishop Colman "a greater controversy arose about the observance of Easter, and the rules of ecclesiastical life" (HE 7918–7920). Then: "The controversy being there started, concerning Easter, or the tonsure, or other ecclesiastical affairs, it was agreed, that a synod should be held in the monastery of Streaneshalch, which signifies the Bay of the Lighthouse, where the Abbess Hilda, a woman devoted, to God, then presided ; and that there this controversy should be decided. The kings, both father and son, came thither" (HE 7955–7963). The tonsure is the shaved part of a cleric's head; the two sides cut it differently. LATER (~70 years after the synod). Documented.

M14. **Hild's side.** HE: "Bishop Colman with his Scottish clerks, and Agilbert with the priests Agatho and Wilfrid, James and Romanus were on their side ; but the Abbess Hilda and her followers were for the Scots, as was also the venerable Bishop Cedd, long before ordained by the Scots, as has been said above, and he was in that council a most careful interpreter for both parties" (HE 7963–7970). The English punctuation blurs the sides. The Latin is clear: "Cohnan [read Colman] cum clericis suis de Scottia, Agilberctus cum Agathone et Uilfrido preshyteris [read presbyteris]. lacobus [read Iacobus] et Romanus in horum parte erant ; Hild abbatissa cum suis in paiie [read parte] Scottorum" (HEL 21408–21414). So Colman led the Scots' side, Agilbert and Wilfrid the other, and Hild with her people was on Colman's side. King Oswy ran the debate: "he then commanded his bishop, Colman, first to declare what the custom was" (HE 7981–7983). Bede records no words spoken by Hild. LATER. Documented. **DEFENDED**: the Easter and customs her teachers from Iona kept.

M15. **The ruling.** Wilfrid argued for the Roman side; the king asked whether the keys of heaven were given to Peter (HE 8192–8203), and ruled: "he is the door-keeper, whom I will not contradict" (HE 8205–8206). "Tlie [read The] king having said this, all present, both great and small, gave their assent, and renouncing the more imperfect institution, resolved to conform to that which they found to be better" (HE 8214–8218). Colman "perceiving that his doctrine was rejected, and his sect despised, took with him such as would not comply with the Catholic Easter and the tonsure ... and went back into Scotland" (HE 8227–8233). Cedd changed sides (HE 8233–8236). LATER. Documented. Bede does not say what Hild herself said or did after the ruling: see D4.

M16. **Stephen also places the meeting in her presence.** "in coenobio quod Streuneshalgh dicitur, presente ganctimoniale [read sanctimoniale] matre piissima Hilde, presentibus quoque regibus, et duobus, Colmanno et /Egilberhto [read Ægilberhto], episcopis, de Paschali ratione conquirebant" (VW 5081–5087; a footnote marker and Raine's margin notes left out). In plain English: "in the monastery called Streuneshalgh, in the presence of the nun, the most devout mother Hild, and in the presence of the kings and of two bishops, Colman and Agilbert, they inquired into the reckoning of Easter". LATER (~30–45). Documented. Stephen gives no side for Hild.

M17. **Caedmon.** Caedmon chapter: "There was in this abbess's monastery a certain brother, particularly remarkable for the grace of God, who was wont to make pious and religious verses, so that whatever was interpreted to him out of Scripture, he soon after put the same into poetical expressions of much sweetness and humility, in English, which was his native language" (HE 11139–11145). He had "lived in a secular habit till he was well advanced in years" and could not sing; at feasts "when he saw the instrument come towards him, he rose up from table and returned home" (HE 11154–11164). One night, in "the stable, where he had to take care of the horses that night" (HE 11167–11168; Latin "ad stabula iumentorum", HEL 25189, the stalls of the beasts of burden), "a person appeared to him in his sleep" (HE 11170; Latin "adstitit ei quidam i>er [read per] somnium", HEL 25191, someone stood by him in a dream). It said: ""Caedmon, sing some song to me."" (HE 11171–11172), then ""Sing the beginning of created beings,"" (HE 11177). "Hereupon he presently began to sing verses to the praise of God, which he had never heard" (HE 11178–11180). Bede gives only the sense, and says so: "This is the sense, but not the words in order as he sang them in his sleep" (HE 11187–11188). LATER (~50). Documented as Bede's account. WONDER: the gift in a dream; Bede's chapter heading calls it "the gift of writing verses ... bestowed by heaven" (HE 11136–11137), and Bede says "he did not learn the art of poetry from men, but from God" (HE 11150–11151).

M18. **Hild receives Caedmon.** "In the morning he came to the steward, his superior, and having acquainted him with the gift he had received, was conducted to the abbess, by whom he was ordered, in the presence of many learned men, to tell his dream, and repeat the verses, that they might all give their judgment what it was, and whence his verse proceeded. They all concluded, that heavenly grace had been conferred on him by our Lord. They expounded to him a passage in holy writ, either historical, or doctrinal, ordering him, if he could, to put the same into verse. Having undertaken it, he went away, and returning the next morning, gave it to them composed in most excellent verse ; whereupon the abbess, embracing the grace of God in the man, instructed him to quit the secular habit, and take upon him the monastic life ; which being accordingly done, she associated him to the rest of the brethren in her monastery, and ordered that he should be taught the whole series of sacred history" (HE 11196–11218). Latin: "Undo [read Unde] mox abbatissa amplexata gratiam Dei in uiro, saecularem illum habitum relinquere, et monachicum suscipere jDropositum [read propositum] docuit" (HEL 25241–25243). LATER. Documented. Note: the steward brought him; the learned men judged; then she acted. Bede does not date this. The "[a.d. 680.]" on the chapter heading is the editor's (HE 11137).

M19. **What Caedmon then sang.** "Thus Cædmon, keeping in mind all he heard, and as it were chewing the cud, converted the same into most harmonious verse ; and sweetly repeating the same, made his masters in their turn his hearers. He sang the creation of the world, the origin of man, and all the history of Genesis" (HE 11219–11224; OCR "C^dmon"), then Exodus, the life of Christ, the apostles, the judgment (HE 11224–11233). LATER. Documented.

M20. **Six years of fever.** "Falling into a fever, she fell into a violent heat, and was afflicted with the same for six years continually ; during all which time she never failed either to return thanks to her Maker, or publicly and privately to instruct the flock committed to her charge" (HE 11045–11050). LATER. Documented.

M21. **Her death, 17 November 680.** "In the seventh year of her sickness, the distemper turning inwards, she approached her last day, and about cockcrowing, having received the holy communion to further her on her way, and called together the servants of Christ that were within the same monastery, she admonished them to preserve evangelical peace among themselves, and with all others ; and as she was making her speech, she joyfully saw death approaching, or if I may speak in the words of our Lord, passed from death to life" (HE 11053–11062). "Servants of Christ" renders Latin "ancellis Christi" (HEL 25046), handmaids of Christ: the women of the house. "Evangelical peace" means the peace of the Gospel. Bede gives her words only as reported speech, not as a quotation. Bede's annal: "The same year also the Abbess Hilda died at Streaneshalch" (HE 15362–15363). The Chronicle: "And the same year died Hild abbess in Whitby" (ASC 2827–2828). LATER. Documented.

M22. **Begu's vision (wonder).** "That same night it pleased Almighty God, by a manifest vision, to make known her death in another monastery, at a distance from hers, which she had built that same year, and is called Hackness" (HE 11064–11067). The nun Begu, "having dedicated her virginity to God, had served Him upwards of thirty years" (HE 11068–11070), heard "the well-known sound of a bell in the air" (HE 11072–11073), saw "the top of the house open, and a strong light pour in from above" (HE 11076–11077), and "saw the soul of the aforesaid servant of God in that same light, attended and conducted to heaven by angels" (HE 11078–11084). At dawn "the brothers came with news of her death" (HE 11098–11099). Bede adds a second report: "It is also reported, that her death was, in a vision, made known the same night to one of the holy virgins who loved her most passionately, in the same monastery where the said servant of God died" (HE 11111–11114). LATER. Documented as Bede's account. WONDER, in Bede's voice; the second vision is in Bede's words "It is also reported".

M23. **Her mother's dream (wonder).** "for it was necessary that the dream which her mother, Bregusuit, had, during her infancy, should be fulfilled" (HE 11020–11022). Seeking her banished husband in a dream, "she found a most precious jewel under her garment, which, whilst she was looking on it very attentively, cast such a light as spread itself throughout all Britain ; which dream was brought to pass in her daughter that we speak of, whose life was a bright example, not only to herself, but to all who desired to live well" (HE 11028–11034). LATER. Documented as Bede's account. WONDER, in Bede's voice (a dream he reads as fulfilled).

## 3. What came of her (heaviest first)

I1. **A school for bishops.** The heaviest: it is the fruit Bede himself gives for her teaching, and he follows the men through his later books. "In short, we afterwards saw five bishops taken out of that monastery, and all of them men of singular merit and sanctity, whose names were Bosa, Hedda, Oftfor, John, and Wilfrid" (HE 10973–10976). Latin: "Denique V ex eodem monasterio postea episcopos uidimus, et hos omnes singularis meriti ac sanctitatis uiros, quorum haec sunt nomina, Bosa, Aetla, Oftfor, lohannes [read Iohannes], et Uilfrid" (HEL 24946–24949; Plummer's margin note "underher." left out). Their sees: Bosa at York; the second at Dorchester; John at Hagulstad (Hexham); Wilfrid at York (HE 10976–10984; HEL 24949–24954). Oftfor studied "in both the monasteries of Hilda", then went to Archbishop Theodore in Kent and to Rome, and became bishop of the Hwicce (HE 10983–11003). A sixth, "Tatfrid, a most learned and industrious man", was chosen bishop "from the same abbess's monastery" but died before he could be ordained (HE 11008–11013). Bede carries two of them on: John's miracles and his move from Hexham to York after Bosa's death (HE 12160–12167, 12255–12260), and John, in old age, "ordained Wilfrid, his priest, bishop of the church of York" (HE 12538–12541). LATER. Documented. (The second name: see D3. This Wilfrid is not Wilfrid of Ripon: see L9.)

I2. **Caedmon and sacred song in English.** Bede: "By his verses the minds of many were often excited to despise the world, and to aspire to heaven. Others after him attempted, In the English nation, to compose religious poems, but none could ever compare with him" (HE 11145–11150). Bede says he wrote "to turn away all men from the love of vice" (HE 11234–11236). Plummer in 1896 counts "the tale of the cowherd of St. Hilda's monastery, who received his gift of song" among the parts of Bede that "will live as long as Englishmen have any care for their country and their Church" (HEL 3149–3156). LATER (Bede ~50; Plummer ~1200). Documented that Bede gives the story and its reach. Bede does not say Caedmon was the first English poet. Plummer's "cowherd" is not Bede's word (M17).

I3. **Whitby went on as a royal house and refuge.** Oswy's daughter Elfleda was at Whitby "first a learner, and afterwards a teacher of the monastic life" (HE 7778–7779). "In that same monastery, she and her father, Oswy, her mother, Eanlleda [read Eanfleda], her mother's father, Edwin, and many other noble persons, are buried in the church of the holy Apostle Peter" (HE 7781–7785). After a defeat in 685 Bishop Trumwine took refuge there (HE 11509–11528), when "The royal virgin, Killed [read Elfled], with her mother, Eanfled ... then presided over that monastery" (HE 11529–11531). LATER. Documented. (Bede does not say who chose Elfleda to rule after Hild.)

I4. **Her name and example.** "Thus this servant of Christ, Abbess Hilda, whom all that knew her called Mother, for her singular piety and grace, was not only an example of good life, to those that lived in her monastery, but afforded occasion of amendment and salvation to many who lived at a distance, to whom the fame was brought of her industry and virtue" (HE 11014–11020). Latin: "quam omnes, qui nouerant, ob msigne [read insigne] pietatis et gratiae matrem uocare consuerant" (HEL 24994–24995). The Chronicle keeps her death year (ASC 2825–2828). LATER. Documented.

I5. **Kept in prayer long after.** In the York litany of the 15th and 16th centuries: "Sancta Hilda ora" (YP 11647; OCR "$ancta Hilda8 ora"), "Saint Hild, pray". The editor notes the York book invokes "Wilfrid, William, or Hilda", whom the Salisbury book does not (YP 1501–1503). LATER (~800). Documented. (A York calendar entry "hilde virginis non mar." stands at late August, YP 4663; the file does not say which Hild it is. Not used.)

I6. **What she defended.** **DEFENDED:** at the synod of 664 she and her people "were for the Scots" (HE 7966–7967): the Easter and customs of Aidan's Iona mission, which had taught her (M6). The king ruled the other way (M15). Lower in weight: Bede records no later act of hers on it. See also D5 (her opposition to Wilfrid, in one letter only).

## 4. Links to other people

L1. **Edwin, king of Northumbria.** Kin: her great-uncle (M1). Took the faith with him (M2). Buried at her monastery (HE 7782–7784).
L2. **Paulinus, first bishop of the Northumbrians.** She took the faith at his preaching (HE 10902–10904). Label: *heard him preach*.
L3. **Aidan, bishop of Lindisfarne.** Called her home, gave her land on the Wear, visited and taught her (HE 10943–10945; HEL 24866–24867). Label: *taught her*.
L4. **Heresuid (Hereswith), her sister.** A nun at Cale; Hild meant to follow her (M4). Label: *kin*.
L5. **Heiu.** Founded Heruteu before her, "the first woman" in Northumbria to take the nun's habit (HE 10934–10937). Label: *came after her as abbess*.
L6. **Oswy, king of Northumbria, and Elfleda his daughter.** Oswy gave Elfleda to her at Heruteu (M7); Elfleda was her pupil, later a teacher and head at Whitby (I3). Labels: *received his daughter*; *taught her*.
L7. **Colman, bishop of Lindisfarne.** Hild was on his side at the synod (M14). Label: *sided with him*.
L8. **Wilfrid of Ripon.** Argued against her side at the synod (M14–M15). Label: *opposed at the synod*. (See D5.)
L9. **The five bishops from her house:** Bosa, Hedda/Aetla, Oftfor, John, Wilfrid (I1). Label: *came from her house*. This Wilfrid is a later man, made bishop of York by John (HE 12538–12541), not Wilfrid of Ripon, who was a priest already in 664 (HE 7964–7965) and bishop at Hexham while John was still alive (HE 12255–12260).
L10. **Cedd, bishop.** On her side at the synod, and interpreter for both parties; changed after (HE 7967–7970, 8233–8236). Label: *sided with her, then changed*.
L11. **Caedmon.** Received into her monastery at her order (M18). Label: *received him*.
L12. **Bede.** Wrote her life (HE 10888–11131). Label: *wrote her life*.
L13. **Theodore, archbishop of Canterbury.** Oftfor went from her houses to study with him (HE 10985–10988). Label: *a pupil of hers went to him*.
L14. **Begu, nun of Hackness.** Saw the vision at her death (M22).

## 5. Where sources disagree, or are later than the life

D1. **Built, or set in order?** Bede III.24 says plainly "she built a monastery there" (HE 7772–7773; Latin "monasterium construxit", HEL 21157). Bede IV.23 says she "undertook either to build or to arrange" it (HE 10950–10951; "construendum siue ordinandum", HEL 24926). Bede himself is not sure. Say "built, or set in order" or simply "ruled".

D2. **Elfleda's age at death.** HE: "being sixty years of age" (HE 7780). Latin: "conpleto undeLX annorum numero" (HEL 21159), fifty-nine years. Not story material.

D3. **The second bishop's name.** HE prints "Hedda" (HE 10975); Bede's Latin prints "Aetla" (HEL 24948). Name only Bosa, John and Oftfor if names are needed, or none.

D4. **Hild after the ruling.** Bede says "all present, both great and small, gave their assent" (HE 8215–8216). He does not name her among them, nor say she disagreed. Cedd's change is named; hers is not. Anything about her feelings or later practice is Inferential-Thin and must not be told.

D5. **Hild against Wilfrid at Rome (one witness).** The letter of Pope John copied in Stephen's *Life* says that under Pope Agatho, when Wilfrid appealed to Rome, his opponents were present, sent "a Theodoro veneramda [read veneranda] memorie archiepiscopo sancte Cantuariorum ecclesi ex hac Apostolica, sede mandato, et Hylde religiose memorie abbatisse, ad ewm [read eum] accusandum" (VW 9422–9426; a footnote marker left out; Raine's note gives the variant "Hilde", VW 9440): "from Theodore, archbishop of revered memory, and from Hild, abbess of religious memory, to accuse him". Bede's text names Hild nowhere in Wilfrid's affairs (all HE lines naming her are listed in this sheet). Single witness, in a partisan source, with no outcome given for her. Documented that the letter says it; Contested as to what it meant. Leave out of the story.

D6. **A different family line, late.** Thomas of Ely (Life of Etheldreda) calls Hereswith "filia fuit Hererici, nepotis Regis Edwini" (AS 94849–94850), agreeing with Bede, but then quotes annals that make Hereric the son of a man the OCR reads as "HDdfridus", son of "Edwinus" (AS 94867–94868), a grandson rather than a nephew. LATER, centuries after; the date of Thomas's Life is not given in the lines I read. Bede is preferred. Not story material.

D7. **The English translation errs in two places that matter.** (a) "Bishop Aidan being recalled home" (HE 10925–10926): the Latin says Hild was called home by Aidan (M5). (b) The sides at the synod (M14). Both are fixed above from the Latin.

D8. **"Cowherd".** Plummer's introduction (HEL 3149–3150) calls Caedmon a cowherd. Bede says he was keeping the horses, or beasts of burden, that night (M17). Bede is the witness; use Bede.
