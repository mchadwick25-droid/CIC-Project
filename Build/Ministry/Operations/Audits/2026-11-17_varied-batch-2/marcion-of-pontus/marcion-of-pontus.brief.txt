Once, Marcion met Polycarp, the bishop of Smyrna, and asked him, "Do you know me?" Polycarp answered, "I do know you: the firstborn of Satan." Irenaeus, who had seen Polycarp in his early youth, tells the story, and it shows how hard the church set itself against this man.

In the middle of the second century, in the church at Rome, a shipmaster from Pontus, on the Black Sea, began to ask a hard question. Everything we know of Marcion comes from the men who wrote against him, and Tertullian says that at first he believed as the Roman church did, giving it money in the warmth of new faith.

Then he brooded over where evil comes from. Jesus taught that a good tree cannot bear bad fruit, yet in Isaiah God says he creates evil. So, in Tertullian's account, Marcion judged the Creator a bad tree and found another, good God in Christ, although his real reasons remain uncertain.

Justin, writing to the emperor while Marcion lived, said: "Even now he is still teaching the people he has won over. He teaches them to believe in some other god, greater than the Creator."

Marcion set the gospel against the law of Moses in a book, now lost, called the Antitheses. As Tertullian put it, "Separating the law from the gospel is Marcion's own work, and his chief one." His opponents say he cut down Luke's Gospel and Paul's letters, but Tertullian reports that Marcion claimed the reverse: the defenders of Judaism had added to the Gospel, and he was restoring it.

Rome expelled him more than once, Tertullian says, and his gift was cast out with him.

The church answered him in book after book, arguing that one God made the world and sent Jesus. Irenaeus defended four Gospels against him, and Tertullian wrote five books. Yet his churches spread, and claimed martyrs of their own. Two centuries later, Epiphanius still found Marcionites in Rome and Italy, in Egypt, Syria and Cyprus, and as far away as Persia.
