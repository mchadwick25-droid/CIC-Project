Once, Marcion met Polycarp, the bishop of Smyrna, and asked him, "Do you know me?" Polycarp answered, "I do know you: the firstborn of Satan." Irenaeus, who had seen Polycarp in his early youth, tells the story, and it shows how hard the church set itself against this man.

In the middle of the second century, in the church at Rome, a shipmaster from Pontus, on the Black Sea, gave money in the warmth of new faith. Everything we know of Marcion comes from the men who wrote against him.

Tertullian says Marcion first believed as the Roman church did, until he began to brood over where evil comes from. Jesus taught that a good tree cannot bear bad fruit, yet in Isaiah God says he creates evil. So, in Tertullian's account, Marcion judged the Creator a bad tree and found another, good God in Christ, although his real reasons remain uncertain.

Justin, writing to the emperor while Marcion lived, said: "Even now he is still teaching the people he has won over. He teaches them to believe in some other god, greater than the Creator."

His lost book, the Antitheses, set the gospel against the law of Moses. In Tertullian's words, "Separating the law from the gospel is Marcion's own work, and his chief one." His opponents say he cut down Luke's Gospel and Paul's letters. Marcion claimed the opposite, Tertullian reports: the defenders of Judaism had added to the Gospel, and he was restoring it.

Tertullian says Rome expelled Marcion more than once, together with another teacher, Valentinus. The money Marcion had given in his first faith was cast out with him.

The church answered him in book after book, arguing that one God made the world and sent Jesus. Irenaeus defended four Gospels against him, Tertullian wrote five books, and Polycarp, Irenaeus says, turned many back at Rome. Yet Justin saw people of every nation follow Marcion, and even Tertullian granted that, as wasps make combs, Marcionites made churches. Those churches also claimed martyrs of their own. Two centuries later, Epiphanius still found Marcionites in Rome and Italy, in Egypt, Syria and Cyprus, as far away as Persia, and elsewhere.
