No minutes survive from the council of Nicaea, so we hear how it decided from men who were there. In 325, at Nicaea, in today's Turkey, bishops from across the Roman Empire met in the emperor Constantine's palace.

Eusebius, a bishop who was there and admired the emperor, tells how Constantine addressed the bishops in Latin, through an interpreter. He gave the floor to "those who presided", whom Eusebius never names, but listened, taking each side's points in turn. "He won some over and shamed others with his words," Eusebius writes.

Eusebius read his church's creed, and the phrase "of one being" was added. Athanasius, who was there as a deacon, says the bishops first chose plain words from Scripture, but their opponents slipped around them, so they wrote again, more distinctly still. Eusebius says each member signed what they agreed, although a few would not accept all of it.

Constantine then feasted the bishops, gave each gifts by rank and sent them home with a plea for peace. Eusebius claims the agreement held, while the decades that followed show it did not. Athanasius, writing later in the council's defence, argued that the creed was no new rule, pointing to its wording. For the date of Easter they wrote "It seemed good", but "about the faith they did not write, 'It seemed good.' They wrote, 'This is what the universal Church believes.'"
