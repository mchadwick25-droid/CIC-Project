# Outline: the Council of Nicaea (325)

Sheet = `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder; tags (K1, S1, M5, I3...) point to its entries, which give file and lines.

- **Length:** about 450–500 words (Mark, 2026-11-17: may run long in Daniel's voice). Reason: a major council that did five kinds of work, with three voices to follow (Constantine, Arius, Eusebius) and an after-history on each decision. The record is rich; do not fill beyond it.
- **Setting, in words to say aloud:** "In 325, in the city of Nicaea, in today's Turkey, bishops from across the Roman Empire met at the call of the emperor Constantine, soon after, in his words, the tyrants' hostility had been 'forever removed'." (S1, S2, S3, S4, S5). Or, from S5: "the churches of the empire, newly free of persecution". Do not give a number of years.
- **Thread:** Called to end a quarrel in Egypt and a split over Easter, Nicaea gave the church a creed calling the Son "of one being with the Father", one Easter, a settlement in Egypt and twenty rules of church life; later councils set its creed first, though for decades it divided the church.
- **Shape:** Council. It includes what called it, the major issue and its story, what else it did, its decisions and its impact, in the order the record tells them (see Story beats).
- **Openings:** (1) Eusebius's picture of the Easter split: on the same feast, some Christians fasting while others feasted (K2). (2) The emperor telling the bishops that strife inside the church was worse to him than any war (M2, Q2). (3) Arius's own complaint, before the council (D-3, Q1).

**Story beats** (ordered as the record tells it: Eusebius for what called it and the opening, then the council's own letter to Egypt, which reports the faith first, then Melitius, then Easter, NC 6338–6406. Each beat names which of the five elements it carries. The major issue, beats 3–5, is about a third of the story, not the whole.)

1. *What called it.* Two troubles reached the emperor. In Egypt, Eusebius writes, bishops clashed with bishops in every city, and some even insulted the emperor's statues. And the churches kept Easter on different days: one party held to the Jewish reckoning, so some fasted while others feasted. K1, K2. Documented. (Eusebius also names a "Theban and Egyptian schism" in Egypt; do not identify it with Melitius, K1.)
2. *What called it.* Constantine's letter to Alexandria failed, so he called bishops "from all quarters", lent them the public post and horses, and met them at Nicaea. He told them, in Latin through an interpreter, that strife inside the church was worse than war. K3, S4, M1, M2, M3. Documented as Eusebius's account. Optional detail: he waited for the bishops to beckon before he sat (M1). Who came: more than 250 bishops by Eusebius's count, from Syria to Spain, a bishop from Persia, two priests for Rome's aged bishop (C1, C2, C3; count Contested).
3. *The major issue.* Bishop Alexander of Alexandria had questioned his presbyters (priests); Arius held that the Son had a beginning and God had none, and Alexander drove him out. D-1, D-3, Q1. Documented. Reason it mattered: whether the Son is eternal like the Father.
4. *Its story.* The debate, told by three men who were there, each differently: Eusebius read his church's creed, and the emperor asked for one word, homoousios, "of one being"; Athanasius says Arius's friends read the bishops' words, like "from God" and "like the Father", as true of all creatures too, and were caught "whispering ... and winking"; Eustathius says a statement from that party was torn up "in the sight of all". M5, M6, M7. Each Documented as its teller's account; Contested as one story (sheet D2).
5. *Its decision.* A creed calling the Son "God of God, Light of Light", "begotten, not made", of one being with the Father, cursing (declaring cut off) anyone who said "there was a time when he was not". Eusebius signed, he told his church, with peace as his aim (Q3). Arius and two bishops, Theonas and Secundus, were condemned; Constantine exiled Arius (that from a historian a century later). M8, M9, M10, M11. Documented (exile Widely Accepted).
6. *What else it did, and decided.* The council's letter turns next to Egypt's other trouble: Melitius, who had ordained clergy of his own, kept "the bare title of his rank" but could ordain no one, and his clergy served under Alexander's. Then Easter, one of the two troubles that called it: one day everywhere, the day kept at Rome and in Alexandria. M12, M13, K2. Documented.
7. *What else it did, and decided.* It also set down twenty rules (canons) for the order of the church: how a bishop is made, councils twice a year in every province, mercy, after years of penance, for those who had given way under Licinius without being forced, the last communion never refused to the dying, clergy forbidden to lend at interest. M14. Documented.

**What came of it** (about a third of the story; heaviest first)
1. The creed became the fixed reference: Constantinople in 381 ruled that the faith of the 318 fathers "shall not be set aside"; Chalcedon in 451 called it "pre-eminent". I1. Documented. **Defended**: Athanasius wrote a whole book defending its words "of the essence" and "of one being". I2.
2. It did not hold at once. Eusebius of Nicomedia and Theognis were brought back, and Arius was recalled. At Rimini in 359, Jerome wrote, "the whole world groaned, and was astonished to find itself Arian." I3. Documented. It divided those who held the word from those who would not.
3. Its rules travelled as church law: about ninety years later in Carthage, bishops read aloud the creed and "the statutes ... in twenty heads" from the copy Caecilian had brought home, and wrote east for exact copies; a Persian synod, dated 410 in the work Braun cites (MAR 894), swore to accept the canons, and its acts set out the Nicene creed. I4. Documented (Carthage); Widely Accepted (Persia).
4. Easter did not settle at once either: a council at Antioch, sixteen years on (341, editor's date), threatened to cast out anyone who set aside "the decree of the holy and great Synod" on Easter. I5. Documented.
End there: on Carthage reading the copy aloud, or on the later councils setting the creed first.

**Quotes** (2 or 3)
- **Q1. Arius** (the major issue, beat 3; his voice). Source: Greek, THDG 70615–70616: "Διωχόμεθα [Διωκόμεθα] ὅτι εἴπαμεν ᾿Αρχὴν ἔχει ὁ Υἱὸς, ὁ δὲ Θεὸς ἄναρχός ἐστι." English, NP3 6045–6046: "We are persecuted, because we say that the Son has a beginning, but that God is without beginning." Rendered from the Greek: **"We are persecuted because we said, 'The Son has a beginning, but God has no beginning.'"** Job: puts the dispute in the condemned man's own words.
- **Q2. Constantine** (why he called it). Source: Greek, EUSG 11167–11169: "ὡς ἔμοιγε παντὸς πολέμου xcl [καὶ] μάχης δεινῆς καὶ χαλεπωτέρα ἡ τῆς ἐχκχλησίας [ἐκκλησίας] τοῦ ϑεοῦ ἐμφύλιος νενόμισται στάσις καὶ μᾶλλον ταῦτα τῶν ἔξωϑεν λυπηρὰ καταφαίνεται." English, EUS 64468–64471: "for, in my judgment, intestine strife within the Church of God, is far more evil and dangerous than any kind of war or conflict; and these our differences appear to me more grievous than any outward trouble." Rendered from the Greek: **"For I count civil strife in the church of God worse than any war or fierce battle. These troubles seem more painful to me than any from outside."** Job: the emperor's stake in the council. Say it is as Eusebius reports it.
- **Q3. Eusebius of Caesarea** (how a hesitant bishop signed). Source: Greek, SOCG 7326–7328 and 7405–7407: "Διόπερ τῇ διανοίᾳ kai [καὶ] αὐτοὶ συντιθέμεθα, οὐδὲ τὴν φωνὴν τοῦ Ouoovciov [ὁμοουσίου] παραιτούμενοι, ToU [τοῦ] τῆς εἰρήνης σκοποῦ πρὸ ὀφθαλμῶν ἡμῖν κειμένου, kat [καὶ] TOU [τοῦ] μὴ τῆς ὀρθῆς ἐκπεσεῖν διανοίας." English, ATH 19741–19744: "On this account we assented to the sense ourselves, without declining even the term “One in essence,” peace being the object which we set before us, and stedfastness in the orthodox view." Rendered from the Greek: **"So we too agree with this meaning. We do not refuse even the word 'of one being'. We kept peace before our eyes as our aim, and also not to fall from the right meaning."** Job: shows agreement won partly for peace. Use Q3 only if room; Q1 and Q2 come first.

**Connection to carry into the story**
- Nicaea to Constantinople (381) and Chalcedon (451): its creed passed to them as the standard they had to keep, "shall not be set aside" (NC 13970–13972) and "pre-eminent" (NC 20217–20220). The listener needs to hear that these later councils were met to settle new disputes and named Nicaea's faith first; say "318 fathers" is their name for Nicaea (I1).
- Nicaea to Carthage: Caecilian, who sat at Nicaea, carried home a written copy of the creed and the twenty canons; ninety years later the African bishops read that copy aloud as law and, when a disputed rule was not in it, wrote to Antioch, Alexandria and Constantinople for exact copies (NC 32523–32545; I4). The listener needs to understand that the canons travelled as written copies, and copies could be checked.
- Nicaea to Persia: Marutha read "the volume of the canons" to Persian bishops, who accepted them under oath (MAR 857–866; I4). Tell it as Braun's report of the Syriac acts.
- Nicaea to Antioch (341): the Easter ruling was enforced by a later council against those who still kept the older day (NC 9438–9452; I5).

**Links:** Constantine the Great (called it, sat in it, wrote its Easter letter); Melitius (limited by its decision); Arius (condemned by it; his letter kept by Theodoret); Alexander of Alexandria (put Arius out; named its "guide and fellow-worker"); Athanasius (there as deacon, by a later historian's account; wrote its defence); Eusebius of Caesarea (there; wrote its account); Eusebius of Nicomedia (Arius's correspondent; exiled, then restored); Eustathius of Antioch (there; wrote an account); Ossius of Córdoba (there); Sylvester of Rome (sent two priests); Caecilian of Carthage (there; brought its rules home); Marutha and Isaac (carried its canons into Persia).

**Who tells it**
- The creed, rules and letter to Egypt are the council's own; its minutes are lost (not held).
- Eusebius of Caesarea was there and admired the emperor; he was slow to accept the word. Aloud: "Eusebius, a bishop who was there and admired the emperor".
- Eustathius was there; Athanasius was there as a deacon, a later historian says; both were Arius's foes. Athanasius wrote years later. Aloud: "Athanasius, who was there as a deacon and wrote later in its defence".
- Arius's letter dates from before the council; it is kept in the history of Theodoret, who was against him.
- Socrates and Theodoret wrote over a century later. Aloud, for the exile or the scars: "a historian writing a century later".

**Do not say**
- "318 bishops came." → "More than 250, by the count of Eusebius, who was there; others who were there said about 270 or about 300. Later tradition fixed the number at 318."
- "The council was unanimous." → "All but a few signed; Arius and two bishops were condemned." Do not give a number of refusers (two or five: the witnesses differ).
- "Constantine presided" / "Constantine wrote the creed" / "the pope presided." → Eusebius says the emperor approved his creed and asked for one word to be added; the record does not say who presided. Rome's bishop did not come.
- "Ossius presided" / "Eustathius presided." → Eusebius speaks only of "those who presided" and names no one (EUS 64501).
- "The 318 fathers came to Nicaea." → When quoting Constantinople or Chalcedon, say "318 fathers" is the name later councils used for Nicaea, not a count.
- "Constantine made Christianity the empire's religion at Nicaea" / "Constantine was baptized there." → Nothing in the record. He called the council, sat in it and spoke at it.
- "Arius said Jesus was only a man" / "a mere creature." → Arius called the Son "perfect God" but said that before he was begotten "he was not", that he "has a beginning". The council's letter charged him with calling the Son a creature. Keep the two voices apart.
- "Homoousios means equal" / "the same as" / "similar." → "of one being (one substance) with the Father".
- "The creed churches say today was written at Nicaea." → The Nicaea creed is shorter: its statement of belief stops at "and in the Holy Spirit", and its curses follow; the creed said in churches today is the longer one tied to the council of Constantinople. If unsure, do not mention it.
- "The council decided which books belong in the Bible." → Nothing in the record. Do not say it.
- "The council fixed Easter by the full moon and the spring equinox." → It set one day for Easter everywhere, so that those in the East who had followed the Jewish custom would keep it on the same day as Rome and Alexandria.
- "Easter was settled for the sake of unity." alone → If a reason is given, the council's decision is credited to the council; the open contempt for the Jews is in Constantine's letter and is credited to Constantine, never carried forward into the narrator's voice or quoted. Eusebius's plainer reason (K2: some fasting while others feasted) may be used.
- "The council said the Jews ..." / "the bishops condemned the Jews." → The council's own letter says only that those in the East who followed the Jewish custom would now keep Easter with Rome and Alexandria (NC 6402–6406). The hostile words are Constantine's.
- "The council fixed the date of Easter for ever." → It set one day; a council at Antioch had to enforce it sixteen years later (I5).
- "The Theban and Egyptian schism" = Melitius. → Eusebius does not say so; keep them apart (K1).
- "Twenty canons on the Trinity" / "rules about doctrine." → The canons are about church order: making bishops, provincial councils, penance, the dying, clergy moving and lending at interest (M14). The faith is in the creed.
- "Canon 6 made Rome the head of the church." → It upholds old customs for Alexandria, Rome and Antioch side by side (M14); do not rank them.
- "Arius was executed" / "burned" / "excommunicated and killed." → He was condemned and sent into exile, as a historian a century later reports; later he was recalled.
- "This ended the Arian controversy." → Conflict went on for decades (Jerome on Rimini, 359; Constantinople, 381).
- "Athanasius led the bishops" / "Athanasius won the debate." → He was there as a deacon, a later historian says, and opposed Arius's friends; he wrote his account years later.
- Eusebius of Caesarea and Eusebius of Nicomedia are two different men. Eusebius of Caesarea signed; Eusebius of Nicomedia was Arius's friend and was exiled and later restored.
- "Eusebius of Nicomedia was exiled with Arius at the council." → Only Socrates says so, and his editor says Socrates gives the wrong reason for it (sheet D4). Say he was exiled and later restored.
- "Eustathius tore up Arius's creed." → Eustathius says "the formulary of Eusebius" was torn up "in the sight of all"; he does not say who tore it, and does not name which Eusebius. Theodoret, later, says it was a statement by Arius's friends.
- "The council met for two months, from 20 May to 25 August." → Say only "in 325"; the days are disputed.
- "Bishops with gouged eyes and burnt hands filled the hall." → Only Theodoret, a century later, says so. If used, name him.
- "Bishops wept / cheered / the emperor was moved." → No feelings beyond what Eusebius writes.
- "A Persian bishop and a Scythian" are Eusebius's words; do not name them.

- Constantine's banquet (M15): vivid, but it crowds the after-history; usable only in Eusebius's voice if room remains.
- The scars of the confessors (S3): a century later; include only with Theodoret named.
- Constantine's anti-Jewish invective in the Easter letter: not quoted, not paraphrased into the narrator's voice; if mentioned, credited to Constantine.
