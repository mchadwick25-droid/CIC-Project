# Outline: the Council of Nicaea (325)

Sheet = `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder; tags (S1, M5, I3...) point to its entries, which give file and lines.

- **Length:** stretch toward 3 minutes (about 400–450 words). Reason: a major council with three voices to follow (Arius, Constantine, Eusebius) and a long, divided after-history that must carry a third of the story. The record is rich; do not fill beyond it.
- **Setting, in words to say aloud:** "In 325, in the city of Nicaea, in today's Turkey, bishops from across the Roman Empire met at the call of the emperor Constantine, only a few years after the persecutions had ended." (S1, S2, S3, S4, S5). "A few years" rests only on Constantine's speech calling the tyrants' hostility "forever removed" (S3); do not give a number of years.
- **Thread:** Nicaea gave the church a creed calling the Son "of one being with the Father", which later councils set first of all as the faith of "the 318 fathers", although for half a century it divided the church it was meant to unite.
- **Shape:** Council.
- **Openings:** (1) Arius's own complaint, before the council: "We are persecuted because we said, 'The Son has a beginning, but God is without beginning'" (D-3, Q1). (2) The emperor telling the bishops that strife inside the church was worse to him than any war (M2, Q2). (3) A question the record raises: why would an emperor fresh from civil war care about one word?

**Story beats**
1. In Alexandria, Bishop Alexander questioned his presbyters (priests) about a passage of Scripture; Arius held to his answer, and the church split. Arius says Alexander drove him and his friends out of the city because they taught that the Son had a beginning and God had none. Reason: the question was whether the Son is eternal like the Father. D-1, D-3. Documented.
2. Constantine first wrote to both men that the question should never have been asked. When that failed, he called bishops "from all quarters" and lent them the public post and horses for the journey. D-1, D-2, S4. Documented.
3. They came from Syria, Egypt, Libya, Mesopotamia, Greece, even a bishop from Persia and one from Spain. Rome's aged bishop sent two priests. Eusebius, who was there, counts more than 250 bishops. C1, C2, C3. Documented (count Contested).
4. The emperor entered the palace hall in purple and gold, and, Eusebius says, waited for the bishops to beckon before he sat. He spoke in Latin, through an interpreter: division in the church was worse than war. Then the bishops began accusing one another. M1, M2, M3. Documented as Eusebius's account.
5. The witnesses tell the debate differently. Eusebius read his own church's creed; he says the emperor approved it but asked for one word, homoousios, "of one being". Athanasius says Arius's friends accepted every phrase from Scripture by reading it as true of all creatures, and were caught "whispering ... and winking", so the bishops chose plainer words. Eustathius says a statement from that party was torn up "in the sight of all". M5, M6, M7. Each Documented as its teller's account; Contested as one story (sheet D2).
6. The creed called the Son "God of God, Light of Light", "begotten, not made", of one being with the Father, and cursed anyone who said "there was a time when he was not". To curse (anathematize) meant to declare them cut off from the church. Eusebius signed, he told his church, with "peace" as his aim. M8, M9, Q3. Documented.
7. Arius was condemned; two bishops from Libya and Egypt shared his sentence; Constantine sent Arius into exile (that last from a historian a century later). The council also set one day for Easter everywhere and curbed Melitius, a rival bishop in Egypt. M10, M11, M12, M13. Documented (exile Widely Accepted).

**What came of it** (about a third of the story; heaviest first)
1. Its creed became the fixed reference: Constantinople in 381 ruled that the faith of the 318 fathers "shall not be set aside"; Chalcedon in 451 called it "pre-eminent". I1. Documented. **Defended**: Athanasius wrote a whole book defending its words "of the essence" and "of one being". I2.
2. It did not hold at once. Arius's friends Eusebius of Nicomedia and Theognis were brought back, and Arius was recalled. At Rimini in 359, Jerome wrote, "the whole world groaned, and was astonished to find itself Arian." I3. Documented. It divided those who held the word from those who would not.
3. Its rules travelled as church law: about ninety years later in Carthage, bishops read aloud the copy their bishop Caecilian had brought home, and wrote east for exact copies; a Persian synod, in 410 by Braun's account, accepted the canons and the creed. I4. Documented (Carthage); Widely Accepted (Persia).
End there: on Carthage reading the copy aloud, or on Jerome's line followed by the later councils.

**Quotes** (2 or 3)
- **Q1. Arius** (opening; his voice). Source: Greek, THDG 70615–70616: "Διωχόμεθα [Διωκόμεθα] ὅτι εἴπαμεν· ᾿Αρχὴν ἔχει ὁ Υἱὸς, ὁ δὲ Θεὸς ἄναρχός ἐστι." English, NP3 6045–6046: "We are persecuted, because we say that the Son has a beginning, but that God is without beginning." Rendered from the Greek: **"We are persecuted because we said, 'The Son has a beginning, but God has no beginning.'"** Job: puts the dispute in the condemned man's own words.
- **Q2. Constantine** (why he called it). Source: Greek, EUSG 11167–11169: "ὡς ἔμοιγε παντὸς πολέμου xcl [καὶ] μάχης δεινῆς καὶ χαλεπωτέρα ἡ τῆς ἐχκχλησίας [ἐκκλησίας] τοῦ ϑεοῦ ἐμφύλιος νενόμισται στάσις καὶ μᾶλλον ταῦτα τῶν ἔξωϑεν λυπηρὰ καταφαίνεται." English, EUS 64468–64471: "for, in my judgment, intestine strife within the Church of God, is far more evil and dangerous than any kind of war or conflict; and these our differences appear to me more grievous than any outward trouble." Rendered from the Greek: **"For I count civil strife in the church of God worse than any war or fierce battle. These troubles seem more painful to me than any from outside."** Job: the emperor's stake in the council. Say it is as Eusebius reports it.
- **Q3. Eusebius of Caesarea** (how a hesitant bishop signed). Source: Greek, SOCG 7326–7328 and 7405–7407: "Διόπερ τῇ διανοίᾳ kai [καὶ] αὐτοὶ συντιθέμεθα, οὐδὲ τὴν φωνὴν τοῦ Ouoovciov [ὁμοουσίου] παραιτούμενοι, ToU [τοῦ] τῆς εἰρήνης σκοποῦ πρὸ ὀφθαλμῶν ἡμῖν κειμένου, kat [καὶ] TOU [τοῦ] μὴ τῆς ὀρθῆς ἐκπεσεῖν διανοίας." English, ATH 19741–19744: "On this account we assented to the sense ourselves, without declining even the term “One in essence,” peace being the object which we set before us, and stedfastness in the orthodox view." Rendered from the Greek: **"So we too agree with this meaning. We do not refuse even the word 'of one being'. We kept peace before our eyes as our aim, and also not to fall from the right meaning."** Job: shows agreement won partly for peace. Use Q3 only if room; Q1 and Q2 come first.

**Links:** Constantine the Great (called it, sat in it, wrote its Easter letter); Arius (condemned by it; his letter kept by Theodoret); Alexander of Alexandria (put Arius out; named its "guide and fellow-worker"); Athanasius (there as deacon, by a later historian's account; wrote its defence); Eusebius of Caesarea (there; wrote its account); Eusebius of Nicomedia (Arius's correspondent; exiled, then restored); Eustathius of Antioch (there; wrote an account); Ossius of Córdoba (there); Sylvester of Rome (sent two priests); Caecilian of Carthage (there; brought its rules home); Marutha and Isaac (carried its canons into Persia).

**Who tells it**
- The creed, rules and letter to Egypt are the council's own; its minutes are lost (not held).
- Eusebius of Caesarea was there and admired the emperor; he was slow to accept the word. Aloud: "Eusebius, a bishop who was there and admired the emperor".
- Athanasius and Eustathius were there and were Arius's foes; Athanasius wrote years later. Aloud: "Athanasius, who was there as a young deacon and wrote later in its defence".
- Arius's letter dates from before the council and survives only because a hostile historian, Theodoret, copied it.
- Socrates and Theodoret wrote over a century later. Aloud, for the exile or the scars: "a historian writing a century later".

**Do not say**
- "318 bishops came." → "More than 250, by the count of Eusebius, who was there; others who were there said about 270 or about 300. Later tradition fixed the number at 318."
- "The council was unanimous." → "All but a few signed; Arius and two bishops were condemned." Do not give a number of refusers (two or five: the witnesses differ).
- "Constantine presided" / "Constantine wrote the creed" / "the pope presided." → Eusebius says the emperor approved his creed and asked for one word to be added; the record does not say who presided. Rome's bishop did not come.
- "Arius said Jesus was only a man" / "a mere creature." → Arius called the Son "perfect God" but said that before he was begotten "he was not", that he "has a beginning". The council's letter charged him with calling the Son a creature. Keep the two voices apart.
- "Homoousios means equal" / "the same as" / "similar." → "of one being (one substance) with the Father".
- "The creed churches say today was written at Nicaea." → The Nicaea creed is shorter and ends "and in the Holy Spirit"; the creed said in churches today is the longer one tied to the council of Constantinople. If unsure, do not mention it.
- "The council decided which books belong in the Bible." → Nothing in the record. Do not say it.
- "The council fixed Easter by the full moon and the spring equinox." → It set one day for Easter everywhere, the day kept at Rome and Alexandria, so the churches of the East would stop following the Jewish reckoning.
- "Easter was settled for the sake of unity." alone → The emperor's letter also speaks with open contempt of the Jews. Either leave Easter's reasons out or say both.
- "Arius was executed" / "burned" / "excommunicated and killed." → He was condemned and sent into exile, as a historian a century later reports; later he was recalled.
- "This ended the Arian controversy." → It began half a century of conflict (Jerome on Rimini, 359; Constantinople, 381).
- "Athanasius led the bishops" / "Athanasius won the debate." → He was there as a deacon, a later historian says, and opposed Arius's friends; he wrote his account years later.
- Eusebius of Caesarea and Eusebius of Nicomedia are two different men. Eusebius of Caesarea signed; Eusebius of Nicomedia was Arius's friend and was exiled.
- "Eustathius tore up Arius's creed." → Eustathius says "the formulary of Eusebius" was torn up "in the sight of all"; he does not say who tore it, and does not name which Eusebius. Theodoret, later, says it was a statement by Arius's friends.
- "The council met for two months, from 20 May to 25 August." → Say only "in 325"; the days are disputed.
- "Bishops with gouged eyes and burnt hands filled the hall." → Only Theodoret, a century later, says so. If used, name him.
- "Bishops wept / cheered / the emperor was moved." → No feelings beyond what Eusebius writes.
- "A Persian bishop and a Scythian" are Eusebius's words; do not name them.

**Leave out**
- Socrates's burned petitions (M4): late and side business.
- Canons 6, 8 and 20 (M14) and the later fight over Rome's appeals: card material; too much for one story.
- Constantine's banquet (M15): vivid, but it crowds the after-history; usable only in Eusebius's voice if room remains.
- The scars of the confessors (S3): a century later; include only with Theodoret named.
- Constantine's anti-Jewish invective in the Easter letter: not to be quoted for colour; if Easter's reasons are told, say plainly that the letter speaks with contempt of the Jews.
- The Latin signature lists (C4): of doubtful authenticity.
