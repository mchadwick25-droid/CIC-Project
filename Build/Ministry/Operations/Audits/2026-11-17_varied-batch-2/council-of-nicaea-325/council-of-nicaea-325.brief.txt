In Alexandria, a priest named Arius held that the Son had a beginning while God had none. When his bishop, Alexander, drove him out, Arius protested: "We are persecuted because we said, 'The Son has a beginning, but God has no beginning.'" At stake was whether the Son is eternal like the Father. That question about Jesus was taken up by the council of Nicaea, which met in 325, in today's Turkey, when bishops from across the Roman Empire came at the emperor Constantine's call.

Eusebius, a bishop who was there and admired the emperor, tells what brought them. In Egypt, bishops clashed with bishops in every city. When Constantine's letter to Alexandria failed, he summoned bishops from Syria to Spain. As Eusebius reports it, the emperor told them: "For I count civil strife in the church of God worse than any war or fierce battle."

Three men who were there tell the debate differently. Eusebius says the emperor approved his church's creed but asked for one word to be added: homoousios, "of one being". Athanasius, who was there as a deacon and wrote later in its defense, says Arius's friends were caught whispering and winking. They read the bishops' words as true of every creature, so in his telling a word was needed that could not be read that way. Eustathius says a written statement was torn up "in the sight of all".

The creed called the Son "God of God, Light of Light", "begotten, not made", of one being with the Father. It laid a curse on anyone who said "there was a time when he was not". Eusebius told his church that he signed with peace as his aim. All but a few signed, while Arius and two bishops were condemned, and a historian writing a century later says Constantine exiled Arius.

The council also settled other business. In Egypt, Melitius had ordained clergy of his own, and he kept his title but lost the power to ordain. Easter was the second trouble Eusebius names: the churches kept it on different days, so that on the same feast some Christians fasted while others feasted. Now Easter would fall on one day everywhere, as kept at Rome and Alexandria. Twenty rules set the order of church life, down to the last communion, never refused to the dying.

Its creed became the standard for later councils. In 381 a council met at Constantinople over new disputes, and it ruled that the faith of the "318 fathers", as it called the bishops of Nicaea, "shall not be set aside". The council of Chalcedon, in 451, called it "pre-eminent".

Yet it did not hold at once: Arius, it seems, was recalled from exile. Of a council at Rimini in 359, Jerome wrote that "the whole world groaned, and was astonished to find itself Arian." For decades the word divided those who held it from those who would not, and even the single Easter had to be enforced by a later council at Antioch.

The rules traveled as written copies. Caecilian of Carthage, who sat at Nicaea, carried home the creed and canons. About ninety years later, the African bishops read that copy aloud as law, and when a disputed rule was missing from it, they wrote east for exact copies.
