In Alexandria, when his bishop, Alexander, drove him out, a priest named Arius protested: "We are persecuted because we said, 'The Son has a beginning, but God has no beginning.'" At stake was whether the Son is eternal like the Father. In 325 that question came before bishops from across the Roman Empire, who met at Nicaea, in today's Turkey, at the emperor Constantine's call.

Eusebius of Caesarea, a bishop who was there and wrote in praise of the emperor, says bishops in Egypt clashed with bishops in every city. When Constantine's letter to Alexandria failed, he summoned bishops from Syria to Spain. As Eusebius reports it, he told them: "For I count civil strife in the church of God worse than any war or fierce battle."

Two men who were there tell the debate differently. Eusebius says the emperor approved his church's creed but asked for one word to be added: homoousios, "of one being". Athanasius, who was there as a deacon and later wrote in the creed's defense, says the bishops first chose words from Scripture, such as "from God". But Arius's friends read them as true of every creature too, so the bishops wrote again, more plainly.

The creed called the Son "God of God, Light of Light", "begotten, not made", of one being with the Father. It laid a curse on anyone who said "there was a time when he was not". All but a few signed, while Arius and two bishops were condemned. Historians writing a century later say the emperor ordered exile for anyone who resisted the verdict, and his edict sent Arius into exile.

Then, as their letter to Egypt reports, the bishops turned to other business. Melitius, who had ordained clergy of his own, kept his title but lost the power to ordain. Easter, the second trouble Eusebius names, had some Christians fasting while others feasted; now it would fall on one day everywhere, as at Rome and Alexandria. Twenty rules set the order of church life, down to the last communion, never refused to the dying.

It did not hold at once. Arius, it seems, was recalled from exile, and a later council at Antioch had to enforce the single Easter. For decades the word divided the church. Of a council at Rimini in 359, Jerome wrote that "the whole world groaned, and was astonished to find itself Arian."

In 381 a council at Constantinople, called over new disputes, ruled that the faith of the "318 fathers", as it named the bishops of Nicaea, "shall not be set aside". The longer creed said in churches today is tied to that later council; Nicaea's own was shorter and ended with its curses. At Chalcedon, in 451, bishops called Nicaea's creed "pre-eminent".

Caecilian of Carthage, who sat at Nicaea, carried home the creed and canons. About ninety years later, African bishops read that copy aloud as law, and when a disputed rule was missing, they wrote east for exact copies.
