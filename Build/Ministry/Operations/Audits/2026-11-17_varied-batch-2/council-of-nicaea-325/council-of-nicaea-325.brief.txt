On the same feast, some Christians were fasting while others feasted, because the churches kept Easter on different days. Eusebius, a bishop who was there and admired the emperor Constantine, names this as one of two troubles behind the council of Nicaea. It met in 325, in today's Turkey, when bishops from across the Roman Empire came at the emperor's call.

The other trouble was in Egypt, where bishops clashed with bishops in every city. When his letter to Alexandria failed, Constantine summoned bishops from Syria to Spain and lent them the public post and its horses. As Eusebius reports it, the emperor told them: "For I count civil strife in the church of God worse than any war or fierce battle."

The deepest strife had begun in Alexandria, where a priest named Arius held that the Son had a beginning while God had none. When his bishop, Alexander, drove him out, Arius protested: "We are persecuted because we said, 'The Son has a beginning, but God has no beginning.'" At stake was whether the Son is eternal like the Father.

Three men who were there tell the debate differently. Eusebius says the emperor approved his church's creed but asked for one word to be added: homoousios, "of one being". Athanasius, who was there as a deacon and wrote later in its defence, says Arius's friends twisted the bishops' words and were caught whispering and winking. Eustathius says a written statement was torn up "in the sight of all".

The creed called the Son "God of God, Light of Light", "begotten, not made", of one being with the Father, and it laid a curse on anyone who said "there was a time when he was not". Eusebius signed, he told his church, with peace as his aim. All but a few signed, and Arius and two bishops were condemned.

The council then turned to Egypt's other trouble, leaving Melitius, who had ordained clergy of his own, his title but no power to ordain. Easter would fall on one day everywhere, the day kept at Rome and in Alexandria. Twenty rules set the order of church life, from councils twice a year in every province to the last communion, never refused to the dying.

The creed became the standard. When a council met at Constantinople in 381 to settle new disputes, it ruled that the faith of the "318 fathers", as it named Nicaea, "shall not be set aside". In 451 the council of Chalcedon called it "pre-eminent".

Yet it did not hold at once. Arius was recalled, and of a council at Rimini in 359, Jerome wrote that "the whole world groaned, and was astonished to find itself Arian." For decades the word divided those who held it from those who would not, and even the single Easter had to be enforced by a later council at Antioch.

The rules travelled as written copies. Caecilian of Carthage, who sat at Nicaea, carried home the creed and the twenty canons. About ninety years later, the African bishops read that copy aloud as law, and when a disputed rule was missing from it, they wrote east for exact copies.
