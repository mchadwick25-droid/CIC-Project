"We are persecuted because we said, 'The Son has a beginning, but God has no beginning.'" Arius, a priest of Alexandria in Egypt, wrote this after, as he tells it, his bishop drove him and his friends from the city. The church there had split over one question: is the Son eternal, like the Father?

The emperor Constantine first wrote that the question should never have been asked, and when that failed, he called bishops "from all quarters". So in 325, in Nicaea, in today's Turkey, bishops from the churches of the empire, newly free of persecution, met at his call.

They came from Syria and Egypt, and as far as Persia and Spain. Eusebius, a bishop who was there and admired the emperor, counts more than 250; others there said about 270 or 300, and later tradition fixed the number at 318.

Constantine entered the hall in purple and gold. As Eusebius reports it, he told them, "For I count civil strife in the church of God worse than any war or fierce battle." Then the bishops began accusing one another.

Witnesses tell the debate differently. Eusebius says he read his own church's creed, and the emperor approved it but asked for one word to be added: homoousios, "of one being" with the Father. Athanasius, an enemy of Arius, was there as a deacon and wrote later in its defence. He says Arius's friends were caught "whispering ... and winking", so the bishops chose plainer words.

The creed called the Son "God of God, Light of Light", "begotten, not made". It cut off from the church anyone who said "there was a time when he was not". All but a few signed. Arius was condemned with two bishops, and a historian writing a century later says Constantine sent him into exile.

Yet the settlement did not hold at once. Another Eusebius, bishop of Nicomedia and Arius's friend, was brought back from exile, and Arius was recalled. For decades the word divided those who held it from those who would not. After a council at Rimini in 359, Jerome wrote that "the whole world groaned, and was astonished to find itself Arian."

Athanasius wrote a whole book defending "of one being", and the creed became the fixed reference. Constantinople in 381 ruled that the faith of "the 318 fathers", as later councils named Nicaea, "shall not be set aside", and Chalcedon in 451 called it "pre-eminent".

The council had also made rules for the church, and these travelled too. About ninety years after Nicaea, bishops in Carthage read aloud the copy their bishop Caecilian had brought home, and wrote east for exact copies.
