# Outline: Benedict of Nursia

Sheet references (S, M, R, I, D) point to `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder. File short names: GRG = Gregory's *Dialogues* (English of 1608), GRG-ed = Gardner's notes there, RBL / RBE = the Rule in Latin / in Gasquet's English, MGH = the Frankish councils, PAUL = Paul the Deacon.

- **Length:** 2½ minutes, about 375 words. He is a major figure, and what came of him (councils that made his Rule law, a book read for centuries) needs its full third. The life itself rests on one later witness, so keep the life beats lean and do not stretch them.

- **Setting, in words to say aloud:** "Italy, around the year 500, when Gothic kings ruled it." Gregory sets him "in the time of the Goths" (S3a, GRG 4417–4418); modern editors put his birth "about 480" (S3b, GRG-ed 12628). The tradition, said aloud: "the monks of Italy", "the monasteries of Subiaco and Monte Cassino" (S6a). His birthplace: Nursia, in central Italy. Gregory says only "the province of Nursia" (S2, GRG 3414); Gasquet calls it "a city of Umbria" (RBE-ed 170–171). Add no landscape.

- **Thread:** He left the church a short, moderate Rule for monks living together under an abbot, which later rulers made the law of their monasteries.

- **Shape:** Monk or ascetic. The turn away from Rome, the cave, the others drawn after him, and the pattern they copied: his Rule.

- **Openings:**
  1. A monk named Romanus lowers bread on a long rope down a rock face to a cave, with a little bell tied on, so the young man below knows his food has come (M4, GRG 3508–3512). Not a wonder; safe to open on.
  2. A priest finds him in the cave and says it is Easter; he had lived so long apart that he did not know (M5, GRG 3540–3546). If used, the priest was sent by a vision, in Gregory's words (GRG 3523–3533).
  3. Gregory's own advice: to know Benedict's life, read his Rule, "for the holy man could not otherwise teach, than himself lived" (M18, GRG 5571–5572). This raises the question of how we know him at all.

- **Story beats:**
  1. **He walks out of Rome.** Born in Nursia to a good family, he was sent to Rome for his schooling. He saw many students fall into loose living, so he gave up his books and his father's house and wealth to serve God alone (S2, M1; GRG 3414–3425). Documented as Gregory's account.
  2. **The cave at Subiaco.** He went to a wild place called Subiaco, almost forty miles from Rome. A monk, Romanus, gave him the monk's habit and kept his secret. For three years he lived in a narrow cave, known only to Romanus, who let his bread down on a rope (M4; GRG 3488–3512). Shepherds who first saw him in his clothes of skins took him for an animal; later many came to him, bringing food and taking away teaching (M6; GRG 3557–3567). Documented as Gregory's account.
  3. **The abbot they tried to poison.** DEFENDED: regular discipline. Monks of a nearby monastery whose abbot had died begged him to lead them. He warned them their ways and his would never agree, but at last gave in, and insisted they keep the regular life their rule required. Some of them put poison in his wine. Gregory says that when he made the sign of the cross over it, the glass shattered. He told them calmly to find a father suited to their ways, and went back to the wilderness (M8; GRG 3648–3706). The plot: Documented as Gregory's account. The breaking glass: WONDER, Gregory's voice.
  4. **Twelve houses.** Many now came to him. He built twelve monasteries at Subiaco, set a head over each, placed twelve monks in each, and kept a few with himself. Roman nobles gave him their sons to raise, among them Maurus and Placidus (M9; GRG 3845–3856). When a jealous local priest set out to corrupt his young monks, he left the houses in others' care and moved on with a few monks, so as not to put the young ones at risk (M11; GRG 4091–4098). Documented as Gregory's account.
  5. **Monte Cassino.** Above the town of Cassino stood an old temple where country people still worshipped Apollo, with sacred woods around it. He smashed the idol, overturned the altar and burned the woods, built chapels of St Martin and St John in their place, and by steady preaching, Gregory says, brought the people to the faith (M12; GRG 4167–4184). Modern editors date this about 528 or 529 (S3b, S3c). Documented as Gregory's account; date Dominant Modern Reconstruction.
  6. **The Rule.** Gregory says he wrote a rule for his monks, outstanding for its good judgment (M18). In it he calls the monastery "a school for the Lord's service" and hopes to set down nothing harsh (Q1; R1). A monk promises three things for life: stability (to stay in this one community), a turning of his whole way of life to God, and obedience (R3; RBL 8568–8569). Because "idleness is an enemy of the soul", the Rule sets fixed times for work with the hands and other fixed hours for holy reading (R4; RBE 3113–3116). Every guest is to be received as Christ (R5; RBL 7799–7800). The community chooses its abbot, who must temper all things (Q2; R6). He calls it "this little Rule written for beginners", and points those who want more to Basil's Rule and the Collations, Institutes and Lives of the Fathers (R8; RBE 4290–4300). Documented (OWN). Gregory gives no date or place for the writing (D3).
  7. **Totila, and his death.** Gregory says Totila, king of the Goths, first sent a guardsman dressed in his robes to test whether Benedict was a prophet, then came himself. Seeing Benedict from afar, the king fell to the ground. Benedict lifted him up with his own hands and told him to his face, "Much wickedness do you daily commit"; from then on, Gregory says, the king was less cruel (M13b; GRG 4464–4487). Editors date the meeting probably to 542 (M13c). Six days before he died, Gregory says, he had his grave opened; then a fever took him. On the sixth day he had his monks carry him into the chapel, took communion, and died standing, held up by his disciples, with his hands raised to heaven. He was buried where Apollo's altar had stood (M19; GRG 5582–5591, 5612–5614). No early source gives the year; Benedictine tradition says 543 (S3b, S3c). Documented as Gregory's account; year Contested.

- **What came of them** (about a third of the story):
  1. **His Rule became law for Frankish monasteries.** In 742 the first council of Carloman, ruler of the Franks, held with Boniface, ordered that monks and nuns in the monasteries of the lands he ruled "order and live" and govern their life "by the rule of Saint Benedict" (I1a; MGH 2199, 2324–2325). In 802 Charlemagne gathered the abbots and monks who were at Aachen, where they read the Rule together; he then ordered that whatever had been done in the monasteries against it be put right by it (I1c; MGH 7794–7805). Both LATER (about 200 and 260 years). Documented.
  2. **The Rule outlived the house that made it.** In Gregory's own lifetime the Lombards broke into Monte Cassino by night and plundered it, yet, he says, not one monk was taken (M20; GRG 4657–4661). Paul the Deacon, writing in the eighth century, adds that the monks fled to Rome "carrying with them the manuscript of the Holy Rule" (I2; PAUL 7832–7835). Documented as each writer's account.
  3. **Gregory's book carried his story.** Gregory wrote the Life about fifty years after Benedict's death, from four of his disciples (M2; GRG 3427–3441). Pope Zacharias put it into Greek, and an English bishop put it into Anglo-Saxon for Alfred the Great (I3; GRG-ed 1269–1276). Documented as Gardner's account (EDITOR). Paul the Deacon wrote a verse for each of his miracles (I3; PAUL 3266–3274). Documented (LATER).
  - End on item 1: the Aachen order of 802, in the record's own terms.

- **Quotes:**
  - **Q1.** Source: RBL 1240–1245, Latin: "Constituenda est ergo nobis Dominici ... schola servitu [servitii]; in qua institutione nihil asperum, nihil grave nos constituturos speramus." Rendered by me from the Latin: **"So we must found a school for the Lord's service. In founding it, we hope to lay down nothing harsh and nothing heavy."** Job: his own voice; the gentleness that set his Rule apart. Use in beat 6.
  - **Q2.** Source: RBL 9527–9528, Latin: "sic omnia temperet, ut et sit quod fortes cupiant, et infirmi non refugiant". Rendered by me from the Latin: **"Let him so temper everything that the strong have something to long for, and the weak do not run away."** The "him" is the abbot; the writer says so in the lead-in. Job: the Rule's balance between strong and weak, in one line. Use in beat 6.
  - **Q3 (optional; use only if Q1 or Q2 is cut, to keep to 2 or 3 at most).** Gregory on the Rule. Source, Latin of the first sentence (quoted by Gardner, GRG-ed 12809–12810): "Nam scripsit monachorum regu/am [regulam], discretione praecipuam, sermone luculentam". Rendered by me from the Latin: **"For he wrote a rule for monks, outstanding in good judgment and clear in its language."** Second sentence, 1608 English (GRG 5571–5572; Latin not held): "for the holy man could not otherwise teach, than himself lived." Rendered by me from the 1608 English: **"The holy man could not teach in any other way than he lived."** Job: the narrator of the wonders points us to the Rule as the truer picture of the man. Use as opening 3 or at the start of beat 6.

- **Links:**
  - Gregory the Great — wrote his life (from four of Benedict's disciples; never met him).
  - Constantinus, Valentinianus, Simplicius, Honoratus — his disciples; told Gregory his story.
  - Scholastica — his sister; met him once a year; buried in his grave.
  - Romanus — met; gave him the habit and fed him.
  - Maurus and Placidus — his young disciples.
  - Totila, king of the Goths — met him.
  - Basil of Caesarea — his Rule names Basil's Rule.
  - John Cassian — his Rule has the Collations read and commends the Institutes (the identification is the editors').
  - Carloman and Boniface — their council of 742 made his Rule law.
  - Charlemagne — had his Rule read and enforced at Aachen, 802.
  - Paul the Deacon — wrote verses on his miracles; told of the abbey's fall and the saved manuscript.
  - Pope Zacharias — translated Gregory's Life into Greek.

- **Who tells it:**
  - **Gregory the Great**, *Dialogues*, Book II, written in 593, about fifty years after Benedict died. By his editor's dating Gregory was born only a year or two before Benedict died, and he never met him; he names four disciples as his sources. He wrote to show God's power in a saint, so wonders fill the book. Line to say aloud: **"His life was written down by Pope Gregory the Great, about fifty years after his death, from the memories of four of his monks."**
  - **Benedict himself**, the Rule: his own words, in his own lifetime.
  - **The Frankish councils** of 742 and 802: about two and two and a half centuries later. The 742 text is Carloman's own decree; the 802 report is from the annals of Lorsch, a chronicle of the time, not the synod's own decree.
  - **Paul the Deacon**, a monk-historian of the eighth century, about two and a half centuries after Benedict.
  - **Modern editors** (Gardner 1911, Gasquet 1909) give the dates; say "as modern editors date it" if a year is used.

- **Do not say:**
  - Wrong: "He founded the Benedictine Order." Right: "He founded monasteries at Subiaco and Monte Cassino." The name and the order are later (S6b; "General of his order" at GRG 3439 is the 1608 translator's phrase).
  - Wrong: "He wrote the first monastic rule in the West" / "He invented Western monasticism." Right: his Rule points readers to older guides, Basil's Rule and the Collations, Institutes and Lives of the Fathers, and calls itself "a little Rule written for beginners" (R8).
  - Wrong: "born in 480" / "died in 543" said as fact. Right: "born around 480, as modern editors date it"; for his death, "by Benedictine tradition, in 543", or give no year (S3, D2).
  - Wrong: "He founded Monte Cassino in 529." Right: "around 529" (editors; Gregory gives no year).
  - Wrong: "He wrote the Rule at Monte Cassino" / "in 530" / "in 540". Right: "he wrote a rule for his monks". Gregory gives no place or year (D3).
  - Wrong: "the monks poisoned him" / "all the monks". Right: "some of them put poison in his wine" (GRG 3679–3682). He was not harmed.
  - Wrong: "the glass shattered" as plain fact. Right: "Gregory says the glass shattered when he made the sign of the cross over it." Keep every wonder in Gregory's voice.
  - Wrong: "at Vicovaro" as Gregory's word. Right: "a nearby monastery". The place name is an editor's identification (D5). Do not add "for three years" (Gasquet, not Gregory).
  - Wrong: "twelve monasteries of twelve monks" with nothing more. Right: keep "and he kept a few with himself" if the number of monks is given (GRG 3847–3850).
  - Wrong: "He left Subiaco because he was afraid" / "to escape the priest". Right: to protect his young monks; Gregory says he "gave place to envy" (GRG 4091–4095).
  - Wrong: "He converted all the pagans of the region" / "He tore down a temple." Right: "he smashed the idol, overturned the altar, burned the sacred woods, and built chapels in their place; by steady preaching, Gregory says, he brought the people of those parts to the faith" (GRG 4178–4184).
  - Wrong: "Totila was converted" / "repented" / "made peace with the Church". Right: "from then on, Gregory says, the king was less cruel" (GRG 4486–4487). The prophecy of nine years' reign is Gregory's wonder; leave it out or keep it in his voice.
  - Wrong: "Totila knelt for his blessing." Right: "the king fell to the ground"; he asked Benedict to pray for him (GRG 4466, 4484–4485).
  - Wrong: "Ora et labora" / "Pray and work", as the Rule's motto. Right: these words are not in the Rule. Use "idleness is an enemy of the soul" (R4).
  - Wrong: "discretion" in its modern sense (secrecy, tact). Right: "good judgment", "a sense of measure".
  - Wrong: "conversion of manners" / "good manners". Right: "a turning of his whole way of life to God" (R3).
  - Wrong: "The abbot was appointed." Right: "The community chose its abbot", or a smaller part of it with sounder judgment (R6).
  - Wrong: "Scholastica, his twin sister" / "Abbess Scholastica". Right: "his sister Scholastica". Gregory gives neither "twin" nor "abbess" (M16).
  - Wrong: "His nurse" turned into "his mother". Right: leave the nurse out (sieve story is left out).
  - Wrong: "the monk's day was work and reading" / "his day was divided between work and reading". Right: "set times for work, and other set hours for holy reading" (RBE 3113–3116).
  - Wrong: "forty miles" / "over forty miles" from Rome. Right: "almost forty miles" (GRG 3489–3490).
  - Wrong: "he knew the day he would die" as plain fact. Right: in Gregory's voice ("Gregory says he told his monks the day"), or left out (GRG 5577–5578; WONDER).
  - Wrong: "Benedict saw through the disguise at once" as plain fact. Right: leave the test's outcome out, or "Gregory says he knew the man was not the king" (M13a; WONDER).
  - Wrong: "In 742 the Franks made his Rule law" / "all Frankish monasteries". Right: "Carloman's council, for the lands he ruled" (MGH 2203–2204).
  - Wrong: "Charlemagne made every monastery in Europe follow the Rule." Right: "In 802 Charlemagne gathered the abbots and monks who were at Aachen, where they read the Rule together, and ordered that anything done in the monasteries against it be put right by it" (MGH 7794–7805). It was his realm, not Europe.
  - Wrong: "The Pope ordered the Rule for all monks." Right: it was Carloman's council (742) and Charlemagne's synod (802).
  - Wrong: "Benedictine monks converted Europe" / "Augustine of Canterbury was a Benedictine." Right: no early held source says so; this is Gasquet's 1909 view (D8). Leave out.
  - Wrong: "The Lombards destroyed Monte Cassino in 589" as Gregory's statement. Right: "In Gregory's own lifetime the Lombards sacked it by night"; the year is an editor's, and contested (D6). Not "destroyed and the monks killed": Gregory says not one monk was taken.
  - Wrong: "Gregory knew him" / "his disciple Gregory". Right: Gregory never met him; he wrote from four disciples (M2).
  - Wrong: "He fled the sin of Rome." Right: "he saw many of the students fall into loose living", and left his books and his father's house (GRG 3416–3423).
  - Wrong: "lived in a cave for years alone." Right: "three years", known only to Romanus (GRG 3501–3503).
  - Do not add for colour: his feelings at the cave, the look of the mountain, weather, his appearance, Benedict's age at any event, or any words of his not quoted here.

- **Leave out:**
  - The sieve, the nettles, the blackbird, the devil breaking the bell, the crow and the poisoned loaf, Maurus running on water, the iron rising, the boy raised from the dead, the oil, the dragon, the nuns at Mass, Scholastica's storm, the soul as a dove, the vision of the whole world (M3, M7, M10, M11, M14, M16, M17): a pile of wonders is the shape's trap. Two are kept: the shattering glass and the Totila meeting, both in Gregory's voice.
  - Paul the Deacon's ravens, angels and heavenly voice (D9): late legend.
  - The removal of his bones to France (I4, D7): contested even by Paul; a side story.
  - The council of Estinnes (I1b): the word "rule" there is an editor's supply.
  - The Rule of the Master (D4): no held source argues it.
  - Gasquet's claims about European civilization and missionaries (D8): a modern advocate's view.
  - Dante (I3): card only.
  - Exact dates beyond "around 500" and "around 529" unless voiced as the editors'.
