In a wild place called Subiaco, almost forty miles from Rome, a monk named Romanus used to lower bread on a long rope down a rock face, with a little bell tied on, so that the young man in the cave below would know his food had come. The young man was Benedict, and this was Italy, around the year 500, when Gothic kings ruled it.

His life was written down by Pope Gregory the Great, about fifty years after his death, from the memories of four of his monks. Gregory tells us that Benedict came from a good family in the province of Nursia and was sent to Rome to study. When he saw many of the students fall into loose living, he gave up his books, his father's house and his wealth to serve God alone.

For three years he lived in that narrow cave, known only to Romanus, until shepherds found him and many others began to come, bringing food and taking away teaching. Monks of a nearby monastery whose abbot had died begged him to lead them, although he warned that their ways and his would never agree. When he held them to the regular life their own rule required, some of them put poison in his wine. Gregory says that the glass shattered as Benedict made the sign of the cross over it, and that he calmly told them to find a father suited to their ways.

So many followed him that he built twelve monasteries at Subiaco, with a head and twelve monks in each, and kept a few with himself. Later, around 529 as modern editors date it, he moved to Monte Cassino, where country people still worshipped Apollo. He smashed the idol, overturned the altar, burned the sacred woods and built chapels in their place, and by steady preaching, Gregory says, he brought the people to the faith.

There he wrote a rule for his monks, and it is his own voice: "So we must found a school for the Lord's service. In founding it, we hope to lay down nothing harsh and nothing heavy." Because idleness is an enemy of the soul, it sets times for work and other hours for holy reading. Every guest is to be received as Christ, and the community chooses its abbot. "Let him so temper everything that the strong have something to long for, and the weak do not run away." He called it a little rule for beginners, and sent readers on to older guides. By Benedictine tradition he died in 543, and he was buried where Apollo's altar had stood.

That rule outlived its house. In Gregory's own lifetime the Lombards plundered Monte Cassino by night, yet not one monk was taken, and two centuries later Paul the Deacon wrote that the monks fled to Rome carrying the manuscript of the Holy Rule. Gregory's book carried his story into Greek and Anglo-Saxon. In 742 a council of Carloman, ruler of the Franks, ordered the monasteries of the lands he ruled to live by the rule of Saint Benedict. Then, in 802, Charlemagne gathered the abbots and monks who were at Aachen, where they read the Rule together, and he ordered that whatever had been done in the monasteries against it be put right by it.
