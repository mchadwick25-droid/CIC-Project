"I see many people in this world who rejoice in their children with them. But I, Dhuoda, see myself kept far away from you, my son William." So begins a book written in the 840s at Uzès, a town in the south of France that was then part of the Frankish empire Charlemagne had built.

Dhuoda was the wife of Bernard, a great lord of the south. Her kingdom and homeland were, in her words, "in discord", because after Charlemagne's son Louis died in 840, his sons fought over the empire. In 841 she bore a second son at Uzès. Before the child was baptized, Bernard had him taken from her and brought to him in Aquitaine, with the bishop of Uzès. When she wrote, she did not yet know the baby's name. She stayed on at Uzès "at my lord's command", while William was sent to King Charles, to commend himself as his sworn man.

That November she began a handbook for William. She dictated it and had it written out and sent to him, and she asked him to read it as often as one looks in a mirror. It asks him to pray in short, pure words, to love God first and then his father, and to serve Charles faithfully and help widows and orphans. Nearly every voice that survives from the church of Charlemagne's heirs belongs to one of the clergy. Hers belongs to a noblewoman, and she claims her place as his teacher. "My son, you will have teachers who will teach you more lessons, and greater ones, for your good. But they will not teach on equal terms, with a heart burning in the breast, as I do, your mother."

She worked on it for more than a year. In 844 the Franks judged Bernard guilty of treason, and on Charles's order he was put to death, while in 850 William was killed at Barcelona. The chronicles of her day name her husband and her son, but not her, and no record tells when or where she died.

The book was copied and kept. An early copy survives in fragments, and a later one agrees with it, so her editors could show the text is hers. In 1923, two translators of the Catholic catechism set out to answer the claim that the catechism began with Luther. Among their examples of Christian instruction from the ninth century on, they named "the manual of Dhuoda".
