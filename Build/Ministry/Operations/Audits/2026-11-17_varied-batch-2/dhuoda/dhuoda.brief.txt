"I see many people in this world who rejoice in their children with them. But I, Dhuoda, see myself kept far away from you, my son William." So begins a book written in the 840s, at Uzès, a town in the south of France, in the Frankish empire that Charlemagne had built.

Dhuoda was the wife of Bernard, a great lord of the south. Her kingdom and homeland were, in her words, "in discord", because after Charlemagne's son Louis died in 840, his sons fought over the empire. In 841 she bore a second son, but before the child was baptized, Bernard had the baby brought to him with the bishop of Uzès. She stayed on at Uzès "at my lord's command", while William was sent to King Charles, to commend himself as his sworn man.

That November she began a handbook for William, which she dictated and had written out and sent, to be read as often as one looks in a mirror. It asks him to pray in short, pure words, to love God first and then his father, and to serve Charles faithfully and help widows and orphans. Almost every voice that reaches us from the church of Charlemagne's heirs belongs to the clergy. Hers, a noblewoman's, does not, and she claims her place as his teacher. "My son, you will have teachers who will teach you more lessons, and greater ones, for your good. But they will not teach on equal terms, with a heart burning in the breast, as I do, your mother."

She worked on it for more than a year. In 844 the Franks judged Bernard guilty of treason, and on Charles's order he was put to death, while in 850 William was killed at Barcelona. The chronicles of her day name her husband and her son, but not her, and no record tells when or where she died.

Yet the book was copied and kept, and because an early copy, now in fragments, agrees with a later one, scholars could show the text is hers. Benedictine scholars of the 1700s called it "a respectable monument of literature and piety". When the whole book was printed in 1887, its editor called it the oldest writing by a woman born in France to reach us. In 1923, two translators of the Catholic catechism named "the manual of Dhuoda" among the works of Christian instruction from the ninth century on. With it they answered the claim that the catechism began with Luther, because it showed the faith taught to ordinary people before him.
