# Outline: Dhuoda

Sheet references (S, E, T, I, D) point to `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder. File short names: DH = Bondurand's 1887 edition of her book; NIT = Nithard; AB = *Annals of Saint-Bertin*; TRE = 1923 Roman Catechism translators.

- **Length:** about 2½ minutes (about 375 words). The census marks her a major figure, and her own book is a full, self-dated record. Her life outside the book is thin, though: two lines in the chronicles, and nothing on her death. The extra half-minute goes to the book's voice and to what came of it, not to her life.
- **Setting, in words to say aloud:** "In the 840s, at Uzès, a town in the south of France, in the Frankish empire that Charlemagne had built" (S2, S3, S6, S7). Her times in her own words: a kingdom and homeland "in discord" (S4, DH 2078).
- **Thread:** Kept far from both her sons, Dhuoda dictated a handbook of faith for the elder. It is one of very few voices from the church of Charlemagne's heirs that does not belong to the clergy (S7, CEN 42325).
- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker. Her question: how to shape a son's faith when she cannot be with him. What she taught is the book. Her later readers are its copyists and editors.
- **Openings:** (1) Her first words: many people have the joy of their children, but she is kept far from hers (T1, Q1). (2) A baby, born at Uzès, carried to his father before he was baptized; she did not yet know his name (E4, E6). (3) Her picture of herself as a puppy under the master's table (T3, Q2).
- **Story beats:**
  1. In 824 Dhuoda married Bernard in the emperor's palace at Aachen, as his lawful wife; their son William was born in 826 (E1, E2). Documented. Bernard was duke of Septimania, a great lord in the south (S3).
  2. Louis, Charlemagne's son, died in 840, and his sons fought over the empire (S4, S5). In March 841 Dhuoda bore a second son at Uzès. Before the baby was baptized, Bernard had him brought to him in Aquitaine, with Elefantus, bishop of Uzès (E4). She stayed on at Uzès "at my lord's command" (E5). Documented.
  3. After the battle of 841, Bernard sent William to King Charles. William was to commend himself to Charles, becoming his sworn man, if Charles would grant the lands and offices held in Burgundy. Nithard, writing in those same years, records it (E7). Dhuoda heard of it at Uzès (E7, DH 2303–2304). Documented.
  4. On Saint Andrew's day, 30 November 841, she began a book for William. She dictated it and had it written out and sent (E8, E9). She called it a handbook, to be read as often as one looks in a mirror or plays a board game (T2). Documented.
  5. What it asks of him: seek God, pray in short, pure words, love God first and then his father, serve Charles faithfully, help widows, orphans and pilgrims, and judge fairly (T3, T5, T6, T7). He must read it to his little brother when the boy is old enough (T8). He must pray for his father's dead kin, named in a list (E15). Its opening verses spell, in their first letters, a greeting from Dhuoda to William (T9). Documented.
  6. She writes of illness, of debts she took on to support Bernard, borrowed from Christians and from Jews, and asks William to repay them (E13, E14). She writes her own epitaph and asks him to have it carved on her grave, so that passers-by will pray for her (E17). Documented as her words.
  7. She finished her verses when William turned sixteen, on Saint Andrew's day 842, and the book on 2 February, more than a year after starting (E10, E11). In 844 the Franks judged Bernard guilty of treason, and on Charles's order he was put to death. In 850 William was killed at Barcelona (E19, E20, AB). No record tells when or where she died (E18). Documented, except her death: not recorded.
- **What came of them** (about a third):
  1. **The book was copied and kept.** An early copy, from the age of Charlemagne's heirs, survives in fragments found at Nîmes in 1885. Another, made in the 1600s, came from an old manuscript of La Grasse abbey. The two agree, and her editors used them to show the text is hers (I1). *Defended:* its authenticity.
  2. **Scholars printed and praised it.** Mabillon printed parts in 1677. Eighteenth-century Benedictine scholars called it "a respectable monument of literature and piety" and regretted it was not printed whole (I2). *Defended:* its worth.
  3. **An old error was put right.** One copy has her call the emperor Louis "my brother", so some made her Charlemagne's daughter. Earlier scholars had already doubted it. The older copy reads "the emperor", and the idea was given up (I3). *Defended:* the true reading.
  4. **The whole book was printed at last in 1887.** Its editor, Édouard Bondurand, called it the oldest writing by a woman born in France to reach us (I4). Say it as his claim.
  5. **It was used in an argument.** In 1923 two translators of the Catholic catechism named "the manual of Dhuoda" among the works of Christian instruction from the ninth century on. They used it to answer the claim that the catechism began with Luther (I5). *Defended:* instruction for ordinary people before Luther.
- **Quotes, 2 or 3 at most:**
  - **Q1. Why she writes** (opening). DH 1893–1899, Latin, P only: "Cernens plurimos cum suis in sgeculo gaudere proies, et me Dodanam, ofili Willelme, a te elongatam conspiciens procuLobid quasi anxia, et utilitatis desiderio plena, hoc opusculum ex nomine meo scriptum in tuam specie tenus formam legendum dirigo, gaudens quod, si absens sum cor- pore, iste prsesens libellus tibi ad mentem reducat quid ergame, cum legeris, fili, debeas agere." Rendering (mine, from the Latin): "I see many people in this world who rejoice in their children with them. But I, Dhuoda, see myself kept far away from you, my son William. For this reason I am anxious, and full of the wish to do you good. So I send you this little work to read, written in my name and shaped for you. I am glad of this: though I am absent in body, this little book is here with you. When you read it, my son, it will bring to your mind what you ought to do for me." ("Shaped for you" renders the obscure "in tuam speciem tenus formam"; Bondurand leaves it untranslated, DH 1886–1890.) Job: opens the story in her voice and gives the reason for the book.
  - **Q2. The puppy under the table.** DH 2620–2626, Latin, P only: "Certè et ego, quanquam indigna fragilisque, adumbram, ut valeo, eum quairo, etejus adjutorium, ut scio et intelligo, indesinen- ter precor. Est enim mihi valde per omnia necesse, nam solet fieri ut aliquoties importuna catula, sub mensa domini sui, inter calulos alteros, micas cadentes valeat carpere et mandere" [adumbram = ad umbram; quairo = quaero; calulos = catulos]. Rendering (mine, from the Latin): "I too, unworthy and frail as I am, seek him in the shadows, as best I can. As far as I know and understand, I ask for his help without ceasing. I need it greatly, in everything. For this is a thing that happens now and then. A persistent puppy, among the other puppies under its master's table, manages to snatch the falling crumbs and eat them." Job: shows how she saw herself as a learner of faith, in her own image. She goes on to call the clergy "the puppies, that is, the ministers of the holy altar", from whose crumbs she gathers for herself and William (T3); the writer may paraphrase that.
  - **Q3. No teacher like a mother.** DH 3168–3171, Latin, P only: "Fili, habebis doctores qui te plura et ampliora utilitatis doceant documenta, sed non jfiquali conditione, animo ardentis in pectore, sicut ego genitrix tua." [jfiquali = aequali]. Rendering (mine, from the Latin): "My son, you will have teachers who will teach you more lessons, and greater ones, for your good. But they will not teach on equal terms, with a heart burning in the breast, as I do, your mother." Job: her claim to teach as a layperson and a mother; the turn toward what the book meant.
- **Links:** Bernard of Septimania (husband; married at Aachen). William (son; the book is written to him). The younger son (born at Uzès; she did not yet know his name; editors call him Bernard). King Charles (William's lord; she urges loyalty to him). Louis the Pious (the emperor in her dates). Elefantus, bishop of Uzès (the baby was brought to Bernard with him). Theodoric (William's godfather; on her prayer list). Nithard (wrote of Bernard sending William). Prudentius of Troyes (his annals record Bernard's and William's deaths). Prudentius the poet (she quoted his book). Mabillon (printed parts of her book). Léopold Delisle (read her acrostic; corrected the "daughter of Charlemagne" error). Édouard Bondurand (edited her whole book).
- **Who tells it:** Dhuoda herself, in 841–843, for almost everything (OWN). Nithard, a grandson of Charlemagne writing in those same years, wrote of Bernard sending William to Charles (CONTEMP). The *Annals of Saint-Bertin*, kept year by year at the time, record the deaths of Bernard and William (CONTEMP). The editors and scholars wrote 800 to 1,080 years later (LATER). Line to say aloud: "Almost all we know of her comes from her own book. The chronicles of her day name her husband and her son, but not her."
- **Do not say:**
  - "a cat" or "a cat among the cats" → **"a puppy", "a little dog"**. The Latin is *catula*. Bondurand's French summary wrongly says *chatte* (D3).
  - "she wrote it with her own hand" → **"she dictated it and had it written out"** (E9).
  - "Bernard snatched / stole / tore the baby from her" → **"Bernard had the baby brought to him, with the bishop of Uzès, before he was baptized."** She makes no complaint (E4).
  - "she never knew his name" → **"she did not yet know his name when she wrote, and she never names him in the book"** (E6). Do not call him Bernard in her voice. "Later writers call him Bernard" is allowed.
  - "banished / exiled to Uzès" as fact → **"she stayed at Uzès at her lord's command"**. "She calls herself an exile in her verses" is allowed (D7, T9).
  - "William, aged fifteen, was sent..." → **give no age for the sending.** Say "she gives his age as sixteen when she finished her verses" (D2, E10).
  - "if Charles would give William his father's lands" → **"the lands and offices held in Burgundy"**. The Latin does not settle whose they were (E7).
  - "Bernard was beheaded" → **"In 844 the Franks judged Bernard guilty of treason, and on Charles's order he was put to death"** (E19, D4).
  - "William died avenging his father" → **"William seized Ampurias and Barcelona by trickery. In 850 he was himself captured and killed at Barcelona."** No held annal gives a motive (E20).
  - "she died in 843", "she died soon after", "she died before her husband" → **"No record tells when or where she died"** (E18).
  - "her epitaph is carved on her tomb" or "her grave at Uzès" → **"she asked for it to be carved"**. No record says it was, or where she lies (E17).
  - "she borrowed from Jews" alone → **"from Christians and from Jews"**. Give both, and give her reason: to support Bernard in the March and to keep him from parting from her and William (E14).
  - "she finished it in 843" → **"she worked on it for more than a year"**. The year of completion is missing from the manuscript (E11, D1).
  - "chess" → **"a board game"** (T2).
  - "a hostage" → **"sent to commend himself to Charles, as his sworn man"** (E7). No held source calls William a hostage.
  - "she never saw them again" / "she was dying as she wrote" → **"she writes of illness and says her end is near"** (E13). Nothing records whether she saw her sons again (E18).
  - "Charlemagne's daughter" or "the emperor's sister" → **no. Her family is unknown** (I3, D5).
  - "the first woman writer in France" → **"her editor called it the oldest writing by a woman born in France to reach us"** (I4).
  - "she taught William to honour his father even in this", or any motive for her loyalty to Bernard or to Charles → **say only what she asks.** The book gives no motive and no complaint.
  - "an ordinary believer" or "a common voice" → **"a noblewoman", "not one of the clergy"** (S7).
  - "read for centuries / used to teach children" → **"copied, kept, and printed in part in the 1600s"**. No held record shows it in use between her time and then (section 7 of the sheet).
  - "seventy-three chapters" may be said only as the editors' count (DH 290–292).
- **Leave out:** Henri Martin's story of Bernard and the empress Judith (D6), a 19th-century gossip-history with nothing in her book. The legend of Bernard stabbed by the king at Toulouse (D4). The drowning of Bernard's sister and the blinding of his brother: real events, but side stories. The younger son's later revolt and death (864, 872): the annals do not name his mother. Bondurand's guesses about her family (D5). Her number-lore (God's name in Greek and Latin numerals, finger-counting): rich, but it needs explaining and would crowd the story. The 842 vs 843 completion debate (card). Her slip on Louis's reign years (E3).
