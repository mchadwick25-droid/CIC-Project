# Outline: Martin of Tours

All references are to `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder (S = setting, M = life events, I = impact, L = links, D = doubts). Each one carries the source file and its lines.

- **Length:** about 2½ to 3 minutes (380–450 words). He is a major figure, and the story needs the room. The life has two turns, soldier to monk and monk to bishop. Trier asks for a stand to be explained. The afterlife is large and needs its full third. Do not pad: if it holds in 380 words, stop there.

- **Setting, in words to say aloud:** "in the fourth century" (S3), "in Roman Gaul, in what is now France" (S3, S4), "among the monks and monk-bishops of Gaul" (S5). Born in Pannonia, "in what is now Hungary" (S2). Bishop at Tours, on the river Loire (S4).

- **Thread:** A soldier who would not fight became a bishop who never stopped living as a monk, and the book his friend wrote about him made that pattern a model for later lives of saints (I2d, I3).

- **Shape:** Monk or ascetic. He turned from the army, chose the monk's life and kept it as a bishop, and others followed him (M6, M11, I3). Trap to avoid: a pile of wonders. Keep to the cloak dream (M4) and at most one other, in Sulpicius's voice.

- **Openings:**
  1. The gate of Amiens in a killing winter. A soldier with only his cloak cuts it in two with his sword and gives half to a naked beggar. Some onlookers laugh at him; others groan because they had done nothing (M3).
  2. His words to Caesar at the payday before a battle: "I am Christ's soldier. I am not allowed to fight." (M6, Q1).

- **Story beats:**
  1. **Born to pagans, enlisted in chains.** Born at Sabaria in Pannonia to heathen parents, raised at Ticinum (Pavia) in Italy. At ten, against his parents' wish, he went to the church and asked to become a catechumen, someone under instruction for baptism. At fifteen his father, a soldier who rose to tribune, informed on him under a law that sons of veterans must serve. Martin was seized, chained and made to take the soldier's oath (S2, S6, M1). Documented, as Sulpicius tells it.
  2. **The cloak, and baptism.** Even as a soldier he lived "not so much as being a soldier as a monk". He kept one servant and cleaned that servant's boots himself (M2). At Amiens he shared his cloak (M3). Sulpicius says that the next night Martin saw Christ in a dream, wearing the half he had given, and heard him tell the angels that Martin, "still but a catechumen", had clothed him (M4: a wonder, in Sulpicius's voice). He was baptised at about twenty. He stayed nearly two more years in the army, because his tribune promised to leave the world with him (M5). Documented, as Sulpicius tells it.
  3. **"I am the soldier of Christ."** At Worms the Caesar Julian was handing out a donative, a gift of money to each soldier, before a battle. Martin thought it wrong to take the money if he meant to leave, so he asked for his discharge (Q1). Julian called it cowardice. Martin offered to stand unarmed in front of the battle line the next day, guarded only by the sign of the cross. He was put back in prison. The next day the enemy sent envoys and surrendered, so there was no battle. Sulpicius credits Martin for the peace (M6). Documented as Sulpicius's account. The peace being Martin's doing is Sulpicius's reading of it.
  4. **Hilary, a monastery, and a bishopric he did not seek.** He went to Hilary, bishop of Poitiers. He refused to be made a deacon, saying he was unworthy, but accepted the humble office of exorcist so as not to seem to look down on it (M7). After travels in which Arian opponents had him publicly beaten and driven out of a city, and drove him from a monastery he had set up at Milan, he founded a monastery near Poitiers (M8, M9). The people of Tours wanted him as bishop. A citizen lured him out by pretending his wife was ill. Crowds lining the road took him to the city "under a kind of guard". Some bishops objected that he was mean in looks, clothes and hair (M10). Documented, as Sulpicius tells it.
  5. **A bishop who stayed a monk.** He kept the same humility and the same plain clothes. Visitors crowded his cell at the church, so he built a wooden cell two miles outside the city, between a cliff and the Loire. Eighty disciples joined him, many living in caves cut into the rock. They owned everything in common. The young copied books and the old prayed (M11). Through the villages he tore down pagan temples, and he built churches or monasteries where they had stood. Once he set about cutting down a pine held sacred, and the local people rose against him. Once a crowd drove him off and he was hurt. Often, Sulpicius says, his preaching moved people to pull down their own temples (M13). Documented as Sulpicius's account. The claim that almost no one there was Christian before him is Sulpicius's praise.
  6. **Trier: a stand, and a compromise.** At the court of the emperor Maximus, bishops were prosecuting Priscillian and his followers for heresy. Martin did not defend their teaching. He kept rebuking the accuser Ithacius to drop the charge and begged Maximus not to shed their blood. To be cast out of the churches by the bishops, he said, was punishment enough (Q2). While he was at Trier the trial waited. After he left, Priscillian and his companions were put to death (M15). On a later visit, the emperor was about to send officers into Spain to hunt heretics. Martin won their lives by agreeing to share communion for one day with the bishops who had pressed for the deaths. He would not sign his name to it. He grieved over it, and he never went to a gathering of bishops again (M16). Documented, as Sulpicius and a disciple tell it.
  7. **Death on a peace mission.** He was old, and he knew his end was near. Still he travelled to Candes, a village of his diocese, because its clergy were quarrelling, and he made peace among them (M19). When his brothers begged him not to leave them, he answered with Q3. He lay on ashes, saying a Christian should die among ashes (M19). Documented as reported. Sulpicius was not there and passes on what those present said.

- **What came of them** (about a third; heaviest first):
  1. **His tomb made Tours the chief place of pilgrimage in Gaul** (a historian's judgment, Brehaut; tell it through Gregory's acts below). A later bishop built a great church over his grave because miracles were reported there. Gregory told the faithful to keep a July feast for his church, the moving of his body there and his ordination as bishop, and to remember his burial day, 11 November. Gregory, bishop of Tours almost two centuries later, calls him "our blessed patron". The Frankish king Clovis ordered his army to take nothing but grass and water from Martin's lands. He brought gifts to Martin's church after his victory, and he put on a consul's purple there (I1).
  2. **The book carried him across the Roman world.** Sulpicius's Life was being read eagerly while Martin still lived (I2b). Sulpicius claims, through a friend in his own dialogue, that it sold fast in Rome and was read in Carthage, Alexandria and the Egyptian desert (I2a). Say this is his claim. Nearly two centuries later the poet Fortunatus turned it into verse, writing that "a soldier's white cloak is more than a king's purple" (I2c). A modern historian calls it a model for later saints' lives (I2d).
  3. **Others followed him into the monk's life.** Many of his monks became bishops, and cities wanted their priests from his monastery. At his funeral, it was said, almost two thousand monks gathered (I3, M20).
  4. **DEFENDED, in Sulpicius's telling: no emperor should judge a church case, and no blood for heresy** (Q2, M15, M16). Gregory of Tours later used the Trier story, but for a different point: the punishment of Maximus. Gregory says nothing of heresy or a secular judge. At a council in Paris, when other bishops sat silent before an angry king, Gregory reminded them how Maximus had forced Martin to yield. He told them the eternal King's judgment pursued Maximus, who lost his throne and his life (I4). A strong ending: Gregory, generations later, warning bishops afraid of a king with what happened to Maximus, the emperor who forced Martin's hand.

- **Quotes** (2 or 3; all rendered from the Latin of Halm's edition by the researcher, not from Roberts's English):
  - **Q1** (soldier to Caesar). Latin, HALM 6334–6337 (OCR cleaned): "hactenus, inquit ad Caesarem, militaui tibi: patere ut nunc militem Deo: donatiuum tuum pugnaturus accipiat, Christi ego miles sum: pugnare mihi non licet." Roberts's English, NP11 839–842: "Hitherto I have served you as a soldier: allow me now to become a soldier to God: let the man who is to serve thee receive thy donative: I am the soldier of Christ: it is not lawful for me to fight." **Rendering:** "Until now I have served you as a soldier. Let me now serve God as a soldier. Let the man who is going to fight take your gift. I am Christ's soldier. I am not allowed to fight." Job: the turn of the life, in his own reported words.
  - **Q2** (Trier; reported speech in Sulpicius's Chronicle). Latin, HALM 5903–5907 (OCR cleaned): "satis superque sufficere, ut episcopali sententia haeretici iudicati ecclesiis pellerentur: saeuum esse et inauditum nefas, ut causam ecclesiae iudex saeculi iudicaret." Roberts, NP11 11655–11660: "He maintained that it was quite sufficient punishment that, having been declared heretics by a sentence of the bishops, they should have been expelled from the churches; and that it was, besides, a foul and unheard-of indignity, that a secular ruler should be judge in an ecclesiastical cause." **Rendering:** "It was more than enough that men judged heretics by the bishops should be driven from the churches. It was a cruel wrong, never heard of before, for a judge of this world to try a case of the church." Introduce it as what Martin said, in Sulpicius's report ("he said that..."). The writer may use the second sentence alone. Job: what he defended.
  - **Q3** (deathbed; Ep. III, reported). Latin, HALM 8232–8234 (OCR cleaned): "Domine, si adhuc populo tuo sum necessarius, non recuso laborem; fiat uoluntas tua." Roberts, NP11 2429–2431: "O Lord, if I am still necessary to thy people, I do not shrink from toil: thy will be done." **Rendering:** "Lord, if your people still need me, I do not refuse the work. Your will be done." Job: the bishop who stays at the task. Sulpicius says Martin "is said to" have wept and spoken these words. Keep that it was reported.

- **Links:**
  - Hilary of Poitiers: lived with him; made him an exorcist (L1).
  - Sulpicius Severus: visited him, questioned him, wrote his life (L2).
  - Paulinus (of Nola): Martin held him up as a model; he took the Life to Rome (L3).
  - Emperor Maximus: Martin pleaded at his court (L5).
  - Priscillian: Martin pleaded for his life (L6).
  - Gregory of Tours: bishop of his see; wrote of his death and tomb; kept his feast; used his Trier story (L8, I4).
  - Venantius Fortunatus: wrote his life in verse (L9).
  - Clovis: honoured his church (L10).

- **Who tells it:**
  - Sulpicius Severus, a friend and admirer from Aquitaine, wrote the Life, letters, dialogues and a chronicle. He met Martin and questioned him late in Martin's life, and published the Life while Martin was still alive (sheet, Witness kinds). He was not present for most events, nor for the death. He is a devoted admirer and fills the Life with wonders. Line to say aloud: **"Almost all we know of him comes from one admirer, Sulpicius Severus, who met him late in life and wrote his story while he still lived."**
  - The Trier second visit comes from Sulpicius's Dialogues, spoken by one of Martin's former disciples (M16).
  - Gregory of Tours wrote about 180 to 195 years later. Line to say aloud: **"Gregory, bishop of Tours almost two hundred years later..."**
  - Venantius Fortunatus wrote his verse Life in the same generation as Gregory, retelling Sulpicius.

- **Do not say:**
  - Wrong: "Christ appeared to him." / "That night Christ came to him in his cloak." Right: "Sulpicius says that the next night Martin saw Christ in a dream, wearing the half-cloak" (M4: Martin was asleep, "a vision").
  - Wrong: "The vision converted him" / "the pagan soldier". Right: he was already a catechumen, since the age of ten. After the vision he was baptised (S6, M5).
  - Wrong: "He left the army as soon as he was baptised." Right: "he stayed nearly two more years, for his tribune's sake" (M5).
  - Wrong: "He refused to fight and walked unarmed into battle" / "he deserted". Right: he asked for his discharge, offered to stand unarmed, was jailed, and the enemy surrendered before any battle (M6). Do not say he was released from prison or discharged at once; Sulpicius goes straight to "From that time quitting military service" (M7).
  - Wrong: "He won the battle" / "God stopped the battle for him" as fact. Right: "Sulpicius saw the peace as Martin's victory" (M6).
  - Wrong: "Julian the Apostate" in the story. Right: "the Caesar Julian" (M6). The nickname is Roberts's note, and it pulls focus.
  - Wrong: "Hilary ordained him a priest" / "made him a deacon". Right: "Hilary made him an exorcist, after Martin refused to be a deacon" (M7).
  - Wrong: "founded the first monastery in Gaul" / "father of Western monasticism". Right: "founded a monastery near Poitiers" (M9). Do not name it Ligugé, or the Tours house Marmoutier, as if Sulpicius named them. Those names are a modern editor's (S5, M9, M11).
  - Wrong: "the people kidnapped him" / "seized him". Right: "a citizen tricked him out with a story of a sick wife, and the crowds brought him in under a kind of guard" (M10).
  - Wrong: "exactly eighty monks lived there". Right: "eighty disciples", as Sulpicius counts them at that time (M11). And "almost two thousand monks, it was said," at the funeral, with both "almost" and "it was said" kept (M20).
  - Wrong: "He converted Gaul" / "he gently persuaded the pagans". Right: he tore down temples and built churches in their place. Sometimes his words moved people to pull down their own temples. Once he was driven off and hurt (M13). The near-total conversion is Sulpicius's praise, for "those regions" only.
  - Wrong: "He defended Priscillian" / "he opposed the condemnation". Right: "he accepted that the bishops had judged them heretics. He said casting them out was punishment enough, and begged that their blood not be shed" (M15).
  - Wrong: "He saved Priscillian" / "as long as Martin lived, the trial was put off". Right: "while Martin was at Trier the trial waited; after he left, Priscillian was put to death" (M15, D3). Roberts's English is wrong here.
  - Wrong: "the first execution for heresy". Right: not in the record. Say only that Martin called it a cruel, unheard-of wrong for a worldly judge to try a church's case (Q2).
  - Wrong: "He signed with the other bishops" / "he joined them". Right: "he shared their communion for one day to save lives, and refused to sign his name" (M16).
  - Wrong: "He never met another bishop again." Right: "he never again attended a synod, a gathering of bishops" (M16). Leave out "sixteen years" (D4).
  - Wrong: "he cut down a sacred tree" / "he felled the holy pine". Right: "once he set about cutting down a pine held sacred, and the local people rose against him" (M13). In Sulpicius the pagans fell it themselves, inside the falling-pine wonder, which is left out.
  - Wrong: "two thousand monks walked behind his body" / "followed his coffin". Right: "at his funeral, it was said, almost two thousand monks gathered" (M20). Sulpicius's own image has the shepherd driving his flock before him.
  - Wrong: "Gregory praised Martin's stand against the emperor" / "Gregory held up Martin's defiance". Right: Gregory says Maximus "forced" Martin, who "yielded" to free the condemned, and the point is Maximus's punishment (I4). Tie the principle (no secular judge, no blood) to Sulpicius only (Q2, M15).
  - Wrong: "his feast day is in July". Right: the July feast marks his church, the moving of his body and his ordination as bishop. His burial day is 11 November (I1).
  - Wrong: "Martin won Clovis his victory" / "Clovis was crowned at Tours". Right: "Clovis honoured Martin's lands and church, and after his victory he put on a consul's purple there" (I1). Gregory frames the victory as won with Martin's favour; keep that as Gregory's view.
  - Wrong: "Tours was the heart of the church in Gaul" said as fact. Right: show it through Gregory's acts (the great church, the feast, Clovis), or credit it as a historian's judgment (I1f).
  - Wrong: "the West's picture of a holy man". Right: "a model for later lives of saints", as one modern historian, Brehaut, puts it (I2d).
  - Wrong: "He died on 11 November." Right: 11 November is his burial day, as Gregory gives it. Brehaut gives the death as 8 November 397 (D7). Simplest: "the day of his burial, 11 November, is remembered".
  - Wrong: "He died at Cosne." Right: "at Candes, a village of his diocese" (D6).
  - Wrong: any birth year, or "aged 81", or his age when he left the army. Right: leave them out, because the sources' ages do not fit together (D1).
  - Wrong: "patron saint of France", "Martinmas", the cloak giving us the word "chapel". Right: none of this is in the held record. Gregory calls him "our blessed patron" of Tours (I1).
  - Wrong: "Sulpicius never met him" (the census tile can be read so). Right: "met him late in life" (D10).
  - Wrong: "Sulpicius lived with him for years" / "his lifelong disciple". Right: "a friend who visited him" (M17).
  - Wrong: "the book was read all over the world" as fact. Right: "Sulpicius claims it was read in Rome, Carthage and Egypt" (I2a).
  - Wrong: "Martin wrote..." Right: no writing of his survives in these files. Every word of his is reported by Sulpicius.
  - Do not add weather, feelings or thoughts beyond the record: "a killing winter" is fine, since the cold "was proving fatal to many" (M3). Do not invent the beggar's words or Martin's thoughts at the gate.

- **Leave out:**
  - The robbers in the Alps, the devil on the road, the hellebore on Gallinaria (M8). They are side stories, and the poison cure is a wonder.
  - The raisings of the dead (M9; D11) and the other healings and exorcisms. They are a pile of wonders. The story should hold to the cloak dream, with at most one more.
  - The falling pine and the angels with spears (M13). These are wonders. The temple acts stand without them.
  - The angel on the road from Trier (M16). It is a wonder; the grief and the vow can be told without it.
  - The body passed out of a window and carried off by boat (D9). This is Gregory's story, 180+ years later.
  - The banquet cup (M14). It is a good scene but a side story; use it only if Trier needs a first image of Martin at court.
  - The mercy-to-the-devil passage (I5). It is wrapped in a demon's voice and was disputed by later readers.
  - Modern verdicts on the miracles (D8). These go on the card.
  - Church counts and village names in modern France (Brehaut's note). Modern, and card only.
