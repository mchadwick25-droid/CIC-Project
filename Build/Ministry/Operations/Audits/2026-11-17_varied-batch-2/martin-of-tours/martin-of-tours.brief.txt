At the gate of Amiens, in a killing winter, a soldier met a naked beggar. He cut his cloak in two with his sword and gave the man half, while some onlookers laughed and others groaned, because they had done nothing.

The soldier was Martin, in the fourth century, in Roman Gaul, in what is now France, and his story belongs among the monks and monk-bishops of Gaul. Almost all we know of him comes from one admirer, Sulpicius Severus, who met him late in life and wrote his story while he still lived.

Born to pagans in Pannonia, in what is now Hungary, Martin asked at ten to be prepared for baptism, but his father had him forced into the army. Sulpicius says that after Amiens, Martin saw Christ in a dream, wearing the half-cloak. He was baptised, yet stayed nearly two more years in the army, for his tribune's sake.

When the Caesar Julian was handing out money to his soldiers before a battle, Martin asked for his discharge instead. "I am Christ's soldier. I am not allowed to fight." He offered to stand unarmed before the battle line and was put in prison, but the next day the enemy surrendered. Sulpicius saw the peace as Martin's victory.

Hilary, bishop of Poitiers, made him an exorcist, and later the people of Tours made him their bishop. Even then he lived as a monk, in a wooden cell by the Loire where eighty disciples joined him.

At the emperor Maximus's court in Trier, bishops were prosecuting Priscillian for heresy. Martin did not defend his teaching, but he begged that no blood be shed. He said that "it was a cruel wrong, never heard of before, for a judge of this world to try a case of the church." After Martin left, Priscillian and his followers were put to death. Later, to save other lives, he shared communion for one day with the bishops behind those deaths, though he refused to sign his name and never again went to a gathering of bishops.

A later bishop built a great church over his grave, because miracles were reported there. Gregory, bishop of Tours almost two hundred years later, calls him "our blessed patron", and even the Frankish king Clovis ordered his army to take only grass and water from Martin's lands.

His way of life spread as well, since many of his monks became bishops, and Sulpicius claims his book was read in Rome, Carthage and Egypt. At his funeral, it was said, almost two thousand monks gathered.

Generations later, when bishops at a council in Paris sat silent before an angry king, Gregory reminded them how Maximus had forced Martin to yield. The judgment of the eternal King, he told them, pursued Maximus until he lost his throne and his life.
