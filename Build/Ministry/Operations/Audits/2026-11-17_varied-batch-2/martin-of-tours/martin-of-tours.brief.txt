At the gate of Amiens, in a killing winter, a soldier met a naked beggar. He cut his cloak in two and gave the beggar half, while some onlookers laughed and others groaned, because they had done nothing.

The soldier was Martin, in the fourth century, in Roman Gaul, in what is now France, among its monks and monk-bishops. Almost all we know of him comes from one admirer, Sulpicius Severus, who met him late in life and wrote his story while he still lived.

Born to pagans in Pannonia, in what is now Hungary, Martin asked at ten to be prepared for baptism, but his father had him forced into the army. Sulpicius says that after Amiens, Martin saw Christ in a dream, wearing the half-cloak. He was baptised, but stayed in the army nearly two more years.

When the Caesar Julian handed out money to his troops before a battle, Martin asked for his discharge. "I am Christ's soldier. I am not allowed to fight." Julian called it cowardice, so Martin offered to stand unarmed before the battle line. He was jailed, but the next day the enemy surrendered, a peace Sulpicius saw as Martin's victory.

Out of the army, he founded a monastery near Poitiers, where Hilary was bishop. Later a citizen of Tours lured him out with a story of a sick wife, and crowds brought him in under a kind of guard. As bishop he still lived as a monk, in a wooden cell by the Loire where eighty disciples joined him.

At the emperor Maximus's court in Trier, bishops were prosecuting Priscillian for heresy. Martin did not defend his teaching, but begged that no blood be shed. He said that "it was a cruel wrong, never heard of before, for a judge of this world to try a case of the church." After Martin left, Priscillian and his followers were put to death. To save other lives, he shared communion for one day with the bishops behind those deaths, though he refused to sign it.

A later bishop built a great church over his grave, and even the Frankish king Clovis ordered his army to take only grass and water from Martin's lands.

Many of his monks became bishops, and Sulpicius claims the Life he wrote was read in Rome, Carthage and Egypt. At his funeral, it was said, almost two thousand monks gathered.

At a council in Paris, other bishops sat silent before an angry king. Gregory, bishop of Tours almost two hundred years later, reminded them how Maximus had forced Martin to yield. The judgment of the eternal King, he told them, pursued Maximus until he lost his throne and his life.
