At the gate of Amiens, in a winter so cold it was killing people, a soldier met a naked beggar. With nothing left but his own cloak, he cut it in two with his sword and gave the man half, while some onlookers laughed and others groaned, because they had done nothing.

The soldier was Martin, and this was the fourth century, in Roman Gaul, in what is now France. He would become one of the monks and monk-bishops of Gaul. Almost all we know of him comes from one admirer, Sulpicius Severus, who met him late in life and wrote his story while he still lived.

Martin was born to pagan parents in Pannonia, in what is now Hungary. At ten he asked to be prepared for baptism, but at fifteen his own father, a soldier, had him forced into the army in chains. Sulpicius says that after Amiens, Martin saw Christ in a dream, wearing the half-cloak. He was then baptised, yet he stayed nearly two more years in the army, for his tribune's sake.

Before a battle near Worms, the Caesar Julian was handing each soldier a gift of money, and Martin, who meant to leave, would not take it. "Until now I have served you as a soldier. Let me now serve God as a soldier. Let the man who is going to fight take your gift. I am Christ's soldier. I am not allowed to fight." He offered to stand unarmed before the battle line, so he was put in prison, and the next day the enemy surrendered. Sulpicius saw the peace as Martin's victory.

Hilary, bishop of Poitiers, made him an exorcist after Martin refused to be a deacon, and later Martin founded a monastery near Poitiers. When Tours wanted him as bishop, a citizen tricked him out with a story of a sick wife. As bishop he still lived as a monk, in a wooden cell by the river Loire, where eighty disciples joined him. Through the villages he tore down pagan temples and built churches in their place.

At the emperor Maximus's court in Trier, bishops were prosecuting Priscillian for heresy. Martin did not defend his teaching, but he begged that no blood be shed, and he said this: "It was a cruel wrong, never heard of before, for a judge of this world to try a case of the church." While he was at Trier the trial waited, but after he left, Priscillian and his companions were put to death. Later, to save other lives, Martin shared communion for one day with the bishops behind those deaths, although he refused to sign his name. He never went to a gathering of bishops again.

He died on a journey to Candes, a village of his diocese, where he had gone in old age to make peace among its quarrelling clergy.

A later bishop built a great church over his grave, because miracles were reported there. Gregory, bishop of Tours almost two hundred years later, calls him "our blessed patron" and told the faithful to remember his burial day, 11 November. The Frankish king Clovis ordered his army to take nothing but grass and water from Martin's lands. Sulpicius claims his book was read in Rome, Carthage and Egypt, and many of Martin's monks became bishops. At his funeral, it was said, almost two thousand monks gathered.

Generations later, at a council in Paris, other bishops sat silent before an angry king. Gregory reminded them how Maximus had forced Martin to yield, and how the judgment of the eternal King pursued Maximus until he lost his throne and his life.
