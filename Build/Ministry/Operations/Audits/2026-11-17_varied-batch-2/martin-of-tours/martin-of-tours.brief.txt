At the gate of Amiens, in a killing winter, a soldier met a naked beggar. He cut his cloak in two and gave the beggar half, while some onlookers laughed and others groaned, because they had done nothing.

The soldier was Martin, in Roman Gaul in the fourth century. His story comes to us almost entirely from one admirer, Sulpicius Severus, who met him late in life and wrote it while Martin still lived.

Born to pagans in Pannonia, in what is now Hungary, Martin asked at ten to be prepared for baptism, but his father had him forced into the army. Sulpicius says that after Amiens, Martin saw Christ in a dream, wearing the half-cloak. He was baptized, but stayed in the army nearly two more years.

When the Roman commander Julian handed out money to his troops before a battle, Martin asked for his discharge. "I am Christ's soldier. I am not allowed to fight." Julian called it cowardice, so Martin offered to stand unarmed before the battle line. He was jailed, but the next day the enemy surrendered, a peace Sulpicius saw as Martin's victory.

Out of the army, he founded a monastery near Poitiers, where Hilary was bishop. Later a citizen of Tours lured him out with a story of a sick wife, and crowds brought him in under a kind of guard. As bishop he still lived as a monk, in a wooden cell by the Loire where eighty disciples joined him.

Through the villages, Sulpicius tells, he tore down temples and built churches or monasteries in their place. When he set about cutting down a pine held sacred, the local people rose against him. Elsewhere a crowd drove him off and hurt him. Often too, he says, his preaching moved people to pull down their own temples.

At the emperor Maximus's court in Trier, bishops were prosecuting Priscillian for heresy. Martin did not defend his teaching, but begged that no blood be shed. He said that "it was a cruel wrong, never heard of before, for a judge of this world to try a case of the church." After Martin left, Priscillian and his followers were put to death. To save other lives, he shared communion for one day with the bishops behind those deaths. They pressed him to confirm it in writing, and he refused.

Sulpicius, who was not there, heard that almost two thousand monks gathered at his funeral. Many of his monks became bishops, and Sulpicius claims the Life he wrote was read in Rome, Carthage and Egypt.

A later bishop built a great church over his grave, and even the Frankish king Clovis ordered his army to take only grass and water from Martin's lands.

At a council in Paris, the bishops sat silent before the fury of King Chilperic. Gregory, bishop of Tours almost two hundred years later, warned them that the judgment of the eternal King had pursued Maximus until he lost his throne and his life. Maximus had forced Martin to yield, he said, and Martin gave way only to free the condemned from death.
