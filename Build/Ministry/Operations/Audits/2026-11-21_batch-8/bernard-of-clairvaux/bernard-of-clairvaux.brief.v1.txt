In 1113 a young nobleman of Burgundy named Bernard left his father's house to become a monk, and his older brothers went with him. The eldest, Guy, saw the youngest, Nivard, playing in the street, and told him that all the family land would now fall to him alone. Nivard answered, "So heaven is for you, and earth for me? This is not a fair division."

Nivard soon followed. Bernard, with more than thirty companions, his friend William says, entered Cîteaux, a poor new monastery in Burgundy, where monks kept the old Rule of Saint Benedict strictly. William, who heard him preach, says the house was so small it had almost lost hope of lasting. Popes and kings would come to ask his word.

Two years after he entered, the abbot of Cîteaux sent him with a band of monks to found a new house, and made him their abbot, the head of a monastery. The place was Clairvaux, an old robbers' den, once called the Valley of Wormwood. William says they lived on barley bread and stews of beech leaves.

From 1135 he preached to his monks on the Song of Songs, the Bible's book of love poems, which he read as the love between God and the soul. In the eighty-third sermon he said, "Love seeks no cause beyond itself, and no fruit. Its fruit is the loving itself. I love because I love; I love in order to love."

In 1140 he moved against Peter Abelard, a teacher whose books were read far and wide. Bernard said they let human reason claim everything and leave nothing for faith. At a meeting of bishops at Sens there was no debate: Bernard writes that he read out the charges, and Abelard walked out and appealed to the pope in Rome, over their heads. A hostile pupil of Abelard's mocked the bishops as drunk and half asleep. Pope Innocent II condemned Abelard and ordered him shut in a monastery and his books burned.

In 1145 a monk Bernard had trained became Pope Eugenius III. The king of France had asked him again and again to speak for a new crusade, a great armed pilgrimage to the East. His monk Geoffrey says he refused until the pope ordered him. In 1146 he preached it, promising pardon for every sin confessed with a contrite heart to all who took the cross.

That year a monk of his own order, who had left his cloister, stirred crowds along the Rhine to kill Jews. Bernard wrote that the Jews must not be killed or even driven out. In the same letter he called their scattering a just penalty.

In 1148 the kings turned back from Damascus, the Muslim city of Syria, with nothing done. Bernard wrote that the armies were killed by the sword or by hunger. A historian of the crusader kingdom blamed treachery, but said he could not find out for certain who was guilty. Bernard blamed the crusaders' sins. The blame fell on him, and he wrote to Eugenius, "If one of the two must happen, I would rather people grumble against me than against God. It is good for me if he will use me as a shield. I gladly take on myself the cursing tongues of slanderers and the poisoned darts of blasphemers, so that they do not reach him."

He wrote the pope a book of advice, On Consideration. Peter, whose place the pope held, never rode a white horse or went in gold and silk, he said; and in such things the pope followed not Peter but the emperor Constantine. It was a friend's counsel. In 1413 Jan Hus copied those lines into his book on the Church, to argue that a pope who lives in pomp is no true vicar of Peter.

He died at Clairvaux in 1153. His monk Geoffrey counts nearly seven hundred monks in that valley, and a hundred and sixty monasteries under his care. His editor notes that the eighty-sixth sermon on the Song breaks off where death stopped him.
