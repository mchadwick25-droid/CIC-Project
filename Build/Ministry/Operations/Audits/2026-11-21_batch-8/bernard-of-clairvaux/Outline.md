# Outline: Bernard of Clairvaux

Sheet items (S, E, I, L, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. VP is the Latin *Vita prima* (William of Saint-Thierry, book I; Geoffrey, books III–V) with Alan's *Vita secunda*; PL182 Bernard's letters and *De consideratione* in Latin; PL183 his Song sermons in Latin; E1–E4 Eales's English of Mabillon's edition; ABL Abelard's works with Berengar's *Apology*; WT William of Tyre; JHK Joseph ha-Kohen; HUS Hus, *De Ecclesia*.

- **The essence:** In 1113 Bernard, a young Burgundian noble, brought more than thirty companions, his brothers among them, into the poor new monastery of Cîteaux (S3, E1; Documented, William, who knew him). Two years later he was made abbot of a new house, Clairvaux, and from there, by his sermons and his letters to popes, he became, in the tree's own words, the most listened-to churchman in Europe; popes and kings asked his word (S4, S6, E5, E6; Documented; "most listened-to" is the tree's own judgment, Widely Accepted).
- **Also told:**
  - **The Song of Songs.** From 1135 he preached to his monks on the Bible's love song, reading it as the love between God and the soul: 86 sermons, still unfinished when he died (E2; Documented, his own words; the year and "unfinished" his editor's).
  - **Against Abelard, 1140.** He charged that Peter Abelard's books let human reason claim everything and leave nothing to faith; at Sens, by Bernard's own account, Abelard walked out as the charges were read and appealed to Rome; Pope Innocent II condemned him, and ordered him shut in a monastery and his books burned (E4, E4c; Documented as Bernard's account and the pope's letter). Abelard's pupil Berengar mocked the bishops as drunk and half asleep (E4a; Documented as a hostile pupil's satire).
  - **Letters to popes.** When his own monk became Pope Eugenius III in 1145, Bernard wrote him a book of advice, *On Consideration*, warning that a pope in gold and silk, on a white horse, follows Constantine, not Peter (E5, E12; Documented).
  - **The Second Crusade, told with its failure.** At the pope's order he preached the crusade in 1146, promising pardon of sins to all who took the cross (E6, E7; Documented). When a monk stirred mobs on the Rhine to kill Jews, Bernard wrote that the Jews must not be killed or driven out, while calling their scattering a just penalty (E8; Documented). In 1148 the armies turned back from Damascus with nothing done (E9; Documented). Bernard answered that he would rather be blamed than God (E10; Documented, his own words). Why it failed, the witnesses differ (D4).
  - **What came of him.** About 160 houses under his care and nearly 700 monks at Clairvaux when he died in 1153 (S5; Documented as Geoffrey's figures). His book to Eugenius was taken up by Jan Hus against the popes of his day (I2; Documented).
- **Length:** about 4:30 at most (about 675 words by the scorer), aiming at 4:00. Counted first: the three quotes, about 85 words (Q1 about 14, Q2 about 26, Q3 about 52); the plain sense of the words to explain, about 55 words (Cîteaux, abbot, the Song of Songs, the papal sentence, *On Consideration*, taking the cross, pardon of sins, Damascus); the witnesses credited, about 60 words (William once, Bernard's own account at Sens, Berengar, Geoffrey, a historian of the crusader kingdom, Jan Hus). That is about 200 words before any event is told. Why about four minutes: the job names five turns (the brothers, the Song sermons, Abelard, the popes, the crusade and its failure), and the failure needs its own witnesses.

- **Setting:**
  - Time: born around 1090 (S1; William's age gives about 1090, his editor 1091: say "around 1090"); Cîteaux founded 1098 (S2); entered 1113 (S3); Clairvaux 1115 (S4, editor's year); Song sermons from 1135 (E2); Sens 1140 (E4); Eugenius pope 1145 (E5); the crusade preached 1146 (E6); Damascus 1148 (E9); died 1153 (S5).
  - Place: Fontaines near Dijon, in Burgundy, his father's castle (S1, editor). Cîteaux, in Burgundy (S2). Clairvaux, "in the territory of Langres, not far from the river Aube", in a valley once called the Valley of Wormwood (S4). Today: eastern France (Burgundy and Champagne).
  - Tradition: the tree's name is "the Cistercians" (S6, informalName). The order had begun fifteen years before he came (S3).
  - Language: he wrote and preached in Latin (sheet, Language).

**Words to explain** (the first time each is heard; plain word or short gloss, worked into the sentence)

- **Cîteaux**: "a poor new monastery in Burgundy, founded fifteen years before by monks who wanted to keep the old Rule of Saint Benedict to the letter" (S2, S3; CEN). Say it once, with the gloss.
- **abbot**: "the head of a monastery". The listener knows "monastery"; "abbot" gets its plain sense once.
- **the Song of Songs**: "the Bible's book of love poems between a bride and her beloved, which he read as the love between God and the soul" (E2; Sermon 83's own heading, "the Bridegroom, that is, Christ").
- **council of Sens / appeal**: "a meeting of bishops at Sens", and "appealed to the pope in Rome, over their heads" (E4). Do not use "synod".
- **On Consideration**: say "a book of advice he wrote for the pope, called *On Consideration*" (E5, E12).
- **take the cross**: "put the sign of the cross on their clothes, at the shoulder, as the mark of those going east" (E7, Letter 363: "the sign of the Cross … placed on a devout shoulder").
- **pardon of sins**: Bernard's own words, "pardon for every sin that you confess with contrite heart" (E7). Do not say "indulgence" bare.
- **the Second Crusade**: "the second great armed pilgrimage from western Europe to the East". The census name is not needed.
- **Damascus**: "the great Muslim city of Syria, which the crusaders besieged" (E9). One clause.
- **vicar of Peter**: the one who holds Peter's place. Bernard's own word is 'heir' ('lest he whose seat you hold deny you are his heir', PL182 78509–78510). Hus: 'a true vicar of him whose place he fills' (HUS 6163–6164).
- **Peter / Constantine**: 'the apostle Peter, whose place the pope held' and 'the emperor Constantine'. One clause each, the first time they are heard.
- **Templars**: "knights who took monks' vows to guard the roads to Jerusalem" (CEN link entry), if the link clause is kept.

- **Shape:** Founder or leader. What he built: Clairvaux; at his death, by Geoffrey's count, 160 monasteries under his care (S4, S5, I1). How it ran: hard poverty at the start (S4), the abbot preaching to his own monks (E2). Who it served: his monks first, then popes and kings who asked his word (E5, E6). What outlasted him: the order's spread, the Song sermons, the book to Eugenius (I1–I3). The trap is titles and offices in place of acts; tell each turn through something he did or wrote.
- **Sibling:** none in the batch. Here it is one beat of his crusade preaching, from his own letters, and does not retell the Mainz confrontation.
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** The boy in the street (Q1; E1). In about three sentences: In 1113 Bernard, a young noble of Burgundy, left his father's house to become a monk, and his older brothers went with him. The eldest, Guy, saw the youngest, Nivard, playing in the street, and told him that all their land would now be his. Nivard answered with Q1. Then straight into the essence: in 1113 Bernard and more than thirty companions, his older brothers among them, entered the poor new monastery of Cîteaux.
  2. Not chosen: Bernard's line to himself, "Bernard, Bernard, why have you come?" (E1b; card). Strong, but it does not carry the brothers, which the job names.

**Story beats** (the opening, the essence and beat 1 make the first minute, about 150 words; beats 2 to 7 follow the life once, in order. The essence reaches ahead to "the most listened-to churchman"; the rest does not go back)

1. **Cîteaux and Clairvaux, 1113 to 1115.** He was about twenty-three; his friend William, who heard him preach, says more than thirty came with him, at a time when the house was so small that it had almost lost hope of lasting (S2, S3; D1; Documented). Wives held back their husbands, and friends turned friends away, William says (E1a, the safe part only). Two years later the abbot, Stephen, sent him with a band of monks to found a new house, and made him their abbot, though he was young and weak in body (S4). The place was an old robbers' den, once called the Valley of Wormwood; they lived on barley bread and stews of beech leaves (S4; Documented, William). A little later the boy followed his brothers, and neither his father nor his kin could hold him (E1).
2. **The Song of Songs, from 1135.** Optional clause: he backed Pope Innocent II through an eight-year split over the papacy, from 1130 (E3; editor's summary), so that popes owed him much. Cut first if over. Abbot of Clairvaux, he preached to his monks on the Bible's love song, reading the bride and her beloved as the soul and God (E2). Quote 2 (Sermon 83). The sermons ran to 86 over the years that followed (E2, editor). Documented.
3. **Abelard, 1140.** Peter Abelard, a teacher of logic and theology whose books were read far and wide (E4, Letter 189: "His books fly abroad"), wrote books that Bernard said let human reason claim everything for itself and leave nothing for faith (E4, Letter 188, in the narrator's words). Abelard challenged him to meet before the bishops at Sens. Bernard writes that he went unwillingly, read out the charges, and Abelard walked out and appealed to Rome (E4; Documented as Bernard's account). A pupil of Abelard's wrote that the bishops condemned him after dinner, drunk and half asleep (E4a; credit him as a hostile pupil). Pope Innocent II condemned Abelard and ordered him shut away in a monastery and his books burned (E4c; Documented). The abbot of Cluny took him in, and he died two years later, in 1142 (E4c; editor; the tree's Cluny entry). Do not say he died at Cluny: the editor places his death at Saint-Marcel, near Chalon. Do not tell the reconciliation as more than one clause, and only as the editor's.
4. **Eugenius, 1145, and the call to the crusade.** When the cardinals chose a monk Bernard had trained as Pope Eugenius III, Bernard wrote to them, "May God forgive you what you have done!" (E5; narrator may give the plain words without quotation marks: a man who had chosen to be a door-keeper was made lord of all). In his letters the enemies of the Cross were ravaging the land where Christ had lived (E7, Letter 363). The king of France asked him again and again to speak for a new crusade, and he would not until the pope ordered him, his monk Geoffrey says (E6; D5). He preached it in France and Germany in 1146, by word and letter, promising pardon for every sin confessed to all who took the cross (E6, E7; Documented).
5. **The Rhine, 1146.** A monk of his own order who had left his cloister stirred crowds along the Rhine to kill Jews. Bernard wrote that the Jews must not be persecuted, killed or even driven out, and sent him back to his monastery; in the same letter he called their scattering a just penalty (E8; Documented, his letters; that he sent Ralph back, the editor's, from Otto). Optional clause: a Jewish chronicler four centuries later wrote that without this priest none would have escaped (E8, JHK). Give him by role; if he is named, use "Radulf", as the tree spells him.
6. **The failure, 1148.** The armies, Bernard wrote, were laid low in the desert, killed by the sword or wasted by hunger (E9), and in 1148 the kings turned back from the walls of Damascus with nothing done (E9; Documented: Bernard and William of Tyre). A historian of the crusader kingdom, writing a generation later, blamed treachery, but said he could not find out for certain who was guilty (E9, D4). Bernard, writing to Eugenius, blamed the crusaders' sins: "We said, Peace, and there is no peace" may be given in the narrator's words. Then Quote 3. Optional, cut first: his monk Geoffrey wrote that the East was not freed, but heaven was filled (E11).
7. **His last years and death, 1153.** He went on writing the book to Eugenius, *On Consideration*: a pope, he told him, is either a shepherd or not; Peter never rode a white horse or went in gold and silk; in such things the pope had followed not Peter but Constantine (E12; paraphrase, not in quotation marks). He died at Clairvaux in 1153 (S5; Documented, editor and Geoffrey). He had not finished the Song sermons when he died; the 86th breaks off where death stopped him (E4-ed 33259–33262, the editor's). Give no age: the editor's "sixty-third year" and William's reckoning differ by a year.

**What came of them** (ranked; about a third of the story, told with beats 6 and 7 and the ending)

1. **The order he spread.** At his death, Geoffrey counts nearly 700 monks at Clairvaux and 160 monasteries under his care (S5, I1; Documented as Geoffrey's figures). Tell the numbers as Geoffrey's, told once, at the ending. The places (Ireland, Portugal, Sweden) come from the editor's list of the order's foundations and go to the card.
2. **The book to Eugenius, carried on by Jan Hus.** In 1413 Jan Hus copied Bernard's words about Peter and Constantine into his book on the Church, to argue that a pope who lives in pomp is no true vicar of Peter (I2; Documented). **What he defended**: the pope's office as a shepherd's, after Peter's pattern. The listener must be told that Bernard wrote it to a pope he loved and served; Hus turned it against the popes of his own day.
3. **The Song sermons read on.** One clause, required: his Song sermons were read for centuries; two hundred years on, English writers still quoted him by name (CEN legacy). Optional clause: Calvin, four centuries later, quoted the Song sermons on trusting the mercy of Christ rather than one's own merit (I3; Documented).
4. Keeping today: the tree's Cistercian entry says the order "is still here" and "Cistercian and Trappist houses keep the same rule today" (CEN `cistercian-monasticism` `legacy`). One clause at most, not as the closing line.

**Quotes, 2 or 3 at most**

- **Quote 1, Nivard** (opening). Rendered from the Latin of William's *Life*, book I (VP 16242–16252; E1): "So heaven is for you, and earth for me? This is not a fair division." Job: carries the brothers' going in a boy's voice, and the price, the family's land, in one line. **Damaged scan**: the Latin stands only in a damaged two-column OCR of PL 185; the key word is mended ("rxltiui" read "coelum"). Alan's later *Life*, in the same scan, confirms the sense ("you have heaven, I earth", VP 34739–34743). No other held print. Card: print the English alone unless the page image is checked. Guy's words, if the writer needs them, in the narrator's voice: "all the land we own now falls to you alone".
- **Quote 2, Sermon 83 on the Song of Songs** (beat 2). Rendered from the Latin (PL183 105304–105306), checked against Eales (E4 32161–32164): "Love seeks no cause beyond itself, and no fruit. Its fruit is the loving itself. I love because I love; I love in order to love." Job: shows what the sermons taught, in his own voice: love of God wanted for itself, not for reward.
- **Quote 3, the shield** (beat 6). Rendered from the Latin of *On Consideration*, book II (PL182 75604–75611), confirmed word for word by Geoffrey's quotation of it (VP 20922–20927): "If one of the two must happen, I would rather people grumble against me than against God. It is good for me if he will use me as a shield. I gladly take on myself the cursing tongues of slanderers and the poisoned darts of blasphemers, so that they do not reach him." Job: his own answer to the failure, and to the blame that fell on him.

None is on the README's lifted list; none needs to be. The Constantine line (E12) is told in paraphrase only, so it does not take a fourth quote and stays free for the card.

**Connection to carry into the story:** Bernard wrote *On Consideration* to Eugenius III, a pope who had been his own monk, to call him back from pomp to a shepherd's work: Peter, he wrote, never went in gold or rode a white horse, and in such things a pope follows Constantine, not Peter (E12, PL182 78506–78517). In 1413 Jan Hus copied those very lines into his book on the Church, to argue that a pope who does not live like Peter is not Peter's true vicar (I2, HUS 6135–6164). The listener must be told that this was advice from a loyal friend of the papacy, written for a pope he loved, and that Hus, about two and a half centuries later, used the same words as a charge against the popes of his own time. The order's spread (I1) is told with its numbers as Geoffrey's. Tell it through the two acts of writing, not as "his influence lasted".

(2) The unfinished sermon: his editor notes that the 86th sermon on the Song breaks off where death stopped him (E2; E4-ed 33259–33262; credit the editor).

- "Bernard debated Abelard at Sens" → no debate took place; by Bernard's account Abelard walked out and appealed (E4).
