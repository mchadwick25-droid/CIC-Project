# Source sheet: Jerome

Researcher's sheet for batch 8 (2026-11-21). The job (Mark's, 2026-11-21): the scholar who learned Hebrew to put the Scriptures into Latin from the source, at Damasus's bidding and then in his cell at Bethlehem; his letters, his quarrels with Rufinus and Augustine, and the Roman women who studied with him. Teacher or thinker. New card. The writer uses the outline, the shapes and the standard only; this sheet is the evidence behind the outline.

**Conventions.** Line numbers are the file's own lines. In the XML files, quotes give the text with tags removed and inline notes cut out and marked […].

**Language.** Jerome wrote in Latin. Held in Latin: the *Apology against Rufinus*, *On Illustrious Men* and the polemical works (PL23); Jerome's letters to Augustine as kept in Augustine's collection, Ep. 72, 75 and the others to 123 (AUGL). **Not held in Latin:** Jerome's own letter collection (Letters 1–154 in his numbering), so Letters 22, 39, 45, 84, 108, 123, 125 and 127 are held only in Fremantle's English; and the prefaces to the Gospels, Samuel and Kings, and Daniel. Rufinus's *Apology* is held only in Fremantle's English.

**Witness kinds and gaps.**

- CONTEMP = **Sulpicius Severus**, *Dialogues* I.8–9: the traveller Postumianus, a speaker in Sulpicius's dialogue, says he stayed six months with Jerome at Bethlehem. Written in Jerome's lifetime (it calls him alive, SUL 2889–2900). The visit is told inside a literary dialogue.
- CONTEMP, hostile = **Palladius**, *Lausiac History* (about 420). He sided with Rufinus (PAL 1223, Clarke's note).
- LATER, a generation = **Prosper of Aquitaine**, *Chronicle* (to 455): the death.

## Setting

**S1. Birthplace.** JR 41786–41789, OWN (*On Illustrious Men* 135): "I, Jerome, son of Eusebius, of the city of Strido, which is on the border of Dalmatia and Pannonia and was overthrown by the Goths, up to the present year, that is, the fourteenth of the Emperor Theodosius, have written the following:" Latin PL23 62491–62494: "Hieronymus, patre Eusebio natus, oppido Stridonis, quod, a Gothis eversum, Dalmatiae quondam Pannoniaeque confinium fuit". Documented. Not needed for four minutes; the birthplace goes to the card.

**S2. Birth year: the sources differ.** See D1. Not for the story.

**S3. Rome, 382–385.** JW-ed 2776 "382–85. At Rome." OWN, Letter 45.2 (JW 8614–8615), written as he left: "Such is the company in which I have lived for almost three years." Dates: the editor's (Dominant Modern Reconstruction); "almost three years": Documented, his words.

**S4. Bethlehem, from 386 to his death.** OWN, *Apology* III.22 (JR 55429–55446): sailed from Rome in August, by Cyprus and Antioch to Jerusalem "in the middle of winter", then Egypt. Letter 108.20 (JW 21691–21699): Paula's monasteries at Bethlehem. Dates JW-ed (Letter 45 heading, 8591–8592: "Written on board ship at Ostia, in August, 385"). Documented (journey), editor's year.

**S5. The tradition.** CEN `hieronymian-ascetic-literary`: informalName "The Bethlehem Circle"; dates "c. 382–420 CE"; its `why`: "A circle of wealthy Roman women funded and led a community at Bethlehem where Jerome translated the Bible into Latin". Spoken name: **the Bethlehem Circle**. CEN `jerome`: `why` "Jerome put the Old Testament into Latin from the Hebrew at Bethlehem, in the work later called the Vulgate."

**S6. What the churches read before him.** OWN, preface to the Gospels (the passage after the forger lines; JW 46656–46660): "I am not discussing the Old Testament, which was turned into Greek by the Seventy elders, and […] has reached us by a descent of three steps." JW-ed note 5387: "That is, after being translated from Hebrew into Greek, and from Greek into Latin." Augustine, *City of God* XVIII.43 (CIV 36728–36731): "From this translation [the Septuagint] there has also been made a translation in the Latin tongue, which the Latin churches use." Documented.

## Events

### Learning Hebrew

**E1. In the desert, from a converted Jew.** OWN, Letter 125.12 to Rusticus (JW 25503–25522), written late in life (JW-ed heading of Letter 125 gives 411):
"In my youth when the desert walled me in with its solitude I was still unable to endure the promptings of sin and the natural heat of my blood; and, although I tried by frequent fasts to break the force of both, my mind still surged with [evil] thoughts. […] To subdue its turbulence I betook myself to a brother […] who before his conversion had been a Jew and asked him to teach me Hebrew. Thus, after having familiarised myself with the pointedness of Quintilian, the fluency of Cicero, the seriousness of Fronto and the gentleness of Pliny, I began to learn my letters anew and to study to pronounce words both harsh and guttural. What labour I spent upon this task, what difficulties I went through, how often I despaired, how often I gave over and then in my eagerness to learn commenced again, can be attested both by myself the subject of this misery and by those who then lived with me. But I thank the Lord that from this seed of learning sown in bitterness I now cull sweet fruits."
Documented, his own words. The "[evil]" bracket is Fremantle's. Which desert, and when, is the editor's: JW-ed 2691 "374–79. In Desert of Chalcis." Latin not held.

**E2. The same years, told again in the Daniel preface.** OWN (JW 47197–47208): "when, in lily youth, after reading the flowery rhetoric of Quintilian and Tully, I entered on the vigorous study of this language, the expenditure of much time and energy barely enabled me to utter the puffing and hissing words; I seemed to be walking in a sort of underground chamber with a few scattered rays of light shining down upon me". Documented. Confirms E1; not quoted.

**E3. At Bethlehem, a Jewish teacher by night.** OWN, Letter 84.3 (JW 19006–19013, 399 by JW-ed): "Yet once more I came to Jerusalem and to Bethlehem. What trouble and expense it cost me to get Baraninas […] to teach me under cover of night. For by his fear of the Jews he presented to me in his own person a second edition of Nicodemus." Documented. JW-ed note 2558: "From this Jew Jerome took lessons in Hebrew during the earlier years of his life at Bethlehem." (editor's).

### Rome and Damasus

**E4. Damasus's secretary.** OWN, Letter 123.10 (JW 24188–24191): "A great many years ago when I was helping Damasus bishop of Rome with his ecclesiastical correspondence, and writing his answers to the questions referred to him by the councils of the east and west". Documented.

**E5. The commission: correct the Latin Gospels against the Greek.** OWN, preface to the Gospels, addressed to Damasus (JW 46631–46652; heading JW-ed 46627–46628 "Damasus, a.d. 383"):
"You urge me to revise the old Latin version, and, as it were, to sit in judgment on the copies of the Scriptures which are now scattered throughout the whole world; and, inasmuch as they differ from one another, you would have me decide which of them agree with the Greek original. The labour is one of love, but at the same time both perilous and presumptuous; […] Is there a man, learned or unlearned, who will not, when he takes the volume into his hands, and perceives that what he reads does not suit his settled tastes, break out immediately into violent language, and call me a forger and a profane person for having the audacity to add anything to the ancient books, or to make any changes or corrections therein? […] For if we are to pin our faith to the Latin texts, it is for our opponents to tell us which; for there are almost as many forms of texts as there are copies. If, on the other hand, we are to glean the truth from a comparison of many, why not go back to the original Greek".
Documented. Barred lines: "You urge me to revise the old Latin version" and "the command is given by you who are the supreme bishop" are in `damasus-of-rome` (the first is lifted; not chosen). The "forger" fear is paraphrased in `damasus-of-rome`; do not retell it the same way here.
JW-ed 46600–46614 (editor's): the work was "at the request and under the sanction of Pope Damasus"; Jerome's aim was "to revise the old Latin, and not to make a new version". Also JW-ed 47288–47290: at Rome "he translated only from the Greek—the New Testament from the Greek mss., and the Book of Psalms from the LXX."

**E6. He taught a circle of women at Rome.** OWN, Letter 45.2 to Asella (JW 8617–8620): "It often happened that I found myself surrounded with virgins, and to some of these I expounded the divine books as best I could. Our studies brought about constant intercourse, this soon ripened into intimacy, and this, in turn, produced mutual confidence." Documented. Marcella's questions at Rome are told in `marcella-of-rome` from Letter 127; not retold here.

**E7. Rome turns on him.** OWN, Letter 39.6 to Paula (JW 8109–8117), after the death of her daughter Blaesilla (384, JW-ed): "When you were carried fainting out of the funeral procession, whispers such as these were audible in the crowd. 'Is not this what we have often said. She weeps for her daughter, killed with fasting. She wanted her to marry again, that she might have grandchildren. How long must we refrain from driving these detestable monks out of Rome? Why do we not stone them or hurl them into the Tiber? […]'" Documented as Jerome's report of the crowd. That fasting killed her is the crowd's charge, not a finding.
OWN, Letter 45.2–3 (JW 8625–8640): "No; my sex is my one crime, and even on this score I am not assailed, save when there is a talk of Paula going to Jerusalem. Very well, then. They believed my accuser when he lied; why do they not believe him when he retracts? […] Before I became acquainted with the family of the saintly Paula, all Rome resounded with my praises. Almost every one concurred in judging me worthy of the episcopate." Documented as his own account; the only account held.
OWN, preface to Didymus *On the Holy Spirit*, as Rufinus quotes it (JR 49862–49875): "the whole senate of the Pharisees raised a clamour […] I therefore returned with all speed to Jerusalem […] I am now in sight of Mary's inn and the Saviour's cave." And (JR 49879–49881) "the aforenamed Pontiff Damasus, who had impelled me to undertake this work, now sleeps in the Lord". Documented, his words, kept by his opponent.

**E8. Leaving Rome, August 385.** OWN, *Apology* III.22 (JR 55429–55433): "In the month of August, when the etesian winds were blowing, accompanied by the reverend presbyter Vincentius and my young brother, and other monks […], I went on board ship at the port of Rome, choosing my own time, and with a very large body of the saints attending me". The year is JR-ed note 3182 (385). Against this, Rufinus as Jerome quotes him (JR 55410–55415): "I could unfold a tale as to the manner of your departure from Rome; as to the opinions expressed about you at the time, and written about you afterwards, as to your oath, the place where you embarked, the pious manner in which you avoided committing perjury". See D4.

### Bethlehem: Paula and the work

**E9. Paula's foundations.** OWN, Letter 108.20, Jerome's memorial of Paula (JW 21696–21699): "Besides establishing a monastery for men, the charge of which she left to men, she divided into three companies and monasteries the numerous virgins whom she had gathered out of different provinces". Documented. Told in `paula-of-rome`; a clause here.

**E10. Paula and her daughter studied with him and learned Hebrew.** OWN, Letter 108.26 (JW 22005–22023): "She asked leave that she and her daughter might read over the old and new testaments under my guidance. Out of modesty I at first refused compliance, but as she persisted in her demand and frequently urged me to consent to it, I at last did so and taught her what I had learned not from myself […] but from the church's most famous writers. Wherever I stuck fast and honestly confessed myself at fault she would by no means rest content but would force me by fresh questions to point out to her which of many different solutions seemed to me the most probable. […] While I myself beginning as a young man have with much toil and effort partially acquired the Hebrew tongue and study it now unceasingly lest if I leave it, it also may leave me; Paula, on making up her mind that she too would learn it, succeeded so well that she could chant the psalms in Hebrew and could speak the language without a trace of the pronunciation peculiar to Latin. The same accomplishment can be seen to this day in her daughter Eustochium". Documented as Jerome's claim (memorial written for her daughter). `paula-of-rome` paraphrases the chanting; this story may use the "fresh questions" detail, in paraphrase.

**E11. A hostile witness on Paula.** PAL 475 (*Lausiac History* 41.2), CONTEMP hostile: "Among them was the Roman lady Paula, mother of Toxotius, a woman of great distinction in the spiritual life. She was hindered by a certain Jerome from Dalmatia. For though she was able to surpass all, having great abilities, he hindered her by his jealousy, having induced her to serve his own plan." Documented as Palladius's charge. See D3. (PAL 435, ch. 36: "showed qualities of temper so disastrous that they threw into the shade his splendid achievements": used in `palladius-of-helenopolis`; not quoted here.)

**E12. The Old Testament from the Hebrew, at Bethlehem, at friends' request.** JW-ed 46733–46737 (editor's): "This version was not undertaken with ecclesiastical sanction as was the case with the Gospels, but at the request of private friends […] It was wholly made at Bethlehem, and was begun about a.d. 391, and finished about a.d. 404." Dominant Modern Reconstruction for the dates.
OWN, preface to Samuel and Kings, the first published (JW-ed 46799–46805: "the 'Helmeted Preface' […] addressed to Paula and Eustochium, and published about a.d. 391"), JW 46926–46944: "First read, then, my Samuel and Kings; mine, I say, mine. For whatever by diligent translation and by anxious emendation we have learnt and made our own, is ours. […] albeit I am not in the least conscious of having deviated from the Hebrew original. At all events, if you are incredulous, read the Greek and Latin manuscripts and compare them with these poor efforts of mine, and wherever you see they disagree, ask some Hebrew […] I beg you to confront with the shields of your prayers the mad dogs who bark and rage against me". Documented.
OWN, *On Illustrious Men* 135 (JR 41809–41812): "I translated the New Testament from the Greek, and the Old Testament from the Hebrew". JR-ed note 2570: "The Old Testament from the Hebrew A H 30 31 a e; omit T 25 Her." So the words "Old Testament from the Hebrew" are in some manuscripts only. Do not rest the Hebrew claim on this line; rest it on the prefaces and the letters to Augustine (E17).
JW-ed 47288–47296 (editor's): three stages: at Rome from the Greek; at Bethlehem first from the Septuagint with marks; "the third from a.d. 390–404, in which he translated directly from the Hebrew."

### Rufinus

**E13. The friendship and the break.** JR-ed 46242–46255 (editor's): "They had been close friends in early youth […] In the disputes about Origenism […] they became estranged, Jerome siding with Epiphanius and Rufinus with John". `rufinus-of-aquileia` tells the friendship (lifted "inseparably bound") and the preface "trap"; this story names the break in a clause only.

**E14. Rufinus attacks the translation.** CONTEMP partisan, Rufinus, *Apology* II.32 (JR 50256–50285), 400 (JR-ed 46236):
"Perhaps it was a greater piece of audacity to alter the books of the divine Scriptures which had been delivered to the Churches of Christ by the Apostles to be a complete record of their faith by making a new translation under the influence of the Jews. […] And what are we to do when we are told that the books which bear the names of the Hebrew Prophets and lawgivers are to be had from you in a truer form than that which was approved by the Apostles? […] Which of them would have presumed thus to profane the book of God, and the sacred words of the Holy Spirit? Who but you would have laid hands upon the divine gift and the inheritance of the Apostles?"
And II.33 (JR 50306–50310): "The seventy translators, each in their separate cells, produced a version couched in consonant and identical words, under the inspiration, as we cannot doubt, of the Holy Spirit; and this version must certainly be of more authority with us than a translation made by a single man under the inspiration of Barabbas." Documented as Rufinus's charge. Latin not held.

**E15. Barabbas and Baranina.** Rufinus, *Apology* II (JR 49452–49465): "It is of Barabbas […] whom, unlike me, he took as his teacher from the Synagogue […] For, when Christ and Barabbas were offered for our choice, I in my simplicity made choice of Christ." Jerome's answer, *Apology* I.13 (JR 51608–51621; Latin PL23 31531–31541, "eo quod Barrabam Judaeum dixerim praeceptorem meum. Nec mirum si pro Baranina […] scripseris Barrabam"): "I do not wonder at your writing Barabbas for Baranina, the letters of the names being somewhat similar, when you allow yourself such a license in changing the names themselves, as to turn Eusebius into Pamphilus, and a heretic into a martyr. […] I never spoke of him as my master; I merely wished to illustrate my method of studying the Holy Scriptures by saying that I had read Origen just in the same way as I had taken lessons from this Jew." Documented, each side's words. For the card.

### Augustine

**E16. Augustine's first letter: do not translate from the Hebrew.** Augustine, Ep. 28.2 (AUG 25145–25157; 394 or 395, AUG-ed): "But I beseech you not to devote your labour to the work of translating into Latin the sacred canonical books, unless you follow the method in which you have translated Job, viz. with the addition of notes, to let it be seen plainly what differences there are between this version of yours and that of the LXX., whose authority is worthy of highest esteem." Ep. 28.3 (AUG 25180–25187): on Jerome's commentary on Galatians, "To find there the defence of falsehood undertaken, whether by you, a man of such weight, or by any author […] causes me, I must confess, great sorrow". Documented.

**E17. The uproar at Oea.** Augustine, Ep. 71.5 (AUG 32540–32560; 403, AUG-ed). Latin AUGL 20544–20560:
"Nam quidam frater noster episcopus cum lectitari instituisset in ecclesia, cui praeest, interpretationem tuam, mouit quiddam longe aliter abs te positum apud Ionam prophetam, quam erat omnium sensibus memoriaeque inueteratum et tot aetatum successionibus decantatum, factus est tantus tumultus in plebe maxime Graecis arguentibus et inflammantibus calumniam falsitatis, ut cogeretur episcopus — Oea quippe ciuitas erat — Iudaeorum testimonium flagitare, utrum autem illi inperitia an malitia hoc esse in Hebraeis codicibus responderunt, quod et Graeci et Latini habebant atque dicebant? quid plura? coactus est homo uelut mendositatem corrigere uolens post magnum periculum non remanere sine plebe."
English (AUG 32541–32556): "A certain bishop, one of our brethren, having introduced in the church over which he presides the reading of your version, came upon a word in the book of the prophet Jonah, of which you have given a very different rendering from that which had been of old familiar to the senses and memory of all the worshippers, and had been chanted for so many generations in the church. […] Thereupon arose such a tumult in the congregation, especially among the Greeks, correcting what had been read, and denouncing the translation as false, that the bishop was compelled to ask the testimony of the Jewish residents (it was in the town of Oea). These, whether from ignorance or from spite, answered that the words in the Hebrew Mss. were correctly rendered in the Greek version, and in the Latin one taken from it. What further need I say? The man was compelled to correct your version in that passage as if it had been falsely translated, as he desired not to be left without a congregation,—a calamity which he narrowly escaped."
Documented, Augustine's account. In the same letter (AUG 32566–32570) he thanks God for the Gospels "translated […] from the original Greek, because in almost every passage we have found nothing to object to".

**E18. Jerome's answer: the plant was neither gourd nor ivy.** Jerome, Ep. 75.22 in Augustine's collection (AUG 33970–34003; 404, AUG-ed; Latin AUGL 24249–24306): "Perhaps it is the old dispute about the gourd which has been revived […] I have already given a sufficient answer to this in my commentary on Jonah. At present, I deem it enough to say that in that passage, where the Septuagint has 'gourd,' and Aquila and the others have rendered the word 'ivy' […], the Hebrew Ms. has 'ciceion,' […] It is a kind of shrub having large leaves like a vine […] If, therefore, in translating word for word, I had put the word 'ciceia,' no one would know what it meant; if I had used the word 'gourd,' I would have said what is not found in the Hebrew. I therefore put down 'ivy,' that I might not differ from all other translators. But if your Jews said, either through malice or ignorance, as you yourself suggest, that the word is in the Hebrew text which is found in the Greek and Latin versions, it is evident that they were either unacquainted with Hebrew, or have been pleased to say what was not true, in order to make sport of the gourd-planters." Same letter (AUG 33926–33934): "I have not followed my own imagination, but have rendered the divine words as I found them understood by those who speak the Hebrew language. If you have any doubt of this in any passage, ask the Jews what is the meaning of the original." Documented.
Note: the lines just before (AUG 33906–33914, "in order that Latin readers might know what is found in the original Hebrew") are about his earlier Latin version of the corrected Greek, not the Hebrew one. Do not use them for the Hebrew translation.

**E19. "An old man in his monastic cell."** Jerome, Ep. 72.3 in Augustine's collection (AUG 32660–32663; 404 AUG-ed): "either send me the identical letter in question subscribed with your own hand, or desist from annoying an old man, who seeks retirement in his monastic cell." Context (AUG 32699–32700): Augustine's earlier letter had reached Italy before it reached Jerome: "How comes Italy to possess a treatise of yours which you did not write?" Documented.

**E20. Augustine yields on the word, holds on the churches.** Augustine, Ep. 82.35 (AUG 35730–35751; 405 AUG-ed; Latin AUGL 27673–27720): "my only reason for objecting to the public reading of your translation from the Hebrew in our churches was, lest, bringing forward anything which was, as it were, new and opposed to the authority of the Septuagint version, we should trouble by serious cause of offence the flocks of Christ […] Wherefore, as to that shrub in the book of Jonah, if in the Hebrew it is neither 'gourd' nor 'ivy,' but something else which stands erect, supported by its own stem without other props, I would prefer to call it 'gourd' in all our Latin versions". Documented.

**E21. Reconciled.** Jerome to Augustine, Ep. 195 (AUG 54032–54058; 418 AUG-ed): "You are renowned throughout the whole world; Catholics revere and look up to you as the restorer of the ancient faith, and—which is a token of yet more illustrious glory—all heretics abhor you. They persecute me also with equal hatred". Documented. Latin in Augustine's PL 33 letters (`augustine_epistulae-maurist-order-lat_migne-pl33.txt`), not checked line by line here; not quoted.

### Last years and death

**E22. A traveller's picture.** CONTEMP, Sulpicius, *Dialogues* I.8–9, Postumianus speaking (SUL 2889–2900, 2949–2962; Latin SULL 9041–9050):
SUL 2889–2900: "The presbyter Jerome […] rules the church of this place […] he is a man learned not only in Latin and Greek, but also Hebrew, to such a degree that no one dare venture to compare himself with him in all knowledge."
SUL 2949–2962: "I stayed with Hieronymus six months, who carried on an unceasing warfare against the wicked, and a perpetual struggle in opposition to the deadly hatred of ungodly men. The heretics hate him, because he never desists from attacking them; the clerics hate him, because he assails their life and crimes. But beyond doubt, all the good admire and love him […] He is always occupied in reading, always at his books with his whole heart: he takes no rest day or night; he is perpetually either reading or writing something."
Latin SULL 9047–9049: "totus semper in lectione, totus in libris est: non die neque nocte requiescit: aut legit aliquid semper aut scribit." Documented as Sulpicius's character's report. Legible OCR; the Latin matches the English.

**E23. Death, 420.** LATER, Prosper, *Chronicle* 1274, under the consuls of 420 (PRO 53897–53902): "Hioronimus presbyter moritur anno aotatis suao XCI prid. kal. Octobris." (OCR for "Hieronimus presbyter moritur anno aetatis suae XCI prid. kal. Octobris": died in the ninety-first year of his age, 30 September.) Documented (year); the age see D1. JW-ed 3447: "420. Jerome dies (September 20) at Bethlehem." See D2.

**E24. The attack on the Bethlehem monasteries (416–417).** Innocent to John of Jerusalem (JW 28469–28474): the virgins Eustochium and Paula "have deplored to me the ravages, murders, fires and outrages of all kinds […] they have left untold the name and motive of his human agent." Innocent to Jerome (JW 28438–28440): "as your letters name no individuals and bring no specific charges, there is no one at present against whom we can proceed." That the attackers were Pelagians is JW-ed (28389–28392, and the note "The attack was supposed to have been instigated by Pelagius"). Told in the tradition's tile. For the card.

## What came of him (impact, heaviest first)

**I1. His Latin Bible became the West's standard.**
- Isidore of Seville, *Etymologies* VI.4.5 (ISI 13114–13119), about two centuries later: "Presbyter quoque Hieronymus trium linguarum peritus ex Hebraeo in Latinum eloquium easdem Scripturas convertit, eloquenterque transfudit. Cuius interpretatio merito ceteris antefertur; nam [est] et verborum tenacior, et perspicuitate sententiae clarior [atque, utpote a Christiano, interpretatio verior]." (The square brackets are Lindsay's, marking words not in all manuscripts.) Plain sense: the presbyter Jerome, skilled in three languages, turned the Scriptures from Hebrew into Latin; his translation is rightly preferred to the others, holding closer to the words and clearer in sense. Documented as Isidore's judgment. Isidore's "trium linguarum peritus" repeats Augustine's words in I2.
- Isidore, *On Church Offices* I.12 (ISO 84302–84306), damaged OCR: "8. De Hebraeo autem in Latinum eloqu[…] […]sim usquequaque utuntur, ro eo quod. […] in sententiis et clarior in verbis." The fragment says churches everywhere use it; the scan is too broken to quote, and no other held print confirms it. Not used.
- Council of Trent, Session IV, 8 April 1546 (TRE 12390–12392; decree 12473–12481): "ordains and declares, that the said old and Vulgate edition, which, by the lengthened usage of so many ages, has been approved of in the Church, be, in public lectures, disputations, sermons, and expositions, held as authentic; and that no one is to dare or presume to reject it under any pretext whatever." Documented. The decree does not name Jerome. TRE-ed 4170–4176 (Waterworth, editor): "St. Jerome's version, or the Vulgate, was selected and proposed, as being the most ancient; the most used". Widely Accepted that the Vulgate is chiefly Jerome's work. JW-ed 47354–47359 (editor): the Psalter the churches sang was his revision from the Greek ("the Gallican Psalter"), not his Hebrew translation; "In 1566 it superseded the Roman in all churches except those of the Vatican, Milan, and St. Mark's, Venice."
- CEN `jerome` legacy (the tree's own field): "His Latin Bible became the West's standard text, and in 1546 the Council of Trent declared the Vulgate authentic."

**I2. Augustine's settled verdict: most learned, and yet the Seventy.** *City of God* XVIII.43 (CIV 36730–36746; Latin CIVL 18128–18133): "Our times, however, have enjoyed the advantage of the presbyter Jerome, a man most learned, and skilled in all three languages, who translated these same Scriptures into the Latin speech, not from the Greek, but from the Hebrew. But although the Jews acknowledge this very learned labor of his to be faithful, while they contend that the Septuagint translators have erred in many places, still the churches of Christ judge that no one should be preferred to the authority of so many men". Latin: "presbyter Hieronymus, homo doctissimus et omnium trium linguarum peritus, qui non ex Graeco, sed ex Hebraeo in Latinum eloquium easdem scripturas conuerterit." Documented. Shows that in his own time the churches did not yet take it up.

**I3. His letters are the record of the Roman women.** CEN `hieronymian-ascetic-literary` sourcing: "nearly all of it is one man's own hand"; CEN `jerome` why: "His letters are also the main record of the Roman women who funded that life and shared it." The tree's own sourced fields. Widely Accepted.

**I4. His catalog of Christian writers.** *On Illustrious Men*, continued by Gennadius (JR 41827–41833, "List of the Authors whom Gennadius added, after the Death of the Blessed Jerome"); `ildefonsus-of-toledo` says Ildefonsus carried on the list "that Jerome began and Isidore of Seville continued". For the card.

**I5. The lives of the desert hermits and the ascetic letters** (Lives of Paul, Hilarion, Malchus; Letter 22): for the card.

## Where sources disagree or come late

**D1. When he was born.** Prosper (E23): died in his ninety-first year in 420, so born about 330. JR-ed note 2568: "Born 331, died 420." JW-ed 2549: "345. Jerome born at Stridon". Contested. Not in the story; card gives both.

**D2. The day he died.** Prosper: 30 September ("prid. kal. Octobris"). JW-ed table: "September 20". Prosper is the ancient witness. Card only.

**D3. Paula's study.** Jerome (E10): she pressed him with questions and learned Hebrew. Palladius (E11), on Rufinus's side: he hindered her by his jealousy. Both told, each credited. Contested.

**D4. Why he left Rome.** Jerome (E7, E8): envy, a false accuser who took the charge back; he left "choosing my own time". Rufinus (E8) hints at an oath and a scandal, and says he will "keep back more than I relate". Only Jerome's account is told in full; Rufinus's is a hint. The story credits Jerome's account as his.

**D5. The plant in Jonah, and the Jews of Oea.** Augustine: the Jews, "whether from ignorance or from spite", said the Hebrew agreed with the old version. Jerome: the Hebrew word names a plant that is neither gourd nor ivy; if they said so, they "were either unacquainted with Hebrew, or have been pleased to say what was not true". Augustine later: he would still say "gourd". Each credited; no verdict.

**D6. Was the Greek Old Testament inspired?** Rufinus (E14) and Augustine (I2) held the Seventy's version had the Spirit's authority; Jerome, in his Genesis preface (summarized JW-ed 46764–46767; the preface itself is printed only in his *Apology* II, not checked here), rejected the story of the seventy cells. For the card.

**D7. "The Old Testament from the Hebrew" in *On Illustrious Men* 135** is in some manuscripts only (E12). The Hebrew translation rests on the prefaces and the letters.

**D8. Who attacked Bethlehem in 416.** Innocent names no one; the editor says Pelagians (E24). Card.

**D9. Damasus's death year.** JW-ed note 5386 gives "died 384" and, in the same note, "the pope's death, in 385". Not in the story.
