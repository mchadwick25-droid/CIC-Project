# Outline: Jerome

Sheet items (S, E, I, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. JW is Fremantle's English of Jerome's letters and prefaces (their Latin is not held); JR holds *On Illustrious Men* and the two *Apologies* of Rufinus and Jerome in English; AUG and AUGL are Augustine's letters, with Jerome's to him, in English and Latin; SUL Sulpicius; PAL Palladius; PRO Prosper; ISI Isidore; TRE Trent.

- **The essence:** Jerome, a Latin scholar of the late fourth century, learned Hebrew so that he could put the Old Testament into Latin from the Hebrew itself, not from the Greek translation the churches read; before that, at Rome, Damasus, the bishop of Rome, had set him to correct the Latin Gospels against the Greek (E1, E5, E12, E18; Documented, his own prefaces and letters; the Bethlehem dates 391–404 are the editor's, Dominant Modern Reconstruction). His Latin Bible was fought over in his lifetime, but in time it became the Western church's standard text (I1; Documented as Isidore's judgment and the Trent decree; that Trent's "old and Vulgate edition" is chiefly his work is the editor's and Widely Accepted).
- **Also told:**
  - **The Roman women who studied with him.** At Rome he taught the Scriptures to a circle of women (E6; Documented, his words). Paula followed him east and built monasteries at Bethlehem; she and her daughter read the Bible with him, pressed him with questions when he was stuck, and learned Hebrew (E9, E10; Documented as Jerome's claim). Palladius, on his opponents' side, wrote that he held Paula back by his jealousy (E11, D3; Documented as Palladius's charge; Contested). His translations were made for and dedicated to them (E12; Documented).
  - **His quarrels, in his letters and theirs.** Rufinus, once his close friend, broke with him over Origen and then attacked the new translation as tampering with what the apostles handed down (E13, E14; Documented as Rufinus's charge). Augustine begged him not to translate from the Hebrew, and told him of a church in uproar over one word in Jonah; Jerome answered sharply; years later they were reconciled (E16–E21; Documented, both sides' letters).
  - **The force of it.** Rome turned against him: a crowd at a funeral talked of throwing monks into the Tiber, and he was accused over Paula; he left in 385, by his own account at a time he chose (E7, E8, D4; Documented as his account; year the editor's).
- **Length:** about 4 minutes 5 seconds: about 615 words at 150 a minute, never past 675. Counted first: the three quotes, about 95 words (Q1 in two parts, 36; Q2, 16; Q3, 43); the plain sense of the words to explain, about 75 (the Greek Old Testament and the old Latin, 30; the plant in Jonah, 20; Origen, 10; the Vulgate and Trent, 15); the credited witnesses, about 45 (Rufinus, Augustine, Palladius, Isidore, Trent). That is about 215 words carried before any beat is told, leaving about 400 for the beats and what came of him. What does not fit is in **For the card, not the story**, below.
- **Setting:**
  - **Time:** late fourth and early fifth century: at Rome 382–385 (S3; editor's dates; "almost three years", his words); at Bethlehem from 386 until he died there in 420 (S4, E23). Say "in the 380s" for Rome; "in 420" for the death (Prosper).
  - **Places:** Rome; Bethlehem, "in sight of Mary's inn and the Saviour's cave", his words (E7). Oea: a town in Africa, by Augustine's letter ("one of our brethren", an African bishop). Do not add a modern name.
  - **Tradition:** the Bethlehem Circle (S5, census spoken name), told inside the Latin-speaking West.
- **Words to explain:**
  - **the Septuagint / the Seventy** (S6, E14, E16): "the old Greek translation of the Hebrew Scriptures, the one the apostles' churches read". Say "the old Greek translation"; use "the Seventy" only inside a quote's plain sense.
  - **the old Latin version** (E5, S6): "the Latin Bible the churches already had, translated from the Greek". Jerome's own image, if wanted: it "reached us by a descent of three steps", Hebrew to Greek to Latin (S6).
  - **the plant in Jonah** (E17, E18, Q3): "the plant God grew to shade the prophet Jonah". The old Latin said "gourd"; Jerome wrote "ivy". Give it in one clause before Q3.
  - **Origen** (link): "a Greek scholar of a century and a half before, whose ideas were then under attack".
  - **the Vulgate** (I1): "the Latin Bible the West came to use, built largely on his work". Never say Jerome called it that.
  - **monastery / monk**: known words; no gloss.
  - **presbyter**: say "priest" or leave out.
  - **Barabbas** (card only): the prisoner the crowd chose instead of Jesus.
- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker. The question he was trying to answer: what does Scripture actually say, when every Latin copy differs and the Latin Old Testament came to the churches second-hand, through Greek? What he taught: go back to the source, the Greek for the New Testament and the Hebrew for the Old, and check it with those who read Hebrew. Who learned it: the women who studied with him; then the whole Latin church, slowly. The trap is a list of books: carry the idea through one moment, the uproar at Oea, and his own words on learning Hebrew.

- **Openings:**
  1. **Recommended: his own words on learning Hebrew (E1, Q1a).** As a young man in the desert, Jerome tells us, fasting could not quiet his mind. To tame it he went to a fellow monk who had been born a Jew, and asked him to teach him Hebrew. After years of reading Rome's finest Latin writers, he wrote, Q1a. Then the essence: the Bible he made from that Hebrew.
  2. **The uproar at Oea (E17, Q3).** A bishop in Africa had Jerome's new translation of Jonah read in his church, and one word set the people shouting. If this opens, beat 6 keeps only Jerome's answer and the reconciliation, and Q3 is not told twice.
- **Story beats:**
  1. **Learning Hebrew.** In his youth, in the desert, he took up Hebrew against his own restless thoughts, from a brother "who before his conversion had been a Jew" (E1; Documented, his words, written about 411, JW-ed). Which desert, Chalcis in Syria in 374–379, is the editor's (Dominant Modern Reconstruction); the writer need not name it. Carried by the opening; do not tell it twice.
  2. **Rome, in the 380s: Damasus's commission.** He worked for Damasus, the bishop of Rome, writing his answers to councils of the East and West (E4; Documented). Damasus urged him to correct the old Latin Gospels against the Greek, because, Jerome wrote, there were almost as many forms of the text as there were copies (E5; Documented; the year 383 is the editor's). This was from the Greek, not the Hebrew. Paraphrase only; no quote.
  3. **Rome: the women, and Rome turning.** Women of Rome's great families gathered to study the Scriptures with him, and he taught them (E6; Documented, his words). When Paula's young daughter Blaesilla died, people in the funeral crowd said fasting had killed her, and asked why the monks were not thrown into the Tiber (E7; Documented as Jerome's report of the crowd; the cause of death is the crowd's charge). He was accused over his friendship with Paula; he says his accuser later took the charge back (E7; Documented, his account). In August 385 he sailed from Rome, choosing his own time, he says (E8; Documented; the year is the editor's). Rufinus later hinted at something worse, and said no more (D4; card).
  4. **Bethlehem: the work and the women.** Paula followed him east and built a monastery for men and houses for women at Bethlehem (E9; Documented). He paid a Jewish teacher who came by night, afraid of his own people (E3; Documented, his words). Paula and her daughter read the Bible with him; when he was stuck, he wrote, Paula would not rest but pressed him with fresh questions, and she learned Hebrew (E10; Documented as his claim). Palladius, a monk on his opponents' side, wrote that Jerome held her back by his jealousy (E11; Documented as Palladius's charge; Contested). From about 391 he put the Old Testament into Latin from the Hebrew, book by book, dedicating his prefaces to Paula and her daughter; to doubters he wrote: compare the copies, and ask someone who knows Hebrew (E12; Documented; dates the editor's).
  5. **Rufinus.** Rufinus of Aquileia had been his close friend; they fell out over Origen (E13; editor's summary, Widely Accepted). In 400 Rufinus, writing against him, accused him of changing the Scriptures the apostles had handed down, by a new translation made with the help of Jews (E14; Documented as Rufinus's charge): Q2.
  6. **Augustine and the uproar at Oea.** Augustine, a bishop in Africa, first begged him not to translate from the Hebrew, since the old Greek had such authority (E16; Documented, 394 or 395). In 403 he told him what had happened at Oea, where a bishop had Jerome's Jonah read in church (E17; Documented, Augustine's account): Q3. The Jews of the town, Augustine says, whether from ignorance or spite, said the Hebrew agreed with the old version, and the bishop corrected the reading so as not to lose his congregation (E17). Jerome answered that the Hebrew names a plant that is neither gourd nor ivy, and that he had written "ivy" so as not to differ from every other translator (E18; Documented). He told Augustine to stop troubling an old man in his monastery cell (E19; Documented). Augustine still would not have the Hebrew version read in church (E20). In 418 Jerome wrote that all the world knew Augustine as the restorer of the ancient faith (E21; Documented); they ended as allies. Credit each side; no verdict on the word.
  7. **Death.** He died at Bethlehem in 420 (E23; Documented, Prosper's chronicle). No age.
- **What came of him:**
  1. **His Latin Bible became the West's.** About two centuries later Isidore of Seville wrote that Jerome, skilled in three languages, had turned the Scriptures from Hebrew into Latin, and that his translation was rightly preferred to the others, closer to the words and clearer in sense (I1; Documented as Isidore's judgment). In 1546 the Council of Trent declared the "old and Vulgate edition", long used in the church, the authentic Latin text, and forbade anyone to reject it (I1; Documented, the decree). The decree does not name Jerome; that the Vulgate is chiefly his work is the editor's and the tree's own field (I1; Widely Accepted). What he defended: the Hebrew text as the source.
  2. **At first, even his admirers held back.** Late in life Augustine called him most learned, skilled in all three languages, and granted that Jews judged his translation faithful; yet he held that the churches should keep the old Greek (I2; Documented). Isidore later took up Augustine's very words, "skilled in three languages", for his own praise (I1, I2). Use this as the turn between the quarrels and the later verdict.
  3. **His letters keep the women.** Nearly everything known of the women of the Bethlehem Circle comes through his letters (I3; the tree's own sourced fields). One clause.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** What passed from Jerome was a Latin Old Testament made from the Hebrew, which the Latin churches first resisted and then took as their own (E12, E17, I1, I2). The listener must be told three things, through what people did: (1) the Latin churches read an Old Testament translated from the Greek, which was itself a translation of the Hebrew, and they had sung it for generations (S6, E17); (2) going back to the Hebrew meant changing words people knew by heart and setting aside a Greek text many held to be inspired, which is why Rufinus called it tampering and why a congregation in Africa nearly drove out its bishop (E14, E17, E20); (3) within two centuries a bishop in Spain called his version rightly preferred, and in 1546 the Catholic Church, at Trent, declared the old Latin "Vulgate", built largely on his work, its authentic text (I1). Say "built largely on his work": by the editor's account the Psalter the churches sang was his revision from the Greek, and his New Testament a correction of the old Latin (I1, E5, E12). Tell it through the quarrels and the later verdicts, not in a "this matters because" sentence.
