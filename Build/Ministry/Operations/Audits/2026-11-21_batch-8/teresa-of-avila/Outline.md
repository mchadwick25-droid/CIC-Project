# Outline: Teresa of Ávila

Sheet items (S, E, I, L, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. VIDA is her Life in Spanish (Silverio, from the autograph); LIFE the Life in Lewis's English, with Zimmerman's introduction and Lewis's preface (which prints Báñez's censure); FOUND the Foundations with Zimmerman's introduction; CASTLE the Interior Castle (Stanbrook English); WAY the Way of Perfection; BREV the Roman Breviary's lessons for her feast. The Castle, the Way and the Foundations are not held in Spanish; Báñez's censure is held only in English.

- **The essence:** Teresa of Ávila was a Carmelite nun who, after more than twenty years in a large and easy-going convent in Ávila, founded in 1562 a small, poor house of no more than thirteen nuns, St Joseph's, living by the order's old strict rule, against the city's attempt to close it; then she spent the rest of her life founding more houses like it, seventeen for nuns by her death in 1582, and houses of friars (E10–E12, E16; Documented, her own words; the count Widely Accepted, her editors'). Those houses became the Discalced, or barefoot, Carmelites, still a worldwide order (I1; Documented, the tree's home entry).
- **Also told:**
  - **The Life, for her confessors, under the Inquisition's eye.** She wrote her Life between 1562 and about 1565 because her confessors ordered it, and told them to burn it if it was not sound (E13–E14; Documented, her own words; dates Dominant Modern Reconstruction, D4). In the years when the Inquisition, the church's court for heresy in Spain, was banning devotional books in Spanish, friends warned her she might be reported, and she laughed (E5–E6; Documented). In 1574 a princess she had crossed denounced the book to the Inquisition, and her confessor Báñez handed it over himself; his report of July 1575 found no error and said she was "not a deceiver"; she never saw the book again (E19–E22; Documented as each one's account; the two routes credited, D5).
  - **The Interior Castle.** In 1577, ordered to write about prayer while her reform seemed close to ruin, she pictured the soul as a castle of diamond or crystal with many rooms, God in the innermost one, and prayer as the gate (E24–E25; Documented, her own words; the troubles Widely Accepted, her editor's).
  - **Opposed from inside her order.** In 1575, she wrote, the order's leaders sent her a decree to found no more houses and to stay in one (E18; Documented as her account; who issued it Contested, D10).
- **Length:** about 4 minutes (about 600 words at 150 a minute; never past about 675). Counted first: the three quotes, about 95 words (Q1 about 25, Q2 about 40, Q3 about 30) and the two short phrases, about 8; the plain sense of the words to explain, about 55 (Carmelite, convent rule relaxed, confessor, Inquisition, the Society of Jesus, barefoot or Discalced, the dwelling places, the church's book of daily prayer, declared a saint); the witnesses credited, about 55 (Báñez twice, the princess, her editors once, the church's prayer book, Pascal, Liguori, "those with her" at her death). That is about 215 words before any event is told. The job names four turns (the twenty years, the 1562 house and the many after it, the Life under the Inquisition, the Castle), so the beats get about 325 words, What came about 170 (a third, less the ending), and the ending about 25. Anything more goes to the card (see **For the card, not the story**).
- **Setting:**
  - Time: born 1515 (S1); entered the convent about 1535 (S4, D1; say "about 1535, when she was about twenty"); the house of St Joseph, 24 August 1562 (E10); the Life, 1562 to about 1565 (E13, D4); the book denounced 1574, Báñez's report July 1575 (E19–E21, D5, D6); the Castle, June to November 1577 (E24); a province of their own, 1580 (E28); died October 1582 (E29, D12); declared a saint 1622 (I3).
  - Place: Ávila, a walled city of Castile in central Spain (S1; CEN). The Incarnation, a large convent just outside its walls (S3). Toledo, where she wrote the Castle (E24). Alba de Tormes, a small town near Salamanca, where she died (E29).
  - Tradition: the tree's name is "the Carmelite Reform and the Spanish Mystics" (S2). The story names it once at most; "her reform" is enough after that.
  - Language: she wrote in Spanish (S5).

**Words to explain** (the first time each is heard; plain word or short gloss, worked into the sentence)

- **Carmelite**: "a nun of the Carmelites, an order that took its name from Mount Carmel in the Holy Land" (L2). Once.
- **convent / monastery**: say "convent" or "house" throughout. Her texts say "monastery" for a nuns' house; do not use it.
- **the rule relaxed (the Mitigation)**: "the order's rule, as popes had relaxed it" (S3, E8). Never "the Mitigation" or "mitigated" bare.
- **enclosure**: say what it means: "the nuns stayed inside"; her old convent let them go out to visit (S3).
- **confessor**: "the priest who heard her confession and guided her prayer". Once; after that "her confessors".
- **the Inquisition / Inquisitors**: "the Inquisition, the church's court that tried heresy in Spain"; "its judges".
- **the Society of Jesus / Jesuits**: "priests of the Society of Jesus, the new order Ignatius of Loyola had founded" (L1). Once.
- **Discalced / barefoot**: "the Discalced, or barefoot, Carmelites"; her friend's phrase "nuns like those of the barefoot orders" (E7).
- **the Mansions / Moradas**: "the book she called the Dwelling Places, known in English as the Interior Castle" (E25). Never "mansions" bare (modern sense misleads).
- **provincial / general chapter**: "the head of the order in Castile"; "the order's leaders". Do not use the titles.
- **Breviary**: "the church's book of daily prayer".
- **canonized**: "declared a saint".

- **Shape:** Founder or leader. What she built: small, poor, enclosed houses of thirteen nuns or fewer, living by the old rule, with long hours of silent prayer; how it ran: on alms, without income (E8, E11); who it served: the nuns themselves, and, in her own words, the church in a time of division (E9). The impact is in what outlasted her: the houses and their order (I1), and her books on prayer (I2, I4). The trap is titles and offices; tell her through acts: leaving a house she loved, facing the city council, writing for her confessors, laughing at the warning, going on founding when told to stop.
- **Sibling:** none in the batch. Read for overlap: `carmelite-reform-and-spanish-mystics` (the home; its live text tells the 1562 house, the Inquisition examining her Life, and John of the Cross's prison: here John is one clause by role, and the prison is not told); `ignatius-of-loyola` (live, read; tells his own life to 1556, never names her: no shared event); `the-carmelites` (live, read; tells Albert's rule and Innocent IV's approval of 1247: here one clause, that her reform went back to the order's old rule).
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** Her own words on the twenty years (Quote 1; E2), then: "So Teresa of Ávila wrote of her early years as a nun, in the book her confessors ordered her to write." Then straight into the essence. It carries the job's "twenty years" in her own image, and it belongs to the first beat's time, so nothing is told twice.
  2. Not chosen: the city council meeting to close the new house in August 1562 (E12). Strong, but it belongs in time order at beat 3.

**Story beats** (the opening, the essence and beat 1 make the first minute, about 150 words; beats 2 to 6 follow the life once, in order. The essence reaches ahead to the many houses; the houses after 1562 are told once, at beat 5)

1. **The Incarnation.** Born in Ávila in 1515 (S1). About 1535, at about twenty, she entered the Carmelite convent of the Incarnation, just outside the walls (S3, S4, D1). It was large and pleasant, she wrote, but poor; well over a hundred and fifty nuns lived there, and they often left it to stay with friends; the rule was kept as popes had relaxed it (S3, D3; Documented, her own words; numbers her editors'). The prayer she struggled with there is the opening's quote; do not repeat it.
2. **The turn, and the Jesuits.** One day, in her late thirties, she saw a picture of Christ badly wounded, threw herself down before it in tears, and begged for strength (E3; Documented, her words; no year). As her prayer changed, two advisers in Ávila told her she was deluded by an evil spirit, and sent her to the priests of the Society of Jesus, new to the city (E4; Documented, her words). They judged her prayer sound; one of Ignatius's leading men, Francis Borja, told her not to resist it (E4; Widely Accepted, Lewis's preface; Borja by role). Any vision or voice stays in her voice, or out.
3. **Harsh times, and St Joseph's, 1562.** In 1559 the Inquisitor General banned many devotional books in Spanish, her editor notes, and she grieved for them, she wrote (E5; one clause; the "living book" voice is left out). Then Quote 2 (E6, 1561). A friend's idea of a small house of nuns "like those of the barefoot orders" took hold; a barefoot woman pilgrim taught her that the order's rule, before popes relaxed it, required the nuns to own nothing, so the house would live on alms (E7, E8; Documented; one clause links the-carmelites). On 24 August 1562 the first nuns took the habit at St Joseph's (E10). Two or three days later the city's governor and councillors resolved that it must be broken up; only one Dominican, who disliked its poverty, argued for delay. He was Domingo Báñez, who later became her confessor (E12; Documented, her words and his margin note). She had been ordered back to the old convent. The house stayed, with no more than thirteen nuns, "alone with Him alone" in her phrase (E11; narrator may give the plain sense, "to be alone with God").
4. **The Life, for her confessors.** Two Dominican confessors ordered her to write the story of her life and prayer, which she did between 1562 and about 1565 (E13, D4; say "over the next three years"). She asked that it be burned at once if it did not agree with the Catholic faith (E14; short phrase, rendered from VIDA 9391: "burn it at once"). Keep to one or two sentences: the Inquisition's part comes at beat 5, in time order.
5. **More houses, and the book denounced.** From 1567 she founded house after house across Castile and Andalusia, and in 1568, with a young friar who became the reform's poet, the first house of barefoot friars (E16, E17; John of the Cross by role). By her death there were seventeen houses of nuns, her editors count (E16, D9). A princess whose convent at Pastrana she had closed in 1574, in a quarrel over the princess's place in it, denounced the Life to the Inquisition, a friar close to Teresa wrote, out of anger (E19, D5; name her: "the Princess of Eboli"). Báñez took the manuscript to the Inquisitors himself (E20, D5). In July 1575 he reported that he found nothing in it against the faith, and that she was "not a deceiver"; but it should be shown only to learned men (E21, D6, D7; Documented, his censure, English only). She never saw the book again (E22). The same year, she wrote, the order's leaders sent her a decree to found no more houses and to stay in one (E18, D10; credit "she wrote").
6. **The Interior Castle, 1577.** At Toledo, ordered by her superiors to write on prayer, she began on Trinity Sunday 1577 (E24; Documented). That year friars of her reform were jailed and nuns who voted for her were cut off from the sacraments, her editor writes (E18, E24; one clause, credited: "the reform seemed close to ruin"). Then Quote 3 (E25). In the innermost room God and the soul meet, and the gate into the castle is prayer, she wrote (E25; paraphrase). The book moves through seven such dwelling places (E25).

**What came of her** (about a third of the story; heaviest first)

1. **The houses became an order.** In 1580 the pope let her houses of nuns and friars form a province of their own (E28; Documented, editor). In 1593 they became a separate order (E28). The Discalced Carmelites are still a worldwide order of friars and nuns (I1; the tree's home entry; one clause, never the closing line). Defended: the old rule, poverty and enclosure (E8, E11).
2. **Her books reached print from copies, after her death.** In 1588 an Augustinian professor at Salamanca printed her works; he never saw the autograph of the Life, which the Inquisition still kept (I2; Documented, editors). Her own Spanish of the Life was not printed in full until much later (VIDA, from the Escorial autograph: card). Optional, cut first: his edition dropped the lines in which she named the Jesuits and Ignatius as founder of their Society (E26, I2; Silverio's comparison; this is the connection's Ignatius clause, see Connection).
3. **A saint, read every year.** The pope declared her a saint in 1622 (I3). The church's book of daily prayer read her life aloud on her feast, 15 October, crediting her with thirty-two houses (I3, D9; give the number credited, or leave it to the card).
4. **Read by those who came after.** A century later the French thinker Blaise Pascal wrote that while she lived she was simply a nun, not yet a saint crowned in glory (I4; paraphrase, credited). Alphonsus Liguori called her his patroness and quoted her: humility is truth (I4; paraphrase).

**Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from Teresa was a way of religious life and a way of prayer. The listener must be told three things. First, what the houses were and how the life passed on: small, poor, enclosed houses of no more than thirteen, under the order's old rule (E8, E11), founded one after another and run by her own constitutions (FOUND, Rule and Constitutions appended; card), until in 1580 they became a province of their own and then a separate order, which still exists (E28, I1); so what she started in one house in 1562 was copied in every house after it. Second, how her teaching passed: she wrote for her confessors and her nuns, not for the public (E13, E24); the Life went into the Inquisition's keeping and was printed only after her death, from copies (E22, I2), and readers after her took up her words (I4). Third, the two links on the tree, carried through acts: the order she reformed is the Carmelites, and she took its rule back to before popes relaxed it (L2); the priests who steadied her at the turn were of the Society Ignatius of Loyola founded, and she later named him in the Castle among the founders through whom God won souls (L1, E26). Tell it through these acts; no "this mattered because" sentence.

**Ending:** close on the person. (a) Recommended: her death, after What came: worn out, she reached Alba de Tormes in September 1582 and died there in early October, on the very night the calendar changed, which is why her feast falls on 15 October; as she was dying she thanked God that she was a child of the Church, those with her said (E29, D12; her last words are her editors' account, Widely Accepted: credit "those with her said"). End on those words, in paraphrase. (b) Not chosen: the castle's innermost room, seen again (E25, paraphrase), if (a) runs long.

**Who tells it**

- **Teresa herself**, for nearly all of it: the convent, the twenty years, the turn, the advisers' verdict, the warning about the Inquisitors, the house and the city council, the order to write, the decree of 1575, the Castle. Most of her life is known from her own books, written at her superiors' order; say so once, inside the beat 4 sentence about the Life ("the book in which most of what we know of her early life is told").
- **Domingo Báñez**, Dominican theologian and her confessor, in her own time: his margin note on the city meeting (beat 3); his handing over of the book and his censure (beat 5). Friendly to her but severe; say "her confessor".
- **Jerónimo Gracián**, a friar of her reform, in her own time: that the princess denounced the book out of anger (beat 5). Credit "a friar close to her wrote".
- **Her editors** (Zimmerman, Lewis, Silverio, about 1900): the dates of entry, the number of nuns and houses, the 1559 ban, the 1577 troubles, the 1588 printing. Credit "her editors" once in the story (beat 5, the count); elsewhere let her own lines carry it.
- **The Breviary** (after 1622): the feast and its thirty-two houses (What came 3). Credit "the church's book of daily prayer".
- **Pascal** (about 1660) and **Liguori** (1730s–1750): later readers (What came 4).
- **The doubt that is itself the story:** how the Life reached the Inquisition: an enemy denounced it, and a friend handed it over to save it (beat 5, D5). Tell both, credited; no verdict on the princess beyond Gracián's word.

**Do not say**

- Any word in "Words to explain" used without its plain sense the first time it is heard.
- Not "after twenty years in the convent" or "twenty years later she left". Say "more than twenty years in a large convent"; her "nearly twenty years" is her struggle in prayer (D2).
- Not "in 1533" or "in 1535" as fact for her entry. Say "about 1535, when she was about twenty" (D1).
- Not "a convent of 180 nuns" as fact. Say "well over a hundred and fifty nuns", or "a hundred and eighty, she later wrote" (D3).
- Not "a lax", "corrupt" or "worldly convent" in the narrator's voice. Her words: large, pleasant, poor; the nuns went out; the rule was kept as relaxed (S3).
- Not "she ran away from home to be a martyr": the Breviary's story, not told here.

- Not "the Jesuits saved her" or "Ignatius guided her". Ignatius died in 1556 and never met her; his Society's priests guided her (E4, L1).
- Not "the Inquisition persecuted Teresa" or "she was tried by the Inquisition". Her book was denounced and examined; whether she herself was ever denounced the sources differ (D8, card).
- Not "the Inquisition banned her book" or "condemned it". It was examined, found sound by its examiners, and kept (E21, E22).
- Not "the Princess denounced Teresa as a heretic". She denounced the book (E19).

- Not "the book was locked away and shown to no one" (the home entry's wording). Báñez wrote it should be shown only to learned men (D7).
- Not "on 6 July" or "on 7 July 1575". Say "in July 1575" (D6).
