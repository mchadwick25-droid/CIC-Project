"It seems that God does not exist. For if one of two opposites were without limit, the other would be wholly destroyed. But by the name God we mean a good without limit. So if God existed, no evil would be found. But evil is found in the world. So God does not exist."

So begins one article in the Summa of Theology of Thomas Aquinas, a Dominican friar, who went on to answer it. He answered that argument in Augustine's words. A God supremely good, Augustine wrote, would let evil into his works only if he were so powerful and good that he could bring good even out of it.

Thomas taught theology at Paris in the thirteenth century. The Dominicans, the Order of Preachers, were poor, travelling friars trained in theology to preach. His Summa of Theology is a summary of the whole faith, written as questions. Every question, or article, states the case against before it answers. Inside it he taught that faith and reason cannot truly conflict. The church declared him a saint in 1323.

He was born around 1225 to the counts of Aquino, between Rome and Naples. He studied at Naples, and there he joined the Dominicans.

His family fought it. On the road toward Paris, his kin seized him and shut him in a castle, and the witnesses differ on who and for how long. The order's own book of its friars, written in his lifetime, has a story the editors take to be his. His kinsmen held him about a year, it says, and tried every means to make him leave, but could not make him put off the habit, the friars' robe. William of Tocco, a friar who pressed for his canonization, wrote decades later that his brothers did it at his mother's order. They tore his habit, he says, and sent a woman to tempt him, whom Thomas drove out with a burning stick. A royal official who had known him at Naples told the inquiry of 1319 it was his father, for more than a year. In the end, they let him go.

The order sent him to study under Albert the Great at Cologne, and he became a master of theology at Paris, a full teacher in the university. He wrote the Summa, he said, to teach beginners in good order. His rule for answering those who argued against the faith was this: "Faith rests on a truth that cannot fail. And the opposite of a truth can never be proved. So it is clear that the proofs brought against the faith are not true proofs. They are arguments that can be answered." In the same book, he wrote that heretics who would not turn back could be left to the rulers to be put to death, since corrupting the faith was worse than forging money.

The royal official, under oath, told how his writing ended. Around the feast of Saint Nicholas in December 1273, after Mass at Naples, Thomas wrote and dictated nothing more. His companion Reginald begged him to go on. "Reginald, I cannot. For everything I have written seems to me straw." To Reginald alone he added that it was straw beside what had been shown to him. What he saw, no source says. Reginald told this on his deathbed to another friar, who told the official. Tocco tells it differently, at Thomas's sister's castle, and has him say that what he had written seemed small.

The pope called him to a council of the whole church, at Lyon, where Bonaventure, the Franciscan leader, was also bound. Thomas fell ill on the way, and the official says he asked to be carried to the monastery of Fossanova. He died there on 7 March 1274.

Within forty years, his own order bound its teachers to teach by his teaching and works. Tocco reports that Pope John XXII said Thomas had lit up the church more than all the other teachers, and would gladly declare him a saint, provided some miracles could be found. A pope later ordered his feast kept like those of the four great Doctors, the church's chief teachers. And the English reformer William Tyndale complained that preachers quarrelled, "one followeth Duns, another St Thomas." Duns Scotus was a Franciscan teacher.

The Summa he left is unfinished. Its third part still breaks off in its questions on penance, the sacrament of confession and forgiveness, where he stopped. Others finished it from his earlier books.
