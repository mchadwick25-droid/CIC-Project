# Outline: Leo of Rome

Sheet items (S, E, I, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. LEO is Feltoe's English of Leo's Letters and Sermons, with his Introduction; NC the councils in English (Percival); M5 and M6 Mansi's Latin (damaged scans); PROS Prosper's chronicle in Mommsen's Latin; CM2 Hydatius and other Latin chronicles; JOR Jordanes in English; LP the Book of the Popes in English; ZAC the Syriac chronicle known as Zachariah's; NES Nestorius's *Bazaar*.

- **The essence:** Leo, bishop of Rome from 440 to 461, wrote the letter called the Tome in 449 to say how Christ is one person, fully God and fully human; two years later the Council of Chalcedon took it as its rule of faith (E7, E17, E18, I1; Documented). He held that Peter still spoke and ruled through the bishop of Rome, and he would not accept the council's canon that ranked Constantinople next to Rome (E3, E21, E22; Documented, his own words).
- **Also told:**
  - **The robbers' meeting.** In 449 a council at Ephesus, led by Dioscorus of Alexandria, kept his letter from being read, restored Eutyches and deposed Flavian; Leo begged the emperor for a council in Italy and later wrote that the meeting was "not a trial but a robbery" (E10–E12; Documented as Leo's and Feltoe's accounts; the acts of 449 are not told here, D9).
  - **His letter at Chalcedon.** His envoys presided; some bishops doubted the letter, and it was adopted only at the fourth session (E16–E18; Documented).
  - **The canon.** The council ranked Constantinople next to Rome because both were imperial cities; Leo answered that worldly rank is one thing and the church's another, and declared the canon void by Peter's authority. It stood all the same (E20–E22a, I3; Documented).
  - **Attila, 452.** He went with two Roman officials to meet Attila, and Attila left Italy. Why is told three ways: Prosper credits God and the bishop's presence, a Gothic historian a century later credits the Huns' own fear, and a chronicler in Spain names famine, disease and the eastern emperor's army, and no Leo (E23–E23c, D1; Documented as each one's account; the cause Contested).
  - **What came of him.** The Tome's faith held in the Greek and Latin churches and divided them from Egypt and Syria; each side read him against the other; Rome kept refusing the canon for centuries, then accepted Constantinople's second place (I1–I3; Documented as each one's view).
- **Length:** about 4 minutes 5 seconds (about 615 words at 150 a minute; never past about 675). Counted first: the three quotes, about 65 words (Q1 14, Q2 22, Q3 29) and about 25 more to set them up; the plain sense of the words to explain, about 75 words (the Tome, nature and person, envoys/legates, canon, the rock, Manichaeans, see); the witnesses credited, about 90 words (the minutes once, Prosper with his tie to Leo, the Gothic historian citing Priscus, the chronicler in Spain, Feltoe once, the editors of the canons once, Nestorius and the Syriac chronicle). That is about 255 words before any event is told. Why past four minutes: the job names four turns (the Tome, the robbers, Attila, the canon), and the Attila meeting must carry three witnesses who disagree. Cut first, in order, to come down to about 570: the Syriac chronicle's charge (What came 2, keep Nestorius); the Manichaeans down to one clause (beat 2); Flavian's death (beat 4); the deacon's escape down to "his deacon escaped" (beat 4); Cassiodorus is never told.
- **Setting:**
  - Time: born probably between 390 and 400 (S1, editor; say "born around 390 to 400" only if needed, never a year); bishop from 29 September 440 (E1a); Tome June 449 (E7); Ephesus August 449; Chalcedon October 451; letters on the canon 22 May 452 (E21, E22); Attila 452 (E23); died late in 461 (E25).
  - Place: Rome, his home city by Prosper's word (S2a); Gaul, where he was on a mission when elected (E1); Ephesus and Chalcedon, both in today's Turkey, Chalcedon "on the Bosporus" across from Constantinople (S7; he went to neither, E9); northern Italy for Attila (E23, place disputed, D2).
  - Tradition: the tree's spoken name is "Church and Empire" (S5): the years when bishops sat beside the emperor's throne and argued whether Rome, from Peter, or Constantinople, from the throne, held the final word. Name it once.
  - Language: he wrote in Latin and, his editor infers, knew no Greek; the bishops at Chalcedon spoke Greek (S3, S6).

**Words to explain** (the first time each is heard; plain word or short gloss, worked into the sentence)

- **the Tome**: "his letter to Flavian, bishop of Constantinople, known as the Tome, the book" (E7). Say once that it was a letter.
- **nature / person**: "a nature is what Christ is, as God and as man; a person is who he is" (the `council-of-chalcedon-451` story's own gloss). Here: Eutyches said that after God became man there was one nature only; Leo answered that each nature stays wholly itself in one person.
- **Manichaeans** (E4): "followers of a sect that taught that good and evil were two rival powers" (E4c, editor's gloss). Never bare.
- **legates**: "his envoys". Use "envoys".
- **canon**: "a church rule", "the council's rule". Never "canon 28" bare.
- **the rock** (Q3): the narrator gives the plain sense: "the rock on which, in the Gospel, Christ said he would build his church". Do not say "the rock is Peter" flatly (see Do not say).
- **apostolic see** (E21): "a church founded by an apostle"; Leo said Constantinople was a royal city but could not be made one. Use the plain words, not the term.
- **see** (if used): "the bishop's church and office". Better: "the church of Rome".
- **Huns**: no gloss needed; "Attila, king of the Huns".
- **synod / council**: "a meeting of bishops".
- **excommunicated** (E13), if used: "cut off from communion".

- **Shape:** Founder or leader. What he built: the claim that the bishop of Rome speaks for Peter to the whole church, carried by letters, envoys and emperors' courts, and a statement of faith that a council of Greek bishops took as its rule. How it ran: he never went east; he wrote, sent envoys, and pressed emperors and empresses. Who it served: the churches that accepted Chalcedon. The impact is in what outlasted him: the Tome, and Rome's refusal of Constantinople's rank. The trap is titles and offices in place of acts: show him at work through the letter read at Chalcedon (Q1), the letter naming Ephesus a robbery (Q2), and the letter answering the canon (Q3).
- **Sibling:**
  - `council-of-chalcedon-451` (on the kit; live text read). Its job: the council itself, its definition, its trial of Dioscorus, its canons, and Egypt's refusal. It already tells, in paraphrase, that the Tome taught "the Word works miracles, while the flesh suffers injuries", that Leo called Ephesus "a council of robbers", that the legates threatened to leave, and that Leo declared the canon void. Here: the definition is one clause ("the council's own statement of faith followed his letter"; do not quote it, it is barred); the Tome is told through another line (Q1) and the Lazarus pair in paraphrase, never the miracles-and-injuries line; the canon is told from Leo's side in his own words (Q3), and its later fate.
  - `dioscorus-of-alexandria` (live, read). It quotes Leo's "not of judges but of robbers" (Feltoe's English) as an opponent's verdict on Dioscorus's council. Here, under the README's lift, the same line is rendered from Leo's Latin (Q2) and does a new job: Leo's own verdict in his own story, the hinge between the defeat at Ephesus and the reversal at Chalcedon, and the reason he asked for a new council.
  - `eutyches`, `theodoret-of-cyrrhus`, `hilary-of-arles` (live now, rebuilt in later batches; read). None of their quotes is used. Eutyches is named here as the man the Tome answered; his trial is told there. Theodoret appears only inside the Syriac chronicle's charge (cut first). Hilary of Arles goes to the card. `prosper-of-aquitaine` (live) already tells Gennadius's report that Prosper dictated Leo's letters: here one clause at most, as the reason Prosper is close to Leo.
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** Chalcedon, October 451 (E17, E7a/b). In about three sentences: at a council of bishops who spoke Greek, across the water from Constantinople, a letter from the bishop of Rome was read aloud; its writer had not come. Quote 1. When it ended, the minutes say, the bishops cried out that Peter had spoken through Leo (paraphrase, credited to the minutes; not in quotation marks: three quotes are already spent). Then straight into the essence.
  2. Not chosen: Rome waiting forty days for its new bishop, away in Gaul (E1). True and quiet, but it does not lead to the essence; it is beat 1.

**Story beats** (the opening, the essence and beat 1 make the first minute, about 150 words. The opening reaches ahead to the reading of 451; the reading itself is not told again at beat 5, which tells only the doubts and the adoption)

1. **Elected while away, 440.** He was a deacon of Rome, sent to Gaul to make peace between two Roman generals, when the bishop of Rome died. The city waited more than forty days for him, Prosper says, and made him bishop when he came home (E1; Documented, Prosper; one clause may say Prosper wrote in Leo's own years). No number in the succession (D7). Keep short: about 40 words.
2. **Peter's heir.** On the anniversary of his consecration he told his people that Peter himself was honoured in his person, and that Peter's dignity was not lessened "even in so unworthy an heir" (E3; paraphrase, his own sermon; no quotation marks). Reason for the act: it is how he understood his office, which his editor says he claimed over the whole church from his first letters (E3b; credit "his editor" only if the claim is told). The force, in one or two sentences: in Rome he had the Manichaeans, a sect that taught good and evil were two rival powers, hunted out and exposed before the church, and their books burned, Prosper writes with approval; by the emperor's order they were put out of the army and the city (E4, E4b; Documented; say "in his first years", not a single year, D5).
3. **Eutyches and the Tome, 448–449.** At Constantinople an old monk, Eutyches, head of a monastery, taught that after God became man there was one nature only, and a synod under Flavian, bishop of Constantinople, condemned him (E6; one clause; the trial is the `eutyches` story's). In June 449 Leo wrote to Flavian the letter known as the Tome (E7). What it says, in plain words: Christ is one person, and each nature stays wholly itself; Quote 1 (already heard in the opening, so not repeated) set the human beside the divine. The narrator gives one more of its pairs in paraphrase: weeping for a dead friend is not the act of the same nature as calling him back to life (E7c; paraphrase; the Lazarus story need not be named). Optional, one clause: a writer some forty years later thought Prosper had dictated Leo's letters against Eutyches (E7e; Contested; cut if `prosper-of-aquitaine` is not linked here).
4. **Ephesus, 449: the robbery.** The emperor called a council at Ephesus, with Dioscorus of Alexandria presiding by his order; Leo did not go, but sent envoys and his letter (E8, E9, E10). By Feltoe's account, with soldiers and monks present the meeting grew riotous; the letter was never read; Eutyches was restored, and Flavian deposed (E10; credit Feltoe once, as "his editor" or "a modern editor"; one clause, cut first: Flavian died soon after from rough treatment, "the most accurate version", Feltoe says, E10d). Leo's own letter says his deacon escaped with great difficulty, so as not to be forced to sign (E10b). In October Leo and his bishops begged the emperor "with groans and tears" for a council in Italy (E11; paraphrase; the phrase may be told as narration without quotation marks). Two years later, writing to the empress Pulcheria, he called it what it is still called: Quote 2 (E12; dated 20 July 451; D8). In spring 450 Dioscorus and ten bishops cut Leo off from communion (E13; optional clause).
5. **Chalcedon, 451.** The emperor died in a fall from his horse, and the new rulers, his sister Pulcheria and her husband Marcian, called a council, but in the East, not in Italy as Leo had asked (E14, E15; give the rulers by role unless named for Q2's addressee: "the empress"). His envoys presided (E16). This is the reading the opening told; here only what followed: bishops from Illyria and Palestine said it stressed the two natures too far, and it was adopted only days later, at the fourth session (E18; Documented). Dioscorus was deposed and exiled (E19; one clause; his story tells it). The council's own statement of faith followed Leo's letter (one clause; I1; the definition is the Chalcedon story's).
6. **The canon, 451–452.** Near its end the council passed a rule giving the bishop of Constantinople, "New Rome", the rank next after Rome, because the fathers had honoured old Rome as the imperial city (E20; paraphrase; the canon is the council's text). His envoys protested (E20a). In May 452 he wrote to the emperor Marcian: Quote 3 (E21). Constantinople was a royal city, he said, but it could not be made a church of the apostles (E21; paraphrase). To the empress he wrote that by the authority of the apostle Peter he declared the bishops' vote void (E22; paraphrase). He broke off contact with the bishop of Constantinople until that bishop gave way (E22a; Feltoe). Reason for the act: his own words: the canons of Nicaea could not be changed, and rank in the church rests on the rock, not on the city (E21, E22). The later fate of the canon belongs to What came 3.
7. **Attila, 452.** That summer Attila and the Huns were in northern Italy. Rome's rulers decided to ask for peace through envoys, and Leo went with two high officials, a former consul and a former prefect (E23; Prosper; do not say he went alone, D3). Prosper, who wrote in those years and was close to Leo, says Leo trusted in God's help, and the king was so pleased by the bishop's presence that he stopped the war and went back beyond the Danube (E23; credit Prosper; God's help stays in Prosper's voice). A Gothic historian a century later, Jordanes, citing Priscus (give both by role: "a historian of the Goths, writing a century later, who drew on a writer of Attila's own time"), says the Huns' own men feared what had happened to Alaric, the Gothic king who died soon after sacking Rome, and that Attila still threatened worse unless he was given the emperor's sister (E23a). A chronicler in Spain, writing in the same years, says nothing of Leo: famine and disease, and an army sent by the eastern emperor, drove the Huns home (E23b). Leo himself never tells it (E23c). The doubt is the scene: tell the three accounts side by side and do not settle it.

**What came of him** (about a third of the story; heaviest first)

1. **The Tome as the rule of faith.** Prosper, closing his account of Chalcedon, says the faith confirmed there was the one "preached by the holy pope Leo" (I1; paraphrase). The Greek and Latin churches kept the council and its faith; the churches of Egypt and Syria refused it (the `council-of-chalcedon-451` story and the tradition entry; one clause). Told mostly at beats 3 and 5; here one or two sentences.
2. **Read by each side against the other.** Nestorius, condemned at Ephesus twenty years before, wrote in exile that God had raised up Leo "to preach the truth undaunted" (I2a; paraphrase, credit Nestorius; he means it vindicated his own teaching, so say "Nestorius, whose own teaching had been condemned"). A Syriac chronicle from the churches that refused Chalcedon says Leo wrote the Tome after Theodoret of Cyrrhus won him over with a gift (I2; credit the chronicle; its order of events does not fit the letter's date: if told, say it is the chronicle's charge; cut first).
3. **The canon stood.** Leo's annulling did not hold. The editors of the councils note that bishops of Illyria signed the canon soon after (their reading of a letter of Leo's not held here), and that Rome gave way only centuries later: a council at Rome accepted Constantinople's place next after Rome in 1215, while Latins held Constantinople, and the Council of Florence gave that place to the Greek patriarch (I3; credit "the editors of the councils" once; Documented as their account). Do not give a single year for when Rome "gave way". Defended: the canons of Nicaea, as Leo read them, and Rome's place above every other church.
4. **Attila remembered.** The Book of the Popes, a century later, says simply that he went to meet Attila and "delivered all Italy" (I4; paraphrase). Optional; cut if over length.

Who keeps him today: no held source says it in present terms (Feltoe gives his feasts as of 1895, I5). Leave it out of the story.

**Quotes** (three; each rendered by me from the Latin; each original is held only in a damaged scan, and Feltoe's clean English in LEO confirms each reading, so the card may print the Latin with the English)

Rendered from the Latin, M5 123352–123354 (damaged scan; confirmed by LEO 5415–5416): "To be hungry, to be thirsty, to grow tired and to sleep is plainly human." (14 words.) Not used elsewhere on the tree (searched).
- **Quote 2** (beat 4). Letter 95, to the empress Pulcheria, 20 July 451. Rendered from the Latin, M6 13193–13197 (damaged scan; confirmed by LEO 8637–8640): "Nor need a letter set down everything that could be done at Ephesus, in what was not a trial but a robbery." (22 words.) **Lifted** under Mark's ruling of 2026-11-21 (the README lists Leo's "not of judges but of robbers", used in `dioscorus-of-alexandria`). Rendered from the Latin "non judicio, sed latrocinio", so its English differs from Feltoe's there.
- **Quote 3** (beat 6). Letter 104, to the emperor Marcian, 22 May 452. Job: his answer to the canon, his conviction in his own words: church rank comes from the rock, not from the imperial city. Not used elsewhere on the tree (searched). - **Connection to carry into the story:** What passed on was a sentence and a claim. The sentence: the Tome's account of one person in two natures passed into the Council of Chalcedon's rule of faith (E17, E18, I1), and so into the faith of the Greek and Latin churches, while it divided them from Egypt and Syria. The claim: that the bishop of Rome speaks with Peter's authority over the whole church, which Leo used to reject canon 28 (E21, E22), and which his successors kept using against Constantinople's rank for centuries, until Rome accepted it (I3). What the listener must be told, so as not to infer it: the Tome was a letter to one bishop, written in Latin by a man who knew no Greek, that a council of Greek bishops made its yardstick; and the canon and Leo argued from two different grounds, the canon from the city where the emperor ruled, Leo from the rock of Christ's words to Peter. Carry it through what he did: the letter read aloud, the envoys presiding, the letters to the emperor and empress; never as a "this matters because" sentence.
- **Ending:**
  1. **Chosen.** His death and burial (E25): late in 461, after twenty-one years as bishop, he died, and he was buried in the church of St Peter. One or two sentences; it closes on the claim the story has followed. No comment on it.
  2. Not chosen: Sermon 84's question, who saved the city, "the games of the circus-goers or the care of the saints?" (E24a). Strong, but a fourth quote, and its occasion is uncertain (Feltoe links it to the Vandal sack of 455, which the story does not tell).

## For the card, not the story

- The Vandal king Geiseric, 455: Leo met him outside the gates; Prosper credits his plea, "God acting", for sparing the city fire, slaughter and torture; fourteen days of plunder and many thousands of captives followed (PROS no. 1375). Sermon 84 asks whether the city was saved by "the games of the circus-goers or the care of the saints"; Feltoe links it to 455, with doubt.
- The quarrel with Hilary of Arles, 444; Feltoe's verdict on it stays barred.
- Birthplace: Tuscan by the Book of the Popes, Rome his home by Prosper, uncertain by Feltoe. Prosper counts him the 43rd bishop of Rome, Feltoe the 47th.
- Prosper sets the Manichaean trials under 443, Feltoe under 444; the emperor's ordinance (Letter VIII).
