In October 1414, Sigismund, king of Hungary and the elected head of the empire, wrote a safe-conduct, a royal letter of protection on the road, for a priest from Prague. It ordered everyone to let Jan Hus "pass, stay, remain and return freely." He had already set out for the council at Constance.

Hus was a master of Prague's university and a preacher to the city in Czech. He read the books of John Wyclif, an English scholar dead since 1384 whom the church had condemned, and he would not condemn him. At Constance he refused to swear that he had taught what he said he never taught, and on 6 July 1415 he was burned. His death turned a university quarrel into a nation's movement.

In 1402 he became preacher at the Bethlehem Chapel, built so that preachers in Czech, the people's own tongue, need no longer wander through houses and hidden corners.

In 1410 the archbishop of Prague had Wyclif's books burned and cut Hus off from the church's sacraments. Hus wrote that he hoped Wyclif was among the saved.

In 1412 Prague was put under interdict, a ban that stopped Mass and sermons across the whole city, and Hus left. In the countryside he wrote his book on the Church, which says that Christ alone is its head. Much of it, his translator writes, is taken from Wyclif's own book.

At Constance, a council of the bishops and teachers of the whole Western church met in 1414. Hus was arrested on 28 November. Most of what followed we know from Peter of Mladoňovice, a Czech in Hus's party and his friend. Ulrich Richental, a citizen of Constance who watched from the other side, wrote that the king wanted to help Hus. But the learned told the king that by no law could a heretic hold a safe-conduct, and he let it be.

At his hearing on 7 June, Hus was charged with wishing his soul were with Wyclif's. He answered: "I do not know where John Wyclif's soul is. I hope that he is saved, and I fear that he may be damned. Yet in hope I would wish my soul to be where John Wyclif's soul is." The council laughed at him. The king told him, Peter writes, that he would burn a stubborn heretic with his own hands. From prison Hus defended the cup, the wine at communion, which only priests had long drunk and the council had now forbidden to lay people.

On 6 July the council condemned him. Urged a last time to abjure, to swear publicly to give up the teachings charged against him, he said he feared to lie before God. He was stripped of his priesthood and crowned with a paper hat painted with devils. The council handed him to the king's officers, who had him burned. At the stake he was asked once more to recant. He answered: "God is my witness that I never taught or preached the things falsely charged to me, laid on me by false witnesses. The chief aim of my preaching, and of all my other acts and writings, was only to turn people from sin. I wrote, taught and preached the truth of the gospel from the words and teachings of the holy teachers. In that truth I will gladly die today." He sang a prayer, Peter writes; Richental writes that he cried out badly. The executioners broke up his bones and roasted his heart on a stick, Peter writes, and his ashes were thrown into the Rhine.

Within two months, on 2 September 1415, the lords and knights of Bohemia and Moravia set 452 seals on a letter to the council: "Master John Hus was truly a good, just and catholic man. For many years he lived among us in our kingdom in a way worthy of praise, proven in his life, his conduct and his good name." By catholic they meant true to the church's faith. They vowed to defend the law of Christ and its preachers, whatever it cost them. From his death grew the Hussite and Bohemian Brethren movement, and the cup became its emblem.

The king's letter had promised that Hus could "return freely." In 1520, Martin Luther wrote that Hus was burned against a sworn safe-conduct, and that he had no wish to make a saint or a martyr of him.
