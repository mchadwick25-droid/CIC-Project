# Outline: Gregory Palamas

Sheet items (S, E, I, L, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. PHIL is Philotheos's speech in praise and the 1857 editor's prolegomena (Greek); PG150 and PG151 Migne's Greek and Latin; PHK2 the Philokalia vol. 2 (Greek); KAN1-3 Kantakouzenos's Histories; GRE2-3 Gregoras's history; MM the patriarchal register. Nothing here is held in English: every rendering is mine, from the Greek.

- **The essence:** Gregory Palamas, a monk of Mount Athos, defended the hesychasts, the monks of stillness, when the scholar Barlaam mocked their silent prayer and the light they said they saw (E2, E7; Documented as Kantakouzenos's account and Palamas's own words). He taught that God cannot be shared in his essence, yet gives himself truly in his uncreated energy, so that one who is joined to God is joined to God himself (E8, E9; Documented, his own words; that this is true, the councils' verdict, contested by his opponents, D2).
- **Also told:**
  - **Imprisoned.** During the civil war he was held for years, at the end in a prison inside the palace (E14-E15; Documented). Why is told three ways: the patriarch says for defying the synod and hiding his writings; Kantakouzenos, for the patriarch's envy and his own refusal to condemn Kantakouzenos; Gregoras, for his teaching (D1; Contested).
  - **Upheld by councils.** At Constantinople in 1341 the synod found the monks above Barlaam's charges (E11-E12); in 1347 a synod deposed the patriarch and declared Palamas in line with the theologians (E16); in 1351, with the emperor present, it ruled for him against Gregoras and others (E17). Documented, the winning side's records.
  - **The charge of many gods, credited to Gregoras.** Gregoras, the historian, who was condemned in 1351, wrote that Palamas taught many gods and no god (E19; Documented as Gregoras's charge; the charge itself Contested). Barlaam had first charged "two gods" in 1341 (E11).
  - **What came of him.** His name is acclaimed every year in the Synodikon of Orthodoxy, and a Sunday of Lent keeps his memory (I2; the 1857 editor's account); the Philokalia carried his writings on prayer into Slavonic and Russian (I3; Documented).

- **Setting:**
  - Time: the fourteenth century. Barlaam's attack before the council of 1341 (the Bonn editor's margin at KAN1 32713 reads 1341; the census sources date the Triads 1338-41, S6: neither is a witness's date, so say "before 1341"); prison during the civil war, ordered in the letter of November 1344 (E14) and ended in 1347 (E15: the year is the Bonn editor's margin at KAN2 34246, and the release came just before the council of 1347 that the job dates); councils 1341 (MM 9665, July), 1347, 1351. No year of birth or death in any held early witness (S5): say "he died at sixty-three, Philotheos says", never a year.
  - Place: Constantinople (S1); Mount Athos, "a peninsula of monasteries in northern Greece" (census region, S6); the Lavra of Athanasios on Athos (S3); Thessalonica, the great city of northern Greece, where Barlaam attacked the monks and where Palamas was later archbishop (E2, S4); the Blachernae palace in Constantinople for 1351 (E17).
  - Tradition: the census spoken name is "The Hesychasts and Gregory Palamas" (S6). It carries his own name, so the story says "the hesychasts" or "the monks of stillness" and need not say the tradition's name.
  - Language: all wrote in Greek.

**Words to explain** (the first time each is heard; plain word or short gloss, worked into the sentence)

- **hesychasts / hesychia**: "the hesychasts, from the Greek word for stillness: monks who prayed in silence". Do not say the Greek word more than once.
- **Mount Athos**: "Mount Athos, a peninsula of monasteries in northern Greece".
- **Lavra**: "the Lavra, the great monastery founded by Athanasios the Athonite" (S3, L1). Or "the great monastery of Athanasios".
- **navel-souls** (Q1, E2): Barlaam's sneer that the monks thought the soul sat in the navel. The narrator gives this sense once, as Barlaam's mockery, before Q1, so that Q1's question makes sense.
- **essence**: "what God is in himself".
- **energy / energies**: "his working: the way he acts and gives himself". Palamas's word in Q2 is singular ("his energy"); the narrator may say "energies" for the teaching, as the job and the census do.
- **uncreated**: "not made, not a creature: God's own" (E8).
- **shared / "cannot be shared"** (Q2): the plain sense of ἀμέθεκτος, "that no one can share in or take part in". Not "unknowable" inside Q2 (Do not say).
- **patriarch**: "the patriarch, the bishop of Constantinople".
- **synod / council**: "council" is enough; "a council of bishops at Constantinople" at first mention.
- **Synodikon of Orthodoxy** (What came 1): "a list of praises and condemnations read aloud in church on the first Sunday of Lent". Or leave the title out: "a list read aloud in churches on the first Sunday of Lent".
- **Messalians** (E2): do not use; leave out (it needs its own history).

- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker. The question he tried to answer: if God is beyond all knowing, how can a monk at prayer truly meet him? What he taught: God cannot be shared in what he is, his essence, but gives himself in his uncreated energy, so the monk meets God himself. Who learned it: the councils that made it the church's teaching, and the readers of the Philokalia. The trap is a list of books; show the idea through the navel charge and his answer (Q1) and through one passage of his chapters (Q2).

Links read: `athanasios-the-athonite` (the Lavra's founding: not retold here, one clause), `symeon-the-new-theologian` (his own light and his exile: not retold here; card link only).
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** Kantakouzenos's scene (E2), in about three sentences: at Thessalonica a learned monk from Calabria, Barlaam, asked a simple monk to teach him the way of stillness; he heard that those who pray in quiet feel an unspeakable joy and see a light shining around them with their bodily eyes; then, the emperor John Kantakouzenos wrote, he turned on them all, calling them deceivers and "navel-souls". Then straight into the essence: "One of those monks, Gregory Palamas, answered him." Credit Kantakouzenos in the opening and say once, where he is first heard, that he was Palamas's ally.
  2. Not chosen: Barlaam's own words, "You thunderstruck, foul man…" (E3). Strong, but it is held only as quoted in his opponents' records, and the story already has its three quotes. For the card.
  3. Not chosen: Kantakouzenos thanking him on his release (E15). The 2026-11 Who profile opened there.

**Story beats** (the opening, the essence and beat 1 make the first minute, about 150 words; beats 2 to 7 follow the life once, in order. The opening reaches ahead to the mockery; beat 2 tells only what came after it)

1. **Constantinople to Athos.** Born in Constantinople to a family that had come there from the east, Philotheos says; raised at the imperial court, Kantakouzenos says (S1; one clause each, or one of them). Very young, he left property and the emperor's favor and went with his two brothers to Mount Athos (S2; Kantakouzenos; "very young", no age). He lived at the great Lavra of Athanasios, and later in a cell of quiet just outside it, coming in only for the liturgy (S3; Philotheos). Documented. Keep short.
2. **The answer to Barlaam.** Barlaam wrote his mockery down, "so the insult would stand written for ever", Kantakouzenos says (E2; optional). The monks of Thessalonica wrote begging Palamas to come down from the Mountain; he came unwillingly and first tried, through friends and then face to face, to turn Barlaam away from writing against prayer (E4; Philotheos). Then he wrote: nine treatises in three sets of three, the Triads (E6). Quote 1: his answer to the "navel-souls" charge (E7). Documented, his own words.
3. **The teaching.** What was at stake: Barlaam said the light the monks spoke of was a created thing, below even human thought (E3, one clause, credited to Barlaam's words as his opponents quote them; optional). Palamas answered that there are three in God: his essence, his energy, and the three persons. No one can be united with God's essence; those joined to God are joined through his uncreated energy, God's own (E8; paraphrase). Quote 2 (E9): how to read the Fathers who say both. Then one plain sentence: so what the monk at prayer meets is God, while what God is in himself stays beyond all sharing (E8; the narrator's plain sense of the quotes, not a new claim). Documented, his own words.
4. **The council of 1341.** At a council of bishops in Constantinople, with the emperor presiding, Barlaam accused the monks of saying God's essence could be shared, and when they answered that what is shared is the uncreated grace of the Spirit, he charged them with believing in two gods (E11; the synod's record). The council found the monks above his charges; Barlaam asked pardon, and was told he would be cut off from the Church if he did not truly repent (E12). The emperor died soon after (E12; Kantakouzenos). Documented.
5. **War and prison.** Civil war followed. The empress, ruling for her young son, made war on John Kantakouzenos, with the patriarch as her partner (E13; Gregoras). Another monk took up Barlaam's cause (E14, by role). Palamas was condemned and held, and three witnesses give three reasons (E14): the patriarch himself wrote in 1344 that he ran off when summoned and would not show his writings, so he had to be held; Kantakouzenos says the patriarch envied him and punished him for refusing to condemn Kantakouzenos; Gregoras says the empress's letters gave his teaching as the reason, not politics. Tell all three, each credited; the narrator does not choose (D1; Contested).
6. **1347.** In 1347, as the war ended, the empress had Palamas brought out of a prison inside the palace and sent him to Kantakouzenos to treat for peace (E15). A council deposed the patriarch and declared that Palamas spoke and thought in line with the church's theologians (E16). The new patriarch made him archbishop of Thessalonica, though the city's rulers at first would not let him in (S4; optional clause). Documented.
7. **1351.** Kantakouzenos, now emperor, called a council at the Blachernae palace; for four days Palamas debated his opponents, among them the historian Nikephoros Gregoras (E17; Kantakouzenos). It ruled for Palamas and cut his opponents off (E17, the 1351 tome). Gregoras's charge, credited and in his words: Palamas was "in labor with the teachings of many gods, and at the same time of no god" (E19; short phrase). Then Gregoras's own account of the night it ended: torches burning, his side in tears, and the bishops rising in anger to lay hands on them (E18; his voice, "Gregoras says"). The torches and tears may go if time is short; the bishops' anger stays, in one clause at least, so the winning side's force is not left out. Documented as Gregoras's account; the charge Contested.

**What came of him** (about a third of the story, heaviest first; 95 to 110 words)

1. **The church's teaching.** The councils made his defense of the monks' prayer, and his teaching on God's essence and energy, the teaching of the Orthodox Church (I1; the tomes; "standard" is the tree's tradition text). The 1857 editor of his sermons writes that his name is acclaimed every year in the Synodikon, the list read aloud in churches on the first Sunday of Lent, for having brought down Barlaam's teaching and defended God's uncreated energy and the light that shone from Christ on the mountain (I2; credit "an editor of his sermons"). The next Sunday keeps his memory, the editor writes; the feast was made general under Philotheos, as patriarch, in 1368 (I2). Who keeps him today, one clause: that Sunday is still kept in his memory across the Orthodox world (CEN `legacy`).
2. **His prayer writings carried on.** In the eighteenth century the Philokalia, a book of the old teachers of prayer printed at Venice, gathered his writings (I3; Venice from its title page, PHK1 38; the year 1782 is the Library's header note, so say "in the eighteenth century" or "in 1782" only as the Library dates it); Paisius Velichkovsky put his piece on prayer into Slavonic, and the monks of Optina printed it for Russian readers (I3; optional, cut first).
3. **Not settled for everyone.** His opponents' charge did not die with the council: Gregoras kept writing it into his history, from confinement (E19; GRE3 4450-4451 for his guards; optional, one clause).

Defended: the hesychasts' silent prayer, and their claim that the light they saw was God's own, not a created thing (E7-E9; I2).

**Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from Palamas was a way of saying how a person at prayer can truly meet God, and it passed into the church's own teaching. The listener must be told three things. First, why the monks needed it: Barlaam said what they saw and met in prayer was a created thing, or a delusion; Palamas answered that God cannot be shared in what he is, his essence, but truly gives himself in his uncreated energy, so the monk meets God himself (E3, E8, E9). Second, how it passed: three councils at Constantinople ruled for him, and the church has since acclaimed his name every year in Lent and kept a Sunday for him, an editor of his sermons writes (E12, E16, E17, I2); his writings on prayer went on in the Philokalia and into Slavonic (I3). Third, that it was fought: his opponents said the distinction split God into many gods, the councils that upheld him were his own side's, and he was imprisoned while the quarrel was tangled with a civil war (E14, E19, D2). Tell it through these acts, not a "this matters because" sentence.

**Ending:** close on the person. Recommended: his death, in Philotheos's account: dying, he kept whispering one phrase over and over, Quote 3, "the heavenly to the heavenly", showing, Philotheos says, where he was going; he was sixty-three (E21, S5). Alternative: the opening image seen again: the monks who said they saw a light, and the councils that said it was God's (not recommended: it would end on a verdict).

- Palamas's words to Barlaam, as Philotheos quotes them (E5).
- The nine treatises' arrangement in three sets of three (E6); the Hagioritic Tome (E10); the 150 Chapters' "three in God" (E8).
- The three accounts of the imprisonment in full (E14), with Kalekas's Heraclea detail.
- Kantakouzenos's thanks on his release (E15).
- The 1347 tome's thirty signatures (E16); the four days and the opponents' names in 1351 (E17); Gregoras's night account (E18) if the story drops it.
- The charge of many gods both ways (E19-E20): Gregoras; the Cyprus letters; Philotheos turning it on Barlaam's party; Barlaam's earlier "two gods" (E11); Palamas's retort as Gregoras quotes it.
- His age at death and the twelve years as archbishop (S5); the two reckonings of the death year (Inferential-Thin).
- The feast and the Synodikon, credited to the 1857 editor (I2).
- The Philokalia, Paisius, Optina (I3).
- Card-only items still to be rechecked before use: the captivity among the Turks, the Bollandists and Covel, the July/August month, Combefis's note on 1341.
- The link to Symeon the New Theologian (card only, Mark, 2026-11-21): read by the same circle of hesychasts, who named his chapters on prayer first (L2; PG147 32624-32625, Telikoudes), and tied to Palamas by a later Latin editor, Le Quien, as the story on Symeon's page tells. No held line has Palamas or his councils name Symeon.
- From **Cut first**, whatever the story drops: his two brothers on the way to Athos (S2); his family come from the east, Philotheos says (S1); the emperor's death soon after 1341 (E12); the cell of quiet outside the Lavra, coming in only for the liturgy (S3); the nine treatises in three sets of three (E6); the feast made general under Philotheos in 1368 (I2); his tries through friends and face to face to turn Barlaam from writing (E4); the torches and tears of the 1351 night, Gregoras says (E18).
