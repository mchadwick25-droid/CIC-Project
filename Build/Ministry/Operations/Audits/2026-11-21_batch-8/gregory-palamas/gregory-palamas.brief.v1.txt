The emperor John Kantakouzenos, a friend and ally of Gregory Palamas, tells how the quarrel began. A learned monk named Barlaam, from Calabria in the south of Italy, went to a simple monk at Thessalonica, one of the hesychasts, from the Greek word for stillness: monks who prayed in silence. He heard that those who pray in quiet feel a joy beyond words, and see a light shining around them with their bodily eyes. Then, Kantakouzenos writes, Barlaam turned on them all. He called them deceivers, and "navel-souls."

One of those monks, Gregory Palamas, answered him. Palamas lived on Mount Athos, a peninsula of monasteries in northern Greece. He taught that no one can share in what God is, his essence. Yet God gives himself truly in his energy, his working, the way he acts. That energy is uncreated: not made, but God's own. So a monk joined to God in prayer is joined to God himself.

Raised at the imperial court, Kantakouzenos says, Palamas left his property and the emperor's favor while very young, and lived on Athos in the great monastery of Athanasios.

How he came to Thessalonica is told two ways. Philotheos, who later became patriarch, says in a speech of praise that the monks begged him to come down from the Mountain. Kantakouzenos says he was already in the city, ill. Then he wrote his answer. Barlaam's sneer was that the monks thought the soul sat in the navel. Palamas wrote: "Those who call them navel-souls are, to begin with, plainly slandering the men they accuse. For which of them ever says that the soul is in the navel?"

The great teachers of the early church, the Fathers, had said two things about God. Palamas wrote: "So when you hear the Fathers say God's essence cannot be shared, understand the essence that does not go out and is not shown. When they say again it can be shared, understand the going-out that belongs to God by nature, his showing and his energy. Holding to both in this way, you will agree with the Fathers."

Barlaam then charged the monks with believing in two gods, and brought the case to the patriarch, the bishop of Constantinople. At a council of bishops there, with the emperor presiding, Palamas made the defense. The council found the monks above Barlaam's charges. Barlaam asked pardon, and was warned he would be cut off from the Church unless he truly repented. The records of this council and the two that followed are the winning side's.

Then came civil war. The empress, ruling for her young son, fought Kantakouzenos, with the patriarch beside her. Palamas was condemned and held for years, at the end in a prison inside the palace. Three witnesses give three reasons. The patriarch wrote that Palamas defied the council and would not show his writings. Kantakouzenos says the patriarch envied him, and punished him for refusing to condemn Kantakouzenos. The historian Nikephoros Gregoras, who stood against Palamas, says the empress's letters gave his teaching as the reason, not politics.

In 1347, the empress had Palamas brought out as the war ended. A council deposed the patriarch and declared that Palamas spoke and thought in line with the church's theologians.

In 1351 Kantakouzenos, now emperor, called another council, and Palamas debated his opponents, Gregoras among them. It ruled for Palamas. Gregoras wrote that Palamas was "in labor with the teachings of many gods, and at the same time of no god." He says that on the night it ended, the bishops rose in anger to lay hands on his side.

The councils made Palamas's defense of silent prayer the teaching of the Orthodox Church. An editor of his sermons writes that a list read aloud in churches on the first Sunday of Lent acclaims his name every year, and that the next Sunday keeps his memory.

Philotheos says that as Palamas lay dying, he kept whispering one phrase: "The heavenly to the heavenly." Philotheos takes it as a sign of where he was going. He was sixty-three.
