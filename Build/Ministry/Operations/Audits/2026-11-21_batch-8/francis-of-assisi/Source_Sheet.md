Researcher's sheet for batch 8 (2026-11-21). The job (Mark's, 2026-11-21): the merchant's son who gave up his inheritance, went to the lepers and the roads, and founded friars who owned nothing; his Rule, his Testament and the Canticle of Brother Sun, in his own words. Shape: Monk or ascetic. New person entry and card, under `the-franciscan-movement` (spoken name: "the Franciscan Movement"), era 5. ## Setting

- **S1. Born about 1182, at Assisi.** CEL-ed date table: "1 182. Birth of S. Francis." (CEL 682). Editor's date. I Cel.: "almost until the twenty-fifth year of his age he miserably squandered and wasted his time" (CEL 973–975); I Cel. calls Assisi the city "of which he was a native" (CEL 4009–4010). Born: Inferential-Thin as a year (editor), "around 1182"; Assisi Documented (I Cel.).
- **S2. A trading family; he was himself a trader.** I Cel. calls him "a shrewd trader but a most ostentatious spender" (CEL 983–984; Latin "cautus negotiator", CELL 6730); he rode to Foligno "taking with him scarlet cloths to sell" and sold the horse too (CEL 1204–1209; CELL 7027–7029). Bonaventure: "after gaining some knowledge of letters, was appointed unto a profitable business of merchandise" (BON 532–534); he calls him "the son of Peter Bernardone" (BON 2723). II Cel. names the father "Pietro di Bernardone" (CEL 6630). Later, contemporary within about three years (I Cel.); later, about 35 years (BON). Documented as their accounts. "Merchant's son" rests on these; no source names the father's trade in one word as "cloth merchant" except by the cloth he sold (CEL 1075 "bales of cloth to sell").
- **S3. Place.** Assisi, a hill town of central Italy, in the valley of Spoleto (CEL 919, 2063 "valley of Spoleto"). San Damiano, a ruined church outside the walls (CEL 1219–1223); the Portiuncula, a little church of St Mary near the town (CEL 1629–1633). Documented.
- **S4. Tradition.** Census `the-franciscan-movement`, spoken name "the Franciscan Movement", era 5, "1209-1300", region "Italy, then everywhere" (CEN). The story names it at most once.
- **S5. Language.** He wrote and dictated in Latin (OPU), and made the Canticle in his own Italian (ROB-ed 8482–8486, 8536–8546). He sang to God "in French" in a wood (I Cel., CEL 1445; optional colour, Leave out).
- **S6. Death and canonization.** I Cel.: "In the 1226th year ... on Sunday the fourth day of October, in the city of Assisi ... at S. Maria de Portiuncula, where he first planted the Order of Lesser Brethren ... having fulfilled twenty years from the time when he had perfectly cleaved to Christ" (CEL 4006–4016). CEL-ed table: "1226, 3rd–4th October. Death"; "1228, 16th July. His canonization by Pope Gregory IX" (CEL 725–726). Richard of San Germano, a contemporary chronicler: Gregory went from Perugia to Assisi in July and canonized him "for two miracles he had done openly, on a blind man and a lame man" (RSG 3972–3976); another passage of the same chronicle puts the decision in June at Perugia, with the cardinals' counsel (RSG 4644–4648). Death year Documented; day: Celano's Sunday 4 October is the evening of 3 October by our count (editor's "3rd–4th"). Canonization 1228 Documented.

## Events (time order)

**E1. Youth.** I Cel. paints a wealthy, popular young man, "very rich", in "soft and flowing attire" (CEL 980–981). Later, about two years after his death. Documented as Celano's account; the moralizing is Celano's.

**E2. The lepers: his own account.** Testament (OPU 2823–2830, Latin; ROB 5368–5376, English): "Dominus ita dedit mihi fratri Francisco incipere facere poenitentiam, quia, cum essem in peccatis, nimis mihi videbatur amarum videre leprosos; et ipse Dominus conduxit me inter illos, et feci misericordiam cum illis. Et recedente me ab ipsis, id quod videbatur mihi amarum, conversum fuit mihi in dulcedinem animi et corporis. Et postea parum steti et exivi de saeculo." Own words, dictated in 1226 (E14). Documented. Robinson notes some manuscripts read "feci moram cum illis", "I made a stay with them" (ROB-ed 5385–5388). I Cel. quotes the same lines from the Testament (CELL 7373–7377; CEL 1481–1485): a second print of the Latin. The Testament gives no date, place or name.

**E3. The lepers in the Lives.** I Cel.: once he held his nose at the sight of their houses two miles off; "while still in the habit of the world, he met a leper, and, having become stronger than himself, went near and kissed him" (CEL 1490–1500; CELL 7377–7385). Bonaventure tells the meeting on horseback and the kiss, and that the leper then vanished (BON 710–721). Later (I Cel. two years; BON about 35). Documented as their accounts. See D1 for order.

**E4. His father's force.** I Cel.: townsmen pelted him with mud and stones as mad (CEL 1291–1294); his father "rushed on him like a wolf on a sheep", dragged him home, "shut him up for several days in a dark place", and tried words, "then stripes and chains" (CEL 1316–1325; Latin "verbis, deinde verberibus et vinculis agit", CELL 7186–7193). His mother loosed his chains while the father was away (CEL 1349–1356). Later, two years. Documented as Celano's account.

**E5. Before the bishop.** I Cel.: the father brought him before the bishop "so that by a formal renunciation of all his property in the bishop's presence he might give up all he had" (CEL 1398–1402; Latin "coram episcopo civitatis, ut in ipsius manibus omnibus eius renuntians facultatibus, omnia redderet quae habebat", CELL 7276–7279). "Without waiting to be spoken to and without speaking he immediately put off and cast aside all his garments and gave them back to his father ... stripped himself stark naked before all the bystanders." The bishop covered him with his own mantle (CEL 1407–1420; CELL 7283–7293). Bonaventure: the father wished him to "resign his claim unto his father's inheritance" (BON 966–970), and gives him a speech (see D2). Documented as their accounts; "inheritance" is Bonaventure's word; I Cel. says "all his property".

**E6. San Damiano and the Portiuncula; the Gospel.** I Cel.: he repaired San Damiano, then another church, then the Portiuncula, in hermit's dress with staff and shoes (CEL 1536–1545, 1621–1645). At the Portiuncula he heard the Gospel of Christ sending out the disciples: "not to possess gold, silver, or money, not to carry on their way scrip, wallet, bread or staff, not to have shoes, or two tunics", and cried, "This is what I wish, this is what I am seeking" (CEL 1647–1659). He put off his shoes and staff, and was "content with one tunic" with a cord (CEL 1662–1667). Later, two years. Documented as Celano's account.

**E7. Brothers, and Rome.** Testament: "And when the Lord gave me some brothers, no one showed me what I ought to do, but the Most High himself revealed to me that I should live according to the form of the holy Gospel. And I caused it to be written in few words and simply, and the Lord Pope confirmed it for me" (ROB 5461–5466; OPU 2874–2879, "et ego paucis verbis et simpliciter feci scribi; et dominus papa confirmavit mihi"). Own words. Documented. I Cel.: with eleven brothers he went to Rome; the bishop of Sabina "urged him to embrace the life of a monk or of a hermit", and Francis "refused to yield" (CEL 1971–1985, 2006–2016; CELL 8053–8062, "ut ad vitam monasticam seu heremiticam diverteret suadebat"); Innocent III "granted their request" and told them to "preach repentance to all" (CEL 2024–2037). The earlier Rule's own heading: "This is the life that Brother Francis begged might be conceded to him and confirmed by the Lord Pope Innocent. And he has conceded and confirmed it to him and to his brothers present and future" (ROB 3041–3045; OPU 1134–1137). The Quaracchi heading says Innocent confirmed it "sine bulla", without a bull (OPU 1130; ROB 3014–3015); Robinson: "approved viva voce" (ROB-ed 2690–2691). Date: D4. Documented (the approval); the date Contested among editors.

**E8. The Rule as it was first written is lost.** ROB-ed: the first form "has not come down to us"; the twenty-three-chapter Rule (the earlier Rule, often dated 1221) is the same Rule "in the form it had assumed in the course of twelve years, as a consequence of many changes and additions" (ROB-ed 2733–2738); "the primitive Rule of the Friars in the form approved by Innocent III in 1209" is lost (ROB-ed 10038–10041). Editor's judgment. Widely Accepted (editors agree the first text was "very short and simple", ROB-ed 2750–2752).

**E9. How they lived, his own words.** Testament: "those who came to take this life upon themselves gave to the poor all that they might have, and they were content with one tunic, patched within and without ... with a cord and breeches, and we wished for no more" (ROB 5467–5471; OPU 2879–2884). "I worked with my hands and I wish to work ... And when the price of labor is not given to us, let us have recourse to the table of the Lord, begging alms from door to door" (ROB 5476–5512; OPU 2888–2898). The greeting "The Lord give thee peace" (ROB 5515). Own words. Documented. The earlier Rule, ch. 9: the brothers "ought to rejoice when they live among mean and despised persons, among the poor and the weak, the sick and the lepers, and those who beg by the wayside" (OPU 1550–1553, Latin "inter viles et despectas personas, inter pauperes et debiles, infirmos et leprosos et iuxta viam mendicantes"). Own (Rule) words. Documented. Not quoted in the outline: the tradition entry's documentedStories already prints it (see Library sweep).

**E10. A contemporary sees them, 1216.** Jacques de Vitry, bishop of Acre, writing from Genoa in October 1216 (JDV 5856–5860): at the papal court he found much "contrary to my spirit", but one comfort: "many rich and worldly people of both sexes, leaving everything for Christ, were fleeing the world; they were called Friars Minor (fratres minores). They are held in great reverence by the lord pope and the cardinals" (JDV 5994–5999, paraphrase of damaged OCR). "By day they go into the cities and villages ... by night they return to a hermitage or lonely places, giving themselves to contemplation" (JDV 6015–6017). "The women live together in various hostels near the cities; they accept nothing, but live by the work of their hands" (JDV 6017–6019). "They grieve and are troubled because clergy and laity honour them more than they would wish" (JDV 6019–6021; the subject may be the women or both). They meet once a year, then scatter through Lombardy, Tuscany, Apulia and Sicily (JDV 6021–6027). He sets them against prelates who are "like dumb dogs not able to bark" (JDV 6029–6031). Contemporary, outside the order. Documented as his letter. He does not name Francis here.

**E11. The Rule of 1223.** "The Rule and life of the Minor Brothers is this, namely, to observe the holy Gospel of our Lord Jesus Christ, by living in obedience, without property and in chastity. Brother Francis promises obedience and reverence to the Lord Pope Honorius" (ROB 4571–4576; OPU 2421–2425). Ch. 4: "I strictly enjoin on all the brothers that in no wise they receive coins or money" (OPU 2541–2543). Ch. 6, Latin (OPU 2577–2582): "Fratres nihil sibi approprient nec domum nec locum nec aliquam rem. Et tanquam peregrini et advenae in hoc saeculo in paupertate et humilitate Domino famulantes vadant pro eleemosyna confidenter, nec oportet eos verecundari, quia Dominus pro nobis se fecit pauperem in hoc mundo." English ROB 4774–4795. Own words. Documented. Confirmed by Honorius III on 29 November 1223 (ROB-ed 2722–2726; CEL-ed 719–720). Bonagratia's appeal of 1323 quotes the same words (BFV 39278–39282): a second print.

**E12. Clare and the women of San Damiano (by role).** I Cel.: at San Damiano "the glorious Religion and most excellent Order of Poor Ladies and holy virgins took its happy beginning some six years after blessed Francis' conversion", of which "the Lady Clara, a native of the city of Assisi" was the foundation, "turned to God by the admonitions of the holy man" (CEL 1546–1554). Francis's own "form of life" for them, kept in her Rule: "Since by divine inspiration you have made yourselves daughters and handmaids of the most high sovereign King ... I will, and I promise to have always, by myself and my brothers, a diligent care and special solicitude for you, as for them" (ROB 5177–5214; OPU 2786–2792). His "last wish" to them: "I, little brother Francis, wish to follow the life and poverty of Jesus Christ ... And I beseech you all, my ladies ... to live always in this most holy life and poverty" (ROB 5219–5228; OPU 2801–2808). Clare's Rule, with these words, was confirmed by Innocent IV in 1253 (ROB-ed 5137–5138; CLR-ed 9913). Own words, kept by her. Documented.

**E13. The Canticle of Brother Sun.** The rubric of the Assisi manuscript, as Sabatier gives it: "the praises of the creatures which blessed Francis made ... when he was ill at San Damiano" (SPECL 27198–27200; ROB 8587–8590). ROB-ed: composed "toward the close of the year 1225 in a poor little hut near the Monastery of San Damiano, whither St. Francis had retired on account of his infirmities" (ROB-ed 8505–8508); it is the only one of his songs in Italian known to survive (ROB-ed 8482–8486). Italian (SPECL 27169–27175): "Altissimu, onnipotente, bon Signore ... Laudato sie, Misignore, cum tucte le tue creature, spetialmente messor lo frate sole, lo quale iorno et allumini noi per loi." Death strophe (SPECL 27258–27259): "Laudato si, Misignore, per sora nostra morte corporale, da la quale nullu homo vivente poskappare [pò skappare]." English ROB 8592–8611, 8663–8666. Other manuscripts in the same file read the strophe alike (SPECL 27693, 30326, 30413, 30495). Own words. Documented (authentic: ROB-ed 8547–8549). II Cel. sets the "Praises concerning the Creatures" after a promise of the kingdom given him in sickness (CEL 13443–13451). The Mirror of Perfection says he made it after he was "certified" of the kingdom, set it to music, and taught his companions to sing it, wishing friars to go out "as it were minstrels of the Lord" (SPEC 7307–7386). Its date of writing contested. Eye trouble: SPEC 7276–7283 (sixty days unable to bear light); I Cel. (CEL 4672) "for the treatment of the infirmity of his eyes".

**E14. The Testament, 1226.** Dictated shortly before his death: Gregory IX's bull of 1230 shows it "was published a few days only before his death", ROB-ed 5264–5269; probably at Celle near Cortona in summer 1226, "though some think" at the Portiuncula (ROB-ed 5269–5274). Its commands, own words:
- not to accept churches or dwellings unless they fit holy poverty, "always dwelling there as strangers and pilgrims" (ROB 5516–5521; OPU 2906–2910);
- "they should not dare ... to ask any letter in the Roman curia" for church, place, preaching or protection, "but wherever they are not received let them flee to another land" (ROB 5523–5531; OPU 2912–2918);
- brothers who will not say the Office by the Rule, "or who are not Catholics", are to be handed to the custos, who must "guard him strongly day and night as a prisoner" until he is brought "to the Lord of Ostia, who is master, protector, and corrector of this brotherhood" (ROB 5573–5590; OPU 2942–2960, "sicuti hominem in vinculis die noctuque"). The Lord of Ostia was Cardinal Ugolino, later Gregory IX (ROB-ed 5601);
- "let not the brothers say: This is another Rule"; they are to read it with the Rule at every chapter, "not to put glosses on the Rule or on these words ... but as the Lord has given me to speak and to write the Rule and these words simply and purely, so shall you understand them simply and purely" (ROB 5592–5625; OPU 2961–2985). The Assisi codex reads "without gloss" for "purely" (ROB-ed 5638–5639).
Own words. Documented. The force (prisoners) is part of the record and is not to be left out (outline, Do not say).

**E15. The marks of the wounds (a wonder, Celano's voice).** I Cel.: at La Verna, "two years before he gave back his soul", he saw "a man like a seraph having six wings ... fixed to a cross", and "marks of nails began to appear in his hands and feet" (CEL 4230–4258). Later, two years. Documented as Celano's account; the wonder stays in his voice. Optional; the census `bonaventure` story already names the seraph vision.

**E16. Last months.** I Cel.: he "wished once more to return to the service of lepers, and to be held in contempt as he had once been", saying "Let us begin, brethren, to serve the Lord God, for hitherto we have profited little or nothing" (CEL 4609–4626). Bonaventure tells the same (BON 6789–6796). Later. Documented as their accounts.

**E17. Death.** The Mirror of Perfection: told by a physician he would die, he said "Welcome, my Sister Death!" (SPEC 8975–8979; Latin "Bene veniat soror mea mors", SPECL 27500–27501), and had Angelo and Leo sing the Canticle, adding "before the last verse" the verses "concerning Sister Death" (SPEC 9040–9058; SPECL 27685–27693). Contested date; companions' circle. II Cel. also: "Welcome, my sister Death" (CEL 13584–13586) and "lay me naked on the ground" (CEL 13590). I Cel.: carried from the bishop's palace to the Portiuncula, he had two brothers sing praises "concerning death which was near, or rather life", and himself "broke into that Psalm of David, 'I cried unto the Lord with my voice'" (CEL 4797–4837). Died October 1226 at the Portiuncula (S6). Documented as their accounts.

## Impact (heaviest first: what later witnesses carried on and used)

**I1. His Rule and Testament became the order's quarrel.** Four years after his death Gregory IX, who had known him and "assisted" at the Rule's making, interpreted the Rule in *Quo elongati* (28 Sept. 1230), as quoted in Bonagratia's appeal: "they should have no ownership, either in common or individually", only use of what they were allowed (BFV 39287–39297). Ubertino of Casale, a leader of the strict friars, writing early in 1310, reports the pope's judgment "that the brothers are not bound to keep that command, because he could not bind his successor, an equal having no command over an equal" (EHR3 3080–3083; Ehrle identifies the pope as Gregory IX, EHR3 3119–3120); Ubertino adds that he does not dissent, but that Francis meant by it to explain his intention in the Rule (EHR3 3082–3087). In January 1323 the order's procurator, Bonagratia of Bergamo, stood before John XXII at Avignon to appeal, quoting the Rule's first words and ch. 6 ("fratres nihil habeant seu sibi approprient nec domum nec locum nec aliquam rem") as the matter of a question raised "long ago, about the beginning of the institution and approval of the order" (BFV 39258–39282). Later witnesses (Ubertino about 84 years on; Bonagratia about 97). Documented as their texts. The census entries `fraticelli-spiritual-franciscan-rigorists` and `franciscan-order-era-6` tell the quarrel's end; here a clause.

**I2. His life written and rewritten by his brothers.** Thomas of Celano wrote the First Life between July 1228 and February 1229 "by commission of Pope Gregory IX" (CEL-ed 128–130), and a Second Life for the minister general between 1244 and 1247 (CEL-ed 136–138). Bonaventure, minister general, wrote the *Legenda maior* (BON; census `bonaventure`: written 1260–63, meant to replace the earlier Lives). Editors' dates; the Lives Documented. Link `bonaventure`.

**I3. The women kept his words.** Clare's Rule, confirmed in 1253, holds his form of life and his last wish (E12). Documented.

**I4. The order spread.** Jacques de Vitry, in his history of the West, says there is hardly a Christian province where they do not have brothers (JDVH 19384–19386, long-s OCR), and "we saw the first founder of this order ... a simple and unlettered man, beloved by God and men, named Brother Francis", who went fearless into the camp of the sultan of Egypt at Damietta and preached to him for some days (JDVH 19418–19440). Contemporary witness ("vidimus"). Documented as his account. The sultan is for the card (outline).

**I5. The Canticle.** Kept in the manuscripts in his Italian (SPECL 27169–27268, apparatus 30300–32700); his sole surviving song in the common tongue (ROB-ed 8482–8486). The tradition entry's legacy: "quoted constantly to this day" (CEN, unsourced there; not used).

**I6. Who keeps him today.** The census tradition's `livingEvidence`: the Order of Friars Minor publishes its own dated notices, November 2026 (CEN). Optional one clause.

## Links

- **L1. Innocent III** (`innocent-iii`, live): confirmed the first Rule, by the Rule's own heading (E7). The Innocent story tells the heading in paraphrase; this story tells the visit to Rome from Francis's side (I Cel.'s bishop of Sabina) and does not quote the heading.
- **L2. Bonaventure** (`bonaventure`, live): wrote his Life; gives "inheritance" (E5) and the return to the lepers (E16). The Bonaventure story tells the writing and the suppression of earlier Lives; here a clause.
- **L3. Gregory IX** (not on the tree): as Cardinal Ugolino, "the Lord of Ostia" of the Testament (E14); as pope, canonized him (S6), commissioned Celano (I2), and ruled on the Rule and Testament (I1). Acts; by name or role.
- **L4. Clare of Assisi** (not in the census): by role, "a young woman of Assisi" (E12).
- **L5. Thomas of Celano, Jacques de Vitry, Ubertino of Casale, Bonagratia of Bergamo**: witnesses, not on the tree.

## Where the sources differ or come late

- **D1. Lepers before or after he left home.** The Testament: the Lord led him among lepers, "and afterwards I remained a little and I left the world" (E2): lepers first. I Cel. tells the leper service after the bishop scene, with only the kiss before it, "while still in the habit of the world" (CEL 1478–1500). Tell the Testament's order in his voice; credit Celano for the kiss.
- **D2. What he said before the bishop.** I Cel.: he neither waited for words nor spoke ("nec verba exspectat nec facit", CELL 7284). II Cel. gives him words at the giving back of money: "Henceforth I may freely say 'our Father who art in heaven,' not any more 'father Pietro di Bernardone'" (CEL 6627–6633); Bonaventure puts a like speech in the stripping scene (BON 980–985). Later witnesses add the speech. Leave it out, or credit.
- **D3. The speaking crucifix.** II Cel. (1244–47): at San Damiano "the painted image of Christ crucified moved its lips", saying "Francis, go repair My house" (CEL 6544–6557). I Cel. tells the repair with no voice (CEL 1536–1545); the Testament says nothing of it. For the card.
- **D4. The year of approval.** Robinson: 23 April 1209, with the Bollandists, "far more probable" than 1210 (ROB-ed 2690–2701); Howell's table: 1210 (CEL-ed 693–696); Sabatier and Wadding: 1210 (ROB-ed 2697–2699). Census tradition: "By 1209". Say "in 1209 or 1210" or "around 1209".
- **D5. One Rule or two.** Sabatier held the 1223 Rule "the very antithesis" of the first, the work of the Church; Robinson and the Quaracchi editors answer that it is the same Rule, "shorter, more precise" (ROB-ed 2905–2945). Editors' quarrel; for the card.
- **D6. The Canticle's last strophes.** The Mirror of Perfection says the strophe on pardon was added for a feud between the bishop and the magistrates, and the death strophe near death (ROB-ed 8529–8536; SPEC 9040–9058). The Mirror's date and author are contested (SPEC header). Robinson's dating (end of 1225) is the editor's. Credit "a later book of his companions' memories" for the added verse.
- **D7. The Testament's binding force.** Francis: read it with the Rule, no glosses (E14). The pope (Gregory IX, Ehrle): not binding (I1, Ubertino's report). Tell both, credited.
- **D8. Witness reliability.** The census tradition calls the sources "a famous layered-hagiography problem" (CEN). I Cel. was commissioned by Gregory IX; the Mirror is a later, contested compilation; Bonaventure's Life was written to settle the order's quarrel (census `bonaventure`). His own writings are the undisputed layer (CEN tradition sources note).
