# Outline: Francis of Assisi

Sheet items (S, E, I, L, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. OPU is Francis's Latin writings (Quaracchi 1904); ROB Robinson's English of them (1906); SPECL Sabatier's *Speculum perfectionis* with the Italian Canticle; SPEC its English (Evans); CEL Celano's two Lives in English (Howell), CELL their Latin; BON Bonaventure's Life in English (Salter); JDV and JDVH Jacques de Vitry's letter of 1216 and his history of the West; EHR3 Ubertino of Casale (1310); BFV Bonagratia's appeal (1323).

- **The essence:** Francis, a trader's son of Assisi in central Italy, gave up his family's wealth in the first years of the 1200s, went to serve the lepers he had once loathed, and took to the roads to preach (E2–E6; Documented: his own Testament for the lepers, Celano for the rest). The brothers who joined him, the Friars Minor, the "lesser brothers", were to own nothing, work with their hands and beg when no wage came; they lived among the townspeople, not behind a monastery wall (E9–E11; Documented, his own Rule and Testament; a contemporary bishop saw it, E10).
- **Also told:**
  - **The break with his father.** His father chained him at home, then brought him before the bishop to give up all he had; Francis stripped and handed back his clothes. Bonaventure calls it giving up his claim to his father's inheritance (E4–E5; Documented as Celano's and Bonaventure's accounts).
  - **The Rule.** With eleven brothers he went to Rome; a cardinal urged him to become a monk or a hermit, and he refused; Innocent III confirmed his short rule of life, by the Rule's own words, about 1209 (E7; Documented; the year Contested among editors, D4). Its first text is lost (E8; Widely Accepted). The Rule of 1223 forbade the brothers to own a house, a place or anything (E11; Documented, his own words).
  - **The Canticle of Brother Sun.** Ill at San Damiano, near the end, he made a song of praise in his own Italian for the sun, the moon and the elements, and, a later book says, added a verse for death when he knew it was near (E13, E17; the song Documented, his own words; the added verse Contested, the Mirror of Perfection).
  - **The Testament.** In 1226 he dictated his memory of how it began, and his commands: no letters of privilege from Rome, brothers who strayed to be held like prisoners, and his words to be read "simply", without glosses (E14; Documented, his own words).
  - **What came of him.** Four years after his death, the pope ruled that the Testament's command did not bind; for a century his brothers fought over the Rule's poverty, quoting its words to the pope (I1; Documented as each witness's text). His brothers wrote his Life and rewrote it, Bonaventure among them; Clare's sisters kept his words in their Rule (I2–I3; Documented).
- **Length:** about 4 minutes 5 seconds (about 610 words at 150 a minute; never past 675). Counted first: the three quotes, about 160 words (Q1 about 68, Q2 about 52, Q3 about 40); the plain sense of the words to explain, about 45 words (do penance, left the world, Friars Minor, alms, Rule, Testament, gloss, the Portiuncula); the witnesses credited, about 65 words (Celano twice, Bonaventure twice, Jacques de Vitry, the Mirror of Perfection, a strict friar of 1310, the order's lawyer of 1323). That is about 270 words before any event is told, leaving about 340 for the events. Why it fits: the job names the break, the lepers and roads, the friars, and three of his own texts; his own words carry much of it, and the narrator need not retell what they say. Cut first, if needed: Jacques de Vitry's sentence (beat 5) to one clause; the cardinal's advice (beat 4); the Lives and Clare in What came to one clause each; the sun line of Q3 (keep the death line).
- **Setting:**
  - Time: born around 1182 (S1, editor; never a year as fact); he turned from his old life about 1204–06 (CEL-ed, S1; say "in his early twenties" or "about 1205"); Rome about 1209 (D4); Jacques de Vitry's letter, 1216 (E10); Rule confirmed 29 November 1223 (E11); Canticle late 1225, editor's date (E13); Testament 1226 (E14); died October 1226 (S6); canonized 1228 (S6).
  - Place: Assisi, a hill town in central Italy (S1, S3). San Damiano, a ruined church outside the town; the Portiuncula, a little church of St Mary below it, where the order began and he died (S3, S6). Rome (E7).
  - Tradition: the tree's name is "the Franciscan Movement" (S4); name it once at most.
  - Language: he wrote and dictated in Latin, and made the Canticle in Italian (S5).
  - For the person entry: dates "about 1182–1226"; place Assisi, Italy; region Mediterranean (as the tradition); `activeYear` 1209 (the Rule's approval) or 1223; toldWithin `the-franciscan-movement`; `major: true`.

**Words to explain** (the first time each is heard; plain word or short gloss, worked into the sentence)

- **do penance** (Q1): the narrator gives the plain sense, "to turn his life to God". Do not let "penance" stand alone as punishment.
- **left the world** (Q1): "left his old life in the town", the way of life of family, trade and money.
- **Friars Minor**: "the Friars Minor, the 'lesser brothers'"; friar means brother.
- **alms** (Q2): "gifts of food or help, begged from door to door".
- **Rule**: "a written rule of life for the brothers".
- **Testament**: "a last testament, his parting words to the brothers", dictated in 1226.
- **gloss**: "notes that explain a text away"; say "without notes to explain his words away", or "simply, as he wrote them".
- **Canticle**: "a song of praise"; "the Canticle of Brother Sun, a song of praise in his own Italian".
- **the Portiuncula**: "the little church of St Mary below Assisi"; the name may be left out.
- **custos / Lord of Ostia** (E14): do not use; say "their superiors" and "the cardinal who protected the order".

- **Shape:** Monk or ascetic. The turn away from ordinary life: the lepers, the father, the bishop. The life chosen instead: owning nothing, working, begging, preaching on the roads, among people, not behind walls. The others drawn after him: the brothers, the women of San Damiano, the order and its quarrel. The trap is a pile of wonders: none is told except, at most, one clause on the marks of the wounds in Celano's voice (optional, cut first), and the speaking crucifix is left for the card.
- **Sibling:** none in the batch. Links read first (live text): `innocent-iii` (job: the pope; it tells, in paraphrase, that Francis's first Rule says Innocent confirmed it; this story tells the visit from Francis's side and does not quote the heading); `bonaventure` (job: the minister general and his Life of Francis, with the earlier Lives suppressed; here one clause, and Bonaventure's word "inheritance" credited to him). The tradition entry `the-franciscan-movement` is the home (its longDescription tells the founder in summary, Clare, the third order and the quarrel; this story is the deeper telling and does not retell the third order or the Spirituals' end).
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** His own Testament (Q1). In about three sentences: the quote, then "So Francis of Assisi remembered it, in the testament he dictated in 1226, the year he died", then straight into the essence. The listener hears the turn in his own voice before anything else.
  2. Not chosen: the bishop scene (E5), the naked young man and the bishop's cloak. Strong, but it belongs in time order at beat 2.

**Story beats** (the opening, the essence and beat 1 make the first minute, about 150 words; beats 2 to 6 follow the life once, in order. The opening's quote is the lepers; beat 3 does not repeat it)

1. **Assisi.** Born around 1182 into a trading family of Assisi (S1, S2). Celano, a friar who wrote his Life within three years of his death, calls him rich, open-handed and "a shrewd trader"; he rode out to sell scarlet cloth (S2, E1; credit Celano at first mention, with when he wrote). One or two sentences.
2. **His father, and the bishop.** When he began to give money away, townsmen threw mud and stones at him as a madman; his father dragged him home, shut him in a dark room and used blows and chains to bend him (E4; Documented as Celano's account: the force is told plainly). His mother let him go. Then his father brought him before the bishop of Assisi to give up all he had: he took off all his clothes, gave them back to his father, and stood naked, and the bishop wrapped him in his own cloak (E5). Bonaventure, who led the order a generation later and wrote its official Life, says he gave up his claim to his father's inheritance (E5; credit Bonaventure; link). Reason for the act: the sources give his purpose only as giving up all he had for God; do not add a motive (Do not say).
3. **The lepers and the roads.** He went to the lepers and served them, washing their sores, Celano writes; before, the sight of their houses had made him hold his nose (E3; Celano's voice). The Testament had already said, in the opening, that the Lord led him among them (D1: lepers first in his own order). He repaired the ruined church of San Damiano with his own hands (E6). At the Portiuncula he heard the Gospel where Christ sends the disciples out with no money, no bag, no shoes, and cried, "This is what I want" (E6; Celano; paraphrase, not a quote). He put off his shoes and took to the roads to preach (E6).
4. **Brothers, and Rome.** Men came to share the life; in his own words, no one showed him what to do, but God showed him he must live by the Gospel, and he had it written down in a few simple words (E7; his Testament, paraphrase). With eleven brothers he went to Rome. A cardinal urged him to become a monk or a hermit; he refused (E7; Celano; optional, cut first). Pope Innocent III confirmed the rule, the Rule itself says, by word of mouth and not by a written decree, in 1209 or 1210 (E7, D4; link). That first text is lost (E8; one clause, optional).
5. **How they lived.** By 1216 a bishop at the papal court, Jacques de Vitry, wrote that rich and worldly men and women were leaving everything to follow them; by day the brothers went into towns, by night into lonely places to pray, and the women lived by the work of their hands (E10; credit Jacques; do not call the women Clare's). The Testament: content with one patched tunic, they worked, and begged only when no wage was given (E9). In 1223 the next pope confirmed the shorter Rule that still holds the order; Quote 2 (E11).
6. **The Canticle and the Testament.** Ill and nearly blind, at San Damiano, where a young woman of Assisi and her sisters now lived under his care (E12; Clare by role, one clause), he made a song of praise in his own Italian: Quote 2's companion, Quote 3, first line (E13; the sun). In 1226 he dictated the Testament: no brother was to ask Rome for letters of privilege, even to escape persecution; brothers who would not say the prayers by the Rule, or were not Catholics, were to be held day and night like prisoners and brought to the cardinal who protected the order; and the Rule and these words were to be understood "simply", with no glosses (E14; his own words; the prisoners clause is told, not softened). Optional (cut first): Celano writes that two years before his death, after a vision of a seraph, the marks of nails appeared in his hands and feet (E15; Celano's voice only).

**What came of him** (about a fifth of the story; it carries the connection; heaviest first)

1. **The quarrel over his words.** Four years after his death, the pope ruled that the brothers were not bound by the Testament's command, as a leader of the strict friars reported about eighty years later; he was the cardinal the Testament named, now Pope Gregory IX (I1; credit the strict friar; Ehrle's identification of the pope, D7). For a century his brothers fought over the Rule's poverty: in 1323 the order's own lawyer stood before the pope at Avignon and quoted its words, that the brothers should have nothing of their own, neither house nor place (I1; one sentence; the Fraticelli story tells the end). Defended: his poverty, by the strict friars, with his own words.
2. **His Life, written and rewritten.** Gregory IX made him a saint in 1228 and had Celano write his Life; Celano wrote it again in the 1240s, and Bonaventure wrote the Life the order made its standard (I2, S6; one sentence; link Bonaventure).
3. **His words kept by the women.** The sisters of San Damiano kept his written promise to care for them, and his last wish that they live always in poverty, in their own Rule (E12, I3; one clause).
4. Optional, one clause, not the last line: the Friars Minor still publish their own notices today (I6; the census tradition's living evidence).

**Quotes** (3)

1. **Testament, opening** (OPU 2823–2830, Latin, rendered from it; OCR slips but readable; Celano's Latin quotes the first part, CELL 7373–7377; Robinson's English ROB 5368–5376 agrees). Latin: "Dominus ita dedit mihi fratri Francisco incipere facere poenitentiam, quia, cum essem in peccatis, nimis mihi videbatur amarum videre leprosos; et ipse Dominus conduxit me inter illos, et feci misericordiam cum illis. Et recedente me ab ipsis, id quod videbatur mihi amarum, conversum fuit mihi in dulcedinem animi et corporis. Et postea parum steti et exivi de saeculo." Rendering: "The Lord gave me, Brother Francis, to begin to do penance in this way. When I was in sin, it seemed too bitter to me to see lepers. And the Lord himself led me among them, and I showed mercy to them. And as I left them, what had seemed bitter to me was turned into sweetness of soul and body. After that I waited a little, and I left the world." ("In sin" renders the plural *in peccatis*.) The narrator gives the plain sense of "do penance" (turn his life to God) and "left the world" (left his old life). The writer may end the quote at "soul and body" and tell the last sentence in narration. Job: the opening. The turn in his own voice, without vision or voice from heaven. Not used in any census longDescription; the tradition entry's documentedStories prints these lines in another English (see Gaps).
2. **Rule of 1223, ch. 6** (OPU 2577–2582, Latin, rendered from it; Robinson's English ROB 4774–4795 and Bonagratia's quotation BFV 39281–39282 agree). Latin: "Fratres nihil sibi approprient nec domum nec locum nec aliquam rem. Et tanquam peregrini et advenae in hoc saeculo in paupertate et humilitate Domino famulantes vadant pro eleemosyna confidenter, nec oportet eos verecundari, quia Dominus pro nobis se fecit pauperem in hoc mundo." Rendering: "Let the brothers make nothing their own, not a house, not a place, not anything. As pilgrims and strangers in this world, serving the Lord in poverty and humility, let them go confidently for alms. They need not be ashamed, for the Lord made himself poor for us in this world." The narrator gives "alms" its plain sense. Job: beat 5. What "owned nothing" meant, in his own law for the order; the same words come back in What came 1.
3. **The Canticle of Brother Sun** (Italian, SPECL 27173–27175 and 27258–27259, rendered from it; the Italian is held only in Sabatier's OCR, which has slips: "sořa", "poskappare" for *pò skappare*; other manuscripts in the same file read the death line alike, SPECL 27693, 30326, 30413, 30495; Robinson's English from the same Assisi manuscript, ROB 8598–8606 and 8663–8666, confirms the reading). Italian: "Laudato sie, Misignore, cum tucte le tue creature, spetialmente messor lo frate sole, lo quale iorno et allumini noi per loi." … "Laudato si, Misignore, per sora nostra morte corporale, da la quale nullu homo vivente pò skappare." Rendering: "Be praised, my Lord, with all your creatures, above all Sir Brother Sun, who is the day, and through him you give us light." … "Be praised, my Lord, for our Sister Bodily Death, from whom no one living can escape." ("Sir" renders *messor*, a title of honour; *per* can be read "for" or "through"; the rendering follows Robinson's "for".) Job: the first line at beat 6, the song made ill at San Damiano; the death line in the Ending, the verse the Mirror of Perfection says he added when told he would die. The two lines may be split as two short uses of one quote.

**Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from Francis was a way of life written in his own words, the Rule and the Testament, and with it a question his brothers could never settle: how literally to keep it. The listener must be told three things. First, that the order lived by the Rule he wrote, and the Rule said the brothers should own nothing, not a house, not a place (Q2; E11). Second, that he tried to fix its meaning before he died, by a Testament to be read with the Rule "simply", without glosses, and by forbidding privileges from Rome (E14); and that four years later the pope, the very cardinal he had named as protector, ruled that this command did not bind them (I1). Third, that the quarrel ran on: a century later the order's own lawyer was quoting the Rule's words to a pope (I1). Tell it through these acts: the writing, the commands, the ruling, the appeal. Not as a "this matters because" sentence. The tradition entry tells the split into Spirituals and Conventuals; here it stays at the appeal.

**Ending:** close on the person. Recommended: his death, after What came: told by a physician that he would die, he said, a later book of his companions' memories says, "Welcome, Sister Death", had two companions sing his song, and added a verse for death (Q3, second line; E17, SPEC 8975–8979, 9040–9058); he died in October 1226 at the Portiuncula, the little church where the order began, Celano says (S6, CEL 4006–4016). End on the place, or on the verse. Alternative: Celano's scene, carried to the Portiuncula, he sang the psalm "I cried to the Lord with my voice" (E17, CEL 4836–4837). Add no moral.

**Who tells it**

- **Francis himself**, for the lepers, the Gospel life, the confirmation, the one tunic and the work (Testament), the poverty (Rule), and the Canticle. Credit "he dictated", "his Rule says". His own words are the one layer no one disputes (D8); the story leans on them.
- **Thomas of Celano**, a friar, who wrote the First Life within three years of his death, for the pope who made him a saint, and a Second Life in the 1240s: the trading youth, the father's chains, the bishop, the lepers' sores, the Gospel at the Portiuncula, Rome and the cardinal, the death (beats 1–4, Ending). Name him once with when he wrote; the listener should know the Life was written for the pope.
- **Bonaventure**, a generation later, minister general: "inheritance" (beat 2), the standard Life (What came 2).
- **Jacques de Vitry**, a bishop outside the order, in 1216: how the brothers and the women lived (beat 5). Contemporary.
- **The Mirror of Perfection**, a later book of his companions' memories, whose date and author are disputed: the death verse and "Welcome, Sister Death" (Ending). Say "a later book of his companions' memories".
- **A leader of the strict friars, about 1310** (Ubertino): the pope's ruling on the Testament (What came 1).

**Do not say**

- Any word in "Words to explain" used without its plain sense the first time it is heard.
- Not "the crucifix spoke to him" or "go and repair my house". It is the Second Life's (1240s), absent from the Testament and the First Life (D3); for the card.
- Not "he said, 'From now on I will say Our Father who art in heaven'" at the bishop scene. The First Life says he did not speak (D2). Leave it out.
- Not "he renounced his inheritance" in the narrator's voice as Celano's. Celano: he gave up all he had, at his father's demand; "inheritance" is Bonaventure's word, credited (E5).
- Not "he stripped to protest", "to shame his father", "in defiance". No source gives such a motive (E5).
- Not "Innocent III issued a bull" or "a papal decree". He confirmed it by word of mouth, "without a bull" (E7).
- Not "in 1209" as fact. Say "in 1209 or 1210" or "about 1209" (D4).
- Not "the Rule Innocent approved survives" or that the 1221 Rule is it. Its first text is lost (E8).
- Not "Francis founded the Poor Clares" or "founded a women's order". Celano: the Order of Poor Ladies began at San Damiano, and its foundation was a young woman turned to God by his words; he wrote them a form of life and promised them care (E12). Do not name Clare.
- Not "Jacques de Vitry met Francis in 1216" or that his women were Clare's sisters. The 1216 letter does not name Francis or Clare (E10).
- Not "the friars lived only by begging". They worked; they begged when no wage was given (E9).
- Not "a vow of poverty" as the Rule's wording. Say "to live in obedience, without anything of their own, and chaste" (E11), or "to own nothing".
- Not "he wrote the Canticle on his deathbed". He made it while ill at San Damiano, an editor dates it to late 1225; the death verse was added near death, by the Mirror's account (E13, D6).
- Not "the stigmata" or "he received Christ's wounds" in the narrator's voice. If told: "Celano writes that ... marks of nails appeared" (E15).
- Not "he loved animals and nature" or the sermon to the birds: colour the outline does not hold for this story.
- Not "Francis ordered heretics burned" or "imprisoned". The Testament orders that brothers who will not keep the Office by the Rule, or are not Catholics, be held like prisoners and brought to the cardinal (E14). Tell exactly that.
- Not "the Testament forbade all property" or "the Testament was a new Rule". He said it was not another Rule, but a reminder and exhortation to keep the Rule better (E14).
- Not "the pope overturned Francis" or "betrayed him". Say the pope ruled that the brothers were not bound by the command, as a strict friar later reported (I1).
- Not "the order split in 1323" here. The appeal is told; the split and the burnings are the tradition's and the Fraticelli story's.
- Not "Francis was a priest". The sources do not make him one.
- Not "born in 1182" or "born 1181". Say "born around 1182" (S1).
- Not "he died on 4 October" as our date. Say "in October 1226" or "on the evening of 3 October" (S6).
- Not "Gregory IX canonized him at Perugia" or a place beyond Assisi. Say "in 1228" (S6; the chronicle's two passages differ on place).
- Not "the first Franciscan" or "the first friar" as fact.
- Not "Bonaventure suppressed the earlier Lives". That is the `bonaventure` story's, told there from Salter's note.
