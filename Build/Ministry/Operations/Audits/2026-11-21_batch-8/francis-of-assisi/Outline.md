# Outline: Francis of Assisi

Sheet items (S, E, I, L, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. OPU is Francis's Latin writings (Quaracchi 1904); ROB Robinson's English of them (1906); SPECL Sabatier's *Speculum perfectionis* with the Italian Canticle; SPEC its English (Evans); CEL Celano's two Lives in English (Howell), CELL their Latin; BON Bonaventure's Life in English (Salter); JDV and JDVH Jacques de Vitry's letter of 1216 and his history of the West; EHR3 Ubertino of Casale (1310); BFV Bonagratia's appeal (1323).

- **The essence:** Francis, a trader's son of Assisi in central Italy, gave up his family's wealth in the first years of the 1200s, went to serve the lepers he had once loathed, and took to the roads to preach (E2–E6; Documented: his own Testament for the lepers, Celano for the rest). The brothers who joined him, the Friars Minor, the "lesser brothers", were to own nothing, work with their hands and beg when no wage came; by day they went among the townspeople, and he refused a cardinal's advice to live as a monk or a hermit (E7, E9–E11; Documented, his own Rule and Testament, Celano for the refusal; a contemporary bishop saw how they lived, E10).
- **Also told:**
  - **The break with his father.** His father chained him at home, then brought him before the bishop to give up all he had; Francis stripped and handed back his clothes. Bonaventure calls it giving up his claim to his father's inheritance (E4–E5; Documented as Celano's and Bonaventure's accounts).
  - **The Rule.** With eleven brothers he went to Rome; a cardinal urged him to become a monk or a hermit, and he refused; Innocent III confirmed his short rule of life, by the Rule's own words, about 1209 (E7; Documented; the year Contested among editors, D4). Its first text is lost (E8; Widely Accepted). The Rule of 1223 forbade the brothers to own a house, a place or anything (E11; Documented, his own words).
  - **The Canticle of Brother Sun.** Ill at San Damiano, near the end, he made a song of praise in his own Italian for the sun, the moon and the elements, and, a later book says, added a verse for death when he knew it was near (E13, E17; the song Documented, his own words; the added verse Contested, the Mirror of Perfection).
  - **The Testament.** In 1226 he dictated his memory of how it began, and his commands: no letters of privilege from Rome, brothers who strayed to be held like prisoners, and his words to be read "simply", without glosses (E14; Documented, his own words).
  - **What came of him.** Four years after his death, the pope ruled that the Testament's command did not bind; for a century his brothers fought over the Rule's poverty, quoting its words to the pope (I1; Documented as each witness's text). His brothers wrote his Life and rewrote it, Bonaventure among them; Clare's sisters kept his words in their Rule (I2–I3; Documented).

**Words to explain** (the first time each is heard; plain word or short gloss, worked into the sentence)

- **do penance** (Q1): the narrator gives the plain sense, "to turn his life to God". Do not let "penance" stand alone as punishment.
- **left the world** (Q1): "left his old life in the town", the way of life of family, trade and money.
- **Friars Minor**: "the Friars Minor, the 'lesser brothers'"; friar means brother.
- **alms** (Q2): "gifts of food or help, begged from door to door".
- **Rule**: "a written rule of life for the brothers".
- **Testament**: "a last testament, his parting words to the brothers", dictated in 1226.
- **gloss**: "notes saying how his words ought to be understood"; or say "simply, as he wrote them".
- **Canticle**: "a song of praise"; "the Canticle of Brother Sun, a song of praise in his own Italian".
- **the Portiuncula**: "a little church of St Mary near Assisi"; the name may be left out.
- **letters of privilege** (beat 6, Connection): 'letters from the pope's court granting the brothers special rights, for a church, a place or leave to preach' (ROB 5523–5529; OPU 2912–2918).
- **the Office** (beat 6): "the daily prayers set by the Rule".
- **bull**, if the optional heading in beat 4 is kept: "a sealed papal letter".
- **seraph**, if the optional clause in beat 6 is kept: "a six-winged angel" (CEL 4231–4234).
- **custos / Lord of Ostia** (E14): do not use; say "their superiors" and "the cardinal who protected the order".

- **Shape:** Monk or ascetic. The turn away from ordinary life: the lepers, the father, the bishop. The life chosen instead: owning nothing, working, begging, preaching on the roads, among people, not behind walls. The others drawn after him: the brothers, the women of San Damiano, the order and its quarrel. The trap is a pile of wonders: none is told except, at most, one clause on the marks of the wounds in Celano's voice (optional, cut first), and the speaking crucifix is left for the card.
- **Sibling:** none in the batch. **Story beats** (the opening, the essence and beat 1 make the first minute, about 150 words; beats 2 to 6 follow the life once, in order. The opening's quote is the lepers; beat 3 does not repeat it)

1. **Assisi.** Born around 1182 into a trading family of Assisi (S1, S2). Celano, a friar who wrote his Life within three years of his death, calls him rich, open-handed and "a shrewd trader"; he rode out to sell scarlet cloth (S2, E1; credit Celano at first mention, with when he wrote). One or two sentences.
2. **His father, and the bishop.** Celano writes that one day, while he still dressed as a man of the town, he met a leper, went up to him and kissed him (CEL 1497–1500; Celano's account; Documented as his). When he came back into the town changed, and thin from fasting, townsmen threw mud and stones at him as a madman; his father dragged him home, shut him in a dark room and used blows and chains to bend him (E4; Documented as Celano's account: the force is told plainly). His mother let him go. Then his father brought him before the bishop of Assisi to give up all he had: he took off all his clothes, gave them back to his father, and stood naked, and the bishop wrapped him in his own cloak (E5). Bonaventure, who led the order a generation later and wrote its official Life, says he gave up his claim to his father's inheritance (E5; credit Bonaventure; link). Reason for the act: the sources give his purpose only as giving up all he had for God; do not add a motive (Do not say).
3. **The lepers and the roads.** After the break he went to live among the lepers and served them, washing their sores, Celano writes (E3). Before, the sight of their houses had made him hold his nose (E3; Celano's voice). The Testament had already said, in the opening, that the Lord led him among them (D1: lepers first in his own order). He repaired the ruined church of San Damiano with his own hands (E6). At the Portiuncula he heard the Gospel where Christ sends the disciples out with no money, no bag, no shoes, and cried, "This is what I want" (E6; Celano; paraphrase, not a quote). He put off his shoes and took to the roads to preach (E6).
4. **Brothers, and Rome.** Men came to share the life; in his own words, no one showed him what to do, but God showed him he must live by the Gospel, and he had it written down in a few simple words (E7; his Testament, paraphrase). With eleven brothers he went to Rome. A cardinal urged him to become a monk or a hermit; he refused (E7; Celano). Pope Innocent III confirmed it, the Rule's own opening says, in 1209 or 1210 (E7, D4; link). Optional: the heading printed above it adds "without a bull", that is, without a written papal decree (E7; credit "the heading", not the Rule's own words). That first text is lost (E8; one clause, optional).
5. **How they lived.** By 1216 a bishop at the papal court, Jacques de Vitry, wrote that rich and worldly men and women were leaving everything to follow them; by day the brothers went into towns, by night into lonely places to pray, and the women lived by the work of their hands (E10; credit Jacques; do not call the women Clare's). The Testament: content with one patched tunic, they worked, and begged only when no wage was given (E9). In 1223 the next pope confirmed a shorter Rule; Quote 2 (E11).
6. **The Canticle and the Testament.** Ill, at San Damiano, where a young woman of Assisi and her sisters had lived for years in poverty under his promised care (E12; Clare by role, one clause), he made a song of praise in his own Italian: Quote 3, first line (E13; the sun). Do not say "blind": only the later Mirror of Perfection says he could not bear light for sixty days (E13). In 1226 he dictated the Testament: no brother was to ask Rome for letters of privilege, even to escape persecution; brothers who would not say the prayers by the Rule, or were not Catholics, were to be held day and night like prisoners and brought to the cardinal who protected the order; and the Rule and these words were to be understood "simply", with no glosses (E14; his own words; the prisoners clause is told, not softened). Optional (cut first): Celano writes that two years before his death, after a vision of a seraph, the marks of nails appeared in his hands and feet (E15; Celano's voice only).

**What came of him** (about a fifth of the story; with beat 6's Testament commands, which are the hinge of the connection, about a third; heaviest first)

1. **The quarrel over his words.** In 1230, four years after his death, the order's own leaders asked the pope whether the Testament bound them. Gregory IX, the cardinal the Testament had named, answered in a bull that they were not bound by its command. He believed Francis had meant well, he wrote, but Francis could not bind them without the ministers' consent, or bind the pope who came after him (SPECL 31887–31890, 31933–31943; own text of the pope, contemporary; Documented). For a century his brothers fought over the Rule's poverty: in 1323 the order's own lawyer stood before the pope at Avignon and quoted its words, that the brothers should have nothing of their own, neither house nor place (I1; one sentence; the Fraticelli story tells the end). Defended: his poverty, with his own words: by the order itself before the pope in 1323.
2. **His Life, written and rewritten.** Gregory IX made him a saint in 1228 and had Celano write his Life; Celano wrote it again in the 1240s, and Bonaventure wrote the Life the order made its standard (I2, S6; one sentence; link Bonaventure).
3. **His words kept by the women.** The sisters of San Damiano kept his written promise to care for them, and his last wish that they live always in poverty, in their own Rule (E12, I3; one clause).
4. **The spread.** Optional, one clause: Jacques de Vitry, writing later, says there was hardly a Christian province without its brothers (JDVH 19384–19386; contemporary, long-s OCR).
5. Optional, one clause, not the last line: the Friars Minor still publish their own notices today (I6; the census tradition's living evidence).

**Quotes** (3)

1. **Testament, opening** (OPU 2823–2830, Latin, rendered from it; OCR slips but readable; Celano's Latin quotes the first part, CELL 7373–7377; Robinson's English ROB 5368–5376 agrees). Latin: "Dominus ita dedit mihi fratri Francisco incipere facere poenitentiam, quia, cum essem in peccatis, nimis mihi videbatur amarum videre leprosos; et ipse Dominus conduxit me inter illos, et feci misericordiam cum illis. Et recedente me ab ipsis, id quod videbatur mihi amarum, conversum fuit mihi in dulcedinem animi et corporis. Et postea parum steti et exivi de saeculo." Rendering: "The Lord gave me, Brother Francis, to begin to do penance in this way. When I was in sin, it seemed too bitter to me to see lepers. And the Lord himself led me among them, and I showed mercy to them. And as I left them, what had seemed bitter to me was turned into sweetness of soul and body. After that I waited a little, and I left the world." ("In sin" renders the plural *in peccatis*.) The narrator gives the plain sense of "do penance" (turn his life to God) and "left the world" (left his old life). The writer may end the quote at "soul and body" and tell the last sentence in narration. Job: the opening. The turn in his own voice, without vision or voice from heaven. 2. **Rule of 1223, ch. 6** (OPU 2577–2582, Latin, rendered from it; Robinson's English ROB 4774–4795 and Bonagratia's quotation BFV 39281–39282 agree). Latin: "Fratres nihil sibi approprient nec domum nec locum nec aliquam rem. Et tanquam peregrini et advenae in hoc saeculo in paupertate et humilitate Domino famulantes vadant pro eleemosyna confidenter, nec oportet eos verecundari, quia Dominus pro nobis se fecit pauperem in hoc mundo." Rendering: "Let the brothers make nothing their own, not a house, not a place, not anything. As pilgrims and strangers in this world, serving the Lord in poverty and humility, let them go confidently for alms. They need not be ashamed, for the Lord made himself poor for us in this world." 3. **The Canticle of Brother Sun** (Italian, SPECL 27173–27175 and 27258–27259, rendered from it; the Italian is held only in Sabatier's OCR, which has slips: "sořa", "poskappare" for *pò skappare*; other manuscripts in the same file read the death line alike, SPECL 27693, 30326, 30413, 30495; Robinson's English from the same Assisi manuscript, ROB 8598–8606 and 8663–8666, confirms the reading; for the sun line, other manuscripts in the same file read the verb *giorna* / *iorna*, "who brings the day", SPECL 30207, 30309, 30382, and Robinson has "The which lights up the day", ROB 8603–8604; "who is the day" follows Assisi 338, SPECL 27175, and the other reading is for the card). **Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from Francis was a way of life written in his own words, the Rule and the Testament, and with it a question his brothers could never settle: how literally to keep it. The listener must be told three things. First, that the order lived by the Rule he wrote, and the Rule said the brothers should own nothing, not a house, not a place (Q2; E11). Second, that he tried to fix its meaning before he died, by a Testament to be read with the Rule "simply", without glosses, and by forbidding privileges from Rome (E14); and that four years later, at the request of the order's own leaders, the pope, the very cardinal he had named as protector, ruled in a bull that this command did not bind them (SPECL 31887–31943). Third, that the quarrel ran on: a century later the order's own lawyer was quoting the Rule's words to a pope (I1). Tell it through these acts: the writing, the commands, the ruling, the appeal. Not as a "this matters because" sentence. The tradition entry tells the split into Spirituals and Conventuals; here it stays at the appeal.

**Ending:** close on the person. Recommended: his death, after What came: told by a physician that he would die, he said, a later book said to gather his companions' memories says, "Welcome, my Sister Death", had two companions sing his song, and added a verse for death (Q3, second line; E17, SPEC 8975–8979, 9040–9058); he died in October 1226 at the Portiuncula, the little church where the order began, Celano says (S6, CEL 4006–4016). End on the place, or on the verse. Alternative: Celano's scene, carried to the Portiuncula, he sang the psalm "I cried to the Lord with my voice" (E17, CEL 4836–4837). Add no moral.

**Do not say**

- Any word in "Words to explain" used without its plain sense the first time it is heard.
- Beats 1 to 4 rest on Celano's account (with Bonaventure for "inheritance"). Name Celano once, with when and for whom he wrote, and keep the chains, the mud and stones and the bishop scene in his telling ("Celano writes"), not as bare fact.
- Not "behind monastery walls" as a contrast the sources state. Say they went into the towns by day, and that he refused to become a monk or a hermit (E7, E10).
- Not "the Rule says Innocent approved it by word of mouth". The Rule's opening says he confirmed it; "without a bull" is the printed heading, and "by word of mouth" Robinson's (E7).
- Not "the Rule that still governs the order": no held source says so.
- Not "the crucifix spoke to him" or "go and repair my house". It is the Second Life's (1240s), absent from the Testament and the First Life (D3); for the card.
- Not "he said, 'From now on I will say Our Father who art in heaven'" at the bishop scene. The First Life says he did not speak (D2). Leave it out.
- Not "he renounced his inheritance" in the narrator's voice as Celano's. Celano: he gave up all he had, at his father's demand; "inheritance" is Bonaventure's word, credited (E5).
- Not "he stripped to protest", "to shame his father", "in defiance". No source gives such a motive (E5).
- Not "Innocent III issued a bull" or "a papal decree". He confirmed it by word of mouth, "without a bull" (E7).
- Not "in 1209" as fact. Say "in 1209 or 1210" or "about 1209" (D4).
- Not "the Rule Innocent approved survives" or that the 1221 Rule is it. Its first text is lost (E8).
- Not "Francis founded the Poor Clares" or "founded a women's order". Celano: the Order of Poor Ladies began at San Damiano, and its foundation was a young woman turned to God by his words; he wrote them a form of life and promised them care (E12). Do not name Clare.
- Not "Jacques de Vitry met Francis in 1216" or that his women were Clare's sisters. The 1216 letter does not name Francis or Clare (E10).
- Not "the friars lived only by begging". They worked; they begged when no wage was given (E9).
- Not "a vow of poverty" as the Rule's wording. Say "to live in obedience, without anything of their own, and chaste" (E11), or "to own nothing".
- Not "he wrote the Canticle on his deathbed". He made it while ill at San Damiano, an editor dates it to late 1225; the death verse was added near death, by the Mirror's account (E13, D6).
- Not that he first went near a leper only after the bishop scene. Francis puts the lepers before he left the world, and Celano puts the first meeting, the kiss, before the break and the service after it (D1).
- Not "the stigmata" or "he received Christ's wounds" in the narrator's voice. If told: "Celano writes that ... marks of nails appeared" (E15).
- Not "he loved animals and nature" or the sermon to the birds: colour the outline does not hold for this story.
- Not "Francis ordered heretics burned" or "imprisoned". The Testament orders that brothers who will not keep the Office by the Rule, or are not Catholics, be held like prisoners and brought to the cardinal (E14). Tell exactly that.
- Not "the Testament forbade all property" or "the Testament was a new Rule". He said it was not another Rule, but a reminder and exhortation to keep the Rule better (E14).
- Not 'the pope overturned Francis' or 'betrayed him', and not that the pope acted on his own. The order's leaders asked him. He wrote that Francis meant well, and ruled that the brothers were not bound by the command (SPECL 31887–31943).
- Not that the order's lawyer of 1323 was one of the strict friars. He spoke for the order, and had opposed their leader.
- Not "the order split in 1323" here. The appeal is told; the split and the burnings are the tradition's and the Fraticelli story's.
- Not "Francis was a priest". The sources do not make him one.
- Not "born in 1182" or "born 1181". Say "born around 1182" (S1).
- Not "he died on 4 October" as our date. Say "in October 1226" or "on the evening of 3 October" (S6).
- Not "Gregory IX canonized him at Perugia" or a place beyond Assisi. Say "in 1228" (S6; the chronicle's two passages differ on place).
- Not "the first Franciscan" or "the first friar" as fact.
- Not "Bonaventure suppressed the earlier Lives". That is the `bonaventure` story's, told there from Salter's note.
