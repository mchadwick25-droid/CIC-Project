"The Lord gave me, Brother Francis, to begin to do penance in this way. When I was in sin, it seemed too bitter to me to see lepers. And the Lord himself led me among them, and I showed mercy to them. And as I left them, what had seemed bitter to me was turned into sweetness of soul and body."

So Francis of Assisi remembered it in the testament he dictated in 1226: his parting words to the brothers. To do penance, for him, was to turn his life to God. Soon after, he says, he left the world: his old life of family, trade and money.

A trader's son from Assisi, in central Italy, he gave up his family's wealth in his early twenties, served the lepers, and took to the roads to preach. The brothers who joined him were the Friars Minor, the lesser brothers. They were to own nothing, work with their hands, and beg when no wage came. When a cardinal urged him to become a monk or a hermit instead, he refused.

He was born around 1182. Thomas of Celano, a friar, wrote his Life within three years of his death, for the pope who made him a saint. He says his father dragged him home and used blows and chains to bend him. Then his father brought him before the bishop to give up all he had. Celano says Francis took off every piece of clothing, handed it back, and stood naked, and the bishop wrapped him in his own cloak. Bonaventure, who wrote the order's official Life a generation later, calls it giving up his claim to his father's inheritance.

After that, Celano writes, he lived among the lepers and washed their sores. At the Portiuncula, a little church of St Mary near Assisi, he heard the Gospel where Christ sends out his disciples with no money, no bag and no shoes. He put off his shoes and took to the roads.

With eleven brothers he went to Rome, and Pope Innocent III, the Rule's own opening says, confirmed their short written rule of life in 1209 or 1210.

A bishop at the papal court wrote in 1216 that the brothers went into towns by day and lonely places by night. In 1223 the next pope confirmed a shorter Rule. It said the brothers should go for alms, gifts of food or help begged from door to door. And it said what owning nothing meant: "Let the brothers make nothing their own, not a house, not a place, not anything."

Ill at San Damiano, he made a song of praise in his own Italian, the Canticle of Brother Sun.

Then he dictated the Testament. No brother was to ask Rome for letters of privilege, which grant special rights. Some brothers might not say the daily prayers set by the Rule, or might not be Catholics. They were to be held day and night like prisoners and brought to the cardinal who protected the order. And the Rule and his words were to be read simply, without notes saying how they ought to be understood.

Four years after his death, the order's leaders asked the pope whether the Testament bound them. Pope Gregory IX, that same cardinal, wrote that Francis had meant well, but could not bind them without their leaders' consent. They were not bound. For a century his brothers fought over the Rule's poverty. In 1323 the order's lawyer stood before the pope at Avignon and quoted its words: nothing of their own, neither house nor place. The sisters of San Damiano kept his last wish, that they live always in poverty, in their own Rule.

A later book, said to gather his companions' memories, says a physician told Francis he would die. "Welcome, my Sister Death," he said, and had two companions sing his song, to which, it says, he added a verse.

"Be praised, my Lord, for our Sister Bodily Death, from whom no one living can escape."

He died in October 1226 at the Portiuncula, the little church, Celano says.
