Zurich, January 1523. More than six hundred priests and citizens crowd the hall of the city council. The bishop's spokesman wants the universities to judge the new preaching, and a priest rises to answer him.

"I say we have judges here who cannot err and take no side: the Holy Scriptures, which can neither lie nor deceive. We have them here in Hebrew, Greek and Latin. Let both sides take them as fair and just judges."

So said Huldrych Zwingli, people's priest of Zurich, who preached at the Great Minster, the city's main church. He had asked to defend his preaching in public. The council, the two hundred citizens who made Zurich's laws, ruled that every preacher must preach only what he could defend from Scripture. Within two years Zurich took the images out of its churches and ended the Mass, the Latin service at the altar. The Reformed churches, the family that grew from Zurich and Geneva, look to him as a founder.

He had come to that hall by way of the pulpit. In 1519, instead of the set Sunday readings, he began preaching straight through the Gospel of Matthew.

The council that judged by Scripture also used force. Its summons had warned that anyone who opposed it without Scripture would be dealt with more severely, and any preacher who disobeyed its ruling would be restrained. In 1524 images and organs were taken out of the churches, and on April 12, 1525, the council ended the Mass. A city council, not a bishop, now decided what its church would do.

Some Zurich men pushed the same test further. Historians say they would admit nothing without clear warrant in Scripture, and found none for baptizing infants, so they baptized adults again. They were called Anabaptists, or rebaptizers. On March 7, 1526, the council decreed that anyone who baptized another would be drowned without mercy. Zwingli wrote that same day to a friend that patience had at last broken out. He was grieved by their stubborn daring, and wished the new faith had not begun with such examples. On January 5, 1527, by the council's sentence, one of their leaders, Felix Manz, was bound and thrown into the water to die.

In 1529 the prince of Hesse brought Zwingli and Martin Luther to Marburg to settle communion, the shared bread and wine. Luther held that Christ's own body is truly in the bread; Zwingli answered that a body is in one place, and Christ's is in heaven. They signed fifteen articles together, and the last one says where they stopped.

"We have not agreed at this time whether Christ's true body and blood are bodily in the bread and wine. Yet each side shall show the other Christian love, as far as each conscience can bear. And both sides shall pray earnestly to Almighty God to confirm us, by his Spirit, in the right understanding. Amen."

That May Zwingli urged the leaders of Bern, Zurich's ally, to war on the five Catholic cantons, or states, of central Switzerland.

"This peace that some press for so hard is war, not peace. And the war we press for is peace, not war. For we thirst for no one's blood, nor will we drink it in the turmoil. Our aim is to cut the sinews of the rule of the few."

In 1531 Zurich and Bern cut off the cantons' food. The cantons marched, and on October 11 beat Zurich's army at Kappel. His friend Myconius, writing the next year, says Zwingli went as the army's chaplain, though he had said he would not go. He died on the field. Myconius adds that the victors burned his body.

In 1549 the pastors of Zurich signed one agreement on communion with John Calvin of Geneva, who never met him. In 1554 Calvin wrote that if Zwingli were alive, he would not change a word of it.

In March 1532 the overseers of the Great Minster gave the house of "Master Ulrich, of blessed memory" to the new teacher of the Bible lesson, Heinrich Bullinger. He agreed to share it with Master Ulrich's children until his widow found another home.
