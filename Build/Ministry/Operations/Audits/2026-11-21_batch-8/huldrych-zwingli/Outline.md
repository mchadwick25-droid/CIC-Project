# Outline: Huldrych Zwingli

Sheet items (S, E, I, L, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. JK01 is Jackson's English of the Zurich disputation records; KD Kidd's source book; EG the Zurich council's records; WA the Weimar Luther with the Marburg Articles in German; CU and DW Luther's letters; FX Foxe; CT the 1549 Zurich-Geneva agreement with Calvin's 1554 exposition.

- **The essence:** From 1519 Huldrych Zwingli, people's priest of Zurich's main church, preached the Bible book by book, and in 1523 asked to defend his preaching in a public debate before the city council; the council ruled that every preacher must preach only what Scripture could defend, and over the next two years Zurich removed its images and ended the Mass (E1, E3-E5; Documented, the council's decrees and his own words). The churches called Reformed look to him as a founder; on the tree, their tradition begins at Zurich in 1519 (I2; Widely Accepted, editor and the tree's own entries).
- **Also told:**
  - **The test, and its force.** The council that judged by Scripture also punished: it threatened those who would not prove their case from Scripture, and in 1526 it made rebaptism a crime punished by drowning, and in 1527 it drowned the first of the Anabaptists, believers who baptized adults again, some of them Zurich men who had spoken at his own second debate (KD 24433, 24463; that they pressed his own test further is Kidd's account). He wrote the same day that patience had at last broken out, and that he wished the new faith had not begun with such examples (E2, E4, E6; Documented, council records and his own letter).
  - **Marburg, 1529.** A German prince brought him and Martin Luther together to unite their side. They signed fifteen articles together, but could not agree whether Christ's body is bodily present in the bread and wine, and promised each other Christian love (E7-E9; Documented, the signed text; the scenes Documented as each witness's account).
  - **Kappel, 1531.** He urged war on the Catholic cantons as the only true peace; in 1531 they marched and beat Zurich at Kappel, and he died on the field (E11-E12; Documented, his letter; his death Widely Accepted; what was done to his body is told by his friend Myconius a year later (JK12 1596-1600), and Foxe repeats it (E12)).
  - **What came of him.** Heinrich Bullinger took his place and kept Zurich's church; Luther called his death God's judgment; in 1549 Geneva under John Calvin signed one agreement on the Lord's Supper with Zurich, and Calvin wrote that Zwingli would not change a word of it (I1-I4; Documented as each one's words).
- **Length:** about 4:30 at most (about 675 words by the scorer), aiming at 4:00. Counted first: the three quotes, about 140 words (Q1 about 40, Q2 about 60, Q3 about 40); the plain sense of the words to explain, about 60 words (people's priest, the council of Two Hundred, Mass, Anabaptists, the Lord's Supper, bodily present, the Five Cantons, the rule of the few); the witnesses credited, about 60 words (the council's decree and mandate, his letter, a chronicler, Collin, Luther twice, Foxe, Calvin). That is about 260 words before any event is told. Why this long: the job names four turns (the preaching, the disputations, Marburg, Kappel), and the drowning of the Anabaptists must stand beside the disputations, not be cut.

- **Setting:**
  - Time: people's priest from 1519 (S3; admitted 1 January, Kidd; preaching begun "Christmas day, 1519", Myconius); First Disputation 29 January 1523 (E3, D1); images out 1524, last Mass 12 April 1525 (E5); drowning law 7 March 1526, first drowning 5 January 1527 (E6); Marburg, early October 1529 (E7-E9); Kappel, 11 October 1531 (E12). Born 1 January 1484 (S1, editors): card only.
  - Place: Zurich, a self-governing city of the Swiss Confederation (S4); its Great Minster, "his own church, still open" (I8). Marburg, a castle town of the prince of Hesse in Germany (E7). Kappel, about ten miles south of Zurich (S6, editor).
  - Tradition: the tree's spoken name is "the Reformed cities of Zurich and Geneva" (S5). Name it once.
  - Language: he preached in German and wrote in Latin and German; the disputation was held "in the German language" (JK01 1747-1748, sheet E3).

**Words to explain** (the first time each is heard; plain word or short gloss, worked into the sentence)

- **people's priest** (S3): "the priest who preached to the townspeople at the Great Minster, Zurich's main church". Do not use "Leutpriester".
- **disputation** (E3): "a public debate". Say "debate"; the word "disputation" is not needed.
- **the council / Great Council / Two Hundred** (S4): "the city council, the two hundred citizens who made Zurich's laws". The burgomaster is "the mayor".
- **the Mass** (E5): "the Mass, the Latin service at the altar". Its end: "the Lord's table was set up in its place" (E5).
- **the Lord's Supper**: "communion, the shared bread and wine". Use one name and keep it.
- **Anabaptists** (E6): "Anabaptists, 'rebaptizers', who baptized adult believers again". Never bare.
- **bodily present** (E9, Q2): Luther held that Christ's own body is truly in the bread; Zwingli that a body is in one place, and Christ's is in heaven, so it is not in the bread (E8, both sides' accounts agree on his argument). His own words for his view: Christ's true body is present "by the contemplation of faith" (E10). The narrator gives this in one plain sentence before Q2.
- **the Five Cantons** (E11-E12): "five Catholic cantons, or states, of central Switzerland". Do not list them.
- **oligarchy** (Q3): the narrator gives the plain sense, "the rule of a powerful few". Do not say who they were; the letter does not.
- **Reformed**: "the Reformed churches, the family that grew from Zurich and Geneva" (I2).
- **Ananias** (beat 3): 'a man in the Acts of the Apostles who fell dead when Peter exposed his lie'.
- **relics** (beat 2): "saints' bones and belongings".
- **confession** (What came 1 and 2): "a statement of faith", on first use.
- **the Sacramentaries** (What came 2, cut-first 5), if kept: "Luther's side's name for Zwingli and those who held with him" (KD-ed 14382; CT 583).

- **Shape:** Founder or leader. What he built: a city church in which the council, hearing Scripture argued in public, decided what would be preached and practiced, and then enforced it. How it ran: preaching through whole books in place of the set readings; the Mass replaced by the Lord's table; images and organs removed; the houses of monks and nuns closed and their income turned to schooling and the poor (E5). Who it served: the city and its country parishes, and later the churches that copied it. What outlasted him: Zurich's church under Bullinger, and the Reformed family Geneva joined (I1-I3). The trap is titles in place of acts: tell the debates, the decree, the drowning, the signed article, the war letter.
- **Sibling:** none in the batch. Links read first: `martin-luther` (live; its job: the pardons, Worms, the German Bible, the Peasants' War; it does not tell Marburg) and `john-calvin` (live; its job: Geneva's church order; it does not name Zwingli). Here Luther acts only at Marburg and in one later letter; Calvin only in What came.
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** The hall of the Great Council, 29 January 1523, more than six hundred priests and citizens (E3). The bishop's spokesman wanted the universities to judge; Zwingli answered with Quote 1. Then straight into the essence: "So said Huldrych Zwingli, people's priest of Zurich ..." In about three sentences.
  2. Not chosen: 1 January 1519, a new priest opening the Gospel of Matthew at its first chapter (E1). Plain, but the debate shows the essence (Scripture tested in public before the council) in one scene.

**Story beats** (the opening, the essence and beat 1 make the first minute, about 150 words; beats 2 to 5 follow the life once, in order. The opening reaches ahead to 1523; beat 1 goes back only to set up how he came to that hall, then the story runs forward without returning.)

1. **Zurich, 1519.** He became people's priest at the Great Minster in 1519 (S3; give no day: Kidd has him admitted on 1 January 1519, KD-ed 21198; his friend Myconius says he began the promised preaching on "Christmas day, 1519", JK12 1010; the card credits both). Instead of the set Sunday readings he preached straight through the Gospel of Matthew, then Acts and Paul's letters (S3, E1). He wrote in 1522 that he had preached all of Matthew before he had even heard the names of the faction he was accused of joining (E1; his own claim; Documented as his words). Reason for the act: for Matthew, his friend Myconius says he told the canons he would teach "the history of Christ the Saviour as Matthew the Evangelist describes him", expounded "not according to human reason" but by collating the Scriptures (JK12 993-1004; credit his friend); he added Acts so that Zurich might see how the Gospel spread (E1, his words). Keep to two or three sentences. Since the opening is in 1523, tell this beat as how he came to that hall, then move forward.
2. **The council judges by Scripture, 1523-1525.** After the debate the council ruled that Zwingli should go on preaching as before, and that every preacher in the city and its lands must preach only what he could defend from Scripture; whoever did not obey would be "restrained" (E4; Documented, the decree). Its summons had already warned that whoever opposed it without Scripture would be dealt with more severely (E2). In October 1523 a second debate took up images and the Mass (E5). In 1524 images, relics and organs were taken out, and the houses of monks and nuns closed, their income turned, at his suggestion, to schooling and the poor (E5; editor's summary of the council records). On 12 April 1525 the council ended the Mass by a small majority, historians say (Kidd; Egli, from the chronicler's notes; not a quote); a Zurich chronicler wrote that the Lord's table was set up and the altars stripped, and "what pleased one man well did not please his neighbour" (E5; credit the chronicler). The meaning to carry: a city council, not a bishop, now decided, by Scripture argued in public, what its church would do.
3. **The Anabaptists, 1525-1527.** Some of Zurich's own reformers, led by Conrad Grebel and Felix Manz, pushed the same test further; Grebel had spoken at the second debate (KD 24433, 24463). In Kidd's account their aim was to admit nothing without explicit warrant of Scripture, and infant baptism had none, so they baptized adults again (E6; the aim is the editor's: credit "historians" or tell it as what they did). The council ordered infants baptized, jailed the rebaptizers, and on 7 March 1526 decreed that anyone who baptized another would be "drowned without mercy" (E6; the council's mandate). Zwingli wrote that same day to a friend: patience, tried enough, had at last broken out; he was grieved by their stubborn daring, and wished the new faith had not begun with such examples, but, he went on, it pleases God to guard against evils to come in this way, as when he struck down Ananias for lying (KD 25407-25412; his letter, Latin; paraphrase) (E6; D7). On 5 January 1527, by the council's sentence, Manz was taken out in a boat, bound, his arms pulled down over his knees, and thrown into the water to die (E6; EG 33747-33752; say "the water", as the sentence does). Do not soften: tell the law and the drowning plainly (Do not say).
4. **Marburg, 1529.** Philip, the prince of Hesse, wrote that their enemies said the reformers could not agree among themselves, and brought Zwingli and Luther to Marburg to settle the Lord's Supper (E7; his letter). By the account of a Zurich professor who went with Zwingli, before the debate began Luther had written "This is my body" on his table, so that he would not be drawn away from those words (E8; credit him: he was on Zwingli's side). Zwingli argued that a body is in one place, and Christ's is in heaven, so it is not in the bread; Luther wrote home to his wife that this was Zwingli's best argument (E8; both sides agree on the argument). Optional (cut first, to one clause): Luther wrote that Zwingli asked in tears to be counted brothers, and that he kept telling them they had a different spirit (E8; DW 2670-2672; paraphrase; credit Luther; hostile; damaged-scan German, see Quotes). They signed fifteen articles together; the last agreed on most of the Supper, but not on Christ's bodily presence; then Quote 2 (E9; Documented, the signed text). Luther wrote to his wife that they would not be brothers, but would live at peace (E8; CU 12756-12757; paraphrase).
5. **War, and Kappel, 1529-1531.** He urged the city of Bern, Zurich's ally, toward war on the Five Cantons: Quote 3 (E11; his letter of 30 May 1529). That first war ended in June 1529 in a peace with no battle, and he wrote that God had shown they could win "with no killing" (E11; paraphrase; optional). In 1531 Zurich and Bern cut off the Five Cantons' food; his friend wrote that Zwingli disapproved of the blockade (JK12 1533-1546). They marched, and on 11 October 1531 they beat Zurich's men at Kappel (E12, D4; Kidd and Foxe). Zwingli died on the field (E12; Widely Accepted). Credit why he was there, in one or two clauses: his friend Myconius, writing the next year, says he was chosen as the army's chaplain (Myconius does not say by whom), and that he said he would not go but went (JK12 1530-1533). Foxe, decades later, gives the custom that the city's chief minister marched with its army (FX 26319-26321). The custom line may drop if the length needs it. Myconius's line stays. His friend Myconius wrote the next year that the victors tried his body, cut it in four and burned it (JK12 1597-1600; credit Myconius; optional, cut first).

**What came of him** (about a third of the story; heaviest first)

1. **Zurich kept his church.** Within months the records of the Great Minster name Heinrich Bullinger "in Master Ulrich's place" over its teaching (I1; EG 48403, March 1532; the editors give his choosing as chief pastor in December 1531, and the tree his four decades there). Bullinger led Zurich's church for four decades after him and later wrote its great confession (I1; SHC-ed; say "the confession of 1566" only if named; optional).
2. **Two Protestant families.** Marburg's one unsettled point divided the Protestants. Luther's side made its own articles "against the Sacramentaries", the first of the Lutheran confessions; the churches that held with Zwingli became the Reformed, who look to him as a founder (I2; editors). Say what divided them in one plain sentence, carried by the act: they could not agree what happens at the Lord's table.
3. **Geneva joins Zurich.** In 1549 the pastors of Zurich and John Calvin of Geneva signed one agreement on the Lord's Supper; in 1554 Calvin wrote that if Zwingli were alive he would not change a word of it (I3; Calvin's own claim; paraphrase). Calvin never met him (L2).
4. **Luther's verdict.** In January 1532 Luther wrote that they were seeing God's judgment a second time, first on Münzer and now on Zwingli, and that his followers called him a martyr of Christ (I4; credit Luther; hostile). Optional, one sentence; if kept, name Münzer only as "a rebel preacher Luther had condemned" (Names).
- Optional (one clause, with What came, never the close): his own church, the Great Minster, still stands open in Zurich (I8; the tree's entry).

**Quotes, 3** (none used in another story on the tree; checked against every census `longDescription`, see the last section)

1. **Zwingli at the First Disputation, 29 January 1523** (E3). Rendered from Jackson's English of the Zurich German record (JK01 2185-2187, 2214-2216; the German is not held), checked against Kidd's Latin version (KD 23573-23578), which agrees in every clause:
   > "I say we have judges here who cannot err and take no side: the Holy Scriptures, which can neither lie nor deceive. We have them here in Hebrew, Greek and Latin. Let both sides take them as fair and just judges."
   Job: the opening; his whole method in his own words, at the moment it was tried.
2. **The Marburg Articles, Article 15, signed 3 October 1529 by Luther, Zwingli and seven others** (E9). Rendered from the German (WA 12535-12544; black-letter scan, fair and legible; confirmed by Kidd's Latin version, KD 14892-14899):
   > "We have not agreed at this time whether Christ's true body and blood are bodily in the bread and wine. Yet each side shall show the other Christian love, as far as each conscience can bear. And both sides shall pray earnestly to Almighty God to confirm us, by his Spirit, in the right understanding. Amen."
   Job: the break with Luther, in the words both men signed; it shows the break and the hope together.
3. **Zwingli to the leaders of Bern, 30 May 1529** (E11). Rendered from the Latin (KD 26135-26139; clean print):
   > "This peace that some press for so hard is war, not peace. And the war we press for is peace, not war. For we thirst for no one's blood, nor will we drink it in the turmoil. Our aim is to cut the sinews of the rule of the few."
   Job: why he went toward the war that killed him, in his own voice, with its force not softened.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** What passed from Zwingli was a way of ordering a church: test each practice against Scripture, argue it in public, and let the city's governing council decide and enforce it (E2-E5). Zurich kept it after his death under Bullinger (I1); and his reading of the Lord's Supper, that Christ's body is in heaven and is received by faith, not in the bread (E8, E10), is the point on which he broke with Luther at Marburg and on which Calvin's Geneva later joined Zurich in 1549 (E9, I3). The listener must be told three things: that the council, not a bishop, made the decisions, and used force to keep them (beat 2 and 3); what exactly divided him from Luther, in one plain sentence (beat 4); and that Geneva, under a man who never met him, signed with Zurich on that very point (What came 3). It comes through acts: the decree, the signed article, the 1549 agreement, not a "this matters because" sentence.
