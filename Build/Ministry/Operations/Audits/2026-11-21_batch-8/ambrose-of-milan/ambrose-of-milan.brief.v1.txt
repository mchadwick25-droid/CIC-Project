In 374, the people of Milan, where the western emperors then held court, were fighting over who should be their next bishop. The governor of the province, Ambrose, went into the church to calm the crowd. While he spoke, a shout went up: "Ambrose for bishop!" The whole crowd took it up. His secretary, Paulinus, who later wrote his life, says a child's voice began it.

Ambrose had not yet been baptized. He resisted: Paulinus says that, against his custom, he had men put to torture, and then fled the city by night. Within eight days he was baptized and made bishop. For twenty-three years he held that the church's buildings and its bread and wine were not the emperor's to command. He would not give up a church to the court, and he kept the emperor Theodosius from communion until Theodosius confessed his sin before the whole church after a massacre.

In the mid-380s, the empress Justina, mother of the boy emperor, wanted one of the city's great churches for her party at court. They were Christians who would not say that the Son is of one being with the Father. Ambrose answered that a bishop could not hand over God's temple. "The palaces belong to the emperor, the churches to the bishop," he told the court.

Most of this comes from his own letter to his sister. His crowd seized a man the court's party called a priest, and Ambrose sent clergy to rescue him. The court fined Milan's merchants two hundred pounds of gold and filled the prisons with tradespeople. Then soldiers surrounded the church before dawn, with the people inside. A court secretary asked if he meant to be a tyrant, a man setting up his own power against the emperor's. Ambrose said he had no arms but his own body, and he would not fight. The soldiers, he wrote, came in to pray, and the emperor pulled them back and returned the fines.

In those nights of vigil the people sang hymns. The court's men said his hymns had led them astray. Ambrose answered, "I certainly do not deny it." Every day, he said, the whole people sang their faith in the Father, Son and Holy Spirit. "So they have all become teachers, who could hardly be pupils."

A young teacher named Augustine wrote that Ambrose received him like a father, and at Easter in 387 Ambrose baptized him.

A year later, Christians in a town in the East burned a synagogue, and Theodosius ordered their bishop to rebuild it. Ambrose wrote that he would take the blame himself, and would not go to the altar until the emperor promised to change the order and end the inquiry.

Two years after that, a riot in Thessalonica, a large city of Macedonia, killed one of the emperor's commanders. Theodosius sent soldiers against the people, and many innocent were cut down. When he came to Milan, Ambrose stayed away and wrote to him privately. "I dare not offer the sacrifice if you mean to be there," he said, meaning he would not lead the communion service. "What is not allowed after the blood of one innocent person is shed, is it allowed after the blood of many? I do not think so."

Paulinus says Ambrose would not let the emperor into the church until he had done public penance, staying away from communion for the time set him. The emperor did it. Augustine wrote that the people, seeing their emperor lying on the ground, wept more than they had feared his anger. Theodoret, a Greek historian two generations later, has the two men meeting at the church door. Ambrose's letter and Paulinus do not. We cannot say which is right.

Paulinus says that singing began then, at Milan, and thirty years later it went on through almost all the provinces of the West. Historians in Latin and in Greek told the penance again and again.

When Ambrose lay ill, Paulinus tells, the city's leading men begged him to pray for a longer life. He answered, "I have not lived among you in such a way that I am ashamed to live. Nor am I afraid to die, because we have a good Lord." He died on the night of Good Friday, 397. Paulinus, who was there, says he prayed with his arms stretched out like a cross. His lips moved, but no one heard his voice.
