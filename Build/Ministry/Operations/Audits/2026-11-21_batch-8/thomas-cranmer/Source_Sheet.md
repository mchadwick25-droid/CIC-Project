# Source sheet: Thomas Cranmer

Researcher's sheet for batch 8 (2026-11-21). The job (Mark's, 2026-11-21): the archbishop of Canterbury who served Henry VIII, made the Book of Common Prayer of 1549 and 1552, recanted under Mary, then withdrew his recantations and was burned at Oxford in 1556, putting first into the fire the hand that had signed them; Foxe, a Protestant partisan, is credited for the death. ## Events

### Cambridge and the king's marriage

E1. **The supper at Waltham** (Foxe's story). Plague at Cambridge; Cranmer at Waltham with two pupils; the king's secretary and almoner lodged in the same house; asked of the king's cause, "It were better, as I suppose," quoth Dr. Cranmer, "that the question, whether a man may marry his brother's wife, or no? were decided and discussed by the divines, and by the authority of the word of God … than thus from year to year by frustratory delays to prolong the time" (FOXE8 1167–1174). Foxe dates it "a.d. 1529" (FOXE8 1123); COX-ed dates the stay at Waltham "In 1528" (COX 437). The king: "that that man hath the sow by the right ear" (FOXE8 1233). Witness: Foxe, partisan, about 35 years later. Confidence: Documented as Foxe's account; the year differs between Foxe (1529) and Cox (1528). For the card: the king's line.

E2. **Writing for the king.** Foxe: the king charged him to write his mind; Cranmer wrote "That the bishop of Rome had no such authority, as whereby he might dispense with the word of God and the Scripture" (FOXE8 1312–1315). Sent with the embassy to the pope, 1530 (FOXE8 1331–1335). Confidence: Documented as Foxe's account.

### Archbishop under Henry VIII

E3. **Made archbishop, 1533.** COX-ed: Warham died 23 August 1532; "Although he delayed his return to avoid the appointment, and manifested great reluctance … he was at length compelled to yield to the determination of the imperious monarch, and was consecrated on the 30th March, 1533" (COX 459–464). Witness: editor (his reluctance rests on Strype, COX 464–466 n.1). Confidence: Documented for the date; his reluctance, Inferential-Thin (editor's). Plain words: "archbishop of Canterbury, England's senior bishop" (the Tyndale story's gloss, CEN `william-tyndale`).

E4. **The sentence on the king's first marriage, May 1533** (own words). Letter to Archdeacon Hawkyns: "it was thought convenient by the king and his learned counsel, that I should repair unto Dunstable … where the said lady Katherine keepeth her house, and there to call her before me to hear the final sentence in the said matter. Notwithstanding, she would not at all obey thereunto … saying, that inasmuch as her cause was before the pope, she would have none other judge; and therefore would not take me for her judge" (COX 23797–23810). "And the morrow after Ascension-day I gave final sentence therein, how that it was indispensable for the pope to license any such marriages" (COX 23869–23870). COX-ed: "On the 23rd May, in the same year, the new archbishop pronounced judgment in favour of the king's divorce" (COX 466–467). Witness: own words. Confidence: Documented. Plain words: "ruled that the king's marriage to Katherine had never been lawful, because the pope had no power to allow it". In 1536 "the archbishop divorced her [Anne Boleyn], by command of Henry VIII" (COX-ed 479–481): Documented as the editor's; for the card.

E5. **The English Bible, 1537** (own words; link to Tyndale). Letter to Cromwell, 4 August 1537: "you shall receive by the bringer thereof a bible in English, both of a new translation and of a new print … And as for the translation, so far as I have read thereof, I like it better than any other translation heretofore made … I pray you, my lord, that you will exhibit the book unto the king's highness, and to obtain of his grace, if you can, a licence that the same may be sold and read of every person … until such time that we the bishops shall set forth a better translation, which I think will not be till a day after doomsday" (COX 32931–32961; date COX 32991–32992). Cox's note identifies the book as the 1537 Bible "by Thomas Matthew" (COX 32975–32977); COX-ed: "the bible of Tyndale's translation printed in English … he caused it to be set forth by the king's authority" (COX 484–486). CEN `william-tyndale` tells this event by role: "Within a year of Tyndale's death, the archbishop of Canterbury … praised a new English Bible … editors find that most of it was Tyndale's." Witness: own words; that it was Tyndale's work, editors. Confidence: Documented (letter); Widely Accepted (Tyndale's share). The "doomsday" line is not used anywhere on the tree: for the card.

E6. **Lambert, 1538: the record's force.** COX-ed: "It was in this year that Cranmer had to take a part in the argument against John Lambert on the bodily presence in the sacrament, the result of which was his condemnation and execution as a heretic; which is one of the grounds of accusation urged against the archbishop by his opponents" (COX 518–522). Foxe, his own partisan, says the same of him at his death: God purged "not only … his recantation, but also … his standing against John Lambert and master Allen, or if there were any other, with whose burning and blood his hands had been before any thing polluted" (FOXE8 7544–7548). Witness: a friendly editor and a friendly martyrologist both. Confidence: Documented that he argued against Lambert, who was burned; Foxe vol. V (the trial) not held. Also COX-ed 648–650 n.: the burning of Joan Bocher in 1550, and an editor's statement "relieving the archbishop from the allegation": Contested; for the card only.

### Edward VI: the Prayer Book

E7. **The 1549 book.** COX-ed: in 1548 "steps taken for converting the mass into a communion-service in English … the great work of producing the English Liturgy was finished, and received the final sanction of the legislature on the 15th of January, 1549" (COX 595–598). The book's title for the communion: "THE SUPPER OF THE LORD, AND THE HOLY COMMUNION, COMMONLY CALLED THE MASS" (KET 5273–5281). Who made it: CEN voices "chief architect of the Book of Common Prayer"; COX-ed prints its Preface among his writings as "attributed to him" (COX 48873–48876; index 53458–53462). Confidence: Widely Accepted that he led its making (census and editor); that he wrote the Preface, Widely Accepted (editor's attribution; unsigned).

E8. **The Preface: why English** (Quote 2). "And moreover, whereas St Paul would have such language spoken to the people in the church, as they might understand and have profit by hearing the same; the service in this Church of England (these many years) hath been read in Latin to the people, which they understood not; so that they have heard with their ears only; and their hearts, spirit, and mind, have not been edified thereby" (KET 1628–1637; the same words in COX 48897–48901). Witness: the book's own preface. Confidence: Documented.

E9. **One use for the realm.** "And where heretofore there hath been great diversity in saying and singing in churches within this realm: some following Salisbury use, some Hereford use, some the use of Bangor, some of York, and some of Lincoln: Now from henceforth, all the whole realm shall have but one use" (KET 1680–1685; COX 48943–48947). And: "the curates shall need none other books for their public service, but this book and the Bible" (KET 1691–1693). Plain words: "use" = the local form of the Latin service. Confidence: Documented. Paraphrase in the story; not a quote.

E10. **The rising of 1549** (conflict; the rebels' words as Cranmer quotes them). The eighth article of the rebels of Devon and Cornwall: "We will not receive the new service, because it is but like a Christmas game; but we will have our old service of matins, mass, even-song, and procession in Latin, as it was before. And so we the Cornish men, whereof certain of us understand no English, utterly refuse this new English" (COX 17555–17558). His answer: "I would gladly know the reason why the Cornish men refuse utterly the new English, as you call it, because certain of you understand it not; and yet you will have the service in Latin, which almost none of you understand" (COX 17561–17563). COX-ed: "in Devonshire and Cornwall active opposition was roused by the disaffected, and a formidable revolt broke out, which ended in the signal defeat of the rebels" (COX 599–602). Witness: the rebels' article as he printed it; his own reply; the editor for the outcome. Confidence: Documented. The number killed is not in a held source: do not give one.

E11. **The 1552 book.** COX-ed: "the English Liturgy underwent a revision at the hands of the archbishop, assisted by Ridley, and Cox … in which some of the suggestions of Peter Martyr, Martin Bucer, and others were adopted. The result … was, that the Book of Common Prayer was reduced very nearly into the form in which it stands at the present time" (COX 664–669). The 1552 title drops the word Mass: "THE ORDER FOR THE ADMINISTRATION OF THE LORD'S SUPPER OR HOLY COMMUNION" (KET 16017–16022). The words at the bread, 1552: "Take and eat this, in remembrance that Christ died for thee, and feed on him in thy heart by faith, with thanksgiving" (KET 16785–16787); the Order of Communion of 1548 had said "The body of our Lord Jesus Christ, which was given for thee, preserve thy body unto everlasting life" (KET 1195–1196). The 1552 Act requires every person to attend common prayer "upon pain of punishment by the censures of the Church" (KET 13350–13366). Witness: the books; the editor for his part in it. Confidence: Documented (texts); his leading the revision, Widely Accepted (editor).

### Mary: prison, the submissions, the death

E12. **Arrest and Oxford.** COX-ed: on Mary's accession "in the middle of the following September he was committed to the Tower … attainted of high treason by the parliament … in the month of March, 1554, he was removed to Oxford, with Ridley and Latimer, where disputations were held … On the 20th of April they were condemned, and excommunicated as heretics … eighteen months elapsed … before Ridley and Latimer were burned … five months … between their martyrdom and that of Cranmer" (COX 700–718). Foxe, as Cox's note quotes him: "the old grudges against the archbishop for the divorcement of her mother remained hid in the bottom of her heart … she remembered the state of religion changed; all which was reputed to the archbishop as the chief cause thereof" (COX 42709–42717). Witness: editor; Foxe for the queen's grudge. Confidence: Documented for the dates; the queen's motive, Contested (Foxe's claim). Plain word: "heretic" = "one condemned for teaching the church held false".

E13. **Ridley and Latimer burned, October 1555. He did not watch.** Foxe: "Master doctor Ridley, as he passed toward Bocardo, looked up where master Cranmer did lie, hoping belike to have seen him at the glass-window, and to have spoken unto him. But then master Cranmer was busy with friar Soto and his fellows, disputing together, so that he could not see him, through that occasion" (FOXE7 45574–45579). The census's own documented story says he "had watched Ridley and Latimer burn from the roof of his prison" (CEN tradition `documentedStories`, marked "from reference summaries; primary text not yet read"). No held source says he watched. Confidence: the census line is Not attested in the Library; Foxe says the reverse of the moment. Do not say.

E14. **Degraded; the appeal.** COX-ed: "It was not till Feb. 14th, 1556, that this degradation was carried into execution; and notwithstanding he appealed to a general council, after having formally withdrawn the recantations he had been induced to make, he was led to the stake" (COX 744–746). Plain word: "degraded" = "stripped of his rank as a priest and archbishop". Confidence: Documented (editor).

E15. **The pressure to recant** (Foxe). "the doctors and divines of Oxford busied themselves … about master Cranmer, to have him recant … they had him to the dean's house of Christ's Church … where he lacked no delicate fare, played at the bowls" (FOXE8 6847–6855). "They put him in hope, that he should not only have his life, but also be restored to his ancient dignity … only that he would set his name in two words to a little leaf of paper. But if he refused, there was no hope of health and pardon; for the queen was so purposed, that she would have Cranmer a catholic, or else no Cranmer at all" (FOXE8 6886–6898). Witness: Foxe, reporting what the persuaders said. Confidence: Documented as Foxe's account.

E16. **He signed** (Foxe's own uncertainty). "the archbishop, being overcome, whether through their importunity, or by his own imbecility, or of what mind I cannot tell, at length gave his hand. It might be supposed that it was done for the hope of life … but, as we may since perceive by the letter of his sent to a lawyer, the most cause why he desired his time to be delayed, was that he would make an end of Marcus Antonius [his answer to Gardiner's book] … But howsoever it was, plain it was, to be against his conscience" (FOXE8 6926–6934). Plain words: "recant" = "to take back his teaching in writing". Confidence: that he signed, Documented; his reason, Contested (Foxe himself cannot tell, then offers the book; his own last speech says "for fear of death", E20).

E17. **The submissions as Bonner printed them.** Title: "All the Submissions and Recantations of Thomas Cranmer, late Archbishop of Canterbury, truly set forth both in Latin and English, agreeable to the Originals, written and subscribed with his own hand. VISUM ET EXAMINATUM PER … DOMINUM EDMUNDUM EPISCOPUM LONDON. ANNO MDLVI" (COX 51905–51911). Six follow. The first: "I am content to submit myself to their laws herein, and to take the pope for chief head of this church of England, so far as God's laws and the laws and customs of this realm will permit" (COX 51918–51921). The second, without that limit: "I, Thomas Cranmer … do submit myself to the catholic church of Christ, and to the pope, supreme head of the same church" (COX 51928–51930). The fifth (Latin), witnessed by Villa Garcia and Sydall, accepts the pope as Christ's vicar, transubstantiation and purgatory (COX 51966–52014); Foxe prints its English (FOXE8 6958–7000). The sixth (Latin), dated 18 March: "agnosco me omnium maximum peccatorem" (I acknowledge myself the greatest of all sinners) (COX 52029), and blames his part in the divorce as "omnium hujus regni malorum et calamitatum seminarium" (the seedbed of all this realm's evils) (COX 52038–52040). Witness: the hostile government print. Confidence: Documented that these were printed as his; how many he truly signed, and who drafted the last, Contested (E18).

E18. **How many, and whose words: the editors differ.** FOXE8-ed quoting Strype: "a fourth and a fifth paper of recantation was demanded, … and lastly a sixth"; on the last morning "the friars brought him yet a seventh declaration to subscribe, which he rejected"; and Southey: "The probability is that Cranmer signed an equivocal Recantation, and that the other papers, five in number … were fabricated by Bonner's direction" (FOXE8 7010–7023). COX quoting Todd: "That this outrageous composition [the sixth] was drawn up by Pole, Strype long since assumed" (COX 52294–52296). Witness: modern editors only. Confidence: Contested. The story says "a series of submissions, six in the bishop of London's printed book" and does not settle authorship.

E19. **The morning, 21 March 1556** (Foxe). The friar brought "a paper with articles, which Cranmer should openly profess in his recantation before the people", which he copied and signed; "But yet the archbishop … put secretly in his bosom his prayer with his exhortation written in another paper" (FOXE8 7079–7094). "Cranmer at length cometh from the prison of Bocardo unto St. Mary's church (the chief church in the university), because it was a foul and rainy day" (FOXE8 7118–7120). "There was a stage set over against the pulpit" (FOXE8 7126). "He that late was archbishop, metropolitan, and primate of England, and the king's privy councillor, being now in a bare and ragged gown, and ill favouredly clothed, with an old square cap" (FOXE8 7132–7136). Witness: Foxe. Confidence: Documented as Foxe's account.

E20. **The speech: the renunciation and the hand** (Quote 1). After the prayer, the exhortations and the creed: "And now I come to the great thing, which so much troubleth my conscience, more than any thing that ever I did or said in my whole life, and that is the setting abroad of a writing contrary to the truth; which now here I renounce and refuse, as things written with my hand, contrary to the truth which I thought in my heart, and written for fear of death, and to save my life if it might be; and that is, all such bills and papers which I have written or signed with my hand since my degradation; wherein I have written many things untrue. And forasmuch as my hand offended, writing contrary to my heart, my hand shall first be punished therefor; for, may I come to the fire, it shall be first burned. And as for the pope, I refuse him, as Christ's enemy, and antichrist, with all his false doctrine" (FOXE8 7394–7407; OCR "thouarht" = thought, "oflended" = offended, "pimished" = punished; marginal notes interleaved). Then: "And as for the sacrament, I believe as I have taught in my book against the bishop of Winchester" (FOXE8 7409–7412). **Damaged scan.** Confirmed by a second held print: Perkins (1600), "And now (saith hee) I come to the great thing that so much troubleth my conscience more then any thing that euer I did or said in all my life, & that is the setting abroad of a writing contrarie to the truth; which nowe here I renounce as things written with my hand contrary to the truth which I thought in my heart, & that for feare of death and to saue my life, &c. and forasmuch as my hand offended writing contrary to my heart, my hand shall be first punished therefore: for may I come to the fire it shall be first burned" (PERK 44206–44211, read through the OCR). Perkins orders "shall be first punished"; Foxe "shall first be punished": no change of sense. Witness: Foxe, partisan; Perkins copies Foxe's text, so he is not independent. Confidence: Documented as Foxe's account; that he publicly withdrew the recantations is confirmed by the hostile register (E22).

E21. **The other account: Bonner's book prints the prepared speech.** Bonner's book prints "The Prayer and Saying of Thomas Cranmer, a little before his death, all written with his own hand", which matches Foxe's text through the creed, then ends: "And now I come to the great thing that so much troubleth my conscience … and that is, setting abroad untrue books and writings, contrary to the truth of God's word; which now I renounce and condemn … they be the books which I wrote against the sacrament of the altar sith the death of king Henry VIII. … I say and believe that our Saviour Christ Jesu is really and substantially contained in the blessed sacrament of the altar, under the forms of bread and wine" (COX 52089–52091, 52176–52190; printed by "Johannis Cawodi Typographi Regiae Majestatis. Anno MD.LVI", COX 52193–52195). Cox's note: "the report here printed, as published by Boner, agrees with Foxe's account, except in the last paragraph and the line immediately preceding it" (COX 52125–52128). Todd (quoted by Cox): Bonner "prints only what the martyr was to have spoken, but basely conceals the fact that he did not speak it" (COX 52360–52362). Witness: hostile print; the editor's judgment. Confidence: Documented that two printed versions differ at exactly the turn; which one he spoke is settled by E22.

E22. **The hostile register: he revoked them in public.** Bonner's own Register, as Cox quotes it: "idem Cranmer tempore ejusdem concremationis, et immediate ante illam suam concremationem, publice revocavit recantationes suas antea per eum factas, persistendo in erroribus et haeresibus suis" (the same Cranmer, at the time of his burning and immediately before it, publicly revoked the recantations he had made before, persisting in his errors and heresies) (COX 52204–52210; OCR "erroribtis"). Also a Protestant, Sampson, 1566: "ille fortiter reclamabat vivens pernegabatque" (he protested strongly while living and denied it) (COX 52212–52216). And a London chronicle of 1559, quoted in Todd's note: "after that he had recanted his supposed recantation, was brent at Oxford" (COX 52402–52406). Witness: his enemies' own record (via the editor's quotation; the Register is not held). Confidence: Documented. This is the line that lets the story tell the reversal as fact, and the hand as Foxe's.

E23. **The crowd and the stage** (Foxe). "Here the standers-by were all astonied, marvelled, were amazed, did look one upon another, whose expectation he had so notably deceived. Some began to admonish him of his recantation, and to accuse him of falsehood" (FOXE8 7415–7420). His answer: "Always since I lived hitherto, I have been a hater of falsehood, and a lover of simplicity, and never before this time have I dissembled" (FOXE8 7455–7458). "specially Cole cried out upon him, 'Stop the heretic's mouth, and take him away.' And then Cranmer being pulled down from the stage, was led to the fire" (FOXE8 7462–7465). Cole was the preacher of the sermon (FOXE8 7044–7047). Confidence: Documented as Foxe's account.

E24. **The stake and the hand** (Foxe). At "the place where the holy bishops and martyrs of God, Hugh Latimer and Nicholas Ridley, were burnt before him" (FOXE8 7482–7484). "And when the wood was kindled, and the fire began to burn near him, stretching out his arm, he put his right hand into the flame, which he held so steadfast and immovable (saving that once with the same hand he wiped his face), that all men might see his hand burned before his body was touched … his eyes were lifted up into heaven, and oftentimes he repeated 'his unworthy right hand,' so long as his voice would suffer him; and using often the words of Stephen, 'Lord Jesus, receive my spirit,' in the greatness of the flame he gave up the ghost" (FOXE8 7516–7527). Perkins: "when he was at the fire, first he burnt his right hand" (PERK 44212). Witness: Foxe (Perkins follows him). Confidence: Documented as Foxe's account. The anonymous Catholic eyewitness letter that would confirm the hand is not held (gaps).

E25. **A friar's reading, and Foxe's verdict.** "friar John … thinking it came not of fortitude, but of desperation … ran to the lord Williams of Thame, crying that the archbishop was vexed in mind, and died in great desperation. But he … smiled only" (FOXE8 7529–7535). Foxe: "much more worthy the name of St. Thomas of Canterbury, than he whom the pope falsely before did canonize" (FOXE8 7550–7552). Witness: Foxe. For the card.

## Impact, heaviest first (what later witnesses carried on and used)

I1. **The Prayer Book outlived him.** Elizabeth's book of 1559 was made from his of 1552: CLAY-ed calls it a copy "varying … by no means an unimportant, point from its predecessor of 1552" and cites "a list of differences between Edward's of 1552 and her own" (CLAY 441–444, 486–488). CEN tradition longDescription: "The Book of Common Prayer, first issued in 1549 and revised in 1552, 1559 and 1662, which fixed the words the whole country was required to say together, week after week." CEN tradition `legacy`: "It became the Church of England, and through it the worldwide Anglican Communion … The rhythms of the Book of Common Prayer … shaped worship in English." Words still heard: KET 1549 marriage, "till death us depart" (KET 7934); burial, "earth to earth, ashes to ashes" (KET 8712). Witness: the books; the editor; the census's sourced entry. Confidence: Documented (1559 from 1552); Widely Accepted (the wider legacy, census).

I2. **Foxe made his death a memory.** CEN tradition voices: "John Foxe — martyrologist whose Acts and Monuments gave English Protestantism its memory of the Marian burnings". Foxe closes: "of whom this archbishop was the last, being burnt about the very middle time of the reign of that queen, and almost the very middle man of all the martyrs which were burned in all her reign besides" (FOXE8 7555–7562). Perkins, 1600, teaching on how God lets the faithful fall, uses Cranmer: God "left him for a while to fall from his religion, and to make a dangerous recantation: but so as thereby he prevented many sins, and prepared him to a glorious martyrdom" (PERK 44201–44205). Confidence: Documented.

I3. **His enemies printed the fall; his later enemies kept it.** Bonner's book of 1556 (E17, E21). Three centuries on, Newman, newly Catholic, writes "anathema to a whole tribe of Cranmers, Ridleys, Latimers, and Jewels!" (NEW 10433–10436). Confidence: Documented. Newman is for the card, not the story (name count).

I4. **The English Bible licensed** (link; E5). Confidence: Documented (letter). One clause.

## Where sources disagree or come late

D1. **The hand rests on Foxe.** The renunciation is told in Foxe and confirmed in Bonner's hostile Register (E20, E22). The hand in the fire is in Foxe alone among held texts (E24); Perkins copies Foxe. Credit Foxe.
D2. **Two printed speeches.** Bonner's book ends the speech with belief in the real presence; Foxe's ends with the renunciation, the hand and the pope refused (E21). Both credited; the Register settles that he revoked (E22).
D3. **How many submissions, and who wrote them.** Six in Bonner's book; a seventh on the last morning, Strype says; most fabricated, Southey guessed; the sixth drafted by Pole, Strype assumed (E18). Editors only: Contested.
D4. **Why he signed.** Foxe cannot tell, then offers his wish to finish a book; his own speech in Foxe says "for fear of death, and to save my life if it might be" (E16, E20). Contested; give his own words, credited.
D5. **The queen's motive.** Foxe: revenge for her mother's divorce (E12; FOXE8 7031–7033). Credited, or left out.
D6. **Waltham, 1528 or 1529** (E1).
D7. **Did he watch Ridley and Latimer burn?** The census story says so; Foxe says Ridley looked up and could not see him (E13).
D8. **Who wrote the Prayer Book.** Unsigned; led by him by the census and his editor; the Preface "attributed to him" (E7).
