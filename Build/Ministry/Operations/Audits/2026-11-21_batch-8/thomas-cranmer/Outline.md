# Outline: Thomas Cranmer

Sheet items (S, E, I, L, D) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives the file and line for each. COX is the Parker Society *Miscellaneous Writings and Letters* (1846), with Bonner's printed book of the submissions in its appendix; FOXE8 and FOXE7 are Foxe's *Acts and Monuments* vols. VIII and VII; KET the Prayer Books of 1549 and 1552; CLAY the book of 1559; PERK Perkins (1600). Cranmer wrote in English; every quote is rendered from the sixteenth-century English as these prints hold it.

- **The essence:** Thomas Cranmer was archbishop of Canterbury from 1533 under Henry VIII, and under the boy king Edward VI he led the making of the Book of Common Prayer of 1549 and 1552, which put the church's services into English and gave the whole realm one form of worship (S3, E3, E7–E9, E11; Documented for the books and the office; that he led their making, Widely Accepted, the census and his editor). Under the Catholic queen Mary he signed a series of recantations, then at Oxford in 1556 publicly took them back and was burned; Foxe, the Protestant writer who collected the stories of those burned under Mary, tells that he put into the fire first the hand that had signed them (E17, E20, E22, E24; the recantations and their withdrawal, Documented, his enemies' own print and register; the hand, Documented as Foxe's account).
- **Also told:**
  - **The king's servant.** A Cambridge scholar drawn into Henry VIII's attempt to end his first marriage, he gave the sentence in May 1533 that the marriage had never been lawful, because the pope had no power to allow it (E1, E4; Documented, his own letter). In 1537 he asked the king's minister to win a licence for an English Bible, most of it Tyndale's work (E5; Documented, his letter; Tyndale's share Widely Accepted). And he took part in the case against a man burned as a heretic in 1538, which even Foxe counts against him (E6; Documented, his editor and Foxe).
  - **The Prayer Book.** Its preface said the people had heard the Latin service "with their ears only"; from now on, it said, the whole realm would have one form (E8, E9; Documented). Rebels in Devon and Cornwall rose against it in 1549, calling it "like a Christmas game", and were defeated (E10; Documented, their article as he printed it; the outcome, his editor). The 1552 book went further and dropped the word Mass (E11; Documented).
  - **The fall and the stand.** Imprisoned, tried and stripped of his rank, he signed submissions that the bishop of London printed; Foxe says he could not tell why, and Cranmer's last speech says it was for fear of death (E14–E17, E20; Documented as each one's words; his motive Contested). On the last morning he renounced them instead of reading the text prepared for him; his enemies' register records that he did so (E19–E22; Documented).
  - **What came of him.** Elizabeth's Prayer Book of 1559 was made from his of 1552, and the book went on into the Church of England's worship (I1; Documented, the editor of 1559; the wider legacy Widely Accepted, the census). Foxe's book made his death part of English Protestant memory (I2; Documented).
- **Length:** about 4 minutes (about 600 words at 150 a minute; never past about 675). Counted first: the quotes, about 80 words (Quote 1 about 30, Quote 2 about 43, the rebels' phrase 5); the plain sense of the words to explain, about 55 words (archbishop of Canterbury, the Latin service and its local forms, the Mass, recant, heretic, stripped of his rank, the real presence if used); the witnesses credited, about 60 words (Foxe with who he was, the bishop of London's printed book, the bishop's own register, the rebels, his editor once). That is about 195 words before any event is told, leaving about 400 for the life. Why four minutes and not three: the job names four turns (Henry's service, the two books, the recantations, the fire), and the end is told with two printed accounts that differ, then a hostile record that settles it. Cut first, if needed: Waltham (beat 1) to one clause; the 1552 words at the bread (beat 4); the ragged gown and the stage (beat 6) to one clause; Cole's shout. Never cut: the Lambert clause, the rebels, the fear of death, the two accounts.
- **Setting:**
  - Time: archbishop 30 March 1533 (S3, E3); the sentence on the marriage, May 1533 (E4); the English Bible licensed 1537 (E5); Lambert, 1538 (E6); Edward VI from 1547; the Prayer Book 1549, the rising 1549, the revised book 1552 (E7–E11); Mary's reign from 1553; the Tower 1553, Oxford March 1554, condemned April 1554 (E12); Ridley and Latimer burned 1555 (E13); stripped of his rank 14 February 1556 (E14); burned Saturday 21 March 1556, at 66 (S3; "in the 67th year of his age", COX-ed 747).
  - Place: Cambridge (S1); Canterbury and Lambeth (his see and house); Dunstable (E4); Oxford: the prison called Bocardo, the university church of St Mary, and the stake outside the north wall of the city (E19, E22, E24). Plain words: "Oxford", "the university church of St Mary", "a prison by the city gate" (Bocardo need not be named). Modern country: England, United Kingdom (S2).
  - Tradition: the tree's spoken name is "the English Reformation" (S2; census informalName). Region for the entry: England.
  - Language: he wrote and spoke in English; two of the submissions are in Latin (E17).

**Words to explain** (the first time each is heard; plain word or short gloss, worked into the sentence)

- **archbishop of Canterbury**: "archbishop of Canterbury, England's senior bishop" (the Tyndale story's gloss).
- **the Latin service / "use"** (E9): "until then the service was said in Latin, and each region had its own form of it". Do not say "use" bare.
- **Book of Common Prayer**: "a book of prayer for the whole country, its services set out in English". "Common" means shared; say it so once if needed: "prayer said in common".
- **the Mass**: "the Mass, the old Latin service of the sacrament". In 1552: "the new book dropped the word Mass" (E11).
- **heretic** (E6, E12, E23): "condemned for teaching the church held false" (the Tyndale story's form).
- **recant / recantation / submission** (E15–E17): "to take back his teaching in writing"; "papers taking back what he had taught". Say "submissions" only as "papers of submission to the pope and the old faith".
- **stripped of his rank** for "degraded" (E14): "stripped of his rank as priest and archbishop". Never "degraded" bare.
- **the pope's authority**: "the pope's rule over the church in England".
- **the sacrament of the altar / real presence** (E21), if the Bonner version is quoted in substance: "that Christ's body is truly present in the bread". Better told in plain words than named.
- **"like a Christmas game"** (E10): the narrator gives the plain sense, "no better than a Christmas pastime".
- **martyr** (I2): "someone put to death for the faith".

- **Shape:** Founder or leader. What he built: the English Prayer Book, one service for the whole realm, in its two forms of 1549 and 1552; how it ran: required by law, read in every parish church, resisted by force in the West; who it served: people who had heard the service in a language they did not understand. The impact is in what outlasted him: the 1559 book made from his, and the memory of his death. The trap is titles and offices in place of acts: tell what he did (gave the sentence, asked for the Bible, made the book, answered the rebels, signed, took it back), not the titles he held.
- **Sibling:** none in the batch. On the tree, `william-tyndale` (live, read) shares one event: the 1537 Bible that "the archbishop of Canterbury … praised" and the king allowed to be read, told by role and with no quote. Its job is the translation and its readers; here the event is one clause from Cranmer's side (beat 2), and no Tyndale quote is used. The census tradition entry's own documented story ("Cranmer Puts His Right Hand in the Fire First", in `documentedStories`, not a live story) quotes the hand line; it is not a longDescription, and its "watched Ridley and Latimer burn from the roof" is not held (Do not say).
- **Openings:**
  1. **Chosen.** The Prayer Book's own preface (Quote 2; E8). In about three sentences: Quote 2, then "So said the preface of a new English prayer book in 1549", then "Its chief maker was Thomas Cranmer, archbishop of Canterbury, England's senior bishop", straight into the essence. It opens on the book, the thing he built, and keeps the fire for the end.
  2. Not chosen: the rainy morning at St Mary's, the old archbishop in a ragged gown on a stage, the paper hidden in his clothes (E19). Strong, but it would tell the ending first and then go back; it belongs at beat 6.

**Story beats** (the opening, the essence and beat 1 make the first minute, about 150 words; beats 2 to 6 follow the life once, in order. The essence reaches ahead to the fire; the fire itself is told once, at the Ending)

1. **Cambridge and the king's question.** A Cambridge scholar, he was drawn in when Henry VIII sought to end his marriage to his first queen. Foxe tells that at a supper in a country house, during a plague at Cambridge, he said the question should be settled by the word of God and the universities, not by years of delay at Rome; the king sent for him (E1, E2; Documented as Foxe's account; say "about 1529": Foxe gives 1529, his editor 1528, D6). One or two sentences.
2. **Archbishop, and the king's servant.** Made archbishop in 1533 (E3). In May he sat as judge at Dunstable, where the queen refused to come, saying her case was before the pope; he gave sentence that the marriage had never been lawful, because the pope had no power to allow it (E4; his own letter; Documented). Reason: the king wanted it ended; the sentence is his own words, paraphrased. In 1537 he asked the king's chief minister to win the king's leave for an English Bible to be sold and read by everyone; most of it, editors find, was Tyndale's work (E5; link; one clause; the share is the editors', as the Tyndale story credits it). In 1538 he argued against a man named Lambert in a trial over the sacrament; Lambert was burned as a heretic, and even Foxe, his admirer, counted that blood on his hands (E6; Documented; say it plainly, do not soften).
3. **The Prayer Book, 1549.** Under the boy king Edward VI, from 1547, he led the making of the Book of Common Prayer (E7; Widely Accepted). The preface (Quote 2, already heard in the opening) gave its reasons: the people had heard the Latin "with their ears only", and each region had had its own form; now the whole realm would have one (E8, E9; Documented; E9 paraphrased, not quoted). The parish priest needed only this book and the Bible (E9). In Devon and Cornwall rebels rose against it: they wanted their old Latin service back and called the new one "like a Christmas game"; some Cornish men, they said, understood no English (E10). He answered that they wanted Latin, which almost none of them understood (E10; his own words, paraphrased). The rising was put down (E10; editor; no number of dead).
4. **The second book, 1552.** He revised it, his editor says, with other reformers' advice (E11; Widely Accepted). The new book no longer called the service the Mass, and at the bread it said to take and eat in remembrance that Christ died for you, and to feed on him in your heart by faith (E11; Documented, KET; paraphrase, cut first). The law required everyone to attend (E11). **What came 1 rides here:** after Mary's reign, Elizabeth's book of 1559 was made from this one, and through the Church of England its words shaped worship in English for centuries (I1; say "Elizabeth's book of 1559 was made from it", CLAY-ed; the legacy clause from the census tradition entry). One or two sentences, then back to 1553.
5. **Mary: prison and the papers.** When Edward died in 1553 and his Catholic sister Mary became queen, Cranmer was sent to the Tower, condemned for treason, then taken to Oxford, and in 1554 condemned as a heretic (E12; Documented, editor). Two fellow reforming bishops were burned there in 1555 (E13; by role; Do not say he watched). In February 1556 he was stripped of his rank (E14). Then the pressure, as Foxe tells it: comfort and promises at a college dean's house, and a choice between his life and the fire; the queen, he was told, would have him a Catholic or no Cranmer at all (E15; Foxe's report of what was said; credit Foxe). He signed a series of papers taking back his teaching and accepting the pope; the bishop of London printed six of them, the last calling himself the worst of all sinners (E17; Documented as the bishop's printed book; that he wrote every word, Contested, Do not say). Why he signed, Foxe says he cannot tell (E16; Contested). The queen did not relent (E16, Foxe; credit).
6. **St Mary's, 21 March 1556.** On a foul, rainy morning, Foxe writes, he was brought to the university church and set on a stage to hear a sermon before his burning, in a ragged gown, with a paper hidden in his clothes (E19). He prayed, then came to "the great thing" that troubled his conscience: the papers he had signed, which he now renounced, written, he said, for fear of death, to save his life if he could (E20; Foxe's text; paraphrase this part). Then Quote 1. He refused the pope (E20). **The two accounts, credited here (What came 2):** the bishop of London's printed book gives his speech ending instead with the faith he had been meant to declare; Foxe's gives the renunciation and the hand; and the bishop's own register records that just before the burning he publicly took back his recantations (E21, E22; Documented; D2). The crowd, Foxe says, was amazed; the preacher cried out to stop the heretic's mouth, and he was pulled down from the stage and led out to the fire (E23; Foxe; Cole by role, cut first).

**What came of him** (about a third of the story, told where it is strongest, not as a block; heaviest first)

1. **The Prayer Book outlived him.** Elizabeth's book of 1559 was made from his of 1552 (I1; CLAY-ed). Optional, one clause where it fits at beat 4: the church it served became the Church of England, and through it the worldwide Anglican Communion, and the book's rhythms shaped worship in English (census tradition `legacy`, in its own words; source named in the outline only). Placed at beat 4.
2. **His enemies printed the fall; their own register recorded the reversal.** The bishop of London's book of the submissions, and the prepared speech ending, against the register's line that he publicly revoked them (E17, E21, E22). Placed at beat 6.
3. **Foxe made his death a memory.** Foxe tells the death as a martyr's, counts him the last of the learned martyrs of Mary's reign, and his account was what later Protestants carried: a Cambridge preacher in 1600 quoted the hand speech to teach how God lets the faithful fall and raises them (I2; Perkins by role). Placed at the Ending, in the clause that names Foxe as the witness, or for the card.
4. **The English Bible licensed** (E5). Placed at beat 2, one clause.

Defended: the English service, against the rebels' demand for Latin (E10); his last confession, against the paper he was meant to read (E20).

**Quotes** (2, and one short phrase)

1. **His last speech, as Foxe prints it** (FOXE8 7403–7404; **damaged scan**: OCR "oflended", "pimished", marginal notes run into the lines. Confirmed by another held print, Perkins 1600, PERK 44210–44212, also a damaged scan, which gives the same words with "shall be first punished"). Original: "And forasmuch as my hand offended, writing contrary to my heart, my hand shall first be punished therefor; for, may I come to the fire, it shall be first burned." Rendering: "My hand did wrong when it wrote against my heart. So my hand shall be punished for it first. If I come to the fire, it shall be burned first." Job: beat 6, the turn of the story in his own words, credited to Foxe's account; it sets up the Ending without telling it. Bonner's printed version does not contain these words (E21): the card should print Foxe's English and say so.
2. **The Preface to the Prayer Book, 1549** (KET 1630–1647, clean, across a page break; the same words in COX 48899–48901, where Cox prints the preface as "attributed to him"). Original: "the service in this Church of England (these many years) hath been read in Latin to the people, which they understood not; so that they have heard with their ears only; and their hearts, spirit, and mind, have not been edified thereby." Rendering: "For many years the service in this Church of England has been read to the people in Latin, which they did not understand. So they have heard with their ears only. Their hearts, spirit and mind have not been built up by it." Job: the opening; it says in the book's own words what the book was for, which the narrator's paraphrase cannot. Credit it to the book's preface, not to Cranmer by name (D8).

Short phrase in the narrator's sentence: **"like a Christmas game"** (COX 17555; the rebels' eighth article as Cranmer printed it in his answer). Plain sense given beside it: "no better than a Christmas pastime". Job: beat 3, the rebels' own scorn for the book.

**Connection to carry into the story:** what passed from Cranmer was the English Prayer Book: one service for the whole country, in a language people understood, required in every parish. The listener needs three things. First, what changed: before, the service was in Latin, which most could not follow, and each region had its own form of it; his book put it into English and made one form for all (E8, E9). Second, that it was imposed and resisted, not simply welcomed: the law required attendance, and the West rose against it (E10, E11). Third, how it outlasted him: Mary restored the old faith, but after her, Elizabeth's book of 1559 was made from his of 1552, and it went on to shape worship in English (I1). Tell it through what he did and what the books said, at beats 3 and 4, never as a "this matters because" sentence. The link to Tyndale is the same work at its start: he asked for the English Bible, most of it Tyndale's, to be read by all (E5).

**Ending:** close on the person, at the fire, in Foxe's account (E24): at the place where the two other bishops had been burned, when the fire was lit he stretched out his arm and held his right hand in the flame, still, so that all could see it burn before his body; he kept repeating the words "unworthy right hand", as long as his voice allowed, and, using the words of Stephen, the first martyr, "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit" (Foxe reports the first in his own words, "his unworthy right hand"; do not turn it into "this unworthy right hand", which is the census summary's wording, not Foxe's) (FOXE8 7482–7484, 7516–7527; Documented as Foxe's account; give it in Foxe's voice: "Foxe writes", once, at the start of the scene). End there. No line after it about what it meant; Foxe's verdict ("more worthy the name of St Thomas of Canterbury") and the friar's "desperation" are for the card.
