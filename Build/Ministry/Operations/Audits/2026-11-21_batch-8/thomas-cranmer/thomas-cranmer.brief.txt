"For many years the service in this Church of England has been read to the people in Latin, which they did not understand. So they have heard with their ears only. Their hearts, spirit and mind have not been built up by it." So said the preface of a new English prayer book in 1549. The man who led its making was Thomas Cranmer, archbishop of Canterbury, England's senior bishop.

He held that office from 1533, under King Henry VIII. Under the boy king Edward VI, he led the making of the Book of Common Prayer, a book of prayer for the whole country, its services set out in English. Under Queen Elizabeth, his book of 1552 became the book of 1559, and it went on to shape worship in English. But under the Catholic queen Mary, he signed a series of papers taking back his teaching, and then, at Oxford in 1556, withdrew them in public and was burned. John Foxe, a Protestant who collected the stories of those burned under Mary, tells that he put into the fire first the hand that had signed them.

A Cambridge scholar, he was drawn into the king's wish to end his marriage to his first queen. In May 1533 he sat as judge at Dunstable, and he wrote that he gave sentence that the marriage had never been lawful, because the pope had no power to allow it. In 1537 he asked the king's chief minister to win the king's leave for an English Bible, most of it, editors find, the work of William Tyndale. In 1538 he argued against a man named Lambert, tried over the bread and wine of communion. Lambert was condemned for teaching what the church held false, and he was burned. Even Foxe, Cranmer's admirer, counted that blood on his hands.

Then, from 1547, came the boy king and the prayer book. Until then the service was said in Latin, and each region had its own form of it. Now the whole realm would have one, in English, and the law required everyone to attend. In 1549, rebels in Devon and Cornwall rose against it. They wanted their old Latin service back, and they called the new one "like a Christmas game," no better than a Christmas entertainment. Cranmer printed their article with his reply, that they asked for Latin, which almost none of them understood. The rising was put down.

When Edward died in 1553, his Catholic sister Mary became queen. At Oxford in 1554 he was condemned for teaching what the church held false, and two fellow reforming bishops were burned there in 1555. In February 1556 he was stripped of his rank as priest and archbishop. Then, Foxe says, he was offered a choice between his life and the fire. Cranmer signed a series of papers taking back his teaching and accepting the pope's rule over the church in England, and the bishop of London printed six of them. Why he signed, Foxe says he cannot tell.

On the rainy morning of 21 March 1556, he was brought to the university church of St Mary to hear a sermon before his burning. He did not read the text prepared for him. Instead he renounced the papers he had signed, written, he said, for fear of death, to save his life if he could. Foxe prints his words: "My hand did wrong when it wrote against my heart. So my hand shall be punished for it first. If I come to the fire, it shall be burned first."

Here the record divides. The bishop of London's printed book gives a speech that ends instead with the faith Cranmer had been meant to declare. Foxe gives the renunciation and the hand. Yet the bishop's own register records that just before the burning, Cranmer publicly took the papers back.

It stood outside the city's north wall, where the other two bishops had burned. When the fire was lit, Foxe says, he stretched out his arm and held his right hand in the flame, still, so that all could see it burn before his body. He kept repeating the words "unworthy right hand" as long as his voice allowed. And in the words of Stephen, the first martyr, he said: "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit."
