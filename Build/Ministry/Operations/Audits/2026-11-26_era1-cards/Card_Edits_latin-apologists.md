## Shown dates

Census `dates`: `c. 197-320`. Proposed: **`c. 200 to early 4th c.`**, a change, by the date rule.

- Start: Jerome says Tertullian flourished chiefly under Severus and Antoninus Caracalla (NPNF2-3 40498-40499), reigns Widely Accepted as 193 to 217; the brief's "around 200". The Chronicle sets Tertullian "celebrated in the speech of all the churches" in Severus's sixteenth year (JerChron 43480-43483), about 208 counting from 193: a conversion, shown on the timeline. 197 is the year the ANF and NPNF editors give for the Apology (ANF3 1443-1446; NPNF2-1 10297-10303), a modern reckoning, and is not used.
- End: the last event the sources date is the first day of the persecution at Nicomedia, "in the eighth consulship of Diocletian and seventh of Maximian" (ANF7 10161), which is 303 by the consuls, while Lactantius taught there and then wrote. Jerome puts Lactantius's tutoring of Crispus "in his extreme old age" (NPNF2-3 41052-41054) and files Arnobius in Constantine's twenty-first year (JerChron 45066), so the record runs into the early fourth century, and no source gives a single last year. 320 rests on no held line (Open-Gaps 429).
- `start` 197 and `end` 320 only place the entry on the map and are not edited here (the batch-11 release held `start` 200 for its river-origin effect).

## shortDescription

> At Carthage, Rome and Nicomedia, Christian writers defended their faith in Latin to educated people who looked down on it. They answered the charges against Christians from the pagans' own books.

Two sentences, 31 words. No negative or contrast, no dash, no placeholder opening: it opens on the places. People named as what they were: Christian writers (a pleader, teachers of rhetoric, a presbyter), the educated. It follows the brief's paragraph 2 ("they defended their faith in Latin to the educated who despised it ... theirs answered the charges out of the pagans' own books"); "looked down on" replaces "despised" to meet the reading floor. Sicca is left to the timeline. Readability alone: grade 9.9 (names out 8.4), ease 62.5: pass.

## uncertain

Mapped across word for word from the batch-11 card (14 items). Each checked against the final brief and the census `longDescription`: none contradicts them. No word changed. Item 1 already says no old source dates the Octavius.

1. When Minucius wrote. No old source fixes the date of the Octavius. Lactantius names Minucius before Tertullian, and Jerome names him after.
2. When Arnobius taught. Jerome's Lives of Illustrious Men puts him at Sicca in the reign of Diocletian; Jerome's Chronicle files the same entry among the years of Constantine. Arnobius himself writes that Christians had been in the world almost three hundred years, something less or more.
3. How Arnobius became a Christian. The dreams, the bishop's refusal and the books written as hostages rest on Jerome's Chronicle alone, written about eighty years later.
4. Arnobius's pupil. That Lactantius was Arnobius's pupil rests on Jerome alone, in his Lives and in his letter to Magnus.
5. The charges in full. Caecilius says the Christians gather the dregs of the people, the unlearned and credulous women; that they meet at night; that they worship the head of an ass, and a man punished for his crime, with the wood of his cross; and that they have no altars, temples or images. For the feast he cites a speech of the orator Fronto. Tertullian says Cornelius Tacitus first suggested the ass's head.
6. Caecilius's own words. He says that as Octavius is his conqueror, so he triumphs over error, and puts off what remains until the next day, since the sun is setting.
7. The monthly gift. Tertullian says it also kept old people confined to the house, the shipwrecked, and any in the mines, banished to the islands or shut up in prison for their faith.
8. Tertullian's other quarrels. He names Justin, philosopher and martyr, among the earlier writers against the heretics, and he wrote against Marcion, a teacher who brought in two Gods, the Creator and a god of his own.
9. What Arnobius answered. His books answer the charge that since the Christians came the world has gone to ruin and the gods have left the earth. He asks why the Christians' writings are given to the flames and their meetings broken up, where prayer is made for all in authority, for soldiers, kings, friends and enemies, the living and the dead.
10. Lactantius on the learned. He says the wise and the learned give the Scriptures no credit, because the prophets spoke in common and simple language, so that wisdom and truth need fit heralds; the learned who came over had not been enough to defend it.
11. Lactantius at Nicomedia. Jerome says he had few pupils there, since it was a Greek city, and so turned to writing.
12. Commodian. Only Gennadius tells of him: a man of secular learning who read the Christians' writings, accepted the faith, and wrote against the pagans in barely tolerable semi-versified language. His date is not known.
13. Three persons. Augustine later wrote that many Latin writers of authority said three persons, because they could find no fitter way to put in words what they understood without words. He names none of them.
14. The list of books not to be received. It is carried under the name of Gelasius, and in a second form under Hormisdas; who made it, and when, is disputed.
