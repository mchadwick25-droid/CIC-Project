## Shown dates

Census `dates`: `2nd-5th c.` (set by the batch-11 release from its Card_Check, fix 3). Proposed: **`mid-2nd to 5th c.`**, a change, by the date rule and Mark's ruling on dates from the sources' clues.

- Start: the brief says "in the middle of the second century". The old clues: Justin, writing to Antoninus Pius, says Marcion is "even at this day alive" (ANF1 13961-13963, 14943-14945); Tertullian puts Marcion and Valentinus "in the reign of Antoninus for the most part" (ANF3 21334-21335) and calls Marcion "a heretic of the Antonine period" (23722); Irenaeus has Marcion succeed Cerdo, who came under Hyginus, and flourish under Anicetus (ANF1 33564-33573, 39983-39984). The reign of Antoninus Pius (138-161) is Widely Accepted. No clue gives a single year for Marcion: the Chronicle's third year of Antoninus, about 140, dates the coming of Cerdo, "magister Marcionis", not Marcion (JerChron 42747-42750; Open-Gaps 429). So the start is a century phrase, not a year.
- End: Theodoret's letters, "after twenty-five" and "twenty-six years" as bishop (Thdt 31501, 33158). The fifth century is Widely Accepted for Theodoret (the outline's Setting); the consecration year 423 is Jackson's reckoning and is not used. "5th c." stands as the batch-11 check ruled.
- `start` 140 and `end` 450 only place the entry on the map and are not edited here. 140 stays on Open-Gaps 429 ("Jerome's Chronicle has Cerdo, not Marcion"); this card shows no year for the start.

## shortDescription

> From Rome to Persia, churches followed the teacher Marcion of Pontus. They held that Jesus came from a good Father, above the God who made the world, and they read one Gospel, from Luke, with ten letters of Paul.

Two sentences, 39 words. No negative or contrast, no dash, no placeholder opening: it opens on the places, as the brief's own span does ("from Rome to Persia"). Marcion is named as what he was, a teacher (Irenaeus, "developed his doctrine"; Justin, "teaching"). The belief is told as what they held. The Gospel is "from Luke", not "cut down from Luke's": the cutting is Irenaeus's charge, which the timeline gives in his voice (line 5). Readability alone: grade 7.4 (names out 6.3), ease 84.9: pass, with the scorer's note to check it is not flat; it carries places, a person, the belief and the two books, so it is not.

## timeline

Nine periods, 46 lines, in time order. Dated only where an old source fixes the date or its own clue gives one: the Chronicle's third year of Antoninus for Cerdo's coming (about 140), Tertullian's own fifteenth year of Severus (about 207), the consuls of the Acts of Pionius (250), each marked as a conversion. Otherwise the record's own phrase ("in the time of Hyginus", "while Anicetus was bishop of Rome", "in the reign of Antoninus, for the most part", "on the eleventh day of the month Audynaeus", "after twenty-five years as bishop") or "date unknown". No editor's year is used: McGiffert's 177 for Book V and Jan. 11, 309 for Asclepius, Jackson's 423 for Theodoret, the ANF 207-208 for Against Marcion. No year for the break with Rome (Outline, Do not say). The span of 115 years and six and a half months (line 10) is Tertullian's report of the Marcionites' own reckoning, given as the record's phrase with no AD year. Witness at a period's head only where one witness carries every line (periods 3, 6, 9).

## uncertain

Mapped across word for word from the batch-11 card (13 items), with that card's Card_Check fix 2 ("destruction") already in it. The two double-quoted spans (item 7, "multitude of martyrs for Christ", E-en 29928-29929; item 10, Eusebius's "disguising their real sentiments, crept secretly into the Church.", E-en 66163-66164) stand in their cited files.

1. The year of the break with Rome. No old source used here gives one. Tertullian says Marcion was expelled more than once, and that he went with the two hundred sesterces he had brought into the church.
2. Apelles. Rhodon, who spoke with him when Apelles was an old man, counts him among Marcion's divided followers. Tertullian says Apelles left Marcion's continence for a woman, withdrew to Alexandria, and came back no longer a Marcionite. Both tie him to a maiden, Philumene: Rhodon says she was possessed by a demon, and Tertullian calls her a prostitute. These are enemies' charges.
3. How many first sources. Rhodon says Apelles held one, Potitus and Basilicus two, as Marcion did, and Syneros three. Asked how he knew there was one, Apelles answered that the prophecies refuted themselves. Pressed, he swore that he did not know how there is one unbegotten God, but believed it, and Rhodon says he laughed at him.
4. Their rites. Tertullian says their God still used the Creator's water to wash his people, oil to anoint them, honey and milk to feed them as children, and bread to represent his own body. Epiphanius, writing late in the fourth century, says they held their rites with learners not yet baptized looking on, used water in them, fasted on the Sabbath so as not to keep the rest of the God of the Jews, and let women baptize. He adds that, as he had heard from many, they allowed up to three washings or more.
5. The marriage rule. Tertullian reads it as a condemnation of marriage. Clement of Alexandria, another opponent, says the Marcionites would not marry because they did not wish to fill the world the Creator made. Hippolytus says Marcion called marriage destruction, thinking to grieve the maker by abstaining.
6. Their prayer. Ephrem, at Edessa, reports their claim to pray more than Daniel, and to pray the whole day.
7. Martyrs. An anonymous writer against the Montanists, of the late second century, says the Marcionites claimed a "multitude of martyrs for Christ", and denies that this proves them right. Eusebius tells of a woman at Caesarea, under Valerian, who died after three men were given to wild beasts, and says it was reported that she belonged to the sect of Marcion.
8. Metrodorus. The Acts of Pionius, written by those who were present, call him a presbyter of the sect of the Marcionites, and set Pionius on the right and Metrodorus on the left, both facing east. They date the deaths to a day in March, under the governor of Asia, Julius Proculus Quintilianus. Eusebius says only that Metrodorus appears to have been a proselyte of the Marcionite sect, and sets the deaths in Polycarp's time.
9. Asclepius. Eusebius says he died on the same pyre as Peter, an ascetic also called Apselamus, from the village of Anea, whom the judge and those around him begged many times to spare himself.
10. After the edict. Eusebius says that some of those the heretics had deceived, frightened by the emperor's threats, "disguising their real sentiments, crept secretly into the Church."
11. A charge about the dead. John Chrysostom tells, expecting laughter, that when a learner died among them, a living man hidden under the dead man's couch answered for him that he wished to be baptized, and they baptized him in the dead man's place. It is an enemy's mockery.
12. Theodoret's counts. In one letter he says he led eight villages of Marcionists into the truth; in another, that he rescued more than a thousand souls from the plague of Marcion; in a third, that he converted more than ten thousand of them and brought them to baptism. He also says his district held eight hundred parish churches.
13. A teacher of the early third century. Hippolytus says that in his own day a follower of Marcion, Prepon, an Assyrian, set out his teaching in a work addressed to Bardesanes.
