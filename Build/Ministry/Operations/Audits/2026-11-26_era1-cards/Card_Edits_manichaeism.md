## Shown dates

Census `dates`: `3rd-7th c.`. Proposed: **`216 to c. 1000`**, a change, by the date rule and Mark's ruling on dates from the sources' clues.

- Start: Mani's birth, which Biruni gives from Mani's own book as the year 527 of the era of the Babylonian astronomers, the era of Alexander (BIR 29094-29098). The year 216 is Widely Accepted (Source_Sheet A1; census `start`), and the brief tells it plainly ("Mani was born in 216").
- End: the census end, the 7th century, contradicts the finished brief, which tells the Chinese order of 732 and the edict of 843, and the held record, which runs on: the Fihrist's Samarkand and Baghdad in the 900s (FIH 5406-5431) and Biruni, about 1000, who writes that most of the eastern Turks and of the people of China and Tibet "adhere still to his law" (BIR 29145-29147). "About the year 1000" for Biruni is Widely Accepted (Outline, Setting). The Dunhuang scroll is undated (Le Coq judges it a relatively recent copy, KHU 15444-15447; a modern view, not used), so it does not move the end.
- `start` 216 and `end` 650 only place the entry on the map and are not edited here. `end` 650 is flagged under Entry fields: it no longer matches what the card shows.

## shortDescription

> From Babylonia to Roman Africa and China, the followers of the prophet Mani held that Light and Darkness were mixed in the world. Their Elect lived in strict purity and poverty, and their Hearers fed and served them.

Two sentences, 38 words. No negative or contrast, no dash, no placeholder opening: it opens on the places, the brief's own span ("they spread from Mesopotamia west to Roman Africa and east to the Turks and China"). Mani is named as what the brief calls him, a prophet. The Elect and the Hearers are explained by what each did. The census 'without beginning' is left to the timeline (line 9) to keep the description to what they are. Readability alone: grade 10.1 (names out 7.7), ease 73.8. The full-text grade is a little over 10 from names, with the names-out grade inside the limits: a pass under Mark's ruling ("10.1 is ok for the card as there are a lot of names and references", Header.md), a warning only.

## timeline

Nine periods, 45 lines, in time order. Dated only where an old source fixes the date or its own clue gives one: the era year of Mani's birth (216, Widely Accepted), the consuls of the laws of 372 and 399, of the debate of 392 and of Prosper's 443, and the Chinese reign years of 732, 768, 771, 806 and 843, each marked as a conversion where the AD year is the editors' (the Chinese years are Chavannes and Pelliot's conversions of the reign years printed beside them, and a reign year is the record's own date). Otherwise the record's own phrase ("in his thirteenth year, the second year of Ardashir", "when he had completed his twelfth year", "under the caliph Muqtadir", "in Augustine's twenty-ninth year") or "date unknown". No modern year for Mani's death (the outline's Do not say bars 274 and 276), for Faustus's exile, or for the emperor's letter (the editors' late 842 is not used). 1907 is a modern event, told as an event (Kit Part 1). The ages at the first revelation (twelve in the Fihrist, the thirteenth year in Biruni) sit side by side, with the difference in line 4's note. Witness at a period's head: none, since no period rests on one witness throughout.

## uncertain

Mapped across word for word from the batch-11 card (7 items), with its Card_Check fixes 3, 4 and 7 already in it. Each checked against the final brief and the census `longDescription`: none contradicts them.

1. Who ruled Babylonia when Mani was born. Biruni dates the birth, from Mani's own book, to the fourth year of King Adharban, whom he believes to be Ardawan, the last of the Ashkanians, the kings who held Iraq before Ardashir rose; by Mani's thirteenth year Ardashir was King of Kings.
2. Mani's age at his first revelation. The Fihrist says it came when he had completed his twelfth year; Biruni, from Mani's own book, says in his thirteenth year.
3. How King Shapur received him. The Fihrist, from what the Manichaeans said, has Mani first come forward in public on the day Shapur was crowned, and later has Shapur honor him and grant that his disciples be respected and go freely through his realm, and the Manichaeans add that Mani came in with two lamps of light shining on his shoulders. One of Biruni's informants, the Ispahbadh Marzuban, told him that Shapur banished Mani under Zoroaster's law, which orders false prophets expelled, and that Mani went to India, China and Tibet, came back, and was killed by Bahram for breaking that condition.
4. Whether Mani worked wonders. Biruni says his followers held two opinions: one party that he worked no miracle, the other that he did, and that King Shapur came to believe in him after Mani rose with him into the air between heaven and earth.
5. Which king killed Mani, and how. Biruni names Bahram, and says it is well known that he had Mani flayed, his skin filled with grass and hung at the gate of Gundeshapur; a book by one of Mani's pupils, which Biruni reports, has him chained in prison until he died. The Fihrist says he was killed under Bahram and hung in two halves at two gates of the city, and that others say he died in prison. Alexander of Lycopolis, writing in Egypt about 300, heard that he served under Shapur and was put to death after offending him in some way. The Acts of Archelaus, a staged debate of the 300s, also has the king flay him.
6. Augustine's charges against the Elect. In On the Morals of the Manichaeans he says that in nine years he could not hear of one of their Elect who was not found breaking their rules, or at least suspected of it, and that many were caught at wine and meat, though this, he says, he heard only by report. In the Confessions he mocks their belief that a fig wept when it was plucked, and that the particles of God in it stayed bound unless an Elect set them free with his teeth and belly. These are an opponent's charge and mockery.
7. The deaths of 843. The Chinese chronicle that reports them, compiled in the 1200s, gives seventy women religious at the capital in one chapter and seventy-two in another, and gives no cause.
