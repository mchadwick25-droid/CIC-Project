## Shown dates

Census `dates`: `c. 100-900`. Proposed: **`late 2nd to late 4th c.`**, a change, by the date rule.

- Start: the earliest datable meeting with these books is Serapion's at Rhossus. Eusebius has Serapion bishop of Antioch in the tenth year of Commodus (NPNF2-1 31003, 31060-31061), a reign Widely Accepted as the late second century; Serapion's successor was a confessor "in the persecution" (Schwartz 2369-2371). The brief's "about the year 200" stands within this. No held line gives 100.
- End: the brief's window ends with Egeria's visit, late in the fourth century (Widely Accepted; her date is the editors' reckoning from her own account, `uncertain` item 9). Jerome's Lives of Illustrious Men, with its verdict on the Acts of Paul and Thecla, is dated by his own preface to the fourteenth year of Theodosius (NPNF2-3 38785), about 392, a conversion, shown on the timeline. Jerome's Letter 107 carries no held year and stands as "date unknown".
- The books were copied long after (an eleventh-century manuscript, ANF8 36602-36603, a modern note), but no held line gives 900 (Open-Gaps 429; the batch-11 check flagged it for the census owner). `start` 100 and `end` 900 only place the entry on the map and are not edited here.

## shortDescription

> From Syria to Carthage, Christians wrote and read stories of Jesus and his apostles under the apostles' own names. Churches read these books aloud, and bishops judged them by what the apostles had handed down.

Two sentences, 35 words. No negative or contrast (the census teaser's "never put in the Bible" is not used), no dash, no placeholder opening: it opens on the places. People named as what they were: Christians, churches, bishops. It follows the brief's essence ("Christians wrote and read gospels about Jesus and tales of his first followers, the apostles, under the apostles' own names ... Congregations read them aloud ... bishops and teachers had to judge them against what had been handed down"). Readability alone: grade 9.1 (names out 8.4), ease 66.3: pass.

1. What the teaching at Rhossus was. Serapion was told only that the backers' minds were caught up in some heresy. He writes that he saw what one Marcianus held, a man who contradicted himself, not understanding what he said. He calls those who had used the book from the first, the successors of those who began it, Docetae. He says no more of what either held, and no other writer of his time ties that name to Rhossus.
2. What Serapion did next. He promised the church at Rhossus to come again soon, and the record does not say that he came. He set out for them the things added to the gospel, but Eusebius quotes only the lines about his rule and his reading, so the list is lost.
3. Origen and a Book of James. Origen, in his Commentary on Matthew, reports that some, drawing on the Gospel according to Peter or a Book of James, held that the brothers of Jesus were Joseph's sons by an earlier wife. Those who say so, he writes, wish to keep Mary's virginity honored to the end. Whether his Book of James is the book that survives under James's name, the old sources do not say, and modern editors differ.
4. Joseph in the book under James's name. When Mary is given to him by a sign, Joseph at first refuses, saying that he has children and is an old man. The text printed in English comes from an eleventh-century manuscript, and many other forms of the book exist.
5. Eusebius's reasons. Of the books that heretics cited under the apostles' names, among them the gospels of Peter, Thomas and Matthias and the Acts of Andrew and John, Eusebius says that no church writer in the succession had thought them worth mention. Their style, he says, was unlike the apostles', and their thoughts and purpose were so far from true teaching that they showed themselves to be the fictions of heretics.
6. The presbyter and his judge. Tertullian names no judge, and says that the presbyter left his office. Jerome, nearly two centuries later, says he was convicted by John and resigned his office of presbyter. Jerome also counts the fable of a lion baptized by Paul among the apocryphal writings. In Tertullian's Latin, the words "apud Ioannem", before John, stand only as an early editor's conjecture.
7. Thecla's end. The forms of her book differ. In one, she goes to Seleucia, enlightens many with the word of God and falls asleep there. In the other, she lives in a cave at Seleucia for seventy-two years, and later goes to Rome and is buried near Paul's tomb.
8. Thecla's shrine. Egeria writes of numberless cells of men and women around the church, and of a deaconess named Marthana who ruled the cells of the renouncers and virgins.
9. Egeria. Her name, and the date of her journey in the late fourth century, are editors' reckoning from her own account.
10. The Gospel of Peter found. A fragment of a gospel told in Peter's voice was found in a grave at Akhmim, in Upper Egypt, in 1886, and published in 1892. That it is the book Serapion read at Rhossus is a modern identification.
11. The Apocalypse of Peter. The Muratorian fragment, whose date and place are modern reckonings, says that some would not have it read in the church.
