## uncertain

Mapped across word for word from the batch-11 card (15 items, with Card_Check FIX 1 already in it). Each checked against the final brief and the census `longDescription`: none contradicts them. No word changed.
1. When it began. The elder Eusebius copied says it began when Gratus was proconsul of Asia, and gives no year. Epiphanius says about the nineteenth year of Antoninus Pius. Apollonius wrote in the fortieth year after Montanus began. Eusebius sets a letter from the churches of Gaul about the followers of Montanus in the time of the martyrs of Lyons, but gives none of its judgment.
2. What they believed about God. Tertullian says the quarrel was not over God or Christ. Hippolytus, writing at Rome, allows that they confess God the Father and Creator as the church does, and receive what the Gospel says of Christ, but says some of them followed Noetus in making the Father the Son. Jerome, of the Montanists of his own day, says they force the Trinity into one person. Epiphanius says they think as the catholic church does of Father, Son and Holy Spirit, then quotes oracles of Montanus in which God speaks in the first person, among them the oracle of the lyre, and reads them as claims to be God.
3. Who the Paraclete spoke through. Eusebius says they boasted that Montanus was the Paraclete, and Basil that they gave the title to Montanus and Priscilla. Tertullian says the Paraclete spoke through the prophetess Prisca. The later Against All Heresies printed with Tertullian's works says the Paraclete spoke in Montanus.
4. Apollonius's charges. He says Montanus taught that marriages be dissolved, made laws for fasting, appointed collectors of money, took gifts as offerings and paid his preachers; that the prophetesses left their husbands; and that a prophetess took gold, silver and costly clothes. He says Themiso bought his way out of prison, and wrote a letter to the churches as if he were an apostle, and that Alexander, honored by them as a martyr, had been condemned as a robber. These are an opponent's charges.
5. Other bishops who tried. A letter of Serapion of Antioch carries the signature of a bishop of Debeltum in Thrace, who swears that Sotas of Anchialus wanted to cast the demon out of Priscilla and was not allowed.
6. Martyrs. The elder says they claimed many martyrs, and that at Apamea on the Maeander martyrs from the church would not die in fellowship with theirs.
7. Rome. Only Tertullian tells it: a bishop of Rome, whom he does not name, acknowledged the prophetic gifts of Montanus, Prisca and Maximilla and sent his letter of peace to the churches of Asia and Phrygia, a letter recognizing them as in fellowship with his own. A man newly come from Asia then pressed false charges and the authority of the bishop's predecessors, and the bishop recalled it.
8. Oracles Tertullian keeps. Through Prisca, he says, the Spirit taught that purity brings visions and voices. An oracle of the Spirit urged believers to die as martyrs, not on beds or in fevers. He also says that, as their prophecy foretold, a city hung in the sky over Judaea every morning for forty days, a sign of the heavenly Jerusalem: a wonder in his own voice.
9. Maximilla's oracle of the end. Epiphanius gives it, writing about two centuries later. Holl's print of his Greek reads no prophet after her; Oehler's print reads no prophetess.
10. Which prophetess saw Christ at Pepuza. Epiphanius says it was Quintilla or Priscilla, and he cannot say which.
11. Women among them. Epiphanius says that in a branch of his own day, the Quintillians, also called Pepuzians, women were bishops and presbyters, and seven virgins dressed in white came into their church with lamps to prophesy. He says their women were called prophetesses, though he is not sure whether this was among them or among the Phrygians. He says they were called Artotyrites for setting bread and cheese in their mysteries.
12. A charge of child murder. Cyril of Jerusalem says Montanus killed small children for their mysteries. Jerome prefers not to believe it, and Cyril himself says the same charge was laid on all Christians in time of persecution.
13. After the councils of Asia. Constantine's law took away their meeting houses and forbade them to meet, even in private. Bishops gathered at Iconium, Firmilian says, refused their baptism, and Basil later did the same. Jerome says they set the patriarchs of Pepuza first, then stewards, and bishops third, kept three Lents a year, and held a second marriage to be adultery. Sozomen says that in Phrygia and the lands near it they had been many since Montanus and still were in his day, and that they kept Easter by the sun.
14. Their own books. Hippolytus says they had countless books. Jerome says Tertullian wrote seven books On Ecstasy, the last against Apollonius. None of these is held.
15. The end in Phrygia. Procopius, hostile to Justinian, says Justinian also threatened those who held out with being unable to leave their property to their wives and children, and Justinian's law names the Montanists. Michael the Syrian, writing some six hundred years later, says John of Asia burned their house at Pepuza on the emperor's order, with bones found there in a marble chest marked as those of Montanus and his women.
