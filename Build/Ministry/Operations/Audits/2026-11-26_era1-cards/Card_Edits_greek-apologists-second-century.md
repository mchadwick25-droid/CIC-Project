## Shown dates

Census `dates`: `c. 124-200`. Proposed: **`c. 125 to c. 180`**, a change, by the date rule and Mark's ruling on dates from the sources' clues.

- Start: the first dated defense is Quadratus's, with Aristides's. Eusebius's Chronicle, in Jerome's Latin, sets both in the 226th Olympiad (JerChron 42526) and in the ninth and tenth years of Hadrian by its margin (42528-42535; the coordinator's note reads 42531-42534 the same). Counting Hadrian's reign from 117, Widely Accepted (Eusebius gives Trajan nineteen and a half years before him, npnf201 20993-20994), that is about 125. The year is a conversion, and the timeline says so. 124 is the census's own year and no held line gives it (Open-Gaps 429; the outline barred it from the telling).
- End: the last defense the sources date is Athenagoras's, addressed to Marcus Aurelius and Commodus ruling together (athen-grc 72-74). Eusebius says Commodus received the government after Marcus had reigned nineteen years (npnf201 28467-28469), which is 180 counting Marcus from 161, Widely Accepted. Theophilus wrote after the death of the emperor he calls Aurelius Verus (anf02 11076); the editors' notes give 169 and 180 and are not used, so "c." covers him. The letter to Diognetus has no date (shown as "date unknown"), so it does not move either end.
- 200 rests on no held line. `start` 124 and `end` 200 only place the entry on the map and are not edited here.

## shortDescription

> From Athens and Rome to Sardis and Antioch, Christians who wrote in Greek defended their faith to emperors, and one to a pagan friend. Accused of being godless, of incest and of eating human flesh, they asked to be judged by how they lived.

Two sentences, 44 words. No negative or contrast, no dash, no placeholder opening: it opens on the places, in the brief's own frame ("from Athens and Rome to Sardis and Antioch"). People are named as what they were: Christians who wrote in Greek, emperors, a pagan friend. "One to a pagan friend" is Theophilus to Autolycus; the brief's "pagans who asked them about it" was dropped to meet the reading floor, and the friend is the one such reader the held lines show. Readability alone: grade 9.6 (names out 9.1), ease 68.7: pass.

## uncertain

Mapped across word for word from the batch-11 card (6 items). Each checked against the final brief and the census `longDescription`: none contradicts them. No word changed. Item 5's "Serenus Granius" matches the Chronicle's spelling (JerChron 42535-42536).

1. Which emperor Aristides addressed. Eusebius says Hadrian, and so does the short heading of the Syriac version. Its longer heading names Caesar Titus Hadrianus Antoninus, and Justin addresses Antoninus Pius as Titus Aelius Hadrianus Antoninus.
2. What Aristides says of Christian life. His Greek survives only reworked inside a later Christian romance, Barlaam and Josaphat, and his Syriac version differs from it. Both say that Christians who have give to those who have not, and take a stranger under their roof as a brother. Only the Syriac adds that they fast two or three days to feed the needy, and that they persuade their slaves to become Christians and then call them brothers.
3. What Quadratus saw. Quadratus says that people Christ healed or raised from the dead lived on, some of them to his own day. Jerome, writing much later, says Quadratus had seen many of them.
4. Whether a plot killed Justin. Justin wrote that he expected to be plotted against, perhaps by Crescens, a philosopher at Rome who publicly called Christians atheists and impious to please the crowd. Eusebius, writing in the early 300s, says Justin was put to death through a plot laid by Crescens.
5. Whether the emperors answered. No old source records a reply to any of these defenses. Eusebius's Chronicle, in Jerome's Latin, sets the books of Quadratus and Aristides under Hadrian, then says Hadrian wrote to Minucius Fundanus, moved by a letter from the legate Serenus Granius, not by the defenses. Melito himself told Marcus Aurelius that Hadrian had written to Fundanus and many others, and that his father, while Marcus ruled with him, wrote to the cities forbidding any new measures against Christians.
6. Theophilus as bishop. Only Eusebius, and Jerome after him, calls Theophilus bishop of Antioch. His books to Autolycus do not say so.
