## Shown dates

Census `dates`: `2nd-3rd c.`. Proposed: **`2nd to 4th c.`**, a change, by the date rule.

- Start: Clement sets the rise of the founders of the heresies "in the times of Adrian the king", lasting "to the age of Antoninus the elder" (CL 52955-52958); Irenaeus brings Valentinus to Rome under Hyginus (IR 39969). The Chronicle's own clue gives about 140 for his coming and about 143 for his becoming known (JerChron 42747-42750, 42765-42769), each a conversion. The brief's "around 136" rests on no held line (it is near McGiffert's modern 137-141 for Hyginus): it is not shown.
- End: the held record names Valentinians into the 4th century: Constantine's edict, under Constantine (Widely Accepted), addresses them by name (E-en 66071-66072; VC-gr 13037-13038), and Epiphanius, late in the 4th century, says Valentinus' "seed" still remains in several districts of Egypt (HOLL 29762-29768, "ἔτι ἐν Αἰγύπτῳ περιλείπεται"). So the census "3rd c." end is too early. No old source gives an end year.
- `start` 120 and `end` 300 only place the entry on the map and are not edited here (120 rests on no held line; Clement's Hadrian covers it only as "the times of Hadrian").

## shortDescription

> At Rome and in Egypt, the teacher Valentinus and his students told of a highest God, the Father of all, above a lesser maker of this world. They taught that the Saviour came to make that Father known.

Two sentences, 38 words. No negative or contrast, no dash, no placeholder opening: it opens on the places. Valentinus is named as what he was, a teacher (Tertullian, "an able man both in genius and eloquence"; Irenaeus, "his own school"). The teaching is given in the words of Ptolemy's own letter, so it is theirs, not an opponent's charge; the census's "divine spark" is not used, since no held line opened gives that image. Readability alone: grade 7.7 (names out 5.9), ease 86.6: pass, with the scorer's note to check it is not flat; it carries the places, two kinds of people and the two Gods of their teaching, so it is not.

## timeline

Six periods, 33 lines, in time order. Dated only where an old source fixes the date or its own clue gives one: the Chronicle's third and sixth years of Antoninus (about 140, about 143), marked as conversions. Otherwise the record's own phrase ("in the times of Hadrian", "in the time of Hyginus, bishop of Rome", "in the time of Anicetus", "while Eleutherius is bishop of Rome, the twelfth from the apostles", "at the present time, Tertullian says", "in Epiphanius's own day") or "date unknown". No editor's year is used: McGiffert's 137-141 and 154-167, the ANF's "not earlier than 207" for Against the Valentinians, the brief's "around 136" and "around 180". The Nag Hammadi find of 1945, which the brief tells, has no held line and is not on the timeline. Period placement: Tertullian, Hippolytus and Origen are set in the 3rd century, Widely Accepted for each (Jerome has Tertullian flourish "in the reign of the emperor Severus and Antoninus", npnf203 40498); Valentinus' own fragments sit in the middle of the 2nd century, the span Irenaeus gives him at Rome; Ptolemy follows Irenaeus's Ptolemaeans, with no year claimed. Witness at a period's head: none, since no period rests on one witness throughout.

## uncertain

New (8 items), each from held lines, each stated plainly in its witness's voice.

1. When Valentinus was at Rome. Irenaeus says he came in the time of Bishop Hyginus, flourished under Pius and stayed until Anicetus. Tertullian says he and Marcion were believers in the church of Rome under Bishop Eleutherus before they were expelled.
   - Evidence: Irenaeus III.4.3 (IR 39969-39970); Tertullian, Prescription 30 (TE 21331-21339, "under the episcopate of the blessed Eleutherus").
2. Why he broke with the church. Only Tertullian, an opponent, gives a reason: Valentinus had hoped to become a bishop, and another man won the place by the claim his suffering for the faith had given him. Tertullian names no city, and says such men are often roused by ambition to revenge.
   - Evidence: Tertullian, Against the Valentinians 4 (TE 43109-43115; KR 9633-9634, 9652-9654, "ut solent animi pro prioratu exciti praesumptione ultionis accendi"). No other held witness gives a reason.
3. Where he came from. Epiphanius, late in the 4th century, heard that some said he was born on the coast of Egypt and taught at Alexandria, and he calls the report uncertain. He also says Valentinus left the faith in Cyprus, while Irenaeus says he stayed at Rome until Anicetus.
   - Evidence: Epiphanius, Panarion 31.2 and 31.7 (HOLL 28941-28948, "ἐν ἀμφιλέχτῳ μέν"; 29802-29804); Irenaeus III.4.3 (IR 39969-39970, "remained until Anicetus").
4. His teacher. Clement says the followers of Valentinus claimed he had heard Theudas, a pupil of Paul, and that the followers of Basilides claimed Glaucias, the interpreter of Peter.
   - Evidence: Clement, Stromata VII.17 (CL 52955-52967).
5. What is his and what is his school's. Irenaeus gives a short account of Valentinus' own teaching and a much longer one of Ptolemy's school. Tertullian says Valentinus kept the powers within God, and Ptolemy made them persons outside God.
   - Evidence: Irenaeus I.11.1 (IR 31520-31545) against I, preface 2 (29871-29873); Tertullian, Against the Valentinians 4 (TE 43117-43121; KR 9656-9660).
6. The Gospel of Truth. Irenaeus says those from Valentinus called a recent writing of theirs the Gospel of Truth. The old sources held here do not say who wrote it.
   - Evidence: Irenaeus III.11.9 (IR 41173-41178). The census `voices[0]` "possibly the author of the Gospel of Truth" is a modern view (Not found, below).
7. Teaching in private. Irenaeus and Tertullian, both opponents, say the Valentinians first won hearers in the churches and told them their deeper teaching only later, in private.
   - Evidence: Irenaeus III.15.2 (IR 42121-42141); Tertullian, Against the Valentinians 1 (TE 42950-42974).
8. Their own words. The words of Valentinus, Ptolemy and Heracleon held here survive only as their opponents copied them: Clement, Epiphanius and Origen.
   - Evidence: Clement (CL 34289-34303, 39090-39095, 38952-38957), Epiphanius (HOLL 34007-34012, the letter copied "so that no one think we refute him from hearsay only"), Origen (OR 23164-23170, 27710-27715). The Nag Hammadi books the brief tells of are not held.
