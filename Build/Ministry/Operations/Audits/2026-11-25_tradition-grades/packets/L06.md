## inquisition-record-sects-adamites-and-kin

Inquisition-Record 'Sects' (Adamites and kin)

Some of the 'sects' in the medieval record are known only from the documents that condemned them. Take the Bohemian Adamites of 1421. They are described as a naked band on an island in southern Bohemia, who shared everything and waited for the end of the world. They appear in the chronicles of people who despised them, and in the account of a later outside observer. The Taborites destroyed them, so no voice from inside survives at all. The 'heresy of the Free Spirit' in Germany is a parallel case. Some of these people were certainly real, and some of what they were accused of may well have been true. But the evidence is one-sided. It comes from their enemies, and it may have been shaped by what those enemies expected to find.

## latin-papal-church-avignon-to-lateran-v

The Latin Papal Church (Avignon to Fifth Lateran)

In 1309 Clement V settled the papal court at Avignon. For nearly seventy years the popes governed the Latin church from the Rhône rather than the Tiber. They built the largest Gothic palace in Europe, and an administration to match. The return to Rome in 1377 led to something worse. A disputed election the next year left two popes. An attempt to fix it in 1409 left three. For a generation the schism divided every kingdom, university and religious order in the West. It was ended not by a pope but by a council. The Council of Constance removed the rival claimants and elected Martin V in 1417. It also declared that a general council holds its authority directly from Christ. The decades after that were a contest over whether this was true. By the 1440s it was settled in the papacy's favor. The popes who followed rebuilt Rome. They founded the Vatican Library, commissioned the Sistine Chapel and began the new St Peter's. The Fifth Lateran Council decreed a program of reform that changed very little. It closed on 16 March 1517, seven months before Luther's theses.

## byzantine-church-palaiologan

The Byzantine Church, Palaiologan Era

In its last century and a half, the Byzantine church governed the shrinking remnant of an empire. Even so, it did some of its most lasting work. Synods at Constantinople in 1341, 1347 and 1351 ruled for the hesychast monks. They made Gregory Palamas's theology the standard of Orthodoxy. Then came the harder question. The Ottomans were closing in. The emperor and the patriarch went to Italy, and in 1439 they signed a union with Rome at the Council of Florence. They did it in exchange for military help, which never came in enough strength. Mark of Ephesus refused to sign. At home the union was rejected by monks, by much of the clergy and by the city. When it was at last proclaimed in Hagia Sophia in December 1452, it met open hostility. Five months later, on 29 May 1453, Constantinople fell. The same era produced the Palaiologan revival in art: the mosaics of the Chora church and the painted churches of Mystras. They are some of the finest work Byzantium ever did, made while the state around it was collapsing.

## waldensians-in-the-alps

The Waldensians in the Alps

A century and more after they were condemned, the Waldensians had become something no medieval dissent was supposed to be. They were a lasting, organized church, kept out of sight. In public its members lived as ordinary Catholics of their parish. In private they were something else. They lived in the high valleys of the Cottian Alps, between Piedmont and Dauphiné. There were scattered groups too in Provence, in Calabria and Apulia, and in Germany and Bohemia. What held the church together were the barbes, the 'uncles'. These were preachers who went from place to place in pairs. They knew long parts of the Bible by heart in their own tongue. They carried small books copied by hand, and they made a round of the households. People knew they existed, and they were attacked again and again. In 1487 Innocent VIII called a crusade against them. The campaign that followed tore through valleys on both sides of the Alps. They survived it.

## church-of-the-east-timur-to-hakkari

The Church of the East — Timur's Catastrophe and the Northern Remnant

For centuries the Church of the East had spread wider than any other church on earth. Its provinces ran from Baghdad across Central Asia to India and China. Within a lifetime most of it was gone. The patriarch Yahballaha III, a monk born far to the east among the Öngüd, died in 1317. By then the Mongol rulers of Persia had turned to Islam, and Christians in Mesopotamia were a persecuted minority. Then Timur's campaigns, from the 1380s to 1405, destroyed the lowland dioceses and much of the population that had kept them alive. What survived did so by geography, in the Hakkari mountains and around Lake Van and Lake Urmia. The patriarchs of the late fifteenth century lived on the upper Tigris, at Gazarta and Mosul. By the sixteenth century the office passed within one family, from uncle to nephew. The one province Timur never reached was the community on the Malabar coast of India. It went on receiving bishops from its patriarch, who sent them out in 1490 and again in 1503.

## terminal-christian-nubia

Terminal Christian Nubia (Late Makuria and Dotawo)

Christian Nubia had been a going concern since the sixth century. It had kingdoms with cathedrals and bishops, and a literature in Old Nubian, Greek and Coptic. Its treaties kept Muslim Egypt at a distance. Its ending took four centuries and left an unusually physical record. In 1317 the throne hall of the royal palace at Dongola was turned into a mosque. The stone that records it can still be read. After that Makuria broke apart. A smaller kingdom that followed it, Dotawo, held on downstream around Gebel Adda and Qasr Ibrim. It still issued documents in Old Nubian, and it still had a king and a bishop. The last of its kings anyone can name is King Joel, recorded in 1484. After that the record simply stops. There was no conquest, no massacre and no final synod. The documents just cease, and over the following generations the people became Muslim.

## franciscan-order-era-6

The Franciscan Order — Spirituals Crisis to the Great Division

By 1300 the Franciscans were a huge international order, with universities, libraries and buildings. Yet their founder had told them to own nothing. The strain broke in the 1310s and 20s. The Spirituals, who wanted the Rule kept to the letter, were ordered to conform, and four were burned at Marseille in 1318 for refusing. Then in 1323 John XXII declared it heretical to insist that Christ and the apostles had owned nothing at all. That put the order's own official view of itself on the wrong side of the law. The head of the order, Michael of Cesena, fled the papal court with William of Ockham rather than accept it. The argument did not end so much as change shape. It became a long split between two wings. The Conventuals accepted the order's endowments and town convents. The Observants, from 1368, built up reformed houses that kept the Rule strictly. Leo X ended the pretense of a single order on 29 May 1517, and divided the family formally in two.

## the-bosnian-church

The Bosnian Church (Krstjani)

Between Catholic Hungary and Orthodox Serbia, medieval Bosnia had a church of its own. Its members called themselves krstjani, Christians. It used a Slavonic liturgy and had its own leaders. At the head was a djed, a 'grandfather', with gosts and starci under him. Its communities, called hiže, or houses, were scattered through the countryside. Rome condemned it as heretical again and again, and from time to time sent friars against it. Bosnia's own rulers protected it and made use of it. What its members actually believed is still in real dispute. Since the 1970s most historians have rejected the long-held view that they were Bogomil dualists. The one major document written from inside is the will of Gost Radin, made in 1466. It reads like ordinary Christianity, with a cross drawn down its margin. Bosnia fell to the Ottomans in 1463, and the church did not outlast the kingdom.

## armenian-cilicia-after-the-kingdom

Armenian Christianity after the Kingdom (Sis to Etchmiadzin)

The Armenian kingdom of Cilicia lay on the Mediterranean. Through the fourteenth century it was pressed by Mamluk Egypt and by Latin Europe at the same time. Its kings needed help from the west, and again and again they pushed for a union with Rome. Inside the church, those who saw its independence at stake resisted. The kingdom fell in 1375, when the Mamluks took Sis, and its last king died in exile in Paris in 1393. The catholicos, the head of the church, stayed at Sis for two more generations. He was more and more cut off from most Armenians, who lived far to the east under other rulers. Then in 1441 a national council met at Vagharshapat and elected a catholicos at Etchmiadzin. This was the church's original see, left behind nearly a thousand years earlier, and the center of Armenian church life moved home. Sis did not give up its own succession, so the church has had two catholicoi ever since. Much of what we hear from Armenians of this period comes from colophons. These are the notes scribes wrote at the ends of the books they copied, saying who they were and what was happening around them.

## maronite-christianity-era-6

Maronite Christianity — the Union Formalized

The Maronites were a Syriac-speaking church of the Lebanese mountains. Their patriarch was based in the Qadisha valley, and they had communities on Cyprus as well. The fourteenth century opened badly. Around 1305 Mamluk campaigns against the Kesrwan drove Christians from their homes. For most of this period the church survived largely by being hard to reach. Its monasteries and rock-cut hermit cells were tucked into a gorge that armies found costly to enter. Its tie to Rome, claimed since the crusades, was confirmed twice in this era. On 7 August 1445 a bull of Pope Eugenius IV received the Maronites of Cyprus into union with the Roman church. It was issued at the Council of Florence, which had by then moved to Rome. In 1516 envoys of the Maronite patriarch pledged his obedience at the Fifth Lateran Council. Unlike almost every other Eastern church, the Maronites hold that they were never out of communion with Rome at all. The Roman documents of this era tell it another way. The bull of 1445 says the Maronites of Cyprus had held that Christ had only one will.

## st-thomas-christians-1300-1517

St Thomas Christians, 1300-1517

On the pepper coast of south-west India there had been Christians for longer than anyone could document. They traced their founding to the apostle Thomas and worshipped in Syriac. They belonged to the Church of the East and got their bishops from its patriarch, far away in Mesopotamia. In this window they come into focus. A Syriac book copied at Cranganore in 1301 is the oldest dated Syriac manuscript from India. Its closing note names the patriarch Yahballaha III, which shows the link intact. The community was no small minority on the margins. It was an established trading group of high standing, with privileges from Hindu rulers and a headman of its own. A papal envoy at Kollam in the mid-1300s found that its people owned the pepper gardens and ran the public weighing-house. By the late 1400s the church's home in Mesopotamia lay in ruins, and the link took real effort to keep. In 1490 a delegation went to the patriarch to ask for bishops. The bishops who came back wrote home in 1504. They described the community, and the arrival of Portuguese ships.

## lutheran-wittenberg-and-its-congregations

Lutheran Wittenberg & Its Congregations

Wittenberg was a small Saxon university town when one of its theology professors, an Augustinian friar named Martin Luther, wrote 95 theses against the sale of indulgences and sent them to the archbishop of Mainz on 31 October 1517. Tradition also holds that he nailed a copy to the door of the Castle Church, the university's usual notice board for an academic debate — but that detail comes only from an account written decades later, after Luther's death, and historians still disagree over whether it happened. Within a few years a movement had grown around him that reorganized worship, emptied monasteries, and put the Bible, a catechism and a hymnbook into ordinary German households. What that looked like from the pew is documented unusually well. From 1527-28 the Saxon churches sent visitors out to examine parish after parish, and their reports are frank about how little many villagers actually knew. The movement settled its own arguments in writing — the Augsburg Confession of 1530, then the Book of Concord of 1580. And the churches shaped by it spread north and east across Germany and Scandinavia, where they remain.

## the-reformed-cities-zurich-and-geneva

The Reformed Cities - Zurich & Geneva

Two Swiss city-states, a generation apart, worked out what a whole town would look like if its church were rebuilt from Scripture: Zurich under Huldrych Zwingli from 1519, which stripped images and the Mass out of its churches. And Geneva after 1536 under John Calvin, whose Ecclesiastical Ordinances of 1541 gave the city pastors, teachers, elders and deacons, and a consistory that met every week. Geneva's consistory registers survive, and they are startlingly ordinary — quarrels between neighbors, absent husbands, people who had not been to church. Which makes this one of the best pictures anywhere of what a church actually did about the daily conduct of ordinary people. From these two cities the Reformed pattern traveled: Scotland, France, the Netherlands and English Puritanism all trace back here.

## the-anabaptist-movements

The Anabaptist Movements

In January 1525 a handful of men in Zurich baptized one another as adults. They were impatient with how slowly their own city's reform was moving, and they were hunted for it. Anabaptists held that baptism belonged to those who could confess faith for themselves. The church, they said, was a free body of believers, not a whole society. Most of them also held that a Christian could neither swear oaths nor take up the sword. Catholic, Lutheran and Reformed rulers alike saw them as a threat to order. By one early count more than two thousand were put to death, above all in Catholic lands. The survivors regrouped. In Moravia, a band that pooled its goods in 1528 grew into the Hutterite Brethren. In the Low Countries, a former priest named Menno Simons gathered congregations, and their descendants still carry his name.

## the-english-reformation-and-elizabethan-settleme

The English Reformation & Elizabethan Settlement

England's Reformation began as a king's quarrel — Henry VIII's break with Rome in the 1530s. And only afterwards became a change in what people believed, swinging hard toward Protestantism under Edward VI, back to Rome under Mary I, and settling under Elizabeth I into a church that kept bishops and cathedrals while preaching a Protestant gospel. What actually altered English religion was less a confession of faith than a book. The Book of Common Prayer, first issued in 1549 and revised in 1552, 1559 and 1662, which fixed the words the whole country was required to say together, week after week. The change can be watched from below in churchwardens' accounts and in the wills of ordinary people, whose formulas about the soul and the dead shift decade by decade. And the settlement held, uneasily, until the civil wars of the 1640s broke it open again.

## the-scottish-reformation-and-kirk

The Scottish Reformation & Kirk

In 1560 the Scottish Parliament abolished papal authority and adopted a Reformed confession of faith, and the church that followed set out to remake a whole nation rather than a single city: a minister and a school in every parish, and in each parish a kirk session of elders meeting to deal with quarrels, drunkenness, broken promises and sexual misconduct. Those session registers survive in extraordinary quantity, which makes Scotland the best-documented case anywhere of church discipline as it was actually administered. Usually over small matters, among neighbors who then had to go on living beside each other. The Kirk's covenanting instinct, the idea of a whole people bound to God by public oath, came to a head in the National Covenant of 1638 and in the conflicts that followed it.

## the-huguenots

The Huguenots

France's Protestants organized themselves before any ruler told them to. In 1559 delegates met secretly in Paris to adopt a confession of faith and a book of discipline, creating a national church inside a Catholic kingdom. What followed was nearly a century of civil war and precarious tolerance — the St Bartholomew's Day massacres of August 1572, the Edict of Nantes in 1598 that gave them legal existence and fortified towns, then the slow erosion of that edict until Louis XIV revoked it in 1685. Rather than convert, several hundred thousand left, carrying their trades, their psalters and their memory of what it was to flee into England, the Dutch Republic, Prussia, Switzerland, South Africa and the American colonies.

## recusant-english-catholicism

Recusant English Catholicism

After Elizabeth I's settlement of 1559, English Catholics who would not attend their parish church were fined as recusants — refusers. And from 1585 it was treason for a priest ordained abroad even to set foot in the country. What survived was a church that had retreated into private houses. Mass said in an upstairs room, priests hidden in cavities built into chimneys and stairwells, and households in which the woman of the house, legally less exposed than her husband, effectively ran the congregation. Well over a hundred priests and lay people were executed under these laws, and the community that remained was small, anchored in gentry households, and stubborn.

## puritanism

Puritanism

Puritans were the English Protestants who thought the Elizabethan settlement had stopped halfway. That a church with bishops, surplices and a set liturgy still carried too much of what the Reformation was meant to clear away. Their real distinctiveness lay less in church furniture than in the inner life. They kept diaries, examined their own hearts in writing, and made the conversion narrative — an account of how God had brought you to faith, told aloud before a congregation. A normal thing for ordinary men and women to produce. Pressed at home, thousands sailed for New England from 1630. Those who stayed came to dominate the Long Parliament and the Westminster Assembly, and after the Restoration of 1660 were ejected from the Church of England altogether.

## dutch-further-reformation-and-refuge-culture

Dutch Further Reformation & Refuge Culture

The Dutch Republic won independence from Spain as a Reformed country, but a strange one. The Reformed church was the public church while a large minority never joined it, and the Republic became the printing house and the shelter of much of Europe's religious dissent. English separatists, French and Flemish refugees, Jews expelled from Iberia, and books that could be published nowhere else. Inside the Reformed church itself a movement of preachers pressed for something beyond correct doctrine, urging precise, examined, daily godliness on their congregations; contemporaries called it the Nadere Reformatie, the further or closer Reformation. It was a movement of pastors and their books rather than of institutions, and historians still argue whether it is a tradition in its own right or the devotional temperature of a whole society.

## the-waldensian-reformed-union

The Waldensian-Reformed Union

The Waldensians had already survived three centuries of suppression as an underground movement of lay preachers when, in 1532, their representatives met at Chanforan in the Alpine valleys of Piedmont and agreed to join the young Reformed churches. And to pay for a new French translation of the Bible, which appeared in 1535 as the Olivétan Bible. It was an unusual graft: an old dissenting tradition became a Reformed church with ministers, confessions and printed books, without letting go of its own memory of the centuries before. That memory was tested repeatedly, most brutally in the Piedmont massacre of Easter 1655, which drew protests from across Protestant Europe and a famous sonnet from John Milton.

## the-society-of-jesus

The Society of Jesus

Ignatius of Loyola was a Basque soldier whose leg was shattered by a cannonball in 1521. During a long convalescence he read the lives of the saints, and the notes he made about his own inner experience became the Spiritual Exercises, a structured month of prayer that afterwards shaped everyone the order trained. The Society of Jesus was approved by Rome in 1540 and grew astonishingly fast. Not as a monastic order in a cloister but as a mobile body of preachers, confessors, schoolmasters and missionaries, running dozens of colleges across Europe within a generation, and some two hundred and fifty by 1600, and sending men to India, Japan, China, Brazil and Canada. By 1650 its members were at once the Catholic Church's most effective teachers and its most controversial operators, admired and resented in roughly equal measure.

## tridentine-parish-renewal-borromean-milan

Tridentine Parish Renewal - Borromean Milan

The Council of Trent decided what Catholic reform ought to mean; Milan under Archbishop Charles Borromeo showed what it looked like in a parish. From 1565 Borromeo set about redesigning a whole archdiocese around the question of what a parish priest was actually for — founding seminaries to train them, holding provincial councils and diocesan synods, walking his own visitations through remote alpine valleys, and issuing minutely detailed instructions on everything from the fabric of a church building to the enclosed confessional box, which his diocese did much to standardize. He also mobilized the laity, through Schools of Christian Doctrine in which ordinary men and women taught catechism to children and through confraternities that organized charity. When plague struck Milan in 1576 he stayed in the city, and his reputation as a pastor was made.

## mission-born-christianities-kirishitan-japan-kon

Mission-Born Christianities (Kirishitan Japan; Kongo; China; New Spain/Andes)

Between the 1490s and the middle of the seventeenth century, Christian communities took root far outside Europe under very different conditions. The kingdom of Kongo, whose ruler Afonso I adopted the faith and whose letters survive in his own voice. Japan, where a Jesuit mission from 1549 gathered a large Christian population before a savage prohibition drove the survivors underground. China, where Jesuits and Chinese converts argued out how the Christian God could be spoken of in a Confucian idiom. And Spanish America, where conquest and mission arrived together and Andean and Nahua Christians made something of their own out of what they were given. Almost everything written about these communities was written by missionaries, which makes the deepest question about them a hard one — how much of what survives is the mission's story and how much the converts'. And Japan and Kongo are where converts' own voices most clearly break through.
