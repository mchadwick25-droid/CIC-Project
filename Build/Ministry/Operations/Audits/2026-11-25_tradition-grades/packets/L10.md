## laestadianism-and-the-nordic-revivals

Laestadianism & the Nordic Revivals

Lars Levi Laestadius was a Swedish Lutheran pastor and a serious botanist, of partly Sami descent, serving parishes in Lapland that were being destroyed by alcohol — and in 1844 a conversation with a young Sami woman he called Mary changed his preaching entirely. What followed spread through the Sami and Finnish north: fierce sermons in the local languages, an end to drinking, and intense emotional expression in meetings that startled outsiders. Its most distinctive practice is confession and spoken absolution between ordinary believers — forgiveness declared face to face by a neighbor rather than pronounced from a pulpit. It was never only his: the same century brought Paavo Ruotsalainen's awakening in Finland and, in Sweden, the low-church revivals of Carl Olof Rosenius and P. P. Waldenström — a wider family of Nordic revivals, not just one man's movement.

## plymouth-brethren-and-dispensational-formation

Plymouth Brethren & Dispensational Formation

In the late 1820s a handful of dissatisfied Christians in Dublin — among them a dentist, a lawyer and a young Anglican curate named John Nelson Darby — began meeting to break bread together with no ordained clergy, no denominational name and no set liturgy, and assemblies on the same pattern spread through Britain and then the world. Darby also worked out a way of reading the Bible that divided history into distinct dispensations, kept Israel and the church permanently separate, and expected Christ to remove the church before a final tribulation — a scheme almost unknown before him and enormously consequential after. Alongside the theology ran an equally characteristic practice. George Müller ran orphanages in Bristol for thousands of children on the principle of never asking anyone for money and publishing the accounts. In 1848 the movement split over how far assemblies were answerable to one another, into the Open and Exclusive Brethren, and both halves carried on.

## the-sunday-school-and-bible-institute-movement

The Sunday School & Bible-Institute Movement

In 1780 a Gloucester newspaper proprietor named Robert Raikes hired four women to teach the town's factory children to read on Sunday — their one free day — and then publicized the results in his own paper. The idea traveled with extraordinary speed, and by the middle of the nineteenth century Sunday schools were teaching literacy along with religion to millions of working-class children in Britain and America, staffed overwhelmingly by unpaid women volunteers and organized by unions that supplied lessons, prizes and books at industrial scale. A century after Raikes the same instinct produced the Bible institute: schools like D. L. Moody's in Chicago, opened in 1886, that took ordinary lay people rather than university graduates and trained them in a year or two as evangelists, teachers and missionaries. Together they are the machinery by which most ordinary Christians of the industrial age actually learned the faith.

## the-student-volunteer-movement-and-edinburgh-191

The Student Volunteer Movement & Edinburgh 1910

In the summer of 1886 a hundred students at a conference in Massachusetts signed a declaration that they were willing and desirous to become foreign missionaries, and over the next thirty years the Student Volunteer Movement they founded put something on the order of twenty thousand young Americans and Britons on ships, under the watchword "the evangelization of the world in this generation." Its summit was the World Missionary Conference at Edinburgh in 1910, where twelve hundred delegates met to plan the work as one enterprise instead of a hundred competing ones. Two of the very few non-Western delegates used their few minutes to say the necessary thing: V. S. Azariah of India asked the room, to its face, for friends rather than masters, and Cheng Jingyi of China told it that the Chinese church wanted to be one church and had no use for European denominational divisions. Both were right, and it took the rest of the century to act on it.

## indonesian-christianity

Indonesian Christianity

Christianity had been present in the eastern Indonesian islands since the Portuguese and the Dutch East India Company, but the churches that matter most today were built in the nineteenth century and, very quickly, by Indonesians. German missionaries reached the Batak highlands of North Sumatra in 1861, where Ludwig Nommensen spent more than fifty years. The church that grew there gave itself a written order in 1881 and is now among the largest Lutheran bodies anywhere in the world. Further west, in Java, a Javanese teacher known as Kiai Sadrach gathered thousands of followers into congregations that kept Javanese forms, Javanese leadership and Javanese ways of teaching. Which the Dutch missions regarded with deep suspicion at the time, and which is now read as the archipelago's clearest early case of Christianity on local terms. Almost everything written about all of this in the period was written by the missions; the churches' own records are what let it be read the other way round.

## northeast-india-revivals-and-the-mar-thoma-refor

Northeast India Revivals & the Mar Thoma Reform

In 1836 a teacher at the seminary in Kottayam, Abraham Malpan, revised the Syriac liturgy that Kerala's St Thomas Christians had used for centuries — cutting what he judged unscriptural and putting the service into Malayalam so ordinary people could follow it. The reform divided the ancient community, and after decades of argument and litigation a royal court verdict in 1889 separated the two parties for good. The reforming wing carried on as the Mar Thoma Syrian Church, an Eastern church that kept its ancient rite and its evangelical conscience together, while the larger body continued as the Malankara church. A thousand miles to the northeast, in the Khasi, Mizo and Naga hills, small congregations planted by Welsh and American missions caught fire on their own. Village preachers and singers carried revival through the hills, most famously the Khasi awakening of 1905-06, and hill languages received their first written hymnody out of the same process, composed rather than translated.

## orthodoxy-after-1821-balkan-autocephalies

Orthodoxy after 1821 — the National Churches

Under Ottoman rule, the Orthodox of the Balkans were governed as one religious community. That community answered to the Patriarch of Constantinople. The Greek revolt of 1821 broke this order apart. Weeks after the rising began, Patriarch Gregory V was hanged at the gate of his own home. As each new nation won its freedom, its church claimed the right to govern itself. Greece declared its church free in 1833, and Constantinople formally accepted it in 1850. Bulgaria won a decree from the sultan in 1870 that set up a national church, the exarchate. Serbia won its own church in 1879 and Romania in 1885. In 1872 a council at Constantinople condemned phyletism, which means building the church around a nation rather than a place. Even so, national churches kept on spreading. The century did not simply break one church apart. It made a family of churches. Each had its own synod, its own language of worship and its own claim on a people.

The call for a national church was older. Ivanov says the monk Paisii, writing in 1762, named the lack of a national hierarchy and national schools. He listed them among the causes of his people's spiritual ruin. The exarchate of 1870 was a national church.

Not every new Orthodox church grew out of the Ottoman patriarchate. Fortescue, a Catholic writer, dates a self-governing church of the Vlachs of Hungary to 1864. He calls it Hermannstadt. Today the town is Sibiu.

## catholic-church-long-nineteenth-century

The Catholic Church of the Long Nineteenth Century

Between the fall of Napoleon and the eve of the First World War, the Catholic Church lost a state and gained a papacy. Gregory XVI and then Pius IX met the liberal century head-on: Pius defined the Immaculate Conception in 1854, cataloged the errors of the age in the Syllabus of 1864, and in July 1870 saw the First Vatican Council define papal infallibility. Weeks before Italian troops entered Rome and ended the Papal States. Stripped of territory, the papacy grew in reach instead: bishops and ordinary Catholics looked to Rome as never before, new congregations of sisters and brothers staffed schools and hospitals across the world, and mass devotion gathered around Marian apparitions like Lourdes in 1858. Under Leo XIII the church that had spent decades condemning the modern world began to negotiate with it, most consequentially in Rerum Novarum (1891), which put the church on the side of a worker's right to a living wage and to organize.

Rome still ran the missions through Propaganda. In 1845 it wrote to a vicar apostolic. It called it a grave abuse, not to be borne, to baptize again all whom catechists had baptized (a plain rendering).

Mission work grew far beyond Europe. Charles Lavigerie reached Algiers as archbishop on 16 May 1867. In London on 31 July 1888 he spoke of his missioners, the White Fathers. They were settled in the Sahara and on the high lands of Central Africa.

Daniele Comboni wrote a plan for Africa. The press of Propaganda in Rome printed it in 1867. He asked whether Africa could be won by means of Africa (a plain rendering).

In 1893 Launay printed a story told by a seminary director in Paris. Some priests, he said, had taken his society for the Lazarists, the Jesuits or the Holy Spirit Society. His society, he said, was quite distinct from the others.

His society was the Foreign Missions of Paris. Launay says it was born in 1658. It grew in the persecutions of Tonkin, Cochinchina and China.

In 1814 Pius VII had restored the Jesuits to all states.

The Catholic Church in Africa in the twentieth century has its own entry. It grew from missions like these, though the sources here stop at the White Fathers of 1888.

## victorian-church-of-england

The Victorian Church of England

The Church of England entered the nineteenth century as a comfortable establishment and left it a contested one. Three parties argued for its soul. Evangelicals who had inherited the awakening's fervor and its campaigns against slavery and squalor, high churchmen who rediscovered the church's catholic inheritance, and broad churchmen who wanted room for the new biblical criticism and the new geology. The quarrels went to law: the Gorham case of 1850 over what baptism does, the storm over the essayists of 1860 who applied critical methods to Scripture, ritual prosecutions that put clergymen in prison over vestments and candles. Underneath the controversies the church rebuilt itself, in thousands of new buildings and slum parishes at home and in a mission network abroad that by 1867 had produced enough overseas bishops that a first Lambeth Conference had to be called to hold them together.

Samuel Marsden shows the church reaching overseas. A pamphlet of his, printed in 1826 by his biographer's account, calls him 'principal Chaplain to the Colony of New South Wales'. His biographer dates one letter to 7 October 1814, just before Marsden sailed for New Zealand. In it Marsden wrote that he had 'submitted my views to the Church Missionary Society, and solicited their aid.'

On Christmas Day, in 1814 by the biographer's date, Marsden preached in New Zealand. He wrote, 'In this manner, the Gospel has been introduced into New Zealand'. On 5 June 1815 he wrote from Parramatta that the societies in London could not make up their minds to send out a vessel. So he 'at last determined to purchase one for the purpose on my own account'.

In April 1837 he asked a missionary, 'Have they any meeting in the week-days for prayer and religious instruction?' The quoted words are Marsden's own, printed in a biography of 1913. Three of the dates are the biographer's. The book tells the mission from one side only. The Maori side is not in it.

## russian-church-synodal-century

The Russian Church — the Synodal Century

For this whole century the Russian Orthodox Church had no patriarch. It was governed by a Holy Synod whose chief lay officer, the ober-procurator, answered to the tsar — an arrangement that produced both an immense institution and a cramped one. Metropolitan Filaret of Moscow gave Russia its standard catechism and pressed for decades for a Bible in modern Russian, finished in 1876 over long resistance. Missions went out to the Altai peoples, to Alaska and to Japan. Monasteries and their elders drew crowds, and a parish priest, John of Kronstadt, became the most sought-after confessor in the empire. But under Konstantin Pobedonostsev, ober-procurator for a quarter-century, the church was also the instrument of a policy of religious uniformity that pressed hard on Old Believers, Eastern Catholics and evangelicals alike. When toleration was finally decreed in 1905, the church immediately began preparing the council that would give it back its own head.

The church also governed Georgia. In 1811 the Georgian catholicate ended. Ioselian, a Georgian historian in Malan's English, says the head of the Georgian clergy then took the title "Member of the Synod, and Exarch of Georgia". The first to hold it was Archbishop Balaam. Ioselian calls him a Georgian of a princely family. A Georgian appeal calls him a Russian. In 1814 the emperor gave Balaam the exarchate of Imereti as well. In December 1818 one exarchy was set up for all Georgia. The same appeal says that Georgia's church lost its rights against canon law and that its property was seized. The Georgian church's later story is told in the entry on the restored Georgian church.

Missions went east too. Blackmore put Mouravieff's history into English in 1842. In its preface he wrote of the "marked success" of the missions in Siberia.

## armenian-christianity-zartonk

Armenian Christianity — the Zartonk Century

Zartonk — awakening — is what Armenians call their nineteenth century. A revival of language, schools, printing and civic life carried largely inside the church, because under Ottoman rule the church was the nation's legal body. Its central document is the Armenian National Constitution of 1863, which seated lay delegates in a national assembly and limited what the Patriarch of Constantinople could do alone, an unusual experiment in giving a laity formal power over its own hierarchy. The century also divided the community: converts of American missionaries were expelled from the Apostolic Church and organized as the Armenian Evangelical Church in 1846, recognized by the Ottoman state as a separate Protestant community in 1850, while an Armenian Catholic patriarchate continued alongside. And it ended in blood — the Hamidian massacres of 1894-96 killed tens of thousands and emptied whole districts.

Russia took Erivan and Etchmiadzin in 1828. Armenian volunteers helped.

In 1836 a Russian rule, the Polozhenie, tied the catholicos's rule to the state. Ormanian says it let the state meddle.

It also left the last word on bishops to the tsar. The catholicos named two men, and the tsar chose.

A Catholic work says a papal bull of 1867 caused a schism in Armenia. The bull was called Reversurus.

## old-catholic-churches-union-of-utrecht

The Old Catholic Churches — Utrecht to the Union

There had been a small church at Utrecht out of communion with Rome since an eighteenth-century quarrel over Jansenism left it electing its own archbishops. It stayed a Dutch curiosity until 1870, when the First Vatican Council defined papal infallibility and a body of German, Swiss and Austrian Catholics — professors, priests and laypeople, with the church historian Ignaz von Dollinger at their head — refused to accept it. Excommunicated, they needed bishops, and Utrecht had them: Joseph Hubert Reinkens was consecrated for the German Old Catholics in 1873 by a bishop of that line. In 1889 five bishops signed the Declaration of Utrecht, binding these churches into a union that professed the faith of the undivided church of the first thousand years and rejected the papal claims of 1870. And then went on to drop compulsory celibacy for clergy and to put the liturgy into the language of the people.

## nineteenth-century-methodism

Nineteenth-Century Methodism

A movement that began as a society inside the Church of England spent the nineteenth century becoming a worldwide family of churches, and dividing as it grew. In Britain the Wesleyan connexion consolidated into a respectable denomination, and those who found it too respectable went their own way: the Primitive Methodists, born at an open-air camp meeting on Mow Cop in 1807 and a settled connexion by 1811-12, took the gospel into the pit villages behind working-class preachers, a striking number of them women. In America, Methodism became the largest Protestant body in the country and then split down the middle over slavery in 1844 into northern and southern churches that would not reunite for nearly a century. Black Methodists, refused equal standing, had already built churches of their own — the African Methodist Episcopal Church in 1816, the AME Zion Church in 1821. By 1881 the family was scattered enough that it needed a world conference simply to speak to itself.

## nineteenth-century-baptists

Nineteenth-Century Baptists

Baptists began the century as a scattering of self-governing congregations and ended it as one of the largest Protestant families in the English-speaking world, without ever agreeing to be governed from anywhere. In London, Charles Haddon Spurgeon preached weekly to thousands at the Metropolitan Tabernacle and published sermons that sold in the millions. Late in life he broke with the Baptist Union over what he called the Down-Grade, convinced his fellow ministers were quietly giving up the doctrines they had been ordained to preach. In America the family split in 1845, when Baptists in the South, refused a slaveholding missionary appointment, formed the Southern Baptist Convention. Running alongside was Landmarkism, which held that only true Baptist congregations were churches at all and traced an unbroken Baptist succession back to the apostles.

## nineteenth-century-quakerism-separations

Nineteenth-Century Quakerism — the Separations

The Society of Friends came into the nineteenth century intact and left it in pieces. In 1827-28 the American yearly meetings tore apart over Elias Hicks, a Long Island farmer and traveling minister who held that the Inward Light, not the Bible or a doctrine of atonement, was the heart of the faith. Those who followed him and those who opposed him became separate bodies meeting in separate houses. Within twenty years the evangelical side divided again, between followers of the English banker-minister Joseph John Gurney and the conservative John Wilbur. Out of the Gurneyite stream came something Friends had never had — revival meetings, planned worship and paid pastors. So that by the century's end some Quaker meetings still sat in silence while others sang hymns and listened to a sermon. Through all of it Friends kept up the work they were best known for: abolition, prison reform, and the women's rights movement several of them helped to launch.

## american-unitarianism-universalism-consolidations

American Unitarianism & Universalism — the Consolidations

Two movements had grown up apart in New England. In this century both grew into organized churches. By 1815 the liberal clergy of Boston's Congregational churches were already being called Unitarians. Many complaints were made that the label slandered them. In 1819 William Ellery Channing preached at an ordination in Baltimore. His sermon gave these liberals a platform. It also spoke out against the Trinity. In 1825 they founded the American Unitarian Association. The Universalists taught that God would in the end save everyone. They built their conventions on a track of their own. Within a generation the Unitarian side produced a third thing it had not looked for. Ralph Waldo Emerson left the ministry. In 1838 he told Harvard's divinity students to seek religion in their own souls and in nature. They were not to look for it in a revelation fixed in the past. The Transcendentalists took that further than most of their elders wanted to go. The two movements did not treat creeds alike. The Unitarians bound no one to a creed. The Universalists kept a short one, the Winchester Profession of 1803. In 1870 they made it a test of fellowship. Both had a strong practical bent. They worked hard for schools, prisons and asylums, and to end slavery.

## ruthenian-greek-catholic-polotsk-to-galicia

The Ruthenian Greek Catholic Church — Polotsk to Galicia

Millions of Belarusians and Ukrainians worshipped in the Byzantine rite while in communion with Rome. The arrangement dated from a union of 1596. In the Russian Empire it was ended by decree. At the Synod of Polotsk in February 1839, bishops led by Joseph Semashko declared their church reunited with Russian Orthodoxy. Roughly a million and a half people were moved with a stroke of the pen. The remaining eparchy, Chełm, followed in 1875, and in some villages soldiers broke the resistance by force. What survived did so under Austrian rule in Galicia. There the Greek Catholic Church became more than a church. It was the backbone of Ukrainian national life. Its priests were the teachers, editors and co-op organizers of a people who had almost no other leaders. At the turn of the century Andrey Sheptytsky took the see of Lviv as its metropolitan. His long tenure would carry the church through all that the next fifty years brought.

## philippine-catholicism-gomburza-to-aglipay

Philippine Catholicism — Gomburza to the Aglipayan Schism

Spanish friars had run the Philippine church for three centuries, and the century's great church fight was over whether Filipinos could run their own parishes. The secularization campaign — transferring parishes from Spanish religious orders to diocesan clergy, most of them native-born — turned into a national grievance, and in 1872, after a mutiny at the Cavite arsenal, three priests who had championed it were garrotted: Mariano Gomez, Jose Burgos and Jacinto Zamora, remembered ever since by the single name Gomburza. Their execution did more for the independence movement than their campaign ever had; Jose Rizal dedicated his second novel to their memory. When Spanish rule ended, part of the church went with it. In 1902 the labor organizer Isabelo de los Reyes proclaimed an Iglesia Filipina Independiente and named the former revolutionary chaplain Gregorio Aglipay its head, and for a few years a substantial minority of Filipino Catholics followed it.

A lay movement inside this church met refusal and then force. In 1840, by Montero's account, many people began to meet at Lucban, in Tayabas province, as a Confraternity of St Joseph. Its founder was Apolinario de la Cruz, a native lay servant of a Manila hospital. He told his questioners he was a Roman Catholic and meant to honor the saint. He asked the church authorities for leave to make the group lawful. They refused, and he appealed to the colony's high court.

In October 1841 the governor of Tayabas marched on the group's camp with about 300 men. He was killed. On 1 November troops overran its next camp. On 4 November Apolinario was shot. Montero counts 240 dead in the battle and about 260 more in the pursuit. Govantes, whom he cites, says more than a thousand. Montero also quotes a letter of 16 November from the colony's finance chief. It praised the officer of Batangas for keeping "the fire of rebellion" out of his province.

The movement speaks here only through its enemies. Montero wrote in 1895. He draws on a parish priest's report, an official's letter and a writer named Mas. Montero says Apolinario began it only to live off the gullible. Apolinario said he meant to honor St Joseph. Asked what they wanted, pardoned members answered, as Mas tells it: to pray. Asked what they would have done in victory, they said they would have tied the Spaniards and friars to trees for the women to shoot with arrows.

Montero says the group let in no Spaniards and no Chinese mestizos. He takes that as proof of a political aim. That is his reading. Apolinario's letters were read aloud at the meetings, and were seized in 1841. The Library holds none of them. A modern study, by Reynaldo Ileto, starts its story of these movements in 1840. It is called Pasyon and Revolution (1979).

## church-of-the-east-chaldean-consolidation

The Church of the East & the Chaldean Consolidation

The Church of the East — the church that once sent bishops from Baghdad as far as China — entered this century reduced to the mountains and plains of the Ottoman-Persian borderland and divided between rival patriarchal lines, one in communion with Rome and one not. In 1830 Rome confirmed John Hormizd as patriarch of Babylon, consolidating the Chaldean Catholic Church as a single body. The line that stayed independent kept its seat in the mountain village of Qudshanis in Hakkari, its patriarchate passing from uncle to nephew, and its people came to be known as the Assyrian Church of the East. Both then found themselves courted by outsiders. American Congregationalist missionaries settled at Urmia in the 1830s and gave the region a printing press and schools in the spoken Syriac of the day. From 1886 the Archbishop of Canterbury sent a mission expressly instructed to strengthen the ancient church rather than convert its members.

A third outsider came in the 1890s. Wigram writes that in 1896 the part of the church living in Persia submitted to the mission the Russian Orthodox Church had sent to teach them. They hoped this would bring political protection. Wigram says it did, for as long as Russian power lasted. He adds that the Assyrians in Turkish lands began to think of following.

## confessional-lutheran-revival-emigrations

The Confessional Lutheran Revival & the Emigrations

In 1817 the king of Prussia ordered his Lutheran and Reformed subjects into one united church. Many Lutherans went along. Some would not. They held that a state cannot merge a confession of faith. These Old Lutherans were fined, their services were broken up and their pastors were jailed. Rather than give in, whole churches left. Some went to South Australia in 1838 under Pastor August Kavel. A separate group of Saxon Lutherans sailed for Missouri the same year. Within months of landing it had to depose its own leader for immorality. Out of that chastened remnant, under the young pastor C. F. W. Walther, came the Missouri Synod of 1847. It made assent to the Lutheran confessions the test of fellowship. It built its own schools, seminaries and German-language press to keep them. In Germany the same instinct led to a revival of scholarship at Erlangen. At Neuendettelsau, Wilhelm Löhe trained pastors and deaconesses for German churches overseas.

## malagasy-christianity

Malagasy Christianity — the Self-Carried Church

The London Missionary Society reached Madagascar in 1818, and its most consequential work turned out to be alphabetical. Within a decade Malagasy had a written form, a printing press and, by 1835, a complete Bible. Then the door slammed. Queen Ranavalona I banned Christianity in 1835 and expelled the missionaries. Rasalama, the first Malagasy martyr, was speared in 1837, and for the next quarter-century believers were hunted, enslaved, and thrown from the cliff at Ampamarinana above the capital. The church did not merely survive the persecution. It grew through it, without foreign clergy, on the Bible it had been left and hymns its own people wrote, and when the missionaries were allowed back they found far more Christians than they had left behind. Ranavalona II was baptized in 1869 and burned the royal talismans, and the church became the establishment it had once been martyred by, until the French conquest of 1895 ended the kingdom that housed it.

## east-african-christianity-uganda-martyrs

East African Christianity & the Uganda Martyrs

Christianity came back to East Africa along the coast first — Johann Ludwig Krapf landed at Mombasa in 1844 and began the language and mapping work later missions relied on — and then inland to the kingdom of Buganda, where Anglican missionaries arrived in 1877 and French Catholic White Fathers in 1879. Their converts were largely young men of the royal court, and when the kabaka Mwanga II turned on them between 1885 and 1887 he killed both kinds together: Anglican and Catholic pages executed side by side, the largest group burned alive at Namugongo on 3 June 1886 under the leadership of Charles Lwanga, who is said to have arranged his own pyre. Persecution did what it often does. The church grew, and Baganda converts carried it outward as evangelists into the surrounding kingdoms faster than any mission society could have, so that by the agreement of 1900 that settled Buganda's relations with Britain, it was a Christian kingdom.

## taiping-heavenly-kingdom

The Taiping Heavenly Kingdom

Hong Xiuquan was a village schoolteacher in Guangdong who failed the imperial examinations four times. After one failure he collapsed into a delirium and saw visions he could not interpret until, years later, he reread a Christian tract handed to him in the street. From it he concluded that the old man in his vision had been God the Father, the elder brother beside him Jesus Christ, and that he himself was God's second son, sent to sweep China clean of demons. The God Worshippers he and his cousin Feng Yunshan gathered in Guangxi became an army. They proclaimed the Heavenly Kingdom of Great Peace in 1851, took Nanjing in 1853 and held it for eleven years — banning opium, alcohol and footbinding, destroying idols and ancestral tablets, holding land in common, and printing their own scriptures alongside the Bible. The war fought to destroy them was among the deadliest in human history, with estimates of the dead running to twenty million, and when Nanjing fell in 1864 Hong was already dead and the kingdom went with him.

## japanese-christianity-hidden-emergence-meiji

Japanese Christianity — the Hidden Christians’ Emergence & the Meiji Church

On 17 March 1865, weeks after a French church opened in Nagasaki's foreign settlement, a group of villagers from nearby Urakami came in, and one woman quietly told the priest that the hearts of all present were the same as his, and asked where the statue of Mary was. They were descendants of Christians driven underground in the 1600s, who had kept baptism, prayers and a liturgical calendar for over two hundred years without a single priest. In the months that followed tens of thousands more made themselves known. The government's ban was still in force, and the Urakami villagers were deported and held until 1873. When it was lifted, a Christianity of three kinds grew quickly in Meiji Japan. Catholic parishes rebuilt around the emerging hidden communities, Protestant bands of samurai-class students at Yokohama, Kumamoto and Sapporo, and a Russian Orthodox mission that Nikolai of Japan had begun in 1861 and staffed largely with Japanese clergy. Not all the hidden Christians rejoined: some, the Kakure, kept the tradition exactly as they had received it, and remain separate to this day.

The priests who met them belonged to the Paris Foreign Missions Society. In his history of the church's return, Marnas wrote in 1896 that this Society found the descendants of the old Christians in 1865. He said it was born long ago from the inspiration of Alexandre de Rhodes.
