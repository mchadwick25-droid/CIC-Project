# Packet L12

## the-sayfo-and-the-syriac-diaspora

The Sayfo & the Syriac Diaspora

In 1915 the Syriac Christians of eastern Anatolia and the surrounding regions were massacred and driven out — the Sayfo, the sword. Tur Abdin, the Syriac Orthodox heartland, and Urmia, where the Church of the East had its own communities, were emptied by killing and flight. Survivors testified almost at once: Joseph Naayem published Shall This Nation Die? in 1921, and an Assyro-Chaldean delegation put its case at the Paris peace conference in 1919. What followed was a century of relocation. The Syriac Orthodox patriarchate moved to Homs in 1933 and to Damascus in 1959; Church of the East exile communities gathered in Chicago and elsewhere; By 2010 European diaspora parish registers recorded families whose villages no longer had them. The Assyrian side of the story has its own twentieth century, including the assassination of Mar Shimun XXIII in 1975, a split in 1968, and a Common Christological Declaration with Rome in 1994.

## modern-ethiopian-and-eritrean-christianity

Modern Ethiopian & Eritrean Christianity

The Ethiopian Tawahedo church entered the twentieth century as an ancient national church under a Coptic hierarchy, and spent the century becoming self-governing under a series of shocks. The first Ethiopian bishops were consecrated in 1929; Italy occupied the country from 1936 to 1941, and massacred the monks of Debre Libanos in 1937; the church received its own patriarch in 1959. The Derg's revolution deposed Patriarch Tewophilos in 1976 and killed him in 1979, and the church's own martyrology from those years is its record of what followed. Alongside all this a Protestant evangelical movement, the P'ent'ay, grew fast — the Kale Heywet and Mekane Yesus churches among them, the latter publishing a much-quoted letter on holistic ministry in 1972 — and grew fastest underground under the Derg. In May 1970 Archimandrite Laike Mariam Mandefro, later Archbishop Yesehaq, established the Ethiopian Orthodox Church in Jamaica. Eritrea's church became autocephalous in 1993; in 2006 its Patriarch Antonios was deposed and placed under house arrest.

## the-ukrainian-greek-catholic-underground

The Ukrainian Greek Catholic Underground

In March 1946 a synod was convened at Lviv that dissolved the Ukrainian Greek Catholic Church into the Russian Orthodox Church. Every one of the church's bishops had already been arrested, and none took part. In communist Czechoslovakia the Greek Catholic Church was liquidated between 1948 and 1950. A 'sobor' met at Prešov on 28 April 1950, in an operation called 'Action P'. For forty-three years the Ukrainian church was the largest banned church in the world. It had priests ordained in secret, liturgies in apartments and forests, and bishops in the Gulag. Metropolitan Andrey Sheptytsky's pastoral letters — among them one from 1942 headed 'Thou Shalt Not Kill' — went on being read through the underground years, and his successor Josyf Slipyj spent from 1945 to 1963 in the camps before his release. From 1984 an initiative group circulated a Chronicle of the Catholic Church in Ukraine in samizdat. In 1989 the church was legalized, and it came out with its hierarchy, its parishes and its people intact — which is not what the state had been counting on.

## the-russian-new-martyrs-and-the-catacomb-church

The Russian New Martyrs & the Catacomb Church

The Russian Orthodox Church went into 1917 as an established state church and came out of the Soviet century as the object of the largest persecution of Christians in history. Patriarch Tikhon issued an anathema in 1918. Clergy, monastics and laypeople were shot or sent to the camps in numbers that were still being counted in 2010: the Butovo firing range outside Moscow accounted for thousands in 1937 and 1938 alone, and the Solovki monastery became a prison camp. In 1927 Metropolitan Sergius issued a Declaration of loyalty to the Soviet state, and the church divided over it — those who refused went into the catacomb communities, following bishops like Joseph of Petrograd and refusing to commemorate Sergius in the liturgy. The archives opened after 1991, and with them the case files: names, charges, dates, sentences. In August 2000 the Moscow patriarchate canonized the new martyrs and confessors in their thousands.

## the-post-vatican-ii-parish-world

The Post-Vatican-II Parish Tradition

The Second Vatican Council opened in 1962, and what it changed most was the ordinary Sunday. Sacrosanctum Concilium, the constitution on the liturgy, was promulgated on 4 December 1963; Lumen Gentium on the church followed on 21 November 1964; Dei Verbum and Gaudium et Spes came in 1965; the revised Missal of Paul VI appeared in 1969 and 1970. In parishes this meant Mass in the language people spoke, readers and ministers of communion drawn from the pews, and parish councils. In 1972 the Rite of Christian Initiation of Adults restored the ancient catechumenate — a first-century pattern coming back after fifteen centuries. Not everyone welcomed it: Archbishop Lefebvre published a declaration in 1974, and the argument over the older rite is documented in the church's own successive rulings, Ecclesia Dei in 1988 and Summorum Pontificum in 2007.

## lausanne-era-global-evangelicalism

Lausanne-Era Global Evangelicalism

Evangelicals from around the world met at Lausanne from 16 to 25 July 1974, and what came out of it was not a denomination but a network with a document. The Lausanne Covenant was drafted by a committee chaired by John Stott and adopted at the congress, and the congress's own record, Let the Earth Hear His Voice, was published the following year with the addresses in it — including those of the Latin Americans René Padilla and Samuel Escobar, who argued that evangelism and social responsibility could not be separated. That argument won enough of the room to change the movement's direction, which is why 'integral mission' became a Lausanne word. The movement restated itself twice more, in the Manila Manifesto of 1989 and the Cape Town Commitment of 2010. Together they form a chain of dated statements of what it took itself to be, unusual in its consistency.

## african-diaspora-and-reverse-mission-churches-in

African Diaspora & Reverse-Mission Churches in Europe

Within a single lifetime the mission map turned over. Churches that European missionaries had planted in Africa began planting congregations in Europe, first among migrant communities and then well beyond them. Aladura and Kimbanguist congregations were meeting in European cities from the 1960s. The Redeemed Christian Church of God, founded in Nigeria in 1952, opened British parishes from the 1980s and Jesus House in London in 1994. Matthew Ashimolowo founded Kingsway International Christian Centre in London in 1992. Sunday Adelaja founded the Embassy of the Blessed Kingdom of God for All Nations in Kyiv in 1994. The phrase that attached to them, reverse mission, described a real inversion. Churches once planted by European missionaries were planting congregations back among Europeans.

## ratana-church-and-pacific-adjustment-movements

Ratana Church & Pacific Adjustment Movements

In November 1918 Tahupōtiki Wiremu Rātana had a vision in Aotearoa New Zealand. A healing ministry grew up around him from that year. In 1925 the movement became a church of its own, Te Haahi Rātana. Rātana himself was known as the mangai, the mouthpiece. That role appears in the church's own creed, prayers and hymns, alongside the faithful angels. The movement was political as well as religious from early on. A group took a Treaty of Waitangi petition to London in 1924. An alliance with the Labour Party followed a meeting with Michael Joseph Savage in 1936. By 1943 the movement held all four Māori seats in Parliament. Rātana died in 1939, and the church went on under the tumuaki, the leaders who followed him. Other Pacific movements of a like shape belong beside it. Among them is Silas Eto's Christian Fellowship Church in the Solomon Islands, from around 1960.

## the-armenian-church-after-the-genocide-medz-yegh

The Armenian Church after the Genocide (Medz Yeghern)

In 1915 the Armenian population of Anatolia was destroyed, and the church went with the people — clergy killed, dioceses emptied, whole regions left without a parish. What survived did so by moving. Grigoris Balakian, a priest who lived through it, wrote his account in 1922. The Holy See of Cilicia relocated to Antelias in Lebanon in 1930. Etchmiadzin, the mother see, continued inside the Soviet Union under severe constraint; Catholicos Khoren I was killed in 1938. In the Cold War the two sees divided along the politics of the diaspora, a division dated to 1956 whose records both sides kept. After 1991 the church in Armenia was re-established in a country of its own again.

## the-emerging-church-and-post-evangelical-movemen

The Emerging Church & Post-Evangelical Movement

In the late 1990s a set of conversations began among younger evangelical leaders in North America, Britain and Australia who thought the church they had been formed in was answering questions nobody was asking. It ran through networks rather than institutions — the Young Leaders Network, then Terra Nova, then Emergent Village from around 2001 — and through books and an enormous volume of blogging. Dave Tomlinson's The Post-Evangelical came out in Britain in 1995; Brian McLaren's A New Kind of Christian in 2001 and A Generous Orthodoxy in 2004; Rob Bell's Velvet Elvis in 2005. Phyllis Tickle's The Great Emergence (2008) tried to say what was happening while it was still happening. The movement deliberately held confessional questions open rather than settling them, which is why this map assesses its communities one at a time.

## dispensational-prophecy-culture

Dispensational Prophecy Culture

This is not a doctrine so much as a way of reading, and it reached more people than most denominations did. Its formation is older than the era: the Niagara Bible Conference met annually from the 1870s to 1897 and adopted a fourteen-point creed in 1878, and prophecy conferences from 1878 onward taught a reading of scripture divided into dispensations with a rapture ahead. What carried it to the millions was print. The Scofield Reference Bible of 1909, revised in 1917, put the scheme in the notes at the foot of the page, so that a reader met it every time they opened the text. Clarence Larkin's Dispensational Truth (1918) put it on charts. Then came the bestsellers: Hal Lindsey's The Late Great Planet Earth in 1970 and the sixteen Left Behind novels between 1995 and 2007, whose sales figures are the measure of how far this way of reading actually traveled.

## the-megachurch-and-seeker-movement

The Megachurch & Seeker Movement

From the mid-1970s a particular way of building a church spread out of American suburbs and then around the world. Willow Creek Community Church was founded in 1975 on the premise that the Sunday service should be designed for the person who does not go to church, with the actual formation happening in small groups during the week. Saddleback followed in 1980, and Rick Warren's The Purpose Driven Church (1995) put the method into a book other pastors could work from. Behind both lay the church-growth school, and Donald McGavran's Understanding Church Growth (1970). The Willow Creek Association, from 1992, exported the model by conference and manual. The other great carrier was music: Hillsong, founded in Australia in 1983, became a global publisher of congregational songs, and licensing data shows how widely they were sung. The movement studied itself unusually hard — Willow Creek's own REVEAL research from 2007 questioned whether its programs were forming anyone.

## the-narrative-kingdom-renewal-the-bible-as-one-s

The Narrative-Kingdom Renewal (the Bible as one story fulfilled in Jesus)

A way of reading the Bible as a single story, running from creation to Jesus and outward from him, spread across evangelical and mainline churches from about 1990 without ever becoming an organization. Lesslie Newbigin's The Gospel in a Pluralist Society (1989) set the terms by asking what the church is for in a culture that has heard the story and moved on. Dallas Willard's The Divine Conspiracy (1998) put the kingdom back at the center of discipleship. N. T. Wright's academic volumes — The New Testament and the People of God (1992), Jesus and the Victory of God (1996) — and his popular ones, Simply Christian (2006) and Surprised by Hope (2008), carried it to a general readership.

## fundamentalism-1910-1970s

Fundamentalism

The word arrived in 1920: Curtis Lee Laws, writing in the Watchman-Examiner, proposed calling those willing to do 'battle royal for the Fundamentals' fundamentalists, and the name stuck. The movement was older than the word. The Niagara Bible Conference had met annually from the 1870s until 1897 and adopted a fourteen-point creed in 1878; A. A. Hodge and B. B. Warfield published 'Inspiration' in the Presbyterian Review in April 1881, giving the doctrine of scripture its most exacting American statement. Between 1910 and 1915 twelve volumes called The Fundamentals were published, financed by Lyman Stewart and given away — around three million copies. The Presbyterian General Assembly adopted a five-point doctrinal deliverance in 1910. Then came the public defeats and the separations: the Scopes trial in July 1925, Princeton Seminary reorganized in 1929 with Westminster founded in answer, and new denominations of their own from the 1930s.

## the-social-gospel

The Social Gospel

In 1886 Washington Gladden published Applied Christianity, and in the same year Walter Rauschenbusch took a pastorate on the edge of Hell's Kitchen in New York, where what he saw of tenement life reorganized his theology. He and others formed the Brotherhood of the Kingdom in 1892. His Christianity and the Social Crisis appeared in 1907 and went through thirteen printings in five years, and A Theology for the Social Gospel followed in 1917. Charles Sheldon's novel In His Steps (1896) put the same instinct into a form anyone could use: asking, before each decision, what Jesus would do. In 1908 the newly formed Federal Council of Churches adopted the Social Creed of the Churches — the movement's clearest institutional mark, and the ancestor of the denominational social statements that came after it.

## the-mainline-century-liberal-and-neo-orthodox

The Mainline Century — Liberal Protestantism & Neo-Orthodoxy

This is the world the mainline Protestant seminaries built and the mainline denominations taught. Its reach-back runs to Schleiermacher and to the historical-critical century — the Briggs heresy trial of 1891 to 1893 in America, Harnack's What Is Christianity? in 1900 — and its institutional face arrived with the Federal Council of Churches in 1908. The public rupture came in the 1920s: Harry Emerson Fosdick preached 'Shall the Fundamentalists Win?' on 21 May 1922; J. Gresham Machen answered with Christianity and Liberalism in 1923; and the Auburn Affirmation, released on 9 January 1924 with about 150 signatures and eventually some 1,300, answered from the other side. Then the ground moved again from inside: Karl Barth's Romans commentary of 1919, rewritten in 1922, opened the neo-orthodox turn against the liberal theology that had formed the field, and Reinhold Niebuhr, Rudolf Bultmann and Paul Tillich each reshaped the field in turn.

## new-calvinism-young-restless-reformed

New Calvinism (Young, Restless, Reformed)

In September 2006 Christianity Today ran a piece by Collin Hansen called 'Young, Restless, Reformed', and it gave a name to something already under way: a wave of younger American evangelicals adopting Reformed theology, organized through networks rather than through denominations. Its most-cited earlier text was John Piper's Desiring God (1986). Its church-planting turn came earlier than its name — Mars Hill in Seattle in 1996, and the Acts 29 network founded in 1998 by David Nicholas with Mark Driscoll — and its institutional wave came after: The Gospel Coalition, initiated by D. A. Carson and Tim Keller in 2004, first meeting in 2005, launched with its Confessional Statement in 2007; Together for the Gospel, whose first conference was in 2006. It ran on conferences, seminaries and publishing rather than on denominations, and it crossed a great many of them. Hansen's book followed in 2008.

## indonesian-christianity-the-independent-century

Indonesian Christianity — the Independent Century

Over the twentieth century Indonesia came to hold one of the largest Christian populations in the Muslim-majority world. By 2010 its churches had been running themselves for most of a century. The Batak church, the HKBP, became independent in 1930 — the first self-governing church in the Dutch East Indies. The archipelago's Christianity is regional rather than national: Sumatra, Java, Timor and the eastern islands each have their own story. Two of the century's turning points fall close together. Between 1965 and 1971 a revival ran through Timor, whose best-known account, Mel Tari's Like a Mighty Wind (1971), is disputed for embellishment, and whose most careful evaluation, George W. Peters's Indonesia Revival: Focus on Timor (1973), was written on the ground. And after the violence of 1965 and 1966, in a state that required every citizen to declare a religion, church membership grew very fast — a growth that cannot honestly be told apart from its context.

## northeast-india-and-mar-thoma-twentieth-century

Northeast India & the Mar Thoma Church — the 20th Century

Two very different Indian Christian traditions are carried together here, both of them twentieth-century continuations of older stories. In the northeast hills, revival waves ran through the Mizo country from 1906 and again in 1913, in 1919 and through the 1930s, and out of them came lengkhawm zai — hymns composed in the local language for the local way of singing, which became the churches' ordinary practice rather than a curiosity. Within a few generations Mizoram and Nagaland became, on the usual counts, among the most heavily Christian regions on earth. In Kerala, the Mar Thoma Church — the reform wing of the ancient St Thomas Christians — went through its own century under indigenous leadership, with the annual Maramon Convention as its great gathering, and sent congregations out with the diaspora.

## the-malankara-churches-orthodox-and-jacobite

The Malankara Churches — Orthodox & Jacobite

In 1912 Patriarch Abdul Masih II consecrated a Catholicos of the East for Malankara, and the ancient St Thomas community in Kerala divided into two: an autocephalous party, the Malankara Orthodox Syrian Church, and a patriarchal party, the Jacobite Syrian Christian Church, which held to the Syriac Orthodox patriarchate of Antioch. A constitution followed in 1934. A Supreme Court ruling in 1958 brought a reunion that held for a generation; in 1975 the patriarch deposed the Catholicos and the split reopened. A further Supreme Court ruling came in 1995. This is a century of ecclesiology argued in civil courts — over parishes, properties and endowments as much as over jurisdiction — and both parties wrote the record, since both were litigants throughout.

## the-georgian-orthodox-church-restored

The Georgian Orthodox Church Restored

Georgia's church had been autocephalous since antiquity until that status was abolished in 1811 and the church absorbed into the Russian one. On 12 March 1917 by the old calendar — 25 March by the new — an assembly of Georgian bishops, clergy and laypeople declared the autocephaly restored, without waiting for anyone's permission; a council that September elected Kyrion II as Catholicos-Patriarch. Kyrion was dead by 1918 in circumstances the record itself leaves unresolved. Ambrosi submitted a protest memorandum in 1922 and was put on trial for it. Then came the Soviet decades, which fell on this church as heavily as on any. Recognition came slowly and from two directions: Moscow acknowledged the restored autocephaly on 31 October 1943, and Constantinople issued its tomos on 4 March 1990. Ilia II became Catholicos-Patriarch in 1977 and still held the office in 2010.

## churches-of-christ-twentieth-century

Churches of Christ

In 1906 the United States religious census listed the Churches of Christ separately from the Disciples of Christ for the first time — a bureaucratic act that recorded a division which had been widening for a generation. David Lipscomb's own correspondence with the Census Bureau is part of how that listing came about. What was listed was the a cappella wing: congregations that sing without instruments and recognize no authority above the local congregation. Their journals did the work an office would have done elsewhere — the Gospel Advocate, the Firm Foundation and others carried the arguments, the news and the appeals for money. They founded colleges, sent missionaries widely, and divided again in the middle of the century over whether congregations could support institutions in common. They confessed the content of the historic creeds while refusing to make subscription to any creed a test. They said so plainly.

## disciples-of-christ-and-independent-christian-churches

Disciples of Christ & the Independent Christian Churches

The other side of the 1906 division took the opposite road. The Disciples of Christ kept their instruments and their national organizations, joined the Federal Council of Churches at its founding in 1908 and the World Council of Churches at its founding in 1948, and in 1968 adopted a plan called the Restructure — 'The Design' — that turned a movement of congregations into a denomination. But that step cost them a second division, which had been forming for decades: the independent Christian Churches had been meeting separately at the North American Christian Convention since 1927, and by 1971 they were listed separately in the yearbooks. So one nineteenth-century plea for Christian unity ended up producing three distinct streams.

## the-black-church-in-the-twentieth-century

The Black Church in the Twentieth Century

The Great Migration moved Black Americans out of the rural South in enormous numbers, and the church moved with them: congregations founded in the North and in the cities, and a whole religious culture remade in a generation. The Church of God in Christ, which took its Pentecostal turn in 1907 out of Memphis, rose fast and became one of the century's major bodies. The National Baptist Convention split in 1915. From the 1950s the congregations were the civil-rights movement's infrastructure: Martin Luther King Jr and the Southern Christian Leadership Conference, founded in 1957, worked out of them, and much of what survives of that movement's own voice is sermons. At the end of the 1960s James Cone published the founding works of Black theology, in 1969 and 1970, and opened a field in the seminaries that had not existed before them.
