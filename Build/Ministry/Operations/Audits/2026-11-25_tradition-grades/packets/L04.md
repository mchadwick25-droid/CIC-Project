## scandinavian-conversion-christianity

Scandinavian Conversion Christianity

Christianity came north in fits and starts over nearly three centuries. A Frankish monk named Anskar preached in Denmark and at Birka in Sweden in the 820s and 830s, with modest and short-lived results. Then came the kings. Harald Bluetooth set up a great carved stone at Jelling claiming that he had 'made the Danes Christian'. In Norway there were the two Olafs. As the sagas tell it, their methods included force, and one of them was killed in battle and venerated as a saint almost at once. Iceland took a different road. In the year 1000 the question was put to the Althing, the island's assembly. The pagan law-speaker Þorgeir thought it over for a night. Then he ruled that all Icelanders should be baptized, though some old practices were allowed in private for a time. A whole community changed its religion by public decision rather than by conquest. The window closes in 1104, when the northern church received its own archbishop at Lund. It stopped being mission ground run from Germany. The sagas that tell these stories most vividly were written down two centuries later.

## byzantine-imperial-church-heraclian-iconoclast

The Byzantine Imperial Church (Heraclian & Iconoclast Eras)

This is the imperial church of the empire's worst century and a half. Heraclius recovered the True Cross from Persia in 630 and within a decade had lost Syria, Palestine and Egypt to the Arab armies. His government's attempt to hold the remaining eastern Christians together with a compromise formula — that Christ had two natures but only one will — was pushed as state policy and resisted by a monk, Maximus, who was tried, had his tongue and right hand cut off, and died in exile. In 680 a council at Constantinople condemned the formula and taught that Christ has two wills, though it did not name him. Then came iconoclasm: emperors from Leo III onward removed and destroyed religious images, a council of 338 bishops endorsed the policy in 754, the Council of Nicaea reversed it in 787, a second imperial iconoclasm followed, and only in 843 was the matter closed for good. Through all of it the same institution — palace, patriarchate, councils — kept running, which is why this era looks so different depending on whether you stand with the court or with the monasteries.

## byzantine-imperial-church-macedonian

The Byzantine Imperial Church (Macedonian Era)

With the icons restored in 843, the Byzantine church entered two centuries of confidence. It recovered its learning and sent missions that converted Bulgaria, Moravia and eventually Rus'. Photius is the age's emblem: a layman raised straight to the patriarchate, an enormous reader who summarized 279 books, by his own count, many of which survive nowhere else, and the man who put in its sharpest form the Eastern objection to the Latin West's addition of one clause, the Filioque, to the Creed. That argument, along with disputes over papal authority and over who had jurisdiction among the newly converted Slavs, hardened across the following century and a half, until in 1054 a papal delegation and the patriarch of Constantinople excommunicated one another inside Hagia Sophia. The conventional date for a division that neither side at the time understood as final.

## bagratid-armenian-christianity

Bagratid Armenian Christianity

After two centuries under Arab governors, an Armenian kingdom was restored under the Bagratid dynasty in the 880s. With it came a burst of church building and writing. The catholicos, the head of the Armenian church, moved his seat to the royal capital of Ani. Its cathedral, finished around 1001 by the architect Trdat, still stands roofless on the plain. Trdat may also have been called to Constantinople to rebuild the dome of Hagia Sophia after an earthquake brought it down. The period's most honored voice, though, was not an architect but a monk. Gregory of Narek wrote a book of ninety-five prayers, called the Narek after his monastery. Malachia Ormanian was once Armenian patriarch of Constantinople. In a book put into English in 1912, he wrote that it was used for private prayer. It was, he said, 'regarded as a potent talisman against all kinds of dangers.' The kingdom ended in 1045, when Byzantium annexed Ani. The Seljuk Turks took the city in 1064.

## syriac-orthodox-under-islam

Syriac Orthodox Christianity under Islam

For the West Syriac church the Arab conquest lifted a weight. They had been the losing side under Byzantine emperors who wanted Chalcedon enforced, and under the caliphs they became simply one protected community among others — taxed, but not pressed to change their confession. The centuries that followed were unusually productive. Jacob of Edessa corrected the Syriac Old Testament and, by one later report, wrote the first grammar of his own language. Dionysius of Tel Mahre, patriarch and historian, traveled to Egypt in the caliph al-Ma'mun's own company and wrote a chronicle that later writers mined for centuries; Moses bar Kepha explained the liturgy and the Scriptures for his clergy. The carriers of all this were the monasteries — Mor Gabriel and the other houses of the Tur Abdin plateau, and the Syrian monastery in the Egyptian desert, where monks assembled a library that is now one of the world's great collections of early Syriac manuscripts.

## makurian-nubian-christianity

Makurian Nubian Christianity

Between Egypt and the deserts of the Sudan, the Christian kingdom of Makuria held together for centuries under an arrangement no one else had: the Baqt, a treaty struck with Muslim Egypt in 652 that set terms of exchange rather than conquest and was more or less honored for six hundred years. Under that peace, and especially after Merkurios — whom an Egyptian writer called a new Constantine — took the throne in 697, Nubia built. The cathedral at Faras was rebuilt in 707 and covered, over the following centuries, in wall paintings: enormous saints and archangels, nativities, and portraits of the bishops themselves, several shown with dark skin beside lighter-skinned holy figures. Nubian Christians wrote their own language, Old Nubian, in an alphabet adapted from Coptic. What they did not leave, or what has not survived, is much writing about their inner life; this is a Christianity known mainly by what it built and painted.

## late-anglo-saxon-christianity

Late Anglo-Saxon Christianity

In the Viking wars, Alfred of Wessex wrote, the churches were ravaged and burned, and learning all but died out. Whole dioceses lapsed. When Alfred set out to rebuild, he wrote, he could not think of one man south of the Thames who could translate a Latin letter. His answer was a program of translation. Gregory the Great's Pastoral Care and other books the clergy needed were put into English, so that they could read them at all. And the Chronicle begun in his reign is the first sustained history written in English. A century later came a second rebuilding, led by the bishops Dunstan, Æthelwold and Oswald. They refounded the monasteries on the Rule of Benedict and drew up one agreed customary for the whole country. The English language stayed central throughout. Ælfric wrote sermons in English, well over a hundred by his editors' count, so that ordinary people could hear careful teaching in their own tongue. Archbishop Wulfstan preached in 1014 as the Danish invasions closed in. He told the English that their own sins had brought the calamity on them. The Norman Conquest of 1066 ended the church's independent growth.

## georgian-revival-tao-klarjeti

Georgian Revival — Tao-Klarjeti and Athos

The Arab conquests reduced the old Georgian kingdoms to a handful of mountain principalities. Georgian Christian life was then rebuilt in the highland province of Tao-Klarjeti. There a monk named Grigol founded the monastery of Khandzta around 782. Over a very long life he seeded a whole district with monasteries. What makes him unusual for this period is that we can hear about him from inside. A Georgian monk called Giorgi Merchule wrote his life in 951. It contains the famous sentence that defines Georgia itself as the land where the liturgy is celebrated in the Georgian tongue. The movement also went abroad. Georgian monks lived in Palestine, and probably on Sinai and near Antioch too. Between 980 and 983 they founded Iviron on Mount Athos, a house that turned Greek books into Georgian. It may have worked the other way as well. A Christian retelling of a story from India, the life of the Buddha, may have passed from Georgian into Greek there. As Barlaam and Josaphat, it went on into most of the languages of Europe.

## post-aksumite-ethiopian-christianity

Post-Aksumite Ethiopian Christianity

Aksum had been one of the great powers of the late-antique world, minting its own coins, its kings Christian since the fourth century. Then the trade that sustained it moved away, the coinage stopped, and Ethiopian Christianity went quiet for something close to five hundred years — one of the least documented stretches in the whole history of any ancient church. What can be said with confidence is small but decisive. The church survived, its center shifted south into the highlands, and it went on receiving its chief bishop from the Coptic patriarch in Alexandria, a link that would last into the twentieth century. Later Ethiopian tradition fills the silence with the story of a queen remembered as Gudit, who is said to have destroyed churches around the year 960, but that account comes from much later and largely hostile sources and cannot be verified. When the record resumes under the Zagwe kings in the twelfth century, there is a functioning church with monasteries, a liturgy in Ge'ez and its own saints. Which tells us the intervening centuries were not empty, only silent.

## cistercian-monasticism

Cistercian Monasticism

In 1098 a group of monks walked out of the great Burgundian abbey of Molesme. They thought Benedictine life there had grown too comfortable. They settled in a marshy clearing called Cîteaux to keep the Rule of St Benedict as plainly as they could. That meant bare churches, simple food, no elaborate liturgy, and their own hands in the fields. Within a generation the reform had a constitution, the Carta Caritatis. It bound every daughter house to its mother house through yearly visits and a yearly general chapter. After Bernard of Clairvaux arrived in 1113, the order spread across Europe at extraordinary speed. Much of the actual labor was done by lay brothers, the conversi. They took vows but lived a separate, largely manual life beside the choir monks, on farms called granges. The order never ended. Its writers, Bernard on the Song of Songs and Aelred of Rievaulx on friendship, stayed among the most-read spiritual authors of the Latin West for centuries.

## carthusian-life

Carthusian Life

In 1084 Bruno of Cologne, who had been a leading master of the schools at Reims, gave up his career, took six companions into a high, hard valley in the Alps above Grenoble, and built a life with almost nothing in it: each man in his own cell with a small garden, meeting the others only for the night office, part of the Mass, and a weekly walk. Guigo I, the fifth prior, wrote the customs down around 1127 — cell, silence, and a strict cap on how many could live in any one house — and Guigo II's Ladder of Monks, a short account of reading, meditating, praying and resting in God, became one of the most widely used descriptions of prayer in the Western church. Deliberately, the order produced almost no public figures. The one great exception was Hugh, prior of the English charterhouse at Witham and then bishop of Lincoln, whose chaplain wrote him up in unusual detail. The Grande Chartreuse is still a working monastery, and the order has kept substantially the same shape ever since. A traditional saying has it that the Carthusians were never reformed because they were never deformed.

## canons-regular-victorine-school

Canons Regular / Victorine School

Canons regular were clergy who lived in community under a rule, usually the Rule of St Augustine. But unlike monks, they kept working in the world: serving churches, running hospitals, teaching. The most influential of their houses was the abbey of Saint-Victor on the left bank of Paris. There the master William of Champeaux withdrew from the schools in 1108. His successors built a way of learning that refused to choose between the classroom and the cloister. Hugh of St Victor argued in the Didascalicon that every art and science, rightly ordered, leads toward wisdom. Richard of St Victor mapped the stages of contemplation more carefully than anyone in the Latin West had yet done. Adam of St Victor wrote the sequences the abbey sang. The school's line ran down to Thomas Gallus, who died in 1246. The abbey itself no longer stands.

## the-franciscan-movement

The Franciscan Movement

Francis of Assisi was a cloth merchant's son who gave away his inheritance, took the gospel's instructions to the apostles as a literal rule of life, and began preaching and caring for lepers in the towns of central Italy. By 1209 others had joined him and Pope Innocent III gave the group verbal approval. What made the movement new was that it did not withdraw behind walls — the friars owned nothing, begged, worked, and preached in the streets of the fast-growing commercial towns. Clare of Assisi built the movement's women's branch and wrote her own Form of Life, the first monastic rule known to have been composed by a woman, and a third order let married lay people live a version of the same discipline without leaving ordinary life. Within a generation of Francis's death in 1226 the order was quarreling bitterly over how literally his poverty had been meant. A fight that eventually split it into Spirituals and Conventuals, and that also produced repeated rewritings of his own life story, by his own brothers, within living memory of him.

## the-dominican-order

The Dominican Order

Dominic de Guzmán was a Spanish cathedral canon who, traveling through Languedoc, concluded that the church was losing the argument with dissenters because its own preachers arrived on horseback with baggage while their opponents walked and fasted. His answer was an order built backwards from a purpose: friars trained in theology, living poor and mobile, whose stated reason for existing was preaching. Approved in 1216, the Order of Preachers wrote itself a constitution of unusual sophistication — elected superiors, representative chapters, and a clause stating that the rule did not bind under sin, so that it could never get in the way of study — and planted itself immediately in the new universities at Paris and Bologna. Its convents of women, in the German lands especially, became one of the very few settings in medieval Europe where women wrote theology and were read.

## athonite-and-comnenian-byzantine-monasticism

Athonite & Comnenian Byzantine Monasticism

In 963 the monk Athanasios founded the Great Lavra on the mountain peninsula of Athos in northern Greece. Later accounts say a Byzantine emperor backed him. The loose scatter of hermits already living there began to take the shape it has kept ever since. Athos became a self-governing league of monasteries. Each ran on a written charter, called a typikon. In it the founder set down exactly what the house was for, down to the diet, the liturgy and the charity. In Constantinople at the same time, Symeon the New Theologian preached to his monks. He taught that God can be known directly and consciously, in experienced light. He pressed the claim so hard that he was pushed out of his monastery. Between them, the houses of Athos and the monasteries of the capital kept the practice of inner prayer alive. The next century would gather it up and name it hesychasm.

## the-beguines

The Beguines

From the late twelfth century, women in the towns of the Low Countries and the Rhineland began to live religious lives without joining any order. They took no lifelong vows, kept no rule and lived under no enclosure. They kept their own property or worked, often in cloth. Some lived alone, others in walled courtyard communities called beguinages. They prayed, and nursed the sick and the dying. Some of them wrote, and that is what makes them extraordinary on any map of medieval Christianity. Hadewijch wrote visions and letters. Mechthild of Magdeburg wrote The Flowing Light of the Godhead, and Marguerite Porete wrote The Mirror of Simple Souls. These are among the very few medieval accounts of a religious life told from the inside, by the women living it. Because they fitted no category, they drew suspicion. Porete was burned in Paris in 1310. In 1312 the Council of Vienne condemned errors it laid on beguines and beghards.

## the-waldensians

The Waldensians

Around 1173 a rich merchant of Lyon named Valdes gave away his property. He had parts of Scripture put into the language people actually spoke, and he began preaching in the streets. His confession of faith was wholly orthodox. What he would not do was stop preaching without a license, and in 1184 the bull Ad abolendam condemned him and his followers for exactly that. Driven out of the cities, the movement held on in the Alpine valleys between France and Italy. It lasted through three and a half centuries of on-and-off persecution, with its own traveling ministers and its own books. In 1532 its leaders met the Reformers at Chanforan and threw in with the Reformed churches. So a single line of protest runs from twelfth-century Lyon into the Reformation, though scholars disagree about how unbroken it was.

## zagwe-and-early-solomonic-ethiopian-christianity

Zagwe Ethiopian Christianity

After the old kingdom of Aksum faded, a new line of kings, the Zagwe, ruled the Ethiopian highlands from the twelfth century. Under them the church went on as it had for eight hundred years, with one odd feature. Its head bishop, the abun, was not Ethiopian but a monk sent from the Coptic patriarch in Egypt. So a free kingdom kept its senior churchman on loan from another land. The Zagwe are best known for eleven churches cut downward into solid rock. They stand at the town now called Lalibela, after the king who had them made. Several bear the names of holy places, such as Golgotha and Bethlehem. A Portuguese visitor in the 1520s was shown a tomb there said to copy Christ's tomb in Jerusalem. In 1270 Yekuno Amlak pushed the Zagwe aside, and his line claimed descent from Solomon and the Queen of Sheba. The Kebra Nagast, put together in the generations after he took power, set out that claim. Ethiopian kings leaned on it for the next seven centuries.

## maronite-christianity

Maronite Christianity

The Maronites take their name from a Syriac monk, Maron, and from the monastery built around his memory. By the Middle Ages they were a church of their own, in the West Syriac family of worship. They lived mostly in the mountains and gorges of Lebanon, where they cut their monasteries and hermit cells straight into the cliffs. Their early history is unusually hard to recover. Much of what is told about it was written down later, by people arguing about it. The question was whether the community had once held that Christ had only one will, a view the wider church had rejected. What is clear is that when the crusaders came, the Maronites joined in communion with Rome. William of Tyre describes the union around 1182. In 1215 the Maronite patriarch Jeremiah al-Amshitti went to Rome for the Fourth Lateran Council. After it, a papal bull set out the terms of the union.

## military-religious-orders-templars-hospitallers

Military-Religious Orders (Templars, Hospitallers)

In the decades after the First Crusade, two kinds of institution fused into something the church had never authorized before. The Hospitallers began as a hospital in Jerusalem caring for pilgrims. They called the sick 'our lords the sick', and only later took on a military role. The Templars began as a small band of knights guarding the pilgrim roads. They took monastic vows and were given a rule at the Council of Troyes in 1129. Bernard of Clairvaux wrote their defense, In Praise of the New Knighthood. He argued that a man who killed in this cause was not a homicide but, in his own coined word, a malicide: a killer of evil. Both orders grew enormously rich. They ran castles, estates and banking networks from the Latin East across Europe, and lost their reason for existing when Acre fell in 1291. The Templars were arrested in France in 1307 and made to confess under torture. In 1312 Pope Clement V abolished their order for ever.

## cathars-albigensians

Cathars / Albigensians

From the 1140s, church writers in the Rhineland and then in southern France describe communities with their own ministers and their own rite of entry. They taught that the visible world of matter was not the work of the good God. This dualism placed them outside Christianity as the councils had defined it. In Languedoc they had noble protection and something close to a parallel church. In 1209 the papacy launched a crusade against them. Two decades of war followed, then the inquisition, then the siege of Montségur in 1244. There about two hundred of their ministers walked into a fire rather than recant. Their ministers were called the perfect. The last known perfect of Languedoc, Guillaume Bélibaste, was burned in 1321. Almost everything known about them comes from the people who destroyed them. Historians now argue hard about how much of 'Catharism' was one movement at all. Some of it may have been a label that inquisitors built out of scattered local dissent.

## joachimite-current

Joachimite Current

Joachim of Fiore was a Calabrian abbot who read history as a pattern with a shape: three ages, of the Father, the Son, and the Holy Spirit, the third still to come and not far off. He died in 1202, and the current that carried his reading afterwards was never an order or a church. It was a way of reading the Bible and the calendar together, and it spread especially among Franciscans, who found in it a description of their own movement. In 1254 a young friar published an Introduction to the Eternal Gospel that made the claim explicit and scandalous, and it was condemned. The current then fixed on 1260 as the year the new age would open. The year came and went. Some let it go, and some carried the expectation on into the bitter Franciscan quarrel over poverty, where it did most of its later work.

## free-spirit-heresy

'Free Spirit' Heresy

In the early fourteenth century the church declared that a sect existed. The decree Ad nostrum, issued at the Council of Vienne in 1312, listed eight errors said to be held by beguines and beghards in Germany. Their core claim was that a soul made perfect in this life can no longer sin. Such a soul needs neither the church nor the virtues any longer. One error spoke of people living 'in the spirit of liberty'. Interrogators then went looking for people who held them, and found them, often because the questions supplied the answers. Modern research, above all Robert Lerner's study of 1972, concluded that no organized sect of this kind ever existed. What existed was a wide spectrum of mystical piety, much of it among beguines and beghards. There was also a charge that could be laid against any part of it. The movement named here probably was not real; the charge and its victims certainly were.

## the-georgian-golden-age

The Georgian Golden Age

For two and a half centuries Georgia was a real power in the Caucasus, and its church rose with it. David IV, called the Builder, broke the Seljuk hold at the battle of Didgori in 1121, and in 1106 had begun the monastery of Gelati, which was also an academy: philosophy, astronomy, rhetoric and Greek taught alongside the liturgy, with the Neoplatonist Ioane Petritsi among its scholars. Under his great-granddaughter Tamar the kingdom reached its widest extent, its churches were covered in frescoes, and its language was given a literature to match. The age ended not in decline but in conquest — after the Mongol invasions Georgia became a tributary state, and the death of Queen Rusudan in 1245 closes the age.
