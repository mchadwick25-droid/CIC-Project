## serbian-and-bulgarian-medieval-monasticism

Serbian & Bulgarian Medieval Monasticism

In 1196 the Serbian ruler Stefan Nemanja gave up his throne and became a monk. His youngest son, Rastko, was already a monk on Mount Athos under the name Sava, and Nemanja joined him there. Together they founded Hilandar, a Serbian house on the Holy Mountain. From it the shape of Serbian church life was set. Athonite charters were adapted for Serbian monasteries, and a ruling family produced its own saints and then wrote their lives. In 1219 Sava won a self-governing Serbian church and became its first archbishop. In Bulgaria the restored patriarchate at Tarnovo did much the same. It gathered a school of writers around it, and the cult of hermits like John of Rila. Both churches may have been the road by which hesychast prayer passed from Greek into Slavonic, and from there to Russia. No source quoted here traces that road. At the end of the fourteenth century the Ottoman advance overran Bulgaria, and Serbia followed within two generations.

## byzantine-church-after-1054

The Byzantine Church after the Schism

In July 1054 a papal legate laid a bull of excommunication on the altar of Hagia Sophia during the liturgy and walked out. The patriarch, Michael Cerularius, condemned him in return. It was not yet a final break. But the split between the Greek and Latin churches did not heal, and in 1204 it turned to disaster. A crusading army sacked Constantinople and set up a Latin patriarch. The church of the empire was driven out to Nicaea for nearly sixty years. The Byzantines won back their city in 1261. In 1274 Emperor Michael VIII needed help from the West against his enemies, so he accepted union with Rome at the Second Council of Lyon. His own clergy and monks refused it, and the union fell apart.

## latin-papal-church-gregorian

The Latin Papal Church (Gregorian Reform to Innocent III)

In the middle of the eleventh century, a circle of reformers around Pope Leo IX set out to free the church from lay lords. These lords chose its bishops and sold its offices. The reformers ended by claiming for the papacy a power no bishop of Rome had held before. Gregory VII's Dictatus Papae of 1075 declared, among much else, that the pope may depose emperors. The Investiture Contest that followed ran for half a century. Its most famous scene came in 1077. The German king Henry IV stood barefoot for three days at the gate of Canossa, begging the pope's pardon. What followed was a working papal monarchy. Canon law was gathered into codes, papal legates went everywhere, and great councils met at the Lateran. Innocent III, pope from 1198 to 1216, judged between kings, approved Francis of Assisi's first friars and called crusades. In 1215 his Fourth Lateran Council made law for the whole Latin church. Its rules ran from how the eucharist is defined to a rule that every Christian confess once a year.

## kievan-rus-christianity-1054-1240

Kievan Rus' Christianity, 1054-1240

In the two centuries after Yaroslav the Wise died in 1054, the Christianity that had arrived in Rus' only two generations earlier put down roots of its own. Its center was the Caves Monastery at Kyiv, begun by a hermit named Anthony in a hillside cave above the Dnieper and turned into a community by Theodosius, its third abbot, who gave it a Byzantine rule and a distinctive emphasis on humility, manual work and care for the poor. Out of that monastery came bishops, chronicles, and a book of stories about its own monks. The church was formally a metropolitanate of Constantinople, usually headed by a Greek, but native metropolitans appeared and a native literature and a native calendar of saints grew up around the princely dynasty. It ended abruptly: the Mongols sacked Kyiv in 1240.

## armenian-cilicia

Armenian Christianity in Cilicia

After the Seljuks took the Armenian homeland, Armenians moved to Cilicia, on the coast of the Mediterranean Sea. There a small state grew into a kingdom, and Levon I was crowned in 1198. The Armenian church went with them. In 1151 its head, the catholicos, moved his seat to Hromkla, a fortress above the Euphrates. There Nerses Shnorhali, 'the Gracious', wrote the finest Armenian church poetry of his day. He and Manuel I, the Byzantine emperor, wrote each other long and remarkably generous letters. They wrote about how the Armenian and Greek churches might be one again. That never happened. In the next century the church in Cilicia dealt instead with the Latin crusader states next door. Some Armenians were glad of that closeness, and others deeply resented it. For a long time it shaped the church's arguments with itself.

## syriac-renaissance

The Syriac Renaissance

The Syriac Orthodox Church lived under Muslim rule, and for a while alongside the crusader states, in the country between the Euphrates and the Tigris. And in exactly this period it produced its best writing in four hundred years. Dionysius bar Salibi commented on the whole Bible and on the liturgy. Michael the Syrian, patriarch from 1166, compiled a world chronicle that remains one of the most valuable historical sources for the medieval Middle East, not least because he quotes at length from earlier writers whose books are otherwise lost. And Gregory Bar Hebraeus, who died in 1286, wrote across nearly everything — history, theology, canon law, grammar, astronomy, medicine, and a collection of jokes. In both Syriac and Arabic, and did it while serving as his church's chief bishop for the eastern provinces under Mongol rule.

## church-of-the-east-under-the-mongols

The Church of the East under the Mongols

The Church of the East had spread far into Central Asia centuries earlier, and when the Mongols swept west a good many of the peoples they absorbed — the Kerait and the Öngüd among them — were already Christian in this tradition, as were several of the great khans' wives and mothers. For a few decades the church that Latin Europe called Nestorian had extraordinary access: tolerated and sometimes favored at the Mongol court, and in 1281 a monk who had traveled the whole width of Asia from China was elected its catholicos as Yahballaha III. His teacher, Rabban Bar Sauma, had set out with him on pilgrimage toward Jerusalem and ended up instead as the Ilkhan's ambassador to the West, reaching Rome and the courts of France and England and writing the journey down — one of the very few medieval descriptions of Europe by a traveler from Asia. Yahballaha died in 1317, and the favor did not survive him.

## the-carmelites

The Carmelites

Some time between 1206 and 1214 a group of Latin hermits living in a wadi on Mount Carmel above the bay from crusader Acre asked Albert, the patriarch of Jerusalem — who kept his seat at Acre — for a written way of life. What he gave them was short and severe: separate cells, silence, work, daily Mass, and constant meditation on the law of the Lord. As the crusader states contracted the hermits moved west, into England, France and Italy, where a hermit's rule made little sense in a town — so the life was adapted into a mendicant order alongside the Franciscans and Dominicans, and Innocent IV approved the revised Rule in 1247. The order kept the mountain in its name and in its self-understanding, and traced its origins, in its own later retellings, all the way back to the prophet Elijah.

## balkan-bogomil-continuation

Balkan Bogomilism, 1054-1300

The Bogomils began in tenth-century Bulgaria and were still there, and in Byzantine territory, throughout this period. What their opponents describe is a movement with no church buildings, no clergy in the ordinary sense, no sacraments as the church understood them, and a teaching that the material world and the institutions of the visible church alike belonged to an evil power. Around the turn of the twelfth century the emperor Alexios I had a Bogomil teacher called Basil brought to Constantinople, drew out his beliefs by pretending to be a convert, and had him burned in the Hippodrome. The emperor's daughter Anna Komnene tells the story, and Alexios commissioned the theologian Euthymios Zigabenos to write the movement up for refutation. In 1211 a synod at Tarnovo anathematized them again. Not one line survives that a Bogomil wrote about themselves.

## latin-church-of-outremer

The Latin Church of Outremer

When the First Crusade took Jerusalem in 1099, the conquerors did not simply add a diocese. They built a whole Latin church on top of an older Christian landscape. They set up a Latin patriarch in Jerusalem, and then another at Antioch. There the Greek patriarch soon left for Constantinople. For nearly two centuries this transplanted church ran parishes and cathedrals. It also served a huge flow of pilgrims to the holy places. The canons of the Holy Sepulchre stood at its center, with a liturgy shaped by processions to the sites themselves. Most of the people it ruled were not Latin. They were Greek, Syriac, Armenian and Muslim. Its dealings with them ran from workable peace to plain dispossession. When Jerusalem was lost in 1187 the patriarchate moved to Acre. When Acre fell in 1291, the church of Outremer ended with it.

## coptic-christianity-fatimid-to-mamluk

Coptic Christianity, Fatimid to Mamluk

By this period Egypt's Christians had lived under Muslim rule for four centuries, and the shape of their life was set by that: a poll tax, stretches of tolerance under the Fatimids alternating with sharp episodes of persecution, and a slow shift out of Coptic and into Arabic as the everyday language. What they did with Arabic is the surprise. In the thirteenth century a single Cairo family, the sons of al-Assal, produced a body of work — canon law, theology, biblical scholarship, philosophy, and Coptic grammars and dictionaries written in Arabic to keep the old language from being lost entirely — that amounted to a renaissance of Coptic learning in a new tongue. Their patriarch for part of that stretch, Cyril III ibn Laqlaq, was a learned and combative man who spent his reign gathering the church's authority into his own hands.

## the-humiliati

The Humiliati

In twelfth-century Lombardy, groups of lay wool-workers and weavers began to live a simple, demanding life of penance. They wore plain dress and worked with their hands. They refused the oaths and the violence that feudal society expected. They called themselves the Humiliati, 'the humbled ones.' In 1184 Pope Lucius III condemned the Waldensians for preaching without leave. Later writers say he condemned the Humiliati with them. If so, their story then took the opposite turn. The Waldensians stayed apart for good, but Pope Innocent III took the Humiliati back around 1200. He made them three recognized orders. Two were religious orders. The third was for married lay people who stayed in ordinary working life under a shared rule. For about two centuries they were a real presence in the towns of northern Italy. Their rule and community records still survive. In 1571 a papal bull shut down the order of Humiliati brothers.

## the-english-mystics-and-devout

The English Mystics and Devout

Across a single century, a scattering of English men and women began writing about the inner life of prayer. They wrote in their own language rather than in Latin. Richard Rolle, the Yorkshire hermit, wrote the Fire of Love. Walter Hilton wrote the Scale of Perfection. An author whose name is lost wrote The Cloud of Unknowing. All of them wrote for recluses and devout lay people rather than for scholars. Two of the voices are women's. Julian of Norwich was an anchoress, enclosed in a cell beside a Norwich parish church. She spent decades revising an account of sixteen revelations she had received during a near-fatal illness. In the end she reached the line she is still known for: that all shall be well. Margery Kempe, a mayor's daughter from Bishop's Lynn, ran a brewery of her own for a few years. She could not write. Her own book says she bore fourteen children. She dictated to scribes a book about her pilgrimages, her uncontrollable public weeping and the heresy examinations she survived. It is the closest thing the period leaves us to an ordinary believer's own life story. Carthusian monks copied The Cloud and Hilton's Scale and kept them safe.

## rhineland-mysticism-and-the-friends-of-god

Rhineland Mysticism & the Friends of God

Along the Rhine, a generation of Dominican preachers began to preach in German. Meister Eckhart, Johannes Tauler and Henry Suso spoke to convents of nuns and to devout lay people. To do it, they had to invent words for the inner life that German did not yet have. Eckhart pushed furthest. He spoke of the ground of the soul, and of a birth of God within it. His words got him put on trial for heresy. He died before the case closed. In 1329 a papal bull condemned twenty-eight articles drawn from his work, and that is how his memory reached everyone who came after. Around Strasbourg and Basel, a mixed circle of clergy and lay people called themselves Gottesfreunde, the Friends of God. They carried the same piety outside the cloister. Out of that circle came the Theologia Deutsch, a book whose author is unknown. Much of what survives does so because women kept it. Nuns took sermons down as they were preached. Convents wrote sister-books about the lives of their own communities. And Elsbeth Stagel worked with Suso on the book of his own life.

## devotio-moderna-brethren-of-the-common-life

Devotio Moderna / Brethren of the Common Life

It began with Geert Grote, a wealthy and worldly scholar of Deventer. He gave up his career and opened his own house to a group of devout women. Then he began preaching repentance in the towns of the IJssel valley. After his death in 1384 his followers organized. The Brothers and Sisters of the Common Life lived together and paid their way by copying books and other ordinary work. They took no vows and did not leave ordinary society. Beside them stood a stricter branch, the canons of Windesheim. Its houses kept their own chronicles. Johannes Busch wrote the chronicle of Windesheim, and Thomas à Kempis that of Mount St Agnes. What held them together was less a doctrine than a method: a plain, inward and orderly devotion. They had the habit of keeping a rapiarium, a personal notebook of gathered passages to return to. One such gathering became the Imitation of Christ. It is credited to Thomas à Kempis of Mount St Agnes near Zwolle, though its authorship is disputed. The houses also took in schoolboys and shaped the Latin schools of towns like Deventer and Zwolle. That is how the movement's way of shaping lives reached far more people than ever joined it.

## hesychasm-and-the-palamite-synthesis

Hesychasm & the Palamite Synthesis

On Mount Athos monks had long practiced hesychia, or stillness. They sat alone, held the breath steady, and repeated the Jesus Prayer until it went on by itself. Some of them said they saw an uncreated light. The Calabrian scholar Barlaam mocked the practice. Gregory Palamas, an Athonite monk who later became archbishop of Thessalonica, answered him in the Triads. God cannot be known in his essence, he argued, and yet he is truly given in his energies. So what the praying monk meets is God himself, not some created stand-in. The argument ran for two decades. Councils at Constantinople in 1341, 1347 and 1351 settled it in Palamas's favor, and the hesychast view became the standard of Orthodox theology. Beside it stands a very different voice. Nicholas Cabasilas, a layman of Thessalonica, wrote The Life in Christ. He showed that the same life in God came through the sacraments to people who would never enter a monastery.

## lollardy

Lollardy

Lollardy grew out of the teaching of John Wycliffe, an Oxford theologian. He attacked the church's wealth and the pope's authority. He denied transubstantiation, the belief that the bread and wine become Christ's body and blood. And he said the Bible should be open to all in English. But the movement soon became much wider than him. Most of its followers were ordinary people. They were weavers, smiths, tailors, wives and widows who owned or shared the Bible in English. They met in one another's houses to read it aloud. They doubted images, pilgrimage and the priest's power over the sacrament. The response was severe. In 1401 burning for heresy became law in England. In 1409 Archbishop Arundel's rules banned any English Bible made without a license. Sir John Oldcastle's failed rising in 1414 made Lollardy treason as well as heresy. After that the movement went underground. It lived on in scattered households, linked to each other, into the 1520s. Almost everything known about them comes from the records kept by those who tried them. To convict someone, you first had to write down what they said.

## the-hussite-and-bohemian-brethren-movement

The Hussite & Bohemian Brethren Movement

Jan Hus preached reform in Czech at Prague's Bethlehem Chapel for more than ten years. Then in 1415 the Council of Constance burned him as a heretic. The shock turned a university movement into a national one. Its emblem was the chalice. It stood for the demand that ordinary lay people, and not only priests, should receive the wine at communion. Its program was set out in the Four Articles of Prague in 1420. Crusades were called against Bohemia, and the Hussite armies under Jan Žižka and then Prokop the Bald beat them one after another. In 1436 the Compacts of Basel granted what no other medieval dissent ever won: legal recognition. Two bodies grew out of that settlement. The Utraquist church held the parishes, and for a time, under George of Poděbrady, the crown itself. Then from 1457 came the Unity of the Brethren. It was a smaller, stricter, pacifist body, formed at Kunvald under the influence of the layman Petr Chelčický. By 1501 it had printed what is often called the first hymnbook in a European vernacular.

## the-ethiopian-monastic-flowering

The Ethiopian Monastic Flowering

In 1270 the Solomonic dynasty took the Ethiopian throne. It claimed descent from Solomon and the Queen of Sheba. A wave of monks came with it and carried churches deep into newly conquered lands. Two networks led the way: the houses Tekle Haymanot built in Shewa, and the northern houses of the followers of Ewostatewos. The bond between crown and cloister was contested throughout. The Ewostathians kept Saturday as well as Sunday as a Sabbath. The lives of their own saints say they were persecuted for it for generations. Then, at a council in 1450, the emperor Zara Yaqob gave way. His court chronicle says he ordered the old Sabbath kept as holy as Sunday. Zara Yaqob wrote theology himself. He reordered the church and pressed devotion to Mary on it. When the monks of Estifanos refused to bow down before Mary and the cross, he persecuted them. All of it was written down in Ge'ez: royal chronicles, saints' lives, hymns to Mary, and the Kebra Nagast. That book gave the dynasty its Solomonic charter.

## muscovite-monastic-christianity

Muscovite Monastic Christianity

Sergius of Radonezh withdrew into the woods north of Moscow in the 1340s to live alone. A monastery grew up around him. Within a generation his disciples had founded dozens more, pushing ever deeper into the northern forests. Later Russians called this the northern Thebaid. The texts followed: Epiphanius the Wise's life of Sergius, and the skete rules of Nil Sorsky. So did the art. Andrei Rublev painted his Trinity in Sergius's memory, for Sergius's own monastery. The same years saw the sharpest quarrel in Russian church history. Nil Sorsky and the non-possessors held that monasteries should own no land and no peasants. Monks should live by their own work. Joseph of Volotsk and the possessors held that rich monasteries could feed the poor, train clergy and carry the church's work. The monasteries kept their lands. Soon after 1496 Grand Prince Ivan planned to take them, but the metropolitan talked him out of it. In the middle of all this, in 1448, Russian bishops chose their own metropolitan without waiting on Constantinople. It was the beginning of an independent Russian church.

## observant-reform-and-popular-preaching

Observant Reform & Popular Preaching

The Observance began as an argument inside the religious orders. Its friars said the Rule should be kept as it was written. It should not be kept as years of exceptions had relaxed it. It started small, with Paoluccio Trinci's handful of friars at Brugliano in 1368. Then it spread across the Franciscans, Dominicans, Augustinians and Benedictines alike. In the end, reformed houses formed rival provinces beside the unreformed ones. Its public face was preaching. Bernardino of Siena drew whole towns into the square to hear him for weeks on end. In 1427 he preached forty-five sermons at Siena. A listener in the crowd took them down as he spoke, with his asides and the crowd's reactions. It is about as close as anyone gets to hearing a medieval sermon, not just reading one. John of Capistrano did the same across central Europe. The movement's dark side belongs in the same story. Capistrano also drove the persecution of Jewish communities. In 1447 the pope made him enforcer of the papal laws restricting Jews and Muslims in Italy. The most notorious case was at Breslau in 1453, where Jews were burned.

## late-medieval-lay-parish-piety

Late-Medieval Lay Parish Piety

For most people in late-medieval England, Christianity was the parish. It meant the mass seen from the nave, the yearly round of feasts and processions, and the guild you joined. It meant the light you paid to keep burning before an image, and the church ale that funded the roof. It was not passive. Churchwardens were ordinary laymen, elected and accountable to their neighbors. They collected and spent the money. Their accounts show parishes rebuilding naves, raising towers, buying vestments and hiring carvers on their own initiative. Books of hours, soon printed by the thousand, put the same daily prayers into the hands of any household that could afford them. Sermon collections like John Mirk's Festial show roughly what people in the pews were hearing. Almost none of these people wrote about what they believed. What survives instead is the record of what they did with their money and their time. It is a different kind of evidence, and in some ways a more honest one.

## taborite-radicalism

Taborite Radicalism

In 1420 the most radical wing of the Hussite movement founded a fortified town in southern Bohemia. They named it Tábor, after the mountain of the Transfiguration. They expected Christ to return at any moment, and believed only a few chosen places would be spared. The year passed, the world went on, and the community stayed. Tabor stripped worship down to what it could find in scripture: worship in Czech, no vestments, no purgatory, no cult of the saints. It chose its own bishop, Nicholas of Pelhřimov, and in its early years held property in common. It also fielded the Hussite wars' most effective army. Its wagon forts, under Jan Žižka and then Prokop the Bald, broke crusade after crusade. In the end it was fellow Hussites who destroyed it. Utraquist lords and Prague's Old Town, joined by Catholic lords such as Ulrich of Rosenberg, crushed the Taborite field armies at Lipany in 1434. In 1452 George of Poděbrady, himself a Utraquist, took the town. He made it use the moderate Utraquist service and had the Taborite priest Wenceslas Koranda locked up.

## fraticelli-spiritual-franciscan-rigorists

Fraticelli (Spiritual Franciscan rigorists)

The Franciscans had argued for a century about how literally to take Francis's poverty. In the 1310s and 20s the argument was closed by force. John XXII ordered the strictest friars to conform, and had four of them burned at Marseille in 1318 for refusing. In 1323 he declared it heretical to insist that Christ and the apostles had owned nothing at all. A devotional ideal had become a condemned idea. Those who would not submit became the fraticelli, the 'little brothers'. Their prosecutors used the name as a legal label as much as a description. They lasted another century and a half in the Marches of Ancona, in Provence and in scattered Italian and Greek communities. Lay friends kept them going. Among these were the beguins of Languedoc, who honored the burned friars as martyrs and were hunted for it. In 1467 Pope Paul II had a group of them brought to Rome for public penance. Within a few years the last traces of them are gone.
