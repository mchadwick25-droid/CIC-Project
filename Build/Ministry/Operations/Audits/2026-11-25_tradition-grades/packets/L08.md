## the-moravian-church-at-herrnhut

The Moravian Church at Herrnhut

In 1722 a handful of families slipped over the Bohemian border into Saxony. They were the remnant of the Unity of the Brethren, the Czech reform church. It had survived in hiding for nearly a century after the Habsburgs outlawed it. Count Nikolaus von Zinzendorf let them settle on his estate, and they built a village called Herrnhut. Years of quarreling followed, among the refugees and the other seekers who joined them. Then on 13 August 1727 they shared the Lord's Supper, and they always dated their renewal from that day. What followed was remarkable for a settlement of a few hundred people. They kept a prayer watch in shifts around the clock. They sorted the church into 'choirs' by age and state of life. They wrote up a diary every single day, and thousands of ordinary members set down their own life stories. From 1732 they sent missionaries to the enslaved people of St Thomas, and to Greenland and Suriname. Others went to the Native congregations of Pennsylvania and New York. They were not the first, for Lutherans trained at Halle had reached India in 1706.

The Brethren tried the Cape early. George Schmidt landed there on 9 July 1737. He taught Khoikhoi children to read, and he baptized seven people. The Brethren had to leave in 1745. In 1786 some of them met a woman Schmidt had baptized, and she still prized the Dutch Bible he had given her. The Herrnhut synod of 1789 renewed the plan. Three missionaries reached the Cape on 21 November 1792.

The Brethren also looked to North Africa. La Trobe wrote in 1771 that three Brethren lived at Cairo, with the Copts of Egypt and Abyssinia as their special aim. In 1752 Dr. Frederick William Hooker went to Cairo, on the way to the Christians of Abyssinia. Watson's history says he met the Coptic patriarch, who gave him a letter in reply to one from Count Zinzendorf. In Algiers, Brother Richter went in 1740 to the Christian slaves. He died of the plague. Another Brother stayed there from 1744 to 1748.

Not every Czech Protestant had left. In 1781 the emperor Joseph II issued a Patent of Toleration. It freed the Lutherans and Calvinists. It left out even the Unity of the Brethren. Count Lützow wrote in 1920 that many Bohemians had secretly stayed Protestant. They were glad of the Patent. The Toleration churches after 1781 are not told here.

## the-methodist-revival

The Methodist Revival

John Wesley was an Oxford-trained Church of England priest whose methodical little religious society had earned him a mocking nickname and whose mission to Georgia had ended in failure. Until, at a meeting in Aldersgate Street, London, in May 1738, he felt his heart 'strangely warmed' and began preaching assurance and holiness to anyone who would stand still for it. Shut out of many pulpits, he and George Whitefield preached in fields and at pit-heads to crowds of thousands. But Wesley did something rarer than preaching, which was to organize, dividing converts into 'classes' of a dozen who met weekly to give an account of their lives, licensing lay preachers to ride circuits, and holding an annual conference. His brother Charles wrote the movement's theology into thousands of hymns, so that people who could not have read a treatise sang one instead. Wesley meant all of this to renew the Church of England from inside and insisted to the end that he had not left it. After his death in 1791 the connexion became a separate church anyway.

## the-evangelical-awakening-in-new-england

The Evangelical Awakening in New England

It began in the winter of 1734-35 in Northampton, Massachusetts. There the town's minister, Jonathan Edwards, watched an ordinary church in the Connecticut Valley come apart and remake itself in a wave of conversions. Then he did something unusual: he wrote down what had happened, case by case. His cases included a young woman the town had thought beyond hope and a four-year-old girl. The English preacher George Whitefield toured the colonies from 1739, and scattered local revivals became a general one. Preachers crossed parish lines, and crowds met in the open air. The awakening split New England's churches. The New Lights defended it. The Old Lights, led by Charles Chauncy of Boston, attacked it as 'enthusiasm', false claims to the Spirit, and disorder. Hundreds of Separate congregations broke away. Many of them may later have become Baptist, but how many is unclear. Black and Native Christians of its world also began to write. Among them were the poet Phillis Wheatley and the preacher Samson Occom.

## the-old-believers

The Old Believers

In the 1650s Patriarch Nikon set out to correct the Russian service books and rituals against contemporary Greek practice — two fingers in the sign of the cross became three, the spelling of Jesus's name changed, processions reversed direction. And a great part of the Russian church refused. To them the old rite was not a detail but the faith itself, handed down and now being altered by a church they concluded had fallen; the council of 1666-67 anathematized them, and the state hunted them for generations. Their great voice was Archpriest Avvakum, who wrote his own life story from an underground prison at Pustozersk before he was burned in 1682 — the first sustained autobiography in Russian, plain-spoken, furious and unexpectedly funny. Cut off from bishops, the movement divided between those who found priests and those who concluded the priesthood had ended altogether, and survived in the far north, the forests and Siberia, in some cases by mass self-immolation rather than submission.

## the-hesychast-revival-paisius-and-the-philokalia

The Hesychast Revival - Paisius & the Philokalia

In 1746 a young Ukrainian monk named Paisius Velichkovsky arrived on Mount Athos and began working through its monastery libraries for the old Greek texts on inner prayer that almost nobody was reading any more. He gathered a community, moved with it to Moldavia, and at Neamț built a translation workshop where monks turned those fathers into Slavonic; meanwhile on Athos, Macarius of Corinth and Nicodemus the Hagiorite assembled the same material into an anthology printed at Venice in 1782 under the title Philokalia, 'the love of the beautiful'. Paisius's Slavonic version, the Dobrotolubiye, was printed in Moscow in 1793, and the effect was enormous and largely unplanned: a body of monastic teaching on the Jesus Prayer and the watching of one's own heart, gathered for specialists, went into general circulation and reached ordinary lay readers.

## port-royal-jansenism

Port-Royal Jansenism

Port-Royal was a Cistercian convent outside Paris, reformed into severity by a young abbess, Angélique Arnauld, and it became the center of a movement that read Augustine on grace harder than the Jesuits or the French crown could tolerate. Around it gathered the solitaires — men of rank who gave up their careers to live plainly near the abbey, teach in its small schools and write. And in its orbit stood Blaise Pascal, whose Provincial Letters mocked Jesuit moral reasoning so effectively that the mockery outlived the quarrel. Rome condemned five propositions said to be contained in Jansen's Augustinus, and the nuns were required to sign a formulary declaring both that the propositions were heretical and that they were in the book. A core of them refused, on the ground that no one could compel them to swear to a matter of fact they had no way of knowing. Louis XIV lost patience: the nuns were dispersed in 1709 and the buildings pulled down, down to digging up the cemetery.

## the-welsh-revival-tradition

The Welsh Revival Tradition

The Welsh revival began independently of England and in its own language: in 1735 Howell Harris, a schoolmaster from Trefeca, and Daniel Rowland, a curate at Llangeitho, were converted within months of each other and began preaching to whoever would gather. What grew was not only a wave of feeling but a structure. The seiat, or experience meeting, a small society where members told one another honestly how things stood with their souls, described in a handbook written by the movement's poet, William Williams Pantycelyn. Williams gave Welsh Christianity a hymn corpus that became a national possession, and so, more quietly, did Ann Griffiths, a farmer's daughter who composed her hymns aloud and never wrote them down. They survive because her maid Ruth Evans held them in memory and Ruth's husband set them on paper after Ann died at twenty-nine.

## the-antonian-movement-in-kongo

The Antonian Movement in Kongo

By 1700 the Kingdom of Kongo had been Christian for two centuries — its kings baptized, its capital São Salvador full of churches, its catechism printed in Kikongo. And it was also in ruins, broken by civil war and the slave trade, its capital abandoned. In 1704 a young noblewoman named Kimpa Vita, baptized Beatriz, announced that she had died and been possessed by Saint Anthony of Padua, and that he now spoke through her. The kingdom must be reunited, the capital reoccupied, and Christ and the saints understood as Kongolese rather than European. Thousands followed her back into São Salvador. Two Capuchin missionaries persuaded the restored king to arrest her, and in July 1706 she was burned as a heretic.

## shakers

Shakers

The Shakers began among a small group of dissenters in Manchester who worshipped with shaking, dancing and ecstatic speech, and were transformed by one of them. Ann Lee, a factory-hand who had buried four infant children and came to believe that celibacy was the way out of the world's fallenness. In 1774 she led eight followers to New York, and the communities that grew after her death in 1784 became one of the most successful communal experiments in American history. Property held in common, men and women living apart but governing jointly, work treated as a form of worship. Their published confession identified Christ's second appearing with Ann Lee herself, which set them apart from the churches around them, and their dancing worship gave them the name their neighbors used. What they built was startlingly beautiful: villages of plain, exact buildings, furniture pared down to its function, and thousands of songs.

## swedenborgian-new-church

Swedenborgian New Church

Emanuel Swedenborg was a Swedish mining engineer, anatomist and member of the House of Nobles who, in his mid-fifties, began having visions and spent the rest of his life writing an enormous body of theological work in Latin — accounts of heaven and hell, of the inner meaning of Scripture, and of a Christianity in which God is one person, Jesus Christ, rather than three. He never founded a church and died in London in 1772; the church came afterwards, and it was made by readers. Reading societies formed in Manchester and London in the 1780s, a separate New Church congregation organized in London in 1787, the first General Conference met in 1789, and the books crossed the Atlantic, where societies formed in Baltimore and Philadelphia in the 1790s.

## quietist-controversy-molinos-guyon

Quietist Controversy (Molinos, Guyon)

In 1675 a Spanish priest living in Rome, Miguel de Molinos, published The Spiritual Guide, teaching a prayer of total interior passivity: stop striving, stop even asking, and let God act. It was hugely popular until it was not; Molinos was arrested in 1685 and sixty-eight propositions drawn from his work were condemned in 1687. The quarrel then moved to France, around Jeanne Guyon, a widow whose Short and Easy Method of Prayer taught something similar, and who found a defender in Fénelon, archbishop of Cambrai, and an implacable opponent in Bossuet, bishop of Meaux. Guyon spent years in confinement, including in the Bastille; Fénelon's defense of her was condemned by Rome in 1699, and he read out his own submission from his own pulpit.

## camisard-prophetism

Camisard Prophetism

After Louis XIV outlawed Protestantism in 1685, the Protestants of the Cévennes were left with no pastors, no churches and no legal existence. And out of that vacuum came prophecy. From 1688 ordinary people, many of them children and young women, began falling into trance and speaking in what they took to be the voice of the Spirit. The first widely reported was a shepherd girl, Isabeau Vincent, who preached in her sleep. In 1702 the movement turned to armed revolt. Bands of peasants known as Camisards fought royal troops through the mountains for two years, led by men like Jean Cavalier, a baker's apprentice of twenty, with prophets traveling alongside the fighters and directing them. The rising was broken by 1704 and the last resistance ended by 1710; some of the prophets escaped to London, where they caused a fresh sensation as the 'French Prophets'.

## the-baptists

The Baptists

In 1609 an exiled English congregation in Amsterdam decided that baptism belonged to believers who could profess faith for themselves. Its pastor, John Smyth, baptized himself and then the rest. That was the start of the English Baptists. Two streams grew up. General Baptists held that Christ died for all. Particular Baptists held, with Calvin, that God had chosen who would be saved. Both formed gathered churches: bodies of ordinary people who made a covenant together and chose their own ministers. They disciplined their own members and wrote it all down in minute books, week after week. They were persecuted hard under the Restoration. John Bunyan spent twelve years in Bedford jail for preaching without a license and wrote Grace Abounding there. They became one of the strongest voices in English for freedom of conscience. In America they grew fast late in the eighteenth century. The awakening may have fed them many converts, though the numbers are not known. The movement turned outward in 1792. That year a shoemaker named William Carey persuaded a small group of ministers to found a mission society. Then he sailed for India himself.

## colonial-latin-american-devotional-catholicism

Colonial Latin American Devotional Catholicism

Once the first missionary generation had passed, Catholicism in Spanish America settled into being the ordinary religion of whole societies, and its real institutions were local: the cofradías, lay confraternities that maintained an altar or an image, buried their members and paid for their funerals, organized by trade, by parish and often by race — with indigenous confraternities and Black brotherhoods running their own devotional life. Its saints were homegrown: Rose of Lima, canonized in 1671 as the first saint of the Americas, and Martín de Porres, the mixed-race Dominican lay brother of the same city. Its convents produced writers of the first rank, among them Sor Juana Inés de la Cruz in Mexico, who defended a woman's right to study, and Úrsula de Jesús in Lima, an enslaved woman later freed, whose spiritual diary survives in her own dictated words. Above all it produced Our Lady of Guadalupe, whose shrine at Tepeyac became the devotional center of Mexico and eventually a symbol of Mexican identity itself.

Portuguese America had its own brotherhoods. In Bahia a sermon of 1633 is headed as preached to the brotherhood of the Blacks of a sugar mill, on the feast of St John the Evangelist. It is by the Jesuit António Vieira, in his Maria Rosa Mystica of 1686. In the same book he tells the enslaved workers of a sugar mill that no labor in the world is more like the Cross and Passion of Christ than theirs (a rendering). In 1711 André João Antonil wrote of the mills. He says some masters keep enslaved people for a long time in the cane fields or the mill without baptism. He tells masters not to object when the enslaved choose their own kings. He tells them to let the enslaved keep their morning feasts of Our Lady of the Rosary, of St Benedict and of the patron of the mill chapel. In 1741 Benedict XIV wrote to the bishops of Brazil, Paraguay and the Río de la Plata. His bull Immensa pastorum principis condemned those who enslaved or sold Indians, even baptized ones.

Saint-Domingue, the French colony that became Haiti, is seen here through one long witness. It is the Description of Moreau de Saint-Méry, printed in Philadelphia in 1797. It shows the colony as it stood on 18 October 1789. Moreau was at Le Cap in 1783, and in 1788 the Navy minister gave him a drafting task for the colonies. He writes from the colonists' side, so the picture of the Black people in it is his, not theirs.

He says the parishes of the North first had Capuchins as pastors. In 1704 the Capuchins could not supply enough men and gave up the mission. The Jesuits took it over and kept it until their order was expelled from the colony, at the end of 1763. Secular priests held it until 1768, when the Capuchins came back. In the West, the Dominicans served the parishes.

Moreau judges the Africans of the colony harshly. He says they could not sort religious ideas in their minds, so that all their belief lay in outward signs. In church, he says, they mumbled prayers they knew badly, or slept. Yet he reports that new arrivals from Africa pressed to be baptized. Black people born in the colony claimed to stand far above them because of their baptism, and the newcomers were taunted as "horses". Adults were baptized on two Saturdays of the year, often with no preparation. He says they held a godparent in more respect than their own father and mother.

At Le Cap, after the parish Mass on Sundays, Black worshippers met in the church. They prayed and sang hymns, led by some old men and women. A low Mass followed, which Moreau calls la messe des nègres, the Mass of the Black people. He credits the Jesuit Pierre Boutin, who reached Le Cap in 1705, with teaching them each evening, with the baptism of adults at Easter and Pentecost, and with that Mass.

Guatemala gave this world a brotherhood that ran a hospital. It was called the Bethlemites. Pedro de Betancur founded it and took the name Pedro de San José.

Two books tell his story, and both are hagiography, written to honor him. One is a Life printed at Rome in 1683, by Francisco Antonio de Montalvo. The other is the order's own history of 1723, by Joseph García de la Concepción, a Discalced Franciscan who wrote it for the order.

Montalvo says Pedro was born on Tenerife. He sailed for the Indies in 1650 and reached Guatemala City in 1651. He died in April 1667.

Montalvo writes of the Hospital of Our Lady of Bethlehem. He also writes of a Bethlemite Company that Pedro was to found. It was to shelter the sick, the poor and the abandoned. Members of the Third Order of St Francis, he says, moved over to the new house of hospitality.

Montalvo's Life is full of wonders. In one, he says, the Virgin let Pedro know how welcome his service was, by a favor no less rare than miraculous. Of one such wonder Montalvo says he offers it only as human report, because the Sacred Congregation of Rites is the body that weighs miracles.

García says the brothers sent their constitutions to Brother Antonio de la Cruz at Madrid. He was to go to Rome and seek apostolic confirmation. Antonio went to the Roman Curia and won papal confirmation of the constitutions. García prints the bull of Clement X for it.

García also prints a bull of Innocent XI. It closes with the date 26 March 1687, in the eleventh year of that pope's reign. A later memorial recalls the pope's grace in raising the Company to a regular community under the Rule of St Augustine.

## orthodoxy-under-the-ottomans-1650-1821

Orthodoxy under the Ottomans — the Second Act

For a century and a half after 1650 the Orthodox church of the Ottoman Empire was both a church and an arm of the state: the Ecumenical Patriarch in Constantinople answered to the Sultan for the obedience of all Orthodox subjects, collected their taxes and judged their marriages and inheritances. It was a position of real power and total insecurity — patriarchs were deposed and restored constantly. And it came increasingly to be held under the influence of the Phanariots, the wealthy Greek families of Constantinople's Phanar quarter who supplied the empire's interpreters and the princes of its Danubian provinces. In 1672 the Synod of Jerusalem, under Patriarch Dositheus, issued a confession answering the Calvinist ideas that had been circulating under an Orthodox name — the East's own equivalent of the West's confessional settlements. That power also worked against its neighbors: the Serbian patriarchate of Peć was abolished in 1766 and the archbishopric of Ohrid in 1767, both absorbed into Constantinople. It ended violently — when the Greek revolt broke out in 1821, Patriarch Gregory V was hanged in his vestments from the gate of his own patriarchate.

Not every Orthodox church of these years stood under Constantinople. Fortescue, a Catholic writer, lists two that stood apart. One is Karlovci, made of Orthodox Serbs in Hungary. He dates it to 1765. The other is Montenegro, with one independent diocese on the Black Mountain. He also dates it to 1765. His dates mark independence as he counts it.

A Bulgarian revival began in these years. In 1762 Paisii, a monk of Hilendar, wrote a history of the Bulgarians. Yordan Ivanov printed it in 1914. He says Paisii called to his readers: «Българино! знай своя родъ и езикъ» ("Bulgarian! know your own people and language", a rendering). Ivanov adds that Paisii named three causes of his people's spiritual ruin. They were no freedom, no national hierarchy and no national schools. Ivanov writes in the Bulgarian national tradition. He blames the Greek clergy. No Greek account of this dispute was read.

## catholic-church-ancien-regime

The Catholic Church of the Ancien Régime

This was the Catholic Church at the height of its establishment and on the edge of its worst crisis: enormously wealthy, woven into the monarchies of Europe, and increasingly unable to control them. Rome's condemnation of Jansenism in the bull Unigenitus (1713) turned into a fifty-year political crisis in France, with parlements and bishops fighting over it. Then the Catholic monarchies turned on the church's most effective order, expelling the Jesuits from Portugal, France, Spain and Naples and forcing the pope to suppress the Society altogether in 1773. Then came the Revolution: church property nationalized, clergy required to swear to a Civil Constitution that split them into jurors and refractories, thousands killed or driven abroad, and Pope Pius VI carried off to die a French prisoner in 1799. His successor signed a concordat with Napoleon in 1801, was imprisoned by him in turn, outlived him, and restored the Jesuits in 1814.

Rome ran its missions through Propaganda, the congregation for spreading the faith. Its collected decrees show three acts in these years.

In 1656 the Holy Office ruled on a set of questions. Missionaries in China had sent them to Propaganda in 1645. Chinese Christians could keep certain rites, it said. The cult, it said, seemed merely civil and political.

In 1659 Propaganda sent orders to its vicars apostolic. They were not to urge any people to change its rites, customs and ways. What is more absurd, it asked, than to carry France, Spain or Italy into China (in a plain English rendering)?

In 1726 Propaganda ruled that native priests must swear an oath too. This covered China, Tonkin and Cochinchina. The oath was the one set by the constitution Ex illa die.

## restoration-georgian-church-of-england

The Restoration and Georgian Church of England

When the monarchy came back in 1660 so did the bishops and the Prayer Book, and the Act of Uniformity of 1662 required every minister to accept both. About two thousand refused and were ejected from their livings on a single day, creating English Dissent as a permanent fact. The church that remained was the established church of the long eighteenth century, and it has had a poor press — pluralism, absentee clergy, comfortable sermons. But it was also the church of the Prayer Book as ordinary people actually knew it, of parish registers and charity schools, and of the first Anglican missionary societies, the SPCK in 1698 and the SPG in 1701. It had its own principled casualties. The nonjurors, several hundred clergy including the Archbishop of Canterbury, who would not swear allegiance to William and Mary in 1689 while James II lived, and walked out rather than break an oath. And it was the church John and Charles Wesley were born in, ordained in, and never meant to leave.

## russian-church-nikon-to-holy-synod

The Russian Church — Nikon to the Holy Synod

Patriarch Nikon, elected in 1652, set out to correct Russian service books against Greek models and to assert the priesthood's authority over the tsar's. And lost both fights: the corrections split the church permanently, and Nikon himself was deposed by the council of 1666-67 that condemned his opponents. Half a century later Peter the Great settled the second argument the other way. When the patriarch died in 1700 Peter simply left the office empty, and in 1721 he abolished it, replacing it with a Holy Synod of clergy, watched over by a lay official the tsar appointed. The church became, in effect, a department of the imperial government and stayed one for two hundred years. Catherine II completed the process in 1764 by confiscating the monastic estates. Yet the same church produced Tikhon of Zadonsk's gentle devotional writing, a serious seminary system, and in 1794 sent monks from Valaam across the Pacific to Alaska.

The church followed the Russian frontier east. The council of 1666 and 1667 raised Tobolsk, in Siberia, to a metropolitan see. It also proposed new bishoprics at Tomsk and on the Lena. They lay far from Tobolsk. In Peter the Great's reign, Mouravieff says, Theodore of Tobolsk converted 10,000 Ostiaks. The numbers are Mouravieff's, in an English version of 1842.

The church also reached Peking, by way of a lost fort. In 1685 Chinese troops took the Russian stockade of Albazin, on the Amur. The Cossacks taken there were invited to Peking to serve the emperor. Adoratskii says the captives took their priest, Maksim Leontiev, along by force. Father Maksim turned a Buddhist temple the emperor gave them into a chapel of St Nicholas.

In 1695, Adoratskii says, the metropolitan of Tobolsk wrote to him. In English, the letter says: "though you are in captivity, yet you yourself, with God's help, take captive unknowing men into the knowledge of the gospel's truth."

The first permanent Russian mission set out with the emperor's leave in 1715. It reached Peking at the end of that year or early in 1716. Peter the Great had told the metropolitan of Tobolsk to choose its members. A treaty of 1727 named one priest in the Russian house and three more to come. It also said Russians would not be forbidden to pray and to honor their God in their own faith. In 1732 the Albazinians' church was consecrated as the Dormition church, though it was still called St Nicholas's.

Adoratskii says that from 1745 to 1808 the mission was neglected and in a sorry state. He gives three causes: hard dealings with China, the weakness of some members, and too little money. Yet even then, he adds, it held to the Orthodox faith and to its tasks.

The details are his, from the Synod's archives, in a Russian history of 1887. He was an Orthodox churchman.

## church-of-the-desert-huguenots

The Church of the Desert

When Louis XIV revoked the Edict of Nantes in 1685, French Protestantism was simply abolished. Its churches were pulled down, its pastors expelled, its people forbidden to emigrate and required to attend Mass. Those who stayed called what they did next 'the Desert', after Israel's wilderness — they met at night in ravines, quarries and woods, several hundred at a time, with lookouts posted. In 1715 a twenty-year-old lay preacher named Antoine Court gathered a handful of others and set about rebuilding an actual church. He held a synod, restored ordained ministry and discipline, discouraged the prophesying that had led to the Camisard rising, and founded a seminary at Lausanne to train pastors who went back over the border knowing they could hang for it. The penalties were exactly that — pastors executed, men sent to the galleys for life, women shut up in towers, Marie Durand among them for thirty-eight years. Until the Edict of Versailles in 1787 finally gave Protestants a civil existence again.

## melkite-greek-catholic-church

The Melkite Greek Catholic Split

When the Patriarch of Antioch died in 1724, the Christians of Damascus elected Cyril VI Tanas, who favored union with Rome. Constantinople refused to accept him and consecrated a rival, Sylvester, a Greek monk from Cyprus. The Antiochian patriarchate never reunited. What followed was the permanent division of one Arabic-speaking, Byzantine-rite community into two churches — one in communion with Rome, recognized by the pope in 1729, and one with Constantinople — sharing the same liturgy, the same language and frequently the same families. The Catholic side, based at Damascus, Aleppo and the monasteries of Mount Lebanon, was pressed hard by Ottoman authorities acting on Orthodox complaints, and was not granted a recognized civil standing of its own until well into the nineteenth century.

The split had a long run-up. In 1654 Patriarch Macarius of Antioch was in the Cossack lands. His journey took him to Kyiv and on to Moscow. His son Paul, an archdeacon of Aleppo, wrote the journey down.

Before 1724 some Orthodox bishops of Syria had tried to reach reunion with Rome. Fortescue, a Catholic writer, tells it. Euthymius, bishop of Tyre and Sidon, approached the Holy See. So did the patriarchs Athanasius IV and Cyril of Berrhoea. They hoped to receive the pallium. Rome found their professions of faith too weak. In 1722 a synod at Constantinople sent the Antiochene bishops a warning letter. It listed Latin heresies. No Orthodox account of these approaches to Rome was read.

## armenian-apostolic-church-era-8

Armenian Apostolic Church — Etchmiadzin and Constantinople

Two seats led the Armenian Apostolic Church in these years. The catholicos lived at Etchmiadzin, in lands ruled from Persia. The Persian court had to approve his election. A patriarch led the Armenians of Constantinople, and that city became the center of the nation. Catholic missionaries worked hard to win Armenians to Rome. One of their party once seized the patriarch's seat, but the people drove him out. At the same time a rival bishop had himself proclaimed supreme patriarch of the sultan's lands, against Etchmiadzin. In 1664 the catholicos Hacob IV went to Constantinople to restore order. He died there on a second journey in 1680. The quarrel grew bitter. The patriarch's side had the Ottoman government act against Armenians who had become Catholics. The Catholic side, for its part, used the French ambassadors against the patriarch. In 1703 the patriarch Avedik was put in prison when the French ambassador stepped in. Later he was carried off to France, where he died. Catholic writers record that in 1707 the patriarch John handed the priest Gomidas to the Turks, accusing him of following the Franks. "I am a Christian and a follower of St. Gregory the Illuminator, Armenian by birth," Gomidas told them. He was beheaded. In 1742 the pope approved a separate Armenian Catholic patriarchate. Even so, a Catholic Armenian wrote in 1783 that his people still had to obey the patriarch. They still went to his churches to baptize, marry and bury. In 1722 Afghan armies reached Isfahan, and the Armenians of New Julfa had to ransom their town more than once. In 1736 the catholicos Abraham of Crete went to Nadir's camp on the Mughan plain, where an assembly asked Nadir to rule Persia. Abraham was one of three men chosen to speak for the Armenians, and he came home with seven written orders he had asked for. From 1763 to 1780 the catholicos Simeon of Erevan founded a college, brought in printing and built a paper mill. He also opened the church's first ties with the Russian empire. As Persian rule grew harsher, Armenians began to look to the czar. In 1810 a new catholicos, Ephraim, came to Etchmiadzin under a guard of Russian soldiers.

## armenian-christianity-era-8

Armenian Christianity — the Mekhitarists and the Catholic Patriarchate

Mekhitar of Sebaste founded a religious congregation in 1701 with a purpose as much scholarly as devotional. To gather, edit and print the Armenian inheritance for a people scattered across empires. Driven out of Ottoman territory, he and his monks were given the small island of San Lazzaro in the Venetian lagoon in 1717, and turned it into one of the great centers of Armenian learning — a press, a library of thousands of manuscripts, and the dictionaries and translations that effectively created modern Armenian lexicography. Meanwhile, inside the Ottoman capital, the growing number of Armenians in communion with Rome created a problem the millet system had no room for. In 1742 Benedict XIV confirmed Abraham Petros I as Catholic patriarch of Cilicia, with a seat established at Bzommar in Mount Lebanon in 1750. The Armenian Apostolic Church remained overwhelmingly the larger body, but from that point there were two Armenian churches, and the argument between them was fought out in the politics of Constantinople.

## church-of-utrecht

The Church of Utrecht

The Catholic Church in the Dutch Republic was a church without legal standing, tolerated in hidden chapels, and its clergy had absorbed a good deal of the Jansenist temper of the age. When Rome refused for years to confirm any archbishop for Utrecht, the cathedral chapter fell back on what it held to be its own ancient right and, in 1723, elected Cornelius Steenoven itself. A French missionary bishop, Dominique Marie Varlet — himself already in trouble with Rome — consecrated him in 1724, which gave the small church a valid succession and a permanent breach with the papacy in the same act. It stayed Catholic in everything else, went on electing its own bishops, and survived on a few thousand members for a century and a half.

## english-old-dissent

English Old Dissent — Presbyterians and Congregationalists

On St Bartholomew's Day 1662, about two thousand ministers were put out of the Church of England. They could not accept the restored Prayer Book, or the rule that only bishops could ordain. From then on, Presbyterians and Congregationalists were churches of their own, not parties inside one church. Barred from the universities, they built their own schools. These dissenting academies taught math, science and modern languages when Oxford and Cambridge mostly did not. They trained a striking share of the nation's scientists, factory owners and reformers. Their other great gift was sung. Isaac Watts, a Congregational minister, broke the rule that only the Psalms could be sung in worship, and wrote hymns instead. That is why English-speaking Christians sing 'When I Survey the Wondrous Cross' and 'Joy to the World'. The tradition also had a fault line. At Salters' Hall in London in 1719, most of the ministers would not make others sign a statement on the Trinity. Over the next century many Presbyterian churches moved away from the Trinity. They went first toward Arian and then toward Unitarian views. The Congregationalists kept the old faith. Some of their leaders, like Watts and Doddridge, took up the revival of the 1730s and 1740s.
