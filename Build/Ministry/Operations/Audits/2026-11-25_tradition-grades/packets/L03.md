# Packet L03

## merovingian-gallic-christianity

Merovingian Gallic Christianity

When the Frankish king Clovis accepted baptism as a Nicene Christian around the turn of the sixth century, he brought his people into the same church as the Gallo-Roman bishops rather than into the Homoian one most other barbarian kings held. And the Gaul that resulted is one of the few places in this era where ordinary parish religion can actually be overheard. Caesarius, bishop of Arles, preached short, blunt, repeatable sermons at people who came to church and also consulted soothsayers, kept the old feast days, and slipped out before the sermon. Gregory of Tours, writing two generations later, filled book after book with the shrines, relics and cures that structured everyday devotion, alongside a famously unsparing narrative of Frankish kings murdering one another. Queens became founders — Radegund walked out of a royal marriage to establish a convent at Poitiers — and poets wrote hymns for their relics. It is a Christianity that is fully established and only half converted at the same time, and it says both things about itself out loud.

## insular-irish-monastic-christianity

Insular / Irish Monastic Christianity

Ireland was never Roman, and the church that took root there in the fifth century was built to match. It grew up around monasteries and their abbots, not city bishops, and monastic families and founder-saints were the anchors of whole regions. Two short letters by Patrick survive: one a defense of his own life, the other a furious protest because his converts had been taken as slaves. They are the only voices from the beginning that we can be sure are genuine, and they are startlingly personal. Two things this church made were taken up by the wider church. One was peregrinatio, leaving home for good for God's sake. It sent Columba to Iona in 563, and Columbanus into Gaul and Italy, where he founded Luxeuil and Bobbio and argued with Frankish kings. The other was the penitentials, handbooks that set out the penance for each kind of sin. Its own way of reckoning Easter, and its own style of tonsure, in time clashed with Roman practice. At Whitby in 664 the Northumbrian king ruled against the Irish reckoning.

## aksumite-christianity

Aksumite Christianity

Aksum had been Christian since the fourth century. After Chalcedon it was a serious Red Sea power, with its own coinage and its own script, and its Bible was being translated into Ge'ez. Tradition credits the spread of monasticism and much of that translation work to the Nine Saints. They are said to have been monks from the Roman east, arriving around the turn of the sixth century. In 523 the Jewish king of Himyar, across the water in Yemen, massacred the Christians of Najran. The Aksumite king Kaleb crossed the Red Sea in about 525, killed him, and set up a Christian king who paid Aksum tribute. Not long after, the Aksumite soldiers who had stayed behind set up another king, Abramus, and Aksum could not remove him. A Miaphysite historian of the time counted the Aksumite king as a fellow believer. No text from inside these years shows where its church stood on Chalcedon. Evidence from these years is thin: inscriptions, a few very early gospel books, archaeology. Muslim tradition remembers that around 615 Muhammad sent his first followers to its king for protection.

## georgian-christianity

Georgian Christianity

The kingdom of Kartli, in what is now eastern Georgia, became Christian in the fourth century. It came through the preaching of a woman the tradition calls Nino. Kartli then held on to that faith for three centuries, on a frontier where Persia had every reason to prise it loose. Its oldest surviving book, written in the 470s or 480s, tells the story of Shushanik. She was an Armenian princess married to the local ruler. He left Christianity for Zoroastrianism, and when she would not follow him, he locked her up and beat her until she died. Georgia built its own alphabet, its own monasteries, and an unusually rich life of worship. Georgian manuscripts keep the worship of fifth- to eighth-century Jerusalem more fully than any Greek source does. Around 600, at the third council of Dvin, its church broke with Armenia and took the Chalcedonian side. That is why the two neighboring churches of the Caucasus have belonged to different communions ever since.

## nubian-christianity

Nubian Christianity

Christianity reached the Nubian kingdoms south of Egypt in the 540s, through two competing missions. A Miaphysite priest named Julian went with the empress Theodora's backing, and a Chalcedonian effort went with Justinian's. The story of that race survives only because a Syrian bishop, John of Ephesus, wrote it down from the winning side. What Nubia itself left is not stories but walls. Built a few generations later, the cathedral at Faras lay buried in sand until it was rescued before the Aswan dam flooded the region. It gave up a list of its bishops and more than a hundred painted figures: Nubian bishops, kings and queens, and saints. Some have dark faces and some pale, in a style nobody had seen before. When Arab armies pushed south after taking Egypt, Nubian archers held them at Dongola. In 652 the two sides signed the Baqt, a treaty of exchange rather than of conquest, and it left Christian Nubia standing on its own terms.

## gothic-and-vandal-homoian-christianity

Gothic, Vandal & Lombard Homoian Christianity

Most of the peoples who took over the western Roman provinces — Goths, Vandals, Burgundians, and later the Lombards — were Christians before they arrived, but of a kind the Nicene churches did not recognize. Homoian, holding that the Son is like the Father rather than of one being with him. Their tradition went back to Ulfila, the fourth-century missionary bishop who devised an alphabet for Gothic and translated most of the Bible into it, and it worked as a real church, with clergy, liturgy, scripture in the vernacular, and arguments of its own. In Vandal Africa it was the persecuting party, exiling and maiming Nicene bishops in a campaign its victims documented in detail. In Ostrogothic Italy it lived alongside the Catholic church in separate buildings, some of which still stand in Ravenna. Where it ended, it ended two ways. In Africa and Ostrogothic Italy it was destroyed by Byzantine reconquest. In Spain and among the Lombards it ended by conversion. The Visigothic king Reccared brought his kingdom over at the third council of Toledo in 589, and the last Arian Lombard kings gave way in the seventh century.

## byzantine-imperial-church-justinianic

The Byzantine Imperial Church (Justinianic)

For nearly two centuries after Chalcedon, the church of the eastern Roman Empire was inseparable from the empire itself. Emperors summoned its councils, legislated for its clergy and monasteries, appointed and deposed its patriarchs, and treated doctrinal unity as a condition of political order. Justinian pushed this furthest — rebuilding Hagia Sophia in under six years, codifying Roman law with the church written into it, and in 553 calling a council to condemn the writings of three long-dead theologians, two of whom Chalcedon itself had cleared, in the hope of winning back the Miaphysites. It did not work: the condemnation alienated much of the Latin West without reconciling Egypt or Syria, and each attempted formula produced a fresh schism instead. The same church, in the same years, produced Romanos the Melodist, whose sung sermons in verse are among the finest poetry in Greek, and sent missionaries as far as the Nile kingdoms.

## church-of-caucasian-albania

The Church of Caucasian Albania

Beside Armenia and Georgia there was a third Christian kingdom in the Caucasus. This was Albania, in what is now Azerbaijan and the south of Dagestan. It had its own royal line, its own bishops and its own alphabet. In the late fifth century it held a council at Aghuen. The council made rules for the clergy and for marriage, and set out what the nobles owed the church. Albania also produced a history, put together from older material by a writer known as Movses Daskhurantsi. That is very nearly all the story it left. It was pressed between Persian, Byzantine and then Arab power, and between the rival loyalties of its Armenian and Georgian neighbors. In the early 700s it lost its independent standing, when its catholicos was placed under the Armenian church. Its written language was all but lost for more than a thousand years. Then, in 1996, palimpsests at Saint Catherine's Monastery on Sinai were found to hold Caucasian Albanian scripture. The pages had been scraped and written over with later Georgian text.

## latin-africa-under-byzantium

Latin African Christianity under Byzantium

Belisarius destroyed the Vandal kingdom in 533. The Nicene church of Roman Africa came out of nearly a hundred years of Vandal rule and waves of persecution. It got back its buildings and its bishops' sees. Within twenty years it was among the most stubborn foes Justinian faced anywhere in his own empire. When he condemned the Three Chapters to appease the Miaphysites, the bishops of Africa said no. Facundus of Hermiane wrote twelve books against his decree. Reparatus of Carthage lost his see and was sent into exile. The chronicler Victor of Tunnuna spent years in prison and exile for the same stand. Africa stayed a place where theology got argued at full volume. Maximus the Confessor lived here for some years, and in 645 he debated the ousted patriarch Pyrrhus in Africa. In 646 the councils of Africa threw out the empire's next bid for a compromise on the wills of Christ. Then Arab armies reached Carthage in the 690s. The old African church, which had given the Latin church Tertullian, Cyprian and Augustine, was gone within a few generations.

## nicene-visigothic-iberia

Nicene Visigothic Iberia

For over a century the Visigoths ruled Spain as Homoian Christians, and lived uneasily beside the Nicene people under their rule. Then King Reccared announced his own conversion. At the third council of Toledo in 589, he brought the Gothic bishops with him. Nothing quite like it took place anywhere else: a kingdom's own church council formally dropped its creed and took up the creed of its subjects. What came next was one of the more striking feats of learning of the early Middle Ages. Isidore of Seville set out to gather what was left of classical learning into a single encyclopedia. A circle grew up around him. His brother Leander, Braulio of Zaragoza and Ildefonsus of Toledo wrote letters, liturgy, histories and rules. The councils of Toledo worked as a cross between a synod and a parliament, and made laws for church and kingdom at once. Their record also holds a harsh body of law against Spain's Jews, which grew worse with time. The Gothic kingdom fell at a stroke to the Muslim conquest of 711, but its church lived on under the new rulers.

## british-welsh-christianity

British and Welsh Christianity

Britain had been Christian under Roman rule, and when the province fell away and Anglo-Saxon settlers took the east, that church did not vanish — it survived in the west: in Wales, Cornwall, the north-west, and across the sea in Brittany, which British emigrants gave its name. Its own record is thin and largely furious. Gildas, writing around the 540s, delivered a book-length denunciation of British kings and clergy for the sins he believed had brought the Saxon catastrophe on them. Its life ran through monastic schools and founder-saints whose names still sit on Welsh parishes — David, Illtud, Teilo, Cadoc — and through inscribed memorial stones that are among the few hard traces of the period. When Augustine of Canterbury arrived from Rome and met British bishops around 603, they declined to accept his authority or to join his mission to the Saxons who had displaced them; they were not at the synod of Whitby in 664 either. The Welsh church came round to the Roman dating of Easter on its own, around 768.

## the-church-of-the-east-on-the-silk-road

The Church of the East on the Silk Road

A Syriac-speaking church whose bishops sat in Persia had, by the seventh century, sent monks the whole width of Asia: in 635 a missionary the Chinese records call Alopen reached the Tang capital of Chang'an and was received at the imperial court, and toleration for his religion was proclaimed three years later. What that mission looked like is known mostly through a handful of extraordinary survivals — above all a stone stele erected in 781, inscribed in both Chinese and Syriac, narrating a century and a half of what it calls the Luminous Religion in China, and caches of Christian manuscripts recovered at Dunhuang and Turfan. Between those bright points lie long silences, and much of what is said about the stretches in between is reconstruction rather than record. The reach closes visibly in 845, when an imperial edict aimed at foreign religions ordered clergy out of their monasteries and the Chinese church stops appearing in the sources. Though the same communion carried on in Persia and Central Asia.

## melkite-arabic-christianity

Melkite Arabic Christianity

When the Arab conquests cut Palestine and Syria off from Constantinople, the Chalcedonian Christians of those provinces — Melkites, 'the emperor's people', as their neighbors labeled them — found themselves a minority under a new government and, within a few generations, doing something no church had done before: praying, arguing and writing theology in Arabic. Their intellectual heart was the monastic world of the Judean desert, above all the monastery of Mar Saba, where John of Damascus, from a family that had served in the Muslim administration, wrote the great defense of religious images that Byzantine emperors could not silence because he lived beyond their reach. A generation later Theodore Abu Qurrah was writing theology in Arabic for a mixed audience of Christians and Muslims, and anonymous works such as the treatise now called On the Triune Nature of God were explaining the Trinity in the vocabulary of the world around them. The window closes hard in 1009, when the caliph al-Hakim ordered the Church of the Holy Sepulchre razed.

## coptic-christianity-under-early-islam

Coptic Christianity under Early Islam

Egypt's miaphysite Christians had spent the century before the Arab conquest under Byzantine pressure for refusing the Council of Chalcedon. When Arab armies took Alexandria in 641-42 their patriarch Benjamin came out of hiding and was restored to his see. The church's own History has him come out on the conqueror 'Amr's promise of safety. Michael the Syrian, a Syriac patriarch of the twelfth century, found it told that Benjamin had promised the Arabs Alexandria if they would drive out Cyrus, the Chalcedonian patriarch, and give him back the churches. Two European scholars, Hiob Ludolf in 1691 and Eusèbe Renaudot in 1713, read the Copts as having helped the conquerors. What followed was the long transition that made the Coptic church what it still is. No longer persecuted for its confession but taxed and governed as a protected minority, shifting gradually from Coptic into Arabic, and beginning the long, centuries-long drift toward minority status that conversions would eventually bring. Its own record of those years, the History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria, follows the church bishop by bishop with unusual care, though it says far more about patriarchs and taxes than about ordinary Egyptian Christians. The window ends with the persecution under the caliph al-Hakim in the early eleventh century, the harshest single episode the community had yet faced.

## anglo-saxon-christianity

Anglo-Saxon Christianity

Christianity reached the English twice over and from two directions — from Rome in 597, when Augustine landed in Kent, and from Ireland by way of Iona, whose monks founded Lindisfarne and evangelized Northumbria. And at the Synod of Whitby in 664 the two traditions had to settle publicly which calendar and which customs the English church would keep. What grew out of that settlement was a church with an extraordinary appetite for learning. Bede, writing at Jarrow, finished in 731 the history that is still the main source for the whole period, and monasteries like Whitby, under the abbess Hild, trained bishops. Within a century of being a mission field, England was sending missionaries of its own back across the Channel — Willibrord to Frisia, Boniface into Germany, and with them women such as the abbess Leoba, whose one surviving letter comes down under her own name. The window closes on 8 June 793, when Viking raiders sacked Lindisfarne and a different age began.

## carolingian-reform-christianity

Carolingian Reform Christianity

When Pippin was anointed king in 751 and his son Charlemagne built an empire across western Europe, they treated the condition of the church as a matter of government: schools attached to cathedrals and monasteries, corrected copies of Scripture and the liturgy, one monastic rule for the whole realm, and decrees ordering that every priest be able to explain the Creed and the Lord's Prayer to his people in words they could understand. Scholars were imported to run it, Alcuin of York above all, and the clear round script their scriptoria developed — Carolingian minuscule — is the ancestor of the letters you are reading now. Almost everything that survives, though, is what people were told rather than what they thought. The reform is documented from the top down, and its ordinary interior life has to be inferred from its own instructions. One striking exception is a lay voice, Dhuoda, a noblewoman writing a handbook of faith and conduct for her absent son in the 840s. The imperial framework that carried the reform broke apart after 888.

## iconophile-byzantine-monasticism

Iconophile Byzantine Monasticism

Twice over a century and more, the Byzantine court tried to purge Christian worship of images. Both times the strongest organized resistance came from the monasteries. The monks argued that God had truly become a human being who could be seen, so he could be painted. To refuse the image was, in the end, to refuse the Incarnation. And they paid for the argument. Stephen the Younger, his Life says, was killed in 765. Theodore, abbot of the great Stoudios monastery in Constantinople, tells in his own letters how he was beaten, imprisoned and exiled more than once. Through it all he kept writing letters, talks to his monks, and treatises on the holy icons. Images were restored once at the Council of Nicaea in 787, and for good in 843. The Orthodox churches still keep that day as the Triumph of Orthodoxy. One thing is worth knowing about the story as we have it. The winning side wrote the record. The iconoclasts' own case survives almost entirely in quotations made by the people refuting it.

## cyrillo-methodian-slavic-christianity

Cyrillo-Methodian Slavic Christianity

In 863 two brothers from Thessaloniki — Constantine, better known by his monastic name Cyril, and Methodius — were sent from Constantinople to Moravia at its ruler's request, and did something unusual for their day: rather than teach in Greek or Latin, they devised an alphabet for the Slavic language and translated the Scriptures and the liturgy into it. The idea was fought hard, by clergy who held that God could properly be worshipped only in Hebrew, Greek and Latin, and the brothers traveled to Rome to argue the case, where the pope approved the Slavonic books. After Methodius died in 885 their disciples were driven out of Moravia and carried the work south, where Clement and Naum built schools at Ohrid and Preslav that turned it into a permanent literature. And where the alphabet now called Cyrillic took the shape it still has. A monk writing under the name Hrabar defended the new letters in a short, sharp treatise: a rare case of a tradition arguing publicly, in its own words, that its language was fit for worship.

## kievan-rus-christianity

Kievan Rus' Christianity

In 988 Prince Vladimir of Kyiv was baptized and had his people baptized with him — an act of state that the Rus' chronicle later dressed in one of the great stories of medieval Christianity. Envoys sent out to sample the religions who reported that in Hagia Sophia at Constantinople they had not known whether they stood in heaven or on earth. Within two generations there was a Christian culture to match the decision. Vladimir's son Yaroslav built his own St Sophia in Kyiv and filled it with mosaics; a monastery in the caves above the Dnieper became the school of Rus' monasticism. And around 1049 a native churchman, Ilarion — whom Yaroslav would make the first native-born metropolitan two years later — preached a Sermon on Law and Grace that is the first substantial theology written by a Rus' author. The window is short, barely two generations, but almost the whole later history of Russian, Ukrainian and Belarusian Christianity runs back through it. Most of what tells the story was written down afterwards, by monks looking back.

## mozarabic-christianity

Mozarabic Christianity

After 711 most of Iberia's Christians lived under Muslim rule, kept the old Visigothic liturgy, and — as generations passed — increasingly spoke, wrote and thought in Arabic. In the 850s that quiet accommodation broke open in Córdoba, where some four dozen Christians were executed after publicly denouncing Islam, many of them going out of their way to provoke the sentence. The community then argued with itself about what had happened: the priest Eulogius wrote the martyrs up as heroes and was executed himself in 859. His friend Paulus Alvarus complained bitterly that young Christians were mastering Arabic poetry while their Latin decayed. And other Christians, including bishops assembled in council, judged that the martyrs had thrown their lives away. Very little of the community's ordinary life is recoverable, but that argument survives in the words of the people who were having it. The window closes with the collapse of the Caliphate of Córdoba in 1031.

## cluniac-reform-monasticism

Cluniac Reform Monasticism

In 909 or 910 — the foundation charter's own year is disputed — a duke of Aquitaine endowed a monastery at Cluny in Burgundy and did something unusual with the deed: he renounced all rights over it, for himself and his heirs, and placed it directly under the pope, so that no local lord or bishop could appoint its abbot or spend its money. Freed from that grip, and led by a run of unusually long-lived and capable abbots, Cluny grew into a network of hundreds of houses across Europe answering to a single abbot, with a life built around an elaborate round of prayer — the community's chief work was the liturgy, sung at a length no earlier Western monastery had attempted. Its abbots reached outward too: Odilo spread the commemoration of all the dead on 2 November and lent his weight to efforts to limit private warfare, and by the eleventh century Cluny's church was the largest in Christendom. Its records are institutional almost throughout — charters, customaries, letters of business — so its inner life is easier to describe from the outside than from within.

## st-thomas-christians-of-india

St. Thomas Christians of India

On the Malabar coast of India there was, long before any European arrived, a Christian community that traced itself to the apostle Thomas, worshipped in Syriac, and received its bishops from the patriarch of the Church of the East in Mesopotamia — an Indian church inside an Asian communion, on nobody's European line at all. What it left of itself in this period is mostly not writing but objects and documents drawn up by others: granite crosses carved with inscriptions in Pahlavi, the language of Persia, and above all a set of copper plates issued around 849 in which a local ruler grants land and trading privileges to a church at Kollam and names the Christians as a recognized community with standing among their neighbors. Almost nothing survives in which these Christians describe themselves in their own words before about 1500. Which makes them at once one of the oldest continuously existing Christian communities in the world and one of the hardest to hear.

## paulicians

Paulicians

The Paulicians appear in the record from the seventh to the ninth century, in Armenia and eastern Anatolia. Almost everything said about them was written by people trying to destroy them. Byzantine heresy-hunters say the movement rejected the Old Testament, the cross, the sacraments and the honor paid to Mary. They say it held that the world was split between a good power and an evil one. Whether they truly believed that, or their enemies needed them to, cannot now be settled. What is not in doubt is what was done to them. The empire persecuted them and condemned them to the flames. The survivors built a fortified state of their own at Tephrike on the frontier, and made common cause with the Arabs. By Byzantine accounts they raided the empire's lands until the fortress fell in 878. Later emperors forced whole Paulician groups to move to the Balkans.

## bogomils

Bogomils

The Bogomils take their name from a Bulgarian priest of the mid-tenth century. The earliest account of them is a long sermon written against them by another priest, Cosmas. It describes ascetics who rejected almost all that the visible church was made of: church buildings, the cross, icons, the sacraments, infant baptism and the clergy who gave them. Behind that refusal, the sources say, lay a belief that God did not make the material world or the human body. A fallen power made them, so the whole machinery of bodily religion was a mistake. The movement spread from Bulgaria through the Balkans and into Byzantium. Its story after 1054 continues as Balkan Bogomilism. As with the Paulicians, none of their own writing survives. All we know of them comes from the people who argued against them.
