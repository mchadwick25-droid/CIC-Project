## early-american-unitarians-universalists

Early American Unitarians & Universalists

Two separate movements, often confused with one another, converged in the new American republic. The Universalists came first in organized form. John Murray, an English preacher who had absorbed the doctrine that God will finally save everyone, arrived in America in 1770 and gathered a congregation at Gloucester, Massachusetts in 1779, and a general convention adopted the brief Winchester Profession in 1803. The Unitarian thread came from inside New England Congregationalism and from English precedent — Theophilus Lindsey opened the Essex Street Chapel in London in 1774, and in 1785 King's Chapel in Boston, an Anglican congregation left leaderless by the Revolution, revised its Book of Common Prayer to remove the Trinity. Neither had a denomination yet; those came in the 1820s, after this period closes.

## ruthenian-uniate-18th-century

The Ruthenian Uniate Church — Zamość to the Partitions

A century after the Union of Brest, the Greek Catholic church of the Ruthenian lands — today's Ukraine and Belarus — was at its numerical height, holding the loyalty of most of the peasantry of the eastern Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth. The Synod of Zamość in 1720 consolidated it and also latinized it, importing Roman devotional practices into a Byzantine rite and settling its identity as something distinctly its own rather than a temporary arrangement. Then the political ground vanished: the partitions of Poland between 1772 and 1795 handed most of the church's people to Catherine II's Russia, which regarded the union as an amputation to be reversed, and campaigns of forced reversion from 1794 stripped it of the great majority of its parishes within two years. What survived did so in Austrian Galicia, where the Habsburgs raised the Greek Catholic clergy's status and paid for their education. And where the church became, over the following century, the carrier of Ukrainian national identity.

The Ruthenian church of the Carpathian lands had a union with Rome of its own, apart from the union of Brest. Nilles's heading dates the union at Uzhhorod to 1646. It dates the solemn confirmation to 1652.

In a petition of 15 January 1652, Greek-rite priests tell the pope that they were won back to Rome in the Latin church of the Uzhhorod castle, on 23 April. Their printed text names the year as 1649. Nilles notes that some read it as 1646, because the bishop of Eger who received them died in 1647.

Their earlier bishop at Mukachevo, they say, had left the union.

They joined on three terms. They would keep the Greek rite. They would have a bishop of their own choosing, confirmed by Rome. They would keep their church privileges. In their Latin, as the scan prints it: ritus graecae ecclesiae nobis servare Iice«t.

They asked Rome to confirm Peter Parthenius, the bishop they had chosen.

## philippine-catholicism-era-8

Philippine Catholicism — Era-8 Segment

By the middle of the seventeenth century the Philippines were the one substantially Christian society in East or Southeast Asia, and its basic unit was the friar parish: a Spanish religious — Augustinian, Dominican, Franciscan, Recollect or Jesuit — often the only Spaniard in a town, holding both spiritual and civil authority over a population whose language he had learned and which he alone among the officials could preach in. Filipino Christianity took its own shape inside that arrangement, above all in the pasyon, a long verse narrative of Christ's passion in the vernacular, chanted by ordinary people through Holy Week in their own houses. The earliest surviving text dates from 1704, and the practice continues. The century's strains were institutional: a long fight over whether bishops could inspect the friars' parishes, the British occupation of Manila in 1762-64, the expulsion of the Jesuits in 1768, and above all the question of whether Filipino secular priests could hold parishes at all. Which became the great grievance of the century that followed.

## the-black-church-in-america

The Black Church in America

Before the Civil War most Black Christians in America worshipped under white supervision. And made something else of it anyway, in praise houses, brush arbors and night meetings where the preaching, the singing and the theology were their own. Independent congregations appear from the 1770s (Silver Bluff in South Carolina, First African Baptist in Savannah in 1788), and in 1816 Richard Allen, who had walked out of a Philadelphia Methodist church with the rest of its Black members after a trustee pulled Absalom Jones up off his knees in the segregated gallery, organized the African Methodist Episcopal Church — the first Black denomination of national reach; AME Zion followed in 1821. Because the law in much of the South forbade the enslaved to read, the tradition's theology comes down to us largely in what it sang and told: the spirituals, conversion accounts, and self-published narratives like Jarena Lee's. By 1906 it was a whole world of its own. Denominations, colleges, newspapers and a worship life that was about to reshape global Christianity from a mission on Azusa Street.

In 1874 a church in the United States made a bishop for Haiti, and its mission board asked that he be of the African race. The Spirit of Missions for December 1874, the journal of the Protestant Episcopal Church's mission board, prints a triennial report on the Haitian church. It is signed by J. Theodore Holly as Dean, at Port-au-Prince on 30 June 1874, but Holly's own part is a table of districts and his signature. The story around it is the board's.

The board also wanted him to be a Haitian citizen. The Dean of the Haitian Convocation came to make a covenant with the American church. By it, the Church in Haiti was a foreign church, still aided by the American one.

The Haitian church promised to keep the doctrine, worship and discipline of the American church. The House of Bishops chose Holly as the first bishop. He was consecrated in Grace Church, New York, on 8 November 1874.

## the-second-great-awakening-and-camp-meeting-worl

The Second Great Awakening & Camp-Meeting Tradition

At the turn of the nineteenth century, settlers on the Kentucky and Tennessee frontier began traveling for days by wagon to sacramental gatherings that ran for a week at a time — and something broke loose. At Cane Ridge in August 1801 the crowd was estimated at anywhere from ten to twenty thousand people, camped in the woods around a log meeting house, with several preachers going at once and people weeping, shouting and falling to the ground. Methodist circuit riders and Baptist farmer-preachers carried the pattern across the continent, and by the 1830s Charles Finney was writing up the revival as a method that could be worked deliberately rather than waited for. Whether or not it counts as a tradition of its own, it changed what an entire continent expected conversion to feel like.

## the-stone-campbell-restoration-movement

The Stone-Campbell Restoration Movement

On opposite sides of the Appalachians, two sets of Presbyterians came independently to the same conclusion. That the creeds and denominational names Christians used to define themselves were the very things keeping them apart. Barton W. Stone and his colleagues dissolved their own presbytery in 1804 with a document called the Last Will and Testament of the Springfield Presbytery. Five years later Thomas Campbell published the Declaration and Address, and with his son Alexander built a movement around the plea for no creed but the Bible and no name but Christian. In 1832 the two groups shook hands and merged. Debating was their native genre — Alexander Campbell argued in public for days at a stretch against skeptics, Presbyterians and a Roman Catholic bishop, and published the transcripts himself — and by 1906, when the federal religious census listed the Churches of Christ separately for the first time, a movement founded to end division had recorded a division of its own.

## the-holiness-movement

The Holiness Movement

Methodism had always taught that God could make a believer actually holy in this life, and in the 1830s a New York doctor's wife named Phoebe Palmer took that teaching, simplified it into a step anyone could take at once, and began hosting a Tuesday afternoon meeting in her parlor that ran for decades and drew bishops as well as neighbors. From that parlor the Holiness movement spread through camp meetings organized from 1867, through the Guide to Holiness and a flood of cheap devotional print, and across the Atlantic, where it took a quieter and more Anglican form at the Keswick Convention from 1875. Because sanctification had to be testified to out loud, the movement's native literature is ordinary people's accounts of their own experience rather than the writings of its leaders. Women were prominent throughout, and its single most remarkable voice belongs to Amanda Berry Smith, born enslaved, who preached in England, India and West Africa and wrote her own life.

## the-oxford-movement-and-anglo-catholic-revival

The Oxford Movement & Anglo-Catholic Revival

In July 1833 John Keble preached an Oxford sermon on national apostasy, protesting Parliament's reorganization of the Irish bishoprics, and a group of Oxford dons began issuing Tracts for the Times arguing that the Church of England was not a creature of the state but a branch of the ancient catholic church, with bishops in unbroken succession and a sacramental life to match. The argument split them: John Henry Newman's Tract 90 in 1841 tried to read the Thirty-Nine Articles in a catholic sense, and four years later he left for Rome, while Keble and Edward Pusey stayed and worked. The next generation carried it out of the university and into the parishes — vestments, incense, candles, weekly communion, and slum missions in the worst districts of London and the industrial cities. At the cost of ritual prosecutions in which a few priests went to prison. It also gave England something it had not had since the Reformation: women's religious orders, beginning with the Park Village sisterhood in 1845.

## the-salvation-army

The Salvation Army

William Booth, a Methodist preacher who could not settle inside a denomination, gave up his pulpit in 1861, and by 1865 was preaching in the streets of London's East End to people the churches were not reaching and did not particularly want. In 1878 his Christian Mission renamed itself an Army — with a General, ranks, uniforms, flags and brass bands to drown out the hecklers. And its officers were commissioned regardless of sex from the beginning, on Catherine Booth's insistence and the published argument she had made for it in 1859. It fed and housed as much as it preached: In Darkest England (1890) laid out a whole scheme of shelters, labor yards, rescue homes and emigration, and the Army counted everything it did, soul by soul and meal by meal. Mobs attacked it, magistrates fined it, and by 1906 it was working on every inhabited continent.

## the-missionary-movement-s-native-churches-seramp

The Missionary Movement's Native Churches (Serampore India; Sierra Leone & the Niger; Pacific Islands; China Coast & CIM)

Between 1793 and 1906 European and American missionary societies planted churches across India, West Africa, the Pacific and the China coast. And almost immediately those churches began to be run, staffed and spread by local Christians the societies had not planned for. Samuel Ajayi Crowther, enslaved as a boy in Yorubaland and freed by an anti-slavery patrol, translated the Bible into Yoruba and in 1864 became the first African Anglican bishop, only for the next generation of the same society to force him aside in 1890. In the Pacific it was hundreds of Polynesian teachers, not Europeans, who carried Christianity from island to island, often at the cost of their lives. In China the preacher Liang Fa wrote a tract in 1832 whose stray reading by Hong Xiuquan helped set the Taiping upheaval in motion, and the former opium addict known as Pastor Xi built a network of his own converts inland. What holds these four wings together is a single problem. Nearly everything written about them was written from the mission's side, and the native voices that do survive are what any honest telling has to be built on.

Liberia's settler church belongs to the West African wing. It came from America. Lott Cary was born a slave near Richmond, Virginia, about 1780, and the church there encouraged him to preach. About 1815 he helped rouse the Black Christians of Richmond to take an interest in Africa. The Richmond African Missionary Society that followed gave between one hundred and one hundred and fifty dollars to the African mission for several years. In 1821 Cary sailed for West Africa. In a letter from Free Town he wrote, 'Before we left the United States, we formed ourselves into a church'. On 16 August 1823 he wrote from Monrovia, 'Our little church has been wonderfully blessed of late.' He had baptized two the day before. Cary's words reach us through a biography by J. B. Taylor, a Richmond Baptist pastor, printed in 1837.

Korea lies outside the four wings, but its Protestant church began in these same years. Before the first missionaries came, two Scots at Mukden, Ross and McIntyre, had done introductory work in China among Koreans. Some Koreans there took up the faith. They began work in Korean villages on the China side of the Yalu. Ross and McIntyre translated a number of the Gospels, and later the whole New Testament.

Underwood, who reached Korea in 1885, says in his book of 1908 that Dr. Allen settled in Seoul in September 1884. He adds that the Methodists sent Dr. McClay from Japan to look over the field in that same year.

The first converts, in July and the fall of 1886, were baptized behind closed doors. The old law of death to Christians had never been repealed. It seemed plain that it would not be enforced, but no one knew how far to venture.

A Korean colporteur, Soh Sang Ryun, told Underwood of 'a small company of Christians' at Sorai. By 1890 the two churches had over one hundred converts in all Korea. In 1896 the missionaries' monthly in Seoul spoke of inquirers who had 'finished the study of the Ross catechism'. It was The Korean Repository. All of this comes to us in the missionaries' own telling.

## the-haugean-awakening

The Haugean Awakening

In 1796 a Norwegian farmer's son named Hans Nielsen Hauge had a religious experience while he was working in his father's field. He set out on foot to tell people about it. That was against the law, because Norway's Conventicle Act kept religious meetings for the parish clergy. He wrote thirty-three books and tracts and walked thousands of miles. He founded mills, paper works and trading businesses to support the network of "friends" that grew around him. The state answered by arresting him in 1804. It kept him in prison and under prosecution for most of the next ten years. The movement long outlasted the man. After his death in 1824 the friends' societies went on meeting and trading. In 1842 the Conventicle Act itself was repealed.

## the-reveil-and-inner-mission-awakening

The Reveil & Inner-Mission Awakening

After the wars with Napoleon, a wave of fresh, personal faith ran through Protestant churches in Switzerland, France, the Netherlands and Germany. In Geneva it rose against a comfortable rationalist establishment. In the Netherlands it came in the same years as a split from the state church in 1834. Everywhere it held that faith must be felt, not just handed down. What set it apart was what it built. In Hamburg in 1833, Johann Hinrich Wichern opened the Rauhes Haus for street children. It grew into the Inner Mission, a whole web of Christian social work. In 1836 Theodor and Friederike Fliedner opened a house for deaconesses at Kaiserswerth. It trained women as nurses and teachers. In 1851 Florence Nightingale was one of its guests. Within a generation it had built a lasting network of hospitals, schools, orphan homes and training houses.

## restorationist-apocalyptic-ferment-and-adventism

Restorationist-Apocalyptic Ferment & Adventism

In the 1830s and 40s, thousands of Americans across denominations followed Baptist farmer William Miller's calculation that Christ would return around 1844 — and when the date passed without incident, in what believers still call the Great Disappointment, most walked away disillusioned. A determined remnant didn't: they reread their calculations, concluded the date had marked something other than Christ's visible return, and built lasting communities around that reinterpretation, most enduringly the Seventh-day Adventist Church and a cluster of related Adventist bodies. What makes the story unusual is how openly it was told — the movement wrote down its own failed prediction and its own morning-after reckoning with it. And that same instinct for self-documentation carried into its later history, including its own internal debate over the Trinity in its earliest decades, formally settled in its 1931 statement of belief.

## the-optina-elders-and-russian-ascetic-renewal

The Optina Elders & Russian Ascetic Renewal

At a small monastery in the forest near Kozelsk, south of Moscow, a hermitage was founded in 1821. It became the center of something Russian Orthodoxy had never had at this scale. Elders, called startsy, kept the door open to anyone who walked up to it. Peasants, students, landowners and novelists queued outside the cell of Father Ambrose. The elders could not see everyone, so they answered by letter. They left thousands of pages of spiritual advice. They wrote not to monks but to ordinary people trying to live ordinary lives. They were also publishers. From the 1840s, working with the Slavophile thinker Ivan Kireevsky, Optina ran a press. It printed the Greek monastic fathers for Russian readers. Dostoevsky came in 1878 after the death of his small son. He turned what he found there into the elder Zosima.

## the-kollyvades-and-greek-renewal

The Kollyvades & Greek Renewal

It began as an argument about when to hold memorial services for the dead — Saturday, as tradition had it, or Sunday, as convenience on Mount Athos had made usual. And the monks who insisted on the older practice were nicknamed for the boiled wheat, kollyva, served at those services. The quarrel was small; what came out of it was not. Its leaders argued for frequent communion against a general practice of receiving perhaps once or twice a year, and in 1782 published in Venice the Philokalia, an anthology of Greek ascetic writers on prayer that became one of the most influential books ever printed in Orthodoxy. Under Ottoman rule they also did something with few parallels anywhere in Christian history. They prepared, pastorally and deliberately, people who expected to be executed for refusing to convert, and then wrote up their deaths in a New Martyrologion in 1799. For their trouble the Kollyvades were driven off Athos and scattered through the islands.

## coptic-revival-under-cyril-iv

The Coptic Revival — Cyril IV and After

The Coptic Orthodox Church entered the nineteenth century poor, largely untaught, and reduced in the eyes of most outsiders to a survival. And then in 1854 a young patriarch took office and spent seven years changing that. Cyril IV founded schools, including one for girls at a time when that was nearly unheard of in Egypt, brought in a printing press, built a library, and set about educating clergy who had mostly learned the liturgy by ear. He died suddenly in 1861, not yet fifty, in circumstances still argued about. What he started did not stop. An elected lay communal council from 1874, a Coptic press and newspapers from the 1870s, and the schools that would eventually produce the twentieth-century Sunday School movement out of which the modern Coptic renaissance came.

## latter-day-saints-lds

Latter-day Saints (LDS)

In 1830 a young man in western New York named Joseph Smith published the Book of Mormon — which he said he had translated from golden plates shown to him by an angel — and on 6 April that year organized a church around it. Its members were driven out of Ohio, then Missouri, then Illinois, where a mob killed Smith in the Carthage jail in 1844. Under Brigham Young most of them crossed the plains from 1847 and settled the Salt Lake valley, laying out towns, irrigation systems and eventually a territory largely of their own making. The nineteenth-century church practiced plural marriage openly from 1852, which brought decades of federal prosecution before its president formally renounced the practice in 1890 and Utah entered the Union six years later. Its members documented themselves relentlessly: the Relief Society's minute book, Eliza R. Snow's poems and trail diaries, and Emmeline B. Wells's newspaper make up one of the fullest records any nineteenth-century American religious community left behind.

## jehovah-s-witnesses-bible-students

Jehovah's Witnesses (Bible Students)

In the early 1870s Charles Taze Russell, a young clothing merchant in Pittsburgh, gathered a small circle to study the Bible. They aimed to read it for themselves, free of the teaching handed down by the churches. In 1879 Russell began a magazine, Zion's Watch Tower and Herald of Christ's Presence. A network of readers in many countries grew up around it. They called themselves Bible Students. They handed out Russell's six-volume Studies in the Scriptures by the million. They went door to door as colporteurs, traveling sellers of religious books. From 1893 they met each year at large conventions. The movement was built on print and on handing books from person to person from the very start. That is why its record of itself is so full for a body of its size. The name Jehovah's Witnesses came later, and so did the tightly run central body the movement is known for today. Both came after Russell's death in 1916, under his successor, Joseph Franklin Rutherford.

## christian-science

Christian Science

Mary Baker Eddy was a New England woman in poor health for most of her early life who, after a recovery in 1866 that she attributed to reading the Gospels, spent nine years working out what she had understood and published it in 1875 as Science and Health with Key to the Scriptures. Around the book she organized a church in 1879, a college to train practitioners, and a set of periodicals that carried the healing testimonies of ordinary members. Its congregations have no ordained clergy, no sacraments and no sermons in the usual sense. Two lay readers read aloud from the Bible and from Eddy's book, the same lesson in every branch church in the world on any given Sunday. Eddy governed the movement closely through the Manual of The Mother Church, written in 1895, until her death in 1910. And the practitioners who did most of its actual work were, then as later, overwhelmingly women.

## christadelphians

Christadelphians

John Thomas was an English doctor who emigrated to America in 1832, nearly drowned in a shipwreck on the way, and resolved that if he survived he would work out the Bible's teaching for himself rather than take it on anyone's authority. He concluded that most of what the churches taught had been added long after the apostles, and set out the alternative in Elpis Israel in 1848. His followers organized as "ecclesias" — small congregations with no clergy, where members taught and presided in turn. And in 1864 adopted the name Christadelphians, brothers of Christ, largely so they could claim conscientious-objector status in the American Civil War. Robert Roberts, a Scottish printer's apprentice who became the movement's second great figure, gave them a magazine that has run continuously since 1864 and a written confession, the Birmingham Statement of Faith, that they still use.

## catholic-apostolic-church-irvingites

Catholic Apostolic Church (Irvingites)

In 1830 and 1831, people in the fashionable London congregation of the Scottish preacher Edward Irving began speaking in tongues and prophesying — something almost nobody in Britain had expected to see again. And Irving was deposed by his presbytery for his teaching and died in 1834, before the movement that bears his name had really taken shape. What emerged was a church built on the conviction that Christ was about to return and had restored the apostolate to prepare for it. Twelve men were called as apostles, and around them grew a fourfold ministry, church buildings on a near-cathedral scale, and one of the richest liturgies composed in the nineteenth century. Because the apostles were held to be irreplaceable, no successors were ordained. As they died, the church's ministry simply ran out, and when the last of them died in February 1901 its congregations entered what they called the time of silence. Continuing to worship, with nobody left who could ordain anyone.

## oneida-community

Oneida Community

John Humphrey Noyes taught that a Christian could be wholly free of sin in this life, and in 1848 he gathered a community at Oneida in upstate New York to live as though the kingdom had already arrived. They held property in common, replaced marriage with what they called complex marriage — in which every member was in principle married to every other. Submitted to a formal system of group criticism, and from 1869 ran a selective breeding program they named stirpiculture. They also worked hard and paid their way, manufacturing animal traps, silk thread and eventually tableware. Outside campaigning and internal strain finished it. Noyes left for Canada in 1879, and in 1880 the community voted itself out of existence, reorganizing on New Year's Day 1881 as a joint-stock company.

## spiritualism-new-thought

Spiritualism / New Thought

In March 1848 two young sisters in a farmhouse at Hydesville, New York, said they were communicating with the dead through rapping sounds, and within a few years séances, mediums and spirit circles had spread through the United States and Britain, drawing in reformers, grieving families and scientists who wanted to test the claims. Running alongside it, and partly overlapping, was New Thought. A family of teachers descended from the Maine healer Phineas Quimby who held that mind governs matter and that right thinking heals. Both borrowed Christian vocabulary heavily and grew in the same nineteenth-century soil as the revivals, but neither is a Christian tradition; they are named here only because visitors reasonably ask where they fit. Spiritualism's public credibility took a heavy blow in 1888, when Margaret Fox declared before an audience in New York that she and her sister had produced the original raps by cracking their toe joints — a confession she withdrew a year later.

## korean-catholic-origins

Korean Catholic Origins

Korea is the only country in the world where the Catholic Church began without a missionary. In the late eighteenth century a circle of Confucian scholars near Seoul studied Chinese-language Catholic books, convinced themselves, and sent one of their number, Yi Sung-hun, to Beijing to be baptized in 1784. He came home and baptized the others, and a church of several thousand people existed before a priest had ever set foot in the country. It was written in Korean from the start — Chong Yak-chong's catechism was in the vernacular script rather than in the scholarly Chinese of the elite. Which is part of why it reached women, servants and the poor as fast as it reached the literati. The Choson state read it as treason against the Confucian order, and the church spent a century under persecution, with something on the order of ten thousand Korean Catholics killed in successive purges between 1801 and 1866, until a treaty with France in 1886 finally brought the killing to an end.

The Paris Foreign Missions Society had an establishment in Korea.

In 1866, says the Catholic Encyclopedia of 1908, two bishops and seven priests of the Society were beheaded in March. Only three priests of the Society were left, it says: Ridel, Féron and Calais.
