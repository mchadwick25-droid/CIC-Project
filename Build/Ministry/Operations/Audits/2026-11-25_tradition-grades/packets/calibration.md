# Packet: calibration

## P01

From about 70 to 200 CE, Christians in Antioch, the cities of Asia Minor and Rome gathered in households. No central body ruled them. Letters carried by hand held them together. After the apostles died, these churches had not yet settled who should lead. Ignatius's letters urged one bishop with elders beside him. At Rome, early letters show a council of elders leading. Being a Christian could bring real danger. Ignatius's letters say he was marched under guard toward death.


## P02

From 1517 to 1580, a reform grew out of Wittenberg. Martin Luther taught there at the university.

At the heart of this tradition sits a frightened conscience. The churches of this reform taught that the commandments lead a person to despair of his own powers, and that God's promise then comforts him. In their teaching, a sinner is freely justified for Christ's sake, through faith, and the sacraments carry that promise. Baptism means the old self is drowned by daily sorrow and repentance, and absolution is given as though from God himself. They held that in the Supper Christ's body and blood are truly present, in and under the bread and wine, and they gave no explanation of how.

On 31 October 1517 Luther, a friar, wrote to Albrecht, the Archbishop of Mainz. Preachers were offering indulgences in the archbishop's name, to help build St. Peter's in Rome, and Luther was troubled by what their hearers now believed. He asked the archbishop to withdraw the instruction the preachers worked from, and he sent ninety-five statements for debate with it, the Ninety-Five Theses.

In 1518 Luther was summoned to Augsburg. By his own account, told years later at his table, he knelt before Cardinal Cajetan. He would not say the one word an Italian urged on him, Revoco, which means I recant.

In 1521 Luther stood before the Emperor and the Electors at the Diet of Worms. He told the story years afterward to his students at his own table. By his account, he would not take back his books of teaching, though he was willing to be corrected if he had been too violent in his books against opponents. By the same account, the next day he said he would accept instruction in everything except what touches Holy Scripture.

The reform ran ahead of its founder. In March 1522 Luther left the Wartburg, where he had been hidden since Worms, against the Elector's instruction to stay. Wittenberg was in disorder over how fast to sweep away the old forms of worship. Luther preached eight days running and told his congregation he saw no signs of love among them.

On 1 July 1523 two young monks, John and Henry, were burned at Brussels after they refused to give up their teaching. Within months Luther wrote a ballad about them.

The reform also taught people in their own language. German hymns were sung inside the Mass. Wittenberg's first hymn-book was printed in 1524 and held eight hymns, four of them Luther's. Johann Walter later remembered that Luther kept him at Wittenberg for three weeks. Walter wrote out the notes for some of the Gospels and Epistles until the first German Mass was sung.

The reform's chief tool for forming people was a household catechism, written in 1529. Its three parts were the Ten Commandments, the Creed and the Lord's Prayer. A father was to question his children and servants at least once a week. The young were to recite the parts daily and get neither food nor drink until they could. After each part came the question, what does this mean? At his table, Luther told his students that his wife, Katharina von Bora, had asked him why the old church prayed so hard, and their own household so coldly.

In 1530 the Emperor called a Diet to Augsburg, to deal with the Turk and to hear the quarrels over religion. Melanchthon wrote the Augsburg Confession for it. An elector, several princes and the senates of two cities signed it. Luther's name is not among them. The Confession rejected those who taught otherwise about the Supper.

In 1525 Luther wrote against the peasants' rising, and in 1543 he wrote a treatise against the Jews. The sources behind this account hold the 1525 writings, but not the 1543 treatise.

Luther died in the mid-1540s. In 1580 a fuller book of confessions was gathered, the Book of Concord. It drew together the catechisms and the confession given decades before.


## P06

In July 1526, Afonso I, the Christian king of Kongo, wrote to the king of Portugal. He asked for wine and flour for the Mass. Then he warned that his land was being emptied of its people, who were being carried off to be sold. Contact with Portugal had brought Kongo both the faith and this trade, and in his letters Afonso set the one against the other.

Portuguese ships reached the mouth of the Zaire River in 1491. A report written down a century later says Kongo's king was baptized and took the name Dom João, and that his son, who took the name Afonso, soon followed.

Afonso spoke for himself in letters his scribes wrote down, and they survive, while almost everything else about Kongo's Christians was written by Portuguese and Italian outsiders. In a letter to the pope in 1512, which a Portuguese chronicler later printed, Afonso said Portuguese religious men had led his people away from idols. In 1517 he wrote that he sent his kin to Portugal to learn the faith and bring light to the blind in his kingdom. He wanted them to uphold the faith after his death.

Not everyone at court welcomed the faith. The report says his brother Pango stood against him, and that the rule of one wife turned some nobles and their wives against it. It says Afonso lost his province on a charge that he wanted the throne, and later won it back. When the old king died, the report adds, Afonso's mother kept the death secret for three days so that he could reach the capital in time. Then, it says, Pango brought a great army against him, and Afonso defeated it and kept the throne.

By 1526, the letters turn to a harm that came with Portugal. Afonso wrote that merchants and thieves were carrying off his people every day, including the sons of nobles and his own kin. He asked for priests and teachers for his schools, and for no merchants at all. In his words: "It is our will that in these kingdoms there be no trade in slaves, and no outlet for them." His letter is a plea. It does not tell us what came of it.

In December 1540, fourteen years on, he wrote to Portugal of an attempt on his life. He said a friar had arranged for seven or eight white men to fire at him during Easter Mass. The shot passed by the skirts of his robe, he said. It killed one man and wounded two. That charge rests on his word alone.

He wrote that letter as he wrote the others, calling the king of Portugal his brother and signing himself king.


## P09

This was the Catholic Church at the height of its establishment and on the edge of its worst crisis: enormously wealthy, woven into the monarchies of Europe, and increasingly unable to control them. Rome's condemnation of Jansenism in the bull Unigenitus (1713) turned into a fifty-year political crisis in France. Then the kings of Portugal, France, Spain and Sicily drove out the Jesuits. In 1773 Clement XIV abolished the Society. His bull names quarrels, riots, charges against Jesuit teaching, and greed for property. The kings, it says, wished to stop Christians rising against one another. Then came the Revolution: church property nationalized, thousands killed or driven abroad, and Pope Pius VI carried off to die a French prisoner in 1799. His successor signed a concordat with Napoleon in 1801. In 1809 Napoleon annexed the Papal States, saying that spiritual power joined to rule over land had bred quarrels. He kept the pope captive until 1814. The pope outlived him and restored the Jesuits that year.

Rome ran its missions through Propaganda, the congregation for spreading the faith. Its collected decrees show three acts in these years.

In 1656 the Holy Office answered questions sent from China in 1645. Christian scholars could join the ceremony in the hall of Confucius when they took a degree. That cult, it said, seemed merely civil and political. Converts could also keep rites for their dead, once anything superstitious was removed.

In 1659 Propaganda sent orders to its vicars apostolic. They were not to urge any people to change its rites, customs and ways. What is more absurd, it asked, than to carry France, Spain or Italy into China (in a plain English rendering)?

In 1726 Propaganda ruled that native priests of China, Tonkin and Cochinchina must swear an oath. It was the one set by the constitution Ex illa die. That text seemed to name only Europeans. The oath bound a priest to obey the pope's order on the Chinese rites, without evasion.
