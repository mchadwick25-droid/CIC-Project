## kongo-christianity-afonso-to-1650

Kongo Christianity, Afonso I to 1650

In July 1526, Afonso I, the Christian king of Kongo, wrote to the king of Portugal. He asked for wine and flour for the Mass. Then he warned that his land was being emptied of its people, who were being carried off to be sold. Contact with Portugal had brought Kongo both the faith and this trade, and in his letters Afonso set the one against the other.

Portuguese ships reached the mouth of the Zaire River in 1491. A report written down a century later says Kongo's king was baptized and took the name Dom João, and that his son, who took the name Afonso, soon followed.

Afonso spoke for himself in letters his scribes wrote down, and they survive, while almost everything else about Kongo's Christians was written by Portuguese and Italian outsiders. In a letter to the pope in 1512, which a Portuguese chronicler later printed, Afonso said Portuguese religious men had led his people away from idols. In 1517 he wrote that he sent his kin to Portugal to learn the faith and bring light to the blind in his kingdom. He wanted them to uphold the faith after his death.

Not everyone at court welcomed the faith. The report says his brother Pango stood against him, and that the rule of one wife turned some nobles and their wives against it. It says Afonso lost his province on a charge that he wanted the throne, and later won it back. When the old king died, the report adds, Afonso's mother kept the death secret for three days so that he could reach the capital in time. Then, it says, Pango brought a great army against him, and Afonso defeated it and kept the throne.

By 1526, the letters turn to a harm that came with Portugal. Afonso wrote that merchants and thieves were carrying off his people every day, including the sons of nobles and his own kin. He asked for priests and teachers for his schools, and for no merchants at all. In his words: "It is our will that in these kingdoms there be no trade in slaves, and no outlet for them." His letter is a plea. It does not tell us what came of it.

In December 1540, fourteen years on, he wrote to Portugal of an attempt on his life. He said a friar had arranged for seven or eight white men to fire at him during Easter Mass. The shot passed by the skirts of his robe, he said. It killed one man and wounded two. That charge rests on his word alone.

He wrote that letter as he wrote the others, calling the king of Portugal his brother and signing himself king.

## kirishitan-japan

Kirishitan Japan

A Japanese Christian stood before a governor who was leading an inquiry against the Christians. The governor told him that to follow a faith the lord had forbidden was a capital crime, and that he could still be safe if he gave it up. The man, Jean Ingoro, answered that nothing could give him more joy than to shed his blood for God. The governor said: "I have never seen anyone as happy as you seem to be."

Ingoro was one of Japan's Christians. Their church began in 1549, when Francis Xavier landed at Kagoshima and wrote from there that November. By the early 1600s its members faced prison, exile and execution. A few of them wrote about it in their own words. Most of their letters went to Jesuit priests, and missionaries chose them and printed them in their own books. A French editor translated them in 1870. So we hear these few Christians through the hands of the mission.

Asked where such joy came from, Ingoro told the governor how he had come to believe. He had read the writings of the Japanese sects, and he found nothing in them about a Creator. He heard the preachers and argued with them many times, and he thought no priest could stand up to him. Then a Christian came back from Korea to the town where Ingoro was living. He converted most of its people and nobles, and Ingoro with them, by his own account the most stubborn of all. The governor answered that he did not know whether the missionaries could be trusted, and he had Ingoro jailed.

His letter stops there. A note printed after it adds that the governor accused him of stealing the bodies of four crucified Christians, among them little Luis, to sell them at Nagasaki. Ingoro answered that he had only gathered bones that had fallen, to bury them with Christian rites.

Michel Fieoyemon wrote from prison in January 1609. He had been there four years for confessing the name of God, and had borne trials without number, of every kind. He wrote: "I have borne them with such keen joy and comfort that I seem to have been a prisoner for only one day."

In May 1614, a group of Christians sailed into exile to Tsugaru. The lord of the province, in the name of the country's ruler, ordered them to clear unworked land. They answered that they would obey in everything if they could keep their faith. In their letter to the priests, they asked that one of them visit the next year, though they knew it could not be this year.

An outsider saw the same years. Richard Cocks, an English trader at Hirado, heard in August 1615 that the king of Spain was sending the emperor a gift for his kindness to Christians. The king little knew, Cocks wrote, that the emperor had already banished all the friars, monks, Jesuits and priests.

One more exchange survives. A woman named Maria heard it said that her husband, Jean Pioyemon, had been condemned to die. She asked him to send her his reliquary, a small case for holy relics, and some of his hair to remember him by. She hoped to be there at his death, to say a last farewell. He answered that to die as a martyr was far above what he deserved. He feared he might be set free instead, and he asked at least to have his fingers cut off for God, as other Christians had at Suruga. He would gladly exchange reliquaries with her, he wrote, but she was not to speak of his hair. If by God's grace he were put to death, she would then receive the great gift she asked of him.

## china-mission-born-christianity

Mission-Born Christianity in Ming China

In the Ming court, a Chinese minister wrote to the emperor in defense of foreign priests. He called them, in the translator's English, "worthies and sages," the words China used for its own great teachers. If they were guilty, he wrote, then so was he.

The minister was Xu Guangqi, known in English as Paul Su. He was one of the scholars and officials whom Jesuit missionaries had won to Christianity in Ming China. He argued for the faith in the words of China's own teachers. His memorial is a rare convert's own voice, though it reaches us in an English translation of 1850, and its wording is the translator's.

The mission's own story was written by the Jesuit Nicolas Trigault, from Matteo Ricci's own writings. In September 1583, it tells, Ricci and a fellow Jesuit, Michele Ruggieri, reached the city of Zhaoqing. Ruggieri had stayed there once before and been sent away. Kneeling before the governor, they asked for a place to build a house and a church. He answered kindly, and told them to look about the city for a place.

The memorial answers a report from the Board of Rites, made in 1616 or 1617. The Board had impeached the Jesuits at court. Its report said their teaching was spreading, so that even men of eminence were believing in it.

Xu did not deny it. He wrote that he had talked with the Jesuits about religion for years. He said they had come thousands of miles because they heard that China's own teachers served Heaven through virtue, as theirs served the Lord of Heaven. He called Buddhism false, since it had not reformed hearts in eighteen hundred years. In his view the Jesuits' teaching could repair and strengthen the royal laws and the learning of the scholars, and even correct the Buddhists.

Then he proposed three tests. The Jesuits' books should be translated, and the emperor's ministers should judge whether they were sound. The Jesuits should debate the leading Buddhists and Taoists in writing, and scholar-officials should decide. A summary of their teaching should go before the emperor himself. If the teaching proved wicked, he wrote, the Jesuits should be sent away, and he would accept the punishment due to those who helped deceive the emperor.

Near the end, he wrote that for peace and good government, "nothing can surpass this that is taught by your majesty's courtiers." The courtiers were the Jesuits; he called them the emperor's own. If the Board had its way, he wrote, these men must at once return to their own countries. He had fasted and washed, he said, before he dared to lay their case before the throne.

## new-spain-and-andes-mission-born-christianity

Mission-Born Christianity in New Spain and the Andes

In 1536, a Franciscan friar in Mexico wrote down a number. "I believe more than four million souls were baptized from the time the land was won until the time I write this." The land had been won in 1521. The friar was Toribio de Benavente, called Motolinía.

He wrote the mission's story from the inside. Twelve friars, he says, left Spain in January 1523 for the land of Anahuac, now called New Spain. They came for the conversion of its native peoples. We hear the people they baptized only through the friars' pens.

The land the friars reached had already been struck. Smallpox came with a sick man on a Spanish ship, and in most provinces, Motolinía says, more than half the people died. War killed many others, above all around Mexico City. He counts ten plagues in all. After smallpox and war came hunger, and then the weight of Spanish rule: tribute, slavery and forced work in the gold mines. Many houses, he wrote, stood empty.

Motolinía read this as God's work. He wrote that God had killed many of the lords who would not accept the new law, through those same plagues. Their sons, baptized as small children, then inherited their lands. That is his own reading.

He also told a smaller scene. Friars visited two provinces from their house at Cuauhnahuac, and people came from the small town to the larger one to hear them and to have their children baptized. One night, heavy rain turned a stream into a river. The friars preached from one bank to the people on the other, and the people would not go home until their children were baptized. So they made a poor raft of canes. Half in the water and half in their arms, they carried the children across, and the friars baptized them with great effort, he says, because there were so many.

His counts are his own, and they do not agree. In one book he gives three totals for the same fifteen years: more than four million, close to five million, and more than nine million. "In my judgment, and in truth, more than nine million souls of Indians will have been baptized in the time I speak of." The numbers count baptisms. They do not tell what the people believed.

A Jesuit, José de Acosta, wrote about the Indians of Peru and Mexico some fifty years later, around 1590. He said many of their customs resembled the law of Moses, and some came near the Gospel. In Peru, he wrote, people confessed their sins aloud and washed in water to cleanse them. The Mexica even had a kind of baptism of newborn children. He judged these customs mixed with great abomination, and he named the devil's pride as the cause of their idol worship. But some priests told him that after the Christians came to Peru, people there confessed even their thoughts.

## anti-trinitarian-currents-servetus-polish-brethr

Anti-Trinitarian Currents (Servetus; Polish Brethren/Socinians; Transylvanian Unitarians)

The Reformation's appeal to Scripture alone led a minority to a conclusion the rest found intolerable: that the doctrine of the Trinity was not taught in the Bible. Michael Servetus published against it in 1531 and was burned at Geneva in 1553 with the city council's approval and Calvin's agreement. The idea survived further east, where the Polish Brethren built a printing town and academy at Raków and issued the Racovian Catechism in 1605, and where the Transylvanian diet's Edict of Torda in 1568 gave a Unitarian church legal standing under the preaching of Ferenc Dávid. Poland expelled the Brethren in 1658; the Transylvanian church is still there.

## schwenckfelders

Schwenckfelders

Caspar Schwenckfeld was a Silesian nobleman who welcomed Luther's reform and then broke with it, concluding that outward means — preaching, sacraments, church order — could not do what only the inward work of Christ could do. In 1526 he urged that the Lord's Supper be suspended until Christians could agree what it was, a 'standing still' his followers kept for generations, and he taught a distinctive doctrine of the glorified, heavenly flesh of Christ that Lutherans and Reformed alike condemned. His followers never built a church in the ordinary sense. They met in houses, copied his writings out by hand, and were harried in Silesia for two centuries until a remnant emigrated to Pennsylvania in the 1730s.

## family-of-love-and-spiritualists-franck-niclaes

Family of Love & Spiritualists (Franck, Niclaes)

Some of the Reformation's most radical figures decided that no visible church was worth joining at all. Sebastian Franck, a former priest who became a printer and chronicler, held that the true church had been hidden since the apostles. God's word, he said, spoke inwardly to people of every nation. Hendrik Niclaes, a merchant in the Low Countries, went further and founded a fellowship of his own. This was the Family of Love, a secret group whose members outwardly obeyed whatever church their country required. They kept their real beliefs to themselves. Familists were hunted by royal proclamation in England. And because they blended in so well, historians still argue about how many there were and how long they lasted.

## orthodoxy-under-the-ottomans-parish-millet-and-n

Orthodoxy under the Ottomans - Parish, Millet & Neomartyrs

When Constantinople fell in 1453 the Orthodox Church did not disappear; it was reorganized. The sultan recognized the Ecumenical Patriarch as head of the empire's Orthodox Christians — a civil as well as a religious office, taxing, judging and appointing. And for the next four centuries most Orthodox Christians lived as a tolerated but subordinate community under Muslim rule. Parish and monastic life continued, Mount Athos kept its monasteries, and a distinctive kind of saint appeared. The neomartyr, executed for refusing to convert or for returning to the faith after having converted, sometimes prepared for that death by a confessor beforehand. The period also produced the strangest episode in Orthodox history, the patriarch Cyril Lucaris, whose Calvinist-sounding Confession of 1629 was repudiated by a succession of church councils after his death in 1638.

## st-thomas-christians-under-portuguese-pressure-d

St. Thomas Christians under Portuguese Pressure - Diamper & the Coonan Cross

The Christians of the Malabar coast traced their church to the apostle Thomas. For centuries they had worshipped in Syriac and taken their bishops from the Church of the East. The Portuguese found this puzzling and then intolerable. In 1599 Archbishop Aleixo de Menezes held a synod at Diamper. It placed the community under Latin rule, revised its liturgy, and ordered its Syriac books corrected or burned. Half a century of resentment came to a head on 3 January 1653. At Mattancherry a crowd swore they would no longer obey the Jesuits or the Jesuit archbishop, Francisco Garcia. Tradition says they tied ropes to a stone cross as they swore. This is the Coonan Cross Oath, named for a cross said to have bent under the strain.

## philippine-catholicism

Philippine Catholicism

Spanish rule in the Philippines began in 1565, and with it came friars — Augustinians first, then Franciscans, Jesuits, Dominicans and Recollects. Who baptized, gathered scattered settlements into parishes and built stone churches at their centers. What grew there was not simply transplanted Spanish religion. The first book printed in the islands, the Doctrina Christiana of 1593, appeared in Spanish and in Tagalog written both in Latin letters and in the local baybayin script, and lay confraternities took on much of the work of devotion. Out of that vernacular habit came the tradition the islands are still known for — the pasyon, the Passion of Christ told as a long verse epic and chanted in ordinary houses through Holy Week — though the surviving pasyon texts date from the century after this one.

## the-union-of-brest-and-ruthenian-greek-catholic-

The Union of Brest & Ruthenian Greek Catholic Christianity

In 1596 most of the Orthodox bishops of the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth met at Brest and accepted communion with Rome while keeping their Byzantine liturgy, their Church Slavonic, their calendar and their married clergy. Their opponents met in the same town at the same time and rejected it outright, so what the union produced was not one church but two rival hierarchies claiming the same people and arguing in print for a century. The new church had to explain itself constantly — to Rome, to the Orthodox it had left, and to Latin bishops who never quite treated it as an equal. And that argument became the ordinary condition of its life rather than an occasional crisis. Its most famous figure, Archbishop Josaphat Kuntsevych, was killed by a hostile crowd at Vitebsk in 1623.

## the-kyiv-mohyla-orthodox-revival

The Kyiv-Mohyla Orthodox Revival

Orthodox Kyiv answered the Union of Brest not with a lament but with a school. A lay brotherhood founded one in 1615, and Petro Mohyla, metropolitan from 1633, turned it into a college on the Jesuit model — Latin, rhetoric, philosophy, theology. On the reasoning that a church cannot defend itself against arguments it cannot read. He also gave the Kyivan church its own instruments. An Orthodox Confession of faith, approved in revised form by a council at Iaşi in 1642, a short catechism, and a great service book, the Trebnyk of 1646, which regularized how priests were actually to celebrate the sacraments. It is the clearest case anywhere of a tradition taking up its rivals' tools in order to remain itself.

## the-tridentine-church

The Tridentine Church

The Catholic Church's answer to the Reformation took decades to assemble and then held for four centuries. The Council of Trent met in three widely separated periods between 1545 and 1563, restating Catholic teaching on Scripture and tradition, justification and the sacraments, and — as important in practice — ordering bishops to reside in their dioceses and to found seminaries. What followed was wholesale standardization: a Roman Catechism, a revised Missal and Breviary issued for the whole Latin church, and in 1622 a Roman office for the missions, Propaganda Fide. The same period produced the Roman Inquisition and the Index of Forbidden Books, and a wave of new active orders — Capuchins, Ursulines, Oratorians — doing work the older monastic rules had never been designed for.

## russian-church-stoglav-to-nikon

The Russian Church, Stoglav to the Eve of Nikon

In the century and a half after 1517 the Russian Church became something new: a national church with a patriarch of its own. A great council held in Moscow in 1551, its decisions arranged in a hundred chapters and so known as the Stoglav, tried to regulate everything from icon painting to the conduct of priests. In 1589 the visiting Ecumenical Patriarch Jeremias II consecrated Metropolitan Job as the first Patriarch of Moscow, making formal what Muscovy already believed about itself. Then came the Time of Troubles, when the state effectively collapsed and the church became the country's rallying point — Patriarch Hermogenes died in confinement rather than endorse a foreign claimant to the throne — and afterwards Patriarch Filaret, the young tsar's own father, governed alongside him. All of this stands immediately before the liturgical corrections of the 1650s that would tear the church apart.

## czech-churches-last-century

The Czech Churches' Last Century (Utraquists and Unitas)

A century before Martin Luther, Bohemia already had its own reform-minded national church — the Utraquists, who insisted that the laity receive both bread and wine at communion, and the Unity of the Brethren who grew alongside them. Surviving as a tolerated, functioning body long after the Hussite wars that gave it birth. Its final century opens with real legal standing, the 1609 Letter of Majesty guaranteeing religious freedom, and closes in catastrophe: the 1620 Battle of White Mountain crushed Bohemian Protestant resistance, and by 1627-28 the Habsburgs had outlawed the church outright, forcing its bishop, the scholar and educator Jan Amos Comenius, into permanent exile along with much of his flock. In between, the church produced lasting work — the Bohemian Confession of 1575 and the Kralice Bible, the first complete Czech translation from the original languages — leaving behind a body of literature that outlasted the institution itself.

## ethiopian-christianity-gragn-to-fasilides

Ethiopian Christianity — Gragn to Fasilides

Ethiopian Christianity nearly ended in this period and then reasserted itself twice over. From 1529 the armies of Ahmad ibn Ibrahim al-Ghazi — remembered as Gragn, the left-handed — swept through the highlands, burning churches and monasteries and destroying manuscripts on a scale that still limits what can be known about earlier Ethiopian history. The invasion was broken only at the battle of Wayna Daga in 1543, with Portuguese help. That help brought Jesuits, and after decades of argument Emperor Susenyos converted to Catholicism and tried to impose it, provoking a revolt so fierce that he abdicated in 1632. His son Fasilides expelled the Jesuits, restored the ancient church, and built a new capital at Gondar.

## the-remonstrants-and-dort

The Remonstrants and Dort

The Dutch Reformed minister Jacobus Arminius had doubts about how strictly predestination should be taught, and after his death in 1609 his supporters set them out in five articles — the Remonstrance of 1610. Asking the government to allow their view. The quarrel that followed swallowed the young Dutch Republic, mixing theology with politics until it ended on a scaffold. The statesman Johan van Oldenbarnevelt, the Remonstrants' protector, was executed in 1619. The Synod of Dort, meeting through the winter of 1618-19 with delegates from Reformed churches across Europe, condemned the Remonstrance and answered it with five canons of its own. Some two hundred ministers were deposed and their leaders driven into exile — until toleration returned in the 1630s and they came home to build a church of their own.

## scandinavian-reformation-kingdoms

The Scandinavian Reformation Kingdoms

In Scandinavia the Reformation arrived from the top down and stayed. Sweden's break began at the Diet of Västerås in 1527, when Gustav Vasa took the church's property and its independence, and was only completed at the Uppsala Synod of 1593, which committed the kingdom to the unaltered Augsburg Confession. In Denmark-Norway it came faster and harder. Christian III won a civil war, arrested the bishops outright in 1536, and the church ordinance drawn up with Luther's colleague Johannes Bugenhagen reorganized the churches of Denmark, Norway and Iceland at a stroke. Icelandic resistance ended in 1550 with the execution of the island's last Catholic bishop. What followed everywhere was the long, slow business of translating Bibles and hymnbooks and teaching a rural population its catechism.

## armenian-christianity-new-julfa

Armenian Christianity — Etchmiadzin and New Julfa

Caught between the Ottoman and Safavid empires, Armenians lived through one of the great forced migrations of their history: in 1604-05 Shah Abbas I, pursuing a scorched-earth strategy against the Ottomans, deported the population of Julfa and much of eastern Armenia into Persia, and many died on the road. Those who survived were settled in a new quarter across the river from Isfahan, New Julfa, where the cathedral known as the Vank was established in 1606 and where Armenian merchants built one of the most far-reaching trading networks in the world, from Amsterdam to Manila, funding their church as they went. Meanwhile the mother see at Etchmiadzin, now under Safavid rule, worked to rebuild itself as the center of Armenian church life.

## carmelite-reform-and-spanish-mystics

The Carmelite Reform and the Spanish Mystics

Teresa of Ávila had spent more than twenty years as a Carmelite nun in a large, comfortable, sociable convent when she concluded that the life she had promised was not the life she was living. In 1562 she founded a small house, St Joseph's in Ávila, under a stricter rule — poverty, enclosure, few sisters, long hours of silent prayer. And spent the rest of her life traveling Spain founding others, arguing with officials, and writing books about prayer she had never intended to publish. In 1568 a young friar, John of the Cross, joined the reform on the men's side and became its greatest poet. The Inquisition examined Teresa's account of her life, and both were opposed from inside their own order. John spent nine months imprisoned by his own brethren. Which is when he wrote some of his finest verse.

## church-of-the-east-schism-of-1552

The Church of the East — the Schism of 1552

By the sixteenth century the patriarchate of the Church of the East — once a communion that had reached as far as China — had contracted to the mountains and plains of Mesopotamia, and its highest office had become hereditary, passing from uncle to nephew inside a single family. In 1552 a party opposed to that arrangement elected a monastery superior, Yohannan Sulaqa, and sent him to Rome. He made a profession of faith there, was consecrated patriarch in 1553, returned home, and was arrested and killed in 1555. The line he had begun continued anyway, and from that point the ancient communion has carried two rival patriarchates — a division that has never fully healed.

## the-quakers-religious-society-of-friends

The Quakers (Religious Society of Friends)

In the turmoil of the English Civil War years, a shoemaker's apprentice named George Fox began telling anyone who would listen that Christ could teach his people himself. That every person carried an inward light, that no paid clergy or consecrated building was needed, and that a meeting could sit in silence until someone was moved to speak. Friends refused oaths, hat-honor and titles, refused to fight, and let women preach on the same footing as men. Thousands of them were fined, jailed and transported for it, and they kept a running written record of their own sufferings as they went. By the eighteenth century the movement had settled into a quieter, disciplined life of meetings and plain dress, and turned outward. The New Jersey tailor John Woolman spent decades traveling among Friends, pressing them to free the people they held as slaves, until the American yearly meetings made slaveholding a bar to membership.

## german-pietism-spener-francke-halle

German Pietism - Spener, Francke, Halle

In 1675 a Lutheran pastor in Frankfurt, Philipp Jakob Spener, published a short book called Pia Desideria arguing that correct doctrine correctly preached was not enough. Christians needed to meet in small groups, read Scripture together, and expect their lives to change. What followed was less a new church than a renewal movement inside the Lutheran state churches, and its center became Halle, where August Hermann Francke built an orphanage that grew into a complex of schools, a printing house and a Bible institute, funded by donations and tied to correspondents across Europe, Russia, India and North America. Pietism gave German Protestantism the conversion narrative as a genre and a great body of new hymnody, and through Halle's graduates it launched one of the first organized Protestant missions sent out from Europe, when Bartholomäus Ziegenbalg and Heinrich Plütschau sailed for the Danish trading post at Tranquebar in South India in 1706.

The Tamil church they planted went on. Fenger calls Aaron "the first native ordained" to the ministry. That was on 28 December 1733. By 1813 his list counts eleven Indian ministers.

## radical-pietism-and-the-brethren-dunkers-ephrata

Radical Pietism & the Brethren (Dunkers, Ephrata)

Where mainstream Pietism stayed inside the Lutheran church, a radical wing walked out of it — separatists who held that the true church had fallen away centuries earlier and that no state establishment could be mended. In 1708, at Schwarzenau in the little county of Wittgenstein — one of the few German territories that would leave separatists alone — eight of them baptized one another in the river Eder and became the Brethren, joining Pietist inwardness to Anabaptist convictions: baptism only for believers, plain living, and refusal of the sword. Persecution pushed them to Pennsylvania from 1719, where they became one of the first German-speaking religious cultures in colonial America; and where one of them, Conrad Beissel, founded the celibate community at Ephrata, which produced its own austere music, its own hand-lettered Fraktur manuscripts, and a sisterhood, the Roses of Saron, who wrote and sang their own hymns.
