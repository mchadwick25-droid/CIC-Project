# Packet L02

## armenian-christianity-conversion-era

Armenian Christianity (conversion era)

Armenia's own tradition holds that it was the first kingdom to adopt Christianity as its official religion, when Gregory the Illuminator — imprisoned for years in a pit, by the telling — converted King Trdat and the court around the turn of the fourth century. What followed over the next century and a half is the more remarkable part. An entire national church built from nothing, catechized at first in Greek and Syriac because Armenian had no script of its own, until the monk Mesrop Mashtots devised an alphabet around 405 and the church set about putting the Bible and the liturgy into the people's own language. That translation, made under the catholicos Sahak Partev, is still called by Armenians the Queen of Translations, and it did more than any royal decree to make Christianity Armenian rather than imported. The story of these years ends at the battle of Avarayr in 451, where Armenian nobles fought a Persian attempt to impose Zoroastrianism. A defeat on the field that became the founding story of Armenian religious identity.

## aksumite-christianity-conversion-era

Aksumite Christianity (conversion era)

The kingdom of Aksum, straddling what is now Ethiopia and Eritrea, was a Red Sea trading power with its own script and its own coinage when Christianity arrived by an almost accidental route. A young man from Tyre named Frumentius, shipwrecked and taken into the royal household, rose to be tutor and adviser, then traveled to Alexandria to ask Athanasius for a bishop. And was consecrated for the job himself, around 330. King Ezana's conversion followed, and it is legible in the most concrete way imaginable. His coins stop carrying the disc and crescent of the old religion and start carrying the cross, one of the earliest such declarations by any state anywhere. The faith took root in Ge'ez, the kingdom's own language, in which Scripture and liturgy were eventually rendered, and Frumentius's consecration bound the Ethiopian church to the Egyptian one for the next sixteen centuries. What survives from these years is mostly inscriptions, coinage, and later chronicles rather than the everyday voices of ordinary believers.

## persian-church-of-the-east-early

Persian Church of the East (early)

East of the Roman frontier, in the Persian Empire, lived Christians who had never had a Christian emperor and never would. When Constantine turned Christian, it made things worse for them, not better. It let the Sassanian court read them as a Roman fifth column. Under Shapur II, a long persecution began about 340. It killed clergy and lay people in numbers this church remembered in a whole literature of martyr acts. Among the dead was the church's own head, the catholicos Shemon bar Sabbae. He was put to death in the 340s after he would not collect a punitive double tax from Christians to fund the war against Rome. When toleration came at last, the church set up its own order by design. A synod at Seleucia-Ctesiphon in 410 gave it a legal structure and a recognized head. A further synod in 424 said its bishops would answer to no power outside Persia. Its worship was in Syriac, and it shared the learning of Edessa. Persian students there read Theodore of Mopsuestia's commentaries once they were put into Syriac. The church's later teachers made his Antiochene way of reading their own. And it was already becoming the church that would carry Christianity along the trade roads to India, Central Asia, and China.

## antiochene-exegetical-christianity-chrysostom-ce

Antiochene Exegetical Christianity (Chrysostom-centered)

Antioch was one of the largest cities of the Roman East, and the Christianity that grew there developed a distinctive habit of reading. Where Alexandria hunted for the hidden spiritual sense beneath the text, Antioch's teachers insisted on what the words actually said in their own historical setting — and then pressed hard on what that demanded of the people in front of them. John Chrysostom, trained in rhetoric by the pagan orator Libanius and in Scripture by Diodore of Tarsus, preached in Antioch through the 380s and 390s to congregations of tradesmen, householders, and slaves: on money, on anger, on how you treat your servants, on the theater and the racetrack, on almsgiving as a matter of justice rather than generosity. In 387, after the city's crowds smashed the imperial statues and Antioch waited to learn whether it would be destroyed in reprisal, he preached the sermons On the Statues to a terrified population. And the reason hundreds of his sermons survive at all is largely that people wanted to hear them again. At the end of 397 he was taken to Constantinople and made its archbishop, where he preached the same way to the imperial court, which is roughly why he died in exile in 407.

## jerusalem-liturgical-pilgrimage-christianity

Jerusalem Liturgical-Pilgrimage Christianity

Constantine's builders uncovered what they took to be the tomb of Christ, and in the 330s they raised a great church complex over it. From then on Jerusalem was something no other city was. Here the events of the gospel could be marked on the day and at the spot they were believed to have happened. Its clergy built a liturgy out of exactly that. Palm Sunday became an actual procession down the Mount of Olives with branches in hand. Good Friday brought the veneration of the Cross, and Easter an all-night vigil at the tomb. The crowds who came to see it wrote home about what they saw. One of those visitors was a woman from the Latin west named Egeria. In the early 380s she kept a detailed travel diary of exactly what was done and when. The manuscript disappeared for centuries. It was found again at Arezzo, in Italy, in 1883, and it remains the single most valuable eyewitness account of early Christian worship anywhere. What makes this community hard to pin down is the very thing that made it distinctive. It was always part settled and part passing through: resident clergy and monks, local Christians, and a constant flow of pilgrims from every corner of the Christian world.

## ambrosian-milan-standalone

Ambrosian Milan (standalone)

Milan, not Rome, was the western emperors' working capital in the late fourth century, and in 374 its people acclaimed as their bishop a provincial governor named Ambrose who had not yet even been baptized. He was baptized and consecrated within a week, and spent the next twenty-three years working out — mostly in public confrontations — what a bishop could say to power. He refused to hand a basilica over to the Empress Justina's court in 386, and his congregation occupied the building for days, singing hymns he wrote to keep their spirits up. In 390 he required the emperor Theodosius to do public penance for a massacre of civilians at Thessalonica. He was also a preacher who opened up Scripture for a restless African rhetorician sitting in his congregation — Augustine, whom Ambrose baptized at Easter in 387. And the city he shaped still carries his stamp in the rite that bears his name.

## aquileian-christianity

The Church of Aquileia

Aquileia stood at the head of the Adriatic, where the roads from Italy, the Danube and the Balkans met. In the fourth century it was among the largest cities of the empire, and its church was among the more consequential in the West. Its basilica floor mosaics from the 310s are still there. In the 370s a group of young ascetics gathered in the city, and Jerome later remembered them as a chorus of the blessed. He was one of them. So was Rufinus of Concordia. Thirty years later, the friendship between those two ended in the bitterest literary quarrel the Latin church produced. In 381 Ambrose came up from Milan to a council at Aquileia. The record names the city's own bishop, Valerian, first. Ambrose led the questioning. The council condemned two Homoian bishops of the Danube provinces, Palladius and Secundianus. Around 404 Rufinus, home in Italy after decades in Egypt and Palestine, sat down and wrote out the creed his own church used at baptism. He explained it line by line for people about to say it. That is why anyone knows two things about the Aquileian creed. It called the Father invisible and impassible, where Rome's did not. And it already had the clause about the descent into hell, which Rufinus points out, a little pointedly, that the Roman creed does not have. Rufinus's own words show that the Western churches did not all say the same words at the font.

## gallic-nicene-episcopate

The Gallic Nicene Episcopate

In 356 the emperor Constantius II, who wanted a single creed his bishops would all sign, exiled the bishop of Poitiers to Phrygia for refusing to condemn Athanasius. It was a miscalculation. Hilary spent four years in the Greek-speaking East learning the eastern debates from the inside, and wrote back to the Gallic bishops to explain them - De Synodis, a patient account of what the eastern creeds actually said and where they could and could not be met. He also wrote De Trinitate, a massive Latin answer to Arianism running to some 190,000 words. When he was allowed home around 360, the Gallic church repudiated the Homoian creed that had been imposed at Ariminum, at a council in Paris. Hilary went on to attack Auxentius, the Homoian bishop of Milan, with less success. He died in 367. The province he left behind was securely Nicene, Latin, and in possession of a theological vocabulary it had not had before - and a young ex-soldier named Martin, whom Hilary had ordained, was already living as a monk at Ligugé.

## gallic-monastic-ascetic-christianity

Gallic Monastic-Ascetic Christianity

About 360, a soldier named Martin, later bishop of Tours, told Caesar he was now a soldier of Christ, and asked to leave the army. He gathered a few companions near Poitiers, in western Gaul. Some years later, the people of Tours seized him and made him their bishop, and he kept the habits of a monk even in that office. His story survives because a wealthy Aquitanian lawyer, Sulpitius Severus, gave up his career, attached himself to Martin, and wrote his Life while he was still alive. Two other houses complete the picture. Around 410, Honoratus founded a monastery on a small island off the coast near Cannes, Lérins. Honoratus became bishop of Arles, and Hilary, who succeeded him there, wrote his Life; another monk formed on the island, Faustus, later became bishop of Riez. And around 415, John Cassian, who had spent years among the Egyptian monks, founded two houses at Marseilles and wrote down what he had learned there - the Institutes on how a monastery is run, the Conferences on what the Egyptian elders actually said. It was the monks of Marseilles who resisted Augustine's late teaching on grace and human effort. Augustine answered them in two treatises, after a third one - addressed to a different monastery, in Africa - had first provoked their objection.

## priscillianist-asceticism

Priscillianist Asceticism

In the 370s a Spanish layman named Priscillian began teaching a severe ascetic Christianity in the western provinces. It meant fasting, celibacy, and retreat to the countryside for study and prayer. Lay men and women read Scripture together, outside the bishops' oversight. It spread fast enough to alarm the bishops of Spain and Gaul. They condemned it at a council in Saragossa around 380. His supporters answered by making him bishop of Ávila. He went to Rome and Milan to clear himself, and was turned away by both bishops, Ambrose of Milan among them. His opponents called his teaching the heresy of the Gnostics, accused him of sorcery, and took the case to the emperor's court. Later churchmen likened it to Manichaeism. Around 385, at Trier, the usurping emperor Magnus Maximus had Priscillian and several companions tried and put to death. So far as anyone can tell, it was the first time a Christian state executed Christians over belief. It shocked people at the time. Martin of Tours had begged the emperor not to shed the blood of the accused. A secular ruler, he said, had no place judging a church case. The bishops who had pressed the charges were disgraced for it.

## pelagianism

Pelagianism

Pelagius was a British-born ascetic teacher. He came to Rome in the late fourth century and found the faith of the capital slack. People there made excuses for their sins, on the grounds that human nature simply could not do better. His answer was that God does not command the impossible. If Scripture tells you to be perfect, then you can be, and the excuse is a lie. Serious, high-minded Christians rallied to that belief and formed a movement. It included the combative Caelestius and, later, the bishop Julian of Eclanum. They pushed the argument into hard questions about infant baptism, inherited guilt, and whether grace is help or takeover. Augustine of Hippo fought them for the last twenty years of his life. The African councils condemned them in 418, and the Council of Ephesus did so again in 431. Almost all that Pelagius himself wrote survives only in fragments, in what his foes quoted, or misfiled for centuries under another man's name.

## homoian-arian-christianity

Homoian ('Arian') Christianity

For long stretches of the fourth century, the church the emperors backed was not the one that won in the end. The Homoians — from the Greek homoios, "like" — taught that the Son is like the Father according to the Scriptures. They refused to go further and call him of one being with the Father. Scripture never used that word, they said, and no council should force it on anyone. They did not call themselves Arians; that label came from their opponents. One of their own bishops put it the other way round: Arius had followed the bishops, not the bishops Arius. The emperors Constantius II and later Valens backed this position, and the councils of 359 and 360 adopted it. It stayed the imperial church's official position long enough for Jerome to write that the world groaned to find itself Arian. Its most lasting work was done outside the empire. Ulfila, made a bishop around 341, spent his life among the Goths. He made an alphabet for their language and translated the Bible into it. The Goths became Christians in this form, and the Vandals, Burgundians, and Lombards held it too. Jordanes, a sixth-century historian of the Goths, says they passed it on to "all people of their speech." Homoian kingdoms ruled Italy, Spain, and North Africa for generations after the empire itself had turned Nicene. The Visigoths of Spain went over to the Nicene creed at Toledo in 589. The Lombards of Italy were the last to follow, two generations later.

## anomoean-eunomian-christianity

Anomoean (Eunomian) Christianity

In the 350s a deacon at Antioch named Aetius began teaching the Arian case in its sharpest possible form. Others said the Son was like the Father, or like him in all things. Aetius said he was unlike him - anomoios - in essence. He argued it with a formal rigor that made his opponents call him a sophist and an atheist. His pupil and secretary Eunomius carried the position further. He gave it its most confident claim. God's essence, he said, is simply ingeneracy. This, he held, can be stated, and so a human being can know God's essence as God knows it himself. Eunomius was made bishop of Cyzicus in 360 and driven out within a year. The break with the Homoians began at Constantinople in the same year. Their council there confirmed the Homoian creed and deposed Aetius. Within a few years Eunomius and Aetius had broken away entirely, so to file the two parties together is to misread the dispute. Basil, Gregory of Nyssa and Gregory of Nazianzus wrote some of the most demanding theology of the century against these men, and against that claim about knowing God. The Council of Constantinople in 381 anathematized Eunomians by name in its first canon. Imperial law banned their meetings, and the sect faded through the fifth century.

## pneumatomachian-current

Pneumatomachian ('Macedonian') Christianity

By about 360 the debate had moved on. A body of churchmen in Constantinople and Asia Minor had come to accept, or nearly accept, that the Son is of one being with the Father. But they would not say the same of the Holy Spirit. They held that the Spirit was a creature, or at best a ministering spirit of some third order. Their critics called them Pneumatomachians, Spirit-fighters. The historian Socrates named them after Macedonius, a deposed bishop of Constantinople. But he also says the party was named after Marathonius of Nicomedia. Basil of Caesarea wrote On the Holy Spirit in about 375 to answer them, and he did it with care. He argued the Spirit's divinity mostly from how the church already prayed and baptized, rather than from a bare proof-text. Gregory of Nyssa wrote against them too. The Council of Constantinople in 381 settled the matter. It filled out the creed's third article, which until then had said almost nothing about the Spirit. The result is the clauses still said each week.

## apollinarian-christianity

Apollinarian Christianity

Apollinaris of Laodicea was on the right side of the Arian controversy. He was a friend of Athanasius, a defender of Nicaea, and by all accounts a formidable scholar. The position that took his name came from pressing the unity of Christ harder than anyone else dared. If Christ is one, Apollinaris reasoned, then there cannot be two minds competing in him. So the divine Word must take the place a human rational soul would hold. Christ has a human body and its animating life, but not a human mind. This view was condemned at a council in Rome in the 370s, and again by name at Constantinople in 381. Gregory of Nazianzus wrote the reply that stuck, in a letter to a priest named Cledonius: what is not assumed is not healed. If Christ did not take a human mind, then the human mind, the part that most needs it, is not saved. Apollinaris' followers kept his works in circulation under other men's names, which is why some of them survive at all.

## modalist-monarchianism

Modalist Monarchianism

Around the turn of the third century a way of protecting the oneness of God became briefly popular and then notorious. Noetus of Smyrna, and after him Praxeas and Sabellius at Rome, taught that Father, Son and Spirit are not three who are distinct but one who appears in three ways - so that the Son is the Father under another aspect. The objection was immediate and blunt: then it was the Father who was born, and the Father who died. Tertullian wrote Against Praxeas to say so, in a book that also gave Latin theology much of the vocabulary it would use for the opposite view. Hippolytus wrote against Noetus, and accused Callistus, the bishop of Rome, of sheltering the teaching - an accusation tangled up with his own rivalry for that see. Sabellius' name stuck to the position longest, and modalism is still sometimes called Sabellianism, though very little is known about him.

## bardaisanite-current

Bardaisan and the Bardaisanites

Bardaisan of Edessa was a courtier, an archer of some reputation, a philosopher, and by his own lights a Christian. He wrote against Marcion, and he was baptized in the Edessene church. He and his school made hymns. Ephraim later accused those hymns of sweetening poison for the simple, and he answered them with hymns of his own. What Bardaisan actually taught is hard to recover. The Book of the Laws of Countries was written by his pupil Philip. It shows him arguing that human freedom is real, against the fatalism of the astrologers. That is a thoroughly Christian argument. Ephraim wrote a century and a half later in the same city. He presents Bardaisan as a heretic with a cosmology of primordial beings and a flawed doctrine of the body's resurrection. Both cannot be the whole truth, and the sources that would settle it are gone.

## cyrilline-miaphysite-egyptian-christianity

The Church of Alexandria and Miaphysite Egypt

In 328 Athanasius became bishop of Alexandria. The Coptic church still counts him as its twentieth patriarch. He wrote and fought against the teaching of Arius. His foes had him deposed at Tyre in 335, and the Melitians, a rival church in Egypt, helped bring the charges.

Cyril became bishop in 412. He clashed with Nestorius, bishop of Constantinople, over a name for Mary. The name was Theotokos, the one who gave birth to God. In 431 a council met at Ephesus. Cyril's side began before the bishops from Syria came. They deposed Nestorius, and he was sent into exile.

In 451 the Council of Chalcedon said Christ must be confessed "in two natures". It also deposed Dioscorus, Cyril's successor, who would not come when called three times. In Egypt, clergy, monks and people broke with the bishop the council put in his place. From then on Alexandria had two lines of bishops. One was backed by the emperors. This page follows the other, the line most Egyptians kept.

The monks of the desert stood behind this line. In 457 its people asked the monks to help choose a bishop, and they chose Timothy, once a monk in the desert. Only one bishop could be found to consecrate him, so the people brought in a second, Peter the Iberian. After 518 Severus of Antioch, driven from Syria, lived in exile among them. Over these years Coptic, the language of Egypt, took a larger place beside Greek.

Who were the true heirs of Athanasius and Cyril? That is still disputed. Chalcedon itself took Cyril's letters as a guide to the faith. The Coptic church holds that Dioscorus kept that faith in the see of Saint Mark, and that the council broke it. Both sides claim the same teachers.

Arab armies took Egypt between 640 and 642. Benjamin, the patriarch who had hidden from the emperor's men, came back to Alexandria. The conqueror told him to govern his churches again, and the church went on under Muslim rule.

## syriac-orthodox-west-syriac-christianity

Syriac Orthodox (West Syriac) Christianity

After Chalcedon, the Christians of Syria and Mesopotamia who refused the council's formula lived inside the Roman Empire, as the Egyptian Miaphysites did. But they lost the throne of Antioch, and for long stretches under Justinian they had no legal standing at all. Their leader, Severus of Antioch, was driven from his see in 518. Like many of his bishops, he withdrew to Egypt and went on writing from exile. Their churches kept meeting, and their clergy kept being ordained, mostly because of Jacob Baradaeus. He was made a bishop at the request of the Ghassanid Arab king Harith, with the backing of the empress Theodora. For decades he crossed Syria and Mesopotamia, ordaining priests and bishops for a church the empire would not recognize. He would finish his work in a district in a day and a night, then move on thirty or forty miles. The bishops hunting him never caught him. John of Ephesus was himself a Miaphysite bishop and one of Justinian's own missionaries. He wrote down fifty-eight portraits of these people as it all took place. In 636 the Byzantines lost at Yarmouk, and with that the empire lost its power to enforce against them. What stood on its own was a separate church whose bishops and clergy had never stopped working.

## the-church-of-the-east-under-persia

The Church of the East under Persia

In 424 the bishops of the Persian Empire ruled that their catholicos at Seleucia-Ctesiphon answered to no bishop beyond their own frontier. This church lived under a Zoroastrian shah. It had taken the Nicene creed as its own in 410, but it would answer to no Roman bishop. Its answer to the question of Christ was not Chalcedon's, nor the Miaphysites'. It stood in the line of Theodore of Mopsuestia and held firmly that his divinity and his humanity were distinct. Its opponents fixed the name "Nestorian" on it. Its center of learning was the School of Nisibis. Written statutes there set out the students' day, their lodging and their discipline. Almost nothing else this early has left formation documents like them. Its communion reached far beyond Mesopotamia. In the 550s a traveler named Cosmas reported a bishop appointed from Persia at Kalyan, in western India. He also reported a church on the pepper coast further south. When the Sasanian empire fell to Arab armies by 651, the church carried straight on under new rulers and kept spreading east.

## armenian-christianity-after-avarayr

Armenian Christianity after Avarayr

In 451, while the Roman world's bishops met at Chalcedon, Armenia's nobles met the Persian army at Avarayr rather than accept an imposed return to Zoroastrian worship. They lost the battle and their commander, Vardan Mamikonian, but the resistance went on until Persia conceded the right to remain Christian in 484. And the Armenian church came to read that defeat as a covenant sealed in martyrdom rather than as a loss. Absorbed in that struggle, Armenia had sent no bishops to Chalcedon. When it did take up the council's formula it judged it too close to dividing Christ, and at the second council of Dvin in 554 the church formally set itself outside Chalcedon. By then it had a Bible and a liturgy in an alphabet barely fifty years old, and a body of historical writing — Elishe's and Lazar's accounts of the war. That fixed the story it has told about itself ever since.

## chalcedonian-monasticism-judean-desert-and-gaza

Chalcedonian Monasticism - Judean Desert & Gaza

In the century after Chalcedon, monks settled in the ravines between Jerusalem and the Dead Sea, and in the villages around Gaza. They became almost a second population. In the laurae, hermits lived apart through the week and gathered on Sundays. Shared houses held hundreds. This tradition stands out clearly because its own papers survive. Cyril of Scythopolis wrote carefully dated lives of Euthymius, Sabas and the men who came after them. Two recluses near Gaza, Barsanuphius and John, left roughly 850 letters. They answered monks, married couples, sick people and local officials, one question at a time. Most of the Judean houses came to back Chalcedon. Euthymius took the council's side. His biographer says nearly all the desert monks would have followed him, had a rival monk named Theodosius not turned many of them against it. Near Gaza, Peter the Iberian became bishop for those who rejected the council. The empire pulled the monks hard into its fights over doctrine. One was the purge of teaching drawn from Origen in the 550s, which split their own houses. In 614 the Persian army sacked Jerusalem and tore through the desert houses. Those who lived wrote down what was lost.

## early-benedictine-italian-monasticism

Early Benedictine / Italian Monasticism

Benedict of Nursia left Rome for a cave at Subiaco. Around 529 he ended up founding a monastery on the hill of Monte Cassino. Italy was being torn apart, first by the Gothic wars and then by the Lombard invasions. What he left behind was a rule: seventy-three short chapters on prayer, work, sleep, food, guests, discipline and the election of an abbot. Much of it, most scholars now think, was drawn from an older and harsher text, the anonymous Rule of the Master. Benedict cut it down and softened it, with an eye for what ordinary people could actually keep up. A generation later Gregory the Great, a monk before he was a pope, wrote the only account we have of Benedict's life. He also governed the Italian church through more than 850 letters, which show the same world from the other side. They tell of famine, plague, refugees, Lombard armies, and the daily running of a church trying to hold itself together.

## roman-church-gregorian

The Roman Church in the Age of Gregory the Great

The Rome Gregory was born into around 540 had been besieged, taken, retaken and emptied within living memory. The Gothic wars and Justinian's reconquest had wrecked the city and the countryside that fed it. From 568 the Lombards held most of the peninsula. The imperial government sat at Ravenna and had little left to spend on Rome. Gregory was city prefect and gave it up. He turned the family house on the Caelian into a monastery, and was sent to Constantinople as the pope's representative. He was elected bishop in 590, during a plague, and spent fourteen years doing a job that had quietly become something else. The letters show what it was. He ran the papal estates in Sicily as the city's food supply and audited the men who managed them. He paid soldiers and bargained with Lombard dukes when the exarch would not. He ransomed prisoners. He told bishops in Gaul, Spain, Africa and Illyricum what he thought they were doing wrong, at length. He refused the title of universal bishop when Constantinople's patriarch used it. He already called himself servant of the servants of God, a title Pope Damasus and Augustine had used before him. In 596 he sent a party of his own monks to Kent. Then he wrote to them again and again about what to do with pagan temples, and how far to insist on Roman custom. The Pastoral Rule, written at the start of all this, is the book underneath the rest. It argues that the government of souls is the art of arts, and that different people must be told the same truth in different ways. And it holds that to want the office is already to be unfit for it.
