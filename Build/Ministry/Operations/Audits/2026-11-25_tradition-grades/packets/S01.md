# Packet S01: tradition entries (census longDescription)

## tree/post-apostolic-house-church

Post-Apostolic Household-Church Christianity

In the generations following the apostles, the earliest Christian movement had no documented church buildings and no single leadership structure. They had the Old Testament. The gospels and letters of the apostles moved between communities as copies were made and passed along, though no fixed collection had been settled yet. It was a network of faithful households, scattered across Antioch, Asia Minor, and Rome, meeting wherever they could. New believers were taught the Two Ways on the road to baptism; only then were they brought into the fellowship to share the weekly breaking of bread and prayer. Across long distances, these household communities were held together by carried letters.

Some communities were guided by a single bishop alongside elders and deacons, others by a circle of elders alone—both forms were real, and neither was settled. The questions they wrestled with had no easy answers: How do we make room for genuine doubt? How should conflict within the community be resolved? What did women's leadership look like? Following Jesus routinely meant loss of social standing, suspicion from neighbors, and periodic waves of official violence.

In his surviving letters, Ignatius of Antioch describes writing to sister churches while being marched toward Rome under armed guard — soldiers he called 'ten leopards,' who, in his own words, 'show themselves all the worse' the more kindly he treats them. Justin Martyr, who told his imperial interrogator that he taught above a public bathhouse in Rome, wrote down the earliest record of what their Sunday actually looked like—the reading of the memoirs, the common loaf and cup, and the collection gathered for orphans, widows, and travelers. Justin was executed for refusing to abandon his Lord; tradition has it that Ignatius met the same end.

The earliest outside account of these communities comes from Roman authority, not from inside the movement. Around 112 CE, the governor Pliny the Younger wrote to the emperor Trajan describing an investigation: believers who had recanted told him, under threat of ordinary punishment, that these communities met before dawn to sing a hymn to Christ 'as God,' bound themselves by oath against theft and adultery, then met again later for a meal they called 'common yet harmless.' Still unsatisfied, Pliny had two enslaved women who held office among them — titled ministrae, deacons — questioned under torture. He learned, he wrote, only 'a superstition depraved and immoderate.' Their names do not survive; his letter is the earliest outside evidence that women held a formal title here at all.

## tree/alexandria-catechetical

Alexandrian Catechetical / Christian-Platonist Tradition

Alexandria was the ancient world's great city of libraries and lecture halls, and Christianity there took the same shape: a school. Teachers received inquirers, catechumens preparing for baptism, and educated pagans curious about this new philosophy, and walked them through Scripture as a text with a surface and a depth: the letter first, then the moral sense, then the spiritual meaning that opens onto the Logos himself.

Clement of Alexandria wrote for cultivated Greeks who wanted to know whether a Christian could keep his learning. Origen, who succeeded him, wrote commentaries across most of Scripture and produced the Hexapla, a six-column parallel edition of the Old Testament assembled to see what the Hebrew actually said — before falling out with his bishop and moving his teaching to Caesarea around 231. Historians still argue about how formal the whole thing was — whether Alexandria had one continuous institution with a succession of appointed heads, as Eusebius of Caesarea later described it, or a looser sequence of private Christian schools whose teachers happened to be remembered together. That tidy succession comes almost entirely from Eusebius: Clement's own writings never once describe a formal school that he headed.

The allegorical way of reading Scripture is this tradition's best-known signature, but it was never uncontested even at home. Nepos, a bishop out in the Egyptian countryside at Arsinoe, wrote a book refuting the allegorists and won real support for a plainer, literal reading — until Dionysius, by then bishop of Alexandria, spent three days in patient public conversation with Nepos's own community and brought them back around.

## tree/syriac-edessa-nisibis

Syriac Christianity (Edessa/Nisibis)

The first hard evidence of a Christian community at Edessa is a flood: a church building destroyed when the river overran its banks in 201, recorded in the city's own chronicle. Edessa's Christians told an older story about their beginning too—that King Abgar himself had written to Jesus and been answered—but no text earlier than the fourth century attests it, and it is best heard as the community's own cherished memory, not as history. What the record actually shows is plainer. They were a Christian people who spoke Syriac, close to the language Jesus himself spoke, and who read Scripture as a web of symbols answering each other: Eden answering the cross, the Jordan answering the font. They met the Gospel as one continuous story rather than four separate books, in a harmony a Greek-trained convert named Tatian had woven a generation earlier; Aphrahat quoted it and Ephrem preached from it for as long as this Christian tradition lasted.

At the community's ascetic heart stood the covenant: men and women who vowed themselves to lifelong celibacy while remaining inside their own congregation instead of leaving for the desert, keeping the fast and the vigil and singing what the community believed. Office and vow ran side by side for this whole tradition's life, and who finally spoke for it was never settled. Aphrahat, remembered simply as the Persian sage, once drafted a synodal letter in the name of a council of bishops, yet whether he held that office himself, no one now can say. Persia's own bishops fought the same question in the open: Papa of Seleucia-Ctesiphon claimed to lead them all, and was contested by his own colleagues in his own lifetime. The community also knew its faith by naming what it was not, answering by name the teachings of the philosopher Bardaisan, of Marcion, and of Mani, all preached in the same cities in the same tongue.

In 338, Shapur II of Persia besieged Nisibis; Ephrem, the city's own hymnwriter, remembered its deliverance as an act of God worked through ordinary defenders, while a later account added a swarm of gnats to the story. Bishop Jacob led that defense. The Chronicle of Edessa places his death that same year; Theodoret's own church history instead has him defending the city again in 350, and the record itself never closes the question. Nisibis held until 363, when the Roman emperor Jovian ceded it to Persia and Ephrem moved west to Edessa, where he spent his last year running famine relief before he died. On the Persian side, the persecution already underway since the 340s took bishop Simeon bar Sabbae and his clergy, and left the church's chief seat empty for twenty years, since filling it meant near-certain death. Not until 410, when the Roman bishop Marutha brokered a synod with the favor of Yazdegerd I, the Persian king, did the Persian church finally receive a single settled order—the last thing this tradition's own record has to say.

## tree/desert-monasticism

Desert Monasticism

By the early fourth century the last empire-wide persecution had ended, and Constantine's toleration closed off a path that had once been open to any Christian: dying for the faith. Antony, a young Egyptian landowner, gave away his family's land after hearing one Gospel reading in church — "if thou wouldest be perfect, go and sell that thou hast" — and withdrew by stages: the village's edge, an abandoned fort, then the desert's inner reaches, for the rest of his life. What his biographer Athanasius of Alexandria describes there is not escape but combat: the war once waged against a persecutor from outside, now fought against demons and the thoughts they whispered, from within. Teaching passed mostly in a single exchange — a younger monk asked an elder for "a word," and the elder answered in one sentence, no more. Generations later, those sentences were gathered into what other Christians would call the Sayings of the Desert Fathers and Mothers, arranged by the name of the elder who spoke them, not necessarily as anyone first heard them said.

Not everyone lived Antony's way. Around 320, a man named Pachomius founded the desert's first rule-governed community at Tabennesi in the Thebaid; by his death in 346 it had grown into a federation of nine houses for men and two for women, bound by a written rule and appointed offices rather than one elder's own example. Between the two lay a third pattern: loose settlements at Nitria, Kellia, and Scetis, where ascetics lived mostly alone but gathered weekly for worship and correction. These settlements sat closer to ordinary life than their own rhetoric of total withdrawal suggests: excavation at Kellia has uncovered at least one commercial center operating alongside the ascetic cells, real evidence of trade with the surrounding villages. Which of these actually held authority — a life earned through discipline, or an office conferred by a rule — was never settled. Even Pachomius, the rule's own founder, was remembered afterward less as an officeholder than as a father whose own example, not his rule alone, had formed his community.

What kept any of this from tipping into excess was discernment. It was the skill most prized of all: judging how much fasting and how much solitude one person could truly bear. The tradition's most common story shape is an eager newcomer who asks for an extreme practice and is steered toward something milder. Evagrius of Pontus was the most formally educated man the desert produced. He tried to build the struggle into a scheme of his own. He named eight kinds of tempting thought, and he drew a ladder that rises from practice through passionlessness to contemplation. He died at Kellia in January 399, just before a dispute his own learned circle could not survive. A bishop's letter on how literally to picture God split simple monks from learned ones. Soon after, Evagrius's circle was driven out of Egypt. His writings survived anyway, and part of them was handed down for centuries under another name.

## tree/donatism

Donatism

Caecilian was elected bishop of Carthage in 311 or 312. The sources support both years, and neither can be ruled out. He was consecrated by Felix of Aptungi. Felix was accused of handing over the Scriptures to be burned in the recent persecution. A rival party would not accept a bishop ordained by the hand of such a traditor. They made Majorinus bishop beside him instead, not in his place, and from Majorinus's successor Donatus the movement took its name. The doctrine at stake was narrow and absolute. A sacrament's validity rises and falls on the giver's own unbroken purity. So a tainted ordination gives nothing, and everyone it touches must be washed again by a hand that can be trusted. This was never a fight over the Trinity or whether Christ was God, for both sides held the same Nicene faith. The quarrel was over who could validly give what the church gives. For a century, across much of Roman Africa, this was simply the larger of the two churches. It had its own bishops, its own basilicas, and its own councils, and it fought its rival for the same towns.

The empire did not stay neutral. In 347, imperial commissioners came to force the two churches into one communion, and they met resistance in Numidia. A bishop named Marculus was seized, paraded through several towns, and thrown from a cliff called Novapetra. His church honored him and others killed with him as martyrs, and its own account of his death survives. A rural movement called the circumcellions appears in Optatus's account of these same years. An imperial law of 412 fined them as a class of their own, so they were a real, legally distinct group. But almost everything said about what they did comes from opponents, who had reason to make them look like a mob. The record will support that they existed and had a name. It will not cleanly support a picture of their character.

This church denied that any emperor had the standing to say which church was the true one. Yet it turned to imperial power three times when that served its case. It appealed to Constantine in 313 and to the emperor Julian in 361. In the 390s it turned the same machinery it condemned everywhere else against its own breakaway bishops, the Maximianists. In 411 the emperor finally ordered the whole dispute settled at law. Two hundred and seventy-nine of its bishops met roughly two hundred and eighty-six from the rival church before an imperial judge at Carthage. They refused the chairs he offered them and argued for three days. The verdict went against them, and the laws that followed took their churches by name. The sources say little about this church under the Vandals, but it was still there under Byzantine rule. In 535 Justinian's law for Africa barred Donatists from holding services. The last surviving word on it comes from Pope Gregory the Great. His letters of the 590s pressed for an inquiry into Donatist rebaptism in Numidia.

## tree/cappadocian-nicene-pastoral-monastic-tradition

Cappadocian Christianity

In Cappadocia and neighboring Pontus, from the years after the last empire-wide persecutions to the death of the last of a remarkable generation, one extended family and the friends around it did more than most to settle the words Christians still use about God — how settled that settlement really was is itself now a live scholarly question. Basil of Caesarea, his younger brother Gregory of Nyssa, their elder sister Macrina, and Basil's friend Gregory of Nazianzus argued the hardest question of the century — whether the Son and the Spirit are God in the same sense the Father is — while running dioceses, burying relatives, and feeding the hungry through famine.

Basil's own preaching indicted the region's grain-hoarders and moved them to open their stores. After the famine of 368, he built a complex outside Caesarea with a hospital, a hostel, and a workhouse. It was a genuine undertaking, with almost no precedent. But it was not the first of its kind anywhere, as later legend sometimes claims. Even before that, he had spent years answering a more radical ascetic ferment loose in the region. Households were dissolved, slaves were urged to leave their masters, and fasts were kept against the church's own feasts. He did not condemn the impulse. Instead, he gave it a rule, an obedience, and an open door for guests. Macrina turned the family estate into an ascetic household of her own. In her brother's account of her last days, she taught him theology from her deathbed. That account is the only record of her that survives; no words of her own reach us apart from his telling.

Gregory of Nazianzus carried the argument to Constantinople. There, in 381, the creed most Christians still say took its final shape. Those years were not always peaceful. This Christian tradition buried its own martyr, Eupsychius of Caesarea, who was put to death under the emperor Julian in 362. Under Valens, a hostile church party had Gregory of Nyssa thrown out of office. He lived in exile until 378. Epistle 38 is the letter most often used to show that the three men agreed on the technical terms. Yet even its author is in dispute. Modern scholars still argue over whether Basil or Gregory of Nyssa wrote it.

## tree/imperial-juridical-christianity

Imperial and Juridical Christianity

For nearly three centuries the church had been an illegal association. Then, between Constantine's toleration in 313 and Theodosius's legislation at the century's end, it became first protected, then privileged, then the empire's official religion — and had to work out, very fast, what to do with that. Bishops found themselves hearing civil lawsuits, administering public building funds, and traveling to councils at imperial expense; emperors found themselves summoning those councils and expecting the results to hold.

Three answers competed over where final authority in the church now lay, and this Christian tradition's own window closes without settling which was right. Rome traced its authority to Peter. Constantinople claimed rank because it sat beside the throne. The Council of Chalcedon extended that claim in 451, over the protest of Rome's own delegates. The same council also received a letter Leo of Rome had written two years before. It took that letter as the last word on Christ's two natures. Ambrose of Milan staked a third, quite different claim: the emperor stood inside the church, not over it. He said those exact words in 386, from inside his own basilica. Soldiers of a Homoian imperial court were besieging it, because the court wanted the building for its own worship. He would not give it up, and his congregation stayed inside with him. Augustine, then living in Milan, later wrote that they were 'prepared to die with their bishop'. He also wrote that at this time Milan's people began to sing hymns and psalms in the Eastern way. That custom, he says, lasted and spread to almost all congregations elsewhere. Four years later Ambrose proved the same claim again, against Theodosius himself. He refused the emperor the sacrament until he did public penance for a massacre at Thessalonica.

The hardest evidence against this tradition is not what was done to it, but what it did. In 366, a disputed election to the bishopric of Rome left the city so violent that a hundred and thirty-seven people were killed in a single day — the same Damasus whose reign then built the city's great martyr shrines. The same sacramental refusal that later humbled an emperor over Thessalonica had already been used once before, two years earlier, for the opposite end: protecting a bishop who had incited a mob to burn a synagogue at Callinicum from having to pay for it, and leaving the burned community no recourse at all.

## tree/hieronymian-ascetic-literary

Hieronymian Ascetic-Literary Christianity

In the early 380s, Jerome arrived in Rome. There he joined a circle of ascetic women, serious about Scripture, who already met around the widow Marcella on the Aventine. Paula, another widow, and her daughters Blaesilla and Eustochium belonged to the same circle. All of them took Jerome as their teacher, a caustic, brilliant scholar with a gift for making enemies. In 384 Blaesilla took up the severe fasting Jerome taught, and within four months she was dead. At her funeral, Jerome reports, people in the crowd muttered that the monks should be stoned or thrown in the Tiber. In his letter of comfort to the grieving Paula, he admits that he wrote the whole letter in tears.

Within the year Pope Damasus, Jerome's protector, was also dead, and his position in Rome had become impossible. Paula and Eustochium sold their estates, sailed east, and founded monasteries at Bethlehem — a house for men, houses for women, a hostel for pilgrims — with Jerome installed alongside to work. What came out of it was the Vulgate: a Latin Bible rendered book by book from the Hebrew rather than the Greek everyone was used to, against fierce criticism — in one North African town, a congregation nearly rioted when its bishop read the new wording of a single word in Jonah, and Augustine wrote across the sea to warn Jerome what unsettling a people's prayed-over text could cost.

Not one word that any of these women wrote in her own hand has survived. What is known of them comes only through Jerome's letters, or through others who describe letters now lost. In the community's last years, an attack tied to the Pelagian dispute set fire to the Bethlehem monasteries. Eustochium and her niece Paula sent Rome an account of it. Writing to the bishop of Jerusalem, Pope Innocent praised the "wonderful clemency" with which they had left the attacker's name untold.

## tree/gallic-monastic-ascetic-christianity

Gallic Monastic-Ascetic Christianity

About 360, a soldier named Martin, later bishop of Tours, told Caesar he was now a soldier of Christ, and asked to leave the army. He gathered a few companions near Poitiers, in western Gaul. Some years later, the people of Tours seized him and made him their bishop, and he kept the habits of a monk even in that office. His story survives because a wealthy Aquitanian lawyer, Sulpitius Severus, gave up his career, attached himself to Martin, and wrote his Life while he was still alive. Two other houses complete the picture. Around 410, Honoratus founded a monastery on a small island off the coast near Cannes, Lérins. Honoratus became bishop of Arles, and Hilary, who succeeded him there, wrote his Life; another monk formed on the island, Faustus, later became bishop of Riez. And around 415, John Cassian, who had spent years among the Egyptian monks, founded two houses at Marseilles and wrote down what he had learned there - the Institutes on how a monastery is run, the Conferences on what the Egyptian elders actually said. It was the monks of Marseilles who resisted Augustine's late teaching on grace and human effort. Augustine answered them in two treatises, after a third one - addressed to a different monastery, in Africa - had first provoked their objection.

## tree/lutheran-wittenberg-and-its-congregations

Lutheran Wittenberg & Its Congregations

Wittenberg was a small Saxon university town when one of its theology professors, an Augustinian friar named Martin Luther, wrote 95 theses against the sale of indulgences and sent them to the archbishop of Mainz on 31 October 1517. Tradition also holds that he nailed a copy to the door of the Castle Church, the university's usual notice board for an academic debate — but that detail comes only from an account written decades later, after Luther's death, and historians still disagree over whether it happened. Within a few years a movement had grown around him that reorganized worship, emptied monasteries, and put the Bible, a catechism and a hymnbook into ordinary German households. What that looked like from the pew is documented unusually well. From 1527-28 the Saxon churches sent visitors out to examine parish after parish, and their reports are frank about how little many villagers actually knew. The movement settled its own arguments in writing — the Augsburg Confession of 1530, then the Book of Concord of 1580. And the churches shaped by it spread north and east across Germany and Scandinavia, where they remain.

## tree/the-reformed-cities-zurich-and-geneva

The Reformed Cities - Zurich & Geneva

Two Swiss city-states, a generation apart, worked out what a whole town would look like if its church were rebuilt from Scripture: Zurich under Huldrych Zwingli from 1519, which stripped images and the Mass out of its churches. And Geneva after 1536 under John Calvin, whose Ecclesiastical Ordinances of 1541 gave the city pastors, teachers, elders and deacons, and a consistory that met every week. Geneva's consistory registers survive, and they are startlingly ordinary — quarrels between neighbors, absent husbands, people who had not been to church. Which makes this one of the best pictures anywhere of what a church actually did about the daily conduct of ordinary people. From these two cities the Reformed pattern traveled: Scotland, France, the Netherlands and English Puritanism all trace back here.

## tree/latin-pastoral-congregational-christianity

Latin Pastoral-Congregational Christianity

This is the congregational life of Latin-speaking North Africa. It is the week in, week out work of a bishop with a flock, and the hard disputes that pressed on that work. Cyprian became bishop of Carthage around 248. When the emperor Decius ordered everyone to sacrifice, he went into hiding. He came back to the hardest pastoral problem of the age. Thousands of his own people had complied, and now they wanted back in.

He organized relief through the plague that still bears his name. He told his congregation to nurse pagan neighbors as well as their own. He refused communion to the envoys of Novatian, a rival bishop at Rome. He argued with Stephen of Rome over baptism given outside the church. He was beheaded outside Carthage in 258.

A century and a half later, Augustine was bishop of Hippo Regius from 395 until his death in 430. He preached to the same congregation several times a week for thirty-five years, and stenographers took much of it down. Hundreds of those sermons survive. They argue, joke, scold and console actual people whose lives he knew.

His pastoral work ran through the great disputes of his day. A rival Donatist church stood in the same towns, and he argued with it for decades. He answered the Manichaeans, whom he had followed for nine years in his youth. In his last years he wrote against Pelagius and his followers on grace and human nature.

Between the two bishops lay the ordinary round. Catechumens were taught, and new believers were baptized at Easter. Penitents did public penance. The church cared for widows and the poor. And the bishop sat as judge in his own people's disputes.

## tree/kongo-christianity-afonso-to-1650

Kongo Christianity, Afonso I to 1650

In July 1526, Afonso I, the Christian king of Kongo, wrote to the king of Portugal. He asked for wine and flour for the Mass. Then he warned that his land was being emptied of its people, who were being carried off to be sold. Contact with Portugal had brought Kongo both the faith and this trade, and in his letters Afonso set the one against the other.

Portuguese ships reached the mouth of the Zaire River in 1491. A report written down a century later says Kongo's king was baptized and took the name Dom João, and that his son, who took the name Afonso, soon followed.

Afonso spoke for himself in letters his scribes wrote down, and they survive, while almost everything else about Kongo's Christians was written by Portuguese and Italian outsiders. In a letter to the pope in 1512, which a Portuguese chronicler later printed, Afonso said Portuguese religious men had led his people away from idols. In 1517 he wrote that he sent his kin to Portugal to learn the faith and bring light to the blind in his kingdom. He wanted them to uphold the faith after his death.

Not everyone at court welcomed the faith. The report says his brother Pango stood against him, and that the rule of one wife turned some nobles and their wives against it. It says Afonso lost his province on a charge that he wanted the throne, and later won it back. When the old king died, the report adds, Afonso's mother kept the death secret for three days so that he could reach the capital in time. Then, it says, Pango brought a great army against him, and Afonso defeated it and kept the throne.

By 1526, the letters turn to a harm that came with Portugal. Afonso wrote that merchants and thieves were carrying off his people every day, including the sons of nobles and his own kin. He asked for priests and teachers for his schools, and for no merchants at all. In his words: "It is our will that in these kingdoms there be no trade in slaves, and no outlet for them." His letter is a plea. It does not tell us what came of it.

In December 1540, fourteen years on, he wrote to Portugal of an attempt on his life. He said a friar had arranged for seven or eight white men to fire at him during Easter Mass. The shot passed by the skirts of his robe, he said. It killed one man and wounded two. That charge rests on his word alone.

He wrote that letter as he wrote the others, calling the king of Portugal his brother and signing himself king.

## tree/kirishitan-japan

Kirishitan Japan

A Japanese Christian stood before a governor who was leading an inquiry against the Christians. The governor told him that to follow a faith the lord had forbidden was a capital crime, and that he could still be safe if he gave it up. The man, Jean Ingoro, answered that nothing could give him more joy than to shed his blood for God. The governor said: "I have never seen anyone as happy as you seem to be."

Ingoro was one of Japan's Christians. Their church began in 1549, when Francis Xavier landed at Kagoshima and wrote from there that November. By the early 1600s its members faced prison, exile and execution. A few of them wrote about it in their own words. Most of their letters went to Jesuit priests, and missionaries chose them and printed them in their own books. A French editor translated them in 1870. So we hear these few Christians through the hands of the mission.

Asked where such joy came from, Ingoro told the governor how he had come to believe. He had read the writings of the Japanese sects, and he found nothing in them about a Creator. He heard the preachers and argued with them many times, and he thought no priest could stand up to him. Then a Christian came back from Korea to the town where Ingoro was living. He converted most of its people and nobles, and Ingoro with them, by his own account the most stubborn of all. The governor answered that he did not know whether the missionaries could be trusted, and he had Ingoro jailed.

His letter stops there. A note printed after it adds that the governor accused him of stealing the bodies of four crucified Christians, among them little Luis, to sell them at Nagasaki. Ingoro answered that he had only gathered bones that had fallen, to bury them with Christian rites.

Michel Fieoyemon wrote from prison in January 1609. He had been there four years for confessing the name of God, and had borne trials without number, of every kind. He wrote: "I have borne them with such keen joy and comfort that I seem to have been a prisoner for only one day."

In May 1614, a group of Christians sailed into exile to Tsugaru. The lord of the province, in the name of the country's ruler, ordered them to clear unworked land. They answered that they would obey in everything if they could keep their faith. In their letter to the priests, they asked that one of them visit the next year, though they knew it could not be this year.

An outsider saw the same years. Richard Cocks, an English trader at Hirado, heard in August 1615 that the king of Spain was sending the emperor a gift for his kindness to Christians. The king little knew, Cocks wrote, that the emperor had already banished all the friars, monks, Jesuits and priests.

One more exchange survives. A woman named Maria heard it said that her husband, Jean Pioyemon, had been condemned to die. She asked him to send her his reliquary, a small case for holy relics, and some of his hair to remember him by. She hoped to be there at his death, to say a last farewell. He answered that to die as a martyr was far above what he deserved. He feared he might be set free instead, and he asked at least to have his fingers cut off for God, as other Christians had at Suruga. He would gladly exchange reliquaries with her, he wrote, but she was not to speak of his hair. If by God's grace he were put to death, she would then receive the great gift she asked of him.

## tree/china-mission-born-christianity

Mission-Born Christianity in Ming China

In the Ming court, a Chinese minister wrote to the emperor in defense of foreign priests. He called them, in the translator's English, "worthies and sages," the words China used for its own great teachers. If they were guilty, he wrote, then so was he.

The minister was Xu Guangqi, known in English as Paul Su. He was one of the scholars and officials whom Jesuit missionaries had won to Christianity in Ming China. He argued for the faith in the words of China's own teachers. His memorial is a rare convert's own voice, though it reaches us in an English translation of 1850, and its wording is the translator's.

The mission's own story was written by the Jesuit Nicolas Trigault, from Matteo Ricci's own writings. In September 1583, it tells, Ricci and a fellow Jesuit, Michele Ruggieri, reached the city of Zhaoqing. Ruggieri had stayed there once before and been sent away. Kneeling before the governor, they asked for a place to build a house and a church. He answered kindly, and told them to look about the city for a place.

The memorial answers a report from the Board of Rites, made in 1616 or 1617. The Board had impeached the Jesuits at court. Its report said their teaching was spreading, so that even men of eminence were believing in it.

Xu did not deny it. He wrote that he had talked with the Jesuits about religion for years. He said they had come thousands of miles because they heard that China's own teachers served Heaven through virtue, as theirs served the Lord of Heaven. He called Buddhism false, since it had not reformed hearts in eighteen hundred years. In his view the Jesuits' teaching could repair and strengthen the royal laws and the learning of the scholars, and even correct the Buddhists.

Then he proposed three tests. The Jesuits' books should be translated, and the emperor's ministers should judge whether they were sound. The Jesuits should debate the leading Buddhists and Taoists in writing, and scholar-officials should decide. A summary of their teaching should go before the emperor himself. If the teaching proved wicked, he wrote, the Jesuits should be sent away, and he would accept the punishment due to those who helped deceive the emperor.

Near the end, he wrote that for peace and good government, "nothing can surpass this that is taught by your majesty's courtiers." The courtiers were the Jesuits; he called them the emperor's own. If the Board had its way, he wrote, these men must at once return to their own countries. He had fasted and washed, he said, before he dared to lay their case before the throne.

## tree/new-spain-and-andes-mission-born-christianity

Mission-Born Christianity in New Spain and the Andes

In 1536, a Franciscan friar in Mexico wrote down a number. "I believe more than four million souls were baptized from the time the land was won until the time I write this." The land had been won in 1521. The friar was Toribio de Benavente, called Motolinía.

He wrote the mission's story from the inside. Twelve friars, he says, left Spain in January 1523 for the land of Anahuac, now called New Spain. They came for the conversion of its native peoples. We hear the people they baptized only through the friars' pens.

The land the friars reached had already been struck. Smallpox came with a sick man on a Spanish ship, and in most provinces, Motolinía says, more than half the people died. War killed many others, above all around Mexico City. He counts ten plagues in all. After smallpox and war came hunger, and then the weight of Spanish rule: tribute, slavery and forced work in the gold mines. Many houses, he wrote, stood empty.

Motolinía read this as God's work. He wrote that God had killed many of the lords who would not accept the new law, through those same plagues. Their sons, baptized as small children, then inherited their lands. That is his own reading.

He also told a smaller scene. Friars visited two provinces from their house at Cuauhnahuac, and people came from the small town to the larger one to hear them and to have their children baptized. One night, heavy rain turned a stream into a river. The friars preached from one bank to the people on the other, and the people would not go home until their children were baptized. So they made a poor raft of canes. Half in the water and half in their arms, they carried the children across, and the friars baptized them with great effort, he says, because there were so many.

His counts are his own, and they do not agree. In one book he gives three totals for the same fifteen years: more than four million, close to five million, and more than nine million. "In my judgment, and in truth, more than nine million souls of Indians will have been baptized in the time I speak of." The numbers count baptisms. They do not tell what the people believed.

A Jesuit, José de Acosta, wrote about the Indians of Peru and Mexico some fifty years later, around 1590. He said many of their customs resembled the law of Moses, and some came near the Gospel. In Peru, he wrote, people confessed their sins aloud and washed in water to cleanse them. The Mexica even had a kind of baptism of newborn children. He judged these customs mixed with great abomination, and he named the devil's pride as the cause of their idol worship. But some priests told him that after the Christians came to Peru, people there confessed even their thoughts.
