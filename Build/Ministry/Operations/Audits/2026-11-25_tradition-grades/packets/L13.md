## seventh-day-adventist-church-twentieth-century

The Seventh-day Adventist Church in the 20th Century

Ellen White died on 16 July 1915, and the movement she had helped found carried on without the person whose writings had shaped it — which is itself one of the plainest facts about it. Its confession settled in public over the following decades. In 1931 the church's yearbook carried a statement of twenty-two beliefs, explicitly trinitarian; in 1980 the General Conference session at Dallas adopted the Fundamental Beliefs, twenty-seven of them, restating the same. Between those two documents the church became a genuinely global body, and it kept documenting itself with the thoroughness it had shown since the beginning: General Conference minutes, yearbooks, statistical reports, periodicals. Its own internal arguments, including the long controversy that followed the book Questions on Doctrine, are on that same public record rather than hidden in it. In 1911 Ferdinand and Ana Stahl joined Manuel Camacho at Utawilaya, in Platería district, Puno province, on the Peruvian side of Lake Titicaca. Camacho, an Indigenous visionary, was one of the first Adventist converts on the altiplano. He had opened a free school in his home about 1902. The Stahls lived in an adobe building that served as a school on weekdays and a place of worship on Sabbaths.

## charismatic-renewal-and-the-jesus-movement

Charismatic Renewal & the Jesus Movement

On 3 April 1960 Dennis Bennett told his congregation in Van Nuys, California, that he had spoken in tongues. The announcement is conventionally taken as the start of the charismatic movement inside the historic Protestant churches, and it went where the earlier Pentecostal revival had not: into mainline Protestant parishes, whose members mostly stayed where they were. Bennett published his own account, Nine O'Clock in the Morning, in 1970. Alongside it and overlapping it came the Jesus movement, roughly 1967 to 1972, which reached young people outside the churches altogether and brought a great many of them in — Calvary Chapel, begun in 1965, became its best-known institutional home, and the music that came out of it changed what congregational worship sounded like. The Vineyard association formed in 1982, at the edge of this window, as the wave handed on to what came next.

## the-ecumenical-movement-and-the-wcc

The Ecumenical Movement & the World Council of Churches

The World Missionary Conference at Edinburgh in 1910 left three currents running, and over the next decades they came together. The International Missionary Council was founded in October 1921. Life and Work, which dealt with the churches' common action in society, took shape in 1925. Faith and Order, which dealt with the teachings that divided them, followed in 1927. The last two merged into the World Council of Churches. It was formed at Amsterdam on 22 August 1948 with 147 member churches, and the missionary council joined it at New Delhi in 1961. It was a council of churches, not a church. It had no power over its members and no creed of its own, and each member kept its own. Orthodox churches were members. The Roman Catholic Church was not. In 1928 Pope Pius XI ruled that Catholics could not take part. After the Second Vatican Council, Rome worked with the council in other ways. In the Middle East, the Middle East Council of Churches held its first assembly at Nicosia from 28 to 30 May 1974.

## the-catholic-church-pius-x-to-the-council

The Catholic Church — Pius X to the Eve of the Council

In 1907 Rome moved against what it called modernism: the decree Lamentabili and the encyclical Pascendi condemned it, and in 1910 an anti-modernist oath was required of clergy and teachers. Alfred Loisy and George Tyrrell were at the center of it. The argument was the same one about history, scripture and doctrine that would break American Protestantism apart in the 1920s, running inside Catholicism a generation earlier. Then it turned. Divino Afflante Spiritu, in 1943, reopened the biblical scholarship Pascendi had closed. The worker-priest experiment sent clergy into ordinary workplaces. The nouvelle théologie went back behind the manuals to the fathers and to the liturgy. All of that, contested at the time, became the seedbed of the council. And there were the wars, including the record of Pius XII, which has a historiography of its own and was argued about for decades afterward.

## american-orthodoxy-autocephaly-and-the-convert-wave

American Orthodoxy — Autocephaly & the Convert Wave

Orthodoxy came to North America with immigrants and stayed, for most of a century, an affair of ethnic parishes, each answering to a church somewhere else. In 1970 the Orthodox Church in America received autocephaly, and an immigrant church became, at least in part, an American one. Then something less expected: in 1987 the Evangelical Orthodox Church — seventeen parishes and around two thousand people, formed by former evangelical leaders — was received into the Antiochian Archdiocese. Peter Gillquist told that story in Becoming Orthodox in 1989. A convert literature grew with it, and Ancient Faith Radio began broadcasting in 2004. How large the convert stream was rests on parish reports and journalism, since no independent national count exists.

## hebrew-roots-and-torah-observance-movements

Hebrew Roots & Torah-Observance Movements

From the 1990s a largely gentile current of Christians took up Torah observance. They kept the Sabbath and the biblical feasts in congregations, in Torah study groups and, above all, online. It was not Messianic Judaism, a movement of Jewish believers, and both traditions drew that line in their own writing. Its best-documented body was First Fruits of Zion, founded in 1992, and its own teaching changed in public. In 2009 Boaz Michael and D. Thomas Lancaster set out a shift in Messiah Journal. They moved away from 'One Law' toward what they called Divine Invitation. The One-Law side answered in print. That exchange is the clearest dated record of the argument inside this current. Digital ministries followed, 119 Ministries among them from around 2010. Founding dates for many of them simply cannot be established. An older current kept the Sabbath and the feasts as well. This was the Sacred Name movement of the 1930s. It began among members of the Church of God (Seventh Day), a Sabbath-keeping body with Adventist roots. Its magazine The Faith dates from 1937. How far the newer current drew on it is not clear.

## the-christian-right

The Christian Right

Jerry Falwell, Tim LaHaye, Charles Stanley, D. James Kennedy and Greg Dixon founded the Moral Majority on 6 June 1979. It named four principles of its own: it was pro-life, pro-family, pro-morals and pro-American. From its base in Washington, it worked in politics for those aims. In August 1980 Falwell called it entirely political, not a religious group. Its backers, he said, were not all evangelicals. That month Ronald Reagan spoke in Dallas, at a briefing run by another group, the Religious Roundtable. He told the crowd that he endorsed them and what they were doing. The Moral Majority closed in 1989. That same year Pat Robertson founded the Christian Coalition, with Ralph Reed as its executive director. Reed went on to found the Faith and Freedom Coalition in 2009.

## iglesia-ni-cristo

Iglesia ni Cristo

Iglesia ni Cristo, 'the Church of Christ', was registered with the government of the Philippines on 27 July 1914. It was registered under Felix Y. Manalo, and its own history presented him as God's messenger for the last days. It honored Jesus as Lord and Savior but taught that he was not God. From February 1939 its magazine Pasugo, in Tagalog and English, set out its teaching. It passed through two changes of leader, in 1963 and 2009. By its own counts it was, by 2010, the largest church founded in the Philippines. No independent count is cited here, and independent scholarship on the church is thin.

## the-anglican-communion-twentieth-century

The Anglican Communion in the Twentieth Century

The bishops of the Anglican churches met in the Lambeth Conference in 1920, 1930, 1968 and 1998, among other years. In 1920 they sent out an Appeal to All Christian People. It named the Scriptures, the Nicene Creed, the two sacraments and a shared ministry as the ground of a future unity. In 1930 they voted 193 to 67 on limiting or avoiding parenthood. Where a couple clearly felt a moral obligation to do so, they said, the method must be decided on Christian principles. In 1968 Lambeth proposed an Anglican Consultative Council. It first met at Limuru, Kenya, in 1971. Over the century most of the Communion came to live outside the West. In 1998 the bishops passed a resolution on human sexuality. In June 2008 the first Global Anglican Future Conference (GAFCON) met in Jerusalem. It issued the Jerusalem Declaration.

## the-rescue-mission-movement

The Rescue Mission Movement

In 1872 Jerry McAuley opened a mission on Water Street in New York. Others followed. In 1877 Colonel George Clarke and his wife Sarah opened the Pacific Garden Mission in Chicago, it seems with no tie to McAuley. It aimed to be 'a harbor for wrecked and ruined lives'. In 1906 mission leaders in New York formed a National Federation of Gospel Missions. On 17 September 1913 a wider body followed, the International Union of Gospel Missions, with about fifty missions. The historian Norris Magnuson counts the rescue missions among the revival-based welfare movements of 1865 to 1920. He argues that their welfare work came from their commitment to evangelism and personal holiness. By 1972 the union counted some 350 members. In 2000 it became the Association of Gospel Rescue Missions.

## the-christian-homeschool-movement

The Christian Homeschool Movement

Sociologist Mitchell Stevens traced one root of homeschooling to the Christian day-school movement of the 1960s and 1970s. Alan Peshkin spent eighteen months in one such school, a Baptist school in Illinois, in the late 1970s. He called it a 'total world'. Raymond and Dorothy Moore's Better Late Than Early (1975) argued that formal lessons should wait until a child was older. Around 1982 James Dobson interviewed Raymond Moore about the book on the air. Some families who heard the broadcast began to teach their children at home. In March 1983 two homeschooling fathers, the lawyers Michael Farris and J. Michael Smith, launched the Home School Legal Defense Association with their wives. At the time, homeschooling was legal in only a handful of states. A reviewer of Stevens's study summed the movement up as 'a woman's enterprise, in which men are usually junior partners'.

## the-liturgical-movement

The Liturgical Movement

In 1909 Dom Lambert Beauduin spoke at the National Congress of Catholic Action in Malines, Belgium. Historians date the popular liturgical movement from that address. In 1926 Virgil Michel of Saint John's Abbey in Minnesota founded the journal Orate Fratres, the movement's voice. He also set up the Liturgical Press. Keith Pecklers showed how closely its American leaders tied worship to social justice in the 1930s and 1940s. In England, Gabriel Hebert published Liturgy and Society in 1935. The Church Times later called it 'the Bible of the Parish Communion Movement'. Hebert's collection The Parish Communion (1937) set out a practice that changed the pattern of Sunday-morning worship.

## the-third-wave-and-the-vineyard

The Third Wave & the Vineyard

In 1982 John Wimber and C. Peter Wagner began a course called 'Signs and Wonders' at Fuller Theological Seminary in Pasadena. It was known as MC510. The course ran until 1985, and it drew sharp debate. Fuller's teachers dropped it in early 1986. In 1987 they put out their own study of it, Ministry and the Miraculous. Wagner called the stream 'the Third Wave'. Its people believed that the Holy Spirit still gave power and gifts. But in Wagner's view they did not take on Pentecostal or charismatic beliefs or ways of worship. Wimber set out his method in Power Evangelism in 1985. In 1994 a revival called the Toronto Blessing began at the Toronto Airport Vineyard. In December 1995 the Vineyard association cut its ties with that church. In 1997 Margaret Poloma wrote a study of the revival. Jon Bialecki later studied 'the Third Wave' by name, and the Vineyard as 'an American charismatic movement'.

## promise-keepers-and-the-christian-mens-movement

Promise Keepers & the Christian Men's Movement

On 20 March 1990, Bill McCartney, the University of Colorado football coach, drove with a friend to Pueblo. From that trip grew a group of about seventy men who called themselves Promise Keepers. In 1991, 4,200 men met at the university's basketball arena. In 1996, 22 stadium rallies drew nearly 1.2 million men. Each man made seven promises, published in 1994 as Seven Promises of a Promise Keeper. They pledged purity, strong marriages, and friendship across racial and denominational barriers. On 4 October 1997 a crowd estimated at about a million men gathered in Washington, D.C., for Stand in the Gap. In March 1998 the ministry began laying off 345 paid staff. Sociologist John Bartkowski later traced the rise and fall of this Christian men's movement.

## the-catholic-church-in-africa-twentieth-century

The Catholic Church in Africa in the Twentieth Century

On 25 May 1939 Joseph Kiwanuka was named vicar apostolic of Masaka, in Uganda. Pius XII consecrated him in Saint Peter's on 29 October that year. In 1956 a group of priests published Des prêtres noirs s'interrogent ('Black priests ask themselves questions'). Its writers included A. Abblé, A. Kagame, V. Mulago and M. Hebga. On 31 July 1969 Paul VI closed a symposium of Africa's bishops at Kampala. In his homily he told them they could, and must, have an African Christianity. In Zaire a trial text of the Mass came into use in 1974. Rome approved the Roman Missal for the Dioceses of Zaire on 30 April 1988, and Cardinal Joseph-Albert Malula was its chief promoter. In Katanga, in the Belgian Congo, the Franciscan Placide Tempels began the Jamaa movement. Its name is Swahili for 'family', and Johannes Fabian studied it in 1971 as a charismatic movement. In Rwanda the churches lived through the genocide of 1994. Timothy Longman argued that the churches had aligned with the state, and that church leaders backed the genocide to protect their own power. His thesis is contested. A special assembly of the Synod of Bishops for Africa met in Rome from 10 April to 8 May 1994. John Paul II signed its closing exhortation, Ecclesia in Africa, at Yaoundé on 14 September 1995.

## the-church-struggle-against-apartheid

The Church Struggle against Apartheid

This story opens with the Cottesloe Consultation, which met in Johannesburg from 7 to 14 December 1960. In September 1985 a group who called themselves the Kairos Theologians issued the Kairos Document. It was made public on 25 September. Its full title called it a theological comment on the political crisis in South Africa. The number of people who signed it differs from one edition to another. In October 1982 a synod of the Dutch Reformed Mission Church, meeting at Belhar, accepted a draft confession. The church adopted it as a confession in 1986. Its first article confesses the triune God, who through his Word and Spirit gathers, protects and cares for his church. The movement ended with apartheid in 1994.

## sudanese-christianity-through-the-civil-wars

Sudanese Christianity through the Civil Wars

Roland Werner, William Anderson and Andrew Wheeler wrote the standard history, Day of Devastation, Day of Contentment (2000). It runs to 688 pages and covers the Sudanese church across two thousand years. Marc Nikkel studied the conversion of the Dinka people in Dinka Christianity (2001). His book grew from his Edinburgh doctorate of 1993. Both books appeared in the Faith in Sudan series, from Paulines Publications Africa in Nairobi. Historians usually trace the first civil war to the Torit mutiny of 1955. Øystein Rolandsen says full civil war began only in late 1963. In March 1964 the government expelled foreign missionaries. The order began in the three southern provinces. The government said their activities threatened Sudan's integrity and stability. The war that began in 1983 ended with the peace agreement of 9 January 2005. Jesse Zink has told how many Dinka people embraced Anglican faith in that war.

## latin-american-catholicism-twentieth-century

Latin American Catholicism in the Twentieth Century

In Mexico the Calles Law took effect on 31 July 1926. That day the bishops suspended public worship. Catholics of the National League for the Defense of Religious Liberty, founded in March 1925, took up arms. So did rural fighters called cristeros. The settlement of June 1929, the arreglos, brought an amnesty and the non-enforcement of the law. Jean Meyer told the war's history in the three volumes of La Cristiada (1973-74). On 21 May 2000 John Paul II canonized 25 of its martyrs, Cristóbal Magallanes and his companions. From 25 July to 4 August 1955 Latin America's bishops held their first General Conference, at Rio de Janeiro. There they set up CELAM, their continental council, under Pius XII. Its seat was in Bogotá. Its first president was Cardinal Jaime de Barros Câmara. The second General Conference met at Medellín, Colombia, in August and September 1968. Its document read the church's task in Latin America in the light of the Council. One chapter was titled 'Poverty of the Church'. The third General Conference met at Puebla, Mexico, in 1979. Leonidas Proaño was bishop of Riobamba, Ecuador, from 1954 to 1985. He came to Riobamba on 29 May 1954 and retired on 29 January 1985. Under him, projects for Indigenous people opened in Chimborazo province. One was the Centro de Estudios y Acción Social ('Center for Social Studies and Action'), in 1960. The Escuelas Radiofónicas Populares del Ecuador ('Popular Radio Schools of Ecuador') began on 19 March 1962. Latin American scholars began to write this church's history themselves. One group of them, CEHILA, was founded in 1973 under Enrique Dussel. From 1981 it published a general history of the church in Latin America.

## protestantism-in-latin-america

Protestantism in Latin America

The story starts before the Panama congress. James Thomson, an agent of the British and Foreign Bible Society, arrived at Buenos Aires on 6 October 1818. Soon after, he proposed the Lancasterian system of schools to the government. By 1820 the magistrates had engaged him to put the schools of the city and its neighborhood on that plan. In 1821 he sailed round Cape Horn to Chile. There he wrote that he was in every way the servant of the Society. His letters were printed in London in 1827.

The Panama congress met from 10 to 20 February 1916. A committee formed in 1913 organized it. It drew 304 delegates and visitors from 21 nations. Few of them were Latin Americans. Its reports came out in three volumes in 1917. In November 1969 the first Latin American Congress on Evangelization, called CLADE, met in Bogotá. In 1970 the Latin American Theological Fraternity met for the first time, at Cochabamba in Bolivia. Churches met at Huampaní in Peru in 1977 and at Oaxtepec in Mexico in 1978. They meant to form a council of their own. That council, CLAI, was set up at Huampaní in 1982. One source gives 1983. A more conservative body, CONELA, formed as its alternative. Virginia Garrard-Burnett found that Protestants in Guatemala won few converts until the 1960s. Then military power and violence spread. They weakened people's old ties. Indigenous evangelicals in Ecuador organized themselves too. Julián Guamán is an Indigenous author from Ecuador. He dates the start of that organizing to Chimborazo in 1966. The national council of these evangelicals, FEINE, was founded in 1980. Guamán counts more than 1,500 local congregations among them. Susana Andrade studied why Indigenous people in Chimborazo became Protestants. Her book of 2004 sets out to show how Pentecostalism there took on a Quichua form. It took back themes from Indigenous religion. The themes include prayer, the interpretation of dreams and baptism in lagoons.

## korean-protestantism-twentieth-century

Korean Protestantism in the Twentieth Century

On 17 September 1907 the first presbytery of an independent Presbyterian Church in Korea met. Its 78 members were 38 missionaries and 40 Korean elders. It ordained seven Korean pastors, that year's graduates of the seminary in Pyongyang. It also took up the Twelve Articles of Faith, at first for a year of study. The articles came from the Presbyterian Church in India, which had drawn them up in 1904. In 1938 the Pyongyang seminary was shut down, because it had opposed the shrine worship that Japan enforced. In the 1950s the Presbyterian church divided into several churches, among them Kosin, Kijang, Hapdong and Tonghap. Kijang separated in 1953 over its progressive theology, especially its view of Scripture. Timothy Lee's Born Again (2010) tells the century as a story of evangelicalism in Korea.

## chinese-catholicism-twentieth-century

Chinese Catholicism — the First Chinese Bishops to the Divided Church

In 1926 Pope Pius XI consecrated Chinese bishops in Saint Peter's Basilica. His successor Pius XII later recalled them, in Pius XI's words, as "the flowers and . . . latest buds of the Chinese episcopate". On 18 February 1946 Thomas Tian Gengxin became the first Chinese cardinal. On 11 April 1946 Pius XII set up a full hierarchy in China, of 137 dioceses and prefectures in 20 regions. Chinese clergy led 28 of them. From 17 July to 2 August 1957 the authorities held a national assembly of Catholic representatives in Beijing. It approved the statutes of the Chinese Catholic Patriotic Association. In 1958 Pius XII wrote that missionaries had been driven out, and that bishops, priests and laypeople had been imprisoned. He ruled that bishops elected and consecrated against Rome's orders had no power to teach or to govern. In May 1980 a national assembly under state control set up a bishops' conference. Rome judged such bodies incompatible with Catholic teaching.

## philippine-catholicism-twentieth-century

Philippine Catholicism in the Twentieth Century

This entry opens in 1906. That is where the story of the Aglipayan schism ends. Under Ferdinand Marcos the Catholic Church and the state clashed. Robert Youngblood followed that clash in Marcos against the Church (1990). He tied it to the regime's drive for economic growth. He tied it to its political repression too. That rule ended. Afterward the Filipino Jesuit Antonio Moreno studied the church's part in civil society. His book of 2006 gathered "narratives of engaged citizenship". El Shaddai grew up within the same church. Katharine Wiegele studied it as a change in popular Catholicism. That means the Catholic faith as ordinary people live it.

## philippine-independent-church-twentieth-century

The Philippine Independent Church in the Twentieth Century

The Iglesia Filipina Independiente was proclaimed in 1902, and its birth is told with Philippine Catholicism to 1906. Its founder, Gregorio Aglipay, and other priests had left Rome because they would no longer accept foreign control of the church. Aglipay later took up Unitarian teaching, and published pamphlets defending it. The Episcopal Church's missionary bishop in the Philippines, Norman Binsted, was told that no more than five percent of its people ever accepted that teaching. In October 1946 the church's Supreme Bishop, Isabelo de los Reyes Jr., met Binsted. On 4 and 5 August 1947 its Supreme Council of Bishops and its General Assembly adopted a Declaration of Faith. Both votes were unanimous. The Declaration confessed the Holy Trinity, as the Apostles' and Nicene Creeds contain it. The church then asked the Episcopal Church in the United States to give its bishops the apostolic succession. In 1961 the two churches approved a concordat of full communion.

## japanese-christianity-after-1906

Japanese Christianity after 1906

In wartime, the Kyodan later wrote, the government demanded that religious bodies unite and cooperate with the war effort. Most Japanese Protestants had long wanted a single evangelical church. Their leaders used the demand to enter a church union, and the Kyodan was formed. It marked its twenty-fifth anniversary in October 1966. On 26 October 1954 its Eighth General Assembly enacted a Confession of Faith. It called the Bible "the unerring standard of faith and life", and it confessed the Apostles' Creed. On Easter Sunday, 26 March 1967, its moderator, Suzuki Masahisa, issued a confession on the church's wartime responsibility. The church, it said, "should not have aligned itself with that war effort". It asked forgiveness from the peoples of Asia and from their churches. Beside the Kyodan grew Japan's own Christian movements, which Mark Mullins studied in Christianity Made in Japan (1998).
