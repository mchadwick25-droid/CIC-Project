## the-russian-orthodox-church-council-patriarchate-and-reunion

The Russian Orthodox Church — Council, Patriarchate and Reunion

A Local Council of the Russian church sat in 1917 and 1918. On 4 November 1917 it restored the patriarchate and ruled that a Patriarch would head the church's government. The church then lived under the Soviet regime. Dimitry Pospielovsky's history follows it from 1917 to 1982. Mikhail Shkarovsky and Tatiana Chumachenko have studied how it dealt with the state under Stalin and Khrushchev. In August 2000 the Bishops' Council adopted the Basis of the Social Concept of the Russian Orthodox Church. Its first section sets out the theology behind the church's social teaching. On 17 May 2007, in Moscow, Patriarch Alexy II and Metropolitan Laurus signed the Act of Canonical Communion. It restored communion between the church in Russia and the Russian Church Abroad. Orthodox Christians also lived in Central Asia. Sébastien Peyrouse (2004) found Orthodox, Catholic, Armenian and Protestant minorities there. Most belonged to European minorities that came with Russian colonization in the eighteenth and nineteenth centuries.

## polish-catholicism-wyszynski-and-john-paul-ii

Polish Catholicism — Wyszyński and John Paul II

Cardinal Stefan Wyszyński wrote the Jasna Góra Vows of the Polish Nation while interned at Komańcza. He finished them on 16 May 1956. On 26 August 1956 Bishop Michał Klepacz read them at Jasna Góra. On 18 November 1965, in Rome, the Polish bishops sent a message to their German brother bishops. Archbishop Bolesław Kominek drafted it, and 34 bishops signed it, Wyszyński and Karol Wojtyła among them. On 2 June 1979 Wojtyła, now Pope John Paul II, preached in Victory Square in Warsaw. He prayed for the Spirit to come down and renew the face of the earth, and of this land. A lay renewal grew inside the church too. The Light-Life movement began with a first oasis retreat in 1954, led by Father Franciszek Blachnicki. From 1969 it was called the Movement of the Living Church, and in 1976 it took the name Light-Life. In neighboring Lithuania, Father Sigitas Tamkevičius produced the Chronicle of the Catholic Church in Lithuania at Simnas. Its first issue is dated 19 March 1972, and its 81 issues ran to 19 March 1989.

## orthodoxy-in-the-communist-balkans

Orthodoxy in the Communist Balkans — the Serbian, Romanian, Bulgarian and Albanian Churches

On 12 September 1920, at Sremski Karlovci, the Serbian Orthodox Church was declared united. Constantinople recognized it in a Tomos, a formal decree, dated 19 February 1922. Its first patriarch was Dimitrije. Sources differ on the date of his election. In Romania the Holy Synod voted for a patriarchate on 4 February 1925. A law followed on 25 February. A Tomos from Constantinople followed on 30 July. Miron Cristea was enthroned as the first patriarch on 1 November 1925. In 1945 the schism between the Bulgarian church and Constantinople was lifted. On 10 May 1953 a church-people's council restored the Bulgarian patriarchate. It elected Kiril of Plovdiv. In Bucharest a prayer movement called the Burning Bush met at the Antim monastery from about 1945. It was outlawed in 1948. Its members were arrested in June 1958 and tried that November. Among them were Sandu Tudor, later Father Daniil, Benedict Ghiuș and Sofian Boghiu.

## the-greek-church-and-the-ecumenical-patriarchate

The Greek Church and the Ecumenical Patriarchate in the Twentieth Century

In 1907 the Brotherhood of Theologians Zoe, meaning 'Life', was founded in Greece. Amaryllis Logotheti has studied its place in the religion and politics of post-war Greece. That was the start of the Cold War. On 7 December 1965 Pope Paul VI and Ecumenical Patriarch Athenagoras I issued a Joint Catholic-Orthodox Declaration. It removed from memory the sentences of excommunication of 1054. Rome's legates had excommunicated Patriarch Michael Cerularius, and his synod had condemned the legates in turn. The declaration said those sentences struck persons, not the churches. Lucian Leustean's Eastern Christianity and the Cold War, 1945–91 (2010) gives the Ecumenical Patriarchate a chapter of its own.

## the-romanian-greek-catholic-church-suppression-and-return

The Romanian Greek Catholic Church — Suppression and Return

Decree 358 of 1 December 1948 dissolved the Romanian Greek Catholic Church. Its property passed to the state, and most of its church buildings came into Orthodox use. For forty-one years it had no legal existence. On 31 December 1989 Decree-Law 9 restored it. On 14 March 1990 John Paul II named Alexandru Todea metropolitan of Făgăraș and Alba Iulia.

## melitian-schism

The Melitian Schism

In the last great persecution, Melitius was bishop of Lycopolis in Upper Egypt. He began to ordain clergy in other bishops' lands. Four bishops wrote to him in protest. Peter, bishop of Alexandria, wrote that Melitius had made clergy of his own even in the prison. Peter told his people to hold no communion with him.

Why the quarrel began is disputed. Athanasius and Socrates, a church historian, say Peter deposed Melitius. The charge, they say, was that he had offered sacrifice to idols. Both wrote as his opponents. H. Idris Bell, who edited the Melitians' own letters in 1924, read it as a quarrel over the lapsed. The question, he wrote, was how long to wait before taking them back.

Melitius went on making bishops of his own. In 325 the Council of Nicaea let him keep the title of bishop but forbade him to ordain anyone. The peace did not last. Before he died, Melitius named a friend, John, as his successor, and his church stood apart again.

Under Athanasius, bishop from 328, the fight grew bitter. Socrates says his foes used Melitians to bring charges against him. In 335 a synod at Tyre voted to depose him. A man once a Melitian bishop signed the vote. Around then a Melitian monk named Callistus wrote home from Alexandria. Men on Athanasius's side, he says, came with soldiers. They beat Melitian monks and locked some up.

That letter belongs to a small set of papyri from the 330s, published in 1924. Most are letters to Apa Paieous, a priest and confessor. He led a large community of Melitian monks at Hathor, in the eastern desert. Some letters are in Coptic. They speak of prayers for one another, of care for brothers in trouble, and of bread and wheat.

The Melitians lasted long after Athanasius. In the fifth century Theodoret wrote that bodies of their monks still kept their own ways. Papyri of 512 show a Melitian priest buying a monastery near Arsinoe. The History of the Patriarchs says Patriarch Damian, from 569, drove them out of the monasteries of Wadi Habib. Bell, citing a later part of the History of the Patriarchs, puts the last report of them under Michael I, patriarch from 744 to 768.

## luciferians

The Luciferians

Lucifer was bishop of Cagliari in Sardinia. At a council in Milan he refused to condemn Athanasius of Alexandria, and he stood up to the emperor Constantius II to his face. For this he was sent into exile from 355 to 361. He spent most of those years at Eleutheropolis in Palestine. From there he wrote pamphlets to the emperor himself. How could Christians meet, he asked, with the false bishops of the emperor's sect, the Arians? One pamphlet bears the title We Must Die for the Son of God.

In 362, after Constantius died, the exiles came home. A council met at Alexandria under Athanasius. It agreed that bishops who had only given way could stay in their sees. Lucifer did not attend. Two of his deacons signed its letter for him. He went instead to Antioch and made Paulinus bishop of one of the rival parties there. When his friend Eusebius of Vercelli did not approve, Lucifer broke off communion with him. The church historian Socrates says that many followed Lucifer, and a separate sect arose.

Lucifer himself went home to Sardinia and died there in 371. His followers held on. Hilary, a deacon of Rome, went further than Lucifer. He set up his own font and baptized again those who came over from the Arian side. In about 379 Jerome wrote a dialogue against the group. Lucifer was a good shepherd, he said. But in a time of wolves he took a few sheep away and left the rest of the flock to the beasts.

In Rome the group had its own bishop, Ephesius, and a priest, Macarius, who held night vigils in a private house. In 383 or 384 two of its priests, Marcellinus and Faustinus, sent a petition to the emperors. It says that the clergy of Damasus, bishop of Rome, broke up one of Macarius's vigils and dragged him over the stones. The two priests refused the name Luciferians. Christ is our teacher, they wrote, so the only name we bear is Christian.

The emperors answered them. Their letter called the two men priests full of faith. It told the prefect Cynegius to shield Gregory in Spain, Heraclida in the East and priests like them from attack. The group never grew large. Augustine wrote that they had cut themselves off from the unity of the church. Socrates, writing in the next century, says that those who first joined Lucifer's quarrel still stood apart. After that they fade from view.

## carthusians-later-middle-ages

The Carthusians — The Later Medieval Charterhouses

After 1300 the Carthusians kept the life Bruno had begun, each monk alone in his cell under a strict rule. Thomas à Kempis, writing in the Low Countries, thought the light of the heavenly life still shone among them, though hidden. To worldly men their rule seemed harsh, he wrote, but men of fervent spirit chose it gladly. In England the order had houses at Mount Grace in Yorkshire, at Beauvale, at Sheen near the royal palace of Richmond, and in London.

The English houses became keepers of books. Walter Hilton, an Augustinian canon who died in the 1390s, was close to the order. A letter of his is addressed to Adam Horsley, a monk of Beauvale. Carthusian scribes copied his Scale of Perfection with care. In 1499 Grenehalgh, a monk of Sheen, signed a fine copy of it with his own hand. Another copy bears a note that it was a book of the London Charterhouse.

The Cloud of Unknowing went the same way. Richard Methley, a monk of Mount Grace, probably made a Latin version of it. William Tregoose of the London Charterhouse, who died in 1514, wrote out another copy. A note in its margin praises Hilton, whom the notes take to be the author: O most holy Hilton, great is your humility!

Nicholas Love, prior of Mount Grace, wrote an English book that was copied many times. Before 1410 he made The Mirror of the Blessed Life of Jesus Christ out of a Latin life of Christ. He says he wrote it in English for lay men and women of simple understanding. Such readers, he says, need the milk of light teaching. The book is also aimed at the Lollards. Love breaks off to rebuke the Lollard who laughs at the church's miracles of the sacrament. Its editor says it was presented in 1410 to Archbishop Arundel, a hard foe of the Lollards.

Across the sea the order touched another movement. A Carthusian prior of Monnikhuizen, near Arnhem, was an old friend of Geert Grote. He met Grote in Utrecht and urged him to change his life. Grote went to the Carthusians at Monnikhuizen and was given a cell as a guest. Thomas à Kempis tells the story in his life of Grote, the first of the founders of the New Devotion.

## new-france-mission-born-christianity

Mission-Born Christianity in New France

On 24 June 1610 a chief named Membertou was baptized at Port Royal in Acadia. About twenty of his family and people were baptized with him. A French priest named Fléché baptized them, about a year before the first Jesuits came. The first report in the Jesuit Relations collection is from that year.

Near Quebec, the Jesuits set up a settlement at Sillery in 1637 and 1638. One evening a man named Negabamat came to hold Father Le Jeune to his word. Had he not promised a house for him and another family? Two Algonquin families settled there, and within a year most of the settlers were Christians. Negabamat took the name Noël.

In 1640 the Christians of Sillery met at night, with no priest present. Negabamat told the others that the French taught that "we all are brothers". An old captain, Jean Baptiste Etinechkavat, said he had weighed the faith well before he took it. One man who was not baptized spoke against them. He would not give up the old right to take more than one wife.

The same Christians agreed among themselves that anyone who sinned should be jailed and made to fast. They told the Jesuits only the next morning. The priests said they were too severe, but the converts kept to their plan. Charles Meiachkawat, just baptized, told them to bind him if he ever lost courage.

Among the Hurons, Joseph Chihwatenhwa was for months the only Christian in his village, with his family. His people spoke hard words against him. The Jesuits called him "Our Christian" and wrote down his speeches. In the summer of 1640 he was killed while he worked in his field. The Jesuit report says two Iroquois killed him.

By 1650, war with the Iroquois had broken the Huron nation. The survivors gathered on the Island of Saint Joseph. There two old captains came in secret to the Jesuit superior. "More than ten thousand have been snatched away by death," they told him. They asked him to lead the rest to Quebec. "There, our faith will not die out," they said. About three hundred went down to Quebec with the Jesuits that year.

Every word of theirs comes to us through the Jesuits. The priests wrote each year for readers in France, to build them up in faith. What the converts meant, and what they doubted, we see only through those reports.

## swedish-lutherans-of-new-sweden

The Swedish Lutherans of New Sweden

In 1638 Swedish settlers built Fort Christina on the Delaware River. Their pastor, Reorus Torkillus, came from East Gothland in Sweden. The colony stayed small, and its churches were built of logs.

Swedish rule ended in 1655, and one Swedish pastor, Lars Lock, stayed on. In 1677 the people called a second pastor, Jacob Fabritius, who preached in Dutch. By 1691 he was too old to serve. Then, Acrelius writes, an old man read sermons out of a book in one church, and Charles Springer read in another. "The people waited, but no clergyman came."

Springer had a strange road to the river. He was seized in London, shipped to America and sold as a servant in Virginia. Once free, he joined his own people on the Delaware. In 1693 he wrote for them to Sweden. They asked for "two Swedish ministers", for Bibles, and for two hundred catechisms. They were farmers, they said, who lived "according to the laudable old Swedish customs". They were ready to seal their faith "with our own blood".

The letter reached King Charles XI. In 1697 three pastors arrived with a commission from the king and the archbishop. The people built two brick and stone churches. Trinity Church at Christina was consecrated in 1699, and Gloria Dei at Wicaco in 1700.

In the 1740s Moravian preachers came among the Swedes. In 1744 one of them, Bruzelius, was set to preach at Raccoon church. His opponents held the key, so his friends broke in through a window. A panel of twenty-five men later ruled against him. Moravian ministers were then barred from the Swedish and English churches.

Acrelius came as provost in 1749 and went home in 1756. He urged the people to keep the Swedish language and teach it to the young. Yet the English tongue was gaining, and some services were already held in English.

## english-unitarianism

English Unitarianism

Theophilus Lindsey was a Church of England clergyman at Catterick, in Yorkshire. He grew uneasy in his post and thought of leaving it. Others in that church were trying to end the rule that clergy must sign its Thirty-Nine Articles. Lindsey left, and on 17 April 1774 he opened a chapel in Essex Street, in London.

In his first sermon there, Lindsey said why they had come together. They were "a separate congregation, distinct from that of our national church". They met so that they "may be at liberty to worship God alone". No one, he said, should make others sign his own reading of Scripture. The prayers that day spoke to the Father: "thou art God alone".

Joseph Priestley came from the other side, the old Dissent. He and his fellow tutors at a Dissenting academy, he wrote, "were likewise all Arians". At Leeds he became what was called a Socinian, holding that Christ was a man and not God. In London he spent his Sundays at Lindsey's chapel and sometimes led its worship. He wrote a pamphlet every year in defense of "the unitarian doctrine".

In Birmingham, Priestley also argued with the new Swedenborgian church. His letters of 1791 rejected its teaching that the one God is Jesus Christ. Robert Hindmarsh, a leader of that church, answered him in 1792. A Socinian, he wrote, had no right to the name of a Christian.

On 14 July 1791 a mob in Birmingham burned the church where Priestley preached. Then they burned his home. It wrecked the houses of many Dissenters too. Priestley moved to Hackney, near London. A church there asked him to be its minister. In April 1794 he sailed for America. Thomas Belsham taught at the college in Hackney. He later served the Essex Street chapel.

The same belief had English voices long before Lindsey. In 1653 John Biddle put a life of Socinus, the Polish Brethren's teacher, into English. In 1777 Joshua Toulmin, a Baptist pastor at Taunton, wrote a new life of Socinus. A Baptist editor wrote in 1910 that some Baptist churches in the west of England turned Unitarian too.

The chapel's prayer book crossed the sea. In 1786 the minister of King's Chapel in Boston wrote to Lindsey about his new liturgy. He said he had tried to bring in Lindsey's whole liturgy. He also credited Lindsey's books for the spread of Unitarian belief in New England.

## hutterite-brethren

The Hutterite Brethren

In 1528 Jakob Widman led his followers out of Nikolsburg in Moravia. They were some two hundred people, not counting the children. They had parted from the Anabaptists there who kept the sword. In a deserted village on the road, their leaders spread a cloak on the ground. Everyone laid down their goods on it, freely and unforced. They pointed to the prophets and the apostles.

Jakob Hutter led the community for more than two years. In 1535 he was betrayed and seized at Klausen, and taken to King Ferdinand's government at Innsbruck. He was tortured and then burned alive early in 1536. The Brethren took his name, and their chronicle says they were not ashamed of it.

The communities spread across Moravia. By 1622, the Brethren said, they had lived there more than eighty years and served many lords well. That October the emperor Ferdinand drove them out of all Moravia. They left twenty-four households behind, with their grain, wine and herds. They went into Hungary with almost nothing, as winter set in.

In Hungary and Transylvania they lived on for some decades more, growing fewer. From 1757 the government broke up the last communities, and most members became Catholics. A few families would not give up their fathers' faith. They moved to Wischenka in Russia, and in 1784 others fled to join them.

## mennonites-and-amish-after-1650

The Mennonites and the Amish

After 1650 the heirs of the Swiss and Dutch Anabaptists lived on as settled churches. Many were called Mennonites, after Menno Simons. Their Dordrecht Confession of 1632 stayed their rule of faith. In 1660 Thieleman van Braght of Dordrecht published the Martyrs Mirror. It told of those "Who Baptized Only Upon Confession of Faith". In a prayer dated at Dort in 1659, he asked to be like "my slain fellow brethren and sisters".

In Pennsylvania the Mennonites wanted their great martyr book in German, not Dutch. The Ephrata community in Lancaster County did the work. Fifteen of its brothers were set to it, and the job took three years. The edition ran to 1300 copies.

The old hymn book of the Swiss Brethren, the Ausbund, was printed again and again in Pennsylvania. Its songs, the title says, were written in the prison at Passau. A Lancaster printing of 1856 calls itself the seventh in Pennsylvania. In 1886 Mennonite publishers brought out the Martyrs Mirror in English. English was spreading "year by year" among "our Mennonite people", its preface says.

In 1890 a manual from Elkhart set out the church's forms. A minister was chosen by lot. A slip with a verse from Proverbs went into one of several like books. The man whose book held it was ordained. Some congregations, "especially among the Amish and Russian Mennonites", chose by the members' vote instead.

The Mennonites also had a Russian chapter. Gerhard Wiebe, an elder of Bergthal, told it in 1900. A call came from Russia, he writes, and the first families left Prussia in 1788 or 1789. By 1790 eight villages had been settled. In the Molotschna colony thirty-nine villages were settled between 1804 and 1824. In the early 1870s the Mennonites read in the newspapers that they would be drafted. They sent men to St Petersburg. Many then left for America. Wiebe says his last brethren came from Russia in 1876.

The Hutterites, a church of their own, have a Russian chapter too. Josef Beck, who edited their chronicles, wrote in 1883 that single families who would not give up their fathers' faith went to Wischenka in Russia. In 1784, he says, part of the new converts fled to join them. The emigrants later moved on from Little Russia. Beck's preface says they went to the Crimea. His own note places their colony, Hutersthal, near the Sea of Azov, in the province of Taurida. In 1855, he says, the community stood under a grandson of the last bishop.

The two churches had been in touch before, by letter. In 1749 a Mennonite preacher, Franz Simon Sporer, wrote to the Brethren at Levar in Hungary. In 1757 another, Peter Weber, wrote to Zacharias Walter. He offered to send Menno's books.

## melkite-greek-catholic-church-after-1815

The Melkite Greek Catholic Church after 1815

Patriarch Macarius Tauil died on 15 December 1815. Only four bishops were left. In June 1816 they met at the monastery of Saint Anthony on Mount Lebanon. There they chose the priest Moses Qattan as patriarch.

Under Qattan the church's affairs went from bad to worse. So Rome's mission office, the Propaganda, told its delegate to gather all the bishops. They met late in 1831 at the Annunciation monastery at Zuq Mikhail.

Maximos III Mazloum was elected patriarch in 1833. He wrote to the pope at once to ask for confirmation. In 1835 he held a national council at Ain Traz. From 1843 to 1848 he worked in Constantinople. There he won the sultan's decree of 7 January 1848. It gave the Melkite Catholics full civil standing of their own.

In 1849 Maximos presided over a council of his church at Jerusalem. He died at Alexandria in August 1855, and the bishops chose Clement Bahus. In 1858 Clement asked all his people to keep the Gregorian calendar. Four of his bishops rose against him. In 1859 they met at Zahle and declared him stripped of all authority.

The synods went on choosing patriarchs. Peter Geraigiri was elected at the monastery of Sarba in 1898. After his death, Cyril VIII Geha was elected on 27 June 1902. Rome confirmed him a year later, and he still led the church in 1911.

## the-coptic-church-under-the-ottomans

The Coptic Church under the Ottomans: the Patriarchate and the Archons

In 1517 the Ottomans took Egypt. A list of the Coptic patriarchs, compiled by E. A. Wallis Budge and printed in 1928, runs on without a break through that year. It shows John XIII in office to 1524, and Gabriel VII from 1526 to 1569.

Volney traveled in Egypt and Syria from 1783 to 1785. He says the Copts had become the keepers of the registers of the lands and tribes. He says that at Cairo they served as the government's stewards, secretaries and tax collectors. He writes that the Turks they served despised them, and the peasants they pressed hated them. Volney was no friend of the Copts.

These lay leaders are called archons. One was Ibrahim al-Jawhari, chief clerk of the finance office. Butcher says he was so well liked by Muslim and Christian that he escaped a persecution under Hassan Pasha in the 1780s. Through his influence the churches and monasteries could be restored, and he gave much land and money to the church.

Edward Lane wrote his account of Egypt in the years 1833 to 1835. He reckoned the Copts at about 150,000. He writes that the Coptic patriarch judged small cases among his people in Cairo.

Rome built a Catholic line among the Copts. In 1741 the pope made a Copt named Athanasius a metropolitan.

He lived in Jerusalem. Butcher calls him the first with real power in Egypt. He named a priest as his vicar there. Butcher wrote in 1897 and did not favor Rome's claim.

The patriarch also sent the abun to Ethiopia. A decision of his reached Tigray, as Gobat's journal shows.

## catholics-of-the-ottoman-balkans

Catholics of the Ottoman Balkans: the Bosnian Franciscans

The Catholics of Bosnia and the lands north of it were served by the friars of the Bosnian province. A calendar of their documents, in Latin, was printed at Zagreb in 1892. It is the main source for these years.

On 1 January 1689 the fathers of the Bosnian province met at Požega, in Slavonia. The calendar says they spoke in their own name and the people's. They declared that the bishops of Zagreb had never held jurisdiction in the Slavonian parts. The clergy and the Catholic people between the Sava and the Drava said that no bishop but the bishop of Bosnia had built up and ruled the church of Požega, for at least 170 years.

The Crown took a hand. In 1690, from Vienna, King Leopold ordered that the bishop of Bosnia be kept and defended in his rights in the district of Požega. This was to last until a judge had settled the questions.

In 1746 Franjo Ivanović wrote from Vienna to the guardians of Slavonia. He was provincial of Bosnia Argentina. That means Silver Bosnia (a plain rendering). He asked them to gather testimonies from nobles and faithful. The province would use them at the court of Vienna. They were to defend its rights in the Slavonian parishes.

These are records of fights about parishes. They show the friars speaking for their people. They do not show how the people themselves saw the friars.

## chinese-catholicism-1650-1906

Chinese Catholics Under Edict and Treaty

The Jesuit mission of the Ming years went on at the Qing court. The Jesuits served the emperor as astronomers and interpreters. The Catholic Encyclopedia of 1908 says they stood high in favor. In March 1692, it adds, a note from the president of the Board of Rites said it seemed unfair to ban the Europeans' religion. An imperial decree approved the note. Copies went to all the provincial governors.

The Rites quarrel asked what a Chinese Christian might do for Confucius and the dead. In 1645 missionaries in China sent questions to Rome. The Holy Office answered them on 23 March 1656. It let Chinese Christians join the rite in the Confucius hall. It seemed, the ruling says, to be worship that is only civil and political.

In 1658 Rome named two French priests, Pallu and Lambert de la Motte, to lead the missions of China. The seminary they asked for was given a site in Paris in 1663 and opened in 1664. The encyclopedia calls it the beginning of the Paris Foreign Missions Society.

Propaganda wrote to them in 1659. It asked what could be more absurd than to carry France, Spain or Italy into China.

Rome later turned against the rites.

Father Ricci was the Jesuits' first leader in China. He was tolerant of the Chinese rites, the encyclopedia says. Rome, not China, decided the question. Maigrot was a vicar apostolic in Fujian and a priest of the Paris Foreign Missions. In 1693 he condemned the rites in a mandate. A bull of Benedict XIV settled the matter on 11 July 1742. It was, the encyclopedia says, "a terrible blow to the missions in China".

In 1723 officials in Fujian asked the emperor to send the Europeans out of the provinces. Europeans at the court could stay.

On 16 October 1724 the Jesuit de Mailla wrote from Peking. He said the holy faith was entirely banned in China. Churches were pulled down or turned to common uses. An order of 1723 complained of girls who were kept from marriage and called virgins.

The church went on in the provinces. In 1726 Propaganda ruled that Chinese-born priests must swear the same oath as the Europeans. In 1736 an edict of the Ch'ien-lung emperor forbade the preaching of Christian doctrine on pain of death. A persecution in Fujian in 1746 killed Bishop Sanz and four Spanish Dominicans.

In 1755 Propaganda warned the missionary at Peking for letting Mass be said in Chinese on feast days. It forbade the practice for the future. In 1766 the Holy Office was told that Christians in China were few and could not avoid their non-Christian neighbors' tables. It allowed them to eat food set before them, even if offered to idols, outside any temple and with no sign of worship.

In 1811 a decree ordered a search for foreign preachers. In 1844 a treaty was signed at Whampoa. On 20 February 1846 the emperor ordered that the old Christian buildings be restored.

Treaties in 1858 and 1860 gave missions special privileges. These were the Treaty of Tientsin and the Peking Convention. Anger followed in 1870. A Holy Office instruction of the 1860s dealt with trouble in Hunan. Non-Christians, it says, were angry at a treaty and drew crosses on the roads so that Christians had to step on them. At Tientsin on 21 June 1870, twenty-one people died, among them a Lazarist and a Chinese priest named Hu.

The Boxer rising of 1900 brought "sad days for the missions". The encyclopedia says five bishops were among the dead.

## church-of-the-east-three-patriarchates

The Church of the East — Three Patriarchates

The Church of the East began these years with two rival patriarchs. One lived in the mountains of Hakkari. Malech says a patriarch of that line, Denkha Shimon, later moved its seat to the village of Qudshanis. The other lived on the plain, at Alqosh and Mosul.

Each patriarch of the plain held an order from the Sultan, called a firman. It confirmed his power over his people in the empire. The mountain patriarchs had no such order. Badger writes that the Kurdish emirs tolerated and protected them.

Rome then added a third line. In 1672 Joseph, the church's archbishop at Diyarbakır, broke with the patriarch Elias. Labourt says he followed the advice of Capuchin missionaries. In 1681 Pope Innocent XI gave him the pallium and the title of patriarch. Wigram dates the new line to 1680.

Five men named Joseph held this see in turn. Badger says neither the old bishops nor the Ottoman government recognized the line at first. It did not reach beyond Diyarbakır.

In 1778 the line of the plain began to turn to Rome. Patriarch Elia died of plague at Alqosh on 29 April. His nephew Hormizd, a metropolitan, wrote the next part himself, in Badger's English: “So on the 30th of April I embraced all that the holy Church believes.” Another nephew, Yeshua-yau, was to be patriarch. Within a year he turned back, and the two became enemies. In 1782 Pope Pius VI let Hormizd act as patriarch, but not use the patriarch's seal.

The sources disagree on when the plain line truly joined Rome. Wigram puts it in 1778. Labourt puts it in 1830, after Rome had cut Hormizd off in 1818. Wigram says the Joseph line was then allowed to lapse, though its last holder lived until 1828.

## ethiopian-christianity-gondar-to-the-zemene-mesafint

Ethiopian Christianity — Gondar to the Zemene Mesafint

By 1650 the Jesuits were gone. Hiob Ludolf, writing in 1682, says that Fasilides drove them out of all his lands, together with their patriarch. Ludolf adds that Europeans knew little more of the emperor after that, because he would have no more dealings with them.

The head of the church was the abun, and he came from Egypt. James Bruce, who was in Ethiopia in the early 1770s, calls him a Coptic monk raised in the desert of St Macarius. Bruce says people looked on the abun as the patriarch of their church, and that they knew little of the Coptic patriarch of Alexandria. He adds that the abun seldom understood the language of the country.

The monks held great weight. Bruce says the Itchege, the chief of the monks of Debra Libanos, mattered more than the abun in troubled times. He names the monks of Gojam, who followed St Eustathius, as the low church of Ethiopia, and says they leaned toward turmoil in matters of religion. Bruce was an outside witness. He wrote decades after some of the events he tells.

Councils of clergy met when a new king took the throne or a new abun came from Egypt. Bruce tells of one in the reign of David. The head of the monks of Debra Libanos brought a complaint against three Catholic priests and their Ethiopian interpreter, also named Abba Gregorius. The council asked whether they accepted the Council of Chalcedon and the authority of Pope Leo. They said that they did, and the crowd cried out, "Stone them." The missionaries were dragged to a place behind the church of Abbo and stoned to death.

The Catholic Encyclopedia of 1907 also tells of three missionaries stoned in a public square at Gondar. It dates this to 1752. No other source read here gives that date.

The article also says that in 1846 Rome made Abyssinia a vicariate. Rome gave it to the Lazarists. It says one of them, Justin de Jacobis, stirred the jealousy of the clergy. These accounts are Catholic. They speak for the missionaries.

The Church Missionary Society had long turned its eyes toward Ethiopia. It sent Samuel Gobat and Christian Kugler into Egypt in 1826 to seek a way in. On 26 February 1830 Gobat left Adigrate, in Tigray, and set out for Gondar.

Gobat's journal also shows how the church settled a quarrel in Tigray. A letter of Kugler's tells that Girgis and the chief priest of Tigray, Guebra Mariam, came from Cairo. They brought the decision of the Coptic patriarch on the priests' quarrels. One priest, Guebra Messih, held that Christ had three births. On 28 September the dispute was settled in the presence of the Tigray ruler Sebagadis.

The next entry says that for a century before 1855 the emperors were only figureheads, while regional lords ruled. No source read for this entry tells of those years.

## georgian-church-1650-1811

The Georgian Church — Between Persia, the Ottomans and Russia

For a century and a half the Georgian church lived between great powers. Dowling says Georgia "suffered severely from the attacks of Persians and Turks". Akhaltsikhe stayed Ottoman. In 1795 a Persian army took Tiflis and burned it. Ioselian says the churches were full of killers.

Catholic monks came too. Theatines came in 1625, and Capuchins followed. At first the kings used them as teachers. Ioselian says they "perverted" many.

Catholicos Antoni I left Georgia in 1755. The accounts differ on why. Ioselian says the king feared the catholicos meant to leave the Orthodox faith, so a synod gave him a choice: leave, or give up his office. Dowling says Antoni opposed the Latin innovations and was deposed for it. Both say he went to the Russian Synod. Ioselian says the Synod found his confession of faith fully Orthodox. He became archbishop of Vladimir and later came back to his old see.

The church still made its own books and hymns. Macrina, sister of King Teimuraz I, wrote hymns that were still sung when Ioselian wrote. Catholicos Antoni I translated many works. Under King Heraclius II the ties with Russia grew closer, and the catholicos took a seat in the Russian Holy Synod. He kept his title.

That ended in 1811. A Georgian appeal printed by Dowling says the last catholicos was called to St Petersburg in 1810 and never allowed home. In 1811 the office was abolished. An exarch took its place.

## hungarian-and-transylvanian-protestantism-1650-1815

Hungarian and Transylvanian Protestantism, 1650 to 1815

Two sources tell this story. Bauhofer, a Protestant, writes of long suffering that ends in freedom. A Catholic work writes of a fight over churches and laws. This entry uses both.

In 1671 the bishop took the Protestant church of Kashaw, after breaking its doors. The government set up a special court at Presburg to try Protestants.

Forty-one prisoners were taken away. Only thirty entered the galleys at Naples, on 7 May. They were sold for fifty Spanish piasters apiece and chained to the benches. Ten others gave up their Protestant faith and joined the Church of Rome on 19 February 1676.

On 11 February 1676 the chaplain of the Dutch fleet came for the galley prisoners. They left their prison singing the 46th, the 114th and the 125th Psalms. Twenty-six of the thirty were still alive.

The Diet of 1681 ordered some churches returned to the Protestants. In 1687 Leopold said that by his own free favor he would keep the liberties granted in 1681. The Peace of Szathmar, signed on 10 May 1711, kept the laws of 1681 and 1687 in force.

On 21 March 1731 Charles VI gave rules the churches had long awaited. Bauhofer says their hopes were badly hurt. Private worship was allowed everywhere. Public worship was allowed only where the law of 1681 named a place.

The two churches did not always act together. In 1732 the Lutherans refused to join the Calvinists in a petition to the king. Soon after, both sent a joint statement to the court. Bauhofer reports a Count Pallfy as saying that the Protestants must have either God or the Devil for their friend. Each time their ruin seemed sure, some untoward event stopped it.

Joseph II's Edict of Toleration went to all the bishops of Hungary on 24 October 1781, in Bauhofer's account. The Catholic Encyclopedia says it was issued on 25 October. Where a hundred families could pay for it, they might build a church, a school and a manse. The churches could have neither bell nor spire, and no door onto the street.

The Diet of 1790 and 1791 gave the churches full freedom to run their own affairs, says the Catholic Encyclopedia. It counts about two million Protestants and five million Catholics in 1805.

## japans-hidden-christians

Japan's Hidden Christians

In 1614 Japan banned Christianity, and the entry before this one tells how it began. Marnas wrote the history of the church's return in 1896. He says that in 1666 the government at Edo set up an inquiry in every town and village to learn what each family believed. Once a year, he adds, every Japanese had to tread on a cross laid on the ground, or on an image of the Virgin and the saints.

This was the e-fumi, the treading of the image. Marnas says its inventors meant it to expose the surviving Christians or force them to give up the faith.

Almost everywhere the hidden Christians kept the same order, Marnas says. Most villages had two chiefs. The chief of prayer led the Sunday prayers and went to the dying.

The baptizer gave baptism and trained a pupil to take his place. Marnas credits these offices, oral teaching and a few kept books and images for the survival of the faith.

In 1865 the missionaries studied the papers the villagers had kept.

They included prayer books, written baptism formulas and Christian calendars.

One treatise, Contrition, was dated 1603 and written wholly in Japanese. The baptizer Domingo brought it to the missionaries. It had escaped a search at Urakami eight years before. Then twenty-eight Christians were jailed and most of their books were burned. On 17 March 1865, at about half past twelve, twelve to fifteen people stood at the door of the church of the Twenty-Six Martyrs in Nagasaki.

Three women knelt beside Father Petitjean. One said, with her hand on her heart, that the hearts of all of them were the same as his. Then she asked, "Sancta Maria no gozowa doko?", "Where is the image of Holy Mary?" (our rendering). Within weeks the missionaries counted about 3,800 Christians.

About 1,300 lived at Urakami and 2,500 in the hills and on the nearby islands.

## kongo-christianity-after-1650

Kongo Christianity after 1650

In the late 1660s two Capuchin friars, Dionigi Carli and Michelangelo Guattini, served in Bamba, in Kongo. Carli's book came out in 1671. He writes that a headman sent word through the village to bring the children for baptism, because the adults were all baptized. The people called the friar "Ngamga," and Carli adds that they put a word after it to honor him.

Giovanni Antonio Cavazzi was a Capuchin. He printed his history of Kongo, Matamba and Angola in 1687. He writes that priests rarely came to remote places to keep the law of Christ in force. The people there still took pride in it.

Cavazzi tells of the queen Njinga, whom he calls queen of the Jaga. He says she turned her back on the true law and joined the Jaga. He says that for twenty-eight years she followed a way of life he calls the most beastly of all. Her later return to the church is not shown in the text read here.

The Antonian movement of Dona Beatriz Kimpa Vita, from 1704 to 1706, falls inside this window. John Thornton's study of 1998 treats it, and how far it grew from the older church rests on that one study.

Almost nothing was read for the years from about 1720 to 1879. How the church lived then waits on sources not yet read.

Bentley's list dates the Baptist station at San Salvador to 1879. The Portuguese priests there first came in 1881. Bentley judged from what he heard that the people were about evenly split between the priests and the Baptists.

## protestant-churches-of-the-voc-world

Protestant Churches of the VOC World

In 1605 the Dutch East India Company, the VOC, reached Ambon and drove out the Portuguese priests. Its charter of 1623 bound it only to maintain the public faith. In two centuries it sent at least 900 ordained ministers to its lands. In the Moluccas the church ran on village teachers, called mester. The study of Aritonang and Steenbrink calls the teacher the backbone of Ambonese Christianity. He led the Sunday service and read a Malay sermon. An ordained minister was meant to visit a few times a year. The schools grew from 32 in 1633 to 54 in 1700, with 5,190 pupils. In 1673 the Ambon church ordered its teachers to come to town every two weeks, or at least every month.

Pressure also came from above. In 1677 the governor attended the mass baptism of the village of Kamarian, and the Company sent rice for the feast. In 1692 a minister complained that only the schoolchildren, the village chief and his wife came to Sunday service.

In Batavia the ministers were all Dutch, except Cornelis Senen of Banda, who was only a probationer. About 5,000 people were full members around 1700. In 1706 the church had 34 evangelists. They taught 4,873 slaves and free people. Of these, 4,440 were taught in Portuguese, 323 in Malay and 110 in Dutch. Teachers in Batavia had to agree with the Heidelberg Catechism.

Georg Werndly translated that catechism. The Malay Bible came out at Amsterdam in 1731 to 1733.

In 1753 the minister at Semarang had 1,767 Christians. Nearly half were Lutherans or Catholics. He tried not to offend them. In Ceylon the Dutch tried to make Catholic converts Reformed.
