# Packet L11

## haitian-church-rupture-to-concordat

The Haitian Church — Rupture to Concordat

When Haiti declared independence in 1804 as the first Black republic, its church was cut adrift. Rome would not recognize the new state, the French clergy had fled or been killed, and for more than half a century no properly appointed bishop and almost no seminary-trained priest arrived. The state filled the gap, appointing its own clergy, and the parishes were served by men of irregular standing. Some genuine priests under suspension, some who had simply assumed the title. In that long vacancy Haitian religious life took the shape it has largely kept: Catholic sacraments, feast days and saints woven together with Vodou practice inherited from West Africa, held by most Haitians as one thing rather than two. The rupture formally ended on 28 March 1860, when Haiti and the Holy See signed a concordat restoring a regular hierarchy. And began a much longer argument about what to do with everything that had grown up in the meantime.

## alaskan-mission-herman-veniaminov

The Alaskan Mission — Herman’s Decades & Veniaminov

Russian Orthodoxy reached Alaska with the fur trade, and two very different men made it stay. Herman, a lay monk who had come with the first mission in 1794, spent his last decades alone on Spruce Island near Kodiak, running a school and an orphanage for Alutiiq children and quarreling with the trading company over its treatment of them. In 1824 a young married priest, Ioann Veniaminov, arrived at Unalaska and did the work of a linguist. He devised an alphabet for Aleut, translated the Gospel of Matthew and a catechism into it, and wrote an account of the islands and their people that scholars still use. Widowed, he became a monk, was made bishop in 1840 as Innocent, and rose eventually to be Metropolitan of Moscow. What the two of them built proved unexpectedly durable. When Russia sold Alaska in 1867 and the Russians went home, the church stayed, in the hands of Alaska Native clergy and lay readers who kept the services going in their own languages.

## nineteenth-century-latin-american-catholicism

Nineteenth-Century Latin American Catholicism

The Spanish crown had run the church in its American empire under a papal grant, appointing the bishops and collecting the tithes. When the colonies won independence the new republics claimed exactly the same right, and Rome — which had backed the crown — took decades to recognize either the governments or their nominees, leaving dioceses vacant and seminaries shut for a generation. What followed was a long fight between liberals and clericals over land, schools, cemeteries and marriage. Sharpest in Mexico, where the Reform laws of the 1850s stripped the church of its property and drove it out of public life, and loudest in Brazil, where two bishops were tried and imprisoned in the 1870s. Rome answered by tightening its grip, importing European clergy and religious orders, rebuilding seminaries on Roman lines, and in 1899 summoning the bishops of the whole continent to Rome for a first Latin American Plenary Council. Underneath all of it the devotional life of ordinary Catholics — confraternities, patronal feasts, pilgrimage shrines, household saints — carried on much as before, often with very few priests.

The rising of 1810 in Mexico began with a priest. He was Miguel Hidalgo, the priest of Dolores. A history printed in 1843 says he began the revolution on 15 September 1810. José María Morelos was the priest of Carácuaro. He left his parish on 25 October 1810. The Inquisition of Mexico later charged that the rebel priest of Dolores, Hidalgo, had sent him to raise troops in the south.

In November 1815 the Inquisition tried Morelos. It called him a degraded priest. It said he had joined «el hereje Cura de Dolores», the heretic priest of Dolores. It said this brought on him the excommunications of some bishops, and its own edicts of 1810 and 1811.

Another popular movement of the 1890s is known from a novel. Heriberto Frías's Tomóchic, in its edition of 1906, tells of a village in Chihuahua. Its people said the name of the Santa de Cabora, Teresa Urrea, with veneration. The novel follows a young officer in the army sent against them, and dates its scenes to October 1892. A novel is not a record of events.

## ethiopian-christianity-tewodros-to-menelik

Ethiopian Christianity — Tewodros to Menelik

For a century Ethiopia's emperors had been figureheads while regional lords ruled. Then in 1855 a soldier from the provinces took the throne as Tewodros II. He set out to remake the empire, and its church too. He seized church lands and tried to bring the clergy under his hand. His quarrel with Britain ended in 1868, when a British army stormed Magdala and he took his own life. Yohannes IV took the throne a few years later. He settled an argument that had split Ethiopian scholars for two centuries. At the Council of Boru Meda in 1878 he ruled for the Tewahedo teaching on how God and man are united in Christ. He then made it the one teaching of the empire. Menelik II did what no other African ruler managed in that century. At Adwa in 1896 he crushed an invading Italian army and kept his country free. Ethiopia entered the twentieth century as an ancient Christian state that had never been ruled by Europeans. Its church still took its head, the abun, from the Coptic patriarch in Egypt.

Missionaries kept coming. Donald Crummey wrote about them in a study of the missions from 1830 to 1868. It is called Priests and Politicians.

A Catholic source of 1907 says Tewodros made Catholics suffer. It says Yohannes brought them twenty years of trouble.

It says Menelik, crowned in March 1889, restored calm to the missions. This is a Catholic account, not the church's own.

## dutch-neo-calvinism-secession-churches

Dutch Neo-Calvinism & the Secession Churches

In 1834 a village pastor in Groningen, Hendrik de Cock, was suspended for preaching the old Reformed doctrines against what he took to be the liberalism of the national church, and his congregation walked out with him. The Afscheiding that followed was a secession of mostly poor and rural people, who were fined and had soldiers billeted on them for holding illegal services. Many emigrated, and in 1857 their American cousins founded what became the Christian Reformed Church in North America. Fifty years after de Cock, Abraham Kuyper — journalist, politician, theologian — led a second and larger secession, the Doleantie of 1886, and in 1892 brought most of the two streams together. Kuyper's idea was always bigger than a church. That Christ's claim covers every sphere of life, each with its own proper authority, so believers should build their own schools, university, newspaper and political party rather than either withdraw from society or dissolve into it. He founded the Free University in Amsterdam in 1880 and served as prime minister of the Netherlands from 1901 to 1905.

## russian-evangelicals-stundists

Russian Evangelicals — Stundists to the Baptist Union

In the 1860s Ukrainian peasants in the south worked beside German colonists. They began meeting for Bible studies that their neighbors called Stunden, hours. They read the Bible for themselves, in the Russian translation then coming out. Reading became conversion, and conversion became congregations. Then the movement met a second one, coming from the other end of society. From 1874 in St Petersburg, nobles won by the preaching of the English evangelist Lord Radstock gathered around Colonel Vasily Pashkov. He opened his house to meetings where servants and nobles sat side by side. In 1884 Pashkov invited the leaders of every strand to his house to unite. On the fourth day the police broke up the meeting. That same year the Baptist churches of the south formed a Russian Baptist Union. Pashkov's followers stayed a separate stream. The state answered with everything it had. Pashkov was sent into exile and meetings were broken up. Children were taken from Stundist parents, and whole families were deported. Leaving the Orthodox Church was a crime as well as a sin. The toleration decree of 1905 gave them their first legal breathing space in forty years.

## azusa-street-and-early-pentecostalism

Azusa Street & Early Pentecostalism

The revival on Azusa Street in Los Angeles began in April 1906, and it came out of two streams already running — the Holiness movement, and Black church worship. What made it travel was that the mission printed its own newspaper. The Apostolic Faith ran to thirteen issues between 1906 and 1908, went out free from Los Angeles, and was read by people who then started meetings of their own on the strength of it. William J. Seymour led the mission and set down how it was to be ordered in The Doctrines and Discipline of the Azusa Street Apostolic Faith Mission in 1915. The city's press mocked it from the first weeks — the Los Angeles Times ran 'Weird Babel of Tongues' on 18 April 1906 — and participants answered with accounts of their own, Frank Bartleman's among the best known. The mission itself declined after 1909, and Seymour died in 1922. By then what began there was long past its own address.

## the-1904-07-revival-family-wales-mukti-india-pyo

The 1904-07 Revival Family (Wales; Mukti India; Pyongyang Korea)

Three revivals broke out within a few years of each other in three parts of the world, and none of them descended from the others or from Azusa Street. In Wales the revival ran through 1904 and 1905 and was reported almost daily in the Western Mail by a correspondent writing as 'Awstin', with Evan Roberts as its public face; W. T. Stead published The Revival in the West in 1905. At Pandita Ramabai's Mukti mission in India it came in 1905, and Minnie Abrams, who worked there, described what happened in The Baptism of the Holy Ghost and Fire the following year. In Korea it began in January 1907, at a Bible conference at the Presbyterian seminary in Pyongyang, on ground that Korean colporteurs and foreign missionaries had been working since the 1880s. Three separate beginnings, recorded almost verbatim as they happened, which is why this map keeps them together rather than tracing them to a single source.

## the-east-african-revival-balokole

The East African Revival (Balokole)

The East African Revival began around 1930 at the mission station at Gahini in Rwanda, where African and missionary Christians started confessing sin to one another out loud and in public. Those who joined were called balokole, the saved ones. It spread through fellowship meetings and through conventions, at Kabale from 1935 and then elsewhere, with testimony given openly and the hymn 'Tukutendereza Yesu' sung wherever they gathered. It ran through Uganda, Kenya and Tanzania as well as Rwanda, and it was led by Africans inside the mission Anglicanism that had planted the church — often against it, since a movement that asked bishops and catechists alike to confess in public was not always welcome. Its record is unusually communal: the testimony culture generated its own written material, and oral-history projects have gathered more of it from people who remembered it.

## african-initiated-churches-harrist-aladura-zioni

African-Initiated Churches (Harrist; Aladura; Zionist/ZCC; Kimbanguist)

From the 1880s onward Africans began founding churches of their own rather than staying inside the missions. Nehemiah Tile left the Methodists to form the Thembu National Church in the 1880s, and in November 1892 Mangena Mokone founded the Ethiopian Church in South Africa. Later waves went further. William Wadé Harris walked through the Ivory Coast and the Gold Coast between 1913 and 1915 preaching and baptizing, and the churches that followed him bear his name. In Nigeria the Aladura churches grew from the 1920s and 1930s — the Cherubim and Seraphim, founded in 1925 by Moses Orimolade and Christianah Abiodun; the Christ Apostolic Church around Joseph Babalola's revival of 1930; the Celestial Church of Christ from 1947. In the Congo, Simon Kimbangu's ministry began on 18 March 1921 and ended within months before a colonial military court. In 1972 Martial Sinda studied Kimbanguism beside Matsouanism, the movement around André Matswa in Congo-Brazzaville. In South Africa, Engenas Lekganyane founded the Zion Christian Church in 1924. In Uganda, Reuben Spartas Mukasa broke with the Anglicans in January 1929. In 1946 he visited Alexandria and was made the patriarch's trustee, and from then his church was linked to the Greek Orthodox Patriarchate there. In Zimbabwe, the apostolics of Johane Masowe set aside the Bible as a written text. Matthew Engelke studied them in 2007. In Northern Rhodesia, now Zambia, Alice Lenshina's Lumpa Church ended in conflict and a ban in 1964. Each of these carried its founder's legacy forward differently, which is why this map takes them one at a time.

## the-chinese-church-indigenous-and-suffering

The Chinese Church - Indigenous & Suffering

From the 1910s Chinese Christians began founding churches of their own, free of the foreign missions. Wei Enbo founded the True Jesus Church in Beijing in 1917. Watchman Nee's Little Flock assemblies date from 1922, and the Jesus Family from 1927. Their leaders wrote a great deal. Nee's The Spiritual Man appeared in 1928, and Wang Mingdao published his own account of fifty years in 1950. The traveling preacher John Sung kept diaries that his family later put together. After 1950 the Christian Manifesto and the Three-Self Patriotic Movement brought Protestant church life under the state. Wang Mingdao published 'We, Because of Faith' in 1955. He was imprisoned from 1955 until 1980, and Watchman Nee from 1952 until 1972. After that, much church life went on outside registered structures. In November 1998 house-church leaders published a joint Confession of Faith. No living person and no specific community is named here. What is known is thinnest, and least safe to make public, exactly where the growth was greatest. That is a fact about the church itself, not only about the available sources.

## the-confessing-church

The Confessing Church

When the Nazi state and the pro-Nazi 'German Christians' moved to reorganize the German Protestant churches in 1933, a minority refused. Pastors organized an emergency league; a synod at Barmen in May 1934 adopted a declaration, drafted by Karl Barth, that the church has one Word to hear and obey, Jesus Christ, and no other; and the Dahlem synod that October declared itself the lawful church. Dietrich Bonhoeffer ran an illegal seminary at Finkenwalde from 1935 until it was shut down in 1937, and wrote Discipleship and Life Together out of that community. In 1936 the movement sent a memorandum to Hitler. Its own record refuses to flatter it: the church struggle was largely about the church's own freedom, and the near-silence about what was being done to Jews is named by the movement's own historians as part of the story. In October 1945 the survivors issued the Stuttgart Declaration of Guilt.

## catholic-lay-renewal-worlds-catholic-worker-foco

Catholic Lay Renewal Traditions (Catholic Worker; Focolare; Charismatic Renewal; L'Arche)

Four Catholic movements, all begun by lay people, all still active in 2010. Dorothy Day and Peter Maurin founded the Catholic Worker in 1933: a newspaper, houses of hospitality, and a refusal of war that cost them readers. Chiara Lubich began Focolare in 1943. The Catholic Charismatic Renewal began on a retreat weekend at Duquesne University in February 1967 and reached parishes across the world within a decade; Cardinal Suenens's Malines Documents, from 1974, worked out how it and the wider church would live together. In Brazil the renewal began at Campinas in 1969, brought by the Jesuits Haroldo Rahm and Edward Dougherty. Andrew Chesnut's study of 2003 follows it in Brazil, Mexico and Guatemala. Jean Vanier founded L'Arche in 1964, communities in which people with and without intellectual disabilities share a household. The map treats these as four distinct movements rather than one tradition, because that is what they are — and it does not tell L'Arche's story without the abuse committed by its founder, documented later in the inquiry L'Arche International commissioned and published in 2020 and in the independent study commission's report of 2023.

## latin-american-base-communities

Latin American Base Communities

From the 1960s, in Brazil and then across Latin America, small groups of poor Catholics began meeting to read the Bible together, often led by a lay coordinator or a catechist, and usually reading the gospel against their own week. The bishops gave the practice their backing at Medellín in 1968, a meeting told in the entry on Latin American Catholicism. Ernesto Cardenal published four volumes of the conversations his peasant neighbors at Solentiname had about the Sunday gospel, beginning in 1975 — one of the few records anywhere of poor people doing theology in their own words, printed as they said them. Gustavo Gutiérrez had named the theology in 1971. Many of these communities' people were killed: Rutilio Grande in 1977, Óscar Romero at the altar in March 1980, four American churchwomen later that year, six Jesuits of the UCA in 1989. Rome's doctrinal office issued two instructions on liberation theology, in 1984 and 1986.

## emigre-orthodoxy-and-the-paris-school

Emigre Orthodoxy & the Paris School

The Russian revolution scattered a whole church's worth of thinkers, monks and parishioners across Europe, and a remarkable number of them landed in Paris. The St Sergius Institute was founded there in 1925 and became the center of it. Sergei Bulgakov's theology drew a controversy of its own between 1935 and 1937, argued in print on both sides; Georges Florovsky's Ways of Russian Theology (1937) called the emigration back to the Greek fathers; Vladimir Lossky's Mystical Theology of the Eastern Church, written in French and published in 1944, put Orthodox theology in front of Western readers who had never had it. YMCA Press printed what they wrote. Mother Maria Skobtsova ran a hostel in Paris, sheltered Jews under the occupation, and died at Ravensbrück on 31 March 1945. The next generation — Alexander Schmemann, John Meyendorff — carried the work to America, and Schmemann's journals from 1973 to 1983 record what that cost and what it was for.

## the-coptic-monastic-and-sunday-school-revival

The Coptic Monastic & Sunday-School Revival

Two renewals ran together in the Coptic Orthodox Church and remade it. The first was catechetical. Habib Girgis, born in 1876, taught and wrote from the 1890s and built a church-wide Sunday-school movement, organized around 1918, that trained a generation of lay teachers and then of bishops. The second was monastic: from the middle of the century the desert monasteries, which had been emptying, were repopulated, and the movement gathered pace under Kyrillos VI, patriarch from 1959 to 1971. Matthew the Poor, of the monastery of St Macarius, wrote a body of spiritual work that came to be read well outside Egypt. Shenouda III, who came out of the Sunday-school generation, became patriarch in 1971 and published steadily from then to 2010.

## global-south-pentecostal-christianity

Global South Pentecostal Christianity

By the second half of the twentieth century the weight of Pentecostal faith had moved south. Most of the churches that carried it were founded in the Global South by people from there. The Assembleias de Deus in Brazil date from 1911. The Redeemed Christian Church of God was founded in Nigeria in 1952. Yoido Full Gospel Church in Seoul dates from 1958, and the Igreja Universal in Brazil from 1977. They are not one tradition. Only a close look, at one body in one country in one generation, shows anything real. In South India a Pentecostal movement grew through the twentieth century. Michael Bergunder wrote its history in 2008. The family's sharpest inside argument was over prosperity teaching. It is on the public record in the sermons that teach it. It is also in critiques by the Lausanne movement and by African evangelical writers who rejected it.

## persecuted-church-worlds-of-the-communist-era-in

Persecuted-Church Traditions of the Communist Era (Initsiativniki Baptists; Romania's Lord's Army)

Under Communist rule, believers who would not accept the terms the state offered went underground, and left an extraordinary paper trail doing it. In the Soviet Union a group of Baptist ministers broke in 1961 with the registered union over state control of church life; they were called the Initsiativniki, after their initiative group, and they ran their own press, Khristianin. Their Council of Prisoners' Relatives — the relatives of imprisoned believers — began issuing bulletins in 1964 listing arrests, sentences, searches and confiscations, name by name. Georgi Vins's Testament from Prison reached the West in translation in 1975. In Romania the Lord's Army, founded in 1923 by the priest Iosif Trifa around his weekly paper, went on under Communist rule; Traian Dorz wrote hymns and prison memoirs. When the archives opened after 1991, the persecutors' own files could be read alongside the persecuted's.

## messianic-judaism

Messianic Judaism

Jewish believers in Jesus are as old as the church, but the modern congregational movement belongs to the later twentieth century. From the 1960s and 1970s, Jewish believers began forming congregations that kept Jewish practice — Sabbath, festivals, Hebrew liturgy — rather than joining gentile churches. The Hebrew Christian Alliance of America, founded in 1915, renamed itself the Messianic Jewish Alliance of America in 1975, and the change of name was also a change of self-description. The Union of Messianic Jewish Congregations followed in 1979 and set out its position in a document called 'Defining Messianic Judaism' in 2005. Jews for Jesus, founded in 1973, took a more evangelistic shape. The movement's own writers have argued about what it is: David Stern's Messianic Jewish Manifesto appeared in 1988 and his Complete Jewish Bible in 1998, and Mark Kinzer's Postmissionary Messianic Judaism in 2005. It was argued about from outside in two directions at once, by Jewish communal bodies and in Christian debates over supersessionism.

## ecumenical-community-worlds-taize-and-iona

Ecumenical Community Traditions - Taize & Iona

Two communities, founded two years apart, built a shared life of work and prayer. George MacLeod founded the Iona Community in 1938. He set it to rebuilding the abbey on Columba's own island, a work that took until 1965. He held that worship, labor and politics belong together. Roger Schutz began the community at Taizé in France in 1940. Its Rule was written in 1954. From the 1970s young people came in large numbers. A Council of Youth met in 1974, and European meetings were held every year from 1978. Both communities are ecumenical by design. Their members come from many churches, with none of the usual boundaries between them. Brother Roger was murdered on 16 August 2005, and the community carried on under Brother Alois.

## oneness-pentecostalism

Oneness Pentecostalism

In April 1913, at the Arroyo Seco camp meeting, R. E. McAlister preached on baptism. He noted that the baptisms in the book of Acts are done in the name of Jesus. The next year Frank Ewart and Glenn Cook baptized each other on that basis. The question tore through the young Pentecostal movement. In October 1916 the Assemblies of God adopted its Statement of Fundamental Truths, a plainly trinitarian creed. The ministers who could not sign it left. Several bodies followed. The oldest is the Pentecostal Assemblies of the World, Oneness from 1916, and G. T. Haywood was among its leaders. For decades it had Black and white members together, while other Pentecostal bodies split by race. Its own telling includes the splits of 1924 and the part race played in the merger of 1945. The United Pentecostal Church International was formed by a merger vote on 25 September 1945. It printed its Articles of Faith in a Manual each year. In Mexico the Apostolic revival began in 1914. Romanita Carbajal de Valenzuela had become a Pentecostal in Los Angeles. That year she went home to Villa Aldama in Chihuahua. The Iglesia Apostólica de la Fe en Cristo Jesús grew from it.

## iglesia-ni-cristo-the-way-international-unificat

The Way International; Unification Church; La Luz del Mundo

Three separately founded bodies share this entry, but not a confession. Each is described from its own published texts. The Way International began in 1942 as Victor Paul Wierwille's Vesper Chimes radio ministry. It took its later name in 1955. His Power for Abundant Living appeared in 1971, and Jesus Christ Is Not God in 1975, a title that states its own position. Sun Myung Moon founded the Unification Church in Seoul on 1 May 1954. It was later known as the Family Federation for World Peace and Unification. Moon's Divine Principle appeared in Korean in 1966. Eusebio Joaquín González founded La Luz del Mundo in Guadalajara on 6 April 1926. From 1964 a second apostle led it, in succession from him.

## branch-davidians-and-apocalyptic-splinters

Branch Davidians & Apocalyptic Splinters

In 1929 Victor Houteff began circulating a reform message inside Adventism that his church rejected, and published it as The Shepherd's Rod in 1930 and 1932; his followers settled at Mount Carmel outside Waco, Texas, in 1935. A Branch group formed under Ben Roden in 1955 and was later led by his wife Lois. David Koresh took leadership in the 1980s. On 28 February 1993 a federal raid began a siege that ended on 19 April in a fire that killed seventy-six people, and Koresh's exposition of the seven seals was left unfinished. What was already an extreme case of authority resting on one person became known to most people through the fire — and the surviving record of the ending is largely the state's: congressional hearings, and the Danforth Report of 2000.

## progressive-christianity-a-broad-label-not-one-m

Progressive Christianity (a broad label, not one movement)

This is a label rather than a movement, and the map treats it as one. By 2010 'Progressive Christianity' was claimed by mainline congregations, by post-evangelical networks, by individual writers and by organizations with no relation to one another. The communities it covered differed from each other as much as they differed from anyone else. Some were old mainline parishes with a century of denominational history behind them. Some were congregations founded in the 2000s, and some were simply readers of particular books. Some organizations gave the label a content. The Center for Progressive Christianity, founded in 1994, published a set of points and revised it. Each version is its own dated document. But no confession, no founder and no membership covered the label as a whole. That is why this map never judges it as a category, and asks about a specific community's own published statement instead.
