# Packet S05: person and council stories (census longDescription)

## tree/liberatus-of-carthage

Liberatus of Carthage

The emperor Justinian sent two bishops to Rome to consult Pope John, called Mercurius. They came to oppose Cyrus and Eulogius, envoys of the monks called the Acoemetae. These two denied that Mary is truly and properly the Mother of God. Liberatus of Carthage writes that he was in Rome when the pope upheld this teaching by letter.

Liberatus was a deacon of the church of Carthage under Justinian. Pope Agapetus, writing to Reparatus, bishop of Carthage, says two bishops and Liberatus the deacon came to speak for Reparatus. Otto Günther dates the letter to 535. His life is known almost only from this letter and his book.

His book is the Breviarium, a short history of the quarrels over Nestorius and Eutyches. He wrote it, he says, worn out by the hardships of a journey. He wrote to answer the followers of both heresies, who do not tell the truth about their own leaders.

Liberatus blames a later fight on Theodore, bishop of Caesarea in Cappadocia. Theodore was a fierce defender of Origen, he says. Grieved when Origen was condemned, he urged the emperor to condemn Theodore of Mopsuestia and a letter of Ibas of Edessa. These were two of the Three Chapters. So Justinian wrote a book against the Three Chapters, Liberatus says, for our sins. Jean Garnier, whose edition Migne reprints, counts him among the African defenders of the Three Chapters.

The African chronicler Victor of Tunnuna writes that Reparatus would not condemn the Three Chapters, and died in exile at Euchaida. Garnier takes the journey in Liberatus's preface to be this exile, which Liberatus shared until his bishop died.

At the close he writes that bishops who agreed to condemn them were rewarded with gifts, and those who refused were deposed and exiled. Some hid in flight and found a happy end in their hardship. All this is known to everyone, he says, so he thinks he should now be silent.

## tree/longinus-bishop-of-the-nobadae

Longinus, bishop of the Nobadae

For three years Longinus was watched in Constantinople. At last, John of Ephesus writes, "he disguised himself, and put a wig on his head,—for he was very bald ;—and taking with him two servants, he fled."

Longinus was a bishop of the party that refused the council of Chalcedon. He was sent to the Nobadae, on the eastern borders of the Thebais beyond Egypt, whom the presbyter Julian had first taught. He was consecrated as soon as the patriarch Theodosius of Alexandria died. John says the empress Theodora had ordered it; his chapter heading says Theodosius did. Almost all of this comes from John, of his own party.

Among the Nobadae, Longinus built a church, ordained clergy and taught them the order of worship. Their envoy came to the emperor's court. There, John says, "in the presence of myself", he praised Longinus: "Though we were Christians in name, yet we did not really know what Christianity was until Longinus came to us."

After about five years he was called north, to help make a patriarch for his party at Alexandria. Back among the Nobadae, he went on to the Alodaei, a powerful people many score leagues beyond them. The Alexandrians sent word that he was a heretic and deposed. "We will receive him who baptized the Nobadæ," the Alodaei answered, "and by him will we be baptized."

John gives part of a letter Longinus wrote from that land. On the road, Longinus says, they all fell ill, and "we lost no less than seventeen camels." Then the king "came out in person to meet us, and received us with great joy." He baptized the king and his nobles. "For I desire neither silver, nor gold, nor dresses, as God is my witness," he writes.

John dates these things to a year R. Payne Smith, his translator, reckons as 580. No source tells of Longinus after that, or of his death.

## tree/mar-aba-i

Mar Aba I

Mar Aba and his bishops came into the district of Kashkar. Two men there had each claimed to be its bishop in the years of quarrel. At the town of Zizwarda, the record of his synod says, the people of Kashkar agreed and chose one bishop, Samuel. The two rivals lost their titles and gladly served under Samuel as priests.

Mar Aba was catholicos, head of the Church of the East in the Persian empire. The French scholar J.-B. Chabot dates his election to January 540, from the synod's own dates.

Mari ibn Sulayman, who wrote his church's history in the 1100s, says Aba had been a magus, a follower of the old Persian religion. Mari tells his conversion as a wonder. Aba refused a Christian scholar named Joseph a place in his boat on the Tigris. Each time he refused, winds held the boat back, until he took Joseph aboard.

A Life of Mar Aba survives, which Chabot says needs careful critical study. It has him baptized in the village of Akda, a student at Nisibis, a learner of Greek at Edessa, and later a teacher.

Before him, his church had two rival patriarchs, Narses and Elisha. Mari says each ordained his own bishops, so that two altars stood in every church. The synod's record opens by saying that split had wholly ended. A letter in Mar Aba's name calls marriage a bond of love in the chaste and lawful union of a man and a woman. It forbids marriage to a father's wife and other close kin, as the magi did.

The magi turned against him, Mari says, because he had been one of them and changed their customs. He spent seven years in prison in Adorbigan, Mari writes, and kept caring for his flock. He told the king, Mari says, to kill him if the king wanted him dead.

How he died is told two ways. The Life has him regain the king's favor and die of illness, as head of his church. Bar Hebraeus, of the rival Syriac Orthodox church, says the king chained him when he returned to Seleucia, and he died in prison. Chabot gives the year as 552.

The Life has him buried in the monastery of Seleucia. Mari says his disciple Qyura carried his body to Hira and built a monastery over the grave. Chabot notes that his church honors him as a saint.

## tree/marcellinus-comes

Marcellinus Comes

On a Sunday in Constantinople, two men named Marinus and Plato stood in a church pulpit and changed the hymn of the Trinity. Marcellinus Comes says they did it at the order of the emperor Anastasius. Many of those he calls orthodox kept singing the old words and shouted the new singers down. They were struck down in the church, he writes, and taken to prison, where they died.

Marcellinus tells what came next in his Latin chronicle. More were killed the next day in the court of the church of Saint Theodore. Then crowds filled the forum of Constantine. They threw down the emperor's statues and called for a man named Areobindus to rule. On the third day Anastasius sent them home with what Marcellinus calls his usual false oaths. He promised them everything and did nothing. Theodor Mommsen, who edited the chronicle, puts this in 512.

Marcellinus wrote at Constantinople, but Latin was his own tongue, Mommsen says. Cassiodorus, a Latin writer of the same age, calls him Marcellinus of Illyricum. He says Marcellinus is reported to have served Justinian as a court clerk before Justinian became emperor.

In his preface Marcellinus calls himself a count and a man of high rank. He says he followed the Eastern empire only. His book carries on the chronicle of Eusebius of Caesarea and Jerome. Mommsen dates it from 379 to 518. Later Marcellinus added sixteen more years, up to 534. He calls it "meum rusticum opus", my rustic work.

Mommsen says hatred of heretics breathes from every page, above all against the followers of Nestorius. He also notes the writer's warm support for Justinian. One of the two families of manuscripts runs on to 548. Mommsen judges that part to be by another hand. Cassiodorus does not know it, and the preface does not mention it.

In his guide to Christian reading, Cassiodorus names Marcellinus right after Eusebius and Jerome among the writers of chronicles.

## tree/marius-of-avenches

Marius of Avenches

In the year Theodor Mommsen counts as 563, Marius of Avenches writes, the mountain of Tauredunum in the Valais suddenly fell. It buried a nearby fort and villages with everyone in them, and shook the whole lake until the water broke over both shores. The flood swept away old villages with their people and cattle. It carried off the bridge at Geneva with its mills and men, then burst into the city and killed many.

Marius was bishop of Avenches, in the Burgundian lands, in the later 500s. His Chronicle carries on that of Prosper of Aquitaine from 455 to 581.

At the council of Mâcon, in King Guntram's twenty-fourth year, Marius signed as bishop of the church of Avenches. His epitaph comes down through Conon, provost of Lausanne, writing in 1235. It says he was of noble birth and a cleric from his early years. He made sacred vessels for the church and worked his lands with his own hands, it says. He fed the poor and ate sparingly himself.

From the epitaph and a church Marius dedicated, Mommsen reckons that he became bishop in 574 and died in 594. Conon gives 581 for his becoming bishop, and a later hand in the annals of Lausanne puts his death in 601. Mommsen rejects both. Andrea Gallandi, after Pierre-François Chifflet, gives his death as 596.

The bishop's seat later moved from Avenches to Lausanne. Mommsen thinks this came after Marius. Gallandi reports Charles Le Cointe's view that Marius moved it himself.

Marius wrote most about Agaune and the Valais, Mommsen says. He records that King Sigismund of Burgundy built the monastery at Agaune, and that its monks tried one night to kill their bishop, Agricola. A single manuscript keeps the Chronicle, which Mommsen says went unused in the Middle Ages.

A Lausanne cathedral book of the dead from about 1510 marked his day, Mommsen notes, with a procession to the church of Saint Marius.

## tree/martin-of-braga

Martin of Braga

Chararic, a king in Galicia, had a gravely ill son, Gregory of Tours writes in a book of the miracles of Martin of Tours. The king sent to Martin's tomb for relics. A man named Martin, from a far country, came into a Galician port with them. The son was healed, and the king confessed the unity of Father, Son and Holy Spirit.

The newcomer was Martin of Braga, a bishop among the Sueves, a people settled in Spain. C. P. Caspari, who edited his sermon, reckons he came there about 550. Isidore of Seville calls him bishop of the monastery of Dumio, which Caspari places near Braga. Gregory says he had gone East to visit the holy places, and was held second to none in learning in his time.

Once the Sueves turned from the Arian teaching to the Catholic faith, Isidore says, Martin set down a rule of faith for them. Isidore credits the change to King Theudemir, backed by Martin, while Gregory names Chararic.

At the Second Council of Braga, Martin, by then bishop of Braga, spoke first. The faith was in no doubt in this province, he told the bishops. So they should correct their own faults, his among them, by the Scriptures and the old canons.

Polemius, bishop of Astorga, asked him for something on the origin of idols, for country people who still honored demons more than God. Martin sent him a sermon to preach in country speech, On the Correction of Peasants. Some who renounced the devil at baptism, it says, now light candles at stones, trees, springs and crossroads, and that is the devil's worship.

Gregory places his death among events that Caspari and the Bollandist editors date to 580. His people mourned him greatly, Gregory says.

In his epitaph Martin speaks to Martin of Tours. Born in Pannonia, it says, he crossed vast seas to Galicia by God's will. Following his patron, he rests in the peace of Christ, the same in name but not in merit.

## tree/moses-of-khoren

Moses of Khoren

Moses of Khoren hurried home from Byzantium, hoping to dance at weddings. Instead, he writes, he found himself at a grave, weeping. His teachers, Sahak the Great and Mesrop Mashtots, had died. He had not come in time to close their eyes or hear their last words.

That lament closes his History of Armenia. Its three books tell his nation's story from its first ancestors to the deaths of those two men. He wrote it for Sahak Bagratuni, who had asked for it. Victor Langlois, his 1869 French editor, takes this patron for the Bagratid prince the Armenians made their governor in 481, in a revolt against Persia.

In the History, Moses says Sahak and Mesrop sent him to Alexandria to study. He passed through Edessa and its archives and prayed at the holy places of Palestine. In Alexandria, he says, a new Plato taught, a teacher he was not found worthy to follow. Langlois's note says he surely means Cyril of Alexandria. The Mekhitarist monks of Venice say perhaps. Contrary winds then drove him to Italy, and after a short stay in Athens he made for Byzantium.

Langlois adds what tradition tells of his later life. Moses lived ten years apart and poor. Then the patriarch Kiud made him bishop of Bagrevand after Eznik of Kolb died. Langlois warns that tradition has wrapped his life in details history has not kept. One Armenian historian he cites, Thomas Artsruni, has him living past a hundred, into the reign of Zeno.

Langlois has him born in the later fourth century and draws his life from the History itself, as the Mekhitarist preface does. Modern work argues for a later date, but the Library holds none of it. Frédéric Macler, a French scholar writing in 1917, says almost nothing precise is known of him.

In his lament Moses asks who will now value his teachers' doctrine, and who will be glad of his own progress.

## tree/peter-the-iberian

Peter the Iberian

Peter the Iberian is known only through his own disciples. No tradition on this map tells his story. He came from the Christian royal house of Kartli, in Georgia, and he led the monks near Gaza who refused Chalcedon.

His father, the Life says, was Bosmarios, king of the Iberians. As a boy of twelve Peter was sent as a hostage to the court of Theodosius II. He grew up in the palace.

He fled to Palestine with his friend John the Eunuch, and there they took up the monk's life. Peter was made a priest in 445.

After the council of 451, the monks of Palestine turned against their bishop, Juvenal, who had signed it. They chose a monk named Theodosius as their new bishop. The people of Maiuma near Gaza seized Peter and took him to Theodosius. Against his will, Theodosius made him their bishop.

When the emperor Marcian moved against them, Peter went to Egypt. At Alexandria the people carried him off to help make Timothy their bishop. Later he lived near Gaza again. He died on 1 December 488.

## tree/philoxenus-of-mabbug

Philoxenus of Mabbug

In exile at Gangra, Philoxenus of Mabbug was shut in a room above the kitchen of a hostel, and the smoke choked him. The Syriac chronicle under the name of Zachariah Rhetor says he told of it in a letter. At last he died there.

Philoxenus, first called Akhsenaya or Xenaias, was a Syriac bishop and writer of the church that refused the Council of Chalcedon. His English translator, E. A. Wallis Budge, says he was born at Tahal in Beth Garmai between about 450 and 475. In 485, by Budge's date, Peter the Fuller, bishop of Antioch, made him bishop of Mabbug, also called Hierapolis.

Theophanes the Confessor, a later Greek chronicler loyal to Chalcedon, calls him Satan's slave. He says Philoxenus was a runaway slave, not yet baptized when he was made bishop. Budge answers from Philoxenus's own words: "For I was baptized in the name of Him that died."

He fought the teaching that Christ has two natures, which he traced to Nestorius. He put ten questions to those who held it. The first asks, "If it be a demonstrable thing that Christ hath two natures, to which of them did the Virgin give birth?" Michael the Syrian says a Syriac version of the New Testament was made at Mabbug through his care.

His long fight with Flavian II of Antioch ended in 512. The chronicle under Zachariah's name, from his own side, says his zeal urged on the monks. There were wounded and dead, and Flavian was driven out. Evagrius Scholasticus, from the other side, heard from very old men that the people of Antioch killed the monks. A great many found their grave in the Orontes. A Syriac chronicle of 846 names Philoxenus among the bishops who ordained Severus of Antioch in Flavian's place.

The Chronicle of Edessa says the emperor Justin, in his second year, drove Severus from Antioch and Philoxenus from Mabbug. A letter of his, Budge says, shows he went first to Philippopolis in Thrace, then to Gangra. No early source used here gives the year he died. The editor François Nau puts the end of his time as bishop in 523.

Palestinian monks of the other side, in a letter Evagrius copies, called him "truly a stranger to God". The chronicle of 846 calls him a great teacher and a martyr.

He wrote in his Discourses, "Faith is the foundation and the architect".

## tree/primasius-of-hadrumetum

Primasius of Hadrumetum

In Constantinople, an African bishop met a fellow countryman, Junillus. The first thing he asked, as was his way, was whether any Greek there burned to study the holy books. Junillus tells this in Latin, in the letter that opens his handbook on the Bible. He told the bishop of a Persian named Paul, taught in the school at Nisibis. The bishop pressed him to write down Paul's rules for reading Scripture, until Junillus gave in.

The bishop was Primasius of Hadrumetum, in the African province of Byzacena. He had come, the German scholar Heinrich Kihn reckons, over the Three Chapters, writings the emperor Justinian wanted the church to condemn. Victor of Tunnuna says the emperor summoned him there with other African bishops. Victor's editor, Theodor Mommsen, gives the year as 551.

In May 553, as the Second Council of Constantinople met, he signed the ruling of Pope Vigilius on the Three Chapters. His signature survives. It names his city as Hadrumetum, also called Justinianopolis.

The rest is told only by Victor of Tunnuna, who defended the Three Chapters and tells it as betrayal. Victor says Primasius was sent to a monastery. When Boethius, the head bishop of Byzacena, died, Primasius agreed to condemn the Three Chapters so that he might take his place. Back home he persecuted those who held what he had once held. His own province's bishops condemned him, and he died an unhappy death. Kihn, writing in 1880, warned that Victor wrote as a party man.

His five books on the Revelation of John survive, as Cassiodorus, a writer of his own time, describes them. In the preface, Primasius says he drew on Augustine of Hippo and also on Tyconius, a Donatist, and cut away his errors. A precious gem lying in dung, he wrote, should be picked up and restored to its worth. In the same way, wherever truth shines, it should be won back for the Catholic Church.

## tree/procopius-of-caesarea

Procopius of Caesarea

Two envoys came from Constantinople to the chief priest of Rome, as Procopius of Caesarea calls the pope. They came about a point of faith that Christians quarreled over. He knew the points well, he writes, but would not record them. "I consider it a sort of insane folly to investigate the nature of God," he explains. Of God he will say only that "He is altogether good and has all things in His power."

Procopius was born at Caesarea in Palestine, late in the fifth century, says H. B. Dewing, his English translator. Procopius trained in law and went to Constantinople. He says himself that he was chosen as adviser to the general Belisarius in the war with Persia. He followed Belisarius to Africa and Italy, and saw much of what he tells.

Dewing notes the calm tone of the History of the Wars. It tells how Belisarius suspected Silverius, the chief priest of Rome, of plotting with the Goths. He sent him away to Greece and put Vigilius in his place. The Buildings praises Justinian's churches. Procopius writes that the dome of Hagia Sophia seemed "suspended from heaven by the fabled golden chain." Dewing says the Buildings is written in the style of a fawning flatterer, but is still a storehouse of facts.

The Secret History attacks the emperor, the empress Theodora and Belisarius. Dewing finds its slander too extreme to believe. The unnamed English translator of 1896 thinks it exaggerates but gives a faithful picture of the court. Some have doubted that Procopius wrote it. Dewing says it is now believed highly probable that he did. The 1896 translator notes that the first writers to name him as its author lived centuries later.

Dewing says Procopius writes at times as a Christian and at times like a man steeped in the old religion of Greece. The 1896 translator thinks he was a skeptic who used Christian forms under an orthodox emperor.

## tree/reccared

Reccared

King Reccared came among the bishops gathered at Toledo and prayed with them, the council's acts say. Not many days after his father's death, he reminded them, they had learned he had joined the Catholic faith. All he had to say of his faith was written in a book, to be read before them. The king offered it, and a notary read it out in a clear voice.

Reccared was king of the Visigoths in Spain, the son of King Leovigild. The acts date this Third Council of Toledo to the eighth of May in his fourth year. Their Latin is told here in modern words.

The book speaks as the king, though O. M. Dalton, translating Gregory of Tours, says Bishop Leander of Seville composed the speech. God had given him many peoples to rule, it says, yet he remembered he was mortal. It confesses Father, Son and Spirit as one substance.

God stirred the king, burning with faith, to call back a people who served error under the name of religion. The whole Gothic people was there, it says. A great host of Sueves, brought under his kingdom, had also been called back. He offered these noble peoples to God through the bishops' hands, as a holy sacrifice. It condemns Arius and all his teaching. Then Reccared signed, holding this faith in his heart, he wrote, and affirming it with his mouth.

All the witnesses here write from the Catholic side. John of Biclaro, a chronicler of the time, had been exiled by Leovigild. He says Reccared took the throne in peace. In the tenth month of his first year, John says, Reccared became Catholic. Gregory of Tours, a Frankish bishop, tells instead of a debate between the bishops of both sides. Pope Gregory the Great credits Leander's counsel and the merits of Reccared's brother Hermenegild, whom Gregory calls a martyr.

Gregory wrote to Reccared, "I cannot express in words, most excellent son, how much I am delighted with thy work and thy life." Jews offered him money over a law against them, Gregory had heard. Reccared "preferred innocence to gold".

Isidore of Seville, Leander's brother, wrote that Leovigild was irreligious and quick to war, and his son pious in faith and famed for peace. At the end Reccared made a public confession of penance, and died peacefully at Toledo after reigning fifteen years.

## tree/romanos-the-melodist

Romanos the Melodist

One Christmas night, a book of saints says, the Mother of God came to Romanos the Melodist in his sleep. She handed him a roll of paper and told him to take it and eat it. He woke full of wonder, went up into the pulpit and began to sing of the Virgin giving birth that day.

The story comes from one short notice in a Menologion, a book of saints for the church year. Cardinal Jean-Baptiste Pitra, who printed it in 1888, calls it the Menologion of the emperor Constantine Porphyrogenitus. Migne's edition of Theodore the Studite gives the same Vatican manuscript to the emperor Basil. Pitra says almost nothing more is recorded of Romanos anywhere.

The notice says Romanos came from Syria and was a deacon of the church at Berytus, now Beirut. He came to Constantinople in the time of the emperor Anastasius. He lived at a church of the Mother of God and kept night vigils at the shrine of Blachernae. He wrote about a thousand kontakia, long sung poems for the feasts, and then died. A later Greek book of saints, revised by Nikodemos the Hagiorite, names his home city as Emesa.

Pitra asks which Anastasius is meant, the first or the second, and looks for clues in one of the poems. He says it is not at all clear that Romanos lived under the second, who reigned from 713 to 716.

Pitra printed three poems from manuscripts on the island of Patmos: on Stephen the first martyr, on Joseph the chaste, and on the ten virgins. He set a Latin prose version beside the Greek, and the words here are from it. In two, Pitra's headings say, the first letters of the verses name the author as humble Romanos.

Each verse of the poem on the ten virgins ends with the plea of those left outside the bridal chamber. In Pitra's Latin it is "Aperi nobis": open to us.

## tree/ruricius-of-limoges

Ruricius of Limoges

Bishops of Gaul met at Agde under Caesarius of Arles, in 506, and Ruricius of Limoges did not come. Caesarius wrote that he had been deeply troubled by the absence. Ruricius's letter had gone to Agde with a deacon of Verus, bishop of Tours, but Caesarius did not recall receiving it. Ruricius should, he wrote, have sent someone to sign in his place. Yet Caesarius held nothing against him.

Ruricius answered, in modern words, "Infirmity did this, not will." Caesarius himself, he wrote, had seen him worn out at Bordeaux, and in winter, when he was usually stronger.

Ruricius was bishop of Limoges in Gaul. August Engelbrecht, who edited his letters with those of Faustus of Riez, dates his election to 485 from one of Faustus's letters. Venantius Fortunatus wrote an epitaph for him and for his grandson, also bishop of Limoges. It joins the two to the Anicii, a great house of Rome. Sidonius Apollinaris wrote the poem for his wedding to Iberia.

Faustus, Engelbrecht says, drew him to the religious life. Ruricius's first letter to Faustus says he had long known him by fame. "My soul has clung to you, outstanding teacher," he writes, in modern words, borrowing a psalm. He asks that Faustus's letters be harsher food, fit for a sinner's sickness, in place of the sweet dishes sent so far. From exile Faustus rejoiced that Ruricius had turned the prow of salvation toward the harbor of religion.

Two books of his letters survive. Several speak of his poor health. Engelbrecht thinks he died not long after 507, when he was too frail to reach a council at Toulouse. Fortunatus says each Ruricius founded a church in his time, one to Augustine and one to Peter.

Limoges was a small city. Ruricius wrote to Caesarius, in modern words, "It is far better and far more distinguished for a city to become known by its bishop than a bishop by his city."

## tree/samson-of-dol

Samson of Dol

In Cornwall, in a district called Tricurius, Samson of Dol heard people worshipping an idol with a play. On a hilltop stood their image. He went to them with two of his monks and told them to leave it. The writer of his Life adds that he has stood on that hill himself. With his own hand, he says, he traced the cross Samson had carved with an iron tool on a standing stone.

Samson was a British monk and bishop who founded the monastery of Dol in Brittany. His Life says he was taken as a boy to the school of Eltut, the most learned of the Britons. He became an abbot, sailed to Ireland, and was made a bishop. Then he crossed to Brittany and founded Dol and many other monasteries. He died and was buried at Dol.

The Life is a book of miracles, with a dove at his ordination and serpents driven away. Its writer gives no name. He says he wrote for Bishop Tigernomalus, from what an old man, a cousin of Samson, told him, and from accounts the deacon Henoc brought.

When he wrote is disputed. Thomas Taylor, who put the Life into English, accepts 610 to 615. He follows Abbé Duine, who found nothing borrowed from writings later than the 500s. Taylor reports that the scholar Fawtier called the work spurious and rejected what its writer says of its authorship and origin. Duine and Professor Loth answered him.

A second witness stands beside the Life. Friedrich Maassen dates a council at Paris between 556 and 573. One of its bishops signed, "Samson subscripsi et consensi in nomine Christi": I, Samson, have signed and agreed, in the name of Christ. It names no see, and Maassen's note gives Dol with a question mark.

Taylor says later Lives of saints tried to outdo its miracles. The Life, he writes, "actually furnished the mould into which many of them were cast."

## tree/second-council-of-constantinople-553

Second Council of Constantinople

Before the council of 553, the question was put at Constantinople whether it was right to curse the dead. Eutychius, envoy of the bishop of Amasea, said it needed no debate. He pointed to King Josiah, who "broke up the sepulchres of those who had long been dead." The emperor Justinian soon made him the city's bishop.

Evagrius of Epiphania, a historian on Chalcedon's side, tells this decades later. Justinian wanted the Three Chapters condemned. One was Theodore of Mopsuestia, with his writings. Another was what Theodoret of Cyrrhus wrote against Cyril of Alexandria. The third was a letter said to be by Ibas of Edessa.

The emperor's letter to the council says the followers of Nestorius used Theodore to bring in their errors. Facundus of Hermiane, an African bishop, wrote in the Chapters' defense. Chalcedon's enemies were behind the case, he said, and Chalcedon had judged the letter of Ibas catholic.

The council's acts survive, the record of the side that won, and are told here from English extracts. Henry Percival, their editor, notes that some of the most damaging lines about Pope Vigilius are in one Paris manuscript alone.

The council opened on 5 May 553, by the dating of Charles Joseph Hefele, the historian of the councils. Hefele says Eutychius presided. Vigilius was in the city, but refused the emperor's repeated calls to come.

On 14 May Vigilius sent Justinian his ruling, signed with sixteen bishops. He did not dare condemn Theodore, he wrote, nor would he let anyone else. In a session given in full only in the Paris manuscript, the emperor ordered that the pope's name no longer be read in church. The council's final sentence curses the Chapters: "We therefore anathematize the Three Chapters."

In a December letter to Eutychius, Vigilius gave way. Percival says most scholars accept it as his. Vigilius died in Sicily, going home. Victor of Tunnuna, an African bishop exiled for the Chapters, tells of others sent to Egypt for opposing the decrees.

Jérôme Labourt, a French historian of the Persian church, reads the creed of its synod of 585 as an answer to the council's backers.

Gregory the Great of Rome said he honored the fifth council. But writing to Milan, he called it the synod "called by many the fifth." Nothing was done in it, he said, "with respect to the faith, but only with respect to persons."

## tree/severinus-of-noricum

Severinus of Noricum

On the fifth of January, Severinus of Noricum felt a pain in his side. Three days later he called his monks to him at midnight. He forbade them to weep, made the sign of the cross over his body, and told them to sing a psalm. Grief kept them silent, so he began it himself, "Praise ye the Lord in his sanctuary". On the eighth of January, Eugippius writes, "while we could hardly make the responses, he fell asleep in the Lord."

Eugippius, a monk of his community, wrote this Life from the elder brothers' daily stories. He gives no year for the death, which his translator George W. Robinson puts about 482, some thirty years before the Life. It is a saint's Life, full of wonders.

Severinus was a monk among the towns of Riverside Noricum, on the Danube, often raided by barbarians. Eugippius says he came there from the East about the time Attila died. When the Italian priest Primenius asked his country, Severinus said a servant of God gains nothing by naming it, since silence guards him from vainglory. Eugippius heard in his speech a man of purest Latin stock.

He urged the towns to fasting, prayer and alms, and refused to be made a bishop. The young Odoacer, later king of Italy, came to his cell in wretched hides. "Go forth to Italy!" Severinus told him. Giso, queen of the Rugians, tried to rebaptize some Catholics, but her husband, King Feva, would not allow it, out of reverence for Severinus.

He told his monks to take his bones when the Romans left, as Joseph asked of Israel. When Odoacer's brother ordered the Romans to Italy, the monks did so. With Pope Gelasius's leave, Bishop Victor laid him to rest at Naples, in the Lucullan castle.

Eugippius sent the book to the deacon Paschasius, who added nothing. The virtues of teachers, Paschasius wrote back, "are more easily depicted by their pupils."

## tree/severus-of-antioch

Severus of Antioch

In the first year of his reign, the emperor Justin ordered the arrest of Severus, bishop of Antioch. Evagrius of Epiphania, a later historian on the side of the Council of Chalcedon, says some reported that Severus was to lose his tongue. He fled, and E. W. Brooks, the English translator of his letters, dates the flight to 29 September 518. Evagrius seems to give 519, Brooks notes, but Severus's sermons, kept in date order, decide it.

Severus was born at Sozopolis in Pisidia. His fellow student Zachariah Rhetor wrote a Life of him. It says his widowed mother sent him and his two older brothers to Alexandria to study grammar and rhetoric. Later he studied law at Berytus, now Beirut. Brooks says Zachariah wrote to answer enemies who claimed Severus had followed pagan rites in his youth. In Brooks's view, "the answer is a very lame one."

Zachariah says he was baptized at the shrine of the martyr Leontius at Tripolis. He became a monk between Gaza and Majuma, where Peter the Iberian had lived. Nephalius, who had once shared his view and then taken Chalcedon's side, drove him and other monks out. Severus pleaded their cause at Constantinople and won the notice of the emperor Anastasius. Evagrius tells this from a Life of Severus, which Brooks takes for Zachariah's.

He replaced Flavian as bishop of Antioch in 512, Brooks says. Evagrius says his letters as bishop condemned Chalcedon.

After his flight he lived mostly in hiding in Egypt. There, Brooks says, he wrote against Julian of Halicarnassus, who held that Christ's body could not decay. Evagrius says the empress Theodora, who favored the single nature, persuaded Justinian to send for Severus. Brooks says he and Anthimus of Constantinople were expelled from the city in 536 and condemned at a synod that May. Evagrius says Justinian then cursed him by law. Brooks gives his death in Egypt as 8 February 538.

To the chamberlain Misael he wrote that, when he was raised to the see, he wished "not to know even the very name" of interest. Then he saw the church's debts. "I forgot the spiritual laws," he admits.

Brooks reckons his letters once numbered at least 3,759; a Syriac version made in 669 keeps 123. In the letter to Misael, Severus says God alone is "able to heal our church that is groaning under many wounds, and to change need into abundance."

## tree/simeon-of-beth-arsham

Simeon of Beth Arsham

Simeon was a bishop of the Syriac Orthodox side, though he worked inside the Persian empire. John of Ephesus wrote his life. He says Simeon was a Persian, and that he fought the school of Nestorius all his life. Wherever a debate was planned, Simeon would turn up, even from days away.

Once the bishops of the Church of the East called his side to a debate. The judge was a Persian governor. Their leader was the catholicos Babai, who held a synod in 497. Babai told the governor that Simeon's party were the king's enemies. After the debate, his own side made Simeon bishop of Beth Arsham by force.

In 524 he was at the camp of the Arab king Mundhir, with an envoy of the emperor Justin. There he heard of the killing of the Christians of Najran, in Himyar. At Nu'man's camp he met a Christian envoy of the late king of Himyar. He wrote it all down in a letter. He asked that the bishop of Alexandria be told, so that he could ask the king of the Ethiopians to help.

John of Ephesus says Simeon died in Constantinople.

## tree/theodora-wife-of-justinian

Theodora, wife of Justinian

Mare, the exiled bishop of Amida, sent his deacon Stephen from Petra to plead at court. John of Ephesus says God led Stephen to "Theodora who came from the brothel." She was then a patrician, married to Justinian, the emperor's nephew. She begged her husband with tears to ask his uncle for relief. An order came, and the exiles moved to Alexandria.

John wrote from the side she sheltered. R. Payne Smith, who put John's Church History into English, notes that John belonged to the party that refused the Council of Chalcedon. Procopius of Caesarea wrote as her enemy in his Secret History. He says her father kept the bears for the Green faction at Byzantium, and that she went on the stage as a girl. The rest he tells as scandal. H. B. Dewing, who translated his Wars, says we are not forced to believe it. The twelfth-century Michael the Syrian, a patriarch of John's church, made her a priest's daughter from Daman, near Callinicus, raised in chastity.

In the Wars, Procopius gives Theodora a speech in the Nika riot of 532, as the court weighed flight by sea. She ends by approving an ancient saying, "royalty is a good burial-shroud." The words are Procopius's own telling. Dewing notes his love of sounding phrases in speeches.

Evagrius of Epiphania, a later historian on Chalcedon's side, says Justinian upheld the council and Theodora backed the single nature of Christ. Evagrius offers two reasons: true conviction, or an agreement between them. Procopius's Secret History calls it a plan to divide the Christians. Evagrius adds that she persuaded Justinian to send for Severus of Antioch.

John tells what she did for his side. When Justinian raged at Anthimus, who had laid down his office as patriarch of Constantinople, she hid him in her palace for years. She gathered no fewer than five hundred persecuted monks into the palace of Hormisdas. In the sixteenth year of Justinian's reign, the Saracen king Hereth Bar Gabala asked her for bishops. She gave the order, and James was made bishop for Edessa and Theodore for Hirtha of the Saracens.

Michael the Syrian says she died in the twentieth year of Justinian's reign. E. W. Brooks, John's English translator, gives 548. John says she left the emperor a charge to care for her monks.

John calls her the Christ-loving Theodora, "perhaps appointed queen by God to be a support for the persecuted."

## tree/theodosius-i-of-alexandria

Theodosius I of Alexandria

When the patriarch Timothy died, the new bishop kept watch over the body by night. By custom at Alexandria, the successor laid the dead man's right hand on his own head. Then he buried him and put on the pallium of Saint Mark. But before Theodosius could finish, the people and the monks learned what had been done. They drove him out. So says Liberatus of Carthage, a deacon on the side of Chalcedon who wrote while Theodosius still lived.

Theodosius I of Alexandria was patriarch of the church in Egypt that refused the council of Chalcedon. He spent most of his time as patriarch in exile. The Syriac chronicle under the name of Zachariah Rhetor, from his own side, calls him learned, kind and gentle, and a friend of Severus of Antioch. A rival, Gaianus, was set up in his place, though that chronicle puts Gaianus first. The empress Theodora had Theodosius restored. Liberatus says her chamberlain Narses did it by force and fire, and that the city split into Theodosians and Gaianites.

The History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria is a later history written in his own church. It tells how Justinian offered him the governorship of Egypt if he would accept the Tome of Leo. He refused. "The prince has no power except over my body," he says there, "but the Lord Jesus Christ, the true and great prince, has power over my soul and body together."

Summoned to Constantinople, he still refused Chalcedon. John of Ephesus, of his own party, says he and about three hundred bishops and clergy were held at Dercus, a fortress in Thrace. John often visited them there. He would not ordain outside his own see, John says, but by his mandate twelve bishops were made for Egypt. With his permission, Jacob Baradaeus made bishops at Alexandria.

Two of his letters survive in that chronicle. To Anthimus of Constantinople he wrote, "For I, who am feeble, judge that it is on account of my shortcomings that I endure all the troubles which befall me." He accepted, he said, the faith of Nicaea and the emperor Zeno's formula uniting the churches, which aimed to annul Chalcedon.

He died at Constantinople, John says, nine months after Justinian. E. W. Brooks, John's translator, puts that in 566. B. Evetts, who translated the History, gives 567. The History says believers took the name Theodosians "after his name".

## tree/theopistus

Theopistus, deacon of Dioscorus

The emperor Marcian's letter was being read out in Alexandria, the History of Dioscorus says. Its narrator came up from behind and looked at it. He read in it the names of men the emperor praised as his counselors, men he calls evil. He went back to his bishop and said, in the words of the sons of the prophets, that there was death in this letter.

The narrator names himself Theopistus, a disciple of Dioscorus of Alexandria. In the book a brother calls him the deacon of Dioscorus. François Nau, who put the Syriac into French, notes one claim the narrator makes. From the summons to the Council of Chalcedon, by his own account, he never left Dioscorus until the bishop died in exile. He tells how a heretic slapped him for begging an envoy not to mock the old man.

Two months after the death, the book says, the archdeacon Peter wrote telling him to flee and hide. He says he hid in the city of Pentapolis and wrote this history there.

Nau doubts the book is still as Theopistus wrote it. In one of Dioscorus's visions, a saint blesses a small child named Severus. Nau reads this as a sign that Severus will be patriarch of Antioch. Severus took that see in 512, so Nau puts the present text after that year. He thinks Theopistus may have written an account of the council and the exile. It may have been among the stories in Dioscorus's favor written in Egypt soon after his death. After 512, he suggests, a writer trained in rhetoric, of the school of Peter the Iberian, reworked it, probably in Greek. That Greek, he thinks, was later put into Syriac with some further changes.

Near the end the narrator testifies before God that he has written the truth.

## tree/timothy-aelurus

Timothy Aelurus

When news of the emperor Marcian's death reached Alexandria, the people and the monks seized a priest named Timothy. They brought him to the great church called Caesarian to make him their bishop. The canons wanted three bishops for that, and only two were there. So they found Peter the Iberian in the city and carried him on their shoulders, not letting him touch the ground.

This is how the Syriac chronicle known as that of Zachariah of Mitylene tells it, from the side that rejected Chalcedon. It says Timothy, a man of ascetic life, had been brought from the wilderness by force to Cyril of Alexandria, who made him a priest. He was "of the same faith as Dioscorus," the bishop of Alexandria whom Chalcedon had deposed. He was so thin from self-denial that the party of Proterius, Chalcedon's bishop in the city, called him "the Weasel." Evagrius of Epiphania calls him Aelurus, which E. Walford's English gives as "the Cat."

Then Proterius was killed. A petition to the emperor Leo from the Egyptian bishops and the clergy of Alexandria, which Evagrius quotes, blames Timothy. Evagrius, from that report, says some Alexandrians killed Proterius in the baptistery "at the instigation of Timotheus." Zachariah says a Roman soldier stabbed him.

Zachariah gives a petition Timothy sent to the emperor. In it he says, "I do not agree with the transactions of Chalcedon, because I find in them divisions and cleavage of the dispensation."

Zachariah says the clergy of Proterius's party dragged Timothy from the font where he had fled. He was sent to Gangra and then to Cherson. In exile, it says, he wrote against the followers of Eutyches as well as the Nestorians. A long work of his against Chalcedon and the letter of Leo of Rome comes down to us in Armenian.

The emperor Basiliscus called him back. Evagrius says it was in the eighteenth year of his exile, and Zachariah after eighteen years. The History of the Patriarchs of the Coptic Church gives seven years. Zachariah says he brought home the bones of Dioscorus in a silver coffin. When the emperor Zeno meant to expel him, Evagrius says, he was told Timothy was already old and near death. He died soon after.

He had told the emperor that the faith of Nicaea needed nothing more. In his words, "neither does the definition of the faith which the fathers proclaimed require addition."

## tree/venantius-fortunatus

Venantius Fortunatus

Venantius Fortunatus and his friend Felix of Treviso suffered a grievous disease of the eyes. Paul the Deacon, the Lombard historian, tells what they did at Ravenna. They went to the church of Saints Paul and John. There a lamp burned in a window beside an altar of Martin of Tours. They touched their eyes with its oil, and at once the disease was gone. Fortunatus's poem says Martin once gave the light back to Felix and to him.

Fortunatus was a poet from northern Italy. Paul says he was born near Ceneda and Treviso, and trained at Ravenna. For love of Martin he left Italy for the saint's tomb at Tours, a little before the Lombards invaded. The German scholar Bruno Krusch puts his coming to Gaul in 565.

He stayed at Poitiers, Paul says. Radegund, once the wife of King Chlothar the First, lived there in her monastery of the Holy Cross. Fortunatus wrote poems to her and to its abbess, Agnes. He called Agnes a mother in honor and a sister in sweet love, and said his love was of the spirit, with Christ as witness.

He wrote verse for kings and bishops, and Lives of saints. Gregory of Tours, who calls him his venerable fellow servant, says he told Martin's life in four books of verse. O. M. Dalton, Gregory's English translator, says that when Radegund's house received a relic of the Cross, Fortunatus marked it with hymns. One begins "Vexilla Regis prodeunt", in plain words, the banners of the King go forward.

Paul says he was ordained at Poitiers, first priest and then bishop. Baudonivia, a nun of Holy Cross, also calls him bishop. Dalton notes that Gregory never calls him bishop. Dalton thinks Fortunatus became bishop only after Gregory died.

Paul came to his grave to pray. At the request of Aper, the abbot, he wrote an epitaph: "Famous in talent, quick to perceive and in speech ever gentle."
