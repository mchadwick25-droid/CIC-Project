# Packet L05: person and council stories (census longDescription)

## paulinus-of-milan

Paulinus of Milan

A few days before Ambrose of Milan took to his bed, he was dictating the forty-third Psalm. Paulinus of Milan was taking it down. Suddenly, Paulinus writes, "a fire in the shape of a small shield covered his head," and entered his mouth. The bishop's face became like snow. Paulinus froze and could not write until the vision had passed.

Paulinus tells this in his Life of Ambrose, written at the request of Augustine of Hippo. He had lived in Ambrose's household. Later he moved to Africa, "in which we now live and write." Sister Mary Simplicia Kaniecka, who translated the Life in 1928, says little else is known of him.

Paulinus drew on men who served Ambrose before him, and on Ambrose's sister Marcellina. He begged readers to trust him, "since indeed it is better to say nothing at all than to set forth something false." Kaniecka calls it a saint's life, written to build up the reader, and says it pays no heed to dates.

At Carthage in 412, by B. B. Warfield's date, a deacon named Paulinus brought a written charge against Caelestius, a follower of Pelagius. Marius Mercator, a Latin writer against the Pelagians, calls the accuser a deacon of Ambrose. Kaniecka takes him to be the author of the Life. Augustine quotes the council's record. Caelestius said a presbyter at Rome had denied that sin passes down from Adam, and others said the same. "Tell us their names," Paulinus pressed. "Is not one priest enough for you?" Caelestius answered. The council condemned him.

In 417 Zosimus, bishop of Rome, heard Caelestius again and called Paulinus to Rome. Paulinus sent a short petition instead, which survives in Latin. In it he thanks Zosimus for ordering Caelestius to condemn what Paulinus had charged. What he had always wanted for Caelestius, he writes, was his correction.

He closed the Life by asking Augustine to pray for "the most humble man and sinner Paulinus."

## pelagius

Pelagius

About 413, Pelagius wrote to Demetrias, a young Roman noblewoman who had vowed herself to Christ and given up marriage. God made human nature good and free to choose, he told her, and her birth and wealth were her family's. "But spiritual riches no one can give you except yourself. In these, then, you are rightly praised. In these you are rightly set above others. They cannot exist except from you and in you."

Pelagius, a monk from Britain who taught at Rome, held that God made human nature able, by God's gift, to choose the good and to live without sin. Bishops in Palestine cleared him in 415. But Augustine of Hippo answered him book by book, and in 418 bishops in North Africa and the bishop of Rome condemned him over grace, God's help freely given. The teaching that bears his name was ruled out of the church.

He lived long at Rome, where Caelestius became his follower. About 411 he crossed to Africa, then sailed on to Palestine. Apart from Pelagius's own letter and answers, his story comes to us mostly through Augustine and others who wrote against him.

In December 415, two bishops from Gaul charged him in writing but could not come. Fourteen Greek-speaking bishops heard him through an interpreter, at Diospolis. Some charges were Caelestius's views: that Adam's sin harmed only Adam, and newborn babies are as Adam was before he sinned. Pelagius said these were not his, and condemned them. Yet a man can be without sin, he said, and keep God's commandments if he wishes. The bishops declared him a member in good standing of the church. Augustine, reading the record, granted that the judges were right on what they heard, but to him Pelagius did not appear cleared.

Augustine reports, as something said, that followers of Pelagius had beaten the monks and nuns in Jerome's care at Bethlehem, killed a deacon and burned their houses. Early in 417, at the African bishops' urging, Innocent, the bishop of Rome, condemned the Pelagians, as his followers came to be called.

Pelagius wrote to Innocent that he always added the help of God's grace. The next bishop of Rome, Zosimus, had letters from him read aloud. In September 417 he asked the Africans, "Is there any passage in which God's grace or help has been left out?" Some who heard them, he wrote, could barely hold back their tears that men of such complete faith had been slandered. For a few months, Rome itself took his side.

Writing with a fellow bishop, Augustine warned Demetrias's mother against Pelagius's letter. "'These good things can only be in you': that was very well and very truly said. That is clearly food. But when he says 'only from you', that is wholly poison." Goodness might live in her, they meant, but did not come from her.

On 30 April 418 the emperor ordered Caelestius and Pelagius driven from Rome, and anyone spreading their teaching tried and, if convicted, exiled. Pelagius was then in the East. The next day some two hundred African bishops met at Carthage. God's grace, they ruled, does not only forgive past sins, and does not only show us what to do: it helps us do it. Rome, under Zosimus, condemned him too, Augustine says. That year Augustine judged that Pelagius made grace mean teaching, forgiveness and Christ's example, and never help to the will itself.

The sentence held. By 429, a chronicler says, a Pelagian was spreading the teaching in Britain's churches, and Germanus of Auxerre was sent against it. About 430 John Cassian, a monk in Gaul, charged the bishop of Constantinople with the Pelagians' teaching. Both, he wrote, made Christ a mere man who lived without sin, so others could too. In 431 the council at Ephesus wrote to Rome upholding the sentence on the Pelagians, Pelagius among them.

The sources we have do not say where or when Pelagius died. But his letter to Demetrias outlived him. It long passed as Jerome's, and was printed among the works of Jerome, who had written against the Pelagians.

## philostorgius

Philostorgius

At twenty, Philostorgius came to Constantinople and saw Eunomius of Cyzicus, the leader of his party. In his history he praised the man's looks and speech. He admitted that Eunomius lisped, then made even the lisp a sign of grace. Photius, a later patriarch of Constantinople, summed up the history. He scorned it. Philostorgius, he says, "is not ashamed to exalt this lisp into a mark of great grace and elegance."

His book is lost. It survives through Photius's summary, and Photius was hostile throughout. He calls Philostorgius an "impious contriver of lies."

Through Photius, Philostorgius tells of his family. His mother's father, Anysius, was a priest at Borissus in Cappadocia. His mother, Eulampia, came from families who held "the consubstantial faith" of Nicaea. His father, Carterius, followed Eunomius and won her over. She then won "her brothers, then her father," and in time the rest of her family.

Photius names Aetius of Antioch and Eunomius as leaders of those who held that the Son is unlike the Father in substance. Philostorgius praised them both. He rated Aetius higher in argument, and Eunomius higher for "a style well suited to learners." He even faulted Arius, whom he praised, for teaching that God cannot be known, "not even by His own only-begotten Son." He wrote a book in praise of Eunomius, and another against Porphyry of Tyre "on behalf of the Christians."

His history ran in twelve books, from the quarrel of Arius with Alexander of Alexandria to 425, by Edward Walford's date. The first letters of the books spelled his name. Walford, who translated the summary, notes his love of far lands and wonders.

A later Greek dictionary keeps a passage in his own words. In it he praises three foes of his party, Basil of Caesarea, Gregory of Nazianzus and Apollinaris of Laodicea. They argued so well for the Nicene faith, he says, that "Athanasius must be judged a mere child in comparison with them."

## possidius-of-calama

Possidius of Calama

The Vandals had shut Hippo in by land and sea. Possidius of Calama and other bishops had taken refuge there with Augustine of Hippo. One day at table, Augustine told them what he asked of God. He asked God to free the city, or to make his servants brave to bear his will. Or else, he said, "that He may take me from this world unto Himself." In the third month of the siege, Augustine fell ill with fever.

Possidius tells this in his Life of Augustine, in Herbert Weiskotten's 1919 translation. He had lived beside Augustine, he says, "for almost forty years." He wrote to build up the church, and set down "the things that I have seen in him and heard from him."

In 408 pagans at Calama stoned the church three times, then tried to burn it, Augustine writes. Its bishop forced himself into a crevice, "in which he lay folded double," while they hunted him. Augustine does not name him there. A later letter to the same man, Nectarius of Calama, points to Possidius. He told Nectarius that Possidius loved the town's people in a way more likely to do them good than his own. To another bishop he wrote that in Possidius "you will find one who is very much the same as myself."

At the Carthage conference of 411, Possidius of Calama was one of seven bishops named to speak for the Catholic side.

Possidius was with Augustine when he died in 430, "while we stood by and watched and prayed." He wrote the Life and added a list of Augustine's books, so readers could seek copies in the library at Hippo. Prosper of Aquitaine names a Posidius among the bishops whom the Vandal king Geiseric drove from their cities in 437. Weiskotten takes him to be the same man.

Possidius closed by asking readers to pray that he might "continue to emulate and imitate him in this world."

## priscillian

Priscillian

Priscillian carried a book to Damasus, bishop of Rome. In it he wrote what he and his friends had always urged in the churches. "No one should be forbidden, if he despises parents, children, wealth and rank, even his own life, and would rather love God than the world."

A rich and learned layman of Spain, Priscillian called men and women to that strict life, and became bishop of Abila. Other bishops accused him of heresy, and an emperor who had seized the throne had him tried. In about 385 he was put to the sword, and his followers honored him as a martyr.

Nearly all we know of him comes from Sulpicius Severus, a writer from Gaul. Sulpicius called the teaching the heresy of the Gnostics, a heresy of secret knowledge, and repeats that Priscillian was believed to have practiced magic from boyhood. Yet he adds: "My own view is that the accused displease me as much as the accusers."

In his own account, Priscillian and his friends gave themselves wholly to God after baptism, because Scripture says that whoever loves anyone more than God cannot be his disciple. The same book gives his creed and curses every heresy, the followers of the Persian teacher Mani above all. Nobles, common people and many women came to him, Sulpicius says. The bishop of Emerita, he adds, harassed them without restraint, and only fanned the fire.

Bishops met at Saragossa, where, a modern historian thinks, they banned Sunday fasting and barred women from private meetings. Sulpicius says Priscillian was condemned in his absence as a heretic, still a layman. Priscillian wrote that no charge was laid against his group there, and reminded Damasus that Damasus's own letter forbade judging the absent and unheard.

His enemies won an order from the emperor that drove all heretics from the cities and beyond his lands. They asked for it, Priscillian wrote, against false bishops and Mani's followers. Priscillian went to Rome to clear his name: in a cause of faith, he wrote, he preferred the judgment of holy men to the world's. But Damasus would not even see him, and at Milan Ambrose was just as opposed.

The emperor Maximus came to Trier, where Bishop Ithacius accused Priscillian before him. At a council at Bordeaux, Priscillian appealed from the bishops to the emperor, to escape their judgment, Sulpicius says. Ithacius, he adds, called anyone who loved reading or fasting Priscillian's disciple. Martin, bishop of Tours, begged the emperor not to shed their blood, since the bishops' sentence was enough.

After Martin left, a prefect heard Priscillian twice and found him guilty of sorcery. Sulpicius says Priscillian did not deny studying obscene teachings, holding night meetings of women, and praying naked. These are the court's findings; no account from his side survives. The emperor ordered death. Priscillian was killed with several companions.

His followers, Sulpicius says, now revered him as a martyr. They carried the bodies home to Spain for grand funerals. Fifteen years later, Spanish bishops at Toledo still heard of clergy calling him a holy martyr.

The deaths split the other side. Its bishops above all quarreled for fifteen years, Sulpicius says, and Ithacius, attacked for bringing the case before the courts, lost his bishopric.

Yet sixty years on, Leo, bishop of Rome, wrote against the Priscillianists. Their teaching, he wrote, put each life under the power of demons and stars. He praised the killing. "Even the rulers of the world loathed this unholy folly. So they cut down its founder, with most of his followers, by the sword of the public laws." In 561 a council at Braga still cursed teachings it called his, among them that souls sinned in heaven and were cast down into bodies.

For centuries Priscillian was known only through his enemies. In 1885, a scholar opened an old manuscript at Würzburg and found eleven unsigned treatises. He judged they could be no one's but Priscillian's. Among them was the book Priscillian had carried to Damasus, with his call in his own words again.

## prosper-of-aquitaine

Prosper of Aquitaine

Near the end of Augustine of Hippo's life, a man in Gaul named Prosper wrote to him in Latin. They had never met, he said, but had traded letters through a deacon, Leontius. Many servants of Christ in Marseilles, he wrote, thought Augustine's teaching on how God calls those he has chosen went against the Fathers.

Gennadius, a priest of Marseilles, calls Prosper a man of Aquitaine, "scholastic in style and vigorous in statement." No ancient source used here gives his city, birth or death, and later writers disagree on whether he was a layman. Edgar Gibson, who translated John Cassian, says he was. Theodor Mommsen, who edited his chronicle, thinks he was a cleric, since Prosper calls the deacon Leontius his brother.

Gibson dates the letter to 429. Prosper then wrote a long poem, On the Ungrateful, against those he saw as enemies of grace. After Augustine died, Gibson says, Prosper and a layman named Hilary appealed to Pope Celestine. This Hilary had also written to Augustine. Gibson keeps him apart from Hilary of Arles. Mommsen takes the letter's writer for Hilary of Arles, writing before he became bishop. They won a letter from the pope to the bishops of Gaul.

Prosper also wrote against Cassian, whom Gibson calls the leading priest of Marseilles. Gennadius thought an unsigned book against Cassian was Prosper's. Gennadius, who sided with Cassian, says Prosper "brands as injurious" works that the church finds helpful. Gibson calls the book powerful, "although not scrupulously fair."

He also kept a chronicle. One entry, which Mommsen's edition sets in 429, has Pope Celestine send Germanus, bishop of Auxerre, to win the Britons back from the Pelagians. Gennadius adds that Leo of Rome's letters against the monk Eutyches were thought to have been dictated by Prosper.

In the chronicle Prosper wrote of Augustine's death. Augustine died during the Vandal siege, he says, still answering Julian of Eclanum, and still defending the grace of Christ.

## rufinus-of-aquileia

Rufinus of Aquileia

Jerome heard, in 374, that his friend Rufinus was traveling among the monks in the far parts of Egypt. He wrote to draw him to Syria, and ended: "A friendship that can end was never real." Within thirty years, a book Rufinus translated had ended theirs.

Rufinus of Aquileia, a monk from northern Italy, spent nearly thirty years among the monks of Egypt and at Jerusalem. Then he came home and put Greek Christian books into Latin. One was First Principles, by Origen, a Greek Bible scholar of the 200s, much admired and by now much attacked. The other was Eusebius's history of the church, which Rufinus carried on in two books of his own. First Principles and many of Origen's other works survive chiefly in his reworded Latin, his editors note. And the preface to First Principles broke his friendship with Jerome.

He spent eight years in Egypt, he writes, learning from the desert monks. When the emperor turned on the monks there for their creed, he says, he was jailed and exiled. At Jerusalem, in the 390s, a bishop from Cyprus charged that Origen taught souls had sinned in heaven before birth, that the devil would be saved, and that this flesh would not rise. Jerome took his side, Rufinus the other, but they made peace before Rufinus sailed home.

At home he argued in a short book that heretics had falsified Origen's writings. Then he translated First Principles, Origen's book on God and creation. Its preface praised, without naming him, a friend who had translated Origen before and pared away what jarred with the faith. He said he followed that method. "Where I found anything in his books against the truth about the Trinity, which he teaches soundly elsewhere, I left it out as not his own. Or I set it down to agree with the rule of faith, which we find him holding to again and again." The rule of faith is the church's summary of its belief.

Jerome's friends at Rome sent him the translation, saying it was falsely translated. Jerome took the preface as aimed at him. In the Greek, Jerome later wrote, Origen ranked the Son below the Father, the Spirit below the Son. He charged that Rufinus had made the Trinity sound to put readers off guard against Origen's other teachings. He made his own literal translation: "In the books On First Principles, I simply set down what stood in the Greek. I did not want the reader to believe what I translated. I wanted him not to believe what you had translated before."

Rufinus told the bishop of Rome that he was no champion of Origen, and had cut only what he believed others added. He affirmed that this very flesh would rise, and the devil burn forever. But the bishop of Rome wrote to the bishop of Jerusalem that Origen meant to undermine the faith, and that the emperors had banned his books. Rufinus was wholly apart from Rome and must answer to God, he wrote, and he neither knew nor wished to know where Rufinus lived. Each wrote a full defense against the other, and the friendship did not recover.

When the Goths broke through into Italy, Chromatius, bishop of Aquileia, asked Rufinus to translate the Church History. Chromatius hoped it would help listeners forget their sufferings a little. He did.

The few Greek fragments of First Principles show, his editors note, that he altered Origen's wording. His History became the Latin West's Eusebius through the Middle Ages, an editor judges. The Greek historian Socrates followed it, then rewrote his first two books, trusting more a bishop who had suffered in those events.

Rufinus's last work came in Sicily, about 410 by his editor's date. The barbarian had set fire to Rhegium, just across the strait. On quiet nights, he wrote, as a comfort in their troubles, he gathered Origen's sermons on Numbers into Latin. He hoped to add Deuteronomy, he said, if God gave him back his eyesight. He died soon after, his editors judge.

## socrates-of-constantinople

Socrates of Constantinople

After riots between pagans and Christians at Alexandria, many pagans fled the city. Two of them, the grammarians Helladius and Ammonius, came to Constantinople. Helladius boasted that he had killed nine men in the fighting. Ammonius complained that Theophilus, bishop of Alexandria, had kept one statue only to make the old religion look ridiculous. Socrates of Constantinople tells this in his Church History. He was their pupil, he says, "in my youth at Constantinople."

He lived in that city, "where I was born and educated." He wrote for a man he calls "O holy man of God, Theodore." He carried the story of the church on from where Eusebius of Caesarea left off. His seven books run from Constantine to the year 439.

He wrote his first two books following Rufinus of Aquileia. Then he read the writings of Athanasius of Alexandria, and judged "that more credit was due to him who had suffered." So he rewrote them and added the emperors' letters and the bishops' decisions. He chose a plain style, he says, because ordinary readers want the facts. He expected zealots to blame him for not calling bishops "Most holy."

Auxanon, a very old priest of the Novatian church, had gone to Nicaea as a youth. He told Socrates his stories when Socrates was "a mere youth." A. C. Zenos, who revised the English translation, lists the many places where Socrates writes kindly of the Novatians, a strict church that stood apart. Baronius and others held that Socrates was one of them. Valesius and most later writers put it down to his fairness.

He is called Scholasticus, a word that could mean a lawyer. Zenos and Valesius think he was one. Harnack, Zenos reports, finds no sign of legal knowledge in his book.

He ended with a prayer that the churches "may live in peace; for as long as peace continues, those who desire to write histories will find no materials for their purpose."

## sozomen

Sozomen

Under the emperor Julian, Christians had to flee from city to city. "My grandfather and many of my ancestors were compelled to flee in this manner," Sozomen writes in his Church History. His grandfather was born to pagans. He and his family, with that of Alaphion, were the first in Bethelia, a town near Gaza, to become Christians. It is said, Sozomen adds, that the monk Hilarion of Gaza won them by driving a demon out of Alaphion.

His grandfather was loved in Gaza and Ascalon for explaining hard places in Scripture. Members of "the other family" founded the first churches and monasteries there. Chester Hartranft, who revised the English translation, thinks it probably means Alaphion's. Sozomen saw some of its good men in his youth, "but they were then very aged."

He became an advocate. A friend was healed at the Michaelium, a shrine near Constantinople. Sozomen calls him "an advocate in the same court of justice as that to which we belong." He says the shrine helped him too.

He sent his history to the emperor, the younger Theodosius. He asked him to "purify it by thy labors," adding or cutting as he saw fit. The dedication says it would run to the emperor's seventeenth consulship. Hartranft dates that to 439, and notes that the history stops at 425.

He searched laws, synod records and letters. Each party, he warns, kept only "such epistles as favored their own heresy."

Sozomen never names Socrates of Constantinople, who wrote a few years before him. Hartranft finds the use of Socrates sure, from stories both tell almost alike. One view, he reports, is that Sozomen plagiarized Socrates. Hartranft calls that charge "wholly unfair" by the custom of the time.

Sozomen also wrote of the first monks. He wanted to leave "such a record of their manner of life that others, led by their example, might attain to a blessed and happy end."

## sulpicius-severus

Sulpicius Severus

Sulpicius Severus went to Tours to see its bishop, Martin. He already meant to write Martin's life. Martin gave him water to wash his hands. At evening he washed his guest's feet himself. Sulpicius says he had not the courage to stop him. Martin talked of leaving the world behind, and held up Paulinus of Nola, a rich man who had sold everything for the poor.

Gennadius of Marseilles says Sulpicius was a presbyter from Aquitaine, "distinguished by his birth," poor by choice and humble. Alexander Roberts, who translated him, says without naming a source that he gave up a name at the bar after his wife died young. Roberts puts his life at about 363 to 420. Ernest Cushing Richardson, in a note to Gennadius, gives after 353 to about 410.

The Life of Martin was done before Martin died. In a letter, Sulpicius tells of a morning dream. Martin appeared, smiling, and held out "the small treatise which I had written concerning his life." When Sulpicius woke, a servant said two monks had come from Tours, and "they have brought word that Martin is dead." Later he wrote Dialogues, where friends talk of the monks of the East and of Martin. He also wrote a Chronicle from the Creation to his own day.

The Chronicle closes with the trial of Priscillian of Abila at Trèves. Sulpicius wanted the heretics put down. Of the bishops who pressed the charges, he writes that "the accusers were as distasteful to me as the accused." Martin, he says, begged the emperor Maximus not to shed their blood.

Gennadius adds that in old age Sulpicius "was led astray by the Pelagians," the followers of Pelagius. He then kept silent until death, to atone. Roberts finds "no ground for any such conclusion" in the writings.

Paulinus of Nola, in a letter Roberts quotes, called Martin blessed for having "a historian worthy of his faith and of his life."

## theodoret-of-cyrrhus

Theodoret of Cyrrhus

Theodoret of Cyrrhus wrote to his friend, the deposed Nestorius. "I will never agree to what was done against you, unjustly and wickedly, not even if someone cuts off both my hands, as God's grace works with me and strengthens my weak soul."

In the fifth century, Theodoret was a leading voice of Antioch's teaching that Christ is one, God and man, with neither mixed away into the other. He answered Cyril of Alexandria's twelve curses and was deposed in 449, and at Chalcedon, two years later, he was let back only after he cursed Nestorius by name. He was a historian too. His Church History carried the church's story on from Eusebius, and his Religious History kept the Syrian monks he knew.

Much of his life comes from letters he wrote to answer his accusers, who said he split Christ into two sons, and to win Leo of Rome's help. He says he lived in a monastery and was made bishop of Cyrrhus, a small town in northern Syria, "unwillingly". He led villages of Marcion's followers, a rival church that set aside the Old Testament, into his own and, he says, was stoned for it many times.

Cyril had sent out twelve curses against Nestorius's teaching, solemn acts that cut a person off from the church. The first fell on anyone who would not call Mary Mother of God. Theodoret charged that they mixed God and man. Asked by John of Antioch to answer them, he wrote: "The form of God was not changed into the form of a servant. It stayed what it was, and took the form of the servant." The form of a servant is Paul's phrase for a human life: God stayed God, and took one on. So Mary is rightly Mother of God, he wrote, not because she bore God by nature, but because of the union. At Ephesus in 431, his English translator says, Cyril's side condemned Nestorius without waiting for John's bishops; Theodoret stood with John's side.

In 433 the two sides made peace, and Theodoret found that Cyril's letter agreed with his faith. But he would not accept that Nestorius had been rightly removed.

His Religious History told of many monks he says he saw and heard. Of Simeon, who stood on a pillar, he writes: "First he ordered it cut six cubits high, then twelve, after that twenty-two, and now thirty-six. For he longs to fly up to heaven, and to be set free from this life on earth." A cubit is the length of a forearm.

An order from the emperor then kept him in Cyrrhus, with no charge and no accuser, he wrote, only for calling bishops together. The council of 449 at Ephesus, led by Dioscorus of Alexandria, deposed him in his absence. Its acts, his opponents' record, say he had written against Cyril, and ask the emperors to burn his books.

At Chalcedon, the council's acts record, the bishops at the altar rails demanded that he curse Nestorius. He said he condemned him, but asked to explain his faith first; they shouted for a plain curse and cried that he was a heretic. Then he said it: "Anathema to Nestorius", a curse on his friend and on all who split the one Son into two. He was given back his church.

About 140 years later Evagrius, a Greek historian, called his History and three others "a most excellent record", and carried it on. A Syriac chronicle from the churches that refused Chalcedon's teaching of two natures, God and man, begins where he and two others stopped, so both sides carried his story on. Yet that same chronicle says he won Leo over with a bribe. In 553 a council at Constantinople condemned what he had written against Cyril.

To Leo he had written that in all his years as bishop he had gained not a house, not a piece of ground, not a coin, "not a tomb". He died in the reign of the emperor Leo, a later writer says; the year is not known.

## tyconius

Tyconius

"Of all the things that occur to me, I consider none so necessary as to write a little book of rules." So Tyconius opened his Book of Rules. His seven rules would be keys and windows into "the secret places of the law." With them, a reader crossing "the vast forest of prophecy" would not lose the way.

Tyconius was an African of the late fourth century and a Donatist. The Donatist church had split from the main body in Africa over those who lapsed in the Great Persecution. F. C. Burkitt edited the Book of Rules. Burkitt says the little known of Tyconius's life comes chiefly from Augustine of Hippo, a Catholic bishop who wrote against the Donatists.

Augustine says Tyconius wrote "in defence of the Church" against his own party. He reported a Donatist council that took back those who had handed over the Scriptures, without baptizing them again. Parmenian, the Donatist bishop of Carthage, answered him and warned him not to write such things. Tyconius still kept apart from the Catholics. Augustine calls this "a most inconsistent disposition."

Augustine set out the seven rules in his guide to the Bible, On Christian Doctrine. The second rule held that the Lord's body has two parts, good and bad, joined for now. Tyconius found it in the Song of Songs, where the bride says she is dark and beautiful. Augustine renamed the rule "the mixed Church." He faulted the third rule for saying that faith does not come to us from God. The book "must be read with caution," he warned, chiefly for what its author taught as a Donatist.

Gennadius of Marseilles says Tyconius also explained the whole Apocalypse, "regarding nothing in it in a carnal sense." Burkitt says that commentary is believed to be lost, though later writers copied much of it. Augustine commended the Book of Rules to students, "for it is of very great assistance in understanding Scripture."

## ulfila

Ulfila

Ulfila had been bishop of the Goths for seven years when persecution drove him out, after many men and women had died as martyrs. He led a great crowd of believers across the Danube, and they were received with honor on Roman soil, under the emperor Constantius. His pupil Auxentius, who wrote in his praise and on whom nearly all of this life rests, told it as a new Exodus.

"Through Moses, God freed his people from the power and violence of Pharaoh and the Egyptians. He made them cross the sea, and made sure they would serve him. So too, through this man, God freed those who confessed his holy only Son from the barbarians' land. He made them cross the Danube, and serve him in the mountains, after the saints' example."

Those who confessed had held to the faith under persecution, and the barbarians' land was the Goths' country. Ulfila gave the Goths letters and the Scriptures in their own tongue. To his death he taught them a creed that called the Son like the Father, according to the Scriptures. His opponents called that faith Arian, after Arius, a priest who taught that the Son had a beginning.

Auxentius says he was made bishop at thirty. Philostorgius, a historian of the next century, says it was at the emperor's court, by Eusebius and other bishops. A modern historian takes this Eusebius for the bishop of Nicomedia.

Among the Goths, Auxentius says, Ulfila taught a people starved of preaching and made Christians into true Christians.

Philostorgius says Ulfila invented letters for the Goths and translated all of Scripture except the Books of Kings. Those told of war, he explains, and the Goths were too fond of war already. Two church historians, Socrates and Sozomen, credit him with both. Fragments of a Gothic Bible survive without a translator's name, but their editor in 1908 had no doubt they were Ulfila's.

His pupil records what he taught. The Father alone is unbegotten, never begotten by anyone, without beginning, and the one true God. The Son is God and maker of all creation, yet has the Father as his God, and is like the Father, according to the Scriptures. The Greek for like, homoios, gives these churches the name Homoian. Nicaea had called the Son of one being with the Father, sharing his very being, and Ulfila spurned that as a devilish invention.

In 360, by the usual date, he joined the bishops at Constantinople in confirming a creed that banned the word "substance" for God, saying Scripture never uses it. But when did he take that faith? It matters because the Goths still held that faith in the historians' day. Socrates puts it down to the emperor Valens, whose help in a Gothic civil war won a chief over to his faith. But Sozomen says Ulfila was why the barbarians on the Danube followed Arius.

Socrates says Ulfila took the creed only in 360, holding Nicaea's faith before. Sozomen says he signed from thoughtlessness, and changed sides years later, under Valens, on an embassy seeking land for the Goths. The other side's leaders, he heard, promised help if he joined them. He did, under pressure or perhaps from conviction. Theodoret also puts it under Valens, when a bishop of the emperor's party won him over with eloquence and bribes.

Under Valens, Socrates says, Ulfila also taught the people of the Gothic chief Athanaric. The chief took this as an attack on his ancestors' religion and punished the Christians, so that many died as martyrs.

Later the emperor ordered Ulfila to Constantinople for a debate, to stop his opponents teaching in his churches. As he entered the city, he fell ill at once. He died there, about 383.

He left his faith in writing to his people, by testament. Of the Father, he wrote, "alone unbegotten"; of the Son, "subject and obedient in all things" to his God the Father. He wrote that he had always believed so, and that in this faith alone he made his crossing to his Lord.

## agathias

Agathias of Myrina

After a great winter earthquake at Constantinople, Agathias writes, the people turned good for a while. Some left everything for a lonely life in the mountains. At night the rich walked the streets, giving food and clothes to the poor. As soon as the fear passed, most went back to their old ways. We taste good works, he says, only while need drives us and fear presses. His Greek is told here from an old Latin version.

Agathias gives his own name, town and trade. He came from Myrina in Asia, the son of Memnonius, and he practiced Roman law. B. G. Niebuhr, who edited him in 1828, says he studied at Alexandria and pleaded cases in Constantinople to earn his living. He turned from love poems to history, he says, so that his life would be of some use to the public.

His five books carry on the history of Procopius of Caesarea, as Evagrius Scholasticus records. Church matters come in only in passing. He tells how Justinian raised the fallen middle of the dome of Hagia Sophia higher and made it stronger. He says the Franks are all Christians, with priests in their cities, and keep the feasts just as we do. Of peoples who worship trees and rivers he writes that those who stray from the truth deserve pity rather than hatred.

Whether he was a Christian is disputed. G. J. Vossius thought him a pagan, partly because he tells of the martyr Stephen only as something they say. Niebuhr thinks one of his poems, dedicated to the archangel, puts it beyond doubt that he professed the faith. But he guesses that Agathias was born to a pagan father and joined the Christians to escape the laws, not from belief.

Niebuhr thinks he died before 582. He finds the style poor, but says Agathias wrote free of attack and of flattery alike, and was a much better man than Procopius.

## augustine-of-canterbury

Augustine of Canterbury

King Ethelbert of Kent would not meet the strangers indoors. Bede of Jarrow says he feared they might work magic on him, so he sat in the open air on Thanet. Augustine of Canterbury and his companions came singing a litany. They bore a silver cross for a banner and a picture of Christ painted on a board. The king called their words fair but new, and would not leave the faith his people had long kept. He let them stay and preach.

Augustine had been provost of Pope Gregory the Great's own monastery, Gregory's letters say. Bede says Gregory sent him to the English in 596, and the mission landed the next year. On the way, Bede tells, the monks lost heart at the thought of a fierce people whose language they did not know. They sent Augustine back to ask to be excused. Gregory sent him on with a letter: "When Augustine, your chief, returns, whom we also constitute your abbat, humbly obey him in all things."

At Canterbury the king believed and was baptized. Bede says Augustine then went to Arles to be made archbishop of the English. In Bede's telling, Aetherius of Arles ordained him; the editor Nicola Turchi names Vergilius. Gregory wrote to Eulogius of Alexandria that more than ten thousand English were reported baptized one Christmas.

Augustine sent questions to Rome, and Bede copied them with Gregory's answers. One asks: "Whereas the faith is one and the same, why are there different customs in different churches?" Gregory told him to choose what was good from every church, "for things are not to be loved for the sake of places, but places for the sake of good things." Ludwig Hartmann, editing Gregory's letters, says these answers have often been doubted, and judges them genuine.

Gregory also put all the bishops of Britain in his care. Bede tells of two meetings with British bishops, the first at Augustine's Oak. Before the second, a hermit told them to follow him if he rose to greet them. Augustine stayed seated, and they refused him. If they would not preach to the English, Augustine is said to have warned, they would suffer death at English hands. Bede, no friend of the Britons, calls them "that perfidious nation".

Augustine died on the twenty-sixth of May. His epitaph gives the day but not the year, and calls him the first archbishop of Canterbury.

## avitus-of-vienne

Avitus of Vienne

King Gundobad of the Burgundians came to believe that the Son and the Holy Spirit are equal to the Father. He asked the bishop of Vienne to anoint him in secret. Gregory of Tours, writing later in the same century, gives the bishop's answer. The king should not fear his people, he said, but lead them. "For thou art the head of the people; the people is not thy head." Gregory says the king never confessed it in public.

That bishop was Avitus of Vienne, in Gaul. An old Life of him says he followed his father Isicius as bishop. His editor, Rudolf Peiper, puts his birth about 460. Gundobad's church was Arian, and the Life says Avitus fought that teaching with great labor.

When Clovis, king of the Franks, was baptized, Avitus wrote to him in Latin. He had not been there in body, he said, but had shared the joy. In choosing for yourself, he told the king, you judge for everyone, and your faith is our victory. Peiper dates the letter to 496 or 497. The year and place of the baptism are disputed. O. M. Dalton, Gregory's English translator, follows those who hold Reims in 496, against Bruno Krusch's Tours in 507 or 508.

At Gundobad's request, Gregory says, Avitus wrote against the teaching of Eutyches. Agobard of Lyon, writing three centuries later, says he argued with the king in person and by letter. Once Gundobad was lost in his unbelief, Agobard says, Avitus turned his son and heir Sigismund to the Catholic faith. Peiper tentatively dates Avitus's letter to the pope about that conversion between 513 and 516, while Gundobad still lived. In 517 Avitus signed first at the council of Epaone.

His Life says he died under the emperor Anastasius, and gives no year. Peiper corrects the emperor to Justin.

Gregory wrote of his letters that "as they once quelled heresy, so now they edify the Church of God."

## babai-the-great

Babai the Great

Thomas of Marga tells how the Persian king Khosrau swore by the Sun, his god, that while he lived, the Christians of his land would have no new head of their church. He kept his word for close to twenty years. In that time the bishops, the leaders over each city and its region, turned to a man who was not even a bishop.

He was Babai, a monk vowed to prayer, poverty and an unmarried life, and abbot, or head, of the Great Monastery on Mount Izla, near Nisibis. For the Church of the East, the church of the Persian empire, he drove out of its monasteries those the bishops judged heretics, people teaching what the church had condemned. He also wrote the Book of the Union, on how God and man are joined in the one Christ. His church kept and honored his books, and historians judge that they helped settle its teaching on Christ.

Thomas, who tells most of his story two centuries later, says some monks at Izla were found living with wives. Babai and the senior monks stripped them of the monk's robe, drove them out with their families and burned their huts. Babai then drove out a gentle monk named Jacob, unjustly, Thomas says, and condemned him in writing. The monks rebuked their abbot, and many left, and founded monasteries across the East, among them Jacob's, where Thomas would later write.

In 612, Babai says, the king told the bishops to show him first which faith was true, and then they would have a head. In Christ, they wrote, are two natures, what makes him God and what makes him human, each real and complete, in one person. But while they honored Nestorius, their teacher, a bishop of Constantinople condemned in 431, the king answered, they would have no head. Babai's Book of the Union says: "Here too, in the same way, we say that Christ was crucified, that Christ died and rose again. But we do not say that God was crucified and died." The man Jesus suffered and God did not, yet both are one Christ. That teaching set his church apart from its neighbors. Two centuries on, Thomas counted eighty-four of his works still honored in the church.

In 628, after the king was murdered, the bishops all begged Babai, Thomas says, to become head of the church. He refused, choosing to end his days in his hut. Thomas tells that an angel on a white horse, with a fiery sword, then stood before it. It had stood beside him, it said, all the years he took the place of the head of the church, and now must go to the new one. Babai answered: "If I had known you were with me, I would have taken on this great work with all care. But now go in peace, and pray for me." Babai, by then an old man, died about that time.

## barhadbeshabba-arbaya

Barhadbeshabba 'Arbaia of Halwan

The bishops of the Church of the East met at the Persian king's court to choose a new head. J.-B. Chabot, who edited their acts, dates the meeting to April 605. The acts say King Khosrau the Second had the distant bishops brought on his own beasts. They chose Gregory of Prat. The twenty-seventh to sign is Bar Hadbeshabba, bishop of Halwan.

Addai Scher and François Nau take this bishop to be Barhadbeshabba 'Arbaia, who wrote the Cause of the Foundation of the Schools. The title Scher prints calls him bishop of Halwan. Scher says his second name marks him as a man of Beth 'Arbaye, and that he studied at Nisibis under Henana of Adiabene.

The Cause tells of the schools through which God taught angels and people, from Adam to the schools of Edessa and Nisibis.

The Cause speaks of Henana as a living master. It praises his humility and prays that God will lengthen his days. Henana's own church, Scher says, always charged him with leaving Theodore of Mopsuestia's reading of Scripture. Scher thinks the author probably hid the truth to praise him.

The Chronicle of Seert tells of a walk-out from the school of Nisibis. Some three hundred students left when the head of the church took Henana's side. It names among them a Hadbeshabba 'Arbaya, later made metropolitan of Halwan. Scher takes him for the Cause's author.

Part of a history of the holy Fathers persecuted for the truth survives from the same school. Nau gives it to Barhadbeshabba 'Arbaia, a priest of the school of Nisibis, and dates it to the end of the 500s. He thinks it not impossible that this writer is the bishop of Halwan.

Of the school that Narsai of Nisibis founded, the Cause says, in R. H. Connolly's English, "Edessa was darkened, but Nisibis shone forth; and the dominions of the Romans were filled with error, but those of Persia with the knowledge of true religion".

## barsanuphius-and-john-of-gaza

Barsanuphius and John of Gaza

Dorotheus of Gaza put a question to his teacher, the abbot John of Gaza. How could he hold back his too free manner and rule his tongue? By mourning, the answer said. Then how, he asked, could he keep mourning while dealing every day with people of the world? Mourning does not come through tears, the answer ran. Tears come through mourning.

J.-P. Migne prints the questions and answers among Dorotheus's writings, in Greek and Latin. Their heading sends them to the great old man Barsanuphius and his disciple, the abbot John. It says John was called the Prophet for his gift of insight.

The church historian Evagrius Scholasticus calls Barsanuphius an Egyptian, shut in a cell near Gaza. No one had seen him for over fifty years, Evagrius says. Dorotheus came to know the two old men when he entered the monastery of the abbot Seridus. So says a Life of Dositheus, as Andrea Gallandi's notice reports it.

Dorotheus later told monks how he took everything to John. When a thought told him not to trouble the old man, since he knew the answer already, Dorotheus cursed the thought and went anyway. Often John gave the very answer the thought had foreseen. When Dorotheus worried that he felt no hardship, he asked John by letter. Whoever gives himself to obedience, John told him, gains this rest from God.

Only the first question is marked as sent to John, so the text does not show which old man gave each later answer.

A preface that Migne prints without an author's name says there were two men named Barsanuphius and two named Dorotheus. One pair, it says, defended the errors of Severus of Antioch, and the other kept sound teaching. It calls Theodore the Studite as its witness. Theodore says the men who were condemned were others.

Barsanuphius's image, Theodore writes, stood in the Great Church beside the holy fathers Antony and Ephrem.

## barsauma-of-nisibis

Barsauma of Nisibis

The Church of the East gathered its synods, its councils of bishops, into one book. Its compiler, about three centuries later, left one out on purpose. Barsauma had held it at Beth Lapat, and he himself had said it was held "out of ill will and human passion."

Barsauma was bishop of Nisibis, a city on the border between the Roman and Persian empires, in the late 400s. His church, the church of the Persian empire, had governed itself for sixty years; its synods now fixed its teaching and rules. Barsauma and his side stood for two things: ministers free to marry, and the Greek Bible teacher Theodore of Mopsuestia as the church's sure guide. In 484 he led a synod against the catholicos, the head of his church, and later took it back in writing. Yet his church made both causes its law, while neighboring churches remembered him as the man who set it apart.

At Nisibis he welcomed the teacher Narsai and asked him to found a school, which counts them both as its founders. By his own account, "Satan stirred up a quarrel" between him and the head of the church, Babowai. At Beth Lapat, in April 484, he and the bishops on his side drew up a written case against Babowai and made rules on ministers' marriage. That year the Persian king put Babowai to death. A later historian of Barsauma's own church says Barsauma passed Babowai's letter to the king and then regretted it. Writers of the rival Syriac church say he had him killed. His own letters speak only of the quarrel.

His marriage is told two ways. The same historian says the king later ordered him to take a wife, and he married a nun, a woman vowed to a single life. A rival writer, later still, says instead that he kept a woman and called her his wife. Another charges he made the marriage rule so he would not be blamed for her.

The year after the synod, with Acacius as the new head of the church, Barsauma took back everything done at Beth Lapat, its rules included. His letters to Acacius, the nearest witness to his life, show a frontier pressed by drought, raiding tribes and a Roman army. He asked Acacius to cut his city's rebels off from the church, "like the doctor who amputates," and to threaten to report them to the king.

A synod early the next year, which he stayed away from but accepted in advance, confessed Christ fully God and fully human, the two never mixed. It sent wandering men in monks' black, whom it called frauds, back to the monasteries. It barred bishops from stopping their ministers marrying, and expected new assistant ministers to take a wife.

From 491, he and Acacius were cutting each other off from the church.

In 497 a synod let every minister, from the head of the church down, marry one wife openly. It accepted Beth Lapat "but only on this point."

The Armenian bishops refused the rules that Acacius and Barsauma had set up. Writers of the rival Syriac church drew on a letter written from old men's memories about a century and a half later. They said he told the Persian king his Christians would never be loyal until their faith differed from the Romans'. With the king's soldiers, they said, he killed thousands who refused.

More than a century after Beth Lapat, a synod made Theodore's writings required study for every teacher in the church. It quoted Beth Lapat's ruling on Theodore, and called the synod left out of the book "the divine assembly." It named him with the title Mar, which means my lord. To those bishops he was "the blessed Mar Barsauma."

## baudonivia

Baudonivia

"I chose you, daughters. You are my light, my life, my rest and all my happiness. You are the new planting." Radegund spoke those words to the nuns of the convent of the Holy Cross, at Poitiers, in Frankish Gaul. She had been a queen, the wife of King Chlothar, and after leaving him she built the house. A little after the year 600, one of her daughters, a nun named Baudonivia, wrote her life for her sisters, at the bidding of her abbess and the whole community. The poet Fortunatus had already written one, and she meant to tell what he had passed over.

She tells, from what the house had heard, how Radegund, still a queen, had a pagan shrine of the Franks burned. Franks came with swords and clubs to defend it, but she sat her horse unmoved until it burned. When the Frankish kingdoms moved against each other, Radegund sent each king the same letters, that they make peace and the homeland not perish. She set her nuns to pray for them.

She tells, too, how Radegund sought a piece of the wood of the Cross from the emperor, for the safety of the whole homeland. It was kept out of Poitiers, and Baudonivia names no one, blaming the enemy of mankind through his agents. Radegund fasted and wept, her nuns wept every day, and at last the king had the bishop of Tours bring it in. Radegund left it in the house, for the honor of the place and the saving of the people. "What she did in the land of the East," Baudonivia says of the empress Helena, who in the old story found the Cross, "blessed Radegund did in Gaul."

In most manuscripts her book follows Fortunatus's, so later readers met the queen as her daughters remembered her.

Radegund died in 587, with her nuns round the bed crying that their light was taken. "Her eyes were closed, and ours were darkened." Since none of them went out of the gate, they wept from the wall as her body passed below.

## benedict-of-nursia

Benedict of Nursia

Pope Gregory the Great filled his life of Benedict with wonders, and then pointed his readers somewhere else, to the rule Benedict wrote for monks. "For the holy man could in no way teach otherwise than he lived."

Benedict lived around the year 500, and founded houses of monks in Italy, twelve at Subiaco, then one at Monte Cassino. For monks living together under an abbot they choose, he wrote a rule. "So we must found a school for the Lord's service. In founding it, we hope to lay down nothing harsh and nothing heavy." Yet the promise binds for life: a monk vows to stay in one community, to turn his whole way of life to God, and to obey. About two centuries later, Frankish rulers ordered the monasteries of their lands to live by it.

Gregory records that in his own lifetime the Lombards sacked Monte Cassino. Paul the Deacon later wrote that the monks fled to Rome with the manuscript of the Rule. In 742, Carloman's council bound the monks and nuns of his lands to the rule of Saint Benedict. And in 802, at Aachen, Charlemagne gathered the abbots and monks who were there, and they read it together. Then he ordered that whatever in the monasteries went against it be put right by it.

Because the Rule calls idleness an enemy of the soul, it sets times for work with the hands and other hours for holy reading. Even the last monk to join may be chosen as abbot. Of the abbot, the Rule says: "Let him so temper everything that the strong have something to long for, and the weak do not run away." Every guest is received as Christ. Benedict called it a little rule for beginners.

Benedict's life was written down by Gregory, about fifty years after his death, from the memories of four of his monks. Gregory says that in Rome, Benedict saw many students fall into loose living. So he left his books and his father's house to serve God alone, and spent three years in a cave near Subiaco.

Later, monks of a nearby house begged him to lead them. He held them to its regular life, and some of them put poison in his wine. Gregory says the glass shattered when Benedict made the sign of the cross over it.

Gregory says that at Monte Cassino, where country people still worshipped Apollo, Benedict smashed the idol, overturned the altar, burned the sacred woods and built chapels. He tells too how Totila, king of the Goths, threw himself to the ground before Benedict, who raised him and told him to stop his evil. From then on, Gregory says, the king was less cruel.

At the end, Gregory says, Benedict died standing in the chapel, held up by his disciples, his hands raised to heaven.

## boethius

Boethius

Boethius stood before Theodoric, the Ostrogothic king of Italy, to defend a fellow senator, Albinus, accused of writing to the emperor Justin against the king. A Latin chronicle, the Anonymus Valesianus, gives his answer, told here in modern words. The charge is false, he said, but if Albinus did it, then I and the whole Senate did it together. Its editor, Theodor Mommsen, calls its author a fierce enemy of the Arians.

Boethius was a Roman senator under Theodoric and was consul in 510. A letter Cassiodorus wrote in Theodoric's name praises him for putting Pythagoras, Ptolemy the astronomer, Euclid, Plato and Aristotle into Latin.

He also wrote short books on the faith. Writing against Eutyches and Nestorius, he defined a person as "The individual substance of a rational nature." Christ, he argued, is two natures in one person, as the Catholic faith holds. He closed one with a plea: "if possible, reconcile faith and reason." Thomas Hodgkin, who put Cassiodorus's letters into English, reports that scholars of his day doubted these books were his. Hodgkin answered them from a fragment drawn from a lost work of Cassiodorus, which names a book on the Holy Trinity and one against Nestorius.

In 522 his two sons were consuls together, and he praised the king in the Senate. Soon after, he fell. Condemned, he wrote the Consolation of Philosophy, in which Philosophy visits him as a woman. He set down the charges there: "I am said to have desired the Senate's safety." "Now being conveyed five hundred miles off, dumb and defenceless, I am condemned to death," he wrote.

Marius of Avenches says he was killed in the district of Milan, in the year Mommsen dates to 524. The Anonymus Valesianus has him tortured with a cord twisted on his forehead and clubbed to death. The Book of the Popes says the king killed him with the sword. Procopius of Caesarea writes that Theodoric later wept for the wrong he had done.

The Consolation never names Christ, Hodgkin notes, so some have read Boethius as a pagan. His English editors, H. F. Stewart and E. K. Rand, call him without doubt a Christian, and perhaps a martyr. The Bollandists, in their Acta Sanctorum of saints' days, list him as a martyr at Pavia.

Of his own fall he wrote that "it is the most unhappy kind of misfortune to have been happy."
