# Packet S02: person and council stories (census longDescription)

## tree/jovinian

Jovinian

Jovinian is known only through Jerome and Ambrose. No tradition on this map tells his story, and none of his own writing survives whole. What remains is what his opponents chose to quote.

Jerome's editor calls him a Roman monk, or rather a solitary, who put off the monk's dress and lived like other men. He wrote a book in Latin at Rome. Jerome quotes four points from it. Baptized virgins, widows and wives are of equal merit. Those reborn in baptism with full faith cannot be overthrown by the devil. Fasting is no better than eating with thanks. And all who keep their baptismal vow share one reward in heaven.

Pammachius, a friend of Jerome, brought the book to Siricius, bishop of Rome. By the editors' account, synods at Rome and at Milan condemned it about 390. Ambrose and his synod wrote back to Siricius to join the sentence. In a letter to the church at Vercelli, Ambrose warned against two monks who said there is 'no merit in abstinence' and none in virginity. His editor takes them for followers of Jovinian.

In 393 Jerome answered the book with two books of his own, Against Jovinianus. He calls Jovinian 'the Epicurus of Christianity'. Even Jerome's editor gives a warning here. He says Jerome's charges about the man's life are guesses drawn from his views. They are not known facts.

## tree/koriun

Koriun

Two Armenians named Leon and Koriun arrived at Constantinople. There they joined their companion Eznik of Goghp, who had been raised with them like a brother. Together they finished their work. Then they went home to Armenia. They took true copies of the Scriptures and the Church Fathers, with the canons of the councils of Nicaea and Ephesus.

This scene comes from the Life of Mesrop Mashtots, the monk who made the Armenian letters, and of Sahak, patriarch of the Armenians. The Life names Koriun only this once, in the third person. A note in Victor Langlois's 1869 French edition takes him for its author. So does Lazar of Pharp, a later Armenian historian, who calls Koriun a disciple of Mashtots. Langlois calls the Life the main source for Armenian literary history from the late fourth century into the fifth.

Koriun says he wrote of Sahak and Mesrop, men he saw with his own eyes. He writes of himself as an attentive witness of their preaching and a servant of these teachers. He left out many of their good deeds, he adds, and told only the chief things worth knowing. His Life is also the work of a disciple honoring a saint. It has demons put to flight and a cross of light over the house where Mesrop died.

Who asked him to write it is told two ways. Lazar says it was King Vramshapuh. Langlois says it was Joseph, who led the Armenian church after Sahak. The held French text names no patron.

Langlois also tells what is said of Koriun's later life. Sahak, he says, made him a bishop of the Georgians and sent him to their land. Koriun is believed to have died there, Langlois adds. But nothing certain can be said, since writers of his time give no details.

Koriun says he wrote as an example for the spiritual children of Sahak and Mesrop, and for all who would teach their nation the truth.

## tree/liberius-of-rome

Liberius of Rome

The emperor Constantius wanted Liberius, bishop of Rome, to condemn Athanasius of Alexandria. A record of their talk, which Theodoret of Cyrrhus copied into his Church History, gives his words. Asked why he stood alone, he said, "My standing alone does not make the truth a whit the weaker." Given three days to change his mind, he said, "Neither three days nor three months can change my sentiments. Send me wherever you please." He was banished to Beroea in Thrace. When the emperor sent him gold for the journey, he sent it back. The emperor, he said, needed it "to pay his troops."

Ammianus Marcellinus, a historian outside the church, tells the same refusal. He says Liberius was taken from Rome by night, for fear of the people, "among whom he was exceedingly popular." Theodoret says a deacon, Felix, was put in his place.

What happened in exile is disputed. Athanasius, his ally, says that after two years Liberius "gave way, and from fear of threatened death subscribed," and he blames those who forced him. Jerome says Liberius, worn out by exile, signed heresy and entered Rome as if he had won. Sozomen, a church historian of the next century, says he signed a document drawn up by Eastern bishops at Sirmium, with his own statement added. Theodoret tells of no lapse at all. Letters in which Liberius says he gave up Athanasius survive in Latin in a dossier gathered by Hilary of Poitiers. Feder, who edited the dossier, marks those letters as doubtful.

Theodoret says the noblewomen of Rome begged Constantius to bring their bishop back. The emperor proposed that Liberius and Felix share the church. The crowd cried, "One God, one Christ, one bishop." Liberius returned, and Felix withdrew.

Ambrose of Milan passed on the words Liberius spoke at Christmas in Saint Peter's, when Ambrose's sister made her profession as a virgin. "You," he said, "my daughter, have desired a good espousal."

## tree/lucifer-of-cagliari

Lucifer of Cagliari

In exile in Palestine, Lucifer of Cagliari wrote a book against the emperor Constantius and sent it to him to read. Jerome of Strido, in his Lives of Illustrious Men, says he wrote it "with wonderful constancy and willingness to meet martyrdom." Lucifer was bishop of Cagliari in Sardinia. Liberius of Rome had sent him to the emperor. With him went the Roman priest Pancratius and the deacon Hilary.

The emperor wanted the Western bishops to condemn Athanasius of Alexandria. Lucifer refused at a council in Milan. W. H. Fremantle, who translated Jerome, says he opposed the emperor with much violence. He was exiled from 355 to 361. He spent most of it at Eleutheropolis in Palestine, and the rest in Egypt. His pamphlets from exile survive in Latin, with titles such as Of Apostate Kings.

When Constantius died in 361, the emperor Julian let the exiles go home. Theodoret of Cyrrhus says the church at Antioch was split in two, though both sides held the same creed. Lucifer skipped the council at Alexandria and went straight to Antioch. There he made Paulinus, a presbyter who led one party, its bishop. Eusebius of Vercelli, who had shared his exile, disapproved. Socrates of Constantinople says Lucifer took this as an insult and broke with him.

Lucifer went home to Sardinia. Fremantle says he refused church office to anyone who had given way to the Arians. Socrates writes that "a distinct sect arose under the name of 'Luciferians.'" It had died out by early in the next century, he adds. Jerome's Chronicle, Fremantle notes, gives his death in 371.

Jerome had been ordained by Paulinus, the bishop Lucifer made. About 379 he wrote a Dialogue against the Luciferians. In it he calls Lucifer "that saintly man," yet blames him for the split. "He himself was a good shepherd," Jerome writes, "but he was leaving a vast spoil to the beasts of prey."

## tree/marcella-of-rome

Marcella of Rome

When Rome was taken in 410, soldiers broke into the house of an old widow named Marcella. Asked for gold, she is said to have pointed to her coarse dress to show she had none. They did not believe her, and "scourged her and beat her with cudgels." Jerome of Strido tells this in a memorial letter, written in her praise to her friend Principia. He was in Palestine and writes, he says, "what holy men have seen."

Marcella came from a noble Roman family. She was widowed before seven months of marriage. An old nobleman, Cerealis, then offered her his fortune. She told him that "a young man may indeed die early, but an old man cannot live long."

Clergy of Alexandria fled to Rome, Athanasius among them. From them Marcella heard of Antony, the desert monk, and of Pachomius's monasteries in Egypt. No highborn lady at Rome, Jerome says, had yet dared "publicly to call herself a nun." Marcella took up that life, and the virgin Eustochium was trained in her cell.

In the 380s Jerome lived in Rome, and Marcella questioned him about Scripture at every visit. Seventeen of his letters to her are held, several on Hebrew words. After he left, "recourse was had to her to settle" disputes about Scripture. She gave her answers as his or another's, because Paul had written, "I suffer not a woman to teach."

A Latin version of Origen's First Principles later reached Rome. The notes in W. H. Fremantle's English edition name its translator as Rufinus of Aquileia. He and Jerome later wrote against each other. Marcella opposed its teachers in public. "She it was who originated the condemnation of the heretics," Jerome writes. He admits some may think him "giving vent to my own rancour."

A few days after the soldiers came, she died in Principia's arms. Two years later Jerome wrote of her as "Marcella the glory of her native Rome."

## tree/marcellus-of-ancyra

Marcellus of Ancyra

In Rome, Marcellus, bishop of Ancyra in Galatia, wrote to Julius, bishop of the city. He had waited there a whole year and three months, he says. At the Council of Nicaea, he reminds Julius, he had refuted the men now writing against him. He confesses the Word who always exists with the Father, and Christ's kingdom, which will have no end. The letter survives only in Greek, in a book against heresies by Epiphanius of Salamis.

His opponents' charges explain why he was in Rome. Socrates of Constantinople says Marcellus wrote against Asterius, a writer on the side of Arius. In doing so he fell into the opposite error and taught, as Paul of Samosata had, that Christ was a mere man. Sozomen says he taught that the Son began when he was born of Mary, and that his kingdom would end. Bishops meeting at Constantinople deposed him and put Basil in his place at Ancyra.

Julius received him, and the Western bishops at Sardica cleared him. They said he had written that Christ's kingdom had neither beginning nor end. The Eastern bishops, in a letter quoted in A. C. Zenos's notes to Socrates, called him "the most execrable of all heretics."

Whether Athanasius of Alexandria stood by him is disputed. Epiphanius once asked him. Athanasius only smiled, Epiphanius says, hinting that Marcellus was not far from error, yet treating him as cleared. The dossier of Hilary of Poitiers says Athanasius refused him communion. E. W. Watson, who introduced the English Hilary, says it is "notorious that he never did so."

In 381 the Council of Constantinople condemned the Marcellians by name. Hefele, quoted in Henry Percival's edition, says Marcellus himself came back to the truth. A Benedictine note to Basil's letters thinks he erred at the end of his life.

Epiphanius left the judgment open. The hidden things of Marcellus's mind, he wrote, are known to God.

## tree/marius-mercator

Marius Mercator

In 429, by the consuls its title names, a Latin writer named Marius Mercator gave the church of Constantinople a short warning, written in Greek. Its title says it concerned Caelestius, a follower of Pelagius. After it was read, the title goes on, Caelestius and Julian of Eclanum were driven from the city with their companions by the emperor's order. They were later condemned at the Council of Ephesus.

Garnier, his first editor, notes that even Gennadius of Marseilles leaves him out of his list of church writers. Augustine of Hippo wrote to him as "my son." In that letter, held here in Latin, Augustine says Mercator's letters reached him at Carthage. He had not known, he says, how far Mercator had grown.

Where he came from is disputed. Garnier guessed southern Italy, since Mercator knew the private lives of Caelestius and Julian so well. Baluze, a later editor, guessed Africa. Schoenemann, a later scholar, says both are guesses. Garnier thought him a layman, since Augustine called him son and not brother. Hefele, the historian of the councils, calls him a layman living in Constantinople.

His chief work was translation. He put sermons of Nestorius, the deposed bishop of Constantinople, into Latin, with letters of Nestorius and of Cyril of Alexandria. He wanted Latin readers to know these sayings and avoid them. Hefele says Nestorius's twelve anathemas against Cyril survive only in Mercator's Latin.

He wrote as a partisan, and Garnier gathers from him rumors about Caelestius and Julian. In a book against Julian, Mercator traced the error back to Theodore of Mopsuestia.

No held line gives his death. Garnier thought he wrote nothing after the Council of Chalcedon. Baluze thought he did, since he names the followers of Eutyches.

In one preface he set out his method. He had tried to turn the Greek word for word, he wrote. He did not want to be proved a forger rather than a true translator.

## tree/melania-the-elder

Melania the Elder

A Roman noblewoman lost her husband, and before he was buried, two of her sons died. Jerome of Strido tells it to Paula of Rome. Not a tear fell, he says. She threw herself at the feet of Christ. "Henceforth, Lord," she said, "I will serve Thee more readily, for Thou hast freed me from a great burden." She gave all she had to her only son and sailed for Jerusalem.

She was Melania, called the Elder because her granddaughter shared her name. Palladius of Helenopolis says she was widowed at twenty-two and sailed first to Alexandria. In Egypt she spent half a year with the monks of Nitria. When their leaders were exiled to Palestine, she followed and fed them, dressed as a slave. The governor threw her in prison. She told him whose daughter and wife she was, "but I am Christ's slave." He apologized and released her.

She then founded a monastery in Jerusalem, Palladius writes, and led fifty women there for twenty-seven years. Rufinus of Aquileia lived there with her. They welcomed pilgrims and healed a split among some four hundred monks. Jerome's Chronicle says her humility earned her the name of Thecla, an early woman saint.

Rufinus, her companion and by then Jerome's opponent, says Jerome cut her from every copy of the Chronicle, for disapproving of some of his deeds. In 415 Jerome wrote of "her whose name bears witness to the blackness of her perfidy." The notes to W. H. Fremantle's edition read this as a pun on her name, meaning black.

At sixty, Palladius says, she sailed back to Rome to guard her family from false teaching, and urged them to give up their wealth. To senators who opposed her she said, "Little children, it was written 400 years ago, It is the last hour." She returned to Jerusalem and died within forty days, leaving "both a monastery in Jerusalem and an endowment for it."

## tree/melitius-of-lycopolis

Melitius of Lycopolis

Epiphanius of Salamis tells of a prison in Egypt during the Great Persecution, early in the fourth century. Peter, bishop of Alexandria, was held there with Melitius of Lycopolis and other clergy. They split over Christians who had given way. Peter wanted to be gentle with them. Melitius wanted to be strict. Peter hung a cloak across the prison like a curtain. A deacon called out that Peter's friends should come to his side, and the others to Melitius. Most of the clergy and monks, Epiphanius says, went to Melitius.

Epiphanius, a foe of every heresy, wrote some seventy years later. Archibald Robertson, who edited Athanasius, calls this the Melitians' own account. H. Idris Bell, who published papyri of their church in 1924, thinks Epiphanius had good sources.

Melitius was bishop of Lycopolis, in Upper Egypt. Two letters of the time speak against him. Four bishops in prison scolded him for making priests in their churches. One of them was Phileas of Thmuis. Peter wrote that Melitius had "ordained in the prison several unto himself." He told his flock to shun him.

Athanasius of Alexandria later held Peter's see. He wrote that Peter put Melitius out for many crimes, among them "offering sacrifice to idols." Bell doubts this. He calls Athanasius an unbending foe of the Melitians. Such charges came easily after a persecution, he says. He and Robertson agree the split began over church order. It did not begin over doctrine.

In 325 the Council of Nicaea let Melitius keep the title of bishop. But he could not ordain. Its letter, kept by Socrates of Constantinople, left him "a nominal dignity." Sozomen, the church historian, says that near the end he named his friend John to follow him. This broke the council's terms.

His followers called themselves the Church of the Martyrs, Epiphanius says. Later they sided with the Arians against Athanasius. Bell traces them in Egypt into the eighth century.

## tree/mesrop-mashtots

Mesrop Mashtots

A Syrian bishop's letters could not carry the sounds of Armenian, and Mesrop Mashtots prayed with tears, day and night, for letters that could. Then, his pupil Koriun writes: "At once he saw them. It was not in a dream while he slept, nor in a vision while he was awake, but in his heart. There they appeared to the eyes of his mind, traced by the wrist of a right hand writing on stone."

The letters, Koriun means, came whole, as an inner sight. Nearly all we know rests on this pupil, who says he saw these men with his own eyes. By his account, this royal secretary turned monk made the letters of the Armenian language, so that the faith could be preached, written and taught in Armenian. He worked with the king and with Sahak, the catholicos who led the Armenian church. Together they began putting the Scriptures into Armenian and founded schools on both sides of the Persian and Roman border.

As a monk he lived on herbs and slept on bare ground.

In Koghten he preached to people who kept pagan ways, but with no letters for Armenian, preaching was hard. On the Persian side of the divided country, Koriun says, only Syriac learning was allowed.

King Vramshapuh sent Mesrop to Syrian Mesopotamia, the Syriac lands to the south. Mesrop made new letters with a hermit named Rufinus, who shaped them on his directions, after the Greek alphabet.

Translation began at once, with Proverbs. In the early 400s he brought the letters to Sahak and the king. "They rejoiced over them as if they were the divine Tablets," Koriun writes: the tablets of the Law that God gave Moses. "Mesrop gathered children who were bright and well taught, with sweet voices and long breath," and schools opened in every province and village.

Koriun says he then gave the Georgians an alphabet and renewed that of the Albanians, a Christian people of the eastern Caucasus. The Georgian chronicle gives Georgia's script to an ancient king.

Back in Armenia, because of that ban, Koriun says, Sahak and Mesrop went west into Roman Armenia. Sahak wrote to the emperor and the bishop of Constantinople and sent Mesrop to them. In Roman Armenia, children studied the letters at public cost. Koriun tells of a warm welcome; a later historian, Moses of Khoren, says the letters were first refused. In the emperor's letter Moses gives, Sahak is blamed for serving a pagan king and for turning to Syrians, not to Constantinople's scholars.

Mesrop fought the Borborites, a sect Koriun calls rash and insolent. Koriun never says what they taught. Those who would not yield, he writes, were put in prison and chains and tormented; those who still refused were burned, shut up and driven from the land.

Sahak and Mesrop sent pupils to Edessa, a center of Syriac learning, and from there they went to Constantinople. They brought home true copies of the Scriptures, and the first hasty translations were corrected. When the council of Ephesus warned of a man it had shut out, whose books, Koriun says, followed Nestorius, Sahak and Mesrop drove him out. That council's quarrel turned on whether Mary could be called Mother of God; Nestorius urged Mother of Christ instead.

When Sahak died, Mesrop wept, finding no one to share the work of Armenia. After a short illness, he sat up among his disciples, raised his arms, blessed the Trinity and entrusted them to God. Koriun says a cross of light was seen over the house.

Every later Armenian witness we have names him the letters' maker, some beside Sahak. Lazar, a later historian who credits the translating to Sahak, says once the letters and Scriptures came, copyists multiplied, and men and women crowded the churches. Mesrop's pupils became the next writers: Koriun, Eznik, and, by his own account, Moses of Khoren.

The hand Koriun saw writing on stone had left letters, and those pupils carried them on.

## tree/olympias-of-constantinople

Olympias of Constantinople

On the day John Chrysostom was sent into exile, the cathedral of Constantinople burned. Socrates of Constantinople says some of John's followers set the fire. The church historian Sozomen says each side blamed the other. The deaconess Olympias was brought before the prefect and asked why she had done it. "My past life ought to avert all suspicion from me," she answered, "for I have devoted my large property to the restoration of the temples of God."

Nothing could be proved, Sozomen says, so the prefect pressed her and other women to take communion with the new bishop, Arsacius. The others gave way. Olympias told him, "I will not hold communion with those from whom I ought to secede." He fined her heavily, and she left the city for Cyzicus.

Palladius of Helenopolis says she was the daughter and granddaughter of high imperial officials. She married Nebridius, a former prefect of the city, and was soon widowed. The Dialogue on John's life, which its translator Herbert Moore gives to Palladius, says the emperor Theodosius wanted her to marry his kinsman Elpidius. She refused, and he put her wealth in trust until she was thirty. She answered that he would do better to have it "dispersed among the poor, and the Churches." Later he gave it back.

Sozomen says the bishop Nectarius made her a deaconess, though she had been widowed young. She gave to anyone who asked, and John told her to give "with economy." Palladius says she "instructed many women." The Dialogue names bishops she gave land and money, Epiphanius of Salamis among them.

Seventeen of John's surviving letters are to her. In the first he writes, "there is only one thing, Olympias, which is really terrible, only one real trial, and that is sin." W. R. W. Stephens, John's translator, says nothing definite is known of her later life. The people of Constantinople, Palladius writes, "reckon her life among the confessors."

## tree/ossius-of-cordoba

Ossius of Cordoba

Ossius, bishop of Cordoba in Spain, wrote to the emperor Constantius, a son of Constantine. "I was a Confessor at the first, when a persecution arose in the time of your grandfather Maximian," he began. The emperor had pressed him to sign against Athanasius of Alexandria. Ossius told him to "remember that you are a mortal man." "Intrude not yourself into Ecclesiastical matters," he wrote. God had given the emperor his kingdom, and the bishops the affairs of the church.

The letter survives only in Athanasius's History of the Arians. It defends Athanasius, who praises Ossius all through, so his account takes a side. He says Ossius had been a bishop for "more than sixty years."

Socrates of Constantinople says Constantine sent Ossius with a letter to Alexander of Alexandria and Arius. The Latin lists of those who signed at the Council of Nicaea put "Osius" first. Theodoret of Cyrrhus calls him the "most highly distinguished" bishop there. Whether he led that council is disputed. Athanasius asks when a council was ever held "in which he did not take the lead." Henry Percival, who edited the council records, finds no proof that he was in the chair.

At the council of Sardica, about 343 by Percival's date, Theodoret says he held "the first place." Its canons open with his words against bishops who move from a small city to a larger one. The eastern bishops met apart and deposed him, says Sozomen, a church historian of the next century.

Athanasius says Constantius held him for a whole year at Sirmium. Ossius was "now a hundred years old." At last, broken by suffering, he agreed to share communion with the bishops his letter had warned against. Even so, he would not sign against Athanasius. In Socrates's telling, he was beaten and tortured until he signed their creed.

As death drew near, Athanasius says, "he bore witness to the force which had been used towards him, and anathematized the Arian heresy."

## tree/palladius-of-helenopolis

Palladius of Helenopolis

At Nitria, in Egypt, Palladius of Helenopolis heard his teacher Evagrius Ponticus wish to know what kind of man John of Lycopolis was. Next day he shut his cell and set off for the Thebaid. He arrived eighteen days later, on foot and by river boat.

John, a hermit, spoke to visitors through a window. He asked if Palladius wanted to be a bishop. Palladius joked that he was bishop of the kitchen pots. "This is my bishopric, for gluttony ordained me." John smiled. "You have to be ordained bishop, and toil much and be afflicted."

Palladius tells this in his Lausiac History. He lived as a monk near Alexandria and in the desert. Illness took him to Palestine and Bithynia, where he was made bishop, "having become embroiled in the disturbance connected with the blessed John." W. K. Lowther Clarke, its translator, takes this John for John Chrysostom, bishop of Constantinople.

A Dialogue on Chrysostom's life tells what followed. Herbert Moore, who translated it, follows Cuthbert Butler, who edited the Lausiac History, in giving it to Palladius. Emeritus Bigot, its first editor, suggested another Palladius. In it, Palladius reaches Rome with a copy of an edict against John's friends. Later he is under guard at Syene, far to the south.

Foes in the quarrel over Origen attacked a Palladius. Epiphanius of Salamis, in a letter Jerome of Strido put into Latin, warned that "Palladius of Galatia" taught "the heresy of Origen." Jerome wrote that a Palladius, "no better than a villainous slave," tried to revive the same heresy. Notes to W. H. Fremantle's edition of Jerome take both for this Palladius. The historian Tillemont, they report, thought another Palladius may be meant. Palladius wrote that Jerome's temper threw "into the shade his splendid achievements."

He wrote the Lausiac History for Lausus, a court chamberlain. Its prologue says, "I welcomed all the hardship of the way so long as I might meet some man that loved God."

## tree/paula-of-rome

Paula of Rome

Paula came from a noble Roman family. Jerome says it traced its line to the Gracchi and the Scipios. She married Toxotius and bore five children, among them Eustochium, Rufina and a son, Toxotius. When her husband died, Jerome writes, her grief nearly killed her. She then gave herself to the service of the Lord.

Later she sailed east from Portus with Eustochium. On the shore her small son held out his hands, and Rufina begged her to wait until she was married. Jerome says her eyes stayed dry. She toured the holy places, then settled at Bethlehem and built cells and monastic buildings there.

She set up a monastery for men. She also gathered women from many provinces and divided them into three houses, which met for psalms and prayer. She read the Old and New Testaments under Jerome's guidance. She learned Hebrew well enough to chant the psalms in it. Jerome says she died after twenty years at Bethlehem, aged fifty-six.

## tree/paulinus-of-milan

Paulinus of Milan

A few days before Ambrose of Milan took to his bed, he was dictating the forty-third Psalm. Paulinus of Milan was taking it down. Suddenly, Paulinus writes, "a fire in the shape of a small shield covered his head," and entered his mouth. The bishop's face became like snow. Paulinus froze and could not write until the vision had passed.

Paulinus tells this in his Life of Ambrose, written at the request of Augustine of Hippo. He had lived in Ambrose's household. Later he moved to Africa, "in which we now live and write." Sister Mary Simplicia Kaniecka, who translated the Life in 1928, says little else is known of him.

Paulinus drew on men who served Ambrose before him, and on Ambrose's sister Marcellina. He begged readers to trust him, "since indeed it is better to say nothing at all than to set forth something false." Kaniecka calls it a saint's life, written to build up the reader, and says it pays no heed to dates.

At Carthage in 412, by B. B. Warfield's date, a deacon named Paulinus brought a written charge against Caelestius, a follower of Pelagius. Marius Mercator, a Latin writer against the Pelagians, calls the accuser a deacon of Ambrose. Kaniecka takes him to be the author of the Life. Augustine quotes the council's record. Caelestius said a presbyter at Rome had denied that sin passes down from Adam, and others said the same. "Tell us their names," Paulinus pressed. "Is not one priest enough for you?" Caelestius answered. The council condemned him.

In 417 Zosimus, bishop of Rome, heard Caelestius again and called Paulinus to Rome. Paulinus sent a short petition instead, which survives in Latin. In it he thanks Zosimus for ordering Caelestius to condemn what Paulinus had charged. What he had always wanted for Caelestius, he writes, was his correction.

He closed the Life by asking Augustine to pray for "the most humble man and sinner Paulinus."

## tree/pelagius

Pelagius

About 413, Pelagius wrote to Demetrias, a young Roman noblewoman who had vowed herself to Christ and given up marriage. God made human nature good and free to choose, he told her, and her birth and wealth were her family's. "But spiritual riches no one can give you except yourself. In these, then, you are rightly praised. In these you are rightly set above others. They cannot exist except from you and in you."

Pelagius, a monk from Britain who taught at Rome, held that God made human nature able, by God's gift, to choose the good and to live without sin. Bishops in Palestine cleared him in 415. But Augustine of Hippo answered him book by book, and in 418 bishops in North Africa and the bishop of Rome condemned him over grace, God's help freely given. The teaching that bears his name was ruled out of the church.

He lived long at Rome, where Caelestius became his follower. About 411 he crossed to Africa, then sailed on to Palestine. Apart from Pelagius's own letter and answers, his story comes to us mostly through Augustine and others who wrote against him.

In December 415, two bishops from Gaul charged him in writing but could not come. Fourteen Greek-speaking bishops heard him through an interpreter, at Diospolis. Some charges were Caelestius's views: that Adam's sin harmed only Adam, and newborn babies are as Adam was before he sinned. Pelagius said these were not his, and condemned them. Yet a man can be without sin, he said, and keep God's commandments if he wishes. The bishops declared him a member in good standing of the church. Augustine, reading the record, granted that the judges were right on what they heard, but to him Pelagius did not appear cleared.

Augustine reports, as something said, that followers of Pelagius had beaten the monks and nuns in Jerome's care at Bethlehem, killed a deacon and burned their houses. Early in 417, at the African bishops' urging, Innocent, the bishop of Rome, condemned the Pelagians, as his followers came to be called.

Pelagius wrote to Innocent that he always added the help of God's grace. The next bishop of Rome, Zosimus, had letters from him read aloud. In September 417 he asked the Africans, "Is there any passage in which God's grace or help has been left out?" Some who heard them, he wrote, could barely hold back their tears that men of such complete faith had been slandered. For a few months, Rome itself took his side.

Writing with a fellow bishop, Augustine warned Demetrias's mother against Pelagius's letter. "'These good things can only be in you': that was very well and very truly said. That is clearly food. But when he says 'only from you', that is wholly poison." Goodness might live in her, they meant, but did not come from her.

On 30 April 418 the emperor ordered Caelestius and Pelagius driven from Rome, and anyone spreading their teaching tried and, if convicted, exiled. Pelagius was then in the East. The next day some two hundred African bishops met at Carthage. God's grace, they ruled, does not only forgive past sins, and does not only show us what to do: it helps us do it. Rome, under Zosimus, condemned him too, Augustine says. That year Augustine judged that Pelagius made grace mean teaching, forgiveness and Christ's example, and never help to the will itself.

The sentence held. By 429, a chronicler says, a Pelagian was spreading the teaching in Britain's churches, and Germanus of Auxerre was sent against it. About 430 John Cassian, a monk in Gaul, charged the bishop of Constantinople with the Pelagians' teaching. Both, he wrote, made Christ a mere man who lived without sin, so others could too. In 431 the council at Ephesus wrote to Rome upholding the sentence on the Pelagians, Pelagius among them.

The sources we have do not say where or when Pelagius died. But his letter to Demetrias outlived him. It long passed as Jerome's, and was printed among the works of Jerome, who had written against the Pelagians.

## tree/philostorgius

Philostorgius

At twenty, Philostorgius came to Constantinople and saw Eunomius of Cyzicus, the leader of his party. In his history he praised the man's looks and speech. He admitted that Eunomius lisped, then made even the lisp a sign of grace. Photius, a later patriarch of Constantinople, summed up the history. He scorned it. Philostorgius, he says, "is not ashamed to exalt this lisp into a mark of great grace and elegance."

His book is lost. It survives through Photius's summary, and Photius was hostile throughout. He calls Philostorgius an "impious contriver of lies."

Through Photius, Philostorgius tells of his family. His mother's father, Anysius, was a priest at Borissus in Cappadocia. His mother, Eulampia, came from families who held "the consubstantial faith" of Nicaea. His father, Carterius, followed Eunomius and won her over. She then won "her brothers, then her father," and in time the rest of her family.

Photius names Aetius of Antioch and Eunomius as leaders of those who held that the Son is unlike the Father in substance. Philostorgius praised them both. He rated Aetius higher in argument, and Eunomius higher for "a style well suited to learners." He even faulted Arius, whom he praised, for teaching that God cannot be known, "not even by His own only-begotten Son." He wrote a book in praise of Eunomius, and another against Porphyry of Tyre "on behalf of the Christians."

His history ran in twelve books, from the quarrel of Arius with Alexander of Alexandria to 425, by Edward Walford's date. The first letters of the books spelled his name. Walford, who translated the summary, notes his love of far lands and wonders.

A later Greek dictionary keeps a passage in his own words. In it he praises three foes of his party, Basil of Caesarea, Gregory of Nazianzus and Apollinaris of Laodicea. They argued so well for the Nicene faith, he says, that "Athanasius must be judged a mere child in comparison with them."

## tree/possidius-of-calama

Possidius of Calama

The Vandals had shut Hippo in by land and sea. Possidius of Calama and other bishops had taken refuge there with Augustine of Hippo. One day at table, Augustine told them what he asked of God. He asked God to free the city, or to make his servants brave to bear his will. Or else, he said, "that He may take me from this world unto Himself." In the third month of the siege, Augustine fell ill with fever.

Possidius tells this in his Life of Augustine, in Herbert Weiskotten's 1919 translation. He had lived beside Augustine, he says, "for almost forty years." He wrote to build up the church, and set down "the things that I have seen in him and heard from him."

In 408 pagans at Calama stoned the church three times, then tried to burn it, Augustine writes. Its bishop forced himself into a crevice, "in which he lay folded double," while they hunted him. Augustine does not name him there. A later letter to the same man, Nectarius of Calama, points to Possidius. He told Nectarius that Possidius loved the town's people in a way more likely to do them good than his own. To another bishop he wrote that in Possidius "you will find one who is very much the same as myself."

At the Carthage conference of 411, Possidius of Calama was one of seven bishops named to speak for the Catholic side.

Possidius was with Augustine when he died in 430, "while we stood by and watched and prayed." He wrote the Life and added a list of Augustine's books, so readers could seek copies in the library at Hippo. Prosper of Aquitaine names a Posidius among the bishops whom the Vandal king Geiseric drove from their cities in 437. Weiskotten takes him to be the same man.

Possidius closed by asking readers to pray that he might "continue to emulate and imitate him in this world."

## tree/priscillian

Priscillian

Priscillian carried a book to Damasus, bishop of Rome. In it he wrote what he and his friends had always urged in the churches. "No one should be forbidden, if he despises parents, children, wealth and rank, even his own life, and would rather love God than the world."

A rich and learned layman of Spain, Priscillian called men and women to that strict life, and became bishop of Abila. Other bishops accused him of heresy, and an emperor who had seized the throne had him tried. In about 385 he was put to the sword, and his followers honored him as a martyr.

Nearly all we know of him comes from Sulpicius Severus, a writer from Gaul. Sulpicius called the teaching the heresy of the Gnostics, a heresy of secret knowledge, and repeats that Priscillian was believed to have practiced magic from boyhood. Yet he adds: "My own view is that the accused displease me as much as the accusers."

In his own account, Priscillian and his friends gave themselves wholly to God after baptism, because Scripture says that whoever loves anyone more than God cannot be his disciple. The same book gives his creed and curses every heresy, the followers of the Persian teacher Mani above all. Nobles, common people and many women came to him, Sulpicius says. The bishop of Emerita, he adds, harassed them without restraint, and only fanned the fire.

Bishops met at Saragossa, where, a modern historian thinks, they banned Sunday fasting and barred women from private meetings. Sulpicius says Priscillian was condemned in his absence as a heretic, still a layman. Priscillian wrote that no charge was laid against his group there, and reminded Damasus that Damasus's own letter forbade judging the absent and unheard.

His enemies won an order from the emperor that drove all heretics from the cities and beyond his lands. They asked for it, Priscillian wrote, against false bishops and Mani's followers. Priscillian went to Rome to clear his name: in a cause of faith, he wrote, he preferred the judgment of holy men to the world's. But Damasus would not even see him, and at Milan Ambrose was just as opposed.

The emperor Maximus came to Trier, where Bishop Ithacius accused Priscillian before him. At a council at Bordeaux, Priscillian appealed from the bishops to the emperor, to escape their judgment, Sulpicius says. Ithacius, he adds, called anyone who loved reading or fasting Priscillian's disciple. Martin, bishop of Tours, begged the emperor not to shed their blood, since the bishops' sentence was enough.

After Martin left, a prefect heard Priscillian twice and found him guilty of sorcery. Sulpicius says Priscillian did not deny studying obscene teachings, holding night meetings of women, and praying naked. These are the court's findings; no account from his side survives. The emperor ordered death. Priscillian was killed with several companions.

His followers, Sulpicius says, now revered him as a martyr. They carried the bodies home to Spain for grand funerals. Fifteen years later, Spanish bishops at Toledo still heard of clergy calling him a holy martyr.

The deaths split the other side. Its bishops above all quarreled for fifteen years, Sulpicius says, and Ithacius, attacked for bringing the case before the courts, lost his bishopric.

Yet sixty years on, Leo, bishop of Rome, wrote against the Priscillianists. Their teaching, he wrote, put each life under the power of demons and stars. He praised the killing. "Even the rulers of the world loathed this unholy folly. So they cut down its founder, with most of his followers, by the sword of the public laws." In 561 a council at Braga still cursed teachings it called his, among them that souls sinned in heaven and were cast down into bodies.

For centuries Priscillian was known only through his enemies. In 1885, a scholar opened an old manuscript at Würzburg and found eleven unsigned treatises. He judged they could be no one's but Priscillian's. Among them was the book Priscillian had carried to Damasus, with his call in his own words again.

## tree/prosper-of-aquitaine

Prosper of Aquitaine

Near the end of Augustine of Hippo's life, a man in Gaul named Prosper wrote to him in Latin. They had never met, he said, but had traded letters through a deacon, Leontius. Many servants of Christ in Marseilles, he wrote, thought Augustine's teaching on how God calls those he has chosen went against the Fathers.

Gennadius, a priest of Marseilles, calls Prosper a man of Aquitaine, "scholastic in style and vigorous in statement." No ancient source used here gives his city, birth or death, and later writers disagree on whether he was a layman. Edgar Gibson, who translated John Cassian, says he was. Theodor Mommsen, who edited his chronicle, thinks he was a cleric, since Prosper calls the deacon Leontius his brother.

Gibson dates the letter to 429. Prosper then wrote a long poem, On the Ungrateful, against those he saw as enemies of grace. After Augustine died, Gibson says, Prosper and a layman named Hilary appealed to Pope Celestine. This Hilary had also written to Augustine. Gibson keeps him apart from Hilary of Arles. Mommsen takes the letter's writer for Hilary of Arles, writing before he became bishop. They won a letter from the pope to the bishops of Gaul.

Prosper also wrote against Cassian, whom Gibson calls the leading priest of Marseilles. Gennadius thought an unsigned book against Cassian was Prosper's. Gennadius, who sided with Cassian, says Prosper "brands as injurious" works that the church finds helpful. Gibson calls the book powerful, "although not scrupulously fair."

He also kept a chronicle. One entry, which Mommsen's edition sets in 429, has Pope Celestine send Germanus, bishop of Auxerre, to win the Britons back from the Pelagians. Gennadius adds that Leo of Rome's letters against the monk Eutyches were thought to have been dictated by Prosper.

In the chronicle Prosper wrote of Augustine's death. Augustine died during the Vandal siege, he says, still answering Julian of Eclanum, and still defending the grace of Christ.

## tree/rufinus-of-aquileia

Rufinus of Aquileia

Jerome heard, in 374, that his friend Rufinus was traveling among the monks in the far parts of Egypt. He wrote to draw him to Syria, and ended: "A friendship that can end was never real." Within thirty years, a book Rufinus translated had ended theirs.

Rufinus of Aquileia, a monk from northern Italy, spent nearly thirty years among the monks of Egypt and at Jerusalem. Then he came home and put Greek Christian books into Latin. One was First Principles, by Origen, a Greek Bible scholar of the 200s, much admired and by now much attacked. The other was Eusebius's history of the church, which Rufinus carried on in two books of his own. First Principles and many of Origen's other works survive chiefly in his reworded Latin, his editors note. And the preface to First Principles broke his friendship with Jerome.

He spent eight years in Egypt, he writes, learning from the desert monks. When the emperor turned on the monks there for their creed, he says, he was jailed and exiled. At Jerusalem, in the 390s, a bishop from Cyprus charged that Origen taught souls had sinned in heaven before birth, that the devil would be saved, and that this flesh would not rise. Jerome took his side, Rufinus the other, but they made peace before Rufinus sailed home.

At home he argued in a short book that heretics had falsified Origen's writings. Then he translated First Principles, Origen's book on God and creation. Its preface praised, without naming him, a friend who had translated Origen before and pared away what jarred with the faith. He said he followed that method. "Where I found anything in his books against the truth about the Trinity, which he teaches soundly elsewhere, I left it out as not his own. Or I set it down to agree with the rule of faith, which we find him holding to again and again." The rule of faith is the church's summary of its belief.

Jerome's friends at Rome sent him the translation, saying it was falsely translated. Jerome took the preface as aimed at him. In the Greek, Jerome later wrote, Origen ranked the Son below the Father, the Spirit below the Son. He charged that Rufinus had made the Trinity sound to put readers off guard against Origen's other teachings. He made his own literal translation: "In the books On First Principles, I simply set down what stood in the Greek. I did not want the reader to believe what I translated. I wanted him not to believe what you had translated before."

Rufinus told the bishop of Rome that he was no champion of Origen, and had cut only what he believed others added. He affirmed that this very flesh would rise, and the devil burn forever. But the bishop of Rome wrote to the bishop of Jerusalem that Origen meant to undermine the faith, and that the emperors had banned his books. Rufinus was wholly apart from Rome and must answer to God, he wrote, and he neither knew nor wished to know where Rufinus lived. Each wrote a full defense against the other, and the friendship did not recover.

When the Goths broke through into Italy, Chromatius, bishop of Aquileia, asked Rufinus to translate the Church History. Chromatius hoped it would help listeners forget their sufferings a little. He did.

The few Greek fragments of First Principles show, his editors note, that he altered Origen's wording. His History became the Latin West's Eusebius through the Middle Ages, an editor judges. The Greek historian Socrates followed it, then rewrote his first two books, trusting more a bishop who had suffered in those events.

Rufinus's last work came in Sicily, about 410 by his editor's date. The barbarian had set fire to Rhegium, just across the strait. On quiet nights, he wrote, as a comfort in their troubles, he gathered Origen's sermons on Numbers into Latin. He hoped to add Deuteronomy, he said, if God gave him back his eyesight. He died soon after, his editors judge.

## tree/socrates-of-constantinople

Socrates of Constantinople

After riots between pagans and Christians at Alexandria, many pagans fled the city. Two of them, the grammarians Helladius and Ammonius, came to Constantinople. Helladius boasted that he had killed nine men in the fighting. Ammonius complained that Theophilus, bishop of Alexandria, had kept one statue only to make the old religion look ridiculous. Socrates of Constantinople tells this in his Church History. He was their pupil, he says, "in my youth at Constantinople."

He lived in that city, "where I was born and educated." He wrote for a man he calls "O holy man of God, Theodore." He carried the story of the church on from where Eusebius of Caesarea left off. His seven books run from Constantine to the year 439.

He wrote his first two books following Rufinus of Aquileia. Then he read the writings of Athanasius of Alexandria, and judged "that more credit was due to him who had suffered." So he rewrote them and added the emperors' letters and the bishops' decisions. He chose a plain style, he says, because ordinary readers want the facts. He expected zealots to blame him for not calling bishops "Most holy."

Auxanon, a very old priest of the Novatian church, had gone to Nicaea as a youth. He told Socrates his stories when Socrates was "a mere youth." A. C. Zenos, who revised the English translation, lists the many places where Socrates writes kindly of the Novatians, a strict church that stood apart. Baronius and others held that Socrates was one of them. Valesius and most later writers put it down to his fairness.

He is called Scholasticus, a word that could mean a lawyer. Zenos and Valesius think he was one. Harnack, Zenos reports, finds no sign of legal knowledge in his book.

He ended with a prayer that the churches "may live in peace; for as long as peace continues, those who desire to write histories will find no materials for their purpose."

## tree/sozomen

Sozomen

Under the emperor Julian, Christians had to flee from city to city. "My grandfather and many of my ancestors were compelled to flee in this manner," Sozomen writes in his Church History. His grandfather was born to pagans. He and his family, with that of Alaphion, were the first in Bethelia, a town near Gaza, to become Christians. It is said, Sozomen adds, that the monk Hilarion of Gaza won them by driving a demon out of Alaphion.

His grandfather was loved in Gaza and Ascalon for explaining hard places in Scripture. Members of "the other family" founded the first churches and monasteries there. Chester Hartranft, who revised the English translation, thinks it probably means Alaphion's. Sozomen saw some of its good men in his youth, "but they were then very aged."

He became an advocate. A friend was healed at the Michaelium, a shrine near Constantinople. Sozomen calls him "an advocate in the same court of justice as that to which we belong." He says the shrine helped him too.

He sent his history to the emperor, the younger Theodosius. He asked him to "purify it by thy labors," adding or cutting as he saw fit. The dedication says it would run to the emperor's seventeenth consulship. Hartranft dates that to 439, and notes that the history stops at 425.

He searched laws, synod records and letters. Each party, he warns, kept only "such epistles as favored their own heresy."

Sozomen never names Socrates of Constantinople, who wrote a few years before him. Hartranft finds the use of Socrates sure, from stories both tell almost alike. One view, he reports, is that Sozomen plagiarized Socrates. Hartranft calls that charge "wholly unfair" by the custom of the time.

Sozomen also wrote of the first monks. He wanted to leave "such a record of their manner of life that others, led by their example, might attain to a blessed and happy end."

## tree/sulpicius-severus

Sulpicius Severus

Sulpicius Severus went to Tours to see its bishop, Martin. He already meant to write Martin's life. Martin gave him water to wash his hands. At evening he washed his guest's feet himself. Sulpicius says he had not the courage to stop him. Martin talked of leaving the world behind, and held up Paulinus of Nola, a rich man who had sold everything for the poor.

Gennadius of Marseilles says Sulpicius was a presbyter from Aquitaine, "distinguished by his birth," poor by choice and humble. Alexander Roberts, who translated him, says without naming a source that he gave up a name at the bar after his wife died young. Roberts puts his life at about 363 to 420. Ernest Cushing Richardson, in a note to Gennadius, gives after 353 to about 410.

The Life of Martin was done before Martin died. In a letter, Sulpicius tells of a morning dream. Martin appeared, smiling, and held out "the small treatise which I had written concerning his life." When Sulpicius woke, a servant said two monks had come from Tours, and "they have brought word that Martin is dead." Later he wrote Dialogues, where friends talk of the monks of the East and of Martin. He also wrote a Chronicle from the Creation to his own day.

The Chronicle closes with the trial of Priscillian of Abila at Trèves. Sulpicius wanted the heretics put down. Of the bishops who pressed the charges, he writes that "the accusers were as distasteful to me as the accused." Martin, he says, begged the emperor Maximus not to shed their blood.

Gennadius adds that in old age Sulpicius "was led astray by the Pelagians," the followers of Pelagius. He then kept silent until death, to atone. Roberts finds "no ground for any such conclusion" in the writings.

Paulinus of Nola, in a letter Roberts quotes, called Martin blessed for having "a historian worthy of his faith and of his life."
