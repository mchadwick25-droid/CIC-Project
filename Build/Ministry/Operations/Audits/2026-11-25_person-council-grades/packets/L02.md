# Packet L02: person and council stories (census longDescription)

## pontius-the-deacon

Pontius the Deacon

When Cyprian, the bishop of Carthage, was put to death, his people wanted to hear everything about him. So writes the man who set out to tell them, in a book that bears no name. Jerome, about 130 years later, names him as Pontius, the bishop's deacon.

His church was one of the Latin-speaking churches of North Africa. Pontius chose to share his exile, and was with him on his last night. Soon after, he wrote The Life and Passion of Cyprian: the story of his life, and of his suffering and death as a martyr. He wrote it, he says, so that Cyprian's example would reach those who came after. A German church historian, Adolf Harnack, called it the oldest Christian biography that survives. A French historian called it a speech of praise. We know Pontius only from this book, and much of Cyprian's last year comes to us through it; he writes as an admirer.

In 257 the governor sent Cyprian away. Pontius went with him to Curubis, a small coastal town. Cyprian chose him, he writes, as "a willing exile", though he wished he had been chosen to share his death as well.

On their first day there, Cyprian told them a dream, which Pontius gives in Cyprian's words. A young man of more than human size led him before the governor. The governor wrote a sentence without asking him anything, and the young man, standing behind the governor, read it. "He spread his hand out flat, like a sword blade, and mimed the stroke of the usual execution. What he wanted understood, he made as clear as plain speech. I understood the sentence still to come: my martyrdom." Cyprian begged a day's delay to put his affairs in order, and it was granted.

Pontius read the day as a year, and says Cyprian died a year later to the day. Harnack found that reading unconvincing, and said it was not Cyprian's meaning.

When friends later urged Cyprian to withdraw, Pontius says he refused. Cyprian's own last letter says he did leave his gardens for a while, so that he would not be taken to another city to die. His companions, Pontius writes, were with him on the night after his arrest, and the next day, 14 September 258, he was beheaded.

Earlier Christians, Pontius says, had recorded in great detail how ordinary believers died as martyrs. It would be hard, he writes, if so great a bishop were passed over, when he had so much to teach beyond his death. "I have decided to write down a few things, in brief. Not that the life of so great a man is hidden from anyone, even the pagans. It is so that this great, matchless example may be handed on to those after us too, into undying memory."

He began at Cyprian's baptism, the day he was born of God, and passed over everything before it. He tells no wonders but the dream, and says nothing of Cyprian's councils, or of his quarrel with Rome over whether those baptized outside the church must be baptized again. Scholars differ on why.

By 359 the book was listed with Cyprian's own writings and copied with them. A Belgian scholar saw in it a blend of biography and praise.

No early source says how or when Pontius died. His book ends torn between joy and grief. "Shall I grieve that I was not his companion? But his victory must be celebrated. Shall I celebrate his victory? But I grieve that I am not his companion. Yet I must confess to you plainly what you know too: this is how I felt. I rejoice much, even too much, in his glory. But I grieve more that I remained behind."

## suetonius

Suetonius

In his life of the emperor Claudius, the Roman scholar Suetonius lists what Claudius did. He freed the people of old Troy from tax, and he let German envoys sit with the senators at the theater. Between the two comes one sentence: "He drove from Rome the Jews who were constantly rioting at the urging of Chrestus."

Suetonius wrote his lives of twelve of Rome's rulers around 120, decades after Claudius, and the Christians get this line and one other: under Nero, they were punished. Yet these are among the very few notices of the first Christians at Rome from outside the church, and readers still argue over who Chrestus was.

Suetonius was a close friend of Pliny the Younger, and served for a time as secretary to the emperor Hadrian, until Hadrian dismissed him.

The Claudius line gives no year and no source. In the New Testament, the Acts of the Apostles tells of a Jew named Aquila and his wife, whom the apostle Paul met at Corinth. They had come from Italy "because Claudius had commanded all the Jews to depart from Rome," and historians read the two as one event. But Suetonius never says who Chrestus was, a name in use among Greeks and Romans and close in sound to Christus, the Latin for Christ. Editors read it as Christ, placed in the wrong city and reign, though one allows a Jew of that name.

The Nero line sits in another list, between rules for food shops and a ban on chariot drivers' pranks. It reads: "Punishments were inflicted on the Christians, a kind of people given to a new and harmful superstition." Superstition was his word of contempt for their religion. He names no charge and no kind of punishment, and he counts this among Nero's acts that were "beyond criticism." Elsewhere he blames Nero for the great fire, but never ties it to the Christians. Another Roman, Tacitus, tells in full of Christians put to death under Nero.

Historians still weigh both lines, and his English editors noted "how little" even this well-informed Roman knew. Long before, Christian writers had complained that Romans said their name wrong. Around 200, Tertullian, defending the Christians, told them so. "And even when you wrongly say 'Chrestianus', since you do not even know the name for certain, it is formed from sweetness or kindness." The Greek chrestos means kind, or good. Tertullian never names Suetonius; editors set the two side by side.

Seven Latin words, between old Troy's tax and the Germans' theater seats, from a man not writing about Christians at all, still read word by word for one name.

## tacitus

Tacitus

When daylight ended in Nero's gardens, people were burned to light the dark. Others, covered in animal skins, were torn apart by dogs or nailed to crosses, while Nero, dressed as a charioteer, mixed with the crowd. The Roman who tells it, the historian Tacitus, thought they were criminals.

Writing about fifty years after the great fire of Rome in 64, Tacitus gives the fullest account outside the church of what Nero did to the Christians. In it he names their founder, Christus, put to death under Pilate. He despised them and said so. Yet his one chapter became what later historians built on.

After the fire, no prayers or offerings to the gods could stop the belief that it had been set on an order. To kill the rumor, Nero put the guilt on people the crowd called Christians, hated, he says, for their shameful acts, and he had them tortured.

Tacitus stops to explain the name, and gives Christ in its Latin form. "Christus, from whom the name came, was put to death in the reign of Tiberius by one of our governors, Pontius Pilatus." A superstition, he calls it, and a deadly one. It began in Judaea, the Roman province around Jerusalem, and broke out even in Rome, where, he says, everything shameful in the world gathers.

First those who admitted it were seized, and then, on their word, a huge number more. They were convicted, he says, less of setting the city on fire than of hatred of the human race. Historians a century ago read that to mean the inquiry proved them Christians, not that they set the fire. Their deaths were made a show, as Nero held games at the chariot-racing ground.

Almost everything known of these deaths rests on this one chapter, and Tacitus never says where he learned of Christus.

About the year 200, Tertullian, defending the Christians, answered him by name over another of his histories. Tacitus had written there that the Jews honored an ass's head, a charge Tertullian thought had passed to the Christians. Yet, he wrote, "that same Cornelius Tacitus, who truly talks more lies than anyone," also says a Roman general found no image in the temple at Jerusalem.

About 400, Sulpicius Severus, a Christian writing a history of the world, told the same deaths in the same order. But where Tacitus called them criminals, he called them innocent.

Later historians made the chapter their main outside record of these deaths, and a Roman's witness that Christus was put to death under Pilate. In an 1876 debate, a speaker arguing that the Bible was not divine said the passage was open to challenge. The historians who weighed it treated it as Tacitus's own. In 1890, a translator of Eusebius's history of the early church rejected the view of some historians that the victims were Jews, not Christians.

Back in the gardens at night, Tacitus ends with his own judgment. "So even for criminals who deserved to be punished harshly, as an example to all, a feeling of pity arose. For it seemed they were being destroyed not for the public good, but to feed one man's cruelty."

## tatian

Tatian

Do not sneer at the barbarians, as you call all who are not Greek, Tatian told the Greeks, for which of your arts did not come from them? You owe the stars to the Babylonians, geometry to the Egyptians, and your very alphabet to the Phoenicians.

Tatian was born in the land of the Assyrians, east of the Euphrates, yet he had mastered Greek learning. At Rome, in the middle of the second century, he became a Christian and heard the teacher Justin. Then, in a book addressed to the Greeks, he turned on all he had studied, calling it borrowed and younger than Moses. Later he broke with the church, and his weaving of the four Gospels into one story became the gospel most churches speaking Syriac, a form of Aramaic, heard read.

He had been admitted to the mysteries, the secret rites of a god's cult, and at Rome, he says, he saw a Jupiter who delighted in human blood. Searching alone for the truth, he found old writings he calls barbarian writings, which his editors take to be the Hebrew Scriptures. He trusted their plain language and their one God. A philosopher at Rome, he adds, tried to have both Justin and him put to death.

His book mocks the philosophers as vain and greedy, while Christians, he says, teach the poor for free, and their women study philosophy. Then comes his turn: "So I said goodbye to the boasting of the Romans and the lifeless talk of the Athenians, teachings that do not hang together. I made our barbarian philosophy my own." He then sets out to prove, from Greek, Babylonian and Egyptian writers, that Moses was older than Homer.

Irenaeus, who wrote as his opponent, says that after Justin's martyrdom, about 165, Tatian left the church, puffed up with wanting to be a teacher. He taught invisible heavenly powers, Irenaeus says, as the followers of Valentinus did, and called marriage corruption, as Marcion did. He was also the first, Irenaeus says, to teach that Adam was not saved. A church historian dates the break to about 172, and later writers called him the founder of the Encratites, "the self-controlled".

A later writer says Tatian went east, to the land between the Tigris and the Euphrates, and only that writer puts him there. Tatian wove the four Gospels into one continuous story, the Diatessaron, the fourfold gospel, though whether in Greek or Syriac, and before or after the break, is disputed.

In the churches that spoke Syriac it was widely read aloud as the Gospel, and Ephrem the Syrian wrote a commentary on it. In the 400s, two bishops moved against it. A bishop of Edessa ordered the separate Gospels read in every church, and the words we have of his order give no reason. Theodoret of Cyrrhus, in northern Syria, charged that it cut out Jesus's descent from David, a charge the surviving texts only partly bear out. People used it in all innocence, he says, as a handy short book. Then he tells what he did, and by evangelists he means the four Gospel writers. "I myself found more than two hundred such books, held in honor in our churches. I gathered them all and put them away. In their place I brought in the Gospels of the four evangelists."

Still, the fourfold gospel outlived them, and it survives in Arabic and Latin forms. Near the close of his book to the Greeks, the man from the land of the Assyrians stands before them once more. "Now that I know who God is, and what he has made, I stand before you, ready to be questioned on what I teach. I hold to the way of life that follows God, and I will not deny it."

## tertullian-s-voice

Tertullian

In about 197, Tertullian wrote to the governors who sat in judgment for Rome. If they dared not look into the charges openly, he said, "Then let the truth reach your ears at least by the hidden road of silent writing." He pleaded: "One thing only she asks for now: not to be condemned unknown."

Tertullian of Carthage, in Roman Africa, was a convert trained in Roman law, as Eusebius says, who became the foremost early writer of the faith in Latin. He defended the Christians before Rome's courts, claimed the Scriptures for the apostles' churches, and put to work Latin words for God as one substance in three persons. Then he turned to a strict movement of prophets, the New Prophecy, and two centuries later Augustine counted his followers, the Tertullianists, among the heretics.

That book was his Apology, a defense of the Christians. The governors, he argued, hated and judged the name of Christian without learning what Christians did, while crowds blamed them for every flood, drought, famine or plague. He closed defiant: the more you cut us down, the more we grow; "the blood of Christians is seed."

Against teachers who built new doctrines on Scripture, he borrowed a lawyer's plea that throws a case out before it is argued. Their arguments should not be heard until it was settled whose the Scriptures were. The faith, he said, passed from Christ to the apostles, then to their churches, such as Ephesus and Rome, which anyone could visit. "By what right, Marcion, do you cut my timber? By whose leave, Valentinus, do you turn my springs aside?" He went on, "This property is mine. I have held it long; I held it first." And he ended, "I am the heir of the apostles."

The New Prophecy came from Phrygia, in today's Turkey, and its prophets said the Paraclete, the Helper Jesus promised, the Holy Spirit, was speaking anew through them. They asked for stricter fasts, no marrying again after a spouse had died, and no flight from persecution. His defense of the Paraclete, he writes, set him apart from those he called the unspiritual. Why he turned, two later writers disagree. Jerome blames the envy and insults of the clergy at Rome. Augustine says he joined prophets he had once argued against, then broke from them too and gathered his own followers.

After his turn, he wrote against Praxeas, who, by Tertullian's account, taught at Rome that the Father himself was born and suffered. At creation, he said, God commands the light and the Word, the Son, makes it, so the two are distinct. "I have already said how you should take the word 'another'. It names a person, not a substance. It marks a distinction, not a division. I hold everywhere one substance in three who are joined together." Substance is what God is, one and undivided; person is who is distinct: Father, Son and Spirit. His text holds the Latin words trinitas, persona and substantia, which, later writers say, became the Latin church's words for God.

A bishop offered to forgive adultery after repentance. In what is probably his last surviving book, Tertullian replied that only the church of the Spirit could forgive it, "not the church which consists of a number of bishops."

Novatian wrote a book on the Trinity that Jerome took for a summary of Tertullian's, though a modern historian doubts it. Most of Tertullian's life rests on his own books and on Jerome. Jerome heard, at two removes, that Cyprian, the next great bishop of Carthage, read him daily and would ask, "Give me the master." A century on, Lactantius said his Apology had already pleaded the Christians' cause in full.

His followers at Carthage bore his name down to Augustine's day, when the last few joined the Catholic church, the main body, and gave up their building. Augustine named Tertullian a heretic for going over to the New Prophecy.

Tertullian, Jerome says, is said to have lived to a great old age. No one records when or how he died.

## valentinus

Valentinus

Valentinus, a teacher at Rome, once wrote that the heart is like an inn on the road. Its guests leave it rutted and full of dung, since they care nothing for a place that is not theirs. So it is, he wrote, with a heart no one looks after: "It is unclean, the home of many demons. But when the Father, who alone is good, visits it, it is made holy and shines with light." The words survive because Clement of Alexandria quoted them to argue against him.

Irenaeus places him at Rome in the middle of the second century, from the time of one bishop there to another, some twenty years. He founded a school, a circle of students around a teacher. By his opponents' account, it taught that a hidden Father lived beyond the world, that heavenly powers came from him in pairs, and that a lesser maker made this world. Almost everything we know of him comes from the men who wrote against him.

Tertullian, writing at Carthage about fifty years later, alone tells the turn in his life: "Valentinus had hoped to become a bishop, for he was able in mind and in speech. But another man won the place, by the claim his suffering for the faith had given him. Angry at this, Valentinus broke away from the church of the true rule." By that Tertullian meant the church that kept the true teaching. He puts the break down to ambition and a wish for revenge. He adds that the church at Rome expelled Valentinus more than once.

As Irenaeus tells the teaching, a pair beyond any name, Unspeakable and Silence, gave rise to Father and Truth, then Word and Life, and more powers after them. When one of the last fell away, its fall brought forth the world and its maker. Hippolytus, another opponent, reports the school's teaching differently: the Father was alone, and he was all love, and "love is not love unless there is something to love." Tertullian says Valentinus kept these powers inside God, as his thoughts and feelings, until his pupil Ptolemy made them separate beings. The accounts do not agree, and we cannot always tell the teacher from his students.

In a sermon, Valentinus told his hearers they were immortal from the beginning, children of eternal life. They wished for death to be given to them, he said, so that "death may die in you." Clement answered that this made some people saved by their nature, not by Christ's coming.

No source says when or how he died. His students carried the teaching on, and Hippolytus says the school split into an Italian branch and an Eastern one. Irenaeus, Tertullian and Hippolytus wrote long books against it, and in answering they set out what the church held, that one God made the world. For eighteen centuries the school was known almost only through those same books, until its own writings were found in Egypt in 1945.

By Tertullian's day, he says, Valentinus himself was now nowhere, though his followers still bore his name. Only one of them, Axionicus at Antioch, kept his teaching whole.

## aetius-of-antioch

Aetius of Antioch

About the year 350, at Antioch, Bishop Leontius made a man named Aetius a deacon and let him preach. Flavian and Diodorus, who led the orthodox party in the city, raised such an uproar that Leontius had to stop him. Archibald Robertson, who edited Athanasius in English, tells the story. Aetius spoke so boldly about God, Sozomen, the church historian, says, that many people called him Atheus, the godless one.

Philostorgius, a later historian who admired him, says Aetius came from Coele-Syria and was poor. His history survives only in a hostile summary by Photius, a later patriarch of Constantinople. In it Aetius works as a goldsmith to keep himself and his mother, then turns to logic. Socrates of Constantinople, who did not admire him, says he leaned on Aristotle's book of Categories. He built arguments, Socrates says, that deceived himself.

Aetius taught that the Son is not like the Father. If God alone is unbegotten in his being, he reasoned, then the Son, who is begotten, cannot share that being. He set this out in a short book of claims, the Syntagmation. It survives because Epiphanius of Salamis copied it out to refute it. Those who followed Aetius were called Anomoeans, from the Greek word for "unlike."

Many Arians found this too much. They liked to say the Son was like the Father, and to stop using the word "substance" at all. A council met at Constantinople in 360. There his friend Eudoxius, bishop of Antioch, gave him up. In Robertson's words, Aetius was "a sacrifice to the Emperor's scruples." The council took away his office of deacon.

When the emperor Julian came to power, he called Aetius back from exile and sent him warm letters. Philostorgius says Julian gave him land on the island of Lesbos. Aetius later lived at Constantinople, where he died. His pupil Eunomius of Cyzicus carried the teaching on, and in time the sect took his name.

## alexander-of-alexandria

Alexander of Alexandria

Alexander, bishop of Alexandria, lay dying. He called for his deacon Athanasius, who was away. Another man of that name answered, but Alexander kept still. He called again. Then he cried, "O Athanasius, thou thinkest to escape, but thou wilt not escape." Sozomen, a church historian of the next century, tells this story. He took it from a writer he calls "Apolinarius, the Syrian."

Alexander led the church of Alexandria in the time of Constantine. Arius was one of his priests.

The historians, who all held to Nicaea, tell three stories of how the two fell out. Socrates of Constantinople says Alexander once taught his clergy about the Trinity in too fine a way, perhaps. Arius thought he heard the old error of Sabellius in it. Theodoret of Cyrrhus says Arius was jealous that Alexander had been made bishop. Sozomen says Alexander first let both sides argue in front of him, and seemed to lean one way and then the other. Chester Hartranft, who revised Sozomen's English, doubts this. All three agree that Alexander at last put Arius out of the church.

Some of his letters survive in the histories. One went out in his name to bishops everywhere. Archibald Robertson, who edited Athanasius, says John Henry Newman thought Athanasius wrote it. It says that nearly a hundred bishops of Egypt and Libya met and condemned Arius. It tells them to pay no heed to Eusebius of Nicomedia, who had taken up Arius's cause. To Alexander, bishop of Constantinople, he wrote that the Son was never made out of nothing. The Father and the Son cannot be parted, he said.

At the Council of Nicaea, Theodoret says, Athanasius came "as one of the retinue of Alexander." Theodoret has Alexander die five months after the council. Robertson reads an old index of the Easter letters as giving the 17th of April, 328.

Theodoret remembered him as "that admirable bishop, who had successfully withstood the blasphemies of Arius."

## ambrose-of-milan

Ambrose of Milan

In 374, the people of Milan, where the western emperors then held court, were fighting over their next bishop. The governor of the province, Ambrose, went into the church to calm the crowd. While he spoke, a shout went up: "Ambrose for bishop!" The whole crowd took it up. His secretary, Paulinus, who later wrote his life, says a child's voice began it.

Ambrose had not yet been baptized. He resisted: Paulinus says that, against his custom, he had men put to torture, and then fled the city by night. Within eight days he was baptized and made bishop. For twenty-three years he held that the church's buildings and its bread and wine were not the emperor's to command. He would not give up a church to the court, and he kept the emperor Theodosius from communion until Theodosius confessed his sin before the whole church after a massacre.

In the mid-380s, the empress Justina, mother of the boy emperor, wanted one of the city's great churches for her party at court. They were Christians who would not say that the Son is of one being with the Father. "The palaces belong to the emperor, the churches to the bishop," Ambrose told the court.

Most of what follows comes from his own letter to his sister. His crowd seized a man the court's party called a priest, and Ambrose sent clergy to rescue him. The court fined Milan's merchants two hundred pounds of gold and filled the prisons with tradespeople. Then soldiers surrounded the church before dawn, with the people inside. A court secretary asked if he meant to be a tyrant, a man setting up his own power against the emperor's. Ambrose said he had no arms but his own body, and he would not fight. The soldiers, he wrote, came in to pray, and the boy emperor pulled them back and returned the fines.

In those nights of vigil the people sang hymns, and Paulinus says the custom began then, at Milan. The court's men said his hymns had led them astray. Ambrose answered, "I certainly do not deny it." Every day, he said, the whole people sang their faith in the Father, Son and Holy Spirit. "So they have all become teachers, who could hardly be pupils."

A young teacher named Augustine wrote that Ambrose received him like a father, and at Easter in 387 Ambrose baptized him.

A year later, Christians in a town in the East burned a synagogue, and Theodosius ordered their bishop to rebuild it. Ambrose wrote that he would take the blame himself, and would not go to the altar until the emperor promised to change the order and end the inquiry.

In 390 a riot in Thessalonica, a large city of Macedonia, killed one of the emperor's commanders. Theodosius sent soldiers against the people, and many innocent were cut down. When he came to Milan, Ambrose stayed away and wrote to him privately. "I dare not offer the sacrifice if you mean to be there," he said, meaning he would not lead the communion service. "What is not allowed after the blood of one innocent person is shed, is it allowed after the blood of many? I do not think so."

Paulinus says Ambrose would not let the emperor into the church until he had done public penance, staying away from communion for the time set him. The emperor did it. Augustine wrote that the people, seeing their emperor lying on the ground, wept more than they had feared his anger. Theodoret, a Greek historian two generations later, has the two men meeting at the church door. Ambrose's letter and Paulinus do not.

Paulinus, writing some thirty years later, says the singing still went on through almost all the provinces of the West. Historians in Latin and in Greek told the penance again and again.

When Ambrose lay ill, Paulinus tells, the city's leading men begged him to pray for a longer life. He answered, "I have not lived among you in such a way that I am ashamed to live. Nor am I afraid to die, because we have a good Lord." He died on the night of Good Friday, 397. Paulinus, who was there, says he prayed with his arms stretched out like a cross. His lips moved, but they did not hear his voice.

## ammianus-marcellinus

Ammianus Marcellinus

In Rome in the 360s, two men fought to become bishop. Damasus and Ursinus, writes Ammianus Marcellinus, formed parties that came to blows. "It is certain that on one day one hundred and thirty-seven dead bodies were found in the Basilica of Sicininus, which is a Christian church."

Ammianus calls himself "a Greek by birth, and formerly a soldier." His English translator, Charles Duke Yonge, draws the rest from the history. He served under the general Ursicinus and fought in the emperor Julian's war on Persia. At Rome he wrote a history ending with the emperor Valens's death in 378. Yonge says even his religion is uncertain. The general belief is that he kept the old Roman faith.

Ammianus also mocks the bishops of Rome, "enriched by offerings from matrons, riding in carriages, dressing splendidly." Yet he praises provincial priests for their plain dress and strict fasting. He says Julian gave the Christian sects freedom, expecting it to deepen their quarrels. For Julian had found that "no wild beasts are so hostile to men as Christian sects in general are to one another." Still, he calls Julian's law on teachers of rhetoric and grammar "a cruel action, and one deserving to be buried in everlasting silence."

Ammianus believes he never "knowingly perverted" the truth. Edward Gibbon, whom Yonge quotes, called him "an accurate and faithful guide." Professor Ramsay, whom Yonge also quotes, says the age's belief in omens had some hold on him. Ammianus reports that when Julian tried to rebuild the Temple at Jerusalem, "fearful balls of fire burst forth" and stopped the work. The scholar E. A. Abbott, quoted in Blomfield Jackson's notes to Theodoret of Cyrrhus, judges him impartial. But Abbott accepts only that people "generally believed" a fiery sign had stopped it.

A mob at Alexandria killed its bishop, George. Ammianus says the Christians might have saved him, "if both parties had not been equally exasperated by hatred of George."

## antony-the-great

Antony the Great

A young man named Antony, in a village in Egypt, walked into church thinking of the apostles who left everything to follow Jesus. Inside, he heard the Gospel tell a man to sell what he had and give to the poor, and he took it as spoken to him. He gave his family's fields to the villagers and sold the rest for the poor.

He went ever further into the Egyptian desert to live by prayer, work with his hands, and fasting. At last he walled himself into a ruined fort for nearly twenty years. When many wanted to live as he did, his friends forced the door. He persuaded many to take up that life and directed them as a father, until the desert became a city of monks.

One book carried that life far from Egypt. Most of what we know comes from it, a Life written soon after his death, which the church has long credited to Athanasius, bishop of Alexandria. It was written for monks in other lands, as a pattern for their own life, and it was soon put into Latin. Preaching later in the same century, Gregory of Nazianzus called it the laws of the monastic life, set out as a story.

The Life tells how crowds came to him for healing, and how he moved three days further into the desert, to a mountain. There he lived on into the time of the emperor Constantine and his sons, who wrote to him. The Arians taught that the Son of God was created. When they claimed he agreed with them, he came down to Alexandria and taught that the Son was not created.

A historian writing nearly a century later says his disciples flourished in Egypt, Libya, Palestine, Syria and Arabia. And in Augustine's own account, two officials of the emperor's court at Trier found the Life in a monks' cottage and read it. That very hour they resolved to leave the court and serve God. Pontitianus, a fellow official who was there that day, told the story to Augustine in Milan. Augustine never met Antony, but in a garden there, in tears, he remembered him. Then Augustine opened Paul's letters and read, and his doubt was gone. Later he wrote of it in a book addressed to God.

"For I had heard this of Antony. He had come in by chance during the Gospel reading, and was urged on by it. It was as though the words read were spoken to him. 'Go, sell all that you have, give to the poor, and you will have treasure in heaven. And come, follow me.' By such a divine word he was at once turned to you."

## apollinaris-of-laodicea

Apollinaris of Laodicea

When the emperor Julian barred Christian children from the schools of the Greek poets, a scholar at Laodicea in Syria answered with poems of his own. The historian Sozomen says he wrote an epic of Hebrew history in twenty-four books, to stand in for Homer, with plays and odes beside it. His name was Apollinaris, and he was already known as a friend of Athanasius.

In the fourth century he stood with Athanasius for the creed of Nicaea, which said the Son is of one being with the Father. The Arians, who followed Arius, held that the Son was created and lower. Then he taught that in Christ the divine Word, the Son of God as John's Gospel names him, took the place of a human mind. The church condemned that teaching at Rome and at Constantinople in 381, and his followers formed churches of their own.

He taught rhetoric, as his father taught grammar. Bishop George put him out of the church. Sozomen says the cause was his friendship with Athanasius, whom George's party shunned. Socrates says it was friendship with a pagan teacher, or, as some told it, a quarrel over George's wavering creed. In 362 Apollinaris sent envoys to a meeting of bishops that Athanasius held at Alexandria, and its letter calls him bishop.

Under Julian, Socrates says, father and son rewrote Scripture: the father the books of Moses in epic verse, the son the Gospels as dialogues like Plato's. Sozomen gives all of it to Apollinaris alone.

Almost all we have of Apollinaris comes through opponents, or under other names. Gregory of Nyssa quoted a book of his to refute it: "If God was joined to a man, complete to complete, they would be two. One would be Son of God by nature, and one adopted." So, he held, the Word replaced the human mind. Epiphanius of Salamis, who had loved him, at first would not believe the reports.

Jerome heard him lecture at Antioch, and says he learned Scripture from him but never took his teaching. His followers formed churches in many cities under their own bishops, and sang his songs. Sozomen says: "Men sang his songs at drinking parties and at work. Women sang them at the loom."

Power came down on them. A council at Rome under Damasus condemned him and his pupil, in the 370s. After the council at Constantinople in 381, Gregory of Nazianzus answered that without a human mind Christ could not heal ours: what he did not take on, he did not heal. Laws of the emperor Theodosius from 383 forbade his followers to meet or to ordain. In 388 their bishops lost the name of bishop and were barred from the cities. Jerome says he died under Theodosius; the year is not known.

His books lived on in disguise. Men who shared his views but stayed inside the main church put honored bishops' names, Athanasius among them, on his writings. So, says a German scholar who gathered his fragments, they saved them. One was a statement of faith to the emperor Jovian, carried as Athanasius's. It says Christ is not two natures but "one nature of God the Word made flesh", that is, God the Word and his flesh are one nature. In 429 Cyril of Alexandria cited it as Athanasius's. Modern editors give it to Apollinaris; one thought it might be Athanasius's. If they are right, the man condemned in 381 gave Cyril's followers, unseen, what one of them calls a watchword of orthodoxy.

In the 540s the emperor Justinian charged the one-nature party of his day with passing such writings off under the names of honored early teachers. He said: "They cunningly push aside the name of Apollinaris, because it plainly proves ungodliness. They put the names of the holy Fathers on his writings and on their own inventions."

Sozomen thought that, but for people's love of old books, his writings would be prized like the ancients'. Socrates said the poems and dialogues from Julian's years were now as if never written.

## arius

Arius

"We are persecuted because we said: the Son has a beginning, but God has no beginning." So wrote Arius, a priest of Alexandria in Egypt, in the early three-hundreds.

In his letters, God alone was without beginning. The Son, begotten by the Father before time as perfect God, was not eternal beside him. The question split the church: was the Son God in the same full sense as the Father, or a being the Father made before all else? In 325 the council of Nicaea answered: the Son is "begotten, not made", "of one substance with the Father". Yet his teaching lived on, in later forms and under a name its bishops refused, as the faith of emperors for a time and of the Goths for two centuries.

Early on, Arius reasoned that two beings without beginning would make two first principles, and a Son who was part of the Father would make the bodiless God divisible. Around 318 he clashed with his bishop, Alexander, whose council deposed him and his allies as "enemies of Christ". His words survive only because opponents copied them, Athanasius to mock his verse.

Nicaea's creed closed by cursing anyone who said "before he was begotten he was not", his own letter's phrase. The emperor Constantine exiled him and ordered his books burned, with death for anyone who hid one.

Constantine took him back after he gave a creed without the disputed phrases. In Constantinople, the bishop refused to admit him, praying that God would take either Arius or himself. Arius died suddenly the day before his reception, in 336 by the usual dating. Athanasius, who was not there, saw God's judgment in it.

Opponents called the bishops who accepted him Arians. At Antioch in 341 they replied: "We have not been followers of Arius. For how could we, who are bishops, follow a priest?" Athanasius answered that they disowned a hated name.

In 359 and 360, under the emperor Constantius, who favored them, councils dropped Nicaea's word "substance" as unscriptural and called the Son "like the Father". Jerome, an opponent, wrote that at Ariminum, the first, the world "groaned, and was astonished to find itself Arian". Ulfila, bishop of the Goths, accepted that creed, and under the emperor Valens gave them letters and the Scriptures in their tongue. His creed calls the Father "alone unbegotten", in Arius's words, and the Son our Lord and God, yet "subject and obedient in all things" to the Father.

In 589 the Gothic bishops of Spain entered the catholic church at Toledo. They cursed all who still held "the faith and communion that came from Arius", held, they said, until then. Churches that had refused his name gave up their faith under it.

## athanasius-of-alexandria

Athanasius of Alexandria

One night in 356, soldiers surrounded a church in Alexandria, in Egypt, as its people prayed. Their bishop, the leader of the city's church, later wrote: "I thought it wrong to leave the people in such an uproar, rather than face the danger for them first." He sent the people home, and then monks, men who had left ordinary life for prayer, dragged him out past the soldiers.

This was Athanasius, bishop for forty-five years, who had to leave his city five times. At Nicaea, in today's Turkey, bishops agreed a creed, a statement of faith, in 325: God's Son is "of one being" with the Father, fully God. He held to it against the party of Arius, who said the Son had a beginning. The Son became human, he taught, so that people might be made divine, joined to God and freed from death. Only a Son who was truly God could do that. He also wrote the life of a hermit, a monk living alone, and listed the books to be read as God's word.

At Nicaea he was a deacon, an officer under the bishop, says Socrates, a later historian. He became bishop in 328. When Arius was allowed home, he refused him communion, the shared bread and wine of one church, and the emperor Constantine threatened to remove him.

Most of our record comes from Athanasius or writers who used him, but a monk of the Melitians, a breakaway church in Egypt, gave the other side. In 335 he wrote that Athanasius's followers came with soldiers and beat their monks bloody. That year a council of bishops at Tyre, a meeting that judged church matters, removed him from office. Constantine sent him far away to Trier, now in Germany.

He came home in 337, and when a rival bishop came in 339 he fled to Rome, returning in 346. After that night in 356, under Constantine's son, now emperor, he hid for six years. A chronicle of his church records that searchers tortured a woman vowed to God to find him. From hiding he wrote his defenses. Yet in 359 he wrote that those who accepted all of Nicaea's statement but its one word should be met "as brothers with brothers". Those who rejected the council he still called enemies of Christ. In those years, by his editor's dating, he wrote the Life of Antony, for monks abroad who had asked how Antony lived. The emperor Julian drove him out in 362, and in 365 he hid four months near the city before he was led home.

Each year the bishop of Alexandria wrote to the churches of Egypt and Libya to set the date of Easter. In his Easter letter of 367, Athanasius set out the books passed down as Scripture, those the church reads as God's word. He named twenty-two of the Old Testament and twenty-seven of the New. He set other books apart for new believers. Still others, he said, were made up by heretics, teachers he held false, and passed off as old. Of the true books he wrote: "Let no one add to them, or take anything away from them."

A churchman of Antioch put the Life into Latin, Jerome says, and later it reached readers in Syriac, the language of the churches of Syria and Mesopotamia. In 692 a council made his rulings church law, and Greek copies of that law carry his list. In 1921 a Church of England proposal for sharing communion with the Orthodox churches of the East took the books as Athanasius had defined them.

He died in his own city in 373. Gregory of Nazianzus, preaching a few years later, said that many tears were shed for him.

## augustine-of-hippo

Augustine of Hippo

Milan, where the emperor then held court. A young teacher is preparing a speech in praise of the emperor, in which, he says, he would tell many lies. In the street he passes a beggar, well fed and joking, and sighs: the beggar already has the happiness he is straining after.

The teacher was Augustine. He taught rhetoric, the art of persuasive public speaking. In a garden in Milan, he says, a child's voice sent him to the Bible, and he turned to Christ. He became bishop, the leader of the church, in Hippo, a port town in Roman North Africa. There he wrote the Confessions, his life told as a prayer to God, and the City of God, on those who love God above themselves. He taught that grace, God's free and unearned gift, comes first: no one even begins to believe unless God gives it. The Western churches carried that teaching on, and it both shaped and divided them.

Born in Roman Africa, he taught at Carthage and Rome, then Milan; in his own words, he sold the skill of talking to win. At Milan he heard its bishop, Ambrose, preach. There, in 386, came the moment he tells in his Confessions. Torn and weeping, he threw himself down under a fig tree. Then, he writes: "And look, I hear a voice from the house next door, singing and repeating it again and again, like a boy's or a girl's voice, I do not know which: 'Take it and read, take it and read.'" He opened Paul's letters at the first lines he saw: not drunken revels or quarrels, but put on the Lord Jesus Christ. He told his mother, Monica, who rejoiced, and at Easter Ambrose baptized him, washing him in water to join the church.

Back in Africa, he became bishop of Hippo. Early in those years, a bishop of Milan sent him questions on Paul's letter to the Romans. Answering them, he changed his mind about free will, a person's own power to choose good or evil. Near the end of his life, calling Paul the apostle, he looked back: "In answering this question, I worked hard for the free choice of the human will. But God's grace won. I could only come to see that the apostle spoke the plainest truth: 'Who sets you apart? What do you have that you did not receive?'"

Beside his church stood the Donatists, a rival African church, for a century the larger one. It held that bishops who had handed over the Bible in persecution had lost the church. At first, Augustine wrote in about 408, he held that no one should be forced into the church; argument alone should win. But his fellow bishops pointed to his own town, brought back by fear of the emperors' laws. He came to back those laws to force the Donatists back, citing Jesus's parable: compel them to come in.

In the same years Pelagius, a monk at Rome, held that God had already given human nature the power to do good. Augustine answered that free will and good teaching are not enough: God's Spirit must give the love of the good. His friend Possidius, who later wrote his life to honor him, tells it from the winning side. He says meetings of African bishops moved two bishops of Rome, the leaders of the church there, to condemn Pelagius's followers. The emperor's laws then punished them as heretics, false teachers cut off from the church.

He died in 430, at seventy-six, while the Vandals, a people from beyond the empire's frontier, besieged Hippo. Possidius says he told them to keep the church's library.

Those books carried his teaching on. In 529 a meeting of bishops at Orange, in what is now France, set out rulings on grace drawn largely from his writings, as their modern editor shows. A thousand years later Martin Luther sent an opponent to his book On the Spirit and the Letter. Its letter is the law that commands; its spirit is God's Spirit, giving the love to keep it. John Calvin said he could build a whole volume on God's choice and grace from Augustine's words alone. Both stood against Rome. Yet inside the church united with Rome, the Catholic bishop Cornelius Jansen wrote a book called Augustinus, and Rome condemned it.

Near the end of his account, Possidius recalled a poet's lines: whoever reads the words aloud gives the writer his voice.

## auxentius-of-durostorum

Auxentius of Durostorum

From his earliest years, Auxentius of Durostorum was Ulfila's pupil. His parents gave him to the bishop of the Goths, who taught him the holy writings and raised him in the faith like a son. So he says in a letter written after Ulfila died. He could not praise his teacher enough, he writes, and he did not dare stay silent.

Auxentius was a bishop of the late fourth century, in the party its foes called Arian. Friedrich Kauffmann, who edited the letter in 1899, places Durostorum in Lower Moesia, at modern Silistria. He thinks Ulfila held that see first. Maximinus, a bishop of the same party writing against Ambrose of Milan, kept him apart from Auxentius of Milan. Ambrose had named an Auxentius, he says, without saying which. One was alive, at Durostorum. The other, at Milan, had died without a successor.

The letter survives because Maximinus copied it into his own book, a defense of two bishops condemned at the Council of Aquileia in 381. It comes down in a fifth-century manuscript now in Paris, in the margins of Ambrose's On the Faith. Kauffmann, after Ludwig Traube, dates that hand probably to the sixth century.

Auxentius tells his teacher's life in the numbers of Scripture. Ulfila became bishop at thirty, like David, Joseph and Christ. Seven years among the Goths and thirty-three in Roman lands make forty, the span of the saints he followed. Kauffmann notes that writers of that time often used numbers so, yet he counts them as real years.

In Kauffmann's day, W. Luft held that only the first part keeps Auxentius's own words, and the rest is Maximinus's summary of another work. Kauffmann answered that the whole fragment has one biblical style, unlike Maximinus's.

Auxentius was still alive when Maximinus wrote. He was ready, Maximinus says, to stand with Palladius of Ratiaria wherever the faith was put to a public test.

## basil-of-caesarea

Basil of Caesarea

Years later, defending himself in a letter against men who, he wrote, were spreading lies about him, Basil of Caesarea looked back. He had wasted nearly all his youth, he wrote, in the vain labor of learning a wisdom God had made foolish, the learning of this world. "Then one day, like a man roused from deep sleep, I turned my eyes to the wonderful light of the truth of the gospel."

That waking set the course of his life in Caesarea, an inland city in Asia Minor, in the fourth century. Basil gave up teaching rhetoric, the art of public speaking, for a life of prayer and shared work. He wrote rules that taught monks to live together and serve one another, and he built a great lodging for the poor and the sick a little way outside the city. As its bishop, he defended the Holy Spirit's place beside the Father and the Son, and he died before a council, a meeting of bishops, declared it in its creed.

The old writers differ on what turned him. In his own telling, he read in the gospel of selling goods and sharing with the poor. His brother, Gregory of Nyssa, says their sister Macrina turned him, while the historian Rufinus says his friend Gregory of Nazianzus drew him away from teaching.

In Pontus, near the Black Sea, Basil founded a community and led it. His rules were answers to his brother monks' questions, and one asks whether to live alone or with others. Love does not seek its own good, Basil answers, and Christ washed his disciples' feet, but a man alone has no one to serve. "Whom, then, will you wash? Whom will you care for? Behind whom will you be last, if you live by yourself?"

The bishop of Caesarea made him a priest, but they quarreled, and Basil went back to Pontus. When the emperor Valens, who favored the party that ranked Christ, the Son, below God the Father, moved against the churches that held them equal, Basil came back to serve the bishop.

When the old bishop died, Basil was made bishop, though the bishops of the province fought it out of envy, Gregory of Nazianzus says in his speech in Basil's memory. The emperor's highest official ordered him to accept the emperor's faith, or else lose his goods and face exile, torture and death. In Gregory's telling, Basil answered that a man who owns only rags and a few books cannot be robbed, and that death would only send him sooner to God. When the province was divided, a rival bishop claimed part of his churches and once blocked his road with armed men. Basil answered by making more bishops for the province, Gregory says.

His accusers were filling the governor's ears, and Basil wrote to ask what harm the state suffered from him, or from building a lodging for strangers, travelers and the sick, with doctors and transport. The rich gave to it at his urging. Lepers driven from their towns were taken in, and Basil greeted them as brothers and tended them himself.

Basil closed prayers by giving glory to the Father in two forms: with the Son, together with the Holy Spirit, and through the Son, in the Holy Spirit. The first set the Spirit beside the Father and the Son. Some attacked these as new, contradictory words, holding that the Spirit could not be ranked with the Father and the Son. He answered in a book, On the Holy Spirit, that the church already baptized new believers in the name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit together. A letter of his puts the same thought in two lines: "As we were baptized, so we confess our faith. As we confess our faith, so too we give praise." Gregory says that Basil, out of caution, held back from writing in plain words that the Spirit is God, though he often preached it.

The whole city poured around Basil as he died. In 381, after his death, a council at Constantinople declared in its creed that the Spirit is worshipped and glorified with the Father and the Son. Rufinus put his works, his rules for monks among them, into Latin. In the sixth century Benedict of Nursia closed his own Rule by pointing his monks to Basil's as an example of good living.

## conference-of-carthage-411

Conference of Carthage

On the first of June in 411, the Donatist bishops came into a hall of the Gargilian baths at Carthage, with the Catholics' chosen speakers. Clerks of both churches worked beside the court's scribes and wrote down every word. The judge was Flavius Marcellinus, an officer sent by the emperor Honorius to settle their quarrel.

For a century Africa had held two rival churches. The Donatists said the church had been lost by bishops who handed over the Scriptures in persecution, and by all who shared communion with them. Augustine of Hippo reports their claim that the true church was "the one which suffers persecution, not the one inflicting it." The Catholics answered that the church spread through the whole world. It held good and bad together until the end.

The record survives in Latin, each speech signed by its speaker. It breaks off in the third session. For the end we rely on a summary by Augustine, one of the Catholic speakers. Possidius of Calama, another, later told it in his Life of Augustine. Both write for the winning side.

The clerks counted 266 Catholic signatures, and twenty more Catholic bishops came forward who had not signed. The Donatist list held 279 names, including absent bishops and one who had died. Each side chose seven to speak. Among the Donatist seven were Petilian of Constantina and Emeritus of Caesarea in Mauretania.

Marcellinus offered the bishops seats. Petilian refused. Christ, he said, had stood before the governor, so they would gladly stand. The Donatists first argued about the deadline, their written orders and who was the accuser. Only in the third session did the debate reach the old records about Caecilian, the bishop whose ordination had split Africa.

Marcellinus sent both sides out, wrote his sentence, then called them back and read it. Augustine says it found that every document had proved the Donatists wrong. His edict banned their meetings. The churches he had lent them were to go to the Catholics.

The Donatists said the judge had been bribed and had ruled at night. They appealed, and Possidius says the emperor condemned them as heretics.

Later, at Caesarea, Augustine challenged Emeritus to say whatever he had not been allowed to say. Emeritus answered only that the records "of what was done by the bishops at Carthage contain the proof of whether we were victors or vanquished."

## constantine-the-great

Constantine the Great

In 312 the church had lived through nearly ten years of persecution. That year Constantine marched on Rome against his rival emperor Maxentius, and with him begins Imperial and Juridical Christianity, the church of the emperors. On the march, Eusebius of Caesarea says, he prayed that God would show him who he was. Constantine told Eusebius the rest himself, years later and under oath. At midday a cross of light stood above the sun, with the words "Conquer by this," and that night Christ came to him in sleep. Lactantius, who wrote in Constantine's own day, knows only a dream that told him to mark the sign of Christ on his soldiers' shields.

Maxentius was driven into the Tiber at the broken bridge. Soon Constantine and his fellow emperor Licinius met at Milan and decreed that Christians and all others should be free to follow the religion each thought best. The Christians' meeting places were to be given back, and Eusebius says churches rose again from their foundations.

At Alexandria the presbyter Arius argued that if the Father begat the Son, there was a time when the Son was not. Constantine wrote to Arius and his bishop that the quarrel was trifling. Then he called the bishops to Nicaea and sat among them, "your fellow servant," as he wrote. Eusebius told his church that the emperor urged one added word, homoousios, meaning of the same essence, and an imperial edict then sent Arius into exile.

Years later Arius gave the emperor a written creed, and Constantine ordered Athanasius, by then bishop of Alexandria, to receive him back. Socrates of Constantinople says he did not wish to see the church torn. Athanasius refused, his enemies brought charges against him, and Constantine sent him into exile in Gaul.

Only when he knew he was dying, Eusebius says, did he seek baptism. Sozomen calls him "the first of the emperors who began to be zealous for the Church". He was buried in the Church of the Apostles at Constantinople, and Sozomen says later Christian emperors were buried there too.

## council-of-arles-314

Council of Arles

In 314, by the dating of Charles Joseph Hefele, the historian of the councils, the emperor Constantine wrote to his deputy in Africa. He ordered Caecilian, bishop of Carthage, to travel to Arles in Gaul. Three of his opponents were to come, with bishops from every African province. The state would carry them. All were to arrive "by August 1."

The church of Africa had split over Caecilian. His opponents, later called Donatists, said that the bishop who ordained him, Felix of Aptunga, had handed over the Scriptures in the persecution. To them, his ordination was worthless. They had asked Constantine for "judges from Gaul," which the persecution had spared. Bishops at Rome cleared him in 313. His accusers told Constantine that those bishops "had shut themselves up somewhere" and judged as suited them. So he called a larger meeting.

The full record of Arles is lost. What survives comes from the winning side. It is the bishops' letter to Silvester, bishop of Rome, with their canons, and Constantine's letters. The losing side reaches us mostly through its foes. Its petition survives because Optatus of Milevis, who wrote against it, copied it.

The letter to Silvester opens with thirty-three names, led by Marinus, whom Hefele takes as bishop of Arles. Caecilian is among them. From a list of those present, Hefele finds bishops from Gaul, Italy, Spain and Africa, and one from London. Some old accounts speak of six hundred, a figure Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius, calls wild.

The bishops told Silvester that the accusers "have been either condemned or rejected." They ruled that Easter be kept "upon the same day" everywhere. Africans baptized again anyone who came to them from heretics. Now each would be questioned on the creed. If he had been baptized in the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, "hands shall be laid upon him and no more." Another rule struck at the African quarrel. A cleric proved by public records to have handed over the Scriptures would lose his office. Those he had ordained would keep theirs.

The losers appealed to the emperor. "They ask judgement from me, who am myself waiting for the judgement of Christ," Constantine wrote to the bishops. By Hefele's account, he heard the case at Milan in 316 and cleared Caecilian again.

Augustine of Hippo reminded the Donatists that after Arles they had "appealed to the Emperor himself."

## council-of-chalcedon-451

Council of Chalcedon

Evagrius, a historian on the council's side, wrote that the quarrel turned on one letter: the Greek for "from two natures" against "in two natures". In the autumn of 451, at Chalcedon, just across the water from Constantinople, more than five hundred bishops called by the emperor Marcian answered. "One and the same Jesus Christ, Son, Lord, Only-begotten, is known in two natures: without confusion, without change, without division, without separation."

A nature is what Christ is, as God and as man, and a person is who he is. The bishops confessed one person, fully God and fully human, neither mixed nor split. "From two natures" meant one nature formed out of the two, and they refused to make it the rule. The Greek and Latin churches came to keep this as their fourth council, while the churches of Egypt and Syria, and later Armenia, refused it and still stand apart.

In 448 a synod under Flavian of Constantinople had condemned Eutyches, a monastery head, who said Christ had one nature after the union. A year later a council at Ephesus under Dioscorus of Alexandria restored Eutyches and deposed Flavian. Leo of Rome called it a council of robbers.

Leo did not come, but sent legates and his letter to Flavian, the Tome. It taught that each nature does what belongs to it, in communion with the other: the Word works miracles, while the flesh suffers injuries. As it was read, bishops cried that Leo and Cyril of Alexandria taught the same thing.

Dioscorus stood on trial. As the minutes of 449 were read, he broke in: "I accept 'from two'. 'Two' I do not accept. I am forced to be shameless: my soul is at stake." Leo's legates deposed him for receiving Eutyches, keeping Leo's letter from being read at Ephesus, excommunicating Leo, and refusing three summonses. The minutes, kept by the winning side, record his reply that guards held him back, and his request for the emperor's officials. He was exiled to Gangra.

Most bishops wanted to sign a draft that said "from two natures", but Leo's legates threatened to leave and hold a council in the West. The emperor's judges asked, Leo or Dioscorus? The bishops cried that they believed as Leo, and "two natures" went in. Any other creed was banned. The council also put monks under their bishops, and ranked Constantinople next after Rome, a canon Leo declared void.

Egypt refused at once. Priscus, a historian in Alexandria then, says a crowd burned soldiers alive in the temple of Serapis, and the emperor's troops abused the city's women. The Syriac chronicle says believers were killed at the altar. The Coptic Church's own History says Dioscorus died in exile with the crown of martyrdom, and its calendar of saints keeps his feast.

The Coptic, Syriac and Armenian churches condemned Eutyches too. They confessed Christ as one nature from two, the faith, they said, of Athanasius and Cyril, and to them "two natures" divided him. Their Syriac chronicle says: "That council tore into ten thousand pieces the whole robe of Christ, woven in one piece from the top."

Later emperors set the council aside before the Greek and Latin churches settled on it. Armenia refused it by synod in the sixth century. In 1912 Ormanian, a former Armenian patriarch, still described the Armenian, Syrian, Coptic and Ethiopian churches as one communion, apart from the Greek and Latin.

## council-of-ephesus-431

Council of Ephesus

Years after a council of bishops removed him, Nestorius, once bishop of Constantinople, wrote from exile: "Who was judge? Cyril. Who was the accuser? Cyril. Who was bishop of Rome? Cyril. Cyril was everything." Cyril of Alexandria had led it, in the place of Rome's bishop.

The emperor Theodosius II called that council to Ephesus. Could Mary rightly be called Mother of God, the one who gave birth to God? Nestorius would not simply call her that, offering Mother of Christ, he wrote, to settle his city's dispute. Cyril said she was, since her child was God's own Son, made man. At stake was how God and a man are one in Christ.

In June 431, Cyril opened the council before John of Antioch and Syria's bishops arrived. It deposed Nestorius, removing him from office, and John's assembly deposed Cyril. The verdict held in the empire, but not in Persia.

The bishops had waited sixteen days past Pentecost, the appointed day. Count Candidian, the emperor's officer, begged four days more for John. His protest says: "I warned you of this, then, not once but many times, and humbly begged you, and I gained nothing." They drove him out, he says, with insult and by force.

Nestorius, summoned three times, would not come. By the council's own record, the winning side's, he had soldiers guard his house. The bishops heard the creed of Nicaea, the statement of faith from a century before, and judged that Cyril's letter agreed with it and Nestorius's did not. They removed him and cut him off from the church's fellowship. Nestorius's side told the emperor that agitators surrounded their houses, and that Memnon, bishop of Ephesus, shut the churches to them and threatened death.

When John arrived, his assembly deposed Cyril and Memnon for holding their council apart, against the emperor's orders. It charged that Cyril's twelve curses held old heresies. The curses damned anyone who denied Mary was Mother of God or split Christ in two; Nestorius's answering curses condemned making God and man one nature. Cyril's council cut John's men off in turn.

Cyril's council also banned any creed but Nicaea's, and upheld Pelagius's condemnation: his accusers charged that he said grace only forgives past sins and shows what to do.

In August, a new commissioner had the emperor's letter read "by persuasion and force", as he reported. He proclaimed Nestorius, Cyril and Memnon deposed and arrested all three, and that autumn the emperor dissolved the council.

In 433, John and his bishops sent Cyril a confession of Christ's natures, what he is as God and as man: "A union of two natures has taken place. So we confess one Christ, one Son, one Lord. In keeping with this idea of a union without mixing, we confess the holy Virgin as Mother of God." Cyril accepted it, and John accepted Nestorius's deposition, the condition Cyril had set, says a historian of the councils. Some of John's bishops refused that price: Theodoret of Cyrrhus accepted Cyril's faith, but not the deposition.

In 451 the council of Chalcedon took as its guide Cyril's letter of peace to John's bishops, and his letter to Nestorius.

Wigram, an English historian of the Church of the East, in Persia, wrote in 1910 that it had not accepted this council, nor formally rejected it. It had never been asked to do either.

An English writer on that church's rites was surprised, in 1852, that the rites often censured Nestorius's being cut off, yet never mentioned the council that expelled him.

## council-of-nicaea-325

Council of Nicaea

"There was a time when he was not." That, the council's own letter said, was what Arius, a priest of Alexandria, taught about the Son. It charged him with more: that the Son came from nothing, and that he is "a creature". At stake was whether the Son is God as the Father is, or a being with a beginning.

In 325 the emperor Constantine called bishops to Nicaea. They answered with a creed that calls the Son "begotten, not made", of one being with the Father, and curses anyone who says there was a time when he was not. Arius and two bishops were condemned. Later councils set this creed first, yet for decades it divided the church.

Two troubles had reached the emperor. Eusebius, a bishop who was there and admired the emperor, writes that in Egypt bishops clashed with bishops in every city, and the churches kept Easter on different days. When his letter to Alexandria failed, Constantine called the bishops to Nicaea, in today's Turkey, and let them travel by the imperial post, with horses. More than 250 came by Eusebius's count, though other witnesses gave higher numbers. The emperor told them, as Eusebius reports it: "For I count civil strife in the church of God worse than any war or fierce battle."

Three men who were there tell the debate, each differently. Eusebius says the emperor asked for one word to be added: homoousios, "of one being". Athanasius, who was there as a deacon and later wrote in the council's defense, says Arius's friends read the bishops' words, like "from God", as true of every creature too. They were caught "whispering ... and winking". A third, who also opposed Arius, says a written statement was torn up "in the sight of all".

All but a few signed the creed, and the emperor backed its sentence with force. He sent Arius into exile, a historian a century later says. Constantine's own letter made hiding a book by Arius, rather than burning it, a crime punished by death.

It also settled other business. In Egypt, a leader who had ordained clergy of his own kept his title but could ordain no one. Easter would fall on one day everywhere. And twenty rules, called canons, set the order of the church. They ran from how a bishop is made to mercy for those who had fallen away without being forced.

In 381 a council at Constantinople, meeting over newer heresies, ruled that the faith of the 318 fathers, its name for Nicaea, "shall not be set aside". In 451 Chalcedon called it "pre-eminent". But it did not hold at once. Arius was recalled, and the word "of one being" divided those who held it from those who would not.

The rules traveled as written law, and a Persian synod, a modern scholar reports, swore to accept them. About ninety years after Nicaea, the bishops of Africa, meeting at Carthage, found a disputed rule missing from their copy, and wrote east for exact copies. Their own copy held the creed and the twenty rules, and they read it aloud as law. One of their own bishops had sat at Nicaea and carried it home.
