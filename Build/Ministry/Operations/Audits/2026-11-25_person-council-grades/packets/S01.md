# Packet S01: person and council stories (census longDescription)

## tree/aetius-of-antioch

Aetius of Antioch

About the year 350, at Antioch, Bishop Leontius made a man named Aetius a deacon and let him preach. Flavian and Diodorus, who led the orthodox party in the city, raised such an uproar that Leontius had to stop him. Archibald Robertson, who edited Athanasius in English, tells the story. Aetius spoke so boldly about God, Sozomen, the church historian, says, that many people called him Atheus, the godless one.

Philostorgius, a later historian who admired him, says Aetius came from Coele-Syria and was poor. His history survives only in a hostile summary by Photius, a later patriarch of Constantinople. In it Aetius works as a goldsmith to keep himself and his mother, then turns to logic. Socrates of Constantinople, who did not admire him, says he leaned on Aristotle's book of Categories. He built arguments, Socrates says, that deceived himself.

Aetius taught that the Son is not like the Father. If God alone is unbegotten in his being, he reasoned, then the Son, who is begotten, cannot share that being. He set this out in a short book of claims, the Syntagmation. It survives because Epiphanius of Salamis copied it out to refute it. Those who followed Aetius were called Anomoeans, from the Greek word for "unlike."

Many Arians found this too much. They liked to say the Son was like the Father, and to stop using the word "substance" at all. A council met at Constantinople in 360. There his friend Eudoxius, bishop of Antioch, gave him up. In Robertson's words, Aetius was "a sacrifice to the Emperor's scruples." The council took away his office of deacon.

When the emperor Julian came to power, he called Aetius back from exile and sent him warm letters. Philostorgius says Julian gave him land on the island of Lesbos. Aetius later lived at Constantinople, where he died. His pupil Eunomius of Cyzicus carried the teaching on, and in time the sect took his name.

## tree/alexander-of-alexandria

Alexander of Alexandria

Alexander, bishop of Alexandria, lay dying. He called for his deacon Athanasius, who was away. Another man of that name answered, but Alexander kept still. He called again. Then he cried, "O Athanasius, thou thinkest to escape, but thou wilt not escape." Sozomen, a church historian of the next century, tells this story. He took it from a writer he calls "Apolinarius, the Syrian."

Alexander led the church of Alexandria in the time of Constantine. Arius was one of his priests.

The historians, who all held to Nicaea, tell three stories of how the two fell out. Socrates of Constantinople says Alexander once taught his clergy about the Trinity in too fine a way, perhaps. Arius thought he heard the old error of Sabellius in it. Theodoret of Cyrrhus says Arius was jealous that Alexander had been made bishop. Sozomen says Alexander first let both sides argue in front of him, and seemed to lean one way and then the other. Chester Hartranft, who revised Sozomen's English, doubts this. All three agree that Alexander at last put Arius out of the church.

Some of his letters survive in the histories. One went out in his name to bishops everywhere. Archibald Robertson, who edited Athanasius, says John Henry Newman thought Athanasius wrote it. It says that nearly a hundred bishops of Egypt and Libya met and condemned Arius. It tells them to pay no heed to Eusebius of Nicomedia, who had taken up Arius's cause. To Alexander, bishop of Constantinople, he wrote that the Son was never made out of nothing. The Father and the Son cannot be parted, he said.

At the Council of Nicaea, Theodoret says, Athanasius came "as one of the retinue of Alexander." Theodoret has Alexander die five months after the council. Robertson reads an old index of the Easter letters as giving the 17th of April, 328.

Theodoret remembered him as "that admirable bishop, who had successfully withstood the blasphemies of Arius."

## tree/ammianus-marcellinus

Ammianus Marcellinus

In Rome in the 360s, two men fought to become bishop. Damasus and Ursinus, writes Ammianus Marcellinus, formed parties that came to blows. "It is certain that on one day one hundred and thirty-seven dead bodies were found in the Basilica of Sicininus, which is a Christian church."

Ammianus calls himself "a Greek by birth, and formerly a soldier." His English translator, Charles Duke Yonge, draws the rest from the history. He served under the general Ursicinus and fought in the emperor Julian's war on Persia. At Rome he wrote a history ending with the emperor Valens's death in 378. Yonge says even his religion is uncertain. The general belief is that he kept the old Roman faith.

Ammianus also mocks the bishops of Rome, "enriched by offerings from matrons, riding in carriages, dressing splendidly." Yet he praises provincial priests for their plain dress and strict fasting. He says Julian gave the Christian sects freedom, expecting it to deepen their quarrels. For Julian had found that "no wild beasts are so hostile to men as Christian sects in general are to one another." Still, he calls Julian's law on teachers of rhetoric and grammar "a cruel action, and one deserving to be buried in everlasting silence."

Ammianus believes he never "knowingly perverted" the truth. Edward Gibbon, whom Yonge quotes, called him "an accurate and faithful guide." Professor Ramsay, whom Yonge also quotes, says the age's belief in omens had some hold on him. Ammianus reports that when Julian tried to rebuild the Temple at Jerusalem, "fearful balls of fire burst forth" and stopped the work. The scholar E. A. Abbott, quoted in Blomfield Jackson's notes to Theodoret of Cyrrhus, judges him impartial. But Abbott accepts only that people "generally believed" a fiery sign had stopped it.

A mob at Alexandria killed its bishop, George. Ammianus says the Christians might have saved him, "if both parties had not been equally exasperated by hatred of George."

## tree/auxentius-of-durostorum

Auxentius of Durostorum

From his earliest years, Auxentius of Durostorum was Ulfila's pupil. His parents gave him to the bishop of the Goths, who taught him the holy writings and raised him in the faith like a son. So he says in a letter written after Ulfila died. He could not praise his teacher enough, he writes, and he did not dare stay silent.

Auxentius was a bishop of the late fourth century, in the party its foes called Arian. Friedrich Kauffmann, who edited the letter in 1899, places Durostorum in Lower Moesia, at modern Silistria. He thinks Ulfila held that see first. Maximinus, a bishop of the same party writing against Ambrose of Milan, kept him apart from Auxentius of Milan. Ambrose had named an Auxentius, he says, without saying which. One was alive, at Durostorum. The other, at Milan, had died without a successor.

The letter survives because Maximinus copied it into his own book, a defense of two bishops condemned at the Council of Aquileia in 381. It comes down in a fifth-century manuscript now in Paris, in the margins of Ambrose's On the Faith. Kauffmann, after Ludwig Traube, dates that hand probably to the sixth century.

Auxentius tells his teacher's life in the numbers of Scripture. Ulfila became bishop at thirty, like David, Joseph and Christ. Seven years among the Goths and thirty-three in Roman lands make forty, the span of the saints he followed. Kauffmann notes that writers of that time often used numbers so, yet he counts them as real years.

In Kauffmann's day, W. Luft held that only the first part keeps Auxentius's own words, and the rest is Maximinus's summary of another work. Kauffmann answered that the whole fragment has one biblical style, unlike Maximinus's.

Auxentius was still alive when Maximinus wrote. He was ready, Maximinus says, to stand with Palladius of Ratiaria wherever the faith was put to a public test.

## tree/basil-of-caesarea

Basil of Caesarea

Years later, defending himself in a letter against men who, he wrote, were spreading lies about him, Basil of Caesarea looked back. He had wasted nearly all his youth, he wrote, in the vain labor of learning a wisdom God had made foolish, the learning of this world. "Then one day, like a man roused from deep sleep, I turned my eyes to the wonderful light of the truth of the gospel."

That waking set the course of his life in Caesarea, an inland city in Asia Minor, in the fourth century. Basil gave up teaching rhetoric, the art of public speaking, for a life of prayer and shared work. He wrote rules that taught monks to live together and serve one another, and he built a great lodging for the poor and the sick a little way outside the city. As its bishop, he defended the Holy Spirit's place beside the Father and the Son, and he died before a council, a meeting of bishops, declared it in its creed.

The old writers differ on what turned him. In his own telling, he read in the gospel of selling goods and sharing with the poor. His brother, Gregory of Nyssa, says their sister Macrina turned him, while the historian Rufinus says his friend Gregory of Nazianzus drew him away from teaching.

In Pontus, near the Black Sea, Basil founded a community and led it. His rules were answers to his brother monks' questions, and one asks whether to live alone or with others. Love does not seek its own good, Basil answers, and Christ washed his disciples' feet, but a man alone has no one to serve. "Whom, then, will you wash? Whom will you care for? Behind whom will you be last, if you live by yourself?"

The bishop of Caesarea made him a priest, but they quarreled, and Basil went back to Pontus. When the emperor Valens, who favored the party that ranked Christ, the Son, below God the Father, moved against the churches that held them equal, Basil came back to serve the bishop.

When the old bishop died, Basil was made bishop, though the bishops of the province fought it out of envy, Gregory of Nazianzus says in his speech in Basil's memory. The emperor's highest official ordered him to accept the emperor's faith, or else lose his goods and face exile, torture and death. In Gregory's telling, Basil answered that a man who owns only rags and a few books cannot be robbed, and that death would only send him sooner to God. When the province was divided, a rival bishop claimed part of his churches and once blocked his road with armed men. Basil answered by making more bishops for the province, Gregory says.

His accusers were filling the governor's ears, and Basil wrote to ask what harm the state suffered from him, or from building a lodging for strangers, travelers and the sick, with doctors and transport. The rich gave to it at his urging. Lepers driven from their towns were taken in, and Basil greeted them as brothers and tended them himself.

Basil closed prayers by giving glory to the Father in two forms: with the Son, together with the Holy Spirit, and through the Son, in the Holy Spirit. The first set the Spirit beside the Father and the Son. Some attacked these as new, contradictory words, holding that the Spirit could not be ranked with the Father and the Son. He answered in a book, On the Holy Spirit, that the church already baptized new believers in the name of the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit together. A letter of his puts the same thought in two lines: "As we were baptized, so we confess our faith. As we confess our faith, so too we give praise." Gregory says that Basil, out of caution, held back from writing in plain words that the Spirit is God, though he often preached it.

The whole city poured around Basil as he died. In 381, after his death, a council at Constantinople declared in its creed that the Spirit is worshipped and glorified with the Father and the Son. Rufinus put his works, his rules for monks among them, into Latin. In the sixth century Benedict of Nursia closed his own Rule by pointing his monks to Basil's as an example of good living.

## tree/conference-of-carthage-411

Conference of Carthage

On the first of June in 411, the Donatist bishops came into a hall of the Gargilian baths at Carthage, with the Catholics' chosen speakers. Clerks of both churches worked beside the court's scribes and wrote down every word. The judge was Flavius Marcellinus, an officer sent by the emperor Honorius to settle their quarrel.

For a century Africa had held two rival churches. The Donatists said the church had been lost by bishops who handed over the Scriptures in persecution, and by all who shared communion with them. Augustine of Hippo reports their claim that the true church was "the one which suffers persecution, not the one inflicting it." The Catholics answered that the church spread through the whole world. It held good and bad together until the end.

The record survives in Latin, each speech signed by its speaker. It breaks off in the third session. For the end we rely on a summary by Augustine, one of the Catholic speakers. Possidius of Calama, another, later told it in his Life of Augustine. Both write for the winning side.

The clerks counted 266 Catholic signatures, and twenty more Catholic bishops came forward who had not signed. The Donatist list held 279 names, including absent bishops and one who had died. Each side chose seven to speak. Among the Donatist seven were Petilian of Constantina and Emeritus of Caesarea in Mauretania.

Marcellinus offered the bishops seats. Petilian refused. Christ, he said, had stood before the governor, so they would gladly stand. The Donatists first argued about the deadline, their written orders and who was the accuser. Only in the third session did the debate reach the old records about Caecilian, the bishop whose ordination had split Africa.

Marcellinus sent both sides out, wrote his sentence, then called them back and read it. Augustine says it found that every document had proved the Donatists wrong. His edict banned their meetings. The churches he had lent them were to go to the Catholics.

The Donatists said the judge had been bribed and had ruled at night. They appealed, and Possidius says the emperor condemned them as heretics.

Later, at Caesarea, Augustine challenged Emeritus to say whatever he had not been allowed to say. Emeritus answered only that the records "of what was done by the bishops at Carthage contain the proof of whether we were victors or vanquished."

## tree/council-of-arles-314

Council of Arles

In 314, by the dating of Charles Joseph Hefele, the historian of the councils, the emperor Constantine wrote to his deputy in Africa. He ordered Caecilian, bishop of Carthage, to travel to Arles in Gaul. Three of his opponents were to come, with bishops from every African province. The state would carry them. All were to arrive "by August 1."

The church of Africa had split over Caecilian. His opponents, later called Donatists, said that the bishop who ordained him, Felix of Aptunga, had handed over the Scriptures in the persecution. To them, his ordination was worthless. They had asked Constantine for "judges from Gaul," which the persecution had spared. Bishops at Rome cleared him in 313. His accusers told Constantine that those bishops "had shut themselves up somewhere" and judged as suited them. So he called a larger meeting.

The full record of Arles is lost. What survives comes from the winning side. It is the bishops' letter to Silvester, bishop of Rome, with their canons, and Constantine's letters. The losing side reaches us mostly through its foes. Its petition survives because Optatus of Milevis, who wrote against it, copied it.

The letter to Silvester opens with thirty-three names, led by Marinus, whom Hefele takes as bishop of Arles. Caecilian is among them. From a list of those present, Hefele finds bishops from Gaul, Italy, Spain and Africa, and one from London. Some old accounts speak of six hundred, a figure Arthur McGiffert, who translated Eusebius, calls wild.

The bishops told Silvester that the accusers "have been either condemned or rejected." They ruled that Easter be kept "upon the same day" everywhere. Africans baptized again anyone who came to them from heretics. Now each would be questioned on the creed. If he had been baptized in the Father, the Son and the Holy Spirit, "hands shall be laid upon him and no more." Another rule struck at the African quarrel. A cleric proved by public records to have handed over the Scriptures would lose his office. Those he had ordained would keep theirs.

The losers appealed to the emperor. "They ask judgement from me, who am myself waiting for the judgement of Christ," Constantine wrote to the bishops. By Hefele's account, he heard the case at Milan in 316 and cleared Caecilian again.

Augustine of Hippo reminded the Donatists that after Arles they had "appealed to the Emperor himself."

## tree/council-of-ephesus-431

Council of Ephesus

Years after a council of bishops removed him, Nestorius, once bishop of Constantinople, wrote from exile: "Who was judge? Cyril. Who was the accuser? Cyril. Who was bishop of Rome? Cyril. Cyril was everything." Cyril of Alexandria had led it, in the place of Rome's bishop.

The emperor Theodosius II called that council to Ephesus. Could Mary rightly be called Mother of God, the one who gave birth to God? Nestorius would not simply call her that, offering Mother of Christ, he wrote, to settle his city's dispute. Cyril said she was, since her child was God's own Son, made man. At stake was how God and a man are one in Christ.

In June 431, Cyril opened the council before John of Antioch and Syria's bishops arrived. It deposed Nestorius, removing him from office, and John's assembly deposed Cyril. The verdict held in the empire, but not in Persia.

The bishops had waited sixteen days past Pentecost, the appointed day. Count Candidian, the emperor's officer, begged four days more for John. His protest says: "I warned you of this, then, not once but many times, and humbly begged you, and I gained nothing." They drove him out, he says, with insult and by force.

Nestorius, summoned three times, would not come. By the council's own record, the winning side's, he had soldiers guard his house. The bishops heard the creed of Nicaea, the statement of faith from a century before, and judged that Cyril's letter agreed with it and Nestorius's did not. They removed him and cut him off from the church's fellowship. Nestorius's side told the emperor that agitators surrounded their houses, and that Memnon, bishop of Ephesus, shut the churches to them and threatened death.

When John arrived, his assembly deposed Cyril and Memnon for holding their council apart, against the emperor's orders. It charged that Cyril's twelve curses held old heresies. The curses damned anyone who denied Mary was Mother of God or split Christ in two; Nestorius's answering curses condemned making God and man one nature. Cyril's council cut John's men off in turn.

Cyril's council also banned any creed but Nicaea's, and upheld Pelagius's condemnation: his accusers charged that he said grace only forgives past sins and shows what to do.

In August, a new commissioner had the emperor's letter read "by persuasion and force", as he reported. He proclaimed Nestorius, Cyril and Memnon deposed and arrested all three, and that autumn the emperor dissolved the council.

In 433, John and his bishops sent Cyril a confession of Christ's natures, what he is as God and as man: "A union of two natures has taken place. So we confess one Christ, one Son, one Lord. In keeping with this idea of a union without mixing, we confess the holy Virgin as Mother of God." Cyril accepted it, and John accepted Nestorius's deposition, the condition Cyril had set, says a historian of the councils. Some of John's bishops refused that price: Theodoret of Cyrrhus accepted Cyril's faith, but not the deposition.

In 451 the council of Chalcedon took as its guide Cyril's letter of peace to John's bishops, and his letter to Nestorius.

Wigram, an English historian of the Church of the East, in Persia, wrote in 1910 that it had not accepted this council, nor formally rejected it. It had never been asked to do either.

An English writer on that church's rites was surprised, in 1852, that the rites often censured Nestorius's being cut off, yet never mentioned the council that expelled him.

## tree/council-of-sardica-343

Council of Sardica

At Sardica, the accusers of Athanasius of Alexandria came to the church where Ossius of Cordoba was staying. Ossius later told the emperor Constantius his offer. If they would not speak before the whole council, they could speak "before me alone." If Athanasius was guilty, the bishops would reject him. If he was innocent and they still shunned him, Ossius would take him home to Spain. Athanasius agreed. They refused.

Bishops of the East had deposed him. Julius, bishop of Rome, had received him into communion, as he had Marcellus of Ancyra. Constans, emperor of the West, pressed his brother Constantius, and the two called a council at Sardica.

Socrates of Constantinople and Sozomen date it by a consulship that their editors take as 347. Charles Joseph Hefele, the historian of the councils, works from Athanasius's Easter letters and puts its start late in 343.

Most of the record comes from one side. Athanasius kept the Western letters, and Ossius's, in his own books. The Eastern bishops' letter survives in Latin, in papers gathered by Hilary of Poitiers, who held to Nicaea.

Athanasius says about 170 bishops came. The Eastern letter speaks of eighty from the East, and Hefele puts the West at about ninety-five.

The Eastern bishops would not sit with men they had already condemned. Their letter says they found the two men sharing the sacrament with Ossius. It says Marcellus would "set bounds to the perpetual, eternal, and timeless kingdom of our Lord Christ." The West would not shut them out, so the Easterners left. Socrates says they met on their own at Philippopolis in Thrace.

Each side then condemned the other. The Westerners cleared both men. They deposed their accusers, among them Valens of Mursa and Ursacius of Singidunum. They kept the creed of Nicaea. Under Ossius they also made rules for appeals. A bishop who lost his case could write to Julius, to "honour the memory of Peter the Apostle." The Easterners deposed Julius and Ossius, Sozomen says.

Socrates says East and West shared communion only up to a mountain called Soucis, "which divides the Illyrians from the Thracians." Constans threatened his brother with war, and Athanasius went home in 346, by the index to his Easter letters. Later, Pope Zosimus quoted a Sardica rule to the bishops of Africa as a rule of Nicaea. They could not find it in their copies.

## tree/councils-of-ariminum-and-seleucia-359

Councils of Ariminum and Seleucia

In the winter of 359, at Ariminum in Italy, the prefect Taurus pleaded with tears before the last twenty bishops still refusing. They had been shut in the city for seven months, he said, worn down by winter and want. Sulpicius Severus, a historian from Gaul on the Nicene side, tells the scene.

The emperor Constantius II wanted one creed for the whole church. The Council of Nicaea had called the Son homoousios, "of one substance" with the Father. Many bishops disliked the word, since Scripture does not use it. Constantius split the meeting in two. The Western bishops met at Ariminum, and the Eastern bishops at Seleucia in Isauria.

The council's own acts are lost, apart from letters and decrees kept by the Nicene side, who wrote most of the histories. The other side speaks through its creeds and a statement by Acacius of Caesarea, which the Nicene writers copied.

Athanasius of Alexandria, writing that same year, counts four hundred bishops and more at Ariminum. There Valens of Mursa and Ursacius of Singidunum read out a creed drawn up that May at Sirmium. It called the Son "like to the Father who begat him, according to the Scriptures." It said that "no mention should be made of substance in reference to God." The majority refused it, deposed Valens and Ursacius, and sent envoys to the emperor.

At Nice in Thrace the envoys signed a creed that again left out "substance." Socrates of Constantinople says they used its name to pass the creed off as that of Nicaea. Back at Ariminum the bishops gave way one by one. Jerome later wrote, "The whole world groaned, and was astonished to find itself Arian."

At Seleucia about a hundred and sixty met. Acacius led a smaller party that rejected both homoousion and homoiousion, the words for one substance and like substance. The majority kept an older creed from Antioch and deposed him. At Constantinople, as the new year began, the emperor pressed Seleucia's envoys to sign the creed of Ariminum.

After Constantius died, the bishops of Ariminum said they had been tricked. A council at Rome under Damasus said the size of the meeting at Ariminum settled nothing. But Ulfila, bishop of the Goths, had signed the creed at Constantinople. Long afterwards, at Hippo in Africa, a bishop of that party named Maximinus told Augustine that he held the faith of Ariminum.

## tree/didymus-the-blind

Didymus the Blind

Antony the hermit once came down from the desert to Alexandria to help its bishop, Athanasius, against the Arians. A blind scholar of Alexandria named Didymus came to see him. Jerome of Strido tells the story and places it in his own childhood. They talked of Scripture, then Antony asked whether Didymus missed his eyes. At first Didymus was ashamed to answer. Asked a third time, he said it was a great grief to him. Antony told him a wise man should not grieve for "a faculty which he shares with ants and flies and gnats." Socrates of Constantinople, Sozomen and Rufinus of Aquileia tell the same saying, each listing different animals.

Didymus, called the Blind, taught in that city. Palladius of Helenopolis visited him four times over ten years. Didymus told him he had lost his sight at four. He had never learned to read, Palladius says. Rufinus, who heard him speak, says he listened while others read. When the readers fell asleep, he went over it all again, like a clean animal chewing the cud.

Rufinus says he became teacher of the church's school at Alexandria, with Athanasius's approval. Socrates says he was seen as "the great bulwark of the true faith" against the Arians. Jerome put his book on the Holy Spirit into Latin.

Didymus admired Origen. Socrates says he wrote on Origen's book First Principles. Those who attack Origen, Didymus wrote, cannot grasp "the profound wisdom of that extraordinary man." In the sixth century, the historian Evagrius Scholasticus reports, the emperor Justinian condemned Didymus along with Origen.

Jerome had studied with Didymus at Alexandria. In the later quarrel over Origen, he was blamed for calling him master. He answered that a pupil may call an old and learned man master. He had much to thank him for, Jerome wrote, "for what I did not know I learned from him, and what I knew already I did not forget."

## tree/ephrem-the-syrian

Ephrem the Syrian

Around 372, famine struck Edessa, a Syriac-speaking city of upper Mesopotamia on Rome's border with Persia. Ephrem, a deacon of its Syriac church, from Nisibis or its countryside, left his cell. Palladius of Helenopolis says he asked the rich why they let people die while their wealth went to ruin. They said they had no one honest enough to run the relief, so Ephrem asked what they thought of him. They said they knew him for a man of God, and he answered: "See, on your behalf I appoint myself to take in and care for the needy." Yet he is remembered for something else: he taught the church's faith in hymns.

He had written prose against rival teachers: Mani, Marcion and Bardaisan of Edessa. In Ephrem's telling, Mani taught that Light and Darkness were there from the start and became mixed, and that Darkness made the body a prison for the soul. A later prayer of Mani's own followers names the same two powers. Marcion, Ephrem says, taught that a second God, a stranger to this world and above its Maker, came down to lift souls out of it. In a hymn, Ephrem charged that Bardaisan denied the body would rise. To deny it, he answered, slanders God's justice and grace.

Sozomen says Bardaisan's son Harmonius had set Greek ideas about the soul and the body's birth, death and rebirth to songs the Syrians loved. In his own homily, though, Ephrem blames Bardaisan himself. In Sozomen's account, Ephrem feared the people would absorb those ideas, so he learned the same meters and wrote hymns that held the church's teaching. From that time on, Sozomen says, the Syrians sang the songs of Ephrem. His works were put into Greek in his own lifetime, and Basil of Caesarea admired him. Jerome wrote that some churches read his writings aloud after the Scriptures. In the next century, Theodoret of Cyrrhus says, his hymns still brightened the festivals of the martyrs.

Around 600, Barhadbeshabba, the historian of the school of Nisibis, wrote that Ephrem had opened a school in Edessa, and that after his death his disciples made it grow. Later, he says, Narsai founded a school at Nisibis, by then inside Persia, and most of the students of Edessa came to him. In his telling, the schools that spread through the Church of the East under Persia began with Ephrem.

Ephrem ran the relief for a year, and a month after it ended, Palladius says, he died in his cell.

## tree/epiphanius-of-salamis

Epiphanius of Salamis

Epiphanius called his book the Panarion, which means a medicine chest. In its preface he says what it is for. "We are going to show you the names of the heresies. We will uncover their unholy deeds, which are like venom and poison. Alongside them we will fit antidotes: cures for those who have been bitten, and protection for those who may yet fall into it."

A heresy, to Epiphanius, was a wrong belief held by people led astray ever since Adam, the first man. Epiphanius was a monk from Palestine who became bishop of Salamis, the chief city and port of Cyprus. He named eighty heresies as poisons and set a cure beside each. Later writers, from Augustine to a council at Nicaea four centuries after Epiphanius, took his book as their list. In old age he carried the same fight to Jerusalem and Constantinople, against the followers of Origen, a Christian teacher who had died long before.

He wrote the Panarion about 375. Each chapter names a heresy, tells its teaching and answers it, twenty from before Christ's coming and sixty after. He knew some heresies from books, some by report, some with his own eyes and ears. He copied one opponent's own letter whole, so that no one could say he answered from report alone.

He preached against Origen in the church at Jerusalem. In a letter to the bishop of Jerusalem Epiphanius says why. Origen, he writes, taught that souls were angels who fell into bodies, denied that this flesh would rise from the dead, and read the garden of Eden as hidden meanings. Epiphanius held to the plain sense. Genesis, the first book of the Bible, names the Euphrates among the rivers of Eden, and he writes: "I have also drunk from the great river Euphrates. It was not spiritual water but real water, the kind you can touch with your hand and take into your mouth."

In the same letter he defends what he did to the scholar Jerome's brother, who had several times run away from being made a priest. During a service near Epiphanius's own monastery, he says, he had deacons, the clergy who serve under priests, seize the man and hold his mouth shut. Then he made him a deacon, then a priest, as the man cried that he was unworthy. The monks of Jerome's monastery had complained for a year that no one celebrated the sacraments, the church's holy rites, for them, he explains, and he acted from the fear of God, to provide what the church needed. In a village church he found a curtain painted with an image of Christ or a saint. Holding that an image of a man in church went against Scripture, he tore it apart and asked that such curtains be banned.

Then Theophilus, bishop of Alexandria, wrote to win him to his own campaign against Origen. His letter said he had put a few monks who followed Origen out of the church in Egypt. Epiphanius had the bishops of Cyprus forbid reading Origen's books. The historian Socrates says Theophilus wanted revenge on those monks, the Long Brothers, and used Epiphanius, a man so devout that he was simple in mind and manners. Another historian, Sozomen, says instead that Epiphanius had long loathed Origen's books, and that Theophilus saw no danger in following his example, because the people praised Epiphanius.

At Theophilus's urging, he sailed to Constantinople. There he made a man a deacon in the territory of its bishop, John Chrysostom, against church rules. He would not pray with John unless John condemned Origen's books and drove the Long Brothers from the city. One of the Long Brothers asked him whether he had read any of their books. He had not, and had judged them by report. He sailed for Cyprus and died at sea before he reached home.

Augustine wrote his own book on the heresies in the order of Epiphanius's list. Four centuries on, when Christians quarreled over pictures of Christ and the saints in churches, both sides claimed him, and opponents of images quoted writings under his name. In 787 the council at Nicaea ruled that those writings were not his, arguing that his eighty chapters never listed the making of images. A book written to answer the council's argument replied with his own letter about the torn curtain.

Epiphanius set one thing more after the eighty heresies in his chest of cures: the church, which he calls Christ's holy bride and says was there from the beginning.

## tree/eutyches

Eutyches

Eutyches, the head of a monastery in Constantinople, stood at last before a meeting of bishops. He had said he was ill and old. Pressed to say what Christ is, as God and as man, he answered: "I confess that our Lord was from two natures before the union. But after the union I confess one nature." The union was the moment God the Word became man in the Virgin's womb.

For that sentence he was condemned in 448, restored the next year, and condemned again at Chalcedon in 451. He had fought the teaching of Nestorius, which divided Christ, by holding to one nature. Yet both sides of the coming split would curse his name.

An abbot of many years in the emperor's city, Eutyches wrote that from childhood he had wanted only a quiet life.

A bishop, Eusebius of Dorylaeum, charged Eutyches before Flavian, bishop of Constantinople. Asked to say two natures and curse all who denied them, he refused. He called Christ's body the body of God, yet a human body taken from the Virgin. But his judges recorded that he denied it shared our nature. Nor would he curse the fathers who, he said, had rejected the words "two natures". Flavian's meeting stripped him of his priesthood, his monastery and his place in the church.

Eutyches wrote to Leo, bishop of Rome, that a faction had readied his sentence before the hearing and forced other abbots to sign. He told Leo he had appealed at the trial. Flavian told Leo: "This he certainly never did." Leo, answering in a long letter to Flavian called the Tome, judged Eutyches very unwary and exceedingly ignorant. The letter taught one Christ in two natures, each keeping its own character.

The emperor called a council at Ephesus, under Dioscorus, bishop of Alexandria; its Syriac record says Eutyches's petition moved the emperor to call it. He told the emperor his trial record was false. To the council he wrote that he had been handed to a crowd ready to drag him through the city as a heretic. Flavian, he wrote, had shown him no pity: "He had no regard for my gray hairs. I have grown old with them in wars against heretics, and in confessing the true faith."

That record was read aloud. A bishop who had judged him denied a sentence the record gave as his own: that denying two natures after the union meant mixing God and man. He then said he did not remember. The council shouted that his accuser should be burned alive, and cursed all who said two natures. Its minutes, kept by the side that restored him, say he declared himself right-believing. He was restored, and Flavian deposed, charged with changing the faith.

A new emperor then called a council at Chalcedon. It received Leo's letter against Eutyches's teaching. Egypt's bishops condemned those who said Christ's flesh came from heaven, but would not name Eutyches. The council demanded they curse him and sign Leo's letter. They asked to wait for their new archbishop. So writes Evagrius, on Chalcedon's side, from its records some 140 years later.

The bishops at Chalcedon cried that Leo's opponents were Eutychians. A later writer on their side called the churches that refused the council followers of Dioscorus and Eutyches. Yet those churches were not his followers. They kept what he had defended: one Christ against Nestorius. They rejected his account of Christ's body: in their own chronicle, he would not confess it shared our nature. About 475, Timothy Aelurus returned from exile as bishop of Alexandria. The prefect, that chronicle says, was thought to be a Eutychian, so the people demanded in church that Timothy curse Nestorius and Eutyches. He did so at once, aloud, and was cleared of suspicion.

After 451 the record goes quiet; no source we have tells where or when he died. What was left was his name, cursed by both sides, even by churches still holding the one nature after the union that the council at Ephesus had cheered.

## tree/eznik-of-kolb

Eznik of Kolb

Before anything existed, the Persian magi said, there was a being called Zurvan. Eznik of Kolb retells their story in his Refutation of the Sects. For a thousand years, they said, Zurvan offered sacrifice so that a son, Ormizd, would be born to make heaven and earth. Then he doubted, and two sons were conceived: Ormizd from the sacrifices, and Ahriman from the doubt.

Eznik told the story to take it apart. If Zurvan doubted, he wrote, then he was powerless, imperfect and ignorant, and he himself was the cause of evil. Two springs never flow from one opening, he argued, one sweet and one bitter. He reports, as an opponent, a faith he attacks.

Eznik was a pupil of Sahak the Great and of Mesrop Mashtots, who made the Armenian letters. Koriun says Sahak and Mesrop sent Eznik and a companion, Joseph of Baghin, to Edessa to translate the Scriptures again from Syriac. The two went on to Constantinople to study and became translators of Greek. Koriun and Leon, a priest of Vanant, joined Eznik there, as a brother they had grown up with. They came home with true copies of the Scriptures and the canons of the councils of Nicaea and Ephesus.

Moses of Khoren tells it differently. He says lying letters drew the two to Byzantium without their teachers' order. The new translations made from their copies, he adds, were found faulty in many places.

Eznik was later bishop of Bagrevand. Elishe and Lazar of Pharp both list him among the bishops who answered a Persian edict calling Armenia to the Persian faith. Elishe places the meeting at Artashat and says the edict came from Mihr-Nerseh, the Persian governor. Lazar names King Yazdegerd as its author, and neither gives a year. The introduction to his book in Victor Langlois's collection says no one recorded his birth or death.

The same introduction calls him, beyond question, one of the most famous writers of Armenia in the fifth century.

## tree/fabiola-of-rome

Fabiola of Rome

In the Lateran basilica at Rome, a widow of a noble family stood among the penitents. Before the bishop, the priests and the people, she showed her "dishevelled hair, pale features, soiled hands and unwashed neck." Jerome of Strido says that all of them wept when they saw her weep.

She was Fabiola of Rome. Her story comes from Jerome's letters, above all the letter of praise he wrote after her death to Oceanus. W. H. Fremantle's English edition of Jerome dates her death to 399. Jerome says she had left her first husband for faults so terrible that he will not name them. She then remarried while that husband still lived. Jerome defends the separation but calls the second marriage a fault, perhaps forced on her by need. After her second husband died, she did public penance.

Then she sold her large property for the poor. "She was the first person to found a hospital," Jerome writes in his praise, "into which she might gather sufferers out of the streets." He says she carried the sick on her own shoulders, washed their wounds and fed them by hand. She gave to monasteries on the islands and along the coast. With Jerome's friend Pammachius, she bought a house at Portus as a home for strangers.

Against her friends' advice, she sailed to Jerusalem and was for a time Jerome's guest. She studied Scripture with him and pressed him with questions. When news came that the Huns were near, she went home to Rome.

Jerome says that crowds filled the streets for her funeral, singing psalms. She had once asked him what the halting-places of Israel in the desert meant, and he had promised her a book. He wrote it after her death as a funeral offering. In its pages, he wrote, "she may rejoice that she has passed through the wilderness of this world and has come at last to the land of promise."

## tree/first-council-of-constantinople-381

First Council of Constantinople

At Constantinople in 381, Gregory of Nazianzus stood before the council's bishops and gave up his see. "Farewell, my throne, envied and perilous height," he told them.

Socrates of Constantinople says the Arians had held the city's churches for forty years. In November 380 the emperor Theodosius drove out their bishop. He then called a council to establish the Nicene Creed and appoint a bishop for the capital.

Some who called the Son fully God would not say the same of the Holy Spirit. Their view reaches us only through opponents, chiefly Gregory and the synod of 382. Gregory put their question in an oration: "From whence are you bringing in upon us this strange God, of Whom Scripture is silent?"

The council's minutes are lost. Its creed, its canons and a short letter to Theodosius survive. The creed comes down to us in the acts of the Council of Chalcedon, seventy years later, as Henry Percival, the council's English editor, notes. The histories of Socrates, Sozomen and Theodoret of Cyrrhus all take the Nicene side.

Socrates counts a hundred and fifty bishops. Theodoret says the emperor summoned only those of his half of the empire. By the reading of Charles Joseph Hefele, the historian of the councils, Meletius of Antioch led them at first. He died while they sat. Thirty-six bishops of the Macedonian party, led by Eleusius of Cyzicus, also came. They would not call the Son "of one substance" with the Father, and they left.

Some bishops, chiefly from Egypt, objected to Gregory as the city's bishop. That was when he gave his farewell and withdrew. Theodosius then chose Nectarius, an official who had not yet been baptized, Sozomen says.

The council ruled that the faith of Nicaea "shall not be set aside, but shall remain firm." It condemned heresies by name, among them the Pneumatomachi, whose name means fighters against the Spirit, and the Apollinarians. The creed calls the Spirit "the Lord and Giver-of-Life," worshipped and glorified with the Father and the Son. Hefele thinks the bishops reworked a creed already in use. F. J. A. Hort argued they issued none. A canon ranked the bishop of Constantinople next after Rome, "because Constantinople is New Rome."

Rome accepted the creed but long refused the canons, Hefele says. Later Gregory the Great of Rome counted this council among four that he compared to the four Gospels.

## tree/frumentius

Frumentius

Two small boys, Frumentius and Edesius, were found under a tree, going over their lessons, Rufinus says. Everyone else on their ship had just been killed. The people of that coast spared them out of pity and took them to their king.

Frumentius, from a family of Tyre, grew up a captive at that court and rose to share in ruling it. He gathered the Christian traders there to pray, then went to Alexandria to ask its bishop, Athanasius, for a bishop, and came back as that bishop. Rufinus calls the land India. But a later emperor's letter, kept by Athanasius, names its city Auxumis, that is Aksum, in what is now Ethiopia and Eritrea.

Rufinus of Aquileia wrote the story down around 400, and the whole early life rests on him. In the times of Constantine, he writes, a philosopher from Tyre sailed there with two boys, his relatives, whom he was teaching. Homeward, the ship put in at a harbor for water. The people there had heard their treaty with Rome was broken, and by custom they killed every Roman they found.

The king made Edesius his cup-bearer, and gave Frumentius charge of his accounts and records. Dying, the king set them free. But the queen begged them, Frumentius above all, to stay and help her rule until her son grew up.

With the kingdom in his hands, Rufinus says, God stirred him to seek out Christians among the Roman traders. He urged them to set up meeting places to pray in, so that a Christian seed might grow there.

When the prince came of age, they handed everything back and came back to the Roman world. Edesius went to his parents at Tyre. Rufinus names him as his source: "We learned that these things happened so, not from common talk, but from Edesius himself, who told it. He had been Frumentius's companion, and was later made a priest at Tyre."

Frumentius went to Alexandria instead, saying the Lord's work must not be hidden, and urged Athanasius to send a worthy man as bishop. Athanasius answered, Rufinus reports: "What other man like this could we find, with the Spirit of God in him as in you, who could carry it through in this way?" He made Frumentius bishop and sent him back.

The date is disputed. Rufinus gives no year, but sets it just after Athanasius became bishop, which was in 328. Athanasius's English editor says the story's timeline rules that out. He puts it no earlier than late 346.

Back as bishop, Rufinus writes, it was said that signs like the apostles' were done through him, and countless people turned to the faith.

That tie to Alexandria was fought over. About 356, with Athanasius driven from his city, the emperor Constantius wrote to the rulers of Auxumis, demanding the same teaching in their churches as in the empire's. Athanasius called the emperor's bishops Arians, after Arius, who taught that the Son had a beginning, before all time, and God had none. Athanasius held that the Son shares the Father's own being. The letter ordered: "So send Frumentius the bishop to Egypt with all speed." There the emperor's bishop in Athanasius's place would judge him, and restore him only if found fit. The letter named who had made him: "This Frumentius was set in this rank by Athanasius, a man guilty of countless crimes." The emperor's side had charged Athanasius, among other things, with once turning the emperor's brother against Constantius, and writing to a rebel emperor. He denied it all. We do not know how the rulers answered, or what Frumentius did.

The church of Aksum kept taking its bishops from Alexandria. Three Greek church historians retold the story in the century after Rufinus.

The Ethiopian church's book of saints, written down about a thousand years later, tells his story again. It says he preached the peace of Christ across Ethiopia, and was called Abba Salama for it, Abba meaning father. After he had greeted the people of Ethiopia, it says, he died in peace.

## tree/germanus-of-auxerre

Germanus of Auxerre

Saxons and Picts had joined forces against the Britons. Much of the British army had just been baptized at Easter. Now, Constantius of Lyon writes, Germanus, a bishop from Gaul, made himself leader of the battle. He hid the men in a valley ringed by hills. As the enemy came near, the bishops shouted Alleluia three times, and the whole army took up the cry. The enemy fled in terror.

Constantius wrote his Life of Germanus about 480 or a little later, by the date of its editor, Wilhelm Levison. It is a saint's Life, full of wonders, and Levison knows no other record of that raid.

Germanus was born at Auxerre to noble parents, Constantius says. He studied law at Rome and pleaded in the prefect's courts. The state made him a governor of provinces. Then clergy and people with one voice demanded him as their bishop, and he gave way against his will.

Why he first went to Britain is told two ways. Prosper of Aquitaine's chronicle, in an entry its editor sets in 429, says a Pelagian named Agricola was corrupting the British churches. Pope Celestine, urged by the deacon Palladius, sent Germanus to act for him. Constantius never mentions Celestine. He says the Britons asked the bishops of Gaul for help, and a large synod chose Germanus and Lupus of Troyes. Levison calls every attempt to join the two accounts guesswork.

When the heresy spread again, Constantius says, Germanus went back with a bishop named Severus. Bede, writing in England, tells both journeys, but Levison says Bede copied Constantius almost word for word, so he is no second witness.

At last he went to Ravenna, where Placidia ruled the empire. He fell ill there and asked that his body be sent home, which Placidia granted against her wish.

A Gallic chronicle of 452 says that Germanus, bishop of Auxerre, was famous for his miracles and for the strictness of his life.

## tree/hilarion-of-gaza

Hilarion of Gaza

A woman named Aristaenete came to a hermit in the desert near Gaza, her three sons near death. She had just been to see Antony in Egypt. Now she begged this monk to come to the city and heal them. He answered that he never left his cell. She would not go until he promised to come after sunset. He made the sign of the cross over each boy, and by Jerome's account they recovered within the hour.

The monk was Hilarion. Jerome of Strido wrote his life about 390, and it is the main record. W. H. Fremantle's English edition of Jerome warns that the Life "contains, amidst much that is legendary, some statements which attach it to genuine history." Jerome tells it as the life of a saint.

Hilarion was born at Thabatha, near Gaza, late in the third century. He studied at Alexandria, then went to see Antony in the desert, and stayed about two months. When he came home his parents had died. He gave away his share and settled in the coastal scrub to live as Antony did. Jerome calls him the first monk in Palestine. "It was he who originated this mode of life and devotion," he writes, and sets him beside his master. "The Lord Jesus had in Egypt the aged Antony: in Palestine He had the youthful Hilarion."

As his fame grew, crowds came for healing, and Hilarion grieved at it. "I have returned again to the world," he said, "and have received my reward in my lifetime." He left Palestine and moved from place to place, through Egypt, Sicily and Dalmatia, and died on Cyprus at about eighty.

Sozomen, the church historian, had a family reason to remember Hilarion. He says Hilarion cured a man named Alaphion near Gaza "by simply calling on the name of Christ." It was said, Sozomen writes, that the cure made Christians of Alaphion's household and his own grandfather's.

## tree/hilary-of-arles

Hilary of Arles

Celidonius, a bishop removed from office in Gaul, went to Rome and said he had been condemned unjustly. Hilary, bishop of Arles, heard of it and set out after him on foot. He hurried into Rome without horse or pack animal, says the Life of Hilary. He told Leo, bishop of Rome, that he had come to pay his respects, not to argue a case.

The Life takes Hilary's side, and it survives here only as a passage quoted in a collection of council records. The editors of that collection give it to Honoratus of Marseilles, or to whoever else wrote it.

Leo tells it the other way. Writing to the bishops of the province of Vienne, he says Celidonius proved he had been wrongly deposed. Hilary, with no good answer, poured out words "such as no layman could make and no priest listen to." He rushed through the provinces like a courier, Leo says, with soldiers behind him. Leo took from him his power over the province of Vienne. Leo was a party to the quarrel, and Charles Lett Feltoe, who translated his letters, calls it his "not very creditable encounter with Hilary."

Hilary preached a sermon on Honoratus of Arles, who had led him to the monks' island of Lérins. In it he says Honoratus came back to their homeland for his sake, and that he fought against him. Hilary later followed Honoratus as bishop of Arles. Honoratus, he says, sought in him a vein of faith, as of his own blood, and his editors take them for kinsmen.

As bishop, Gennadius of Marseilles says, he chose poverty and farmed beyond his strength. He was of noble birth and had been brought up very differently. By August 449 Leo was writing of "Hilary of holy memory."

In the sermon Hilary gave thanks for his own turning. Good Jesus, he prayed, you broke my chains, moved by the prayers of your servant Honoratus.

## tree/honoratus-of-arles

Honoratus of Arles

The island off the coast lay empty. Hilary of Arles says no one lived there because it was wild and people feared its poisonous snakes. Honoratus, later bishop of Arles, went in unafraid, repeating the psalm about treading on the asp and the basilisk. The snakes gave way, Hilary says, and Honoratus set up a monastery there. Eucherius of Lyons, a friend on a nearby island, names the island Lérins and Honoratus its founder.

Almost all we know of his life comes from one sermon in his memory. Its author, Hilary, was his disciple and later his successor as bishop. It is a sermon of praise, and tells a saint's life, wonders and all.

His family, Hilary says, had risen to the consulship. His father kept him from baptism as long as he could, afraid that faith would carry him off entirely. As a young man Honoratus cut his hair short, fasted and gave to the poor. His elder brother, Venantius, followed him. The two sold their lands for the poor and left home with an old holy man, Caprasius, as their guide. They sailed for Greece, and Venantius died there, at Methone.

Honoratus came back by way of Italy. On the island he was made a presbyter, against his will, Hilary says. A church and cells for monks went up. People came to him from many lands, speaking many tongues.

Hilary was one of those he won. For Hilary's sake he went back to his home country, and wept and prayed until Hilary gave way. Later, Arles called Honoratus to be its bishop. His first care, Hilary says, was peace in a church split over the choice of a bishop. His last sermon was on the feast of the Epiphany. Edgar Gibson, who translated John Cassian, dates his death to January 429.

Hilary remembers his letters. When one reached Eucherius on wax tablets, Eucherius said that Honoratus had given the wax its honey back.

## tree/john-cassian

John Cassian

Two monks of southern Gaul wanted more of the Egyptian desert. One wanted his monks taught its rules; the other longed to sail there, leaving a land frozen, as John Cassian put it, with the cold of Gaul. Cassian, who had lived among those monks, wrote seven Conferences for the two, so that one need not make so risky a journey.

The books made the journey instead. At Marseilles, Cassian carried Egypt's teaching into Latin for the monks of the West. His Conferences were talks with the old monks of the desert. A century later, Benedict's Rule named the Conferences first for the monks' evening reading. But his thirteenth Conference, on grace, God's free help, and free will, our own power to choose, drew an attack from Prosper of Aquitaine.

Most of his early life comes from his own books. As a young man he joined a monastery at Bethlehem with his friend Germanus. The two sailed to Egypt, promising a quick return, but stayed seven years, went back to keep their word, then returned to the desert of Scete.

At Constantinople, Bishop John Chrysostom made him a deacon, in the word of Gennadius, a later priest of Marseilles. When John was sent into exile, Germanus and Cassian carried his clergy's letters to the bishop of Rome. They wrote that bishops had plotted and soldiers helped drive their bishop out.

At Marseilles, Cassian became a priest and founded two monasteries, one for men and one for women. For a bishop whose province had none, he wrote the Institutes, on the dress, prayers and psalms of the Egyptian houses. Eight of its books give the eight chief faults, from greed for food to pride, and their remedies.

In the 420s came twenty-four Conferences. In the thirteenth, as Cassian writes him, an old hermit says God gives the first good thought. Yet when God sees a good will stirring, he strengthens it, whether he planted it or it grew from our effort, as with Zacchaeus and the thief on the cross. "So these two, the grace of God and free will, seem opposed to each other. But really they are in harmony."

In 429 Prosper told Augustine that many at Marseilles thought Augustine's teaching on God's choosing ran against the Fathers. If God chose before time who would be saved, they said, effort was in vain. The letter does not name Cassian.

Leo, later bishop of Rome, asked him to answer Nestorius, the new bishop of Constantinople, who would not call Mary the Mother of God. In seven books, the year before a council at Ephesus removed Nestorius, Cassian charged him with saying Christ was born a mere man. He tied that error to Pelagius's followers, who, he wrote, said people could live without sin if they liked.

Prosper then wrote a book against "the Conferencer", as its title calls him, defending Augustine. By the translator's account, it takes twelve sentences from the thirteenth Conference and accepts only the first, that God begins every good thought. It rejects the rest, among them that a good will may sometimes begin in us, and never names Cassian directly. He died at Marseilles, but no year is known.

Benedict's Rule names no author for its Conferences, but the title is Cassian's. Those monks did not come from his houses; they read his books.

Gennadius, on Cassian's side, took the unnamed attack to be Prosper's, and said some of their views on grace and free will were at odds. Of Cassian's works and Prosper's attack he wrote, "The church of God finds them wholesome, but he brands them as harmful."

Cassiodorus, a century and more later, told his monks to read Cassian with care and hear him gladly on the eight faults. But on free will, he warned, Prosper had rightly blamed him.

Cassian had named his own hope when the first Conferences went out. "May we set the men themselves before you, embodied, as it were, in their own rules. And, what matters more, may they be speaking in Latin."

## tree/john-chrysostom

John Chrysostom

The emperor's chief chamberlain, Eutropius, had a law passed that stripped people who fled into churches of the right to shelter there. Soon after, in 399, he fell from favor and himself fled into the cathedral of Constantinople, the emperor's city. As he lay trembling under the altar, the bishop, John Chrysostom, climbed into the pulpit and preached against him. Some thought he was attacking a man already brought low. John said he spoke to soften the people's hearts, for the church had sheltered its enemy: "When everyone ignored him in his desolation, she alone, like a loving mother, hid him behind her curtains. She stood against the emperor's anger, the people's fury and unbearable hatred."

He preached plainly about the poor, and his blunt words to the powerful led to his exile. His sermons were read for centuries, and the followers who left the church over his fall came back only when his body came home.

John was born at Antioch, in Syria, and as a priest there for twelve years he preached on what the rich owed the poor. They decked the altar, where the bread of Communion lies, which Christians call Christ's body, with gold cups and silk. In one sermon he told them to honor Christ in the poor first: "Do you want to honor Christ's body? Do not neglect him when he is naked. While you honor him here with silk clothes, do not neglect him outside, dying of cold and nakedness." He published some of his sermons, and shorthand writers took down others as he spoke. Both kinds were kept and read.

In 398 the court chose him as bishop of Constantinople, and Eutropius made Theophilus, bishop of Alexandria, ordain him. As bishop he cut the bishop's own spending and gave the money to a hospital. He corrected his clergy sharply over how they lived and put some of them out of the church, and many came to hate him.

When a Gothic general asked for a church inside the city for the Arians, who taught that the Son of God had a beginning, John spoke against it before the emperor. Socrates, a church historian of the city writing a generation later, says Theophilus also held a grudge, since his own choice for bishop had lost out to John, and that he resolved to unseat him.

Theophilus and his bishops met at a place called the Oak, outside Chalcedon, and summoned John. His accusers charged that he had sold church property, insulted the clergy, struck a man and eaten greedily alone. John refused to come before judges he called his enemies, and asked for a council of the whole church. Socrates says they removed him from office only for refusing to come. A later bishop of Constantinople summed up the meeting's own written record, and they tell it differently: some charges were heard, and then all forty-five bishops voted to remove him. The emperor ordered him into exile, and John gave himself up, but he was soon called back.

Public games were held at a silver statue of the empress Eudoxia, just across the street from the cathedral. John mocked those who allowed them, calling the games an insult to the church, and the empress took this as aimed at her and sought another council. It condemned him for taking back his place as bishop, after his removal, without a council's leave. Since two councils had condemned him, the emperor barred him from the church, and John was silenced. His followers kept Easter in the public baths, and from then on they met apart from the church. In 404 he was taken into exile by force.

He was first exiled to Armenia. Palladius, who wrote John's life and took his side, says bishops in Syria, seeing the church of Antioch still looking to John, had him moved to Pityus, a more distant place on the Black Sea. In his telling, the soldiers hurried John on, saying they had been told his death would bring them promotion. Near Comana he asked for white clothes, took Communion and prayed the words he always used: "Glory to God for all things." He died there, in September 407.

In 438 the bishop of Constantinople had the emperor's leave to bring John's body home from Comana. He carried it through the city in procession and laid it in the Church of the Apostles, and those who had left the church over John's fall came back.
