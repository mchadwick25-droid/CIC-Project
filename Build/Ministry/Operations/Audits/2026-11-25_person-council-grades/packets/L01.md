# Packet L01: person and council stories (census longDescription)

## anatolius-of-alexandria-and-laodicea

Anatolius of Alexandria and Laodicea

In a besieged quarter of Alexandria, the bread had run out. A Christian, Anatolius of Alexandria and Laodicea, spoke to the city council. "I advise you to send out of the gates those who are surplus, of no use to us at all: old women, small children and old men. Let them walk wherever they wish. Why keep them here for nothing, when they will soon be dead?" Yet the people he called useless were among those he got out: almost all the starving, his own church first.

Later he was kept as bishop of Laodicea, in Syria, and he is the first Christian known to reckon Easter on a table that repeats every nineteen years. Almost four centuries later, in England, both sides of a quarrel over Easter claimed him.

Eusebius, the church historian, ranked him first among the most learned men of his time. Writing a generation later, he tells the siege as something people said.

It came in the 260s or early 270s, though the sources disagree on the year. The palace quarter of the city held out against a Roman army. When bread failed, Anatolius sent word to a deacon outside the lines, who won from the Roman general a promise to spare anyone who came over.

Anatolius first urged the council to make peace with Rome, which angered its members, then made the proposal heard at the start. He voted first that all not needed for the fighting should leave, and all agreed. Under cover of that decree, Eusebius says, the church's members went out first, then others, and with them a multitude more beyond the decree, secretly dressed in women's clothes. By night they reached the Roman camp, where the deacon nursed them back to health.

After the war he left Alexandria. At Caesarea in Palestine, its bishop made him a bishop beside himself, meaning him to take over when he died. Then he was called to Antioch, to a meeting of bishops judging its own bishop, Paul of Samosata, for his teaching about Christ. On the way he passed through Laodicea, a port on the coast of Syria. Its bishop, that same deacon, had died, and around 268 to 270 the believers kept Anatolius in his place.

Easter's date hangs on the Passover moon, the full moon on the fourteenth day of the first spring month. Anatolius wrote that this month must not begin before the spring equinox, the day in March when day and night are equal. To begin it earlier, he said, is no small mistake. He added that the Jews kept this rule even before Christ, and cited their writers for it. He set the rule on a table of nineteen years, after which the moon's phases fall on the same days again. The editors say the cycle was soon taken up at Alexandria, though changed first.

In 664 the Easter quarrel came to Hild's monastery at Whitby. In Bede's account, written two generations later, Colman, the bishop who led the Irish side, claimed Anatolius for keeping Easter from the fourteenth day of the moon to the twentieth. That rule is found in a Latin book under his name. Wilfrid, a priest who spoke for Rome's side, answered: "It is agreed that Anatolius was a most holy and most learned man, most worthy of praise. But what have you to do with him, when you do not even keep his rules? For he followed the rule of truth in his Easter, and set down a cycle of nineteen years. Either you do not know it, or you know it and treat it as nothing, though the whole church of Christ keeps it."

That book may not be his. An American scholar who translated Eusebius takes it as an early translation of his work; a German historian, as a forgery made in the sixth century or earlier. The king ruled for Rome.

Of the man who sent the starving out of Alexandria by night, no early writer tells how he died. Before the Great Persecution, another man led his church.

## bardaisan-of-edessa

Bardaisan of Edessa

Some pupils were visiting a friend when their teacher, Bardaisan, came in and asked, as he always did, what they had been talking about. One of them spoke for a newcomer among his pupils, Avida, who was too shy to ask. If God made us and wants us to do right, why did he not make us unable to do wrong?

Bardaisan was a Christian teacher in Edessa, a city in what is now south-eastern Turkey, around the year 200, and his answer was that people are free. Nature, he granted, rules the body, and the stars have power over fortune, but not over what we do. A pupil set down the conversation, the Book of the Laws of Countries. In it his proof is that each nation keeps its own laws under the same stars, and that Christians keep one law in every land.

That pupil, most editors judge, was Philip, one of those in the room, and his record is the only one we have in Bardaisan's voice. Born about 155 beside the river Daisan, which gave him his name, he taught in Syriac, a form of Aramaic and the language of Edessa. Eusebius of Caesarea, a century later, says he wrote other such conversations against the followers of Marcion. Marcion taught that the God of the law of Moses, who made the world, was not the Father of Jesus. His many pupils, Eusebius adds, put his works into Greek.

Had God made people unable to do wrong, Bardaisan told Avida, they would be like a harp that another plays, or a ship that another steers. The sun never says that it will not rise, but people can, and so the good they do is their own.

He admitted that he had once followed the astrologers, and he still gave fate its share. Fate, the course God gave the powers of the heavens, hands out wealth, sickness and children unevenly from the hour of birth. "So we humans are ruled by nature all alike, by fate each in a different way, and by our freedom each as we choose."

He gives his examples. Under the same stars the Brahmans of India eat no meat while Persians marry their sisters, and some peoples stone and kill where others refuse to. At Edessa, he says, when King Abgar became a believer, he ordered that any man who castrated himself in a pagan rite should have his hand cut off. Then he turns to the Christians, found in every land: "Wherever we are, we are all called by the one name of Christ: Christians."

He died about 222, in his late sixties. His argument outlived him: a later Christian book reworked its chapters, most editors judge, and a Latin scholar, about 170 years on, called it the strongest of his books. Yet his name passed into the lists of those the church condemned. Eusebius praised him as a powerful defender of the faith, yet says he began among the followers of Valentinus and never wholly washed off the stain of that old heresy. Valentinus was a teacher at Rome whose school taught a heaven of paired divine powers. A bishop writing about 150 years after him, Epiphanius, says the reverse. To him Bardaisan was a fine churchman who later joined Valentinus's followers and denied that the body will rise again, like a fine ship wrecked at the harbor's edge.

In Edessa itself, at about the same time, Ephrem the Syrian attacked his teaching on the soul, the body and the first beings he said the world was made from. He cursed anyone who said, as Bardaisan did, that rain and dew come from the Seven, powers Ephrem says Bardaisan confessed. He also answered his followers' songs with hymns of his own, in the same meters.

The dialogue never records Avida's last word. It ends on the world to come: "When that new world is set up, all evil turmoil will cease, and every rebellion will end. The foolish will be convinced, and all that is lacking will be filled."

## clement-of-alexandria

Clement of Alexandria

Many Christians, Clement wrote, were frightened of Greek philosophy, the way children are frightened of masks. Some wanted "bare faith alone," which to him was like wanting grapes without tending the vine. This is his account; their own words have not come down to us.

Clement was a presbyter, an elder of the church, and he taught in Alexandria, in Egypt. Around the year 200, he answered those Christians in his longest book, which he called Miscellanies. Perhaps, he suggested, God gave philosophy to the Greeks as he gave the Law to the Hebrews. In his words, "it was a guide to bring the Greek mind to Christ, as the Law brought the Hebrews." The word Clement borrowed from Paul meant the household servant who walked a child to his teacher.

Eusebius tells us that Origen was among his pupils, and Jerome says so too. By Jerome's account, Origen followed Clement's example and wrote his own Miscellanies, setting the views of Christians and of philosophers side by side.

Clement took no one school whole. He kept "whatever has been well said" by each school, but would never call divine what men had falsified. Two centuries on, a critic charged Jerome with defiling the church by quoting pagan books. Jerome pointed to Clement, "in my judgment the most learned of men," and named his books. Then he asked, "Is learning missing from these books? Or are they not rather drawn from the very heart of philosophy?"

In his own time, Clement wrote, martyrs, people who died rather than deny Christ, were burned, impaled and beheaded in plain sight every day. Yet he taught that no one should hand himself over to the judge.

The last we hear of Clement alive comes from a bishop named Alexander. Held in prison, Alexander sent a letter to the church at Antioch by Clement's hand, praising him as a man who had built up the church. In a later letter, Alexander counted Clement among the blessed men who had gone on ahead of him. By then, Clement had died.

Most of what we know of Clement's life comes from Eusebius, a church historian writing about a hundred years later. In Clement's own words, he went from teacher to teacher, in Greece, in southern Italy and in the East. Eusebius thought the last teacher Clement found was Pantaenus. Here Clement gives him no name: "I tracked him down where he was hidden in Egypt, and I found rest."

## councils-of-rome-and-carthage-251

Councils of Carthage and Rome on the lapsed

In 250 the emperor Decius ordered everyone to sacrifice to the gods before the officials. At Carthage, their bishop Cyprian wrote later, many Christians did not wait for arrest but ran to the forum, the main square where the officials sat. As evening came and officials put some off, they begged not to be.

When the persecution ended, many of the lapsed, those who had given way, asked to come back. In 251, councils at Carthage, in what is now Tunisia, and then at Rome chose a middle way. The fallen could return after long penance, a time of public sorrow and prayer, with each case examined. A stricter party refused them all and set up Novatian as a rival bishop of Rome, whose church lasted for centuries.

Some had sacrificed, while others had bought a certificate, a paper from an official saying they had. Their hands were clean, Cyprian held, their consciences stained.

Honored voices pressed to take them back at once. Confessors, jailed or tortured for the faith without giving way, were handing out written pardons granting peace, the church's word for sharing the Lord's Supper again. Cyprian says thousands went out a day, none examined. A deacon, Felicissimus, and five elders offered peace without penance: "another persecution", Cyprian called it.

Cyprian, leading from hiding by letter, would not decide alone. The bishops must first meet and fix a sentence "tempered with discipline and mercy". Meanwhile the dying who repented would receive peace, and Rome's clergy agreed.

The African bishops met at Carthage, while Rome already had two bishops, Cornelius and Novatian. Cyprian, who called the council and defended it, is our only witness. "We weighed out a balance, with healing moderation," he wrote. "Hope of communion and peace was not to be wholly denied to the lapsed, so that despair would not make them fall away further." Yet none would rush straight back, and penance would be long. "And the cases, wishes and needs of each person would be examined."

Certificate holders were taken back case by case, and the sacrificed received peace at death, but those who first asked on their sickbed, never having repented, were refused. Felicissimus and the five elders were cut off from the church's fellowship. The council's own book is lost.

Socrates, writing two centuries later and kind to Novatian's church, reports his rule: "They should not admit those who had sacrificed to the sacred rites. They should urge them to repent, but leave their pardon to God, who is able, and has the power, to forgive sins." The sacred rites meant the Lord's Supper. Socrates says Novatian broke away because Cornelius took back those who had sacrificed, but Cyprian calls that a false rumor.

Later that year sixty bishops under Cornelius at Rome, by Eusebius's account of his letters, agreed with Carthage. Novatian's followers were "strangers", outsiders, and the fallen would be healed by penance, "with the medicines of repentance".

Novatian's church kept its rule: in Socrates's day, Phrygia and Paphlagonia held it "to the present day". At Nicaea in 325, its clergy who came over had to share communion with the lapsed who did penance.

In 252, by a historian's reckoning, with a new persecution expected, the African bishops gave peace to all who had kept repenting, so they could stand firm. "In this time we give peace not to those who sleep, but to those who keep watch. We give peace not for luxury, but for arms. We give peace not for rest, but for the battle line."

## cyprian-of-carthage

Cyprian of Carthage

In Carthage, in today's Tunisia, people crowded the doors of a house, waiting for Cyprian. Newly baptized, he had tried to step aside for older men, but they wanted him as bishop, says his deacon Pontius, who wrote his life as an admirer.

Cyprian had taught public speaking, and became bishop about two years after his baptism, by modern reckoning. For ten years he led the church of Roman Africa through persecution, plague and division; some eighty letters show how. He taught that the church is one mother, and died by the sword, the city's first martyr bishop, Pontius says.

In 250 the emperor Decius ordered everyone to sacrifice to the gods. Crowds were shouting for him, he wrote, and to keep the peace he withdrew from the city and hid, leading his church by letter. Clergy at Rome wrote to Carthage of the good shepherd who lays down his life and the hired man who runs. Cyprian replied that his acts had been reported "less than plainly and faithfully", and Pontius later had to argue that fear was not the cause.

When Novatian was set up at Rome as a rival bishop, Cyprian would not receive his envoys as church members. He wrote: "Whoever does not have the Church as mother cannot have God as Father." The church, he meant, bears and feeds Christians like a mother.

When plague struck, about 252, people left their own sick and Cyprian urged care for all, Pontius says. He wrote that it tested everyone: "Do the healthy serve the sick? Do relatives love their kin as they should? Do masters pity their sick slaves? Do doctors stand by the sick who beg for help?"

Cyprian and the African councils held that washing in groups cut off from the church was no baptism, and baptized those who came over. Stephen, bishop of Rome, as Cyprian quotes him, said to keep to what was handed down: hands laid on them as a sign of repentance. Cyprian called his letter proud and his view an error. Eighty-seven bishops met in council, probably in 256. Cyprian opened it: "For none of us sets himself up as a bishop of bishops, or drives his fellow bishops by a tyrant's terror to obey." No bishop, he meant, may force another; each answers to Christ. A bishop in Cappadocia, on Cyprian's side, reported that Stephen refused the African envoys a roof and called Cyprian a false Christ. Stephen died in 257, with no settlement recorded.

That year, says a Christian account built on the court record, he told the Roman governor he was "a Christian and a bishop", and refused the Roman rites. He was sent into exile, and in 258 a harsher law ordered bishops killed. When officers were sent to take him to Utica, he left his gardens on friends' advice, he wrote, to declare his faith before his own people. Pontius says he refused friends' offers of refuge. The account says he stayed at his gardens.

On 14 September 258 he again refused the rites: in so just a cause, he said, there was nothing to think over. The sentence named him an enemy of the Roman gods and leader of a wicked conspiracy, whose blood would be an example to his followers. Sentenced to the sword, he answered, "Thanks be to God," and the crowd cried that they would be beheaded with him. The account says two clergymen tied the cloth over his eyes; Pontius says he bound them himself.

Later, the Donatists, a breakaway African church, quoted his letters and council to defend baptizing again. Augustine of Hippo, about 140 years after Cyprian's death, answered that Cyprian, though wrong on baptism, never left the church's unity and cut off no one who differed. He quoted the "bishop of bishops" line again and again, asking, "What could be more kind? What more humble?" So both sides claimed him.

The night he died, the account says, his body was carried with candles and torches, in prayer and great triumph, to a burial ground outside the city.

## dionysius-of-alexandria

Dionysius of Alexandria

Soldiers had seized Dionysius, the bishop of Alexandria, and were holding him in a small town near the city. A countryman on his way to a wedding feast told the guests, who ran shouting to the house to carry him off, and the soldiers fled. "Then I understood why they had come. I cried out, begging and pleading with them to go away and leave us alone. If they wanted to do a kindness, I asked them to act before the men leading me away, and cut off my head themselves."

A pupil of Origen, Dionysius ran the church's school at Alexandria, where people were taught the faith. From about 247 he led its church as bishop, through two persecutions and a plague. His letters, which Eusebius of Caesarea copied into his history, are our eyewitness record of those years.

About 250, the emperor Decius set out to make Christians give up their faith and return to the old gods. Dionysius stayed at home four days while an officer hunted him. Then God told him to go, he says, and he left, but was caught at sunset, and after the guests carried him off, he hid in the Libyan desert. He told all this to answer a fellow bishop who accused him of running away, so the story rests on his own word.

In one book, Dionysius turned to the Revelation, the last book of the Bible. He would not reject it, but he set its rough Greek beside the polished Greek of the Gospel and letter of John. "That he was called John, and that this book is the writing of a John, I will not dispute. For I agree it is the work of someone holy and inspired by God. But I would not easily grant that this was the apostle, the son of Zebedee, the brother of James." That is John, one of the twelve, and he guessed it was another John.

In the churches of Libya, some bishops taught that the Father and the Son were one and the same. To show the Son was not the Father, Dionysius used blunt images. As his opponents later cited them, the Son was as different from the Father as a vine from its farmer, or a boat from its builder. Some of his own church, Athanasius of Alexandria says, went over his head to the bishop of Rome. The bishop wrote against both errors and told Dionysius what was said against him. Dionysius answered in four books that there was never a time when God was not Father, and that the Son comes from him like light from an eternal light.

When plague came, about 263, many Christians, he wrote, nursed the sick without fear and caught it themselves, and he counted their deaths as dying for the faith. "So even this kind of death, which came from great devotion and strong faith, seemed to fall nothing short of martyrdom." The pagans, he wrote, pushed their sick into the streets half dead. He died, old and still bishop, about 264 or 265.

Nearly a century later came the followers of Arius, who taught that there was once a time when the Son did not exist. They quoted the vine and the boat against Nicaea's creed. Dionysius had written two generations before Arius, against the opposite error, yet his words were now read as support for theirs. Athanasius, bishop of the same city, wrote a book to answer them. Dionysius did write those words, he granted, but his four books showed his real faith, and to claim him was a slander. Basil of Caesarea compared him to a woodman who pulls a crooked sapling so hard the other way that he bends it wrong on the other side. Whether the Arians had any ground, later readers disagree.

For Athanasius, the proof was how Dionysius ended: no bishops ever condemned him or drove him out, and he never left the church. He died, Athanasius wrote, with honor inside it, and his memory was kept among the fathers.

## eusebius-of-caesarea

Eusebius of Caesarea

"I confess that it is beyond my power to write a perfect and complete history. Since I am the first to enter on this subject, I am trying to travel, as it were, a lonely and untrodden path." So wrote Eusebius, bishop of Caesarea in Roman Palestine, opening his Church History.

He set out to tell the whole church's story from its own letters, lists and books, copying their words into his pages. Many early Christian writings, his English editor wrote, survive only in his quotations.

Born around 260, Eusebius studied under Pamphilus, a friend whose name he took.

In 303 the emperors began a persecution, ordering the churches torn down. Eusebius, whose own pages tell most of his life, saw it: "With our own eyes we saw the houses of prayer thrown down from top to ground, to their very foundations. We saw the inspired and sacred Scriptures handed over to the fire in the middle of the market squares. We saw the shepherds of the churches hiding shamefully here and there, and some of them caught in disgrace and mocked by their enemies."

By houses of prayer he meant churches, and by shepherds the clergy. Pamphilus was imprisoned and died a martyr for the faith.

After the persecution Eusebius became bishop of Caesarea and, his editor reckons, wrote most of his History then. He traced each great church's bishops back to the apostles, drawing letters from Jerusalem's church library. He set a rule: those who gave way in the persecution he would leave out, keeping only what would profit his own time and those after.

He had seen the young Constantine, and later knew him as emperor.

At the Council of Nicaea in 325, by his own account, his church's statement of faith was read. The bishops added words, among them "of one being" with the Father. He signed, and wrote home that all agreed "not without inquiry", for the sake of peace. Socrates, a historian a century later, says he held back briefly first. Athanasius of Alexandria, an opponent who was there, says he refused the day before.

Ten years later the emperor called bishops to Tyre to judge Athanasius. Eusebius sat among the judges, says Epiphanius, writing some forty years later on Athanasius's side. In his account Potamo, an Egyptian bishop who had lost an eye in the persecution, cried out: "You sit there, Eusebius, and Athanasius, an innocent man, is judged by you?" And then: "Tell me, were you not with me in prison in the persecution? I lost an eye for the truth. But you seem to have no part of your body maimed. You did not suffer as a martyr; you stand here alive, with nothing cut away. How did you get out of prison, unless you promised our persecutors to do something unlawful, or did it?"

Eusebius rose in anger and broke up the court, Epiphanius says, and the council deposed Athanasius. About three years later the bishops of Egypt wrote that he had been "accused" of offering sacrifice to the Roman gods. Rufinus, writing later in Latin, tells a like scene at Tyre that does not name Eusebius. These were charges; no source shows he sacrificed.

After Constantine died in 337, Eusebius wrote a Life of the emperor, telling, by his own rule, only what showed his faith. Its account of Tyre names neither Athanasius nor Potamo. Socrates judged it more praise than accurate history.

The letter of Polycrates of Ephesus on Easter survives only because Eusebius copied it. He is the first known to quote Josephus on Jesus, and he retold, from a Latin writer, Pliny the Younger's letter about the Christians. Socrates began his history where Eusebius stopped, and Rufinus put the Church History into Latin.

Athanasius said Eusebius had written that Christ was not true God, while Socrates defended him. Historians have counted him both with Arius, a priest who taught that the Son had a beginning, and with Nicaea.

Eusebius died about 339. His pupil and successor as bishop wrote his life. That book is lost.

## hermas

Hermas

Hermas, a Christian in Rome, begins his book by saying that the one who raised him sold him to a woman named Rhoda. Years later he saw her bathing in the Tiber, drew her out, and thought how happy he would be with such a wife. Then, he writes, she appeared to him from the heavens to accuse him of that one desire.

He wrote in the second century, and all he tells of himself comes from his visions. In them the Church, the whole Christian people seen as a woman, and then an angel dressed as a shepherd, called Rome's Christians to repent while there was still time. To repent was to turn from sin and live rightly again. A Christian who sinned after baptism, the washing that made a person a Christian, would have one more chance, and only one. His book, the Shepherd, was read for centuries, quoted as Scripture by some and shut out by others.

He writes that the Church, as an old woman, gave him a book that said a day had been fixed. Those who repented with all their heart before it would be forgiven, but after it a believer who sinned would not be saved. Later he saw her as a tower being built, where stones set aside could still be used if their owners repented before it was finished. Rich believers were round stones that had to be cut square, and she turned this on Hermas himself: "When you were rich, you were of no use. But now you are useful, and fit for life."

Then, he says, an angel sent to teach repentance came to him dressed as a shepherd, and gave the book its name. Hermas asked him: "Sir, I have heard some teachers say this. There is no other repentance than the one when we went down into the water, and our past sins were forgiven." The angel agreed that the baptized ought to sin no more. But the Lord, knowing human weakness, had granted one repentance to those already called, and someone who kept sinning and repenting gained nothing: "with difficulty will he live."

Some churches read it aloud. Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon, quoted it with the words "the Scripture declared", and a teacher in Alexandria carried on its second repentance. An old list of the church's books found it too recent to stand with the Scriptures: "So it ought indeed to be read. But it cannot be read out to the people in church."

Then its question became a fight. When a bishop forgave Christians who had committed adultery and shown their repentance, Tertullian, in Carthage, scorned the book as "the Shepherd of adulterers". By the late fourth century it was kept for people preparing to become Christians, and the Latin West had almost forgotten it.

Near the end of his book, Hermas says he makes God's great works known to everyone. He hopes that all who have sinned before may hear these words, repent, and receive life again.

## hippolytus-of-rome

Hippolytus of Rome

Hippolytus, one of the elders who led the church at Rome with its bishop, set down what, he said, his own bishop, Callistus, taught about Noah's ark. "He also said that Noah's ark was made as a sign of the church. In it were dogs and wolves and ravens, everything clean and unclean. So, he says, it should be with the church." Hippolytus wrote it to show how far, in his eyes, that bishop had fallen.

In the early two hundreds Hippolytus was a Greek-writing teacher at Rome. He wrote a book against the teachings he held false, the Refutation of All Heresies. In it he charged Callistus with forgiving sinners too easily and with holding that Father and Son were one and the same. In 235 he was exiled to Sardinia with Pontianus, the bishop of Rome. Whether he led a rival church against Rome's bishops is still disputed.

A teacher from Smyrna, Hippolytus says, had taught that Christ was the Father himself, so that the Father was born and suffered. When the teaching reached Rome, Hippolytus answered from Christ's words. "Christ did not say, 'I and the Father am one,' but 'are one.' The word 'are' is not said of one person. It speaks of two persons and one power."

Nearly all we know of Callistus comes from this enemy. He says that Callistus, as adviser to the bishop of the day, let the teaching spread and called Hippolytus's side "worshippers of two gods." He paints him as a slave who made away with Christians' savings and was later sentenced to forced labor in the mines of Sardinia, a sentence for convicts. Historians judge the story true at its base but greatly overdrawn.

Once Callistus became bishop in 217, he forgave every sin, Hippolytus says, to anyone who came over to him and would not remove a bishop even for grave sin. He called Callistus's following a school, not a church, and accused him of teaching adultery and murder. For himself, he claimed to be a successor of the apostles, sharing their "high-priesthood." Many historians think his followers made him a rival bishop at Rome; others make him bishop of Portus, Rome's harbor town, and some doubt there was any split. After Callistus died, his Refutation traced each teaching Hippolytus held false back to Greek philosophy, and ended with Callistus himself.

Then, in 235, a list of Rome's bishops records that Pontianus and "the elder Hippolytus," as it calls him, were sent to Sardinia, "the unhealthy island." He had told of Callistus's sentence there, and now he went himself, with Rome's bishop. How he died is not recorded, though historians think he probably died on the island. Rome's calendar of martyrs kept both men on the same day, 13 August.

His Greek books soon dropped out of sight at Rome, where the clergy now wrote Latin. The Refutation turned up in a manuscript from Mount Athos in 1842 and was first printed under the name of Origen. Most scholars have since given it to Hippolytus, though some doubted it.

About 140 years after the exile, Damasus, bishop of Rome, set a verse on his tomb. "It is said that Hippolytus, an elder, while the tyrant's orders pressed hard, had stayed all along in the split of Novatus." The tyrant is the persecuting emperor. By Novatus, Damasus means Novatian, whose party split Rome over a like question in 251, refusing to take back those who had denied the faith. On his way to death, the verse says, Hippolytus told the people to follow the catholic faith, the faith of the one true church. Historians judge that Damasus confused two different splits. "Damasus tells what he has heard. Christ tests it all."

## ignatius-of-antioch

Ignatius of Antioch

"From Syria all the way to Rome I fight with beasts, by land and by sea, by night and by day." Ignatius, a prisoner, wrote that early in the second century. The beasts on his road, he explains, were his guards, a squad of ten soldiers who grew worse the better they were treated. The real beasts waited at Rome.

He was bishop of the church at Antioch in Syria, sentenced to be killed by wild animals in the arena. On the road he wrote seven letters, most of them to churches that came out to meet him, and almost all we know of him comes from them. Polycarp, the bishop of Smyrna, sent on the ones he held, and later writers quoted them.

Of his life before his arrest nothing is known.

At Smyrna, on the west coast of today's Turkey, he met Polycarp face to face, and bishops came to him from three other cities. He wrote back to their churches that each should act as one around its bishop, its deacons and the elders who led it with him. They should come together at one altar, breaking one bread.

He also warned them against teachers who said that Christ only seemed to have a body and to suffer. Christ, he wrote, was truly born, truly crucified under Pontius Pilate, and truly raised. "Some who are without God, that is, unbelievers, say that he only seemed to suffer. They themselves only seem to exist. But if they are right, why am I in chains? Why do I long to face the wild beasts? Do I die for nothing, then?"

From Smyrna he also wrote ahead to the church at Rome. He begged them not to work to spare him but to let him be food for the beasts, which he would coax, and force if they held back. "I am God's wheat, and let me be ground by the teeth of wild beasts, so that I may be found the pure bread of Christ." As grain is ground into bread, his torn body would become an offering to God.

At Troas, a port on the same coast, he called the teachers he opposed beasts in human shape, who cared nothing for the widow, the orphan or the hungry. To another church he wrote that he had cried aloud among them: do nothing without the bishop. Some thought he had heard of their quarrels, but the words, he said, came from God's Spirit, not from any man.

No early witness saw his death. Polycarp, writing soon after to the church at Philippi, which Paul had once written to, held up "the blessed Ignatius" as a model of patience. He also asked for any news of him. He sent on the letters he held, as Philippi had asked. Within a lifetime Irenaeus quoted the wheat line, without naming him, and two centuries later Eusebius wrote that "report says" he was given to the beasts at Rome. Later bishops used his words on Christ's real flesh in their own quarrels.

Because the letters show each church led by one bishop, some scholars thought them too developed for so early a date, and they argued for centuries over which were his. Fifteen letters had come down under his name. By 1890 most scholars accepted seven as his, though some still did not.

The last we hear in his own words, he has to sail suddenly from Troas. The prisoner who fought beasts all the way from Syria sails on toward the real ones at Rome.

## irenaeus-of-lyon

Irenaeus of Lyon

"It is as if a skilled artist had carefully made a fine portrait of a king from choice small stones. Then someone broke up the man's likeness. He moved those stones, fitted them together again, and made the shape of a dog or a fox, and a poor one at that."

The portrait is a mosaic, a picture made of small colored stones, and he insists the fox is still the king. That, wrote Irenaeus, bishop of Lyon in Roman Gaul, today's France, is what the followers of Valentinus did with Scripture. Valentinus had taught at Rome a generation earlier, and his followers claimed, Irenaeus says, that their secret teaching went deeper than the apostles'.

As a boy Irenaeus had heard the old bishop Polycarp of Smyrna, and he became bishop himself after a persecution in 177. His five books against these teachers, written about 180, are the fullest answer we still have. Against their secret teaching he set three open things: the faith received at baptism, the churches the apostles founded, and four Gospels.

In the year 177 a persecution struck the Christians of Lyon and Vienne, on the river Rhône. Their bishop, Pothinus, more than ninety years old, the survivors wrote, was beaten and died in prison. From prison they asked Irenaeus, one of the elders who led the church with its bishop, to carry their letters to Rome. Others were put to death, and their burned remains were swept into the Rhône, so that, the persecutors said, they could not hope to rise again.

Irenaeus became bishop after Pothinus, Eusebius says, and most of what we know of his life comes from that historian, writing more than a century later. The new bishop read his opponents' books. They taught, he says, that the world's maker was a lesser power below a perfect, hidden God. He called their system madness and blasphemy against Christ, and likened them to wolves in sheep's clothing.

His first answer was the faith received from the apostles, in one God the maker, in Christ Jesus who became flesh, suffered and rose, and in the Holy Spirit. A Christian received this short summary at baptism, he says, as a rule to measure by. "The church has received this preaching and this faith, as I said. Though it is scattered through the whole world, it guards them with care, as if it lived in one house. It believes them alike, as if it had one soul and one heart. It preaches them, teaches them and hands them on in harmony, as if it had one mouth." From Germany and Spain to Egypt and Libya, he says, the churches hand on no other.

His second answer anyone could check. If the apostles had secret teachings, he argued, they would have given them to the men they left in charge of the churches. Those bishops could be counted, one after another, to his own day. He counted Rome's, down to the bishop of his own time, in "the twelfth place from the apostles". Historians still argue whether Rome then had a single bishop.

His third answer was four Gospels, by Matthew, Mark, Luke and John, for as there are four winds, he says, the church has four pillars. The followers of Valentinus claimed more, and Marcion fewer.

Florinus, a man Irenaeus had seen as a boy at Polycarp's side, had become an elder at Rome. He seemed, Eusebius says, to teach that God was the author of evil, and he was being drawn toward Valentinus. Irenaeus wrote to him that the elders before them, who had known the apostles, never handed on such teachings. "I remember that time more clearly than recent years. For what boys learn grows with the mind and becomes one with it. So I can describe the very place where the blessed Polycarp sat as he taught." He could describe, he wrote, how Polycarp came and went, how he lived and looked, and his talks to the people. "And I can tell how he spoke of his time with John and the others who had seen the Lord."

Later writers drew on his book for generations: Hippolytus called him "the blessed elder", and Tertullian "a very exact inquirer". About two centuries on, Epiphanius copied his opening account of the Valentinians word for word, and that copy is how much of its Greek survives. For eighteen centuries the Valentinians were known almost only through opponents such as him, until books of their own were found in Egypt in 1945.

A Christian who holds the rule received at baptism, Irenaeus wrote, will know the stones of the broken mosaic, but will not take the fox for the king. He puts each one back in its place, and the false picture falls apart.

## josephus

Josephus

In 67 the Romans took Jotapata, a walled town in Galilee in the north, and found its commander, Josephus, hiding in a cave with forty men. The Roman general Vespasian, later emperor, promised him his life. Josephus prayed in secret to God: "I declare it openly: I do not go over to the Romans as a deserter from the Jews, but as a servant sent by you."

He was a priest of the Temple in Jerusalem by birth, who had led the Jewish forces in Galilee when the Jews rose against Rome. He spent the rest of his life at Rome, under the emperors' favor, writing his people's history, which holds a few lines each on John the Baptist, Jesus and James. Christians quoted those lines for centuries as the word of a Jewish historian outside the church, and argued over the passage on Jesus.

Back in the cave at Jotapata, his companions would rather die than surrender, and when they turned their swords on him, he proposed that they kill each other by lot. One by one they bared their necks. He and one other man were left, by chance, he writes, or by God's guiding hand, and he persuaded that man to live. His own books, written partly to answer the charge of treachery, are the only account.

Brought before Vespasian, he foretold that the general would be emperor, as the Roman writer Suetonius also records, and in 69 the new emperor freed him. At the siege of Jerusalem in 70, he called on the defenders to surrender. The Jews wanted him punished, he writes, while the Romans, whenever they lost, blamed his "treachery".

At Rome, about 93, he finished his history of the Jewish people. In it, the ruler Herod kills John the Baptist, "a good man", fearing his hold on the crowds. And of Jesus: "About this time there lived Jesus, a wise man, if it is right to call him a man. For he did astonishing deeds, and taught people who gladly accept the truth." The passage goes on, "He was the Christ", the Messiah the Jews awaited, crucified under the Roman governor Pilate and seen alive on the third day. Elsewhere the chief priest of the Temple has James, "the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ", stoned. Each passage is only a few sentences.

A century and a half later, the scholar Origen cited Josephus on John and James: "And this is the remarkable thing. He did not accept that our Jesus was the Christ. Yet he still bore witness that James was so upright." Origen never cites the passage on Jesus.

Early in the fourth century, Eusebius of Caesarea was the first writer known to quote the passage on Jesus. He used it against forged records of Jesus's trial, because here was a historian who was himself a Hebrew. Eusebius also told the fall of Jerusalem, from Josephus, as God's judgment on the Jews for what they did to Christ. Jerome's Latin, later in the fourth century, reads that Jesus was believed to be the Christ.

By 1890 an editor of Eusebius wrote that the passage had been disputed since the sixteenth century. Some, he wrote, thought a Christian copyist had added it all, others only part, and a few still held it wholly his own. The lines on John and James, he wrote, were widely accepted as his own.

Josephus ended the story of his own life on the emperors' favor and the charges he had outlived, and left the verdict to his readers: "let others judge of my character."

## justin-martyr

Justin Martyr

In the middle of the second century, Justin wrote to the emperor on behalf of people of every race who were unjustly hated, himself among them.

A philosopher from a Roman town in Samaria, he taught anyone who came to him in Rome. He was a Christian when the name itself was the charge. A name, he argued, is neither good nor bad, so judge Christians by what they do. The Greek logos means both "word" and "reason", and Justin called Christ that Word.

"We have been taught that Christ is the firstborn of God. We have shown already that he is the Word, in whom every race of people has had a share. And those who lived with the Word are Christians, even if they were counted godless."

He counted Socrates among them, and he set out openly what Christians did on Sunday. About the year 165, he was beheaded at Rome for this faith.

On Sunday, he wrote, Christians heard the memoirs of the apostles or the prophets, prayed, shared bread and wine, and gave for orphans, widows, prisoners and strangers.

His road to that faith ran through the philosophers' schools. Then, walking alone by the sea, he met an old man who told him that the prophets, older than any philosopher, had seen and spoken the truth. At once, he says, a fire was lit in his soul. He had found "the only philosophy that is sure and useful", and so he still called himself a philosopher.

He also wrote up his argument with a Jew, Trypho, that the prophets had spoken of Jesus, and told him that the sufferings of his people came to them justly. Yet the two parted as friends.

The account of his trial, by an author we do not know, has him stand with six companions before Rusticus, the prefect of Rome. Rusticus asked, "So you suppose that you will go up into the heavens, to receive some good rewards?" Justin answered, "I do not suppose it. I know it for certain, and I am fully sure of it." All who refused to sacrifice were scourged and beheaded.

Within a generation, Irenaeus was quoting his book against Marcion, defending the one God who made the world. Irenaeus and Hippolytus say that one of his hearers was Tatian, a man from Assyria. In his own book, Tatian praised him and carried on his attack on the gods and the philosophers. Irenaeus, hostile to Tatian, says that after Justin's death he left the Church. But Eusebius says Tatian wove the four Gospels into one book, and a later Syriac account of the church at Edessa says the people came to hear it read.

The church kept the name Tertullian gave him: Justin, philosopher and martyr.

## lactantius

Lactantius

At first light, as Lactantius's own account tells it, officials forced the doors of the church at Nicomedia, burned its Scriptures and looted the rest. The emperor Diocletian, watching from his palace, feared fire would spread, so guards with axes leveled the church within hours. Lactantius, a teacher of Latin speech from Africa, was teaching in Bithynia, the province around Nicomedia.

It was 303, and the Great Persecution had begun, the attack on Christians that Diocletian and his junior emperor Galerius started there. Lactantius answered with the Divine Institutes, a handbook of the true religion. It argued the faith to educated pagans from their own poets and philosophers, and held that religion cannot be forced. A second book told how the persecutors died. Late in life, Jerome says, he taught Constantine's son Crispus in Gaul.

Jerome, our witness for his life, writing some seventy years later, says he was summoned in Diocletian's reign to teach rhetoric, the art of public speaking, in Latin. But Nicomedia spoke Greek, pupils were few, and he turned to writing. When he became a Christian is not known.

The day after the church fell, an edict, an emperor's public order, stripped Christians of rank and laid them open to torture. Fire broke out in the palace. Lactantius blames agents of Galerius. Eusebius of Caesarea says he does not know how it began, and Constantine, who was in the city, later said lightning. Priests and servants were killed, some burned in crowds, some drowned with millstones around their necks.

While the church lay in ruins, two men read out books against the faith before Lactantius. One was a philosopher who preached poverty but dined like a lord, the other a judge who had urged the persecution. He wrote: "Spurred on by their proud ungodliness, by my awareness of the truth itself, and, as I believe, by God, I took up this task."

Of the Latin defenders before him, Tertullian, he judged, had been obscure, and Cyprian had answered a pagan with Scripture the pagan thought false. A newcomer, he says, needs milk before solid food and must be refuted from his own authorities.

His seven books argue that the gods were once men, the philosophers contradict each other, and wisdom and religion belong together. True worship is a pure mind, not blood or incense, and the world will run as it is for six thousand years. He argues from Cicero, Rome's great orator, and from the Sibyls, pagan prophetesses whose verses the Romans revered. Amid the persecution he wrote, "Religion must be defended not by killing but by dying, not by cruelty but by patient endurance, not by crime but by faithfulness."

As Lactantius tells it, Galerius, dying of a rotting disease, ordered in 311 that Christians might be Christians again. Two years later the edict of Milan returned their churches. Lactantius copied the text of both into his book.

That book, On the Deaths of the Persecutors, was written around 314 for a friend tortured nine times, so later ages might learn how God dealt with persecutors. It ends with the victor killing their families, a boy of eight and a girl of seven among them. Thus, Lactantius says, the wicked received what their deeds had earned. Most scholars hold it his; some have doubted.

How and when Lactantius died is not known.

Jerome praised an eloquence like Cicero's, but wished Lactantius had been as ready to teach Christian doctrine as to pull down pagan belief. He charged that Lactantius denied the Holy Spirit was a person, yet asked who could forbid reading the Institutes. Augustine of Hippo took the Sibyl's verses Lactantius had quoted and set them out together in the City of God. His book on the persecutors keeps the edict of Milan's Latin, a modern editor notes. A modern historian calls it the most exact account of those years, and a partisan one.

Jerome wrote of the man, "He was the most eloquent man of his time, but so poor in this life that he often lacked even the necessities."

## marcion-of-pontus

Marcion of Pontus

"There is a certain Marcion, from Pontus. Even now he is still teaching the people he has won over. He teaches them to believe in some other god, greater than the Creator." Justin, a Christian opponent, wrote this to the emperor while Marcion lived, in the middle of the second century.

At Rome, Marcion taught that the God of the law of Moses and the prophets, who made the world, was not the Father of Jesus. He kept a Scripture of his own, one Gospel and Paul's letters, and he founded churches of his own, which spread far. Against him, writer after writer argued that the God who made the world is the Father of Jesus. Everything we know of him comes from the men who opposed him.

"Separating the law from the gospel is Marcion's own work, and his chief one," Tertullian writes of his book, the Antitheses, now lost. His opponents say he cut his Scripture down from Luke's Gospel and Paul's letters. He claimed, Tertullian reports, that "the defenders of Judaism" had added to the Gospel and he was restoring it.

Tertullian calls him a shipmaster from Pontus, on the Black Sea. At first, he says, Marcion believed as the church at Rome did, and gave it money, two hundred sesterces, in the first warmth of his faith. Then Rome expelled him more than once, Tertullian says, for a restless curiosity that spread to others. He says this of Valentinus too, who taught at Rome with him. Marcion's gift, Tertullian says, was cast out with him.

Still his churches spread. Justin says that, with the demons' help, he had led many "in every race of people". Tertullian says with scorn that the Marcionites make churches as wasps make combs. Two centuries later, Epiphanius still found them from Rome and Italy through Egypt, Syria and Cyprus, and even in Persia.

The answers kept coming, from Justin in Marcion's lifetime to Tertullian some sixty years later. In a book that Irenaeus quotes, Justin said he would not have believed the Lord Himself if He had announced any God but our maker. Tertullian's own Against Marcion ran to five books. Irenaeus argued that the Gospel is fourfold, told in four books, and he named Marcion among those who make it fewer.

Irenaeus tells, too, of a single meeting between Marcion and Polycarp, the bishop of Smyrna. Marcion asked, "Do you know me?" Polycarp answered, "I do know you: the firstborn of Satan."

## methodius-of-olympus

Methodius of Olympus

Around the year 300, Methodius was a bishop in Lycia, in the south of Asia Minor. He belonged to the Anatolian church of the third century. Origen had been dead some fifty years, and his books still had critics and defenders. Methodius wrote against him, and two of Origen's admirers wrote about what he did.

Socrates of Constantinople says Methodius attacked Origen in many places and then, as if taking it all back, praised him in a dialogue. Eusebius of Caesarea, in a defense of Origen that Jerome quotes, asked how Methodius dared to attack Origen now, after all he had said of his teaching. Both men admired Origen, so neither is a neutral witness.

Little else about Methodius is certain. Jerome says he was bishop of Olympus and later of Tyre. Methodius wrote a Banquet of the Ten Virgins, in which ten women in turn praise the chaste life.

His chief quarrel with Origen was over the body. Origen had taught, Photius of Constantinople reports, that the soul was given a body as a chain after its fall. In On the Resurrection, Methodius answered that the body is no chain, and that a person is soul and body joined. He pictures sin as a wild fig tree grown into a temple's stones. Death tears the tree out so the temple can stand again. Then the flesh is raised, he writes, "with the same parts, uninjured and immortal, while sin is utterly and entirely destroyed." Decades later, Epiphanius of Salamis wrote against Origen and copied Methodius word for word instead of arguing it himself.

Methodius also answered Porphyry, who had written against the Christians. That reply is lost, but Jerome counted it among the answers that had met Porphyry well. A century and more later, Theodoret of Cyrrhus kept a passage from his sermon on the martyrs. He named him among the bishops and martyrs whose words witness to the church's faith in Christ. Jerome says Methodius died a martyr at Chalcis.

## novatian

Novatian

In 250, under the emperor Decius, many Christians gave way in persecution, offering sacrifice to the gods or buying a certificate to avoid it. Rome's church had no bishop, and one of its elders, Novatian, wrote for its clergy to Carthage; Cyprian, the bishop there, names him the writer.

The fallen must wait in tears for a bishop and a council, he wrote, though the dying who repented might be helped. "Let them knock at the doors, by all means, but certainly not break them down."

Novatian was a leading elder at Rome, and his book On the Trinity set out what the church believed of Father, Son and Spirit. Later he was made a rival bishop of Rome, set up against the first. His church would not take the fallen back to communion, the bread and cup the church shares, and left their pardon to God. That church lasted for centuries.

The book follows the rule of truth, the church's short summary of belief. It sets out God the Father, maker of all, and the Son, truly man and truly God. Father and Son are two, not one person under two names. Then it turns to the Spirit. Its date is uncertain.

In 251 Cornelius was chosen bishop, and a stricter party, with confessors jailed for the faith who had not given way, set Novatian against him. Almost all we know of him comes from the men who fought him, above all his rival Cornelius.

In the persecution, Cornelius charged, Novatian had shut himself away and refused to help the brothers. He had sworn terrible oaths never to seek the office. Then, Cornelius says, he fetched three simple bishops from a corner of Italy, and, with them drunk and sick, forced them to lay hands on him to make him bishop. An American historian who edited the record calls the refusal and the drunkenness slander.

Novatian said he was pushed into it against his will. Dionysius of Alexandria wrote to him: "If you were drawn in unwillingly, as you say, you will prove it by withdrawing willingly. One ought to suffer anything at all rather than tear apart the church of God."

At Rome sixty bishops declared Novatian outside the church. Cornelius made a graver charge: at the communion table, Novatian held each person's hands and made him swear, by Christ's body and blood, never to go back to Cornelius. Cyprian answered that Novatian's rule told the fallen to weep day and night, and then die outside the church.

Novatian sent bishops of his own to many cities. His followers called themselves the Katharoi, Greek for "the pure", and baptized a second time those who joined them.

In 325 the emperor Constantine called Acesius, a bishop of Novatian's church, to Nicaea, the great council of bishops. Acesius accepted the creed and the Easter date, saying nothing new had been decided. Asked why he stayed apart, he gave the old rule from Decius's day. Those who commit a sin unto death after baptism, a grave sin such as denying Christ, should be urged to repent. They must look to God for pardon, not the priest.

Constantine answered: "Set up a ladder, Acesius, and climb to heaven alone." Socrates of Constantinople, a historian kind to Novatian's church, had this from an old man who went to Nicaea as a youth. Socrates gives no reading; a later historian took it as blame. The council's eighth canon set terms for taking in their clergy: they must accept the twice married, and the fallen after a set time of repentance.

Novatian's church flourished at Rome, Socrates says, until the 420s, when a bishop of Rome took their churches and drove their bishop into private houses.

How Novatian died is uncertain. Socrates, writing almost two centuries later, says he died a martyr in a later persecution. No other early writer confirms it.

## origen

Origen

Around the year 202, the emperor Severus began to persecute the churches, and believers from all over Egypt were brought to Alexandria, where many died. Its church was Alexandrian Christianity, and Origen's father was among those arrested. Eusebius of Caesarea says the boy longed for martyrdom. His mother hid his clothes to keep him home, so he wrote to his father in prison: "Be careful not to change your mind because of us."

His father died a martyr. In his eighteenth year Origen took charge of teaching the faith in Alexandria, because everyone else had fled. In 231 he left for Caesarea.

His great work was on Scripture. Jerome says he learned Hebrew, against the habit of his time. He set the Hebrew text beside the Greek translations in the Hexapla and marked where the old Greek fell short. When critics attacked Jerome's own Latin Job, Jerome defended it by pointing to those marks. Origen also taught that Scripture, like a person, has body, soul and spirit. Its plain story feeds simple believers, and its deeper senses feed those who have gone further. The Philocalia, a book of passages chosen from him, opens with those chapters on how Scripture should be read.

He held that the Son had always been with the Father, and a century later Athanasius of Alexandria, defending the council of Nicaea, called on "the hardworking Origen" as a witness. He also turned lovers of Greek learning toward the faith. Eusebius says two brothers he taught at Caesarea, Gregory and Athenodorus, both became bishops in Pontus.

Late in life, under the emperor Decius, Eusebius says he was chained and tortured, his feet stretched in the stocks for many days. Jerome says he died at Tyre.

Some of his ideas, above all that souls lived before they were born into bodies, drew attack. Methodius of Olympus wrote against him, and Pamphilus of Caesarea and Eusebius wrote in his defense. Three centuries after his death, the acts of a council at Constantinople list his name among condemned heretics.

## pamphilus-of-caesarea

Pamphilus of Caesarea

About eighty years after Pamphilus of Caesarea died, the scholar Jerome found twenty-five volumes of Origen's commentaries, his books explaining Scripture, copied, he wrote, in Pamphilus's own hand. "If it is a joy to have one letter of a martyr," he wrote, "how much more to have so many thousand lines! He seems to me to have marked them with the traces of his own blood."

Pamphilus was a presbyter, one of the elders who served the church beside its bishop, in Caesarea, a port in Roman Palestine. Origen, the great Bible scholar of the century before, had taught there. Pamphilus gathered a library of sacred books for his church, and Jerome says he copied most of Origen's books himself. Arrested in the last and greatest persecution of Christians by Rome's emperors, he worked from prison with his pupil and friend Eusebius on a book defending Origen. He was put to death for his faith about 310.

Eusebius writes that he gave his inheritance to the poor; Jerome, that he bought every book Origen had written, at great cost, for the church.

In a Life of him, now lost but quoted by Jerome, Eusebius wrote: "Who among those eager to learn was not a friend of Pamphilus? If he saw any of them lacking what they needed to live, he gave freely what he could. He gave the holy Scriptures too, most readily, not only to read but to keep. He gave them not only to men, but also to women he saw devoted to reading. So he kept many books ready, to give to those who wanted them whenever need arose."

Late in 307, the governor of Palestine ordered him to offer sacrifice to the Roman gods. When he refused, the governor had his sides scraped in long torture and jailed him for two years.

Eusebius writes that the two worked on this Defense of Origen "jointly", Pamphilus from prison. The Defense speaks of the ideas that came to Origen from the Scriptures. "When he explains these things, he often adds and declares that he is not pronouncing them as a final verdict, or settling them as fixed doctrine. He is searching as far as his strength allows, and examining the sense of the Scriptures. He does not claim to have grasped it wholly and perfectly."

In February of 309 or 310, a new governor condemned the prisoners who still would not obey. Twelve died in all. Pamphilus, the only presbyter among them, was put to death by the sword.

About 392, Jerome listed the Defense under Pamphilus's name. In 397 Rufinus of Aquileia put its first book, all that survives, into Latin as Pamphilus's, though he called it the book Pamphilus "is said to have written". Around 400, Jerome began to doubt it was Pamphilus's, and Rufinus answered that Jerome no longer spared even the martyrs. Jerome then named Eusebius as the author, and charged that Rufinus had put a martyr's name on it so that readers would trust its praise of Origen. His proof was Eusebius's own Life, which says Pamphilus wrote nothing but letters to friends.

Almost all we know of Pamphilus rests on that same Eusebius, who had called the Defense their joint work. Five centuries later, a scholar in Constantinople who read all six books said Pamphilus wrote five in prison, working with Eusebius, and Eusebius the sixth alone. Modern editors read it as joint work.

The library outlived him. Eusebius drew much of his history from it, his English editor judged, and Jerome worked from it. The Defense became a weapon in the quarrel over Origen. Jerome complained that readers ignored the bishops who condemned Origen, "since they think a martyr praised him." The book defended the good name of a teacher half a century dead.

And at Caesarea lay the books themselves, many thousand lines in Pamphilus's hand, which Jerome had once said seemed marked with his blood.

## peter-of-alexandria

Peter of Alexandria

At the fourth Easter of the persecution, the fourth year since it began, Peter, bishop of Alexandria, wrote about Christians tortured until they gave way. "They did not come to this by their own choice. The weakness of the flesh betrayed them. For they show the marks of Jesus in their bodies." He meant the scars of their torture.

Peter led the churches of all Egypt through the Great Persecution. In 306 he set rules for taking back those who had given way, weighing what each had suffered and chosen, and in 311 he was beheaded. During the persecution, too, one of his own bishops, Melitius, broke away, starting a rival church that outlived him. That church came before the first great council of the whole church, at Nicaea.

Peter became bishop around 300, and in 303 the emperors ordered the churches pulled down. Soon every Christian was told to sacrifice to the gods, burning incense as the officials demanded, and bishops of Egypt were jailed.

Those who had given way now asked for communion again, to share the Lord's Supper with the church. Peter set each case a time of fasting and prayer first. The tortured, some already mourning into a third year, needed forty more days, the days Christ fasted, while prison without torture meant a year more. Fear alone meant longer, like the gospel's barren fig tree, spared one more season to bear fruit. Those who tricked the officials, by walking past the altar, handing in a written statement or sending a pagan in their place, needed six months. Slaves forced by their masters needed a year, and the masters three. Fallen clergy kept communion, but were not to return to their office.

In those same years Melitius, a bishop in Upper Egypt, began making priests in other bishops' churches. In a letter of the time, four imprisoned bishops rebuked him, since no bishop may ordain outside his own church. An account kept with it says that after they died for the faith, he came to Alexandria, put out the men Peter had left there and ordained his own. Peter, probably in hiding, wrote to his people to hold no communion with Melitius until he could meet him with wise men. Athanasius, writing decades later as a bitter foe of the followers of Melitius, says Peter's council of bishops removed him for many crimes, among them sacrifice to the gods.

Epiphanius, writing seventy years on, says the two were in prison together and split over the fallen. In his telling, Melitius would take them back only after the persecution, and not as clergy, while Peter begged to receive them now. "Peter's case was for mercy and kindness. The case of Melitius and those with him was for truth and zeal." Yet a historian of the councils answered that Peter's own rules kept fallen clergy out of office, the very strictness Epiphanius gives Melitius. Historians still weigh the two.

The persecution eased, then returned, and in its ninth year, 311, it reached Peter. Eusebius, who lived through it, tells how. "He was seized for no cause, when no one expected it. All at once and with no reason given, as though on Maximinus's order, he was beheaded." Maximinus was the emperor, and many other bishops of Egypt, Eusebius adds, died with Peter.

His rules outlived the persecution. In 692 a council at Constantinople sealed them with the rules of other fathers, and the Greek books of church law kept them.

The split outlived him too. In 325 that council, at Nicaea, took up this same quarrel. Its letter left Melitius only the title of bishop, with no power to ordain, yet the rival church lived on.

Athanasius, who fought that rival church all his life, still called Peter blessed, a bishop and a martyr. And Alexandria kept the day of his memory.

## pliny-the-younger

Pliny the Younger

Pliny the Younger wrote to the emperor Trajan with an admission. He had never once attended a trial of Christians, so he did not know what was punished, or how hard to search, though he had already been trying them.

That was around the year 112, in Bithynia, a Roman province on the Black Sea in today's Turkey. Pliny, a man of letters, was governing it for the emperor, and his letter and Trajan's short reply are a Roman judge's own record of the church on trial.

He asked each of the accused whether they were Christians. "Those who confessed, I asked a second and a third time, and I threatened them with punishment. Those who held firm, I ordered to be led away. For I did not doubt that, whatever it was they confessed, their stubbornness and unbending obstinacy surely deserved punishment." He does not say what became of them, though later Christians took it that they died. Roman citizens he sent to Rome for trial.

As the trials went on, the charges spread, and an anonymous list was posted with many names. Those who denied being Christians he released once they had prayed to the gods, burned incense and poured wine before Trajan's statue, and cursed Christ. Real Christians, he had heard, cannot be made to do that.

Others said they had once been Christians but had stopped, and what he knew of the worship came from them. On a fixed day, they said, they had met before dawn to sing a hymn to Christ as to a god. They swore a solemn oath, not to any crime, but never to steal, rob or commit adultery. Later they met to eat ordinary, harmless food, until he banned private clubs on Trajan's orders. Then he had two slave women who were called ministers tortured, and found nothing but a twisted, excessive cult. What became of the women is not recorded.

So many were in danger, of every age and rank, in towns and countryside alike, that he stopped the hearings and wrote. Yet the temples, almost deserted, were beginning to fill again, he claimed.

The letter fills about a page, and Trajan's reply runs to five sentences. No fixed rule could be laid down for every case, the emperor wrote. "They are not to be hunted out. If they are reported and proved guilty, they are to be punished." Whoever denied being a Christian and prayed to the gods was to be pardoned, and anonymous lists did not belong to "our age".

Less than a century later, Tertullian wrote his Defense of the Christians, addressed to Rome's governors. He used Pliny's findings as proof that they had done no crime, and left out the list, the cursing and the tortured women. Then he turned on the ruling: "What a ruling, confused by necessity!" Need, in other words, had forced it to contradict itself. "It forbids a search for them, as if they were innocent, and it orders them punished, as if they were guilty. It spares and it rages; it looks away and it punishes."

Eusebius of Caesarea, writing in Greek two centuries after Pliny, took the story from Tertullian, and wrote that the ruling checked the persecution to a degree. His Pliny was "disturbed by the great number of martyrs", though Pliny gives no number. In Jerome's Latin of Eusebius's year-by-year history, he "had killed very many Christians". Historians have argued since whether the reply protected Christians, and Arthur McGiffert, Eusebius's translator, read it as making the faith itself a crime.

The governor who had never attended a trial of Christians had ended his letter with a hope. A great crowd could be set right, he wrote, if there were room for repentance.

## polycarp-of-smyrna

Polycarp of Smyrna

"For eighty-six years I have served him, and he has done me no wrong. How can I blaspheme my King, who saved me?"

Polycarp, an old man, gave that answer in a stadium in Smyrna, in the second century. The Roman governor had offered to free him if he would curse Christ. Smyrna was a city in the Roman province of Asia, and he was the bishop of its church. Irenaeus, who heard him as a boy, wrote that Polycarp had known people who had seen Jesus.

Earlier, Polycarp had visited Rome, where Anicetus was bishop. The two could not agree about keeping Easter. Polycarp kept it as he had with John and the other apostles he had known. Anicetus said he must follow the customs of the elders before him. "Even so, they shared communion," Irenaeus wrote, and the two parted in peace.

In a time of persecution, the crowd had shouted, "Away with the atheists!", meaning the Christians, who would not worship their gods. They demanded that Polycarp be found.

Because many wished it, he withdrew to a farm, and then to another. There searchers seized two young slaves of his household, and one gave him away under torture. When the police came, he could have escaped. He said, "Let the will of God be done." He had food and drink set before them and asked for an hour to pray. He prayed for two hours, and many were sorry they had come for such a godly old man.

The governor threatened him with wild beasts, and then with fire. Polycarp answered, "You threaten fire that burns for an hour and soon goes out." The crowd cried out against him as the destroyer of their gods. They asked for a lion, but the beast shows were over, so they shouted for him to be burned.

The church in Smyrna says its own people saw the flames curve around him like a ship's sail. The fire could not consume his body, they say, so an executioner killed him with a dagger. Later they laid his bones in a fitting place. They wrote that the Lord would let them gather there to "keep the birthday of his martyrdom," the day he died for Christ. They would do this "to remember those who have already competed," the martyrs before him, "and to train and prepare those still to come."

Decades after that visit, Victor, another bishop of Rome, tried to cut off the churches of Asia over the Easter date. Irenaeus wrote to remind him that Polycarp and Anicetus had kept communion though they differed.

Polycarp's letter to the church at Philippi survives. More than two hundred years later, Jerome wrote that it was still read when the churches of Asia met. It calls its readers back from false teaching to "the word handed down to us from the beginning."

## polycrates-of-ephesus

Polycrates of Ephesus

"We keep the day exactly, neither adding nor taking away. For in Asia, too, great lights have fallen asleep." By great lights he meant the apostles and saints buried there, in the Roman province of Asia, in today's Turkey. Near the end of the second century, Polycrates, bishop of Ephesus, wrote this to Victor, bishop of Rome. The day he meant was Easter, the feast of Christ's death and rising.

Polycrates led the bishops of the province in keeping Easter on the day of the Jewish Passover, whatever day of the week it fell. The other churches whose letters the historian Eusebius saw kept it only on a Sunday. When Victor pressed for one custom, Polycrates refused, and Victor tried to cut Asia off from communion, refusing to share the Lord's Supper with its churches.

Asia ended the fast before Easter on the fourteenth day of the Jewish month, counted by the moon, when the Jews sacrificed the Passover lamb. The others ended it only on Sunday, the day of the resurrection, and each side claimed the apostles. At Victor's wish, bishops met from Palestine to Gaul and decided for Sunday, while the bishops Polycrates gathered held to the old custom.

He wrote for them all. The great lights he named included Philip, whom he calls one of the twelve apostles, and John, who leaned on the Lord's breast, buried at Ephesus. With them he named Polycarp of Smyrna, who died for the faith, and other bishops and martyrs. All of them, he wrote, had kept the fourteenth day, and so had his own family: seven of his relatives were bishops, and he was the eighth. Then he answered Victor: "So, brothers, I have lived sixty-five years in the Lord. I have met the brothers all over the world, and I have gone through all of holy Scripture. I am not frightened by what is said to terrify us. For those greater than I have said, 'We must obey God rather than human beings.'"

The bishops of Asia agreed to his letter, and then, Eusebius writes, Victor at once tried to cut off the churches of all Asia as straying from the true faith. He wrote declaring them all out of communion. Some bishops rebuked him sharply, and Irenaeus of Lyon, who held that Easter belonged on Sunday, urged him not to cut off whole churches for an old custom. Whether the break took hold, historians disagree, and no source says how it ended.

The letter survives only because Eusebius copied it more than a century later, and the whole story rests on him. He first quoted it to show where John was buried, and later writers read it the same way, but the custom it defended lost. At Rome a generation later, Hippolytus put its keepers in his book against heresies, and in 325 the Council of Nicaea set one day for Easter everywhere. Later councils did what Victor had tried, and cast out any bishop who kept Easter with the Jews.

The great lights of Asia, Polycrates wrote, lie asleep, waiting for the day the Lord comes with glory from heaven. Of his own death and grave, no early writer says anything.
