# Packet L11: person and council stories (census longDescription)

## michael-i-of-alexandria

Michael I of Alexandria

On the bank of the Nile, the caliph Marwan faced the patriarch Michael. "Art thou the chief of the enemies of our religion?" he asked. "I am not the chief of wicked men, but of good men," Michael answered. Marwan had the hair of his beard pulled out and thrown into the river. "I saw it with my own eyes floating on the water," writes John the deacon, who stood near him.

Michael I of Alexandria, also called Khail, was a priest at the church of Saint Macarius. B. Evetts's English of the History of the Patriarchs, a later Arabic history of the Coptic church, sets his years at 744 to 768. Michael's life in it is told by an eyewitness who shared his prisons. He calls himself "John, the sinner," and calls Moses, bishop of Wasim, his spiritual father.

Under the governor Abd al-Malik, son of Musa, Michael was jailed for the taxes on his churches. Letters from Egypt reached Cyriacus, the Christian king of Nubia, while Michael was in prison. The governor then seized Michael again, John says. The king marched north with a hundred thousand horsemen. Michael was freed and wrote the king letters of peace, and the army turned back.

When Marwan came to Egypt, his general seized Michael in Alexandria and demanded money. "There is nothing in my church; therefore I give myself instead of the money," Michael said. Led out to be beheaded, he drew his cap over his face and stretched out his neck. The general spared him. Later he lay in chains at Giza until Marwan was killed.

When the caliph made a bishop patriarch of Antioch, Michael would not accept him. Sword, fire, lions, exile or captivity did not trouble him, he said. He had signed with his own hand a ban "that no bishop shall become patriarch."

He died on the 16th of the Coptic month Barmahat, after twenty-three and a half years, by a record in the library of Saint Macarius.

The Arabic Synaxarium is the Coptic church's book of saints' days. In its notice of a bishop who shared his sufferings, it calls Michael a martyr who shed no blood.

## moses-bar-kepha

Moses bar Kepha

Someone may ask, a Syriac bishop wrote, in what part of the world paradise lies. Is it toward the east or the west, the south or the north? Basil, the brother of Gregory of Nyssa, said we face east to pray, to train our eyes on paradise. Others, the bishop added, think church builders prove it, since they lay out churches to face the sun rising in the month of Nisan. In that month, they say, the sun rises over paradise. Ephrem said paradise circles the whole earth beyond the Ocean, as a ring circles the moon.

The bishop was Moses bar Kepha, of the Syriac Orthodox Church. The historian Bar Hebraeus calls him Severus, bishop of Beth Raman, also called Moses bar Kepha, a commentator on the holy Scriptures.

Bar Hebraeus's editors, J. B. Abbeloos and T. J. Lamy, add more from an anonymous Syriac life that Joseph Assemani reports. Moses lived about ninety years, it says. For forty years he held three sees in the region of Nineveh: Beth Raman, Beth Ceno and Mosul. For ten years he was also visitor of the church of Tagrit, which then had no bishop of its own.

Bar Hebraeus puts his death on the twelfth of Shebat, in February, and his burial at the monastery of Mar Sergius, called Moallak. One part of his chronicle gives the year 913, and another gives 903. His editors take 903, and call 913 a slip in the numeral.

His book on paradise, addressed to Ignatius, whom he calls his brother, survives in the Latin of Andreas Masius, printed in 1569. He meant it to be exact and clear, he wrote, with each chapter set apart, so that readers would understand it all and forget nothing.

In his book of church law, Bar Hebraeus lists Moses's commentaries among the books of the teachers read in the church.

J. Rendel Harris and Alphonse Mingana quote a line from Moses's tract on baptism, as a Mr. Aytoun gives it. It tells the one baptized that "thou goest down to baptism one person, and comest up another instead, the new instead of the old".

## muirchu

Muirchu maccu Machtheni

A writer setting out to tell the life of Saint Patrick began with an apology for his own skill. Many had tried before him, he wrote, and none had found one sure track of history. He had brought "the infantile row-boat of my feeble brain" into a deep and dangerous ocean. No boat had ventured there, "save only that of my father Cogitosus". He wrote, "My skill is small; my authorities are uncertain".

He was Muirchu maccu Machtheni, an Irish writer of the late seventh century. He wrote at the command of Aedh, bishop of Sletty, in Leinster. Newport White, who translated the Life in 1920, calls it the oldest Life of Patrick that survives. White dates at least the first book before Aedh's death in 699. Louis Duchesne, the French church historian, puts that death in 698.

Muirchu says almost nothing about himself. White says he and Aedh both signed the acts of Adomnán's synod at Birr, between 695 and 697. His father's name rests on a correction. Bishop Graves read the Latin words "cognito si" as the name Cogitosus, and White calls this generally accepted. White takes this father for the Cogitosus who wrote the Life of Brigid of Kildare.

Muirchu tells of Patrick's Easter fire. On that night, he says, no one might light a fire before the king's own at Temoria, which White calls Tara. Patrick lit one on the plain. The king's magicians warned that unless it was put out that night, "it will not be put out for ever." At last King Loiguire told his elders, "It is better for me to believe than to die."

Tirechan, who also wrote on Patrick, says the king was unable to believe, and White finds that account more credible. Aedh had submitted to Armagh's claim to head the Irish church, White says. He thinks the Life was probably a tribute from Leinster to Armagh.

Muirchu has Patrick die on the 17th of March, after 120 years, "as it is celebrated in all parts of Ireland."

## nennius

Nennius

A Latin history of the Britons begins with its writer's prologue and apology. In J. A. Giles's English, he calls himself "Nennius, disciple of St. Elbotus". He says he gathered "all that I could find" from Roman annals and the chronicles of the sacred fathers. He also drew on the annals of the Scots and Saxons, and on "our ancient traditions". He asks pardon for writing "like a chattering jay".

The book is the Historia Brittonum, and its author is disputed. A heading says it was "edited by Mark the anchorite, a holy bishop of that people". Louis Duchesne, editor of the early lives of the popes, lists a Vatican copy that gives it to Mark. He calls the writer pseudo-Nennius, that is, a false Nennius. Hugh Williams, editor of Gildas, notes that some copies give it to that older British writer. W. H. Stevenson, editor of Asser's Life of Alfred, calls Nennius a Welsh writer of the ninth century.

The book's own dates do not agree. The prologue says it was put together in 858, in the twenty-fourth year of Mervin, king of the Britons. Its list of years reaches 976, "the fifth year of Edmund, king of the Angles". Giles notes hardly two manuscripts give that list alike. John Williams ab Ithel, editor of the Welsh annals, dates a first edition to 674, with the genealogies added later.

Giles makes Elbotus the Elvod who was bishop of Bangor in 755 and first brought a new way of fixing Easter into the Welsh church.

The history runs from Adam to the English kings, through the British king Vortigern, Saint Patrick and the bishop Germanus of Auxerre. In this book's account, Arthur led the Britons in twelve battles, though many were more noble than he. At the hill of Badon, it says, "nine hundred and forty fell by his hand alone".

It angered him, he wrote, that his people's name should sink into oblivion. As he put it, "I had rather myself be the historian of the Britons than nobody".

## niketas-stethatos

Niketas Stethatos

On the feast of John the Baptist's birth in 1054, the monk Niketas stood before the emperor Constantine Monomachos at the Studios monastery in Constantinople. At the urging of the pope's envoys, he condemned a book against the Latin church that bore his name, and the emperor had it burned. The next day, the envoys' own record says, Niketas went to them of his own accord. He took their answers, again condemned every word and act against the see of Rome, and became their close friend.

Niketas, called Stethatos, was a monk and priest of Studios. The compilers of the Philokalia, a Greek collection on prayer, say he lived in 1030 and was the truest disciple of Symeon the New Theologian. He took on his teacher's virtues so fully, they write, that he shone beside him like a second sun.

His own three hundred chapters stand in the Philokalia. Not every tear is compunction, he writes. Tears come from regret for old sins, like boiling water that cleans the heart. Compunction comes down from the Spirit like dew, once the soul has gone deep into humility. Humility brings about the blessed life, he writes elsewhere. Holy compunction is its nurse and mother, the vision of the divine light its friend and sister, and its end the Holy Trinity.

The burned book, which survives in Latin, says the Latins use unleavened bread, which is lifeless, fast on Saturdays, and forbid their priests to marry. Cornelius Will, its editor, suspects the patriarch Michael Cerularius was behind it. The reply printed under the name of Humbert of Silva Candida, one of the envoys, ends by telling Niketas to learn at last to be silent.

Michel Le Quien, John of Damascus's editor, reasons that Niketas wrote against the Latins on the Holy Spirit after the envoys left. A Latin writer, who calls him abbot of Studios, complained that the Greeks still read his name in the liturgy.

Unceasing prayer, Niketas writes, is not seen in lifted hands or the sound of the tongue. It is known in the inner work of the mind and the remembrance of God.

## paul-the-deacon

Paul the Deacon

A Lombard named Lopichis was carried off to the Avars as a boy. As a grown man he fled, with only a quiver, a bow and a little food. Then, his great-grandson writes, "a wolf came to him and became the companion of his journey and his guide." At last he reached Italy and the house where he was born. It had no roof and was full of brambles. Inside its walls grew a large ash tree, and he hung his quiver on it.

The great-grandson was Paul the Deacon, who tells this family story in his History of the Langobards, the Lombards. He was born in Friuli between 720 and 730, by the reckoning of W. D. Foulke, who translated the History in 1907. His father was Warnefrit and his mother Theudelinda, Paul writes. He became a monk, in all likelihood at Monte Cassino, Saint Benedict's house. The one sure point, Foulke says, is that it was before 782.

Around then Paul went to the court of Charlemagne, king of the Franks. Foulke says he taught Greek there. By the king's order, Foulke adds, he gathered a book of the church fathers' sermons, perhaps begun at court and finished at Monte Cassino. After a few years he went home to his monastery.

There he wrote the History of his people. He tells how Queen Theudelinda won gifts for the church, and how Pope Gregory the Great sent her his books. Through her, Paul says, King Agilulf held the Catholic faith. Foulke thinks Paul is probably mistaken in this. Paul also says that under King Rothari almost every city had two bishops, one Catholic and one Arian, which Foulke's notes call doubtful.

He tells how the Lombards sacked Monte Cassino by night, and how Petronax of Brescia brought monks back some hundred and ten years later.

Foulke says Paul died on the thirteenth of April, but the year is unknown. His History stops after the death of King Liutprand. Franks, he writes, carried off the bones of Benedict to their own country. Even so, Paul says, Benedict's "venerable mouth, sweeter than all nectar, and the eyes beholding ever heavenly things" remained "to us, although decayed."

## paulus-emeritanus

Paulus Emeritanus

A dying boy named Augustus, at Mérida in Spain, told a deacon what he had seen. A Lord more beautiful than all the rest, he said, had taken his hand. He had led the boy into a lovely garden, where a stream ran with water the color of glass. The deacon questioned him and told his abbot. Then he went to pray at the basilica of Saint Mary, five miles from the city. When he came back at evening, the boy was dead.

The deacon tells this himself, in his Lives of the Fathers of Mérida, written in Latin. He never gives his name in the text. The book's titles call him Paul, a deacon of Mérida, and he is known as Paulus Emeritanus, from Emerita, the city's Latin name. He calls himself a Levite of Christ, unworthy and the first of sinners. He wrote the boy's words down, he says, in other words but with the very same sense.

Most of the rest of his book tells of the bishops of Mérida. For these older stories he relies, he writes, on the report of many. Paul, a Greek doctor, came from the East and became bishop. His nephew Fidelis followed him. Then came Masona. Paul the deacon calls the Arian king Leovigild most savage and most cruel. He tells how Masona beat the Arian bishop Sunna in open debate. Masona refused to take the Arian faith or to hand over the tunic of Saint Eulalia, and the king sent him into exile.

The book ends with the death of Bishop Renovatus. No line of Paul's gives a year for himself or for his book. Tomás Tamayo de Vargas, who edited it in the 1600s, reasoned that Paul died soon after Renovatus. He notes that Renovatus's successor, Stephen, signed the Fourth Council of Toledo, and that Paul does not name Stephen.

At the close Paul asks scornful readers to read his little book first and despise it afterward. He wrote, he says, driven by love of Christ and of his lady Eulalia, and set down what was true.

## photius-of-constantinople

Photius of Constantinople

In October 869, a council sent for Photius of Constantinople, the city's former patriarch. He came under compulsion, its acts say, and stood in the last place. Asked whether he accepted the decrees of the fathers and the popes, he said nothing. Pressed, he answered that God heard his voice even when he was silent. Silence would not save him, the Roman legates said. He replied that Jesus, too, kept silent and was condemned.

This record comes from the council that condemned him, in the Latin of Anastasius, the Roman see's librarian.

Photius had risen at court as chief secretary. Nicetas the Paphlagonian, who wrote the Life of Photius's rival, the patriarch Ignatius, was an enemy too. Yet he calls Photius the first scholar of his age, in grammar, poetry, rhetoric, philosophy and medicine. He bought books in great numbers, Nicetas says, and read whole nights through, for love of glory. When Ignatius was driven out, Photius replaced him. Nicetas says he went from layman to patriarch in six days, taking the throne on Christmas Day. J. H. Freese, his English translator, dates this to 858.

Pope Nicholas I refused to accept him. He wrote to Photius that he had leapt to that honor from a layman's dress, without climbing the church's grades. Photius wrote to the Eastern patriarchs against the Latins. He listed their fasting on Saturdays and their contempt for married priests. Worst, he wrote, they tried to corrupt the Creed with added words. They taught that the Spirit proceeds from the Son as well as the Father. Cornelius Will, who printed part of the letter in Latin, dates it to 866.

Symeon the Logothete says Photius called the new emperor, Basil, a robber and a murderer at communion, and that Basil then put him out. After Ignatius died, says the chronicle that continues Theophanes, Basil lawfully restored him. In 879 a council received him, with legates of Pope John VIII. Its own acts curse anyone who adds to the Creed. Severinus Binius, in his notes on the councils, calls it a false synod.

When Leo VI followed Basil, Symeon says, he sent Photius to the monastery of Gordon, where he died.

His Library sums up 279 books he had read, for Tarasius, whom he calls his beloved brother. "It is no easy matter to undertake to read each individual work, to grasp the argument, to remember and record it", he wrote.

## pisentius-of-coptos-and-john-the-elder

Pisentius of Coptos and John the Elder

When the Persians were masters of Egypt, Pisentius, bishop of Coptos in Upper Egypt, fled to the mountain of Djeme and hid there. "And at that time I John went with him," writes his disciple, John the Presbyter, "and I was with him in the capacity of a servant." When their water ran out, Pisentius said he thought there was water in one of the vessels, which they had forgotten. John found them full to the brim, a wonder, he says, "which I myself saw with my own eyes."

John wrote the Life of Pisentius as a sermon for the bishop's feast day. He is also a witness inside it, so the two share this profile. E. A. Wallis Budge translated it in 1913 from a Coptic manuscript copied in 1006.

Some things John only heard, and he says so. The young Pisentius, "it is said," saw a pillar of fire while he kept his father's sheep. Later, as a monk in his cell, Pisentius recited the whole of Jeremiah and then Ezekiel while his brother waited outside.

When the clergy came to make him bishop, he hid. They found him at last in the region of Djeme. He cried out, in the words of the great John of Constantinople, that he loved the life of peaceful contemplation. As the men who found him told it, John writes, "it was not he who ran in pursuit of the honour, but it was the honour which ran in pursuit of him."

As bishop he gave to the poor in every town and village as far as Syene, John says. He also wrote to his people, warning that God would bring on them "a nation fierce of visage and cruel" unless they repented. The letter names no nation, and Budge takes it to mean the Persians.

He died at sunset on the thirteenth of the month Epep, which Budge reckons as the seventh of July. John gives only "this fifth year", which Budge reads as a year of the indiction, a repeating count that does not fix the year. Pisentius had kept one coin since his years as a monk, to pay for his shroud.

Near the end, John asked how they would go on serving the poor if he left them nothing. Pisentius answered that "whatsoever thou givest — everything — to the poor, the Lord will give unto us twofold."

## prudentius-of-troyes

Prudentius of Troyes

Wenilo, archbishop of Sens, sent Prudentius of Troyes nineteen chapters from a book by John Scotus Eriugena, and asked him to answer each error. Prudentius found the poison of Pelagius in them, he wrote, and at times the madness of Origen. He trembled, he confessed, to see old errors, long asleep with their authors, sprouting again in his own day.

Prudentius was bishop of Troyes in Frankish Gaul, and he died in 861. On his death, the annals he had kept name him Galindo, called Prudentius, a Spaniard by birth. In his own verses for a Gospel book, Prudentius calls himself born in Hesperia and brought to the Celts. Nicolás Antonio, a Spanish scholar writing after 1650, takes Hesperia to be Spain.

He kept the annals of the Frankish kings' deeds, now called the Annals of Saint-Bertin, from the 830s until his death. G. H. Pertz, in Waitz's edition, gives the abbé Lebeuf's signs of his hand. Prudentius calls Epiphany the Apparition of the Lord, by the custom of his homeland. He notes eclipses, comets, floods and the ravages of wolves, and he credits good and bad alike to God.

The great quarrel of his day was over predestination. In 849, the annals record, the monk Gottschalk was flogged in public and made to burn his own books. Prudentius took the side Hincmar of Reims opposed. He taught, Antonio says, that God freely chose some for life, and by a justice past searching out destined others for punishment. Under 859 he wrote that Pope Nicholas the First had confirmed a double predestination. Hincmar complained to Egilo, archbishop of Sens, that he had heard this from no one else and read it nowhere.

After Prudentius died, Hincmar took over the annals, Pertz says. The entry for 861 says Prudentius had once resisted Gottschalk, but later turned in bitterness into a fierce defender of the heresy. Antonio calls this a slander, and blames an unnamed annalist on Hincmar's side. He says Prudentius never opposed Gottschalk.

Troyes, Antonio adds, names him a saint in its lists of bishops.

## sebeos

Sebeos

When the emperor Heraclius brought the Holy Cross back to Jerusalem, the crowd was so moved that no one could sing the hymns. An Armenian history tells it so. There was weeping and sighing, it says, and a great flame in every heart, from the king and his princes to the people of the city. Heraclius set the Cross back in its place.

The history is known as the History of Heraclius, by Bishop Sebeos. Frédéric Macler put it into French in 1904. He calls Sebeos the only Armenian writer of the seventh century who tells of the first Arab invasions of Armenia. Macler says he wrote in the third quarter of that century, and saw most of what he tells.

Stephen of Taron, an Armenian historian of the tenth century, lists the History of Heraclius as written by Bishop Sebeos. The printed editions place three books under his name. Macler says the first is plainly not his. The second copies Moses of Khoren and also Stephen, whose work, Macler notes, came three centuries after Sebeos. Macler translates only the third, which he takes to be the whole of Sebeos's history. It runs to 661, when the caliph Muawiya came to the throne.

After the Greeks crossed the Jordan to fight the Arabs, Sebeos writes, a terror sent by the Lord seized their army. Macler takes him to see the Arab invasion as God's punishment of the Greeks, for not keeping one faith with the Armenians. Sebeos never says so outright, Macler adds.

For the first Arab wars, Sebeos says who his witnesses were. We learned these things, he writes, from prisoners who came from Arabia and had seen them. Of the conquests he says he will tell the evils that came in our time. A burning, deadly wind blew on us, he writes, and burned the fine young trees of the gardens. And we had deserved it, for we sinned against the Lord.

Near the end, Sebeos says he speaks in vain. He sets out his words by the feeble thought of his mind, he writes, and not by the dignity of learning.

## severus-ibn-al-muqaffa

Severus ibn al-Muqaffa

A dog walked past while Severus, bishop of al-Ushmunain in Upper Egypt, stood before the chief judge. Was the dog a Christian or a Muslim, someone asked. Ask the dog, Severus said, and it will answer for itself. Today is a fast day, he went on. Christians eat no meat, and in the evening they drink a little wine. Muslims do not fast today, and drink no wine. So set meat and wine before the dog. If it eats the meat, it is a Muslim. If it leaves the meat and drinks the wine, it is a Christian.

Eusèbe Renaudot, a Catholic scholar who wrote a Latin history of the Coptic patriarchs in 1713, tells this story. The bishop was Severus ibn al-Muqaffa, and Renaudot says he was famous for his mastery of Arabic. Under the caliph al-Mu'izz, a Jew named Moses, a friend of the caliph's adviser, asked to debate the patriarch Abraham the Syrian. The patriarch chose Severus, who said such a debate was beneath the caliph's dignity, and it went no further. Little else of his life is recorded.

His name is joined to the History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria, and a preface in that History speaks in his voice. Severus says he asked Christian brothers to help him translate the histories written in Coptic and Greek into Arabic. Most people in Egypt, he writes, "are ignorant of the Coptic and the Greek."

B. Evetts, the History's translator, says no one can know how far Severus's work of translation reaches. From the seventh century, Evetts says, its lives were written by men of their own time, such as John the deacon. Renaudot names later writers who carried it on. He also doubts that Severus knew any Greek. Evetts himself changed his date for Severus. He placed him in the ninth century in 1895, and near the end of the tenth in 1904.

The preface under his name ends with a prayer: "In thy mercy be not angry with me for that which I have written, but pardon and forgive the faults therein caused by my negligence."

## simon-i-of-alexandria

Simon I of Alexandria

John, head of the monastery of Az-Zajaj, was handsome, and the Amir Abd al-Aziz agreed to make him patriarch. Then one bishop stood up, says the History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria. "This man shall not be our patriarch," he said. "Simon is worthy of this degree."

Simon I of Alexandria was a Syrian, from the East. The Amir asked why they could not choose a man of their own country. "The matter belongs to God, and in the second place to thee," the bishops replied. The Amir had him ordained.

The History is a later Arabic history of the Coptic church. B. Evetts, who translated it, dates Simon's patriarchate 689 to 701. The History says Simon's parents had given him to the church as a boy, like Samuel, at John's monastery. John the deacon, who wrote the History's life of Michael I, says Simon's archdeacon George wrote the history of these years.

Some of Simon's own priests poisoned his cup, but it did him no harm. Then they gave him poisoned figs while he fasted, and he lay forty days in pain. When the Amir ordered the poisoners burned, Simon fell on his face. He could not stay patriarch if they died, he said, and the priests were set free. The Ethiopic Synaxarium, a book of saints' days, says instead that they were sent into exile.

A priest came from India asking for a bishop. Simon would not ordain one without the Amir's command. The Gaianites, a rival sect, ordained a man anyway. The caliph Abd al-Malik had the hands and feet of the new bishop and his two priests cut off. He wrote to the Amir that the patriarch had sent news of Egypt to India. The Amir questioned Simon at night, and Simon said: "If a voice came from heaven, bidding me deviate from the truth, I would say no otherwise." The Amir gave him three days. At sunset on the second day, Simon's spiritual son found the Indian priest. The next morning the priest told the Amir all that had happened, and the Amir saw that Simon had told the truth.

Before he died, he went to see the monks of Wadi Habib. "I shall not see them again after this time in the body," he said.

## sophronius-of-jerusalem

Sophronius of Jerusalem

Stephen, bishop of Dor, told a synod at Rome in 649 how Sophronius had once led him to Calvary in Jerusalem. There, at the place of the cross, Sophronius bound him with an oath. You will answer to God, crucified here for us, he said, if you hold back while his faith is in danger. He would go himself, he said, but the Saracen invasion hindered him. So Stephen was to go from end to end of the earth until he reached the apostolic see at Rome.

Sophronius of Jerusalem tells his own beginnings in a short poem, printed in Angelo Mai's Latin. He came from Damascus, and his parents were Myro and Plinthas. He never married, and became a monk in the monastery of Theodosius, near Jerusalem. John Moschus, his teacher, calls him the Sophist, a man of learning.

At Alexandria, still a monk, he opposed the patriarch Cyrus's teaching that Christ acted with one operation, or energy. Sergius, patriarch of Constantinople, wrote that Sophronius held two operations. Sergius says Sophronius could not bring texts of the Fathers that spoke of two in those very words. In the end, he writes, it was agreed that Sophronius would speak no more of one or two.

He was then made patriarch of Jerusalem. In the letter he sent out on taking office, he wrote how dear quiet was to him now. The clergy, monks and people of the city, he said, had forced him into office like tyrants. In a Christmas sermon he mourned that Bethlehem lay close by, but fear of the Saracen sword kept him and his people at home.

Theophanes, writing in the early ninth century, says Sophronius won from the caliph Umar a pledge of safety for Palestine. When Umar asked to be shown the temple Solomon built, Theophanes says, Sophronius wept. This is truly the abomination of desolation, he said, standing in the holy place.

The Third Council of Constantinople, in 680 and 681, found his letter true to the faith. It ruled that his name be placed in the diptychs, the lists of names read out in the liturgy.

## symeon-the-new-theologian

Symeon the New Theologian

When Symeon was ordained priest, he saw the Holy Spirit come down on him, simple and formless, as light. In a passage printed with his chapters in the Philokalia, he tells it as another monk's story, with a metropolitan reading the prayer. His disciple Niketas Stethatos, in his Life of Symeon, names the patriarch Nicholas Chrysoberges as his ordainer. The vision came to him at the altar, Niketas says, through forty-eight years as a priest.

Symeon, called the New Theologian, was a monk of Constantinople. He lived about the year 1000, say the compilers of the Philokalia, a Greek collection on prayer. Niketas's Life is known through a long summary by François Combefis, a Dominican scholar.

Symeon came from Galata in Paphlagonia, Niketas says, and served young at the imperial court. Advised by a monk of Studios, Symeon called the Pious, he entered that monastery. When he would not soften his strict life, the superior put him out. His teacher sent him to the house of Saint Mamas, where he later became abbot.

His chapters speak often of light. A man may strip his whole body bare, he writes, but if a veil covers his eyes he still cannot see. So one who gives up everything will never see the spiritual light, Christ, unless he frees the eye of the soul from worldly cares.

Symeon kept a yearly feast for his teacher, with hymns and an icon. The patriarch Sergius read the hymns, Niketas says, and praised them. Sixteen years on, Stephen, once metropolitan of Nicomedia and now the patriarch's aide, began to attack the feast. Niketas puts it down to envy. A synod had the word saint blotted out on the icon, and when Symeon would not stop, he was exiled near Chrysopolis. Recalled, he was offered a high see if he would give way. He refused, and was left free to keep the feast. Sergius said, in words Niketas reports, that Symeon had not erred in the church's teaching.

Michel Le Quien, John of Damascus's editor, called him the father of the Palamite error, named for the later theologian Gregory Palamas. Combefis defended him. Gregory of Sinai tells those who seek stillness to read the New Theologian and his disciple.

Some mocked such claims, Symeon wrote. Who has seen God, they asked. Of those who take up the cross, he answered, many saw God before, and more, he thinks, see him now.

## theodore-of-tarsus

Theodore of Tarsus

Pope Vitalian needed an archbishop for the English. Hadrian, an abbot near Naples, would not take the post, and in the end he put forward a monk he knew. Bede of Jarrow describes this monk, then in Rome, as "born at Tarsus in Cilicia" and learned in Greek and Latin. He was "venerable for age, being sixty-six years old". The pope agreed, but sent Hadrian with him. Hadrian was to see that he did not, "according to the custom of the Greeks, introduce anything contrary to the true faith".

He was Theodore of Tarsus, archbishop of Canterbury in England from 668 until he died in 690. Bede, writing about forty years later, is the main witness. Stephen, biographer of Bishop Wilfrid of Ripon, adds more from Wilfrid's side.

Soon after reaching Canterbury he visited all the English peoples, teaching the canons' way of keeping Easter. "This was the first archbishop whom all the English church obeyed," Bede writes.

In 673 he called the bishops to Hertford. The acts speak in his own voice: "myself, Theodore, the unworthy bishop of the see of Canterbury". He showed them ten chapters he had marked in a book of canons. Among them were Easter, bishops keeping to their own dioceses, and a synod each year.

A book of penances for sinners went under his name. Its compiler calls himself a disciple of the Northumbrians. Most of it, he reports, came from questions the priest Eoda put to Theodore. F. W. H. Wasserschleben, who edited it in 1851, holds that Theodore wrote no such book himself.

In 678, Bede says, Egfrid, king of the Northumbrians, drove Wilfrid from York, and Theodore ordained new bishops for his diocese. Stephen says the king and queen won Theodore over with gifts. In Stephen's telling, the old archbishop later confessed that he had wronged Wilfrid.

Theodore died in 690, aged eighty-eight. In Bede's words, the English churches gained more in his time "than ever they had done before."

## thomas-of-marga

Thomas of Marga

In the Arab year 217, a young man came to the monastery of Beth 'Abhe. He began asking the old monks about the holy men who had lived there. He tells it himself. By reason of the fervor that burned in him, he writes, he made inquiries about each of them from the old men he found there. Of one holy man he says he "did not know that I should become the historian" of his deeds, and so had not asked more.

He was Thomas of Marga, a monk of the Church of the East. E. A. Wallis Budge, who translated his Book of Governors in 1893, reckons his arrival as 832. In 837, Budge says, he was secretary to the catholicos Abraham, the head of his church. Thomas recalls copying letters "before the Patriarchal throne" in the days of his youth. Budge says Abraham made him bishop of Marga, and that he later became metropolitan of Beth Garmai.

A monk named 'Abhd-Isho' had urged him for many years to write. Thomas says he would not, for two reasons. Writing histories is severe labor, and it keeps a man from his daily work. In the end he wrote the histories of the holy men and monks of Beth 'Abhe. He names his sources. Among them are an old deacon of Shalmath, the letters of the patriarch Timothy I, and Abraham himself, who "related to me fully" one story. His stories, he writes, are "not vain imaginations of my own".

Budge's title dates the book to 840. Jérôme Labourt, the French historian, says he wrote between 832 and 850.

Mari ibn Sulayman, a later historian of that church, tells of Thomas in his life of the patriarch Theodosius. The caliph al-Mutawakkil held the patriarch in prison for three years and six months. All that time, Mari says, the patriarch's brother Thomas, metropolitan of Beth Garmai, stood by him and cared for him.

Thomas pictured his work as a harvest. He would go into the gardens of their deeds as into a field, he wrote, and gather "the choice and well-filled ears of corn" into one sheaf.

## timothy-i-of-baghdad

Timothy I of Baghdad

Early in 781, Christians in China's capital set up a great stone. On it, in Syriac, the language of their church, they named their patriarch, the head of the whole church, far away in Mesopotamia. But he was dead, and a new patriarch had been installed months before; the news, it seems, had not yet reached them.

The new patriarch was Timothy. For more than forty years he led the Church of the East, the church of the Christians of Mesopotamia and Persia. He led it from Baghdad, under five caliphs, the Muslim rulers of the empire. Historians of his church say he argued for his faith at the caliphs' court. And he sent monks out as bishops, the church's leaders for a region, and let them make more bishops where they were. His letters show a church that reached toward the Turks and China.

Timothy was already a bishop when the patriarch before him died. Mari, a historian of his church writing more than three centuries later, says Timothy showed the electors sacks he said held money for them, and won. When the electors asked for their money, he answered, "The priesthood is by no means sold for money," and showed them the sacks held only stones. Other later histories tell the trick differently.

The chief bishop of Elam, a rival for the post, refused to worship or work with him. Then the chief bishop of Elam and thirteen bishops declared Timothy cursed and cut off from the church, and he cut them off in turn. The split lasted about two years, by Timothy's own count, and in it Christians fought, killed and jailed one another.

A list of his writings, made five centuries later, names a debate with the caliph al-Mahdi. Another historian of his church, five centuries later, says the next caliph, al-Hadi, sent for him on most days to put hard questions to him about religion.

Thomas of Marga, a monk who wrote about twenty years after Timothy's death and had read his letters, tells of the bishops Timothy sent. Two brothers from one monastery, Kardagh and Yahballaha, went as chief bishops to Gilan and Dailom, south of the Caspian Sea. When many peoples there came to the faith, Yahballaha asked leave to make bishops. But to ordain a bishop, to make one, took three bishops, and the brothers were two, far from Baghdad, where a new bishop normally went to have his making confirmed.

Timothy wrote back. The book of the four Gospels, the life of Jesus, read in worship, would stand in for the missing bishop, laid on the throne, the bishop's chair in the church. "Make bishops of whomever you and the pious Kardagh choose. In place of the third bishop, let the Book of the Gospels be laid on the throne at the right hand. So, by the hand of God, ordain the first bishop." They made seven bishops. From Timothy's letters, Thomas learned of a chief bishop of Beth Sinaye, which scholars take to be China.

He died in the early 820s, under the fifth caliph; one chronicle gives 9 January 823. Mari says he was ninety-five. He was buried at Baghdad, in the monastery he had restored and made his home.

## tirechan

Tirechan

An Irish bishop, writing down the churches of Saint Patrick, stopped to say what moved him. His heart, he wrote in Latin, thought of love for Patrick. He saw deserters, arch-robbers and warriors of Ireland who hated Patrick's paruchia, the churches under his authority, because they had taken what was his. They feared, he said, that if Patrick's heir claimed that paruchia, he could win back almost the whole island.

The bishop was Tirechan. His notes on Patrick are kept in the Book of Armagh, which Ferdomnach, the scribe of Armagh, copied between 807 and 846. Newport White, who translated parts of them in 1920, dates the work no earlier than 664, possibly about 670. Whitley Stokes, who printed the Latin in 1887, takes the latest plagues it mentions to be those of 683, "apparently".

Tirechan tells little of himself. A heading says he wrote from the mouth or the book of Bishop Ultan, whose pupil he was. Tirechan says Ultan fostered him, and that he found four names of Patrick written in a book kept by Ultan. White makes Ultan bishop of Ardbraccan in Meath, and Tirechan a native of Tirawley, in County Mayo.

His book follows Patrick from place to place, naming the churches he founded. White says its purpose was to record what belonged to Armagh, against the claims of rival churches. In it Patrick fasts forty days on Mount Egli, "observing the discipline of Moses, and of Elijah, and of Christ." Two daughters of King Loiguire, Ethne the fair and Fedelm the golden-haired, find the bishops at a well at dawn. "Who is God?" the elder asks. "And where is God?"

Of a magician's stone near Tara he writes, in White's English, "I have seen it with mine own eyes."

Louis Duchesne, the French church historian, saw in it little but local traditions, much embellished over two centuries of telling, with Armagh's interests at stake. To White, the book gives the impression of "an honest author who had no gift of composition".

## wilfrid-of-ripon

Wilfrid of Ripon

In Rome, an old English bishop stood trial before the pope. Bede of Jarrow says the acts of an earlier synod under Pope Agatho were read aloud at the hearing. His name was found in them. The hearers asked one another "who that Bishop Wilfrid was?" A man who had been a bishop near forty years, they agreed, ought not to be condemned.

He was Wilfrid of Ripon, bishop of York in northern England, who died in 709. Stephen, a priest of his own circle, wrote his Life in Latin, from Wilfrid's side. Bede tells it more briefly.

At fourteen he went to the monks of Lindisfarne. Bede says he judged the way of the Scots, as Bede calls the Irish, not perfect, and set out for Rome. At Lyon in Gaul he wished to die with the bishop who had taken him in. The executioners spared him, Bede says, because he was a stranger, of the English nation. Back in England, Alfrid, son of King Oswy of the Northumbrians, gave him the monastery of Ripon.

At Whitby in 664 he spoke for the Roman Easter. In Bede's account he set the few "in a corner of the remotest island" against "the universal church of Christ throughout the world". King Oswy ruled for Peter, the keeper of heaven's door.

As bishop of York, Stephen says, he built great churches at Ripon and Hexham. In 678, Bede says, Oswy's son King Egfrid drove him from his see. Stephen blames the queen's envy. Wilfrid appealed to Rome, and Stephen says Agatho's synod ordered him restored. Kept out by the king's enmity, Bede says, he preached to the South Saxons, and taught the starving people to fish. Restored after Egfrid's death, he was driven out again five years later and went back to Rome.

He died at Oundle and was buried at Ripon, after forty-five years as bishop, by Bede's count. Stephen says he died as his monks, singing the psalms, reached one verse. It asks God to send forth his spirit and renew the face of the earth.

## willibrord

Willibrord

In the margin of a calendar he used, a bishop named Clement Willibrord wrote a short note about himself in Latin. He came over the sea to Francia in 690, he says. In 695 Pope Sergius ordained him bishop in Rome, and he calls himself unworthy of it. Louis Duchesne, editor of the early lives of the popes, prints the note.

Willibrord came from Northumbria, in northern England, and became archbishop of the Frisians, across the sea. Bede of Jarrow wrote of him in about 731, while he was still alive. Alcuin of York wrote his Life, full of miracle stories, more than fifty years after his death, by the reckoning of Froben, its editor.

Alcuin says that as soon as the boy was weaned, his father Wilgis gave him to the brothers of Ripon. He later spent twelve years in Ireland. Bede says he went to preach in Frisia with others, twelve in all.

Bede says Pippin, duke of the Franks, sent him to Pope Sergius, who consecrated him archbishop of the Frisians in 696 and named him Clement. Duchesne trusts the year in Willibrord's own note over Bede's. Pippin gave him a see at a fort that Bede says the French call Utrecht.

Alcuin tells of an island the pagans held sacred to their god Fosite. No one dared touch its cattle, and water was drawn from its spring only in silence. Willibrord baptized three men there and had the cattle killed for food. Radbod, king of the Frisians, cast lots to punish him, and one companion was put to death. Willibrord told the king his god was a devil. Radbod sent him back to Pippin.

Alcuin says he built the monastery of Echternach and was buried there after he died on 7 November. He gives no year. In a sermon for his feast, Alcuin names the patron saints of many cities, from Rome to Paris. To the monks Willibrord gathered, he says, God gave no small help in him.

## yahya-ibn-said-of-antioch

Yahya ibn Said of Antioch

On the Monday before Pentecost in 966, a crowd attacked the Church of the Resurrection in Jerusalem. The governor of Jerusalem had demanded more gifts from the patriarch John than custom allowed. John paid at first, but refused a new demand at Pentecost and shut himself in the church. The governor's followers burned its doors, and its dome fell in. The next day they found the patriarch hiding in an oil cistern in the church. They killed him, tied his body to a pillar and burned it.

Yahya ibn Said of Antioch tells this story. I. Kratchkovsky and A. Vasiliev, who edited his history in 1924, call him an Arab Christian historian of the eleventh century. He wrote his book as a sequel to the history of Sa'id ibn Bitriq, patriarch of Alexandria, also called Eutychius. Sa'id's history stops in the Arab year 326, which is 937 to 938, and Yahya takes up the story there.

His preface sets out his plan. He would record every account of past times that reached him and seemed sound, down to his own day. He wrote for someone he does not name, who had asked him for the book.

Later, he says, chronicles he had not known came into his hands, so he rewrote the whole book. Then he moved to Antioch, in the Arab year 405, which is 1014 to 1015. There he found more chronicles and revised part of it again. He set this down so that, if other copies turned up that differed, the reason would be known.

His preface is all the sources used here give of his life. None gives his birth or his death.

Frédéric Macler, translating the Armenian historian Stepanos of Taron, quotes Gustave Schlumberger's history of Byzantium in the tenth century. Schlumberger says the eastern historians, Yahya among them, are almost always very exactly informed about Asia, though much less about the distant war in Bulgaria. Yet only lately, he writes, has Yahya's chronicle shown that one campaign of the emperor Basil II in Bulgaria lasted four whole years.

Yahya promised a middle way, neither too long nor too brief. Minds seek most eagerly, he wrote, to know the events closest to their own time.

## bernard-of-clairvaux

Bernard of Clairvaux

In 1113 a young nobleman of Burgundy named Bernard left his father's house to become a monk, and his brothers went with him. The eldest, Guy, saw the youngest, Nivard, playing in the street, and told him that all the family land would now fall to him alone. Nivard answered that heaven was for them and earth for him, and the division was not fair.

Bernard, with more than thirty companions, his friend William, who heard him preach, says, entered Cîteaux, a poor new monastery, where monks kept the old Rule of Saint Benedict strictly. Nivard followed a little later. The house was so small it had almost lost hope of lasting. In time, popes and kings would come to ask Bernard's word.

Two years later, the abbot of Cîteaux sent him with a band of monks to found a new house, and made him their abbot, the head of a monastery. The place was Clairvaux, an old robbers' den. They lived on barley bread and stews of beech leaves.

From 1135 he preached to his monks on the Song of Songs, the Bible's book of love poems, which he read as the love between God and the soul. He said, "Love seeks no cause beyond itself, and no fruit. Its fruit is the loving itself. I love because I love; I love in order to love." His Song sermons were read for centuries.

In 1140 he moved against Peter Abelard, a teacher whose books were read far and wide. Bernard said they let human reason claim everything and leave nothing for faith. At a meeting of bishops at Sens, Bernard writes, he read out the charges, and Abelard walked out and appealed to the pope in Rome, over their heads. A hostile pupil of Abelard's mocked the bishops as drunk and half asleep. Pope Innocent II condemned Abelard and ordered him shut in a monastery and his books burned.

In 1145 a monk Bernard had trained became Pope Eugenius III. The king of France had asked him again and again to speak for a new crusade, a great armed pilgrimage to the East. His monk Geoffrey says he refused until the pope ordered him. In 1146 he preached it, promising pardon for every sin confessed with a contrite heart. That pardon was for all who took the cross, putting its sign on their shoulder as the mark of those going east.

That year a monk of his own order, who had left his cloister, stirred crowds along the Rhine to kill Jews. Bernard wrote that the Jews must not be killed or even driven out. In the same letter he called their scattering a just penalty.

In 1148 the kings turned back from Damascus, the Muslim city of Syria, with nothing done. Bernard wrote that the armies were killed by the sword or by hunger. A historian of the crusader kingdom blamed treachery, but said he could not find out for certain who was guilty. Bernard blamed the crusaders' sins. The blame fell on him, and he wrote to Eugenius, "If one of the two must happen, I would rather people grumble against me than against God. It is good for me if he will use me as a shield. I gladly take on myself the cursing tongues of slanderers and the poisoned darts of blasphemers, so that they do not reach him."

He wrote the pope a book of advice, On Consideration. Peter, whose place the pope held, never rode a white horse or went in gold and silk, he said. In such things the pope followed not Peter but the emperor Constantine. It was a friend's counsel. In 1413 Jan Hus copied those lines into his book on the Church, to argue that the popes of his own day, living in pomp, did not truly hold Peter's place.

He died at Clairvaux in 1153. Geoffrey counts nearly seven hundred monks in that valley, and a hundred and sixty monasteries under his care. His editor notes that the eighty-sixth sermon on the Song breaks off where death stopped him.
