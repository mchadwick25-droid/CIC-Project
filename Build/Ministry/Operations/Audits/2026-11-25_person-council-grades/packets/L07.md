# Packet L07: person and council stories (census longDescription)

## gennadius-of-marseilles

Gennadius of Marseilles

After the people of Alexandria killed their bishop, Proterius, Timothy Aelurus was made bishop in his place by a single bishop, willingly or not. To defend himself, Gennadius of Marseilles writes, Timothy sent the emperor Leo a very persuasive book. It claimed that Pope Leo, the Council of Chalcedon and all the Western bishops were Nestorians at heart. At the brothers' request, Gennadius put the book into Latin "for learning's sake", and "prefixed a caveat".

Gennadius was a priest of Marseilles, in Gaul, late in the fifth century. Little more is known of his life. He carried on the Lives of Illustrious Men by Jerome, a list of Christian writers. Ernest Cushing Richardson, who translated both into English, judges most of Gennadius's part was written about 480. Gennadius also put Greek works into Latin, among them those of the monk Evagrius, a disciple of Macarius the Egyptian.

Richardson finds in him glimpses of a point of view that he calls semi-Pelagian. Gennadius thinks Prosper of Aquitaine wrote an unsigned book against works of John Cassian. He says Prosper "brands as injurious" works "which the church of God finds salutary". Some editions add a line on Augustine of Hippo. Because he said so much, it runs, Solomon's saying came true of him, "In the multitude of words there wanteth not sin." The line drew much comment on his bias, Richardson says, though it almost certainly does not go back to Gennadius.

"I Gennadius, a presbyter of Marseilles, have written eight books Against all heresies," begins a notice at the end of the work. It also names a letter on his creed sent to Gelasius, bishop of Rome. Richardson thinks this notice is a later hand's. He and Blomfield Jackson, Theodoret's translator in the same volume, give 496 for his death.

Of Faustus of Riez he wrote, "There are still other works by him, but as I have not read, I do not care to mention them."

## gildas

Gildas

For ten years or more, Gildas says, he held back. "I have kept silence, I confess, with infinite sorrow of heart," he writes, "for the past ten years or even longer." At last he wrote a long letter in Latin to the rulers and clergy of Britain, "more in tears than in denunciation," as he puts it.

That letter, The Ruin of Britain, is almost all that survives from this British churchman of the sixth century. Hugh Williams, who put it into English, says it is "in no way a history". He calls Gildas "a preacher, a revivalist".

Gildas was born in the year of the siege of Badon Hill, almost the last great British victory over the Saxons. He writes of "the forty-fourth year, with one month now elapsed". Bede took the years from the coming of the Saxons. Williams follows the scholar Ussher, who counts them from Badon, so Gildas wrote some forty-three years after his birth. Williams puts that birth about 500, a date no early source gives. He dates the book a few years after 540, and before 547, while a king it attacks, Maglocunus, was alive. The Welsh annals put that king's death in a great plague, in Williams's year 547.

"Kings Britain has, but they are as her tyrants," Gildas writes. He names five, and calls Maglocunus "the island dragon", "last in my writing, first in wickedness". Then he turns on the clergy: "Priests Britain has, but foolish ones." He spares "a very few", the church's "only real children", who he says hold up his own weakness.

Readers abroad soon knew his name. The Irish saint Columbanus wrote to Pope Gregory the Great about bishops whom "the writer Giltas" calls pests. Williams dates the letter between 595 and 600. Bede later called Gildas the Britons' "own historian".

Two Lives tell much more, but they are late. Williams dates the core of the Breton Life, by a monk of Ruys, to the ninth century. Caradoc of Llancarfan wrote the Welsh Life in the twelfth. Williams calls its Glastonbury chapters "a piece of literary fiction". The Breton Life says that Gildas, dying, asked to be laid in a boat on his stone pillow and pushed out to sea.

Alcuin of York, writing in the 790s by Williams's dates, called him the wisest of the Britons. He told his readers to learn from Gildas why the Britons lost their land.

## gregory-of-tours

Gregory of Tours

In 573 Gregory of Tours became bishop of Tours, in the church of Merovingian Gaul, a land split among rival Frankish kings at war with one another. People lamented, he says, "Alas for our times, because the study of literature has died out among us," so he wrote ten books of History himself, though his speech, he admits, was rude.

He opened them with his creed, "so that anyone who reads may have no doubt that I hold the catholic faith," and a king put that faith to the test. Chilperic decreed that the Trinity be called only God, with no distinct Persons, and told Gregory, "I want you and the other teachers of the churches to believe this." Gregory refused. Only the Son was made man and suffered, he answered, so that He might be offered for the world, and the king must hold to "what Hilary and Eusebius taught." These were Hilary, the bishop of Poitiers, and Eusebius of Vercelli, his great helper against heresy. The king said he would find wiser men, and put the decree to Salvius, bishop of Albi, who would have torn the paper up. "So the king gave up this plan," Gregory writes. In his telling, the bishops' refusal ended a king's attempt to set the faith of his church. He does not say his stand cost him anything.

He held the same ground against the Arians of Spain. When their king's envoy Agilan argued at Tours that the Son was lesser than the Father, Gregory answered that the Father was never without wisdom or light, and so never without the Son. Agilan swore he would take no blessing from a priest of Gregory's faith, though later, Gregory reports, sickness in Spain drove him to that faith.

Two centuries later the historian Paul the Deacon sent readers to "the books of that revered man Gregory, bishop of Tours." Much of what is known of the church in Gaul under the first Frankish kings comes from those books, and for his stands against Chilperic and Agilan, his is the only account we have.

## gregory-the-great

Gregory the Great

Gregory the Great came from a Roman family of rank. When his father died, he kept only a small part of what he inherited. He founded six monasteries in Sicily, and a seventh in his own house on the Caelian hill in Rome. He entered that one as a monk, and later became its abbot.

He was not left there long. The pope made him a deacon and sent him to Constantinople as his envoy, probably from 578 to 585. There he met Leander, bishop of Seville. At Leander's urging he began the Moralia, a long commentary on the Book of Job.

In 590 Pope Pelagius II died of the disease then raging in Rome, and the clergy and people chose Gregory to follow him. He did not want the office. His biographers say he wrote to the emperor and begged him not to confirm the choice. He was ordained on 3 September 590.

His pen did not stop while he governed. The Pastoral Rule teaches bishops how to govern souls, which it calls the art of arts. The Dialogues gather the lives and miracles of the saints of Italy, Benedict among them. In his last months gout left him at times hardly able to speak, yet he still wrote letters. He died on 12 March 604.

## henana-of-adiabene

Henana of Adiabene

About three hundred students walked out of the school of Nisibis, the Chronicle of Seert says. They carried Gospels and crosses on black veils, with censers, and sang the hymns of the Rogations. They were protesting against their master, Henana of Adiabene.

Henana led the school of the Church of the East at Nisibis, in the Persian empire. Addai Scher, who edited the chronicle, reckons he became its master in 572. The teaching he was condemned for is known only through his opponents, though a pupil's praise of the man survives.

The chronicle, which writes against him, says he explained Scripture against Theodore of Mopsuestia, whom his church called the Interpreter. Abdisho of Nisibis, listing Henana's books, says many were rejected because of Theodore.

In the chronicle's telling, Gregory of Kashkar, metropolitan of Nisibis, condemned Henana's books. Sabrisho, head of the church, took Henana's side. Gregory shook the dust off his sandals at the gate and left. The students followed. But Timothy I, a later head of the church, says Sabrisho condemned Henana's writings.

The sharpest charges rest on Babai the Great alone, abbot on Mount Izla. In his Life of the martyr George of Izla, a monk says Henana taught that fate rules everything and denied the resurrection of the body. Babai calls him a Chaldean, which Oskar Braun reads as astrology, and a follower of Origen. Braun calls Babai passionate and speaks of his overstatements. J. Labourt calls Babai Henana's antagonist. He cannot say if the charge about fate is false, but finds it plausible.

Henana's pupil Barhadbeshabba wrote the Cause of the Foundation of the Schools while his master lived, Scher says. Scher thinks the pupil probably hid the truth to praise his master.

Barhadbeshabba calls him gentle, merciful and patient, a man who does not seek his own glory. He says his master worked day and night to read and explain the Scriptures. He prays that God will lengthen his days.

## isidore-of-seville

Isidore of Seville

It was the seventh year, Braulio of Zaragoza thought, since he had first asked Isidore of Seville for his Etymologies. Now he would turn his prayers into a complaint, he wrote. "Give back, give back what you owe," he pleaded. Their Latin is told here in modern words. One of Isidore's letters to him tells how the book went. Braulio's letters had found him at Toledo, on his way to a council. Weak and tired, Isidore sent the Etymologies on from the road, uncorrected because of his illness.

Isidore was bishop of Seville in the Visigothic kingdom of Spain. Ildefonsus, a later bishop of Toledo, says he followed his brother Leander of Seville in the see. Leander's Rule for their sister Florentina calls Isidore the younger brother, left a child when their parents died.

Ildefonsus says he held the see for almost forty years, from King Reccared's time to King Sisenand's. His speaking, Ildefonsus writes, had such richness that it left his hearers struck with wonder. Theodor Mommsen sets his death in 636.

Under King Sisebut, Isidore is named first among the bishops who met in council at Seville. Their acts tell of a Syrian bishop, as he claimed, from the sect they call the Acephali. He denied two natures in Christ and said the Godhead could suffer. After long debate he gave up his teaching and confessed two natures in one person.

At the Fourth Council of Toledo, in 633, Isidore signed first among sixty-six bishops of Spain and Gaul. The council ruled that from then on no Jew should be forced to believe. Jews already forced into baptism under King Sisebut, it added, must be made to keep the faith they had received. In his History of the Goths, Isidore wrote that Sisebut had zeal, but not according to knowledge. The king compelled by force, he says, those he ought to have drawn to the faith by reason.

Braulio says Isidore wrote the Etymologies at his request and left the work unfinished. Braulio himself divided it into twenty books. In it, he says, Isidore "has gathered well-nigh everything that ought to be known", in Ernest Brehaut's English. Ildefonsus says Isidore seemed to reach his last day still at work on it.

Its first book, on grammar, explains why the origins of words are worth knowing. "For every consideration of a thing is clearer," Isidore wrote, in Brehaut's English, "when its etymology is known."

## jacob-baradaeus

Jacob Baradaeus

Horsemen were hunting a bishop across the roads of the empire. Often they met him on the way, and did not know him. "Have you heard any secret news of that deceiver James anywhere there?" they asked. He named a place well behind him. "If you set out," he told them, "perhaps you will catch him." John of Ephesus, whom James made a bishop, tells the story.

Jacob Baradaeus, whom John calls James, was the bishop who built up the church that rejected the council of Chalcedon in Syria and far beyond. Born at Tella, John says, he trained in the monastery of Fsiltha. He went up to Constantinople, where the empress Theodora gave him a house. He lived alone in a cell for about fifteen years. He cut one thick, ugly cloak in two and made his clothing from the halves. From this, says E. W. Brooks, who edited John, came his nickname, Burd'ana, the man of the patchwork garment.

Bishops of his party had grown few. Harith bar Gabala, king of the Saracens, asked Theodora to have two or three made. In the sixteenth year of Justinian, John says, James was made bishop for Edessa, and Theodore for Hirtha of the Saracens.

Then James went on foot through Syria, Armenia, Cappadocia, Asia and the islands, as far as Alexandria. He would finish his work in a district in a night and a day, then move thirty or forty miles on. The emperor paid gold to anyone who promised to catch him. John counts twenty-seven bishops he ordained, and two patriarchs. If anyone says he ordained a hundred thousand clergy, John adds, "I do not think he will depart from the truth."

In old age he was drawn into a bitter quarrel over Paul of Antioch, a patriarch he had made. John calls him "a simple man", swayed by the violent men around him. Michael the Syrian, a twelfth-century patriarch of the same church, says those who stood with the old man were called Jacobites. Brooks thinks the name arose from that quarrel.

He died on the road to Alexandria, at the monastery of Cassianus on the border of Egypt, by John's first account. John also gives a report that their ship sank, and says, "which of these stories is true, God knows." Michael gives the day as the thirtieth of Tammuz, which is July, and Brooks the year as 578.

## john-climacus

John Climacus

John, abbot of the monastery of Raithu, wrote to the abbot of Mount Sinai. He asked him to write down what he had seen on that mountain, as Moses once did. Such a book, he wrote, would carry those who strive for heaven up as if by the steps of a ladder. Jacob, only a keeper of sheep, had seen a ladder. A teacher of a flock that can reason, he argued, could show everyone the way to God.

The abbot answered that only the fear of casting off obedience made him take on a task beyond his strength. From his book, the Ladder, he was called John Climacus, from the Greek word for a ladder. Philippe Labbe, writing in the 1600s, places him late in the 500s.

His life comes mainly from a short saint's Life by Daniel, a monk of Raithu, near the Red Sea. At sixteen John gave himself to God on Sinai, under a teacher. When his teacher died, he went to live alone at a place called Tholas, five miles from the church, and stayed forty years.

Daniel tells how John, dozing in his cell, saw a man who rebuked him for sleeping safely while his disciple Moses was in danger. John rose and prayed. That evening Moses said a huge rock had nearly crushed him in his sleep. Later the monks made John abbot by force.

The Ladder climbs in thirty steps, from leaving the world to faith, hope and love. Christ was baptized at about thirty, John writes at its end, and so took the thirtieth step, since God is love.

An unnamed monk of Sinai tells that John, as he died, promised his brother George that George would follow him within the year. Labbe reports a form of this tale tying George's death to that of Peter, patriarch of Jerusalem. If so, he argues, John died between 560 and 565. Yet he says hardly anything about the year is certain. Gregory the Great wrote to a John, abbot of Mount Sinai, in a letter its editors, Paul Ewald and Ludwig Hartmann, date to 600. Gregory's English translator, James Barmby, takes this John to be Climacus, still alive then.

In the 1400s, Thomas à Kempis remembered John Ketel, a cook in the House of Florentius at Deventer. "Sometimes he read the book of John Climacus", Thomas writes. Ketel explained it so well, Thomas says, that wise men marveled.

## john-moschus

John Moschus

One noon in Alexandria, John Moschus and his friend Sophronius, later patriarch of Jerusalem, went to study with the philosopher Stephen. He was asleep, so they waited at the Tetrapylon, where three blind men told how each had lost his sight. The third had once stripped a richly dressed corpse in its tomb. The dead man sat up, he said, and tore out his eyes. Sophronius nodded, and they left. "Truly, Abba John, let us study no more today," he said, in modern words. "We have been edified enough."

Moschus, a monk of Palestine, tells this in the Spiritual Meadow, his book of stories about monks of his time, written for Sophronius.

An old notice of his life was set before the Meadow. Daniel Papebroch, a Bollandist scholar, says Photius of Constantinople copied much of it. It says Moschus became a monk at the monastery of Saint Theodosius. He lived long in the desert by the Jordan, then at the New Laura of Saint Sabas. After the emperor Maurice was murdered and the Persians pressed in, he went to Antioch and then Alexandria. Hearing that the holy places had been taken, he sailed for Rome with Sophronius.

There, the notice says, he saw his death coming, wrote the Meadow and gave it to Sophronius. He asked to be buried on Mount Sinai, or if barbarians barred the way, at Saint Theodosius. Raiders barred the road to Sinai, so Sophronius buried him at Saint Theodosius. Papebroch reckons that year as 620.

Johann Albert Fabricius says the Meadow often attacks the followers of Severus of Antioch, who rejected the Council of Chalcedon. It has its share of fabulous tales, he adds. John of Damascus and the Second Council of Nicaea, in 787, cited it as Sophronius's book.

In the dedication, Moschus says he picked the fairest flowers from these lives and wove a crown from a meadow that never fades. "I offer it to you, and through you to all," he writes, in modern words.

## john-of-beth-aphthonia

John of Beth Aphthonia

A man named Dometios asked for a Life of Severus of Antioch, since the wise and eloquent had left this world. The writer protested, in words told here in modern English. Asking him was like hiring a worker in lead to make a royal crown. He wrote it all the same, in Greek, and it survives in a Syriac translation, which M.-A. Kugener put into French.

The heading names the writer as John, superior of the monastery of Beith Aphthonia. He calls himself small and not learned in letters.

Who this John was is disputed. One manuscript, which holds a short extract, gives the Life to the archimandrite John bar Aphthonia, and Kugener calls that false. The Life itself tells of John bar Aphthonia's death as another man's. J.-B. Chabot and Rubens Duval write of the Life as John bar Aphthonia's, and E. W. Brooks names him its author.

John bar Aphthonia was a learned man of Edessa, the Syriac chronicle under Zachariah's name says. When the monks of Saint Thomas at Seleucia were driven from their house, he settled them at Kenneshre on the Euphrates. A hymn heading calls him its first abbot. Michael the Syrian calls this house the monastery of Bar Aphthonia. John bar Aphthonia sat with the bishops who refused the Council of Chalcedon at debates in Constantinople. He wrote a record of them, which the chronicles mention but do not give. In hymns he wrote that Severus fought with his hand as well as his tongue, shooting arrows of ink and pen.

Near death, the Life says, Severus wrote a long letter in his own hand to the archimandrite John bar Aphthonia. Through him it went to all the monks of the East. John lived fifteen days after it.

Michael the Syrian says Jacob of Edessa later became a monk at the monastery of Aphthonia and studied the Psalms there in Greek. Bar Hebraeus calls it the Kenneshre monastery.

## john-of-biclaro

John of Biclaro

In the royal city of Constantinople, John of Biclaro writes, a plague of groin sores broke out. He saw many thousands of people die of it, he says. That same year, envoys of a people he calls the Maccurritae came to the city. They brought the emperor Justin the Younger elephant tusks and a giraffe as gifts.

John was a Goth, born at Scallabis in Lusitania, Isidore of Seville writes. His life is known mainly from Isidore's notice of him. As a young man John went to Constantinople and was schooled there in Greek and Latin learning. He came home to Spain after seventeen years, Isidore says. Some copies of Isidore read the seventh year. Theodor Mommsen, who edited John's Chronicle, holds to seventeen, since its first ten years report the news of Constantinople.

King Leovigild was then pressing the Arian teaching. John refused it, and the king exiled him to Barcelona, where he suffered ten years of plots from the Arians. He later founded a monastery called Biclaro and wrote a rule for its monks. Isidore says he became bishop of Gerona. Mommsen takes him to be the John of Gerona who signs the acts of a council at Barcelona and a decree of King Gundemar.

His Chronicle carries on from that of the African bishop Victor of Tunnuna. It runs from Justin's first year as emperor to King Reccared's fourth. John tells the revolt of Leovigild's son Hermenegild as a seizure of power, urged on by Leovigild's queen, Gosuintha. At the Third Council of Toledo, he says, Reccared's presence renewed what Constantine had done at Nicaea. Mommsen notes that John writes of Leovigild, the king who exiled him, with great admiration.

In his preface John says he wrote down the deeds of his own times for those who would come after. He set down partly what he saw with his own eyes, and partly what he learned from the reports of faithful men.

## john-of-ephesus

John of Ephesus

Every son born to the parents of John of Ephesus had died by the age of two. When John fell ill too, they carried him, weeping, to Maro, a holy man who lived on a pillar. "Be quiet, be quiet," Maro told them. "John shall not die before Maro." John tells this himself, as his parents used to tell it to him, in his Lives of the Eastern Saints.

John was a bishop and historian of the party that refused the council of Chalcedon. E. W. Brooks, who edited his Lives, says he was born about 507 in the country around Amida, and grew up in monasteries there. R. Payne Smith, who put his Church History into English, gives about 516.

In Justinian's time, John writes, he was made teacher of the heathen in the provinces of Asia, Caria, Phrygia and Lydia. In the hills above Tralles alone he converted many thousands and built twenty-four churches. Brooks says the emperor set one condition: that the converts be brought to the faith of Chalcedon.

His Lives record that Jacob Baradaeus made him bishop of Ephesus. Brooks finds nothing to show he ever lived there. He led his party in Constantinople, and he was jailed more than once. In one prison he told the emperor's envoys, "Even though you eat me roasted, if I be but quit of the sight of you, I am ready on these terms to be delivered to a painful death."

In his Lives he says he writes only of people he saw with his own eyes. Payne Smith joined the History's broken pieces with lines of his own, he says. Brooks says John is not a literary writer and that his dates are often loose.

His history ends in 585, Brooks says, and he probably died the next year. Payne Smith values the History because its writer was "a busy actor in the scenes which he describes."

## john-rufus

John Rufus

One day in Jerusalem, John Rufus walked the road from Siloam up the valley with a notable of the city. Below the mountain he saw a large old monastery falling into ruin. His companion smiled and said it had been the monastery of Juvenal, bishop of Jerusalem. It became a desert, he said, when the council of Chalcedon met. John answered that Juvenal had been the companion of Judas, as Dioscorus of Alexandria had said.

John tells this in his Plerophories, a book of testimonies against Chalcedon. François Nau, its French editor, draws John's life from the chapters where he speaks of himself. By Nau's account John was an Arab of southern Palestine, probably from Ascalon, and studied law at Beirut. Peter the Fuller, patriarch of Antioch, whose close aide he had been, made him a priest there.

When Peter the Fuller was driven out, John went to Palestine. There, Nau says, he came to know Peter the Iberian, the anti-Chalcedonian bishop of Maiuma near Gaza. In his Life of Severus, Zachariah Rhetor says that after Peter died the altar was kept for John, called Rufus. The book's heading calls John bishop of Maiuma.

Nau says John wrote in Greek soon after 512, while Severus was patriarch of Antioch. It survives in Syriac, in 89 short chapters of visions and wonders, most of them told by Peter the Iberian. In one chapter John says he checked a story with people who had seen it. Nau calls the book a new kind of collection of sayings, written to fight the council and its defenders.

A Greek Life of Peter the Iberian from about 500, held in Syriac, names no author. Its German editor, Richard Raabe, asks whether the author of the Plerophories wrote it too, and leaves the question open.

The book's first chapter begins with a story Peter the Iberian used to tell, and John calls Peter his father and bishop.

## joshua-the-stylite

Joshua the Stylite

In the year Joshua the Stylite counts as 812, famine held Edessa. The poor slept in the porticoes and streets, he writes, wailing night and day from hunger. Their bodies wasted away, and they began to die where they lay. William Wright, his English translator, gives that year as 500 to 501.

Joshua wrote a short Syriac chronicle of those years around Edessa and Amid. He wrote it for Sergius, a priest and abbot near Edessa. Sergius had asked him for a record of the locusts, the famine and plague, and the war between the Romans and the Persians.

Wright says we know little more of Joshua than he chose to tell. His name and title come from a note by a later copyist, Elisha of the convent of Zuknin near Amid. The note asks mercy on "the priest Mar Yêshûa (Joshua) the stylite, from the convent of Züknin." A stylite was a holy man who lived on top of a pillar.

Joshua counts his years in the old Greek era, and Wright adds our own years. By Wright's reckoning the last date in the book is 28 November 506. Wright and the German scholar Theodor Noeldeke place the writing in the months after.

Joshua says plainly why he wrote. The disasters were sent for the people's sins, he argues, and their memory should teach readers to repent. "I am a plain man in speech," he writes, "and I record in this book those things which all men that are in our country can testify to be true."

Wright says the Chronicle is generally counted among the most valuable sources for its time. It survived inside a later Syriac chronicle. Wright took its compiler for the patriarch Dionysius of Tell-Mahre. The French scholar François Nau calls him the Pseudo-Dionysius, writing in 775.

Joshua ends by asking Sergius to pray for him. He promises to send on whatever else is worth recording, "if I remain alive."

## julian-missionary-to-the-nobadae

Julian, missionary to the Nobadae

The empress Theodora wrote to the duke of the Thebais, John of Ephesus says. She and her husband Justinian had each planned an embassy to the Nobadae, a people beyond the Thebais, at the edge of Egypt. She was sending a blessed man named Julian, and her envoy had to arrive first. If the duke let the emperor's envoy through before hers, she warned, "your life shall answer for it; for I will immediately send and take off your head." So the duke held back the emperor's envoy, saying he still needed pack animals and men who knew the deserts.

Julian was a presbyter and an old man, one of the clergy of Theodosius of Alexandria. John says Julian longed to make Christians of this people and took the plan to Theodora. Justinian disliked it, John writes, because Julian opposed the Council of Chalcedon. So the emperor told bishops of his own side in the Thebais to go and teach them.

All that is known of Julian comes from John. John's English translator, Payne Smith, notes that John belonged to the party that refused Chalcedon, and John tells the story from Julian's side. The later chronicle of Michael the Syrian shortens John's account.

The king of the Nobadae received Julian with joy. He and his nobles gave up the gods of their fathers. Julian then told them of Chalcedon, and of Theodosius, who had lost his throne rather than accept the council. When the emperor's envoy came, they took the gifts and refused the faith. They would follow "the example of pope Theodosius," they answered.

Julian stayed two years. He used to say that "from nine o'clock until four in the afternoon" he sheltered in caves full of water, wearing only a linen cloth like the local people. Even so he taught, and baptized the king, his nobles and many people. He left them in the care of Theodore, an old bishop from the Thebais, whom John later calls bishop of Philae.

Julian returned safely to Constantinople, where Theodora received him with honor. He died a short time before Theodosius. John gives no year for any of it.

Afterwards a priest named Longinus was made bishop for the Nobadae, as Theodora had ordered. John calls Julian "their teacher."

## justinian-i

Justinian I

Justinian ruled from Constantinople from 527 to 565. His Novels are the laws he issued in his own name. Many of them deal with the church. In a preface to Epiphanius, the archbishop of Constantinople, he writes that the greatest gifts of God among men, given by mercy from above, are priesthood and empire. The one serves divine things. The other rules over human things and takes care of them.

In 535 he made a law for Africa, which his armies had won back. It named the Donatists with Jews, pagans and Arians, and barred them from holding services. Another law confirmed the sentence on Severus of Antioch. It ordered his books burned and kept him out of the capital.

In 553 he called a council to condemn the writings of three theologians long dead. He hoped to win back the Miaphysites, and it did not work. His enemies wrote bitterly of him. Procopius says he told the Montanists to give up their beliefs, and that those in Phrygia shut themselves in their churches and burned them.

## lazar-parpetsi

Lazar Parpetsi

After the battle of Avarayr, the Persian king held Armenian princes captive. Lazar Parpetsi, also called Lazar of Pharp, says he saw some of them with his own eyes. He writes that they set up schools to learn church teaching. They said the psalms in their minds as they took up their weapons.

Lazar was an Armenian historian. Victor Langlois, who edited his History in French, places him at the end of the fifth century or the start of the sixth. Langlois says the name Parpetsi came from a long stay at the monastery of Pharp, a village in Ararat, and not from his birthplace.

Most of his life comes from Langlois's introduction, which draws partly on a letter of Lazar's that the Library does not hold. Langlois calls him a childhood companion of Vahan Mamikonian. He says Lazar studied in Greece with leave from the patriarch Sahak the Great and from Mesrop Mashtots. When Vahan governed Armenia for the Persian king, he put Lazar in charge of the mother church at Etchmiadzin.

Langlois says jealous clergy accused his teaching of going against the true faith, and Vahan listened to them. His enemies drove him out and would not let him take the Greek books he had gathered. From Tigranocerta he wrote Vahan a long letter answering each charge. Langlois says it seems to have worked, for Vahan called him back.

Lazar says Vahan ordered him to write the History, which ends on the day Vahan took the government of Armenia. Lazar doubted some stories in Faustus of Byzantium, suspecting that another writer had added them or a poor copyist had altered the text. He also tells that his patron Vahan, worn down by slander, weakened in his faith at the Persian court.

He called his History the third part of Armenia's annals, after the histories of Agathangelos and Faustus. In his own phrase, it was the work of his weakness.

## leander-of-seville

Leander of Seville

Pope Gregory the Great remembered where he came to know Leander of Seville. "When I knew you long since at Constantinople, my most blessed brother," he wrote. Leander had come on an embassy "on counts touching the faith of the Wisigoths". Monks who had followed Gregory there begged him to explain the book of Job, and Leander added his voice. Gregory's letter to him opens the finished Moralia on Job.

Leander was bishop of Seville in the Visigothic kingdom of Spain. His younger brother, Isidore of Seville, followed him as bishop and wrote of him. Through Leander's faith and work, Isidore says, the Gothic people turned from the madness of Arius's teaching to the Catholic faith. Isidore writes as his brother and from the Catholic side.

In exile abroad, Isidore says, Leander wrote two books against the heretics. Isidore names no place or year. Gregory the Great says King Leovigild had greatly persecuted Leander.

Travelers from Spain told Gregory that Leander turned the king's son Hermenegild to the Catholic faith. Gregory of Tours, a Frankish bishop, credits Hermenegild's wife Ingund.

Near death, Gregory the Great writes, Leovigild entrusted his other son Reccared to Leander. The historian Altamira, as O. M. Dalton reports, found no proof of this in records of the time.

Reccared became Catholic, and in 589 his bishops met at Toledo for its Third Council. John of Biclaro, a chronicler of the time, says its business lay chiefly with Leander and Eutropius, abbot of Servitanum. A homily under Leander's name is told here in modern words. It calls this the most solemn of feasts, because the conversion of so many peoples is new. We once groaned at their harshness, it says, and now rejoice at their faith.

He also wrote a rule for his sister Florentina. He searched for riches to leave her as his heir, he says, and found nothing under the sun worthy of her. Christ is her bridegroom, brother and friend, and her share of the inheritance. He asks her not to forget their younger brother Isidore, left a child when their parents died.

When Leander died is disputed. Isidore says only that it was under Reccared. An epitaph's date comes to 603 in Theodor Mommsen's reckoning, and he doubts the epitaph. Nicola Turchi gives 600 or 601, and Edmund Gardner 599.

Gregory once wrote to him of carrying "the image of thy countenance" stamped deep within his heart.

## liberatus-of-carthage

Liberatus of Carthage

The emperor Justinian sent two bishops to Rome to consult Pope John, called Mercurius. They came to oppose Cyrus and Eulogius, envoys of the monks called the Acoemetae. These two denied that Mary is truly and properly the Mother of God. Liberatus of Carthage writes that he was in Rome when the pope upheld this teaching by letter.

Liberatus was a deacon of the church of Carthage under Justinian. Pope Agapetus, writing to Reparatus, bishop of Carthage, says two bishops and Liberatus the deacon came to speak for Reparatus. Otto Günther dates the letter to 535. His life is known almost only from this letter and his book.

His book is the Breviarium, a short history of the quarrels over Nestorius and Eutyches. He wrote it, he says, worn out by the hardships of a journey. He wrote to answer the followers of both heresies, who do not tell the truth about their own leaders.

Liberatus blames a later fight on Theodore, bishop of Caesarea in Cappadocia. Theodore was a fierce defender of Origen, he says. Grieved when Origen was condemned, he urged the emperor to condemn Theodore of Mopsuestia and a letter of Ibas of Edessa. These were two of the Three Chapters. So Justinian wrote a book against the Three Chapters, Liberatus says, for our sins. Jean Garnier, whose edition Migne reprints, counts him among the African defenders of the Three Chapters.

The African chronicler Victor of Tunnuna writes that Reparatus would not condemn the Three Chapters, and died in exile at Euchaida. Garnier takes the journey in Liberatus's preface to be this exile, which Liberatus shared until his bishop died.

At the close he writes that bishops who agreed to condemn them were rewarded with gifts, and those who refused were deposed and exiled. Some hid in flight and found a happy end in their hardship. All this is known to everyone, he says, so he thinks he should now be silent.

## longinus-bishop-of-the-nobadae

Longinus, bishop of the Nobadae

For three years Longinus was watched in Constantinople. At last, John of Ephesus writes, "he disguised himself, and put a wig on his head,—for he was very bald ;—and taking with him two servants, he fled."

Longinus was a bishop of the party that refused the council of Chalcedon. He was sent to the Nobadae, on the eastern borders of the Thebais beyond Egypt, whom the presbyter Julian had first taught. He was consecrated as soon as the patriarch Theodosius of Alexandria died. John says the empress Theodora had ordered it; his chapter heading says Theodosius did. Almost all of this comes from John, of his own party.

Among the Nobadae, Longinus built a church, ordained clergy and taught them the order of worship. Their envoy came to the emperor's court. There, John says, "in the presence of myself", he praised Longinus: "Though we were Christians in name, yet we did not really know what Christianity was until Longinus came to us."

After about five years he was called north, to help make a patriarch for his party at Alexandria. Back among the Nobadae, he went on to the Alodaei, a powerful people many score leagues beyond them. The Alexandrians sent word that he was a heretic and deposed. "We will receive him who baptized the Nobadæ," the Alodaei answered, "and by him will we be baptized."

John gives part of a letter Longinus wrote from that land. On the road, Longinus says, they all fell ill, and "we lost no less than seventeen camels." Then the king "came out in person to meet us, and received us with great joy." He baptized the king and his nobles. "For I desire neither silver, nor gold, nor dresses, as God is my witness," he writes.

John dates these things to a year R. Payne Smith, his translator, reckons as 580. No source tells of Longinus after that, or of his death.

## mar-aba-i

Mar Aba I

Mar Aba and his bishops came into the district of Kashkar. Two men there had each claimed to be its bishop in the years of quarrel. At the town of Zizwarda, the record of his synod says, the people of Kashkar agreed and chose one bishop, Samuel. The two rivals lost their titles and gladly served under Samuel as priests.

Mar Aba was catholicos, head of the Church of the East in the Persian empire. The French scholar J.-B. Chabot dates his election to January 540, from the synod's own dates.

Mari ibn Sulayman, who wrote his church's history in the 1100s, says Aba had been a magus, a follower of the old Persian religion. Mari tells his conversion as a wonder. Aba refused a Christian scholar named Joseph a place in his boat on the Tigris. Each time he refused, winds held the boat back, until he took Joseph aboard.

A Life of Mar Aba survives, which Chabot says needs careful critical study. It has him baptized in the village of Akda, a student at Nisibis, a learner of Greek at Edessa, and later a teacher.

Before him, his church had two rival patriarchs, Narses and Elisha. Mari says each ordained his own bishops, so that two altars stood in every church. The synod's record opens by saying that split had wholly ended. A letter in Mar Aba's name calls marriage a bond of love in the chaste and lawful union of a man and a woman. It forbids marriage to a father's wife and other close kin, as the magi did.

The magi turned against him, Mari says, because he had been one of them and changed their customs. He spent seven years in prison in Adorbigan, Mari writes, and kept caring for his flock. He told the king, Mari says, to kill him if the king wanted him dead.

How he died is told two ways. The Life has him regain the king's favor and die of illness, as head of his church. Bar Hebraeus, of the rival Syriac Orthodox church, says the king chained him when he returned to Seleucia, and he died in prison. Chabot gives the year as 552.

The Life has him buried in the monastery of Seleucia. Mari says his disciple Qyura carried his body to Hira and built a monastery over the grave. Chabot notes that his church honors him as a saint.

## marcellinus-comes

Marcellinus Comes

On a Sunday in Constantinople, two men named Marinus and Plato stood in a church pulpit and changed the hymn of the Trinity. Marcellinus Comes says they did it at the order of the emperor Anastasius. Many of those he calls orthodox kept singing the old words and shouted the new singers down. They were struck down in the church, he writes, and taken to prison, where they died.

Marcellinus tells what came next in his Latin chronicle. More were killed the next day in the court of the church of Saint Theodore. Then crowds filled the forum of Constantine. They threw down the emperor's statues and called for a man named Areobindus to rule. On the third day Anastasius sent them home with what Marcellinus calls his usual false oaths. He promised them everything and did nothing. Theodor Mommsen, who edited the chronicle, puts this in 512.

Marcellinus wrote at Constantinople, but Latin was his own tongue, Mommsen says. Cassiodorus, a Latin writer of the same age, calls him Marcellinus of Illyricum. He says Marcellinus is reported to have served Justinian as a court clerk before Justinian became emperor.

In his preface Marcellinus calls himself a count and a man of high rank. He says he followed the Eastern empire only. His book carries on the chronicle of Eusebius of Caesarea and Jerome. Mommsen dates it from 379 to 518. Later Marcellinus added sixteen more years, up to 534. He calls it "meum rusticum opus", my rustic work.

Mommsen says hatred of heretics breathes from every page, above all against the followers of Nestorius. He also notes the writer's warm support for Justinian. One of the two families of manuscripts runs on to 548. Mommsen judges that part to be by another hand. Cassiodorus does not know it, and the preface does not mention it.

In his guide to Christian reading, Cassiodorus names Marcellinus right after Eusebius and Jerome among the writers of chronicles.

## marius-of-avenches

Marius of Avenches

In the year Theodor Mommsen counts as 563, Marius of Avenches writes, the mountain of Tauredunum in the Valais suddenly fell. It buried a nearby fort and villages with everyone in them, and shook the whole lake until the water broke over both shores. The flood swept away old villages with their people and cattle. It carried off the bridge at Geneva with its mills and men, then burst into the city and killed many.

Marius was bishop of Avenches, in the Burgundian lands, in the later 500s. His Chronicle carries on that of Prosper of Aquitaine from 455 to 581.

At the council of Mâcon, in King Guntram's twenty-fourth year, Marius signed as bishop of the church of Avenches. His epitaph comes down through Conon, provost of Lausanne, writing in 1235. It says he was of noble birth and a cleric from his early years. He made sacred vessels for the church and worked his lands with his own hands, it says. He fed the poor and ate sparingly himself.

From the epitaph and a church Marius dedicated, Mommsen reckons that he became bishop in 574 and died in 594. Conon gives 581 for his becoming bishop, and a later hand in the annals of Lausanne puts his death in 601. Mommsen rejects both. Andrea Gallandi, after Pierre-François Chifflet, gives his death as 596.

The bishop's seat later moved from Avenches to Lausanne. Mommsen thinks this came after Marius. Gallandi reports Charles Le Cointe's view that Marius moved it himself.

Marius wrote most about Agaune and the Valais, Mommsen says. He records that King Sigismund of Burgundy built the monastery at Agaune, and that its monks tried one night to kill their bishop, Agricola. A single manuscript keeps the Chronicle, which Mommsen says went unused in the Middle Ages.

A Lausanne cathedral book of the dead from about 1510 marked his day, Mommsen notes, with a procession to the church of Saint Marius.
