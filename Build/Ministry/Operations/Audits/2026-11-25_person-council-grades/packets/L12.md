# Packet L12: person and council stories (census longDescription)

## bonaventure

Bonaventure

Glassberger writes that the chapter at Rome elected Bonaventure minister general while he was away. He gives the year as 1256. The Quaracchi editors say that is the old count, and that by ours it was 1257. In 1273 Pope Gregory X named him a cardinal and bishop of Albano. The editors say he then went with the pope to Lyon for the council. Glassberger says he fell ill during the council and died there. The friars buried him in their church at Lyon.

In the prologue to his Life of Francis, Bonaventure says the urging of the Chapter General moved him to write it. He says that as a child he was snatched from death by calling on Francis, and by Francis's merits. To learn the true story, he went to the place of Francis's birth and talked with friends of his who were still alive. Salter's note says he wrote it between 1260 and 1263, and meant it to replace earlier Lives. She adds that another general chapter ordered the earlier Lives suppressed.

He did not found the movement. The third chapter of his Life treats the founding of Francis's Religion. In the prologue to the Itinerarium, he writes that he withdrew to Mount Alverna, near the day of Francis's death, to seek peace of spirit in a quiet place. There Francis had seen the vision of the seraph. The Quaracchi editors date that visit to 1259.

## francis-of-assisi

Francis of Assisi

"The Lord gave me, Brother Francis, to begin to do penance in this way. When I was in sin, it seemed too bitter to me to see lepers. And the Lord himself led me among them, and I showed mercy to them. And as I left them, what had seemed bitter to me was turned into sweetness of soul and body."

So Francis of Assisi remembered it in the testament he dictated in 1226: his parting words to the brothers. To do penance, for him, was to turn his life to God. Soon after, he says, he left the world: his old life of family, trade and money.

A trader's son from Assisi, in central Italy, he gave up his family's wealth in his early twenties, served the lepers, and took to the roads to preach. The brothers who joined him were the Friars Minor, the lesser brothers. They were to own nothing, work with their hands, and beg when no wage came. When a cardinal urged him to become a monk or a hermit instead, he refused.

He was born around 1182. Thomas of Celano, a friar, wrote his Life within three years of his death, for the pope who made him a saint. He says his father dragged him home and used blows and chains to bend him. Then his father brought him before the bishop to give up all he had. Celano says Francis took off every piece of clothing, handed it back, and stood naked, and the bishop wrapped him in his own cloak. Bonaventure, who wrote the order's official Life a generation later, calls it giving up his claim to his father's inheritance.

After that, Celano writes, he lived among the lepers and washed their sores. At the Portiuncula, a little church of St Mary near Assisi, he heard the Gospel where Christ sends out his disciples with no money, no bag and no shoes. He put off his shoes and took to the roads.

With eleven brothers he went to Rome, and Pope Innocent III, the Rule's own opening says, confirmed their short written rule of life in 1209 or 1210.

A bishop at the papal court wrote in 1216 that the brothers went into towns by day and lonely places by night. In 1223 the next pope confirmed a shorter Rule. It said the brothers should go for alms, gifts of food or help begged from door to door. And it said what owning nothing meant: "Let the brothers make nothing their own, not a house, not a place, not anything."

Ill at San Damiano, he made a song of praise in his own Italian, the Canticle of Brother Sun.

Then he dictated the Testament. No brother was to ask Rome for letters of privilege, which grant special rights. Some brothers might not say the daily prayers set by the Rule, or might not be Catholics. They were to be held day and night like prisoners and brought to the cardinal who protected the order. And the Rule and his words were to be read simply, without notes saying how they ought to be understood.

Four years after his death, the order's leaders asked the pope whether the Testament bound them. Pope Gregory IX, that same cardinal, wrote that Francis had meant well, but could not bind them without their leaders' consent. They were not bound. For a century his brothers fought over the Rule's poverty. In 1323 the order's lawyer stood before the pope at Avignon and quoted its words: nothing of their own, neither house nor place. The sisters of San Damiano kept his last wish, that they live always in poverty, in their own Rule.

A later book, said to gather his companions' memories, says a physician told Francis he would die. "Welcome, my Sister Death," he said, and had two companions sing his song, to which, it says, he added a verse.

"Be praised, my Lord, for our Sister Bodily Death, from whom no one living can escape."

He died in October 1226 at the Portiuncula, the little church, Celano says.

## gregory-vii

Gregory VII

Gregory VII was pope from 1073 to 1085. In his prayer to Peter, he says the Roman church dragged him to its helm against his will. He did not take the seat as a prize, he says. He would rather have ended his life as a pilgrim than seize Peter's place by worldly cunning, for the glory of the world.

A list of papal claims, the Dictatus Papae, is entered in his Register for 1075. One says the pope may depose emperors. Another says no one may judge him. Mirbt notes Sackur's view that Cardinal Deusdedit wrote it. In 1076, at the Roman synod of Lent, Gregory took the rule of the German and Italian kingdoms from Henry IV. He freed all Christians from the oaths they had sworn, or would swear, to the king and bound Henry with anathema.

In January 1077 Henry did penance at Canossa. Gregory's own report says the abbot of Cluny, Hugh, helped confirm the king's sworn promises. The wider story of the reform is told in the Latin papal church's entry.

## innocent-iii

Innocent III

Innocent III became pope in 1198. In October of that year he wrote to Acerbus. God, the maker of all things, set two great lights in the sky, he says. The greater rules the day and the lesser the night. In the same way he set two great offices in the church. The greater rules souls, as if they were days. The lesser rules bodies, as if they were nights. These are the papal authority and the royal power.

The moon takes its light from the sun, he goes on, and is truly less than it in size and quality, in place and in effect. In the same way, royal power takes the splendor of its rank from the papal authority.

Francis of Assisi's first Rule says Francis asked Innocent to grant and confirm his way of life. It says the pope did so for Francis and his brothers. In 1205 Innocent wrote that the church of Constantinople had lately come back to the unity of the Roman see. In 1215 he held the Fourth Lateran Council. Its rules ran from the meaning of the eucharist to yearly confession.

## thomas-aquinas

Thomas Aquinas

"It seems that God does not exist. For if one of two opposites were without limit, the other would be wholly destroyed. But by the name God we mean a good without limit. So if God existed, no evil would be found. But evil is found in the world. So God does not exist."

So begins one question in the Summa of Theology of Thomas Aquinas, his summary of the whole faith, where each question sets out the case against, then answers it. He answered this argument in Augustine's words. A God supremely good, Augustine wrote, would let evil into his works only if he were so powerful and good that he could bring good even out of it.

Thomas was one of the Dominicans, the Order of Preachers, poor traveling friars trained in theology to preach, and he taught theology at Paris in the thirteenth century. In the Summa he taught that faith and reason cannot truly conflict, because nothing true can be proved false. The church declared him a saint in 1323.

He was born around 1225 to the counts of Aquino, between Rome and Naples. He studied at Naples, and there he joined the Dominicans.

On the road toward Paris, his kin seized him and shut him in a castle. The order's own book of its friars, written in his lifetime, has a story the editors take to be his. His kinsmen held him about a year, it says, and tried every means to make him leave, but could not make him put off the habit, the friars' robe. William of Tocco, a friar who pressed for him to be declared a saint, wrote decades later that his brothers did it at his mother's order. They tore his habit, he says, and sent a woman to tempt him. A royal official who had known him at Naples told the inquiry of 1319 it was his father, for more than a year. In the end, they let him go.

The order sent him to study under Albert the Great at Cologne, and he became a master of theology at Paris, a full teacher in the university. He wrote the Summa, he said, to teach beginners in good order. His rule was this: "Faith rests on a truth that cannot fail. And the opposite of a truth can never be proved. So it is clear that the proofs brought against the faith are not true proofs. They are arguments that can be answered." He also wrote there that heretics who would not turn back could be left to the rulers to be put to death. Corrupting the faith, he said, was worse than forging money.

By the royal official's sworn account, around the feast of Saint Nicholas in December 1273, after Mass at Naples, Thomas wrote and dictated nothing more. His companion Reginald begged him to go on. "Reginald, I cannot. For everything I have written seems to me straw." To Reginald alone he added that it was straw beside what had been shown to him. What he saw, no source says. Reginald told this on his deathbed to another friar, who told the official. Tocco tells it differently, at Thomas's sister's castle, and has him say that what he had written seemed small.

The pope called him to a council of the whole church, at Lyon. Thomas fell ill on the way, and died at the monastery of Fossanova on 7 March 1274.

Within forty years, his own order bound its teachers to teach by his teaching and works. Tocco reports that Pope John XXII said Thomas had lit up the church more than all the other teachers. A pope later ordered his feast kept like those of the four great Doctors, the church's chief teachers. And the English reformer William Tyndale complained that preachers quarreled, "one followeth Duns, another St Thomas." Duns Scotus was a Franciscan teacher.

The Summa still breaks off, in its third part, in its questions on penance, the sacrament of confession and forgiveness, where he stopped.

## catherine-of-siena

Catherine of Siena

Avignon, the summer of 1376. Catherine of Siena stands before Pope Gregory XI, and her confessor, Raymond of Capua, the priest who hears her confessions and guides her, interprets. When the pope asks how she can know the ways of his court after a few days, Raymond records her answer: "To the honor of almighty God, I dare to say it. While I was still in the city where I was born, I smelled the stench of the sins committed in the papal court. I smelled it more than the men who have committed them, and commit them every day, smell it themselves." The pope, Raymond says, said nothing.

Catherine was a dyer's daughter who never entered a convent. She gathered a circle of followers and dictated letters to popes, cities and rulers. For nearly seventy years the popes had kept their court at Avignon, in southern France, and she pressed Gregory to take it back to Rome. That September he left. How far she moved him is disputed.

Pressed by her family to marry, she sought the habit of the Sisters of Penance of St Dominic. They were laywomen who wore the dress of the Dominican order but lived in their own homes. She did not write her letters but spoke them aloud, and young men of her circle wrote them down, two at once, Raymond says. Raymond saw crowds come down from the hills to hear her.

By 1375 she was writing to Gregory to uproot the bad priests, come back to Rome, and raise a crusade, a holy war against the Muslim powers. She called the pope's governors in Italy "incarnate demons." Florence rose against them, and the pope answered with harsh sentences and then war. Before going herself, she sent Raymond with a letter: "I tell you: come, come, come. Do not wait for time, because time does not wait for you."

Florence sent her to Avignon to make peace. Raymond heard the pope put it in her hands, but Florence's envoys would not deal with her, and the peace failed. The pope left that September and reached Rome the following January.

Did she bring him back? Raymond, writing about ten years after her death to show her holiness, says the pope decided to return with her leading him. Her own letters tell it differently. In 1375 she was already urging him to finish a return he had resolved on, and at Avignon she wrote that he had "decided on your return and announced it." A German chronicler of the next generation credited revelations that came to a Swedish visionary in Rome and reached the pope through a hermit. A later biographer of the popes said a bishop, rebuked by the pope for living away from his own diocese, asked why the pope did not go back to his own church. The answer, the biographer says, hastened the return.

Gregory died in March 1378. Back in Siena that year, she dictated a book called the Dialogue, in which her soul puts four requests to God and God answers. Her secretaries say she spoke it while lost to her senses. One of them wrote: "To hear it told, this seems a thing past believing. But to those who wrote it down and heard it, it does not seem so. And I am one of them."

That September the cardinals declared the new pope, Urban VI, wrongly elected and chose a rival, so two men claimed to be pope. Urban called her to Rome, and she dictated letters for him. Her secretaries put the Dialogue into Latin, and it was copied, printed in Italian by 1472, and put into English in 1519. Raymond wrote her Life, and about eighty years after her death Pope Pius II declared her a saint.

She died in Rome at the end of April 1380, with the split between the two popes unhealed. A secretary who was with her wrote that she cried out for the blood again and again. Then, as Christ did on the cross, she gave her soul into her Father's hands, bowed her head and died.

## gregory-palamas

Gregory Palamas

John Kantakouzenos, later emperor and an ally of Gregory Palamas, tells how a learned monk named Barlaam, from the south of Italy, went to a simple monk at Thessalonica. The monk was a hesychast, from the Greek word for stillness: one who prayed in silence. He heard that those who pray in quiet feel a joy beyond words, and see a light shining around them with their bodily eyes. Then, Kantakouzenos writes, Barlaam turned on them all, calling them deceivers and "navel-souls."

One of those monks, Gregory Palamas, answered him. Palamas lived on Mount Athos, a peninsula of monasteries in northern Greece. He taught that no one can share in what God is, his essence. Yet God gives himself truly in his energy, the way he acts. That energy is uncreated: not made, but God's own. So a monk joined to God in prayer is joined to God himself.

Raised at the imperial court, Kantakouzenos says, Palamas left his property and the emperor's favor while very young, and lived in the great monastery of Athanasios.

Philotheos says in a speech of praise that the monks of Thessalonica begged him to come down from the Mountain. But Kantakouzenos says he was already in the city, ill. Then he wrote his answer. Barlaam's sneer was that the monks thought the soul sat in the navel. Palamas wrote: "Those who call them navel-souls are, to begin with, plainly slandering the men they accuse. For which of them ever says that the soul is in the navel?"

The great teachers of the early church, the Fathers, had said two things about God. Palamas wrote: "So when you hear the Fathers say God's essence cannot be shared, understand the essence that does not go out and is not shown. When they say again it can be shared, understand the going-out that belongs to God by nature, his showing and his energy. Holding to both in this way, you will agree with the Fathers."

Barlaam then charged the monks with believing in two gods, and brought the case to the patriarch, the bishop of Constantinople. At a council of bishops there in 1341, Palamas made the defense. The council found the monks above Barlaam's charges. Barlaam asked pardon, and was warned he would be cut off from the Church unless he truly repented. The records of this council and the two that followed are the winning side's.

Then came civil war. The empress, ruling for her young son, fought Kantakouzenos, with the patriarch beside her. Palamas was condemned and held for years, at the end in a prison inside the palace. Three witnesses give three reasons. The patriarch wrote that Palamas defied the council and would not show his writings. Kantakouzenos says the patriarch envied him, and punished him for refusing to condemn Kantakouzenos. The historian Nikephoros Gregoras, who stood against Palamas, says the empress's letters gave his teaching as the reason, not politics.

In 1347, the empress had Palamas brought out as the war ended. A council deposed the patriarch and declared that Palamas spoke and thought in line with the church's theologians.

In 1351 Kantakouzenos, now emperor, called another council, and Palamas debated his opponents, Gregoras among them. It ruled for Palamas. Gregoras wrote that Palamas was "in labor with the teachings of many gods, and at the same time of no god." On the night it ended, he says, the bishops rose in anger to lay hands on his side.

The councils made Palamas's defense of silent prayer the teaching of the Orthodox Church. An editor of his sermons writes that a list read aloud in churches on the first Sunday of Lent acclaims his name every year. The next Sunday, he adds, keeps his memory. In the eighteenth century, the Philokalia, a Greek book of the old teachers of prayer, gathered his writings.

Philotheos says that as Palamas lay dying, he kept whispering one phrase: "The heavenly to the heavenly." It showed, Philotheos says, where he was going. He was sixty-three.

## jan-hus

Jan Hus

In October 1414, Sigismund, king of Hungary and the elected head of the empire, wrote a safe-conduct, a royal letter of protection, for a priest from Prague. It ordered everyone to let Jan Hus "pass, stay, remain and return freely." He had already set out for Constance, where a council of the bishops and teachers of the Western church was to meet.

Hus, a master, or teacher, at Prague's university, preached to the city in Czech. He read the books of John Wyclif, an English scholar dead since 1384, whose books the church had condemned, and he would not condemn him. At Constance he refused to swear that he had taught what he said he never taught, and on 6 July 1415 he was burned. His death turned a university quarrel into a nation's movement.

In 1402 he became preacher at the Bethlehem Chapel, built so that preachers in Czech need no longer wander through houses and hidden corners.

In 1403 the university forbade anyone to teach Wyclif's articles, and Hus protested. In 1410 the archbishop of Prague burned Wyclif's books and cut Hus off from the church's sacraments. Hus wrote that he hoped Wyclif was among the saved. In 1412 he spoke against papal pardons said to cancel the punishment for sin. His ban was made heavier. No one was to give him food or drink or speak to him, and the city was put under a ban that stopped Mass, sermons and church burials. He appealed from the pope to Christ, and in October he left Prague. In exile he wrote his book on the Church: Christ alone is its head. Much of it, his translator writes, comes from Wyclif's own book.

At Constance, Hus was arrested within weeks. A papal official at the council wrote that he had gone on teaching Wyclif's doctrine at his lodging after he was warned to stop. Most of what followed comes from Peter of Mladoňovice, a Czech in Hus's party and his friend. Ulrich Richental, a citizen of Constance who watched from the other side, wrote that the learned told the king no heretic could hold a safe-conduct, and he let it be.

At his hearing on 7 June, Hus was charged with wishing his soul were with Wyclif's. He answered: "I do not know where John Wyclif's soul is. I hope that he is saved, and I fear that he may be damned. Yet in hope I would wish my soul to be where John Wyclif's soul is." The council laughed. The king told him, Peter writes, that he would burn a stubborn heretic with his own hands. From prison Hus defended the cup, the wine at communion, which only priests had long drunk and the council had just forbidden to lay people.

On 6 July the council condemned him. Urged to abjure, to give up publicly the teachings charged against him, he said he feared to lie before God. He was stripped of his priesthood and crowned with a paper hat painted with devils. The council handed him to the king's officers, and at the king's command the city's executioners burned him. Asked at the stake once more to recant, he answered: "God is my witness that I never taught or preached the things falsely charged to me, laid on me by false witnesses. The chief aim of my preaching, and of all my other acts and writings, was only to turn people from sin. I wrote, taught and preached the truth of the gospel from the words and teachings of the holy teachers. In that truth I will gladly die today." He sang a prayer, Peter writes; Richental writes that he cried out badly. The executioners broke up his bones and roasted his heart on a stick, Peter writes, and his ashes were thrown into the Rhine.

Within two months, the lords and knights of Bohemia and Moravia set 452 seals on a letter to the council: "Master John Hus was truly a good, just and catholic man. For many years he lived among us in our kingdom in a way worthy of praise, proven in his life, his conduct and his good name." By catholic they meant true to the church's faith. They vowed to defend the law of Christ and its preachers, whatever it cost them. From his death grew the Hussite and Bohemian Brethren movement, and the cup became its emblem.

The king's letter had promised that Hus could "return freely." In 1520, Martin Luther wrote that Hus was burned against a sworn safe-conduct, and that he had no wish to make a saint or a martyr of him.

## john-wyclif

John Wyclif

In fourteenth-century England, friars said it was heresy to write God's law in English for lay people, everyone who was not clergy. An English tract that goes under John Wyclif's name answers them: "So Christ and his apostles taught the people in the tongue the people knew best. Why should people not do the same now?"

For him, God's law belonged to everyone, in their own tongue, and Scripture was the test of the church's wealth and power. His circle made the first whole Bible in English. A hostile Leicester chronicler of his own day says Wyclif put the Gospel into English himself, for lay people and women who could read. In the chronicler's words, "And so the Gospel's pearl is scattered and trampled by pigs." The Bible's editors of 1850 thought the New Testament was probably his own work, but a later scholar found no real proof, and thought his students made it at his wish.

He taught at Oxford, and from 1374 was priest of Lutterworth, in the English Midlands. He wanted the clergy as poor as the apostles and told the pope to leave worldly lordship to the secular power.

The church taught that at the priest's words the bread of the Mass changed in substance, leaving only its appearance. In 1381 Wyclif held that Christ's body truly is that bread, yet it is still bread. Oxford's ban on his teaching was read out while he sat in the master's chair, arguing the opposite.

In 1382 a London council condemned twenty-four of his conclusions as an earthquake shook the city. Banished from Oxford, he went on writing at Lutterworth until he died there after a stroke, at the end of 1384.

His followers, called Lollards in scorn, carried it on. About 1408 Archbishop Arundel forbade anyone to translate Scripture into English, or read a translation from Wyclif's time, without a bishop's leave. The Bible was copied by hand, not yet printed, and scarce, so a family might own a single gospel. In 1521 the bishop's records show John Grosar examined for an English gospel book, passed to him by a priest, which he lent on. At Iver, old Durdant sent a boy outside, so he could not hear and tell, before reciting passages of Paul and the Gospels to his family.

Long after, in 1526, William Tyndale took up the same work from the Greek and printed it, writing that he had no one's English to follow.

Earlier, in 1415, the Council of Constance declared Wyclif a heretic and ordered his bones dug up. Jan Hus of Prague had built his own book on the Church from Wyclif's. He told that council: "Yet in hope I would wish my soul to be where John Wyclif's soul is."

## thomas-a-kempis

Thomas à Kempis

"Whoever follows me does not walk in darkness," says the Lord. So opens the Imitation of Christ, a fifteenth-century book that says these words call us to imitate Jesus's life and ways. It puts a changed heart above argument: "I would rather feel sorrow for sin than know how to define it." And it turns inward: "Learn to scorn outward things and to give yourself to inward things. And you will see the kingdom of God come within you."

The book is credited to Thomas à Kempis, a canon who spent almost all his life in one small house, Mount St Agnes, near Zwolle. There he copied books, among them the house's Bible, and wrote its Chronicle and the Lives of the New Devotion's founders. Johannes Busch, a canon of his own order and time, names him as the book's author. Early copies named John Gerson, a Paris theologian, and scholars still disagree.

The book calls the cross the "royal way", the only road to life and inner peace. That way is dying to self each day: denying oneself, and willingly bearing the pain of body or trouble of spirit that comes.

Born about 1380 in Kempen, near Cologne, Thomas went to school at Deventer. Florens Radewijns, who led the Brothers of the Common Life there, took him in, placed him in the school, gave him books and found him lodging. He lived among the Brothers, who shared all they had and spent their days copying books. In 1399 he came to Mount St Agnes and asked to stay, and there he became a canon, living in community under the Rule of St Augustine.

When a dispute over the bishop of Utrecht brought a pope's ban on church services, the canons kept it. In 1429, for keeping it, they were insulted and driven out, and the community spent three years in Frisia. Thomas died in 1471, in his ninety-second year.

The book's call crossed the lines that later divided the church. Ignatius of Loyola, founder of the Jesuits, knew it as "the little Gerson". He told a fellow Jesuit that after finding it he wanted no other book of devotion, and read one chapter a day. Two centuries later, reading it, the young John Wesley "began to see that true religion was seated in the heart". His Methodist Conference advised its preachers to read it, among other books, between four and five in the morning. The Quaker John Woolman, though told Thomas was a Roman Catholic, judged him "a man of a true Christian spirit".

## hugo-grotius

Hugo Grotius

Grotius was a jurist on the Remonstrant side. In 1617 he published at Leiden a treatise on theology.

It answers Faustus Socinus, who denied that Christ's death satisfied divine justice. The treatise opens by promising to state first the doctrine which the church of Christ has defended 'with unshaken faith', as drawn from Scripture. It then sets that doctrine beside the view of Socinus. The Library holds it only in Foster's English.

Dort condemned the Remonstrants. Foster, who put the book into English, says Grotius shared the ban laid on his fellow Arminians. He adds that the English Arminians gave Grotius a kinder reception.

## huldrych-zwingli

Huldrych Zwingli

Zurich, January 1523. More than six hundred priests and citizens crowd the hall of the city council. When the bishop's spokesman wants the universities to judge, the preacher, Huldrych Zwingli, answers him.

"I say we have judges here who cannot err and take no side: the Holy Scriptures, which can neither lie nor deceive. We have them here in Hebrew, Greek and Latin. Let both sides take them as fair and just judges."

Since 1519 Zwingli had preached the Bible book by book as people's priest at the Great Minster, the city's main church. He had asked to defend that preaching in public. The council, the two hundred citizens who made Zurich's laws, ruled that every preacher must preach only what he could defend from Scripture. Within two years Zurich removed its images and ended the Mass, the Latin service at the altar. The Reformed churches, the family that grew from Zurich and Geneva, look to him as a founder.

The council that judged by Scripture also used force. Its summons had warned that anyone who opposed it without Scripture would be dealt with more severely, and any preacher who disobeyed its ruling would be restrained. A city council, not a bishop, now decided what its church would do.

Some Zurich men, historians say, pushed the same test further. They would admit nothing without clear warrant in Scripture, and found none for baptizing infants, so they baptized adults again. They were called Anabaptists, or rebaptizers. On March 7, 1526, the council decreed that anyone who baptized another would be drowned without mercy. Zwingli wrote that same day to a friend that patience had at last broken out. He was grieved by their stubborn daring, and wished the new faith had not begun with such examples. But, he went on, it pleases God to guard in this way against evils to come. On January 5, 1527, by the council's sentence, one of their leaders, Felix Manz, was bound and thrown into the water to die.

In May 1529 Zwingli urged Bern, Zurich's ally, to war on the five Catholic cantons, or states, of central Switzerland.

"This peace that some press for so hard is war, not peace. And the war we press for is peace, not war. For we thirst for no one's blood, nor will we drink it in the turmoil. Our aim is to cut the sinews of the rule of the few."

That autumn the prince of Hesse brought Zwingli and Martin Luther to Marburg to settle communion, the shared bread and wine. Luther held that Christ's own body is truly in the bread; Zwingli answered that a body is in one place, and Christ's is in heaven. They signed fifteen articles together, and the last one says where they stopped.

"We have not agreed at this time whether Christ's true body and blood are bodily in the bread and wine. Yet each side shall show the other Christian love, as far as each conscience can bear. And both sides shall pray earnestly to Almighty God to confirm us, by his Spirit, in the right understanding. Amen."

In 1531 Zurich and Bern cut off the cantons' food. The cantons marched, and on October 11 beat Zurich's army at Kappel. His friend Myconius, writing the next year, says Zwingli went as the army's chaplain, though he had said he would not go. He died on the field.

In 1549 the pastors of Zurich signed one agreement on communion with John Calvin of Geneva, who never met him. In 1554 Calvin wrote that if Zwingli were alive, he would not change a word of it.

In March 1532 the records of the Great Minster name Heinrich Bullinger over its teaching in Zwingli's place; he led Zurich's church for four decades. Its overseers gave the house of "Master Ulrich, of blessed memory", as the record calls Zwingli, to the new teacher of the Bible lesson. He agreed to share it with Zwingli's children until the widow found another home.

## ignatius-of-loyola

Ignatius of Loyola

Ignatius of Loyola was born in 1491 at the castle of Loyola, and grew up a page and then a soldier. At the siege of Pamplona a cannon ball broke one of his legs. In his slow recovery he read the lives of Christ and the saints, because the house had no other books.

He set out as a pilgrim. At Manresa he lived on the alms he begged each day. He reached Jerusalem, but he could not stay there. So he went back to school, to be better fitted to help souls.

At Paris he took his master's degree in March 1534. Francis Xavier and Peter Faber were his fellow students there. He and his companions meant to spend their lives in Jerusalem. If they could not, they would offer themselves to the pope.

No ship could take them, so they went to Rome. In 1540 Pope Paul III approved their Society. From Rome Ignatius sent out letters and instructions, among them orders for his men at the Council of Trent in 1546. He died on 31 July 1556.

## john-calvin

John Calvin

In August 1536 a young French scholar, already the author of a book that defended his persecuted fellow believers, stopped at Geneva for one night, on his way to Strasbourg. The city's preacher, William Farel, begged him to stay, then did something more frightening. The scholar told it himself, twenty years later: "When he saw that asking got him nowhere, he went as far as a curse. He called on God to curse my quiet life if I held back from helping in so great a need. Struck by that terror, I gave up the journey I had begun."

The scholar was John Calvin. His book was the Institutes, a book of instruction in the Christian faith. He stayed, and gave Geneva a written plan for its church, with four kinds of office. Churches in France and Scotland built theirs the same way, and his name became the label for a family of churches begun at Zurich before him.

Born in France in 1509, he says he left it to hide in some quiet corner. But believers burned there were being slandered as rebels, so he wrote a short handbook to the French king to clear their name.

In 1538 Geneva's citizens voted him and Farel out for defying a ban on their preaching. In 1540 Geneva's councils wrote begging him back. He went back in 1541, but would take up his post only once the city swore to keep a catechism, a book of questions and answers on the faith. It also swore to keep a church discipline, a system for correcting members' lives.

His rules set pastors to preach, baptize and give communion, the shared bread and wine of worship. Teachers taught sound teaching, and deacons cared for the poor and sick. Twelve elders, laymen from the city's councils, watched over people's lives and met weekly with the pastors as the consistory, a church court that questioned and warned. But the councils kept the power to punish. Who could bar people from communion stayed in dispute between them until 1555. In 1547, while that dispute went on, the city beheaded a man for speaking against God, for acts against the city's government, and for threatening the pastors.

By then the much larger Institutes taught that God chooses beforehand who will be saved. In Calvin's words, "Some are ordained beforehand to eternal life, others to eternal damnation," to life with God, or to being lost forever. In 1551, as Geneva's pastors recorded it, a doctor of medicine, Jérôme Bolsec, rose at their weekly Bible meeting. He said this made God a tyrant. He was jailed, and when Geneva asked other Swiss churches, Bern's pastors wrote that some said he was not so bad a man, and hoped for agreement. Geneva still sent him away for good.

In 1553 the city council burned Michael Servetus alive for books against infant baptism and against the teaching that God is three persons in one. It had first asked the advice of four Swiss churches. Calvin had written that if Servetus came, he would not leave alive, and during the trial he hoped for a death sentence, though a less cruel one.

Others built the same offices with no friendly city behind them. In 1559, when going to Geneva could mean death, French churches met secretly to adopt a statement of belief and a rulebook with pastors, elders and deacons. In 1562 Scotland's church ruled to give baptism and communion, marry and bury as Geneva's church did.

Gravely ill in 1564, Calvin spoke to the city's pastors, and one of them wrote it down: "I have lived here amid extraordinary battles. In mockery, I was greeted in the evening outside my door with fifty or sixty gunshots. Think how that could shake a poor, timid scholar, as I am and always have been, I confess." He died that May.

## martin-luther

Martin Luther

On 31 October 1517, a friar, living under religious vows, wrote to the archbishop of Mainz, a great prince of the church. Pardons, letters said to cancel punishment for sin, were being sold in the archbishop's name for the pope's new church in Rome. People believed, he warned, that a pardon made them sure of heaven.

The friar was Martin Luther, who taught at Wittenberg, a university town in eastern Germany. He taught that forgiveness is grace, God's free gift, received by faith, by trusting what God promises, so it could not be bought. Condemned by the pope, he would not take back his books before the emperor, ruler of the German lands and more, unless Scripture, the Bible, or clear reason proved him wrong. Made an outlaw, whom anyone could seize, he was hidden away and put the New Testament, the gospels and letters of the early church, into German. The Lutheran churches, grown from his teaching, carry his name.

With his letter he sent theses, short statements for a university debate. One put his teaching in a line: "Every true Christian, living or dead, has a share in all the good things of Christ and the church. God gives it to him, even without letters of pardon."

In June 1520 the pope's decree condemned his books, ordered them burned and gave him sixty days to take them back. In December 1520 he burned the popes' book of church law, and editors say the pope's decree against him burned with it. In January the pope cut him off from the church.

In April 1521, under the emperor's promise of safe passage there and back, he came to Worms. His friends printed his answer: "Unless I am convinced by the witness of the Scriptures or by clear reason … I cannot and will not take back anything." It ends, "God help me. Amen." "Here I stand, I cannot do otherwise" appears only in prints from one Wittenberg press.

In May the emperor, Charles V, made him an outlaw: no one was to house or feed him. Hidden on his friends' advice, he made that German New Testament, printed in 1522. He wrote that it must be in the simplest language, so that all could understand it. He wanted the people to sing in their own language, not only to hear trained singers. By one editor's count, four of the eight hymns in Wittenberg's first hymnbook, in 1524, were his.

In 1525 peasants across the German lands rose against their lords. Luther first warned both sides. The rulers, and above all the bishops, had only themselves to thank for the rising, he wrote, for they did nothing but rob their people. To the peasants he said that whoever takes the sword will perish by the sword. Then armed bands began robbing and burning. He urged rulers, and anyone who could, to strike them down: "So let everyone who can strike, kill and stab, in secret or in the open. Remember that nothing can be more poisonous, harmful or devilish than a rebel."

One friend wrote to him that people who favored him found it strange that he let the tyrants kill without mercy. In July he held to his call: rulers should strike quickly, guilty or innocent. But once they had won, he said, they should show mercy even to the guilty, and he condemned the "furious, raving, senseless tyrants" who could not get their fill of blood. A later, hostile chronicle of the Anabaptists, a church of believers baptized as adults, said he had leaned on worldly power.

The whole Bible followed in German by 1534, finished with his colleagues, editors say. His churches kept his songs, and two centuries later the hymnbook of Halle, a Lutheran town in Germany, still printed them.

In 1539 he looked back on putting the Bible into German. He had hoped for less writing and more reading of Scripture, so that each might "drink for himself from the fresh spring".

## teresa-of-avila

Teresa of Ávila

"I crossed this stormy sea for almost twenty years, with these falls, and rising again, and badly, for I would fall once more." So Teresa of Ávila wrote of her long struggle in prayer as a nun, in the book her confessors, the priests who guided her prayer, ordered her to write.

She was a nun of the Carmelites, an order that took its name from Mount Carmel in the Holy Land. She spent more than twenty years in a large, easy-going convent in Ávila. Then, in 1562, she founded a small, poor house of no more than thirteen nuns, living by the order's old rule. The city tried to close it. She kept founding more, for nuns and friars. They became the Discalced, or barefoot, Carmelites, still an order today.

Born in Ávila in 1515, she had entered the Incarnation, a convent outside the city, at about twenty. It was large and pleasant, she wrote, but poor. The nuns often left it to stay elsewhere, and the rule was kept as popes had relaxed it.

As her prayer changed, two advisers told her an evil spirit was deceiving her. They sent her to a priest of the Society of Jesus, the new order Ignatius of Loyola had founded. Its priests judged her prayer sound.

The Inquisition, the church's court that tried heresy in Spain, was banning devotional books in Spanish, and people warned her. She wrote: "People came to me in great fear. They told me the times were harsh, and that someone might bring a false charge against me and go to the Inquisitors. This struck me as funny, and made me laugh, for in this I was never afraid."

On 24 August 1562 the first nuns joined the house, St Joseph's. Days later the city's governor and councilors resolved that it must be broken up. Only one Dominican, Domingo Báñez, spoke for delay, though he disliked its poverty. He later became her confessor. She had been ordered back to her old convent, but the house stayed.

In the years after 1562 she wrote her life. It is the book in which most of what we know of her early years is told. If it did not agree with the Catholic faith, she told her confessors, they should "burn it at once."

From 1567 she founded house after house, seventeen for nuns by her death, her editors count. Then the Princess of Eboli, whom she had crossed, denounced the book, out of anger, a friar close to Teresa wrote. Báñez, by his own later account, carried the manuscript to the Inquisitors himself, to keep it safe. He had told her to burn it, since women's writings should not become public, and she had agreed. The Inquisition gave it back to him to judge, an editor writes. In July 1575 he reported that he found no error in its teaching, and that she was "not a deceiver." He added that only learned men should see it. She never saw the book again. That year, she wrote, the order's leaders sent her a decree to found no more houses and to stay in one.

Two years later, at Toledo, ordered to write about prayer while her reform seemed close to ruin, her editor writes, she began the Interior Castle. She wrote: "I thought of the soul as like a castle, made of a single diamond or of very clear crystal. It has many rooms, just as in heaven there are many dwelling places." God is in the innermost room, and prayer is the gate.

In 1580 the pope let her houses form a province, a branch of the order with its own head, and later an order. After her death, once the Inquisition agreed, her books were printed at Salamanca in 1588. In 1622 the pope declared her a saint.

Worn out, she reached Alba de Tormes in September 1582, and died there in early October, on the night the calendar changed. As she was dying, her editors tell, she thanked God that she was a child of the Church.

## thomas-cranmer

Thomas Cranmer

"For many years the service in this Church of England has been read to the people in Latin, which they did not understand. So they have heard with their ears only. Their hearts, spirit and mind have not been built up by it." So said the preface of the Book of Common Prayer, the new English prayer book for the whole country, in 1549. Thomas Cranmer, archbishop of Canterbury, England's senior bishop, led its making under the boy king Edward VI.

Under the Catholic queen Mary, he signed a series of papers taking back his teaching, then withdrew them in public and was burned at Oxford in 1556. John Foxe, a Protestant who wrote the lives of those burned under Mary as martyrs, says he put the hand that signed them into the fire first. After her, his book of 1552 became Queen Elizabeth's of 1559, which went on to shape worship in English.

Cranmer was a Cambridge scholar drawn into King Henry VIII's wish to end his marriage to his first queen. Made archbishop in 1533, that May he gave sentence, he wrote, that the marriage had never been lawful, because the pope had no power to allow it. In 1537 he asked the king's chief minister to win the king's leave to publish an English Bible, most of it, editors find, the work of William Tyndale. In 1538 he argued against a man named Lambert, tried over the bread and wine of communion. Lambert was condemned for teaching what the church held false, and burned. Even Foxe, Cranmer's admirer, counted that blood on his hands.

Until the prayer book, the service had been said in Latin, and each region had its own form of it. Now the whole realm would have one, in English. In 1549, rebels in Devon and Cornwall rose against it. They wanted their old Latin service back, and they called the new one "like a Christmas game," no better than a Christmas entertainment. Cranmer answered that they asked for Latin, which almost none of them understood. The rising was put down. His revised book of 1552 dropped the word Mass, the old Latin service of holy communion, and a new law required everyone to attend.

When Edward died in 1553, his sister Mary became queen. At Oxford in 1554 Cranmer was condemned for teaching what the church held false, and two fellow reforming bishops were burned there in 1555. In February 1556 he was stripped of his rank as priest and archbishop. Then, Foxe says, he was offered a choice between his life and the fire. Cranmer signed a series of papers taking back his teaching and accepting the pope's rule over the church in England. The bishop of London printed six. Why he signed, Foxe says he cannot tell.

On the rainy morning of 21 March 1556, Foxe writes, he was brought to the university church to hear a sermon before his burning. Instead of reading the text prepared for him, he renounced the papers he had signed, written, he said, for fear of death, to save his life if he could. Foxe prints his words: "My hand did wrong when it wrote against my heart. So my hand shall be punished for it first. If I come to the fire, it shall be burned first."

The bishop of London's printed book gives a speech that ends instead with the faith Cranmer had been meant to declare. Foxe gives the renunciation and the hand. Yet the bishop's own register records that just before the burning, Cranmer publicly took the papers back.

The fire was set where the other two bishops had burned. When the fire was lit, Foxe says, he stretched out his arm and held his right hand in the flame. He held it still, so that all could see it burn before his body. He kept repeating the words "unworthy right hand" as long as his voice allowed. And in the words of the first martyr, he said: "Lord Jesus, receive my spirit."

## william-tyndale

William Tyndale

In 1525, at Cologne on the Rhine, an English New Testament, the second part of the Bible, was on the press. A German churchman, an enemy of Luther, told years later how he heard the printers boasting of it over their wine and had the city council stop the press. Two Englishmen, he wrote, fled up the river with the printed sheets.

One was William Tyndale, a priest ordained to lead worship, and a scholar. He was putting the New Testament, its gospels and letters, into English straight from the Greek they were first written in, not the church's Latin. At home that needed leave from a bishop, a senior church officer, so he printed abroad and had the books smuggled in, where the king ordered them burned. He would offer that king his life for the Bible's words alone, and in 1536 he was put to death.

Only this, he wrote, moved him to translate: people outside the church's ordained ranks could hold no truth unless the Bible lay plain before their eyes in their own tongue. At Worms in 1526 he printed the Testament in thousands.

Tyndale then wrote about kings and their power, and one book taught that the king answers to God alone. The king praised it, in the story told by John Foxe, who later collected the stories of Protestants put to death. In 1530 Tyndale printed the first five books of the Bible, put into English from the Hebrew they were written in. Another book, on the bishops' dealings with kings, angered the king most, Tyndale told the king's envoy. And he called the churchmen who fought his translation hypocrites.

In 1531 that envoy met him outside Antwerp and asked him to come home. Tyndale would not come home on any promise. He asked only for the bare text of Scripture, the Bible's words without his notes. The envoy reported his answer: "Suppose the king, of his grace, grants his people only the bare text of the Scripture … in the translation of anyone he pleases. Then I will at once make a faithful promise never to write again. … I offer my body to suffer whatever pain or torture, yes, whatever death the king wills, if only this is granted."

No answer from the king is known, and within a year the king's envoys were ordered to seize him. In 1535, as Foxe tells it, an Englishman who had won his trust pointed him out to waiting officers in Antwerp. After more than a year in the castle of Vilvoorde, near Brussels, he was condemned under the law of the emperor, whose officers ruled there. He was tied to a post and strangled, and his body was burned. Foxe says he cried out, "Lord, open the king of England's eyes."

Foxe kept the readers' stories too, of people whipped, jailed and questioned for the book. John Maundrel, a Wiltshire farmer who could not read, had others read the Testament to him until he knew most of it by heart. Some readers were later burned as heretics, for teaching the church condemned as false. Maundrel himself was burned at Salisbury in 1556, when the old church was restored under Queen Mary. Smuggled in faster than bishops could burn it, the book had reached people no one had allowed to have it.

Within a year of Tyndale's death, the archbishop of Canterbury, England's senior bishop, praised a new English Bible, and the king allowed it to be bought and read. It bore another man's name, but editors find that most of it was Tyndale's.

In 1533 he had written to a friend in prison. He called God to witness that he had never changed one syllable of God's word against his conscience, and never would, for all the pleasure, honor or riches on earth.

## john-wesley

John Wesley

One May evening in 1738, John Wesley went very unwillingly to a society meeting, a weekly prayer group inside the church, in Aldersgate Street, London. As someone read Luther's preface to Paul's letter to the Romans, Wesley felt his heart warmed.

He wrote, "I felt that I did trust in Christ, in Christ alone, for salvation. And I was given an assurance that he had taken away my sins, even mine, and saved me from the law of sin and death." He meant a felt certainty of God's forgiveness, and freedom from sin's hold and from death, its end. That night, his brother wrote, he declared, "I believe."

He was a priest of the state Church of England, a minister formally set apart, or ordained. From 1739 he preached outdoors to thousands. He gathered his converts into societies, split into weekly classes of about twelve under a leader who asked how each lived. Mocked as Methodists, a nickname at first, they became the Methodist churches.

In 1739 George Whitefield called him to Bristol, a port city in the west of England, where Whitefield had begun preaching outdoors. Wesley could barely accept it, for he had thought saving souls, bringing people to faith and forgiveness, almost a sin outside a church. Yet on 2 April, in a brickyard by the city, he preached to about three thousand people.

By his own account, the bishop of Bristol, the senior churchman over that region, told him he had no business preaching in his area and advised him to go. Wesley stayed.

The class came by accident. In 1742 the Bristol society agreed to pay off a building debt at a penny a week from each member who could. To collect it, they divided into classes of about twelve, each with one collector. When the collectors told him some members were not living as they should, he saw in it the very thing they had long wanted. From then on each leader saw every member weekly, and advised, rebuked or comforted them. Some changed, and some were put out.

The work drew violence. At Wednesbury, in 1743, a mob surrounded the house where he was writing, crying, "Bring out the minister." That night a second mob dragged him through Walsall, many crying out to knock his brains out. That winter, one of his preachers reported, mobs in that district beat Methodists and plundered their houses.

His preachers were ordinary men who had not been ordained. At a yearly meeting with his preachers, first held in 1744, they allowed such men only in cases of necessity.

After American independence, in 1784, Wesley ordained two preachers for America himself, though only bishops normally did so.

That year the Americans formed their own Episcopal church, meaning one led by bishops. In 1791 his preachers counted more than 130,000 members in Europe and America.

An American preacher later wrote that the American Methodists kept the written Rules Wesley had drawn up for the societies, with very little alteration. Their rule book still set out classes under a leader. Richard Allen, who later founded the African Methodist Episcopal Church, chose Methodism for its good discipline. Yet he wrote that some preachers became tyrants to Black members, turning them out without a trial.

The day before he died, too weak to make himself understood, he gathered his strength and said twice, "The best of all is, God is with us." A woman who kept his house wrote it down. He died the next morning, 2 March 1791, nearly eighty-eight, with friends kneeling around the bed.
