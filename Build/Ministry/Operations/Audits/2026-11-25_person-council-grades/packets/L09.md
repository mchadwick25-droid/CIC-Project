# Packet L09: person and council stories (census longDescription)

## venantius-fortunatus

Venantius Fortunatus

Venantius Fortunatus and his friend Felix of Treviso suffered a grievous disease of the eyes. Paul the Deacon, the Lombard historian, tells what they did at Ravenna. They went to the church of Saints Paul and John. There a lamp burned in a window beside an altar of Martin of Tours. They touched their eyes with its oil, and at once the disease was gone. Fortunatus's poem says Martin once gave the light back to Felix and to him.

Fortunatus was a poet from northern Italy. Paul says he was born near Ceneda and Treviso, and trained at Ravenna. For love of Martin he left Italy for the saint's tomb at Tours, a little before the Lombards invaded. The German scholar Bruno Krusch puts his coming to Gaul in 565.

He stayed at Poitiers, Paul says. Radegund, once the wife of King Chlothar the First, lived there in her monastery of the Holy Cross. Fortunatus wrote poems to her and to its abbess, Agnes. He called Agnes a mother in honor and a sister in sweet love, and said his love was of the spirit, with Christ as witness.

He wrote verse for kings and bishops, and Lives of saints. Gregory of Tours, who calls him his venerable fellow servant, says he told Martin's life in four books of verse. O. M. Dalton, Gregory's English translator, says that when Radegund's house received a relic of the Cross, Fortunatus marked it with hymns. One begins "Vexilla Regis prodeunt", in plain words, the banners of the King go forward.

Paul says he was ordained at Poitiers, first priest and then bishop. Baudonivia, a nun of Holy Cross, also calls him bishop. Dalton notes that Gregory never calls him bishop. Dalton thinks Fortunatus became bishop only after Gregory died.

Paul came to his grave to pray. At the request of Aper, the abbot, he wrote an epitaph: "Famous in talent, quick to perceive and in speech ever gentle."

## victor-of-tunnuna

Victor of Tunnuna

Victor of Tunnuna wrote a chronicle. In it he tells how he was kept under guard and beaten, first in a monastery at Mandracium, then on the island of Aegimurus. Then he was sent to Alexandria with Theodore, bishop of Cebarsussi in Africa, for defending the Three Chapters. There he was thrown into prison in the fort of Diocletian. Theodor Mommsen, who edited the chronicle, puts this in 555.

Victor was a bishop in Roman Africa. How the name of his town should be spelled is not clear, Mommsen says. He thinks it lay perhaps not far from Carthage.

The Three Chapters were writings of three earlier churchmen. The emperor Justinian condemned them. Victor stood with the African bishops who defended them. He writes that they cut off Vigilius, bishop of Rome, for condemning them, but left him room to repent.

After fifteen days of argument in the governor's court, Victor and Theodore were sent to a monastery at Canopus, twelve miles from Alexandria. Later the emperor called them to Constantinople. They stood before Justinian and Eutychius, the bishop of the city, and would not give way. They were split up and held in the city's monasteries. Theodore died, Victor writes, in the same month and on the same day as Justinian.

Isidore of Seville says the chronicle ran from the creation of the world to the first year of the emperor Justin the Younger.

Victor writes as a partisan. To him the empress Theodora was the enemy of the council of Chalcedon, and she died covered in a cancer. Mommsen calls him and Liberatus, another African, fierce defenders of Chalcedon.

Isidore says Victor died still a prisoner in a monastery of Constantinople, as people say, he adds. No early source gives the year.

Isidore praised the chronicle as most worthy of memory. Joseph Mesnage, a French scholar, calls it very precious for the church history of Africa in that century.

## victor-of-vita

Victor of Vita

Felix, bishop of Abbir, had been a bishop for forty-four years when King Huneric sent him into exile. He was paralyzed and could neither feel nor speak. Victor of Vita writes that he and others asked that the dying man be left at Carthage. The king, Victor reports, answered that wild oxen should be yoked to drag him with ropes. So they carried Felix the whole way, Victor says, tied across a mule like a log.

Victor wrote the History of the Persecution in Africa under the Vandal kings Geiseric and Huneric. Most manuscripts of his book call him a bishop of Vita; one reads Utica. He calls Pampinianus the bishop "nostrae civitatis" (of our city), and Karl Halm, who edited the Latin, takes that to mean Vita.

He wrote in the sixtieth year since the Vandals crossed from Spain. He calls them a cruel and savage people, and their faith the Arian heresy. His book is the polemic of a victim and an enemy of their church.

Some of it he lived through. Four thousand nine hundred and sixty-six bishops, priests, deacons and other church members were sent into the desert, he writes. At Sicca and Lares they shouted that they were Christians and Catholics. Victor and others were let in to encourage them and celebrate the liturgy.

At Tipasa, Victor says, the king had the tongues and right hands of Catholics cut off, and they still spoke. Anyone who doubts it, he adds, can find one of them, the subdeacon Reparatus, at Constantinople. Procopius of Caesarea, the Greek historian of the Vandal War, also writes of many whose tongues Huneric cut out. They "even up to my time were going about in Byzantium having their speech uninjured."

Near the end he calls on the angels of God to look at Africa. Once it was held up by so many churches, he says, and now it sits a widow, cast down.

## vigilius-of-rome

Vigilius of Rome

On Saint Cecilia's feast, 22 November, an officer sent by the empress Theodora found Pope Vigilius in Cecilia's church in Rome. The officer took him to the Tiber and put him on a ship. As it moved off, the crowd threw stones and sticks and cried, "Your hunger go with you!"

The scene comes from the Book of the Popes, a record of the popes' lives. Its life of Vigilius tells of Romans who called him a murderer. It also shows him stand up to the empress. Louise Loomis, its translator, finds the life full of mistakes and the scene told nowhere else.

Vigilius, a Roman deacon, had been the pope's envoy at Constantinople. In 537 the general Belisarius sent Pope Silverius away, fearing he was plotting with the Goths. The historian Procopius of Caesarea says Belisarius "appointed another man, Vigilius by name, to the office of chief priest."

How he won the post is disputed. Liberatus of Carthage says Theodora had him promise in secret to set aside the Council of Chalcedon, and that she offered him gold. Liberatus and Victor of Tunnuna both copy a letter from Vigilius to three bishops Rome had condemned. In it he says he shares their faith. Theodor Mommsen, who edited Victor, says both men hated Vigilius, and with reason. Loomis, citing the French scholar Louis Duchesne, thinks the letter most likely forged; Mommsen leaves it open.

At Constantinople the emperor Justinian wanted the Three Chapters condemned. These were writings of Theodore of Mopsuestia and Theodoret of Cyrrhus, and a letter of Ibas of Edessa. Loomis says Vigilius condemned them in 548, in a ruling called the Judicatum, but held to Chalcedon.

A council met in the city in May 553 without his consent, Bishop Hefele records. On 14 May Vigilius issued his Constitutum. He did not dare condemn Theodore, he wrote, nor would he let anyone else condemn him. The council condemned them anyway.

That December, writing to Eutychius, bishop of Constantinople, Vigilius gave way and condemned the Three Chapters. Henry Percival, who prints it in English, says some have doubted it is real, but most scholars accept it.

Vigilius died of the stone in Sicily on his way home, the Book of the Popes says.

In that letter to Eutychius, Vigilius pointed to Augustine of Hippo, who had corrected his own writings. When further study shows the truth, he wrote, "there ought to be no shame."

## zachariah-rhetor

Zachariah Rhetor

Zachariah Rhetor came to Berytus, now Beirut, to study civil law, and he expected trouble. In his Life of Severus of Antioch he says new students were teased by the others, and he feared Severus most of all. On his first day in the law school of Leontius, he found Severus among the students. Severus greeted him kindly.

Zachariah came from the port of Gaza, as he says in that Life. He had studied beside Severus at Alexandria, hearing the same teachers. E. W. Brooks introduced the English translation of the chronicle that bears Zachariah's name. From the Life, he places their studies in the emperor Zeno's reign and says Zachariah later worked as an advocate in Constantinople.

He wrote the Life to defend his friend, who was by then bishop of Antioch. It opens with a friend who comes to him from the bookstalls of the Royal Portico in Constantinople, upset by a pamphlet that mocks Severus. Brooks says enemies charged that Severus had followed pagan rites in his youth. Brooks finds Zachariah's answer "a very lame one."

Zachariah also wrote a church history in Greek for Eupraxius, who served at the imperial palace, ending in 491 by Brooks's reckoning. Its preface attacks the Council of Chalcedon. A later Syriac compiler used the history in a longer chronicle. Brooks says the compiler followed Zachariah in books three to six only, and that later writers wrongly gave him the whole work. Land, its editor of 1870, gives him book seven as well.

Brooks also takes him for the Zachariah Scholasticus who, by the heading of a Greek dialogue, later became bishop of Mitylene. A bishop of that name was at a synod in 536, Brooks says. Brooks suggests, with a "perhaps," that he had come over to Chalcedon's side when he became bishop.

In that preface Zachariah says the council "tore into ten thousand rents the perfect Robe of Christ, woven from the top throughout."

## abbess-bugga

Abbess Bugga

Around 720, an Englishwoman wrote to Boniface of Mainz, the English missionary, to give thanks for his success. His letter had told her that Rathbod, king of the Frisians, "that enemy of the Catholic Church", had been laid low. With the messenger she sent "fifty shillings and an altar pall".

Her greeting names her "Bugga, a lowly handmaiden". Another letter, from her mother Eangyth, an abbess, names her as "her only daughter Heaburg, called Bugga". Both are in Edward Kylie's English translation of 1911. Mother and daughter wrote of the souls "of different sex and age committed to us", and of poverty.

Bugga became an abbess. About 725, by Kylie's uncertain date, Boniface wrote "To the lady Bugga, abbess". She had asked whether to go abroad. He would not urge it strongly, he wrote, but if she found no quiet at home, going seemed better. Their sister Wiethburga, at the shrine of Saint Peter, advised waiting until the dangers "from the Saracens" in Italy were over, and Boniface agreed.

In her old age, Boniface heard of "the storms of tribulation" that had come on her. She had laid down "the greater anxiety of caring for the monasteries", he wrote, and heavier sorrows had followed.

Ethelbert the Second, king of Kent, wrote to Boniface of "the venerable Abbess Bugga", his kinswoman, and Kylie takes her for the same woman. She had prayed at the holy places of Rome, the king said, and had met Boniface there. She came home to "the monastery of consecrated women, which formerly she governed well under the law of the Church".

Bregwin, archbishop of Canterbury, later wrote to Lul, who followed Boniface at Mainz. Bregwin and his church, he wrote, kept the burial day of an abbess named Bugga, 27 December. While she lived, she had asked that Mainz be told and keep it too, since Boniface had been her father in Christ. The Bollandists, editors of the Acta Sanctorum, report a belief that Bugga was another name of Abbess Eadburga, to whom Boniface wrote about a vision. Kylie treats them as two women.

In her letter of thanks Bugga had told Boniface that "no temporal vicissitudes can move my mind from its steady guardianship of thy love."

## adam-jingjing

Adam (Jingjing)

"Behold the unchangeably true and invisible, who existed through all eternity without origin." So begins the Chinese text of a stone set up in China in 781. It praises "our eternal true lord God, triune and mysterious in substance". He "appointed the cross as the means for determining the four cardinal points".

The stone is the Xi'an stele, raised by the Church of the East in Tang China. In A. Wylie's English, its text names its author: "Composed by King-Tsing, A Priest of the Syrian Church." Henri Cordier, in his notes to Henry Yule's Marco Polo, joins this Chinese name to the Syriac name Adam. Apart from one note of Cordier's, everything the sources say of Adam comes from the stone.

His text sets out his church's faith in Chinese words. The Messiah was born of a virgin in Syria, it says, and "Twenty-seven sacred books" were left. Of the church's people he wrote that "they do not keep slaves, but put noble and mean all on an equality." Then the text tells how Alopen, a man from Syria, brought the books to the capital, Chang-an, in 635, and how emperors honored him.

Lines of Syriac run down the sides of the stone. Joseph Assemani, writing in 1728, reads one as naming Adam priest and chorepiscopus. Athanasius Kircher, who published the stone in Latin, had rendered chorepiscopus once as archbishop and once as a bishop's vicar. Once he also made Adam a deacon, which Assemani calls a misreading. Henry Yule calls him "Bishop and Pope" of China.

The stone names a second Adam, a deacon, son of the priest Jazedbuzid. In the Syriac, as Assemani reads it and as Malech gives Kircher's reading, Jazedbuzid set the stone up. Cordier writes that King-tsing erected it.

Cordier adds that King-tsing appears also to have helped a Buddhist translate a Buddhist sutra, under Te-Tsung, the emperor of the stone's day. He takes this from a paper by J. Takakusu.

No source gives Adam's birth, his death or where he came from.

Of his church's teaching he wrote: "It is difficult to find a name to express the excellence of the true and unchangeable doctrine."

## adomnan-of-iona

Adomnán of Iona

Ceolfrid, abbot of Wearmouth and Jarrow, once asked a guest why his head was shaved like that of the man Saint Peter had cursed. The guest was Adomnán of Iona, his people's envoy to King Aldfrith of Northumbria. He answered that "though I have Simon's tonsure, according to the custom of my country, yet I utterly detest and abhor the Simoniacal wickedness". Ceolfrid, who argued for the Roman tonsure, told the story in a letter that Bede copied into his History.

Wentworth Huyshe, who translated Adomnán's Life of Columba, says he was born in 624 in Donegal, of Columba's kin. By Huyshe's account he became a monk under his kinsman Seghine, abbot of Iona, and in 679 became abbot himself. Huyshe adds that the exiled Aldfrith was called his foster-son.

The Life is a saint's Life of prophecies, miracles and visions. Adomnán asks readers to attend "to the matter rather than to the words, which, as I think, seem uncultured and rude." He names his tellers. Abbot Failbeus, his predecessor, told him how King Oswald of Northumbria saw Columba in a vision before a battle. Adomnán writes that on a visit to "my friend, King Aldfrid," plague was still killing in England, yet none of his company fell ill.

Bede says Adomnán wrote a book on the holy places, from what Arculf, a bishop from Gaul, told him. A storm had driven Arculf onto Britain's west coast on his way home. In its preface, Adomnán says Arculf dictated the account to him. L. Cecil Jane, in notes to Bede, doubts that Adomnán wrote it.

In Aldfrith's kingdom, Bede says, learned men urged Adomnán not to keep an Easter against the custom of the whole Church. He changed his mind, but could not win his monks of Iona. In Ireland he brought many to the Roman Easter. He went home and died before the next year came round. Bede calls him "a good and wise man, and remarkably learned in Holy Scripture."

Newport White, who translated Patrick's writings, says Adomnán held a synod at Birr in the late 690s. A note in the Lebar Brecc, a late medieval Irish manuscript, counts Adomnán's rule among four rules of Ireland, "not to slay women".

At the close of his book on the holy places, Adomnán asks each reader to pray for Arculf. He asks them to pray for him too, the poor sinner who wrote it down.

## agathon-of-alexandria

Agathon of Alexandria

Under the emperor Heraclius, a priest of Alexandria went about the city at night in a layman's clothes. He comforted the faithful who were in hiding and gave them the sacrament. By day he carried a basket of carpenter's tools on his shoulder. He passed for a carpenter, so that his opponents would not stop him at the doors of the faithful. He kept this up for ten years, until the Muslims came.

So says the History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria, in its life of the patriarch Benjamin. The History is a later Arabic history of the Coptic church. The priest was Agathon of Alexandria, a native of Mareotis. When Benjamin came back to his see, he took Agathon as his son in governing the church. Agathon later followed him as patriarch. B. Evetts, who translated the History, heads his life 661 to 677.

As patriarch, Agathon bought captives whom Muslim raiders had brought from Sicily, and set them free, the History says.

Then Theodore, a Chalcedonian, paid Yazid, son of Muawiyah, the Muslim ruler at Damascus, for power over Alexandria and Mareotis. The Chalcedonians held to the council of Chalcedon, which Agathon's church refused. Theodore ordered that anyone who saw "the pope of the Theodosians" going out, by night or by day, might stone him to death. The Theodosians were the History's name for Agathon's own church. Agathon stayed hidden in his cell and prayed for Theodore, as the Gospel bids.

Agathon died on the 16th of the Coptic month Babah, after seventeen years, the History says. The Ethiopic Synaxarium, a book of saints' days, gives nineteen. His body was laid with Benjamin's.

Benjamin's life in the History holds an account told in Agathon's voice. It tells how Benjamin consecrated a church of Saint Macarius in the desert. Eusèbe Renaudot was a Catholic who wrote a Latin history of these patriarchs in 1713. He calls their line heretical, and that account a fable under Agathon's name. Early in the account, its writer calls himself the sinner Agathon. "I was the son of the Father Benjamin," he says, "and knew much of his virtues through my intimacy with him."

## aidan-of-lindisfarne

Aidan of Lindisfarne

Bede passes on a report of a council among the elders of the Scots, as he calls the Irish. King Oswald of Northumbria had asked them for a bishop, but the first man they sent came home calling the English stubborn and barbarous. Aidan, a monk of Iona, told him he had been too severe, and had not first given his hearers "the milk of more easy doctrine". The elders made Aidan a bishop and sent him.

He came to the English from Iona in the 630s. Oswald made Lindisfarne, an island at high tide, the seat of Aidan's bishopric. Aidan spoke little English, so the king, who had learned the Scots' tongue in exile, interpreted for him. Nearly all we know of Aidan comes from Bede, who wrote some eighty years after his death, learning from those who knew him.

Bede says Aidan taught no otherwise than he lived. He walked through town and country, urging people to believe and to give alms. Gifts from the rich went to the poor, or to ransom people wrongly sold as slaves. Many of those he freed he taught and made priests.

King Oswin of Deira gave him a fine horse, and Aidan gave it to a beggar. When the king complained, he answered, "Is that foal of a mare more dear to you than the Son of God?" Oswin later knelt for his pardon, and Aidan wept and told his priest the king would not live long. Oswin was killed soon after, and Aidan died twelve days later, on 31 August 651, Bede says.

Aidan kept Easter Sunday by his country's custom, between the fourteenth and twentieth day of the moon. Bede, who followed the Roman Easter, says he detests this. Charles Plummer, who edited Bede in 1896, calls it "sad" that Bede breaks off his praise of Aidan to say so. Yet Bede grants that Aidan always kept it on a Sunday. While Aidan lived, he adds, all bore the difference patiently.

An unnamed Lindisfarne monk wrote a saint's Life of Cuthbert of Lindisfarne. He says the young shepherd Cuthbert saw a soul carried to heaven, and days later heard that Aidan had died that hour. Bede's Life of Cuthbert says he learned it the next morning.

Bede praises Aidan's love of peace and his care for the afflicted and the poor. "These things I much love and admire in the aforesaid bishop," he writes.

## al-baladhuri

al-Baladhuri

The Christians of Damascus still held their church of Saint John. The caliph Mu'awiyah wanted to add it to the city's mosque, and they refused. The caliph 'Abd al-Malik offered them money, and they refused again. Then his son, the caliph al-Walid, threatened to tear it down. Someone warned him that whoever "tears down a church will lose his wits". Angered, al-Walid called for a spade. He began breaking the walls with his own hand, in "a robe of yellow silk", and workmen pulled the rest down.

Al-Baladhuri tells this story in his Futuh al-Buldan. He was a Muslim historian who died in 892, well over a century after Mu'awiyah's reign. Philip Hitti, who put the book into English in 1916, draws on al-Fihrist and the lives by Yakut and al-Kutubi. From these, he writes, al-Baladhuri was Ahmad ibn Yahya ibn Jabir, a native of Baghdad of Persian descent. He died deranged, Hitti writes, from drinking the juice of the anacardia, in Arabic baladhur, which gave him his name.

When 'Umar ibn 'Abd al-'Aziz became caliph, the Christians complained, and he ordered the part taken from their church returned. The Muslims of the city objected. In the end the Christians gave up their claim to Saint John's. In return they received the churches of al-Ghutah, an orchard district of Damascus, that had been taken by force.

During the siege of Damascus, al-Baladhuri reports, the commander Khalid wrote terms at the bishop's request. They promised the people "security for their lives, property and churches". Al-Baladhuri also gives the terms the Christians of Najran made with Muhammad. "No attempt shall be made to turn a bishop from his office as a bishop," they say.

Hitti weighs how far al-Baladhuri can be trusted. He says al-Baladhuri tries, wherever he can, to rest each fact on its own chain of named reporters. Sometimes, as al-Baladhuri says himself, he pieces reports together into one account. The Saint John's story is one he opens only with "It is reported". Hitti finds the book "free from exaggeration", but says the author scarcely judges what he reports.

Hitti quotes the later historian al-Mas'udi: "We know of no better work on the history of the Moslem conquests".

## alcuin-of-york

Alcuin of York

Liudger, a Frisian deacon, traveled to York to study under its master, Alcuin. Altfrid, a ninth-century bishop of Münster, tells it in his Life of Saint Liudger. A Frisian merchant killed a local count's son in a quarrel, and the Frisians hurried to leave. Alcuin sent Liudger home with them, Altfrid says, for he would rather die than see his dear son suffer deadly harm.

Alcuin was born in the province of York, of noble English stock, Jean Mabillon writes in his seventeenth-century essay. Mabillon draws on an older Life. He adds that Alcuin was raised from boyhood at York's main church and later ran its school.

In 780, Mabillon writes, Alcuin went to Rome on behalf of Eanbald, the new bishop of York. On the way back, at Parma, he met Charlemagne, who invited him to Francia. Einhard, who wrote Charlemagne's Life, calls Alcuin a deacon from Britain and a man learned in every field. Charlemagne learned rhetoric, logic and astronomy from him, Einhard says, as Mabillon quotes him.

Alcuin wrote that he came at Charlemagne's call. His teacher, he says, had charged him to defend the Catholic faith wherever new sects arose. Felix, bishop of Urgel, taught that Christ in his human nature was God's adopted Son. The Royal Frankish Annals record that teaching condemned at Regensburg in 792 and at Frankfurt in 794. The Frankfurt decrees end with Charlemagne asking the council to take Alcuin into its fellowship and prayers.

Elipandus of Toledo, who had first put the question to Felix, warned Alcuin not to corrupt the king. Alcuin answered that the king could not be corrupted, and that he had come to help him in the faith. He wrote seven books against Felix. In Charlemagne's thirty-second year as king, Alcuin says, Felix was summoned to Aachen, came willingly and confessed the true faith.

In 796, by Mabillon's reckoning, Charlemagne made Alcuin abbot of Saint Martin's at Tours. The older Life, as Mabillon reports it, puts his death in May 804. Mabillon argues instead that Alcuin lived on past 810.

From Tours Alcuin described his teaching to Charlemagne, in a letter Mabillon quotes. To some he served the honey of the Scriptures, and others he plied with the old wine of ancient learning. Some he fed on the apples of grammar. Others he longed to light up with the order of the stars, like the painted roof of a great house.

## alexander-ii-of-alexandria

Alexander II of Alexandria

Alexander II, the Coptic patriarch of Alexandria, went to Misr to greet the new governor, Abd Allah, as patriarchs did. "What is this man?" Abd Allah asked. He held the patriarch until he paid three thousand dinars. Then George, a deacon from Dimru, went to the governor. "Dost thou desire the life of the patriarch or money?" he asked. "I wish for the money," Abd Allah answered. George took Alexander round the North for two months, begging, until they had the sum.

The History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria, a later Arabic history of the Coptic church, tells it this way. Alexander was a monk of the monastery of Az-Zajaj. He was consecrated on the feast of Saint Mark, after the see had stood empty three years. B. Evetts, the History's translator, gives his years as 705 to 730.

The next governor, Kurrah, sent by the caliph al-Walid, arrested him as well. "Our Law bids us not to lay up treasure," Alexander told him. Alexander asked leave to beg in Upper Egypt, and Kurrah let him go. "Since the time of the Father Benjamin we have not seen a patriarch in Upper Egypt," the people said.

Kurrah took the church's gold and silver vessels. At Easter, the people and clergy of Alexandria demanded their dues. He told them they now used chalices of glass and patens of wood. They reviled him, the History says, and he bore it.

The History says that under the governor Usamah, monks were branded on the left hand with a ring-shaped iron. Eusèbe Renaudot, a Catholic scholar writing in Latin in 1713, says instead that they had to wear an iron ring on the right hand. The governor Ubaid Allah had the Christians counted and marked, and tried to brand Alexander too. Alexander refused. He asked for three days, and he prayed to die rather than be branded. On the third day he fell sick.

"Prepare the boat at sunset that we may depart, for to-morrow the Lord Jesus Christ will visit me," he told the brothers. They fled down the river and reached Tarnut by morning, and there he died. Shamul, bishop of Wasim, was the only bishop with him.

## alopen

Alopen

In 1625, workmen digging outside Xi'an, in China, came upon a great stone with a cross carved at its top and strange writing beside the Chinese. It told of Christians who had taught in that city a thousand years before, with the favor of the Tang emperors.

The stone named the man who first brought the books of their church, the Church of the East. We know him as Alopen, and his is the earliest Christian mission to China that the record describes. In the year 635 he reached Chang-an, the Tang capital — today's Xi'an — coming from the far west. The emperor Taizong sent his chief minister to meet him outside the capital and bring him to the palace.

The books were translated in the imperial library, and the emperor studied the teaching and ordered it spread. In 638 his decree, quoted on the stone, said of this faith: "It helps every creature, and it does people good. Let it spread freely through the empire." Officials built a monastery in the capital for twenty-one priests, and a foreign faith now had the emperor's open license.

Under the next emperor, the stone says, its monasteries filled a hundred cities. In the years after, Buddhists spoke out against it and others mocked it, but its priests held it together. In 781 they set up the stone, about 146 years after he came, and told his story: his church looking back, in words that also praise the emperors. It pictures his journey: "Watching the blue clouds, he carried the true sacred books. Watching the way the winds blew, he faced hardships and dangers." That is the stone's way of saying the road was long and hard, and he kept the books with him.

After 781, Chinese history keeps only slight traces of the church. One is an edict of 845 that sent more than three thousand foreign teachers, Christians and others, back to ordinary life. The stone lay in the ground until 1625. Then a Chinese Christian official sent a copy to the Jesuit missionaries, and they circulated it through the empire as proof that Christianity had come to China early. So a church the world had forgotten was known again.

## antony-of-choziba

Antony of Choziba

A young man left his father's house in secret, with a single companion and none of his family knowing. The two meant to go to the monks of Raithou. Saracens were raiding the public roads, so on the advice of holy fathers they went to the monastery of Choziba instead. There they were received and soon given the monk's tonsure. Later his companion slipped away to Raithou, and he was left grieving.

He tells it himself. He is Antony of Choziba, a monk who lived through the Persian attack on Jerusalem. He wrote the Life of his teacher, George of Choziba, in Greek. The Bollandists edited it in 1888, with a Latin translation. In the Life, George calls him his child Antony, and when dying he asks where Antony is.

When Antony wanted to follow his companion, George took him aside one Sunday. It is not the place they look for in a monk, he said, but the way of life. And not father or mother, kin or friends, but the will. On that great and fearful day, no one would stand by us but each man's deeds.

Antony tells the Persian invasion through George. The Persians besieged the holy city, and some fled to Arabia. In a ravine the soldiers killed abba Stephen the Syrian, an old man of a hundred or more. Seeing George poor, weak and modest, they let him go.

After the invasion the monks settled in the guesthouse at Jericho, and Antony was made cellarer, the keeper of stores. There, he writes, dealing with worldly people and women, he was fiercely tempted. He went in secret to George in the holy city and told him his struggle. George prayed over him, Antony says, and by word and deed healed him and kept him near.

The Bollandists' volume also prints, under his name, miracles of the Mother of God at Choziba. At the end he says he is not wise, nor able in speech. Yet God, rich and generous, did not disdain to pour on him, useless and foolish, this small drop of blessing.

## athanasios-the-athonite

Athanasios the Athonite

The general Nikephoros Phokas was fighting the Arabs of Crete when he sent for a monk from Mount Athos. The monk tells it in the rule he wrote for his monastery. Nikephoros wrote to him many times, and when he would not come, wrote more often still. On the island he begged the monk to build a lavra, a house of monks, where he too could one day become a monk. The monk held back. He was used to living free and alone, he wrote. He thought it enough if he could save himself.

The monk was Athanasios the Athonite. Philipp Meyer, who edited his writings in 1894, tells his early life from a Life written under his successor. Meyer knew it only in a Modern Greek version of the seventeenth century. By that Life, Athanasios came from Trebizond, was orphaned young, and became a teacher in Constantinople. He became a monk under Michael Maleinos, abbot of Mount Kyminas and uncle of Nikephoros. When Michael wanted him as his successor, the Life says, he fled to Athos.

Meyer dates the visit to Crete to 960 or 961. A manuscript cited in the notes to Leo the Deacon's history says Athanasios never went, and only foretold the victory. Meyer calls that a legend.

By Meyer's reckoning, the church was half built when news came, in 963, that Nikephoros had been crowned emperor. Athanasios went to Constantinople, his rule says, and blamed him to his face. From the start, he told him, you planned one thing and promised us another. The emperor swore he would still come to the Mountain. He asked only that the church not be left half finished. He was murdered in 969, and Athanasios called his death a martyr's end.

Nikephoros's charter, which Athanasios quotes in his rule, made the house self-governing, under its own abbot. Later, the Life says, monks of Athos accused Athanasios of changing the Mountain's ways. A ruling of 972 by the emperor John Tzimiskes records their complaint, and Athanasios signed it as abbot of the greatest lavra. Meyer says his rule became the standard for the other houses of Athos.

The Life says he died with six of his monks when a dome they were finishing fell in. Meyer puts it between 997 and 1011.

In his testament he wrote that he had spent all his days in great sorrow, knowing his weak soul unfit to lead others.

## bede-of-jarrow

Bede

On his last evening, Bede was dictating to a boy named Wilbert. The boy said one sentence was still unwritten. "Well, then, write it," Bede said. When the boy said it was written, Bede agreed, "it is finished." He asked the boy to hold his head while he sat facing the place where he used to pray. He died on the floor of his cell singing "Glory be to the Father."

The scene comes from a letter by his pupil Cuthbert to a fellow pupil, Cuthwin. Charles Plummer, who prints it, holds that Bede died on the eve of Ascension Day in 735, though the Saxon Chronicle gives 734.

Bede was a monk and priest of Wearmouth and Jarrow, in northern England. At the close of his History, finished in 731, he tells his own life. He was born on the monastery's land. At seven he was given to Abbot Benedict Biscop, and afterwards to Ceolfrid, to be educated. He became a deacon in his nineteenth year and a priest in his thirtieth. "I always took delight in learning, teaching, and writing," he wrote.

An anonymous History of the Abbots tells of a plague at Jarrow. It killed every monk who could read or sing the services, except Abbot Ceolfrid and one small boy he had raised. The two kept up the psalms with their antiphons until others were trained. Plummer dates the plague to 686, and says the boy can hardly be anyone but Bede.

His own list names commentaries on many books of the Bible, drawn from the Fathers. It also lists saints' lives, hymns, and books on nature and the reckoning of time. Bede wrote that rowdy peasants at their drink charged him with heresy over his book On the Times, which Plummer dates to 703. He answered in a letter to Plegwin, a Hexham monk, on the six ages of the world.

Bede names his sources in the History. Nothelm, a London priest, searched the papal archives and found letters of Gregory the Great. For Northumbria, Bede leaned on "the faithful testimony of innumerable witnesses." L. Cecil Jane, who edited the 1903 English edition, calls the History practically the only authority for England from the Saxon invasion to 732.

He closed the History with a prayer to "good Jesus." He asked that he, who had been given "the words of thy wisdom," might "come to thee, the fountain of all wisdom."

## benjamin-i-of-alexandria

Benjamin I of Alexandria

'Amr, the Arab commander, had taken Alexandria. A Christian duke named Sanutius told him about the patriarch of the Copts, a fugitive from the emperor's men. 'Amr wrote to the provinces of Egypt that the patriarch could come out safely and govern his church. When Benjamin came before him, 'Amr told his friends he had never seen a man of God like this man.

So says the History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria, a later Arabic history of Benjamin's own church. B. Evetts, the History's translator, heads his life 622 to 661. Benjamin I of Alexandria led the Egyptian church that refused the council of Chalcedon. He came from a rich family at Barshut and became a monk under an old man named Theonas. The patriarch Andronicus made him a priest and named him his successor.

The emperor Heraclius sent Cyrus, bishop of Phasis, to Egypt as prefect and patriarch, to bring the Egyptians over to Chalcedon. The History says an angel told Benjamin to flee and to warn his bishops to hide. He walked by night to the desert monasteries of Wadi Habib, then hid in Upper Egypt. Heraclius had Benjamin's brother Mennas tortured and drowned for refusing to call Chalcedon good.

R. H. Charles thinks John, the Coptic bishop of Nikiu whom he translated, was born about the time of the conquest. John says Benjamin came back in the thirteenth year after his flight. People said, John writes, that the Muslims won because of Heraclius's persecution through Cyrus.

Michael the Syrian, a twelfth-century patriarch of the Syriac Orthodox church, tells it differently. He found in histories that Benjamin promised the Arabs Alexandria if they would drive out Cyrus and give him back the churches.

Back in his see, the History says, Benjamin won back by gentleness most of those Heraclius had led astray. His spiritual son Agathon tells how he consecrated a church of Saint Macarius in Wadi Habib. Some of Benjamin's rules for its sanctuary end in curses. "Think not, my brethren, that I have written these anathemas for this generation," he told those around him. Eusèbe Renaudot, who wrote a Latin history of these patriarchs in 1713, calls their line heretical. He calls the account a fable of later Copts.

Benjamin died after thirty-nine years as patriarch. Agathon, who succeeded him, remembered him as one "who understood the interpretation of the scriptures, and dwelt in the desert, and grasped many mysteries."

## beuno

Beuno

Beuno was traveling near a ford on the river Severn when he heard a voice across the water. An Englishman was urging his dogs after a hare, shouting "Cergia". Beuno turned back at once. "My sons, put on your clothes and your shoes, and let us leave this place," he told his disciples. The man's nation "has a strange language, and is abominable", he said, and "they have invaded this place, and it will be theirs".

The story is told in the Welsh Life of Saint Beuno. W. J. Rees printed it in 1853 from two manuscripts, with an English translation. It is a late telling. Rees thought the Lives of the Welsh saints were probably compiled about the twelfth century. The few other accounts of him are late as well.

The Life makes him a Welsh clergyman and a founder of churches. A note in W. J. Rees's edition, quoting a Professor Rees, places his life in the sixth century and into the seventh. The Life says he was born to parents in old age and taught by a saint named Tangusius at Caerwent. Welsh kings and princes gave him land, estate after estate, and he built churches on it.

Cadwallon, king after his father Cadvan, gave him land in Arvon, and Beuno began to build. A woman came to him with a crying baby. The land, she said, belonged to the child's father. Beuno took her to the king. The child "who is in that woman's arms owns that land, and is heir thereof", he said, in W. J. Rees's English. The king refused, and Beuno left him under a curse. A cousin of the king gave Beuno a township called Kelynauc.

The Life also says he raised the girl Winefred from the dead, after a king cut off her head. Where her blood fell, it says, a spring rose that is called Winefred's Well. W. J. Rees places it in Flintshire.

The same note says Beuno founded the religious house at Clynog Fawr in 616, and it counts eleven churches dedicated to him.

## boniface-of-mainz

Boniface of Mainz

Boniface, archbishop of Mainz, sailed down the Rhine toward Frisia for the last time. Edward Kylie, who translated his letters in 1911, says he had a winding-sheet packed in the chest with his books. On the fifth of June 754 at Dokkum, he writes, a pagan band surrounded his tents, and he forbade his companions to resist. The Royal Frankish Annals record that pagans killed him in Frisia as he preached.

He was English, born Wynfrith between 675 and 680, by Kylie's reckoning. For his early life, Kylie's modern account is the main witness. At Nursling, near Winchester, he lectured on the Bible. A first mission to Frisia in 716 met resistance. In Rome in 718, Kylie says, his English name took a Latin form.

In Hesse, Kylie writes, he felled the oak at Geismar sacred to Thor and built a chapel to Saint Peter from its ruins. No missionary before him had called women to his aid, Kylie says. He wrote to Lioba, Tecla and Cynehilda, "I beseech, nay all but command you," and asked them to pray for him.

About 737, by Kylie's date, he asked all the English to pray that God would turn the hearts of the pagan Saxons. Have pity on them, he wrote, for they themselves say, "We are of one blood and one bone."

In 742 Carloman, prince of the Franks, called a synod. His decree sets Archbishop Boniface, the envoy of Saint Peter, over the bishops of the cities, and orders a synod every year. In a letter printed with the decree, Boniface told Pope Zacharias that Carloman had promised to put right something of the church's order. It had been trodden down, he wrote, for no less than sixty or seventy years.

After a synod Kylie dates to 747, he sent its decrees to Cuthbert, archbishop of Canterbury. He likened himself to a watchdog that sees robbers breaking in, but with no one to help him "only complains and grieves." When Pippin was made king of the Franks, the Royal Frankish Annals say, Boniface anointed him.

Cuthbert later wrote to Boniface's helper Lul that an English synod had fixed the day of his martyrdom as a yearly feast.

Kylie quotes Boniface's greeting to a new pope, Stephen. "If I have accomplished anything for the good of the Church during the thirty-six years of my Roman mission I still wish to complete and perfect it."

## braulio-of-zaragoza

Braulio of Zaragoza

In seventh-century Visigothic Spain, Braulio, bishop of Zaragoza, was losing his sight when King Chindaswinth sent for his archdeacon, Eugenius. A part of his soul was being taken from him, he wrote, and he begged the king not to part them. The king answered that he trusted this was God's will, and that Eugenius had been born in the city that now needed a bishop. Braulio sent him. The bond between them had burst, he wrote, rather than been cut.

Ildefonsus of Toledo, a later bishop there, tells of a cleric of the royal church named Eugenius. He fled to Zaragoza to live as a monk, until the king's force brought him back to be bishop of Toledo. Ildefonsus does not name Braulio.

Ildefonsus says Braulio followed his brother John as bishop of Zaragoza and served about twenty years, under four kings. He signed at three councils of Toledo in the 630s.

He was the friend and editor of Isidore of Seville. In Ernest Brehaut's English, Braulio says that Isidore left the Etymologies unfinished. Braulio divided it into twenty books, "since he wrote it at my request."

In 638 Braulio wrote to Pope Honorius for the bishops of a council of Toledo. The pope had urged them to act more firmly against the faithless. The council had just praised King Chintila, who would let no one who was not Catholic live in his kingdom. The council's canon on the Jews made every later king swear to keep it so. Braulio defended the bishops' slower way. They would bend by Christian coaxing, he wrote, those whom hard discipline could hardly move. The pope's rebuke had called them dumb dogs that cannot bark. That did not fit them at all, Braulio answered.

Braulio once teased a priest named Taio, who answered with insults. Braulio could bite back if he wished, he wrote. He would rather follow Christ, who gave his back to the whips and did not curse in return.

## cedd-and-chad

Cedd and Chad

Cedd chose a site for a monastery, on land given by King Ethelwald of the Deiri. Bede says its crags and distant hills looked more like haunts of robbers and wild beasts than homes. First Cedd meant to cleanse it by prayer and fasting. Through Lent he fasted each day but Sunday until evening, and then ate only "a little bread, one hen's egg, and a little milk mixed with water."

The place was Lastingham, in the north of England. Cedd and Chad were brothers, two of four who all became priests, which Bede calls "a rare thing to be met with." Bede tells their lives together, as he learned them from the monks of the one house they shared.

King Oswy sent Cedd to preach to the East Saxons, and Finan of Lindisfarne made him their bishop. He built churches and gathered monks at Ithancestir and at Tilbury, on the Thames. At the synod of Whitby in 664 he stood with the Irish party on Easter. Bede says he was also "a most careful interpreter for both parties." Afterward he accepted what Bede calls "the Catholic observance of Easter." He fell sick and died at Lastingham in a time of plague.

He left Lastingham to Chad, a disciple of Aidan of Lindisfarne. Oswy then sent Chad to be made bishop of York, while Wilfrid of Ripon was overseas. Chad went about on foot, Bede says, "after the manner of the apostles." Theodore of Tarsus came as the new archbishop. When he told Chad he had not been properly consecrated, Chad offered to resign, "for I never thought myself worthy of it." Bede says Theodore completed his ordination instead. Stephen, who wrote Wilfrid's Life and takes his side, says Theodore ordered Chad removed from another man's see. Even so, Stephen calls him a true and most gentle servant of God.

At Theodore's request, Chad became bishop of the Mercians, at Lichfield. When Chad would not ride, Theodore lifted him onto the horse with his own hands. He died there during a plague, on the second of March.

Trumbert, one of Bede's teachers, had been a monk under Chad. He said that when a strong wind rose, Chad prayed for mercy for all mankind. If the wind grew stronger, "he closed his book, and prostrating himself on the ground, prayed still more earnestly."

## cogitosus

Cogitosus

In the great church of Kildare, in Ireland, two tombs stood on either side of the altar. Brigid lay in one and Bishop Conleath in the other, under crowns of gold and silver hung above them. Painted boards adorned the church, and one wall bore pictures. The bishop came to the altar by one door, and the abbess with her virgins and faithful widows by another. So, the writer says, a great people in its ranks prayed with one mind to God.

The writer was Cogitosus. His Latin Life of Brigid of Kildare is a saint's Life, mostly miracles. Migne's collection of the Latin Fathers prints him under the year 591. Jacques Basnage, whose note Migne prints, thought him much later. Cogitosus did not see Brigid alive, Basnage argues, and the painted images he describes belong to later centuries.

The brothers compelled him to write, Cogitosus says, and the task was beyond his small skill. He tells a few things out of many, handed down by elders and the most learned. He has seen miracles with his own eyes, he adds, in the monastery where her body lies.

He makes Brigid's house the head of almost all the Irish churches, its jurisdiction reaching across Ireland from sea to sea. Brigid, he says, called a hermit from the wilderness to govern the church with her as bishop. When Cogitosus wrote, an archbishop and an abbess still ruled it in unbroken succession.

In his Life of Patrick, Muirchu names his own father Cogitosus, on Bishop Graves's correction of the Latin. Newport White, Muirchu's translator, takes that father for Brigid's biographer. Since White puts Muirchu in the 690s, that would place Cogitosus before about 700. Whitley Stokes, in an index, dates Cogitosus between 800 and 835.

He signs the Life asking the brothers to pray for "Cogitoso nepote culpabili", Cogitosus the guilty nepos. Nepos can mean grandson or nephew, and Basnage could not say in what sense he used it.
