# Packet L04: person and council stories (census longDescription)

## jerome

Jerome

Jerome was a young man in the desert when fasting failed to quiet his restless mind. So he went to a brother who, before his conversion, had been a Jew, and asked to be taught Hebrew. Years later he wrote of what it cost a scholar fresh from Rome's great Latin writers. "I began to learn my letters all over again, and worked at sounding out words that were harsh and throaty."

Jerome put the Old Testament into Latin from the Hebrew itself, not from the old Greek translation the churches read, from which their Latin version had been made. His Bible was fought over in his lifetime. In time it became the Western church's standard text.

In the 380s Damasus, the bishop of Rome, urged him to correct the Latin Gospels the churches already had against the Greek. Jerome wrote that there were almost as many forms of the text as copies.

Most of what we know of Jerome, and of the women around him, comes from his own letters. At Rome, he wrote, he often found himself among women vowed to God, and to some he explained the Scriptures. Then Rome turned on him. When the young daughter of Paula, a Roman widow of a great family, died, people in the funeral crowd, he wrote, said fasting had killed her. They asked why the monks were not thrown into the Tiber. He was accused over his friendship with Paula, though he says his accuser later took it back. He sailed in August, at a time he chose, he says.

Paula followed him east and built a monastery for men and houses for women at Bethlehem. When he was stuck over the Bible, he wrote, she pressed him with fresh questions, and she learned Hebrew. Palladius, a monk on his opponents' side, charged instead that Jerome held her back by his jealousy. From about 391 he addressed many prefaces to Paula and her daughter.

Augustine, a bishop in Africa, begged him not to translate the Scriptures afresh unless he marked where he differed from the old Greek, which Augustine held in the highest esteem. Rufinus of Aquileia, once Jerome's close friend, had fallen out with him over Origen, a Greek scholar whose ideas were under attack. In 400 Rufinus wrote against him. The new translation, he charged, was made under the influence of the Jews, and it tampered with the Scriptures as the apostles handed them down. "Who but you would have laid hands on God's gift, and on the inheritance of the apostles?"

In 403 Augustine told Jerome how a bishop at Oea had Jerome's Jonah read in church. The old Latin said the plant God grew to shade the prophet was a gourd. Jerome had written ivy. Augustine reported, and by the Greeks he means the Greek-speakers in the town: "The Greeks above all found fault with the reading, and fanned the charge that it was false. Such an uproar broke out in the congregation that the bishop was forced to demand the Jews' testimony. The town was Oea." The Jews, Augustine says, whether from ignorance or spite, said the Hebrew agreed with the old version, and the bishop corrected the reading so as not to lose his congregation.

Jerome answered sharply that the Hebrew names a plant that is neither gourd nor ivy. Augustine still would not have the Hebrew version read in church. Yet in 418 Jerome wrote that Catholics revered Augustine as the restorer of the ancient faith.

Jerome died at Bethlehem in 420. Two centuries later a bishop in Spain wrote that his translation was rightly preferred to the others. In 1546 the Council of Trent declared the "old and Vulgate edition," long used in the church, the authentic Latin text, and forbade anyone to reject it. The decree does not name Jerome, but that Latin Bible was built largely on his work.

About 411 he wrote of that first hard lesson in Hebrew: "But I thank the Lord that from this seed of learning, sown in bitterness, I now gather sweet fruit."

## john-cassian

John Cassian

Two monks of southern Gaul wanted more of the Egyptian desert. One wanted his monks taught its rules; the other longed to sail there, leaving a land frozen, as John Cassian put it, with the cold of Gaul. Cassian, who had lived among those monks, wrote seven Conferences for the two, so that one need not make so risky a journey.

The books made the journey instead. At Marseilles, Cassian carried Egypt's teaching into Latin for the monks of the West. His Conferences were talks with the old monks of the desert. A century later, Benedict's Rule named the Conferences first for the monks' evening reading. But his thirteenth Conference, on grace, God's free help, and free will, our own power to choose, drew an attack from Prosper of Aquitaine.

Most of his early life comes from his own books. As a young man he joined a monastery at Bethlehem with his friend Germanus. The two sailed to Egypt, promising a quick return, but stayed seven years, went back to keep their word, then returned to the desert of Scete.

At Constantinople, Bishop John Chrysostom made him a deacon, in the word of Gennadius, a later priest of Marseilles. When John was sent into exile, Germanus and Cassian carried his clergy's letters to the bishop of Rome. They wrote that bishops had plotted and soldiers helped drive their bishop out.

At Marseilles, Cassian became a priest and founded two monasteries, one for men and one for women. For a bishop whose province had none, he wrote the Institutes, on the dress, prayers and psalms of the Egyptian houses. Eight of its books give the eight chief faults, from greed for food to pride, and their remedies.

In the 420s came twenty-four Conferences. In the thirteenth, as Cassian writes him, an old hermit says God gives the first good thought. Yet when God sees a good will stirring, he strengthens it, whether he planted it or it grew from our effort, as with Zacchaeus and the thief on the cross. "So these two, the grace of God and free will, seem opposed to each other. But really they are in harmony."

In 429 Prosper told Augustine that many at Marseilles thought Augustine's teaching on God's choosing ran against the Fathers. If God chose before time who would be saved, they said, effort was in vain. The letter does not name Cassian.

Leo, later bishop of Rome, asked him to answer Nestorius, the new bishop of Constantinople, who would not call Mary the Mother of God. In seven books, the year before a council at Ephesus removed Nestorius, Cassian charged him with saying Christ was born a mere man. He tied that error to Pelagius's followers, who, he wrote, said people could live without sin if they liked.

Prosper then wrote a book against "the Conferencer", as its title calls him, defending Augustine. By the translator's account, it takes twelve sentences from the thirteenth Conference and accepts only the first, that God begins every good thought. It rejects the rest, among them that a good will may sometimes begin in us, and never names Cassian directly. He died at Marseilles, but no year is known.

Benedict's Rule names no author for its Conferences, but the title is Cassian's. Those monks did not come from his houses; they read his books.

Gennadius, on Cassian's side, took the unnamed attack to be Prosper's, and said some of their views on grace and free will were at odds. Of Cassian's works and Prosper's attack he wrote, "The church of God finds them wholesome, but he brands them as harmful."

Cassiodorus, a century and more later, told his monks to read Cassian with care and hear him gladly on the eight faults. But on free will, he warned, Prosper had rightly blamed him.

Cassian had named his own hope when the first Conferences went out. "May we set the men themselves before you, embodied, as it were, in their own rules. And, what matters more, may they be speaking in Latin."

## john-chrysostom

John Chrysostom

The emperor's chief chamberlain, Eutropius, had a law passed that stripped people who fled into churches of the right to shelter there. Soon after, in 399, he fell from favor and himself fled into the cathedral of Constantinople, the emperor's city. As he lay trembling under the altar, the bishop, John Chrysostom, climbed into the pulpit and preached against him. Some thought he was attacking a man already brought low. John said he spoke to soften the people's hearts, for the church had sheltered its enemy: "When everyone ignored him in his desolation, she alone, like a loving mother, hid him behind her curtains. She stood against the emperor's anger, the people's fury and unbearable hatred."

He preached plainly about the poor, and his blunt words to the powerful led to his exile. His sermons were read for centuries, and the followers who left the church over his fall came back only when his body came home.

John was born at Antioch, in Syria, and as a priest there for twelve years he preached on what the rich owed the poor. They decked the altar, where the bread of Communion lies, which Christians call Christ's body, with gold cups and silk. In one sermon he told them to honor Christ in the poor first: "Do you want to honor Christ's body? Do not neglect him when he is naked. While you honor him here with silk clothes, do not neglect him outside, dying of cold and nakedness." He published some of his sermons, and shorthand writers took down others as he spoke. Both kinds were kept and read.

In 398 the court chose him as bishop of Constantinople, and Eutropius made Theophilus, bishop of Alexandria, ordain him. As bishop he cut the bishop's own spending and gave the money to a hospital. He corrected his clergy sharply over how they lived and put some of them out of the church, and many came to hate him.

When a Gothic general asked for a church inside the city for the Arians, who taught that the Son of God had a beginning, John spoke against it before the emperor. Socrates, a church historian of the city writing a generation later, says Theophilus also held a grudge, since his own choice for bishop had lost out to John, and that he resolved to unseat him.

Theophilus and his bishops met at a place called the Oak, outside Chalcedon, and summoned John. His accusers charged that he had sold church property, insulted the clergy, struck a man and eaten greedily alone. John refused to come before judges he called his enemies, and asked for a council of the whole church. Socrates says they removed him from office only for refusing to come. A later bishop of Constantinople summed up the meeting's own written record, and they tell it differently: some charges were heard, and then all forty-five bishops voted to remove him. The emperor ordered him into exile, and John gave himself up, but he was soon called back.

Public games were held at a silver statue of the empress Eudoxia, just across the street from the cathedral. John mocked those who allowed them, calling the games an insult to the church, and the empress took this as aimed at her and sought another council. It condemned him for taking back his place as bishop, after his removal, without a council's leave. Since two councils had condemned him, the emperor barred him from the church, and John was silenced. His followers kept Easter in the public baths, and from then on they met apart from the church. In 404 he was taken into exile by force.

He was first exiled to Armenia. Palladius, who wrote John's life and took his side, says bishops in Syria, seeing the church of Antioch still looking to John, had him moved to Pityus, a more distant place on the Black Sea. In his telling, the soldiers hurried John on, saying they had been told his death would bring them promotion. Near Comana he asked for white clothes, took Communion and prayed the words he always used: "Glory to God for all things." He died there, in September 407.

In 438 the bishop of Constantinople had the emperor's leave to bring John's body home from Comana. He carried it through the city in procession and laid it in the Church of the Apostles, and those who had left the church over John's fall came back.

## jovinian

Jovinian

Jovinian is known only through Jerome and Ambrose. No tradition on this map tells his story, and none of his own writing survives whole. What remains is what his opponents chose to quote.

Jerome's editor calls him a Roman monk, or rather a solitary, who put off the monk's dress and lived like other men. He wrote a book in Latin at Rome. Jerome quotes four points from it. Baptized virgins, widows and wives are of equal merit. Those reborn in baptism with full faith cannot be overthrown by the devil. Fasting is no better than eating with thanks. And all who keep their baptismal vow share one reward in heaven.

Pammachius, a friend of Jerome, brought the book to Siricius, bishop of Rome. By the editors' account, synods at Rome and at Milan condemned it about 390. Ambrose and his synod wrote back to Siricius to join the sentence. In a letter to the church at Vercelli, Ambrose warned against two monks who said there is 'no merit in abstinence' and none in virginity. His editor takes them for followers of Jovinian.

In 393 Jerome answered the book with two books of his own, Against Jovinianus. He calls Jovinian 'the Epicurus of Christianity'. Even Jerome's editor gives a warning here. He says Jerome's charges about the man's life are guesses drawn from his views. They are not known facts.

## koriun

Koriun

Two Armenians named Leon and Koriun arrived at Constantinople. There they joined their companion Eznik of Goghp, who had been raised with them like a brother. Together they finished their work. Then they went home to Armenia. They took true copies of the Scriptures and the Church Fathers, with the canons of the councils of Nicaea and Ephesus.

This scene comes from the Life of Mesrop Mashtots, the monk who made the Armenian letters, and of Sahak, patriarch of the Armenians. The Life names Koriun only this once, in the third person. A note in Victor Langlois's 1869 French edition takes him for its author. So does Lazar of Pharp, a later Armenian historian, who calls Koriun a disciple of Mashtots. Langlois calls the Life the main source for Armenian literary history from the late fourth century into the fifth.

Koriun says he wrote of Sahak and Mesrop, men he saw with his own eyes. He writes of himself as an attentive witness of their preaching and a servant of these teachers. He left out many of their good deeds, he adds, and told only the chief things worth knowing. His Life is also the work of a disciple honoring a saint. It has demons put to flight and a cross of light over the house where Mesrop died.

Who asked him to write it is told two ways. Lazar says it was King Vramshapuh. Langlois says it was Joseph, who led the Armenian church after Sahak. The held French text names no patron.

Langlois also tells what is said of Koriun's later life. Sahak, he says, made him a bishop of the Georgians and sent him to their land. Koriun is believed to have died there, Langlois adds. But nothing certain can be said, since writers of his time give no details.

Koriun says he wrote as an example for the spiritual children of Sahak and Mesrop, and for all who would teach their nation the truth.

## leo-of-rome

Leo of Rome

In October 451, at Chalcedon, across the water from Constantinople, Greek-speaking bishops heard a letter read aloud. Its writer, Leo, bishop of Rome, had not come. The letter said: "To be hungry, to be thirsty, to grow tired and to sleep is plainly human." When it ended, the minutes say, the bishops cried out that Peter had spoken through Leo.

That letter is called the Tome. Leo of Rome wrote it in 449 to say that Christ is one person, fully God and fully human, and the council's own statement of faith followed it. Yet Leo also held that Peter still spoke and ruled through the bishop of Rome, and he refused the council's rule that ranked Constantinople next to Rome. This was the age of Church and Empire.

Leo was a deacon, away in Gaul, when he was chosen bishop of Rome in 440. In his first years he had the Manichaeans, followers of a sect that taught good and evil were two rival powers, hunted out and exposed before the church. Prosper of Aquitaine, who wrote in those years and praises Leo throughout, writes with approval that their books were burned.

In the East, the monk Eutyches taught that after God became man there was one nature only. A nature is what Christ is; a person is who he is. A meeting of bishops under Flavian, bishop of Constantinople, condemned him. In June 449, in the Tome, Leo told Flavian that each nature stays wholly itself in one person. He wrote in Latin and, his English editor thinks, knew no Greek.

The emperor then called a council at Ephesus, with Dioscorus of Alexandria presiding. Leo sent envoys and his letter. By that editor's account, with soldiers and monks present, the meeting turned riotous, and the letter was never read. Eutyches was restored and Flavian deposed, and Flavian, that editor says, died soon after from rough treatment. Prosper says many bishops were forced by violence and fear to agree. Leo begged the emperor, with groans and tears, for a new council in Italy.

After the emperor died, his successors called one in the East. In July 451 Leo accepted it and sent his envoys. To the empress he wrote: "Nor need a letter set down everything that could be committed at Ephesus, in what was not a trial but a robbery." His envoys presided. Some bishops thought the Tome stressed the two natures too far, and it was adopted only at the fourth session, days later.

The council also ranked Constantinople, the new imperial city, next after Rome, because the fathers had honored old Rome as the imperial city. In May 452 Leo wrote to the emperor: "Yet worldly things stand on one footing, and the things of God on another. No building will stand firm except on the rock the Lord laid as its foundation." That rock is the one on which, in the Gospel, Christ said he would build his church. To the empress he wrote that, by the authority of Peter, he declared the vote void. He broke off contact with the bishop of Constantinople until that bishop wrote to make peace. The rule stood.

That year, Attila, king of the Huns, was in northern Italy. Leo went to him with two high Roman officials. Prosper says Leo trusted in God's help, and that the king, pleased by the bishop's presence, stopped the war and went home. A historian of the Goths, writing a century later, says the Huns already feared the fate of the Gothic king who sacked Rome. When Leo came, Attila put aside his fury. A chronicler in Spain never mentions Leo and names famine and disease sent from heaven, and an army sent by the eastern emperor.

The faith of the Tome held in the Greek and Latin churches, and the churches of Egypt and Syria refused it. The editors of the councils write that Rome gave way on Constantinople's rank only centuries later.

Leo died late in 461, after twenty-one years as bishop. He was buried in the church of St. Peter.

## liberius-of-rome

Liberius of Rome

The emperor Constantius wanted Liberius, bishop of Rome, to condemn Athanasius of Alexandria. A record of their talk, which Theodoret of Cyrrhus copied into his Church History, gives his words. Asked why he stood alone, he said, "My standing alone does not make the truth a whit the weaker." Given three days to change his mind, he said, "Neither three days nor three months can change my sentiments. Send me wherever you please." He was banished to Beroea in Thrace. When the emperor sent him gold for the journey, he sent it back. The emperor, he said, needed it "to pay his troops."

Ammianus Marcellinus, a historian outside the church, tells the same refusal. He says Liberius was taken from Rome by night, for fear of the people, "among whom he was exceedingly popular." Theodoret says a deacon, Felix, was put in his place.

What happened in exile is disputed. Athanasius, his ally, says that after two years Liberius "gave way, and from fear of threatened death subscribed," and he blames those who forced him. Jerome says Liberius, worn out by exile, signed heresy and entered Rome as if he had won. Sozomen, a church historian of the next century, says he signed a document drawn up by Eastern bishops at Sirmium, with his own statement added. Theodoret tells of no lapse at all. Letters in which Liberius says he gave up Athanasius survive in Latin in a dossier gathered by Hilary of Poitiers. Feder, who edited the dossier, marks those letters as doubtful.

Theodoret says the noblewomen of Rome begged Constantius to bring their bishop back. The emperor proposed that Liberius and Felix share the church. The crowd cried, "One God, one Christ, one bishop." Liberius returned, and Felix withdrew.

Ambrose of Milan passed on the words Liberius spoke at Christmas in Saint Peter's, when Ambrose's sister made her profession as a virgin. "You," he said, "my daughter, have desired a good espousal."

## lucifer-of-cagliari

Lucifer of Cagliari

In exile in Palestine, Lucifer of Cagliari wrote a book against the emperor Constantius and sent it to him to read. Jerome of Strido, in his Lives of Illustrious Men, says he wrote it "with wonderful constancy and willingness to meet martyrdom." Lucifer was bishop of Cagliari in Sardinia. Liberius of Rome had sent him to the emperor. With him went the Roman priest Pancratius and the deacon Hilary.

The emperor wanted the Western bishops to condemn Athanasius of Alexandria. Lucifer refused at a council in Milan. W. H. Fremantle, who translated Jerome, says he opposed the emperor with much violence. He was exiled from 355 to 361. He spent most of it at Eleutheropolis in Palestine, and the rest in Egypt. His pamphlets from exile survive in Latin, with titles such as Of Apostate Kings.

When Constantius died in 361, the emperor Julian let the exiles go home. Theodoret of Cyrrhus says the church at Antioch was split in two, though both sides held the same creed. Lucifer skipped the council at Alexandria and went straight to Antioch. There he made Paulinus, a presbyter who led one party, its bishop. Eusebius of Vercelli, who had shared his exile, disapproved. Socrates of Constantinople says Lucifer took this as an insult and broke with him.

Lucifer went home to Sardinia. Fremantle says he refused church office to anyone who had given way to the Arians. Socrates writes that "a distinct sect arose under the name of 'Luciferians.'" It had died out by early in the next century, he adds. Jerome's Chronicle, Fremantle notes, gives his death in 371.

Jerome had been ordained by Paulinus, the bishop Lucifer made. About 379 he wrote a Dialogue against the Luciferians. In it he calls Lucifer "that saintly man," yet blames him for the split. "He himself was a good shepherd," Jerome writes, "but he was leaving a vast spoil to the beasts of prey."

## macrina-the-younger

Macrina the Younger

After eight years apart, Gregory came to his sister's house and was led to her. She lay on a sack spread over a board, and she lifted herself on one elbow but could not rise.

The house, in Pontus, in what is now Turkey, was the making of Macrina, the eldest of the family of Basil of Caesarea and Gregory of Nyssa. She had led her mother to live as an equal with the slave women, eating the same food, sleeping on the same kind of bed, and praying night and day. For them, Gregory says, this prayer was their work, and what others call work was rest.

On her deathbed she taught Gregory about the soul, death and rising again, and he wrote her teaching down as a conversation, with her as the Teacher. Almost all we know of her comes from her brother Gregory, who sat with her as she died, and how much of the teaching is hers, no one can say.

More than a thousand years later, her words were still being quoted. At Ferrara in 1438, Latin and Greek church leaders argued over what happens to souls after death, and the Latins quoted Macrina from Gregory's book. When the Greeks replied that Gregory was a man and could err, the Latins answered with his family: he was the brother of Basil the Great and of blessed Macrina.

Her life had turned on a refusal. When the man her father chose for her died before the wedding, she would take no other. He was not dead, she said, but only away, because of the hope of rising again.

She would not leave her mother. Over the years their house grew into one community, the women under her and the men under Peter, the brother she had raised. In a famine she took in babies left to die, and a young widow came to live under her care.

In 379, as scholars date it, she lay dying, and at evening she prayed. "You, Lord," she said, "have set us free from the fear of death." When her voice failed in the evening thanksgiving, she finished it in her heart, and her life and her prayer ended together.

## marcella-of-rome

Marcella of Rome

When Rome was taken in 410, soldiers broke into the house of an old widow named Marcella. Asked for gold, she is said to have pointed to her coarse dress to show she had none. They did not believe her, and "scourged her and beat her with cudgels." Jerome of Strido tells this in a memorial letter, written in her praise to her friend Principia. He was in Palestine and writes, he says, "what holy men have seen."

Marcella came from a noble Roman family. She was widowed before seven months of marriage. An old nobleman, Cerealis, then offered her his fortune. She told him that "a young man may indeed die early, but an old man cannot live long."

Clergy of Alexandria fled to Rome, Athanasius among them. From them Marcella heard of Antony, the desert monk, and of Pachomius's monasteries in Egypt. No highborn lady at Rome, Jerome says, had yet dared "publicly to call herself a nun." Marcella took up that life, and the virgin Eustochium was trained in her cell.

In the 380s Jerome lived in Rome, and Marcella questioned him about Scripture at every visit. Seventeen of his letters to her are held, several on Hebrew words. After he left, "recourse was had to her to settle" disputes about Scripture. She gave her answers as his or another's, because Paul had written, "I suffer not a woman to teach."

A Latin version of Origen's First Principles later reached Rome. The notes in W. H. Fremantle's English edition name its translator as Rufinus of Aquileia. He and Jerome later wrote against each other. Marcella opposed its teachers in public. "She it was who originated the condemnation of the heretics," Jerome writes. He admits some may think him "giving vent to my own rancour."

A few days after the soldiers came, she died in Principia's arms. Two years later Jerome wrote of her as "Marcella the glory of her native Rome."

## marcellus-of-ancyra

Marcellus of Ancyra

In Rome, Marcellus, bishop of Ancyra in Galatia, wrote to Julius, bishop of the city. He had waited there a whole year and three months, he says. At the Council of Nicaea, he reminds Julius, he had refuted the men now writing against him. He confesses the Word who always exists with the Father, and Christ's kingdom, which will have no end. The letter survives only in Greek, in a book against heresies by Epiphanius of Salamis.

His opponents' charges explain why he was in Rome. Socrates of Constantinople says Marcellus wrote against Asterius, a writer on the side of Arius. In doing so he fell into the opposite error and taught, as Paul of Samosata had, that Christ was a mere man. Sozomen says he taught that the Son began when he was born of Mary, and that his kingdom would end. Bishops meeting at Constantinople deposed him and put Basil in his place at Ancyra.

Julius received him, and the Western bishops at Sardica cleared him. They said he had written that Christ's kingdom had neither beginning nor end. The Eastern bishops, in a letter quoted in A. C. Zenos's notes to Socrates, called him "the most execrable of all heretics."

Whether Athanasius of Alexandria stood by him is disputed. Epiphanius once asked him. Athanasius only smiled, Epiphanius says, hinting that Marcellus was not far from error, yet treating him as cleared. The dossier of Hilary of Poitiers says Athanasius refused him communion. E. W. Watson, who introduced the English Hilary, says it is "notorious that he never did so."

In 381 the Council of Constantinople condemned the Marcellians by name. Hefele, quoted in Henry Percival's edition, says Marcellus himself came back to the truth. A Benedictine note to Basil's letters thinks he erred at the end of his life.

Epiphanius left the judgment open. The hidden things of Marcellus's mind, he wrote, are known to God.

## marius-mercator

Marius Mercator

In 429, by the consuls its title names, a Latin writer named Marius Mercator gave the church of Constantinople a short warning, written in Greek. Its title says it concerned Caelestius, a follower of Pelagius. After it was read, the title goes on, Caelestius and Julian of Eclanum were driven from the city with their companions by the emperor's order. They were later condemned at the Council of Ephesus.

Garnier, his first editor, notes that even Gennadius of Marseilles leaves him out of his list of church writers. Augustine of Hippo wrote to him as "my son." In that letter, held here in Latin, Augustine says Mercator's letters reached him at Carthage. He had not known, he says, how far Mercator had grown.

Where he came from is disputed. Garnier guessed southern Italy, since Mercator knew the private lives of Caelestius and Julian so well. Baluze, a later editor, guessed Africa. Schoenemann, a later scholar, says both are guesses. Garnier thought him a layman, since Augustine called him son and not brother. Hefele, the historian of the councils, calls him a layman living in Constantinople.

His chief work was translation. He put sermons of Nestorius, the deposed bishop of Constantinople, into Latin, with letters of Nestorius and of Cyril of Alexandria. He wanted Latin readers to know these sayings and avoid them. Hefele says Nestorius's twelve anathemas against Cyril survive only in Mercator's Latin.

He wrote as a partisan, and Garnier gathers from him rumors about Caelestius and Julian. In a book against Julian, Mercator traced the error back to Theodore of Mopsuestia.

No held line gives his death. Garnier thought he wrote nothing after the Council of Chalcedon. Baluze thought he did, since he names the followers of Eutyches.

In one preface he set out his method. He had tried to turn the Greek word for word, he wrote. He did not want to be proved a forger rather than a true translator.

## martin-of-tours

Martin of Tours

In the fourth century, before a battle at Worms, the Caesar Julian was paying each of his soldiers a gift of money. A young soldier named Martin asked for his discharge instead. As his friend Sulpicius Severus reports it, he said: "I am Christ's soldier. I am not allowed to fight."

When Julian called it cowardice, Martin offered to stand unarmed before the battle line, and was put back in prison. The next day the enemy surrendered before any battle, and Sulpicius credits Martin with that peace.

From the army Martin went to Hilary, bishop of Poitiers, in Roman Gaul, in what is now France, and later set up a monastery nearby. Years after that, the people of Tours made him bishop, yet he lived on as a monk, in a wooden cell with eighty disciples who held everything in common. Sulpicius wrote his life while he still lived, a book a modern historian calls a model for later lives of saints.

Cities wanted their priests from Martin's monastery, and Sulpicius saw many of his monks made bishops. Almost all we know comes from that one admirer, who met him late in life. Martin's tomb made Tours a place kings and bishops honored, as Gregory, bishop of Tours almost two hundred years later, tells it.

Martin was born to pagans in what is now Hungary. At ten, against their wish, he asked to be taught for baptism. He was fifteen when his father informed on him, and he was chained and sworn into the army.

One winter, at the gate of Amiens, the cold was killing people. Martin had already given away the rest of his clothes, so with his sword he cut his cloak in two and gave half to a man with no clothes. Sulpicius says the next night Martin saw Christ in a dream, wearing the half he had given, and heard him say Martin, still only a catechumen, had clothed him. He was baptized at about twenty, but stayed nearly two more years in the army, because his tribune promised to leave the world with him.

After the army, Arian opponents had him beaten in public and driven from a city, and expelled him from a monastery he had set up at Milan. At his election, some bishops scorned his poor looks, clothes and hair, yet as bishop he kept that plain dress. He tore down pagan temples and built churches where they had stood. Sometimes his preaching moved people to pull down their own, but once a crowd drove him off and he was hurt.

At Trier, bishops were prosecuting Priscillian and his followers for heresy before the emperor Maximus. Martin did not defend their teaching, but he begged that their blood not be shed. In Sulpicius's report he said: "It was a cruel wrong, never heard of before, for a judge of this world to try a case of the church." The trial waited while he was there, but after he left, Priscillian and his companions were put to death. On a later visit, Martin won the lives of men already condemned by sharing communion for one day with the bishops behind the deaths. He refused to sign his name, grieved over it, and never again went to a gathering of bishops.

Old, and knowing his end was near, he went to Candes, a village of his diocese, to make peace among quarreling clergy. He died there, and at his funeral, it was said, almost two thousand monks gathered.

## melania-the-elder

Melania the Elder

A Roman noblewoman lost her husband, and before he was buried, two of her sons died. Jerome of Strido tells it to Paula of Rome. Not a tear fell, he says. She threw herself at the feet of Christ. "Henceforth, Lord," she said, "I will serve Thee more readily, for Thou hast freed me from a great burden." She gave all she had to her only son and sailed for Jerusalem.

She was Melania, called the Elder because her granddaughter shared her name. Palladius of Helenopolis says she was widowed at twenty-two and sailed first to Alexandria. In Egypt she spent half a year with the monks of Nitria. When their leaders were exiled to Palestine, she followed and fed them, dressed as a slave. The governor threw her in prison. She told him whose daughter and wife she was, "but I am Christ's slave." He apologized and released her.

She then founded a monastery in Jerusalem, Palladius writes, and led fifty women there for twenty-seven years. Rufinus of Aquileia lived there with her. They welcomed pilgrims and healed a split among some four hundred monks. Jerome's Chronicle says her humility earned her the name of Thecla, an early woman saint.

Rufinus, her companion and by then Jerome's opponent, says Jerome cut her from every copy of the Chronicle, for disapproving of some of his deeds. In 415 Jerome wrote of "her whose name bears witness to the blackness of her perfidy." The notes to W. H. Fremantle's edition read this as a pun on her name, meaning black.

At sixty, Palladius says, she sailed back to Rome to guard her family from false teaching, and urged them to give up their wealth. To senators who opposed her she said, "Little children, it was written 400 years ago, It is the last hour." She returned to Jerusalem and died within forty days, leaving "both a monastery in Jerusalem and an endowment for it."

## melitius-of-lycopolis

Melitius of Lycopolis

Epiphanius of Salamis tells of a prison in Egypt during the Great Persecution, early in the fourth century. Peter, bishop of Alexandria, was held there with Melitius of Lycopolis and other clergy. They split over Christians who had given way. Peter wanted to be gentle with them. Melitius wanted to be strict. Peter hung a cloak across the prison like a curtain. A deacon called out that Peter's friends should come to his side, and the others to Melitius. Most of the clergy and monks, Epiphanius says, went to Melitius.

Epiphanius, a foe of every heresy, wrote some seventy years later. Archibald Robertson, who edited Athanasius, calls this the Melitians' own account. H. Idris Bell, who published papyri of their church in 1924, thinks Epiphanius had good sources.

Melitius was bishop of Lycopolis, in Upper Egypt. Two letters of the time speak against him. Four bishops in prison scolded him for making priests in their churches. One of them was Phileas of Thmuis. Peter wrote that Melitius had "ordained in the prison several unto himself." He told his flock to shun him.

Athanasius of Alexandria later held Peter's see. He wrote that Peter put Melitius out for many crimes, among them "offering sacrifice to idols." Bell doubts this. He calls Athanasius an unbending foe of the Melitians. Such charges came easily after a persecution, he says. He and Robertson agree the split began over church order. It did not begin over doctrine.

In 325 the Council of Nicaea let Melitius keep the title of bishop. But he could not ordain. Its letter, kept by Socrates of Constantinople, left him "a nominal dignity." Sozomen, the church historian, says that near the end he named his friend John to follow him. This broke the council's terms.

His followers called themselves the Church of the Martyrs, Epiphanius says. Later they sided with the Arians against Athanasius. Bell traces them in Egypt into the eighth century.

## mesrop-mashtots

Mesrop Mashtots

A Syrian bishop's letters could not carry the sounds of Armenian, and Mesrop Mashtots prayed with tears, day and night, for letters that could. Then, his pupil Koriun writes: "At once he saw them. It was not in a dream while he slept, nor in a vision while he was awake, but in his heart. There they appeared to the eyes of his mind, traced by the wrist of a right hand writing on stone."

The letters, Koriun means, came whole, as an inner sight. Nearly all we know rests on this pupil, who says he saw these men with his own eyes. By his account, this royal secretary turned monk made the letters of the Armenian language, so that the faith could be preached, written and taught in Armenian. He worked with the king and with Sahak, the catholicos who led the Armenian church. Together they began putting the Scriptures into Armenian and founded schools on both sides of the Persian and Roman border.

As a monk he lived on herbs and slept on bare ground.

In Koghten he preached to people who kept pagan ways, but with no letters for Armenian, preaching was hard. On the Persian side of the divided country, Koriun says, only Syriac learning was allowed.

King Vramshapuh sent Mesrop to Syrian Mesopotamia, the Syriac lands to the south. Mesrop made new letters with a hermit named Rufinus, who shaped them on his directions, after the Greek alphabet.

Translation began at once, with Proverbs. In the early 400s he brought the letters to Sahak and the king. "They rejoiced over them as if they were the divine Tablets," Koriun writes: the tablets of the Law that God gave Moses. "Mesrop gathered children who were bright and well taught, with sweet voices and long breath," and schools opened in every province and village.

Koriun says he then gave the Georgians an alphabet and renewed that of the Albanians, a Christian people of the eastern Caucasus. The Georgian chronicle gives Georgia's script to an ancient king.

Back in Armenia, because of that ban, Koriun says, Sahak and Mesrop went west into Roman Armenia. Sahak wrote to the emperor and the bishop of Constantinople and sent Mesrop to them. In Roman Armenia, children studied the letters at public cost. Koriun tells of a warm welcome; a later historian, Moses of Khoren, says the letters were first refused. In the emperor's letter Moses gives, Sahak is blamed for serving a pagan king and for turning to Syrians, not to Constantinople's scholars.

Mesrop fought the Borborites, a sect Koriun calls rash and insolent. Koriun never says what they taught. Those who would not yield, he writes, were put in prison and chains and tormented; those who still refused were burned, shut up and driven from the land.

Sahak and Mesrop sent pupils to Edessa, a center of Syriac learning, and from there they went to Constantinople. They brought home true copies of the Scriptures, and the first hasty translations were corrected. When the council of Ephesus warned of a man it had shut out, whose books, Koriun says, followed Nestorius, Sahak and Mesrop drove him out. That council's quarrel turned on whether Mary could be called Mother of God; Nestorius urged Mother of Christ instead.

When Sahak died, Mesrop wept, finding no one to share the work of Armenia. After a short illness, he sat up among his disciples, raised his arms, blessed the Trinity and entrusted them to God. Koriun says a cross of light was seen over the house.

Every later Armenian witness we have names him the letters' maker, some beside Sahak. Lazar, a later historian who credits the translating to Sahak, says once the letters and Scriptures came, copyists multiplied, and men and women crowded the churches. Mesrop's pupils became the next writers: Koriun, Eznik, and, by his own account, Moses of Khoren.

The hand Koriun saw writing on stone had left letters, and those pupils carried them on.

## moses-the-black

Moses the Black

Around the year 400, the desert fathers of Egypt counted Moses among their great ones. He was a priest at Scete, out in the desert, and he left seventy disciples. Yet he began as a household slave, whom Palladius, a bishop writing around 420, calls an Ethiopian by race, and black. His master drove him out for immorality and robbery, and he was even said to have murdered. Palladius tells of these crimes to show what his repentance could do.

People said Moses went on to lead a band of robbers by the Nile. In time, Palladius says only, one of his adventures left him conscience-stricken, and he gave himself to a monastery.

Four robbers once attacked him in his cell, not knowing who he was. Moses tied them up, carried them on his back to the church, and asked the brothers, "I am not allowed to hurt anyone. So what do you tell me to do with these?" When the robbers learned who had caught them, they confessed. "If a man so great and powerful in robbery has feared God," they said, "why should we put off our salvation?" They left the world because of his conversion.

His own fight was long, and by his own account his old lust nearly made him give up. For six years he stood all night in his cell, but still he could not master it. In the end Isidore, the priest of Scete, told him his dreams had ceased and sent him to Communion. Two months later, Moses said he suffered nothing more.

Sayings gathered in the 600s remember the fathers of Scete scorning him. "Why does this Ethiopian come and go among us?" they said. He kept silent, and afterwards said he had been afraid but had not said a word. Once a bishop, wishing to test him, had the clergy drive him out of the sanctuary. Outside, he said to himself, "They have treated you rightly, you whose skin is dark and black. You shall not go back in as if you were a man."

Palladius says he died at Scete, aged seventy-five.

## nestorius

Nestorius

A book about Christ came down to us under the name of Heracleides, a man who lived near Damascus. Its translator into Syriac, the Aramaic language of a church in Persia, explains why: "The writer chose to give the book this man's name. He feared that his own name, which many hated, would stop them from reading it and turning to the truth." The writer was Nestorius.

From 428 he was bishop, the leader of the churches, in Constantinople, the empire's capital. He taught that Christ is God and man, the two kept distinct yet worshiped as one Christ. So he would not simply call Mary "Mother of God", the one who gave birth to God. In 431 a council at Ephesus, a meeting of bishops called to settle teaching, was led by his enemy, Cyril of Alexandria. It judged that he split Christ in two and removed him from office. He denied it, and died in exile, forced to live far away under guard. Historians still argue over what he really taught.

Socrates, a historian of the city in his lifetime, tells of the first sermon this newcomer from Antioch preached. In it he asked the emperor for "the earth purged of heretics", people the church judged to teach falsely. Later he had the emperor take churches from a rival Christian group.

Socrates also says he rejected the name "Mother of God" outright. Nestorius says he offered a name drawn from the Gospel's own words, "Mother of Christ".

In a sermon kept by his opponents, he said: "God is not separated from the one who is seen. So I do not separate the honor of the one who is not separated. I separate the natures, but I unite the worship." The natures are what Christ is as God and as man, two, yet Christians bow in worship to one Christ. Socrates, who blames his temper, says he did not deny that Christ was God, but feared the name "as though it were some terrible phantom".

In 435, four years after the council, the emperor's law ordered his writings burned. Banished the next year to southern Egypt, he was dragged to and fro by soldiers, old and sick, with "a mangled hand and side", his own letters say.

Near the end, perhaps in 451, he wrote his defense in Greek under the borrowed name. In it he offered his own name to be cut off under the church's solemn curse: "So let Nestorius be anathema. Only let them say of God what I beg them to say."

His Greek is lost. The book survives because the Church of the East, the church of Persia beyond the Roman empire, put it into Syriac, perhaps a century later. Its rites, its set services, call him "the persecuted martyr", one who suffered for the faith. Yet that church would not be called his. In 1298 its chief bishop of Nisibis wrote that Nestorius had never been its patriarch, its head. They had held the faith in two natures already, he wrote, and they refused to cut him off: "Nestorius, then, followed them, and not they him."

When and how he died is not known. In his last book he bids the desert rejoice for him. He calls the land of his exile his mother, which will guard his body until the resurrection, when the dead rise at the end of time.

## olympias-of-constantinople

Olympias of Constantinople

On the day John Chrysostom was sent into exile, the cathedral of Constantinople burned. Socrates of Constantinople says some of John's followers set the fire. The church historian Sozomen says each side blamed the other. The deaconess Olympias was brought before the prefect and asked why she had done it. "My past life ought to avert all suspicion from me," she answered, "for I have devoted my large property to the restoration of the temples of God."

Nothing could be proved, Sozomen says, so the prefect pressed her and other women to take communion with the new bishop, Arsacius. The others gave way. Olympias told him, "I will not hold communion with those from whom I ought to secede." He fined her heavily, and she left the city for Cyzicus.

Palladius of Helenopolis says she was the daughter and granddaughter of high imperial officials. She married Nebridius, a former prefect of the city, and was soon widowed. The Dialogue on John's life, which its translator Herbert Moore gives to Palladius, says the emperor Theodosius wanted her to marry his kinsman Elpidius. She refused, and he put her wealth in trust until she was thirty. She answered that he would do better to have it "dispersed among the poor, and the Churches." Later he gave it back.

Sozomen says the bishop Nectarius made her a deaconess, though she had been widowed young. She gave to anyone who asked, and John told her to give "with economy." Palladius says she "instructed many women." The Dialogue names bishops she gave land and money, Epiphanius of Salamis among them.

Seventeen of John's surviving letters are to her. In the first he writes, "there is only one thing, Olympias, which is really terrible, only one real trial, and that is sin." W. R. W. Stephens, John's translator, says nothing definite is known of her later life. The people of Constantinople, Palladius writes, "reckon her life among the confessors."

## ossius-of-cordoba

Ossius of Cordoba

Ossius, bishop of Cordoba in Spain, wrote to the emperor Constantius, a son of Constantine. "I was a Confessor at the first, when a persecution arose in the time of your grandfather Maximian," he began. The emperor had pressed him to sign against Athanasius of Alexandria. Ossius told him to "remember that you are a mortal man." "Intrude not yourself into Ecclesiastical matters," he wrote. God had given the emperor his kingdom, and the bishops the affairs of the church.

The letter survives only in Athanasius's History of the Arians. It defends Athanasius, who praises Ossius all through, so his account takes a side. He says Ossius had been a bishop for "more than sixty years."

Socrates of Constantinople says Constantine sent Ossius with a letter to Alexander of Alexandria and Arius. The Latin lists of those who signed at the Council of Nicaea put "Osius" first. Theodoret of Cyrrhus calls him the "most highly distinguished" bishop there. Whether he led that council is disputed. Athanasius asks when a council was ever held "in which he did not take the lead." Henry Percival, who edited the council records, finds no proof that he was in the chair.

At the council of Sardica, about 343 by Percival's date, Theodoret says he held "the first place." Its canons open with his words against bishops who move from a small city to a larger one. The eastern bishops met apart and deposed him, says Sozomen, a church historian of the next century.

Athanasius says Constantius held him for a whole year at Sirmium. Ossius was "now a hundred years old." At last, broken by suffering, he agreed to share communion with the bishops his letter had warned against. Even so, he would not sign against Athanasius. In Socrates's telling, he was beaten and tortured until he signed their creed.

As death drew near, Athanasius says, "he bore witness to the force which had been used towards him, and anathematized the Arian heresy."

## pachomius

Pachomius

In the Greek Life of Pachomius, by a monk of the next generation, the great hermit Antony speaks to Pachomius's monks after his death. He tells them: "When I first began to live as a monk, there was no community anywhere in which anyone cared or feared for the salvation of others. Once the persecution was over, each of the early monks trained himself in the monk's life on his own. Later, with God's help, your father did this great good."

Before him, in Upper Egypt, each monk lived by his own way, a hermit in a cell, sometimes with a pupil. Pachomius drew such monks into one shared life under one written rule. His brothers lived in houses inside a walled monastery, each house under a head. They wore the same clothes, ate at one table and worked at their trades together. Nine monasteries came to form one fellowship.

Born to pagan parents, he was taken at about twenty for Constantine's war, early in the fourth century. Locked up, he asked who the strangers feeding the recruits were. His own communities' Coptic Life gives the answer: "They are Christian people. They treat us this way out of love for the God of heaven." That night he vowed to serve people all his life. Freed and baptized, he became a hermit's pupil, until at a deserted village called Tabennisi he began to build.

He would have no priests among his monks, and when a bishop wanted him ordained, he hid. At Esneh, monks and bishops questioned him over claims that he read hidden thoughts. As he finished, a man driven by an evil spirit burst in with a sword to kill him, but the Greek Life says the Lord saved him with the help of those present.

In 346 or 348, as plague swept the monasteries, he fell ill and asked for a lighter blanket. When he saw it was finer than the brothers' own, he sent it back: "Take this blanket away from here at once. It is not right that I should be better off in anything than the other brothers." After his death the fellowship split, though Theodore's letters urged the breakaways back.

Palladius, writing about seventy years later, counted some seven thousand monks under the rule. Latin-speaking monks there could not read its Egyptian or Greek, so priests sent Jerome the books, pressing him. About 404 he dictated a Latin version for them, and for his own brothers and Eustochium's nuns. Through that Latin the rule reached the West. In the early ninth century the abbot Benedict of Aniane set it under the chapters of Benedict's Rule, as one of the old rules it agreed with.

## palladius-of-helenopolis

Palladius of Helenopolis

At Nitria, in Egypt, Palladius of Helenopolis heard his teacher Evagrius Ponticus wish to know what kind of man John of Lycopolis was. Next day he shut his cell and set off for the Thebaid. He arrived eighteen days later, on foot and by river boat.

John, a hermit, spoke to visitors through a window. He asked if Palladius wanted to be a bishop. Palladius joked that he was bishop of the kitchen pots. "This is my bishopric, for gluttony ordained me." John smiled. "You have to be ordained bishop, and toil much and be afflicted."

Palladius tells this in his Lausiac History. He lived as a monk near Alexandria and in the desert. Illness took him to Palestine and Bithynia, where he was made bishop, "having become embroiled in the disturbance connected with the blessed John." W. K. Lowther Clarke, its translator, takes this John for John Chrysostom, bishop of Constantinople.

A Dialogue on Chrysostom's life tells what followed. Herbert Moore, who translated it, follows Cuthbert Butler, who edited the Lausiac History, in giving it to Palladius. Emeritus Bigot, its first editor, suggested another Palladius. In it, Palladius reaches Rome with a copy of an edict against John's friends. Later he is under guard at Syene, far to the south.

Foes in the quarrel over Origen attacked a Palladius. Epiphanius of Salamis, in a letter Jerome of Strido put into Latin, warned that "Palladius of Galatia" taught "the heresy of Origen." Jerome wrote that a Palladius, "no better than a villainous slave," tried to revive the same heresy. Notes to W. H. Fremantle's edition of Jerome take both for this Palladius. The historian Tillemont, they report, thought another Palladius may be meant. Palladius wrote that Jerome's temper threw "into the shade his splendid achievements."

He wrote the Lausiac History for Lausus, a court chamberlain. Its prologue says, "I welcomed all the hardship of the way so long as I might meet some man that loved God."

## paula-of-rome

Paula of Rome

Paula came from a noble Roman family. Jerome says it traced its line to the Gracchi and the Scipios. She married Toxotius and bore five children, among them Eustochium, Rufina and a son, Toxotius. When her husband died, Jerome writes, her grief nearly killed her. She then gave herself to the service of the Lord.

Later she sailed east from Portus with Eustochium. On the shore her small son held out his hands, and Rufina begged her to wait until she was married. Jerome says her eyes stayed dry. She toured the holy places, then settled at Bethlehem and built cells and monastic buildings there.

She set up a monastery for men. She also gathered women from many provinces and divided them into three houses, which met for psalms and prayer. She read the Old and New Testaments under Jerome's guidance. She learned Hebrew well enough to chant the psalms in it. Jerome says she died after twenty years at Bethlehem, aged fifty-six.
