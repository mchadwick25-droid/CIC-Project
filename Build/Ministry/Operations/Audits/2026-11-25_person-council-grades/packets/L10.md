# Packet L10: person and council stories (census longDescription)

## cuthbert-of-lindisfarne

Cuthbert of Lindisfarne

One night a young shepherd kept watch over his flock in the hills. As he prayed, he saw light pour down from the sky, and choirs of angels carry a soul up to heaven. In the morning he learned that Aidan, bishop of Lindisfarne, had died at that hour. He gave the sheep back to their owners and set out for a monastery.

The young man was Cuthbert of Lindisfarne, a monk and bishop in Northumbria, in northern England, in the seventh century. Bede of Jarrow tells this in his Latin prose Life of him. A monk of Lindisfarne wrote an earlier Life, in which the news came some days later. Both are saints' Lives, full of miracles.

He rode up to Melrose, on the river Tweed, with a spear in his hand. The prior Boisil, at the door, said only, in Bede's Latin, "Ecce servus Dei": here is a servant of God.

After the meeting at Whitby in 664, the Scots, as Bede calls the Irish, left Lindisfarne. Eata, abbot of Melrose, became its abbot too, and later moved Cuthbert there to teach the monks' rule. Then Cuthbert went to live alone on the island of Farne. The bank around his cell was so high, Bede says, that he could see nothing but heaven.

A synod at Twyford, under Archbishop Theodore of Tarsus, chose him bishop. When letters failed, King Egfrid of Northumbria crossed to Farne with Bishop Trumwine and the brothers. They knelt and wept until they drew him, weeping too, from his cell.

After two years as bishop he went back to Farne to die. Herefrid, abbot of Lindisfarne, told Bede his last words. Keep peace among yourselves, he said, and have no fellowship with those who keep Easter at the wrong time. He would rather they dug up his bones and left than bowed to schismatics.

Eleven years after his burial, Bede says, the brothers opened his grave. They found his body whole, "more like one asleep than a dead person".

## cyril-and-methodius

Cyril and Methodius

The emperor asked a tired scholar to go to the Slavs. He would go gladly, the scholar said, "if they have letters for their language." Without them, he asked, "who can write words on water, and who wants to be called a heretic?"

The scholar was Constantine, and the Slavs had no letters, so he made them. With his brother Methodius he put the Gospel, the Psalms, the readings from the apostles and chosen services into Slavic. When Latin clergy said God could be praised only in Hebrew, Greek and Latin, Constantine answered that every people may praise God in its own language. Their work began in Moravia, in what is now the Czech Republic. After Methodius died, a Life of their disciple Clement says, the younger disciples were sold as slaves, and the teachers were chained, stripped and driven out. Clement, Naum and Angelarius, it says, carried the letters to Bulgaria.

That Life was written in Bulgaria a generation or more later. In it Prince Boris thanks God for sending them, and Clement becomes "the first bishop of the Bulgarian tongue." Writing in Kiev more than two centuries later, the chroniclers said this Slavic writing was found among the Rus.

It had begun in the 860s, when Rastislav, prince of Moravia, told the emperor that his people had teachers from Italy, Greece and Germany, but none in their own language. The emperor chose the two brothers because they came from Thessalonica, a Greek city where Slavic was spoken too.

Most of what we know comes from two Lives written by their own followers. They survive only in copies made centuries later. In Venice, his Life says, Constantine asked the Latin clergy: "Does God's rain not fall on all alike?"

In Rome, the Lives tell, Constantine fell ill, became a monk named Cyril, and died in 869. His last words to Methodius were: "Look, brother, we were partners plowing a single furrow. And I fall in the field, having finished my day." Methodius loved the monks' mountain, but Constantine asked him not to give up his teaching for its sake.

Methodius went back as a bishop, and his Life says Frankish bishops held him prisoner for two and a half years. A Salzburg writer of his day complained that this "Greek" with "newly invented Slavic letters" had partly cheapened, in the people's eyes, the Latin Masses and Gospels. Pope John VIII repeated a ban on Slavonic Mass in 879, but in 880 he allowed it, with the Gospel read first in Latin. He wrote: "For he who made the three chief languages, Hebrew, Greek and Latin, also himself created all the others, for his praise and glory."

Methodius died in 885. His Life says his disciples held the funeral in Latin, Greek and Slavonic, and crowds came, rich and poor, free and slave.

## dhuoda

Dhuoda

"I see many people in this world who rejoice in their children with them. But I, Dhuoda, see myself kept far away from you, my son William." So begins the handbook of faith that a Frankish noblewoman, living at Uzès in the south of France, dictated for her elder son. She began it in November 841, when William had just turned fifteen and had been sent to serve King Charles. That March she had borne a second son, and before he was baptized, her husband Bernard had the baby brought to him, with the bishop of Uzès.

She meant the book to stand in her place. Reading it and praying, she wrote, William could see her, "mind and body, as in a picture in a mirror." She was a noblewoman, not one of the clergy. Other teachers would teach him more, "but they will not teach on equal terms, with a heart burning in the breast, as I do, your mother." And she told William to show the book to his little brother when the boy could read.

She worked on it for more than a year, and in seventy-three chapters she set out a Christian life for a young man at court. He must love God first and then his father, and pray in short, pure words. He must serve Charles faithfully and honor priests, help the poor, widows, orphans and pilgrims, and judge without taking gifts. And he must pray for the Church, for kings and for the dead.

When the emperor Louis, Charlemagne's son, died in 840, his sons, Charles among them, went to war over the empire. Bernard, duke of Septimania, was in Aquitaine, and Dhuoda stayed on at Uzès, as she says, at her lord's command.

In 844 the Franks judged Bernard guilty of treason, and on Charles's order he was put to death. In 848 William seized Ampurias and Barcelona by trickery, and in 850 he was captured by greater trickery and killed at Barcelona.

Her book outlived the family. It was copied and kept, and printed in part in 1677 and whole in 1887. Its editor called it the oldest writing by a woman born in France to reach us. In 1923 two translators of the Catholic catechism named her manual among early works of Christian instruction, to answer the claim that the catechism began with Luther.

Almost all we know of her comes from her own book. The chronicles of her day name her husband and her son, but not her. In the book she writes her own epitaph and asks William to have it written on her grave, so that passers-by will pray for her. No record tells when or where Dhuoda died.

## dionysius-of-tel-mahre

Dionysius of Tel Mahre

In Baghdad, in March 829, the caliph al-Ma'mun let the patriarch Dionysius of Tel Mahre come to him alone in his garden. Lazarus, a deposed bishop of Baghdad, cited the caliph's decree that any ten men of a faith could choose a leader. The Christians' fathers, Dionysius answered, had handed many cities to the Muslims on promises that their laws would stand. A law, he said, cannot be kept without a lawgiver, and the church's leadership was one of those laws. Al-Ma'mun ruled that the church could depose a sinner. It could not shut him out of church and prayer, for sinners above all must pray.

Bar Hebraeus tells this in his thirteenth-century Ecclesiastical Chronicle.

He led the Syriac Orthodox church as patriarch of Antioch from 818 until his death in 845. That church refused the Council of Chalcedon and lived under the caliphs. He was a monk of Kenneshre. In words Bar Hebraeus quotes, he tells how two monks seized him like a criminal. He pleaded, weeping, that he was unfit. The bishops made him deacon on a Friday, priest on Saturday, and patriarch on Sunday.

The emir Abdallah, son of Tahir, had gone down to Egypt, and his brother tore down new church buildings in Edessa. Dionysius sailed after the emir, and a storm drove him into Tanis. The emir scolded him for coming instead of writing, then wrote to his brother forbidding attacks on the churches. Dionysius won the people of Tanis a tax graded by wealth, in place of five dinars a head.

Later al-Ma'mun sent him, with Joseph, patriarch of Egypt, to talk rebel Christians of Lower Egypt into peace. The general Afshin refused terms, burned their villages, killed many, and shipped the rest away as captives. Dionysius told the caliph they were being crushed.

He wrote a history at the request of John, metropolitan of Dara. Michael the Syrian, a twelfth-century patriarch, keeps its preface. There Dionysius protests that John overlooked his old age and the contempt of waiting at princes' doors. He says Theophilus of Edessa, a Chalcedonian, dropped every story that named anyone of Dionysius's church.

The history closed, Bar Hebraeus says, with words to John about the crushing taxes on Christians. He could not sleep, and wept over his life as he watched the church's children suffer. Death was the one release left to him, he wrote, and he sought it as a good thing.

## eadburga-of-thanet

Eadburga of Thanet

Boniface of Mainz, the English missionary in Germany, wrote to an abbess with a request. He asked her "to copy in gold the Epistles of my lord, Saint Peter". The Scriptures would then be honored, he wrote, "when the preacher holds them before the eyes of the heathen." She had often eased his distress already, he said, with gifts of books and vestments.

The abbess was Eadburga, who lived in the eighth century. Edward Kylie, who put Boniface's letters into English in 1911, heads the letters to her as letters to the abbess of Thanet. He dates this one to 735. None of her own letters is in his book. She is known from what others wrote to her and about her.

Boniface wrote to her over many years. About 717, by Kylie's date, he sent her an account she had asked for. A man in the convent of Abbess Milburga at Wenlock had died and come back to life. Boniface wrote down the vision the man told him. Later he thanked her for books of Scripture, which had consoled "an exile in Germany". In a hard time he asked for her prayers. The snares of false brethren, he wrote, "surpass the malice of the heathen."

Leoba, a young woman of Boniface's circle, learned verse from her. She sent Boniface four lines of her own. "The art I have learned from the teaching of Eadburga," Leoba wrote, "who continues without ceasing to search into the divine law." Kylie says Leoba had been sent to Thanet, where Eadburga taught her the art of verse. Lul, Boniface's deacon, wrote to Eadburga too. He sent her a silver stylus, storax and cinnamon, and asked for her prayers.

The Bollandists, editors of the Acta Sanctorum, report a belief that she was an abbess in Kent also called Bugga. Kylie keeps the two women apart. His Bugga is the daughter of another abbess, Eangyth, and her own name was Heaburg.

Boniface opened one letter to her this way: "To the Abbess Eadburga, beloved sister, now long bound to him in the kinship of spiritual love."

## elias-of-nisibis

Elias of Nisibis

Under the Arab year 397, which began in 1006, Elias of Nisibis recorded a split among Christians. The Christians of the West and of the East did not agree on how to reckon the fast before Easter and the feasts. The West set the start of the fast on 17 Shebat. The East set it on the 24th.

Elias was metropolitan of Nisibis in the Church of the East. He set this down in his Chronography, which E. W. Brooks, who put its first part into Latin, dates to 1019. It runs year by year, and for almost every year it names its sources, Greek, Syriac and Arabic works, most of them now lost. For its last years it names none. There, Brooks writes, Elias tells what he remembered or had heard.

A note added after Elias's own entries tells his life, and Brooks marks it as the work of later hands. It says he was born in 975. John, bishop of Senn, ordained him priest in 994 at the monastery of Father Simeon. John later became catholicos, the head of the Church of the East. He made Elias bishop of Beth Nuhadra in 1002, and metropolitan of Nisibis in 1008.

In 1020 Isho'yahb son of Ezekiel was made catholicos. Bar Hebraeus, a later Syriac historian, says Isho'yahb paid the governor five thousand denarii. The governor ordered that all who would not consent be drowned, and many bishops refused to proclaim him. Joseph Assemani, writing in 1725, says Elias stood firm against him. In a letter to the people of Baghdad, Assemani says, Elias called Isho'yahb a buyer of his office.

Elias gathered rules on inheritance from a work of the catholicos Elias I. Ebedjesu of Soba, which is Nisibis, copied them into his book of canons. In his own preface to those rules, Elias prays for the soul of Elias I, who died in 1049. Assemani puts the metropolitan's death after that. J.-B. Chabot says he died soon after. Frédéric Macler gives about 1056.

The second part of the Chronography, on reckoning years and feasts, opens with a prayer for its reader, his beloved brother. May the Lord guide you, Elias wrote, to please him in your thoughts, words and deeds.

## elipandus-of-toledo-and-felix-of-urgell

Elipandus of Toledo and Felix of Urgell

At the end of one July, Elipandus, archbishop of Toledo, received a letter from his friend Felix. He wrote back that it came to him as if fallen from heaven, and that he thanked God with arms stretched out. He was now, he said, in worn-out old age, and had entered his eighty-second year. He still held the teaching for which both men had been condemned.

Felix was bishop of Urgell, a city the Frankish annals place on a ridge of the Pyrenees. In 794 the council of Frankfurt condemned the teaching as the heresy of them both. The sources tell the two men together, so they share one profile. Much of their story comes from the men who fought them.

Elipandus set out his view in a letter to Alcuin of York. Christ is adoptive, he wrote, only in the form of a servant, his humanity, and not in the glory or substance of his divine nature. As God, the Son is Son by nature, not by grace. Elipandus also cited the Toledo prayers for the mass of the Lord's Supper. As he quoted them, they spoke of the passion of the adoptive man.

His opponents heard two sons in this. Pope Hadrian I likened it to Nestorius, who confessed the Son of God a mere man. Alcuin wrote that Felix divided Christ into a proper son and an adoptive one. Elipandus called Alcuin a new Arius.

The Frankish annals say Felix was condemned at Regensburg in 792 and renounced his teaching before Pope Hadrian at Rome. In 799 he came to the palace at Aachen, where Alcuin says he was heard, proved wrong, and returned to the peace of the church. Felix then wrote to his clergy that he came back with no pretense this time, as there had been once, which God knows.

Froben, Alcuin's editor, reports from a book of Agobard of Lyon that Felix still taught some people his errors, and held to them until he died. In a letter Froben dates to 800, Alcuin says Elipandus still held his view. No source of their own time gives the year either man died.

Elipandus had defended his friend to Alcuin. He had known Felix from early youth, he wrote, as a man of the highest charity, chaste and of good conduct. Yet Alcuin, he wrote, now hunted him in the mountains and the caves.

## gregory-of-narek

Gregory of Narek

Messengers came from the city of Ani to the convent of Narek. An assembly of chiefs and clergy had sent them to bring its abbot, Gregory, to answer for his faith. He welcomed them and served roasted pigeons. They objected that it was Friday, a fast day. Then, the story goes, he told the birds to rise and go, and they flew away.

Gregory of Narek was a monk of tenth-century Armenia. Michael Chamich tells this story in his History of Armenia, printed in 1784. James Issaverdens retells it in 1875. Both were Mekhitarists, monks of an Armenian order at Venice. "We, however, report this merely as an anecdote," Issaverdens adds, "as it shews the public esteem towards the holy man."

Chamich says Gregory was raised from infancy at Narek, in the land of the Rushtunians, and in time became its abbot. Giovanni de Serpos, writing at Venice in 1786, calls Gregory the pupil of Anania, a learned abbot of Narek.

Chamich and Issaverdens say Gregory tried to unite the Armenians and the Greeks by bringing his people to accept the council of Chalcedon. Malachia Ormanian, a former Armenian patriarch of Constantinople, names their order among those that won Armenians over to the Catholic side. Serpos says Gregory worked to calm the quarrels over Chalcedon. Stepanos of Taron, of Gregory's own century, wrote Armenian history. He says teachers including Anania of Narek ruled that anyone baptized in the Chalcedonian faith be baptized again.

Chamich says Gregory prayed long to see the Virgin with her Son, and was granted the sight. Then he wrote the book of prayers called Narek. Chamich has him comment on the Song of Songs and on Job at twenty-six. Serpos says twenty, for a Bagratid king.

Two of his prayers to the Holy Spirit stand in the Armenian Liturgy, as Issaverdens translates it. In the first, said at the foot of the altar, the one leading the service prays, "we implore Thee with out-stretched arms and deep groans." In one breviary manuscript edited by F. C. Conybeare, a meditation of his follows the prayers before sleep. It begins, "Accept, O powerful Lord God, in thy loving-kindness the prayer of him that has turned again to thee."

Chamich says he died at fifty, and Ormanian gives the year as 1003. Ormanian writes that the Narek is used for private prayer, and is "regarded as a potent talisman against all kinds of dangers."

## heraclius

Heraclius

Macler's note to Sebeos says the patriarch Sergius crowned Heraclius on 6 October 610. A note in Mansi says he died on 11 February 641. Sebeos writes that the king received the holy Cross and brought it to Jerusalem, with all the church vessels that had escaped the enemy in Byzantium. There he set it back in its place, and put the church's objects back where they belonged. He says it stayed in the holy city until the sons of Ishmael took Jerusalem the second time.

The Ecthesis opens as a statement of faith made by Heraclius, because a dispute had arisen over the question of operation. It says it agrees with the five holy councils. It forbids anyone to speak of or teach one operation or two. It also calls it impious to confess two wills in Christ that come against each other.

The Lateran fathers of 649 say the Ecthesis was written in the name of Heraclius, then emperor, by enemies of the faith. They say Sergius persuaded Heraclius, then emperor, to put his name to it. A letter quoted in the Mansi notes has Heraclius say the Ecthesis is not his. He says he did not dictate it or order it made. Sergius wrote it five years earlier, before Heraclius came back from the East. When the emperor reached the city, Sergius asked him to set it out in his name, with his signature, and he agreed. The Lateran council anathematized the Ecthesis.

## hild-of-whitby

Hild of Whitby

Caedmon, a man of Hild's house and no longer young, would leave the feast whenever the harp came near, because he could not sing. One night, Bede says, someone stood by him in his sleep and told him to sing the beginning of created things. In the morning he was brought to the abbess, Hild. Learned men judged the gift a grace from God, and she made him a monk and had him taught sacred history, which he turned into English song.

Hild ruled a monastery of men and women at Whitby, in Northumbria, and ran it on peace and charity. After the example of the earliest church, all goods were shared, so that no one there was rich and no one poor. She made her people study the Scriptures, so that many were fit to be priests.

Of her house Bede wrote, "Later we saw five bishops come from that same monastery. All of them were men of rare merit and holiness." In those days a bishop led the church of a whole region, and the English church was still young. Ordinary people, Bede writes, and sometimes even kings and princes, sought her advice.

Almost all we know of her comes from Bede, a monk in the same kingdom, who wrote about fifty years after she died. She was the daughter of King Edwin's nephew, and took the faith with King Edwin at the preaching of Paulinus. After thirty-three years of ordinary life she chose the monastic life, and after Bishop Aidan called her home from the East Angles, she ruled first Hartlepool, then Whitby.

The newly converted English had their faith from Rome and from the Irish monks of Iona, whom Bede calls the Scots, and the two kept Easter on different dates. In 664 the kings met in her house to settle the date of Easter. Bishop Colman led the Scots' side, and Hild and her people held with it, while the priest Wilfrid argued for Rome. King Oswy ruled for Rome's side, for Peter, keeper of heaven's keys, and Bede says all present agreed. Colman, his teaching rejected, went home with those who would not accept it, but Bede does not say how Hild took the ruling.

A fever held Hild for six years, and she never stopped teaching her people. In the seventh year, in 680, she called the women together at cockcrow and urged them to keep the peace of the Gospel among themselves and with all others. All who knew her called her Mother.

## hincmar-of-reims

Hincmar of Reims

In 882 the Northmen were burning the country around Laon and making for Reims. Its archbishop, Hincmar, fled by night with the body of Saint Remigius and the treasures of his church. He was so weak he had to be carried in a chair. His canons, monks and nuns scattered, and he barely reached Épernay, beyond the Marne. No wall or human hand defended the city, he wrote, but God's power and the merits of the saints kept the raiders out.

His annals end that year, the year he died, Theodor Mommsen says. Hincmar was archbishop of Reims, in Frankish Gaul. A table of dates in Mansi's council volumes puts his ordination in 845. From 861 he carried on the annals that Prudentius of Troyes had kept. G. H. Pertz, an earlier editor, held that Hincmar wrote them, and warned that they need care where his own affairs are concerned.

He fought for years with his nephew and namesake, Hincmar, bishop of Laon. The annals call the nephew a bishop in name only, a man of singular insolence, who rebelled against the king and scorned his archbishop. At Douzy in 871 a synod deposed him.

Its bishops, with the uncle named first, wrote to Pope Hadrian II. The uncle had raised the nephew as an orphan, they said, taught him his letters and made him a bishop. If anyone tricked Rome into restoring him, they warned, he would do as he liked, knowing they could not send every case to Rome. The pope had blamed the uncle for leaving his nephew without help. In his own letter, the uncle wished he had never seen the man, still less ordained him. Hadrian rejected the sentence, a note in Mansi says, but Pope John VIII later wrote that it was just.

In his own complaint to Pope John, the nephew says he was seized on the road and exiled. He was kept for a time in chains, he says, and after nearly two years he was blinded. At Troyes in 878, the uncle's annals say, the nephew's supporters dressed the blind man in his vestments. Without the pope's order, they led him before the pope and into the church, singing.

Pertz calls the archbishop deeply versed in church law and in the affairs of his own time. Bruno Krusch, who edited his Life of Saint Remigius, judged him vain and deceitful, and his long sentences painful to read.

## humbert-of-silva-candida

Humbert of Silva Candida

On a Saturday in July, the clergy of Hagia Sophia in Constantinople were ready for Mass. Three envoys from Rome came in and laid a sentence of excommunication on the high altar. They went out, shook the dust from their feet, and cried out that God should see and judge.

Their own account names Humbert, cardinal bishop of Silva Candida, first. Pope Leo IX had sent him with Peter, archbishop of Amalfi, and Frederick, the chancellor. Cornelius Will, who edited it, believes Humbert wrote it. Will reads its date as 16 July 1054, and Carl Mirbt gives 13 July.

In 1053, Will says, Leo, archbishop of Ohrid, wrote against the Latins at the urging of Michael Keroularios, patriarch of Constantinople. Will dates the embassy to January 1054, and Pope Leo's death to 19 April. Wibert, Pope Leo's biographer, says Humbert wrote a Dialogue in the city, against the Greek charges.

A monk of the Studios monastery, Nicetas, had written a book against the Latins, on unleavened bread at Mass, the Sabbath and married priests. On 24 June, the account says, Nicetas cursed his own book before the emperor, and it was burned.

The envoys' account says the patriarch avoided them. Michael wrote that they came to him but would not greet him, bow, or sit below his bishops. Their sentence likens his party to the heretics of old. They sell the gift of God, it says, and they have cut from the creed the Spirit's going forth from the Son. It curses Michael, Leo of Ohrid and their followers, unless they repent.

The edict of Michael's synod calls the envoys men from the darkness of the West, who burst into the city like a wild boar. The Greeks will not corrupt the creed, it says, for they hold that the Spirit proceeds from the Father. The pope never sent the envoys, the edict adds. They came on the advice of Argyrus, the Greek governor in Italy, and forged the pope's letters. On 20 July, by Will's reading, the synod cursed the paper and its makers.

The envoys' account says Michael falsified the Greek version of their sentence to turn the crowd against them. Will takes their side and says the edict heaps lie on lie.

Will wrote that the sentence of 1054 made the split between the churches complete, and that it has lasted.

## ildefonsus-of-toledo

Ildefonsus of Toledo

The boy Ildefonsus had run away to the monastery of Agali to become a monk. His father came after him in a fury. Only a single wall stood between them. The father pushed on into the inner rooms and passed the place where the boy was hiding. Then he attacked the monastery, sword in hand, found nothing, and went home to mourn a lost son.

Ildefonsus became bishop of Toledo, in Visigothic Spain, in the seventh century. The story comes from Julian of Toledo, who lived in his time and later became bishop of the city. Julian's account is a short tribute to him. Julian says the boy came back to Agali once his father had given up, and lived there as a monk for many years. He built a convent for women and endowed it from his own wealth. Then he became abbot of Agali. An abbot Ildefonsus signed the acts of two councils of Toledo in the 650s.

The king's force brought him back to Toledo, Julian says, and he became bishop after Eugenius the second. He was made bishop in the ninth year of King Reccesvinth and held the office for nine years and about two months. He died on 23 January and was buried in the church of Saint Leocadia, at the feet of the bishop before him.

Julian lists many books. Among them is On the Virginity of Holy Mary, written, Julian says, against three unbelievers. Ildefonsus also carried on the list of famous church writers that Jerome began and Isidore of Seville continued. He wrote it, he says, so that silence would not hide the memory of the great men of his church.

A Life written later by Cixila, a bishop of Toledo under Muslim rule, adds a wonder. Leocadia, the virgin saint of the city, rose from her tomb before the king and the people. She is said to have thanked God that her Lady lived through the life of Ildefonsus.

## isaac-of-alexandria

Isaac of Alexandria

An archbishop wanted a secretary, and he sent for a young monk of the desert of Scetis. Asked to write a letter, the monk wrote it badly on purpose, because he loved solitude. The archbishop saw through it. The second letter came out wonderfully. The monk was let go back to the desert, on condition that he spend a month each year writing the rules for the feasts.

So says the Life of Isaac by Mina, a monk of Saint Macarius who later became a bishop. Isaac of Alexandria followed John III of Samannud as patriarch. B. Evetts, who translated the History of the Patriarchs, heads his life there 686 to 689. That History, a later Arabic history of the Coptic church, gives him a short life. E. Porcher, who edited Mina's Life, says it was written to edify. It is not history as we would write it today, he says.

Mina says Isaac was a clerk before he was a monk. His parents placed him with a kinsman, Meneson, to train as a secretary. Meneson served the governor of Egypt. The History says John III called Isaac and made him his partner in governing the church.

When John died, some bishops tried to seat George, a deacon of Sakha, without asking the Amir Abd al-Aziz. Mark, the archdeacon of the city, would not take part unless they waited for Sunday, as the canons require. The Amir's officers found that John had not named George, the History says, and the Amir ordered Isaac appointed.

Isaac wrote to the king of the Abyssinians and the king of the Nubians, bidding them make peace. Slandered, he was summoned by the Amir to be killed. The History says the secretaries had swapped his letters for others, so the Amir found nothing in them. Mina names the kings of Makuria and of a land he calls Maurotania. In Mina, the apostles Peter and Mark walk beside Isaac, and the Amir sees them and is afraid.

Mina says Isaac told the Amir that whoever afflicts the Church afflicts God.

## jacob-of-edessa

Jacob of Edessa

Leaving his see at Edessa, Jacob argued with the patriarch Julian the Roman and the bishops over keeping the church's canons. No one listened, and all told him to bend to the times. So he burned the book of canons at the door of the patriarch's monastery. He cried out that he was burning, as useless, the canons they trampled underfoot.

Michael the Syrian, a twelfth-century patriarch, tells this, and so does Bar Hebraeus a century later. Both belonged to Jacob's own Syriac Orthodox church, which refused the Council of Chalcedon.

Jacob, who lived around the year 700, came from 'Ayndaba, a village in the country of Antioch. He became a monk at Kenneshre, where he studied Greek, then gathered learning in Alexandria. Back in Syria he settled at Edessa, and the patriarch Athanasius of Balad made him its bishop. After four years, troubled by clergy he had barred for their disorders, he gave up the see.

He withdrew to a monastery at Kaisum. There he wrote two tracts of reproach, against the church's shepherds and against those who break its canons. For eleven years the monks of Eusebona had him teach Greek, whose study had died out. Brothers who hated the Greeks made trouble for him, and he left. At the monastery of Tell 'Ada he spent nine years correcting the Old Testament.

In his chronicle's preface, which Michael keeps, Jacob says no one since Eusebius of Caesarea had carried his tables forward. Joseph Assemani reports that Elias of Nisibis called Jacob the first to write a grammar of Syriac.

In about 701 he revised the Syriac of Severus of Antioch's sermons against the Greek. M.-A. Kugener and his student Triffaux, who edited one, say he kept the older version wherever it needed no change.

When the next bishop died, Edessa called Jacob back. He held the see four months, then went to fetch his books and pupils. At Tell 'Ada he sent the books ahead, but died there. Its monks hurried to bring the books back before they crossed the Euphrates. Bar Hebraeus dates his death to the fifth of June 708. Michael's manuscript puts it four years earlier in one place, and Assemani gives 710.

Kugener and Triffaux print his note on how Greek and Syriac name the days of the week. Whoever reads these words with love of the work, Jacob wrote, may perhaps understand why Severus's words vary here.

## john-iii-of-samannud

John III of Samannud

In Holy Week, the patriarch John III of Samannud was held at the house of Samad, the governor of a castle. The Amir Abd al-Aziz, governor of Egypt, had handed him over until he paid a hundred thousand denarii. John answered calmly that God's law did not command him to save anything for himself. Samad ordered a brass pan of burning coals brought for his feet, until he promised to pay.

That night, the History of the Patriarchs says, the Amir's wife fell gravely ill. She sent her eunuch to Samad: "Take heed that thou do no harm to that man of God." At cock-crow Samad told the Amir he had not put the patriarch to the torture.

Samad came down to fifty thousand. "The only things that I can dispose of are my garments which are upon my body," John answered. Samad kept lowering his price, to ten thousand in the end. "I will not promise what I cannot perform," John said. He was freed on the Thursday of Holy Week, and he washed the feet of the people.

John followed Agathon as patriarch of Alexandria. The History is a later Arabic history of the Coptic church. B. Evetts, who translated it, dates John's patriarchate 677 to 686. The writer of John's life says he was with him at the end, "for he was his spiritual son", but gives no name.

Theodore, the Chalcedonian governor of Alexandria who had harassed Agathon, died of dropsy, the History says. The Chalcedonians held to the council of Chalcedon, which John's church refused. Theodore's son became governor and was like a son to John.

John rebuilt the church of Saint Mark in Alexandria in three years. He fed the poor through a famine of three years. Dying, he was carried to the altar of Saint Mark's, where he stood and said the whole prayer of thanksgiving. He died on the first of the Coptic month Kiahk, after nine years.

To Samad he had said: "My body is in thy hands, but my soul and body are both in the hands of my Lord Jesus Christ."

## john-of-nikiu

John of Nikiu

When the Muslim army took the town of Nakius, in Egypt, it found no soldiers to stop it, a Coptic chronicle says. The army put to the sword all it found in the streets and in the churches, men, women and infants, "and they showed mercy to none." Soon after, the chronicler writes: "Let us now cease, for it is impossible to recount the iniquities".

The chronicle is by John of Nikiu, a later Coptic bishop of that same town. It survives only in an Ethiopic version made in 1602 from an Arabic one now lost. R. H. Charles, who put it into English in 1916, says it was first written in Greek. He thinks John was born about the time of the Arab conquest.

A John, bishop of Nikiu, appears in the History of the Patriarchs of Alexandria, a later Arabic history. R. H. Charles takes him for the chronicler. This John was among the bishops at the deathbed of the patriarch John III, and met with them to choose the next. The patriarch Simon later gave him charge of the monasteries, since he knew the monks' life and rules.

Then some monks carried a virgin off from her convent. John had the guilty monk beaten, and ten days later the monk died. The bishops deposed John, the History says, for going beyond what humanity required of him. He was silent while they did it. Then he cried out that the Lord would make them, in his words, "strangers to your sees".

Charles dates the election after John III to 690. B. Evetts, who put the History into English, heads the life of the patriarch then chosen, Isaac, 686 to 689.

In his Chronicle, John writes that people blamed the conquest on the emperor Heraclius, and on his persecution of the Orthodox through the patriarch Cyrus. Of the Arab general 'Amr he says both that he "had no mercy on the Egyptians" and that "he took none of the property of the Churches".

In his preface he calls himself "wanting in eloquence beyond all authors." He adds, "I have been honest".

## jonas-of-bobbio

Jonas of Bobbio

For three years, Jonas of Bobbio wrote, he was carried along the shores of the Ocean. He sailed the river Scheldt, and the slow marsh of the Elnone often soaked his feet. He had gone to help Bishop Amand, who was curbing the old errors of the people there with the blade of the Gospel.

Jonas says so in the preface to his Life of Columbanus, the Irish monk who founded Luxeuil in Gaul and Bobbio in Italy. Jonas was a monk of Bobbio in the seventh century. His translator, Dana Munro, says he entered it three years after Columbanus died. Some three years before the preface, Jonas had promised at Abbot Bertulf's command to write the saint's deeds. Many who had seen them were still alive, Jonas says, and he learned more from Attala and Eustasius, the abbots who followed Columbanus.

It is a saint's Life, and its miracles are many, as Munro notes. Bruno Krusch, an editor of the Frankish saints' Lives, dates it to about 640. Jonas often names the man who told him a story. "Theudegisil himself told us of this", he writes of one wonder, in Munro's English.

In the third year of King Clothar, which Krusch reckons as 659, Jonas was an abbot himself. On a journey ordered by the king and his mother, Queen Balthild, he rested a few days at the monastery of Réomé. Its monks begged him to write down what the disciples of their founder, John, and those after them had passed on, and he did so. Krusch says two forgers of the Carolingian age later reworked the book, and one claimed to have lived before Jonas.

Krusch believes Jonas also wrote the older Life of Vedast, bishop of Arras, since its style and one of its wonders match his.

If readers found him praising someone still living, Jonas wrote, they should not call him a flatterer. He meant only to commend to memory what deserved it.

## joseph-i-of-alexandria

Joseph I of Alexandria

For years the see of Alexandria had no patriarch. Some bishops had taken gifts from a married official who sought the see. The others prayed, and a priest of the desert named Joseph came to mind. They asked God for a sign, that they would find his door open. They found it open, and they seized him and bound him. "He is worthy, He is worthy," they cried, in E. A. Wallis Budge's English of the Ethiopic Synaxarium, a book of saints' days.

Joseph I of Alexandria, also called Yusab, came from a leading family of Manuf, says the Arabic Synaxarium of the Coptic church. Orphaned, he gave most of his fortune to the poor and became a monk. The History of the Patriarchs, a later Arabic history of the Coptic church, has a life of him. That life is not among the texts used here. The fullest account held is the Latin retelling by Eusèbe Renaudot, a Catholic scholar, in 1713. He dates Joseph's ordination to 831.

In Joseph's time the Bashmurites, Christians of Lower Egypt, rose against the tax collectors. Renaudot says Joseph wrote to tell them that whoever resists authority resists the order of God. The caliph al-Ma'mun came to Egypt with Dionysius of Tel Mahre, patriarch of the Syriac Orthodox church, and sent both patriarchs to the rebels. They failed, and the general Afshin crushed the revolt.

Bar Hebraeus, a thirteenth-century Syriac historian, quotes what Dionysius wrote of Joseph's church. Dionysius found the Copts chaste, sincere and humble. But no one could reach high office without paying two or three hundred gold pieces. When Dionysius rebuked this, Joseph said he had found the abuse already there.

Later two bishops whom Joseph's synod had cut off accused him to the governor. A man came with a sword to kill him. The sword struck a pillar and broke, both Synaxaria and Renaudot say. In the Ethiopic Synaxarium and in Renaudot, Joseph also begged the governor to spare the two bishops.

He died after nineteen years as patriarch, both Synaxaria say.

The Ethiopic Synaxarium greets him as Joseph of the city of Manuf, "unto whom was given the knowledge of Letters."

## julian-of-toledo

Julian of Toledo

On the feast of the Lord's Passion, Julian of Toledo slipped away to a quiet room with his friend Idalius, bishop of Barcelona. They lay on separate beds and read the Gospel story of the Passion. At one passage, which Julian later could not recall, both men were shaken. They groaned and sighed, and tears came. Idalius was worn by gout, Julian wrote, but lifted far more by the hope of contemplating God.

They began to ask how the souls of the dead fare before the last resurrection. That same day, at Idalius's urging and if he remembered rightly, Julian called a secretary and set down the questions. He wrote the book, the Prognosticon, when the king rode out to war and the crowds left the city.

Julian, a seventh-century bishop of Toledo in Visigothic Spain, tells this in his letter to Idalius at the head of the work. Felix, a later bishop of Toledo who lived in his time, wrote a short Life of him. Felix says Julian was born and baptized at Toledo and taught by its bishop Eugenius.

Julian became bishop after Quiricus. His name stands first among the signatures at four councils of Toledo, from 681 to 688.

At King Ervig's command he wrote On the Proof of the Sixth Age, against Jews of his day. In the opening letter, Julian says they counted the years from Creation. By that count, they said, the world was still in its fifth age, so Christ had not yet come. He says he believes the king follows the surgeon, who cuts away rot before it reaches healthy limbs. That rot, he writes, he would call the tongues of the Jews.

In 688 the bishops met again at Toledo. Pope Benedict, now dead, had raised points against a statement of their faith two years before. One section of their answer bears Julian's name. If their critics still disagreed with the Fathers, they wrote, there was no point in arguing further.

Felix calls him a man full of the fear of the Lord.

## leoba

Leoba

A woman wrote to Boniface of Mainz, the English missionary in Germany, and greeted him as her kinsman. She asked him to pray for her father Dynne, who had died more than eight years before. Her mother Aebbe was still alive and long ill. "I am the only daughter of these my parents," she wrote. She sent a small gift, "that the bond of our love may hold firm for ever." She asked him to "correct the homely style of this letter".

Her name was Leoba, and she lived in the eighth century. Her letter survives among Boniface's letters, in Edward Kylie's English translation of 1911. It is her only letter in his book, and much of the rest of her story is Kylie's own account.

At the end of the letter she added four lines of verse. She had tried, she said, to follow "the rules of poetic art". She learned the art from Eadburga, who, she wrote, never stops searching into the divine law. Kylie calls Eadburga the abbess of Thanet and says Leoba was sent to her there.

Kylie says the friendship led her into Boniface's service, and that she ruled a convent at Bischofsheim. In Kylie's heading, Boniface writes to her as abbess there. The priest Torhthat had her consent for a certain girl to be taught for a time, if Boniface agreed. Whatever she chose, Boniface answered, had his "assent and approval". In another letter he begged Leoba, Tecla, Cynehilda and their sisters to pray that he "may not die barren without some fruit of the gospel".

When Boniface left for Frisia for the last time, Kylie writes, he told her to stay at her work in Germany. She was to be buried beside him. None of the letters in Kylie's book tells of this. The Roman Martyrology, in the Marquess of Bute's English, remembers her as Lioba on 28 September, "eminent for the gift of miracles".

Her verses end with a prayer for Boniface: "Keep thee unharmed in His justice eternal."

## martin-i-of-rome

Martin I of Rome

In June 653, Pope Martin I lay sick before the altar of the Lateran church in Rome. Soldiers came in with lances, swords and bows ready. The candles fell under their weapons, he later wrote, like leaves in a winter wind. He gave himself up. He judged it better to die ten times, he said, than to let anyone's blood be shed.

He told the story in a letter to his friend Theodore. Martin was born at Todi in Tuscany and became bishop of Rome in 649.

The emperor Constans had issued an edict, the Typos, forbidding anyone to say that Christ had one will or two. Martin's own speech to his synod blames Paul, patriarch of Constantinople, for urging the edict. Paul had also overturned the Roman see's altar in that city, so its envoys could not say Mass. In October 649 Martin gathered his synod at the Lateran. Its bishops condemned Cyrus of Alexandria and Sergius, Pyrrhus and Paul, patriarchs of Constantinople, for teaching one will.

After Olympius, governor of Italy, failed to seize him, the Book of the Popes says, his successor, Theodore Calliopas, took him. Martin wrote that he spent a year on the island of Naxos.

An account sent to the West by one of his clergy says he reached Constantinople on 17 September. After ninety-three days in prison he was carried to his trial, gravely ill. Witnesses swore that he had plotted with Olympius. When Martin began to speak of the Typos, the prefect Troilus cut him off. Do not bring the faith in here, Troilus said; the charge was rebellion.

Before a crowd he was stripped of his priestly cloak and led through the city in irons. Spared death, he sailed for Cherson the next spring, on Holy Thursday. There, he wrote, people spoke of bread but never saw it. His own church of Saint Peter had forgotten him, he complained. He prayed that God would keep its people firm in the faith, and above all the shepherd who now led them.

The clergyman says Martin died on 16 September and was buried outside Cherson. Bede says a Roman abbot sent by Pope Agatho brought "the decision of the synod of the blessed Pope Martin and 105 bishops" to Wearmouth.

The Lord would care for his poor body, Martin wrote, in endless trouble or in a little relief. The Lord was near, so he need not be anxious.

## maximus-the-confessor

Maximus the Confessor

At sunset, guards took the old monk Maximus the Confessor and a companion off a ship at Constantinople, barefoot and without cloaks. Days later, before the senate, the emperor's treasurer angrily asked whether he was a Christian. By the grace of Christ, he answered, he was. The treasurer said that was not true. You say so, Maximus replied, but God knows I am a Christian and remain one.

The scene comes from his own side's record of that hearing. Louis Duchesne, editor of the Book of the Popes, dates it to 655. Maximus alone, the treasurer said, had handed Egypt, Alexandria and Africa to the Saracens.

His Greek Life, written in his honor, says he was born in Constantinople. The emperor Heraclius made him first among his secretaries. He left the court for a monastery at Chrysopolis as the teaching that Christ had one will spread.

In Africa he debated Pyrrhus, a former patriarch of Constantinople. Later the emperor Constans forbade talk of one will or two in an edict, the Typos.

His questioners asked why he shunned communion with Constantinople's church. He named the Typos and an earlier statement by the patriarch Sergius. By them, he said, its leaders had cast out the four councils and deposed themselves. One must speak of two wills and two workings in Christ, he said, since the Fathers teach that no nature exists without its own working.

The chronicler Theophanes the Confessor says Maximus went from Africa to Rome and stirred Pope Martin I to act. Martin's synod of 649 condemned Sergius and Pyrrhus.

At Bizya in Thrace, a bishop promised the Typos would be withdrawn if Maximus took communion. Maximus asked that the Roman synod's verdict be accepted first. The bishop said it lacked force without the emperor's order. If emperors' orders made synods valid, Maximus answered, the synods against the Nicene faith would count too.

A synod then condemned him with two companions, both named Anastasius. The sentence ordered their tongues cut out and their right hands cut off. They were paraded through the city and exiled among the Lazi.

There, Anastasius the envoy wrote, guards carried Maximus on a litter of woven branches to a fort near the Alans. He foretold the day of his death, the envoy adds, and died on it, 13 August.

He had told his questioners he held no teaching of his own, only the common teaching of the Catholic church.
