# Packet S03: person and council stories (census longDescription)

## tree/theodoret-of-cyrrhus

Theodoret of Cyrrhus

Theodoret of Cyrrhus wrote to his friend, the deposed Nestorius. "I will never agree to what was done against you, unjustly and wickedly, not even if someone cuts off both my hands, as God's grace works with me and strengthens my weak soul."

In the fifth century, Theodoret was a leading voice of Antioch's teaching that Christ is one, God and man, with neither mixed away into the other. He answered Cyril of Alexandria's twelve curses and was deposed in 449, and at Chalcedon, two years later, he was let back only after he cursed Nestorius by name. He was a historian too. His Church History carried the church's story on from Eusebius, and his Religious History kept the Syrian monks he knew.

Much of his life comes from letters he wrote to answer his accusers, who said he split Christ into two sons, and to win Leo of Rome's help. He says he lived in a monastery and was made bishop of Cyrrhus, a small town in northern Syria, "unwillingly". He led villages of Marcion's followers, a rival church that set aside the Old Testament, into his own and, he says, was stoned for it many times.

Cyril had sent out twelve curses against Nestorius's teaching, solemn acts that cut a person off from the church. The first fell on anyone who would not call Mary Mother of God. Theodoret charged that they mixed God and man. Asked by John of Antioch to answer them, he wrote: "The form of God was not changed into the form of a servant. It stayed what it was, and took the form of the servant." The form of a servant is Paul's phrase for a human life: God stayed God, and took one on. So Mary is rightly Mother of God, he wrote, not because she bore God by nature, but because of the union. At Ephesus in 431, his English translator says, Cyril's side condemned Nestorius without waiting for John's bishops; Theodoret stood with John's side.

In 433 the two sides made peace, and Theodoret found that Cyril's letter agreed with his faith. But he would not accept that Nestorius had been rightly removed.

His Religious History told of many monks he says he saw and heard. Of Simeon, who stood on a pillar, he writes: "First he ordered it cut six cubits high, then twelve, after that twenty-two, and now thirty-six. For he longs to fly up to heaven, and to be set free from this life on earth." A cubit is the length of a forearm.

An order from the emperor then kept him in Cyrrhus, with no charge and no accuser, he wrote, only for calling bishops together. The council of 449 at Ephesus, led by Dioscorus of Alexandria, deposed him in his absence. Its acts, his opponents' record, say he had written against Cyril, and ask the emperors to burn his books.

At Chalcedon, the council's acts record, the bishops at the altar rails demanded that he curse Nestorius. He said he condemned him, but asked to explain his faith first; they shouted for a plain curse and cried that he was a heretic. Then he said it: "Anathema to Nestorius", a curse on his friend and on all who split the one Son into two. He was given back his church.

About 140 years later Evagrius, a Greek historian, called his History and three others "a most excellent record", and carried it on. A Syriac chronicle from the churches that refused Chalcedon's teaching of two natures, God and man, begins where he and two others stopped, so both sides carried his story on. Yet that same chronicle says he won Leo over with a bribe. In 553 a council at Constantinople condemned what he had written against Cyril.

To Leo he had written that in all his years as bishop he had gained not a house, not a piece of ground, not a coin, "not a tomb". He died in the reign of the emperor Leo, a later writer says; the year is not known.

## tree/tyconius

Tyconius

"Of all the things that occur to me, I consider none so necessary as to write a little book of rules." So Tyconius opened his Book of Rules. His seven rules would be keys and windows into "the secret places of the law." With them, a reader crossing "the vast forest of prophecy" would not lose the way.

Tyconius was an African of the late fourth century and a Donatist. The Donatist church had split from the main body in Africa over those who lapsed in the Great Persecution. F. C. Burkitt edited the Book of Rules. Burkitt says the little known of Tyconius's life comes chiefly from Augustine of Hippo, a Catholic bishop who wrote against the Donatists.

Augustine says Tyconius wrote "in defence of the Church" against his own party. He reported a Donatist council that took back those who had handed over the Scriptures, without baptizing them again. Parmenian, the Donatist bishop of Carthage, answered him and warned him not to write such things. Tyconius still kept apart from the Catholics. Augustine calls this "a most inconsistent disposition."

Augustine set out the seven rules in his guide to the Bible, On Christian Doctrine. The second rule held that the Lord's body has two parts, good and bad, joined for now. Tyconius found it in the Song of Songs, where the bride says she is dark and beautiful. Augustine renamed the rule "the mixed Church." He faulted the third rule for saying that faith does not come to us from God. The book "must be read with caution," he warned, chiefly for what its author taught as a Donatist.

Gennadius of Marseilles says Tyconius also explained the whole Apocalypse, "regarding nothing in it in a carnal sense." Burkitt says that commentary is believed to be lost, though later writers copied much of it. Augustine commended the Book of Rules to students, "for it is of very great assistance in understanding Scripture."

## tree/ulfila

Ulfila

Ulfila had been bishop of the Goths for seven years when persecution drove him out, after many men and women had died as martyrs. He led a great crowd of believers across the Danube, and they were received with honor on Roman soil, under the emperor Constantius. His pupil Auxentius, who wrote in his praise and on whom nearly all of this life rests, told it as a new Exodus.

"Through Moses, God freed his people from the power and violence of Pharaoh and the Egyptians. He made them cross the sea, and made sure they would serve him. So too, through this man, God freed those who confessed his holy only Son from the barbarians' land. He made them cross the Danube, and serve him in the mountains, after the saints' example."

Those who confessed had held to the faith under persecution, and the barbarians' land was the Goths' country. Ulfila gave the Goths letters and the Scriptures in their own tongue. To his death he taught them a creed that called the Son like the Father, according to the Scriptures. His opponents called that faith Arian, after Arius, a priest who taught that the Son had a beginning.

Auxentius says he was made bishop at thirty. Philostorgius, a historian of the next century, says it was at the emperor's court, by Eusebius and other bishops. A modern historian takes this Eusebius for the bishop of Nicomedia.

Among the Goths, Auxentius says, Ulfila taught a people starved of preaching and made Christians into true Christians.

Philostorgius says Ulfila invented letters for the Goths and translated all of Scripture except the Books of Kings. Those told of war, he explains, and the Goths were too fond of war already. Two church historians, Socrates and Sozomen, credit him with both. Fragments of a Gothic Bible survive without a translator's name, but their editor in 1908 had no doubt they were Ulfila's.

His pupil records what he taught. The Father alone is unbegotten, never begotten by anyone, without beginning, and the one true God. The Son is God and maker of all creation, yet has the Father as his God, and is like the Father, according to the Scriptures. The Greek for like, homoios, gives these churches the name Homoian. Nicaea had called the Son of one being with the Father, sharing his very being, and Ulfila spurned that as a devilish invention.

In 360, by the usual date, he joined the bishops at Constantinople in confirming a creed that banned the word "substance" for God, saying Scripture never uses it. But when did he take that faith? It matters because the Goths still held that faith in the historians' day. Socrates puts it down to the emperor Valens, whose help in a Gothic civil war won a chief over to his faith. But Sozomen says Ulfila was why the barbarians on the Danube followed Arius.

Socrates says Ulfila took the creed only in 360, holding Nicaea's faith before. Sozomen says he signed from thoughtlessness, and changed sides years later, under Valens, on an embassy seeking land for the Goths. The other side's leaders, he heard, promised help if he joined them. He did, under pressure or perhaps from conviction. Theodoret also puts it under Valens, when a bishop of the emperor's party won him over with eloquence and bribes.

Under Valens, Socrates says, Ulfila also taught the people of the Gothic chief Athanaric. The chief took this as an attack on his ancestors' religion and punished the Christians, so that many died as martyrs.

Later the emperor ordered Ulfila to Constantinople for a debate, to stop his opponents teaching in his churches. As he entered the city, he fell ill at once. He died there, about 383.

He left his faith in writing to his people, by testament. Of the Father, he wrote, "alone unbegotten"; of the Son, "subject and obedient in all things" to his God the Father. He wrote that he had always believed so, and that in this faith alone he made his crossing to his Lord.

## tree/agathias

Agathias of Myrina

After a great winter earthquake at Constantinople, Agathias writes, the people turned good for a while. Some left everything for a lonely life in the mountains. At night the rich walked the streets, giving food and clothes to the poor. As soon as the fear passed, most went back to their old ways. We taste good works, he says, only while need drives us and fear presses. His Greek is told here from an old Latin version.

Agathias gives his own name, town and trade. He came from Myrina in Asia, the son of Memnonius, and he practiced Roman law. B. G. Niebuhr, who edited him in 1828, says he studied at Alexandria and pleaded cases in Constantinople to earn his living. He turned from love poems to history, he says, so that his life would be of some use to the public.

His five books carry on the history of Procopius of Caesarea, as Evagrius Scholasticus records. Church matters come in only in passing. He tells how Justinian raised the fallen middle of the dome of Hagia Sophia higher and made it stronger. He says the Franks are all Christians, with priests in their cities, and keep the feasts just as we do. Of peoples who worship trees and rivers he writes that those who stray from the truth deserve pity rather than hatred.

Whether he was a Christian is disputed. G. J. Vossius thought him a pagan, partly because he tells of the martyr Stephen only as something they say. Niebuhr thinks one of his poems, dedicated to the archangel, puts it beyond doubt that he professed the faith. But he guesses that Agathias was born to a pagan father and joined the Christians to escape the laws, not from belief.

Niebuhr thinks he died before 582. He finds the style poor, but says Agathias wrote free of attack and of flattery alike, and was a much better man than Procopius.

## tree/augustine-of-canterbury

Augustine of Canterbury

King Ethelbert of Kent would not meet the strangers indoors. Bede of Jarrow says he feared they might work magic on him, so he sat in the open air on Thanet. Augustine of Canterbury and his companions came singing a litany. They bore a silver cross for a banner and a picture of Christ painted on a board. The king called their words fair but new, and would not leave the faith his people had long kept. He let them stay and preach.

Augustine had been provost of Pope Gregory the Great's own monastery, Gregory's letters say. Bede says Gregory sent him to the English in 596, and the mission landed the next year. On the way, Bede tells, the monks lost heart at the thought of a fierce people whose language they did not know. They sent Augustine back to ask to be excused. Gregory sent him on with a letter: "When Augustine, your chief, returns, whom we also constitute your abbat, humbly obey him in all things."

At Canterbury the king believed and was baptized. Bede says Augustine then went to Arles to be made archbishop of the English. In Bede's telling, Aetherius of Arles ordained him; the editor Nicola Turchi names Vergilius. Gregory wrote to Eulogius of Alexandria that more than ten thousand English were reported baptized one Christmas.

Augustine sent questions to Rome, and Bede copied them with Gregory's answers. One asks: "Whereas the faith is one and the same, why are there different customs in different churches?" Gregory told him to choose what was good from every church, "for things are not to be loved for the sake of places, but places for the sake of good things." Ludwig Hartmann, editing Gregory's letters, says these answers have often been doubted, and judges them genuine.

Gregory also put all the bishops of Britain in his care. Bede tells of two meetings with British bishops, the first at Augustine's Oak. Before the second, a hermit told them to follow him if he rose to greet them. Augustine stayed seated, and they refused him. If they would not preach to the English, Augustine is said to have warned, they would suffer death at English hands. Bede, no friend of the Britons, calls them "that perfidious nation".

Augustine died on the twenty-sixth of May. His epitaph gives the day but not the year, and calls him the first archbishop of Canterbury.

## tree/avitus-of-vienne

Avitus of Vienne

King Gundobad of the Burgundians came to believe that the Son and the Holy Spirit are equal to the Father. He asked the bishop of Vienne to anoint him in secret. Gregory of Tours, writing later in the same century, gives the bishop's answer. The king should not fear his people, he said, but lead them. "For thou art the head of the people; the people is not thy head." Gregory says the king never confessed it in public.

That bishop was Avitus of Vienne, in Gaul. An old Life of him says he followed his father Isicius as bishop. His editor, Rudolf Peiper, puts his birth about 460. Gundobad's church was Arian, and the Life says Avitus fought that teaching with great labor.

When Clovis, king of the Franks, was baptized, Avitus wrote to him in Latin. He had not been there in body, he said, but had shared the joy. In choosing for yourself, he told the king, you judge for everyone, and your faith is our victory. Peiper dates the letter to 496 or 497. The year and place of the baptism are disputed. O. M. Dalton, Gregory's English translator, follows those who hold Reims in 496, against Bruno Krusch's Tours in 507 or 508.

At Gundobad's request, Gregory says, Avitus wrote against the teaching of Eutyches. Agobard of Lyon, writing three centuries later, says he argued with the king in person and by letter. Once Gundobad was lost in his unbelief, Agobard says, Avitus turned his son and heir Sigismund to the Catholic faith. Peiper tentatively dates Avitus's letter to the pope about that conversion between 513 and 516, while Gundobad still lived. In 517 Avitus signed first at the council of Epaone.

His Life says he died under the emperor Anastasius, and gives no year. Peiper corrects the emperor to Justin.

Gregory wrote of his letters that "as they once quelled heresy, so now they edify the Church of God."

## tree/barhadbeshabba-arbaya

Barhadbeshabba 'Arbaia of Halwan

The bishops of the Church of the East met at the Persian king's court to choose a new head. J.-B. Chabot, who edited their acts, dates the meeting to April 605. The acts say King Khosrau the Second had the distant bishops brought on his own beasts. They chose Gregory of Prat. The twenty-seventh to sign is Bar Hadbeshabba, bishop of Halwan.

Addai Scher and François Nau take this bishop to be Barhadbeshabba 'Arbaia, who wrote the Cause of the Foundation of the Schools. The title Scher prints calls him bishop of Halwan. Scher says his second name marks him as a man of Beth 'Arbaye, and that he studied at Nisibis under Henana of Adiabene.

The Cause tells of the schools through which God taught angels and people, from Adam to the schools of Edessa and Nisibis.

The Cause speaks of Henana as a living master. It praises his humility and prays that God will lengthen his days. Henana's own church, Scher says, always charged him with leaving Theodore of Mopsuestia's reading of Scripture. Scher thinks the author probably hid the truth to praise him.

The Chronicle of Seert tells of a walk-out from the school of Nisibis. Some three hundred students left when the head of the church took Henana's side. It names among them a Hadbeshabba 'Arbaya, later made metropolitan of Halwan. Scher takes him for the Cause's author.

Part of a history of the holy Fathers persecuted for the truth survives from the same school. Nau gives it to Barhadbeshabba 'Arbaia, a priest of the school of Nisibis, and dates it to the end of the 500s. He thinks it not impossible that this writer is the bishop of Halwan.

Of the school that Narsai of Nisibis founded, the Cause says, in R. H. Connolly's English, "Edessa was darkened, but Nisibis shone forth; and the dominions of the Romans were filled with error, but those of Persia with the knowledge of true religion".

## tree/barsanuphius-and-john-of-gaza

Barsanuphius and John of Gaza

Dorotheus of Gaza put a question to his teacher, the abbot John of Gaza. How could he hold back his too free manner and rule his tongue? By mourning, the answer said. Then how, he asked, could he keep mourning while dealing every day with people of the world? Mourning does not come through tears, the answer ran. Tears come through mourning.

J.-P. Migne prints the questions and answers among Dorotheus's writings, in Greek and Latin. Their heading sends them to the great old man Barsanuphius and his disciple, the abbot John. It says John was called the Prophet for his gift of insight.

The church historian Evagrius Scholasticus calls Barsanuphius an Egyptian, shut in a cell near Gaza. No one had seen him for over fifty years, Evagrius says. Dorotheus came to know the two old men when he entered the monastery of the abbot Seridus. So says a Life of Dositheus, as Andrea Gallandi's notice reports it.

Dorotheus later told monks how he took everything to John. When a thought told him not to trouble the old man, since he knew the answer already, Dorotheus cursed the thought and went anyway. Often John gave the very answer the thought had foreseen. When Dorotheus worried that he felt no hardship, he asked John by letter. Whoever gives himself to obedience, John told him, gains this rest from God.

Only the first question is marked as sent to John, so the text does not show which old man gave each later answer.

A preface that Migne prints without an author's name says there were two men named Barsanuphius and two named Dorotheus. One pair, it says, defended the errors of Severus of Antioch, and the other kept sound teaching. It calls Theodore the Studite as its witness. Theodore says the men who were condemned were others.

Barsanuphius's image, Theodore writes, stood in the Great Church beside the holy fathers Antony and Ephrem.

## tree/boethius

Boethius

Boethius stood before Theodoric, the Ostrogothic king of Italy, to defend a fellow senator, Albinus, accused of writing to the emperor Justin against the king. A Latin chronicle, the Anonymus Valesianus, gives his answer, told here in modern words. The charge is false, he said, but if Albinus did it, then I and the whole Senate did it together. Its editor, Theodor Mommsen, calls its author a fierce enemy of the Arians.

Boethius was a Roman senator under Theodoric and was consul in 510. A letter Cassiodorus wrote in Theodoric's name praises him for putting Pythagoras, Ptolemy the astronomer, Euclid, Plato and Aristotle into Latin.

He also wrote short books on the faith. Writing against Eutyches and Nestorius, he defined a person as "The individual substance of a rational nature." Christ, he argued, is two natures in one person, as the Catholic faith holds. He closed one with a plea: "if possible, reconcile faith and reason." Thomas Hodgkin, who put Cassiodorus's letters into English, reports that scholars of his day doubted these books were his. Hodgkin answered them from a fragment drawn from a lost work of Cassiodorus, which names a book on the Holy Trinity and one against Nestorius.

In 522 his two sons were consuls together, and he praised the king in the Senate. Soon after, he fell. Condemned, he wrote the Consolation of Philosophy, in which Philosophy visits him as a woman. He set down the charges there: "I am said to have desired the Senate's safety." "Now being conveyed five hundred miles off, dumb and defenceless, I am condemned to death," he wrote.

Marius of Avenches says he was killed in the district of Milan, in the year Mommsen dates to 524. The Anonymus Valesianus has him tortured with a cord twisted on his forehead and clubbed to death. The Book of the Popes says the king killed him with the sword. Procopius of Caesarea writes that Theodoric later wept for the wrong he had done.

The Consolation never names Christ, Hodgkin notes, so some have read Boethius as a pagan. His English editors, H. F. Stewart and E. K. Rand, call him without doubt a Christian, and perhaps a martyr. The Bollandists, in their Acta Sanctorum of saints' days, list him as a martyr at Pavia.

Of his own fall he wrote that "it is the most unhappy kind of misfortune to have been happy."

## tree/brendan-of-clonfert

Brendan of Clonfert

Four founders of monasteries once came over from Ireland to visit Columba of Iona on the island of Hinba, Adomnan of Iona writes. While Columba celebrated Mass at their request, one of them, Brendan Mocu Alti, saw a blazing globe of fire over his head. It rose like a pillar until the rite was done, as Brendan later told two of the others.

Adomnan was abbot of Iona from 679 to 704, his translator Wentworth Huyshe says, and Huyshe puts Columba's death in 597. Huyshe takes this Brendan to be Brendan of Clonfert, founder of that Irish monastery. Adomnan names him once more. A devout man, Aidan son of Fergno, is said to have served him for twelve years. Huyshe gives Brendan's years as born 482, founded Clonfert 559, and died 577, without saying where they come from.

Adomnan also tells of a second Brendan, the founder of Birr, whom Huyshe warns must be kept apart from the first.

Most of what is told of Brendan of Clonfert comes from Lives written centuries later. The Irish Life in the Book of Lismore calls him Brenainn son of Finnlug. Whitley Stokes, its editor, says the book was compiled in the later 1400s. In that Life an angel promises Brendan the Land of Promise. He sails for years, keeps Easter on a whale's back and is shown the gate of hell. Then the nun Ita, his foster mother, tells him he will never find that holy land in boats of dead hides. So he builds a great ship, and at last he reaches it.

A Latin Life printed by W. J. Rees calls him the father of nearly three thousand monks, and breaks off as he plans his voyage. Rees thinks the Lives in his book were probably compiled about the twelfth century.

The Lismore Life likens him to Noah. As Noah raised the ark over the flood, it says, Brendan will raise his monks and households over the fire of Judgment Day.

## tree/brigid-of-kildare

Brigid of Kildare

In her great church on the plains of the Liffey, Cogitosus writes, Brigid and Bishop Conleath lay on either side of the altar. Their tombs were adorned with gold, silver and precious stones, and crowns of gold and silver hung above them. A painted wall crossed the church. The bishop and his clergy passed through one door to the altar, and the abbess and her nuns through another.

Brigid founded the monastery of Kildare in Ireland. Cogitosus calls it the head of almost all the Irish churches. He wrote her Life in Latin long after her death, at the brothers' request, from what he says elders had handed down. It is hagiography, and mostly miracles.

Cogitosus says she was born to noble Christian parents, Dubtach and Brocca. She called a holy hermit from his solitude to govern the church with her as bishop. In one story she gives the poor the foreign robes Conleath wore at the altar. When the feast comes, a two-wheeled chariot brings others just like them.

Cogitosus gives no year. He says she died on the first of February. The Welsh annals put her death in the year Saint Columcille was born. Extracts from Nennius, printed by Whitley Stokes, put four years between his birth and her death.

How long after her Cogitosus wrote is disputed. Migne prints the Life under the year 591. Basnage, in the notice printed with it, says he set Cogitosus in his own edition by following others rather than his own judgment. He thought Cogitosus wrote much later, since he describes painted images and miracles in her church. In his Life of Patrick, Muirchu names his father Cogitosus. His editor Newport White takes the father for Brigid's biographer.

A later Irish Life, in the fifteenth-century Book of Lismore, makes her mother a bondmaid named Broicsech.

On her feast, Cogitosus says, crowds came to her church. Some came for the food, the sick for healing, some to watch the crowds, and some with great gifts.

## tree/caesaria-of-arles-the-elder

Caesaria of Arles the Elder

Caesarius, bishop of Arles in Gaul, built a convent for women beside a church in his city. Then he called his sister Caesaria back from a convent at Marseille. He had sent her there, his disciples write, so that she might learn what she would teach, and be a pupil before she was a teacher. She moved into the rooms made ready for her with two or three companions.

Caesaria of Arles was the first abbess of that house. Her story comes almost wholly from her brother's Life. It was written after his death by his disciples, the bishops Cyprian of Toulon, Firminus and Viventius, with the priest Messianus and the deacon Stephen. It is a saint's Life, and no words of her own survive in it.

Bruno Krusch, who edited the Life, dates the dedication of the convent to 512. He takes the house at Marseille to be the one John Cassian founded. The Life itself names neither.

Crowds of young women came, giving up their goods and families. They were enclosed so that none might leave by the convent door until they died. Caesarius wrote a Rule for them.

He built a church where the women would be buried. Not long after, the Life says, he buried his sister there, the mother of the convent. He laid her among the women who had died before her, beside the tomb he had made ready for himself. Another Caesaria followed her as abbess. When Caesarius died, this second Caesaria led more than two hundred of them.

The two abbesses share a name and have been confused. The Life was written for the second, who was still living. A Bollandist commentary, printed in Jacques-Paul Migne's Latin edition, says some writers wrongly took it for the sister.

In the Life, the women who came to the convent sought the care of Caesarius their father and Caesaria their mother.

## tree/caesarius-of-arles

Caesarius of Arles

After a siege of Arles in Gaul, the Goths came back into the city with a great crowd of captives. Caesarius, bishop of Arles, gave the church's silver to buy them free. His disciples report what he often said of clergy who would not do the same. In modern words, he said God would not mind the vessels of his service buying people back, since Christ gave himself to redeem us.

His Life was written by the bishops Cyprian of Toulon, Firminus and Viventius, with the priest Messianus and the deacon Stephen. They wrote for Caesaria, the abbess who then led the convent he founded. It is a saint's Life by men who called him father.

He came from near Chalon, they say. At about eighteen he asked its bishop, Silvester, to take him into the church's service, without his family knowing. Later he joined the monks on the island of Lérins. Fasting wore down his body, and his abbot sent him to Arles to recover. Eonius, bishop of Arles, ordained him and chose him as his successor. The Life says Caesarius hid among the tombs to escape it.

Arles was then under Alaric, the Arian king of the Visigoths. One of Caesarius's own notaries, the Life says, accused him of plotting to give the city to the Burgundians, and he was exiled to Bordeaux. After Arles passed to the Ostrogoths, he was taken under guard to their king, Theodoric, at Ravenna. The king gave him a silver dish, and he sold it to free more captives.

His teaching on grace had rivals in Gaul, the Life says. At a meeting of bishops in Valence, Cyprian of Toulon argued for him. No one can make any progress toward God by himself, Cyprian said, unless God's grace first calls him. Boniface, bishop of Rome, upheld Caesarius. Caesarius signed first at a council in Orange, whose chapters on grace and free will came from Rome. Friedrich Maassen, who edited its acts, dates Orange to 529 and leaves open which council came first.

He wrote rules for monks and for nuns. He sent sermons as far as Italy and Spain for priests to preach, and led the church of Arles for forty years.

His Rule for the nuns opens with a request. When they entered the kingdom with the wise virgins, he asked, they should pray that he would not be left outside with the foolish.

## tree/cassiodorus

Cassiodorus

In his ninety-third year, Cassiodorus says, his monks came to him with a complaint. What good was it, they asked, to study the old books and the ones he had added? They did not know how to spell what they read. Thomas Hodgkin, who put his letters into English, tells it. So the old man wrote them a book on spelling.

Cassiodorus was a Roman statesman in Italy under the Gothic kings, and later the founder of a monastery. Hodgkin and Theodor Mommsen say Senator was part of his own name. A fragment drawn from a lost tract of his own, Hodgkin says, tells how he praised Theoderic, king of the Goths. He spoke so well that the king made him quaestor. He rose to consul, master of the offices and praetorian prefect. In his own chronicle he notes that in his consulship the clergy and people of the Roman church were united again. Mommsen dates that year 514.

He wrote the letters of the Gothic kings for them, and later gathered them in twelve books, the Variae. In the preface he says an author is told to keep his writings nine years, "but I have not as many hours, hardly as many moments." He also wrote a History of the Goths in twelve books, now lost. Jordanes condensed it, he says, from a three days' reading.

With Pope Agapetus, Cassiodorus tried to set up a Christian school at Rome, with paid teachers, as at Alexandria and Nisibis. War stopped it, he says. He then withdrew to Scyllacium in the far south, where his family came from, Hodgkin says. There he built a monastery named for its fishponds, Vivarium, and a retreat for hermits on the hill above.

For his monks he wrote a guide to sacred and secular reading, the Institutions. In it he praises the copyist: "With his fingers he gives life to men and arms them against the wiles of the devil." He made them lamps that fed their own flame, with a sundial and a water-clock. Hodgkin finds no mention of Benedict of Nursia in all his writings, and doubts he was ever formally abbot.

No early source gives the years of his birth or death. Hodgkin, following a writer who died in 1516, puts them near 480 and 575.

Hodgkin calls him "a well-read scholar, and a devout Christian; but he was apt to crouch before the possessors of power."

## tree/ciaran-of-clonmacnois

Ciaran of Clonmacnois

Ciaran planted the first stake at Clonmacnois, with Diarmait son of Cerball beside him. "Let, O warrior, thy hand be over my hand, and thou shalt be in sovranty over the men of Ireland," he told him. That night, the Life says, the king Tuathal Maelgarb was killed, and Diarmait came to rule.

The story comes from the Irish Life of Ciaran in the Book of Lismore. Whitley Stokes, its editor and translator, says the book was put together in the late 1400s. He says the Life was composed at Clonmacnois itself. It is a saint's Life, the house's own telling of its founder, written down long after him.

Two writers from the decades around 700 confirm the man and his monastery. Tirechan, who wrote of Patrick, says the deacon Justus baptized Ciaran, the craftsman's son, from Patrick's book. Adomnan of Iona tells of Columba visiting "the Clonoensian monastery of St. Ceran".

The Life makes him the son of Beoit, a craftsman. His mother would not give him a cow to take to school, it says. So he blessed one, and Ciaran's Dun followed him to Findian of Clonard. Her milk fed the whole school, and her hide was later kept at Clonmacnois. The Life then takes him to Enda of Aran. Before Clonmacnois he led a house on the island of Inis Angin for three years.

He died young, the Life says, in his thirty-third year, seven months after settling at Clonmacnois. Looking up at the sky, he said, "Awful is this way above." The Annales Cambriae note his death in their hundredth year, which their editor, John Williams ab Ithel, sets at 544. John O'Donovan, editor of the Annals of the Four Masters, gives 549.

He is a different man from Ciaran of Saigir, and the Book of Lismore keeps the two apart. In that book's Life of Columba, Columba came to Clonmacnois with a hymn he had made for Ciaran.

## tree/columbanus

Columbanus

Columbanus was writing to his monks from near the Bretons when a messenger came in. A ship was being made ready, the messenger said, to carry him back to his own country against his will. If he were thrown into the sea like Jonah, he wrote, they should pray that someone would row their Jonah back to land.

Columbanus was an Irish monk who founded monasteries in Frankish Gaul and Lombard Italy. Jonas, a monk of Bobbio, wrote his Life after his death. He names companions of the saint who told him its stories. It is a saint's Life, full of wonders, and it takes his side against his enemies.

Jonas says he was born in Leinster and entered Bangor, the monastery of Abbot Comgall. After many years he longed to live among strangers, and sailed with twelve companions.

In the Vosges wilderness he built houses at Anegray, Luxeuil and Fontaines, and wrote a rule for them, which survives. Each day, it says, a monk must fast, pray, work and read. His Penitential holds that different sins need different penances, as doctors treat wounds and sicknesses in different ways.

Jonas says he rebuked King Theuderic of Burgundy for keeping concubines. When Brunhild, the king's grandmother, brought him the sons of these unions, he refused to bless them. Jonas calls her a second Jezebel. His Irish reckoning of Easter had given offense in Gaul, James Barmby notes. Columbanus wrote to Pope Gregory the Great to defend it. "Error is manifestly ancient," he wrote, in Barmby's English, "but truth which reproves it is ever more ancient still."

About twenty years after he came, Jonas says, the king's men took him to Nantes to ship him home. The ship ran aground until his goods were put ashore, which Jonas reads as God's will that he stay. He preached among the Swabians near Bregenz, then crossed into Italy, where Agilulf, king of the Lombards, received him. The Lombard historian Paul the Deacon tells the same. Jonas says he died at his new monastery of Bobbio after a single year. Jonas's translator, Dana Munro, gives the year as probably 615.

Writing to a council of Gallic bishops, he had asked to live in silence in the Vosges, by the bones of seventeen dead brothers. "Let Gaul, I pray, hold us together," he wrote, in modern words, "whom the kingdom of heaven will hold, if our merits are good."

## tree/constantius-of-lyon

Constantius of Lyon

Bishop Patiens of Lyon built a new church, and verses were written for its walls beside the altar. Constantius of Lyon wrote some of them. Wilhelm Levison, who edited Constantius's work, reports this from the letters of Sidonius Apollinaris, bishop of Clermont.

Constantius lived at Lyon in Gaul in the later fifth century, and little else is known of his own life. Levison cannot say whether he was a cleric or a layman given to the religious life. Constantius had urged Sidonius to publish his letters, and Sidonius dedicated them to him. About 473, by Levison's date, Constantius helped make peace in a quarrel at Clermont. Sidonius praised him as a man grave with age and venerable in religion.

Patiens, Constantius writes, ordered him again and again to set down the Life of Germanus, bishop of Auxerre. So much time had passed, he says, that what was known of it could scarcely be gathered. He sent it to Patiens and to Censurius, a later bishop of Auxerre. When Censurius ordered it sent beyond a close circle, it made him, Constantius says, the accuser and the betrayer of his own fault. Levison dates the Life about 480 or a little later.

Levison judges Constantius wrote less as a biographer than as a writer of saints' Lives. Wonders of little weight fill much of the book, as in the Life of Martin of Tours by Sulpicius Severus, which Constantius knew. With so few other witnesses, Levison holds that the book should be used with care and must not be scorned. Bede copied large parts of it almost word for word into his History of the English.

At its close Constantius asks the reader's pardon for his rough words and for the book's length. Then he writes, in modern words, "And yet I call God, who knows what is hidden, to witness that I have left out many deeds of my lord Germanus that were known and proved."

## tree/cosmas-indicopleustes

Cosmas Indicopleustes

Cosmas was sailing for Further India, he writes, when his ship came near the mouth of the ocean. A great flock of birds followed it, and everyone on board was afraid. Seasoned passengers and sailors cried to the pilot: "Steer the ship to port and make for the gulf, or we shall be swept along by the currents and be carried into the ocean and be lost."

Cosmas was a merchant on the Mediterranean, the Red Sea and the Persian Gulf. Later he became a monk and wrote his Christian Topography in Greek. His English translator, J. W. McCrindle, thinks he most probably came from Alexandria. He is called Indicopleustes, the Indian Navigator.

At Adulis, the port of the Ethiopian kingdom of Axum, Cosmas copied old Greek inscriptions for the governor. The king was then preparing a war across the gulf. McCrindle dates the visit to 525 and the writing to about 547.

Photius of Constantinople, a later patriarch, sums up the book's views: the earth is a flat oblong, and the sky a vault over it. Cosmas took the tabernacle of Moses as a copy of the whole world. He learned this, he says, from Scripture and from "that most divine man and great teacher Patricius," who had become Bishop Catholic of all Persia. McCrindle takes Patricius for Mar Aba, head of the Church of the East. He thinks Cosmas most probably belonged to that church, following the earlier scholar De La Croze. Yet Cosmas once calls Mary the Mother of God, a title McCrindle says that church found abhorrent.

In Ceylon, Cosmas says, there is "a Church of Christians, with clergy and a body of believers." At Calliana in India there is "a bishop, who is appointed from Persia."

"This I avouch to be the veritable fact," Cosmas writes of the gospel's spread, "from what I have seen and heard in the many places which I have visited."

## tree/council-of-dvin-609

Council of Dvin

Abraham, the catholicos or head of the Armenian church, did all he could to win the Iberians to his teaching. When he failed, he drew the spiritual sword and cursed them, with the assent of all the Armenians. So Stephen of Taron, an Armenian historian writing around the year 1000, tells it. Stephen names no council and gives no year.

Malachia Ormanian, a former Armenian patriarch of Constantinople, calls these Iberians the Georgians. Their own church head, Kyrion, had been made catholicos by Movses, who led the Armenian church before Abraham. Shortly before Movses died, Kyrion came to doubt what Stephen calls the orthodox faith. He accepted the Council of Chalcedon. Ormanian says he did it to win the emperor's favor.

No record of the council survives among the books this account draws on, and no letter between the two church heads. Of the writers drawn on here, only modern ones tell of a synod at Dvin. The Georgian chronicle, in Marie-Félicité Brosset's French, does not name Kyrion.

Kyrion's own words reach us only through James Issaverdens, a priest of the Armenian Catholic monastery of San Lazzaro in Venice, writing in 1875. He praises Kyrion's learning and piety. He says Abraham sent Kyrion three letters. He gives Kyrion's reply: "I am astonished that you are at variance with all other churches of Christ." Issaverdens names no source for it.

Issaverdens says Abraham called a synod of bishops at Dvin, the chief city of Persian Armenia. They cursed Kyrion "and all those who accepted it," meaning Chalcedon. No writer names the bishops or counts them.

The year is disputed. Ormanian has Abraham elected on 30 April 607 and the council at Dvin in 609. Brosset, in a note of 1876, puts it in 596. Stephen and Issaverdens both set Abraham's quarrel in the reign of the emperor Maurice.

Sebeos, an Armenian bishop of the 600s, tells of a debate before the Persian king. In it the catholicos of Georgia and Albania took Chalcedon's side. Issaverdens adds that Kyrion complained to Maurice, who called bishops of both sides to Constantinople. No other writer tells of that meeting.

Ormanian says the Georgian church, with Kyrion at its head, "became part of the Greek Church."

## tree/cyril-of-scythopolis

Cyril of Scythopolis

John, archbishop of Jerusalem, called the monks into the city by night, Cyril of Scythopolis writes. Those who counted the crowd put it at ten thousand. Anastasius, the duke of Palestine, had come to make John accept Severus of Antioch and condemn the Council of Chalcedon. On the Sunday, in the church of Saint Stephen, John climbed the pulpit with the abbots Theodosius and Sabas beside him. For hours the people shouted for him to condemn the heretics and confirm the council. The three condemned Severus and others who refused the council, and the duke, afraid of the monks, fled to Caesarea.

Cyril tells this in his Life of Sabas. Its passages on Severus were put into French by M.-A. Kugener. Kugener says Cyril was born at Scythopolis in Galilee about 523 and died after 557. He calls the Life of Sabas a source of the greatest value for the church of Jerusalem and the monks of Palestine. Cyril writes for the side that accepted Chalcedon.

Jean-Baptiste Cotelier printed his Life of Euthymius the Great with a Latin translation in 1681. In it Cyril says he often saw Euthymius's tomb flowing with miracles, and wondered how the saint had won such confidence with God. He met many old monks of the desert who had heard the story of Euthymius, some of it from Sabas. He wrote down what each one told him. He names the anchorite Cyriacus as the source of some stories.

Still he could not begin the book, he says. Then Euthymius and Sabas appeared to him. Sabas told Euthymius that Cyril, his son, held the paper but could not set down even a first word. Euthymius dipped a small spoon in a silver jar and put it in Cyril's mouth. Cyril woke with its sweetness on his tongue.

Then he began the Life of Euthymius, and says he was eager to write the deeds of Sabas in another work.

## tree/david-of-wales

David of Wales

A great synod of the British church had gathered at Brefi, the Lives of David say, to answer a revival of the Pelagian heresy. No preacher could make the crowd hear. Paulinus, an old bishop who had once taught David, told them to send for him. He would not stand on the hill. He began to preach from the flat ground, and, says the Welsh Life, "the ground arose as a high mount under his feet".

David, called Dewi in Welsh, was a British bishop whose death the annals place around the year 600. Almost all that is told of him comes from Lives written centuries later. The Latin Life is signed by Ricemarch. Hugh Williams calls him a writer of the eleventh century and quotes a Welsh chronicle that names him under 1096.

The Lives make David the son of Sant and of Non, a nun. They tell how he founded twelve monasteries and settled in the valley of Rosina, which the Britons call Hodnant. His monks plowed without oxen, the Latin Life says, for "every one is an ox to himself". The Life says he lived one hundred and forty-seven years.

Williams thinks the David of the wonders is "the creation, probably, of Ricemarcus", meant to magnify the see of Saint David's. W. J. Rees, who printed both Lives, cites the view that such Lives were rooted in general truth but written to stir devotion, perhaps by exaggerating.

A list of Irish saints, which Williams dates to the 700s, says their second order received the mass from Bishop David and the writer Gildas. The Welsh annals give his death in the year their editor numbers 601. The Irish annals that Williams quotes give 588.

The Welsh Life gives his words after his last sermon. "Lords, brethren, and sisters, be joyful, and keep your faith and belief," he said, "and perform the small things which you have heard and seen with me."

## tree/dionysius-exiguus

Dionysius Exiguus

Cassiodorus remembered a monk who could take any Greek book and run through it in Latin without a stumble. He could read Latin books back again in Greek. You would think, Cassiodorus says, that the words pouring from his mouth had already been written down.

That monk was Dionysius Exiguus, who worked at Rome in the early 500s. Cassiodorus says he was a Scythian by birth but wholly Roman in his ways. The two studied logic together. Dionysius never set himself above the lowest servants, Cassiodorus writes, though he was fit to speak with kings.

Bishop Stephen of Salona asked him to put the church's canons, or council rulings, into Latin from Greek copies. Dionysius set the canons called the Apostles' first, and noted that many did not readily accept them. Cassiodorus says the Roman church used his canons widely, and he told his own monks to read them often.

For Julian, a Roman priest, Dionysius gathered the rulings of past popes. He also put Gregory of Nyssa's book on the making of man into Latin for the priest Eugippius. Some points in it could rightly be faulted, he warned. He had set them out as a translator, he said, and had not approved them as a censor.

At the urging of Bishop Petronius, Dionysius set out the rule for Easter. He wrote that he followed the bishops of Nicaea and their nineteen-year cycle, which Cyril of Alexandria had kept. Louis Duchesne says Nicaea made no such ruling, and that Dionysius drew it from a later letter from Alexandria. Cyril had counted his years from the emperor Diocletian, but Dionysius would not tie his tables to the memory of a wicked persecutor. He numbered the years from the Incarnation instead. That way, he wrote, the start of our hope would be better known, and the Redeemer's passion would shine more clearly.

Later writers differ over his part in the count of years from Christ. William Cave says Dionysius was the first to reckon years this way. Johann Wilhelm Janus agrees, but notes that some credit Bede. Charles Plummer says Bede was the first chronicler to date events from Christ's birth, and so brought the Dionysian era into Western Europe.

Cassiodorus wrote after his death that Dionysius used to pray with them. He asked Dionysius to plead for them, so that those once upheld by his prayers might be helped by his merits.

## tree/dioscorus-of-alexandria

Dioscorus of Alexandria

At the Council of Chalcedon in 451, the minutes of Eutyches's trial were read. Dioscorus, bishop of Alexandria, broke in. The acts record him saying, "I receive 'the of two;' 'the two' I do not receive." He added, "I am forced to be impudent, but the matter is one which touches my soul."

He had followed Cyril of Alexandria as bishop. Charles Lett Feltoe, who translated Leo of Rome's letters, says Leo wrote to him in 445 to lay down church rules.

In 449, Feltoe says, Dioscorus presided at a council at Ephesus by the emperor's order. Its minutes, in S. G. F. Perry's English, give his sentence on Flavian, bishop of Constantinople, and Eusebius of Dorylaeum. He judged them "alien from all the Honor of the Priesthood and of the Episcopate." Flavian answered, "I repudiate you. I protest." Leo later called that meeting one "not of judges but of robbers."

Evagrius of Epiphania, a historian born some eighty years later, gives the charges from the council's own acts. By that record, Chalcedon convicted Dioscorus of hiding Leo's letter, and of having bishops sign a blank paper to depose Flavian. Called three times, he did not come, and the council wrote that he was deposed on the thirteenth of October. Evagrius says he was sent to live at Gangra in Paphlagonia.

The Syriac chronicle known as that of Zachariah of Mitylene, from the side that rejected Chalcedon, tells it another way. It says he was deposed for teaching that Christ became incarnate from two natures, and for refusing two after the union. Asked to sign and regain his throne, he said, "Sooner would Dioscorus see his own hand cut off, and the blood falling on the paper, than do such a thing as that." In Egypt, it says, priests, monks and many of the people broke with his successor.

A History of him goes under the name of his disciple Theopistus. Its editor, François Nau, does not vouch for many details. In it Dioscorus tells the empress Pulcheria she may take his throne of wood, but not the one the Messiah has prepared for him in heaven. Nau and Feltoe date his death in exile to 454.

The History of the Patriarchs of the Coptic Church says, "He preserved the orthodox faith, which persists in the see of the evangelist Saint Mark to this day and for ever."

## tree/dorotheus-of-gaza

Dorotheus of Gaza

While Dorotheus of Gaza lived with the abbot Seridus, the attendant of the abbot John of Gaza fell sick. Seridus sent Dorotheus to serve John. Each evening, as Dorotheus left, the old man gave him a saying of the Fathers. Before each, he asked God to keep their love for one another. One night the saying was to flee these human things and be saved. Another night, it was that if we bear one another's burdens, we fulfill the law of Christ. He kept them all, he says, to guard his life. He served John nine years.

He tells this in his Teachings. Elsewhere in them he calls John the disciple of blessed Barsanuphius. The scholar Andrea Gallandi calls Dorotheus a Palestinian archimandrite, the head of a monastery. The Bollandist scholars, Gallandi reports, place him late in the sixth century.

His example of cutting off one's own will is the blessed Dositheus. A Greek letter printed before the Teachings calls Dositheus his first disciple. Dositheus came, Dorotheus says, from a soft and pampered life, taught nothing of divine things. By obedience alone, he reached the height of perfection in a short time.

Once a leading man of Gaza heard the monks talking of humility, and asked how this could be. Dorotheus asked where he stood among his fellow citizens. As a noble, the man said. At Caesarea he would rank a little below its nobles, and at Antioch he would be a peasant. Before the king at Byzantium, he would be a poor beggar. Holy men are the same, Dorotheus said, and the nearer they come to God, the lower they think themselves.

A later Greek book of saints, revised by Nikodemos the Hagiorite, says he founded his own monastery after John died. The scholar François Nau instead makes him a later head of Seridus's monastery.

Gallandi calls his twenty-four Teachings an outstanding work, most worth reading, and says Theodore the Studite approved them highly.
