What did the first Christians look like to a Roman who despised them? One answer survives.

It comes from Cornelius Tacitus, a Roman historian who wrote histories of Rome, among them the Annals. He wrote as a Roman, and he called Rome's governors "our" men. One chapter of the Annals tells what the emperor Nero did to the Christians of Rome after fire burned the city.

After the fire, Tacitus says, prayers were offered to the gods, but they did not end the rumor. "People still believed the fire had been started by order." The suspicion fell on Nero, though in this chapter Tacitus does not say that Nero did it.

"So, to put an end to the rumor, Nero put forward others to take the blame." Tacitus calls them "people hated for their shameful acts, whom the common people called Christians." Then he explains the name. "Christus, from whom the name came, was executed in the reign of Tiberius by one of our procurators, Pontius Pilatus." A procurator governed for the emperor. Tacitus does not say where he learned this, but Christian writers name the same ruler and the same governor.

He had no good word for the faith. He calls it "a deadly superstition" that began in Judaea and spread "even in Rome."

First, he says, those who pleaded guilty were arrested. Then, "on their information, a huge number of people were convicted. They were found guilty less of setting fire to the city than of hatred of the human race."

"Every kind of mockery was added to their deaths. Some were dressed in the skins of animals, torn apart by dogs, and died. Others were nailed to crosses. Others were condemned to the flames and burned, to serve as lights at night when daylight had gone." Nero, he writes, "offered his gardens for the show."

Tacitus still calls the Christians criminals, yet he records pity for them. "So pity arose, even for criminals who deserved the harshest punishment, as an example to others. For it seemed they were being destroyed not for the public good, but to satisfy one man's cruelty."

Christian writers remembered Nero as the first emperor to persecute them, though most do not mention the fire. Tertullian wrote, "Consult your own records. There you will find that Nero was the first to rage against this sect with the emperor's sword."

Tertullian also answered Tacitus by name, over what another of his books said about the Jews. Tertullian told his readers, "you have dreamed that our god is an ass's head. Cornelius Tacitus planted the suspicion of such a god." He called Tacitus "truly the most talkative of liars."

One later Christian writer, Sulpicius Severus, does tell of the fire. He gives the same three deaths in the same order: the dogs, the crosses, the fires at night. Where Tacitus wrote "criminals," Sulpicius wrote "the innocent."
