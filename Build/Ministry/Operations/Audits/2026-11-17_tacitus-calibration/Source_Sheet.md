## 1. Setting

S1. **His name and his works, from held texts.** Tertullian names him "Cornelius Tacitus" and cites "the fifth book of his histories", where Tacitus begins "the (narrative of the) Jewish war with an account of the origin of the nation" (*Apology* 16, ANF3 2566–2572; Latin "Cornelius Tacitus … in quinta historiarum suarum bellum Iudaicum exorsus ab origine gentis", OEH 17833–17835; Q9). *To the Nations* I.11 says "the fourth book" (ANF3 10081–10082; "in quarta Historiarum suarum", CSEL20 4886; Q10). *To the Nations* II.12 lists him among writers of ancient history, as a source on Saturn: "We read of him in Cassius Severus and in the Corneliuses, Nepos and Tacitus, and, amongst the Greeks also, in Diodorus, and all other compilers of ancient annals" (ANF3 11789–11793). The file heading of the passage itself: "The Annals by Tacitus / Book 15 / 44" (TAC 62–66). CHR; TAC. Documented.

S2. **What he was, in held texts.** No held ancient text gives his office, family, birthplace, dates or career. "Roman senator and historian" appears only in the TAC file header (TAC 5), a vendoring note. Card C1; Library gap G2. The passage itself shows only that he writes as a Roman of the governing side: Pilate is "one of our procurators" (T7, TAC 68).

S3. **His view of the Christians, in his own words (TAC 68).** They are "a class hated for their abominations" (T6); their faith is "a most mischievous superstition" and Judaea "the first source of the evil" (T7); Rome is where "all things hideous and shameful from every part of the world find their centre and become popular" (T7); they were convicted "of hatred against mankind" (T8); he calls them "criminals who deserved extreme and exemplary punishment" (T12). TAC. Documented. Hostile.

S4. **The time, in his own markers.** He dates by reigns, not years. Christus died "during the reign of Tiberius" (T7). The fire and the punishments come under "the emperor" Nero (T5, T6, T11). He writes of Nero in the past tense throughout. When he wrote is not in any held ancient text (N2). TAC. Documented as his account.

S5. **The place.** Rome: the Capitol, "the nearest part of the coast" (T3), "the city" (T8), Nero's "gardens" and "the circus" (T11). Judaea as the faith's first home (T7). Where Tacitus himself was, then or when writing, is not said (N2). TAC. Documented as his account.

S6. **The file is one chapter only.** "SCOPE … THIS IS ONE CHAPTER, NOT THE ANNALS … Nothing here licenses a claim about what Tacitus says elsewhere - not Annals 15.38-43 on the fire itself, not the Histories on Judaea" (TAC 37–43). T1 opens mid-story: "Such indeed were the precautions of human wisdom" refers back to measures told in the lost-to-us earlier chapters (not held).

S7. **Plain spoken forms**, with their support:
- "a Roman historian, Cornelius Tacitus" — support: S1 (Tertullian's name for him; the file heading).
- "who wrote histories of Rome, among them the Annals" — support: S1. "Histories" and "annals" are both held names; the writer should not claim more about the works than S1 shows.
- "a Roman senator" — **card only**: support is the TAC file header alone (S2, C1). No held ancient text says it.
- "writing in the early second century, some fifty years after the fire" — **card only**: rests on the header's "c. 116" and "64 CE" (TAC 8–9).
- "he wrote as a Roman, of 'our' governors" — support: T7.
- "he was hostile to the Christians and their faith" — support: S3.

## 2. Recorded moments

In the order the passage tells them, then the later witnesses. Headings quote the record.

M1. **"The precautions of human wisdom … propitiating the gods."** After the fire, the Sibylline books were consulted, prayers made to Vulcanus, Ceres and Proserpina, Juno entreated by the matrons, and banquets and vigils held (T1–T4, TAC 68; Q1). TAC. Documented as his account; no other held witness tells it.

M2. **"The sinister belief that the conflagration was the result of an order."** Nothing ended the belief that the fire was ordered (T5, TAC 68; Q2). TAC. Documented that he reports the belief. Whether Nero ordered the fire: Contested (D3).

M3. **"Nero fastened the guilt … called Christians by the populace."** To end the rumour, Nero blamed and tortured the Christians (T6, TAC 68; Q3). TAC; retold by Sulpicius (Q18). Documented as his account. The link between the fire and a punishment of Christians: Contested (D2).

M4. **"Christus … suffered the extreme penalty during the reign of Tiberius at the hands of one of our procurators, Pontius Pilatus."** (T7, TAC 68; Q4). TAC. Christian writers give the same ruler and the same governor (Tertullian, *Apology* 21, Q12; *Apology* 5, ANF3 1828–1835; Eusebius, HE II.2, EUS 10189–10199; Jerome, *Chronicle*, Q19c). Documented.

M5. **"A most mischievous superstition, thus checked for the moment, again broke out … even in Rome."** (T7, TAC 68; Q4). TAC. Documented as his account. That Christians were at Rome under Nero: Tertullian (Q11: "cum maxime Romae orientem", "just then rising at Rome"), Sulpicius (Q17, Q18), Lactantius (Q20). Documented.

M6. **"An arrest was first made of all who pleaded guilty; then, upon their information, an immense multitude was convicted."** (T8, TAC 68; Q5). TAC. Documented as his account. What they pleaded guilty to, and how many: Contested (D4, D5).

M7. **"Covered with the skins of beasts, they were torn by dogs … nailed to crosses … burnt, to serve as a nightly illumination."** (T9–T10, TAC 68; Q6). TAC; retold, the same three deaths in the same order, by Sulpicius (Q18). Documented as his account.

M8. **"Nero offered his gardens for the spectacle."** Nero hosted the killings in his gardens and drove or rode in the circus among the people (T11, TAC 68; Q7). TAC. Documented as his account; single witness.

M9. **"There arose a feeling of compassion."** Even for those he calls criminals, pity arose, because they seemed to die "to glut one man's cruelty" (T12, TAC 68; Q8). TAC. Documented as his account; single witness.

M10. **"Cornelius Tacitus first put this notion into people's minds."** Tertullian answers Tacitus by name, twice, on the charge that Christians worship an ass's head (*Apology* 16; *To the Nations* I.11; Q9, Q10). CHR. Documented. Tertullian does not mention the Annals passage.

M11. **"Nero was the first who assailed with the imperial sword the Christian sect."** Tertullian, *Apology* 5 (Q11); also *Apology* 21 (Q12), *To the Nations* I.7 (Q13), *Scorpiace* 15 (Q14). Melito, in Eusebius (Q15). Eusebius, HE II.25 (Q16). Sulpicius (Q17). Jerome (Q19). Lactantius (Q20). Augustine (Q21). CHR. Documented. None of these but Sulpicius connects it with the fire.

M12. **"It happened that Rome was destroyed by fire, while Nero was stationed at Antium."** Sulpicius retells the fire, the blame on Nero, the turn against the Christians, and the three punishments; he adds laws and edicts against the faith and the deaths of Peter and Paul (*Chronicle* II.29, Q18). CHR, about 403 (HALM header, line 9). Documented as his account. That he drew on Tacitus: Inferential-Thin (D7).

## 3. Quotes

Every quote gives the printed English verbatim with its lines, the Latin where the Library holds it, and Rendering 1. Rendering 1 follows the Latin where it and the printed English differ, and says so in a note.

### The passage itself (TAC 68, all on one line)

**Q1. T1–T4: the rites after the fire**
- TAC 68: "Such indeed were the precautions of human wisdom. The next thing was to seek means of propitiating the gods, and recourse was had to the Sibylline books, by the direction of which prayers were offered to Vulcanus, Ceres, and Proserpina. Juno, too, was entreated by the matrons, first, in the Capitol, then on the nearest part of the coast, whence water was procured to sprinkle the fane and image of the goddess. And there were sacred banquets and nightly vigils celebrated by married women."
- Latin: not held.
- Rendering 1: Such were the safeguards that human wisdom could take. Next they sought ways to win the gods' favour, and turned to the Sibylline books. As the books directed, prayers were offered to Vulcanus, Ceres and Proserpina. The married women also pleaded with Juno, first on the Capitol, then on the nearest stretch of coast. From there they drew water to sprinkle the goddess's temple and statue. Married women also held sacred banquets and night-long vigils.
- Note: "fane" is an old word for a temple; "matrons" are married women. "Precautions" points back to measures told in the chapters before, which are not held (S6).

**Q2. T5: the belief that the fire was ordered**
- TAC 68: "But all human efforts, all the lavish gifts of the emperor, and the propitiations of the gods, did not banish the sinister belief that the conflagration was the result of an order."
- Latin: not held.
- Rendering 1: But no human effort, none of the emperor's lavish gifts, and no offering to the gods could drive out the dark belief. People still believed the fire had been started by order.
- Note: the sentence does not say whose order. T6 ("to get rid of the report") shows the suspicion fell on Nero.

**Q3. T6: Nero blames the Christians**
- TAC 68: "Consequently, to get rid of the report, Nero fastened the guilt and inflicted the most exquisite tortures on a class hated for their abominations, called Christians by the populace."
- Latin (Maran's quotation, MAR 12176, 12188–12189): "Ergo abolendo rumori Nero subdidit reos, et quaesitissimis poenis affecit, quos per flagitia invisos vulgus Christianos appellabat."
- Rendering 1: So, to put an end to the rumour, Nero put forward others to take the blame. He punished them with the most carefully devised tortures. They were people hated for their shameful acts, whom the common people called Christians.
- Note: "subdidit reos" is "put forward accused people in his place"; the printed "fastened the guilt" is close. "Exquisite" today mostly means beautiful, which misleads; "quaesitissimis" means "most carefully sought out, most refined". "Abominations" stands for "flagitia", "disgraceful deeds"; Tacitus does not say which deeds (N6). Maran's Latin is a later scholar's quotation, not an edition of Tacitus (G1).

**Q4. T7: Christus, Pilate, and the superstition**
- TAC 68: "Christus, from whom the name had its origin, suffered the extreme penalty during the reign of Tiberius at the hands of one of our procurators, Pontius Pilatus, and a most mischievous superstition, thus checked for the moment, again broke out not only in Judaea, the first source of the evil, but even in Rome, where all things hideous and shameful from every part of the world find their centre and become popular."
- Latin: the first half (Christus, Tiberius, Pilate, "checked for the moment") is **not held**; Maran leaves it out without marking the gap (F5). The second half (MAR 12189–12192): "Exitiabilis superstitio rursus erumpebat non modo per Iudaeam, originem eius mali, sed per urbem etiam, quo cuncta undique atrocia aut pudenda confluunt celebranturque."
- Rendering 1: Christus, from whom the name came, was executed in the reign of Tiberius by one of our procurators, Pontius Pilatus. Checked by this for the moment, a deadly superstition broke out again. It spread not only in Judaea, where the evil began, but even in Rome. There all things horrible and shameful from every part of the world flow together and find favour.
- Note: **"mischievous" misleads.** Today it means playfully naughty. The Latin Maran prints, "exitiabilis", means deadly or ruinous. If the writer quotes the printed English (as Mark's ruling does, "mischievous superstition"), the modern sense will mislead a listener; Rendering 1 says "deadly". "Superstition" ("superstitio") was the Roman word for a foreign or excessive cult; the modern sense, belief in luck, only partly fits. A "procurator" was a Roman official governing on the emperor's behalf; the passage gives the title only. "A most" in the printed English is an intensive; Maran's Latin has no superlative. The first half of the sentence rests on the English alone.

**Q5. T8: the arrests and the charge**
- TAC 68: "Accordingly, an arrest was first made of all who pleaded guilty; then, upon their information, an immense multitude was convicted, not so much of the crime of firing the city, as of hatred against mankind."
- Latin: not held.
- Rendering 1: So first, those who pleaded guilty were arrested. Then, on their information, a huge number of people were convicted. They were found guilty less of setting fire to the city than of hatred of the human race.
- Note: the sentence does not say what they pleaded guilty to (D5). Without the Latin, Rendering 1 keeps the printed sense.

**Q6. T9–T10: the deaths**
- TAC 68: "Mockery of every sort was added to their deaths. Covered with the skins of beasts, they were torn by dogs and perished, or were nailed to crosses, or were doomed to the flames and burnt, to serve as a nightly illumination, when daylight had expired."
- Latin: not held.
- Rendering 1: Every kind of mockery was added to their deaths. Some were dressed in the skins of animals, torn apart by dogs, and died. Others were nailed to crosses. Others were condemned to the flames and burned, to serve as lights at night when daylight had gone.
- Note: "some … others" renders the printed "or … or". Sulpicius's Latin has "ferarum", wild beasts (Q18); the printed Tacitus says only "beasts".

**Q7. T11: Nero's gardens and the circus**
- TAC 68: "Nero offered his gardens for the spectacle, and was exhibiting a show in the circus, while he mingled with the people in the dress of a charioteer or stood aloft on a car."
- Latin: not held.
- Rendering 1: Nero offered his gardens for the show. He was also staging games at the circus. There he mixed with the people dressed as a chariot driver, or stood high up in a chariot.
- Note: the Roman "circus" was a chariot-racing ground, not a modern circus; "car" here means a chariot. Both modern senses mislead.

**Q8. T12: the compassion**
- TAC 68: "Hence, even for criminals who deserved extreme and exemplary punishment, there arose a feeling of compassion; for it was not, as it seemed, for the public good, but to glut one man's cruelty, that they were being destroyed."
- Latin: not held.
- Rendering 1: So pity arose, even for criminals who deserved the harshest punishment, as an example to others. For it seemed they were being destroyed not for the public good, but to satisfy one man's cruelty.
- Note: Tacitus still calls them criminals here; the pity is for how and why they died, not a judgement that they were innocent.

### Christian writers who name Tacitus (Tertullian only)

**Q9. Tertullian, Apology 16: Tacitus and the ass's head**
- ANF3 2566–2580: "For, like some others, you are under the delusion that our god is an ass's head. Cornelius Tacitus first put this notion into people's minds. In the fifth book of his histories, beginning the (narrative of the) Jewish war with an account of the origin of the nation; and theorizing at his pleasure about the origin, as well as the name and the religion of the Jews, he states that having been delivered, or rather, in his opinion, expelled from Egypt, in crossing the vast plains of Arabia, where water is so scanty, they were in extremity from thirst; but taking the guidance of the wild asses, which it was thought might be seeking water after feeding, they discovered a fountain, and thereupon in their gratitude they consecrated a head of this species of animal."
- ANF3 2583–2586: "But the said Cornelius Tacitus (the very opposite of tacit in telling lies) informs us in the work already mentioned, that when Cneius Pompeius captured Jerusalem, he entered the temple to see the arcana of the Jewish religion, but found no image there."
- Latin (OEH 17832–17835, 17915–17919): "Nam et, ut quidam, somniastis caput asininum esse deum nostrum. Hanc Cornelius Tacitus suspicionem eiusmodi dei inseruit. Is enim in quinta historiarum suarum bellum Iudaicum exorsus ab origine gentis etiam de ipsa tam origine quam de nomine et religione gentis quae voluit argumentatus …" and "At enim idem Cornelius Tacitus, sane ille mendaciorum loquacissimus, in eadem historia refert Gneum Pompeium, cum Hierusalem cepisset proptereaque templum adisset speculandis Iudaicae religionis arcanis, nullum illic reperisse simulacrum."
- Rendering 1: For, like some others, you have dreamed that our god is an ass's head. Cornelius Tacitus planted the suspicion of such a god. In the fifth book of his Histories he began the Jewish war with the origin of the nation. He argued as he pleased about its origin, and about its name and religion. … But that same Cornelius Tacitus, truly the most talkative of liars, reports in the same history about Gnaeus Pompey. Pompey took Jerusalem and went into the temple to look into the secrets of the Jewish religion. He found no image there.
- Note: **"(the very opposite of tacit in telling lies)" is the translator's.** Tertullian's Latin is "sane ille mendaciorum loquacissimus", "truly the most talkative of liars". The Latin name Tacitus is also the Latin word for "silent", which is the play the translator brings out. **"First" is the translator's too**: the Latin says Tacitus "inseruit", planted, the suspicion, with no "first". The printed "Cor<pb …/>nelius" is split by a page tag at ANF3 2570.

**Q10. Tertullian, To the Nations I.11: the same charge, the same answer**
- ANF3 10080–10086: "for some among you have dreamed that our god is an ass's head,—an absurdity which Cornelius Tacitus first suggested. In the fourth book of his histories, where he is treating of the Jewish war, he begins his description with the origin of that nation, and gives his own views respecting both the origin and the name of their religion."
- ANF3 10093–10098: "The same Cornelius Tacitus, however,—who, to say the truth, is most loquacious in falsehood—forgetting his later statement, relates how Pompey the Great, after conquering the Jews and capturing Jerusalem, entered the temple, but found nothing in the shape of an image, though he examined the place carefully."
- Latin (CSEL20 4883–4887, 4912, 4918–4923): "nam, ut quidam, somniastis caput asininum esse deum nostrum: hanc Cornelius Tacitus suspicionem fecit. is enim in quarta Historiarum suarum, ubi de bello Iudaico digerit, ab origine gentis exorsus …" and "at enim idem Cornelius Tacitus, sane ille mendaciorum loquacissimus, oblitus affirmationis suae in posterioribus refert Pompeium Magnum de Iudaeis debellatis captisque Hierosolymis templum adisse et perscrutatum nihil simulacri reperisse. ubi ergo is deus fuerit?" (Also OEH 33299–33316.)
- Rendering 1: For some of you have dreamed that our god is an ass's head. Cornelius Tacitus started this suspicion. In the fourth book of his Histories, where he treats the Jewish war, he begins with the origin of the nation. … But that same Cornelius Tacitus, truly the most talkative of liars, forgot his own claim. Later in the work he reports that Pompey the Great conquered the Jews and took Jerusalem. He went into the temple, searched it, and found no image. Where, then, was this god?
- Note: "first" is again the translator's ("fecit", "made", has no "first"). "Forgetting his later statement" in the printed English: the Latin "oblitus affirmationis suae in posterioribus refert" is "forgetting his own claim, he reports later on". The book number differs from Q9 (fourth here, fifth there): card C5.

### Christian writers on Nero's persecution (who do not name Tacitus)

**Q11. Tertullian, Apology 5: Nero the first**
- ANF3 1835–1841: "Consult your histories; you will there find that Nero was the first who assailed with the imperial sword the Christian sect, making progress then especially at Rome. But we glory in having our condemnation hallowed by the hostility of such a wretch. For any one who knows him, can understand that not except as being of singular excellence did anything bring on it Nero's condemnation."
- Latin (OEH 13006–13011): "Consulite commentarios vestros, illic reperietis primum Neronem in hanc sectam cum maxime Romae orientem Caesariano gladio ferocisse. Sed tali dedicatore damnationis nostrae etiam gloriamur. Qui enim scit illum, intellegere potest non nisi grande aliquod bonum a Nerone damnatum."
- Rendering 1: Consult your own records. There you will find that Nero was the first to rage against this sect with the emperor's sword, just as it was rising at Rome. But we even take pride in such a founder of our condemnation. Anyone who knows him can see that Nero condemned nothing that was not some great good.
- Note: "commentarios vestros" is "your records" (printed "your histories"). Tertullian names no writer here (N8). Eusebius quotes this sentence in Greek translation (Q16).

**Q12. Tertullian, Apology 21: Pilate, Tiberius, and Nero's sword**
- ANF3 2940–2945: "at last they brought Him before Pontius Pilate, at that time Roman governor of Syria; and, by the violence of their outcries against Him, extorted a sentence giving Him up to them to be crucified."
- ANF3 2977–2987: "All these things Pilate did to Christ; and now in fact a Christian in his own convictions, he sent word of Him to the reigning Cæsar, who was at the time Tiberius." … "at last by Nero's cruel sword sowed the seed of Christian blood at Rome."
- Latin (OEH 20543, 20848, 20853–20854): "Pontio Pilato, Syriam tunc ex parte Romana procuranti"; "… Christianus, Caesari tunc Tiberio nuntiavit"; "Romae postremo per Neronis saevitiam sanguinem Christianum seminaverunt."
- Rendering 1: At last they brought him before Pontius Pilate, who then governed Syria for Rome. … Pilate, already a Christian in his own conscience, reported him to the emperor of the day, Tiberius. … At last, at Rome, through Nero's cruelty, the disciples sowed Christian blood.
- Note: "procuranti" is the verb of the procurator's office, the same office Tacitus names (Q4). Tertullian places Pilate over "Syria"; Tacitus says only "one of our procurators". "Cruel sword" in the printed English: the Latin is "saevitiam", cruelty, with no sword.

**Q13. Tertullian, To the Nations I.7: "under Nero it was ruthlessly condemned"**
- ANF3 9467–9481: "This name of ours took its rise in the reign of Augustus; under Tiberius it was taught with all clearness and publicity; under Nero it was ruthlessly condemned, and you may weigh its worth and character even from the person of its persecutor. If that prince was a pious man, then the Christians are impious; if he was just, if he was pure, then the Christians are unjust and impure; if he was not a public enemy, we are enemies of our country: what sort of men we are, our persecutor himself shows, since he of course punished what produced hostility to himself. Now, although every other institution which existed under Nero has been destroyed, yet this of ours has firmly remained—righteous, it would seem, as being unlike the author (of its persecution). Two hundred and fifty years, then, have not yet passed since our life began."
- Latin (CSEL20 4191–4200): "principe Augusto nomen hoc ortum est, Tiberio disciplina eius inluxit, sub Nerone damnatio inualuit, ut iam hinc de persona persecutoris ponderetis: si pius ille princeps, impii Christiani, si iustus, si castus, iniusti et incesti Christiani, si non hostis publicus, nos publici hostes; quales simus, damnator ipse demonstrauit, utique aemula sibi puniens. et tamen permansit erasis omnibus hoc solum institutum Neronianum, iustum denique ut dissimile sui auctoris. igitur aetati nostrae nondum anni ccl." (Also OEH 32091–32098.)
- Rendering 1: This name of ours arose under the emperor Augustus. Under Tiberius its teaching shone out. Under Nero its condemnation took hold, so you may judge us from the person of our persecutor. ## 4. Impact as the record gives it

Ranked heaviest first. Weight is what later witnesses carried on and used of what his passage tells, not what is easiest to tell. The ranking is about the record, not about Tacitus's standing.

I1. **"Nero fastened the guilt … on a class … called Christians."** Nero as the first to punish Christians at Rome is the one thing in his passage that almost every later Christian witness carries: Tertullian four times (Q11–Q14), Melito (Q15), Eusebius (Q16), Sulpicius (Q17, Q18), Jerome (Q19b), Lactantius (Q20), Augustine (Q21). None of them cites Tacitus for it; Tertullian sends readers to "your records" (Q11). TAC; CHR. — Rank 1. Carrying quotes: Q3 (Tacitus), Q11 (the Christian side).

I2. **"Christus … during the reign of Tiberius … Pontius Pilatus."** The same ruler and the same governor stand in Christian witnesses, without reference to Tacitus: Tertullian (Q12; *Apology* 5, ANF3 1828–1835), Eusebius (HE II.2, EUS 10189–10199, on Tertullian's word), Jerome (Q19c, on Tertullian's word). TAC; CHR. — Rank 2. Carrying quote: Q4.

I3. **"Covered with the skins of beasts … nailed to crosses … burnt, to serve as a nightly illumination."** The fire, the blame shifted to the Christians, and the three deaths in the same order are carried by one held Christian witness, Sulpicius, about 403 (Q18), who adds Antium, laws, edicts, and Peter and Paul. TAC; CHR. — Rank 3. Carrying quotes: Q6, Q18.

I4. **"Cornelius Tacitus, sane ille mendaciorum loquacissimus."** The one place a held Christian writer answers Tacitus by name: Tertullian, twice, calls him "the most talkative of liars", and turns his own report of Pompey's visit to the temple against him (Q9, Q10). It concerns the *Histories* on the Jews, not the Annals passage. CHR. — Rank 4. Carrying quote: Q9.

I5. **"Hatred against mankind" and "abominations."** The charges Tacitus names without detail are the charges the apologists answer: "enemies of the human race" (Q23); child-killing and incest (Q24); "so many infants have been slain; so many loaves steeped in blood" (*To the Nations* I.7, ANF3 9482–9485). Neither text names Tacitus; the link is Inferential-Thin. — Rank 5. Carrying quote: Q23.

I6. **"An immense multitude."** A great number of Christians at Rome under Nero is carried in other forms: Sulpicius's "abundante iam Christianorum multitudine" before the fire (Q18), Lactantius's "great multitude" of converts (Q20). First Clement's "great multitude of the elect" (Q22) may speak of the same dead, but does not say so (Inferential-Thin). — Rank 6.

**Card only, not for the story**

I7. **"Superstitio exitiabilis" in Sulpicius II.46** (Q25): the same two words, used of a heresy; the borrowing is an editor's claim (C6).
I8. **Modern weight of the passage.** The NPNF note at HE II.25 calls the persecution "the terrible Neronian persecution which Tacitus describes so fully" and argues for "Tacitus' accuracy in this case" (EUS 13843–13857). The TAC header calls it "the standard public-domain English of this chapter" (TAC 20–21); the Library registry row says four project records cited it (`cic/texts/REGISTRY.yaml` 1101–1110). Modern, not held ancient witness.

## 5. Disputes and unclear points

D1. **How far can he be trusted on Christ?**
- (a) What he says: Christus, executed under Tiberius by the procurator Pontius Pilatus; the name comes from him (Q4). He names no source (N1). TAC.
- (b) Christian writers agree on ruler and governor, without citing him: Tertullian (Q12), Eusebius on Tertullian's word (EUS 10189–10199), Jerome on Tertullian's word (Q19c). CHR. They add what Tacitus does not: Pilate's report to Tiberius and Pilate "a Christian in his own convictions" (Q12).
- (c) Christian writers differ among themselves on who killed him: Tertullian says the Jews "extorted a sentence" from Pilate (Q12); Lactantius, in the same work as Q20, says "Jesus Christ was crucified by the Jews" (ANF7 10000). Tertullian makes Pilate governor "of Syria" (Q12); Tacitus does not name the province he ruled. CHR.
- (d) Tertullian calls Tacitus "the most talkative of liars", but on the Jews' origins, not on Christ (Q9, Q10). CHR.
- (e) Modern: the TAC header says the passage's "authenticity is not seriously disputed" (TAC 53–54). MOD, card C2.

D2. **Was the punishment of Christians tied to the fire?**
- (a) Yes: Tacitus (Q3, Q5). TAC. Sulpicius, about 403 (Q18). CHR.
- (b) Nero's persecution told without the fire: Tertullian (Q11–Q14), Melito (Q15), Eusebius (Q16), Augustine (Q21). Jerome's *Chronicle* has both the fire and the persecution, as separate entries far apart (Q19a, Q19b). CHR.
- (c) Another reason given: Lactantius says Nero acted because "a great multitude revolted daily from the worship of idols" (Q20). Sulpicius gives both: the faith's growth (Q17) and the fire (Q18). CHR.
- (d) Even Tacitus says they were convicted "not so much of the crime of firing the city, as of hatred against mankind" (Q5). TAC.
- (e) Modern: the TAC header names a "live, non-fringe debate" on whether a fire-linked persecution "of Christians AS A NAMED GROUP actually occurred in 64" (TAC 53–59). MOD, card C2. Suetonius lists the punishment of Christians among Nero's regulations, apart from the fire (card C4). PAG.

D3. **Did Nero start the fire?**
- (a) Tacitus reports a "sinister belief" and a "report" (Q2, Q3); he does not say in this chapter that Nero did it. TAC.
- (b) Sulpicius: "the opinion of all" blamed him; Nero was at Antium (Q18). CHR.
- (c) Jerome: Nero set fire to most of Rome "to see a likeness of burning Troy" (Q19a). CHR.
- (d) Suetonius says Nero set fire to the city (card C4). PAG.

D4. **How many died?**
- (a) "An immense multitude" convicted (Q5). TAC. No number.
- (b) Sulpicius: the Christians were "very many" before the fire, and "most" were kept to be burned as lights (Q18). No number. CHR.
- (c) First Clement: "a great multitude of the elect" (Q22), not tied in its text to Nero or Rome's fire. CHR. Inferential-Thin as a witness to these events.

D5. **What did the first ones "plead guilty" to?**
- (a) The printed English gives no object (Q5). The Latin of this sentence is not held (G1). TAC.
- (b) The same sentence says the many were convicted less of arson than of "hatred against mankind" (Q5). TAC.
- (c) Tertullian complains that Christians are tortured "not to make them confess what they do, but to make them deny what they are" (*Apology* 7, ANF3 1956–1958): the name itself on trial. CHR. Whether that is the case Tacitus tells: Inferential-Thin.

D6. **The punishments.**
- (a) Tacitus: mockery; animal skins and dogs; crosses; burning as night lights; in Nero's gardens, with a show in the circus (Q6, Q7). TAC.
- (b) Sulpicius: the same three deaths, as "new kinds of death"; adds laws and edicts making it unlawful to be a Christian, and Paul beheaded, Peter crucified (Q18). CHR.
- (c) Tertullian, Eusebius, Jerome, Lactantius tell of Peter and Paul under Nero (Q14, Q16, Q19b, Q20); Tacitus names neither (N7). CHR.
- (d) Tacitus calls them criminals (Q8); Sulpicius calls them innocent (Q18). TAC; CHR.

D7. **Did Sulpicius draw on Tacitus?** He does not name him. His account matches Tacitus's sentences in order (blame, shift to the Christians, three deaths, lights at night), and shares the words "superstitio exitiabilis" elsewhere (Q25). Halm's apparatus and the NPNF note point to Tacitus (card C6). Inferential-Thin.

D8. **What is "this one Neronian institution" in Tertullian?** The Latin "permansit erasis omnibus hoc solum institutum Neronianum" (Q13) can be read as the Christian body that outlived Nero (the printed English: "this of ours has firmly remained") or as Nero's condemnation of the Christians that outlived him. The sentence does not settle it. CHR.

D9. **The name "Christians".** Tacitus says the populace called them so and that the name came from Christus (Q3, Q4). The TAC header notes a manuscript reading "chrestianos" corrected to "christianos" (TAC 45–51), which the English cannot show; Maran's Latin prints "Christianos" (Q3). MOD, card C2. Suetonius has "Chrestus" in another context (card C4).

## 6. What the record does not tell us

N1. Where Tacitus got his information about Christus, Pilate and the Christians. He names no source, record or informant (Q4).
N2. When and where he wrote, and whether he saw any of the events. The held ancient texts give no date for him; "c. 116" is the file header's (C1).
N3. His office, family, birthplace, career or death. No held ancient text gives them; "senator" is the file header's (S2, C1).
N4. The Latin of most of the passage (T1–T5, the first half of T7, T8–T12), and the rest of the Annals and the Histories. So the sheet cannot check Tertullian's account of the *Histories* (Q9, Q10), nor the chapters on the fire itself (S6).
N5. Any victim's name, the number of the dead, where the Christians met, or what they believed. Tacitus says nothing of their teaching beyond the name from Christus.
N6. Which "abominations" (flagitia) he means (Q3). The apologists list charges (Q24), but not as his.
N7. Peter and Paul. Tacitus names no Christian. The later link of the apostles' deaths to Nero is the Christian writers' (D6).
N8. Which "records" or "lives of the Caesars" Tertullian means (Q11, Q14). He does not say they are Tacitus's.
N9. Any held early Christian text that quotes or names the Annals passage. None was found: Tertullian names Tacitus only for the *Histories*; Sulpicius retells without naming; Eusebius names Tertullian as his witness.
N10. How Christians in Rome remembered the crowd's compassion (Q8). No held Christian witness mentions it.
N11. Not read for this sheet, though held: the Latin of *Apology* 7 (OEH). Searched and not found: the Greek of First Clement.
