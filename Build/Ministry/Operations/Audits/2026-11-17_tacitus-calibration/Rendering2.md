**Q2. T5: the belief that the fire was ordered**
- Rendering 2: Yet nothing could banish the sinister belief that the fire had been ordered. Not human effort, not the emperor's lavish gifts, not the offerings made to win over the gods.

**Q3. T6: Nero blames the Christians**
- Rendering 2: So, to put an end to the rumour, Nero put others in his place as the accused. He inflicted the most carefully devised punishments on people hated for their shameful deeds. The common people called them Christians.
- Note: "subdidit reos" means he substituted culprits, not simply "fastened the guilt". The source file's front matter warns that the Medicean manuscript reads "chrestianos"; Maran prints "Christianos", so the name cannot be leaned on.

**Q4. T7: Christus, Pilate, and the superstition**
- Rendering 2: Christus, from whom the name comes, was put to death in the reign of Tiberius by one of our procurators, Pontius Pilate. Checked for the moment, the deadly superstition broke out again. It spread not only through Judaea, where this evil began, but through Rome itself. There everything horrible or shameful flows in from every side and becomes fashionable.
- Note: The first sentence and "checked for the moment" are rendered from the English, since that Latin is not held. "Superstitio" to a Roman meant an excessive, foreign or illicit cult, not a mistaken belief; "per urbem" is "the City", i.e. Rome. "Procurator" is the Roman title Tacitus gives Pilate; it may need a gloss in the brief.

**Q5. T8: the arrests and the charge**
- Rendering 2: So first those who confessed were arrested. Then, on their information, a huge number were convicted. The charge was not so much setting fire to the city as hatred of the human race.
- Note: Latin not held. What they confessed to (being Christians, or the arson) is not stated. "Hatred of the human race" can mean their hatred of humanity or humanity's hatred of them; the English keeps the first sense.

**Q6. T9–T10: the deaths**
- Rendering 2: Every kind of mockery was added to their deaths. Some were wrapped in the skins of beasts and torn apart by dogs. Some were nailed to crosses. Others were set aside for burning, to serve as lights at night once daylight had gone.
- Note: Latin not held. "Doomed to the flames" is rendered as "set aside for burning".

**Q7. T11: Nero's gardens and the circus**
- Rendering 2: Nero offered his gardens for the spectacle. He also put on games at the racetrack. There he mingled with the crowd dressed as a charioteer, or stood high in a chariot.
- Note: Latin not held. "Circus" here is the Roman chariot-racing track, and "car" is a chariot.

**Q8. T12: the compassion**
- Rendering 2: So pity arose, even for guilty people who deserved the harshest punishment as a warning to others. It seemed they were being destroyed not for the public good, but to feed one man's cruelty.
- Note: Latin not held. "Exemplary" means punishment that makes an example of someone, not "excellent".

**Q9. Tertullian, Apology 16: Tacitus and the ass's head**
- Rendering 2: Like some others, you have dreamed that our god is an ass's head. Cornelius Tacitus planted this suspicion about such a god. In the fifth book of his Histories he starts on the Jewish war from the origin of the nation. He argues as he likes about that origin, and about the nation's name and religion. He reports that the Jews were set free from Egypt, or, as he thought, driven out. They were wasting away with thirst in the vast, waterless wastes of Arabia. Wild asses, thought perhaps to be going to drink after grazing, showed them the way to a spring. In gratitude for this, they consecrated the likeness of that animal.
- Rendering 2 (second passage): Yet this same Cornelius Tacitus, truly the most talkative of liars, reports something else in the same history. Gnaeus Pompey took Jerusalem and so went into the Temple to examine the secrets of the Jewish religion. He found no image there.
- Note: The sheet's Latin stops at "argumentatus"; the rest is rendered from the same Oehler text, 17836 and 17911–17913. "(narrative of the)" in the English is the translator's insertion and is dropped. "Superficiem" is "likeness" or "face"; the English "head" follows Tertullian's charge. "The very opposite of tacit" is the translator's spelling-out of a pun the Latin makes only by calling Tacitus ("silent") "most talkative".

**Q11. Tertullian, Apology 5: Nero the first**
- Rendering 2: Look in your own records. There you will find that Nero was the first to rage with the emperor's sword against this sect, just then rising, above all at Rome. But we even take pride that such a man began our condemnation. Anyone who knows him can see that Nero condemned nothing unless it was some great good.
- Note: "Cum maxime" can mean "just then" or "especially"; the rendering keeps both. "Commentarios" are the Romans' own records or chronicles.

**Q18. Sulpicius Severus, Chronicle II.29: the fire and the punishments retold**
- Rendering 2: Meanwhile, when the Christians were already very numerous, Rome happened to burn while Nero was staying at Antium. But everyone turned the blame for the fire back on the emperor. People believed he had sought the glory of renewing the city. Nothing Nero did could stop them thinking he had ordered the fire. So he turned the blame onto the Christians. The cruellest tortures were inflicted on the innocent. New kinds of death were even devised. Covered in the hides of wild beasts, they died torn apart by dogs. Many were fastened to crosses or burned with fire. Most were kept back for one purpose: once the day had ended, they were burned to give light at night. This is how the savagery against the Christians began. Later their religion was also forbidden by laws. Edicts were posted publicly, and it was not lawful to be a Christian. At that time Paul and Peter were condemned to death. One had his neck cut through by the sword; Peter was raised on a cross.
- Note: "Plerique" means "most" or "very many", not the English "not a few". "Quaestiones" are interrogations under torture. The OCR "^tur" before "uertit" is most likely "igitur" ("so"), matching the English "therefore".
