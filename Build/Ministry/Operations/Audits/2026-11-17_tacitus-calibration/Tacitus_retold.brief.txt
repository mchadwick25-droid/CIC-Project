What did the first Christians look like to a Roman who despised them? One answer survives.

It comes from Cornelius Tacitus, a Roman historian who wrote histories of Rome, among them the Annals. He wrote as a Roman, and he counted Rome's governors as his own side. One chapter of the Annals tells what the emperor Nero did to the Christians of Rome after fire burned the city.

After the fire, Tacitus says, the Romans prayed to their gods, and the emperor gave lavish gifts. Still the rumor would not die. People believed the fire had been started by order, and the suspicion fell on Nero. In this chapter, Tacitus does not say that Nero did it.

To end the rumor, he writes, Nero put forward others to take the blame. They were hated for shameful acts, and the common people called them Christians. Tacitus explains the name. "Christus, from whom the name came, was executed in the reign of Tiberius by one of our procurators, Pontius Pilatus." A procurator governed for the emperor. Tacitus does not say where he learned this, but Christian writers name the same ruler and the same governor.

He had no good word for the faith. He calls it "a deadly superstition." The execution checked it for a moment, he says. Then it broke out again, in Judaea where it began, and even in Rome.

First, those who pleaded guilty were arrested. On their word, a huge number were convicted. Tacitus says they were found guilty less of setting fire to the city than of hatred of the human race.

Nero had them punished with carefully devised tortures, and they were mocked as they died. "Some were dressed in the skins of animals, torn apart by dogs, and died." Others were nailed to crosses. Others were burned as lights when night fell. Nero gave his own gardens for the show.

To Tacitus they were still criminals who deserved the harshest punishment. Yet he records that pity arose for them. "For it seemed they were being destroyed not for the public good, but to satisfy one man's cruelty."

Christian writers remembered Nero as the first emperor to persecute them, though most do not mention the fire. Tertullian told his readers to look in their own records, and they would find it there.

Tertullian also answered Tacitus by name, over another book. In his Histories, Tacitus had written about the origin of the Jews. Tertullian says this planted the suspicion that the Christians' god was an ass's head. He calls Tacitus "truly the most talkative of liars."

One later Christian writer, Sulpicius Severus, does tell of the fire. He gives the same three deaths in the same order: the dogs, the crosses, the fires at night. But where Tacitus wrote of criminals, Sulpicius wrote of "the innocent."
