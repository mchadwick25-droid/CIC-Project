Cornelius Tacitus was no friend of the Christians. He was a Roman historian, and he called their faith "a deadly superstition." Yet his Annals tell how the Christians of Rome died under the emperor Nero. This is the story as he gives it.

Rome had burned. Afterward the city prayed to its gods. Married women held sacred banquets and kept vigils through the night. The emperor gave lavish gifts. None of it drove out a dark belief. People still thought the fire had been started by order, and the suspicion fell on Nero.

To end the rumor, Nero put forward others to take the blame. They were a hated group, "whom the common people called Christians." He punished them with tortures devised with great care.

Here Tacitus stops to explain the name, and he gives no source for what he says. "Christus, from whom the name came, was executed in the reign of Tiberius by one of our procurators, Pontius Pilatus." That checked the faith for a moment. Then it broke out again, in Judaea where it began, and even in Rome.

The arrests came in two waves. First, those who pleaded guilty were seized. Then, on their information, a huge number of people were convicted. "They were found guilty less of setting fire to the city than of hatred of the human race."

They died as a show. Some were dressed in animal skins and torn apart by dogs. Others were nailed to crosses. Others were burned as lights when daylight had gone. Nero offered his own gardens for it. He staged games as well, and mixed with the people dressed as a chariot driver.

To Tacitus they were still criminals who deserved the harshest punishment. Even so, pity arose for them. "For it seemed they were being destroyed not for the public good, but to satisfy one man's cruelty." Only Tacitus tells of that pity.

Christians did not forget Nero. They remembered him as the first emperor to turn his sword on them. Tertullian, a Christian writer, pointed the Romans to their own records for it. He also answered Tacitus by name. Some Romans said the Christians' god was an ass's head. Tertullian traced that tale to what Tacitus had written about the Jews, and called him "truly the most talkative of liars."

Later Sulpicius Severus told the fire again in his Chronicle, with the same three deaths in the same order. He added that Paul was beheaded then, and Peter raised on a cross. But where Tacitus saw criminals, Sulpicius saw "the innocent." Of those deaths he wrote, "That was how the savagery against the Christians began."
