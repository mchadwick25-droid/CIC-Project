**Who the main witnesses are.** Socrates of Constantinople, a church historian of the city, writing a generation after (his history "stops with the year 439", Zenos's introduction, SS 1265); critical of John's temper and of Theophilus alike. Sozomen, the same generation, working "for the most part independently" of Socrates (Hartranft's note, SS 42654-42656); admiring. The Dialogue (PALL), by a bishop of John's party written soon after his death, a partisan. The minutes of the Oak, written by his enemies, known only from Photius's ninth-century summary. Theodoret, writing about 438-450 (Jackson's note, THDT 17855-17864), admiring.

## 1. Setting

**S1. Antioch, his birthplace; Constantinople, his see.** SS 17130-17132 (Socrates VI.3), later (a generation). Documented.
> "John was a native of Antioch in Syria-Cœle, son of Secundus and Anthusa, and scion of a noble family in that country."
- Spoken: "Antioch, in Syria" and "Constantinople, the emperor's city". Constantinople as the capital: Socrates VI.2 "the court" chooses its bishop (SS 17097-17098).

**S2. Schooling: Libanius, then Diodore and the literal sense.** SS 17132-17133, 17170-17185 (Socrates VI.3); SS 42688-42698 (Sozomen VIII.2). Later. Documented.
> Socrates: "He studied rhetoric under Libanius the sophist, and philosophy under Andragathius the philosopher." ... "under the guidance of Diodorus ... and Carterius, who then presided over a monastic institution. The former of these was subsequently elevated to the bishopric of Tarsus, and wrote many treatises, in which he limited his attention to the literal sense of scripture, avoiding that which was mystical."
> Sozomen: "He had as teachers of this philosophy, Carterius and Diodorus, two celebrated presidents of ascetic institutions. Diodorus was afterwards the governor of the church of Tarsus, and, I have been informed, left many books of his own writings in which he explained the significance of the sacred words and avoided allegory."
- Zenos's note (SS 17173-17179) calls this "the corner-stone of the Antiochian system of interpretation": the modern editor's label, not the old sources'. Modern, makes a claim of its own.

**S3. Twelve years a presbyter at Antioch.** PALL 3443-3446 (the Dialogue, ch. V). Contemporary partisan. Documented (single witness for the number).
> "Bishop Flavianus therefore ordained him presbyter. For twelve years he was a shining light in the Church of Antioch, lending dignity to the priesthood there by the strictness of his life"
- Reader, then deacon at Antioch: SS 17211-17215 (Socrates VI.3): "appointed him a reader in the church at Antioch ... Meletius having not long after conferred on him the rank of deacon, he produced his work On the Priesthood".

**S4. The Matthew sermons were preached at Antioch.** N110 5301-5303 (Homily VII, own words). Documented.
> "ye, when there is a question of precedence, claim to take place of the whole word, forasmuch as our city first crowned itself with the name of Christian"
- The city of Acts 11:26 (Prevost's and Riddle's note, N110 5303-5305). The preface's "undoubtedly delivered at Antioch" (N110 533-535) is the editor's; the homily's own words carry it. Homily 50 itself names no city.

**S5. Dates the old sources fix, by consuls.** Documented each; the AD figure is the editor's conversion.
- Ordained bishop: "the 26th of February, under the following consulate, which the Emperor Honorius celebrated" (SS 17109-17115, Socrates VI.2; note 398).
- Eutropius's fall in his own consular year (SS 17362-17365, Socrates VI.5; note 399).
- Second exile: the fire "on the 20th of June, under the sixth consulate of Honorius" (SS 18500-18502; note 404).
- Death: "on the 14th of September, in the following consulate, which was the seventh of Honorius, and the second of Theodosius" (SS 18609-18612; note 407).
- Body brought back: "the 27th of January, in the sixteenth consulate of the Emperor Theodosius" (SS 21469-21471; note 438).
- **No old source dates the Oak.** "403" is Freese's note citing Hefele (PHOT 3862-3864); the statue's 403 is an editor's note from Prosper and Marcellinus (SS 18429-18431). Neither year for the story.

**S6. Tradition.** Census `antiochene-exegetical-christianity-chrysostom-ce`: shortName "Antioch", informalName "Antioch in Chrysostom's Day". Plainest spoken word: "the church of Antioch". "Antiochene school" or "system" is the modern editors' label (S2 note).

## 2. Events, in order

**E1. At Antioch he preached that honoring Christ meant feeding the poor.** N110 29931-29939, 29948-29950 (Homily 50 on Matthew), own words. Documented. Greek not held; English printed 1888.
> "Wouldest thou do honor to Christ’s body? Neglect Him not when naked; do not while here thou honorest Him with silken garments, neglect Him perishing without of cold and nakedness. For He that said, “This is my body,” and by His word confirmed the fact, “This same said, “Ye saw me an hungered, and fed me not;” and, “Inasmuch as ye did it not to one of the least of these, ye did it not to me.” ... For This indeed needs not coverings, but a pure soul; but that requires much attention."
> "Even so do thou honor Him with this honor, which He ordained, spending thy wealth on poor people. Since God hath no need at all of golden vessels, but of golden souls."
- Explain it: "Christ's body" here is the bread of Communion on the altar, which the rich honored with gold cups and silk cloths (N110 29907-29914, 29959-29965). He sets beside it Christ's words that whatever is not done for the hungry is not done for him (Matthew 25, quoted at 29935-29937).
- His hearers: Sozomen says his boldness "pleased the people, but grieved the wealthy and the powerful, who were guilty of most of the vices which he denounced" (SS 42723-42728). Later. Documented.

**E2. 398: the court chooses him; Eutropius makes Theophilus ordain him.** SS 17097-17109 (Socrates VI.2); SS 42750-42780 (Sozomen VIII.2). Later. Documented (the ordination); the threat: Socrates plainly, Sozomen "they say" (Widely Accepted).
> Socrates: "The court however gave the preference to John: and inasmuch as many had revived the accusations against Theophilus, and prepared for presentation to the bishops then convened memorials of various charges, Eutropius ... the chief officer of the imperial bed-chamber collected these documents, and showed them to Theophilus, bidding him ‘choose between ordaining John, and undergoing a trial on the charges made against him.’ Theophilus terrified at this alternative, consented to ordain John."
> Sozomen: "He feared Eutropius, who was artfully eager for this ordination. Eutropius then presided over the imperial house, and they say he threatened Theophilus, that unless he would vote with the other bishops, he would have to defend himself against those who desired to accuse him"
- Taken from Antioch secretly: Sozomen VIII.2, SS 42739-42748 ("Asterius acted very prudently in sending for John before the citizens of Antioch knew what was about to occur"). Card.

**E3. As bishop he moved the bishop's own spending to the hospital.** PALL 3745-3753, 3773-3775 (the Dialogue, ch. V); Greek PG47 9226-9234. Contemporary partisan. Documented (single witness).
> "Then he examined the account books of the Church treasurer, and found expenditure which was of no benefit to the Church; these grants he ordered to be stopped. This brought him to another financial question—the bishop’s expenditure. Here he found extraordinary extravagance, and ordered the large sums so spent to be transferred to the hospital. As the need of treatment was very great, he erected other hospitals, over which he appointed two devout priests, as well as doctors and cooks"
- Greek check (PG47 9231-9233), as printed: "χαὶ χελεύει μετενεχθῆναι τὴν πολυτέλεια. / πούτων εἰς τὸ νοσοχομεῖον, Περισσούστες δὲ τῦς / χρείας, χτίζει πλείονα νοσοχομεῖα". Normalized: καὶ κελεύει μετενεχθῆναι τὴν πολυτέλειαν τούτων εἰς τὸ νοσοκομεῖον ... κτίζει πλείονα νοσοκομεῖα ("orders the extravagance of these to be moved to the hospital ... builds more hospitals").
- "Extravagance" is Palladius's judgment of the household before John (blame); the act (cutting the bishop's spending, giving it to the hospital) can be told plainly.
- Moore's note ties this to Basil's hospital at Caesarea (PALL 3765-3767): the translator's, modern. Not for the story.

**E4. He rebuked his clergy, and expelled some.** SS 17261-17276 (Socrates VI.4); SS 42791-42800 (Sozomen VIII.3). Later. Documented.
> Socrates: "John was led to conduct himself toward his clergy with more than proper superciliousness, designing to correct the morals of the clergy under him ... the bishop also not long after expelled many of them from the church, some for one cause, and some for another."
- Sozomen agrees: "He also ejected some of the clergy from the Church" (SS 42798-42800), and the clergy hated him "on account of his free boldness" (SS 43185-43188). The record's force on his own side; for the story in one clause.

**E5. 399: Eutropius's law against sanctuary; his fall; the sermon over him.** SS 17324-17360 (Socrates VI.5); SS 43127-43137 (Sozomen VIII.7); N109 17689-17706, 17708-17727 (Homily I on Eutropius, own words); PG52 775-793, 812-827 (its Greek). Documented.
> Socrates: "Eutropius was the chief eunuch of the imperial bed-chamber, and the first of all eunuchs that was admitted to the dignity of consul. He, desiring to inflict vengeance on certain persons who had taken refuge in the churches, induced the emperors to make a law ... excluding delinquents from the privilege of sanctuary, and authorizing the seizure of those who had sought the shelter of the sacred edifices. But its author was punished for this almost immediately; for scarcely had the law been promulgated, before Eutropius himself, having incurred the displeasure of the emperor, fled for protection to the church. ... The bishop therefore, while Eutropius trembling with fear lay under the table of the altar, mounting the pulpit ... from which he was accustomed to address the people in order to be the more distinctly heard, uttered an invective against him: wherefore he seemed to create greater displeasure in some, as he not only denied compassion to the unfortunate, but added insult to cruelty."
> Sozomen: "While he was lying beneath the table, John pronounced a discourse, in which he reprehended the pride of power, and directed the attention of the people to the instability of human greatness. The enemies of John hence took occasion to cast reproach on him, because he had rebuked instead of compassionating, one who was suffering under the calamities of adverse fortunes."
> John (N109 17699-17706): "Now I say these things not by way of reproaching him, or insulting his misfortune, but from a desire to soften your minds towards him, and to induce you to compassion, and to persuade you to be contented with the punishment which has already been inflicted. For since there are many inhuman persons amongst us who are inclined, perhaps, to find fault with me for having admitted him to the sanctuary, I parade his sufferings from a desire to soften their hardheartedness by my narrative."
> John (N109 17721-17727): "in that having received her enemy as a captive, she spares him, and when all have despised him in his desolation, she alone like an affectionate mother has concealed him under her cloak, ... opposing both the wrath of the king, and the rage of the people, and their overwhelming hatred."
> Greek (PG52 822-827), as printed: "ὅτι τὸν πολέμιον αἱ- / χμάλωτον λαδοῦσα, δείδεται, καὶ πάντων αὐτὸν ἐν / ἐρημίᾳ παριδόντων, μόνη χαθάπερ μήτηρ φιλόστορ- / γος, ὑπὸ τὰ παραπετάσματα αὑτῆς ἔχρυψε, χαὶ πρὸς / βασιλιχὴν ὀργὴν ἔστη, πρὸς δήμου θυμὸν, χαὶ πρὸς / υἷσος ἀφόρητον"
- Normalized: ὅτι τὸν πολέμιον αἰχμάλωτον λαβοῦσα, φείδεται, καὶ πάντων αὐτὸν ἐν ἐρημίᾳ παριδόντων, μόνη καθάπερ μήτηρ φιλόστοργος, ὑπὸ τὰ παραπετάσματα αὑτῆς ἔκρυψε, καὶ πρὸς βασιλικὴν ὀργὴν ἔστη, πρὸς δήμου θυμὸν, καὶ πρὸς μῖσος ἀφόρητον.
- OCR: "δείδεται" for φείδεται ("spares"), "υἷσος" for μῖσος ("hatred"), chi for kappa throughout; Stephens's English confirms each. "παραπετάσματα" is "curtains"; Stephens renders "cloak" and notes the curtain before the altar (N109 17724-17725).
- Explain it, sanctuary: the right of anyone in danger to take refuge in a church; Eutropius's law took it away (Socrates, above; Sozomen, "enacting that no one should seek refuge in churches, and that those who had already fled thither should be driven out", SS 43127-43129).
- Explain it, the sermon: from his enemies' side, he mocked a fallen man (Socrates "some"; Sozomen "the enemies of John"); from his own, he showed the man's terror to move the hard-hearted to mercy, and the church sheltered its enemy (John, above). Both sides Documented.
- Eutropius beheaded: SS 17360-17364 (Socrates); SS 43137-43138 (Sozomen). Documented.

**E6. He rebuked men in power: the Gothic general Gainas and his request for a church for the Arians.** SS 17368-17374 (Socrates VI.5); SS 42884-42905 (Sozomen VIII.4). Later. Documented (Socrates "it is said"; Sozomen tells it plainly and at length).
> Socrates: "It is said that John afterwards used the same license towards Gaïnas also, who was then commander-in-chief of the army; treating him with characteristic rudeness, because he had presumed to request the emperor to assign the Arians, with whom he agreed in sentiment, one of the churches within the city. Many others also of the higher orders, for a variety of causes, he censured with the same unceremonious freedom, so that by these means he created many powerful adversaries."
> Sozomen: "He was a Christian, and, like the rest of the barbarians, had espoused the Arian heresy. ... he applied to the emperor to place one of the churches of the city in the hands of the Arians. ... John did not remain inactive when made acquainted with these proceedings. He assembled all the bishops who were then residing in the city, and went with them to the palace. He spoke at great length in the presence of the emperor and of Gaïnas, reproached the latter with being a stranger and a fugitive ... he showed the law which Theodosius had established, forbidding the heterodox to hold a church within the walls."
- Explain it, the Arians: followers of Arius, who taught that the Son of God "had a beginning of existence" (Socrates I.5, SS 2094-2096).
- What his rebukes cost him: Socrates, "As long as John was in conflict with the clergy only, machinations against him were utterly powerless; but when he proceeded to rebuke many of those in public office also with immoderate vehemence, the tide of unpopularity began to set against him with far greater impetus" (SS 17317-17321). Sozomen agrees: "he was hated by the clergy and the powerful on account of his free boldness, for he never failed to rebuke the clergy when he detected them in acts of injustice, nor to exhort the powerful to return to the practice of virtue when they abused their wealth" (SS 43185-43191). The two agree: Documented, narrator's voice.

**E7. Theophilus turns against him (motive: Socrates's reading).** SS 17081-17086, 17375-17381 (Socrates VI.2, VI.5); SS 17578-17592, 17613-17693 (VI.7); SS 17790-17842 (VI.9); SS 42750-42770 (Sozomen VIII.2). Later. The expulsion of the monks and their coming to John: Documented. The motive: Contested.
> Socrates VI.5: "Wherefore Theophilus bishop of Alexandria, immediately after his ordination, was plotting his overthrow ... For it was not so much the boldness with which John lashed whatever was obnoxious to him, that affected Theophilus, as his own failure to place his favorite presbyter Isidore in the episcopal chair of Constantinople."
> Socrates VI.9: "John, on being informed of the facts, gave the men an honorable reception, and did not exclude them from communion at prayers, but postponed their communion of the sacred mysteries, until their affairs should be examined into. Whilst matters were in this posture, a false report was brought to Theophilus’ ears, that John had both admitted them to a participation of the mysteries, and was also ready to give them assistance; wherefore he resolved not only to be revenged on Isidore and Dioscorus, but also if possible to cast John out of his episcopal chair." (SS 17832-17840)
> Sozomen VIII.2 hedges the Isidore motive: "But whether there was really any truth in this report, or whether Theophilus desired to ordain this man because of his excellence, it is certain that he eventually yielded to those who decided for John." (SS 42767-42770)
- Explain it, the monks' quarrel: the question "whether God is a corporeal existence, and has the form of man" (SS 17578-17579). Theophilus taught God has no body (SS 17586-17589), then, Socrates says, turned the simpler monks against the four brothers he had quarreled with by calling them followers of Origen, and drove them from Egypt (SS 17655-17693). Socrates: Theophilus held "the very same opinion" as they (SS 17660-17663).
- Socrates on Theophilus as a witness: he calls him "of a hasty and malignant temperament" (SS 17648-17649). A hostile witness to Theophilus.

**E8. The Synod of the Oak: the charges.** PHOT 3843-3848, 3865-3937 (Codex 59, the minutes summed up). The minutes: contemporary, his enemies' record; Photius, more than four centuries later, calls the synod unlawful. Documented that the charges were laid; nothing held proves them.
> "the third, that he had sold much valuable Church property ... the fifth, that he had reviled the clergy as dishonourable, corrupt, useless in themselves, and worthless ... the twenty-fifth, that he ate gluttonously alone, living like a Cyclops ; ... the twenty-seventh, that he struck Memnon with his fist in the church of the Apostles, and while he bled at the mouth celebrated the communion" (PHOT 3872-3876, 3929, 3933-3934)
> "Read the proceedings of the synod that was unlawfully summoned against St. John Chrysostom. The presidents were Theophilus, bishop of Alexandria, Acacius of Beroea, Antiochus of Ptolemais, Severian of Gabala, and Cyrinus of Chalcedon, who were bitterly hostile to Chrysostom, and constituted themselves judges, accusers, and witnesses." (PHOT 3843-3848)
- Socrates: "no mention was now made of Origen, but all were intent on urging a variety of criminations, many of which were ridiculous" (SS 18272-18274).
- Place: "one of the suburbs of Chalcedon, a place called ‘The Oak,’" (SS 18275-18276).
- The eating alone: Socrates, "His reasons for not eating with others no one knew with any certainty" (SS 17280-17281); Palladius's reasons in Zenos's note (SS 17282-17292). Card.

**E9. He refuses, and is deposed; the two accounts differ on the ground.** SS 18282-18297 (Socrates VI.15); PHOT 3939-3946, 4024-4031. Contested between the two.
> Socrates: "And since John taking exception to those who had cited him, on the ground of their being his enemies, refused to attend, ... and demanded a general council, without delay they repeated their citation four times in succession; and as he persisted in his refusal to meet them as his judges, always giving the same answer, they condemned him, and deposed him without assigning any other cause for his deposition but that he refused to obey the summons."
> The minutes: "He was four times summoned, but refused to appear. He declared that, if the synod would remove his open enemies from the list of judges, he was ready to appear and defend himself" ... "The first and second counts were then investigated" ... "The members present, forty-five in all, then recorded their opinion ... It was unanimously decided that Chrysostom should be deprived of his episcopate." (PHOT 3939-3943, 3946, 4029-4032)
- Both agree: four summonses, his refusal of judges he called enemies, a deposition. They differ on whether charges were heard before the vote.
- The people's watch and the first exile: "they kept watch all night, and would by no means suffer him to be removed from the church ... A decree of the emperor, however, commanded that he should be immediately expelled, and sent into exile; which as soon as John was apprised of, he voluntarily surrendered himself" (SS 18298-18307). Documented.
- The recall: Socrates gives the people's anger, made worse by Severian's sermon (SS 18316-18342); Theodoret gives an earthquake that terrified the empress (THDT 17687-17704). They differ on the cause; the recall itself is Documented. Card for the causes.
- Theodoret's version of the Oak: "So Chrysostom, without having heard the charges brought against him, or brought forward his defence, was forced as though convicted on the accusations advanced against him to quit Constantinople" (THDT 17677-17680). Admiring, later.

**E10. The silver statue; the second council; silenced.** SS 18427-18444, 18467-18490 (Socrates VI.18); SS 43906-43949 (Sozomen VIII.20); THDT 17708-17716. Documented (three witnesses agree on the ground).
> Socrates: "At this statue public games were accustomed to be performed; these John regarded as an insult offered to the church, and having regained his ordinary freedom and keenness of tongue, he employed his tongue against those who tolerated them. ... employing abusive language he ridiculed those who had enjoined such practices. The empress once more applied his expressions to herself as indicating marked contempt toward her own person: she therefore endeavored to procure the convocation of another council of bishops against him."
> Socrates: "the bishops present, setting aside all other matters, said they would confine themselves to this sole consideration, that he had on his own responsibility, after his deposition, again seated himself in the episcopal chair, without being authorized by an ecclesiastical council." ... "the emperor therefore sent to tell John that he could not go to the church, because two Synods had condemned him. Accordingly Chrysostom was silenced, and went no more to the church"
> Sozomen: "In a public discourse to the people John charged that these proceedings reflected dishonor on the Church. This remark recalled former grievances to the recollection of the empress, and irritated her so exceedingly at the insult that she determined to convene another council." (SS 43916-43920)
> Theodoret: "they endeavoured to enact punishment, not for the forged indictment, but for his taking part in divine service after his deposition." (THDT 17708-17711)
- Place of the statue: "neither near nor far from the church named Sophia, but one-half the breadth of the street separated them" (SS 18434-18436).
- "Again Herodias raves" (SS 18456-18457; Sozomen SS 43923-43924): Zenos's note says Savile thought the sermon spurious (SS 18452-18453). Leave out.
- John's answer that the rule was an Arian canon (SS 18476-18484; Sozomen SS 43943-43949): card.

**E11. His followers leave the church; 404, exile by force.** SS 18490-18502 (Socrates VI.18); SS 43980-43991, 44032-44042, 44056-44069 (Sozomen VIII.21-22). Documented.
> Socrates: "but those who were of his party celebrated Easter in the public baths which are called Constantianæ, and thenceforth left the church. Among them were many bishops and presbyters, with others of the clerical order, who from that time held their assemblies apart in various places, and were from him denominated ‘Johannites.’ For the space of two months, John refrained from appearing in public; after which a decree of the emperor sent him into exile. Thus he was led into exile by force"
> Sozomen: "He made no other censure than that, in being sent into banishment without a legal trial or any of the forms of the law, he was treated more severely than murderers, sorcerers, and adulterers." (SS 44039-44042)
- The fire: Socrates says Johannites set it (SS 18497-18500); Sozomen says each side blamed the other (SS 44059-44065). Contested. The sibling `olympias-of-constantinople` tells it; leave out.
- Cucusus in Armenia, his first place of exile: Sozomen, SS 44066-44069; Theodoret, THDT 17716-17718.

**E12. In exile; moved toward Pityus.** SS 44455-44465 (Sozomen VIII.27); PALL 6338-6356, 6392-6407, 6422 (the Dialogue); SS 44494-44497 (Sozomen VIII.28). Documented (the transfer); the soldiers' orders and the bishops' motive rest on the Dialogue (partisan).
> Sozomen: "John acquired great celebrity even in his exile. ... he purchased the liberty of many captives from the Isaurian robbers, and restored them to their families. ... Hence he was exceedingly beloved not only in Armenia, where he dwelt, but by all the people of the neighboring countries, and the inhabitants of Antioch and of the other parts of Syria" (SS 44455-44464)
> Dialogue: "Then the flames of malignity blazed yet more fiercely in Severianus and Porphyrius and other bishops of Syria, and they set to work to get him again transferred elsewhere ... So when they saw the Church of Antioch migrating to the Church of Armenia, and the gracious philosophy of John chanted from there back again to the Church of Antioch, they longed to cut short his life." (PALL 6339-6353)
> Dialogue: "John was to be transferred to Pityus, a desolate spot in Tzane, situated on the shore of the Black Sea. The soldiers of the pretorian prefect who formed his escort accordingly hurried him over the ground at forced speed. They said that such were their instructions; if he died by the roadside, the higher the promotion they would earn." (PALL 6399-6406)
> "This most distressing journey took three months" (PALL 6421-6422)
- Sozomen gives the edict after Rome's envoys were sent away: "John was at the same time condemned by an imperial edict to a remoter place of banishment, and soldiers were sent to conduct him to Pityus" (SS 44494-44496).

**E13. 407: his death near Comana, "Glory to God for all things".** PALL 6439-6443, 6457-6461, 6495-6500 (the Dialogue); PG47 11677-11683 (its Greek); PG47 5810-5815 (the Life under the name of Theodore of Trimithus, heading PG47 3952-3958); SS 18609-18612 (Socrates VI.21); THDT 17721-17722. ## 3. What came of him, heaviest first

**I1. His sermons were kept, read and quoted.** Documented.
- Socrates: "How eloquent, convincing, and persuasive his sermons were, both those which were published by himself, and such as were noted down by short-hand writers as he delivered them, why should we stay to declare? Those who desire to form an adequate idea of them, must read for themselves, and will thereby derive both pleasure and profit." (SS 17303-17308)
- John Cassian (430), whom John made a deacon, sets him before Constantinople against its bishop Nestorius: "As for John the glory of the Episcopate of Constantinople, whose holy life obtained the reward of martyrdom without any show of Gentile persecution, hear what he thought and taught on the Incarnation" (CASS 55076-55081); "For I was admitted into the sacred ministry by the Bishop John, of blessed memory" (CASS 55143-55144); "John a marvel of faith and purity. ... Remember him, I say. Follow him." (CASS 55169-55172). Later (about 25 years). The passage he quotes "has not been identified" in John's works (Gibson's note, CASS 55084-55086).
- Augsburg Confession, 1530: "Chrysostom says that the priest stands daily at the altar, inviting some to the Communion and keeping back others." (AUGC 880-881).
- Calvin, 1536 on: "I have always been exceedingly delighted with the words of Chrysostom, " The foundation of our philosophy is humility ;"" (CALV1 18508-18510); and against him: "the Greek Fathers, above others, and especially Chrysostom, have exceeded due bounds in extolling the powers of the human will" (CALV1 18125-18128).
- The Library holds 38 works of his own (census card `sources.ownWords`).

**I2. His name restored, his body brought home, his followers reconciled.** Documented.
- Theodoret: "This great wrong was regarded with special detestation by the bishops of Europe, who separated themselves from communion with the guilty parties." (THDT 17765-17769) "On the death of the great teacher of the world, the bishops of the West refused to embrace the communion of the bishops of Egypt, of the East, of the Bosphorus, and in Thrace, until the name of that holy man had been inserted among those of deceased bishops." (THDT 17773-17777) At Antioch, "By Alexander the name of the great John was first inscribed in the records ... of the Church." (THDT 17832-17842)
- Socrates VII.45: "Not long after this, Proclus the bishop brought back to the Church those who had separated themselves from it on account of Bishop John’s deposition, he having soothed the irritation by a prudent expedient. ... Having obtained the emperor’s permission, he removed the body of John from Comana, where it was buried, to Constantinople, in the thirty-fifth year after his deposition. And when he had carried it in solemn procession through the city, he deposited it with much honor in the church termed The Apostles. By this means the admirers of that prelate were conciliated, and again associated in communion with the [catholic] Church. This happened on the 27th of January, in the sixteenth consulate of the Emperor Theodosius." (SS 21459-21471)
- Theodoret V.36: the emperor, son of Arcadius and Eudoxia, "After first gazing upon the bier he laid his head against it, and prayed for his parents and for pardon on them who had ignorantly sinned" (THDT 17864-17867). The boats and torches: THDT 17850-17854.
- Socrates's "thirty-fifth year after his deposition" (SS 21465) is also "the thirty-fifth year after his death" (SS 21478-21479): it fixes no year for the Oak.

**I3. The hospital and the poor.** E3 above. **Defended:** the poor's claim on the church's wealth (E1, E3).

**I4. Shelter in the church for an enemy.** E5 above. **Defended:** sanctuary, even for the man who abolished it.

**I5. The churches of the city kept from the Arians.** E6 (Sozomen). **Defended.**

**I6. His last words, carried on.** PG47 5813-5814 (the later Life); PALL 6498-6499.
- The rite called by his name: `cic/texts/liturgies-eastern_mark-james-clement-chrysostom-basil-malabar_neale-1870s.txt` (Neale's English). The authorship rests on its title and on later tradition; no held text of his time says he wrote it. Card at most.

## 5. Where the sources differ or come late

- **The ground of his deposition at the Oak:** Socrates, refusal alone; the minutes, counts examined then a vote (E9).
- **Theophilus's motive:** Socrates, the lost see and revenge; Sozomen leaves the Isidore report open (E7).
- **The recall in 403:** Socrates, the people's anger; Theodoret, an earthquake (E9).
- **The fire of 404:** Socrates, his followers; Sozomen, each side blamed the other (E11).
- **The place of death:** Socrates, Comana; the Dialogue, a shrine five or six miles out (E13).
- **The trigger of the Oak:** Socrates, a sermon against women taken as aimed at Eudoxia (SS 18239-18249); the Dialogue reports his enemies' charge that he called the queen Jezebel (PALL 5205-5207). Card.
- **The name "Chrysostom", "golden-mouthed":** no held writer of his lifetime gives it to him. It appears in the Dialogue's title (PALL 734-736; PG47 7070, "τοῦ Χρυσοστόμου") and the chapter headings of Sozomen (SOZG 26525). Sozomen says some called another bishop, Antiochus of Ptolemais, "Chrysostom" for his fine voice (SS 43264-43267; SOZG 28792-28793). Inferential-Thin as a name his hearers used; Documented as the name the record gives him.
- **Late witnesses:** Photius (ninth century) on the minutes; the Life under Theodore of Trimithus (later; date not held).
