# Outline: John Chrysostom (`john-chrysostom`)

File short names and full paths are in `Source_Sheet.md` (top). Item codes (S1, E5, I2 ...) point to the sheet, which gives every passage verbatim with its lines. This outline replaces batch 10's notes (`Outline.batch10.md`) for the approved story; its approved beats are kept where the sources hold them, and its Do not say lines that still hold are carried below.

**The essence** (Documented): "He preached plainly about the poor, and his blunt words to the powerful led to his exile." (batch 10's approved wording, kept.) Then one added sentence for what it changed: his sermons, some taken down by shorthand writers as he spoke, were read and quoted for centuries after, and the followers who broke away over his fall came back only when his body was brought home.
- Poor: N110 29931-29950 (E1). Powerful and exile: SS 17317-17321, 43185-43191 (E6); SS 18486-18490 (E10). Sermons: SS 17303-17308 (I1). Followers: SS 21459-21469 (I2).
- Why the sentence is added: the kind kit's essence names what he did, what it changed and why he is on the tree; the approved sentence gives only the first.

**Also told:**
1. What he preached (Documented, his own words). Honor the bread of Communion by feeding Christ in the poor; God needs golden souls, not golden cups (N110 29931-29950, E1).
2. What he built (Documented, one partisan witness). He cut the bishop's own spending, gave it to the hospital and built more (PALL 3746-3753, 3773-3775, E3).
3. Whom he rebuked (Documented). A fallen minister in sanctuary; a general asking a church for the Arians; many others in office (E5, E6).
4. How his fall came (Contested in motive and ground). Theophilus's campaign, the Oak, the statue, the second council (E7-E11).

**Setting:**
- Time: the late fourth century to the early fifth. Fixed by consuls: ordained bishop 398, Eutropius's fall 399, exiled 404, died 407, body brought back 438 (S5). Antioch: twelve years a presbyter, no years given (PALL 3444-3446). Spoken: "In 398", "in 399", "in 404", "in 407", "in 438"; no year for the Oak or the statue.
- Places: born at Antioch, in Syria (SS 17130-17131). Bishop of Constantinople, the emperor's city (SS 17097-17098). The Oak, a suburb of Chalcedon (SS 18275-18276). Exile at Cucusus in Armenia (SS 44066-44069), then toward Pityus on the Black Sea (PALL 6399-6401); died near Comana (SS 18609-18610; PALL 6439-6442). Spoken: "Antioch, in Syria"; "Constantinople"; "a place called the Oak, outside Chalcedon"; "Armenia"; "near Comana".
- Tradition: census `antiochene-exegetical-christianity-chrysostom-ce`, spoken "Antioch" / "Antioch in Chrysostom's Day" (S6). Plainest spoken word: "the church of Antioch". Not "Antiochene", not "school".

- the literal sense of Scripture: "the plain meaning of the words, not hidden meanings" (SS 17184-17185; SS 42697-42698).
- Christ's body (Q2): "the bread of Communion on the altar, which the rich honored with gold cups and silk" (N110 29907-29914, 29959-29965).

- Johannites (avoid the word): "his followers, who met apart from the church" (SS 18490-18496).

- presbyter: say "priest".

**Shape:** Teacher or thinker. The question: what does a Christian owe the poor and the powerful? One idea shown through his own words (Q2) and a moment (Q1). His readers carry the impact.

**Openings:**
1. *(Recommended; batch 10's approved hook.)* Eutropius, the emperor's chief chamberlain, had a law made that took away sanctuary from people who fled into churches. Soon after, in 399, he fell from favor and fled into the cathedral himself, and lay trembling under the altar table (SS 17324-17347). The bishop, John Chrysostom, climbed to the reading desk and preached over him. Some thought he had added insult to cruelty (SS 17357-17360; SS 43132-43137); John said he spoke to soften their hearts, and that the church, like a mother, was hiding its enemy (N109 17699-17706; Q1). Then the essence. If this is the opening, Beat 3 does not tell the sermon again.
2. His own words at Antioch. He told the rich who honored the altar with silk and gold to honor Christ in the poor (Q2; N110 29931-29939). Then the essence. If this is the opening, Beat 1 gives only his training and his years as a priest, and the Eutropius scene stands whole in Beat 3.

**Story beats:**
1. **Antioch.** Born at Antioch (SS 17130-17131). Trained under Diodore, who kept to the plain meaning of Scripture (SS 17170-17185; SS 42693-42698). A priest there for twelve years (PALL 3443-3446). He preached on what the rich owed the poor; Q2 here unless it opened the story (N110 29931-29950; his city, N110 5301-5303). Sozomen: his boldness pleased the people and grieved the wealthy (SS 42723-42728). Documented.
2. **398: bishop of Constantinople.** The court chose him; Eutropius made Theophilus, bishop of Alexandria, ordain him (SS 17097-17109; Sozomen "they say", SS 42775-42780). Reason for "made": Eutropius threatened Theophilus with a trial on charges against him (SS 17098-17108). Card for the threat's detail. As bishop he cut the bishop's own spending and gave the money to the hospital, and built more (PALL 3746-3753, 3773-3775). He corrected his clergy sharply and put some of them out of the church, and many came to hate him (SS 17261-17276; SS 42798-42800; SS 43185-43188). Documented (the hospital on one partisan witness; the clergy, both historians). This is the record's force on his own side: it stays.
3. **399-400: the powerful rebuked.** After the sermon over Eutropius (the opening; do not retell it), he rebuked other men in office. When the Gothic general Gainas asked the emperor for a church inside the city for the Arians, John went to the palace with the bishops and spoke against it (SS 42884-42905; Socrates "it is said", SS 17368-17372). He rebuked many others of high rank "for a variety of causes" (SS 17372-17374). Those rebukes made him powerful enemies (SS 17317-17321, 17374; SS 43185-43191). Documented. If Opening 2 is used, the Eutropius scene and Q1 stand here first, in 399, with Eutropius's beheading after (SS 17360-17364).
4. **Theophilus turns.** Credit Socrates: Theophilus had never forgiven losing the see for his own candidate (SS 17375-17381). Monks he had driven out of Egypt, in a quarrel over whether God has a human body, came to John for help (SS 17578-17592, 17655-17693; 17825-17832). John received them kindly but held back Communion until their case was heard; Theophilus heard, falsely, that John had admitted them, and resolved to unseat him (SS 17832-17840). The monks' arrival: Documented. Theophilus's motive: Contested (Sozomen leaves it open, SS 42767-42770). Explain it, the quarrel: as in Words to explain; Socrates says Theophilus himself taught God has no body (SS 17586-17589, 17660-17663).
5. **The Oak.** He and his bishops met at a place called the Oak, outside Chalcedon, and summoned John (SS 18274-18283). His accusers charged that he had sold church property, insulted the clergy, struck a man and eaten greedily alone (PHOT 3872-3876, 3929, 3933). He refused to come before judges he called his enemies, and asked for a general council (SS 18286-18293; PHOT 3939-3943). The two accounts differ, and the story credits each: Socrates says they deposed him only for refusing to come (SS 18293-18297); the synod's own minutes, as a later patriarch summed them up, say some charges were heard and then all forty-five voted to depose him (PHOT 3946, 4028-4032). The people kept watch all night (SS 18298-18301); the emperor ordered him into exile; he gave himself up; he was soon called back (SS 18302-18307, 18341-18365). Documented (the charges laid, the refusal, the deposition, the recall); Contested (the ground). This is the first place past the essence where exile is told.
6. **The statue and the second council.** A silver statue of the empress Eudoxia stood just across the street from the cathedral, and public games were held at it. John mocked those who allowed them, calling it an insult to the church; she took his words as aimed at her and sought another council (SS 18427-18446; SS 43906-43920). That council condemned him for taking back his see after his deposition without a council's leave (SS 18467-18472; SS 43934-43938; THDT 17708-17711). The emperor told him he could not go to the church, since two synods had condemned him, and he was silenced (SS 18486-18490). His followers kept Easter in the public baths and from then on met apart (SS 18490-18496; SS 43980-43991). In 404 he was taken into exile by force (SS 18496-18502). Documented.
7. **Exile and death.** Exiled to Cucusus in Armenia (SS 44066-44069). Credit Palladius here: he says bishops in Syria, seeing the church of Antioch still turning to John, had him moved to Pityus, far off on the Black Sea, and that the soldiers hurried him on, saying they had been told his death would bring them promotion (PALL 6339-6353, 6399-6406). Three months on the road (PALL 6421-6422). Near Comana he asked for white clothes, took Communion and prayed his usual words, Q3, and died (PALL 6439-6442, 6457-6460, 6495-6499; Theodore's Life, PG47 5812-5814). Date: September 407 (SS 18609-18612). Documented (the death); the soldiers' orders and the bishops' motive: the Dialogue only.

**What came of them** (about a tenth of the story, heaviest first; told in time order before the ending):
1. **His sermons were kept and read.** Some he published; others were taken down by shorthand writers as he spoke, and Socrates told readers to read them for themselves (SS 17303-17308). Cassian, the deacon he ordained, held him up to Constantinople against its bishop Nestorius (CASS 55076-55081, 55143-55144, 55167-55172; Gennadius, THDT 43243-43245; Cut first 2). The Reformers quoted him: the Augsburg Confession (AUGC 880-881) and Calvin, who also said he gave the human will too much power (CALV1 18508-18510, 18125-18128). Card for the Reformers; the essence carries "for centuries".
2. **His name was put back.** The bishops of the West would not share Communion with the eastern bishops until his name was restored to the list of bishops remembered in prayer; at Antioch, Bishop Alexander first did it (THDT 17765-17777, 17832-17842). Documented (Theodoret, later).
3. **His followers came back** when his body was brought home in 438 (the Ending; SS 21459-21471).
- **Defended:** the poor's claim on what the church spent (E1, E3); shelter in the church even for the man who took it away (E5); the city's churches kept from the Arians (E6).
- Who keeps him today: the rite called the Liturgy of St John Chrysostom is held (Neale's English), but no held text of his time says he wrote it. Leave out.

**Connection to carry into the story:** Two things passed from him. First, his sermons, and in them a demand: that the church honor Christ in the poor before it decks its altar, and that a bishop speak as plainly to a general or an empress as to his own clergy (E1, E5, E6). People kept them, and later readers argued from them (I1). Second, his fall split his own church, and the split did not heal by any ruling. His followers met apart rather than accept the new bishops (SS 18490-18496; SS 44089-44097); the bishops of the West refused Communion with the East until his name was put back (THDT 17773-17777); his followers came back only when a later bishop brought his body home and laid it with honor (SS 21459-21469). The listener must understand that many who knew him did not accept his condemnation, and that in the end the church honored him by what it did: his name restored, his body brought home. Tell it through those acts, never as "this matters because" or a verdict that he was innocent.

**Ending:**
1. *(Recommended; batch 10's approved close.)* In 438 Proclus, bishop of Constantinople, had leave from the emperor to bring John's body home from Comana. He carried it through the city in procession and laid it in the Church of the Apostles, and those who had left the church over John's fall came back (SS 21459-21471). The emperor, the son of the rulers who had exiled him, laid his head against the coffin and prayed for his parents' pardon (THDT 17864-17867). Paraphrase; the emperor's prayer is optional (Cut first 7). Do not say "thirty-five years after his deposition": Socrates also calls it the thirty-fifth year after his death (SS 21465, 21478-21479), so it fixes no year.
2. His death and last words (Beat 7, Q3), if What came of them is cut to the card. Then the 438 return goes to the essence's reach-ahead only.

**Who tells it:**
- **John himself**, in his own time: Homily 50 on Matthew at Antioch (old; Prevost's English, modern translator); Homily I on Eutropius (old; Greek held, Stephens's English). Credit beside: Q1, Q2, his reason for the Eutropius sermon. He speaks in the story; no citation needed.
- **Socrates of Constantinople**, a church historian of the city, writing a generation later (old). Critical of John's temper and of Theophilus alike. Tells nearly every event in the narrator's voice where Sozomen or Theodoret agree. **Spoken credit only twice:** Theophilus's motive (Beat 4) and the ground of the deposition (Beat 5).
- **Sozomen**, the same generation, admiring, working mostly apart from Socrates (old). No spoken credit: he confirms the boldness, Gainas, the statue, the second council and the followers apart.
- **The synod's own minutes**, his enemies' record (old), known only through **Photius**, a patriarch writing more than four centuries later who calls the synod unlawful (old, late). Spoken credit: "the synod's own minutes, as a later patriarch summed them up", Beat 5, beside Socrates. The accusers' charges are told as "his accusers charged". Photius's verdicts stay on the card.
- **The Dialogue on his life**, by a bishop of his party, soon after his death (old, partisan). Its title gives it to Palladius of Helenopolis; Moore notes it is anonymous as it stands (PALL 730-736). Spoken credit: "Palladius", once, Beat 7, beside the bishops' motive and the soldiers' orders. The hospital (Beat 2) and twelve years (Beat 1) are told plainly, without its judgment words.
- **Theodoret**, a bishop writing about 438-450, admiring (old). No spoken credit: the West's demand and the return.
- **The Life under the name of Theodore of Trimithus** (old, later; date not held): only as the second print of Q3.
- **John Cassian** (430), **the Augsburg Confession** (1530) and **Calvin**: what came of him.
- **Modern:** Zenos, Hartranft, Stephens, Prevost, Moore, Freese, Jackson: translators; their notes clarify an old text (the curtain, the custom of the list, consular years) and are never credited. Zenos's "Antiochian system" and Freese's "403" make claims of their own and stay out.

**Do not say:**
- "Socrates says" anywhere but Beats 4 and 5. Say the facts Socrates and Sozomen agree on in the narrator's voice: his rebukes made him enemies; Gainas; the statue; the second council; the followers apart.
- Any word of his exile before Beat 5, except in the essence. The Eutropius hook and Beats 1-4 stay in their own time.
- "Do not say his preaching on the poor led to his exile. Socrates ties the exile to his rebukes of men in power." (batch 10, word for word)
- "Do not say Theophilus held a grudge as fact. It is Socrates's reading." (batch 10, word for word)
- "Do not call John's or Diodore's reading of Scripture Antiochene, or a school or system. That is the modern editors' label (Zenos's note, `npnf202` 17173-17179; Prevost, `npnf110` 730-732). The old sources say only that Diodore kept to the literal sense (Socrates VI.3, `npnf202` 17180-17183)." (batch 10, word for word; the Socrates lines are 17182-17185 by this sheet's count)
- "Do not give the Synod of the Oak a year. No held old source dates it. The year 403 comes from Freese's note citing Hefele (Photius, 3862-3864) and Moore's chronology (Palladius, 678)." (batch 10, word for word)
- "Do not date the march to Pityus, and do not give Palladius a year. Palladius gives none. The year 407 is the year of John's death, from Socrates VI.21 (`npnf202` 18609-18612)." (batch 10, word for word)
- "Do not call Palladius John's companion, or say he stood by John to the end. The Dialogue is anonymous; its author's identity with Palladius of Helenopolis is the translator's view (Moore, introduction, 730-756, 895-903). Say he wrote John's life and took his side (Photius, 9984-9986; Palladius 6411, 6418)." (batch 10, word for word)
- "They deposed him for refusing" as the narrator's claim, or "he was condemned on the charges". Say both accounts, credited (Beat 5).
- "Theophilus believed God has a human body." Socrates says he taught the opposite (SS 17586-17589, 17660-17663).
- "John was charged with following Origen." No mention of Origen was made at the Oak (SS 18272).
- "Theophilus called the Oak himself." Say "he and his bishops"; Socrates says the emperor authorized it (card).
- Gainas as the cause of the turn. The turn is his rebukes of many in office; Gainas is one.
- "The bishop's lavish spending" or "extravagance" in the narrator's voice. That is Palladius's judgment; say "the bishop's own spending".
- "He told the rich to stop giving gold cups to the church", or "instead of decking the altar". He says he does not forbid such gifts, but asks for alms with them and before them (N110 29948-29951). Say he told them to honor Christ in the poor first.
- "His hearers called him golden-mouthed." No held writer of his lifetime gives him the name; later titles do, and Sozomen says some gave it to another bishop.
- "Antioch, where he preached the Homilies on Matthew to Constantinople", or any sermon of Constantinople placed at Antioch. Homily 50 is Antioch's (N110 5301-5303); Homily I on Eutropius is Constantinople's.
- "He died on the way to his exile at Cucusus." He died on the way from Cucusus toward Pityus, a farther place.
- "He died in Comana." Say "near Comana": the Dialogue puts it at a martyr's shrine five or six miles out.
- "The martyr appeared to him and told him he would die." A wonder; leave out, or keep in the Dialogue's voice.
- "The soldiers were ordered to kill him." Say, crediting Palladius: they said they had been told his death would bring them promotion.
- "His followers burned the cathedral." Leave the fire out (the Olympias page tells it; Socrates and Sozomen differ).
- "The church cleared his name", "he was declared innocent" or "rehabilitated". Say what was done: his name was put back on the list; his body was brought home.
- "Eudoxia had him exiled" or "Eudoxia called the Oak." Say only that she took his words at the statue as aimed at her and sought another council (Beat 6).
- "He wrote the Liturgy of St John Chrysostom."
- "Calvin admired him" alone. Calvin praised one saying and faulted his view of the will.
- "The emperor asked John's forgiveness." Theodoret says he prayed for his parents and for pardon on those who had sinned in ignorance.
- Any phrase of this outline in quotation marks, outside Q1-Q3, spoken as a quote ("added insult to cruelty", "for a variety of causes", "golden souls", "a marvel of faith and purity"). Paraphrase, credited where Who tells it says.

**For the card, not the story** (each item from **Cut first** the draft drops, kept here so nothing is lost):
- "Chrysostom" means "golden-mouthed"; the name comes from the Dialogue's title and the chapter headings of Sozomen, and Sozomen says some called Antiochus of Ptolemais by it (PALL 734-736; PG47 7070; SOZG 26525; SS 43264-43267).
- John Cassian, whom he ordained deacon, held him up to the people of Constantinople, against their bishop Nestorius, as the teacher to remember and follow (CASS 55143-55144, 55167-55172; Gennadius, THDT 43243-43245).
- After the Oak the people kept watch all night and would not let him be taken from the church (SS 18298-18301).
- He built more hospitals, with priests, doctors and cooks to serve them (PALL 3773-3775).
- Pityus was a desolate spot on the Black Sea (PALL 6399-6401).
- The Gothic general was Gainas; the bishop who brought the body home was Proclus (SS 17368-17372; SS 21459-21460).
- At the coffin the emperor, son of the rulers who exiled him, laid his head against it and prayed for his parents and for pardon on those who had sinned in ignorance (THDT 17864-17867).
