Years later, defending himself in a letter against men who, he wrote, were blackening his name, Basil of Caesarea looked back. He had wasted nearly all his youth, he wrote, in the vain labor of learning a wisdom God had made foolish, the learning of this world. "Then one day, like a man roused from deep sleep, I turned my eyes to the wonderful light of the truth of the gospel."

That waking set the course of his life in Caesarea, an inland city in Asia Minor. Basil gave up teaching rhetoric, the art of public speaking, for a life of prayer and shared work. He wrote rules that taught monks to live together and serve one another. He built a great lodging for the poor and the sick a little way outside the city. As its bishop, he defended the Holy Spirit's place beside the Father and the Son, and he died before a council, a meeting of bishops, declared it in its creed.

He taught rhetoric, the historian Rufinus says. In his own telling, he read in the gospel of selling goods and sharing with the poor. His brother, Gregory of Nyssa, gives the credit to their sister Macrina. Rufinus says his friend Gregory of Nazianzus took him from the teacher's chair.

In Pontus, near the Black Sea, Basil founded a community and led it. His rules were answers to his brothers' questions, and one asks whether to live alone or with others. Love does not seek its own good, Basil answers, and Christ washed his disciples' feet, but a man alone has no one to serve. "Whom, then, will you wash? Whom will you care for? Behind whom will you be last, if you live by yourself?"

The bishop of Caesarea made him a priest, though they quarreled. When the emperor Valens, who favored the party that ranked the Son below the Father, moved against the churches that held them equal, Basil came back to serve the bishop.

When the old bishop died, Basil was made bishop, though the bishops of the province fought it out of envy, Gregory of Nazianzus says in his speech in Basil's memory. The emperor's highest official ordered him to accept the emperor's faith, on pain of losing his goods, exile, torture and death. In Gregory's telling, Basil answered that a man who owns only rags and a few books cannot be robbed, and that death would only send him sooner to God. Later a rival bishop claimed part of his churches and once blocked his road with armed men. Basil answered by making more bishops, Gregory says.

His accusers had the governor's ear, and Basil wrote to ask what harm it did the state to build a lodging for strangers, travelers and the sick, with doctors and transport. The rich filled it at his urging. Lepers driven from their towns were taken in, and Basil greeted them as brothers and tended them himself.

Basil closed prayers with praise to the Father with the Son, together with the Holy Spirit, and at other times through the Son, in the Holy Spirit. Some attacked these as new, contradictory words. The Spirit, they held, could not be ranked with the Father and the Son. He answered in a book, On the Holy Spirit, from what the church already did in baptism. A letter of his puts the same thought in two lines: "As we were baptized, so we confess our faith. As we confess our faith, so too we give praise." Gregory says that in writing Basil held back the bare words that the Spirit is God, from caution, though he often preached it.

In 381, after his death, a council at Constantinople declared in its creed that the Spirit is worshipped and glorified with the Father and the Son. Rufinus put his works, the Rule among them, into Latin. In the sixth century Benedict of Nursia closed his own Rule by pointing his monks to Basil's as an example of good living.

The whole city poured around Basil as he died.
