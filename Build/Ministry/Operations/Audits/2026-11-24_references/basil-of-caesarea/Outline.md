# Outline: Basil of Caesarea (`basil-of-caesarea`)

Line references are to `Source_Sheet.md` rows (S, E, R, P, B, H, Sp, D, I), each of which gives the file and its lines. Files are under `cic/texts/`.

- **The essence:** Basil of Caesarea gave up teaching rhetoric for a life of prayer and shared work. He wrote the rules that taught monks to live together and serve one another, built a great lodging for the poor and the sick a little way outside Caesarea, and as its bishop defended the Holy Spirit's place beside the Father and the Son. He died before a council at Constantinople, in 381, declared in its creed that the Spirit is worshipped and glorified with the Father and the Son. (E3-E4, R3-R7, H1-H4, B1, Sp1-Sp7, D1-D2, I5)
- **Also told:**
  - The rules: monks should live together, not alone, because love does not seek its own good and a man alone has no one to serve. They are answers to his brothers' questions. (R5-R7; Documented)
  - The house: a lodging for strangers, travellers and the sick, with doctors and transport, stocked by the rich at his urging; lepers driven from their towns were taken in. (H1, H3, H4; Documented; H4 is Gregory's report of a fact, told plainly)
  - The Spirit: he was attacked for closing prayer in two forms, 'with the Son together with the Spirit' and 'through the Son in the Spirit', as new words that contradicted each other. He answered from what the church already did: as we are baptized, so we believe and so we praise. His friend says he held back the bare words "the Spirit is God" in writing, from caution. (Sp1, Sp4, Sp6, Sp7; Documented, Sp7 in Gregory's voice)
  - The cost: an emperor's official threatened him with loss of goods, exile, torture and death; he faced a quarrel with his own bishop and a rival bishop who blocked his road. (B2-B4, P2; Documented as Gregory's account, B3 confirms)
  - Checked against the Library: all held. "Outside Caesarea" is held as Gregory's "a little way from the city" (H3); "suburbs" is the editor's (H6). "Rules that shaped Greek monks" is held as his friend's "legislation" (R3) and the council of 787 (I3); the Rules' date is not held (R8).

- **Setting:**
  - Time: the fourth century. Bishop in the reign of the emperor Valens (S4, B2); died eight years after he became bishop (D1), before the council of 381 (D2). Told in no sentence, since only modern editors give them: born 329 or 330 (S1, Jackson); bishop from 370 (B1, Jackson); died 1 January 379 or at the end of 378 (D2, Jackson, Clarke, Gwynn).
  - Place: Caesarea in Cappadocia, an inland city (S2, S3), in what is now Turkey (S6). Plain gloss, not from a source: in the middle of Asia Minor. His retreat: Pontus, by the river Iris (E7, R1). Plain gloss: near the Black Sea.
  - Tradition: "Cappadocian Christianity" (S5).
  - Power: the emperor Valens favoured the party that ranked the Son below the Father (S4).

- **Openings:**
  1. His own words (E4, Quote 1): years later, defending himself in a letter against men who, he wrote, were blackening his name among the brothers (`npnf208...xml` 39108-39112), Basil looked back. He had wasted nearly all his youth, he wrote, in the vain labour of learning a wisdom God had made foolish. Plain sense, inside that sentence: the learning of this world, which he came to see as useless (same letter, 39133-39137). Then Quote 1. Leads straight to the essence.
  2. Gregory's new city (H3): a little way out of Caesarea, Gregory of Nazianzus told the mourners, stood a "new city", filled by the rich at Basil's urging and open to the sick. Use only if Opening 1 is not used; Beat 5 then drops H3.
- **Story beats:**
  1. **Athens and the turn (c. 350-358).** He studied at Athens with Gregory of Nazianzus (E1), and came home to Caesarea, where the city welcomed him (E2). He taught rhetoric, the historian Rufinus says (E3). Three old accounts say what turned him from it: tell them one after another, each with its witness, and put no question to the listener. In his own telling, he woke to the gospel: he read in it of selling goods and sharing with the poor, and went looking for monks in Egypt, Palestine, Syria and Mesopotamia (E4). If Opening 1 is used, do not tell the waking again. Go straight to 'he read in the gospel of selling goods and sharing with the poor, and went looking for monks'. His brother Gregory of Nyssa gives the credit to their sister Macrina (E5). Rufinus says Basil's friend Gregory of Nazianzus took him from the teacher's chair (E6). Reason: the gospel, in his own telling. Confidence: the turn Documented; who caused it Contested; teaching rhetoric Widely Accepted (Rufinus).
  2. **Pontus and the rules.** In Pontus, near the Black Sea, he founded a community and led it (R1). Rufinus says he went through the towns and countryside, teaching people to come together, build monasteries, pray, and care for the poor (R2). His friend calls his rules "legislation" for monks (R3), and says he brought hermits and communities together rather than walling them apart (R4). The rules answer his brothers' questions; one asks whether to live alone or with others (R5). Basil answers that love does not seek its own good (R6), and that Christ washed his disciples' feet: Quote 2 (R7). Plain sense, told before the quote inside its setup, never as a line after it: a man alone has no one to serve. Confidence: Documented; the Rules' date not held (R8).
  3. **Priest, quarrel and famine (c. 364-369).** The bishop of Caesarea made him a priest (P1). They quarrelled; Gregory will not say why. The monks wanted to break away with him, but Basil went back to Pontus rather than split the church (P2). When the emperor Valens moved against the churches that held the Son equal to the Father, he came back and served the bishop (P3, S4). In a great famine he moved the owners of grain to open their stores, gathered the hungry and, with his servants, fed them soup and meat and tended them himself (P4). Confidence: Documented; the famine's year 368 is the editors' (P4).
  4. **Bishop, and the emperor's official.** The old bishop died in Basil's arms, and Basil was made bishop, though the bishops of the province fought it out of envy, Gregory says (B1). The emperor's highest official ordered him to accept the emperor's faith, and threatened him with loss of his goods, exile, torture and death. In Gregory's telling Basil answered that a man who owns nothing but rags and a few books cannot be robbed, every land is God's, and death would only send him sooner to God. The official said no one had spoken to him so boldly; perhaps, said Basil, he had never met a bishop (B2). A later historian, Sozomen, tells the same threat of death and refusal (B3). When the province was divided, a rival bishop claimed part of Basil's churches and once blocked his road with armed men (B4). Basil answered by making more bishops for the province, Gregory says, and his friend Gregory of Nazianzus was made one of them, at Sasima: a hurt, he says, that time never healed (B5; Sasima named by Socrates, `npnf202...xml` 21065-21066). No old line makes his brother's bishopric at Nyssa part of this answer; do not tell it here. Confidence: Documented as Gregory's account; B3 Widely Accepted; Sasima as part of the answer rests on Gregory's "appendage to this scheme" (Or. 43.59).
  5. **The new city (c. 372).** His accusers had the governor's ear; Basil asks what harm the state suffers from him, and whether his building does it any (H2; `npnf208...xml` 30010-30016). Basil wrote to him: whom does it harm to build a lodging for strangers, for travellers and for the sick, with doctors and transport? (H1, H2, paraphrase; the letter's date is the editors'.) It stood a little way from the city, stocked by the rich at Basil's urging (H3). Lepers, once driven from their towns and families, were taken in. Basil greeted them as brothers and went to tend them himself (H4). Confidence: Documented; H4 is Gregory's report of a fact, told plainly.
  6. **The Spirit (c. 374-375).** Praying with his people, Basil closed with praise to God the Father "with the Son, together with the Holy Spirit", and at other times "through the Son, in the Holy Spirit" (Sp1, paraphrase). Some who were there attacked him for new words that contradicted each other (Sp1). They held, he reports, that the Spirit could not be ranked with the Father and the Son, being lower in nature and rank (Sp4). At a friend's request he wrote On the Holy Spirit (Sp2, Sp3). His answer came from what the church already did (Sp5): in the book he says they must teach him either not to baptize as he received, or not to believe as he was baptized, or not to give glory as he believed (`npnf208...xml` 14168-14170). A letter of his puts the same thought in two lines: Quote 3 (Sp6). Tell the letter as his words elsewhere, never as his reply to the attackers. Plain sense: the church baptizes in the name of Father, Son and Spirit, so it must praise them alike. His friend Gregory of Nazianzus says he held back the bare words "the Spirit is God" in writing; defending him, Gregory says enemies were waiting for them to drive him out, and that Basil preached and confessed it (Sp7). Confidence: Documented; Sp7 in Gregory's voice.
- **What came of them** (ranked; about a third of the story):
  1. **His rule.** Rufinus of Aquileia put his works, the Rule among them, into Latin (I1). In the sixth century Benedict of Nursia, closing his own Rule, pointed his monks to "the Rule of our holy Father Basil" as an example of good living (I2, paraphrase, not a quote). In 787 a council told the double monasteries still standing to keep the rule of Basil (I3). Optional, with the connection: the tree's own tradition entry says his rules remain foundational for Eastern Orthodox monasticism (I9).
  2. **What he defended: the Spirit's place.** A council at Constantinople in 381, after his death, declared in its creed that the Spirit is worshipped and glorified with the Father and the Son (I5). State it beside his fight; do not say the council followed him. His brother carried on his answer to Eunomius (sibling clause; I6 for the card).
  3. **His house.** Two generations later Sozomen wrote that it still bore his name, the Basileias, and was the most famous lodging for the poor (I7).
