# Outline: Epiphanius of Salamis (`epiphanius-of-salamis`)

File short names and full paths are in `Source_Sheet.md` (top). Item codes (S1, E4, I1 ...) point to the sheet, which gives every passage verbatim with its lines.

**The essence** (Documented): Epiphanius, a monk from Palestine who became bishop of Salamis in Cyprus, wrote the Panarion, his "medicine chest", which named eighty heresies as poisons and set a cure beside each; later writers, from Augustine to a council at Nicaea four centuries on, used it as their list of the heresies. In old age he carried the same fight against the followers of Origen, to Jerusalem and then to Constantinople, against its bishop, John Chrysostom.
- Medicine chest, eighty, cures: HOLL1 12387-12395 (E4). Augustine: AUG 2411-2426 (I1). Nicaea, 787: MEN 26273-26281 (I3). Jerusalem: J6 40982-41015 (E8). Constantinople, John: SS 18078-18118, 18191-18230; SS 43502-43631 (E12-E16).

**Also told:**
1. What the book holds (Documented). It names each heresy, tells its teaching and answers it: twenty before Christ's coming and sixty after, by his own list (HOLL1 12512-12536). He says he knew some from books, some by hearsay, and some with his own ears and eyes (HOLL1 13426-13430, E5). He copied his opponents' own writings so no one could say he answered hearsay (HOLL1 34006-34011, E6). He listed the teaching of the followers of Apollinaris, a man "always dear to us": that Christ took no human mind (PG42 48828-48833, 48853-48857, E7). After the eighty he set one more: the church, Christ's holy bride (HOLL1 12395-12397).
2. Why he fought Origen (Documented as his charges; a foe's reading of Origen). As he read him, Origen taught that souls were angels who fell into bodies, and turned the garden of Eden into hidden meanings (J6 10813-10818, 10899-10905). He held to the plain sense: the rivers of Eden are rivers a man can drink from (J6 10914-10924). He says he defended the rising of this flesh (J6 10838-10840, 10899-10901).
3. The force he used (Documented, his own words and the historians'). He had Jerome's brother seized, his mouth stopped, and made him a priest (J6 10689-10700). He tore down a church curtain with a painted image (J6 11099-11110). At Constantinople he ordained a deacon in another bishop's territory and would not pray with John (SS 18082-18084, 18197-18200).
4. How others used him (Contested in motive). Theophilus of Alexandria, who had once accused him of thinking God has a human form, wrote to win him; Epiphanius had the bishops of Cyprus ban Origen's books (SS 17868-17890; SS 43502-43514; J6 19716-19728). Socrates says Theophilus used a simple man for his own revenge (SS 17886-17899); Sozomen says Epiphanius had long loathed Origen's books (SS 43510-43512).
5. A monk's question (Documented as Sozomen's report). One of the Long Brothers asked if he had read any of their books. He had not; he judged them by report (SS 43604-43610).
6. What later readers did with him (Documented). Augustine followed his order of heresies (AUG 2414-2415). The council at Nicaea in 787 argued from his eighty chapters, and a book written to answer it replied with his curtain letter (MEN 26273-26281, 26282-26316).

**Setting:**
- Century: the fourth century, from about 310 to the early 400s. No held ancient source gives his birth or death year; editors give 310 and 403, or 402 (J3 41528; J6 3193; S5). Spoken: "in the fourth century"; his Panarion "about 375", told plainly with no credit: his own regnal date fixes it (HOLL1 13424-13426), and the year is arithmetic on it (Holl's note 13447 clarifies).
- Places: born at Besauduc near Eleutheropolis, in Palestine (SS 39793-39794). Trained mostly in Egypt (SS 39795-39796). Bishop of the chief city of Cyprus, "a large city and ... a seaport" (SS 39798-39802); the city is Salamis, also called Constantia (J6 20957-20958). Then Jerusalem (J6 40982-40991) and Constantinople (SS 18078-18083). Spoken: "Salamis, the chief city and port of Cyprus".
- Tradition: the census's Palestinian Monasticism ("The Monks of Gaza and the Judean Desert"). Spoken: "the monks of Palestine". He lived as a monk near his birthplace (SS 39793-39794); Jerome calls his house there "the old monastery" (J6 18736-18739); he knew Hilarion, whom the tree calls the first monk of Palestine (J6 30110-30112).

**Words to explain:**
- Panarion: "a medicine chest", his own gloss (HOLL1 12392-12393; heading 13363-13364).
- heresy: in his own terms, a wrong belief spread by people led astray "from when man was formed on earth until our own time" (HOLL1 13420-13425). So his list begins before Christ's coming, with Barbarism, the Greek schools such as the Platonists and the Stoics, and the sects of the Jews, and only then the Christian sects (HOLL1 12512-12536). Plain words: "a wrong belief, as he judged it". The judging is his.
- Origen: "a Christian teacher who had died long before" (Socrates, SS 17898; give no number).
- allegory (avoid the word): "reading a story as a picture of hidden meanings" (J6 10819-10821, 10905-10906).
- the Euphrates in Eden: Genesis names it among the rivers of paradise; he cites Genesis on Eden's rivers: the letter's "four heads" of the river of Eden (J6 10914-10917), read with Genesis 2:14, which names the Euphrates.
- ordain, presbyter: "make him a priest".
- synod: "a meeting of bishops".
- another bishop's territory / the canons: "church rules", which Socrates says he broke by ordaining in John's church (SS 18191-18193). Why it mattered, in John's own message as Socrates gives it: he had ordained in churches under John's authority, and led services there without John's appointment (SS 18212-18215).
- the Long Brothers: "monks from Egypt whom Theophilus had driven out" (SS 17899; J6 19729-19733, 19746-19748; SS 43604-43605). Why: Theophilus called them followers of Origen, "Origenist serpents" (his letter, J6 19716-19718); Socrates says his grudge began when they left him over his greed, and that he set other monks on them (SS 17636-17652, 17689-17693).
- the council of 787: "a council of bishops at Nicaea in 787" (MEN header; MEN 26327-26331).
- the quarrel over holy images: whether pictures of Christ and the saints belong in churches and may be honored (Hadrian's letter to the council, in Mendham's note, MEN 8494-8495). Its opponents quoted him against bringing images into churches (MEN 26231-26239); the council of 787 defended "the making of venerable images" (MEN 26219-26227).
- the Caroline Books: "a book written to answer the council's argument" (its own reply, in Mendham's note, MEN 26283-26291). Do not name Charlemagne: in the corpus only Mendham's title page does (MEN header line 1).

**Openings:**
1. *(Recommended)* His book's name and its first promise. He called his book against the heresies the Panarion, a medicine chest. In its preface he tells what it is for (Q1). Then the essence. (HOLL1 12387-12395; 13363-13364.)
2. The young man and the sect. As a young man, he writes, women of a sect tried to draw him in, as Potiphar's wife tried Joseph. He read their books, then gave the bishops of that place about eighty names, and they were driven from the city. Then the essence. (HOLL1 22680-22686, 22740-22747.) If this is the opening, Beat 1 does not tell it again.

**Story beats:**
1. **Youth in Palestine and Egypt.** Born at Besauduc near Eleutheropolis; taught from youth by the most famous monks, mostly in Egypt (Sozomen, SS 39793-39796). As a young man he met a sect, which he calls the Gnostics; its women tried to draw him in. He read their books and gave the local bishops about eighty names, and they were driven from the city (his own words, HOLL1 22680-22686, 22740-22747; his heading, HOLL1 21041). He lived as a monk near his birthplace (SS 39793-39794; J6 18736-18739) and knew Hilarion, whose praises he wrote (Jerome, J6 30110-30112). Reason, in his words: God rescued him, and after reading their books he set his mind on the truth and was not drawn in; then he exposed them (HOLL1 22740-22747). Documented (the sect episode rests on his word alone).
2. **Bishop of Salamis.** The people of Cyprus chose him as bishop of their chief city, a seaport (Sozomen, SS 39797-39802). He held it thirty-six years (Palladius's Dialogue, PALL 8919-8921). He gave his whole inheritance to the shipwrecked and the needy, and then the church's funds (Sozomen, SS 42340-42345). Monks came to Cyprus "from all parts of the world" for love of him (Jerome, J6 21024-21027). Documented.
3. **The Panarion, about 375.** The medicine chest: each heresy a snake's poison, each answer its antidote (HOLL1 12387-12395). Eighty heresies, twenty before Christ's coming and sixty after (his own list, HOLL1 12512-12536). Some known from books, some by hearsay, some with his own eyes and ears (HOLL1 13426-13430). He set out an opponent's own letter whole, so no one could say he answered hearsay (HOLL1 34006-34011, 34016-34746). He listed the followers of Apollinaris, though he called Apollinaris "always dear to us" (PG42 48853-48857). Their teaching, in his words: Christ took flesh and a soul but no mind, so not a complete man (PG42 48828-48833). Reason: his remedy was to name and expose, then answer. Documented.
4. **Jerusalem, in his old age: the sermon and the river.** He preached against Origen in the church at Jerusalem. Jerome, who was there and on his side, says the bishop's party mocked him as an old fool, while crowds pressed to kiss his feet (J6 40982-40996). When the bishop spoke against those who think God has a human body, Epiphanius agreed, and asked that Origen be condemned too (J6 41000-41015). In a letter to the bishop he set out why: Origen, he says, taught that souls fell from heaven into bodies, and turned Eden into hidden meanings (J6 10813-10818, 10899-10906). His answer is Q2 (J6 10917-10924). Reason: he read Scripture at its plain word and believed Origen's reading undid the resurrection (J6 10838-10840, 10899-10901). Documented (Jerome's account is a partisan's; the letter's account of Origen is a foe's).
5. **Force in Palestine.** In the same letter he defends two acts. Jerome's brother had fled the priesthood several times. While the service was going on at the church beside Epiphanius's monastery, he had deacons seize him and "stop his mouth", so he could not beg in Christ's name to be let go, and ordained him deacon and then priest as he cried that he was unworthy (J6 10676-10700). His reason, he says: the monks there had no priest, and he feared they would be cut off from the church and its ancient faith (J6 10655-10659, 10704-10705). He says the monastery was not under the bishop of Jerusalem (J6 10653-10654, 10713-10714). Earlier, travelling with the bishop to Bethel, he found at a village church called Anablatha a curtain painted with "an image either of Christ or of one of the saints"; he tore it apart, sent a better one from Cyprus, and asked that such curtains be banned (J6 11096-11098, 11099-11120). Reason: he saw an image of a man in church as against Scripture (J6 11106-11107). Documented (his own words).
6. **Theophilus and the ban in Cyprus.** Theophilus, bishop of Alexandria, had once accused him of thinking God has a human form; now he wrote to win him against Origen (Socrates, SS 17868-17885; Sozomen, SS 43502-43510; Theophilus's letter, J6 19716-19728). Epiphanius called the bishops of Cyprus, who forbade the reading of Origen's books; they condemned the books, not Origen himself (SS 17886-17890, 18080-18082). Socrates says Theophilus wanted revenge on the monks he had driven out, and used a man of "simple mind and manners" (SS 17886-17899); Sozomen says Epiphanius had long loathed Origen's books (SS 43510-43512). He wrote to Jerome that the Lord had blessed his last days, because so great a bishop now approved what he had always taught (his own letter, J6 19779-19782). Contested (motives); Documented (the ban).
7. **Constantinople, and the voyage home.** At Theophilus's urging he sailed to Constantinople, carrying the Cyprus decree (Socrates, SS 18078-18081; Sozomen, SS 43527-43532). He landed outside the city and ordained a deacon there, against church rules (SS 18082-18084; Socrates on the canon, SS 18191-18193; John's message, SS 18212-18215). He refused John Chrysostom's welcome and would not pray with him unless John condemned Origen's books and sent the Long Brothers away (SS 18197-18200; SS 43552-43556). A bishop from Scythia refused to sign, read aloud from one of Origen's books and said he knew of no evil teaching in them (SS 18105-18116). Socrates says Epiphanius then denounced them before the people; Sozomen says he was warned off at the church door (SS 18203-18207; SS 43565-43572). One of the Long Brothers asked if he had read any of their works; he had not, and judged by report (Sozomen, SS 43604-43610). He sailed for Cyprus. Sozomen reports his farewell to the bishops at the shore (Q3; SOZG 30108-30116). He died at sea before he reached home (SS 18227-18228; SS 43622-43623). Documented (the voyage and death at sea); the farewell is hearsay.

**What came of them** (about a third of the story, heaviest first):
1. **His list became the one later writers used.** Augustine, bishop of Hippo (AUG 975), took fifty-seven heresies from it, and followed "his order, not his measure"; he calls him a man the Greeks counted among the great (AUG 2411-2430). In the ninth century Photius, a patriarch of Constantinople (file header, PHOT 3), judged it fuller than any earlier book against the heresies, though poor in style (PHOT 11652-11654, 11678-11683). It keeps whole the writings it attacks (HOLL1 34016-34746). **Defended:** the rising of this flesh and a garden of Eden that was real (J6 10899-10901, 10922-10930).
2. **His name gave weight to the move against Origen's books.** Sozomen says Theophilus saw no danger in following the example of a man "who was the object of popular praise" (SS 43516-43520). John was driven from his see soon after; Socrates tells it as the event fulfilling the parting words, not as Epiphanius's doing (SS 18225-18230). Theophilus, who had once called him "a heretic, or a paltry schismatic", later called him "a most holy saint" (Palladius's Dialogue, PALL 8921-8925), and in his own letter "a bright star among bishops" (J6 22472-22474).
3. **Four centuries on, both sides over holy images claimed him.** The quarrel was whether pictures of Christ and the saints belong in churches and may be honored (MEN 8494-8495, 26219-26227, 26231-26239). Opponents of images quoted writings under his name. The council at Nicaea in 787 said the passage was "none of his": his treatise of eighty chapters passed over no heresy, and did not list the making of images (MEN 26231-26239, 26273-26281). A book written to answer the council's argument replied with his own curtain letter (MEN 26282-26316).
4. **Remembered as a saint.** Sozomen reports it was said that demons were driven out and diseases healed at his tomb (SS 42335-42337). Jerome wrote that his books were read by the learned for their matter "and also by the plain people, on account of their language" (J3 41529-41531).
- Who keeps him today: no held source says so plainly; leave out.

**Connection to carry into the story:** He passed on a list and a way of using it. He gave each heresy a name, an origin and an answer, and set out its own words beside them. Later writers took the list as a standard. Augustine kept his order (AUG 2414-2415). The bishops at Nicaea in 787 argued from what the eighty chapters did not contain: if Epiphanius had thought images against Christ, he would have listed them (MEN 26276-26281). Their opponents answered from his own letter (MEN 26282-26316). The listener must understand that his book became a reference both sides could argue from, centuries after him, and that his own acts became evidence in a quarrel he never saw. The second thread is his standing: Sozomen says Theophilus followed his example because the people praised him (SS 43516-43520). Tell both through what people did with the book and the name, not through a "this matters because" line.

- **Modern, making a claim of its own** (kept out of the story and the card): the editors' years for his birth, death and the Jerusalem visit, and the decade "the 390s" (J3 41528; J6 3042, 3193, 40439-40440); the editors' "about 428" for Augustine's book (AUG 1098); "compiled by order of Charlemagne" (Mendham's title page, MEN header line 1); "the monastery which he had founded" (J6 40447-40448); "the iconoclastic spirit" (J6 10625); Hartranft's view of Sozomen's sources (SS 39771-39773); Lietzmann's Antioch visit.
- Where the two historians differ (Theophilus's motive; the denunciation), the story says so and credits each.

**Do not say:**
- "Epiphanius was tricked by Theophilus." Say: Socrates says Theophilus used him; Sozomen says he already loathed Origen's books.
- "He believed God has a human body" or "he was an Anthropomorphite." Say: Theophilus once accused him of it (Socrates, Sozomen); Jerome shows him condemning that teaching in public.
- "He called the ban a great blessing." Say: he wrote that the Lord had blessed his last days, because Theophilus now approved what he had always taught (J6 19779-19782).
- "He condemned Origen as a heretic in Cyprus." Say: the bishops of Cyprus forbade reading Origen's books; Socrates says they condemned the books, not Origen himself.
- "He denounced John in the Church of the Apostles." Say: Socrates says he did; Sozomen says he was warned off at the door.
- "He caused John's fall." Say: John was driven out soon after; no source makes Epiphanius the cause.
- "This is where he learned to hunt heretics." Say only what he says he did.
- "In Egypt, Gnostic women seduced him." Say: in his youth women of the sect tried to draw him in; he says God rescued him; he names no city ("that place").
- "He gagged him." Say, after his own words: he had deacons seize him and stop his mouth.
- "He tore down an icon of Christ." Say: a painted curtain with "an image either of Christ or of one of the saints"; he did not remember whose.
- Say "tore it apart", not "tore it down".
- "After the quarrel he tore down a curtain." Say: he tore it while travelling with the bishop of Jerusalem (J6 11096-11098); the letter gives no date.
- "The council of 787 condemned Epiphanius." Say: it ruled that the anti-image passage under his name was "none of his".
- "The Caroline Books were a council" or "written at Charlemagne's order". Say: a book written to answer the council's argument.
- "Pope Epiphanius." Jerome's "pope" means father; do not use it.
- "John of Jerusalem" beside "John Chrysostom." Say "the bishop of Jerusalem" for the first.
- Any year or decade for his birth, his death, the Jerusalem visit or the ban, "in the 390s" included (only an editor's chronology gives it, J6 3042). Editors disagree; say "in the fourth century" or "in old age".
- "Three books." The counts differ (three, seven, six); say only "eighty heresies".
- "He founded a monastery near Eleutheropolis." Say: he lived as a monk near his birthplace; Jerome calls his house there "the old monastery".
- "The Panarion is reliable" or "fair." It is a foe's account; say so if its reports are told.
- "Augustine counted twenty heresies before Christ and sixty after." Say plainly, with no credit: Epiphanius set twenty before Christ's coming and sixty after (HOLL1 12512-12536).
- "A heresy is a teaching the church judged false." Say: a wrong belief, as he judged it, held since the first man; his list begins with peoples and Greek schools before Christ (HOLL1 13420-13425; 12512-12536).
- A narrator's line after Q2 drawing its lesson ("If the rivers were real, so was Eden"). If it is told, give it as his own argument: where there are rivers to see and drink, he writes, there are trees too (J6 10922-10924).
- "He copied his opponents' writings so no one could say he answered hearsay." Say: some heresies he knew by hearsay, he says; he copied an opponent's own writing so that no one could say he answered from hearsay alone (HOLL1 13426-13430; 34006-34011).
- "Wonders happened at his tomb." Say: Sozomen says it was said.
- "Ammonius proved him wrong." Say: Sozomen says he was "measurably convinced" and sent them away.
- "He died in 403 on the way to Cyprus." Say: he died at sea before he reached Cyprus.
- Any phrase of this outline in quotation marks, outside Q1-Q3, spoken as a quote (for example "silly old man", "a bright star among bishops", "none of his", "his order, not his measure", the plain-people line). They are here so the writer can check the sense. Paraphrase them, credited to their witness.
- "The monks there had no priest." Say: for a year they had complained that no one celebrated the sacraments for them, because its priests, Jerome and Vincent, held back from it out of humility (J6 10671-10676, 10706-10708).

**Leave out:**
- The beggar who feigned death, and the treasurer's bag of gold (Sozomen 7.27): wonder stories; the tomb line is enough.
- The empress's sick son (Sozomen 8.15): a side story.
- The warning against "Palladius of Galatia": the sibling's job.
- Rufinus's jibe about six thousand books, and Jerome's quarrel over translating Letter 51: card.
- Paula's ten days in Cyprus; Olympias's gifts: card.
- The creed in the Ancoratus and its likeness to the creed of 381: the link is the editors' argument.
- John of Damascus's summary of the Panarion: an editor's note only.
- The Antioch meeting with Vitalis: an editor's reconstruction.
- The Panarion's stories of Peter and Melitius, Potamon at Tyre, Marcellus and Athanasius: told on their own pages; Potamon's words are barred.
- The Ethiopian and Latin feast days: late legend; card.
- Whether the anti-image writings under his name are his: told only as the council's ruling and its opponents' answer.

**For the card, not the story** (each item from **Cut first** the draft drops, kept here so nothing is lost):
- Photius, a ninth-century patriarch of Constantinople, judged the Panarion fuller than any earlier book against the heresies, though poor in style (PHOT 3; PHOT 11652-11654, 11678-11683).
- He knew Hilarion and wrote his praises (Jerome, J6 30110-30112).
- He was bishop for thirty-six years (Palladius's Dialogue, PALL 8919-8921).
- Monks came to Cyprus "from all parts of the world" for love of him (Jerome, J6 21024-21027).
- Augustine took fifty-seven heresies from the Panarion and called him a man the Greeks counted among the great (AUG 2411-2426).
- Jerome: his books were read by the learned for their matter, and by the plain people for their language (J3 41529-41531).
- Sozomen reports it was said that demons were driven out and diseases healed at his tomb (SS 42335-42337).
- He was taught from youth by the most famous monks, mostly in Egypt (Sozomen, SS 39795-39796).
- Sozomen says he gave his whole inheritance to the shipwrecked and the needy, and then the church's funds (SS 42340-42345).
- Theophilus, who had once called him "a heretic, or a paltry schismatic", later called him "a most holy saint" (Palladius's Dialogue, PALL 8921-8925), and in his own letter "a bright star among bishops" (J6 22472-22474).
- Q3, his farewell at the shore, as Sozomen reports it: "I leave you the city, the palace and the play-acting. I am going away, because I am in a hurry, a great hurry." (SOZG 30108-30116; Hartranft's English, SS 43625-43626.)
- Jerome, who was there and on his side, says the bishop of Jerusalem's party mocked him as an old fool while he preached against Origen, and crowds pressed to kiss his feet (J6 40982-40996).
- He was born near Eleutheropolis and lived as a monk near his birthplace; the people of Cyprus chose him as their bishop (Sozomen, SS 39793-39799; J6 18736-18739).
- He listed the followers of Apollinaris, whom he called always dear to him, for teaching that Christ took flesh and a soul but no human mind (PG42 48828-48833, 48853-48857).
- Theophilus had once accused him of thinking God has a human form (Socrates, SS 17868-17870; Sozomen, SS 43502-43508).
