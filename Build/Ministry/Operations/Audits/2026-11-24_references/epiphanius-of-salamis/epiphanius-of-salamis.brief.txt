Epiphanius called his book the Panarion, which means a medicine chest. In its preface he says what it is for. "We are going to show you the names of the heresies. We will uncover their unholy deeds, which are like venom and poison. Alongside them we will fit antidotes: cures for those who have been bitten, and protection for those who may yet fall into it."

A heresy, in his book, is a wrong belief, as he judged it, held by people led astray since the first man. Epiphanius was a monk from Palestine who became bishop of Salamis, the chief city and port of Cyprus. He named eighty heresies as poisons and set a cure beside each. Later writers, from Augustine to a council at Nicaea four centuries on, took his book as their list. In old age he carried the same fight to Jerusalem and Constantinople, against the followers of Origen, a Christian teacher who had died long before.

He wrote the Panarion about 375. Each chapter names a heresy, tells its teaching and answers it, twenty from before Christ's coming and sixty after. He knew some heresies from books, some by hearsay, some with his own eyes and ears. He copied one opponent's own letter whole, so that no one could say he answered from hearsay alone.

In his old age he preached against Origen in the church at Jerusalem. In a letter to the bishop of Jerusalem Epiphanius says why. Origen, he writes, taught that souls were angels who fell into bodies, denied that this flesh would rise again, and read the garden of Eden as hidden meanings. Epiphanius held to the plain sense. Genesis names the Euphrates among the rivers of paradise, and he writes: "I have also drunk from the great river Euphrates. It was not spiritual water but real water, the kind you can touch with your hand and take into your mouth."

The same letter defends two acts of force. Jerome's brother had fled the priesthood several times. During a service near his monastery, he says, he had deacons seize the man and stop his mouth. Then he made him a deacon, then a priest, as the man cried that he was unworthy. The monks there had no priest, he explains, and he feared they would be cut off from the church. In a village church he found a curtain painted with an image of Christ or a saint. Holding that an image of a man in church went against Scripture, he tore it apart and asked that such curtains be banned.

Then Theophilus, bishop of Alexandria, wrote to win him against Origen. Epiphanius had the bishops of Cyprus forbid reading Origen's books. The historian Socrates says Theophilus wanted revenge on monks he had driven out of Egypt, the Long Brothers, and used a man of simple mind and manners. Sozomen says instead that Epiphanius had long loathed Origen's books, and that Theophilus saw no danger in following his example, because the people praised him.

At Theophilus's urging, he sailed to Constantinople. There he ordained a deacon in the territory of its bishop, John Chrysostom, against church rules. He would not pray with John unless John condemned Origen's books and sent the Long Brothers away. One of those monks asked him whether he had read any of their books. He had not, and had judged them by report. He sailed for Cyprus and died at sea before he reached home.

Augustine followed the order of his list. Four centuries on, Christians quarreled over pictures of Christ and the saints in churches, and both sides claimed him. Opponents of images quoted writings under his name. In 787 the council at Nicaea ruled that the passage was not his, arguing that his eighty chapters never listed the making of images. A book written to answer the council's argument replied with his own curtain letter.

Epiphanius set one thing more after the eighty heresies in his chest of cures: the church, Christ's holy bride, which he says was there from the beginning.
