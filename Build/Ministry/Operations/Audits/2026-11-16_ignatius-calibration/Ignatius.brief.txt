"I am chained to ten leopards, I mean a squad of soldiers." The man who wrote this was Ignatius, a condemned prisoner on the road to Rome.

It was early in the second century, and Ignatius was the bishop of Antioch in Syria. Christians then gathered in households, in Antioch, in the cities of Asia Minor and in Rome. No central body ruled them, and letters carried by hand held them together.

Soldiers were taking Ignatius to Rome, where he expected to face wild beasts. The record does not say why he was arrested, or who condemned him. He says only that he was in chains for the name and the hope that Christians share. Later writers place this in the reign of the emperor Trajan, but the exact year is not known.

His road ran through the Roman province of Asia, and the churches there sent their leaders to meet him. At the city of Smyrna he met its bishop, Polycarp. Along the way he wrote. Seven letters bear his name, though some have doubted that all seven are his.

One letter went ahead to the Christians in Rome, begging them not to save him. "I am God's wheat," he wrote. "Let me be ground by the teeth of the wild beasts, so that I may be found to be Christ's pure bread."

In the other letters he defended two things. Some teachers said that Christ only seemed to suffer, and we have only his side of that quarrel. He answered them with his own chains: "If our Lord did these things only in appearance, then I too am in chains only in appearance." He also urged each church to stay united around one bishop, with its elders and deacons, though not every church was led that way then.

At Troas, the last city he wrote from, news came that his own church in Antioch was at peace. Then he had to sail suddenly, and his letters end. They show only that he expected to die in Rome. Later writers say that he did die there, killed by wild beasts, and the church historian Eusebius gives it as a report.

The letters lived on. Christians in the city of Philippi asked Polycarp for them, and he sent all he had. "They deal with faith and endurance," Polycarp wrote, "and everything that builds us up in our Lord." Over the next three centuries, writers in the church's disputes about Christ brought his words as testimony that Christ truly suffered. They used his words about the wheat to show how a martyr faces death.

Antioch kept his memory too, with a feast day in his honour. Late in the fourth century, the preacher John Chrysostom spoke there on that day. "You sent out a bishop," he told the people, "and you received a martyr."
