# Writing from Polycarp

## What we are making

A story of about two and a half minutes that lets anyone, anywhere, meet one person from two thousand years of the church: who they were, what they did, and why the church still remembers them. It is told from the record of their own time, in plain words, as someone who knows that record well would tell it.

## The model, paragraph by paragraph

The approved Polycarp brief, paragraph by paragraph. Do the same jobs with your own person's record. Do not copy Polycarp's sentences, or his order where the record calls for another.

**Draw the listener in.** Open with what in this person's record pulls a listener in best: a scene, someone else's words about them, a letter or an object, their own words, or a question the record raises. Make it different from the briefs around it. Polycarp opens on his own words; Ignatius opens on a scene.

> "For eighty-six years I have served him, and he has done me no wrong. How can I blaspheme my King, who saved me?"

**Who, where, when.** Name them, their place, their century and their church. Then give the one link that makes them matter, through the witness who gives it.

> An old man gave that answer in a stadium in Smyrna, a city in the Roman province of Asia, in the second century. The Roman governor had offered to free him if he would curse Christ. The old man was Polycarp, the bishop of the church there. Irenaeus, who heard him as a boy, wrote that Polycarp had known people who had seen Jesus.

**What was at stake.** The danger, in the words of the people who brought it.

> In a time of persecution, the crowd had shouted, "Away with the atheists!" They demanded that Polycarp be found.

**The story.** What happened, in order. Each act comes with its reason. The human details are the record's own, and nothing is added for effect.

> Because many wished it, he withdrew to a farm, and then to another. There searchers seized two young slaves of his household, and one gave him away under torture. When the police came, he could have escaped. He said, "Let the will of God be done." He had food and drink set before them and asked for an hour to pray. He prayed for two hours, and many were sorry they had come for such a godly old man.

**The turning point.** The decisive exchange, kept in the record's order, with their own answer.

> The governor threatened him with wild beasts, and then with fire. Polycarp answered, "You threaten fire that burns for an hour and soon goes out." The crowd cried out against him as the destroyer of their gods. They asked for a lion, but the beast shows were over, so they shouted for him to be burned.

**How their own people told it.** A wonder stays in its witnesses' voice ("they say"). Then comes what those witnesses did with it, and why, in their words.

> The church in Smyrna says its own people saw the flames curve around him like a ship's sail. The fire could not consume his body, they say, so an executioner killed him with a dagger. Later they laid his bones in a fitting place. They wrote that the Lord would let them gather there to "keep the birthday of his martyrdom," the day he died for Christ. They would do this "to remember those who have already competed, and to train and prepare those still to come."

**What came of them, part one.** A role beyond their death, shown by an event, not stated as a lesson. Here: unity amid disagreement, and how a later bishop used it.

> Years before, Polycarp had visited Rome, where Anicetus was bishop. The two could not agree about keeping Easter. Polycarp kept it as he had with John and the other apostles he had known. Anicetus said he must follow the customs of the elders before him. "Even so, they shared communion," Irenaeus wrote, and the two parted in peace. Decades later Victor, another bishop of Rome, tried to cut off the churches of Asia over the Easter date. Irenaeus wrote to remind him that Polycarp and Anicetus had kept communion though they differed.

**What came of them, part two, and the end.** What they taught or defended, in their own words. It ends on how far that reached.

> Polycarp's letter to the church at Philippi survives. It calls its readers back from false teaching to "the word handed down to us from the beginning." More than two hundred years later, Jerome wrote that it was still read when the churches of Asia met.

## What the model shows, in one view

A portrait, not a list. The record shows Polycarp in three roles: a martyr, a link to the apostles' generation, and a voice for unity amid disagreement. The brief finds the roles the person's own record shows, and lets events carry them.

The record carries the story. Nothing is explained to the listener, no lesson is drawn, and no drama is made up. Where the witnesses tell a wonder, it stays theirs.

Plain and heard. It is said once and followed once, by a listener anywhere in the world, often in a second language. Scholars, dates and editions go on the card.

## How a brief is made

The way the Polycarp brief was made, with its wrong turns left out.

- Gather the record. A researcher builds a source sheet from the person's own time: what happened, their own words, what came of them ranked by what later witnesses carried on, and where the sources disagree. No prose.

- Settle the quotes. Two independent renderings of every quote into modern English, then a comparison that sets one reading.

- Write. A writer reads the Polycarp model and the sheet, and writes.

- Check against the sources. One listen through, then every sentence checked against the source texts themselves. Fix what is wrong, then stop. Polishing for style comes to an end.

- Mark listens. One at a time at first, then spot checks through each era.

## Three questions before it is done

- Heard once, could someone anywhere follow it?

- Do they come away knowing why this person mattered to the church?

- Can every sentence be shown in the sources of the time?

The reading score is a guard on the first question: grade 10 or below, and reading ease 60 or above. The length runs about two and a half minutes, from two at the shortest to three and a half at the longest.
