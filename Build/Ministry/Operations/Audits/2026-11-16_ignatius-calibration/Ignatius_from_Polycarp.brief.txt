"I am God's wheat. Let me be ground by the teeth of the wild beasts, so that I may be found to be Christ's pure bread."

A prisoner wrote those words early in the second century. He was Ignatius, bishop of Antioch, in Syria. Soldiers were taking him from Syria to Rome, where he expected to face the wild beasts. He tells of the journey himself, in seven letters known under his name, though some have doubted that he wrote them.

The letters name no charge, no judge and no year. Later writers place him in the reign of the emperor Trajan, in a time of persecution. Of the road he wrote, "I am chained to ten leopards, I mean a squad of soldiers."

He came to Smyrna, a city in the Roman province of Asia, where Polycarp was bishop, and the two met. Other churches sent their leaders to see the prisoner. Ephesus sent its bishop, Onesimus, and four others, and Ignatius wrote back to their churches.

Then he wrote ahead to the Christians in Rome and begged them not to save him. "I beg you, do not show me kindness at the wrong time. Let me become food for the wild beasts. Through them I will be allowed to reach God." For his own people he asked this: "Remember in your prayers the church in Syria. It now has God for its shepherd instead of me."

From Smyrna, and then from Troas, he sent warnings to the churches of Asia. Some, he said, taught that Christ only seemed to suffer. He would not write their names, and their side is not heard. He answered with his own chains. "But if they are right, why am I in chains? Why do I long to fight the wild beasts? Do I die for nothing, then?" He urged the churches of Asia to hold together around their bishops, elders and deacons. "Let no one do anything that concerns the church without the bishop," he wrote.

No letter tells what happened in Rome. Polycarp, writing to the church at Philippi, named Ignatius among those who are "in their rightful place with the Lord, with whom they also suffered." In the same letter he asked for any surer news of Ignatius and those with him. About two hundred years later the historian Eusebius wrote, "It is reported that he was sent from Syria to Rome, and became food for wild beasts because of his witness to Christ."

The church at Philippi had asked for his letters. Polycarp replied, "As you asked, we have sent you the letters Ignatius wrote to us, and all the others we have." Across three centuries, later writers quoted those letters in their arguments over who Christ was.

By the late fourth century, Antioch kept his feast, and his remains had been brought home to the city. Preaching there on that day, John Chrysostom said, "You sent out a bishop, and you received a martyr."
