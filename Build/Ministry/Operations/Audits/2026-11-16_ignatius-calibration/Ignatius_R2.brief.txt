"From Syria all the way to Rome I am fighting wild beasts, on land and sea, by night and by day. I am chained to ten leopards, I mean a squad of soldiers."

Ignatius wrote those words as a prisoner, early in the second century. He was the bishop of the church at Antioch in Syria, and soldiers were taking him to Rome, where he expected to face wild beasts.

His road ran through the Roman province of Asia, and churches along the way sent their leaders to meet him. At Smyrna he stayed with its bishop, Polycarp. There, and later at Troas, he wrote seven letters: to churches along his road, to the church in Rome, and to Polycarp himself.

The letter to Rome begged the Christians there not to save him. "Let me become food for the wild beasts," he wrote. "Through them I will be allowed to reach God." He would not give them orders, as Peter and Paul had. "They were apostles; I am a condemned man." Then he wrote the words later writers would repeat: "I am God's wheat. Let me be ground by the teeth of the wild beasts, so that I may be found to be Christ's pure bread."

To the other churches he wrote about what they must hold on to. Some teachers said that Christ only seemed to suffer. Ignatius answered with his own chains: "If our Lord did these things only in appearance, then I too am in chains only in appearance." Some Christians met apart from their bishop, so he urged each church to hold together around its bishop, with its elders and deacons. At Philadelphia, he said, the Spirit gave him the words: "Do nothing without the bishop. Love unity. Avoid divisions."

At Troas, news came that his church at Antioch was at peace. Then he had to sail suddenly, and his letters end.

Polycarp held him up to the church at Philippi as endurance they had seen with their own eyes. When they asked for his letters, Polycarp sent all he had. "They deal with faith and endurance," he wrote, "and everything that builds us up in our Lord." Later writers say Ignatius died at Rome, by the wild beasts. Irenaeus repeated his words about the wheat to show that those who are saved must pass through suffering. Over the next three centuries, writers in the church's arguments about Christ brought his words as witness that Christ had truly come in the flesh.

Antioch kept his memory, with a feast in his honour. Late in the fourth century, the preacher John Chrysostom spoke there on that day. "You sent out a bishop," he told the people, "and you received a martyr."
