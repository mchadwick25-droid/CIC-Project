Eutyches, the aged head of a monastery in Constantinople, stood at last before a meeting of bishops. He had said he was ill and old. Pressed to say what Christ is, as God and as man, he answered: "I confess that our Lord was from two natures before the union. But after the union I confess one nature." The union was the moment God the Word became man in the Virgin's womb.

For that sentence he was condemned in 448, restored the next year, and condemned again at Chalcedon in 451. He had fought the teaching of Nestorius, which divided Christ, by holding to one nature. Yet both sides of the coming split would curse his name.

An abbot of many years in the emperor's city, Eutyches wrote that from childhood he had wanted only a quiet life. By then Nestorius, once the city's bishop, had been deposed, and his teaching was said to be reviving.

A bishop, Eusebius of Dorylaeum, who as a lawyer had once exposed Nestorius, charged Eutyches before Flavian, bishop of Constantinople. Asked to say two natures and curse all who denied them, he refused. He called Christ's body the body of God, yet a human body taken from the Virgin. Nor would he curse the fathers who, he said, had rejected that phrase. Flavian's meeting stripped him of his priesthood, his monastery and his place in the church, for reviving, in his judges' words, old heresies.

Eutyches wrote to Leo, bishop of Rome, that a faction had readied his sentence before the hearing and forced other abbots to sign. He told Leo he had appealed at the trial. Flavian told Leo: "This he certainly never did." Leo, answering in a long letter to Flavian called the Tome, judged Eutyches very unwary and exceedingly ignorant.

The emperor called a council at Ephesus, under Dioscorus, bishop of Alexandria; its Syriac record says Eutyches's petition moved the emperor to call it. He told the emperor his trial record was false. To the council he wrote that he had been handed to a crowd ready to drag him through the city as a heretic, and that Flavian had shown him no pity: "He had no regard for my grey hairs. I have grown old with them in wars against heretics, and in confessing the true faith."

That record was read aloud. A bishop who had judged him denied a sentence the record gave as his own, then said he did not remember. The council shouted that his accuser should be burned alive. Its minutes, kept by the side that restored him, say he declared himself right-believing. He was restored, and Flavian deposed, with Theodoret of Cyrrhus and others.

After that emperor died, a new one called a council at Chalcedon, across the water from Constantinople. It received Leo's letter against Eutyches's teaching. Egypt's bishops condemned those who said Christ's flesh came from heaven, but would not name Eutyches. The council demanded they curse him and sign Leo's letter. They asked to wait for their new archbishop. So writes Evagrius, on Chalcedon's side, from its records some 140 years later.

The bishops at Chalcedon cried that Leo's opponents were Eutychians. Later writers on their side called the churches that refused the council followers of Dioscorus and Eutyches. Yet those churches were not his followers. They kept what he had defended: one Christ against Nestorius, and the creed of Nicaea with nothing added. They rejected his account of Christ's body: in their own chronicle, he would not confess it shared our nature. About 475, Timothy Aelurus returned from exile as bishop of Alexandria. The prefect, that chronicle says, was thought to be a Eutychian, so the people demanded in church that Timothy curse Nestorius and Eutyches. He did so at once, aloud, and was cleared of suspicion.

After 451 the record goes quiet; no source we have tells where or when he died. What was left was his name, cursed by both sides, even by churches still holding the one nature after the union that the council at Ephesus had cheered.
