# Outline: Mesrop Mashtots

File keys as in `Source_Sheet.md`: **T2** = `cic/texts/langlois_collection-historiens-armenie-tome2-fra_1869.txt`; **T12** = `cic/texts/langlois_collection-historiens-armenie-tomes1-2-fra_1867-1869.txt` (cleaner OCR of the same print); **SYN16**, **SYN5**, **MICH**, **KART**, **LIT** as there.

- **The essence:** Mesrop Mashtots, a royal secretary who became a monk, made the letters of the Armenian language, by the account of his pupil Koriun, so that the faith could be preached, written and taught in Armenian. With the catholicos Sahak and the king, he started the putting of the Scriptures into Armenian and set up schools on both sides of the border between Persia and Rome. (T2 3518-3521, 3554-3557, 3626-3656, 3696-3777; Documented as Koriun's claim.)
- **Also told:** (1) Why letters were needed: no Armenian script, so preaching to new converts was hard (T2 3518-3521); on the Persian side only Syriac learning was allowed (T2 3700-3704); and, in Lazar's later telling, church and school ran in a Syriac the people did not understand (T2 36234-36240). Documented. (2) The search and the making: the king sent him to Syrian Mesopotamia; a Syrian bishop's letters failed; he made the letters with a hermit, Rufinus, shaped after the Greek alphabet (T2 3571-3584, 3626-3632). Documented as Koriun's claim. (3) What the letters carried: translation began with Proverbs; Sahak and the king received the letters as a gift from above; schools opened in every province (T2 3634-3656). Documented. (4) Georgians and Albanians: Koriun says he gave the Georgians letters and renewed the Albanians' (T2 3657-3664); the Georgian chronicle gives its script to an ancient king (KART 2228-2230). Contested. (5) The Roman side: Sahak and Mesrop went west, wrote to the emperor and the bishop of Constantinople, and schools were paid from the public treasury (T2 3704-3777). Documented as Koriun's claim; Moses tells a harder welcome (T2 24208-24213, 24315-24337). (6) The force: he fought the Borborites with prison, chains and expulsion (T2 3779-3787). Documented as Koriun's claim. (7) The pupils brought back true copies and the canons of Nicaea and Ephesus, and the first hasty translations were corrected (T2 3805-3877). Documented.
- **Length:** About 645 words, about 4 minutes 18 seconds, once the Cut first list is applied (cap 675; the independent count before cuts is 784, `Length_Count.md`). The record is rich in one witness, not thin; no fill.
- **Setting:**
  - Time: Koriun gives no year, only that "Theodosius the Younger" was emperor (T2 3643-3644). Notes in Langlois's edition date King Vramshapuh to 392-414 and Sahak's see to 390-440 (T2 3608-3612, 3596-3597): editor's dates. Spoken: Koriun's own frame only, "in the days of the emperor Theodosius the Younger" (T2 3643-3644, 3709-3710); no year, century or "early 400s" in the story (Modern pass). The editor's dates go to the card.
  - Place: Armenia, split between Persia and Rome (T2 3700, "à l'époque du partage de l'Arménie"; 3706-3708). His birthplace, the village of Hatsegats in Daron (T2 3440-3441); his first preaching in the district of Koghten (T2 3495-3496); the search in Syrian Mesopotamia (T2 3574); death in the New City, in Ararat (T2 4215-4228).
  - Tradition: The Conversion of Armenia, the Armenian church in the century after it became Christian (census `armenian-christianity-conversion-era`, informalName). Spoken: "the Armenian church".
- **Words to explain:**
  - *catholicos*: the head of the Armenian church (the French says "patriarche"). Gloss on first use.
  - *the divine Tablets*: the stone tablets of the Law that God gave Moses. The plain sense of Koriun's phrase, said alongside the quote.
  - *Borborites*: a religious sect Koriun calls "rash and insolent" (T2 3780). Give only that; the editor's note calling them second-century Gnostics who denied the Last Judgment (T2 3814-3816) is the editor's.
  - *the Albanians* (Koriun's "Aghouank"): a Christian people of the Caucasus (Moses, T2 14134; Eghishe, T2 26944-26946, 29194-29195), neighbours of Armenia, not the Balkan Albanians. Census: "The Church of Caucasian Albania".
  - *Persian and Roman Armenia*: the country was divided; part was ruled by the Persian king, part by the emperor in Constantinople.
  - *vartabed* (only if the liturgy clause is used): "a teacher of the church; in old Armenian, the title of its learned fathers" (editor's note, T2 3757-3764). Or drop the word and say "Mesrop the teacher".
  - *Syrian Mesopotamia* and *Edessa*: "the Syriac-speaking lands south of Armenia" and "a city of those lands, a centre of Syriac learning". Edessa as a seat of Syriac translation stands on T2 3808-3812 (pupils sent there to translate from the Syriac).
  - *Georgians*: the French says "Géorgiens" for Koriun, "Ibères" for Moses; say "the Georgians".
- **Shape:** Missionary or translator.
- **Sibling:** none in the batch. Later: `koriun` and `eznik-of-kolb` (batch 13). On the tree now, `koriun`, `eznik-of-kolb`, `moses-of-khoren` and `lazar-parpetsi` tell their own lives from the same volume; this story's job is Mesrop's work: the letters, the Scriptures, the schools and the mission. It does not retell Koriun's authorship scene, Eznik's journey, or Moses's lament. Link: `council-of-ephesus-431`.
- **Openings:**
  1. The vision, in Koriun's voice. The bishop's letters have failed. Mesrop prays with tears, day and night, for the letters. Then, Koriun writes, he saw them in his heart, written by a right hand on stone, as clear as lines on snow (Q1). Lead straight into the essence: the man who saw them was a former royal secretary, and these became the letters of the Armenian language (T2 3586-3594, 3620-3624, 3626-3627). Do not call the vision a fact; it is Koriun's telling.
  2. The problem. A monk preaches in the district of Koghten to people who still keep pagan ways, and there is no way to write his words in their language (T2 3495-3521). Lead into the essence.
- **Story beats:**
  1. **Secretary to monk.** Son of Vartan, from Daron; he learned Greek as a boy and became chancellor of the king's decrees in the royal archives. He was learned in civil and military affairs, and liked by great and small. Then he gave up his posts and became a monk: herbs for food, a hair shirt, the bare ground for a bed. He gathered disciples. (T2 3439-3493.) Documented as Koriun's claim.
  2. **The need.** In Koghten he preached to people who kept pagan habits (Koriun adds that demons fled toward Media, a wonder in his voice; optional). With no letters for Armenian, preaching to new converts was hard. He prayed for letters and went to Sahak, the catholicos, and found him even more eager. (T2 3495-3562.) Documented. Optional, credited to Lazar, a later historian: services and study were in Syriac, and the people understood nothing (T2 36234-36240).
  3. **The search.** King Vramshapuh, learning of their wish, sent Mesrop and his disciples to Syrian Mesopotamia, where the learned were famous. A Syrian bishop named Daniel promised letters, but they could not render the sounds of Armenian. (T2 3571-3584.) Documented as Koriun's claim. Then, without retelling the vision: rising from prayer, he made the letters with a hermit named Rufinus, who gave them their shapes on Mesrop's directions, after the Greek alphabet (T2 3626-3632). Koriun's text calls Rufinus a man of Samos; editors read Samosata, a Syrian town (T2 3671-3674). Documented, with the place Contested.
  4. **Letters to Scripture, schools, and the neighbours.** Translation began at once with the Proverbs of Solomon; his disciples John and Joseph worked on it while he taught the youngest to write (T2 3634-3641). In the days of the emperor Theodosius the Younger, Koriun's only time mark (T2 3643-3644), he brought the letters to Sahak and the king, who received them like the divine Tablets (Q2; T2 3644-3651). He gathered children (Q3) and set up schools in every province and village, by order of the catholicos and the king (T2 3651-3656). Documented. Then, Koriun says, he went to the Georgians and gave them an alphabet, and to the Albanians, whose alphabet he renewed, leaving teachers in both lands, before he came back to Armenia (T2 3657-3664, 3696). Contested: the Georgian chronicle gives Georgia's script to an ancient king, Pharnavaz (KART 2228-2230), and its translator notes that the chronicle is silent on Mesrop (KART 7040, editor's).
  5. **Across the border, and by force.** On the Persian side Greek learning was barred and Greek books burned, so Sahak and Mesrop went west into the Roman part. Sahak sent Mesrop with letters to the emperor and to the bishop of Constantinople; the bishop named him a preacher (T2 3696-3726). In the emperor's provinces, children were kept at the public treasury's cost to study the letters (T2 3766-3777). Koriun tells this as a warm welcome; Moses of Khoren, a later historian, writes that the letters were first refused there (T2 24208-24213, 24315-24337). Then Mesrop set out to fight the Borborites; those who would not yield were put in prison and chains and tormented; those who still refused were burned, shut up and driven from the land (T2 3779-3787). Documented as Koriun's claim.
  6. **The pupils, and the defence.** Sahak and Mesrop sent pupils, Eznik among them, to Edessa and Constantinople; they came home with true copies of the Scriptures and the canons of the councils of Nicaea and Ephesus, and the first hasty translations were corrected against them (T2 3805-3877). When the council warned of a man shut out from the Council of Ephesus, who came with heretical books, Sahak and Mesrop, "defenders of the true faith" in Koriun's words, drove him out (T2 4098-4110). Through these years, Koriun says, he also lived as a hermit in caves, came down when he was needed, and wrote talks the simplest people could follow (T2 3910-3926, 3954-4011). Documented.
  7. **The end.** Sahak died; Mesrop wept and found no one willing to share "the work of Armenia" (T2 4175-4188). The same year, after a few days' illness in the New City in Ararat, he sat up among his gathered disciples, raised his arms, blessed the Trinity and entrusted them to God. Koriun says a cross of light was seen over the house (T2 4213-4255). Documented as Koriun's telling; the cross is a wonder in his voice.
- **What came of them** (about a third of the story):
  1. **The letters stayed his.** Every later Armenian witness held names him as their maker, some beside Sahak (SYN5 28864-28866). Lazar sends readers to Koriun to learn when, where and through whom the letters were found (T2 36214-36218); a Syriac patriarch of the twelfth century writes that the Armenians gained writing through Mesrop (MICH 30859-30860). The editor writes that "Mesrobian letters" became the usual name for the alphabet (T2 3049-3051, editor's). Widely Accepted.
  2. **A church in its own language.** Lazar, a later historian, says that once the letters and Scriptures came, schools opened, copyists multiplied, and men and women crowded the churches (T2 36490-36496). Lazar gives the translating to Sahak (T2 36473-36488). Credit it to Lazar. Documented (Lazar's claim).
  3. **The pupils became the next writers**: Koriun, Eznik, and in his own account Moses of Khoren, whom Sahak and Mesrop sent to Alexandria (T2 3805-3863; 24738-24740). Documented (each witness's claim).
  4. **Defended:** the faith as the council of Ephesus defined it; he and Sahak drove out the man the council had shut out, who came with heretical books, and his pupils brought home the council's canons (T2 3859-3860, 4098-4110). Marked as what he defended. Documented (Koriun's claim).
  5. **Kept as a saint.** A thirteenth-century Armenian calendar keeps a feast of "the Armenian Translators, Isaac and Mesrop and others" (SYN16 6239). Optional clause on who keeps him: the Armenian liturgy, in an English printing of 1908, asks that "Mesrob the Vartabed" be remembered at the altar (LIT 3341-3347). Not as the closing line. Documented.

Rendering: "At once he saw them. It was not in a dream while he slept, nor in a vision while he was awake, but in his heart. There they appeared to the eyes of his mind, traced by the wrist of a right hand writing on stone. It was just as one sees the smallest strokes of a line on snow." Job: the wonder at the heart of the story, kept in Koriun's voice; the writer may use the first three sentences or all four. Plain sense alongside: the letters came to him whole, as an inner sight.
  - **Q2, the welcome** (beat 4). French, T2 3647-3648; T12 55910-55912: "qui s'en réjouirent, comme si [c'eût été] les Tables divines". Rufinus (acts; "a hermit named Rufinus"). Eznik of Kolb (link; may stand as "Eznik" among the pupils). Moses of Khoren (link; witness). Lazar Parpetsi (link; witness; the koriun and eznik stories call him "Lazar of Pharp", and either is on the tree). Eight named at most; drop Moses or Lazar if a witness is not used. Nestorius (link; named once, as the teacher Koriun says the man's books followed).
- **Connection to carry into the story:** What passed from Mesrop was a script and, through it, Scripture and teaching in Armenian. The listener needs to understand three things. First, before the letters the faith reached Armenians through Syriac, which, Lazar says, the people could not follow, while on the Persian side, Koriun says, Greek learning was barred (T2 3518-3521, 3700-3704; Lazar T2 36234-36240). Second, the search ran through the Syriac world: a king sent him to Syrian Mesopotamia, a Syrian bishop's letters failed, and the letters were shaped after the Greek (T2 3571-3584, 3626-3632). That is one way the Syriac and Greek churches shaped the Armenian one. Third, the letters went into schools and translation at once, and his pupils carried the work on: they went to Edessa and Constantinople and came back with true copies of the Scriptures and the canons of Nicaea and Ephesus (T2 3805-3863). Tell it through what he and his pupils did, never as "this matters because".
- **Ending:**
  1. His death in Koriun's telling: after a few days' illness he sat up among his disciples, raised the arms Koriun says were always stretched toward heaven, blessed the Trinity, and entrusted them to God; a cross of light shone over the house (T2 4230-4255; T12 56468-56491). Wonder in Koriun's voice.
  2. The opening image seen again: the hand writing on stone, now the letters his pupils carried on (Q1 is not repeated; refer back only).

- **Who tells it:**
  - **Koriun**, his pupil, writing after Mesrop's death (his Life tells it, T2 4210-4260); he says he wrote of men he saw with his own eyes, to give an example to their spiritual children (T2 4334-4398). The whole life rests on him: say so once, inside a sentence about Koriun, near the opening or the essence. His Life is a disciple honoring a saint, with demons put to flight and a cross of light; keep those in his voice. Credited beside: the vision, the welcome, the force against the Borborites, the Georgian and Albanian letters, the death. He is held only in a French translation (T2 2856-2865); that is for the card.
  - **Moses of Khoren**, a later Armenian historian who calls himself a pupil of Sahak and Mesrop; his date is disputed (Library note in `cic/corpus-map/armenian-christianity-after-avarayr.yaml`). Credited beside: the first refusal on the Roman side. Optional.
  - **Lazar Parpetsi**, a later Armenian historian who read Koriun (T2 36214-36223). Credited beside: the Syriac services no one understood; the crowded churches afterward.
  - **The Georgian chronicle**, credited beside the rival claim for Georgia's script (KART 2228-2230).
- **Do not say:**
  - "Rufinus of Aquileia", or any hint that this is the translator on the tree → "a hermit named Rufinus". Koriun's text calls him a man of Samos and a disciple of an Epiphanius. He is not Rufinus of Aquileia, whose story is in this batch (slate caution).
  - "The Persian governors burned the Greek books" → Koriun says the books had been burned earlier, by Meroujan, "à l'époque du partage de l'Arménie" (T2 3698-3700). The governors barred Greek learning.
  - "Sahak and Mesrop went to Constantinople" → Sahak sent Mesrop and his nephew Vartan to the emperor and the patriarch (T2 3711-3726). Sahak stayed in the Roman part of Armenia.
  - "He invented the Armenian alphabet" as plain fact → "Koriun says he made the letters", or the vision in Koriun's voice. Never "God gave him the alphabet" in the narrator's voice.
  - "He invented the Albanian alphabet" → Koriun says he "renewed" their alphabet (T2 3662-3663).
  - "He gave the Georgians their alphabet" as settled → Koriun says so; the Georgian chronicle gives Georgia's script to an ancient king.
  - "He went to Edessa and Samosata to make the letters" as Koriun's → Koriun says Syrian Mesopotamia, and a hermit of Samos, which editors read as Samosata. Edessa for the letters is Moses's and Lazar's.
  - "He translated the whole Bible" → translation began with Proverbs, by him and his disciples; later writers give much of the Bible to Sahak.
  - "Born in 361" or "died in 441" or "died at eighty" → those are an editor's note; Koriun gives no years.
  - "In the early 400s", "around 400", "sixteen centuries ago" or any year for the letters → the editor's dates for Vramshapuh (392-414) and Sahak (390-440) (T2 3596-3597, 3608-3612); say "in the days of the emperor Theodosius the Younger" (T2 3643-3644) or nothing (Modern pass).
  - "the eastern Caucasus" for the Albanians → "the Caucasus"; the old sources held place them with the Caucasus mountain peoples (T2 14134) but do not say east (Modern pass).
  - "The Borborites were burned to death" or "at the stake" → the French says they were burned, shut up and driven out; who did it is not said. Do not soften it either: prison, chains, torment and expulsion stay in.
  - "Theodore of Mopsuestia" → the French adds "of Mopsuestia" in brackets; say "a man the council had shut out" or "a man who came with heretical books".
  - "So that they could sing in church" or any reason for the sweet voices and long breath → Koriun gives none.
  - "The tablets of the Ten Commandments" → "the tablets of the Law that God gave Moses".
  - "Mesrop wrote..." with a quote of his own → no writing of his is held.
  - "He served in the army" or "a Mamikonian" → editors' reports; leave out.
  - "The first sentence written was from Proverbs" → Koriun says translation began with Proverbs, not that a first sentence was written.
  - "Koriun was an eyewitness" stated as the narrator's fact → "Koriun says he saw these men with his own eyes".
  - "the rest were burned" → "those who still refused were burned, shut up and driven from the land" (T2 3784-3787). It was the same people, after the torture, not a second group.
  - "sent pupils to Constantinople" → "sent pupils to Edessa; from there they went on to Constantinople" (T2 3818-3828).
