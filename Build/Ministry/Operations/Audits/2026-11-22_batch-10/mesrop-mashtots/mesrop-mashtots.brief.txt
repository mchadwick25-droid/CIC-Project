A Syrian bishop's letters could not carry the sounds of Armenian, and Mesrop Mashtots prayed with tears, day and night, for letters that could. Then, his pupil Koriun writes: "At once he saw them. It was not in a dream while he slept, nor in a vision while he was awake, but in his heart. There they appeared to the eyes of his mind, traced by the wrist of a right hand writing on stone."

The letters, Koriun means, came whole, as an inner sight. Nearly all we know rests on this pupil, who says he saw these men with his own eyes. By his account, this royal secretary turned monk made the letters of the Armenian language, so that the faith could be preached, written and taught in Armenian. With the king and Sahak, the catholicos who led the Armenian church, he began putting the Scriptures into Armenian and founded schools on both sides of the Persian and Roman border.

He came from Daron, learned Greek as a boy, and kept the king's decrees in the royal archives. Then he became a monk, living on herbs, in a hair shirt, sleeping on bare ground.

In Koghten he preached to people who kept pagan ways, but with no letters for Armenian, preaching to new converts was hard. On the Persian side of the divided country, Koriun says, only Syriac learning was allowed. Mesrop went to Sahak, who was even more eager.

King Vramshapuh sent Mesrop to Syrian Mesopotamia, the Syriac lands to the south, where the bishop's letters failed. Rising from prayer, Mesrop made new letters with a hermit named Rufinus, who shaped them on his directions, after the Greek alphabet.

Translation began at once, with Proverbs. In the early 400s he brought the letters to Sahak and the king. "They rejoiced over them as if they were the divine Tablets," Koriun writes: the tablets of the Law that God gave Moses. Then "Mesrop gathered children who were bright and well taught, with sweet voices and long breath," and schools opened in every province and village.

Koriun says he went on to give the Georgians an alphabet and renew that of the Albanians, a Christian people of the eastern Caucasus. The Georgian chronicle gives Georgia's script to an ancient king.

So Sahak and Mesrop went west into Roman Armenia. Sahak sent Mesrop with letters to the emperor and the bishop of Constantinople, who named him a preacher. In the emperor's lands, children studied the letters at public cost. Koriun tells of a warm welcome; a later historian, Moses of Khoren, says the letters were first refused.

He also fought the Borborites, a sect Koriun calls rash and insolent. Those who would not yield, he writes, were put in prison and chains and tormented; those who still refused were burned, shut up and driven from the land.

Sahak and Mesrop sent pupils to Edessa, a center of Syriac learning, and from there they went on to Constantinople. They brought home true copies of the Scriptures and the canons of Nicaea and Ephesus, and the first hasty translations were corrected. When the council of Ephesus warned of a man it had shut out, who came with heretical books, Sahak and Mesrop drove him out. Koriun calls them defenders of the true faith.

When Sahak died, Mesrop wept, finding no one to share the work of Armenia. After a short illness in the New City, he sat up among his disciples, raised his arms, blessed the Trinity and entrusted them to God. Koriun says a cross of light was seen over the house.

Every later Armenian witness we have names him the letters' maker, some beside Sahak. Lazar, a later historian who credits the translating to Sahak, says once the letters and Scriptures came, copyists multiplied, and men and women crowded the churches. His pupils became the next writers: Koriun, Eznik, and, by his own account, Moses of Khoren.

The hand Koriun saw writing on stone had left letters, and Mesrop's pupils carried them on.
