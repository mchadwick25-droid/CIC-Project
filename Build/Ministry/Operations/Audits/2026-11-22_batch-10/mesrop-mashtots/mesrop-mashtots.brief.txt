A Syrian bishop's letters could not carry the sounds of Armenian, so Mesrop Mashtots prayed with tears, day and night, for letters that could. Then, his pupil Koriun writes: "At once he saw them. It was not in a dream while he slept, nor in a vision while he was awake, but in his heart. There they appeared to the eyes of his mind, traced by the wrist of a right hand writing on stone."

Koriun means the letters came to him whole, as an inner sight. Nearly all we know of Mesrop rests on this pupil, who says he saw these men with his own eyes. By his account, this former royal secretary turned monk made the letters of the Armenian language, so that the faith could be preached, written and taught in Armenian. With the king and Sahak, the catholicos, or head of the Armenian church, he began putting the Scriptures into Armenian and set up schools on both sides of the border between Persia and Rome.

He came from Daron, learned Greek as a boy, and kept the king's decrees in the royal archives. Then he became a monk, living on herbs, in a hair shirt, on the bare ground.

In the district of Koghten he preached to people who still kept pagan ways, but with no letters for Armenian, preaching to new converts was hard. On the Persian side of the divided country, Koriun says, only Syriac learning was allowed. Mesrop went to Sahak and found him even more eager.

In the early 400s, King Vramshapuh sent Mesrop and his disciples to Syrian Mesopotamia, the Syriac-speaking lands to the south, where the bishop's letters failed. Rising from prayer, Mesrop made new letters with a hermit named Rufinus, who shaped them on his directions, after the Greek alphabet.

Translation began at once, with the Proverbs of Solomon. He brought the letters to Sahak and the king. "They rejoiced over them as if they were the divine Tablets," Koriun writes: the tablets of the Law that God gave Moses. Then "Mesrop gathered children who were bright and well taught, with sweet voices and long breath," and schools opened in every province and village.

Koriun says he went on to give the Georgians an alphabet, and to renew the alphabet of the Albanians, a Christian people of the eastern Caucasus. The Georgian chronicle disagrees, giving Georgia's script to an ancient king.

On the Persian side Greek learning was barred, so Sahak and Mesrop went west into Roman Armenia. Sahak sent Mesrop with letters to the emperor and the bishop of Constantinople, who named him a preacher, and children in the emperor's lands studied the letters at public cost. Koriun tells of a warm welcome; Moses of Khoren, a later historian, writes that the letters were first refused.

Mesrop also set out against the Borborites, a sect Koriun calls rash and insolent. Those who would not yield, he writes, were put in prison and chains and tormented, and the rest were burned, shut up and driven from the land.

Sahak and Mesrop sent pupils, Eznik among them, to Constantinople and to Edessa, a centre of Syriac learning. They brought home true copies of the Scriptures and the canons of Nicaea and Ephesus, and the first hasty translations were corrected. When that council warned of a man it had shut out, who came with heretical books, Sahak and Mesrop drove him out, as defenders, Koriun says, of the true faith.

When Sahak died, Mesrop wept, finding no one to share the work of Armenia. The same year, after a few days' illness in the New City, he sat up among his disciples, raised his arms, blessed the Trinity and entrusted them to God. Koriun says a cross of light was seen over the house.

Every later Armenian witness names him as the letters' maker, some beside Sahak. Lazar, a later historian who gives the translating to Sahak, says that once the letters and Scriptures came, copyists multiplied, and men and women crowded the churches. His pupils became the next writers: Koriun, Eznik, and, by his own account, Moses of Khoren.

The right hand Koriun saw writing on stone had left letters, and Mesrop's pupils carried them on.
