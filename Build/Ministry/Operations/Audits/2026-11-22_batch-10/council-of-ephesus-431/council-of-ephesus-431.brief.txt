Years after the council that removed him, Nestorius, once bishop of Constantinople, wrote of it from exile: "Who was judge? Cyril. Who was the accuser? Cyril. Who was bishop of Rome? Cyril. Cyril was everything." Cyril of Alexandria had led it, sitting in the place of Rome's bishop.

A council is a meeting of bishops called to settle a question. The emperor Theodosius II called this one to Ephesus, over whether Mary could rightly be called Mother of God, the one who gave birth to God. Nestorius would not simply call her that. Cyril held that she was, because the one born of her was God's own Son, made man. At stake was how God and a man are one in Christ.

In June 431, Cyril opened the council before John of Antioch and the bishops of Syria arrived, over the protest of the emperor's officer, and it deposed Nestorius, removing him from office. John's smaller assembly deposed Cyril in turn, and peace came only two years later. The verdict held in the empire, but the Church of the East, in Persia, never made the council its own.

By the opening day, the bishops had waited sixteen days past Pentecost, the appointed day. Count Candidian, the emperor's officer, read them the emperor's letter and begged them to wait four days more for John of Antioch. In his protest, posted that day, he wrote: "I warned you of this, then, not once but many times, and humbly begged you, and I gained nothing." They drove him out, he says, with insult and by force.

Nestorius was summoned three times and would not come. By the council's own record, the winning side's, he had soldiers guard his house. The creed of Nicaea, the statement of faith made a century before, was read, and the bishops judged that Cyril's letter agreed with it and Nestorius's did not. They removed him and cut him off from the church's fellowship. Nestorius and his bishops told the emperor that agitators surrounded their houses, and that Memnon, bishop of Ephesus, had shut the churches against them and threatened them with death.

When John of Antioch arrived, his assembly deposed Cyril and Memnon for holding their council apart, against the emperor's orders. It charged that Cyril's twelve curses, added to his last letter to Nestorius, held old heresies. Cyril's council cut John's men off in turn, leaving their final sentence to Rome.

The council also ruled that no one may put forward any statement of faith beside Nicaea's, and upheld the condemnation of Pelagius and his followers.

In August, the emperor's new commissioner had the emperor's letter read "by persuasion and force", as he reported. He proclaimed Nestorius, Cyril and Memnon deposed, and arrested all three. That autumn the emperor dissolved the council.

In 433, John of Antioch and his bishops sent Cyril a confession about Christ's natures, what Christ is as God and as man: "A union of two natures has taken place. So we confess one Christ, one Son, one Lord. In keeping with this idea of a union without mixing, we confess the holy Virgin as Mother of God." Cyril accepted it, and John recognized Nestorius as deposed. That, says a historian of the councils, was Cyril's condition. Not all John's bishops paid it: Theodoret of Cyrrhus accepted Cyril's faith, but not the deposition.

Twenty years later, the council of Chalcedon took Cyril's letters, to Nestorius and to the Eastern bishops, as the empire's guide.

Beyond the empire, W. A. Wigram, an English historian of the Church of the East, wrote in 1910 that it never accepted the council, never formally rejected it, and was never asked to do either.

Earlier, in 1852, an English writer on its rites had been surprised. Those rites often referred to Nestorius being cut off, and censured it. Yet they made no mention of the council that expelled him.
