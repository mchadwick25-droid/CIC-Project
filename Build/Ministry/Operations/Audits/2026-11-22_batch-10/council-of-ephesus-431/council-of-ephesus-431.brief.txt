Years after the council that removed him, Nestorius, once bishop of Constantinople, wrote from exile: "Who was judge? Cyril. Who was the accuser? Cyril. Who was bishop of Rome? Cyril. Cyril was everything." Cyril of Alexandria had led it, in the place of Rome's bishop.

The emperor Theodosius II had called that council, a meeting of bishops to settle a question, to Ephesus. Could Mary rightly be called Mother of God, the one who gave birth to God? Nestorius would not simply call her that, but Cyril said she was, since her child was God's own Son, made man. At stake was how God and a man are one in Christ.

In June 431, Cyril opened the council before John of Antioch and the bishops of Syria arrived, over the protest of the emperor's officer. It deposed Nestorius, removing him from office. John's smaller assembly deposed Cyril in turn, and peace came only in 433. The verdict held in the empire, but the Church of the East, in Persia, never made the council its own.

The bishops had already waited sixteen days past Pentecost, the appointed day. Then Count Candidian, the emperor's officer, read the emperor's letter and begged four days more for John of Antioch. His protest that day says: "I warned you of this, then, not once but many times, and humbly begged you, and I gained nothing." They drove him out, he says, with insult and by force.

Nestorius, summoned three times, would not come. By the council's own record, the winning side's, he had soldiers guard his house. The bishops heard the creed of Nicaea, the statement of faith from a century before, and judged that Cyril's letter agreed with it and Nestorius's did not. They removed him and cut him off from the church's fellowship. Nestorius and his bishops told the emperor that agitators surrounded their houses, and that Memnon, bishop of Ephesus, shut the churches to them and threatened them with death.

When John of Antioch arrived, his assembly deposed Cyril and Memnon for holding their council apart, against the emperor's orders. It charged that Cyril's twelve curses, added to his last letter to Nestorius, held old heresies. Cyril's council cut John's men off in turn, leaving their final sentence to Rome.

The council also ruled that no one may put forward any statement of faith beside Nicaea's, and it upheld the condemnation of Pelagius and his followers.

In August, the emperor's new commissioner had the emperor's letter read "by persuasion and force", as he reported. He proclaimed Nestorius, Cyril and Memnon deposed, and arrested all three. That autumn the emperor dissolved the council.

Two years later, John of Antioch's bishops sent Cyril a confession of Christ's natures, what he is as God and as man: "A union of two natures has taken place. So we confess one Christ, one Son, one Lord. In keeping with this idea of a union without mixing, we confess the holy Virgin as Mother of God." Cyril accepted it, and John accepted Nestorius's deposition, the condition Cyril had set, says a historian of the councils. Some of John's bishops would not pay that price. Theodoret of Cyrrhus accepted Cyril's faith, but not the deposition.

In 451 the council of Chalcedon took Cyril's letters, to Nestorius and to the Eastern bishops, as its guide.

W. A. Wigram, an English historian of the Church of the East, wrote in 1910 that it never accepted the council, never formally rejected it, and was never asked to do either.

An English writer on its rites, in 1852, was surprised. They often referred to Nestorius being cut off, and censured it, yet never mentioned the council that expelled him.
