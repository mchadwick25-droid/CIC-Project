# Outline: Theodoret of Cyrrhus

Sheet items (S, E, W, I) are in `Source_Sheet.md`, which gives every file and line. NP3 is Jackson's English (1892) of Theodoret's works and letters, with his Prolegomena (EDITOR); RH the Greek of PG 82 (1864); P449 Perry's English (1881) of the Syriac acts of 449; LEO Feltoe's English (1895) of Leo's letters; NP14 the councils in English (1900); EVW and EVG Evagrius in English (1846) and Greek (1898); M5 Mansi's Latin (damaged OCR); ZACH the Syriac chronicle under Zachariah's name, in English (1899); CASS Cassiodorus's Latin (damaged OCR).

- **The essence:** Theodoret, bishop of Cyrrhus, a small town in northern Syria, was a leading voice of Antioch's teaching that Christ is one, God and man, with neither mixed away into the other. He answered Cyril of Alexandria's twelve curses, was deposed at Ephesus in 449, and was let back at Chalcedon in 451 only after he cursed his friend Nestorius by name. *Documented:* E3, E4, E8, E12. He is on the tree also as a historian: his Church History carried the church's story on from Eusebius, and his Religious History kept the Syrian monks he knew, Simeon on his pillar among them. *Documented:* W1, W3.
- **Also told:**
  - What he held: that God the Word did not turn into flesh but took a human life, and stayed what he was; so the two must not be mixed, and Mary is Mother of God because of the union (E4; *Documented*). Marked: *what he defended.*
  - What he did as bishop, in his own words: bridges, baths, a channel bringing water to the town, villages of rival churches brought in, and stones thrown at him (E2; *Documented* as his own claim, written to answer his accusers).
  - What the Church History is: five books, about 105 years, from the rise of Arius to the death of Theodore of Mopsuestia, beginning where Eusebius stopped (W1; *Documented*).
  - What the Religious History is: lives of the Syrian hermits and monks, many of whom he says he saw and heard himself (W3; *Documented* as his eyewitness claim).
  - What happened to him: confined to his diocese, deposed in his absence in 449 by a council that asked the emperors to burn his books; restored in 451 only after he cursed Nestorius by name before the council (E7, E8, E12; *Documented*).
  - What came of him: his History joined Eusebius's in one continuous story that later writers on both sides of Chalcedon carried on; a century after him, a council condemned what he had written against Cyril (I1, I2; *Documented*).
  Checked against the Library: the census teaser and card hold. One correction for the card: the Religious History is now held, in Greek (RH).
- **Length:** about 4 minutes (about 600 words at 150 a minute; 675 at most). The count it must carry: three quotes, about 95 words; the plain sense of the words to explain (Mother of God, anathema or curse, "the form of a servant", cubit, council, the followers of Marcion), about 55; witnesses credited (his own letters, written to defend himself; the acts of 449; the acts of Chalcedon; Leo; Evagrius; Cassiodorus; the Syriac chronicle; a later writer on his death), about 60. Part by part: the opening about 55 (Quote 1); the essence about 65; beat 1 about 45; beat 2 about 60 (Quote 2); beat 3 about 35; beat 4 about 70 (Quote 3); beat 5 about 65; beat 6 about 60; what came of him about 125; the ending about 30. About 610. The record holds two full strands, the controversy and the histories; both are the heart, so it runs past three minutes. Whatever else the record holds goes to Leave out or the card.

**Setting**
- Century and years: about 393 to about 458; bishop from about 423 (S3; *Widely Accepted*; the birth year is *Contested*). Spoken: "in the fifth century".
- Place: Cyrrhus, a small town in northern Syria, with eight hundred parishes in his care (S1; Letter 113, NP3 33165-33166). Spoken: "Cyrrhus, a small town in northern Syria". Antioch, the great city of the region, where he preached and where the bishop he answered to sat (NP3 31484-31489).
- Tradition: the church of Antioch, its way of reading Scripture for "what the words actually said" (S2, the census entry "Antioch in Chrysostom's Day"). Spoken: "the church of Antioch" or "Antioch's teaching".

**Words to explain**
- **Mother of God**: "Mother of God, the one who gave birth to God" (the Ephesus outline's gloss). Say "Mother of God"; never "Theotokos". In Chalcedon's acts the English reads "Parent of God": use "Mother of God" in the narrator's words.
- **anathema / curse**: "a solemn curse that cut a person off from the church". Use "curse" in the narrator's words; "anathema" only inside the short phrase from the acts, with the gloss beside it.
- **"the form of a servant"** (Quote 2): Paul's phrase, in his letter to the Philippians, for the human life Christ took (NP3 4719-4723). The narrator gives the plain sense beside it: God did not turn into a man; he stayed God and took on a human life.
- **cubit** (Quote 3): "the length of a forearm". Give no conversion into metres.
- **council / synod**: "a meeting of bishops". The councils are named by place and year: "the council of 449 at Ephesus", "Chalcedon, in 451".
- **followers of Marcion**: "a rival church that set aside the Old Testament".
- **"Eusebius of Caesarea"**: "the bishop who wrote the first history of the church" is not in the sources; say "Eusebius of Caesarea, whose history of the church ran to Constantine's day" (W1, NP3 5219-5223).

- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker. The question he tried to answer: how God and a man are one in Christ, without one swallowing the other. What he taught: the counter-statement (Quote 2). Who learned from him: the later historians who carried his History on (I1). The trap is a list of books: the controversy is shown through two moments in his own words (Quote 1, Chalcedon), and the Religious History through one image (Quote 3). Name the Church History and the Religious History; no other titles.

- **Sibling:**
  - `council-of-ephesus-431` (batch; job: the council of 431, its decision and the peace of 433). Here 431 is a clause: he came with John of Antioch's side and was one of their envoys to the emperor. The peace is one or two sentences, told from his side. The Ephesus outline's Quote 3 (the confession of 433) is barred here: paraphrase, never "a union of two natures" in quotation marks.
  - `eutyches` (batch; job: Eutyches's teaching and his three verdicts). Here Eutyches is a clause: the council that deposed Theodoret restored Eutyches. Eutyches's trial is not told, and the Eutyches outline's "grey hairs" line is not used.
  - `council-of-chalcedon-451`, `leo-of-rome`, `dioscorus-of-alexandria` (live) tell 449 and 451. Here only what Theodoret did and suffered there. Leo's "not a trial but a robbery" and "not of judges but of robbers", Chalcedon's definition and the Syriac chronicle's "robe of Christ" stay barred (slate). Do not call the council of 449 a "robbers' council".
  - `nestorius`, `cyril-of-alexandria` (live): every quote barred. Their stories hold Ephesus and the name; here only Theodoret's answer to Cyril and his letter to Nestorius.
  - `second-council-of-constantinople-553` (live) tells the Three Chapters; here one clause.
  - Note for the writer and reviewers: the `dioscorus-of-alexandria` story quotes Dioscorus's "Sooner would Dioscorus see his own hand cut off". Quote 1 here is a different man's different vow; do not echo or compare the two.

**Openings**
1. **Chosen. The vow to his friend** (E6; M5 88759-88763; NP3 38007-38010). In about three sentences: Theodoret wrote to Nestorius, the deposed bishop of Constantinople, that he had found Cyril's writings free of heresy (NP3 37999-38003); give the letter no year. He had accepted Cyril's letter as sound, he said, but on what was done to his friend he would not move: Quote 1. Then straight into the essence, which reaches ahead to the curse at Chalcedon. Credit it as his letter; the Greek is lost and it survives in Latin.
2. **Five and thirty days away** (E9; NP3 33141-33149). Theodoret wrote to Leo of Rome that the bishop of Alexandria had condemned him without a summons, without a hearing, while he was thirty-five days' journey away. Narrator's words only. If this opening is chosen, beat 5 does not retell the letter.

**Story beats** (the opening and the essence are the first minute, about 120 words; then the life once, in order)

1. **Given to God; a bishop against his will** (E1, E2; S1). In his own account, his parents promised him to God before he was born; he lived in a monastery and was made bishop of Cyrrhus "unwillingly" (short phrase; Letter 81, NP3 31496-31501). As bishop, he says, he built bridges and baths and a channel that brought water to the dry town, and led whole villages of rival churches into his own, and was stoned many times for it (NP3 31507-31516). *Documented* as his own claim: credit it once, as written to answer his accusers ("My accusers compel me to speak", NP3 31496). The wonder of his birth stays in his voice if told (RH 108893-108991); it may go.
2. **430-431: the answer to Cyril** (E3, E4, E5). Cyril of Alexandria had sent out twelve curses against Nestorius's teaching. John of Antioch sent them to Theodoret and asked him to answer them in writing (Letter 150, NP3 36088-36090; *Documented*, his own words). He wrote that they renewed the teaching of Apollinarius, "long ago stamped out" (NP3 36107-36109); give Apollinarius by role, as "an old teaching the church had long rejected", and add no account of what it taught. His answer, point by point: Quote 2, with its plain sense. So Mary is rightly Mother of God, he wrote, because of the union, not because she bore God by nature (NP3 4733-4734). *What he defended.* At Ephesus in 431 he stood with John's side and went as one of their envoys to the emperor (E5; Jackson; one clause; the Ephesus story holds the council).
3. **433: the peace, and the one thing he refused** (E6). Two years later Cyril and John's side made peace. Theodoret and his fellow bishops read Cyril's letter and found it agreed with their own faith (Letter 171, NP3 37939-37947). But he would not accept that Nestorius had been rightly removed. Some years later, his English translator says, he found a way to accept the curses on Nestorius's teaching, though not, he said, on the man (NP3 2859-2870; EDITOR, *Widely Accepted*). One clause, credited to the translator. One or two sentences; point back to the opening's letter without quoting it again. The date of the letter is the editors' (*Inferential-Thin*); the refusal is *Documented*.
4. **The histories** (W1, W3). In these years he wrote his Church History. It begins, he says, where Eusebius of Caesarea's history stopped, and runs about 105 years, from the rise of Arius to the death of Theodore of Mopsuestia (NP3 5219-5224, 18270-18274). He wrote it, he says, so that forgetting would not rob good deeds of their fame (NP3 5208-5213; narrator's words). He also wrote lives of the Syrian monks, many of whom he says he saw and heard himself. Of Simeon he writes that the crowds all tried to touch him and take a blessing from his leather clothes, and so he took to standing on a pillar (RH 113729-113734). Give no place for the pillar. Quote 3, kept in Theodoret's voice; the narrator glosses "cubit". He says he saw Arabs come in bands of hundreds to break their idols before him and join the church (RH 113926-113940; "he says"). *Documented* as his eyewitness account. Date: the Church History about 438-440 or a little later by his translator's reckoning (NP3 17855-17864; EDITOR); the Religious History while Simeon still lived ("now thirty-six", in the quote); no held line gives its year, so tell it beside the Church History with "he also wrote", and give it no date. Optional, one clause, as links: his History tells how Ephrem's hymns still enlivened the martyrs' feasts (NP3 15063-15066) and how Chrysostom's body was brought home to Constantinople (NP3 17850-17855).
5. **448-449: confined, deposed, absent** (E7, E8, E9, E10). An imperial order forbade him to leave Cyrrhus. "No indictment was published; no prosecutor appeared", he wrote; yet the sentence was given (Letter 81, NP3 31453-31458; narrator's words, or this short phrase). In August 449 a council at Ephesus, led by Dioscorus of Alexandria, deposed him in his absence. Its own acts say he had written against Cyril, whom it called their blessed father, and ask the emperors to burn his books (P449 15581-15603, 15641-15645; credit the acts, his opponents' record). The same council restored Eutyches (one clause; Jackson NP3 3057-3058; EVW 1786-1787 for Theodoret's deposition). He wrote to Leo of Rome that he had been condemned without a trial, and begged Leo to say whether he must accept the sentence (Letter 113, NP3 33171-33177, 33189-33193). Do not use the letter's "not a tomb" line here: it is kept for the ending. He was allowed to withdraw to his monastery (Letter 119, NP3 33481-33485, his request; that it was granted is Jackson's, NP3 3064-3069).
6. **451: Chalcedon, and the curse** (E11, E12). After the emperor Theodosius died, a new emperor called a council at Chalcedon (Jackson, NP3 3186-3197). Its officials let Theodoret in, saying Leo had restored his see to him; bishops of Egypt and Palestine shouted to cast out "the teacher of Nestorius" (short phrase; NP3 3231-3239). At a later session the bishops stood before the altar rails and demanded he curse Nestorius. He asked for his papers to be read; they wanted nothing read. He offered to condemn Nestorius and Eutyches together, with his faith explained; they shouted for a plain curse. He said he could not speak without setting out his faith in full; they cried that he was a heretic (NP3 3305-3334; narrator's words). Then he said it: "Anathema to Nestorius" (short phrase, with the gloss; NP3 3335; EVG 9077-9079; EVW 5243-5244). He was given back his see (EVW 5248-5249; EVG 9085-9087). *Documented*, from the council's acts and Evagrius. Do not number the session.

**What came of him** (about a third)
1. *Heaviest: his History became part of one continuous story.* About 140 years later Evagrius, a Greek historian, wrote that Eusebius, Sozomen, Theodoret and Socrates had left "a most excellent record", and set out to carry it on (I1; EVW 1106-1116, 1136-1140; EVG 1255-1259). The Syriac chronicle of the churches that rejected Chalcedon begins after "the three Ecclesiastical Histories of Eusebius, Socrates, and Theodoret" (ZACH 932-937). In Italy, Cassiodorus had his History put into Latin with Socrates's and Sozomen's, as one history in twelve books (CASS 83303-83307; confirmed by Zenos, NP2 1695-1699). *Documented.* The point the listener needs: writers on both sides of the split over Chalcedon took up the story where he had left it.
2. *Remembered by the other side as an enemy.* That same Syriac chronicle calls his friends "the Nestorian party" and says he won Leo over "with the gift which blinds the eyes of the soul", a bribe (ZACH 2219-2221, 2272-2273). Credit it as their charge; the narrator does not carry it. *Documented* as a hostile memory.
3. *His writings condemned, the man not.* In 553 a council at Constantinople condemned what he had written against Cyril and in defense of Nestorius (I2; NP14 23656-23661; EVW 9578-9583). One clause; the 553 story tells it. *Documented.* His translator's verdict that he was restored in spite of what he wrote, and his writings condemned in spite of his restoration (NP3 3511-3513), may be given only as Jackson's, in the narrator's words.
4. *The monks' memory.* Evagrius calls his account of Simeon "an eloquent record" (EVW 1961-1963; short phrase). His book also tells of Maron, the monk the Maronites take their name from (RH 109564-109569; census `maronite-christianity`). Optional; one clause each. *Documented.*
5. *Leo's ally.* Leo wrote to him after Chalcedon of the victory "you and we together had won" over Nestorius's and Eutyches's teaching (E14; LEO 10293-10297). Optional, narrator's words; it may go if the count runs over.
6. Who keeps him today: no held source names anyone. Leave it out.

**Quotes** (3)

Only Quotes 1-3 are quoted aloud. "Unwillingly", "No indictment was published; no prosecutor appeared", "the teacher of Nestorius", "Anathema to Nestorius", "a most excellent record", "an eloquent record" and "with the gift which blinds the eyes of the soul" and "long ago stamped out" may stand as short phrases inside the narrator's sentences. Each quote and short phrase was searched against every census story but this one and the card quotations (the `check_tree_overlap.py` story set): none is used in a story on the tree. "With the gift which blinds the eyes of the soul" stands already on the `leo-of-rome` card (legacy, remembered), crediting the same chronicle; it is a card line, not a story quote, so not barred, but the narrator may carry it as "a bribe", credited to the chronicle. None is in the batch outlines for Ephesus, Eutyches, Pelagius or Ulfila. None is barred by the slate. No lifted reuse is used.

1. **The vow to Nestorius.** Latin, Letter 172 as kept in the Synodicon, M5 88759-88763 (damaged OCR, long s as f): "His autem quae contra tuam iniuste et inique facta sunt sanctitatem, nec si ambas manus meas quilibet abscindat, patior assentire, procul dubio divina mihi cooperante gratia et animae imbecillitatem roborante." Confirmed by a second held print, Jackson's English (1892) from the same Latin version, NP3 38007-38010. The Greek is lost (NP3 37972-37974). Rendered by me from the Latin:
   "I will never agree to what was done against you, unjustly and wickedly, not even if someone cuts off both my hands, as God's grace works with me and strengthens my weak soul."
   "Tuam sanctitatem" (your holiness), a bishop's form of address, is rendered "you". The ablative absolute ("cooperante gratia ... roborante") is rendered as an "as" clause, not as a second sentence. Job: the hook; his loyalty in his own words, and the loyalty that Chalcedon later made him curse by name. Not on the tree.
2. **The answer to Cyril.** Counter-statement I, Jackson's English (1892), NP3 4724-4726; the Greek is not held. Rendered by me from that English:
   "The form of God was not changed into the form of a servant. It stayed what it was, and took the form of the servant."
   Plain sense beside it: God did not turn into a man; he stayed God and took on a human life. Job: his one idea, in his own words. Not on the tree.
3. **Simeon's pillar.** Greek, Religious History, chapter on Simeon, RH 113735-113738: "Πρῶτον μὲν ἓξ πήχεων τμηθῆναι κελεύσας, εἶτα δύο καὶ δέκα, μετὰ δὲ ταῦτα δύο καὶ εἴκοσι, νῦν δὲ ἓξ καὶ τριάκοντα· ἀναπτῆναι γὰρ εἰς οὐρανὸν ἐφίεται, καὶ τῆς ἐπιγείου ταύτης ἀπαλλαγῆναι διατριβῆς." One held print (Migne, 1864), legible but with steady OCR letter confusions (χ for κ, δ for β); no second held print of this chapter. Rendered by me from the Greek:
   "First he ordered it cut six cubits high, then twelve, after that twenty-two, and now thirty-six. For he longs to fly up to heaven, and to be set free from this life on earth."
   The narrator says "pillar" in the sentence before, so "it" is clear. Job: the Religious History at work, an eyewitness writing while the man still stood there. Not on the tree. If the check will not accept a single damaged print, tell it in the narrator's words, credited to Theodoret.

Not used: Jackson's "were substantially at one" (editor's verdict, barred). Leo's two "robbery" lines (barred). His Church History lines already on the tree (`alexander-of-alexandria`, `ossius-of-cordoba`): lifted by the slate only to show his History at work; not needed. "Unless I set forth at length my faith I cannot speak" (NP3 3329-3330): told in the narrator's words instead, to keep three quotes.

**Connection to carry into the story:** two things passed from him. First, a continuous history: Eusebius had told the church's story to Constantine; Theodoret took it on from there (W1), and a century and more later historians on both sides of Chalcedon, Evagrius in Greek and the Syriac chronicle of the churches that refused Chalcedon, began where he and his fellow historians stopped, while Cassiodorus had the three put into Latin as one history (I1). The listener must understand that his book became a link in a chain that both sides kept using, even while one side cursed him (I3). Second, a teaching and its price: he defended Christ as one, God and man unmixed (E4), and Leo counted the outcome at Chalcedon a victory they had won together (E14); but his own place there was bought by cursing his friend (E12), and a century later his writings against Cyril were condemned (I2). Tell it through what the historians did with his book and what the councils did to him, never as a "this matters because" sentence.

**Ending** (on the person)
1. **Chosen. Nothing to leave.** In his letter to Leo he wrote that in all his years as bishop he had gained "not a house, not a piece of ground, not an obol, not a tomb" (NP3 33201-33205; narrator's words, or the short phrase "not a tomb"). A later writer, Gennadius, says only that he died in the reign of the emperor Leo; no held source gives the year (NP3 43772-43776; E15). Do not draw a moral from the tomb.
2. **The pillar seen again.** End on the image of Quote 3: the man who wrote down what he saw at the foot of Simeon's pillar. Use only if the ending runs short; do not repeat the quote.

**Do not say**
- Not "he betrayed Nestorius" or "he changed his views". Say he said the words the council demanded. Whether his views changed is disputed (E13).
- Not "Leo restored him" as the narrator's fact. Say "the council's officials said Leo had restored his see to him" (NP3 3234-3235).
- Not "the Robber Council" or "a robbers' council"; not "not a trial but a robbery". Say "the council of 449 at Ephesus".
- Not "he bribed Leo". Say "the Syriac chronicle of the other side says he won Leo over with a bribe".
- Not "he wrote the letter urging Nestorius to accept 'Mother of God'": the editors only say it has been attributed to him.
- Not "born at Antioch" as fact: "his English translator says he was born at Antioch".
- Not "he died in 458" or "in 457". Say "in the reign of the emperor Leo, a later writer says; the year is not known".
- Not "the eighth session" or "the ninth": the sources differ. Say "at a later session".
- Not "he was stoned many times" as the narrator's fact. Say "he says".
- Not "Simeon worked wonders" or "Arabs came in thousands" as the narrator's fact: "Theodoret says he saw", and keep "hundreds, sometimes a thousand" in his voice.
- Not "in 553 Theodoret was condemned". Say "what he had written against Cyril was condemned".
- Not "Theotokos". Say "Mother of God", glossed.
- Not "a union of two natures" in quotation marks (the Ephesus outline's quote).
- Not "the last voice of Antioch" or "Antioch's greatest theologian": no held source says it.
- Not "his teachers Diodore and Theodore": only his translator says it.
- Not "a wretched little town": that is his translator's phrase. Say "a small town".
- Not "in exile" of Nestorius in the opening: he had been sent back to his monastery near Antioch (the Ephesus outline). Say "the deposed Nestorius".
- Not "he refused for eighteen years", "he held out until Chalcedon" or "he broke his vow". His translator says that some years after 433 he found a way to accept the curses on Nestorius's teaching, "not, as he said, on Nestorius" (NP3 2859-2870).

- No year for the letter to Nestorius, and not "after the peace" as fact: tie it to his own words about accepting Cyril's writings.
- Not "his books were burned" or "ordered burned": the council asked the emperors to order it.
- No tears, anguish or silence at Chalcedon: the acts give none.
- Not "he went back to Cyrrhus after Chalcedon": "Some doubt hangs over the question" (NP3 3350).
- Not 'he built baths': he says he 'looked after the public baths'. He built bridges and the conduit (NP3 31507-31511).
