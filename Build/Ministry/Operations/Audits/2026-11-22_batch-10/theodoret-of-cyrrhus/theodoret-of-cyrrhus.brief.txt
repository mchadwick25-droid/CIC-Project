Theodoret of Cyrrhus wrote to his friend, the deposed Nestorius. "I will never agree to what was done against you, unjustly and wickedly, not even if someone cuts off both my hands, as God's grace works with me and strengthens my weak soul."

In the fifth century, Theodoret was a leading voice of Antioch's teaching that Christ is one, God and man, with neither mixed away into the other. He answered Cyril of Alexandria's twelve curses and was deposed in 449, and at Chalcedon, two years later, he was let back only after he cursed Nestorius by name. He was a historian too. His Church History carried the church's story on from Eusebius, and his Religious History kept the Syrian monks he knew, Simeon among them.

Much of his life comes from letters he wrote to answer his accusers and to win Leo of Rome's help. He says he lived in a monastery and was made bishop of Cyrrhus, a small town in northern Syria, "unwillingly". There, he says, he built bridges and a water channel for the town. He also led villages of Marcion's followers, a rival church that set aside the Old Testament, into his own and, he says, was stoned for it many times.

Cyril had sent out twelve curses against Nestorius's teaching, solemn acts that cut a person off from the church. When John of Antioch asked Theodoret to answer them, he wrote: "The form of God was not changed into the form of a servant. It stayed what it was, and took the form of the servant." The form of a servant is Paul's phrase for a human life: God stayed God, and took one on. So Mary is Mother of God, the one who gave birth to God, he wrote, because of the union. At Ephesus in 431 he stood with John's side.

Two years later the two sides made peace, and Theodoret found that Cyril's letter agreed with his own faith. But he would not accept that Nestorius had been rightly removed. Some years later, his English translator says, he accepted the curses on Nestorius's teaching, though not, he said, on the man.

In these years he wrote his Church History, which begins, he says, where Eusebius of Caesarea stopped, in Constantine's day. His Religious History tells of many monks he says he saw and heard himself. Of Simeon, who stood on a pillar, he writes: "First he ordered it cut six cubits high, then twelve, after that twenty-two, and now thirty-six. For he longs to fly up to heaven, and to be set free from this life on earth." A cubit is the length of a forearm.

An order from the emperor then kept him in Cyrrhus, with no charge and no accuser. The council of 449 at Ephesus, led by Dioscorus of Alexandria, deposed him in his absence. Its acts, his opponents' record, say he had written against Cyril, and ask the emperors to burn his books.

At Chalcedon, the council's acts record, the bishops at the altar rails demanded that he curse Nestorius. He offered to condemn Nestorius and Eutyches together, with his faith explained, but they shouted for a plain curse and cried that he was a heretic. Then he said it: "Anathema to Nestorius", a curse on his friend. He was given back his see.

About 140 years later Evagrius, a Greek historian, called his History and three others "a most excellent record", and carried it on. A Syriac chronicle from the churches that refused Chalcedon begins where he and two others stopped, so both sides carried his story on. Yet that same chronicle says he won Leo over with a bribe. In 553 a council at Constantinople condemned what he had written against Cyril.

To Leo he had written that in all his years as bishop he had gained not a house, not a piece of ground, not a coin, "not a tomb". He died in the reign of the emperor Leo, a later writer says; the year is not known.
