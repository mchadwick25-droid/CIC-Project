Theodoret of Cyrrhus wrote to his friend, the deposed Nestorius, that he had found Cyril of Alexandria's writings free of heresy. But on what was done to Nestorius himself, he would not move. His letter, which survives only in Latin, says: "I will never agree to what was done against you, unjustly and wickedly, not even if someone cuts off both my hands, as God's grace works with me and strengthens my weak soul."

In the fifth century, Theodoret was a leading voice of Antioch's teaching that Christ is one, God and man, with neither mixed away into the other. He answered Cyril's twelve curses and was deposed in 449, and at Chalcedon, two years later, he was let back only after he cursed Nestorius by name. He is on the tree also as a historian. His Church History carried the church's story on from Eusebius, and his Religious History kept the Syrian monks he knew, Simeon on his pillar among them.

Much of his life comes from letters he wrote to answer his accusers and to win help from Leo of Rome. He says he was living in a monastery when he was made bishop of Cyrrhus, a small town in northern Syria, "unwillingly". There he built bridges, baths and a channel that brought water to the town, and he led whole villages of the followers of Marcion, a rival church that set aside the Old Testament, into his own. He was stoned for it, he says, many times.

Cyril had sent out twelve curses against Nestorius's teaching, solemn acts that cut a person off from the church. John of Antioch asked Theodoret to answer them, and he did, point by point: "The form of God was not changed into the form of a servant. It stayed what it was, and took the form of the servant." The form of a servant is Paul's phrase for the human life Christ took. God did not turn into a man; he stayed God and took on a human life. Mary is rightly Mother of God, the one who gave birth to God, Theodoret wrote, because of the union, not because she bore God by nature. When the bishops met at Ephesus in 431, he stood with John's side.

Two years later Cyril and John's side made peace. Theodoret found that Cyril's letter agreed with his own faith, but he would not accept that Nestorius had been rightly removed. Some years later, his English translator says, he found a way to accept the curses on Nestorius's teaching, though not, he said, on the man.

In these years he wrote his Church History. It begins, he says, where the history of Eusebius of Caesarea stopped, in Constantine's day, and runs about 105 years from the rise of Arius. He also wrote lives of the Syrian monks, many of whom he says he saw and heard himself. Of Simeon, who stood on a pillar, he writes: "First he ordered it cut six cubits high, then twelve, after that twenty-two, and now thirty-six. For he longs to fly up to heaven, and to be set free from this life on earth." A cubit is the length of a forearm.

In 448 an order from the emperor kept Theodoret in Cyrrhus, though no charge was published and no accuser appeared. Then the council of 449 at Ephesus, led by Dioscorus of Alexandria, deposed him in his absence. Its own acts, his opponents' record, say he had written against Cyril, whom they called their blessed father, and they ask the emperors to burn his books. Theodoret wrote to Leo that he had been condemned without a trial.

At Chalcedon, in 451, bishops of Egypt and Palestine shouted to cast out "the teacher of Nestorius". At a later session, the council's acts record, the bishops stood before the altar rails and demanded that he curse Nestorius. He asked for his papers to be read, but they wanted nothing read. He offered to condemn Nestorius and Eutyches together, with his faith explained, and they shouted for a plain curse. When he said he could not speak without setting out his faith in full, they cried that he was a heretic. Then he said the words, "Anathema to Nestorius", a curse on his friend, and he was given back his see.

About 140 years later Evagrius, a Greek historian, wrote that Eusebius, Sozomen, Theodoret and Socrates had left "a most excellent record", and he set out to carry it on. A Syriac chronicle from the churches that refused Chalcedon also begins where Eusebius, Socrates and Theodoret had stopped. Writers on both sides of the split over Chalcedon took up the story where he had left it. Yet that same chronicle calls his friends "the Nestorian party", and says he won Leo over with a bribe. In 553 a council at Constantinople condemned what he had written against Cyril.

To Leo he had written that in all his years as bishop he had gained not a house, not a piece of ground, not a coin, "not a tomb". A later writer, Gennadius, says only that he died in the reign of the emperor Leo. The year is not known.
