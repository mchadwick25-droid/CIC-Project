About 413, a monk named Pelagius wrote to Demetrias, a young Roman noblewoman who had vowed herself to Christ and given up marriage. God made human nature good and free to choose, he told her, and her birth and wealth were her family's. "But spiritual riches no one can give you except yourself. In these, then, you are rightly praised. In these you are rightly set above others. They cannot exist except from you and in you."

A few years later Augustine of Hippo, writing with a fellow bishop, read the book. Without naming its author, they asked her mother who had written it, and warned her. "'These good things can only be in you': that was very well and very truly said. That is clearly food. But when he says 'only from you', that is wholly poison." Goodness might live in her, they meant, but did not come from her.

Pelagius, a monk from Britain who taught at Rome, held that God made human nature able, by God's gift, to choose the good and to live without sin. Bishops in Palestine cleared him in 415. But Augustine answered him book by book, and in 418 bishops in North Africa and the bishop of Rome condemned him over grace, God's help freely given. The teaching that bears his name was ruled out of the church.

He lived long at Rome, where a younger man, Caelestius, became his follower. About 411 he crossed to Africa, then hurried on across the sea, to Palestine. Augustine answered a book of his on nature, but left his name out, he says, to stay on good terms. Apart from Pelagius's own letter and answers, his story comes to us mostly through the men who wrote against him.

In December 415, two bishops from Gaul charged him in writing but could not come. Fourteen Greek-speaking bishops heard him through an interpreter, at a town called Diospolis, the old Lydda. He read out letters in his favor, Augustine's among them. He said the statements drawn from Caelestius were not his, and condemned them. Yet a man can be without sin, he said, and keep God's commandments if he wishes. The bishops declared him a member in good standing of the church. Augustine, reading the record, granted that the judges were right on what they heard, but to him Pelagius did not appear cleared.

Then Africa and Rome closed in. Augustine reports, as something said, that followers of Pelagius had beaten the monks and nuns in Jerome's care at Bethlehem, killed a deacon and burned their houses. Early in 417, at the African bishops' urging, Innocent, the bishop of Rome, condemned the Pelagians, as his followers came to be called.

Pelagius wrote to Innocent that he always added the help of God's grace. The next bishop of Rome, Zosimus, had letters from him read aloud. In September 417 he asked the Africans, "Is there any passage in which God's grace or help has been left out?" Some who heard them, he wrote, could barely hold back their tears that men of such complete faith had been slandered. For a few months, Rome itself took his side.

It did not last. On 30 April 418 the emperor ordered Caelestius and Pelagius driven from Rome, and anyone spreading their teaching tried and, if convicted, exiled. Pelagius was then in the East. The next day some two hundred African bishops met at Carthage. God's grace, they ruled, does not only forgive past sins, and does not only show us what to do: it helps us do it. Rome, under Zosimus, condemned him too, Augustine says. Reading the letter to Demetrias again that year, Augustine wrote that it had almost persuaded him. But Pelagius, he judged, made grace mean teaching, forgiveness and Christ's example, and never help to the will itself.

The sentence held. By 429, a chronicler of the time says, a Pelagian was spreading the teaching in Britain, and Germanus of Auxerre was sent against it. About 430 John Cassian, a monk in Gaul, charged that the bishop of Constantinople taught what the Pelagians had taught before him. In 431 the council at Ephesus wrote to Rome upholding the sentence on the Pelagians, Pelagius among them.

The sources we have do not say where or when Pelagius died. But his letter to Demetrias outlived him. It long passed as Jerome's, and was printed among the works of Jerome, who had written against the Pelagians.
