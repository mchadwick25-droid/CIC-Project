# Pelagius: outline

Source lines refer to `Source_Sheet.md`'s file short names (PL30, PL33, CSEL57, NPNF105, AVELL, MANSI4, PROSPER, NPNF214, NPNF211, NPNF206, MERC, BEDE-HE, CONST). Words in quotation marks inside the beats are the sources' wording, for checking; only the three numbered quotes below are quoted in the story.

- **The essence:** Pelagius, a monk from Britain who taught at Rome, held that God made human nature able to choose the good and to live without sin, and wrote to the young Demetrias on how to live it. Bishops in Palestine cleared him in 415, but after Augustine of Hippo answered him book by book, bishops in Africa and the bishop of Rome condemned him in 418, and the teaching that bears his name was ruled out of the church.
  - Britain: PROSPER 53725; MERC 7005. Widely Accepted. Monk: NPNF105 10263. Documented. Long at Rome: NPNF105 11614, 10285. Documented.
  - Able to be without sin: his own answer, NPNF105 10381; nature's good measured by its maker, PL30 204-206 / PL33 80912-80913. Documented.
  - Cleared 415: NPNF105 10270. Condemned 418: NPNF214 36418-36421; NPNF105 10621; PROSPER 53876-53878. Documented. "Bears his name": PROSPER 53725 "dogma nominis sui". Documented.
- **Also told:**
  1. What the letter says: first show what human nature can do; God made it good and free to choose; her birth and wealth are her family's, but spiritual riches come only from herself (PL30 168-171, 811-817). He held the power itself was God's gift (NPNF105 10388), and said he always added the help of God's grace (NPNF105 11255). Documented.
  2. How his opponents read it: Augustine and Alypius called "in you" food and "from you" poison (PL33 62421-62424; CSEL57 4512-4515); Augustine later judged that by grace Pelagius meant teaching, forgiveness of past sins and Christ's example, not help within the will (NPNF105 11236, 11255). Contested (what he meant).
  3. Diospolis: he disowned Caelestius's statements and was declared in good standing; Augustine accepted the judges' good faith but said Pelagius was not cleared (NPNF105 10263, 10270, 9486, 10277). Documented.
  4. Rome's turn: in 417 Zosimus had his letters read aloud and asked whether any passage left out God's grace (AVELL 11222-11230); in 418 the emperor ordered him and Caelestius driven from Rome, some two hundred African bishops set out what grace does, and Rome condemned him (MANSI4 44536-44538; NPNF214 36504-36527; NPNF105 10621). Documented.
  5. What came of it: the council at Ephesus upheld the sentence (NPNF214 18748-18753); John Cassian, in Gaul, still named the error after him (NPNF211 48715, 48740); by 429 his teaching was in his home island (PROSPER 54151-54156); and his letter survived only as Jerome's (PL30 3-4, 43-45). Documented.
  The sources hold all of this. They do not hold his birth, his death, or what became of him after 418 (Source sheet S4, E15): the story says so.

- **Setting:**
  - Time: the early 400s to 418; the life we can follow runs from Rome before 411 to the condemnations of 418. "In the early 400s" (card); Warfield's "about the opening of the fifth century" and Fremantle's 409 differ (NPNF105 878; NPNF206 3333).
  - Places: Rome (NPNF105 11614); Africa: Hippo (NPNF105 10286); Carthage, for the council of 418 (NPNF214 36421); Palestine: Jerusalem and Diospolis, "the ancient Lydda" (NPNF105 905, 2027, 10270); Bethlehem (NPNF105 10590). Spoken: "Rome", "North Africa", "Palestine", "a town called Diospolis, the old Lydda".
  - Tradition: the Pelagians (census `pelagianism`, informal name "The Pelagians"). Spoken: "the Pelagians, as his followers came to be called".
  - Britain: Prosper, a chronicler of the time, "Pelagius Britto" (PROSPER 53725); Mercator, "gente Britannum" (MERC 7005). Spoken: "from Britain".
- **Words to explain:**
  - grace: "God's help, freely given". First at the essence or the opening.
  - vowed virgin (Demetrias "virginem Christi", PL30 105-107): "she had vowed herself to Christ and given up marriage". PL30 123-124 holds that she gave up heirs of noble blood for it.
  - monk: known; add once that he was not a priest, "a layman" in Zosimus's word (AVELL 11277).
  - synod: say "a meeting of bishops".
  - "in the communion of the Catholic Church" (NPNF105 10270): "a member in good standing of the church".
  - heresy, heretic: "a teaching the church condemned" / "a teacher the church condemned".
  - Pelagians: "his followers, named after him".
  - free will, free choice: "the power to choose".
- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker.
- **Sibling:** none.
- **Openings:**
  1. (Planned.) About 413, from Palestine, a monk wrote to Demetrias, a young Roman noblewoman who had just vowed herself to Christ; her mother had asked him to (PL30 105-111, 119-120, 157-160; NPNF105 11255 "when I was in the East"; NPNF206 3350 "413. Pelagius in Palestine."). Her birth and wealth, he told her, belonged to her family, not to her: Quote 1. A few years later Augustine, writing with a fellow bishop, Alypius, read the book and, without naming its author, asked her mother who had written it, and warned her: Quote 2 (PL33 62406-62409, 62421-62424; CSEL57 4484-4486, 4504-4507). The name Pelagius comes in with the essence. This opening reaches ahead; the warning to Juliana is not told again.
  2. (Alternative.) Diospolis, December 415: an accused monk stands before fourteen Greek-speaking bishops, his accusers absent through illness, and reads out friendly letters from bishops in his favor, one of them from Augustine, the man who would write against him (NPNF105 9491, 10276, 10464). If used, Beat 3 becomes this opening, and the letter and Quotes 1 and 2 move to a beat between Beats 2 and 3, as a scene set at Demetrias's vow, with Augustine's warning told there.
- **Story beats** (after the hook and the essence, in time order):
  1. **Rome, before 411.** He lived at Rome a long time; Augustine first heard his name there, with great praise of him. He wrote short notes on Paul's letters. A younger man, Caelestius, became his follower, his disciple in Augustine's word. Reason: a monk's teaching on how to live; that is what his letter later sets out. NPNF105 10285, 4627, 11614; PROSPER 53726. Documented. Arrival date: Contested (setting).
  2. **Africa to Palestine, 411 to 415.** About 411 he crossed to Africa; Augustine glimpsed him once or twice, while busy preparing for a great debate with a rival church, and he hurried away across the sea, to Palestine. Caelestius stayed and was condemned by African bishops. A book of Pelagius's on nature reached Augustine through two of his former pupils. Augustine answered it, but left Pelagius's name out, he says, to stay on good terms with him. NPNF105 10286-10287, 10294; date Warfield NPNF105 880. Documented; date Widely Accepted. Do not add Warfield's "fleeing from Alaric" as a cause.
  3. **Diospolis, December 415.** Two bishops from Gaul charged him in writing, but could not come, one of them gravely ill. Fourteen Greek-speaking bishops heard him through an interpreter. He read out letters in his favor, Augustine's among them. He said the statements drawn from Caelestius were not his, and condemned them; he said a man can be without sin and keep God's commandments if he wishes, and that God is the helper of free will whenever it chooses good. The bishops declared him a member in good standing of the church. Augustine, reading the record, says the judges were right on what they heard, but that to him Pelagius did not appear cleared of the charges. Meaning: the first verdict on his teaching went his way. NPNF105 9491, 9524, 10263, 10270, 10276, 10277, 10381, 10464; date NPNF105 905, 10500. Documented; date Widely Accepted.
  4. **416 to early 417: Africa and Rome close in.** Augustine reports, as something said, that followers of Pelagius had beaten the monks and nuns in Jerome's care at Bethlehem, killed a deacon and burned their houses. African bishops met again and wrote to the bishop of Rome, Innocent, who condemned the Pelagians early in 417. NPNF105 10590; AVELL 10875-10879; PROSPER 53816-53818; NPNF105 10621; Warfield NPNF105 920. Documented; the Bethlehem attackers: Inferential-Thin (report only; the tradition entry `pelagianism` rates it the same).
  5. **Rome, September 417: Zosimus.** Innocent died, and Zosimus followed him (PROSPER 53814; NPNF203 42984; Warfield NPNF105 920). Zosimus received letters from Pelagius setting out his faith and had them read aloud. Zosimus wrote to the Africans that some who heard them could barely hold back their tears that men of such complete faith had been slandered: Quote 3. He called Pelagius a layman of long service to God, and turned on his accusers. Meaning: Rome itself, for a few months, took his side. AVELL 11210-11241, 11233-11278; MANSI4 35644-35656, 35693. Documented.
  6. **418: condemned.** On 30 April the emperor, from Ravenna, ordered Caelestius and Pelagius driven from Rome, and anyone found spreading their teaching to be tried and, if convicted, sent into exile. The next day some two hundred African bishops, meeting at Carthage, ruled that God's grace does not only forgive past sins or show what to do, but helps a person to do it. Rome, now under Zosimus, condemned Pelagius and his teaching, Augustine says. In a new book that year Augustine read again the letter to Demetrias. It had almost persuaded him, he writes, but he judged that, in it and in his other books, Pelagius made grace mean teaching, the forgiveness of past sins and Christ's example, and nowhere help to the will itself (NPNF105 11228-11229, 11236, 11255-11256, 11260-11275). Meaning: the West defined grace against him. After 418 no source held here follows him. MANSI4 44395, 44426-44427, 44536-44538, 44559-44560, 44571-44573; NPNF214 36418-36421, 36504-36506, 36522-36527; PROSPER 53876-53878; NPNF105 10621, 11614, 11236, 11255. Documented; what he meant by grace: Contested.
- **What came of them** (heaviest first):
  1. **A condemned teaching under his name, and the sentence held.** The council at Ephesus in 431 wrote to Rome that it upheld the deposition of the Pelagians, naming Caelestius, Pelagius and Julian among them (narrator's words). Two centuries on, a letter from Rome to the Irish churches, kept by Bede, still warned that the Pelagian teaching was springing up again (card only). NPNF214 18748-18753; BEDE-HE 5431-5440. Documented. What Augustine and the African bishops **defended**: grace as God's help inside each act of the will (NPNF105 11229, ch. 37; NPNF214 36522-36527).
  2. **Augustine's long answer.** The books he wrote against him (NPNF105 contents). How the West used those books is `augustine-of-hippo`'s story: a clause here, no more.
  3. **Gaul kept arguing, in his name.** About 430 John Cassian, a monk of Marseilles, attacked the bishop of Constantinople whom Ephesus would depose, saying he taught the same thing the Pelagians had said before him (narrator's words). NPNF211 48715, 48740. Documented.
  4. **Britain.** By 429, a chronicler says, a Pelagian named Agricola was spreading the teaching in the British churches, and Germanus of Auxerre was sent against it. PROSPER 54151-54156. Documented.
  5. **His own words survived, under another name.** The letter to Demetrias long passed as Jerome's and was printed among his works; Bede thought it Julian's; Augustine's quotation of Pelagius's letter to Innocent shows it was his. PL30 3-4, 43-45, 68-69, 73-81; NPNF105 720. Documented. This is the ending.
  What Pelagius **defended**: that God made human nature good and free, that a sinner is himself at fault, and that he never left out God's help (NPNF105 9524, 11255; PL30 204-206).
  Who keeps him today: no held source says. Leave it out.

In his ergo jure laudanda es: in his merito caeteris praeferenda es: quae nisi ex te, et in te esse non possunt."). What stands behind it: an original in a damaged print (OCR), confirmed by two more held prints of the same words as Augustine quotes them (PL33 62417-62419; CSEL57 4493-4496).
     Rendering: "But spiritual riches no one can give you except yourself. In these, then, you are rightly praised. In these you are rightly set above others. They cannot exist except from you and in you."

  2. **Augustine and Alypius, Letter 188, to Juliana, Demetrias's mother.** Rendered from the Latin, CSEL57 4513-4515 and PL33 62422-62424 ("quod dictum est: 'Non possunt esse ista bona nisi in te', optime et verissime dictum est; iste plane cibus est; quod uero ait 'non nisi ex te', hoc omnino uirus est"). What stands behind it: an original in two damaged prints, each confirming the other (PL33 reads "ex u\", CSEL57 "ex te").
     Rendering: "'These good things can only be in you': that was very well and very truly said. That is clearly food. But when he says 'only from you', that is wholly poison."

Plain sense for the narrator to give alongside: goodness may live in her, but it does not come from her; Augustine goes on that a vowed virgin is adorned only by her bridegroom's gifts (PL33 62425-62428; CSEL57 4515-4518). - **Connection to carry into the story:** Pelagius made the Western church say what grace is. His letter told a young woman that her spiritual riches came "from you and in you"; Augustine answered that "from you" was poison. In 418 the African bishops turned that quarrel into rulings: God's grace does not only forgive past sins, and does not only show us what to do; it helps us do it (NPNF214 36504-36506, 36522-36527). Those are exactly the two things Augustine said Pelagius meant by grace (NPNF105 11236, 11255). The listener needs to hear that the rulings answer his reading point by point, so that the condemnation is not a bare verdict; the writer says it through what the bishops ruled, not as "this matters because". Then show that the sentence held: Ephesus upheld it in 431, and Cassian in Gaul still used his name for the error.
- **Ending:** Close on the letter, the opening's image seen again. No source held here tells where or when he died (Source sheet S4). His letter to Demetrias outlived him: it long passed as Jerome's, and was printed among the works of Jerome, who had written against the Pelagians; an English monk three centuries later took it for Julian's; and his own words to the bishop of Rome, kept in Augustine's book, show it is his. PL30 3-4, 43-45, 68-69, 73-81; NPNF105 720, 11255.

- **Who tells it:**
  - Pelagius himself: the Letter to Demetrias (about 413); his answers at Diospolis and Jerusalem, kept in the synod's record and in John of Jerusalem's account, as Augustine quotes them (415); his letter to Innocent, as Augustine quotes it (417). Credited beside Quote 1, the letter's teaching, his disowning of Caelestius's statements, and his claim to add God's grace.
  - Augustine of Hippo: his own time, his chief opponent; nearly everything else comes through him. Credited beside Quote 2 (with Alypius), the Diospolis judgment, the Bethlehem report, the 418 reading of grace. The story says once, in a sentence about Augustine, that apart from Pelagius's letter and answers, his story comes to us mostly from the men who wrote against him.
  - Zosimus: his own time, his defender in 417, then his judge. Credited beside Quote 3 and the tears.
  - The council records: Diospolis (via Augustine), Carthage 418 (canons), the emperor's order of 30 April 418, Ephesus 431 (its letter to Rome). Their texts are named as theirs.
  - Prosper of Aquitaine: his own time, Augustine's partisan: "a chronicler of the time" for Britain and 429.
  - John Cassian: his own time, Gaul: credited beside his charge that the Constantinople bishop repeated the Pelagians, given in the narrator's words.
  - The editors (Warfield, Holmes, Fremantle, the Migne editors) give dates and the letter's printing history; no editor's judgment is carried.
  - The doubt that is itself the story: Diospolis, where the bishops cleared him and Augustine says he was not cleared. Beat 3 tells it as a scene.
- **Do not say:**
  - "Pelagius denied grace" or "taught that people can be good without God". Say: he held that the power to choose good was God's gift and said he always added God's help; Augustine judged that by grace he meant teaching, forgiveness and Christ's example (NPNF105 10388, 11255, 11236).
  - "Augustine wrote to Demetrias's mother" alone. Say: Augustine, writing with a fellow bishop (Alypius). And the letter does not name the author; it asks her who wrote the book (PL33 62406-62409). Do not say he did not know: not "Augustine attacked Pelagius's letter by name".
  - "Pelagius was a priest". Say: a monk; Zosimus calls him a layman (AVELL 11277).
  - "a Welshman", "Irish", "a Scot", "Morgan". Say: from Britain (PROSPER 53725; MERC 7005).
  - "The synod accepted his teaching" or "acquitted Pelagianism". Say: it declared him a member in good standing after he condemned the statements he said were not his (NPNF105 10263, 10270).
  - "He tricked the bishops" or "lied". That is Warfield's and, in part, Augustine's suspicion. Say: Augustine says he does not appear to have been cleared (NPNF105 10277).
  - "Pelagius's followers burned Jerome's monastery" as fact. Say: Augustine says they "are said" to have done it; the victims did not name the man (NPNF105 10590; AVELL 10878). Never "Pelagius ordered".
  - "Zosimus declared him orthodox" in quote marks. That is Warfield's word. Say: Zosimus wrote that men of such complete faith had been slandered (AVELL 11227-11228).
  - "He was banished" or "expelled from Rome" as a thing done to him in person. Say: the emperor ordered him driven from Rome (MANSI4 44536-44538); he was then in the East.
  - "two hundred and fourteen bishops" as settled. Say: some two hundred (Prosper counts 214; Warfield "about two hundred").
  - "The canons of Ephesus condemned Pelagius". Say: the council's letter to Rome upheld the sentence on the Pelagians, Pelagius named among them; its canons name Caelestius (NPNF214 18750, 17745).
  - "semi-Pelagian" for Cassian or Gaul. That is the editors' label.
  - "Rufinus of Aquileia taught him". Mercator's Rufinus is "a Syrian", a different man (MERC 6998-7005); and this story leaves that claim out.
  - "He died in exile", "in Egypt", any death. No held source.
  - "The letter was forged under Jerome's name". Say: it long passed as Jerome's and was printed among his works (PL30 3-4, 43-45).
  - "nun" for Demetrias. Say: a young noblewoman who had vowed herself to Christ.
  - Augustine's "almost persuaded" in quote marks. It is not one of the three quotes; give it in the narrator's words.
  - "Pelagius denied original sin" or "said babies are born without sin". Those are Caelestius's propositions, which Pelagius disowned at Diospolis (NPNF105 10263). The story does not carry them (Leave out).
  - "Augustine debated Pelagius" or "the two men met and argued". Say: Augustine glimpsed him once or twice in Africa, and they never discussed the question (NPNF105 10285-10287).

  - Jerome's insults, "Scots porridge" and "hound of Albion": held only in an editor's summary, and Jerome does not name the man. Card, as the editor's.
  - Warfield's "farce" and "disingenuousness": an editor's verdict.
  - The disputed letter boasting that fourteen bishops approved that a man can "easily" keep God's commandments: Augustine doubts it is his (NPNF105 10381). Card.
  - Caelestius's propositions on Adam and infants, and his own trials: his story, and `paulinus-of-milan`'s.
