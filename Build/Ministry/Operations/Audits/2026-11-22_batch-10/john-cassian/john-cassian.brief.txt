Two monks of southern Gaul wanted more of the Egyptian desert. One wanted his monks taught its rules; the other longed to sail there, leaving a land frozen, as John Cassian put it, with the cold of Gaul. Cassian, who had lived among those monks, wrote seven Conferences for the two, so that one need not make so risky a journey.

The books made the journey instead. At Marseilles, Cassian carried Egypt's teaching into Latin for the monks of the West. His Conferences were talks with the old monks of the desert. Later, Benedict's Rule named the Conferences first for the monks' evening reading. But his thirteenth Conference, on grace, God's free help, and free will, our own power to choose, drew an attack from Prosper of Aquitaine.

Most of his early life comes from his own books. As a young man he joined a monastery at Bethlehem with his friend Germanus. The two sailed to Egypt, promising a quick return, but stayed seven years, went back to keep their word, then returned to the desert of Scete.

At Constantinople, Bishop John Chrysostom made him a deacon, in the word of Gennadius, a later priest of Marseilles. When John was sent into exile, Germanus and Cassian carried his clergy's letters to the bishop of Rome. They wrote that bishops had plotted and soldiers helped drive their bishop out.

At Marseilles, Cassian became a priest and founded two monasteries, one for men and one for women. For a bishop whose province had none, he wrote the Institutes, on the dress, prayers and psalms of the Egyptian houses. Eight of its books give the eight chief faults, from greed for food to pride, and their remedies.

Then came twenty-four Conferences. In the thirteenth, as Cassian writes him, an old hermit says God gives the first good thought. Yet when God sees a good will stirring, he strengthens it, whether he planted it or it grew from our effort, as with Zacchaeus and the thief on the cross. "So these two, the grace of God and free will, seem opposed to each other. But really they are in harmony."

Prosper told Augustine that many at Marseilles thought Augustine's teaching on God's choosing ran against the Fathers. If God chose before time who would be saved, they said, effort was in vain. The letter does not name Cassian.

When the Conferences were done, Cassian wrote, he had almost decided to fall silent. But Leo, later bishop of Rome, asked him to answer Nestorius, bishop of Constantinople, who would not call Mary the Mother of God. In seven books, Cassian charged him with saying Christ was born a mere man. He tied that error to Pelagius's followers, who, he wrote, said people could live without sin if they liked. With that book, Gennadius says, Cassian made an end of writing and of living. He died at Marseilles, but no year is known.

Prosper wrote a book against "the Conferencer", as its title calls him, defending Augustine. A second title sets it against the thirteenth Conference, On the Protection of God, and names Cassian.

Benedict's Rule names no author for its Conferences, but the title is Cassian's. No old source says Benedict's monks came from his houses; they read his books.

Gennadius, on Cassian's side, took the unnamed attack to be Prosper's, and said some of their views on grace and free will were at odds. Of Cassian's works and Prosper's attack he wrote, "The church of God finds them wholesome, but he brands them as harmful."

Later, Cassiodorus told his monks to read Cassian with care and hear him gladly on the eight faults. But on free will, he warned, Prosper had rightly blamed him.

Cassian had named his own hope when the first Conferences went out. "May we set the men themselves before you, embodied, as it were, in their own rules. And, what matters more, may they be speaking in Latin."
