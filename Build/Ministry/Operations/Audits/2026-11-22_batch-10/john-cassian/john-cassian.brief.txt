In the 420s, two monks of southern Gaul, Honoratus and Eucherius, wanted more of the Egyptian desert. One wanted his monks taught its rules; the other longed to sail there, leaving a land frozen, as John Cassian put it, with the cold of Gaul. Cassian, who had lived among those monks, wrote seven of his Conferences for the two, so that the one need not make so risky a journey.

The books made the journey instead. In his Institutes, a book of rules for monks, and his Conferences, talks with the old monks of the desert, Cassian carried Egypt's teaching into Latin for the monks of the West. But his thirteenth Conference, on God's grace and human will, drew an attack from Prosper of Aquitaine.

Most of his early life comes from his own books. As a young man he joined a monastery at Bethlehem, near the cave of Christ's birth, with his friend Germanus. The two sailed to Egypt, promising their elders a quick return, but Egypt held them. After seven years they went back to make peace for the broken promise, then returned to the desert of Scete.

At Constantinople, its bishop, John Chrysostom, made him a deacon, in the word of Gennadius, a priest of Marseilles writing fifty years later. When John was sent into exile, Germanus and Cassian carried his clergy's letters to the bishop of Rome, telling how soldiers had helped drive their bishop out.

At Marseilles, Cassian became a priest and founded two monasteries, one for men and one for women. For a bishop whose province had none, he wrote the Institutes: the dress, prayers and psalms of the Egyptian houses, and eight books on the eight chief faults, from greed for food to pride. He would tell no wonders. Where Egypt's rules were too hard for Gaul's harsher climate or different ways, he would temper them with the customs of Palestine and Mesopotamia.

Then came twenty-four Conferences, written many years after the visits. His translator judges that we cannot know how closely they keep the elders' own words.

In the thirteenth, as Cassian writes him, an old hermit named Chaeremon says God gives the first good thought. Yet when God sees a good will stirring in us, he strengthens it, whether he planted it or it grew from our own effort. Grace is God's free help; free will, our own power to choose. "So these two, the grace of God and free will, seem opposed to each other. But really they are in harmony."

In 429 Prosper wrote to Augustine that many servants of Christ at Marseilles thought Augustine's teaching on God's choosing ran against the Fathers. The letter does not name Cassian.

Then Leo, Rome's chief deacon and later its bishop, asked Cassian to answer Nestorius, the new bishop of Constantinople, whose teaching on Christ was under attack. In seven books, written the year before the council at Ephesus, Cassian tied that error to the followers of Pelagius, who, he wrote, said people could live without sin if they liked.

Prosper then wrote a book against "the Conferencer", as its title calls him, defending Augustine. By the translator's account, it takes twelve sentences from that Conference, accepts only the first, and never names Cassian. No reply is known. He died at Marseilles, Gennadius says, under the emperors Theodosius and Valentinian. No year is known.

His books went on. A century later, Benedict's Rule has a monk read aloud after supper from the Conferences, or the Lives of the Fathers, or something else that builds up the hearers, and on fast days from the Conferences. It names no author, but these are Cassian's titles. Those monks did not come from his houses; they read his books.

Gaul answered Prosper. Gennadius, on Cassian's side, took the unnamed attack to be Prosper's, and said the two men's views on grace and free will were at odds. Cassian's works, he wrote, Prosper attacked: "The church of God finds them wholesome, but he brands them as harmful."

Cassiodorus, a century and more later, told his monks to read Cassian with care and hear him gladly on the eight faults. But on free will, he said, Prosper had rightly blamed him, so read him there with caution. And Eucherius, later bishop of Lyons, condensed some of his works into one volume.

Cassian had named his own hope when the first Conferences went out. "May we set the men themselves before you, embodied, as it were, in their own rules. And, what matters more, may they be speaking in Latin."
