# Outline: John Cassian (`john-cassian`)

Batch 10, half A. File short names (N211, PET, N203 and the rest) are defined at the top of `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder; lines are that file's own lines.

- **The essence:** John Cassian lived for years among the monks of Egypt. Then, at Marseilles, he wrote down their way of life and their talks in Latin, in the Institutes and the Conferences, the books that carried the desert's teaching to the monks of the West. Later, Benedict's Rule named the Conferences first among the books read aloud to monks in the evening, before night prayer. His thirteenth Conference, on God's grace and human will, drew an attack from Prosper of Aquitaine.
  - Holds: Egypt years, own words (N211 42239-42249; 36779-36790), Documented. Marseilles and the two houses, Gennadius (N203 43244-43246), Documented. Institutes and Conferences, own words (N211 16443-16448; 25945-25952), Documented. Benedict's Rule, ch. 42 (RB 6838-6846), Documented; "later", because the Rule names the Conferences (RB 6838-6846); "a century later" rests on Hunter Blair's "About 535 a. d." (RB 150), modern, to the card; that "Collationes" are Cassian's, Widely Accepted (the Rule names no author). Prosper's attack, Gennadius (N203 43669-43672) and the book's title (MOM 42762-42764), Documented.
- **Also told:**
  - What the Institutes hold: the dress, prayers and psalms of the Egyptian houses, and eight books on the eight chief faults and their remedies (N203 43249-43254; N211 16508-16512). He left out the wonders on purpose (N211 16503-16514; PET 7949-7962), and he softened what was too hard for Gaul, for its harsher climate or its different ways (PET 7996-8003). Documented.
  - What Conference XIII says: grace and free will both hold; God gives the start of good, yet sometimes meets a will already turning to good (N211 37572-37578; 37833-37837; 38028-38034). Documented as text; how far it departs from Augustine, Contested.
  - Constantinople: John Chrysostom admitted him to the ministry; he and Germanus carried John's clergy's letters to Rome after John's exile (N211 55142-55145; N203 43244; PAL 2640-2654; N202 44327-44330). Documented.
  - At Leo's request he wrote against Nestorius, and tied Nestorius's error to the followers of Pelagius (N211 48484-48487; 48713-48718; N203 43268-43270). Documented.
  - How later readers used him: read in Benedict's houses, defended by Gennadius, read with caution by Cassiodorus, kept as a rule in Merovingian Gaul (I1-I5 on the sheet).

- **Setting:**
  - Time: born about 360, by his translator's reckoning (N211 15062); in Egypt between about 380 and 400 (N211 15408-15412); at Constantinople about 400-405 (N211 15412-15427); writing at Marseilles in the 420s (N211 15519-15526, 15568-15569); last known 432 or later (N211 15905-15912). All dates are the editor's, built on Cassian's own hints.
  - Place: a monastery at Bethlehem, near the cave of Christ's birth (N211 19691-19696; 36924-36925); the Nile delta and the desert of Scete in Egypt (N211 36789-36790; 42248-42249); Constantinople; Rome; Marseilles in southern Gaul (N203 43244-43246).
  - Tradition: Gallic Monasticism (census `gallic-monastic-ascetic-christianity`, c. 360-450; spoken name "the monks of Gaul"). Castor's province had no monasteries yet, in Cassian's own words (N211 16433-16435).
- **Words to explain:**
  - Institutes: "his book of rules for monks" or "the rules of the Egyptian houses" on first use.
  - Conferences: "talks with the old monks of the desert".
  - grace: "God's free help".
  - free will: "our own power to choose".
  - presbyter: say "priest".
  - the eight principal faults: "eight chief faults, from greed for food to pride" (N211 15347-15350, Gibson's list from Conf. V; Inst. pref. "of which they reckon eight", N211 16510-16511).
  - Nestorius: "the new bishop of Constantinople, whose teaching on Christ was under attack" (N211 48514-48519, Gibson's note at 48516-48519).
  - Pelagius: "whose followers, Cassian wrote, said people could live without sin if they liked" (N211 48713-48718).
- **Shape:** Monk or ascetic.
- **Sibling:** none. (Links in the batch, each kept to its own job: john-chrysostom, pelagius, council-of-ephesus-431. Live beside it: evagrius-ponticus, leo-of-rome. This story tells Chrysostom only as the bishop who admitted Cassian and whose cause he carried to Rome; Ephesus only as the council the book came just before; Pelagius only through Cassian's own charge against his followers; the eight thoughts only as a clause.)
- **Openings:**
  1. *(Recommended.)* In the 420s, two monks of southern Gaul, Honoratus and Eucherius, wanted more of the Egyptian desert. One of them led a large monastery and wanted his monks taught the desert fathers' rules. The other longed to sail to Egypt, to see those monks for himself and leave a land frozen, as Cassian put it, with the cold of Gaul. Cassian, who had lived among them, wrote seven of his Conferences for the two instead, so that the one need not make so risky a journey. (N211 36721-36737, own words; Documented. Cassian does not say which of the two wanted the journey.) Leads straight into the essence: the books made the journey.
  2. A bishop in Gaul named Castor was building a monastery in a province that had none. He asked Cassian to write down the customs of Egypt. Cassian agreed, but said he would tell no wonders, though he had seen some himself; he would write about correcting our ways. (N211 16433-16448, 16503-16514; PET 7949-7962; own words; Documented.)
- **Story beats:**
  1. **Bethlehem and Egypt** (dates: the translator's, about 380-400; card only, not told). As a young man he joined a monastery at Bethlehem, near the cave of Christ's birth, with his friend Germanus. Longing to grow further in the monk's life, they sailed to Egypt, promising their elders a quick return. Egypt held them: they stayed seven years, went back to make peace for the broken promise, then returned to the desert of Scete. Reason: to learn from the desert monks. Source: own words, N211 36779-36790, 19691-19696, 41027-41030, 42239-42249; dates N211 15408-15412. Documented (own words, no second witness); dates Dominant Modern Reconstruction.
  2. **Constantinople and Rome** (dates: the translator's, about 400-405; card only, not told). At Constantinople, Bishop John, John Chrysostom, admitted him to the ministry (years later he wrote that, though absent in body, he was still there in heart; N211 55142-55145), as a deacon in Gennadius's word. When John was sent into exile, the priest Germanus and the deacon Cassian carried letters from John's clergy to the bishop of Rome. The clergy wrote that soldiers had helped drive their bishop out. They brought a receipt too: valuables in gold, silver and clothing had been left with the city's magistrates, to clear John of the charges against him. Source: own words N211 55142-55145; Gennadius N203 43244; Palladius, an eyewitness at Rome, PAL 2640-2654 (Greek PG47 8194-8195); Innocent's reply naming them, kept by Sozomen, N202 44327-44330. Documented. That this deacon is our Cassian, Widely Accepted.
  3. **Marseilles and the Institutes** (date: the translator's, about 419-426; card only, not told). He became a priest at Marseilles and founded two monasteries, one for men and one for women (Gennadius). For Castor, a bishop whose province had no monasteries, he wrote the Institutes: the dress, prayers and psalms of the Egyptian houses, and the eight chief faults with their remedies. He would tell no wonders. Where Egypt's rules were too hard for Gaul, for its harsher climate or its different ways, he would temper them with the customs of Palestine and Mesopotamia. Source: N203 43244-43254; own words N211 16433-16448, 16503-16514; PET 7949-7962, 7996-8003; date N211 15519-15526. Documented; date Dominant Modern Reconstruction.
  4. **The Conferences** (dates: the translator's, about 426-428; card only, not told; "Then" holds, since Cassian's own preface puts the Institutes first, N211 36737-36740). Then came twenty-four Conferences, talks with the old monks of the desert, in three parts, sent to bishops and monks of Provence, among them Honoratus. He wrote them many years after the visits, and how closely they keep the elders' own words cannot be known: his translator's judgment. Source: own words N211 25881-25898, 36717-36747; N203 43255-43268; doubt N211 15403-15407 (editor); dates N211 15544-15569. Documented; closeness to the real talks, Contested.
  5. **Conference XIII and the doubts at Marseilles** (dates, the translator's: Conference about 426, letter 429; card only, not told). In the thirteenth Conference, in Cassian's telling, the old hermit Chaeremon says God gives the first good thought and the good will. But he also says that when God sees the first stirrings of a good will in us, he strengthens it, whether God planted it or it grew from our own effort; he points to Zacchaeus and the thief on the cross. Then the quote (Q2). Prosper of Aquitaine wrote to Augustine that many servants of Christ at Marseilles thought his teaching on God's choosing ran against the Fathers. Reason: Cassian, by his translator's reading, meant to defend human effort (N211 15684-15691, editor; tell as the translator's view or not at all). Source: N211 37572-37578, 37833-37837, 37995-37999, 38028-38034; GOLD 16932-16936. Dates: 429, N211 15724-15726 (editor: "This fixes the date of the correspondence as 429"), Dominant Modern Reconstruction; Conference about 426, N211 15546-15552 (Part II, "not later than that year"), Dominant Modern Reconstruction. Text Documented; meaning Contested. Prosper's letter does not name Cassian: re-checked in the letter itself, GOLD 16888-17450, which holds no "Cassian" (Gibson's note N211 15721-15724 agrees).
  6. **Against Nestorius** (date: the translator's, 430; card only, not told). He had meant to fall silent after the Conferences, he wrote. But Leo, then archdeacon of Rome (its chief deacon) and later its bishop, asked him to answer Nestorius, bishop of Constantinople (Socrates, N202 20520-20556; "new" rests on Gibson's note N211 48516-48519, to the card). In seven books he called Nestorius's error the same one Pelagius's followers had taught. Source: own words N211 48477-48487, 48713-48718, 48738-48741; Gennadius N203 43268-43270; date N211 15599-15601 (editor). Documented; date Dominant Modern Reconstruction.
  7. **Prosper's attack and his death** (date: the translator's, 432 or later; card only, not told; no "then" tying it after the Nestorius book). Prosper wrote a book against "the Conferencer", as its title calls him, in defence of Augustine; a second title sets it against the Conference "On the Protection of God" (the heading of Conference XIII, N211 37475-37479) and names "Cassiani presbyteri" (MOM 42762-42764). What the book says inside is not told: it is held only in the translator's summary (N211 15774-15785), to the card. No reply from Cassian is known. He died at Marseilles in the reign of Theodosius and Valentinian, Gennadius says, and no year is given. Source: MOM 42762-42764; N211 37475-37479; N203 43270-43272. Attack and its titles Documented; its contents, Dominant Modern Reconstruction (N211 15774-15785, from a text not held), card only; no reply, Inferential-Thin, not told; year of death unknown.
- **What came of them** (ranked, about a third of the story):
  1. **Benedict's Rule.** After supper, the Rule says, the monks sit together and one reads aloud: the Conferences, or the Lives of the Fathers, or something else that builds up the hearers. On fast days it is the Conferences, four or five pages, or as much as time allows. Its last chapter names the Conferences of the Fathers and their Institutes, with Basil's Rule, as tools by which good monks reach virtue. Narrator's words; no quote. The Rule names no author; these are Cassian's titles. RB 6838-6846, 6880-6893, 10409-10412, 10444-10448. Documented; identification Widely Accepted.
  2. **Gaul answered Prosper** *(what he defended: a place for human will beside God's grace)*. Gennadius, a priest of Marseilles (N203 43968-43969: "I Gennadius, a presbyter of Marseilles") writing some fifty years later, took the attack for Prosper's, and said the two men's views on grace and free will were at odds (Q3). N203 43663-43672; MOM 42882-42885. Documented as Gennadius's view; Gennadius is on Cassian's side.
  3. **Read, with a warning.** Later, Cassiodorus told his monks to read Cassian with care and hear him gladly, for his eight chief faults. But on free will, he said, Prosper had rightly blamed him, so read him with caution there. An African bishop had already cleaned his books of their faults. CAS 83928-83951 (damaged print; the Victor sentence confirmed by Gibson's clean Latin, N211 16008-16010). Documented. Narrator's words only.
  4. **Carried on in Gaul.** Eucherius, one of the two he wrote seven Conferences for, later bishop of Lyons (N211 36721; N203 43288-43297), condensed some of his works into one volume (Gennadius, N203 43290-43297). Late in the sixth century, Gregory of Tours tells of a monastery in Gaul that kept Cassian's rule beside Basil's (GT-L 34530-34532; GT-E 22152-22155). Documented.
  - Who keeps him today: no held source says. Leave it out.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** what passed is a way of life and its teaching, in books. Cassian saw the Egyptian houses himself, then wrote their rules and their elders' talks in Latin for new monasteries in Gaul that had none of their own (N211 16433-16448; 25950-25952). The books were the bridge: he wrote so that one of his friends need not sail to Egypt (N211 36733-36737). Those Latin books were then read where Latin monks lived: Benedict's Rule named the Conferences for the monks' evening reading (RB 6838-6846), and Gregory of Tours knew a house that kept Cassian's rule (GT-L 34530-34532). The listener must understand that this is influence through reading, not descent: the record shows later monks reading his books, not coming from his houses. The writer must also make clear that the books were read with a reservation: Cassiodorus kept them but warned against their teaching on free will (CAS 83942-83945), because of the very Conference Prosper attacked. Carry this through what readers did, not in a "this matters because" line.
