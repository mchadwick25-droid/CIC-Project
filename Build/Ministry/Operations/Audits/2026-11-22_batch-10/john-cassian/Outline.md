# Outline: John Cassian (`john-cassian`)

Batch 10, half A. File short names (N211, PET, N203 and the rest) are defined at the top of `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder; lines are that file's own lines.

- **The essence:** John Cassian lived for years among the monks of Egypt. Then, at Marseilles, he wrote down their way of life and their talks in Latin, in the Institutes and the Conferences, the books that carried the desert's teaching to the monks of the West. A century later, Benedict's Rule had the Conferences read aloud to monks every evening. His thirteenth Conference, on God's grace and human will, drew an attack from Prosper of Aquitaine.
  - Holds: Egypt years, own words (N211 42239-42249; 36779-36790), Documented. Marseilles and the two houses, Gennadius (N203 43244-43246), Documented. Institutes and Conferences, own words (N211 16443-16448; 25945-25952), Documented. Benedict's Rule, ch. 42 (RB 6838-6846), Documented; that "Collationes" are Cassian's, Widely Accepted (the Rule names no author). Prosper's attack, Gennadius (N203 43669-43672) and the book's title (MOM 42762-42764), Documented.
- **Also told:**
  - What the Institutes hold: the dress, prayers and psalms of the Egyptian houses, and eight books on the eight chief faults and their remedies (N203 43249-43254; N211 16508-16512). He left out the wonders on purpose (N211 16503-16514; PET 7949-7962), and he softened what Gaul's harsh climate made impossible (PET 7996-8003). Documented.
  - What Conference XIII says: grace and free will both hold; God gives the start of good, yet sometimes meets a will already turning to good (N211 37572-37578; 37833-37837; 38028-38034). Documented as text; how far it departs from Augustine, Contested.
  - Constantinople: John Chrysostom admitted him to the ministry; he and Germanus carried John's clergy's letters to Rome after John's exile (N211 55142-55145; N203 43244; PAL 2640-2654; N202 44327-44330). Documented.
  - At Leo's request he wrote against Nestorius, and tied Nestorius's error to the followers of Pelagius (N211 48484-48487; 48713-48718; N203 43268-43270). Documented.
  - How later readers used him: read in Benedict's houses, defended by Gennadius, read with caution by Cassiodorus, kept as a rule in Merovingian Gaul (I1-I5 on the sheet).
- **Length:** 4 minutes, about 600 words (cap 675). Count of what the story must carry: three quotes, about 55 words; glosses (Institutes, Conferences, grace, free will, the eight faults, Nestorius, Pelagius), about 45 words; credited witnesses (Gennadius twice, Palladius, Innocent's reply, the translator twice, Prosper's letter, Benedict's Rule, Cassiodorus, Gregory of Tours), about 70 words. Seven beats of about 45 words each, about 315; what came of him, about 170. Opening, essence and ending, about 120. Allowing for glosses and credits that sit inside beats, about 600. The record is rich in his own words and in later use; the cut material is named under Leave out.
- **Setting:**
  - Time: born about 360, by his translator's reckoning (N211 15062); in Egypt between about 380 and 400 (N211 15408-15412); at Constantinople about 400-405 (N211 15412-15427); writing at Marseilles in the 420s (N211 15519-15526, 15568-15569); last known 432 or later (N211 15905-15912). All dates are the editor's, built on Cassian's own hints.
  - Place: a monastery at Bethlehem, near the cave of Christ's birth (N211 19691-19696; 36924-36925); the Nile delta and the desert of Scete in Egypt (N211 36789-36790; 42248-42249); Constantinople; Rome; Marseilles in southern Gaul (N203 43244-43246).
  - Tradition: Gallic Monasticism (census `gallic-monastic-ascetic-christianity`, c. 360-450; spoken name "the monks of Gaul"). Castor's province had no monasteries yet, in Cassian's own words (N211 16433-16435).
- **Words to explain:**
  - Institutes: "his book of rules for monks" or "the rules of the Egyptian houses" on first use.
  - Conferences: "talks with the old monks of the desert".
  - grace: "God's free help".
  - free will: "our own power to choose".
  - presbyter: say "priest".
  - the eight principal faults: "eight chief faults, from greed for food to pride" (N211 15347-15350, Gibson's list from Conf. V; Inst. pref. "of which they reckon eight", N211 16510-16511).
  - Nestorius: "the new bishop of Constantinople, whose teaching on Christ was under attack" (N211 48514-48519, Gibson's note at 48516-48519).
  - Pelagius: "whose followers, Cassian wrote, said people could live without sin if they liked" (N211 48713-48718).
- **Shape:** Monk or ascetic.
- **Sibling:** none. (Links in the batch, each kept to its own job: john-chrysostom, pelagius, council-of-ephesus-431. Live beside it: evagrius-ponticus, leo-of-rome. This story tells Chrysostom only as the bishop who admitted Cassian and whose cause he carried to Rome; Ephesus only as the council the book came just before; Pelagius only through Cassian's own charge against his followers; the eight thoughts only as a clause.)
- **Openings:**
  1. *(Recommended.)* In the 420s, two monks of southern Gaul, Honoratus and Eucherius, wanted more of the Egyptian desert. One of them led a large monastery and wanted his monks taught the desert fathers' rules. The other longed to sail to Egypt, to see those monks for himself and leave a land frozen, as Cassian put it, with the cold of Gaul. Cassian, who had lived among them, wrote seven of his Conferences for the two instead, so that the one need not make so risky a journey. (N211 36721-36737, own words; Documented. Cassian does not say which of the two wanted the journey.) Leads straight into the essence: the books made the journey.
  2. A bishop in Gaul named Castor was building a monastery in a province that had none. He asked Cassian to write down the customs of Egypt. Cassian agreed, but said he would tell no wonders, though he had seen some himself; he would write about correcting our ways. (N211 16433-16448, 16503-16514; PET 7949-7962; own words; Documented.)
- **Story beats:**
  1. **Bethlehem and Egypt (about 380-400, the translator's dates).** As a young man he joined a monastery at Bethlehem, near the cave of Christ's birth, with his friend Germanus. Longing to grow further in the monk's life, they sailed to Egypt, promising their elders a quick return. Egypt held them: they stayed seven years, went back to make peace for the broken promise, then returned to the desert of Scete. Reason: to learn from the desert monks. Source: own words, N211 36779-36790, 19691-19696, 41027-41030, 42239-42249; dates N211 15408-15412. Documented (own words, no second witness); dates Dominant Modern Reconstruction.
  2. **Constantinople and Rome (about 400-405).** At Constantinople, Bishop John, John Chrysostom, admitted him to the ministry, as a deacon in Gennadius's word. When John was sent into exile, the priest Germanus and the deacon Cassian carried letters from John's clergy to Innocent, bishop of Rome. The clergy wrote that soldiers had helped drive their bishop out. They brought a receipt too: the church's gold, silver and robes had been handed to the city's officials, to clear John of the charges against him. Source: own words N211 55142-55145; Gennadius N203 43244; Palladius, an eyewitness at Rome, PAL 2640-2654 (Greek PG47 8194-8195); Innocent's reply naming them, kept by Sozomen, N202 44327-44330. Documented. That this deacon is our Cassian, Widely Accepted.
  3. **Marseilles and the Institutes (written about 419-426).** He became a priest at Marseilles and founded two monasteries, one for men and one for women (Gennadius). For Castor, a bishop whose province had no monasteries, he wrote the Institutes: the dress, prayers and psalms of the Egyptian houses, and the eight chief faults with their remedies. He would tell no wonders. Where Egypt's rules were too hard for Gaul's harsh climate, he would temper them with the customs of Palestine and Mesopotamia. Source: N203 43244-43254; own words N211 16433-16448, 16503-16514; PET 7949-7962, 7996-8003; date N211 15519-15526. Documented; date Dominant Modern Reconstruction.
  4. **The Conferences (about 426-428).** Then came twenty-four Conferences, talks with the old monks of the desert, in three parts, sent to bishops and monks of Provence, among them Honoratus. He wrote them many years after the visits, and how closely they keep the elders' own words cannot be known: his translator's judgment. Source: own words N211 25881-25898, 36717-36747; N203 43255-43268; doubt N211 15403-15407 (editor); dates N211 15544-15569. Documented; closeness to the real talks, Contested.
  5. **Conference XIII and the doubts at Marseilles (Conference about 426; letter 429).** In the thirteenth Conference, in Cassian's telling, the old hermit Chaeremon says God gives the first good thought and the good will. But he also says that when God sees the first stirrings of a good will in us, he strengthens it, whether God planted it or it grew from our own effort; he points to Zacchaeus and the thief on the cross. Then the quote (Q2). In 429 Prosper of Aquitaine wrote to Augustine that many servants of Christ at Marseilles thought his teaching on God's choosing ran against the Fathers. Reason: Cassian, by his translator's reading, meant to defend human effort (N211 15684-15691, editor; tell as the translator's view or not at all). Source: N211 37572-37578, 37833-37837, 37995-37999, 38028-38034; GOLD 16932-16936. Text Documented; meaning Contested. Prosper's letter does not name Cassian (N211 15721-15724, editor).
  6. **Against Nestorius (430).** He had meant to fall silent after the Conferences, he wrote. But Leo, then archdeacon of Rome (its chief deacon) and later its bishop, asked him to answer Nestorius, the new bishop of Constantinople. In seven books, written the year before the council at Ephesus, he called Nestorius's error the same one Pelagius's followers had taught. He wrote that Bishop John had admitted him to the ministry, and that though absent in body he was still there in heart. Source: own words N211 48477-48487, 48713-48718, 48738-48741, 55139-55147; Gennadius N203 43268-43270; date N211 15599-15601 (editor). Documented; date Dominant Modern Reconstruction.
  7. **Prosper's attack and his death (432 or later).** Prosper wrote a book against "the Conferencer", as its title calls him, in defence of Augustine and against the Conference "On the Protection of God". By his translator's account it takes twelve sentences from that Conference, accepts only the first, and never names Cassian directly. No reply from Cassian is known. He died at Marseilles in the reign of Theodosius and Valentinian, Gennadius says, and no year is given. Source: MOM 42762-42764; N211 15774-15785, 15905-15912 (editor; Prosper's book is not held); N203 43270-43272. Attack Documented; its contents Dominant Modern Reconstruction (from a text not held); no reply, Inferential-Thin; year of death unknown.
- **What came of them** (ranked, about a third of the story):
  1. **Benedict's Rule.** After supper, the Rule says, the monks sit together and one reads the Conferences aloud, four or five pages, or as much as time allows. Its last chapter names the Conferences of the Fathers and their Institutes, with Basil's Rule, as tools by which good monks reach virtue. Narrator's words; no quote. The Rule names no author; these are Cassian's titles. RB 6838-6846, 6880-6893, 10409-10412, 10444-10448. Documented; identification Widely Accepted.
  2. **Gaul answered Prosper** *(what he defended: a place for human will beside God's grace)*. Gennadius, a priest of Marseilles writing some fifty years later, took the attack for Prosper's, and said the two men's views on grace and free will were at odds (Q3). N203 43663-43672; MOM 42882-42885. Documented as Gennadius's view; Gennadius is on Cassian's side.
  3. **Read, with a warning.** Cassiodorus, a century and more later, told his monks to read Cassian with care and hear him gladly, for his eight chief faults. But on free will, he said, Prosper had rightly blamed him, so read him with caution there. An African bishop had already cleaned his books of their faults. CAS 83928-83951 (damaged print; the Victor sentence confirmed by Gibson's clean Latin, N211 16008-16010). Documented. Narrator's words only.
  4. **Carried on in Gaul.** His friend Eucherius, later bishop of Lyons, condensed some of his works into one volume (Gennadius, N203 43290-43297). Late in the sixth century, Gregory of Tours tells of a monastery in Gaul that kept "the Rule of Cassian" beside Basil's (GT-L 34530-34532; GT-E 22152-22155). Documented.
  - Who keeps him today: no held source says. Leave it out.
- **Quotes** (3):
  - **Q2, rendered:** "So these two, the grace of God and free will, seem opposed to each other. But really they are in harmony."
    Source: Conference XIII.11, N211 38028-38030, spoken by Chaeremon in Cassian's telling. Stands on: English printed before 1931 (Gibson, 1894); the Latin Conferences are not held. Rendered from Gibson's English. Job: the heart of the teaching in his own book's words, before the attack. Not used elsewhere on the tree.
  - **Q3, rendered:** "The church of God finds them wholesome, but he brands them as harmful."
    Full line: "an anonymous book against certain works of Cassianus, which the church of God finds salutary, but which he brands as injurious". Source: Gennadius 85, N203 43669-43671; Latin MOM 42882-42883 (damaged at the verb: "ille intamat nova"). Stands on: English printed before 1931 (Richardson, 1892), with the held Latin confirming all but the damaged words. Rendered from Richardson's English. **Slate reuse (lifted):** used in prosper-of-aquitaine and gennadius-of-marseilles; Gennadius touched both men. New job here: how Gaul answered Prosper's attack, told from the side of the man attacked.
  - **Q1, rendered (for the ending):** "May we set the men themselves before you, embodied, as it were, in their own rules. And, what matters more, may they be speaking in Latin."
    Source: Conferences, preface to Part I, N211 25950-25952 ("setting before you the men themselves incorporated, as it were, in their own Institutes, and what is more to the point, speaking in the Latin tongue"). Stands on: English printed before 1931 (Gibson, 1894); Latin not held. Rendered from Gibson's English; "Institutes" here means the elders' own rules of life, so "rules", not the book's title. Job: his own statement of what he was doing, the essence in his voice. Not used elsewhere on the tree.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** what passed is a way of life and its teaching, in books. Cassian saw the Egyptian houses himself, then wrote their rules and their elders' talks in Latin for new monasteries in Gaul that had none of their own (N211 16433-16448; 25950-25952). The books were the bridge: he wrote so that one of his friends need not sail to Egypt (N211 36733-36737). Those Latin books were then read where Latin monks lived: Benedict's Rule fixed them as nightly reading (RB 6838-6846), and Gregory of Tours knew a house that kept Cassian's rule (GT-L 34530-34532). The listener must understand that this is influence through reading, not descent: later monks did not come from his houses; they read his books. The writer must also make clear that the books were read with a reservation: Cassiodorus kept them but warned against their teaching on free will (CAS 83942-83945), because of the very Conference Prosper attacked. Carry this through what readers did, not in a "this matters because" line.
- **Ending:**
  1. *(Recommended.)* His own hope for the Conferences, Q1: the desert men themselves, set before Gaul, speaking in Latin. Own words, N211 25945-25952.
  2. His own promise to Castor, in the narrator's words: where a rule is kept within what is possible, the same perfection can be reached even where the strength is unequal. Own words, N211 16532-16534; PET 8001-8003.

- **Who tells it:**
  - Cassian himself, in his prefaces and Conferences, written in the 420s and 430: almost all of his life before Marseilles. Credit beside beats 1, 3, 4, 6. The story should say once, inside a sentence, that his early life comes from his own books.
  - Gennadius of Marseilles, about 480, fifty years later, from Cassian's own city and on his side: deacon by John, priest at Marseilles, the two houses, the death, and Gaul's answer to Prosper (beats 2, 3, 7; what came 2, 4).
  - Palladius, a bishop and Chrysostom's partisan, who was at Rome when the letters came (about 408), and Innocent's own reply, kept by Sozomen (beat 2).
  - Prosper, to Augustine in 429 (beat 5); his book against Cassian is known only by its title, Gennadius's report and the translator's summary (beat 7).
  - Edgar Gibson, his Victorian translator (1894): the dates, the doubt on the Conferences, Prosper's twelve sentences. Credit him by role where his reckoning is told.
  - Benedict's Rule (sixth century), Cassiodorus (about 550), Gregory of Tours (late sixth century): later use.
  - The doubt as scene: the Conferences' speakers. Beat 5 says "in Cassian's telling" and beat 4 gives the translator's doubt once.
- **Do not say:**
  - "semi-Pelagian" or "Semi-Pelagianism", as his word or his party's. It is a later name; no held text from his time uses it. Say: Prosper attacked his teaching on grace.
  - "Cassian taught that people save themselves" or "denied grace". Say: he held that grace and free will work together, and that God sometimes meets a will already turning to good.
  - "Chaeremon said ..." as a record of the old man's words. Say "in Cassian's telling" or "as Cassian writes him".
  - "founder of Western monasticism" or "brought monasticism to Gaul". Martin and others had houses before him (N211 15443-15447, editor). Say: he set down the Egyptian rules for new houses in Gaul.
  - "Benedict names Cassian" or "Benedict recommends Cassian". Say: the Rule orders the Conferences read, and names the Conferences and Institutes of the Fathers; these are Cassian's titles.
  - "Prosper named him" or "Prosper called him John Cassian". Say: the book's title calls him "the Conferencer"; his translator says the text never names him directly.
  - "born in Scythia" or "born in Provence". Say: where he was born is uncertain.
  - "studied under Jerome at Bethlehem". The translator rules it out (N211 15073-15078).
  - "a disciple of Evagrius" or "learned the eight faults from Evagrius". Cassian never names him.
  - "ordained priest at Rome" or "by Innocent". Not in any held source; only a guess by the translator.
  - "a monk of Lérins" or "lived at Lérins". Only the late Irish tract says it.
  - "two monasteries" without "one for men and one for women".
  - Castor as "pope". Cassian calls him "papa", a title then used for bishops (N211 16417-16419, editor).
  - "Pontus and Mesopotamia". The held Latin has Palestine and Mesopotamia.
  - "condemned", "a heretic", or "the Council of Orange condemned him". No held primary text says so.
  - Any year of death, or "he died soon after". Say: no year is known.
  - "Eucherius gave up his journey", or which of the two friends wanted to go. The record says only why Cassian wrote, and does not say which friend was which.
  - Cassiodorus said "read him often". He said "read with care, and hear gladly".
  - "Gennadius knew him". He wrote some fifty years later.
  - The receipt as Cassian's own act alone: Palladius gives it to both men ("they").
