Two monks of southern Gaul, Honoratus and Eucherius, wanted more of the Egyptian desert. One wanted his monks taught its rules; the other longed to sail there, leaving a land frozen, as John Cassian put it, with the cold of Gaul. Cassian, who had lived among those monks, wrote seven Conferences for the two, so that one need not make so risky a journey.

The books made the journey instead. At Marseilles, Cassian carried Egypt's teaching into Latin for the monks of the West. His Institutes were rules for monks; his Conferences, talks with the old monks of the desert. A century later, Benedict's Rule named the Conferences first for the monks' evening reading. But his thirteenth Conference, on grace, God's free help, and free will, our own power to choose, drew an attack from Prosper of Aquitaine.

Most of his early life comes from his own books. As a young man he joined a monastery at Bethlehem with his friend Germanus. The two sailed to Egypt, promising a quick return, but stayed seven years, went back to make peace, then returned to the desert of Scete.

At Constantinople, Bishop John Chrysostom made him a deacon, in the word of Gennadius, a later priest of Marseilles. When John was sent into exile, Germanus and Cassian carried his clergy's letters to the bishop of Rome, telling how soldiers had helped drive their bishop out.

At Marseilles, Cassian became a priest and founded two monasteries, one for men and one for women. For a bishop whose province had none, he wrote the Institutes, on the dress, prayers and psalms of the Egyptian houses. Eight of its books give the eight chief faults, from greed for food to pride, and their remedies. He would tell no wonders. Where Egypt's rules were too hard for Gaul's harsher climate or different ways, he would temper them with the customs of Palestine and Mesopotamia.

In the 420s came twenty-four Conferences. In the thirteenth, as Cassian writes him, an old hermit named Chaeremon says God gives the first good thought. Yet when God sees a good will stirring, he strengthens it, whether he planted it or it grew from our effort, as with Zacchaeus and the thief on the cross. "So these two, the grace of God and free will, seem opposed to each other. But really they are in harmony."

In 429 Prosper told Augustine that many servants of Christ at Marseilles thought his teaching on God's choosing ran against the Fathers. The letter does not name Cassian.

Leo, later bishop of Rome, asked him to answer Nestorius, the new bishop of Constantinople, whose teaching on Christ was under attack. In seven books, the year before the council at Ephesus, Cassian tied that error to Pelagius's followers, who, he wrote, said people could live without sin if they liked.

Prosper then wrote a book against "the Conferencer", as its title calls him, defending Augustine. By the translator's account, it takes twelve sentences from that Conference, accepts only the first, and never names Cassian directly. He died at Marseilles, but no year is known.

His books went on. The Rule's last chapter names the Conferences and Institutes of the Fathers as tools for reaching virtue. It names no author, but these are Cassian's titles. Benedict's monks did not come from his houses; they read his books.

Gennadius, on Cassian's side, took the unnamed attack to be Prosper's, and said some of their views on grace and free will were at odds. Of Cassian's works he wrote, "The church of God finds them wholesome, but he brands them as harmful."

Cassiodorus, a century and more later, told his monks to read Cassian with care and hear him gladly on the eight faults. But on free will, he warned, Prosper had rightly blamed him.

Cassian had named his own hope when the first Conferences went out. "May we set the men themselves before you, embodied, as it were, in their own rules. And, what matters more, may they be speaking in Latin."
