In 399 the bishop of Constantinople, the eastern capital, was John, a preacher from Antioch. Not long before, the emperor's chief eunuch, Eutropius, had a law passed that stripped refugees of the right to shelter in churches. Then he fell from favor and fled into the cathedral himself. As he lay trembling under the altar table, John climbed into the pulpit and preached against him. Socrates of Constantinople says some thought John added insult to cruelty. John himself said he spoke to soften the people's hearts toward him, for the church, like a mother, had hidden her enemy under her cloak.

At Antioch John had trained under Diodore, who kept to the literal sense of Scripture, as Antiochene Christians did. There, in a sermon on Matthew, he told his listeners that if they wanted to honor the body of Christ, they must not neglect Him when He is naked.

In 398 Eutropius made Theophilus of Alexandria ordain him. Palladius of Helenopolis, who stood by John to the end, says he moved the bishop's extravagant spending to the hospital.

He rebuked men in office too. Socrates says that turned the tide against him. It is said he was as blunt with the Gothic general Gainas, who asked for a church in the city for the Arians. They taught that the Son of God had a beginning. Theophilus held a grudge, since his own favorite had lost the see to John. Monks Theophilus had driven out of Egypt, in a quarrel over whether God has a human body, came to John for help. Socrates says that when Theophilus heard, falsely, that John had taken them into communion, he resolved to unseat him. In 403 he and his bishops, meeting at the Oak near Chalcedon, cited John to appear. His accusers charged that he had sold church property, insulted the clergy, struck a man and eaten greedily alone. When John refused them as his enemies, they deposed him, Socrates says, for refusing. The people rose, and he was recalled. Public games were held at a silver statue of the empress Eudoxia beside the cathedral. John mocked those who allowed them, and she took this as aimed at her. A second council silenced him for taking back his see after his deposition without a council's leave, and in 404 an imperial decree sent him into exile. In 407, Palladius says, soldiers hurried him toward Pityus, hoping he would die. He died on the way, with his usual words, "Glory to God for all things."

In 438 Bishop Proclus carried John's body back to Constantinople. By this, Socrates says, he brought back to the church's communion the followers who had left it over John's deposition.
