# Priscillian — Review

## The test, from one hearing

- **Who:** Priscillian, a rich, learned layman of Spain who called men and women to give up family, wealth and rank for a strict life given to God, and was made bishop of Abila.
- **What happened:** Bishops accused him. Damasus and Ambrose turned him away. He appealed to the emperor, and at the usurper Maximus's court at Trier a prefect found him guilty of sorcery. He was put to death by the sword in about 385.
- **Why it matters:** His death split the church. His followers kept him as a martyr. His accusers' own side quarreled over it for years and Ithacius was removed. Sixty years later a bishop of Rome praised the sword. His own words came back only in 1885.

All three lines come clear from one hearing.

**Essence and what else is told.** From the census and the slate, the essence is the strict life he preached, his bishopric, the trial at Trier, the death, and the martyr's cult. Also told: his own book to Damasus, Saragossa from both sides, Gratian's order, Damasus and Ambrose turning him away, the appeal, Ithacius's suspicion of fasters, Martin's plea, the court's findings, and the aftermath (cult, Toledo, the bishops' quarrel, Leo, Braga, the 1885 find). All are present with their weight.

**Timing.** The hook is his own call, from the book he carried to Damasus (about 50 words). It leads straight into the essence, which is complete by about word 105. Under 150: holds.

**Order.** After the essence the life runs once, in order: portrait and witness, the call, Saragossa and Abila, Rome and Milan, Trier, the trial, the death, then the aftermath. The opening reaches ahead to Damasus, as a hook may. No event is told twice and nothing jumps back.

**Ending.** It ends on the person: the found book, with his call in his own words again. Holds.

**Names heard once that neither act nor link:** none that lose the listener. Gratian and Euchrotia are each named once, and both act. The load of places is high: twelve of them, among them Emerita, Saragossa, Bordeaux, Aquitaine, Toledo, Braga and Würzburg (see MINOR 2).

**Where I got lost:** nowhere badly. "When his group went to Emerita" takes a moment, until the listener links it back to "the bishop of Emerita" two paragraphs earlier. Nothing reads as a children's book or talks down.

## Source check

I traced every sentence to the outline and checked it against Sulpicius (Roberts's English and Halm's Latin), Schepss's Priscillian, Leo's Letter 15, the Toledo and Braga lines on the Source Sheet, and Schepss's preface. All hold. Points checked closely:

- Quote 1 (Pr 4415-4417): "nec prohibere si quis contemptis parentibus liberis facultatibus dignitate et adhuc et anima sua deum maluerit amare quam saeculum." The rendering is faithful. Its framing, "what he and his friends had always urged in the churches", matches "semper hoc in eclesiis et admonuimus et admonemus" (Pr 4394).
- Quote 2 (SulpL 5890-5891): "mihi tam reos quam accusatores displicere." The rendering is faithful, and it is the slate's listed reuse, used for the job the slate names. Martin's barred line is not used. His plea is paraphrased from SulpL 5901-5905.
- Quote 3 (Leo 3276-3278): the rendering keeps every clause of Feltoe's English, "most of his disciples" included, inside the quote.
- The court's findings are credited to the court and to Sulpicius ("maleficii", SulpL 5914). The story does not say he was the first Christian put to death on a church accusation, as the batch ruling requires.
- "While Martin was in Trier" follows the Latin "quoad usque Martinus Treueris fuit", not Roberts's slip. "Bishop of Abila" is right, not Roberts's "Arles".
- The bishops' quarrel is placed on Sulpicius's own side ("inter nostros", SulpL 6003-6008), as the Do not say list requires.
- Schepss "judged" the treatises Priscillian's (Pr 80-93). The story does not say he proved it.

No factual error found.
