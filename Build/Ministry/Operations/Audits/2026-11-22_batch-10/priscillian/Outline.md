# Priscillian — Outline

File keys and line numbers are those of `Source_Sheet.md` (top of that file). All texts are under `cic/texts/`.

**The essence:** Priscillian, a rich and learned layman of Spain, called men and women to give up family, wealth and rank for a strict life given wholly to God, and was made bishop of Abila. Accused by other bishops, he was tried at the usurper Maximus's court at Trier and put to death by the sword in about 385, and his followers honored him as a martyr. (SulpE 11463-11471; Pr 4365-4366, 4415-4417; SulpL 5736-5741; SulpE 11662-11692; SulpL 5974-5976. Confidence: Documented; the year Widely Accepted.)

**Also told:**
- What he taught, in his own book to Damasus: the ascetic call open to all, his creed, his curse on every heresy, the Manichaeans above all. (Pr 4347-4370, 4394-4417, 4483-4485, 4565-4567. Documented, attribution Schepss's.)
- How the bishops fought him: condemned at Saragossa in his absence, Sulpicius says, though Priscillian denies anyone was; driven out under an order of the emperor Gratian; turned away by Damasus at Rome and by Ambrose at Milan. (SulpE 11502-11564; Pr 4382-4392. Documented, with the Saragossa outcome Contested.)
- The trial: Priscillian's own appeal to the emperor, Ithacius's suspicion of everyone who fasted or read, Martin's plea, the prefect's two hearings and the charge of sorcery, and the deaths. (SulpE 11580-11705; SulpL 5857, 5895-5920. Documented, as Sulpicius tells it.)
- What came of it: a martyr's cult in Spain; bishops quarreling for fifteen years and his accusers cut off; a bishop of Rome, sixty years on, praising the sword that killed him; councils in Spain still cursing his name in 561; and his own writings found in 1885. (See What came of them.)

**Length:** 4 minutes 25 seconds, about 674 words after the Cut first list (721 before); approved ceiling 675 (Mark, 2026-11-23). What the story must carry: three quotes, about 75 words with their framing; plain senses for sorcery (maleficium), usurper, communion and, if used, Manichaeans and ascetic, about 30 words; credited witnesses (Sulpicius with his view of both sides, Priscillian's own book and its 1885 find, the council at Toledo, Leo, the council at Braga, a later chronicler), about 60 words; the two Saragossa accounts side by side and the trial's charges credited to the court, about 70 words. By the count's method (a fact 11, a gloss 8, a witness credit 6, quotes as worded), the outline carries 721 words before the Cut first list and 674 after, with the added gloss for Gnostics. The record is full on both sides and the story is a stand, its cost and a memory the church split over, so it runs past the three-minute aim but inside the ceiling of 675.

**Setting:**
- Fourth century, the 370s and 380s; he rises under Gratian (Pros 53140-53145, dated 379); dies about 385 (JerVir 41626 note; JerEp 2970 gives 386; Hyd 1143-1145 a later year). Say "in about 385."
- Spain, in the Roman Empire's west; his town is Abila (SulpL 5740; JerVirL 61819; Hyd 1133 "Avila"). Spoken: "Abila, in Spain."
- Trier, in Gaul, where Maximus held court after seizing power in Britain (SulpE 11605-11614). Spoken: "Trier." (The census place label spells it Trèves; the live Martin story says Trier.)
- Tradition: The Priscillianists (census informalName). His followers in Spain, and the movement the bishops named after him.

**Words to explain:**
- *ascetic* — a life of fasting, sleeplessness and self-denial for God; gloss once: "a strict, self-denying life."
- *Manichaeans* — followers of Mani, a third-century Persian teacher whom Christians and the empire treated as outside the faith; gloss: "followers of the Persian teacher Mani."
- *Gnostics*: Sulpicius's name for the heresy, taken from older teachers who claimed a secret knowledge (Jerome names Basilides and Mark, JerVir 41630-41631). Gloss once: 'a heresy of secret knowledge'.
- *usurper* — "an emperor who had seized the throne" (SulpE 11605-11607; Jerome's "tyrant").
- *maleficium* — Sulpicius's Latin for the crime the prefect found (SulpL 5914); say "sorcery"; if a gloss is needed, "sorcery and magic" (Hef2 23504, Hefele reporting Bernays).
- *communion* — "the church's fellowship at the Eucharist; to be cut off from it is to be put out of the church's fellowship."
- *martyr* — a grown listener knows it; no gloss.

**Shape:** Martyr. (The other fit, Monk or ascetic, is carried by the opening and beat 1.)

**Sibling:** none. Martin of Tours is live and tells Martin's plea at Trier and the later communion. This story tells the plea in one clause from Priscillian's side and quotes none of Martin's words.

**Openings:**
1. His own words, from a book he brought to Damasus, bishop of Rome, when he went there to clear his name (Pr 4672-4676). He told the bishop what he and his friends had always urged in the churches: Quote 1. Then the essence: the man who wrote it was put to death a few years later. (Pr 4394-4396, 4415-4417; SulpE 11673-11674.)
2. The verdict: at Trier a prefect heard Priscillian twice and found him guilty of sorcery; the emperor ordered his death. A Spanish bishop, a rich layman before, who had cursed sorcerers in his own writings. (SulpL 5913-5920; Pr 3833-3836.) Opening 1 is preferred: it lets him speak first, and opening 2 tells the ending early.

**Story beats:**
1. **The call, and the witness.** A rich, well-born layman of Spain, very learned and a gifted speaker; he could go without sleep, food and drink, and cared little for money (SulpE 11463-11471). Nearly all of this comes from Sulpicius Severus, a writer from Aquitaine in Gaul, writing some years after the deaths (SulpL 6003-6005; "about fifteen years" is Inferential-Thin, from his 'fifteen years'): say so here, once, inside the sentence that brings him in. Sulpicius thought the teaching the heresy of the Gnostics and repeats that Priscillian "was believed" to have practised magic from boyhood (SulpE 11452-11453, 11475-11476); yet he disliked the accusers as much: Quote 2 (SulpL 5890-5891). In Priscillian's own words, he and his friends, after baptism, gave themselves wholly to God, because Scripture says that whoever loves anyone more than God cannot be his disciple (Pr 4365-4368, narrator's words). The same book gives his creed and curses every heresy, the Manichaeans above all, glossed as 'the followers of the Persian teacher Mani' (Pr 4483-4485, 4565-4567, narrator's words). Documented, attribution Schepss's. Nobles, common people and many women came to him; two bishops bound themselves to him by a kind of oath (SulpE 11477-11490). Why it mattered: the bishop of Emerita harassed them "without measure," Sulpicius says, and only fanned the fire (SulpE 11490-11497). Documented, as Sulpicius's portrait and Priscillian's own account.
2. **Saragossa and Abila.** Bishops met at Saragossa. Sulpicius says Priscillian's party did not come and he was condemned in his absence, still a layman (SulpE 11502-11510). Priscillian wrote that no one of his group was accused, convicted or condemned there, and reminded Damasus that Damasus's own letter forbade judging people absent and unheard (Pr 4382-4392). Two bishops then made him bishop of Abila (SulpL 5736-5741). He says that when he and his friends went to Emerita in peace, they were beaten in the church (Pr 4622-4625, one side's account). Saragossa Contested; Abila Documented.
3. **Rome and Milan.** His enemies turned to the emperor's judges, and an order of the emperor Gratian drove all heretics from the cities (SulpE 11526-11541). Priscillian went to Rome; in a cause of faith, he wrote, he preferred the judgment of holy men to the judgment of the world (Pr 4672-4674, narrator's words). Damasus would not even see him, and at Milan Ambrose was just as opposed (SulpE 11561-11564). Then, Sulpicius says, by bribery at court he won back his church (SulpE 11564-11576, credited, hostile). Documented.
4. **The appeal, and Martin's plea.** Maximus seized power in Britain and came to Trier, and the bishop Ithacius accused Priscillian before him (SulpE 11605-11615). At a council at Bordeaux, Priscillian appealed from the bishops to the emperor (SulpE 11624-11625; SulpL 5857). Sulpicius says he did it to avoid being judged by the bishops (SulpL 5857; credited, hostile). Ithacius, Sulpicius says, accused anyone who loved reading or fasting of being Priscillian's disciple (SulpE 11646-11649). Martin, bishop of Tours, begged the emperor not to shed their blood: a bishops' sentence was enough (SulpL 5901-5905). Documented.
5. **The trial and the death.** While Martin was in Trier the case waited (SulpL 5907-5908). After he left, the prefect Evodius heard Priscillian twice and found him guilty of sorcery. Sulpicius says Priscillian did not deny studying obscene teachings, holding night meetings of women, and praying naked: these are the court's findings, and no account from his side survives (SulpL 5913-5917). The emperor ordered death. In about 385 Priscillian was killed by the sword with several companions, among them Euchrotia, a woman of Aquitaine who had taken the party in on her land (SulpL 5785; SulpE 11554) (SulpE 11688-11692; Hyd 1143-1145). Documented.

**What came of them** (ranked heaviest first; about a third of the story):
1. **A martyr.** His followers, who had honored him as a saint, now revered him as a martyr; the bodies went home to Spain for grand funerals; people swore oaths by his name (SulpE 11722-11729, narrator's words, credited to Sulpicius; the tradition's own story already quotes these lines). Fifteen years later, at Toledo, Spanish bishops heard of clergy who had called him a holy martyr (Mansi3 96780-96784). *What he defended, marked:* the right of anyone to leave family, wealth and rank for God (Pr 4415-4417); a hearing before judgment, with no sentence on the absent and unheard (Pr 4390-4392); in a cause of faith, the bishops' judgment sought first (Pr 4672-4674), though he did not refuse a public court (Pr 4668-4669).
2. **His death divided the bishops.** Sulpicius says the quarrel among his own side, the bishops above all, ran fifteen years and could not be calmed (SulpL 6003-6005). Ithacius lost his bishopric (SulpE 11718-11719); a later chronicler says Ithacius and another accuser were cut off from communion for his death (Pros 53318-53319).
3. **The sword became a precedent.** Sixty years on, Leo, bishop of Rome, wrote to a bishop in Spain against the Priscillianists, and praised the rulers who had killed him: Quote 3 (Leo 3273-3278). He called that severity a long help to the church's gentleness (Leo 3282-3286).
4. **Still cursed.** Imperial law named the Priscillianists in 407 (CTh 87374-87380); a council at Braga in 561 cursed teachings it called his, among them that souls sinned in heaven and were cast down into bodies (Bruns 2513-2515).
5. **His own voice back.** For centuries he was known through his enemies. In 1885 a scholar, Georg Schepss, found at Würzburg eleven unsigned treatises and judged them his (Pr 80-93). This is the ending (see Ending).
- *Who keeps him today:* no held source names a living community. Leave out.

**Quotes, 2 or 3 at most:**
1. **Priscillian, Book to Damasus (Tractate II).** Rendered from Schepss's Latin, Pr 4415-4417: "nec prohibere si quis contemptis parentibus liberis facultatibus dignitate et adhuc et anima sua deum maluerit amare quam saeculum."
   *Modern rendering:* "No one should be forbidden, if he despises parents, children, wealth and rank, even his own life, and would rather love God than the world."
   *Stands behind it:* an original in the only held print (Schepss 1889), legible in the OCR with letter misreadings (OCR: "nee", "parenti-T)us", "aiiima", and the codex spelling "malluerit"); to be read against the page image at second reading. *Job:* the opening and the essence, in his own voice: the call he was killed for spreading. Not used elsewhere on the tree.
2. **Sulpicius Severus, Chronicle II.50.** Rendered from Halm's Latin, SulpL 5890-5891: "ac mea quidem sententia est, mihi tam reos quam accusatores displicere."
   *Modern rendering:* "My own view is that the accused displease me as much as the accusers."
   *Stands behind it:* an original in a damaged held print (Halm's OCR reads "senteatia", "dispUcere"), confirmed by Roberts's English of 1894 (SulpE 11642-11643). *Job:* credits the one witness the story rests on, and tells the listener he is hostile to both sides. This is the slate's listed reuse (sulpicius-severus's old text; Priscillian's own old text), lifted for exactly this job.
3. **Leo of Rome, Letter 15 to Turribius, 447.** Rendered from Feltoe's English, Leo 3276-3278: "for even the leaders of the world so abhorred this profane folly that they laid low its originator, with most of his disciples, by the sword of the public laws."
   *Modern rendering:* "Even the rulers of the world loathed this unholy folly. So they cut down its founder, with most of his followers, by the sword of the public laws."
   *Stands behind it:* English printed before 1931 (Feltoe, 1895); the Latin is not held. *Job:* what the church later did with his death: a bishop of Rome praising the execution as right. Not used elsewhere on the tree; Leo's live story does not mention Priscillian.

**Connection to carry into the story:** His death passed into two opposite memories, and both are what the church received from him. His followers in Spain took the bodies home and kept him as a martyr: fifteen years on, Spanish bishops still heard clergy call him a holy martyr (SulpE 11725-11728; Mansi3 96780-96784). His chief accuser, Ithacius, lost his bishopric (SulpE 11718-11719); Martin held that a bishops' sentence was enough (SulpL 5901-5905), and Sulpicius that the bishops should never have handed the case to the emperor (SulpE 11625-11633). Yet sixty years later Leo, bishop of Rome, held up the same execution as rightful help to the church (Leo 3273-3286). The listener needs to understand that the church itself was split over whether a ruler's sword belonged in a church quarrel, and that his death was used on both sides of that question. Tell it through what the followers, the bishops and Leo did, not as a "this mattered because" sentence.

**Ending:**
1. The manuscript. For fifteen centuries the judgment on him hung on his enemies; in 1885 a scholar opened a fifth- or sixth-century book at Würzburg and found eleven treatises with no author's name; he judged they could be no one's but Priscillian's (Pr 80-93). End on him speaking again, the opening's words seen in the found book, without repeating the quote. Preferred.
2. The funerals: the bodies carried home to Spain and buried with great pomp, his followers now calling him martyr (SulpE 11726-11728). Use only if What came of them is placed before the death is told in full, which the time order forbids; so Ending 1 is preferred.

**Who tells it:**
- **Sulpicius Severus**, a Gallic writer and Martin's disciple, writing some years after the deaths ("about fifteen years" is Inferential-Thin, from his 'fifteen years'). Hostile to Priscillian's teaching and to his accusers. Almost the whole life rests on him; a later chronicler, Prosper, briefly confirms the appeal and the trial. Say so once, in beat 1, inside the sentence that introduces him. Credited beside: the portrait, the rumour of magic, Saragossa's sentence, the bribery, the appeal, Ithacius's suspicion of fasters, the court's findings, the martyr's cult and the bishops' quarrel.
- **Priscillian himself**, in a book he brought to Damasus at Rome, known only since 1885 (Schepss's attribution). Credited beside: the ascetic call (Quote 1), his denial of Saragossa, the beating at Emerita, his reason for going to Rome.
- **The council at Toledo**, in about 400: the clergy who called him a holy martyr.
- **Leo of Rome**, in 447: Quote 3.
- **The council at Braga**, in 561: the curses on teachings it called his.
- **A later chronicler** (Prosper, fifth century, by role): the appeal at Bordeaux and the trial at Trier (Pros 53284-53291); the accusers cut off from communion (Pros 53318-53319).
- **Georg Schepss**, the editor, 1889: the find and the attribution.
- The doubt is part of the story at Saragossa: tell it as two accounts set side by side in beat 2.

**Do not say:**
- "He was the first Christian put to death on a church accusation" (or "for heresy", or "the first time a Christian state executed Christians over belief"). Say instead: the verdict was the court's, for sorcery: a prefect convicted him and the emperor ordered his death. The card carries the editors' claim and Bernays's view (as Hefele reports it), each credited (Mark, Decision-Log, "Batch 10 hearts approved", item 5).
- "He confessed to sorcery" or "he admitted magic." Say: the prefect found him guilty of sorcery; Sulpicius says he did not deny studying obscene teachings, night meetings with women and praying naked; these are the court's findings.
- "He practised magic from boyhood." Say: Sulpicius says it "was believed."
- "Women flocked to him" as the narrator's words. Say: many women came to him, Sulpicius says; or "men and women."
- "He was a Gnostic" or "a Manichaean" in the narrator's voice. Say: his enemies called his teaching the heresy of the Gnostics; he himself cursed the Manichaeans.
- "Martin defended his teaching." Say: Martin begged that their blood not be shed; a bishops' sentence was enough.
- "Ambrose met him" or "Damasus heard him." Say: Damasus would not even see him; at Milan Ambrose was just as opposed.
- "He was condemned at Saragossa" as a fact. Say: Sulpicius says so; Priscillian says no one was.
- "Bishop of Arles" (Roberts's slip). Say: bishop of Abila.
- "The trial was delayed as long as Martin lived." Say: while Martin was in Trier.
- "Beheaded with most of his disciples." "Most" is Leo's claim, kept inside Quote 3. Say: with several companions.
- "In 385" as certain. Say: in about 385.
- "The quarrel between his followers and the Catholics lasted fifteen years." Sulpicius's Latin puts it among the bishops of his own side. Say: his own side quarreled for fifteen years, the bishops above all.
- "He had sinful relations with Procula" or any of the rumours about the women: Sulpicius gives it as common talk. Do not say it.
- "A noblewoman" or "a rich widow" for Euchrotia. Say instead: a woman of Aquitaine, on whose land they had stayed.
- "He appealed to escape the bishops" as narrator's fact. Say: Sulpicius says so.
- "Schepss proved the treatises his." Say: Schepss judged them his.
- Do not add irony or a moral to his curse on sorcerers or his appeal to the emperor; if told, tell them plainly and let them stand.
- Leaving out what the book to Damasus teaches beyond the ascetic call. Say also: the book gives his creed and curses every heresy, the followers of the Persian teacher Mani above all (Pr 4483-4485, 4565-4567).

**Leave out:**
- The "first execution for heresy" claim (see Do not say): editors' views only, and contested. Mark for Mark on the hearts page; the census tradition text states it, and the card can carry the editors' views with their names.
- Sulpicius's derivation of the teaching from Marcus of Memphis, Agape and Helpidius: a hostile genealogy; card.
- The rumour about Procula: gossip Sulpicius marks as such; card if anywhere.
- The bribed officials (Macedonius, Volventius) and the tangle of rescripts, beyond one clause on bribery credited to Sulpicius: detail past what the story needs.
- Martin's later visit to Trier, his communion with the accusers and the tribunes for Spain (Dialogues III.11-13): Martin's story.
- Priscillian's curse on sorcerers "with the sword" (Pr 3833-3836): a strong line, but told beside his sorcery conviction it pulls toward irony the record does not draw; card. Not in the story.
- Orosius's report of his teaching on souls and the stars; Jerome's change of tone between 392 and 414; Augustine's Against Lying: card.
- Ambrose's refusal of communion with the bishops who sought deaths: held only as a quotation inside Mansi's note; card, credited as such.
- Toledo's condemnation of his books and the doubt about the professions' date; the Canons on Paul.
