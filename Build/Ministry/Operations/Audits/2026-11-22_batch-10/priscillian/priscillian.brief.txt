Priscillian carried a book to Damasus, bishop of Rome. In it he wrote what he and his friends had always urged in the churches. "No one should be forbidden, if he despises parents, children, wealth and rank, even his own life, and would rather love God than the world."

A rich and learned layman of Spain, Priscillian called men and women to that strict life, and became bishop of Abila. Other bishops accused him, and an emperor who had seized the throne had him tried. In about 385 he was put to death by the sword, and his followers honored him as a martyr.

Nearly all we know of his life comes from Sulpicius Severus, a writer from Gaul, writing some years after the deaths. He describes a well-born, gifted speaker who could go without sleep, food and drink, and cared little for money. Sulpicius called the teaching the heresy of the Gnostics, a heresy of secret knowledge, and repeats that Priscillian was believed to have practiced magic from boyhood. Yet he adds: "My own view is that the accused displease me as much as the accusers."

In his own account, Priscillian and his friends gave themselves wholly to God after baptism, because Scripture says that whoever loves anyone more than God cannot be his disciple. The same book gives his creed and curses every heresy, the followers of the Persian teacher Mani above all. Nobles, common people and many women came to him, Sulpicius says. The bishop of Emerita, he adds, harassed them without restraint, and only fanned the fire.

Bishops met at Saragossa. Sulpicius says Priscillian stayed away and was condemned in his absence, still a layman. Priscillian wrote that no one of his group was accused or condemned there, and reminded Damasus that Damasus's own letter forbade judging the absent and unheard. Two bishops then made him bishop of Abila. 

His enemies won an order from the emperor that drove all heretics from the cities. Priscillian went to Rome: in a cause of faith, he wrote, he preferred the judgment of holy men to the world's. But Damasus would not even see him, and at Milan Ambrose was just as opposed.

Then Maximus seized the throne in Britain and came to Trier, where the bishop Ithacius accused Priscillian before him. At a council at Bordeaux, Priscillian appealed from the bishops to the emperor, to escape their judgment, Sulpicius says. Ithacius, he adds, called anyone who loved reading or fasting Priscillian's disciple. Martin, bishop of Tours, begged the emperor not to shed their blood, since the bishops' sentence was enough.

While Martin was in Trier, the case waited. After he left, a prefect heard Priscillian twice and found him guilty of sorcery. Sulpicius says Priscillian did not deny studying obscene teachings, holding night meetings of women, and praying naked. These are the court's findings; no account from his side survives. The emperor ordered death. Priscillian was killed by the sword with several companions, among them a woman who had sheltered them.

His followers, Sulpicius says, now revered him as a martyr. They carried the bodies home to Spain for grand funerals, and swore oaths by his name. Fifteen years later, Spanish bishops meeting at Toledo still heard of clergy calling him a holy martyr.

The deaths split the other side too. Its bishops above all quarreled for fifteen years, Sulpicius says, and Ithacius lost his bishopric.

Yet sixty years on, Leo, bishop of Rome, wrote to Spain against the Priscillianists, and praised the killing. "Even the rulers of the world loathed this unholy folly. So they cut down its founder, with most of his followers, by the sword of the public laws." In 561 a council at Braga still cursed teachings it called his.

For centuries Priscillian was known only through his enemies. In 1885, a scholar opened an old manuscript at Würzburg and found eleven unsigned treatises. He judged they could be no one's but Priscillian's. Among them was the book Priscillian had carried to Damasus, with his call in his own words again.
