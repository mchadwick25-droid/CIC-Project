Ulfila had been bishop of the Goths for seven years when a persecution drove him out, with many men and women already dead as martyrs. He led a great crowd of believers across the Danube, and they were received with honor on Roman soil, in the reign of the emperor Constantius. His pupil Auxentius, who wrote in his praise and on whom nearly all of this life rests, told it as a new Exodus:

"Through Moses, God freed his people from the power and violence of Pharaoh and the Egyptians. He made them cross the sea, and made sure they would serve him. So too, through this man, God freed those who confessed his holy only Son from the barbarians' land. He made them cross the Danube, and serve him in the mountains, after the saints' example."

Those who confessed were those who held to the faith under persecution, and the barbarians' land was the Goths' country outside the empire. In the middle of the three-hundreds, Ulfila gave the Goths letters and the Scriptures in their own language. To his death he taught them a creed that called the Son like the Father, according to the Scriptures. His opponents called that faith Arian, and the Greek historians who trace the Goths' faith to him disagree on when he took it.

Philostorgius, a historian of the next century, says Ulfila's forebears were Christian captives from Cappadocia, carried off by Gothic raiders, who won many Goths to the faith. Auxentius says he was made bishop at thirty. Philostorgius sets it on an embassy from the Gothic ruler to the emperor's court, where Eusebius and the other bishops with him consecrated Ulfila. A modern historian takes this Eusebius for the bishop of Nicomedia, and places the scene at the court of Constantine's son Constantius.

For seven years, Auxentius says, Ulfila taught a people who had been starved of preaching, and made Christians into true Christians. Then a godless judge of the Goths raised a persecution with tyrannical terror, and it was this that drove the crossing.

Philostorgius says Ulfila invented letters for the Goths, as part of his care for them. He translated all of Scripture except the Books of Kings, Philostorgius adds, because those were stories of war, and the Goths were too fond of war already. Socrates and Sozomen also credit him with the letters and the translation. Fragments of a Gothic Bible survive without the translator's name, but the editor who printed them in 1908 has no doubt they are Ulfila's, made from the Greek.

What Ulfila taught comes to us in his pupil's words. The Father alone is unbegotten, never begotten by anyone, without beginning, and the one true God. The Son is God and maker of all creation, yet he has the Father as his God, and he is like the Father, according to the Scriptures. The Greek word for like, homoios, gives these churches their modern name, Homoian. Nicaea had said the Son is of one being with the Father, sharing his very being, and Ulfila spurned that teaching as a devilish invention.

In 360, by the usual dating, he joined about fifty bishops at Constantinople who confirmed a creed that banned the word "substance" for God. When did he take that faith? It matters to the Greek historians because they trace the Goths' whole faith to that choice. Socrates says many Goths still held it in his own day, and Sozomen says this was why the barbarians on the Danube followed Arius.

Socrates says Ulfila took the creed only in 360, and before that held Nicaea's faith. Sozomen says he signed in 360 out of thoughtlessness, and joined the other side years later, under the emperor Valens, when he came to Constantinople on an embassy for the Goths. It was under pressure, Sozomen says, or perhaps from conviction. Theodoret also puts it under Valens, when a bishop of the emperor's party won him over with eloquence and bribes.

Under Valens, Socrates says, Ulfila taught the subjects of the Gothic chief Athanaric too, and Athanaric punished the Christians, so that many died as martyrs. Sozomen reports a story that Athanaric's men burned those who refused to sacrifice, together with their tents.

After forty years, as Auxentius counts them, the emperor ordered Ulfila to Constantinople for a debate. As he entered the city, his opponents were having second thoughts about the council the emperor had promised, and at once he fell ill. He died there, about 383.

He left his faith in writing to his people, by testament. Of the Father, he wrote, "alone unbegotten"; of the Son, "subject and obedient in all things to his God the Father." He wrote that he had always believed so, and that in this faith alone he made his crossing to his Lord.
