Two small boys, Frumentius and Edesius, were found under a tree, going over their lessons, Rufinus says. Everyone else on their ship had just been killed. The people of that coast spared them out of pity and took them to their king.

Frumentius, from a family of Tyre, grew up a captive at that court and rose to share in ruling it. He gathered the Christian traders there to pray, then went to Alexandria to ask its bishop, Athanasius, for a bishop, and came back as that bishop himself. Rufinus calls the land India. But the emperor's hostile letter, kept by Athanasius, names its city Auxumis, that is Aksum, in what is now Ethiopia and Eritrea. It calls Frumentius its bishop, made so by Athanasius.

Rufinus of Aquileia wrote the story down around 400, and the whole early life rests on him. In the times of Constantine, he writes, a philosopher from Tyre sailed there with two boys, his relatives, whom he was teaching. Coming home, the ship put in at a harbor for water. The people there had heard their treaty with Rome was broken, and by custom they killed every Roman they found.

The king made Edesius his cup-bearer, and gave Frumentius, whom he found sharp and wise, charge of his accounts and records. Dying, the king set them free, leaving his wife and small son as heirs. But the queen begged them, Frumentius above all, to stay and help her rule until her son grew up.

With the kingdom in his hands, Rufinus says, God stirred him to seek out Christians among the Roman traders. He urged them to set up meeting places to pray in, and gave them sites for buildings, so that a Christian seed might grow there.

When the prince came of age, the two handed everything back and came home. Edesius went to his parents at Tyre, and that is where Rufinus's story comes from. "We learned that these things happened so, not from common talk, but from Edesius himself, who told it. He had been Frumentius's companion, and was later made a priest at Tyre."

Frumentius went to Alexandria instead, saying the Lord's work must not be hidden, and urged Athanasius to send a worthy man as bishop. Athanasius answered, Rufinus reports: "What other man like this could we find, with the Spirit of God in him as in you, who could carry it through in this way?" He made Frumentius bishop and sent him back.

When is disputed. Rufinus sets it just after Athanasius became bishop, in 328 by his editor's reckoning. Athanasius's English editor, Robertson, says the story's own timeline rules that out. He puts it no earlier than the end of 346, as the more natural reading of the emperor's letter.

Back as bishop, it is said, Rufinus writes, signs like the apostles' were done through him, and countless people turned to the faith.

That tie to Alexandria was fought over. About 356, with Athanasius driven from his city, the emperor Constantius wrote to the rulers of Auxumis, demanding the same teaching in their churches as in the empire's. "So send Frumentius the bishop to Egypt with all speed." There George, the emperor's bishop in Athanasius's place, would judge him, and restore him only if found fit. The letter named who had made him: "This Frumentius was set in this rank by Athanasius, a man guilty of countless crimes." No source we hold says how the rulers answered, or what Frumentius did.

The church of Aksum kept taking its bishops from Alexandria. By 1681, a German scholar recorded, the Ethiopians counted ninety-five chief bishops in order from him. Three Greek church historians retold Rufinus's account in the next century, Socrates almost word for word, one editor says.

The Ethiopian church's book of saints, written down about a thousand years later, tells his story again, with new names and turns. It says he preached the peace of Christ across Ethiopia, and was called Abba Salama for it, Abba meaning father. And after he had greeted the people of Ethiopia, it says, he died in peace.
