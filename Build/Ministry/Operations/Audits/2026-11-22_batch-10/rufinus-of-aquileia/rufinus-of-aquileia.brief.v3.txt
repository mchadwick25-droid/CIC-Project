Jerome heard, in 374, that his friend Rufinus was traveling among the monks in the far parts of Egypt. He wrote to draw him to Syria, and ended: "A friendship that can end was never real." Within thirty years, a book Rufinus translated had ended theirs.

Rufinus of Aquileia, a monk from northern Italy, spent nearly thirty years among the monks of Egypt and at Jerusalem. Then he came home and put Greek Christian books into Latin. One was First Principles, by Origen, a Greek Bible scholar of the 200s, much admired and by now much attacked. Rufinus left out or reworded the passages he judged corrupt. The other was Eusebius's history of the church, which Rufinus carried on in two books of his own. First Principles and many of Origen's other works survive chiefly in his Latin, his editors note. And the preface to First Principles broke his friendship with Jerome.

Rufinus was living in a monastery at Aquileia when he was baptized, about thirty years before 400, he says. Jerome belonged to the same circle of young monks.

He spent six years in Egypt and later two more, he writes, learning from the desert monks. When the monks there were persecuted, he says, he was jailed and exiled. At Jerusalem he lived beside Melania the Elder, who led a convent. In the 390s he and Jerome took opposite sides in a dispute over Origen, but made peace before Rufinus sailed home.

At home he argued in a short book that heretics had falsified Origen's writings. Then he translated First Principles, Origen's book on God and the created world. Its preface praised, without naming him, a friend who had translated Origen before and pared away what jarred with the faith. He said he followed that method. "Where I found anything in his books against the truth about the Trinity, which he teaches soundly elsewhere, I left it out as not his own. Or I set it down to agree with the rule of faith, which we find him holding to again and again." The rule of faith is the church's summary of its belief.

Jerome's friends at Rome sent him the translation, saying it was falsely translated. Jerome took the preface as aimed at him. Comparing the Latin with the Greek, Jerome charged that Rufinus had made the Trinity sound to put readers off guard against the rest. He made his own literal translation: "In the books On First Principles, I simply set down what stood in the Greek. I did not want the reader to believe what I translated. I wanted him not to believe what you had translated before."

Rufinus told the bishop of Rome that he was no champion of Origen, and had cut only what he believed others added. But the bishop of Rome wrote to the bishop of Jerusalem that the emperors had banned reading Origen. Rufinus was wholly apart from Rome and must answer to God, he wrote, and he neither knew nor wished to know where Rufinus lived. Each wrote a full defense against the other, and the friendship did not recover.

When Alaric and his Goths broke through into Italy, Chromatius, bishop of Aquileia, asked Rufinus to translate the Church History. Chromatius hoped it would help listeners forget their sufferings a little. He did, adding his own two books.

The few Greek fragments of First Principles show, his editors note, that he altered Origen's wording. His History became the Latin West's Eusebius through the Middle Ages, an editor judges. The Greek historian Socrates followed it for his first two books, then rewrote them, judging that a bishop who had suffered in those events deserved more trust.

Rufinus's last work came in Sicily, about 410 by his editor's date. The barbarian had set fire to Rhegium, just across the strait. On quiet nights, he wrote, as a comfort in their troubles, he gathered Origen's sermons on Numbers into Latin. He hoped to add Deuteronomy, he said, if God gave him back his eyesight. He died soon after, his editors judge.
