Jerome heard, in 374, that his friend Rufinus was traveling among the monks in the far parts of Egypt. He wrote to draw him east, and ended on friendship: "A friendship that can end was never real." Within thirty years, a book Rufinus translated had ended theirs.

Rufinus of Aquileia, a monk from northern Italy, spent nearly thirty years among the monks of Egypt and at Jerusalem. Then he came home and put Greek Christian books into Latin. One was First Principles, by Origen, a Greek Bible scholar of the 200s, much admired and by now much attacked. Rufinus left out or reworded the passages he judged corrupt. The other was Eusebius's Church History, the story of the church from the apostles to Eusebius's own day, which Rufinus carried on in two books of his own. First Principles and many of Origen's other works survive chiefly in his Latin, his editors note. And its preface broke his friendship with Jerome.

Aquileia sits at the head of the Adriatic. Rufinus was living in a monastery there when he was baptized, about thirty years before 400, he says. Jerome belonged to the same circle of young monks.

Later he spent six years in Egypt and then two more, he writes, learning from the desert monks. When the monks there were persecuted, he says, he was jailed and exiled. At Jerusalem he lived beside Melania the Elder, who led a convent of women. In the 390s he and Jerome took opposite sides in a dispute over Origen, but they made peace before Rufinus sailed for Italy.

At home he argued in a short book that heretics had falsified Origen's writings. Then he translated First Principles, Origen's book on God and the order of the created world. Its preface praised, without naming him, a friend who had translated Origen before and pared away what jarred with the faith. He would follow it: "Where I found anything in his books against the truth about the Trinity, which he teaches soundly elsewhere, I left it out as not his own. Or I set it down to agree with the rule of faith, which we find him holding to again and again." The rule of faith is the church's own summary of what it believes.

Jerome's friends at Rome sent him the translation, saying the books had been falsely translated. Jerome took the preface as aimed at him, and from here each man wrote against the other. Comparing the Latin with the Greek, Jerome charged that Rufinus had made the Trinity sound so that readers would not be on guard against the rest. He made his own literal translation, and told Rufinus why: "In the books On First Principles, I simply set down what stood in the Greek. I did not want the reader to believe what I translated. I wanted him not to believe what you had translated before."

Rufinus told the bishop of Rome that he was no champion of Origen, and had cut only what he believed others had added. But the bishop of Rome wrote to the bishop of Jerusalem that the emperors had banned the reading of Origen. Rufinus was wholly apart from them and must answer to God, he wrote, and he neither knew nor wished to know where Rufinus lived. Each man wrote a full defense against the other, and the friendship did not recover.

When Alaric and his Goths broke through into Italy, Chromatius, now bishop of Aquileia, asked Rufinus to translate the Church History. Chromatius wanted listeners to forget their sufferings for a while. He did, and added his own two books.

The few Greek fragments of First Principles show, his editors note, that he altered Origen's wording. His History became the Latin West's Eusebius through the Middle Ages, an editor of Eusebius judges. The Greek historian Socrates followed Rufinus in his own first two books. Then, reading the account of a bishop who had suffered in those events, he judged that the sufferer and those who saw what happened deserved more trust, and rewrote them.

Rufinus's last work came in Sicily, about 410 by his editor's date. The barbarian had set fire to Rhegium, just across the narrowest strait. On quiet nights, he wrote, as a comfort in their troubles, he gathered Origen's sermons on Numbers into Latin. Only the notes on Deuteronomy were left, he said, if God gave him back his eyesight. He died soon after, his editors judge.
