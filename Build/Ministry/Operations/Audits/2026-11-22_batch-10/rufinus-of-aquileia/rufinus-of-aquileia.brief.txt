Jerome heard that his friend Rufinus was traveling among the monks in the far parts of Egypt. He wrote to draw him to Syria, and ended: "A friendship that can end was never real." Years later, a book Rufinus translated had ended theirs.

Rufinus of Aquileia, a monk from northern Italy, spent nearly thirty years among the monks of Egypt and at Jerusalem. Then he came home and put Greek Christian books into Latin. One was First Principles, by Origen, a Greek Bible scholar of the 200s, much admired and by now much attacked. The other was Eusebius's history of the church, which Rufinus carried on in two books of his own. And the preface to First Principles broke his friendship with Jerome.

He spent eight years in Egypt, he writes, learning from the desert monks. When the emperor turned on the monks there for their creed, he says, he was jailed and exiled. At Jerusalem, a bishop from Cyprus charged that Origen taught souls had sinned in heaven before birth, that the devil would be saved, and that this flesh would not rise. His letter prayed that Rufinus be freed from Origen's heresy, and Jerome put it into Latin for the bishop. Still, the two friends made peace before Rufinus set out for home.

At home he argued in a short book that heretics had falsified Origen's writings. Then he translated First Principles, Origen's book on God and creation. Its preface praised, without naming him, a friend who had translated Origen before and pared away what jarred with the faith. He said he followed that method. "Where I found anything in his books against the truth about the Trinity, which he teaches soundly elsewhere, I left it out as not his own. Or I set it down to agree with the rule of faith, which we find him holding to again and again." The rule of faith is the church's summary of its belief.

Jerome's friends at Rome sent him the translation, saying it was falsely translated. Jerome took the preface as aimed at him. In the Greek, Jerome later wrote, Origen ranked the Son below the Father, the Spirit below the Son. He charged that Rufinus had made the Trinity sound to put readers off guard against Origen's other teachings. He made his own literal translation: "In the books On First Principles, I simply set down what stood in the Greek. I did not want the reader to believe what I translated. I wanted him not to believe what you had translated before."

Rufinus told the bishop of Rome that he was no champion of Origen, and had cut only what he believed others added. He affirmed that this very flesh would rise, and the devil burn forever. But the bishop of Rome wrote to the bishop of Jerusalem that Origen meant to undermine the faith, and that the emperors had banned his books. Rufinus was wholly apart from Rome and must answer to God, he wrote, and he neither knew nor wished to know where Rufinus lived. Rufinus and Jerome each wrote a full defense against the other.

When the Goths broke through into Italy, Chromatius, bishop of Aquileia, asked Rufinus to translate the Church History. Chromatius hoped it would help listeners forget their sufferings a little. Rufinus did.

A later church writer wrote that Rufinus opened to the Latin church the greater part of Greek writing, this History among it. The Greek historian Socrates followed it, then rewrote his first two books, because he came to trust a bishop who had suffered in those events more.

Rufinus fled to Sicily. The barbarian had set fire to Rhegium, just across the strait. On quiet nights, he wrote, as a comfort in their troubles, he gathered Origen's sermons on Numbers into Latin. He hoped to add Deuteronomy, he said, if God gave him back his eyesight.
