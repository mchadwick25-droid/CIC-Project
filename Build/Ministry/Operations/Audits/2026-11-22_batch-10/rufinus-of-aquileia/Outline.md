# Outline: Rufinus of Aquileia (`rufinus-of-aquileia`)

Source keys and full verbatim lines: `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder (N203, N206, PL23, MOM, BAE, and the rest). Every line below cites its file and lines.

- **The essence:** Rufinus of Aquileia, a monk from northern Italy, spent nearly thirty years away, among the monks of Egypt and at Jerusalem, then came home and put Greek Christian books into Latin: Origen's First Principles, with passages he judged corrupt left out or reworded, and Eusebius's Church History, carried on in two books of his own. Much of Origen survives mainly in his Latin, and the preface to that Origen broke his long friendship with Jerome. (N203 45950-45955; 45775-45780; MOM 26099-26102; ANF4 22122-22124, editor; N203 44027-44030, editor; N206 18445-18447.) Documented, except "survives mainly in his Latin", which is the editors' (Widely Accepted).
- **Also told:**
  - Why he changed the text: he believed heretics had falsified Origen's books, and said he was doing what Jerome had done before him. (N203 45750-45755, 45759-45765.) Documented as his own account.
  - Jerome's answer from the receiving side: he compared the Latin with the Greek, made a literal translation of his own, and charged that the sound Trinity would put readers off their guard against the rest. (N203 51287-51303; PL23 31077-31091, 31111-31115.) Documented as Jerome's charge; Contested as to intent.
  - The cost: the bishop of Rome reported the emperors' ban on reading Origen, and wrote that Rufinus was wholly apart from them. (N203 46203-46208, 46222-46226.) Documented.
  - The Church History: asked for by his bishop as a comfort while the Goths broke into Italy; it became the West's Eusebius, and the Greek historian Socrates built on it and then corrected it. (MOM 26045-26056; N203 58747-58750; N202 5640-5668; N201 5232-5233, editor.) Documented; "the West's Eusebius" is the editor's (Widely Accepted).
  - His last work, in Sicily, with the city across the strait burning. (BAE 2429-2436; N203 58989-58998.) Documented; the year is the editor's.
  Checked against the census: teaser and job line hold. "Lived for years on the Mount of Olives" is the editor's place (N203 44094-44104); Palladius says only "a monastery in Jerusalem" (PAL 501), so the story says Jerusalem.

- **Setting:**
  - Time: about 370 to 410 (N203 46247-46250 editor; N203 58972-58975 editor). Spoken: "the late 300s", "around 400".
  - Places: Aquileia, at the head of the Adriatic in northern Italy (N203 56738-56740); Egypt and Alexandria (N203 49434-49444); Jerusalem (PAL 501); Italy again, Rome and Aquileia (N203 44204-44252, editor); Sicily (BAE 2429-2431).
  - Tradition: The Church of Aquileia (census `aquileian-christianity`, `name`). Spoken: "the church of Aquileia", where he was baptized and taught the creed (N203 46563-46575). Jerome's Chronicle calls its clergy "like a choir of the blessed" (CHR 46257-46258), for the writer to use as plain narration if it fits.
- **Words to explain:**
  - Origen: a Greek Bible scholar of the 200s (census `origen`, dates "c. 203-253"), much admired and by now much attacked. (N203 45737-45739 for the praise; N203 46203-46208 for the attack.)
  - First Principles: Origen's book on God and the order of the created world; say "his book First Principles". (N203 45769-45775.)
  - rule of faith (inside a quote): the church's own summary of what it believes. (N203 45779.)
  - Church of the Resurrection: the church over Christ's tomb in Jerusalem; "over the slain lamb" means at the Eucharist, the Lord's Supper. (N203 55907-55908; PL23 37480.)
  - Church History: Eusebius's Greek history of the church from the apostles to his own day. (N203 58745-58747, 58788-58789.)
  - The persecution in Egypt: the emperor Valens's order against the monks who held to the creed of Nicaea; say "the party in power in Egypt turned on the monks". (N202 13725-13727.)
  - the Goths: say "Alaric and his Goths" only where Rufinus names them (MOM 26046); in Sicily he says only "the Barbarian" (BAE 2430).
- **Shape:** Missionary or translator. The bridge is Greek books into Latin; the receivers are Latin readers at Rome and Aquileia, Jerome among them, and the Greek historians who read his History. Whose record it is: mostly his own and Jerome's, written against each other.
- **Sibling:** `jerome` (live): tells the quarrel from Jerome's side, through Rufinus's charge against Jerome's Bible translation and the barred line "Who but you ...". This story tells it through the First Principles preface and Jerome's answer to it, and does not retell the Bible charge. `pamphilus-of-caesarea` (live): tells the authorship dispute over the Defence of Origen; here one clause only. `melania-the-elder` (live): tells the pilgrims and the four hundred monks; here one clause only. `frumentius` (in the batch, researched after this outline): Rufinus's History carries the story, from Edesius; here one clause. Read: the live texts of `jerome`, `pamphilus-of-caesarea`, `melania-the-elder`, `frumentius`.
- **Openings:**
  1. (Recommended.) In 374 Jerome, in Syria, heard that his friend Rufinus was travelling among the monks in the far parts of Egypt, and wrote to draw him east. He ended with a line on friendship: Quote 1. Within thirty years, a book Rufinus translated had ended that friendship. (N206 3759-3760 editor date; 3769-3774; 3780-3783; 3936-3942.)
  2. Before Rufinus sailed home in 397, he and Jerome made peace after an earlier rift. Jerome later wrote that they joined hands at the Eucharist in the Church of the Resurrection, and that he walked with him as he set out. (N203 55557-55560, 55905-55908; PL23 37479-37481; N203 46255-46260 editor for the year.) If this opening is used, beat 3's last sentence is not told again.
- **Story beats:**
  1. **Aquileia.** Living in a monastery at Aquileia, he was baptized there, about thirty years before 400, he says. He received the faith from three clergy who later became bishops, Chromatius among them, and one of them taught him the creed. Jerome belonged to the same circle of young monks. (N203 46563-46575; 56738-56740; editor N203 46247-46250.) Documented.
  2. **Egypt.** He stayed six years in Egypt and later two more, he writes, learning from the blind teacher Didymus at Alexandria and the desert monks. When the party in power turned on the monks who held to Nicaea, he says he "underwent imprisonment and exile". That rests on his own word; a Greek historian later repeats it as his claim. (N203 49434-49444; 45966-45969; MOM 29405-29407; N202 13725-13727, 13741-13742.) Documented as his claim.
  3. **Jerusalem.** He lived beside Melania the Elder, who led a convent of women there, and was made a priest. Palladius, a monk who took his side, says they received pilgrims at their own cost, and calls him the most learned and kindest of men. In the 390s he and Jerome took opposite sides in a dispute over Origen; before he sailed for Italy they made peace (if Opening 2 is not used, tell it here in one sentence). (PAL 501; PAL 1223 editor; N206 10976-10979; N203 46255-46260 editor; N203 55557-55560.) Documented.
  4. **The preface.** Home after "nearly 30 years", friends asked him for Origen. He had already put into Latin a defence of Origen that he gave as the martyr Pamphilus's, with a short book of his own arguing that heretics had falsified Origen's writings. Now he translated First Principles. His preface praised, without naming him, the friend who had translated Origen before and pared away what jarred with the faith; he would follow that friend's method: Quote 2. (N203 45950-45955; 45742-45745; 45759-45765; 45721-45726; 45750-45755; 45775-45780.) Documented.
  5. **Jerome's answer.** Jerome read the preface as aimed at him. He compared the Latin with the Greek (PL23 31077-31078), and charged that Rufinus had made the Trinity sound so that readers would not be on guard against the rest. He made his own literal translation: Quote 3. Only fragments of Jerome's version survive, his editors note. (N206 18445-18447; N203 51287-51303; PL23 31077-31091; N203 51325-51331; PL23 31111-31115; ANF4 22126-22128 editor.) Documented as Jerome's charge.
  6. **The cost.** Rufinus wrote to the bishop of Rome that he was "not a defender or a champion of Origen", and had cut only what he believed others had added. Asked about him by the bishop of Jerusalem, the bishop of Rome wrote that the emperors had banned the reading of Origen, that Rufinus must answer to God, and that he neither knew nor wished to know where Rufinus was living. Rufinus and Jerome each wrote a full defence against the other, and the friendship did not recover. (N203 46059-46065, 46070-46071; 46150-46152, 46203-46208, 46222-46226; N203 46229-56448; editor N203 44366-44368, 44414-44416.) Documented; "did not recover" rests on the editor's summary and on Jerome's later words (beat 7, Ending).
  7. **The Church History.** At Aquileia, when Alaric and his Goths broke through into Italy, Chromatius, now bishop, asked him to translate Eusebius's Church History, so that listeners might forget their sufferings for a while. He folded what was history in Eusebius's tenth book into the ninth, and wrote a tenth and eleventh himself, partly from what his elders handed down and partly from his own memory, "like the two fishes to the loaves", running from Constantine to the death of the emperor Theodosius. Among their stories is how Frumentius brought the faith to a king's court in what Rufinus calls India, a story he says he had from Frumentius's own companion. (MOM 26045-26056; N203 58747-58750; MOM 26099-26102; N203 58783-58791; MOM 27383-27385.) Documented; no year (editors give 401 and 402-403).
- **What came of them** (about a third of the story), heaviest first:
  1. **Origen in Latin.** Origen's First Principles survives chiefly in Rufinus's Latin, as do many of his other works, his editors note; they also note, from the few Greek fragments, that he altered Origen's wording. A church writer in Gaul some seventy years later says he "opened to the Latin speaking church the greater part of the Greek literature". (ANF4 22122-22124, 21996-22002, editors; N203 44027-44043, editor; N203 42467-42470, Gennadius.) Widely Accepted (editors); Documented (Gennadius). **Defended:** Origen's books as corrupted by others, and the translator's right to bring them into Latin (N203 45759-45765, 46053-46077).
  2. **The Church History.** His version became the Latin West's Eusebius through the Middle Ages, an editor of Eusebius judges. Socrates of Constantinople wrote his own first two books following Rufinus. Then, reading Athanasius's own account, he decided that the man who had suffered and those who saw the events deserved more trust, and rewrote them, still using Rufinus where he could not be wrong. (N201 5232-5233, editor; N202 5640-5668; HUS 15491-15493, 15529-15543.) Documented (Socrates); Widely Accepted (editor). Socrates's line is lifted on the slate for this job; it is told here in the narrator's words, not quoted.
  3. **The break remembered.** Augustine, in Africa, wrote to Jerome in 404 that if this could happen between Jerome and Rufinus, any friend might be feared as a future enemy. (N101 32949-32951; CSEL34 21514-21516.) Documented. Optional, for the writer to weigh against the word count.
  - Optional, one clause: the same later writer judged his explanation of the creed so good that others counted for nothing beside it (N203 42482-42484). The census tradition `aquileian-christianity` keeps that work; no "who keeps him today" line is held.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** Rufinus carried Greek Christian learning into Latin, and the Latin church read Origen and Eusebius largely through him (N203 42467-42470; ANF4 22122-22124; N201 5232-5233). The writer must make clear what passed: the books themselves, in a Latin he shaped, by his own account, to match the faith he was taught at Aquileia (N203 45775-45780; 46079-46083). And the break with Jerome's tradition came through those same books: Jerome's friends at Rome sent him the translation and its preface, saying the books had been falsely translated (N203 51282-51287), and the bishop of Rome sided with them (N203 46222-46226). The listener needs to understand that the quarrel was about how to translate, not only about who was right about Origen: Rufinus cut what he thought others had added; Jerome translated it all to expose it. Tell it through their acts and their two quotes, not a "this matters because" sentence.
- **Ending:**
  1. (Recommended.) Sicily, about 410 by his editor's date. He had fled Italy with a Christian household. The barbarian had set fire to Rhegium, just across the narrowest strait. On quiet nights, he wrote, as a comfort in their troubles, he gathered Origen's sermons on Numbers into Latin. Only the notes on Deuteronomy were left, he said, if God gave him back his eyesight. It was his last work; he died soon after, his editors judge. (BAE 2429-2436; N203 58989-58998, 59011-59015; editor N203 44447-44449, 58972-58975.)
  2. The opening's image seen again: in Bethlehem, Jerome wrote, without naming him, that "the scorpion" now lay beneath the ground; his editors take it for Rufinus, dead in Sicily. (N206 47992-47998; the name and date are the editors' note.) Use only with Opening 1, and with the editors credited; this is a fourth quoted word, so tell it in the narrator's words.

  - "the Mount of Olives" as Palladius's word. Say: at Jerusalem.
  - "the pope excommunicated (or condemned, or summoned) Rufinus". Say: the bishop of Rome wrote that Rufinus was wholly apart from them and must answer to God; an editor finds no formal sentence.

  - "Most of Origen survives only through Rufinus." Say: First Principles survives chiefly in his Latin, as do many other works, his editors note.
  - "Pamphilus wrote the Defence" as fact. Say: a defence he gave as the martyr Pamphilus's.
  - "Rufinus's History is the only account of Frumentius." Say: Rufinus says he had it from Frumentius's companion; Greek historians retold it after him.
  - "Ethiopia" or "Axum" as Rufinus's word. Say: what he calls India.
  - Any year for his birth, for First Principles, or for the Church History. The Sicily year is "about 410, by his editor's date".

  - Socrates "rejected" Rufinus. Say: he followed him, then rewrote, still using him where he could not be wrong.
  - Rufinus "translated all the Greek fathers". Say: Gennadius's words, credited.
  - Don't call Jerome's literal version a lasting text; only fragments survive.
  - "He followed that (Jerome's) method" in the narrator's voice. Say: he said he followed that friend's method; whether he did is the quarrel (N203 45702-45706, editor; N203 51287-51303, Jerome).
  - "He was going blind" (or "blind"). Say: he hoped God would give him back his eyesight, in his own words (N203 59012-59013); an editor says he was suffering in his eyes (N203 44447-44448).
  - "Chromatius taught him the creed." Say: he received the faith from three clergy, Chromatius among them; the one he calls his teacher in the creed is the third (N203 46566-46575).
  - "The bishop of Rome answered Rufinus." Say: the bishop of Rome wrote to the bishop of Jerusalem, who had asked about him (N203 46149-46152).
  - "He cut Eusebius's tenth book." Say: he left out its praise of bishops and folded its history into the ninth (N203 58776-58783).
