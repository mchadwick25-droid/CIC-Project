# Outline: Rufinus of Aquileia (`rufinus-of-aquileia`)

Source keys and full verbatim lines: `Source_Sheet.md` in this folder (N203, N206, PL23, MOM, BAE, and the rest). Every line below cites its file and lines.

- **The essence:** Rufinus of Aquileia, a monk from northern Italy, spent nearly thirty years among the monks of Egypt and at Jerusalem, then came home and put Greek Christian books into Latin: Origen's First Principles, with passages he judged corrupt left out or reworded, and Eusebius's Church History, carried on in two books of his own. Much of Origen survives mainly in his Latin, and the preface to that Origen broke his long friendship with Jerome. (N203 45950-45955; 45775-45780; MOM 26099-26102; ANF4 22122-22124, editor; N206 18445-18447.) Documented, except "survives mainly in his Latin", which is the editors' (Widely Accepted).
- **Also told:**
  - Why he changed the text: he believed heretics had falsified Origen's books, and said he was doing what Jerome had done before him. (N203 45750-45755, 45759-45765.) Documented as his own account.
  - Jerome's answer from the receiving side: he compared the Latin with the Greek, made a literal translation of his own, and charged that the sound Trinity would put readers off their guard against the rest. (N203 51287-51303; PL23 31077-31091, 31111-31115.) Documented as Jerome's charge; Contested as to intent.
  - The cost: the bishop of Rome reported the emperors' ban on reading Origen, and wrote that Rufinus was wholly apart from them. (N203 46203-46208, 46222-46226.) Documented.
  - The Church History: asked for by his bishop as a comfort while the Goths broke into Italy; it became the West's Eusebius, and the Greek historian Socrates built on it and then corrected it. (MOM 26045-26056; N203 58747-58750; N202 5640-5668; N201 5232-5233, editor.) Documented; "the West's Eusebius" is the editor's (Widely Accepted).
  - His last work, in Sicily, with the city across the strait burning. (BAE 2429-2436; N203 58989-58998.) Documented; the year is the editor's.
  Checked against the census: teaser and job line hold. "Lived for years on the Mount of Olives" is the editor's place (N203 44094-44104); Palladius says only "a monastery in Jerusalem" (PAL 501), so the story says Jerusalem.
- **Length:** about 3 minutes 40 seconds: about 550 words at 150 a minute. The count behind it: three quotes, about 100 words; seven glosses worked into sentences (Origen, First Principles, rule of faith, Church of the Resurrection and the Eucharist, Church History, the Nicene side in Egypt, the Goths), about 70 words; credited witnesses (Rufinus as his own witness, Jerome, Palladius by role, the bishop of Rome, Socrates, Augustine, a later church writer, the editors twice), about 45 words; seven beats and the hook, essence and ending around them. The quarrel has two sides that must both be heard, and the impact has two works in it, so it runs past 450; nothing is planned past what that count carries.
- **Setting:**
  - Time: about 370 to 410 (N203 46247-46250 editor; N203 58972-58975 editor). Spoken: "the late 300s", "around 400".
  - Places: Aquileia, at the head of the Adriatic in northern Italy (N203 56738-56740); Egypt and Alexandria (N203 49434-49444); Jerusalem (PAL 501); Italy again, Rome and Aquileia (N203 44204-44252, editor); Sicily (BAE 2429-2431).
  - Tradition: The Church of Aquileia (census `aquileian-christianity`, `name`). Spoken: "the church of Aquileia", where he was baptized and taught the creed (N203 46563-46575). Jerome's Chronicle calls its clergy "like a choir of the blessed" (CHR 46257-46258), for the writer to use as plain narration if it fits.
- **Words to explain:**
  - Origen: a Greek Bible scholar of the 200s, much admired and by now much attacked. (N203 45737-45739 for the praise; N203 46203-46208 for the attack.)
  - First Principles: Origen's book on God, the world and the soul; say "his book First Principles". (N203 45769-45775.)
  - rule of faith (inside a quote): the church's own summary of what it believes. (N203 45779.)
  - Church of the Resurrection: the church over Christ's tomb in Jerusalem; "over the slain lamb" means at the Eucharist, the Lord's Supper. (N203 55907-55908; PL23 37480.)
  - Church History: Eusebius's Greek history of the church from the apostles to his own day. (N203 58745-58747, 58788-58789.)
  - The persecution in Egypt: the emperor Valens's order against the monks who held to the creed of Nicaea; say "the party in power in Egypt turned on the monks". (N202 13725-13727.)
  - the Goths: say "Alaric and his Goths" only where Rufinus names them (MOM 26046); in Sicily he says only "the Barbarian" (BAE 2430).
- **Shape:** Missionary or translator. The bridge is Greek books into Latin; the receivers are Latin readers at Rome and Aquileia, Jerome among them, and the Greek historians who read his History. Whose record it is: mostly his own and Jerome's, written against each other.
- **Sibling:** `jerome` (live): tells the quarrel from Jerome's side, through Rufinus's charge against Jerome's Bible translation and the barred line "Who but you ...". This story tells it through the First Principles preface and Jerome's answer to it, and does not retell the Bible charge. `pamphilus-of-caesarea` (live): tells the authorship dispute over the Defence of Origen; here one clause only. `melania-the-elder` (live): tells the pilgrims and the four hundred monks; here one clause only. `frumentius` (in the batch, researched after this outline): Rufinus's History carries the story, from Edesius; here one clause. Read: the live texts of `jerome`, `pamphilus-of-caesarea`, `melania-the-elder`, `frumentius`.
- **Openings:**
  1. (Recommended.) In 374 Jerome, in Syria, heard that his friend Rufinus was travelling among the monks in the far parts of Egypt, and wrote to draw him east. He ended with a line on friendship: Quote 1. Within thirty years, a book Rufinus translated had ended that friendship. (N206 3759-3760 editor date; 3769-3774; 3936-3942.)
  2. Before Rufinus sailed home in 397, he and Jerome made peace after an earlier rift. Jerome later wrote that they joined hands at the Eucharist in the Church of the Resurrection, and that he walked with him as he set out. (N203 55557-55560, 55905-55908; PL23 37479-37481; N203 46255-46260 editor for the year.) If this opening is used, beat 3's last sentence is not told again.
- **Story beats:**
  1. **Aquileia.** He was baptized in a monastery at Aquileia, about thirty years before 400, he says, and its future bishop Chromatius taught him the creed. Jerome belonged to the same circle of young monks. (N203 46563-46575; 56738-56740; editor N203 46247-46250.) Documented.
  2. **Egypt.** He stayed six years in Egypt and later two more, he writes, learning from the blind teacher Didymus at Alexandria and the desert monks. When the party in power turned on the monks who held to Nicaea, he says he "underwent imprisonment and exile". That rests on his own word; a Greek historian later repeats it as his claim. (N203 49434-49444; 45966-45969; MOM 29405-29407; N202 13725-13727, 13741-13742.) Documented as his claim.
  3. **Jerusalem.** He lived beside Melania the Elder, a Roman widow who led a convent there, and was made a priest. Palladius, a monk who took his side, says they received pilgrims at their own cost, and calls him the most learned and kindest of men. In the 390s he and Jerome took opposite sides in a dispute over Origen; before he sailed for Italy they made peace (if Opening 2 is not used, tell it here in one sentence). (PAL 501; PAL 1223 editor; N206 10976-10979; N203 46255-46260 editor; N203 55557-55560.) Documented.
  4. **The preface.** Home after "nearly 30 years", friends asked him for Origen. He had already put into Latin a defence of Origen that he gave as the martyr Pamphilus's, with a short book of his own arguing that heretics had falsified Origen's writings. Now he translated First Principles. His preface praised, without naming him, the friend who had translated Origen before and pared away what jarred with the faith; he would follow that friend's method: Quote 2. (N203 45950-45955; 45742-45745; 45759-45765; 45721-45726; 45750-45755; 45775-45780.) Documented.
  5. **Jerome's answer.** Jerome read the preface as aimed at him. He compared the Latin with the Greek, and charged that Rufinus had made the Trinity sound so that readers would not be on guard against the rest. He made his own literal translation: Quote 3. Only fragments of Jerome's version survive, his editors note. (N206 18445-18447; N203 51287-51303; PL23 31077-31091; N203 51325-51331; PL23 31111-31115; ANF4 22126-22128 editor.) Documented as Jerome's charge.
  6. **The cost.** Rufinus wrote to the bishop of Rome that he was "not a defender or a champion of Origen", and had cut only what he believed others had added. The bishop wrote back to Jerusalem that the emperors had banned the reading of Origen, that Rufinus must answer to God, and that he neither knew nor wished to know where Rufinus was living. Rufinus and Jerome each wrote a full defence against the other, and the friendship did not recover. (N203 46059-46065, 46070-46071; 46150-46152, 46203-46208, 46222-46226; N203 46229-56448; editor N203 44366-44368, 44414-44416.) Documented; "did not recover" rests on the editor's summary and on Jerome's later words (beat 7, Ending).
  7. **The Church History.** At Aquileia, when Alaric and his Goths broke through into Italy, Chromatius, now bishop, asked him to translate Eusebius's Church History, so that listeners might forget their sufferings for a while. He cut down Eusebius's tenth book and wrote two more himself, partly from what his elders handed down and partly from his own memory, "like the two fishes to the loaves", running from Constantine to the death of the emperor Theodosius. Into them went stories no one else had written down, among them how Frumentius brought the faith to a king's court in what Rufinus calls India, a story he says he had from Frumentius's own companion. (MOM 26045-26056; N203 58747-58750; MOM 26099-26102; N203 58783-58791; MOM 27383-27385.) Documented; no year (editors give 401 and 402-403).
- **What came of them** (about a third of the story), heaviest first:
  1. **Origen in Latin.** Origen's First Principles survives chiefly in Rufinus's Latin, as do many of his commentaries and sermons, his editors note; they also note, from the few Greek fragments, that he altered Origen's wording. A church writer in Gaul some seventy years later says he "opened to the Latin speaking church the greater part of the Greek literature". (ANF4 22122-22124, 21996-22002, editors; N203 44027-44043, editor; N203 42467-42470, Gennadius.) Widely Accepted (editors); Documented (Gennadius). **Defended:** Origen's books as corrupted by others, and the translator's right to bring them into Latin (N203 45759-45765, 46053-46077).
  2. **The Church History.** His version became the Latin West's Eusebius through the Middle Ages, an editor of Eusebius judges. Socrates of Constantinople wrote his own first two books following Rufinus. Then, reading Athanasius's own account, he decided that the man who had suffered and those who saw the events deserved more trust, and rewrote them, still using Rufinus where he could not be wrong. (N201 5232-5233, editor; N202 5640-5668; HUS 15491-15493, 15529-15543.) Documented (Socrates); Widely Accepted (editor). Socrates's line is lifted on the slate for this job; it is told here in the narrator's words, not quoted.
  3. **The break remembered.** Augustine, in Africa, wrote to Jerome in 404 that if this could happen between Jerome and Rufinus, any friend might be feared as a future enemy. (N101 32949-32951; CSEL34 21514-21516.) Documented. Optional, for the writer to weigh against the word count.
  - Optional, one clause: the same later writer judged his explanation of the creed so good that others counted for nothing beside it (N203 42482-42484). The census tradition `aquileian-christianity` keeps that work; no "who keeps him today" line is held.
- **Quotes, 2 or 3 at most:**
  1. Jerome to Rufinus, 374 (N206 3938, 3941-3942). Rendered from the English of 1893 (Latin not held): **"A friend is long sought, hard to find, and hard to keep. A friendship that can end was never real."** The text: "A friend is long sought, hardly found, and with difficulty kept." and "The friendship which can cease has never been real." The two sentences stand apart in the letter; the writer may set the first, then the second, or the second alone. Behind it: English printed before 1931. Job: the opening's image, the friendship at its warmest, in Jerome's own words; no other story on the tree uses it.
  2. Rufinus, preface to First Principles (N203 45775-45780; second print N206 18367-18372). Rendered from the English of 1892 (Latin not held): **"Where I found anything in his books against the truth about the Trinity, which he teaches soundly elsewhere, I left it out as not his own. Or I set it down to agree with the rule of faith, which we find him holding to again and again."** Behind it: English printed before 1931, confirmed by a second 1893 translation. Job: his method in his own words, the act the whole quarrel turned on.
  3. Jerome, Apology against Rufinus I.7 (PL23 31111-31115; English N203 51325-51331). Rendered from the Latin: **"In the books On First Principles, I simply set down what stood in the Greek. I did not want the reader to believe what I translated. I wanted him not to believe what you had translated before."** Behind it: an original in a fair OCR print (PL23), confirmed by Fremantle's English of 1892. Job: the receiving side's answer, the hostile view in its own words.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** Rufinus carried Greek Christian learning into Latin, and the Latin church read Origen and Eusebius largely through him (N203 42467-42470; ANF4 22122-22124; N201 5232-5233). The writer must make clear what passed: the books themselves, in a Latin he shaped, by his own account, to match the faith he was taught at Aquileia (N203 45775-45780; 46079-46083). And the break with Jerome's tradition came through those same books: Jerome's circle at Rome received the First Principles as heresy brought in under his name (N203 51282-51287), and the bishop of Rome sided with them (N203 46222-46226). The listener needs to understand that the quarrel was about how to translate, not only about who was right about Origen: Rufinus cut what he thought others had added; Jerome translated it all to expose it. Tell it through their acts and their two quotes, not a "this matters because" sentence.
- **Ending:**
  1. (Recommended.) Sicily, about 410 by his editor's date. He had fled Italy with a Christian household. The barbarian had set fire to Rhegium, just across the narrowest strait. On quiet nights, he wrote, as a comfort in their troubles, he gathered Origen's sermons on Numbers into Latin. Only the notes on Deuteronomy were left, he said, if God gave him back his eyesight. It was his last work; he died soon after, his editors judge. (BAE 2429-2436; N203 58989-58998, 59011-59015; editor N203 44447-44449, 58972-58975.)
  2. The opening's image seen again: in Bethlehem, Jerome wrote, without naming him, that "the scorpion" now lay beneath the ground; his editors take it for Rufinus, dead in Sicily. (N206 47992-47998; the name and date are the editors' note.) Use only with Opening 1, and with the editors credited; this is a fourth quoted word, so tell it in the narrator's words.

  - Rufinus, his own prefaces and his two defences, written around 397 to 410; a party to the quarrel. Credited beside: baptism and teacher, Egypt's years, prison and exile, his method, his History's purpose, Sicily. Prison and exile rest on his word alone; the story says "he says".
  - Jerome, his letters (374, 399) and his Apology (402-403); once his friend, then his opponent. Credited beside: the 374 letter, the reading of the preface, the literal translation, the charge, the peace at Jerusalem.
  - Palladius, writing about 419-420, some ten years after Rufinus's death (PAL 657, Clarke's note); took his side (PAL 1223). Credited beside: the life at Jerusalem and the praise.
  - Anastasius, bishop of Rome, letter of 401; hostile. Credited beside: the ban and "wholly apart from us".
  - Socrates of Constantinople, whose History runs to 439 (N202 891-892, editor), about thirty years after; used and corrected him. Credited beside: his use of the History.
  - Augustine, 404; grieved at both. Credited beside: the lament.

- **Do not say:**
  - "Rufinus softened Origen to hide heresy." Say: he cut or reworded what he believed heretics had added, by his own account; Jerome charged that it put readers off their guard.
  - "He was imprisoned and exiled" as plain fact. Say: "he says he was jailed and exiled".
  - "the Mount of Olives" as Palladius's word. Say: at Jerusalem.
  - "the pope excommunicated (or condemned, or summoned) Rufinus". Say: the bishop of Rome wrote that Rufinus was wholly apart from them and must answer to God; an editor finds no formal sentence.
  - "Origen was a heretic" in the narrator's voice. Say: his books were under attack, and the emperors had banned their reading, the bishop of Rome reports.
  - "Most of Origen survives only through Rufinus." Say: First Principles survives chiefly in his Latin, as do many other works, his editors note.
  - "Pamphilus wrote the Defence" as fact. Say: a defence he gave as the martyr Pamphilus's.
  - "Rufinus's History is the only account of Frumentius." Say: Rufinus says he had it from Frumentius's companion; Greek historians retold it after him.
  - "Ethiopia" or "Axum" as Rufinus's word. Say: what he calls India.
  - Any year for his birth, for First Principles, or for the Church History. The Sicily year is "about 410, by his editor's date".
  - "He died in 410" as fact. Say: he died soon after, his editors judge.
  - "Jerome's friends hid his friendly letter." That is an editor's view; leave it out.
  - Socrates "rejected" Rufinus. Say: he followed him, then rewrote, still using him where he could not be wrong.
  - Rufinus "translated all the Greek fathers". Say: Gennadius's words, credited.
  - Don't call Jerome's literal version a lasting text; only fragments survive.
