**The one witness.** Every claim about Alopen goes back to the Xi'an stele, set up in 781 by his own church in the capital. That is about 146 years after he came. No words of his own are held. No Tang court record is held. The Chinese text is not legible in any held file (MAL 13785–13860 is garbled OCR), so the stele is read through two English translations: A. Wylie's (MAL) and James Legge's of 1888 (LEGGE), with Saeki held only as Budge's paraphrase (BUDGE 5171–5236). **Rule for stele lines:** where Wylie and Legge agree, the claim is told; where they differ in meaning, the sheet gives both and the outline follows what both support, or the reading two of the three renderings share, framed as "the stone says".

Witness kinds: **stele** = later, about 146 years, his own church; **19th–20th c. scholar** = modern editor or historian, 1,100 years or more after.

## 1. Setting

**S1. The stele's own heading names its author and church.** MAL 13565–13567. Stele (later, ~146 yrs), via Wylie. Documented.
> Tablet Eulogizing the Propagation of the Illustrious Religion in / China, With a Preface; Composed by King-Tsing, / A Priest of the Syrian Church.

Legge's heading agrees on the faith's name and differs on the author's church. LEGGE 360–368:
> (COMMEMORATING)  THE  DIFFUSION  OF  THE  IL¬ / LUSTRIOUS1  RELIGION  IN  THE  MIDDLE  KINGDOM, / WITH  PREFATORY  NOTICES. / Handed  down  by  Ching-tsing,  a  priest  of  the  Ta  Ts'in / Monastery. / (In  Syriac.)  Adam,  Presbyter  and  Chorepiscopos,  and  Papas / of  China.

Note: "Illustrious Religion" is Wylie's and Legge's English for the stele's name for the faith. Budge gives Saeki's as "Luminous Religion" (BUDGE 5163–5164, 5196).

**S2. Where he came from: Ta-ts'in, rendered Syria.** YULE 2933–2935, 2968. Yule (19th c. scholar). Widely Accepted that the term is Ta-ts'in; Contested what land it means here.
> Ta  T'sin  being  the  term  applied  in  Chinese  literature  to  the  Roman  Empire,

> After  this  comes  a  description  of  Ta-T'sin  (here  apparently / implying  Syria)

Budge: "of the kingdom of Ta-ch'in / (Judea, Syria)" (BUDGE 5173–5174). Wylie renders it "the country of / Syria" outright (MAL 13687–13688). Legge keeps "the  kingdom  of  Ta  Ts'in" (LEGGE 1011, 1245) and in his lecture places Alopen's start only as "Ta  Ts'in,  the / Far  West  from  China" (LEGGE 4117–4118). So "Syria" is a translator's reading: Contested. The *International Review of Missions* (1916) says "from Persia" (`international-review-of-missions_v5_1916.txt` 38827) — late and unsupported; set aside.

**S3. The city: Chang-an, the Tang capital, today's Xi'an.** YULE 2908–2910. Yule. Documented.
> Kenjanfu,  or,  as  Ramusio  gives  it,  Quenzanfu,  is  Si-ngan  fu,  or  as  it  was  called / in  the  days  of  its  greatest  fame,  Chang-ngan,  probably  the  most  celebrated  city  in / Chinese  history,

**S4. The emperor: Taitsung, founder of the Tang line.** MAL 13680–13681. Stele. Documented (that the stele says so).
> In the time of the accomplished Emperor Taitsung, the illustrious and / magnificent founder of the dynasty,

**Correction (Legge):** Wylie's "founder of the dynasty" is wrong. Legge's text, LEGGE 1008–1010: "When  the  Accomplished  Emperor  T'ai  Tsung  (a.  d.  627-649) / commenced  his  glorious  reign  over  the  (recently)  established  dynasty / (of  T'ang)". His note, LEGGE 1033–1034: "T'ai  Tsung  was  not  the  founder  of  the  T'ang  dynasty,  as  Wylie's  and  other / translations  unnecessarily  say.  His  father  Kao  Tsu  was  the  first  emperor". Documented: Taizong, second Tang emperor, reigned 627–649.

Yule calls him "T'ai-Tsung, a very famous prince" (YULE 2966). Note: the stele also has a later "Emperor Taitsung" (MAL 13919), a different ruler of the 760s–770s. The writer should not confuse them.

**S5. The church's spoken name.** Census `toldWithin` = `the-church-of-the-east-on-the-silk-road`, name "The Church of the East on the Silk Road", shortName "Silk Road Church". Spoken: **the Church of the East**. The census floorNote states that "Nestorian" was "given to it by opponents rather than chosen by itself". Wylie's heading says "the Syrian Church" (S1).

## 2. Events

**E1. He came east with sacred books.** MAL 13687–13689. Stele. Documented (stele's words); Widely Accepted (the event).
> men who arrived was the Most-virtuous Olopun, from the country of / Syria. Observing the azure clouds, he bore the true sacred books; / beholding the direction of the winds, he braved difficulties and dangers.

Legge, LEGGE 1011–1012, 1227–1229 (1229 is damaged OCR): "in  the / kingdom  of  Ta  Ts'in  there  was  a  man  of  the  highest  virtue  called / Olopun 8.  Guiding  himself  by  the  azure  clouds,  he  carried  with" … "him  the  True  Scriptures.  Watching  the  laws  of  the" … "rrtade  his  way  through  difficulties  and  perils." (OCR for "made") Legge's "Guiding himself" is stronger than Wylie's "Observing"; both have the clouds, the winds and the dangers. Legge on the honorific, LEGGE 1259–1260: "'  The  Greatly  Virtuous'  is  perhaps  not  more  than  the  equivalent  of  our / 'Reverend,'  or  'Father.'"

Wylie's "among the enlightened and holy / men who arrived was" (MAL 13681–13687) may hint at companions; Legge's text does not.

Note: Assemani says Kircher's reading — that he was led by clouds and charts of the winds — is not in the text, following Renaudot (ASS 35917–35920). The stele's words are figurative; the writer should keep them as the stone's image, not as a travel fact.

**E2. Arrival in 635; the emperor sends his chief minister to meet him.** MAL 13690–13692. Stele. Documented (stele); date Contested between 635 and 636 (see D1).
> In the year A. D. 635 he arrived at Chang-an; the Emperor sent his / Prime Minister, Duke Fang Hiuen-ling; who, carrying the official staff / to the west border, conducted his guest into the interior

Legge, LEGGE 1231–1236: "In  the  ninth  year  of  the  period  Chang-kwan  (a. d." … "635),  he  arrived  at  Ch'ang-an.  The  emperor  sent  his" … "duke  Fang  Hsiian-ling" … "bearing  the  staff  of" … "office,  to  the  western  suburb,  there  to  receive  the  visi¬ / tor,  and  conduct  him  to  the  palace." (Margin glosses run into these OCR lines.) Budge (Saeki): "the Emperor sent his minister, / Fang Hstan-ling, with a guard of honour to / bring him to the palace" (BUDGE 5175–5177). **Two of three** (Legge, Saeki) place the welcome at the capital and end it at the palace; Wylie alone has "the west border". Follow the western suburb. Note: "635" is the translators' conversion of the ninth Chang-kwan year.

**E3. The books are translated in the imperial library; the emperor studies them and orders the faith spread.** MAL 13692–13695. Stele. Documented (stele).
> the sacred books / were translated in the imperial library, the sovereign investigated the / subject in his private apartments; when becoming deeply impressed with / the rectitude of the religion, he gave special orders for its dissemination.

Legge, LEGGE 1236–1239: "The  Scriptures / were  translated  in  the  Library2.  (His  Majesty)  questioned  him  about / his  system  in  his  own  forbidden  apartments,  became  deeply  convinced / of  its  correctness  and  truth,  and  gave  special  orders  for  its  propagation." Legge's note, LEGGE 1268: "This  can  only  mean  that  portions  of  the  Scriptures  were  translated." Budge: "became convinced of its truth" (BUDGE 5180). "Deeply impressed" is Wylie's alone. Legge alone has the emperor questioning Alopen in person.

Note: the stele does not say who translated. Williams reads it as Alopen's work, "in part / or in whole" (MAL 14278–14279) — the editor's reading, marked as his. The stele does not say the emperor became a Christian.

**E4. The decree of 638.** MAL 13696–13711. Stele, quoting an imperial decree. Documented (as the stele's quotation); the decree itself Widely Accepted, not independently held.
> In the seventh month of the year A. D. 638 the following imperial procla- / mation was issued:

> "Right principles have no invariable name, holy men have no in- / variable station; instruction is established in accordance with the locality, / with the object of benefiting the people at large. The Greatly-virtuous / Olopun, of the kingdom of Syria, has brought his sacred books and / images from that distant part, and has presented them at our chief cap- / ital.

> it is beneficial to all creatures; it is advantageous to / mankind. Let it be published throughout the Empire, and let the proper / authority build a Syrian church in the capital in the I-ning May, which / shall be governed by twenty-one priests.

Note: "I-ning May" is OCR for I-ning Fang, a ward of the capital. Yule: "the  Square  of  Peace  and  Justice  {I-ning  Fang),  at  the  capital" (YULE 2967). Budge (Saeki): "This / Teaching is helpful to all creatures, and beneficial / to all men. So let it have free course throughout / the Empire." (BUDGE 5206–5209).

Legge, LEGGE 1240–1244: "In  the  twelfth  Chang-kwan  year  (638),  in  autumn,  in  the  seventh  month, / the  following  proclamation  was  issued  : — '  Systems  have  not  always / the  same  name ;  sages  have  not  always  the  same  personality.  Every / region  has  its  appropriate  doctrines,  which  by  their  imperceptible / influence  benefit  the  inhabitants." Wylie's "holy men have no invariable station" (place) and Legge's "sages have not always the same personality" (person) differ in meaning; the Chinese is not held to settle it.

Legge, LEGGE 1252–1254: "(the  system)  is / helpful  to  (all)  creatures,  and  profitable  for  men  : — let  it  have  free / course  throughout  the  empire". Legge's note, LEGGE 1279: "Here  I  think  the  words  of  the  proclamation  should  terminate."

The building, in Legge, is an act after the decree, LEGGE 1256–1257, 1477: "The  proper  officers  forthwith,  in  the  capital  in  the  Ward  of / Righteousness  and  Repose6,  built  a  Ta  Ts'in  monastery7,  sufficient" … "to  accommodate  twenty-one  priests." Note, LEGGE 1285: "Not  'a  church;'  one  of  its  halls  would  serve  the  part  of  one." Budge (Saeki): "A Ta-ch'in monastery was built / in the capital and 21 priests were appointed to / it." (BUDGE 5209–5211). **Two of three:** a monastery, built by officials after the decree, with twenty-one priests. Wylie's "governed by" is his alone.

**E5. The emperor's portrait is placed in the church.** MAL 13716–13718. Stele. Documented (Wylie's reading).
> Orders were then issued to the authorities to have a true portrait / of the Emperor taken; when it was tranferred to the wall of the / church,

Yule agrees: "The  Emperor's / portrait  was  to  be  placed  in  the  church" (YULE 2967–2968). Assemani's Latin summary, "cujus etiam effigiem depingendam muroque affigendam curavit" (ASS 35912–35913, OCR cleaned in this note), can be read as Alopen's portrait. Legge agrees it was the emperor's: "officers  were  further  ordered  to  take  a  faithful  likeness  of  the  emperor, / and  have  it  copied  on  the  walls  of  the  monastery." (LEGGE 1486–1490). Follow Wylie, Yule and Legge, and leave it out of a one-minute piece.

**E6. Under the next emperor, Kautsung: churches in every province, and a high title for Alopen.** MAL 13738–13744. Stele. Documented (stele's claim); the scale is Inferential-Thin as fact (no second witness).
> The Emperor Kautsung respectfully succeeded his ancestor, and was / still more beneficient toward the institution of truth. In every province / he caused Illustrious churches to be erected, and ratified the honor con- / ferred upon Olopun, making him the great conservator of doctrine for / the preservation of the State. While this doctrine pervaded every chan- / nel, the State became enriched and tranquility abounded. Every city was / full of churches,

Legge, LEGGE 1633–1643: "The  great  emperor  Kao  Tsung  (650-683)  reverently  continued" … "he  caused  monasteries  of  the  Illustrious  (Re-" … "bgion)  to  be  erected  in  every  one  of  the  Prefectures, / and  continued  the  favour  (of  his  father)  to  Olopun, / raising  him  to  be  Lord  of  the  Great  Law,  for  the  preservation  of / the  state." … "Monasteries  filled  a / hundred  cities". Budge (Saeki): "caused / monasteries to be built in every prefecture, and / made A-lo-pén the Great Patron and Spiritual / Lord of the Empire" (BUDGE 5211–5214). **Two of three:** monasteries in every prefecture; Legge's "a hundred cities" against Wylie's "Every city was full of churches". Follow Legge. The title's wording is Contested (see D3). This is the last time Alopen is named in the stele's narrative. Nothing of his death is held.

## 3. What came of him — ranked heaviest first

Weight = what later witnesses carried on and used.

**I1. The stone itself: his church's memory of him, carved in 781 and found again in 1625.** Heaviest, because every later witness held here knows him only through it.
- Date and makers. MAL 14141–14142 (stele, Documented):
  > This was erected in the 2d year of Kienchung, of the Tang dy- / nasty (A. D. 781), on the 7th day of the 1st month,. being Sunday.
- Syriac at the foot, as Kircher translated it. MAL 14162–14166 (stele via Kircher, Documented as Kircher's reading):
  > "In the year of the Greeks one thousand and ninety-two, the Lord / Jazedbuzid, Priest and Vicar-episcopal of Cumdan the royal city, son / of the enlightened Mailas, Priest of Balach a city of Turkestan, set / up this tablet, whereon is inscribed the Dispensation of our Redeemer, / and the preaching of the apostolic missionaries to the King of China."
- Yule on the Syriac: the patriarch named, "Mar  Hanan  Ishua  (dead / in  778,  but  the  fact  apparently  had  not  reached  China),  that  of  Adam,  Bishop  and  Pope  of / Tzinisthan  {i.e.  China)" (YULE 2993–2995); "sixty-seven  names  of  persons  in / Syriac  characters,  most  of  whom  are  characterised  as  priests" (YULE 2996–2997). Legge: "about  seventy  names  in / Syriac  of  individuals," … "of  various / ranks,  from  bishop  down  to  deacon," (LEGGE 3549–3551).
- Found by chance. YULE 2929–2931 (Yule, Widely Accepted):
  > The  stone  slab,  about  7J  feet  high  by  3  feet  wide,  and  some  10  inches  in  thickness,1  which  bears / this  inscription,  was  accidentally  found  in  1625  by  some  workmen  who  were  digging  in  the  Chang-ngan / suburb  of  the  city  of  Singanfu.
- What its finding showed. YULE 2914–2916 (Yule):
  > the  site  of  flourishing  Christian  Churches  in  the  7th / century,  as  well  as  of  the  remarkable  monument,  the  discovery  of  which  a  thousand / years  later  disclosed  their  forgotten  existence.
- Genuine. MAL 14212–14217 (Williams, Widely Accepted):
  > The only record yet found in China itself of the labors of the Nes- / torians is the celebrated monument which was discovered at Si-ngan fu / in Shensi, in 1625; and though the discussion regarding its authenticity / has been rather warm between the Jesuits and their opponents, the weight / of evidence, both internal and external, leaves no doubt regarding its ver- / ity.

> When the pure, bright Illustrious Religion / Was introduced to our Tang dynasty, / The Scriptures were translated, and churches built, / And the vessel set in motion for the living and the dead ;

  ("Brand" is OCR, likely for "grand".) Defended — but by Lohan and Kie-leih, not by Alopen.

**I4. The end in China: the edict of 845.** Widely Accepted; the edict is not held. **The figure covers two foreign religions.** Legge quotes the edict from Du Halde, LEGGE 4346–4350: "'  As  to  the  religions / of  foreign  nations,  let  the  men  who  teach  them,  as  well / those  of  Ta  Ts'in  as  of  Mu-hu-pi,  amounting  to  more  than / 3,000  persons,  be  required  to  resume  the  ways  of  ordinary / life," Legge on the Mu-hu-pi, LEGGE 4389–4390: "perhaps  Mohbeds  or  Guebers, / the  worshippers  of  fire," Williams (MAL 14249–14252) gives all of them to the Christians, which is misleading:
> In / the year 845 an edict of Wu-tsung commanded the priests that belonged / to the sect that came from Ta Tsin, amounting to no less than three / thousand persons, to retire to private life.

Yule: "the  Emperor  Wu-Tsung / issued  an  edict,  still  extant,  against  the  vast  multiplication  of  Buddhist  convents, … A  clause  in  the  edict  also  orders  the  foreign  bonzes  of  Ta-T'  sin  and  Mubupa  (Christian / and  Mobed  or  Magian  r)  to  return  to  secular  life." (YULE 3289–3292). Yule also gives Pauthier's guess that the stone "may  have  been  buried  in  845" (YULE 3288–3289) — a supposition, Inferential-Thin. Legge makes the same guess: "I  venture  to  suppose  that  it  was  then  buried  by  some  of / the  Christians  themselves" (LEGGE 4382–4383).

**I5. Later scholars name him first.** Budge's index: "A-lo-pen, introducer of Chris-" (BUDGE 12436). Chabot (French) cites the inscription as proof that Nestorianism "avait été apporté par un prêtre syrien nommé / Olopen" (CHAB 1343–1344). These are modern labels; light weight.

## 4. Links to other people

- **Adam / King-Tsing** (`adam-jingjing`) — wrote the Chinese text of the stele that tells Alopen's coming (MAL 13566–13567). Cordier: "Adam  {King-tsing),  who  erected  the  monument" (YULE 3000). Kind: wrote his story (on the stone). The stone's own heading pairs the Chinese author with the Syriac "Adam,  Presbyter  and  Chorepiscopos,  and  Papas / of  China" (LEGGE 364–368), and Cordier agrees; Widely Accepted.
- **Jazedbuzid / Yazdbozid**, priest of the capital, son of a priest of Balkh — set the stone up (MAL 14162–14165; BUDGE 5232–5235). Not on the tree as far as checked.
- **Hanan-Isho**, patriarch named in the Syriac (YULE 2993–2994). No link to Alopen in the record.
- **Timothy I** and **Thomas of Marga** (census people of the same tradition): Thomas, from Timothy's letters, names a David as "Metropolitan of Beth Sinaye" (`thomas-of-marga_book-of-governors-v2-english_budge1893.txt` 20159–20161), glossed China (20186). No source ties either to Alopen. No link.
- **Rabban Sauma and Yahballaha**: Rockhill, via Cordier, notes they "do  not  once  refer  to  Hsi-anfu  or / Chang-an" (YULE 3007–3008). No link.

## 5. Where sources disagree or come late

**D1. The year.** 635: Wylie's stele (MAL 13690), Yule (YULE 2939, 2964), Budge's Appendix (BUDGE 5174), Chabot (CHAB 1344, for the church and 21 priests). 636: Assemani "annoChrifti 6^6" (ASS 35905, OCR; read 636), "anno Chrifti (S3 6" (ASS 55233); Abbeloos–Lamy "jam ab anno Christi 636" (BH 9523); Budge's introduction "arrived therein 636" (BUDGE 1882); Brine (`brine_taeping-rebellion-in-china_1862.txt` 2244). About 630: Wigram (WIG 4188–4190). 685: IRM 1927 (`international-review-of-missions_v16_1927.txt` 23128) — evidently a misprint. Legge: the ninth Chang-kwan year, 635 (LEGGE 1231–1233); Legge's lecture guesses he set out "perhaps  about  630" (LEGGE 4156). Contested; tell 635, the stele's year as Wylie and Legge give it.

**D2. His name.** Yule: "Oi.opAn  (probably  a  Chinese  form  of  Rybban  — Monk)" (YULE 2939, OCR for "Olopan … Rabban"). Hirth: "0-lo-pen  =  Ruben,  Rupen?" (YULE 2944). Havret sides with Yule (YULE 3035–3039). Assemani: from Syriac "Jab-allaha" (ASS 35913–35914). Legge: "Olopun  is  a  Syriac  name." (LEGGE 1036). Contested. No Syriac form of his name is held from a Syriac text.

**D3. His title under Kautsung.** Wylie: "great conservator of doctrine for / the preservation of the State" (MAL 13741–13742). Saeki via Budge: "Great Patron and Spiritual / Lord of the Empire" (BUDGE 5213–5214). Legge: "Lord  of  the  Great  Law,  for  the  preservation  of / the  state" (LEGGE 1639–1640). Chavannes–Pelliot discuss it as "un titre conféré en Chine par l'empereur au religieux A-lo-pen" (`manichaean-texts_traite-manicheen-retrouve-en-chine-pt1-in-journal-asiatique-10-18-fra_chavannes-pelliot1911.txt` 29506–29507, OCR). Contested wording; Documented that a high title is given.

**D4. His rank and who sent him.** Not held. "Monk" is Budge's word (BUDGE 1881) and Yule's reading of the name; "prêtre" Chabot's (CHAB 1343); "Episcopus" Assemani's (ASS 55233). The stele calls him only "Most-virtuous" / "Greatly-virtuous" (MAL 13687, 13701). Absent detail.

**D5. Translator.** Stele: passive, "were translated in the imperial library" (MAL 13693). Williams: "in part / or in whole translated by Olopun" (MAL 14278–14279). Editor's reading.

**D6. Reading the stone.** Williams quoting Bridgman: "Were a hundred Chinese / students employed on the document they would each give a different view / of the meaning in some parts of the inscription." (MAL 14231–14233).

**D6b. Legge's lecture guesses** (LEGGE 4146–4169, 4240–4246): "I  like  to  think  of" Olopun starting "from  somewhere  in  Mesopo¬ / tamia,  perhaps  about  630"; "My  idea  is  that  he  journeyed  at  the  head  of  a  con¬ / siderable  band  of  other  monks"; "I  cannot  help / supposing  that  there  had  been  some  previous  communica¬ / tion  between  Olopun  and  the  Emperor"; and "He  would  translate,  or  superintend  the  translating / of,  short  and  important  passages  of  the  Bible". Legge's own guesses; none is in the stone. Inferential-Thin.

**D6c. The finding.** Legge: "some  workmen,  digging  the  ground  in  a  suburb / of  Ch'ang-an,  for  a  grave  or  for  some  other  purpose  (for / there  are  different  accounts  of  the  matter)" (LEGGE 3664–3666).

**D7. Wigram's version** (WIG 4184–4198): "Emperor Tsai", "about the / year 630", an emperor with "an intellectual wife", the Gospels translated "for the Empress's library". None of this is in Wylie or Saeki-via-Budge. Late and unreliable; set aside entirely.

**D8. "First Christian in China."** Brine: "the first Christian / that planted his foot in China" (brine 2243–2244). Williams gives grounds for earlier sees, "as early as A. D. 505" (MAL 14202–14207). Neither is solid; do not claim "first".
