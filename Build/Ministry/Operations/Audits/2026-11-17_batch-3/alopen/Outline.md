# Alopen — Outline

Line references are to files in `cic/texts/`. MAL = `malech_history-of-the-syrian-nation-and-old-evangelical-apostolic-church-of-the-east_1910.txt` (A. Wylie's English of the stele); LEGGE = `legge_nestorian-monument-of-hsi-an-fu_trubner1888.txt` (James Legge's English, 1888, with notes); YULE = `marco-polo_book-of-ser-marco-polo-v2_yule-cordier1903.txt`; BUDGE = `yahballaha-sauma_monks-of-kublai-khan_budge1928.txt` (Saeki's reading, paraphrased). Full lines and verbatim text: `Source_Sheet.md`. Where Wylie and Legge differ, this outline tells what both support, or the reading two of the three renderings share, as "the stone says".

- **Length:** about 1 minute (about 150 words). The record is thin. One witness holds everything: a stone his own church carved about 146 years after he came. No words of his own survive, and he drops out of the record after the next emperor honours him. Do not fill.

- **Setting:**
  - Time: 635, his arrival; 638, the emperor's decree (MAL 13690, 13696; LEGGE 1231–1233, 1240). Spoken: "in the year 635".
  - Place: Chang-an, capital of the Tang emperors; today's Xi'an (YULE 2908–2910). Spoken: "Chang-an, the Tang capital". He came from what the stone calls Ta-ts'in, the Chinese name for the Roman lands far to the west (YULE 2933–2935). Wylie renders it Syria (MAL 13687–13688); Legge keeps "Ta Ts'in" (LEGGE 1011, 1245) and says only "the Far West from China" (LEGGE 4117–4118); Yule "apparently implying Syria" (YULE 2968–2969). Contested. Spoken: "from the far west — the land the Chinese called Ta-ts'in, which his translators read as Syria".
  - Tradition: the Church of the East (census name: the Church of the East on the Silk Road). The stone's own name for the faith, in Wylie's and Legge's English: "the Illustrious Religion" (MAL 13565; LEGGE 360–361); Saeki, via Budge, "the Luminous Religion" (BUDGE 5163–5164).
  - Emperor: Taizong (Wylie "Taitsung", Legge "T'ai Tsung"), second emperor of the Tang, reigned 627–649 (LEGGE 1008–1010, 1033–1034). Spoken: "the emperor Taizong".

- **Thread:** He carried the Church of the East's sacred books to the Tang court, where they were translated, the faith was allowed to spread and a monastery was built for it in the capital — the beginning his own church carved in stone a century and a half later.

- **Shape:** Missionary or translator. Whose record: entirely the receiving church's own stone, set up by his successors in Chang-an in 781 — not the sender's, and not the Chinese court's.

- **Openings:**
  1. The stone's own image of his journey: watching the clouds and the winds, carrying the books (MAL 13688–13689; LEGGE 1012, 1227–1229). Quote 1.
  2. A stone found by workmen digging in a suburb of Xi'an in 1625, which told a forgotten story (YULE 2929–2931, 2914–2916; LEGGE 3664–3666).

- **Story beats:**
  1. **He came east with books.** In Taizong's reign, a man the stone calls by the honorific "Most-virtuous" (Legge: about "Reverend" or "Father", LEGGE 1259–1260), Olopun, came from Ta-ts'in (Wylie: "Syria") carrying "the true sacred books", through hardship and danger. Why he came, who sent him, and whether others came with him are not recorded. MAL 13680–13689; LEGGE 1008–1012, 1227–1229. Documented (stele's words).
  2. **Received outside the capital, 635.** He reached Chang-an in 635. The emperor sent his chief minister, Fang Xuanling (Wylie "Fang Hiuen-ling", Legge "Fang Hsüan-ling"), carrying his staff of office, to the western edge of the capital to receive the visitor and bring him to the palace — an honour for a guest. MAL 13690–13692; LEGGE 1231–1236; BUDGE 5175–5177. Documented (stele); year Contested 635/636, tell 635.
  3. **The books translated at court.** The books were translated in the imperial library. The emperor looked into the teaching in his palace rooms, was satisfied it was right, the stone says, and ordered it spread. The stone does not say who translated, or that the emperor became a Christian. MAL 13692–13695; LEGGE 1236–1239; BUDGE 5177–5181. Documented (stele).
  4. **The decree of 638.** The emperor's proclamation, quoted on the stone: teachings do not always have one name; each region has the teaching that suits it; this one does good to creatures and to people; let it have free course through the empire. The officials then built a monastery in the I-ning ward of the capital (Legge: "the Ward of Righteousness and Repose"), with twenty-one priests. MAL 13696–13711; LEGGE 1240–1257, 1477; BUDGE 5206–5211. Documented as the stele's quotation; the court's own record is not held.
  5. **Honoured by the next emperor.** Under Kao Tsung (Wylie "Kautsung", reigned 650–683), the stone says, monasteries were built in every prefecture and filled "a hundred cities", and Alopen was given a high title. This is the last the stone says of him. MAL 13738–13744; LEGGE 1633–1643; BUDGE 5211–5214. Documented as the stele's claim; scale Inferential-Thin.

- **What came of them** (about a third of the piece):
  1. **The stone itself, heaviest.** In 781 his church in the capital set up a carved stone telling how the faith came, naming him first. Its Chinese text was written by the priest King-Tsing (Legge "Ching-tsing"), paired in Syriac with Adam, "Papas of China" (MAL 13566–13567; LEGGE 364–368); the Syriac at its foot names the priest Jazedbuzid, who set it up (MAL 14162–14166), and the stone names about seventy clergy, from bishop to deacon (LEGGE 3549–3551; YULE 2996–2997). Lost, it was found by chance in 1625 and revealed churches the world had forgotten (YULE 2914–2916, 2929–2931). Everything we know of him comes from it. Widely Accepted that it is genuine (MAL 14212–14217).
  2. **A community that lasted.** For nearly 150 years after him the stone traces it: around 699 Buddhists spoke out against it in the eastern capital, and about 712–713 others mocked and slandered it in the western; the priests Lohan and Kie-leih (Legge: Lo-han, Chi-lieh) held it together — defended, by them, not by Alopen (MAL 13744–13751; LEGGE 1645–1647, 1863–1871). A new priest came from Ta-ts'in in 744 (MAL 13766–13769; LEGGE 2145–2146). The priest-official I-sz' (Legge "I-sze") each year gathered the priests and fed the hungry, clothed the naked, cared for the sick and buried the dead (MAL 13997–13999; LEGGE 2512, 2924–2930). Documented as the stele's account.
  3. **The end in China.** In 845 an edict of the emperor Wu-tsung sent the foreign teachers of Ta-ts'in and of the Mu-hu-pi (perhaps Zoroastrian priests, as Legge and Yule think), more than three thousand in all, back to ordinary life (LEGGE 4339–4350, 4389–4390; YULE 3289–3292). Widely Accepted; the edict is not held.

- **Quotes** (from Wylie's or Legge's English, as marked; the Chinese is not held in a legible text):
  1. Wylie, MAL 13688–13689. Original: "Observing the azure clouds, he bore the true sacred books; beholding the direction of the winds, he braved difficulties and dangers." **Rendering:** "Watching the blue clouds, he carried the true sacred books. Watching the way the winds blew, he faced hardships and dangers." Job: the opening — the stone's own picture of the journey, the only one it gives. (Legge has the same clouds, winds and perils, LEGGE 1012, 1227–1229; "Watching" fits both.)
  2. Legge, LEGGE 1241–1244 (the 638 decree). Original: "Systems have not always the same name; sages have not always the same personality. Every region has its appropriate doctrines, which by their imperceptible influence benefit the inhabitants." **Rendering:** "Teachings do not always have the same name. Sages are not always the same person. Every region has the teaching that suits it, and its quiet influence does the people good." Job: why an emperor could welcome a foreign faith, in the court's own terms.
  3. Legge, LEGGE 1252–1254 (same decree). Original: "(the system) is helpful to (all) creatures, and profitable for men: — let it have free course throughout the empire". **Rendering:** "It helps every creature, and it does people good. Let it spread freely through the empire." Job: the licence itself; use if Quote 2 is not used, or with it if room allows.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** What passed from Alopen was the books and their welcome. He brought sacred books to the court; they were translated in the imperial library; the emperor's decree let the faith spread, and officials built a monastery in the capital for twenty-one priests (MAL 13692–13711; LEGGE 1236–1257, 1477). That community outlived him, and in 781 its priests carved the whole story on stone, starting with his arrival (MAL 14030–14035, 14141–14166). The listener must understand that Alopen is known *only* because his successors remembered him this way: the stone is his church looking back on its own beginning, not a report from his own time. Carry this by telling who set the stone up and when, not by a "this matters because" line.

- **Links:**
  - Adam (King-Tsing) — wrote his story (the stele's Chinese text, which tells his coming). The stone's heading pairs the Chinese author with the Syriac "Adam, Presbyter and Chorepiscopos, and Papas of China" (LEGGE 364–368; MAL 13566–13567); Cordier agrees (YULE 3000).
  - No other tie on the tree is supported. Timothy I and Thomas of Marga (same tradition) have no recorded tie to him.

- **Who tells it:**
  - **The Xi'an stele, 781** — set up in Chang-an by his own church, about 146 years after he came; Chinese text by the priest King-Tsing. The listener must know it is the church's own retrospect, and praise of emperors is part of how it is written. Read in two 19th-century English translations: A. Wylie's (reprinted in Malech, 1910) and James Legge's (1888, with notes). Where they differ, the outline says which it follows.
  - **Henry Yule and Henri Cordier (1903), James Legge (1888), S. Wells Williams (reprinted 1910), E. A. Wallis Budge (1928)** — modern scholars, for the stone's finding, the edict of 845 and the readings of the name and title. Not needed by name in the audio unless a disputed point is voiced.

- **Do not say:**
  - "the founder of the Tang dynasty" → "the Tang emperor Taizong". His father was the first Tang emperor (LEGGE 1033–1034).
  - "from Syria" as plain fact → "from the far west, the land the Chinese called Ta-ts'in". "From Persia" → leave out.
  - "met him at the frontier / the border" → "met him outside the capital and brought him to the palace".
  - "the first Christian in China" → "the first missionary the stone names" or simply "came to China". Earlier claims exist (MAL 14202–14207); none is secure.
  - "Nestorian" for his church → "the Church of the East". Wylie's English heading says "the Syrian Church"; Legge's says "a priest of the Ta Ts'in Monastery" (LEGGE 364–365).
  - "monk", "bishop" or "priest" → no rank: "a man from the far west", or the stone's honorific "Most-virtuous", framed as a title of respect, not praise of his character. His rank is not recorded.
  - "sent by the patriarch" / "sent by his church" / "a Syriac missionary sent by the church in Persia" → nothing; who sent him, and from where, is not recorded.
  - "he came alone" / "he led a band of monks" / "the emperor had sent for him" → none of these is in the stone; the band of monks and the prior contact are Legge's guesses (LEGGE 4158–4169).
  - "Chinese records / Tang court records say" → "the stone says". No Tang court record is held.
  - "his name means Rabban, 'monk'" → leave out; it is one scholar's guess among several.
  - "the clouds guided him" / "led by a sign in the sky" → keep the stone's image: "watching the clouds and the winds".
  - "Alopen translated the Bible / the scriptures" → "the books were translated in the imperial library". Who translated is not stated. "The whole Bible / all the scriptures were translated" → leave out (Legge: only portions, LEGGE 1268).
  - "the emperor questioned him in private" as fact → "the emperor looked into the teaching"; the private questioning is Legge's reading alone.
  - "the emperor became a Christian" → "the emperor studied the teaching and ordered it spread".
  - "he led the twenty-one priests" / "he built the church" / "a church governed by twenty-one priests" → "officials built a monastery in the capital, with twenty-one priests".
  - "churches in every province" / "every city was full of churches" → "the stone says monasteries were built in every prefecture, in a hundred cities", always as the stone's claim.
  - A title in quotes ("Great Patron", "great conservator", "Lord of the Great Law") → "a high title"; translators word it differently.
  - "attacks" / "violence" in 699 and 713 → "spoke out against it", "mocked and slandered it".
  - The church fed and clothed the poor → it was I-sz''s yearly work.
  - Alopen's portrait in the church → leave out; the portrait was the emperor's (MAL 13716–13718; LEGGE 1486–1490).
  - "he died in China", or any date or place of death → nothing is known.
  - "three thousand Christian priests" → "more than three thousand foreign teachers, Christians and others". "Persecuted", "killed", "martyred" for 845 → "sent back to ordinary life".
  - "the stone was hidden / buried in 845 to save it" → leave out; it is Pauthier's and Legge's guess (YULE 3288–3289; LEGGE 4382–4384).
  - "workmen digging a grave" → "workmen digging"; Legge says the accounts differ (LEGGE 3664–3666).
  - "he travelled the Silk Road", "across deserts", "by caravan" → leave out; the stone gives only clouds, winds, hardship and danger.
  - 636 or 630 → 635, the stone's year as Wylie and Legge give it.

- **Leave out:**
  - The year dispute (635/636/630), the name etymologies (Rabban, Ruben, Yahballaha, Legge's "a Syriac name") and the title wordings: card material.
  - The emperor's portrait, the Ta-ts'in geography passage (MAL 13721–13735), and the later emperors' gifts: side detail with no room at one minute.
  - The Syriac name lists and Adam's titles ("Pope of China"): Chavannes and Pelliot doubt the "pope" title (chavannes-pelliot 1911, 29500–29502); not needed.
  - Legge's lecture guesses (a start from Mesopotamia about 630, a band of monks, prior contact with the emperor): his own suppositions.
  - Wigram's account (empress's library, "about 630"): not in the stele; unreliable.
  - The claimed meeting with the Buddhist teacher Zendo (IRM 1916, 38830): speculation.
  - The authenticity dispute beyond one clause: settled in Williams's view, and the card carries it.
