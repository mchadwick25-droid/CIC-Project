# Alopen — Outline

Line references are to files in `cic/texts/`. MAL = `malech_history-of-the-syrian-nation-and-old-evangelical-apostolic-church-of-the-east_1910.txt` (A. Wylie's English of the stele); LEGGE = `legge_nestorian-monument-of-hsi-an-fu_trubner1888.txt` (James Legge's English, 1888, with notes and lecture); YULE = `marco-polo_book-of-ser-marco-polo-v2_yule-cordier1903.txt`; BUDGE = `yahballaha-sauma_monks-of-kublai-khan_budge1928.txt` (Saeki's reading, paraphrased). Full lines and verbatim text: `Source_Sheet.md` (H1–H6 are the lines added for this revision). Where Wylie and Legge differ, this outline tells what both support, or the reading two of the three renderings share, as "the stone says".

- **The heart of his life:** Alopen led the earliest Christian mission to China that the record describes. In 635 he brought the sacred books of the Church of the East to Chang-an, the Tang capital (MAL 13687–13690; LEGGE 1011–1012, 1227–1233). The books were translated in the imperial library; the emperor Taizong studied the teaching and ordered it spread (MAL 13692–13695; LEGGE 1236–1239). In 638 his decree let the faith "have free course throughout the empire", and officials built a monastery in the capital for twenty-one priests (LEGGE 1240–1257, 1477). The church that grew from that welcome lasted about two centuries, then faded from view (LEGGE 4346–4350, 4251–4253; I4). It survived only on a stone his church set up in 781. When workmen dug that stone up in 1625, it gave the world back a church it had forgotten (YULE 2914–2916, 2929–2931; LEGGE 3662–3679).
  - *Confidence.* Arrival, translation, decree, monastery: Documented as the stone's account; no Tang court record is held. "Earliest mission the record describes": the stone's story of the faith in China begins with him, and its title marks the faith's "introduction" (YULE 2932–2933; H1), Documented; that this was the first mission, Widely Accepted (LEGGE 3876–3877; Gibbon in LEGGE 3815–3817; H2). Whether any Christian reached China before him is Contested: Williams's grounds for sees "as early as A. D. 505" name no mission and no person (MAL 14202–14207). The finding and its effect: Widely Accepted.
  - *Commonly said, not held:* "the first Christian in China" (not held; say "the first Christian mission to China on record"); "he translated the Bible" (the stone gives no translator; Legge: only portions, LEGGE 1268); "the emperor became a Christian" (not in the stone); "sent by the patriarch" (not recorded).

- **Length:** 2 minutes, about 300 words (Neo, about 150 a minute): a major figure (census `major: true`) whose heart has two halves, the welcome in 635–638 and the stone's finding in 1625 that is the only reason we know of it; the thin record (one witness, no words of his own) keeps it from stretching further. Do not fill past this.

- **Setting:**
  - Time: 635, his arrival; 638, the decree; 781, the stone; 1625, its finding (MAL 13690, 13696, 14141; YULE 2930). Spoken: "in the year 635", "in 1625".
  - Place: Chang-an, capital of the Tang emperors; today's Xi'an (YULE 2908–2910). Spoken: "Chang-an, the Tang capital — today's Xi'an". He came from what the stone calls Ta-ts'in, the Chinese name for the Roman lands far to the west (YULE 2933–2935). Wylie renders it Syria (MAL 13687–13688); Legge keeps "Ta Ts'in", "the Far West from China" (LEGGE 1011, 4117–4118). Contested. Spoken: "from the far west".
  - Tradition: the Church of the East (census: the Church of the East on the Silk Road). The stone's own name for the faith, in Wylie's and Legge's English: "the Illustrious Religion" (MAL 13565; LEGGE 360–361).
  - Emperor: Taizong, second emperor of the Tang, reigned 627–649 (LEGGE 1008–1010, 1033–1034). Spoken: "the emperor Taizong".

- **Shape:** Missionary or translator. Whose record: entirely the receiving church's own stone, set up by his church in Chang-an in 781, and its finders' accounts in 1625 — not the sender's, and not the Chinese court's.

- **Openings:**
  1. **Recommended.** The finding: in 1625 workmen digging in a suburb of Xi'an came on a great stone with a cross carved at its top and strange writing beside the Chinese. It told of Christians teaching in that city a thousand years before, with the favour of the Tang emperors (LEGGE 3662–3679; YULE 2929–2932). Plain heard once, and it leads straight into who first brought their books. Keep it to two sentences.
  2. Plain arrival: in 635 a man from the far west reached the Tang capital carrying sacred books (MAL 13687–13690). Use only if the finding is moved to the end; then the finding must still come inside the first minute as the reason we know.

- **Story beats** (the heart is told by beat 4, inside about 150 words; beats 5–7 deepen it):
  1. **A stone dug up, 1625.** Workmen digging outside Xi'an found it by chance. Its Chinese text said that, long ago, foreign teachers had taught there with the emperors' blessing (LEGGE 3662–3679; YULE 2929–2931). It named the man who first brought their books. Widely Accepted. One sentence to place it: the stone was set up by his own church in 781, about 146 years after he came (MAL 14141–14142). This is the how-we-know; no more than that here.
  2. **He came with books, 635.** In Taizong's reign, Olopun — the stone's form of the name — came from the far west carrying "the true sacred books", through hardship and danger (MAL 13687–13689; LEGGE 1011–1012, 1227–1229). The emperor sent his chief minister to the western edge of the capital to receive him and bring him to the palace, an honour for a guest (LEGGE 1231–1236; BUDGE 5175–5177). Why he came, who sent him, his rank and his companions are not recorded. Documented (stele).
  3. **The books translated.** The books were translated in the imperial library. The emperor looked into the teaching in his palace and, the stone says, was convinced it was right, and ordered it spread (MAL 13692–13695; LEGGE 1236–1239). Documented (stele).
  4. **The decree of 638.** The emperor's proclamation, quoted on the stone: teachings do not always have one name; each region has the teaching that suits it; this one does people good; let it spread freely (LEGGE 1240–1254). Officials then built a monastery in the capital with twenty-one priests (LEGGE 1256–1257, 1477; BUDGE 5209–5211). Why it mattered: a foreign faith had the emperor's open licence. Documented as the stele's quotation. **The heart is complete here.**
  5. **Honoured by the next emperor.** Under Gaozong (Wylie "Kautsung", Legge "Kao Tsung", reigned 650–683), the stone says, monasteries were built in every prefecture — a prefecture is a district of the empire — and filled "a hundred cities"; Alopen was given a high title. This is the last the stone says of him (MAL 13738–13744; LEGGE 1633–1643). Documented as the stele's claim; scale Inferential-Thin.
  6. **A church that held on, and carved its memory.** Around 699 Buddhists spoke out against it, and about 712–713 others mocked and slandered it; the priests Lohan and Kie-leih held it together (MAL 13744–13751; LEGGE 1645–1647, 1863–1871). In 781 its priests set up the stone, telling the story from his coming, with about seventy names on it, most of them clergy, from bishop to deacon (MAL 14141–14166; LEGGE 3549–3551; YULE 2996–2997). Documented.
  7. **Forgotten, then found.** In 845 an imperial edict sent more than three thousand foreign teachers, Christians and others, back to ordinary life (LEGGE 4346–4350, 4389–4390; YULE 3289–3292). After 781 Chinese history keeps only very slight traces of the church (LEGGE 4251–4253); the edict of 845 is one of them. When the stone came out of the ground in 1625, a Chinese Christian official sent a copy to the Jesuit missionaries, who met it with "a spiritual jubilee" and circulated copies through the empire as proof that Christianity had come to China early (LEGGE 3685–3710). Widely Accepted; the edict itself is not held.

- **What came of them** (about a third of the piece), heaviest first:
  1. **The stone, and its finding.** His church remembered him on stone in 781, and that stone is the only record of his mission held (MAL 14212–14217, Williams: "The only record yet found in China itself"). Dug up in 1625, it "disclosed their forgotten existence" (YULE 2914–2916): the Tang church came back into knowledge, and the Jesuits then in China used it at once as proof of the faith's early coming (LEGGE 3685–3710). Widely Accepted that it is genuine (MAL 14212–14217; LEGGE 3819–3825, Legge affirming Gibbon that the forgery charge fails).
  2. **A church that grew and lasted.** From twenty-one priests in one monastery, by the stone's claim, to monasteries across the prefectures; by 781, about seventy names on its sides, most of them clergy, from bishop to deacon (MAL 13738–13744; LEGGE 3549–3551; YULE 2996–2997). It held its ground against opposition around 699 and 712–713 — defended by Lohan and Kie-leih, not by Alopen (MAL 13744–13751).
  3. **The end in China.** The edict of 845, and near silence after (LEGGE 4346–4350, 4252–4253).

- **Quotes** (from Legge's English; the Chinese is not held in a legible text):
  1. LEGGE 1241–1244 (the 638 decree). Original: "Systems have not always the same name; … Every region has its appropriate doctrines, which by their imperceptible influence benefit the inhabitants." **Rendering:** "Teachings do not always have the same name. Every region has the teaching that suits it, and its quiet influence does the people good." The middle clause is cut: Legge's "sages have not always the same personality" (LEGGE 1242) and Wylie's "holy men have no invariable station" (MAL 13699–13700) differ in meaning, and the Chinese is not held. Wylie agrees on name, locality and benefit (MAL 13699–13701). Job: why an emperor could welcome a foreign faith, in the court's own terms.
  2. LEGGE 1252–1254 (same decree). Original: "(the system) is helpful to (all) creatures, and profitable for men: — let it have free course throughout the empire". **Rendering:** "It helps every creature, and it does people good. Let it spread freely through the empire." Job: the licence itself — the turning point of the heart. If only one quote is used, use this one.
  - The stone's image of the journey ("Observing the azure clouds…", MAL 13688–13689) is dropped: heard once, it does not make plain sense (project lead, 2026-11-18).

- **Connection to carry into the story:** Two handings-on, one inside the other. First, what passed from Alopen to his church: the books and the emperor's welcome. He brought the books; they were translated; the decree let the faith spread and a monastery was built (LEGGE 1236–1257). That community outlived him, and in 781 its priests carved its story on stone, starting with his coming (MAL 14141–14166; LEGGE 3876–3877). Second, what passed from that stone to the world of 1625: the knowledge that the church had ever been there (YULE 2914–2916; LEGGE 3662–3679, 3685–3710). The listener must understand that we know Alopen only because his church, about 146 years later, remembered him this way, and that the church itself was forgotten until their stone was found. Carry it through the acts — who carved the stone, who found it, what the Jesuits did with it — not through a "this matters because" line.

- **Links:**
  - Adam (King-Tsing), `adam-jingjing` — wrote his story: the stone's Chinese text, which tells his coming (MAL 13566–13567; LEGGE 364–368; YULE 3000).
  - No other tie on the tree is supported. Timothy I and Thomas of Marga (same tradition) have no recorded tie to him. Semedo and Kircher are not on the tree.

- **Who tells it:**
  - **The Xi'an stone, 781** — set up in Chang-an by his own church, about 146 years after he came; Chinese text by the priest King-Tsing (Adam). The listener needs one sentence: it is his church looking back, not a report from his own time. Praise of emperors is part of how it is written. Read in Wylie's English (in Malech, 1910) and Legge's (1888).
  - **The finding, 1625** — told by James Legge (1888) from the Jesuit accounts of Alvarez Semedo, who saw the stone in 1628, and Athanasius Kircher (LEGGE 3690–3714); and by Henry Yule (1903). Not needed by name in the audio.
  - How-we-know: one or two sentences, placed with the finding in beat 1. Here the finding is part of the heart, not a footnote: tell it as an event, not as source criticism.

- **Do not say:**
  - Any version of the clouds-and-winds image ("watching the blue clouds") → leave out; it does not make plain sense heard once.
  - "the first Christian in China" / "he brought Christianity to China" as plain fact → "the first Christian mission to China on record", or "the earliest mission the record describes". Earlier claims exist (MAL 14202–14207); none is secure.
  - "the stone was lost" / "hidden" / "buried in 845 to save it" → "it lay in the ground until workmen dug it up in 1625". How it came to be buried is not recorded; the 845 burial is Pauthier's and Legge's guess (YULE 3288–3289; LEGGE 4382–4384).
  - "the Jesuits found / discovered the stone" → workmen found it; a Chinese Christian official sent a copy to the Jesuits (LEGGE 3685–3690).
  - "it proved Christianity had come early" in the narrator's voice → "the Jesuits circulated it as proof that Christianity had come to China early" (their use).
  - "1635" → 1625 (Legge's "1635" at 4255 is a slip; he gives 1625 at 3663, as does Yule).
  - "workmen digging a grave" → "workmen digging"; Legge says the accounts differ (LEGGE 3664–3666).
  - "the church survived in China until the stone was found" → after 781 Chinese history keeps only very slight traces of it, the edict of 845 among them (LEGGE 4251–4253, 4346–4350).
  - "sages are not always the same person" / "holy men have no fixed place" → leave out; the translations differ (LEGGE 1242; MAL 13699–13700).
  - "the emperor had the books translated" → "the books were translated in the imperial library" (MAL 13692–13693; LEGGE 1236–1237).
  - "Alopen built churches across China" / "he founded monasteries in every province" → the stone credits Gaozong's reign, and always as the stone's claim (MAL 13738–13740; LEGGE 1633–1636).
  - "the stone was carved by Alopen's own disciples" → "set up by his church, about 146 years after he came" (MAL 14141–14166).
  - "the founder of the Tang dynasty" → "the Tang emperor Taizong". His father was the first Tang emperor (LEGGE 1033–1034).
  - "from Syria" as plain fact → "from the far west". "From Persia" → leave out.
  - "met him at the frontier / the border" → "met him outside the capital and brought him to the palace".
  - "Nestorian" for his church → "the Church of the East".
  - "monk", "bishop" or "priest" for Alopen → no rank: "a man from the far west". If the stone's honorific "Most-virtuous" is used, frame it as a title of respect.
  - "sent by the patriarch" / "sent by his church" / "a Syriac missionary sent by the church in Persia" → nothing; who sent him is not recorded.
  - "he came alone" / "he led a band of monks" / "the emperor had sent for him" → none of these is in the stone (Legge's guesses, LEGGE 4158–4169).
  - "Chinese records / Tang court records say" → "the stone says". No Tang court record is held.
  - "his name means Rabban, 'monk'" → leave out.
  - "Alopen translated the Bible / the scriptures" → "the books were translated in the imperial library". "The whole Bible" → leave out (only portions, LEGGE 1268).
  - "the emperor questioned him in private" as fact → "the emperor looked into the teaching".
  - "the emperor became a Christian" → "the emperor studied the teaching and ordered it spread".
  - "he led the twenty-one priests" / "he built the church" → "officials built a monastery in the capital, with twenty-one priests".
  - "churches in every province" / "every city was full of churches" → "the stone says monasteries were built in every prefecture, in a hundred cities", always as the stone's claim.
  - A title in quotes ("Great Patron", "great conservator", "Lord of the Great Law") → "a high title".
  - "attacks" / "violence" in 699 and 713 → "spoke out against it", "mocked and slandered it".
  - "the stone tells of Christ's death and rising" → it does not mention the crucifixion (LEGGE 3919–3920); leave the stone's doctrine out, or say only that it sets out the faith's teaching in Chinese.
  - Alopen's portrait in the church → leave out; the portrait was the emperor's (MAL 13716–13718; LEGGE 1486–1490).
  - "he died in China", or any date or place of death → nothing is known.
  - "three thousand Christian priests" → "more than three thousand foreign teachers, Christians and others". "Persecuted", "killed", "martyred" for 845 → "sent back to ordinary life".
  - "he travelled the Silk Road", "across deserts", "by caravan" → leave out.
  - 636 or 630 → 635.
  - An after-life that is all favour, with no opposition and no end → tell the opposition of 699 and 712–713 and the edict of 845 (from Review.md, 2026-11-18).

- **Leave out:**

  - The year dispute (635/636/630), the name etymologies, the title wordings: card material.
  - The forgery dispute (Voltaire, La Croze; LEGGE 3815–3817) and the long silence argued against the stone (LEGGE 4255–4256): card material; it is settled in Williams's and Legge's view.
  - The stone's doctrinal section (God three-in-one, the virgin birth, twenty-seven books; MAL 13574–13575, 13609, 13623): it is the church of 781 speaking, not Alopen, and there is no room. At most one clause.
  - The Jesuits' hiding from persecution in Hangzhou, the governor and the Taoist temple: side detail.
  - The emperor's portrait, the Ta-ts'in geography passage (MAL 13721–13735), later emperors' gifts, the priest I-sz''s charity, the priest Kih-ho of 744: no room.
  - Adam's titles ("Pope of China"): doubted (Chavannes–Pelliot 1911, 29500–29502); not needed.
