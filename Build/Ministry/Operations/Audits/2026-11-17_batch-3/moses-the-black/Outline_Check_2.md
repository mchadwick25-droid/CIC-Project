# Outline check, round 2: Moses the Black

## My account of the heart of his life (written before opening Outline.md)

Moses was a black house-servant, an Ethiopian by race, driven out by his master for immorality and brigandage, and by report a murderer and robber chief by the Nile (LH 333). Struck in conscience, he gave himself to a monastery, and his life became the desert's great proof of what repentance can do: Palladius says plainly he tells the crimes "to show the virtue of his repentance" (LH 333). The essence of his work is that repentance, lived out at Scete: the old violence turned into a refusal to hurt anyone (the four robbers carried to church, who then became monks because of him), a long, near-defeated fight with his old lust under Isidore's guidance, ended not by effort but by Isidore's word and Communion, and humble hidden service (filling the old men's water-pots by night). He became a priest, was numbered among the great fathers, and left seventy disciples (LH 337). The sayings add the contempt he bore as "this Ethiopian" in silence (PAR2 164, 1028) and keep him as a guide to brothers (PAR2 88). His major contribution: the people drawn after him (robbers, seventy disciples) and the pattern of his repentance, which later teachers used to show that grace can call suddenly and that labour alone does not heal (PAR2 2766, 2850), and which the Greek and Ethiopian calendars still keep.

## Verdict: PASS

No blocking findings. Four minor corrections below.

## The heart, compared

The outline's heart has the same substance as mine. It makes him a slave and criminal who became one of the great fathers of Scete, a priest who left seventy disciples. It says his life was told from the first as proof of what repentance can do (LH 333, 337), and that others followed that life, starting with the robbers he caught (LH 335). Beats 1 to 3 bring all of this in by about word 150 to 170. The heart line itself stops at "proof of what repentance can do" and does not say what the repentance was. But beats 4 and 5 tell it in full: the long fight, the hidden service, the rest that came through Isidore's word and Communion. Impact item 3 carries the later teaching drawn from it (PAR2 2766–2768). No major contribution is missing. The sources hold the outline's account.

## Checks

- **Beats, setting, links** were checked against LH 331–337, LH 197/713, LH 657 note 13, LH 711 note 40, SOZ 39459–39525, PAR2 88/96/164/1028/2766–2768/2850/2874, ESX-M 37092–37196, NIK 41886–41957 and the PAR2 header. Every one is supported, says no more than its source and is graded correctly. The report forms ("they used to say", "he was said to"), the wonder at the well and the late Ethiopian death are all kept in their witnesses' voices.
- **Quotes.** These are my own renderings of Clarke's English, set against the outline's. (1) "Since I'm not allowed to hurt anyone, what do you want me to do with these?" The outline's version keeps every clause. "Tell" for "bid" is right. (2) "If a man so great and powerful in robbery has feared God, why should we put off our salvation?" This is the same as the outline's. (3) "I will never stop until the demons stop appearing." The same. Every source text is verbatim in Clarke (LH 335, 337).
- **Impact and defence.** The ranking holds by what later witnesses carried on. Palladius's stated purpose, Nikodemos and the Ethiopian Synaxarium's closing all carry the repentance (NIK 41915–41919; ESX-M 37189–37192). "Defended nothing" is right.
- **Shape and length.** Monk or ascetic fits, and so does a 2-minute story of about 300 words. "The desert fathers" and "Abba" do not mislead.
- **Connection.** It is specific: the four robbers recognized him and renounced the world "because of his conversion", and their reasoning is in their own words (LH 335). The Do not say list rightly keeps them from becoming his disciples.
- **Do not say.** This is thorough: the qualifiers, the ethnicity, the cause of the conversion, the demon, the martyrdom, the barbarian saying, the Ethiopian ordination, the two Isidores, Cassian's Moses, 70 against 75, and the timing of the swim.

## Findings

1. **MINOR: the heart says "Brothers came to him for a rule of life".** PAR2 88 tells of one brother. Correction: "A saying remembers a brother coming to him for a rule of life (PAR2 88)."
2. **MINOR: beat 4 runs Isidore's counsel straight into the six-year vigil.** In LH 337 the all-night vigil came from "another one of the saints". Isidore's word was only to endure. A writer could make Isidore the one who prescribed the vigil. Add to Do not say: "Isidore told him to stand all night" → "Isidore told him to endure; the all-night vigil was another elder's advice (LH 337)."
3. **MINOR: Sozomen's "sudden".** Beat 2 credits it to Sozomen. But a writer could drop the credit and have the narrator say "suddenly", which Palladius does not say. Add to Do not say: "He suddenly turned" in the narrator's voice → "Palladius names no cause; Sozomen calls it sudden" (LH 335; SOZ 39502).
4. **MINOR: the Library sweep missed two later Latin witnesses.** Neither changes any claim. Dorotheus of Gaza, Doctrina XIV (`dorotheus-gaza_doctrinae-barsanuphius-climacus-agathias-grc-lat_migne-pg88.txt` 136342), has "abbas Moyses" rebuking himself: "Sterquilinium cum sis et cinis, non homo; cur ad homines ingrederis?" This is close to the sanctuary saying, but it does not name him the Ethiopian. Theodore the Studite, PG 99 (`theodore-studite_opera-omnia-pg99-grc-lat_migne1860.txt` 72552–72560), retells the two-boats vision of Arsenius and Moses (Y5). Correction: list both under the sheet's Library sweep as later witnesses that were read and set aside, because neither names him the Ethiopian and neither is used.
