They used to say he once swam the flooded Nile at night, his sword in his mouth, to kill a shepherd who had foiled him.

This was Moses the Black, a desert father of Egypt in the late 300s. Palladius, the bishop who wrote the earliest account, calls him an Ethiopian by race, and black. A slave driven out by his master, he was said to have killed and to have led a band of robbers. Then one of his adventures left him conscience-stricken, and he gave himself to a monastery.

When four robbers attacked his cell, Moses tied them up and carried them to the church. "I am not allowed to hurt anyone," he told the brothers. "So what do you tell me to do with these?" When the robbers learned that their captor was Moses, the famous robber, they confessed and left the world to become monks themselves. "If a man so great and powerful in robbery has feared God," they said, "why should we put off our salvation?"

At Scete, a desert settlement of monks, his own fight with lust lasted years. He stood all night in his cell and secretly drew water for the older monks, until one night, Palladius says, a demon struck him down at the well. Isidore, the priest of Scete, told him his dreams had ended and sent him to Communion, and two months later Moses said he suffered no more.

Later sayings recall that when the fathers asked why this Ethiopian came among them, he kept silent. When a bishop tested him by having him driven from the sanctuary, Moses told himself they had treated him rightly. His skin was dark and black, he went on, and he must not go back in as if he were a white man.

He became a priest and left seventy disciples. Palladius says only that he died at Scete at seventy-five, while a later Ethiopian tradition remembers raiders killing him there.
