Around the year 400, the desert fathers of Egypt counted Moses among their great ones. He was a priest at Scete, out in the desert, and he left seventy disciples. Yet he began as a household slave, an Ethiopian by race and black, whose master drove him out for immorality and robbery, and Moses was said to have killed. Palladius, a bishop writing about the desert monks around 420, tells of these crimes to show what his repentance could do.

People said Moses went on to lead a band of robbers by the Nile. In time one of his adventures left him conscience-stricken, and he gave himself to a monastery, though Palladius names no cause.

Four robbers once broke into his cell, not knowing whose it was. Moses tied them up, carried them on his back to the church, and asked the brothers, "I am not allowed to hurt anyone. So what do you tell me to do with these?" When the robbers learned who had caught them, they confessed. "If a man so great and powerful in robbery has feared God," they said, "why should we put off our salvation?" They left the world because of his conversion.

His own fight was long, and by his own account his old lust nearly made him give up. For six years he stood all night in his cell, but still he could not master it. Then one night, as he drew water for the old monks, he was struck down, and Palladius says a demon did it. Afterwards Isidore, the priest of Scete, told him his dreams had ceased and sent him to Communion. Two months later, Moses said he suffered nothing more.

Sayings from the 600s remember the fathers of Scete asking why this Ethiopian came among them. He kept silent, though he said afterwards he had been afraid. Once a bishop put him out of the sanctuary as a test. Moses told himself they had treated him rightly, since his skin was dark and black, and he must not act like a white man.

Palladius says he died at Scete, aged seventy-five.
