They used to say that Moses the Black once swam the flooded Nile, his sword in his mouth, to kill a shepherd who had foiled him one night.

He lived among the desert fathers of Egypt in the late 300s. The bishop Palladius, his first biographer, calls him an Ethiopian by race, and black. A slave driven out by his master, he was said to have killed and to have led a band of robbers. Then one of his adventures left him conscience-stricken, and he gave himself to a monastery.

When four robbers attacked his cell, he tied them up and carried them to the church. "I am not allowed to hurt anyone," he told the brothers. "So what do you tell me to do with these?" When the robbers learned that their captor was Moses, the famous robber, they confessed and became monks themselves. "If a man so great and powerful in robbery has feared God," they said, "why should we put off our salvation?"

At Scete, a desert settlement of monks, his own fight with lust lasted years. He stood all night in his cell and secretly drew water for the older monks, until one night, Palladius says, a demon struck him down at the well. Isidore, the priest of Scete, sent him to Communion, and two months later Moses said he suffered no more.

The monks' sayings remember contempt as well. The fathers at Scete insulted him, asking why this Ethiopian came and went in their midst, and he kept silent. A bishop once tested him by having the clergy drive him from the sanctuary: "Get outside, O Ethiopian." Moses told himself they had treated him rightly. His skin was dark and black, he went on, and he must not go back in as if he were a white man.

He became a priest and left seventy disciples. Palladius says only that he died at Scete at seventy-five, while a later Ethiopian tradition remembers raiders killing him there.
