"Whoever follows me does not walk in darkness," says the Lord. These are the opening words of the Imitation of Christ, a little book of the fifteenth century, which says they urge us to imitate the life and ways of Jesus. "So let our chief study be to ponder the life of Jesus Christ."

The book sets a changed heart above clever argument: "I would rather feel sorrow for sin than know how to define it." Then it turns inward: "Learn to scorn outward things and to give yourself to inward things. And you will see the kingdom of God come within you." It calls the cross the royal way, the only road to life and inner peace, which means denying yourself each day and willingly bearing the pain or trouble that comes. Later parts let Christ speak to the soul, and turn to Holy Communion.

The book is credited to Thomas à Kempis, and Johannes Busch, a canon of his own order writing in his lifetime, names him as its author. But early copies gave it to John Gerson, a Paris theologian, and scholars still disagree.

Thomas was born about 1380 in Kempen, near Cologne. At school in Deventer he was taught by the Brothers of the Common Life, of the movement called the New Devotion, who shared all they had and copied books. In 1399 he came to Mount St Agnes, near Zwolle, in what is now the Netherlands, and asked to stay. He became a canon of that small house, living in community under the Rule of St Augustine, and wrote its Chronicle and the lives of its founders. In 1429, when the canons stopped their services to keep a pope's ban, they were insulted and driven out, and the community spent three years in Frisia. He died in 1471, in his ninety-second year.

Ignatius of Loyola, founder of the Jesuits, knew the book as "the little Gerson." He told a fellow Jesuit, who wrote it down, that after finding it he wanted no other book of devotion, and read a chapter a day. Two centuries later the young John Wesley, reading it in English, began to see that true religion was seated in the heart. In 1746 his Methodist Conference told its preachers to read it between four and five in the morning. And the Quaker John Woolman, though told its author was a Roman Catholic, judged him a man of a true Christian spirit.
