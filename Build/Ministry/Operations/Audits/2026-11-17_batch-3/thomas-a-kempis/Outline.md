# Outline: Thomas à Kempis

Files are in `cic/texts/`; short names and full lines are in `Source_Sheet.md`.

- **Length:** about 2 minutes 30 seconds (about 375 words). Reason to stretch: the record of his life is modest, but the book credited to him is one of the most widely read in the Western church, and its authorship is disputed. The dispute must be told plainly, and the reach crosses Catholic, Methodist and Quaker lines. Do not fill past this.

- **Setting:**
  - Century: the fifteenth. He came to the house in 1399 (Chronicle 1103–1106) and died in 1471 (Chronicle 3413–3417). Born about 1380 (derived from Chronicle 2381 and 3415; Benham 490–491).
  - Place: born in Kempen, "in the diocese of Cologne" (Chronicle 1192). Schooled at Deventer (Founders 8990–8995). Lived at Mount St Agnes, a small house of canons near Zwolle (Chronicle 1103–1106), in the Low Countries, today the Netherlands. Spoken: "Kempen, near Cologne"; "Deventer"; "Mount St Agnes, near Zwolle, in what is now the Netherlands".
  - Tradition: the New Devotion, the movement of Geert Grote and Florens Radewijns (Founders 104–107; census shortName "Devotio Moderna", informalName "The Brethren of the Common Life"). Spoken: "the New Devotion", "the Brothers of the Common Life". He was schooled by the Brothers but became a canon regular: a priest living in community under the Rule of St Augustine, in the Windesheim order (Chronicle 1045–1048, 1189–1195).

- **Thread:** He wrote down how a small community lived and taught its novices to live it, and the little book credited to him carried that inward way of following Christ to readers across the divided churches.

- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker.

- **Openings:**
  1. A scholar from Deventer walks to Zwolle in 1399 for the pope's pardons, goes on to a half-built house on a sandy hill, and asks to stay. He tells it himself (Chronicle 1103–1106; the building and the levelling of the sandy hill, Chronicle 1052–1055, 1068–1077).
  2. A line from the book's first chapter, set against arguing about the Trinity without humility: "I would rather feel sorrow for sin than know how to define it" (Pohl *Opera* II 699–706; Benham 1141–1148).

- **Story beats:**
  1. **Deventer, as a youth.** On the advice of his brother John, a canon at Windesheim, he went to Florens Radewijns, the priest who led the Brothers at Deventer. Florens put him in the school, gave him books and found him free lodging. Thomas laid Florens's table and brought poor scholars to his house to eat. He watched men who shared all they had and spent their days copying books: "Never before could I recollect to have seen such men." Source: Founders 8990–9016, 9031–9033, 6506–6510, 6752–6754. Documented (his own words, written long after).
  2. **1399, Mount St Agnes.** He came to Zwolle "to gain indulgences": pardons the pope had granted to the penitent at St Michael's church. Then he went on, "glad at heart", to Mount St Agnes and asked to be allowed to stay. His brother John had just become its first prior. The house was poor; the prior himself carried the hod of mortar, and brothers wrote books for sale. Source: Chronicle 1047–1048, 1089–1096, 1100–1106. Documented. A brother's later notice says Florens sent him, at twenty (Chronicle 3419–3421).
  3. **1406, clothed as a canon.** On the feast of Corpus Christi, 1406, he received the habit, after some six years of waiting and testing. "Invested" means clothed as a member of the house. Source: Chronicle 1189–1195, 3421–3424. Documented.
  4. **1429, exile.** A disputed election to the bishopric of Utrecht brought a papal interdict, a ban on church services. The canons kept it, stopped singing, were insulted, and chose exile over breaking it. Twenty-four of the household sailed to a sister house in Frisia and stayed three years. Thomas went as sub-prior, the prior's deputy. Thomas was later sent to nurse his brother John at Bethany, near Arnhem, and stayed fourteen months, until he closed his eyes in November 1432. Source: Chronicle 1819–1829, 1892–1896, 1908–1915, 1923–1924, 2010–2026. Documented.
  5. **Teacher of the young.** He gathered into one book the talks made "for our novices", the men in their trial year, and called them plain speech "wishing to please simple minds". At sixty-seven he was again elected sub-prior, protesting he was not fit. He wrote the house's Chronicle and the Lives of its founders as "a memorial for them that come after". Source: Pohl VI 252–261 (Scully 1011–1020); Chronicle 2379–2386; Chronicle 292–300; Founders 2063–2069. Documented. That the sub-prior trained the novices is Scully's claim (Scully 241–251): Dominant Modern Reconstruction.
  6. **The book.** Johannes Busch, a canon of Windesheim writing in Thomas's lifetime, names him as the author of the devout little treatise that begins "He who follows me", on the imitation of Christ. Thomas's own Chronicle tells the same night's dream but leaves the dreamer unnamed. Early copies and printings gave the book to John Gerson of Paris; later scholars split. Source: Busch 5410–5416; Chronicle 1728–1734; Ingram 1176–1186; Exercises 3101–3105; Neale 4716–4736; Pohl I 18010–18013. Contested.
  7. **1471, death.** He died after Compline, the last prayer of the day, in his ninety-second year. The brother who carried on his Chronicle wrote that he copied the Bible the house used, and wrote little books for the young, plain in style but mighty in matter. Source: Chronicle 3409–3437. Documented.

- **What came of them** (heaviest first):
  1. **Ignatius of Loyola.** Ignatius told Luis Gonçalves da Câmara, who wrote it down in 1555, that at Manresa he first saw the book he called "the little Gerson". After it he wanted no other book of devotion; he read a chapter a day and commended it to everyone he dealt with (Scripta 9403–9408). His Spiritual Exercises tell those making them, from the second week on, to read "the Imitation of Christ, or the Gospels, or the lives of saints" (Exercises 24995–25000). Documented. He read it under Gerson's name (Scripta 9431).
  2. **John Wesley and the Methodists.** At about twenty-two, reading "Kempis's Christian Pattern", Wesley "began to see that true religion was seated in the heart". He was "very angry at Kempis for being too strict", yet found comfort in him (Wesley 960–968). "Christian Pattern" is the name Wesley gives the Imitation in the English he read, "Dean Stanhope's translation" (Wesley 962, 965–966). In 1746 the Methodist Conference set "Kempis" among the books its preachers should read between four and five in the morning (Methodist minutes 2643–2653). Documented.
  3. **Across the divide.** The Lutheran Pietist Spener set the Imitation beside Tauler and the Theologia Germanica (Spener 1706, 5855–5857). The Quaker John Woolman, told that Thomas "lived & died in the profession of the Roman Catholick Religion", judged from his writings that he was "a man of a true Christian spirit" (Woolman 12189–12192). Documented.
  4. **The first English**, made for Margaret, mother of King Henry VII, and printed in 1504, again under Gerson's name (Ingram 168–178, 1176–1182). Documented.
  5. **The inside record of the New Devotion.** His Chronicle and Lives remain the movement's own account of its houses and founders; another brother carried the Chronicle on after him (Chronicle 3409–3410). Documented.
  - **What he defended:** obedience to the pope's interdict, at the price of three years' exile (Chronicle 1892–1896).

- **Quotes** (rendered by the researcher):
  1. "I would rather feel sorrow for sin than know how to define it."
     - Rendered from the Latin, Pohl's *Opera* II (`kempis_opera-omnia-sermones-de-compunctione-lat_pohl1902.txt`) 704–706: "Opto magis sentire compunctionem : quam scire eius definitionem." From the Imitation, book I, chapter 1. "Compunction" is avoided because its modern sense, a mild twinge of guilt, misleads; the Latin means the heart pierced with sorrow for sin (Benham 1148 has "contrition"; the Old English version, Ingram 1470–1471, keeps "compunccion").
     - Job: shows the book's one idea, lived faith over learned argument, in its own words. It must be framed as the book credited to him.
  2. "Thinking of the Lord's suffering filled his heart with love. He was a wonderful comfort to people who were troubled or tempted."
     - Rendered from Arthur's English, Chronicle 3432–3433 (the Latin is not held): "The thought of the Lord's passion filled his heart with love, and he was wondrous comfortable to the troubled and the tempted".
     - Job: the teacher as his own house saw him, in a brother's voice, at his death.
  3. "At Manresa he had first seen the little Gerson. After that he never wanted to read any other book of devotion. He commended it to everyone he dealt with. He read one chapter a day, in order."
     - Rendered from the Spanish of Câmara's Memoriale, from what Ignatius told him, Scripta 9403–9406: "que en Manrresa auia visto primero el Gerçonzito, y nunca más auia querido leer otro libro de deuoción; y este encomendaua á todos los que trataua, y leya cada dia vn capitulo por orden".
     - Job: carries the connection, and shows in Ignatius's own naming that the book travelled under another man's name.

- **Connection to carry into the story:** The book credited to Thomas reached Ignatius of Loyola at Manresa, where, as he told Câmara, he read a chapter a day (Scripta 9403–9406), and Ignatius wrote it into his Spiritual Exercises as reading for those making them (Exercises 24995–25000). The listener needs three things made plain: the Spiritual Exercises are the retreat method written by Ignatius, who founded the Jesuits; the book Ignatius loved was the Imitation, which he knew as "the little Gerson", because copies then gave it to John Gerson, a Paris theologian; and a canon of Thomas's own order, writing in Thomas's lifetime, named Thomas as its author (Busch 5413–5415); early copies and printings gave it to Gerson, and scholars still disagree (Ingram 1176–1186; Neale 4716–4722). The same book later turned the young John Wesley toward "religion seated in the heart" (Wesley 962–963), so the teacher of a few novices near Zwolle was read on both sides of the Reformation split. Let this come through what Ignatius and Wesley did with the book.

- **Links:**
  - Florens Radewijns: heard him teach; lodged and schooled by him; wrote his life (Founders 8990–9016).
  - Geert Grote: wrote his life (Founders 2063–2072).
  - John of Kempen, his brother: gave him his investiture; closed his eyes (Chronicle 1189–1193, 3421–3422, 2024–2026).
  - Johannes Busch: named him as author of the Imitation (Busch 5413–5415).
  - Ignatius of Loyola: read the book credited to him, and wrote it into the Spiritual Exercises (Scripta 9403–9408; Exercises 24995–25000).
  - John Wesley: read his book; Wesley's Conference set it for preachers (Wesley 960–968; Methodist minutes 2653).
  - John Woolman: read his book (Woolman 12189–12192).
  - Philipp Jakob Spener: commended his book (Spener 1706, 5855–5857).

- **Who tells it:**
  - Thomas himself: the Chronicle and the Lives, written in old age about his youth and his house. Own words, in Arthur's English.
  - An unnamed brother of Mount St Agnes: the death notice, written in 1471 by someone who lived with him.
  - Johannes Busch: a canon of Windesheim, born 1399/1400, writing in Thomas's lifetime; the earliest held witness to his authorship. The listener needs to know Busch was of his own order and his own time.
  - Luis Gonçalves da Câmara: a Jesuit recording what Ignatius told him in 1555.
  - John Wesley: his own words, looking back in 1738 on 1725.
  - Neale (1858), Benham (1886), Ingram (1893), Pohl (1902), Arthur (1905): later editors who disagree on the authorship. Not named in the audio; the card carries them.

- **Do not say:**
  - "Thomas wrote the Imitation of Christ" as fact. Say "the book credited to him", "Busch, a canon of his own order, names him as its author", and say plainly that it is disputed.
  - "Thomas was a Brother of the Common Life." Say he was schooled by the Brothers at Deventer and became a canon of Mount St Agnes.
  - "Ignatius kept Thomas à Kempis's book" without the name problem. Ignatius knew it as "the little Gerson".
  - "The most widely read book after the Bible" in the narrator's voice. That is an editor's judgment (Neale 4716–4720: "probably"). Keep it as a judgment or leave it out.
  - "He spent seventy years copying books" or "he copied the Bible four times". The notice says he copied "that complete copy of the Bible which we use" and many other books.
  - "He was master of novices." Say he gathered sermons for the novices and twice served as sub-prior; the link between the two offices is an editor's.
  - "Thomas spent three years in Frisia." Say the community spent three years in Frisia; Thomas spent the last part of the exile with his dying brother.
  - "Ignatius wrote that he first read it at Manresa." Say Ignatius told Luis Gonçalves da Câmara, who wrote it down in 1555.
  - "Kempis converted Wesley" or "Kempis changed Wesley's life." Wesley tells it as a stage before his conversion, and also credits "a religious friend" (Wesley 968). Say reading it, Wesley began to see that true religion was seated in the heart.
  - That he went to Mount St Agnes "because Florens sent him" as the only account. His own words say he came for the indulgences and asked to stay; the later notice says Florens sent him. Give his own account; if the other is used, credit it to the notice.
  - "Born in 1380." Say "about 1380".
  - Do not give him a motive for the exile beyond what the Chronicle states: obedience to the pope's interdict, "exiles for justice' sake".
  - "Sorrow for sin" must not become "guilt" or "regret".

- **Leave out:**
  - The Gersen-of-Vercelli argument and the Carthusian and Hilton claims in detail (Benham 606–661; Founders 1519–1524; Ingram 313–318): the card carries the debate; the audio says only that it is disputed.
  - His parents' trades (Benham 491–492): no source given.
  - The partridge nickname (Scripta 24346): a later witness, its meaning unexplained.
  - The dates of the Bible copy (1439 or 1459): the sources disagree.
  - The Life of Lydwine of Schiedam: his part in it is uncertain.
  - His other devotional books by title: a list of books is this shape's trap.
