# Outline: Thomas à Kempis

Files are in `cic/texts/`; short names and full lines are in `Source_Sheet.md` (the book's teachings are in its section 3b).

- **The heart of their life:** A little book of four parts, the Imitation of Christ, credited to him, and the life of the house that he wrote down.
  - **What the book teaches,** in its own words (Documented as text; Contested as his):
    1. *Follow Christ by copying his life.* It opens with Jesus's words, "Whoever follows me does not walk in darkness", and says these words urge us to imitate his life and his ways; so our chief study should be to ponder the life of Jesus. To understand Christ's words, a person must shape his whole life to them (Pohl II 677–684, 690–699; Benham 1121–1129). This first chapter, "Of the imitation of Christ", gave the whole book its name (Pohl II 671–673; Benham 1118–1119).
    2. *A changed heart over clever learning.* Arguing about the Trinity profits nothing without humility; "I would rather feel sorrow for sin than know how to define it"; "love to be unknown" (Pohl II 699–706, 784–786; Benham 1141–1148, 1257–1260).
    3. *Turn inward, and take up the cross.* "The kingdom of God is within you": scorn outward things, give yourself to inward ones, and the kingdom will come within you (Pohl II 2822–2833; Benham 3540–3553). Its "royal way of the holy cross" says there is no other way to life and inner peace than the cross and dying to self each day: denying oneself, and willingly bearing the pain of body or trouble of spirit that comes (Pohl II 3717–3738, 3758–3760, 3764–3769; Benham 4597, 4604–4608, 4622–4624, 4652–4654, 4664–4670).
  - **Its shape:** four little books: counsels for the spiritual life; counsels on the inner life; a conversation in which Christ speaks to the soul ("my son") and the soul answers ("Speak, Lord, your servant is listening"); and a book on Holy Communion, in the usual printed order; the 1441 copy puts the Communion book third (Benham 165–166, 222–223, 255–258, 425–426, 5044, 7825; Pohl II 576–579, 6195–6199, 6247, 7458; Ingram 173–175). Documented.
  - **His documented work:** a canon of one small house, Mount St Agnes near Zwolle, from 1399 to 1471, except the exile years. He copied the house's Bible and many books for use and sale; wrote little books for the young, "plain and simple in style, but mighty in the matter"; gathered his talks for the novices; and wrote the house's Chronicle and the Lives of the New Devotion's founders (Founders 2063–2072) "as a memorial for them that come after" (Chronicle 3426–3430, 292–300; Founders 2063–2069; Pohl VI 252–261). Documented.
  - **Census claims checked:** "credited with the most copied Christian book after the Bible" (census teaser) is not held. The held form is an editor's judgment: next to Scripture it "has probably been read more widely than any other in the Western Church" (Neale 4716–4720). Keep it as Neale's, or leave it out. "Ignatius kept it on his desk" (census legacy) rests on Oliver Manare, a later Jesuit witness (Scripta 24338–24347): Widely Accepted, not Documented. "Copied books in one small house all his life": true but for three years' exile (Chronicle 1908–1915).

- **Length:** about 2 minutes 30 seconds to 2 minutes 40 (about 375–400 words, at Neo's 150 a minute). Reason to stretch past 2: a major figure; the book needs room for three teachings in its own words, and its reach crosses Catholic, Methodist and Quaker lines. The authorship doubt is told briefly. Do not fill past this.

- **Setting:**
  - Century: the fifteenth. He came to the house in 1399 (Chronicle 1103–1106) and died in 1471 (Chronicle 3413–3417). Born about 1380 (derived from Chronicle 2381 and 3415; Benham 490–491).
  - Place: born in Kempen, "in the diocese of Cologne" (Chronicle 1192). Schooled at Deventer (Founders 8990–8995). Lived at Mount St Agnes, a small house of canons near Zwolle (Chronicle 1103–1106), in the Low Countries. Spoken: "Kempen, near Cologne"; "Deventer"; "Mount St Agnes, near Zwolle, in what is now the Netherlands".
  - Tradition: the New Devotion, the movement of Geert Grote and Florens Radewijns (Founders 104–107; census shortName "Devotio Moderna", informalName "The Brethren of the Common Life"). Spoken: "the New Devotion", "the Brothers of the Common Life". He was schooled by the Brothers but became a canon regular: one who lives in community under the Rule of St Augustine, in the Windesheim order (Chronicle 1045–1048, 1189–1195).

- **Shape:** Teacher or thinker.

- **Openings:**
  1. The book's first words, which give it its name: "Whoever follows me does not walk in darkness" (Pohl II 677–678). Lead at once into what it asks: to imitate Christ's life. Recommended: it is plain heard once, and it is the heart.
  2. Ignatius of Loyola at Manresa, reading one chapter a day of a little book, and opening it at other hours to find what his heart needed then, without knowing it as Thomas's (Scripta 9403–9408). Use only if the next sentence says what the book teaches.

- **Story beats.** The first minute (about 150 words) carries both halves of the heart: beats 1 and 2, in about 75 words each. By word 150 the listener must have heard the book's call, Thomas named with his own certain work, and that the credit is disputed. Beats 3 to 6 deepen it.
  1. **What the book asks** (about 75 words). It opens with Jesus's words, "Whoever follows me does not walk in darkness." It says these words call us to imitate his life and his ways, so our chief study is to ponder the life of Jesus. It sets a changed heart above clever argument: better to feel sorrow for sin than to know its definition. Its second part turns inward: scorn outward things, and the kingdom of God will come within you. Source: Pohl II 671–684, 699–706, 2822–2833. Documented.
  2. **Whose book, and his own work** (about 75 words). It is credited to Thomas, a canon who spent almost all his life in one small house, Mount St Agnes near Zwolle. There he copied books, among them the house's Bible, and wrote down the history of his house and of his movement, the New Devotion: the house's Chronicle and the Lives of the movement's founders (Chronicle 3426–3430, 292–300; Founders 2063–2072). "Almost": the exile years were spent away (Chronicle 1908–1915). Johannes Busch, a canon of his own order writing in Thomas's lifetime, names him as its author; early copies gave it to John Gerson, a Paris theologian, and scholars still disagree. Keep the Busch and Gerson part to one or two short sentences. Source: Busch 5410–5416; Ingram 1176–1182; Neale 4716–4722; Ingram 1183–1186. Contested (the credit); Documented (his work).
  3. **The cross, and the book's other parts.** It names the cross the "royal way": no other road to life and inner peace than the cross and dying to self each day. "Dying to self" here means denying oneself, and willingly bearing the pain of body or trouble of spirit that comes, rather than escaping it (Pohl II 3720–3722, 3764–3769; Benham 4604–4608, 4664–4670). Its later parts let Christ speak to the soul and turn to Holy Communion, in the usual printed order (Benham 255–258, 425–426); the 1441 copy puts the Communion book third. Source: Pohl II 3717–3738, 3758–3760, 6195–6199. Documented. The later-parts sentence yields first if the length runs over.
  4. **Where he learned that life.** Born about 1380 in Kempen, near Cologne, he went to school at Deventer. Florens Radewijns, who led the Brothers of the Common Life there, kept him for a while in his house, placed him in the school, gave him books and found him free lodging. He lived among the Brothers and saw their lives daily: men who shared all they had and spent their days copying books, in sacred study and devout meditation: "Never before could I recollect to have seen such men." In 1399 he came to Mount St Agnes and asked to stay; in 1406 he was clothed as a canon. Source: Founders 8990–9017, 9031–9033, 9052–9055; Chronicle 1103–1106, 1189–1195. Documented.
  5. **The house's teacher, and the exile.** He gathered his talks for the novices, the men in their trial year, in plain speech "wishing to please simple minds" (Pohl VI 252–261; Scully 1011–1020). When a dispute over the bishop of Utrecht brought a pope's ban on church services, the canons kept it and stopped their singing; in 1429 they were insulted and driven out, and the community spent three years in Frisia; he went as sub-prior, the prior's deputy. Source: Chronicle 1819–1829, 1892–1896, 1908–1915, 1923–1924. Documented. One or two sentences; the novices clause yields if the length runs over.
  6. **1471, death.** He died after Compline, the last prayer of the day, in his ninety-second year. The brother who carried on his Chronicle wrote that he wrote little books for the young, plain in style but mighty in matter. Source: Chronicle 3409–3430. Documented.
  - **What yields to keep about 375–400 words:** first the later-parts sentence in beat 3; then the novices clause in beat 5; then Spener (What came of them, item 3), the early English version (item 4) and the inside-record item (item 5, already told in beat 2). Never cut the Busch/Gerson doubt, the exile sentence (what he defended), Ignatius, Wesley or Woolman.

- **What came of them** (heaviest first; about a third of the story):
  1. **Ignatius of Loyola.** Ignatius told Luis Gonçalves da Câmara, who wrote it down in 1555, that at Manresa he first saw the book he called "the little Gerson". After it he wanted no other book of devotion; he commended it to everyone he dealt with, and read one chapter a day, in order. Opening it at other hours, he always came on what his heart needed then (Scripta 9403–9408; paraphrase from the researcher's rendering: "At Manresa he had first seen the little Gerson. After that he never wanted to read any other book of devotion. He commended it to everyone he dealt with. He read one chapter a day, in order."). His Spiritual Exercises advise those making them, from the second week on, that it helps greatly to read at times in the Imitation of Christ, the Gospels or the lives of saints (Exercises 24995–25000). Documented. He knew it under Gerson's name (Scripta 9431).
  2. **John Wesley and the Methodists.** At about twenty-two, reading "Kempis's Christian Pattern", Wesley "began to see that true religion was seated in the heart, and that God's law extended to all our thoughts as well as words and actions". He was "very angry at Kempis for being too strict", yet found comfort in him (Wesley 960–968). "Christian Pattern" is the English title Wesley read it under, "Dean Stanhope's translation" (Wesley 962, 965–966). In 1746 the Methodist Conference advised its preachers to read "Kempis", among other books, between four and five in the morning (Methodist minutes 2643–2653). Documented.
  3. **Across the divide.** The Quaker John Woolman, told that Thomas "lived & died in the profession of the Roman Catholick Religion", judged from his writings that he was "a man of a true Christian spirit", who worked to promote "the Inward Spiritual Religion" (Woolman 12189–12192, 12219–12223). The Lutheran Pietist Spener set the Imitation beside Tauler and the Theologia Germanica (Spener 5855–5857). Documented.
  4. **An early English version,** made by William Atkynson for Margaret, mother of King Henry VII, and printed by Wynkyn de Worde in 1504, again under Gerson's name (Ingram 168–178, 1176–1182). Documented.
  5. **The inside record of the New Devotion.** His Chronicle and Lives remain the movement's own account of its houses and founders; another brother carried the Chronicle on after him (Chronicle 3409–3410). Documented.
  - **What he defended:** obedience to the pope's ban, at the price of three years' exile for his community (Chronicle 1892–1896).

- **Quotes** (rendered by the researcher, from Pohl's Latin *Opera* II, `kempis_opera-omnia-sermones-de-compunctione-lat_pohl1902.txt`):
  1. "'Whoever follows me does not walk in darkness,' says the Lord. These are the words of Christ. They urge us to imitate his life and his ways, if we want to be truly given light and freed from all blindness of heart. So let our chief study be to ponder the life of Jesus Christ."
     - Latin, Pohl II 677–684: "Vi sequitar [Qui sequitur] me non ambulat in tenebris: dicit Dominus. Haec sunt verba Christi quibus admonemur, quatenus vitam eius et mores imitemur: si velimus veraciter illuminari, et ab omni caecitate cordis liberari. Summum igitur studium nostrum sit: in vita Iesu Christi meditari." "Ponder" for *meditari*, because "meditate" now suggests a technique of emptying the mind; "given light" for *illuminari*, because "enlightened" suggests the Enlightenment or Buddhism. The writer may use the first sentence alone, or the first and last.
     - Job: opens the story, explains the title, and states the book's central call in its own words.
  2. "I would rather feel sorrow for sin than know how to define it."
     - Latin, Pohl II 704–706: "Opto magis sentire compunctionem : quam scire eius definitionem." Book I, chapter 1. "Compunction" is avoided: its modern sense, a mild twinge of guilt, misleads; the Latin means the heart pierced with sorrow for sin (Benham 1148 has "contrition").
     - Job: shows the book's preference for a changed heart over learned argument; the line Wesley's "seated in the heart" later echoes.
  3. "Learn to scorn outward things and to give yourself to inward things. And you will see the kingdom of God come within you."
     - Latin, Pohl II 2831–2833: "Disce exteriora contemnere et ad interiora te dare : et videbis regnum Dei in te venire." Book II, chapter 1. "Scorn" for *contemnere*, as the first chapter's title names "contempt of the world" (Pohl II 671–673); "despise" is avoided, whose modern sense is hatred. The chapter opens "'The kingdom of God is within you,' says the Lord" (Pohl II 2827–2828), which the narrator may give before the quote.
     - Job: the inward turn, which Woolman named "the Inward Spiritual Religion" and Wesley "religion seated in the heart"; it lets the reach in "What came of them" land as the same teaching carried on.
  - Further rendering for the narrator to paraphrase, not to quote (the cross teaching, Pohl II 3735–3738, 3758–3760): "Why then are you afraid to take up the cross, which is the way to the kingdom? In the cross is salvation, in the cross is life." "There is no other way to life and to true inner peace than the way of the holy cross, and of dying to self each day." "Dying to self" for *cotidianae mortificationis*, because "mortification" now suggests embarrassment.
  - Dropped from the last outline: the Chronicle brother's line on the Lord's passion (about Thomas, not the book; unused) and the Câmara quote as a quote (its rendering stays above, for paraphrase).

- **Connection to carry into the story:** The book's call to follow Christ inwardly reached readers on both sides of the Reformation split. Ignatius of Loyola, founder of the Jesuits, read a chapter a day at Manresa and found in it what his heart needed (Scripta 9403–9408), and in his Spiritual Exercises, the retreat he wrote, advised those making them, from the second week on, that it helps greatly to read at times in the Imitation of Christ, the Gospels or the lives of saints (Exercises 24995–25000). Two centuries later the young John Wesley, reading it in English, "began to see that true religion was seated in the heart" (Wesley 962–963), and his Methodist Conference advised its preachers to read it between four and five in the morning (Methodist minutes 2643–2653). The Quaker John Woolman, though told Thomas was a Roman Catholic, judged him a man of a true Christian spirit (Woolman 12189–12192). The listener needs two things made plain: these readers took up the same inward teaching the book itself gives (beat 1 and quote 3), so tell the teaching before the readers; and Ignatius knew the book as "the little Gerson", because copies then gave it to John Gerson (Scripta 9431). Let it come through what they did with the book.

- **Do not say:**
  - "Thomas wrote the Imitation of Christ" as fact. Say "the book credited to him", "Busch, a canon of his own order, names him as its author", and say plainly that it is disputed.
  - "He wrote the book from what he saw at Deventer" or "the book distils the New Devotion". No held source says so. Tell the Brothers' life and the book's teaching side by side; draw no cause between them.
  - "The book teaches us to despise the world" without the inward turn beside it. Say it asks the reader to scorn outward things and turn to inward ones.
  - "Dying to self" must not become "punishing the body" as a practice the book prescribes, and must not lose its suffering either. Say: denying oneself, and willingly bearing the suffering that comes, each day.
  - "The first English translation" for the 1504 printing. An older English version by an unknown author survives in manuscript (Ingram 167–169, 182). Say "an early English version".
  - "Thomas was a Brother of the Common Life." Say he was schooled by the Brothers at Deventer and became a canon of Mount St Agnes.
  - "Ignatius kept Thomas à Kempis's book" without the name problem. Ignatius knew it as "the little Gerson".
  - "Ignatius kept it on his desk" in the narrator's voice. That is Manare's later report; leave it out.
  - "The most widely read book after the Bible" or "the most copied", in the narrator's voice. That is an editor's judgment (Neale 4716–4720: "probably"). Keep it as a judgment or leave it out.
  - "He spent seventy years copying books" or "he copied the Bible four times". The notice says he copied "that complete copy of the Bible which we use" and many other books.
  - "He was master of novices." Say he gathered his talks for the novices and twice served as sub-prior; the link between the two offices is an editor's.
  - "Thomas spent three years in Frisia." Say the community spent three years in Frisia; Thomas spent the last part of the exile with his dying brother.
  - "Ignatius wrote that he first read it at Manresa." Say Ignatius told Luis Gonçalves da Câmara, who wrote it down in 1555.
  - "Kempis converted Wesley" or "Kempis changed Wesley's life." Wesley tells it as a stage before his conversion, and also credits "a religious friend" (Wesley 968). Say reading it, Wesley began to see that true religion was seated in the heart.
  - That he went to Mount St Agnes "because Florens sent him" as the only account. His own words say he came for the indulgences and asked to stay; the later notice says Florens sent him. Give his own account; if the other is used, credit it to the notice.
  - "Born in 1380." Say "about 1380".
  - Do not give him a motive for the exile beyond what the Chronicle states: obedience to the pope's ban, "exiles for justice' sake".
  - "Sorrow for sin" must not become "guilt" or "regret".
  - "Do not tell the 1429 exile as a free choice alone. Say the canons stopped singing, were insulted and were driven out for keeping the ban, and that the community stayed three years in Frisia." (added from Review.md, 2026-11-18)
  - "Do not gloss 'canon' as 'a priest' at his 1406 clothing. He was invested as a clerk and was a priest only later (Chronicle 1189–1192, 3417). Say 'a canon, living in community under the Rule of St Augustine'." (added from Review.md, 2026-11-18)

  - "Do not say the Brothers of the Common Life taught him at school, or ran the school at Deventer. Florens placed him in the school, gave him books and found him lodging; he lived among the Brothers and saw their lives daily (Founders 9005–9017)." (added from Review_2.md, 2026-11-18)
  - "Told" or "set" for what the Methodist Conference did. Say it "advised its preachers to read it, among other books, between four and five in the morning" (Methodist minutes 2647–2653). The same for the Exercises: they advise that it helps greatly (Exercises 24997–25000). (added from Review_2.md, 2026-11-18)
  - "The lives of its founders" after the house. The Lives are of the New Devotion's founders, Geert Grote, Florens Radewijns and their followers (Founders 2063–2072). Say "the Lives of the New Devotion's founders". (added from Review_2.md, 2026-11-18)
  - "In 1429, when the canons stopped their services to keep a pope's ban". The singing stopped under the ban from the Utrecht dispute; 1429 is when they were driven out. Say: "When a dispute over the bishop of Utrecht brought a pope's ban on church services, the canons kept it. In 1429 they were insulted and driven out, and the community spent three years in Frisia." (Chronicle 1819–1829, 1892–1896) (added from Review_2.md, 2026-11-18)
